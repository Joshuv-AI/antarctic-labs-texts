Robotic versus laparoscopic liver resection for the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma: an observational study
Authors: Chenyue Yang, Junhao Zheng, Yewei Zhang, Meijie Chen, Zefeng Shen, Liye Tao, Shihao Li, Haoyu Pan, Yangyang Xie, Yang Liu, Chao Song, Haijing Guan, Xiao Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompared to laparoscopic liver resection (LLR), robotic liver resection (RLR) can offer patients better outcomes but higher costs. And currently, real-world observational evidence from China regarding this topic remains understudied. This study aimed to compare the cost difference between RLR and LLR and its association with surgical difficulty, thereby providing new insights for surgical decision-making.MethodsWe retrospectively collected data from patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) who underwent liver resection by a single medical team at one hospital in China, from January 2016 to July 2023. Patients were divided into the RLR group and the LLR group. We compared perioperative outcomes and medical costs before and after propensity score matching (PSM), then conducted subgroup analyses with surgical difficulty as a covariate.ResultsA total of 277 patients were included (175 in LLR group and 102 in RLR group). After PSM, 162 patients (81 in each group) were included for further analysis. The results showed that RLR group had less intraoperative bleeding, fewer postoperative complications, shorter postoperative hospital stay, and better surgical safety compared to LLR group. RLR group had higher medical costs (¥82,885.3 vs. ¥58,643.8, P<0.001); however, the non-surgical costs of RLR group were significantly lower than LLR group. Additionally, there was no significant difference in costs between the two surgical methods in high-difficulty liver resections.ConclusionsFor patients with HCC, RLR had better surgical safety and higher medical costs compared to LLR. However, the non-surgical costs of RLR were lower. Furthermore, the increase in surgical difficulty reduced the cost difference between the two surgical methods, recommending RLR for patients with high surgical difficulty.

Highlight box

Key findings

• For hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients, although the total cost of robotic liver resection (RLR) is higher than that of laparoscopic liver resection (LLR), the non-surgical costs of RLR are significantly lower than those of LLR. Additionally, as the difficulty of the surgery increases, the cost difference between RLR and LLR decreases.

What is known and what is new?

• Compared to LLR, RLR can offer patients better outcomes but higher costs. And currently, real-world observational evidence from China regarding this topic remains understudied.

• As the cost of the surgery itself decreases, RLR is expected to become a preferable alternative to LLR, especially in patients with high surgical difficulty.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Considering the superiority of robotic surgery and the fact that surgical costs are the primary reason for RLR being more expensive than LLR, reducing surgical costs in the future is critically important. Additionally, we observed that for patients requiring high-difficulty hepatectomy, there was no significant cost difference between choosing RLR or LLR. This finding provides economic support for surgeons to select the surgical approach (RLR vs. LLR) based on the technical complexity of the hepatectomy.

Introduction

Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a malignant tumor originating from hepatocytes. As the sixth most common malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, HCC poses a significant threat to human health (1). Liver resection is considered the most effective treatment for HCC, and the surgical techniques have continuously evolved over the past few decades.

Rationale and knowledge gap

In 1991, Reich and colleagues performed the first laparoscopic liver resection (LLR) to remove benign liver lesions (2). In the subsequent decades, LLR developed rapidly and gained widespread acceptance worldwide. The 2008 Louisville Statement highlighted the feasibility of LLR, and the 2018 Southampton European Guidelines Meeting on Laparoscopic Liver Surgery provided surgeons with numerous findings and detailed protocols, promoting the development of safe and feasible strategies for LLR (3,4). Compared to open surgery, LLR is associated with reduced postoperative complications, shorter hospital stays, and comparable oncological outcomes (5,6). Currently, LLR has become a standard procedure in many centers. However, laparoscopic surgery still has some inherent drawbacks, including unstable video views, the rigidity of laparoscopic instruments limiting access to the liver surface, and poor ergonomic design forcing surgeons into uncomfortable positions during long surgeries (7).

In 2003, Giulianotti et al. first reported robotic liver resection (RLR) (8). Compared to LLR, robotic arms have seven degrees of freedom, providing greater flexibility and precision for surgeons. Additionally, robots can magnify and visualize the surgical area in three dimensions, facilitating precise tissue dissection and intraoperative suturing. Robotic systems enhance hepatobiliary surgery by enabling complex resections, anastomoses, and reconstruction while mastering critical anatomies (porta hepatis/retrocaval), ultimately shortening learning curves, reducing trauma, accelerating recovery, and decreasing complications (9,10). A meta-analysis published in 2022, which included 36 clinical studies, indicated that RLR is safe and feasible in liver surgery, particularly evident in patients’ short-term outcomes. Therefore, RLR holds great promise in future liver surgeries (11).

However, while RLR offers better perioperative outcomes for HCC patients, it also incurs higher costs. A recent meta-analysis, pooling data from four relevant studies, revealed that the cost of RLR ($20,205.92) is significantly higher than that of LLR ($15,789.75). Cost is thus a crucial limiting factor for the adoption of RLR (12). However, with the advancement of modern medicine, surgery is not only aimed at curing but also at improving quality of life. In 2020, Mejia et al. reported on 214 liver resection patients, indicating that compared to LLR, RLR, despite its higher cost, resulted in shorter hospital stays and was a better choice for patients undergoing small-scale liver resection (13). However, in 2016, Chinese scholars, based on data from 39 patients undergoing robotic and laparoscopic left lateral liver resection, indicated that for left lateral liver resection, RLR was more expensive than LLR, but there was no statistically significant difference in efficacy and safety (14). Currently, there is limited reporting on health economic studies related to RLR both domestically and internationally. Therefore, whether RLR can enhance quality of life while delivering sufficient clinical and economic value remains an ongoing investigation.

International Experts Consensus Guidelines on Robotic Liver Resection in 2023 pointed out that compared to LLR, RLR has unique therapeutic value in liver-related diseases, and its cost-effectiveness merits further investigation in the future (15). In disciplines such as urology and colorectal surgery, studies have suggested that robotic surgery is cost-effective or have identified areas where cost reductions would facilitate increased utilization of robotic technology (16,17).

Objective

Therefore, considering the dual aspects of better clinical benefits and higher treatment costs associated with RLR, we aim to explore whether RLR is better choice compared to LLR, providing a new perspective for the choice of surgical approach. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://hbsn.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/hbsn-2025-231/rc).

Methods

Study populations, inclusion and exclusion criteria

Retrospective data collection of hospitalized patients diagnosed with HCC from January 2016 to July 2023 within the single medical group of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University. All included patients underwent hepatectomy for HCC, with detailed surgical procedures along with the instruments used for both approaches provided in the Table S1. Additionally, detailed descriptions of minimally invasive techniques (Appendix 1) and the selection framework (Figure S1) are provided in the supplementary materials. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. KY2022-079-B). As this was a retrospective study utilizing anonymized clinical data, a waiver of informed consent was granted.

Data collection

Retrospective collection and analysis of baseline characteristics, intraoperative and postoperative clinical indicators, and costs of hospitalized patients diagnosed with HCC within the single medical group of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University, from January 2016 to July 2023. Patients were divided into LLR group and RLR group according to their surgical methods.

Baseline characteristics: (I) age; (II) gender; (III) body mass index (BMI); (IV) type of medical insurance; (V) place of residence; (VI) history of laparotomy; (VII) history of liver resection; (VIII) history of neoadjuvant therapy; (IX) number of tumors; (X) tumor size; (XI) platelet count (PLT); (XII) serum albumin (ALB); (XIII) total bilirubin (TBIL); (XIV) serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST); (XV) serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT); (XVI) alpha-fetoprotein (AFP); (XVII) plasma prothrombin time (PT); (XVIII) international normalized ratio (INR); (XIX) liver cirrhosis; (XX) Child-Pugh classification; (XXI) portal hypertension; (XXII) vascular infiltration status; (XXIII) IWATE surgical difficulty grading; (XXIV) ASA classification.

Clinical outcome indicators: (I) operation time; (II) intraoperative blood transfusion; (III) intraoperative blood loss; (IV) conversion to open surgery during operation; (V) status of surgical margins; (VI) postoperative complications; (VII) Clavien-Dindo classification of complications; (VIII) postoperative hospital stay; (IX) total hospital stay/length of stay (LOS); (X) readmission within 30 days postoperatively due to complications; (XI) reoperation during hospitalization; (XII) perioperative mortality.

Cost outcome indicators: (I) hospitalization costs; (II) out-of-pocket costs; (III) drug costs; (IV) surgical costs; (V) examination costs; (VI) nursing costs; (VII) consumables costs; (VIII) other costs. All costs underwent unified costing via China’s Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) system, ensuring standardized auditable accounting.

Propensity scorematch

Propensity score matching (PSM) is a research method aimed at eliminating bias and balancing variables. In recent years, it has been widely used in the scientific research field (18). In real-world studies, PSM is often used to balance baseline characteristics between study groups. By assigning values to each baseline indicator, individuals with similar scores are matched between groups, thereby reducing the impact of confounding factors on study results. In this study, PSM was employed to match baseline characteristics between the LLR and RLR groups to eliminate the influence of baseline indicator differences on the study results. In IBM SPSS Statistics 26 software, variables including age, BMI, AFP, INR, ALB, AST, TBIL, Child-Pugh classification, vascular invasion status, and surgical difficulty were included as covariates in the model. The LLR and RLR groups were matched in a 1:1 ratio with a caliper value set at 0.04.

Subgroup analysis

In 2014, the second international consensus conference on LLR established the IWATE difficulty scoring system for liver resection. This system quantifies the difficulty of liver resection surgeries and is currently widely used (19). In this study, patients were categorized into four subgroups (“Low”, “Moderate”, “Advanced” and “Expert”) based on the IWATE surgical difficulty grading system. Within each subgroup, the differences in perioperative outcomes and medical costs between the LLR and RLR groups were compared.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables following a normal distribution are described as mean ± standard deviation, while those following a skewed distribution are presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are described as frequencies and percentages. One-way analysis of variance is used to determine if continuous variables are normally distributed. The Student’s t-test is used for comparing normally distributed continuous variables, while the Mann-Whitney U test is used for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables are analyzed using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses are performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 software.

Results

Baseline characteristics

After screening based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 277 patients were included in this study, divided into the LLR group (175 cases) and the RLR group (102 cases) according to the surgical approach. Before PSM, significant differences were observed between the LLR and RLR groups in terms of BMI, AFP, PLT, INR, ALB, AST, liver cirrhosis, Child-Pugh classification, portal hypertension, and IWATE surgical difficulty classification (all P<0.05). Other indicators showed no significant differences. Following PSM to balance baseline characteristics, 162 patients (81 in each group) were included for further analysis, with no significant differences observed in baseline characteristics between the LLR and RLR groups (Table 1) (Figure 1) (Figure S2).

Clinical outcomes

Before PSM, the LLR group had significantly higher intraoperative blood loss [100.0 (50.0–400.0) vs. 50.0 (50.0–112.5) mL, P<0.001], intraoperative transfusion rate [33 (18.8%) vs. 10 (9.8%), P=0.045], postoperative complication rate [35 (20.0%) vs. 7 (6.8%), P=0.003], conversion to open surgery rate (20 (11.4%) vs. 0 (0.0%), P=0.001), postoperative hospital stay [6.0 (4.0–7.0) vs. 5.0 (3.8–6.2) days, P=0.001], and LOS [13.0 (10.0–16.0) vs. 9.5 (7.0–13.0) days, P<0.001] compared to the RLR group. Cost outcomes

Before PSM, the LLR group had significantly lower total hospitalization costs [57,150.9 (44,313.0–76,302.3) vs. 81,432.5 (74,644.9–90,934.2) ¥, P<0.001], out-of-pocket costs [16,875.0 (9,911.2–23,013.9) vs. 50,333.4 (46,274.6–57,632.8) ¥, P<0.001], and surgical costs [6,916.0 (6,302.0–7,834.3) vs. 43,424.9 (42,808.6–43,897.9) ¥, P<0.001] than the RLR group. However, the LLR group had significantly higher drug costs [15,879.4 (11,219.3–23,459.2) vs. 9,955.6 (7,687.4–14,007.0) ¥, P<0.001], examination costs [1,260.0 (930.0–2,153.0) vs. 1,160.0 (673.0–1,752.8) ¥, P=0.01], nursing costs [1,164.0 (879.0–1,521.0) vs. 989.6 (784.0–1,291.3) ¥, P=0.004], and consumable costs [21,113.4 (15,486.0–31,411.4) vs. 12,094.4 (10,839.8–18,034.8) ¥, P<0.001]. After PSM to balance baseline characteristics, 162 patients (81 in the LLR group and 81 in the RLR group) were included in the study. The LLR group had significantly lower total hospitalization costs [58,643.8 (45,171.2–75,899.8) vs. 82,885.3 (75,617.3–90,501.2) ¥, P<0.001], out-of-pocket costs [15,972.7 (8,999.7–23,056.8) vs. 50,706.2 (46,796.8–57,640.6) ¥, P<0.001], surgical costs [6,616.0 (6,165.0–7,481.4) vs. 43,424.9 (42,754.1–43,994.5) ¥, P<0.001], and other costs [341.0 (182.0–683.4) vs. 535.0 (276.5–863.0) ¥, P=0.004] than the RLR group. However, the LLR group had significantly higher drug costs [16,517.6 (11,994.0–24,028.5) vs. 9,975.0 (7,861.8–14,117.4) ¥, P<0.001], examination costs [1,365.0 (1,075.0–2,340.0) vs. 1,115.0 (659.0–1,602.0) ¥, P=0.01], nursing costs [1,174.0 (832.5–1,555.0) vs. 988.6 (779.9–1,255.1) ¥, P=0.001], and consumable costs [21,565.4 (15,899.2–32,842.0) vs. 12,069.4 (10,898.8–19,094.2) ¥, P<0.001] than the RLR group (Table 2).

Results of subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis using IWATE surgical difficulty grading as a covariate showed that in the “Low”, “Intermediate”, and “Advanced” subgroups, the total hospitalization costs for the LLR group were significantly lower than those for the RLR group [“Low”: 46,125.7 (38,707.6–58,047.4) vs. 76,647.9 (72,722.6–87,785.8) ¥, P<0.001; “Intermediate”: 52,692.8 (43,604.6–78,370.6) vs. 76,428.8 (72,783.8–82,939.0) ¥, P=0.003; “Advanced”: 67,548.3 (44,759.2–77,887.4) vs. 84,725.0 (78,034.6–90,070.9) ¥, P<0.001]. However, in the “Expert” subgroup, there was no significant difference in total hospitalization costs between the LLR and RLR groups [75,709.0 (64,022.6–101,275.4) vs. 88,292.6 (82,954.8–98,554.0) ¥, P=0.32] (Figure 2). To examine differences in clinical outcomes between different IWATE difficulty strata, we established two distinct subgroups: a low surgical difficulty cohort (unifying ‘Low’ and ‘Intermediate’ ratings, score range 1–6) and a high surgical difficulty group (aggregating ‘Advanced’ and ‘Expert’ ratings, score range 7–12). Critically, the homogeneity within these consolidated groups was statistically confirmed: In the low surgical difficulty group, identical median scores of 5 were observed for both RLR (IQR 3–5.5) and LLR (IQR 3–5, P=0.88); within the high surgical difficulty group, equivalent medians of 9 were recorded for RLR (IQR 8–10) and LLR (IQR 9–10, P=0.90). This distributional alignment across surgical approaches within each consolidated stratum substantiates the methodological appropriateness of our subgroup aggregation.

The results showed that in both surgical difficulty subgroups, the LLR group had significantly higher intraoperative blood loss [low surgical difficulty: 100.0 (50.0–200.0) vs. 50.0 (20.0–150.0) mL, P=0.01; high surgical difficulty: 200.0 (80.0–400.0) vs. 100.0 (50.0–137.5) mL, P=0.02], longer postoperative hospital stay [low surgical difficulty: 5.0 (4.0–7.0) vs. 4.0 (3.0–5.5) days, P=0.01; high surgical difficulty: 6.5 (5.0–9.0) vs. 5.0 (4.0–7.0) days, P=0.046], and longer LOS [low surgical difficulty: 12.0 (9.0–16.0) vs. 10.0 (7.0–12.0) days, P=0.005; high surgical difficulty: 13.5 (10.0–16.0) vs. 9.5 (8.0–12.0) days, P<0.001] compared to the RLR group. There were no significant differences between the LLR and RLR groups in other outcome indicators in either surgical difficulty subgroup (Table 3).

Discussion

In recent years, liver surgery has developed rapidly. Compared with open surgery, LLR surgery offers better safety, similar oncological outcomes, and comparable costs. Over the past decade, it has become the primary minimally invasive surgical option for patients with benign and malignant liver lesions (20). However, LLR for HCC in anatomically challenging lesions requiring complex procedures faces inherent constraints, including visual instability and restricted instrument maneuverability. RLR overcomes these limitations through enhanced articulation and tremor filtration, demonstrating particular advantages in technically demanding major hepatectomies. This technological evolution correlates with improved short-term patient outcomes. Nevertheless, RLR’s elevated costs continues to present adoption barriers. The dual nature of RLR—combining substantial clinical benefits with significant costs—continues to fuel ongoing debate regarding its comparative value versus LLR.

Currently, there are few studies comparing the medical costs of robotic versus LLRs, both domestically and internationally. This study investigates the clinical value and medical costs of using medical robots versus laparoscopes for HCC resection, providing evidence-based medicine to inform surgical treatment strategies for HCC patients. Additionally, this study is the first to explore diverse value of RLR across different surgical difficulties using the IWATE difficulty scoring system for liver resection. This helps physicians choose the most appropriate surgical approach based on the health economics benefits of varying surgical complexities. Given the significant variability in robotic surgery costs across different centers in real-world settings, patients in this study were selected from the same medical institution and treated by the same medical team to minimize intergroup differences. Furthermore, consistent surgical performance by the primary surgeon also reduced the impact of surgical skill on patient outcomes. To eliminate the influence of baseline characteristics on the study results, PSM was used to minimize bias. The PSM results showed no significant differences in baseline indicators between the LLR and RLR groups (all P≥0.05).

To investigate RLR’s integrated value proposition versus LLR, we compared the differences in clinical outcomes and cost indicators between the two groups after PSM. The results showed that the two groups were similar in terms of operation time, surgical margin status, intraoperative blood transfusion, conversion to open surgery, and 30-day readmission due to complications. However, the LLR group had significantly higher intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complication rates, length of hospital stay, and postoperative hospital stay compared to the RLR group, demonstrating that RLR is superior to LLR in terms of surgical safety. In terms of costs, the total hospitalization costs and out-of-pocket costs were significantly lower in the LLR group than in the RLR group, further corroborating the higher expenses required for RLR compared to LLR, as also observed in works by Ziogas et al. (12). While the most recent study indicated that RLR demonstrated no significant cost advantage over LLR in terms of total hospitalization costs, our study further explored the differences in specific cost components between these two surgical approaches (21). Considering the composition of total hospitalization costs, we found that the surgical costs in the LLR group were significantly lower than those in the RLR group. However, the costs for drugs, examinations, nursing, and consumables were significantly higher in the LLR group compared to the RLR group. This suggests that the high surgical costs are the main reason for the significantly higher total hospitalization costs in the RLR group compared to the LLR group. Nonetheless, RLR improves patient outcomes by reducing the need for postoperative medication and further examinations, thus lowering other expenses for patients. Therefore, we believe that although the total costs of RLR are higher than those of LLR, patients undergoing RLR achieve better clinical outcomes and incur fewer expenses outside of surgery. In the future, by reducing the surgical costs of RLR, it will become a better choice for surgeons and patients.

Relevant studies have indicated that the high surgical costs of robotic surgery mainly stem from the usage costs of the robotic system itself, as well as the costs of maintenance and disposable supplies (12). This aligns with the experience of our research center, where the startup costs of the robot account for the majority of surgical expenses in robotic surgeries. To address this challenge, we propose concrete cost-reduction approaches: developing reusable robotic instruments to decrease recurrent expenditure by replacing single-use items with sterilizable alternatives; implementing AI-enhanced surgical guidance to shorten operative duration, thereby proportionally reducing time-dependent costs such as operating room and anesthesia costs; adopting predictive maintenance systems that optimize service interventions based on real-time equipment monitoring, cutting unnecessary scheduled maintenance expenses; and establishing localized supply chains to lower import-related costs for consumables. Importantly, these technical and operational innovations are likely to synergize with broader trends—including accumulated robotic system utilization, prospective insurance reimbursement adjustments for robotic procedures, and intensified market competition from new robotic platforms. This multi-dimensional evolution is projected to progressively lower RLR costs, thereby enhancing its clinical adoption in hepatic surgery while narrowing the economic gap with laparoscopic approaches.

Numerous studies have indicated that RLR is superior to LLR in patient populations with high surgical difficulty (9,10). At the same time, higher surgical difficulty often demands higher medical expenses. This study has found that the total hospitalization costs for RLR are significantly higher than those for LLR. However, is there a difference in the cost disparity between RLR and LLR among patients with varying levels of surgical difficulty? To investigate this issue, this study defined patient surgical difficulty based on the IWATE surgical difficulty classification and further conducted subgroup analysis with surgical difficulty as a covariate. Results showed that in the “Low”, “Intermediate”, and “Advanced” subgroups, the total hospitalization costs for the LLR group were significantly lower than those for the RLR group. However, in the “Expert” subgroup, there was no significant difference in total hospitalization costs between the LLR and RLR groups [75,709.0 (64,022.6–101,275.4) vs. 88,292.6 (82,954.8–98,554.0) ¥, P=0.32]. Therefore, we believe that as surgical difficulty increases, the cost difference between RLR and LLR decreases. Further comparison of clinical indicators between RLR and LLR within each surgical difficulty subgroup showed that, regardless of the subgroup, the LLR group had significantly higher intraoperative blood loss, postoperative hospital stay, and LOS compared to the RLR group, with no significant differences in other outcome indicators between the two groups. This suggests that RLR’s clinical outcomes are comparable to those of LLR, regardless of surgical difficulty. Therefore, combining the reduced cost gap between the two surgical methods with increasing surgical difficulty, this study concludes that for higher difficulty HCC resection surgeries, RLR is a better choice compared to LLR.

This study also has certain limitations. First, patients were selected from a single center and a single medical team. Given the variations in robotic surgery costs across different centers, our results may not be generalizable to other medical institutions. The technical proficiency of the surgeon can also impact the cost of the surgery, which is why we selected patients operated on by the same lead surgeon within a single medical team. However, this also limits the applicability of our findings to other medical teams. Secondly, in current health economic evaluations, particularly when assessing new drugs, treatments, diagnostic tools, or public health interventions, cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) is commonly used. CEA often incorporates quality-adjusted life years (QALY) to evaluate patient quality of life, ultimately using cost and QALY to calculate the cost required to improve a unit of QALY, thus assessing the cost-effectiveness of the intervention (22,23). However, this study is an observational study and did not prospectively evaluate the postoperative quality of life of patients. Accordingly, we will conduct prospective studies to further consolidate our conclusions. Additionally, the outcome indicators used in this study are short-term outcomes, so QALY was not calculated, and the cost-effectiveness of patients could not be assessed. Moreover, while existing literature suggests that maintenance and startup costs constitute part of robotic surgery costs, our evaluation did not incorporate these costs due to the absence of standardized methodologies for their amortization (12). This represents a potential limitation regarding cost comprehensiveness. Lastly, the study did not include the cost of purchasing the robot as an indirect cost in the analysis. This was mainly because the laparoscopic equipment, used as a comparison, was purchased earlier, and the purchasing power at different times is not directly comparable. Furthermore, the cost of purchasing the robot varies among different centers.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that for patients with HCC, RLR offers better surgical safety but incurs relatively higher medical costs compared to LLR. However, RLR results in lower non-surgical costs. Additionally, as the surgical difficulty increases, the cost difference between the two procedures decreases, making RLR a better choice for HCC patients with high surgical difficulty.
