Cardiomegaly of Unknown Origin: Why Morphology Still Matters in the Genetic Era - A Case of Cardiac Fabry Disease
Authors: Igor MAKAROV, Yulia ROY, Lubov MITROFANOVA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a 72-year-old man with long-standing hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and ischemic heart disease, in whom autopsy, following a fatal acute coronary syndrome, revealed marked cardiomegaly (heart weight, 911 g) and concentric myocardial hypertrophy (left ventricular wall thickness up to 2.4 cm) that appeared disproportionate to the expected degree of hypertensive and ischemic remodeling. Histological examination showed diffuse vacuolization of cardiomyocytes with a negative periodic acid-Schiff reaction; amyloidosis and glycogen storage diseases were excluded. Electron microscopy, α-galactosidase A activity, lyso-globotriaosylceramide measurement, and genetic testing were not available. Sudan III staining of frozen myocardial sections demonstrated intracellular lipid inclusions, providing supportive but non-specific evidence of a suspected non-classical (cardiac) variant of Fabry’s disease. This case highlights the importance of careful morphological evaluation in the differential diagnosis of unexplained severe left ventricular hypertrophy and illustrates both the diagnostic utility and the limitations of conventional histopathological methods in autopsy practice.

INTRODUCTION

Fabry’s disease (Anderson-Fabry disease) is an X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by deficiency of α-galactosidase A, resulting in progressive accumulation of globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) and its derivatives in lysosomes of various cell types, including cardiomyocytes and vascular endothelium1. The disease is characterized by marked clinical heterogeneity, ranging from classical early-onset multisystem involvement to late-onset forms with isolated organ manifestations, most commonly affecting the heart or kidneys in adulthood.

This phenotypic variability poses significant diagnostic challenges. In non-classical presentations, Fabry disease may closely mimic more prevalent conditions such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, hypertensive heart disease, or chronic kidney disease of unclear etiology, frequently leading to delayed or missed diagnoses. Although enzymatic assays and genetic testing are considered key diagnostic tools, their interpretation may be limited in late-onset forms and in the presence of variants of uncertain significance2.

Two major phenotypic forms are recognized: the classical type, associated with little or no residual enzyme activity (<3% of normal) and systemic substrate accumulation from early life, and the non-classical type, characterized by partial residual activity (approximately 3-10%) and often predominant involvement of a single target organ3.

CASE REPORT

A 72-year-old man with a long-standing history of arterial hypertension (maximum blood pressure of 170/100 mmHg; target values of 130-140/70 mmHg) and type 2 diabetes mellitus was admitted. According to the medical history, he sustained an anterior-apical non-Q-wave myocardial infarction in 2016, without subsequent symptomatic heart failure. Until 2025, available medical records revealed no evidence of extreme left ventricular hypertrophy; prior echocardiographic examinations described only moderate changes consistent with hypertensive heart disease, without severe wall thickening.

The patient remained free of recurrent anginal symptoms until August 2025, when an ST-segment-elevation acute coronary syndrome occurred following exposure to high temperatures in a sauna. Coronary angiography demonstrated diffuse, multivessel, calcified coronary artery disease, with stenoses of 60-80% and chronic occlusions. Transthoracic echocardiography, performed for the first time on August 17, 2025, revealed marked concentric left ventricular hypertrophy (mid-ventricular wall thickness of 25-28 mm), reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (39%), and akinesia of the apical segments. The acute myocardial infarction was complicated by pulmonary edema, cardiogenic shock, and sinoatrial block.

Staged coronary revascularization was performed in October-November 2025 and included stenting of the right coronary artery and balloon angioplasty. The patient was admitted to our center on November 20, 2025, for planned stenting of the left anterior descending and diagonal arteries. The procedure was complicated by type 4a myocardial infarction, hemopericardium, and acute encephalopathy. Six days later, on November 26, 2025, the patient developed sudden cardiac arrest due to thrombotic occlusion of the left main coronary artery, left anterior descending artery, and right coronary artery, confirmed by emergency coronary angiography. Resuscitation efforts were unsuccessful, and death was pronounced on November 27, 2025 (Figure 1).

Autopsy confirmed thrombotic occlusion of the coronary circulation, an acute myocardial infarction lasting up to 3 hours, and severe three-vessel coronary artery disease with incomplete revascularization. In addition to the expected findings, marked cardiomegaly was noted, with a heart weight of 911 g.

Gross examination of the heart using the “bread loaf” technique revealed a poorly demarcated subendocardial circumferential area of acute myocardial infarction, predominantly involving the apical region and the inferior third of the left ventricle. Marked hypertrophy of all cardiac chambers was present, with a corresponding reduction in ventricular cavity size. The left ventricular wall thickness was up to 2.4 cm, the interventricular septum ranged from 1.2 cm in the basal third to 2.2 cm in the mid-septum, and the right ventricular wall was 0.6 cm (Figure 2).

Given the patient’s advanced age and long-standing hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and ischemic heart disease, secondary left ventricular hypertrophy remained an important consideration. However, the severity of myocardial hypertrophy and cardiomegaly in the absence of valvular abnormalities appeared disproportionate, prompting further evaluation for alternative etiologies, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, amyloidosis (particularly transthyretin amyloidosis), chronic myocarditis, and storage disorders.

Histological samples were obtained from the interventricular septum, from the anterior and lateral walls of both the left and right ventricles, and from morphologically preserved myocardium outside the infarction zone. Histological examination of the myocardium revealed diffuse vacuolization of cardiomyocytes with a “lace-like” appearance. The cytoplasmic vacuoles were negative on periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining, which argues against glycogen accumulation and therefore against glycogen storage disease. No myofiber disarray was identified, making primary hypertrophic cardiomyopathy less likely. Likewise, neither inflammatory infiltrates nor myocyte necrosis outside the acute infarction was observed, providing no histological evidence of chronic myocarditis. Congo red staining, examined under polarized light, demonstrated no amyloid deposition, thereby excluding morphologically detectable cardiac amyloidosis. Importantly, no significant pathology was observed in other organs except diabetic nephropathy (class I-II), a finding that reduced the likelihood of systemic storage diseases.

Based on the clinical history (concentric hypertrophy with restrictive hemodynamics, reduced right ventricular systolic function, and marked wall thickening) and the absence of histological evidence of amyloidosis or glycogen storage disease, primary hypertrophic cardiomyopathy was considered unlikely because of the atypical late presentation and absence of characteristic morphological and functional features. Accordingly, a suspected non-classical (cardiac) variant of Fabry disease was considered the most likely explanation for the observed morphological findings, although definitive confirmation was not possible.

Because electron microscopy could not be performed (all tissue was formalin-fixed and the postmortem interval exceeded 24 hours) and genetic testing was unavailable, the only available confirmatory method was Sudan III staining of frozen myocardial sections obtained from non-ischemic areas. This demonstrated the presence of orange-red inclusions within cardiomyocytes, consistent with intracellular lipid accumulation. However, Sudan III staining is not specific for Fabry disease and is interpreted only as supportive morphological evidence in the absence of ultrastructural, enzymatic, and genetic confirmation (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

The non-classical (late-onset) cardiac variant of Fabry’s disease remains one of the most diagnostically challenging forms of the disorder. It typically presents between the fourth and sixth decades of life with left ventricular hypertrophy and often mimics hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, frequently resulting in delayed diagnosis and postponement of disease-specific therapy4, 5. The prevalence of Fabry’s disease among patients with an hypertrophic cardiomyopathy phenotype has been reported to be up to 6%, underscoring the importance of systematic differential diagnosis in this population6, 7.

Despite the widespread use of non-invasive imaging techniques, neither echocardiography nor cardiac magnetic resonance imaging can reliably confirm or exclude Fabry’s disease in non-classical forms. Echocardiographic findings are non-specific and may be observed in hypertension, aortic stenosis, and other forms of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy8. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging with T1 mapping is considered an important diagnostic tool; however, in late-onset variants its findings may be inconsistent4. Marked heterogeneity in native T1 values has been demonstrated even among patients with identical α-galactosidase A mutations, which limits the standalone diagnostic value of this technique without comprehensive clinical correlation9.

Genetic testing, although central to the diagnostic algorithm, does not always provide definitive conclusions. More than 1000 variants of the α-galactosidase A gene have been described, a substantial proportion of which are classified as variants of uncertain significance. Their interpretation remains challenging, particularly in isolated cardiac phenotypes, and does not always allow confident assessment of pathogenicity without morphological correlation. Measurement of α-galactosidase A activity may also be inconclusive in non-classical Fabry’s disease, where enzyme levels may be only mildly reduced or even fall within the normal range, particularly in female patients. Due to lyonization (random X-chromosome inactivation), α-galactosidase A activity in women may remain normal, leading to false-negative biochemical screening results10, 11.

In this context, histological evaluation becomes particularly important in patients with unclear clinical and genetic findings. Endomyocardial biopsy can reveal characteristic morphological features of Fabry’s disease. Light microscopy typically demonstrates diffuse cardiomyocyte vacuolization with a “lace-like” appearance and cellular hypertrophy, reflecting lysosomal accumulation of glycosphingolipids12. Electron microscopy remains the gold standard for morphological confirmation, allowing visualization of pathognomonic lamellar inclusions (“zebra bodies”) within cardiomyocytes13.

In settings where electron microscopy or molecular genetic testing is unavailable, lipid-specific staining techniques (e.g., Sudan III) may serve as an additional supportive methods. Detection of intracellular lipid inclusions in cardiomyocytes by light microscopy, after exclusion of amyloidosis and glycogen storage diseases, may represent a screening marker for Fabry’s disease and may justify further comprehensive diagnostic evaluation.

The differential diagnosis of severe left ventricular hypertrophy in elderly patients is particularly challenging because multiple factors, including acquired conditions such as long-standing arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and ischemic heart disease, as well as advanced age, may all contribute to increased myocardial mass. Consequently, gross examination remains an important first step in the pathological evaluation. Among macroscopic parameters, cardiovascular pathologists regard heart weight as a more reliable and reproducible indicator of myocardial hypertrophy than linear wall-thickness measurements, which may be substantially influenced by postmortem contraction, ventricular dilatation, and sampling level. Several validated reference models have therefore been proposed to estimate expected heart weight based on body size and underlying cardiovascular disease. According to the predictive model developed by Vlasov and Volkov14, the expected heart weight for a patient with combined hypertensive and ischemic heart disease would be approximately 693 g. Similarly, the large postmortem study by Kumar et al.15 reported a mean heart weight of 550±140 g in patients with cardiovascular disease, indicating that even after allowing for the modest increase associated with an acute myocardial infarction, heart weights rarely exceed approximately 800 g. In the present case, the heart weighed 911 g, substantially exceeding these expected values. This marked discrepancy suggested that hypertensive and ischemic remodeling alone could not adequately account for the degree of cardiomegaly, and it prompted a systematic histopathological differential diagnosis, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, cardiac amyloidosis, chronic myocarditis, and lysosomal storage disorders such as Fabry’s disease.

This clinicopathological case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of non-classical (cardiac) Fabry’s disease in elderly patients with multiple cardiovascular comorbidities. In this case, severe concentric myocardial hypertrophy with a restrictive diastolic filling pattern and extreme cardiomegaly could not be fully explained by hypertensive or ischemic heart disease alone, necessitating an extended differential diagnostic workup.

In the absence of electron microscopy and molecular genetic analysis, the combination of gross anatomical findings, histological features, and histochemical results—including diffuse cardiomyocyte vacuolization, negative PAS and Congo red staining, and intracellular lipid inclusions on Sudan III staining—supported a suspected diagnosis of a non-classical Fabry’s disease variant with predominant myocardial involvement.

This case emphasizes the continued importance of morphological assessment in the diagnosis of rare cardiomyopathies, particularly in postmortem settings, resource-limited environments, or cases involving variants of uncertain significance. Lipid staining may serve as an adjunct to guide further diagnostic evaluation.

Study Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, the diagnosis of Fabry’s disease was not confirmed by electron microscopy, the gold standard for identifying the characteristic lamellar inclusions in cardiomyocytes. Second, molecular genetic testing and measurement of α-galactosidase A activity were not available, precluding definitive confirmation of the underlying disorder. Finally, Sudan III staining is not specific for Gb3 accumulation and should be interpreted strictly within the context of the overall clinical, morphological, and histochemical findings.
