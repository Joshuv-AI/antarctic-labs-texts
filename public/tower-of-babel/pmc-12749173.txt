The local anesthetic, lidocaine, suppresses the growth of colon cancer SW480 cells by decreasing the voltage-gated sodium channel subunit Nav1.5
Authors: Keiko Haraguchi-Suzuki, Toru Suzuki, Chigusa Nakasone, Reika Kawabata-Iwakawa, Shigeru Saito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lidocaine is an inhibitor of voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs) expressed in excitable cells, such as myocardial cells and sensory neurons, and is used in many clinical situations, including as an anti-arrhythmic and analgesic agent. Clinical studies have suggested that its perioperative application in cancer patients improves their prognosis. Nav1.5 encoded by the SCN5A gene, which forms the α subunit of VGSC and is a pharmacological target of lidocaine, is expressed in various types of cancer cells. The SCN5A gene is also identified as an upstream regulator of the invasive activity of colon cancer cells, and its aberrant expression correlates with malignancy. Although we previously demonstrated that lidocaine treatment inhibits the growth of cancer cell lines, whether lidocaine induces cell growth suppression through inhibition of Nav1.5 is still unresolved. Here, we demonstrated that lidocaine induces growth suppression of colon cancer SW480 cells along with a decrease of Nav1.5. Additionally, lidocaine decreased cyclin D1, a cell cycle promoter, and mutant p53, which has transforming activity in SW480 cells. Since the expression level of Nav1.5 is associated with the invasiveness of colon cancer, the decrease of Nav1.5 with the administration of lidocaine has potential as a novel therapeutic strategy for cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29505-1.

Introduction

With the increase in the number of cancer patients, it is globally one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality1. Simultaneously, cancer therapies, such as molecular-targeted therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors, have been rapidly developing over the past several decades2,3. Nevertheless, surgery is still one of the mainstays of cancer therapy, despite the pain that is inherent to surgery. Thus, appropriate and sufficient analgesia is essential during the perioperative period. Local anesthetics enable pain control through inhibition of the activity of voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs) expressed in sensory neurons. Nav1.1-1.9, which consists of nine analogues of VGSCs that are encoded by the SCN1A-5 A and 8 A-11 A genes, form the α subunit of VGSCs, which form the channel pore. Nav1.1-1.9 are associated with one or more Navβ1-4, which are the β subunits of VGSCs that are encoded by SCN1B-4B genes and modulate the trafficking and gating kinetics of VGSCs4,5.

Local anesthetics, including lidocaine, exert their analgesic effects by binding to the channel pores of Nav1.1-1.9 and preventing the intracellular inflow of sodium ions, inhibiting the generation of action potentials in sensory neurons6,7. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that the α subunit of VGSCs, such as Nav1.5, which is encoded by the SCN5A gene, is expressed not only in excitable cells, but also in various types of cancer cells8–10. Additionally, its aberrant expression is associated with the metastatic ability of breast and colon cancer cells9,11,12. Colon cancer cell lines, such as SW620, HT29 and SW480 cells, express functional Nav1.5 on the plasma membrane, which allosterically activates the sodium/hydrogen exchanger type 1 (NHE-1) protein, which, in turn, promotes metastasis through activating extracellular matrix degrading enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)9,13,14. Therefore, local anesthetics, such as lidocaine, by inhibiting Nav1.5 activity, have potential therapeutic efficacy for cancer. In fact, several clinical studies have demonstrated that local anesthetics, including lidocaine, administered as regional anesthesia or intravenously to cancer patients, decreased the incidence of recurrence or metastasis15–18. Lidocaine reportedly suppresses the growth and invasiveness of human ovarian cancer A2780 and SKOV3 cells through inhibition of Nav1.5 and the focal adhesion kinase (FAK) signaling pathway19. Conversely, another study demonstrated that lidocaine inhibits cell motility in lung cancer NCI-H838 cells through inactivation of the Src signaling pathway, independent of VGSC inhibition20. Hence, although these studies have indicated anti-tumor effects of local anesthetics, it is still controversial whether or not these effects are mediated by the α subunit of VGSC, such as Nav1.5.

Our previous study revealed that lidocaine treatment suppresses colony formation of cancer cell lines, including colon cancer SW480 cells. Further, although a significantly higher concentration of lidocaine was necessary for growth suppression of SW480 cells compared to that of the other cancer cell lines examined, it is as yet unresolved whether the lidocaine-induced growth suppression of these cells depends on the inhibition of VGSC21. The present study examined the expression levels of SCN1A-5 A and 8 A-11 A genes encoding Nav1.1-1.9 in four lidocaine-untreated and treated cancer cell lines. Thereafter, since the mRNA level of the SCN5A gene encoding Nav1.5 in SW480 cells was significantly higher in the examined cancer cell lines, we examined the correlation between Nav1.5 levels and the growth of lidocaine-treated SW480 cells. Furthermore, we examined the expression of the cell cycle promoter, cyclin D1, in addition to mutant p53, which has transforming activity, in these cells.

Results

Expression levels of SCN1A-5 A and 8 A-11 A genes encoding Nav1.1-1.9 in cancer cell lines

Lidocaine treatment suppressed colony formation of cancer cell lines in a dose-dependent manner in our previous study21. In that study, the concentration of lidocaine required for inhibition of colony formation was different among the four cancer cell lines examined. Therefore, we examined the correlation between lidocaine-induced growth suppression and the expression of SCN1A-5 A and 8 A-11 A genes encoding Nav1.1-1.9. For this, we first examined the expression of SCN1A-5 A and 8 A-11 A genes by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using cancer cell lines, including cervical cancer HeLa, osteosarcoma U2OS, colon cancer SW480 and hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 cells. Comparison of the mRNA levels of these genes between the four cancer cell lines are demonstrated in Fig. 1. SCN5A and SCN8A mRNA were mainly expressed in U2OS and SW480 cells. The level of SCN5A mRNA in SW480 cells was significantly elevated, to a level that was approximately 2.8-fold higher than that in U2OS cells. On the other hand, SCN9A mRNA was mainly expressed in HeLa and HepG2 cells, although its level in HeLa cells was lower compared to that in HepG2 cells. The mRNA levels of SCN1A, SCN10A and SCN11A were low in all four cell lines. These results indicate that the subtypes and levels of SCN1A-5 A and 8 A-11 A mRNA expressed in cancer cell lines varied depending on the cell type (Fig. 1).

Expression levels of SCN mRNA changed in lidocaine-treated cancer cell lines

We used the data of comprehensive transcriptome sequence analysis (RNA-seq) of lidocaine-untreated and treated cancer cell lines to assess the types and levels of SCN mRNA expressed in the cancer cell lines21. The differential expression levels of these genes were demonstrated as a heatmap (Fig. 2A). SCN5A and SCN8A genes were predominantly expressed in lidocaine-untreated SW480 and U2OS cells, and the expression levels of these genes increased after lidocaine treatment. The SCN9A gene was primarily expressed in lidocaine-untreated HeLa and HepG2 cells. On the other hand, the level of SCN9A mRNA was decreased in lidocaine-treated HepG2 cells. The expression levels of SCN1A, SCN10A and SCN11A genes were low in all four cell lines. RNA-seq data suggested that the level of SCN mRNA expression in cancer cell lines had a tendency to change with lidocaine treatment.

Among the subtypes of SCN genes and the cancer cell lines examined, SCN5A mRNA encoding Nav1.5 was significantly expressed in SW480 cells, with the level increasing in a lidocaine-dose dependent manner, along with increased expression of SCN8A and SCN4A after lidocaine treatment (Figs. 1 and 2A). We performed qPCR analysis using lidocaine-untreated and treated SW480 cells. Compared to lidocaine-untreated SW480 cells, the level of SCN5A mRNA increased by approximately 2.2-fold and 5.6-fold after 1 mM and 5 mM lidocaine treatment, respectively (Fig. 2B). As in SW480 cells, the level of SCN mRNA, such as SCN9A in HeLa and SCN5A in U2OS cells, increased with lidocaine treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). On the other hand, the level of SCN9A mRNA expressed in HepG2 cells decreased after lidocaine treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1C). These results suggest that the expression level of SCN mRNA in cancer cell lines changed with lidocaine treatment, consistent with the results of RNA-seq.

Two splice variants, an adult and neonatal form of SCN5A mRNA, are generated by alternative splicing of exon 6 in the SCN5A gene, with the neonatal form reportedly contributing to metastatic activity in lung and triple-negative breast cancers (Fig. 2C)11,12,22. Since the level of SCN5A mRNA in qPCR using SCN5A primers (SCN5A general-1) included both splicing variant forms (Figs. 1 and 2B), we performed qPCR using primers to identify the neonatal form of SCN5A mRNA, referring to the primer design described by Guzel RM et al. 23. Since it was difficult to identify the adult form alone, another primer (SCN5A general-2) amplifying both forms was also applied for qPCR (Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 2D, the neonatal form of SCN5A mRNA was expressed in lidocaine-untreated SW480 cells. Further, the levels of both splice forms of SCN5A mRNA amplified using general-1 or 2 primers were approximately 3-fold higher than that of the neonatal form in lidocaine-untreated SW480 cells, indicating that the adult form of SCN5A mRNA is expressed more than the neonatal form in these cells. Furthermore, after 5 mM lidocaine treatment, the mRNA level of the neonatal form increased by approximately 3.7-fold, while that of both forms increased about 11-fold compared to that of the neonatal form alone in lidocaine-untreated SW480 cells. These results indicated that lidocaine treatment increased the adult form of SCN5A mRNA more than the neonatal form, which is supposed to have metastatic activity (Fig. 2D).

Expression of Nav1.5 decreased after lidocaine treatment in SW480 cells

Since the level of SCN5A mRNA encoding Nav1.5 increased in lidocaine-treated SW480 cells, we next examined the level of Nav1.5 protein in lidocaine-untreated and treated SW480 cells by western blot analysis using polyclonal anti-Nav1.5 antibodies. Although Nav1.5 was detected in both lidocaine-untreated and treated SW480 cells, it was difficult to distinguish the adult and neonatal forms of Nav1.5 using polyclonal anti-Nav1.5 antibodies, because there are only seven amino acid differences between these variants23. After lidocaine treatment, the level of Nav1.5 in SW480 cells decreased in contrast to the increase of SCN5A mRNA (Fig. 3A).

Reportedly, the expression level of Nav1.5 in myocardial cells is regulated by the ubiquitin-proteasome system, and administration of a proteasome inhibitor, MG132, increases Nav1.5 levels24,25. In order to examine whether Nav1.5 levels in lidocaine-treated SW480 cells are regulated by the ubiquitin-proteasome system, we treated SW480 cells with a proteasome inhibitor, 5 µM MG132 together with 5 mM lidocaine for 24 h. Although p21 levels which are regulated by the proteasome-ubiquitin system increased by MG132 treatment, the decrease in Nav1.5 levels after lidocaine treatment was not inhibited by MG132 treatment, indicating that Nav1.5 was not decreased through the ubiquitin-proteasome system in lidocaine-treated SW480 cells26 (Fig. 3B). Further, we examined whether the level of Nav1.5 decreased through autophagy after lidocaine treatment. The levels of p62 which is the marker of autophagy increased after treatment with 200 nM Bafilomycin A1, which is an autophagy inhibitor27. Nav1.5 decreased in SW480 cells treated by 5 mM lidocaine together with 200 nM Bafilomycin A1 for 24 h, indicating that the decrease in Nav1.5 was not mediated by autophagy after lidocaine treatment (Fig. 3C).

A previous study revealed that Nav1.5 is primarily expressed in the cell membrane of colon cancer cell lines, including SW620 and SW480 cells9. Hence, we also examined Nav1.5 levels using cytoplasm and membrane fractions purified from SW480 cells by western blot analysis. ATP1A, consisting of the ATPase Na+/K+ transport subunit, is ubiquitously expressed in the cell membrane, and is suitable as a membrane protein marker28. Although there was slight permeation of ATP1A into the cytosol fraction because of its abundant expression, it was significantly detected in the membrane fraction. We also detected growth differentiation factor (GDF)-15 in both the cytoplasm and cell membrane, with an increase of its level in the cell membrane after lidocaine treatment, as we previously reported21. Thus, we used these cellular fractions for western blot analysis. Evaluation showed that Nav1.5 was dominantly expressed in the membrane fraction, while it was hardly detected in the cytoplasmic fraction. Furthermore, Nav1.5 in the membrane fraction of lidocaine-treated SW480 cells decreased compared to that in lidocaine-untreated SW480 cells, indicating that lidocaine treatment decreased Nav1.5 levels in the cell membrane of SW480 cells (Fig. 3D).

Lidocaine reduced cyclin D1 and mutant p53 levels with a decrease of Nav1.5 in SW480 cells

Previous studies, including our report, demonstrated that lidocaine-treated cervical cancer HeLa cells and lung adenocarcinoma A549 and H1299 cells increased in the population of G0/G1 phase cells in a lidocaine-concentration dependent manner29,30. Hence, we examined the level of cyclin D1, which promotes the progression of the G1 phase of the cell cycle, in lidocaine-untreated and treated SW480 cells. In lidocaine-treated SW480 cells, the level of cyclin D1 decreased along with the decrease in Nav1.5 levels (Fig. 4A). The p53 gene functions as a tumor suppressor and is frequently mutated in cancer patients. Further, SW480 cells are known to express a mutant p53, which is associated with cell transformation, resulting in tumor development31. The lidocaine-treated SW480 cells demonstrated a decrease in both mutant p53 and Nav1.5 levels (Fig. 4A).

To examine the significance of lidocaine-induced Nav1.5 reduction, its expression was knocked down using the siRNA method. For this, two kinds of SCN5A siRNAs (SCN5A siRNA-1, 2) were designed. qPCR using total RNAs extracted from SW480 cells transfected with SCN5A siRNA-1 or siRNA-2 revealed that SCN5A siRNA-2 more-effectively suppressed the expression of SCN5A mRNA as compared to SCN5A siRNA-1 (Fig. 4B). Additionally, the cell lysates of SW480 cells transfected with SCN5A siRNAs were used for western blot analysis. The level of Nav1.5 encoded by the SCN5A gene was effectively decreased by transfection with SCN5A siRNA-2 rather than SCN5A siRNA-1, consistent with the results of qPCR in Fig. 4B,C). Using SCN5A siRNA-2, we examined the effect of Nav1.5 expression on cyclin D1 and mutant p53 levels. As shown in Fig. 4D, Nav1.5 decreased along with cyclin D1 and mutant p53 levels after 5 mM lidocaine treatment in control siRNA-transfected SW480 cells (Fig. 4D, lane 2; control siRNA, lidocaine (+)). On the other hand, in SCN5A siRNA-2-transfected SW480 cells without lidocaine treatment, levels of cyclin D1 and mutant p53 did not decrease, although Nav1.5 levels decreased in these cells (Fig. 4D, lane 3; SCN5A siRNA-2, lidocaine (-)). Lidocaine treatment led to a decrease in the levels of cyclin D1 and mutant p53, along with Nav1.5, in SCN5A siRNA-2-transfected SW480 cells (Fig. 4D, lane 4; SCN5A siRNA-2, lidocaine (+)). These results suggested that although lidocaine treatment decreased cyclin D1 and mutant p53 levels along with Nav1.5 levels, the decrease in cyclin D1 and mutant p53 was independent of Nav1.5-related signaling pathways in SW480 cells.

Lidocaine-induced growth suppression of SW480 cells is accompanied by a decrease in Nav1.5

Since increased expression of Nav1.5 is associated with cancer malignancy, and its expression was decreased by lidocaine treatment, we further investigated the correlation between Nav1.5 level and proliferation of SW480 cells8–10. We examined the concentration of lidocaine required to inhibit the proliferation of SW480 cells using a cell growth assay kit. The results showed that proliferation of SW480 cells was inhibited by 50 ± 3.8% with 3 mM lidocaine treatment, and it was almost completely suppressed with 5 mM lidocaine treatment (Fig. 5A, left panel). Use of lysates of lidocaine-treated SW480 cells revealed that Nav1.5 decreased in a lidocaine concentration-dependent manner, suggesting that lidocaine-induced growth suppression correlated with the decrease in Nav1.5 levels (Fig. 5A, right panel).

We next performed an immunofluorescence assay using lidocaine-untreated and treated SW480 cells. Nav1.5 was detected in the cell membrane in both lidocaine-untreated and treated SW480 cells, and its level decreased after lidocaine treatment, consistent with the results of western blot analysis using lidocaine-treated lysates of SW480 cells (Fig. 3D). We simultaneously examined the level of Ki-67, which is expressed in the nucleus of proliferating cells32. Ki-67 was detected in the nucleus in both lidocaine-untreated and treated SW480 cells, with its expression decreasing with lidocaine treatment (Fig. 5B). For quantification of the Ki-67 level, we counted Ki-67 positive cells in lidocaine-untreated and treated SW480 cells. In lidocaine-untreated SW480 cells, 88.8 ± 1.9% of cells expressed Ki-67, while 53.3 ± 5.7% of 3 mM lidocaine-treated SW480 cells expressed Ki-67, indicating that the Nav1.5 level correlated with lidocaine-induced growth suppression (Fig. 5C).

Expression level of Nav1.5 is partly associated with lidocaine-induced growth suppression in SW480 cells

To further examine whether lidocaine-induced growth suppression was due to induction of apoptosis, we performed western blot analysis to detect cleaved-caspase 3, which is one of the regulators of apoptosis. In contrast to lidocaine-untreated SW480 cells, cleaved caspase 3 was detected in SW480 cells after 5 mM lidocaine treatment, indicating that lidocaine-induced growth suppression was mediated by apoptosis (Fig. 6A).

Since lidocaine treatment led to a decrease in Nav1.5 levels, we investigated whether the Nav1.5 level was related to lidocaine-induced apoptosis. To examine this, we compared the expression levels of Nav1.5 in four cancer cell lines by western blot analysis. Nav1.5 was significantly expressed in SW480 and U2OS cells in contrast to HeLa and HepG2 cells, which was consistent with the levels of SCN5A mRNA in each cell line in Figs. 1, 6B). Subsequently, we detected apoptosis in lidocaine-treated SW480 and HeLa cells using an Annexin V-FITC plate assay kit. Lidocaine treatment induced apoptosis in these cells in a dose-dependent manner. After treatment with 3 mM lidocaine, apoptosis was more effectively induced in HeLa cells with only slight Nav1.5 expression than SW480 cells expressing a high level of Nav1.5, implying that its expression levels were opposite to those of lidocaine-induced apoptosis (Fig. 6C).

To further examine the effect of Nav1.5 overexpression on lidocaine-induced growth suppression, SW480 cells were transfected with Nav1.5 expression vector. Western blot analysis revealed that these cells significantly expressed Nav1.5 compared with cells transfected with a control vector (Fig. 6D, arrow). After treatment with 3 mM lidocaine, a control vector-transfected SW480 cells showed a decrease in Nav1.5 levels, while SW480 cells overexpressing Nav1.5 showed no decrease in Nav1.5 levels (Fig. 6D). Using these SW480 cells, we examined the cell viability by MTT assay. As shown in Fig. 6E, after treatment with 3 mM lidocaine, growth of SW480 cells that were transfected with a control vector was inhibited. On the other hand, lidocaine-induced growth inhibition was suppressed in Nav1.5 overexpressed-SW480 cells compared to a control vector-transfected SW480 cells. However, lidocaine-induced growth inhibition was not completely suppressed by Nav1.5 overexpression, indicating that lidocaine-induced growth suppression was partly mediated by a Nav1.5-related pathway (Fig. 6E).

Collectively, these results suggest that lidocaine induced growth suppression of colon cancer SW480 cells by decreasing Nav1.5 levels. Additionally, cyclin D1 and mutant p53, which promote cell growth, were decreased by lidocaine treatment, which was independent of the Nav1.5-related signaling pathway (Fig. 7).

Discussion

In the present study, we showed that lidocaine treatment induced growth suppression of colon cancer SW480 cells with a decrease of Nav1.5, which is encoded by the SCN5A gene. The level of SCN5A mRNA was significantly higher in SW480 cells as compared to the other cancer cell lines examined, and its level increased after lidocaine treatment (Figs. 1 and 2B). Although SCN4A and SCN8A mRNA also increased in lidocaine-treated SW480 cells, SCN9A mRNA primarily expressed in HepG2 cells decreased with lidocaine treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1C). In thyroid cancer MZ-CRC-1 cells, the mRNA level of SCN5A reportedly increased, while that of SCN9A decreased after treatment with the sodium channel inhibitor SVV188, indicating that the expression levels of SCN mRNA vary depending on the cancer cell type and type of sodium channel blocker33.

A previous study involving colon cancer HT29, HCT116 and SW620 cells reported an increase in SCN5A mRNA levels, although with unchanged Nav1.5 levels, in hypoxia-exposed cell lines34. Similarly, in this study, although the level of SCN5A mRNA increased, Nav1.5 decreased in lidocaine-treated SW480 cells (Figs. 2B and 3A). This discrepancy between mRNA and protein levels is frequently observed in cancer cells, suggesting the involvement of post-transcriptional or post-translational regulation of gene expression35,36.

The ubiquitin-proteasome system is known as one of the post-translational regulations. Treatment with a proteasome inhibitor, MG132, did not increase Nav1.5 levels in lidocaine-treated colon cancer SW480 cells though p21 levels which are regulated by the proteasome-ubiquitin system increased in these cells, indicating lidocaine-induced decrease in Nav1.5 levels are independent of this system26. On the other hand, Bahrudin U and colleagues reported that lidocaine inhibits the 20 S proteasome by directly binding to its β5 subunit in gastric cancer MKN45 cells37. Since treatment with lidocaine did not increase p21 levels in our study, lidocaine-related inhibition of the proteasome activity might be dependent on cancer cell types or lidocaine-concentration.

Autophagy is also known to be associated with post-translational regulation of protein levels. Actually, cancer cell migration is inhibited by autophagy-related degradation of the small GTPase RhoA protein, which upregulates Nav1.5 in highly aggressive breast cancer MDA-MB-231 cells, indicating that an Nav1.5-related pathway is regulated by autophagy-related protein expression38,39. However, in this study, treatment with an autophagy inhibitor, Bafilomycin A1, did not inhibit a decrease in Nav1.5 in lidocaine-treated SW480 cells though p62 levels which is the marker of autophagy increased by treatment of Bafilomycin A1 in these cells27 (Fig. 3C). These results suggest that the lidocaine-induced decrease in Nav1.5 is not regulated by post-translational mechanisms, at least through the ubiquitin-proteasome system and autophagy in SW480 cells.

MicroRNA is a non-coding small RNA that post-transcriptionally regulates gene expression. MicroRNA inhibits protein production through binding to the mRNA of targeted genes contributing to physiological processes and pathogenesis, including cancer40. Sun D and colleagues reported that lidocaine induces the expression of microRNA 382-5p (miR-382-5p), which promotes cell death through downregulation of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) in ovarian cancer SCOV-2 and breast cancer T47D cells41. Lidocaine-induced microRNA 520a-3p (miR-520a-3p) also inhibits the proliferation of colorectal cancer SW480, HCT116 and NCM460 cells by downregulating epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)42. Thus, the discrepancy between SCN5A mRNA and Nav1.5 protein levels in lidocaine-treated SW480 cells might be explained by the inhibition of Nav1.5 expression by lidocaine-induced microRNA. Further study is necessary to identify microRNAs that regulate the expression of the SCN5A gene in lidocaine-treated SW480 cells.

In this study, lidocaine treatment decreased not only Nav1.5 levels, but also cyclin D1 and mutant p53 in SW480 cells (Fig. 4A). Cyclin D1 promotes cell proliferation by accelerating the transition from the G1 to S phase of the cell cycle12. The lidocaine-induced decrease in cyclin D1 likely explains previous reports about the increase in the G0/G1 phase cell population in lidocaine-treated cervical cancer HeLa and lung adenocarcinoma A549 and H1299 cells29,30. p53 is a key tumor suppressor protein, and its mutant form expressed in SW480 cells is associated with cell transformation, resulting in tumor development31,43. In this study, the level of mutant p53 decreased in lidocaine-treated SW480 cells (Fig. 4A). Conversely, wild type p53 increases in lidocaine-treated gastric cancer MKN45 cells, indicating that lidocaine-induced changes in p53 levels might be influenced by the p53 status and cancer type31,37,44.

According to a previous transcriptional network analysis-based study, the SCN5A gene encoding Nav1.5 is thought to act as an upstream regulator of the proliferation and invasive activity of colon cancer cells14. Importantly, although siRNA-mediated knockdown of Nav1.5 did not effectively decrease cyclin D1 and mutant p53 levels in lidocaine-untreated SW480 cells in this study, their levels decreased in lidocaine-treated SW480 cells with suppression of Nav1.5 expression by SCN5A siRNA (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that lidocaine-inducible genes that are independent of the Nav1.5-related signaling pathway might be indispensable for regulating the levels of cyclin D1 and mutant p53 in SW480 cells. Actually, in this study, lidocaine-induced growth suppression was not completely inhibited in Nav1.5-overexpressing SW480 cells (Fig. 6D,E). Further studies to identify the genes that mediate lidocaine-induced growth suppression, which is independent of the Nav1.5-related signaling pathway, is important for understanding the mechanism of lidocaine-related anti-tumor effects.

In prostate cancer PC3 cells, treatment with the synthetic sodium channel blockers, S0154 and S0161, decreased Nav1.6 and Nav1.7 levels, along with reduction of metastatic activity45. Hence, medications such as lidocaine that decrease Nav1.5 expression in cancer cells are potential candidate drugs in the treatment of cancer. Since lidocaine is already available for use clinically, finding a newer anti-tumor use for this drug will surely be advantageous. Further experiments evaluating the mechanism of lidocaine-related anti-tumor effects are necessary for its application in cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell cultures and reagents

Human colon cancer SW480 and osteosarcoma U2OS cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (VA, USA). Human cervical cancer HeLa (RCB0007) and hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 (RBC1648) cells were provided by the RIKEN BioResearch Resource Center (BRC) through the National Bio-Resource Project of MEXT, Japan. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco Modified Eagle medium (Fujifilm-Wako, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Atlas Biologicals, CO, USA) at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Lidocaine hydrochloride isotonic solution (2%w/v) (Aspen, Tokyo, Japan) was added to the culture medium to achieve the test concentrations. PBS was used to adjust the total dose of lidocaine in each sample. MG132 and Bafilomycin A1 were purchased from Fujifilm-Wako (Osaka, Japan) and LKT Laboratories (MN, USA), respectively.

Transcriptome expression analysis (RNA-seq)

RNA-seq was performed in our previous study21. Briefly, cancer cell lines were treated with different concentrations of lidocaine for 24 h. The concentration of lidocaine applied to the cells was determined based on the concentration necessary to suppress colony formation21. Total RNAs from cells were extracted and were applied for preparing sequence libraries. The generated libraries and 1% PhiX spike-in were then subjected to paired-end sequencing of 43-bp reads using a NextSeq500 System (Illumina Inc., CA, USA) with a NextSeq500 High Output v2.5 kit (Illumina). The reads were aligned with the UCSC human reference genome 19 (hg19) for calculation of expression values (read counting and TPM (Transcripts Per Million) normalized value) using the STAR (version 2.5.3a)-RSEM (version 1.3.3) pipeline. RNA-seq data are available from ArrayExpress under the accession number E-MTAB-11,320 (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/arrayexpress/experiments/E-MTAB-11320/).

Quantitative RT-PCR (qPCR)

Cells were cultured at the indicated lidocaine concentration for 24 hours. The concentration of lidocaine applied to the cells was determined based on that applied in RNA-seq analysis21. RNA was extracted from these cells using the FastGene RNA Premium kit (Nippon Genetics Inc., Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription of 200 ng of total RNA purified from cells was performed using PrimeScript (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. PCR was performed under the following conditions: a PCR activation step was performed at 50°C for 2 minutes, followed by 95°C for 2 minutes and 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 seconds, 50°C for 15 seconds and 72°C for 1 minute, using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix on a StepOne Real Time PCR system (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA). The sequences of primers of SCN1A-5A and 8A-11A were referred to from the study by Lopez-Charcas O et al. and Guzel RM et al.9,23. The primer sequences were as follow: forward of SCN1A was 5’-TTCATGGCTTCCAATCCTTC-3’ and reverse of SCN1A was 5’-TAGCCCCACCTTTGATTTTG-3’, forward of SCN2A was 5’-GCCAGCTTATCAATCCCAAA-3’ and reverse of SCN2A was 5’-TCTTCTGCAATGCGTTGTTC-3’, forward of SCN3A was 5’-CAAAGGGAAGATCTGGTGGA-3’ and reverse of SCN3A was 5’-AAAGGCCAATGCACCACTAC-3’, forward of SCN4A was 5’-TCAACAACCCCTACCTGACC-3’ and reverse of SCN4A was 5’-ACGGACGAGTTCCCATCATA-3’, forward of SCN5A (general-1) was 5’-CACGCGTTCACTTTCCTTC-3’ and reverse of SCN5A (general-1) was 5’-CATCAGCCAGCTTCTTCACA-3’, forward of SCN5A (general-2) was 5’-CTGCACGCGTTCACTTTCCT-3’ and reverse of SCN5A (general-2) was 5’-CAGCCAGCTTCTTCACAGACT-3’, forward of SCN8A was 5’-CGCCTTATGACCCAGGACTA-3’ and reverse of SCN8A was 5’-GTGCCTCTTCCTGTTGCTTC-3’, forward of SCN9A was 5’-GGCTCCTTGTTTTCTGCAAG-3’ and reverse of SCN9A was 5’-TGGCTTGGCTGATGTTACTG-3’, forward of SCN10A was 5’-ACCTGGTGGTGCTTAACCTG-3’ and reverse of SCN10A was 5’-TGCTGAAGAAGCTGCAAAGA-3’, forward of SCN11A was 5’-CTGTGGTCCTGGTCATTGTG-3’ and reverse of SCN11A was 5’-TGCATTCGCTTCTTGCATAC-3’, forward of GAPDH was 5’-AGGCTAGCTGGCCCGATTTC-3’ and reverse of GAPDH was 5’-TGGCAACAATATCCACTTTACCAGA-3’. To identify the neonatal form of SCN5A mRNA, the following primers were used: forward of SCN5A (general-1) and reverse of neonatal SCN5A, the sequence of which was 5’-GACAAATTGCCTAGTTTTATATTT-3’.

Western blotting

SW480 cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% deoxycholate and 1% Triton X-100) supplemented with 2 mM PMSF, a protease inhibitor cocktail and 1 mM sodium orthovanadate (SantaCruz Biotech, TX, USA). The cell lysates of the cytosol and membrane fractions were prepared using Mem-PER™ Plus Membrane Protein Extraction Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The lysate was subjected to SDS-PAGE, and the separated protein was transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (IPVH00010, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were blocked with 3% non-fat dry milk dissolved in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20 (TBS-T). A primary antibody reaction using Can Get Signal Immunoreaction Enhancer Solution (NKB101, Toyobo Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Osaka, Japan) was performed for 1 h at room temperature, and the membranes were washed with TBS-T. An HRP-conjugated secondary antibody reaction was performed at room temperature for 1 h. After washing the membranes, Western Lighting Plus (Perkin Elmer, MA, USA) and Merstham Imager 600 (Cytiva, Tokyo, Japan) were used for protein detection. Quantification of protein levels was performed using Image J software (https://imagej.net/ij/index.html).

Antibodies

The antibodies used in this study were rabbit anti-Nav1.5 antibody (Proteintech, IL, USA, 23016-1-AP), mouse anti-Ki67 antibody (SantaCruz Bio, TX, USA, sc-23900), rabbit anti-ATP1A1 antibody (Proteintech, IL, USA, 55187-1-AP), rabbit anti-GDF-15 antibody (Proteintech, IL, USA, 27455-1-AP), rabbit anti-cyclin D1 antibody (Proteintech, IL, USA, 26929-1-AP), mouse anti-p53 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA, #2524), anti-rabbit cleaved caspase-3 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA, #9664), mouse anti-GAPDH antibody (Fujifilm-Wako, Osaka, Japan, 014-25524), mouse α-tubulin antibody (DM1A) (SantaCruz Bio, TX, USA, sc-32293), goat Cy3-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody, and donkey Cy2-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, PA, USA).

RNA interference

SW480 cells were cultured in a six-well plate at the concentration of 1 × 105 per well for 24 hours, and transfected with siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Twenty-four hours after transfection, the cells were treated with or without lidocaine for another 24 hours and then assayed. The human SCN5A siRNA-1 and siRNA-2 sequences were 5’-AGAUCUACAUGAUCUUCUUdTdT-3’ (NM_000335.5, position 1369) and 5’-ACGGAUGUCUUCAGGAACU dTdT-3’ (NM_000335.5, position 1649). Each siRNA sense and antisense strand was synthesized and annealed by Nippon Gene Inc. (Toyama, Japan). Control siRNA was purchased from Nippon Gene Inc.

Cell proliferation assay

Cells were plated at the concentration of 5 × 103 in a 96-well plate and cultured for 48 h, and were treated with the indicated concentration of lidocaine. After 24 h, the cells were incubated with Cell counting Kit solution (Doujin Kagaku, Kumamoto, Japan) for 1 h at 37 °C following the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm.

Immunofluorescence assay

SW480 cells were cultured on poly-D-Lysine coated cover glasses (neuVitro, WA, USA). The cells were fixed with ice cold methanol for 10 min at -20 °C. Blocking was performed using 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 60 min at room temperature. The cells were incubated with the primary antibody for 60 min at room temperature and washed three times with PBS, and with the fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody for 60 min at room temperature and washed three times with PBS. DNA was stained with 300 nM 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Invitrogen, MA, USA) for 15 min at room temperature. The images were observed by an immunofluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ni-E, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with NIS-Elements Ver 5.1 software.

Annexin V-FITC plate assay

Cells were plated at the concentration of 5 × 103 in a 96-well black plate with a clear bottom, and cultured for 24 h, following which they were treated with an indicated concentration of lidocaine for 30 h. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, cells were incubated with Annexin V-FITC working solution for 15 min at 37 °C, and were then incubated with quenching buffer to extinguish the excess fluorescence. The fluorescence intensity of Annexin-FITC and the background were measured at 488 nm and 525 nm, respectively.

MTT assay

Cells plated at the concentration of 5 × 103 in a 96-well plate were transfected with the Nav1.5 expression vector of pRP-CMV-hSCN5A [NM_001099404.2]) or empty vector as a control using Lipofectamine 2000 following the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen, MA, USA). The Nav1.5 expression vector was purchased from VectorBuilder (Yokohama, Japan). After 24 h, the cells were treated with 3 mM lidocaine for a further 24 h. The cells were then incubated with MTT solution for 4 h at 37 °C following the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance of each sample and the background were measured at 570 nm and 630 nm, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Values are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significant differences among experimental samples were assessed using Bonferroni’s method. p-Values of < 0.05 were considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.
