Self-adaptive cleaning technology for artillery laser ignition windows based on current feedback
Authors: Zebang Sun, Jianwei Yang, Ruixia Kang, Shixuan Li, Ning Liu, Boyi Zhao, Liangliang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaser ignition is a novel artillery ignition technology. However, residues from propellant combustion adhere to the surface of the laser ignition window, obstructing the laser beam. To ensure the reliability of subsequent ignition, the window must be cleaned using a wire brush within an extremely short time after firing. After repeated use, the bristles of the wire brush tend to bend and deform, leading to performance degradation and reduced cleaning effectiveness. To address this issue of weakened cleaning capability due to brush performance degradation, this paper proposes a self-adaptive cleaning mechanism for artillery laser ignition windows based on current feedback. First, a computational model was established between the compression of the wire brush bristles and the current, and the effective cleaning current range was determined based on experimental results. Subsequently, the adaptive cleaning process was defined. Finally, an experimental platform for the adaptive cleaning mechanism was constructed to validate its cleaning performance. The results indicate that the effective cleaning current range is 4.3 A to 5.5 A. The maximum error between the experimental and theoretical results for the total cam rotation angle was 1.9%. Furthermore, repeated cleaning did not cause damage to the window surface. This demonstrates that the proposed adaptive cleaning mechanism can achieve non-destructive cleaning of the window and provides theoretical guidance for the future design and optimization of cleaning methods.Keywords: Current feedback, Artillery laser ignition, Adaptive, Cleaning mechanismSubject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Laser ignition technology is an advanced ignition method that utilizes a high-energy-density laser beam as an external excitation source to precisely and controllably ignite combustible mixtures1,2. The application of this technology to artillery systems can significantly simplify the structure of the ignition unit and effectively enhance operational performance, making it a key research direction for future advanced artillery ignition systems3–5. However, due to the specific structural constraints of artillery, the laser beam must pass through a dedicated laser ignition window to reach and ignite the propellant within the chamber. This design results in the window being directly exposed to high-temperature and high-pressure combustion gases during projectile launch6,7. Under these extreme conditions, substantial residues from propellant combustion adhere to the window surface, leading to a reduction in light transmittance. This degradation can cause ignition failures and severely compromise the reliability of laser ignition10–12. Therefore, performing effective cleaning of the laser ignition window after firing is crucial for ensuring reliable ignition13,14.

Currently, non-contact cleaning methods—such as lasers, ultrasonic waves, or fluid purging—to remove adhered residues still pose certain risks and limitations. Extensive preliminary experiments have demonstrated that employing a high-speed rotating wire brush in direct contact with the window surface can effectively clean the residues. This is achieved through the mechanical actions of the bristles, which fracture and detach the residue deposits15–19.

During the cleaning process, the bristles of the wire brush are subjected to both compressive forces from the window and alternating bending stresses. This combined loading can readily cause the bristles to bend, deform, or even break and detach, leading to performance degradation of the wire brush20,21. Consequently, it is necessary to adaptively adjust the relative position between the wire brush and the laser ignition window surface based on the brush’s degradation state to ensure effective removal of propellant residues.Currently, adaptive control methods are widely applied in grinding and polishing fields. Pan et al.22 investigated adaptive matching of polishing process parameters based on an SPSO–BP neural network. Their results indicated that, depending on different base conditions and requirements for polishing quality and efficiency, the SPSO–BP prediction model could adaptively match polishing parameters to achieve results closer to the target. Chen et al.23 introduced a fuzzy adaptive impedance control method to study the polishing performance of a curved-surface robot. The results showed that compared with the traditional impedance control model, this algorithm reduced surface roughness by 59.7% after polishing and significantly improved polishing quality. Rei et al.24 and Gundogdu et al.25 employed impedance and neural network control strategies, established a complete electropneumatic model, and used a PID controller to regulate pneumatic pressure for contact force control, effectively accomplishing derusting and paint removal tasks. An et al.26 and Li et al.27 conducted research on polishing techniques under adaptive control methods. Their findings demonstrated that the adoption of adaptive methods effectively improved polishing precision, efficiency, and reduced mean square form (MSF) error.

In summary, adaptive control methods have found extensive application in precision machining fields such as polishing and grinding. However, their application in the specific domain of cleaning artillery laser ignition windows remains unexplored28,29.

This study addresses the performance degradation of wire brushes after repeated cleaning of artillery laser ignition windows. By integrating adaptive control methodology, a self-adaptive cleaning mechanism and corresponding strategy are proposed for these windows. Following brush degradation, the proposed mechanism automatically adjusts the relative position between the brush and the window. This adjustment ensures consistent cleaning effectiveness and enhances the reliability of artillery laser ignition technology.

Composition and working principle of the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism

This section describes the composition and working principle of the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism. It defines the method and the governing principle for adjusting the relative position between the wire brush and the laser ignition window.

Composition of the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism

The self-adaptive cleaning mechanism designed in this paper mainly consists of a wire brush, a rotation motor, a translation motor, a gearbox, a cam, and a translation mechanism. Its structure is shown in Fig. 1.

Structure and extension/retraction principle of the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism. (a) External view of the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism; (b) Extended state and working principle (with the cam pointer pointing to the left); (c) Retracted state and working principle (with the cam pointer pointing to the right).

The structure and extension/retraction principle of the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism are illustrated in Fig. 1. The rotation motor drives the wire brush to rotate via the gearbox. The rotating brush contacts the window surface, and the fracturing and peeling action of its bristles removes the deposits adhering to the window. A cam is mounted on the output shaft of the translation motor. The guide slots on both sides of the gearbox engage with the guide rails on the translation mechanism. As the cam rotates, it pushes the gearbox to slide along the translation mechanism. Specifically, when the cam pointer points to the left, the wire brush is extended; when the pointer points to the right, the wire brush is retracted. This extension and retraction motion adjusts the relative position between the wire brush and the window, thereby realizing the self-adaptive cleaning function.

Working principle of the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism

The self-adaptive cleaning mechanism is mounted on the breech of the artillery. Its arrangement and working process are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Arrangement and working process of the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism (a) Closed breech state; (b) Open breech state.

During artillery preparation for firing, the breech is closed. The self-adaptive cleaning mechanism remains separated from the laser ignition window. Upon projectile launch, the propellant combustion gas acts directly on the window surface, as shown in Fig. 2a.

After firing is completed, the breech opens upward. The wire brush of the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism extends to make contact with the laser ignition window, thereby performing the cleaning operation, as illustrated in Fig. 2b.

Based on the extension and retraction motion principle of the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism, the stroke of the wire brush is governed by the cam rotation angle. Consequently, the moving distance of the wire brush can be readily adjusted by varying the cam rotation angle. Therefore, to achieve precise control of the stroke, it is necessary to establish the governing relationship for this adjustment. Torque and current variation characteristics of the rotation motor

During the cleaning process, as the distance between the wire brush and the window surface decreases, the compression of the bristles increases. This causes the bristles to press more tightly against the window, thereby increasing the rotational resistance encountered by the brush. Consequently, the operating current of the rotation motor rises. This indicates that the magnitude of the motor current can reflect the degree of bristle compression. Therefore, the rotation motor current can be utilized as a feedback signal. By adjusting the extension of the wire brush based on the current level, the self-adaptive control function is achieved.

This section first conducts a force analysis on the wire brush bristles to establish the relationship between the rotation motor torque and the bristle compression. Then, based on the relationship between the motor current and torque, the correlation between bristle compression and current is determined. This establishes the foundation for using the motor current as feedback to achieve self-adaptive adjustment of the wire brush position.

Self-adaptive cleaning control strategy

During operation, the wire brush experiences performance degradation due to bristle deformation, wear, and detachment over time. This degradation reduces the cleaning effectiveness for residues on the laser ignition window surface. Therefore, it is necessary to adjust the compression U based on the degree of brush performance degradation to ensure that the rotation motor current meets the required specifications. Section 3 of this paper has demonstrated a corresponding relationship between the compression U and the rotation motor current It. This relationship allows the motor current to be utilized as a feedback signal for the adaptive adjustment of the brush compression U. Based on the characteristics of the motor current variation, this section designs a self-adaptive control strategy utilizing rotation motor current feedback.

The cleaning operation of the wire brush consists of two primary actions: extension for cleaning and retraction upon completion. Consequently, two key positions are defined along the brush’s travel path: the initial position and the effective cleaning position, as illustrated in Fig. 9.

Schematic diagram of wire brush retractable path position marking.

As shown in Fig. 9, the initial position is the initial state of the wire brush. The effective cleaning position is defined as the location where the brush ensures the rotation motor current It remains within the range of 4.3 A to 5.5 A.

The position calibration process involves two primary stages of brush movement: Stage P1, extending from the initial position to the effective cleaning position; and Stage P2, retracting from the effective cleaning position back to the initial position. The specific control strategy is as follows:

Stage P1: Extending from the initial position to the effective cleaning position.

Stage P1: Extending from the initial position to the effective cleaning position.

To precisely locate the effective cleaning position, the wire brush moves in a stepwise manner during Stage P1. Let the fixed forward displacement per step be xB. The step displacement per forward movement is a key parameter in the proposed adaptive control strategy. Based on extensive experimental results, xB=0.1 mm was selected to achieve a balance between positioning accuracy and response speed. A smaller step (e.g.,0.05 mm) would improve accuracy but prolong the extension time. Given that at high artillery firing rates, the cleaning time available for the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism to clean the laser ignition window is extremely limited, the extension time cannot be too long. Conversely, a larger step (e.g., 0.2 mm) would shorten the response time but risks exceeding the effective cleaning current range, which could easily damage the laser ignition window. The selected step of 0.1 mm ensures that the extension time remains within a reasonable range while keeping the cleaning current controllable, enabling the system to reliably detect entry into the 4.3 A to 5.5 A effective range without overshoot.

During each step, the rotation motor current IBi is sampled. When IBi reaches the range of 4.3 A to 5.5 A, it indicates that the wire brush has arrived at the effective cleaning position. According to Eq. (2), given the step displacement xB, the corresponding cam rotation angle αBn after n step is

According to Eq. (19), αBn−1 is the cam rotation angle after the n−1 step. It is evident that the relationship between the cam rotation angle and the displacement is nonlinear.

During Stage P1, the rotation motor current IBi is sampled at a frequency of 1 kHz (i.e., one sample per millisecond). At each step, the current is measured after the brush has settled for 10ms to avoid transient effects caused by mechanical vibrations and motor startup. The current value is then compared with the effective cleaning range. If IBi falls within 4.3 A to 5.5 A for three consecutive samples (to ensure measurement stability), the brush is considered to have reached the effective cleaning position.

Once the cleaning operation is completed, the wire brush retracts to the zero position in a single continuous motion, thereby completing one cleaning cycle. The stepwise strategy proposed in this paper offers two advantages. First, it eliminates the need for precise system modeling, as the adaptive adjustment is based solely on current feedback. Second, it inherently prevents current overshoot by limiting the forward movement of the wire brush incrementally. These features make the proposed strategy particularly suitable for applications such as wire brushes, where system parameters change gradually due to wear and degradation.

Experimental verification of the self-adaptive cleaning effectiveness

While the adaptive control parameters and workflow for the cleaning mechanism have been established as described above, experimental verification is still required to demonstrate that the mechanism can automatically adjust the bristle compression to maintain the current within the effective cleaning range, even after wire brush performance degrades. To this end, an experimental platform for the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism was constructed, and its adaptive functionality was tested and validated in this section.

Experimental platform for the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism

As shown in Fig. 11, the experimental platform for the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism primarily consists of a base plate, a mount for the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism, a mount for the laser ignition window, window position adjustment bolts, and locking nuts.

Structure of the test platform for the adaptive cleaning mechanism.

As shown in Fig. 11, the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism is mounted on its dedicated mount, and its position is fixed by a positioning slot on the mount, which maintains a fixed relative position between the wire brush and the laser ignition window. The laser ignition window is installed on its corresponding mount. The distance between the wire brush and the window surface can be adjusted by rotating the window position adjustment bolts (M10 bolts, thread pitch: 1 mm). Specifically, one full rotation of the adjustment bolt moves the laser ignition window forward or backward by 1 mm. Once the position of the laser ignition window is set, the adjustment bolts are locked using locking nuts to keep the window position fixed, thereby ensuring the stability and repeatability of the experimental results.

Experimental verification of the self-adaptive control strategy

Prior to the experiment, a test wire brush was installed on the self-adaptive cleaning mechanism. The initial image of the unused brush and the corresponding curve of cam rotation angle variation were recorded. After performing 300 cleaning cycles with the same brush, its image and the cam rotation angle curve were recorded again. The deformation and detachment state of the bristles were observed, and the number of stepwise movements of the cam was compared. This comparison was used to evaluate whether the self-adaptive cleaning strategy could automatically adjust the brush position after performance degradation, thereby maintaining the rotation motor current within the effective cleaning range (4.3 A to 5.5 A).

The wire brush at 0 and 300 cleaning cycles is shown in Fig. 12.

Images of the wire brush at 0 and 300 cleanings operations (a) Wire brush in its initial state (without cleaning operations); (b) Wire brush after 300 cleaning operations.

As shown in Fig. 12, with an increasing number of cleaning cycles, the wire bristles exhibit significant performance degradation, including bending, deformation, fracture, and detachment. This indicates that the cleaning capability of the wire brush gradually diminishes as more cleaning cycles are performed. If the cleaning mechanism lacked self-adaptive capability and maintained a constant relative position between the brush and the window, the cleaning effectiveness would also progressively decline, which could readily lead to ignition failures.

During the 1 st and the 300th cleaning cycles, 150 frames of data (each with a duration of 10 ms) were collected. The corresponding number of stepwise movements of the cam is presented in Fig. 13.

Stepping motor rotation steps of the cam at 0 and 300 cleanings operations (a) Stepping motor rotation steps of the cam at 1 cleanings operations; (b) Stepping motor rotation steps of the cam at 300 cleanings operations.

As shown in Fig. 13, during the first cleaning cycle, the cam advanced by 11 steps, whereas by the 300th cycle, it required 15 steps. This increase in the number of steps demonstrates that as cleaning cycles accumulate and brush performance degrades, the self-adaptive control strategy effectively compensates by automatically adjusting the cam’s step count. This adjustment ensures that the rotation motor current remains within the effective cleaning current range (4.3 A to 5.5 A), thereby guaranteeing consistent cleaning effectiveness of the window. It should be noted that the actual cam rotation angle shown in Fig. 13 does not exhibit an ideal stepwise pattern. The cam is driven by a servo motor. During operation, resistance and inertia inevitably cause overshoot, i.e., the actual rotation angle temporarily exceeds the commanded value. The encoder integrated with the servo motor detects this overshoot and applies real-time correction to maintain the rotation angle within the allowable error range of ± 0.1°. As a result, although the adaptive control strategy theoretically demands a stepwise cam rotation profile, the actual rotation angle is continuously adjusted in real time due to the dynamic effects of resistance and inertia. Consequently, the measured cam rotation angle curve is not strictly stepwise, as observed in Fig. 13. This behavior does not compromise the cleaning performance, as the control system successfully keeps the rotation angle within the specified tolerance.

Cleaning effectiveness test

Having confirmed the effectiveness of the self-adaptive cleaning strategy, it remains necessary to verify its cleaning performance. As it is impractical to simulate the actual process of residue adhesion on the artillery window surface in the laboratory, paint was used to simulate propellant residues for the cleaning effectiveness test. In the test, the same wire brush was used for its 1 st and 300th cleaning cycles on a window coated with paint. This was done to verify whether effective cleaning could still be achieved under the guidance of the adaptive control strategy after the brush had undergone performance degradation. The cleaning results for the 1 st and 300th cycles are shown in Fig. 14.

Cleaning effect after the 1 st and 300th cleanings with the wire brush (a) Cleaning effect before the first cleaning cycle; (b) Cleaning effect after the first cleaning cycle; (c) Cleaning effect before the 300th cleaning cycle; (d) Cleaning effect after the 300th cleaning cycle.

As shown in Fig. 14, the window surface was uniformly coated with paint prior to cleaning. After both the 1 st and the 300th cleaning cycles performed by the test wire brush, almost no paint residue remained on the window surface. This result demonstrates that even after brush performance degradation, the self-adaptive control strategy—by automatically adjusting the relative position between the brush and the window—ensures that the rotation motor current is maintained within the effective cleaning range. Consequently, effective window cleaning is achieved. This approach successfully addresses the issue of diminished cleaning effectiveness caused by brush performance degradation and significantly enhances the reliability of laser ignition window cleaning.

Conclusion

This paper presented a current feedback-based self-adaptive cleaning mechanism. The relationship between bristle compression and rotation motor current during contact with the window surface was analyzed, a corresponding adaptive control strategy was developed, and an experimental platform was constructed. Experiments were conducted to validate the control strategy and evaluate cleaning effectiveness under varying degrees of brush performance degradation. The main conclusions are as follows:

Based on the experimentally determined effective bristle compression range, the corresponding effective operating current range for the rotation motor was calculated to be 4.3 A to 5.5 A, providing a basis for the design of similar laser ignition window cleaning systems.
