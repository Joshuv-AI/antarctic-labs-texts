Determinants of environmentally related physical activity in adolescents: integration of self-determination and planned behavior theories
Authors: Yannis Theodorakis, Charalampos Krommidas, Aristea Karamitrou, Aikaterini Violatzi, Maria Angeli, Evangelos Galanis, Mary Hassandra, Nikos Comoutos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/objectiveEco-friendly behaviors (EFB) represent an important domain at the intersection of health, quality of life, and environmental sustainability. The present study focuses on two related subdomains: PA-related energy-saving behaviors (PA–RESB) and energy-saving behaviors (ESB). More specifically, this study tested an integrated Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) model to identify the psychological determinants of PA–RESB and ESB among adolescents.MethodsA cross-sectional sample of 391 Greek adolescents (12–15 years) completed measures of basic psychological need satisfaction (autonomy, competence, relatedness), TPB constructs (attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, intentions), and PA–RESB and ESB. Two structural equation models were tested (one for each outcome).ResultsIn both models, competence had the strongest association with TPB constructs, showing positive associations with attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. Across models, perceived behavioral control and attitudes significantly predicted intention, and intention significantly predicted PA–RESB and ESB. The direct perceived behavioral control→behavior paths were not significant.ConclusionIntegrating SDT and TPB helps explain adolescents’ eco-friendly behaviors in both physically active and household energy-saving domains. Interventions that strengthen perceived competence and perceived behavioral control may support stronger intentions and greater engagement in PA–RESB and the ESB.Keywords: Eco-friendly behavior, Energy conservation, Physical activity, Self-determination theory, Theory of planned behavior

Background

Regular engagement in physical activity (PA) during adolescence is a key determinant of physical health, psychological well-being, and long-term health-related behaviors [1]. Despite the well-established benefits of PA, global levels of PA among adolescents remain suboptimal, highlighting the need to better understand the psychological and contextual factors that promote sustained engagement in physically active lifestyles. In this context, forms of PA embedded in daily life, such as walking, cycling, and active transportation, represent accessible and health-enhancing behaviors that can be maintained throughout the lifespan.

Environmentally related physical activities, such as walking, cycling, and other forms of active transportation, are important categories of health-enhancing PA [2]. These behaviors contribute to daily energy expenditure, cardiovascular fitness, and health and well-being, while simultaneously supporting environmentally sustainable lifestyles [3]. From an exercise science perspective, such activities are increasingly recognized as viable targets for PA promotion, particularly among adolescents, as they integrate movement into everyday routines rather than relying solely on structured exercise.

Eco-friendly behaviors (EFB) represent an important domain at the intersection of health, quality of life, and environmental sustainability. The present study focuses on two related subdomains: PA-related energy-saving behaviors (PA–RESB) and energy-saving behaviors (ESB). While both contribute to environmental sustainability, they differ conceptually and operationally. ESB primarily reflect stationary, habit-based, and routine household actions, such as switching off lights when not needed or relying on natural light. In contrast, PA-RESB, such as walking, cycling, using public transportation, and spending time in natural environments serve a dual function by enhancing individual health while simultaneously fostering environmentally responsible lifestyles [3, 4]. Treating PA-RESB and ESB as separate outcomes, rather than as indicators of a single higher-order construct, allows for a more granular understanding of how adolescents adopt environmentally responsible lifestyles that either require physical effort or consist of simple household habits.

Engagement in PA and eco-friendly behaviors is shaped by psychosocial factors, including motivation, attitudes, perceived competence, and environmental awareness [5]. The global scientific community increasingly recognizes that environmentally harmful behaviors pose not only ecological threats but also significant public health risks [6]. Growing climate-related concerns have intensified interest in behavior-change and motivational approaches that support sustainable lifestyle choices and relative policies [7, 8]. The intersection between psychosocial functioning and pro-environmental behavior highlights the value of integrating behavior-change and motivational mechanisms into strategies that promote active and sustainable lifestyles.

Self-Determination Theory (SDT) posits that satisfaction of the needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness supports internalization and self-regulation of behavior [9]. In the physical activity domain, need satisfaction is consistently associated with more autonomous motivation, greater enjoyment, and more sustained engagement in active lifestyles [10]. Similarly, in pro-environmental and sustainability-related behaviors, need-supportive contexts are linked to stronger internalization and maintenance of environmentally responsible actions [11, 12]. Accordingly, autonomy, competence, and relatedness are plausible motivational antecedents of adolescents’ eco-friendly behaviors, including both PA–RESB and ESB, as follows.

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) explains behavior through attitudes, subjective norms (SN), and perceived behavioral control (PBC), which predict intentions and subsequent behavior [13]. TPB has been widely applied to physical activity, active transportation and pro-environmental behaviors, including in adolescent populations, where attitudes and PBC are typically strong predictors of intentions to be active.[14, 15] TPB has also been used to explain energy-saving and other environmentally responsible behaviors, indicating that the same social–cognitive mechanisms can shape conservation-related intentions and actions [16, 17]. Therefore, TPB provides an appropriate framework for examining adolescents’ PA–RESB and ESB in the present study.

Exposure to supportive natural and built environments (e.g., green/blue spaces and activity-supportive settings) has been linked to higher physical activity and indicators of psychological well-being, and may also reinforce environmentally responsible orientations [18, 19, 20]. At the population level, pro-environmental attitudes, perceived efficacy, and related social–cognitive factors are consistently associated with environmentally responsible behaviors and intentions, providing additional support for focusing on lifestyle domains where health and sustainability co-benefit [21, 22]. Active mobility (walking and cycling) is a prominent example of this overlap, with evidence supporting its relevance for both health promotion and sustainable transport policy [3].

SDT and TPB are complementary frameworks: SDT highlights motivational antecedents through basic psychological need satisfaction, whereas TPB specifies the social–cognitive predictors of intention and behavior. In PA research, need-supportive social contexts (e.g., perceived autonomy support) and autonomous motivation have been linked to more favorable attitudes and stronger intentions, suggesting that motivational processes can shape TPB determinants [14, 23, 24] Similarly, autonomous motivation has been associated with a greater likelihood of adopting active modes of transportation, indicating relevance to everyday eco-friendly behavior domains [25].

In the integrated framework of the present study, the satisfaction of basic psychological needs is conceptualized as the foundational basis for the development of socio-cognitive beliefs. For instance, when teenagers feel genuinely capable and effective in their environmental efforts (competence), they naturally tend to view these actions more favorably (attitudes) and feel a stronger sense of personal control over them (PBC). A sense of belonging and connection to others (relatedness) makes them more attuned to social expectations (subjective norms). Meanwhile, experiencing true volition (autonomy) helps them bridge their own personal values with a sustainable, positive mindset.

Notably, the existing literature offers different ways to combine these theories. Some researchers, for example, treat autonomous motivation as an active middleman that bridges need satisfaction and TPB constructs [26]. However, in the present study, direct links from basic psychological needs to the TPB variables were deliberately examined. This structural approach was adopted to isolate and identify which underlying need—competence, relatedness, or autonomy—plays the most pivotal role in shaping the specific beliefs necessary for both physically demanding (PA-RESB) and everyday household (ESB) eco-friendly habits.

The study aimed to examine the psychological determinants of eco-friendly behaviors (EFB), operationalized as PA-related energy-saving behaviors (PA–RESB) and energy-saving behaviors (ESB), among adolescents, using an integrated model grounded in SDT and the TPB. From an exercise science perspective, this study focused on physically active behaviors embedded in daily life, such as walking and cycling, which contribute to adolescents’ overall PA levels. It was hypothesized that satisfaction of basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, and relatedness) would positively predict attitudes, PBC, and intentions, which in turn would be associated with greater engagement in both PA–RESB and ESB behaviors.

Methods

Participants and procedure

In this cross-sectional, population-based study, participants were recruited through school administrators, who were interested in exploring issues related to environmental awareness among their students. The target population consisted of children enrolled in schools in central Greece. The sample size was determined a priori via power analysis to ensure adequate statistical power. A quota sampling strategy was employed, stratified by age, gender, and geographic region, to enhance the representativeness of the sample.

The final sample consisted of 391 high school students, aged 12 to 15 years (M = 13.56, SD = 0.93), drawn from eight high school units across five distinct areas of central Greece. In terms of gender distribution, 160 participants identified as boys, 229 as girls, and two students did not report their gender. Regarding school grade, 107 students were enrolled in the first grade, 187 in the second grade, and 87 in the third grade, while ten participants did not report their grade. It is important to mention here that the 12-year-old students were in the first grade of high school. In terms of ethnicity, the majority of students (n = 354) identified as Greek, 18 as Albanian, and 14 reported other ethnicities. Ethnicity data were unavailable for five students.

Initially, the research approval was obtained from by the Bioethics Committee of the authors’ university was obtained (Ref: 09 / Date: 06.02.2024). Parents/guardians and participants were then fully informed about the study’s purpose and that their participation was entirely voluntary. After the participants and their parents/guardians provided written consent, data were collected during a PE class by an experienced researcher. Students were given instructions on how to complete the survey, were assured that there were no right or wrong answers, and were told that they could ask questions if they needed help. To ensure that students submitted valid reports, we used both attention control questions (reverse items) and reading aloud techniques. Finally, a relevant check was performed at the end. In general, no problems were observed during the completion of the questionnaires which lasted approximately 15 min.

Measures

Participants were informed that the study was interested in their opinions on eco-friendly and energy-saving behaviors, with some examples provided. They were then asked to write down any thoughts that came to mind regarding the performance of these behaviors. Participants then completed measures assessing attitudes, subjective norms (SN), perceived behavioral control (PBC), and intentions to perform eco-friendly behaviors. In particular, participants completed the following measures assessing attitudes, SN, PBC, and intentions to perform eco-friendly behaviors, following Ajzen’s [27] recommendations, and their adaptation to the Greek language [28].

Attitude toward eco-friendly behaviors

Attitudes were assessed using five items that asked participants to evaluate the statement “For me, performing eco-friendly behaviors on a regular basis during the next 6 months would be…” using eight 7-point bipolar adjective scales, such as “useless–useful,” “unpleasant–pleasant,” “good–bad,” “happy–sad,” and “pretty–ugly.” It is important to mention here that three items from the factor “Attitudes” (items 2, 4, and 5) were reverse-coded. The Cronbach’s alpha for the scale was 0.78.

PBC

It was measured with two items rated on a 7-point Likert scale: “For me, performing eco-friendly behaviors on a regular basis in the next 6 months would be…” (ranging from “very difficult” to “very easy”), and “I feel that I am able to perform eco-friendly and energy-saving behaviors on a regular basis in the next year” (ranging from “definitely not” to “yes, definitely”). Cronbach’s alpha was 0.68.

SN

It was also assessed with two items on a 7-point Likert scale: “In general, people who are close to me expect me to adopt eco-friendly behaviors on a regular basis during the next 6 months” and “People who are important to me will perform eco-friendly behaviors on a regular basis during the next 6 months.” Participants responded to each item on a 7-point scale ranging from “definitely not” to “yes, definitely.” Cronbach’s alpha was 0.65.

Intentions

Three items were used to assess intention to perform eco-friendly behaviors: “I intent/ I will try/ I am determined/ to perform eco-friendly behaviors on a regular basis during the next 6 months.” Responses were provided on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from “definitely not” to “yes, definitely.” The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.76.

Basic psychological needs

Basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, and relatedness) were measured by using well-established scales, and particularly the relevant instrument related to energy saving behaviors [29, 30].Three items from each subscale were used. For the measurement of perceived autonomy, a sample of items used included: “I decide what I do to save energy” and “I am free to choose what I do to save energy.” For the measurement of perceived competence, the items included: “I feel confident in my ability to save energy”, “I am capable of controlling the amount of energy I use”, and “I feel able to meet the challenge of reducing my energy use.” For the measurement of relatedness, sample items were: “I feel connected with other people who are saving energy”, “I share a common bond with other people who are saving energy,” and “I feel a sense of camaraderie with other people who are saving energy.” Responses to all items were provided on a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Cronbach’s was 0.65, 0.74 and 0.83 respectively.

Eco-friendly behaviors

The instrument used to measure eco-friendly behaviors consists of two distinct subscales [28]. The physical activity–related energy-saving behaviors (PA–RESB) subscale includes six items (e.g., “I go to school by bike or on foot,” “At home, I prefer to use the stairs instead of the elevator,” “In general, for short distances, I prefer to walk, both in winter and summer”). These items were adapted from the Environmentally Significant Behaviors Associated with Physical Activity scale [24] and the pro-environmental behavior scale [31]. The energy-saving behaviors subscale consists of four items (e.g., “I switch off the lights even if I am not using them for a short time,” “I do not switch on the lights if natural light is sufficient”). Participants indicated how frequently they engaged in each behavior using a 7-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (very often). Two items from the factor “PA–RESB” factor (items 1 and 5) were reverse-coded. Cronbach’s alphas were 0.61 and 0.62, respectively. All the scales showed acceptable internal reliability. Based on Aiken’s suggestions [32] that an alpha coefficient of 0.60 is also acceptable the above scales were retained.

Statistical analyses

Initially, descriptive statistics, reliabilities and correlations were performed. Subsequently, separate Confirmatory Factor Analyses (CFA) were used to examine the construct validity of the instruments. To test links between SDT variables, TPB variables, and the two EFB outcomes (PA–RESB and ESB), two Structural Equation Models (SEMs) were tested. In particular, the fit indices used to evaluate the construct validity of the tools (CFA) and the SEM models were the chi-square (χ2), the degrees of freedom (df), the relative Chi-square (χ2/df; acceptable range of less than 3.0) [33], the Comparative Fit Index (CFI; values > 0.90), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI; values > 0.90), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA; values < 0.05), the 90% RMSEA, and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR; values < 0.08) [34, 35]. The Composite Reliability (CR; > 0.70) and the Average Variance Extracted (AVE; > 0.50) were used to further examine the internal validity and the convergent validity of the scales, respectively [36, 37]. Missing data were minimal (< 5%) except for the “Attitudes”, where missing data were 8.6%. All data were handled using the listwise deletion method. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Confirmatory Factor Analyses (CFA)

Separate CFAs were used to examine the instruments’ construct validity. As in previous studies [38], the selection of the items was based on the best possible conceptual representation of the construct. Items for each construct were chosen from the fitting CFA models.

The CFA results of the Basic Psychological Needs for energy saving behaviors scale (autonomy, competence, relatedness) showed acceptable fit-indices [χ2(24) = 46.104, p < 0.001, χ2/df = 1.921, TLI = 0.961, CFI = 0.974, RMSEA = 0.054, 90% RMSEA = 0.030–0.077, SRMR = 0.047]. The factor loadings ranged from 0.53 to 0.72 for autonomy, 0.64 to 0.77 for competence, and 0.74 to 0.85 for relatedness. The correlations between the three factors ranged from 0.17 to 0.51, while the covariates among them ranged from 0.20 to 0.49.

Similarly, the CFA results of the TPB for Energy Saving Behaviors scale (attitudes, PBC, SN, intention) showed acceptable fit-indices [χ2(48) = 103.775, p < 0.001, χ2/df = 2.162, TLI = 0.935, CFI = 0.953, RMSEA = 0.061, 90% RMSEA = 0.045–0.077, SRMR = 0.044]. The factor loadings ranged from 0.59 to 0.70 for attitudes, 0.67 to 0.75 for PBC, 0.74 for both items of SN, and 0.63 to 0.80 for intention. The correlations between the four factors ranged from 0.48 to 0.72, while the covariates among them ranged from 0.50 to 1.09.

Regarding the Eco-friendly Behaviors tool (Environmentally PA-related Behaviors, Energy Saving Behaviors), the initial model did not show acceptable goodness-of-fit indices [χ2(34) = 136.037, p < 0.001, χ2/df = 4.001, TLI = 0.622, CFI = 0.714, RMSEA = 0.097, 90% RMSEA = 0.081–0.115, SRMR = 0.079]. After removing two items (item 1 due to high covariance and item 4 due to low factor loadings of less than 0.30) from the PA–RESB, the final model revealed acceptable fit-indices [χ2(19) = 40.500, p < 0.01, χ2/df = 2.132, TLI = 0.874, CFI = 0.915, RMSEA = 0.060, 90% RMSEA = 0.034–0.085, SRMR = 0.048]. The factor loadings ranged from 0.37 to 0.72 for Environmentally PA-related Behaviors, and 0.37 to 0.72 for Energy Saving Behaviors. The correlation between the two factors was 0.43, while the covariates between them was 0.47.

Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) for eco-friendly behaviors

Two separate structural equation models (SEM) were used to determine the paths between SDT (autonomy, competence, relatedness) and TPB variables (attitudes, PBC, SN, intention) to predict PA-related energy-saving behaviors (PA–RESB) and energy-saving behaviors (ESB), respectively.

Across the two SEM models, significant covariances were observed between competence and relatedness (Estimate = 0.47, S.E. = 0.09, C.R. = 5.254, p < 0.001), autonomy and relatedness (Estimate = 0.11, S.E. = 0.05, C.R. = 2.248, p < 0.05), autonomy and competence (Estimate = 0.38, S.E. = 0.08, C.R. = 4.840, p < 0.001), and the error of attitudes with the error of SN (Estimate = 0.19, S.E. = 0.05, C.R. = 4.097, p < 0.001 for PA-RESB; Estimate = 0.21, S.E. = 0.05, C.R. = 4.258, p < 0.001 for ESB).

Regarding PA-RESB (Model 1), the initial model did not show acceptable goodness-of-fit indices [χ2(258) = 482.927, p < 0.001, χ2/df = 1.872; TLI = 0.892, CFI = 0.908, RMSEA = 0.053, 90% RMSEA = 0.045–0.060, SRMR = 0.063]. After controlling for the high covariance between the error of attitudes and the error of SN, the assessment of the model showed acceptable goodness-of-fit indices [χ2(257) = 459.640, p < 0.001, χ2/df = 1.788; TLI = 0.903, CFI = 0.917, RMSEA = 0.050, 90% RMSEA = 0.042–0.057, SRMR = 0.060]. A review of the standardized regression weights for the indicator variables showed that all loaded significantly (p < 0.001) and substantially on their respective latent constructs, suggesting that the measurement scales were valid (factor loadings ranged from 0.39 to 0.85; Fig. 1).

Paths between SDT and TPB variables, and PA-related energy-saving behaviors. Notes. PA = Physical Activity; PBC = Perceived Behavioral Control; er = error. *p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001

Examining the structural paths for TPB constructs, after controlling for significant covariates among SDT variables, the paths from autonomy to attitudes was not significant (β = 0.094, p = 0.307), nor was the path to SN (β = -0.017, p = 0.853) or to PBC (β = -0.014, p = 0.884). In contrast, competence significantly predicted all the three TPB constructs. A higher sense of competence was strongly associated with more positive attitudes (β = 0.48, p < 0.001) and greater perceived social pressure, as seen in its effect on SN (β = 0.53, p < 0.001). The most powerful relationship from competence was to PBC (β = 0.63, p < 0.001), indicating a strong connection between feeling competent and perceiving control. The relatedness significantly predicted SN (β = 0.63, p < 0.001) suggesting that feeling connected to others influences perceived social norms. However, relatedness did not significantly predict attitudes (β = 0.050, p = 0.496) or PBC (β = 0.122, p = 0.114).

In the next stage of the model, which focused on the predictors of intention, the TPB constructs were examined. After controlling for significant covariates between the error of attitudes and the error of SN, PBC emerged as the strongest predictor of intention (β = 0.59, p < 0.001), showing that an individual’s perception of their ability to performing PA-RESB is the most influential factor in forming their intention. Attitudes was also a strong and significant predictor, where a more positive attitude significantly increased behavioral intention (β = 0.30, p < 0.001). SN was a significant, though weaker, predictor of intention (β = 0.23, p < 0.05), suggesting that social pressure had a modest effect. The final stage of the model confirmed the TPB’s central hypothesis of the TPB by linking intention to actual reported behavior. Intention was a significant predictor of PA-RESB (β = 0.49, p < 0.001), whereas PBC did not have a significant, direct effect on PA-RESB (β = -0.145, p = 0.539).

Regarding energy saving behaviors (Model 2), the initial model did not show acceptable goodness-of-fit indices [χ2(258) = 497.104, p < 0.001, χ2/df = 1.927; TLI = 0.887, CFI = 0.903; RMSEA = 0.054, 90% RMSEA = 0.047–0.061, SRMR = 0.067] (Fig. 2). After controlling for significant covariates between the error of attitudes and the error of SN, and after deleting one item of attitudes (item 5) due to low factor loadings (< 0.30), the analysis of fit revealed that the model showed acceptable fit [χ2(234) = 415.066, p < 0.001, χ2/df = 1.774; TLI = 0.908, CFI = 0.922, RMSEA = 0.049, 90% RMSEA = 0.042–0.057, SRMR = 0.063]. All factor loadings were above the threshold of 0.30, with most being well above 0.60, indicating good convergent validity for the latent constructs.

Paths between SDT and TPB variables, and energy saving behaviors. Notes. PBC = Perceived Behavioral Control; er = error. *p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001

From the standardized regression weights, after controlling for significant covariates among SDT variables, competence appeared to be a strong predictor of attitude (β = 0.48), SN (β = 0.53, p < 0.001), and particularly PBC (β = 0.62, p < 0.001). This may indicate that when people feel competent in their ability to save energy, they develop a more positive attitude, perceive greater social pressure, and feel a stronger sense of personal control over their actions. Relatedness also showed a significant positive effect on SN (β = 0.21, p < 0.01) after controlling for significant covariates among SDT variables, while autonomy did not appear to be a significant predictor of the TPB factors in this model.

Consistent with the TPB, attitudes (β = 0.31, p < 0.001), SN (β = 0.21, p < 0.05), and PBC (β = 0.59, p < 0.001) significantly predicted the intention to save energy, after controlling for significant covariates between the error of attitudes and the error of SN, respectively. PBC was a strong predictor of intention (β = 0.59, p < 0.001), but the direct path from PBC to ESB was weak and negative (β = -0.095, p = 0.698), and notably non-significant in the unstandardized weights, suggesting that its influence is primarily mediated through intention. Subsequently, intention demonstrated a strong, positive, and significant influence on actual ESB (β = 0.59, p < 0.001), confirming that intention is a direct precursor to action.

In conclusion, the results highlight the pivotal role of perceived competence, which was positively associated with attitudes, SN, and PBC across the two models. Furthermore, attitudes and PBC consistently emerged as the strongest predictors of intention, which itself was a significant predictor of both eco-friendly behavior outcomes (PA–RESB and ESB).

Discussion

This study examined an integrated SDT–TPB model to predict adolescents’ PA–RESB and ESB using SEM models. The results indicated a robust pathway between SDT and TPB variables in relation to eco-friendly behaviors (EFB). Perceived competence emerged as the strongest factor among the core components of SDT, showing consistent associations with attitudes, SN, and PBC in both the PA–RESB and ESB models. Motivation has long been recognized as a key factor in attitude formation [39, 40]. Higher perceived competence may strengthen attitudes, SN, and PBC toward eco-friendly behaviors (EFB), including both PA–RESB and ESB, among adolescents. Furthermore, attitudes and PBC played particularly important roles, explaining a substantial proportion of the variance in adolescents’ intentions to engage in both PA–RESB and ESB. This perspective offers valuable insights into the mechanisms through which eco-friendly behaviors are fostered in adolescents, highlighting the pivotal role of attitudes, PBC, and intentions as mediators of eco-friendly behavior. By examining these mediating variables, this study points to new directions for promoting environmental sensitivity within this population.

This study also demonstrates that attitudes are positively associated with intentions and provides compelling evidence for the predictive utility of the TPB in this context [14, 15, 40]. Moreover, higher levels of need satisfaction have been positively linked to the sustained adoption of environmental actions [11, 12]. In the domain of energy-saving behaviors, both theoretical frameworks have shown positive predictive effects [16, 30]. Accordingly, research on environmentally oriented health-related activities, such as active mobility, walking, and cycling, suggests that these behaviors foster individuals’ connections with both natural and built environments, further highlighting the relevance of SDT and TPB in promoting sustainable lifestyle practices [21, 41]. Additionally, engagement in recommended levels of PA has been associated with increased pro-environmental behaviors, including recycling, purchasing seasonal and locally grown products, adopting green travel practices, and encouraging others to engage in these behaviors [4].

The present study revealed a meaningful sequence of relationships within the ESB model: the greater the level of perceived competence among adolescents, the more positive their attitudes and the higher their perceived behavioral control. Specifically, perceived competence positively influenced adolescents’ attitudes toward energy conservation and enhanced their perceived behavioral control over engaging in energy-saving behaviors (ESB). In turn, this increased sense of control substantially contributed to students’ intentions to adopt and maintain ESB, while subjective norms demonstrated only a modest association with intentions.

Previous studies have indicated that when people feel their autonomy is supported, they are much more likely to engage in free-time PA, as a supportive environment actively shapes positive attitudes and firmer intentions, which makes a strong case for integrating autonomy support directly into the TPB framework [25].When individuals are driven by their own genuine interest (autonomous motivation), they tend to develop better attitudes, feel a stronger sense of personal control (PBC), and even perceive more supportive social norms, whereas controlled motivation positively predicted SN. Autonomous motivation, controlled motivation, attitudes, and SN mediated the relationship between perceived autonomy support and behavioral intentions. In addition, the findings indicated that perceived autonomy support from peers was consistently associated with students’ intentions to engage in PA during their leisure time [14]. Similarly, statistically significant relationships have been reported between autonomous motivation and attitudes, PBC, intentions toward, and actual engagement in out-of-school PA [42].

Interestingly, the link between self-determined motivation and perceived control is particularly potent in younger age groups. A likely developmental reason for this is that younger adolescents might not yet fully separate the feeling of “I am choosing to do this” from “I am good at this.” For them, feeling autonomous is deeply intertwined with feeling competent in their work. Finally, researchers have observed that highly self-determined individuals also report stronger subjective norms. While this might initially seem contradictory, it makes sense when we remember that subjective norms essentially represent social pressure; even when teenagers act under their own volition, they remain highly attuned to the external expectations and guiding beliefs of their social world regarding health behaviors [43].

One of our more surprising findings was that perceived autonomy did not significantly predict any of the TPB constructs in either model. While this might seem counterintuitive at first glance, a closer look at the daily reality of adolescents offers a highly practical explanation. During this developmental stage, teenagers actually have very little say over how energy is used at home. Those habits are almost entirely dictated by parental rules and established family routines. The exact same logic applies to their transportation choices. Whether an adolescent actively commutes by walking or biking is heavily restricted by outside factors, such as how far away the school is, neighborhood safety concerns, and ultimate parental permission. As a result, even if a young person deeply cares about sustainability, their personal sense of independence does not strongly influence their attitudes or perceived control over the situation. The structural boundaries of their environment essentially override their personal volition. The unique patterns we observed in these relationships likely stem from the novelty of our approach, as this is the first-time eco-friendly behaviors have been examined through this specific integrated lens. The developmental stage of our participants also provides critical context for these variations. Because adolescents operate under distinct structural limits compared to adults, future interventions should adapt their strategies accordingly. Building on the classification system proposed by Ahmadi et al. [44] practitioners need to move beyond simply presenting cognitive choices, which teenagers often lack the real-world freedom to act upon. Instead, the focus should shift toward cultivating a genuine, personal appreciation and internal value for sustainable habits. It is also plausible that in the family-centric cultural context of Greece, where parental supervision remains high during early adolescence, autonomy is less salient than feeling competent and supported by the community.

Consistent with the TPB, attitudes, subjective norms (SN), and perceived behavioral control (PBC) emerged as significant factors shaping students’ intentions to engage in PA–RESB and ESB. Among these, PBC was the strongest determinant of intention, followed by attitudes, whereas SN exerted the weakest influence.

An important takeaway from our analysis results is that PBC does not directly result in actual behavior in either model. While PBC was the strongest predictor of intention, its impact on ultimate behavior was entirely channeled through that intermediate step. This suggests that feeling capable and in control is crucial for forming the desire to save energy or walk/cycle, but it is not sufficient to bypass the intention stage and directly trigger actions. This dynamic clearly highlights a potential “intention-behavior gap.” Adolescents may know exactly how to save energy or feel perfectly confident riding a bike (reflecting high PBC), but translating this control into immediate action often requires an explicit intention to overcome systemic barriers, such as everyday obstacles. Real-world barriers, such as unsafe streets, poor weather conditions, or just deeply ingrained family routines, can easily disrupt their plans. This finding underscores a crucial distinction between an adolescent’s belief in their own capabilities and the actual, objective autonomy their physical and social environment allows them.

Furthermore, adolescents’ intentions significantly predicted their actual engagement in both PA–RESB and ESB. Consistent with both SEMs, perceived behavioral control influenced PA–RESB and ESB primarily indirectly via intention, as the direct PBC→behavior paths were not significant.

This study extends the existing literature by integrating two well-established yet conceptually distinct psychological theories into a unified framework for examining adolescents’ eco-friendly behaviors. The findings highlight perceived competence as a central psychological determinant that directly influences attitudes, SN, and PBC. By elucidating these relationships, this study offers a more comprehensive understanding of the motivational and cognitive mechanisms that underpinning diverse, yet interconnected, environmentally protective PA behaviors.

Implications

Interventions designed to promote energy conservation and environmentally related physical activity among adolescents should emphasize on enhancing perceived competence and PBC in adopting energy-saving practices. Educational programs may be most effective when they combine self-determined motivational strategies with components that shape positive attitudes and strengthen adolescents’ sense of control over environmentally responsible behaviors. In this context, environmental education delivered within natural settings can be especially beneficial, as it not only fosters ecological responsibility and supports psychological well-being. 20,41 Programs that cultivate a more positive attitude toward nature may further support environmental learning and strengthen pro-environmental orientations that feed into attitudes and intentions [45].

Practical intervention strategies may include goal-setting and skill-building workshops, modeling and peer-learning approaches, and the development of supportive social environments that encourage energy-saving physical activities. Collaborative initiatives involving schools and youth organizations can provide structured opportunities for adolescents to engage in PA-RESB and ESB.

Finally, efforts to promote health-related physical activities, such as walking and cycling should account for the broader built environment, including buildings, neighborhood design, transportation infrastructure, green spaces, and supportive public policies. Sport and PA communities can play an important role in mitigating post-natural disaster contexts [46, 47]. Environmental and policy-level factors shape opportunities for physical activity and can substantially influence population health outcomes, including broader health and well-being outcomes [6, 48, 49, 50].

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. One notable limitation of this study is its reliance on self-reported measures of PA–RESB and ESB, which may be subject to social desirability and recall biases. Several factors, however, help to mitigate this concern: participants were assured of anonymity, the sample size was relatively large, and a broad range of energy-saving behaviors was assessed using validated instruments. Future research should aim to include larger samples drawn from all school grade levels to enhance representativeness.

Furthermore, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. The directional paths tested in our models reflect theoretical assumptions rather than established temporal sequences; thus, reciprocal relationships in which the adoption of eco-friendly behaviors reinforces perceived competence or attitudes are equally plausible. Future longitudinal and intervention-based studies are required to confirm causality.

Regarding the sample, the use of quota sampling through school administrators may have introduced a selection bias. Schools or administrators willing to participate may already possess a stronger pro-environmental ethos, potentially limiting the representativeness of the findings. Furthermore, measurement invariance across gender or age groups was not tested in this study, which is an important next step to ensure the broader generalizability of the integrated model.

In addition, the measurement of certain constructs relies on short scales following the theory’s recommendations [27] (e.g., two to four items for PBC, SN, and EFB outcomes), which yielded marginal internal consistency reliability coefficients (alpha approx. 0.60 to 0.65, and CR < 0.70) and poor convergent validity (AVE < 0.50). While such values are often encountered in early adolescent samples assessing diverse socio-cognitive beliefs, they introduce measurement error that may attenuate the structural paths in the SEM models and should be interpreted cautiously.

Finally, achieving an acceptable model fit required multiple post-hoc modifications, including the deletion of certain items with low factor loadings and the specification of correlated error terms (e.g., between attitudes and SN). Although these modifications were theoretically justifiable—for instance, attitudes and subjective norms often share variance due to broader social desirability or peer influence in adolescence—they represent adjustments based on data. Consequently, the final structural models should be viewed as partly exploratory, and cross-validation with independent samples is necessary to confirm these structural pathways.

Conclusion

By integrating the conceptual frameworks of two prominent psychological theories, this study identified a comprehensive set of key variables, including basic psychological need satisfaction, attitudes, PBC, and intentions, that contribute to adolescents’ engagement in environmentally related PA and energy-saving behaviors. Future longitudinal and experimental studies are warranted to examine the causal pathways among these constructs, which would substantially advance our understanding of the mechanisms underlying environmentally responsible behaviors. Moreover, cross-cultural replications are necessary to evaluate the generalizability of these findings across diverse sociocultural contexts in the future.
