Interplay between sports nutrition knowledge, lifestyle factors, and body mass index among regularly exercising university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ayşe Nur Songür Bozdağ, Eren Canbolat, Emre Duman, Cansu Memiç İnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to comprehensively examine the complex relationships among sports nutrition knowledge, nutritional status (macro- and micronutrient intake), body mass index (BMI), and lifestyle factors (smoking status, dietary habits including salt preference, daily water consumption, dietary supplement use, and exercise characteristics) in university students who engage in regular exercise.MethodsThis cross-sectional analytical study was conducted between December 2023 and April 2024 with 454 university students (172 males, 282 females) enrolled in universities in İzmir, Türkiye, who self-reported engaging in regular exercise. Data were collected using the Abridged Nutrition for Sports Knowledge Questionnaire (A-NSKQ), two non-consecutive 24-hour dietary recalls (one weekday and one weekend day) analysed with BeBiS® v9.0, and sociodemographic/lifestyle questionnaires. Hierarchical multiple linear regression analysis was employed to identify factors predicting BMI and weekly regular exercise duration. Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis, and Spearman correlation tests were used for statistical analyses (p < 0.05).ResultsMost participants (75.1%) demonstrated inadequate sports nutrition knowledge on the A-NSKQ, with this proportion being higher among females (81.2%) than males (65.1%). Male students had significantly higher weekly total regular exercise duration (p < 0.001), A-NSKQ scores (p < 0.001), and BMI (p < 0.001) compared with females. A-NSKQ level was not associated with macronutrient intake (carbohydrates, protein, fat) (p > 0.05); however, higher A-NSKQ was significantly associated with better intake of certain micronutrients, including vitamin E (p = 0.005), riboflavin (p = 0.021), iron (p = 0.033), and zinc (p = 0.034). Gender (β = −0.436), daily energy intake (β = −0.091), smoking status (β = −0.163), and daily water intake (β = 0.159) were identified as significant predictors of BMI (all p ≤ 0.001), with protein intake (%E; β = 0.091, p = 0.030) showing a weaker positive association, while A-NSKQ score and weekly regular exercise duration were not. The final regression model was significant and accounted for a meaningful proportion of the variance in BMI (R² = 0.245; Adjusted R² = 0.231).ConclusionsDespite being physically active, university students exhibit notably low levels of sports nutrition knowledge, particularly among female students. Although knowledge level does not predict BMI or macronutrient intake, it shows a positive association with the intake of key micronutrients. The current situation highlights the disconnect between theoretical knowledge and its application in practice; in other words, a clear knowledge-behaviour gap. The findings confirm that active university students who engage in regular exercise require not merely theoretical, but directly practical and goal-specific nutrition education to achieve their health and performance objectives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01929-w.Keywords: Sports nutrition, Nutrition knowledge level, University students, Physical activity, Body Mass Index, Knowledge–behaviour gap

Introduction

The transition to university is a highly critical period that shapes both young adults’ current lifestyle habits and their long-term health [1]. When new stressors—such as an increased academic workload, efforts towards financial independence and individual decision-making processes—come together, students can often be drawn into an unhealthy lifestyle without realising it [2–4]. Current studies clearly demonstrate that undergraduate students are increasingly adopting a sedentary lifestyle and experiencing a marked deterioration in their dietary habits. This trend not only restricts daily physical activity but is also associated with a significant decline in meeting recommended physical activity levels during the transition to higher education [5–7]. Over time, these changes negatively affect body composition, potentially increasing the risk of developing obesity and associated noncommunicable chronic diseases [8–10].

Although a sedentary lifestyle is a general trend among students, a significant proportion continue to engage in regular exercise [11–13]. The primary reason our study focuses on physically active individuals is that this group has significantly different physiological and metabolic requirements compared to their sedentary peers. Indeed, when it comes to active students, nutrition plays a key role not only in maintaining general health but also in maximising sporting performance and supporting post-exercise recovery processes [3, 14, 15]. Adequate intake of energy, macronutrients and micronutrients is essential to maximise physical performance, accelerate recovery, prevent potential injuries and maintain energy balance [16, 17]. However, the dual pressure created by intensive training programmes and academic responsibilities often makes it difficult to put these ideal nutritional strategies into practice [18–20].

In line with these physiological requirements, adequate knowledge of sports nutrition forms the basis of dietary habits that support health and sporting performance [21–23]. This level of knowledge encompasses critical elements such as meeting macro- and micronutrient requirements, hydration strategies, and pre- and post-exercise nutritional timing [24, 25]. However, studies indicate that the sports nutrition knowledge levels of university students and athletes generally remain at an ‘inadequate’ or, at best, ‘moderate’ level [26–29]. Furthermore, even if theoretical knowledge is at an adequate level, translating this into daily dietary practices is quite challenging [30–32]. This situation, defined in the literature as the ‘knowledge-behaviour gap’, may stem from various practical and psychosocial barriers, such as budget constraints, time constraints and the complexity of the information required for implementation [31, 33–35].

The Body Mass Index (BMI), regarded as a key indicator of these dietary habits, is frequently used in the literature as a standard health indicator [36]. However, the relationship between nutritional knowledge and BMI has not been fully elucidated; existing studies generally present conflicting or inconclusive results [37–40, 41]. In particular, the limitations of BMI in reflecting body composition should be borne in mind in physically active individuals. Indeed, high muscle mass in this group can directly increase BMI values without an actual increase in body fat [42, 43]. Consequently, it should be noted that BMI cannot be explained by dietary information alone; it is shaped by the interaction of multidimensional factors such as genetic background, socioeconomic status, eating behaviours, physical activity and psychological factors [44–46].

One of the most significant factors determining the relationship between health behaviours and body composition is gender [47, 48]. Although male students generally have higher rates of exercise participation, findings regarding sports nutrition awareness vary considerably between the sexes [49–54]. On the other hand, as lifestyle factors such as hydration directly influence metabolic processes, examining the interaction of these variables with nutrition and body composition in active individuals is of great importance [55, 56]. Despite all these multifaceted interactions, research in the literature that adopts a holistic approach to sports nutrition knowledge, detailed macro- and micronutrient intake, and BMI specifically among university students who exercise regularly in Türkiye is quite limited.

The aim of this study is to comprehensively examine the relationships between sports nutrition knowledge, nutritional status, lifestyle factors (smoking, dietary habits, water consumption, supplement use and exercise characteristics) and BMI in university students who engage in regular exercise, thereby addressing this gap in the literature. Mechanistically, nutrition knowledge is likely to affect body weight in an indirect manner, since its impact depends on the extent to which it is converted into daily food choices and improved diet quality. In contrast, lifestyle behaviours such as habitual water intake, smoking and total energy consumption are more directly involved in the regulation of energy balance, which may make them stronger correlates of BMI than knowledge level itself. Considering the current literature, it is hypothesised that an increase in sports nutrition knowledge will be positively associated with more adequate nutrient intake. Furthermore, it was hypothesised that there would be significant gender-based differences in terms of exercise habits, nutritional awareness and anthropometric measurements. In addition, it is thought that lifestyle factors such as energy intake and hydration status would be more decisive in explaining changes in BMI compared to nutritional knowledge alone or duration of exercise. Finally, this study aims to contribute to a better understanding of the knowledge-behaviour gap in the literature by examining the extent to which the theoretical nutritional knowledge acquired by active young adults translates into daily dietary practices and physical health indicators. It is anticipated that the findings will lay the groundwork for the development of targeted nutrition education programmes and provide university health services, nutrition and sports science professionals with important data for practical application.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study was a cross-sectional analytical conducted from December 2023 to April 2024 among students attending higher education in İzmir. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they (i) were aged 18 years or older, (ii) were enrolled as a university student at the time of data collection, and (iii) had been engaging in regular exercise on at least two days per week for a minimum of three consecutive months prior to enrolment. Also, the data collection was completed through face-to-face interviewing that lasted from 15 to 20 min. To make the sample more accurate regarding the overall physical activity dynamics observed in the country, this study was limited to people with an exercise routine. For the purposes of this study, exercise refers to physical activity that is planned, structured, and performed regularly with the goal of maintaining or improving physical fitness. To distinguish regular exercise behaviour from temporary increases in activity, the three-month consistency requirement was applied as a key eligibility threshold. No further exclusions were applied based on weekly frequency beyond this minimum. Exercise type, history, and weekly frequency were all recorded as analytical variables to provide a detailed characterisation of the sample.

Sample size

The G*Power software program (version 3.1.9.7) was used to conduct an a priori power analysis for the sample size determination. The sample size was determined with 5% error probability (α = 0.05) and 95% statistical power (1-β = 0.95) and effect size was selected to be 0.20. The researchers aimed to reach 390 respondents at minimum. As a result of the data collection process, 454 participants who met all criteria and provided complete data were included in the final analysis group, exceeding the targeted sample size.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Ethical approval for the study protocol was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of İzmir Katip Çelebi University (Approval No: 0534, Date: 23.11.2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrolment in the study.

Data collection instruments

Sociodemographic and anthropometric data

During the data collection phase, face-to-face interviews were chosen as the primary method to enhance engagement and data quality. A structured questionnaire (Supplementary File 1) was administered to enable a comprehensive analysis of participants’ demographic profiles, general health profiles and daily living practices. Through this form, in addition to standard parameters such as age, gender and socioeconomic status, information regarding clinical history, dietary patterns, supplement use and sources of information on sports nutrition was gathered. To enhance the accuracy of the statistical analysis and preserve the power of regression models in subsequent stages, data on cigarette consumption was processed as a continuous variable based on ‘daily quantity’. To minimise the risk of reporting errors commonly encountered in fluid intake data, a standard cup size (200 mL) was used as a reference in the questionnaire. Salt preference was assessed using the response categories “unsalted”, “low-salt”, and “salty”. In addition, body weight and height were self-reported by the participants based on their most recent measurements and were not directly measured by the researchers. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). BMI values were categorized according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification into four groups: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²) [57].

Exercise characteristics

Exercise characteristics were self-reported. Participants were asked about the type of regular exercise, duration of regular participation, weekly exercise frequency, and the duration of each exercise session. Weekly exercise duration (min/week) was calculated by multiplying weekly exercise frequency by session duration and was used as a continuous variable in the analyses. No standardized physical activity questionnaire was used, and exercise intensity was not assessed.

24-Hour dietary recall

Participants completed two separate 24-hour dietary recalls (one weekday and one weekend day) to assess a 2-day intake, during researcher-administered face-to-face interviews. The recall form included morning, mid-morning, lunch, afternoon, evening, and night eating occasions. Furthermore, to ensure that the quantities consumed were determined as accurately as possible, standard kitchen measures and photographs of food portions were used during the interviews [58].

Nutrient intake was analysed using BeBiS® v9.0 software (Nutrition Information System, Stuttgart, Germany; Turkish Version). Daily intakes were compared with age- and gender-specific reference values from the 2022 Turkish Nutrition Guide [59]. The Estimated Average Requirement (EAR) was used to assess the risk of micronutrient inadequacy. For nutrients without an EAR, Adequate Intake (AI) was applied (AI for fiber = 25 g/day). To verify data reliability, the consistency between participants’ reported food intakes and their general lifestyle habits was examined qualitatively by the researchers. This verification step enabled a much more robust interpretation of the findings regarding energy intake, whilst also allowing for the consideration of the inherent tendency towards ‘underreporting’ in self-reported questionnaires.

Abridged Nutrition for Sports Knowledge Questionnaire (A-NSKQ)

Participants’ levels of knowledge regarding sports nutrition were assessed using the “A-NSKQ”, originally developed by Trakman et al. [29] and validated for the Turkish language by Temizyürek [60]. The original A-NSKQ comprised 35 items; the validated Turkish version contains 26 items. This questionnaire broadly covers sub-headings such as nutritional requirements, food contents, macro- and micronutrients, hydration, alcohol consumption and dietary supplements. To minimise participants’ reliance on guesswork, the questionnaire includes multiple-choice questions as well as three-option response formats such as “agree/disagree/not sure” or “yes/no/not sure”.

During the data analysis phase, both a raw total score (0–26) and a percentage score [(number of correct answers × 100) / 26] were calculated from participants’ responses (range: 0–100%). A higher score indicates a higher level of knowledge regarding sports nutrition. In line with standard evaluation criteria in the literature, the percentage scores obtained were categorised into four different levels of knowledge proficiency: inadequate (0–49%), moderate (50–65%), good (66–75%) and excellent (76–100%). The Turkish version demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.686) [60].

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). According to distribution characteristics categorical variables were expressed as frequency (n) and percentage (%), continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range [Q1–Q3] in descriptive statistics. The suitability of continuous variables for a normal distribution was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. As the data did not meet the assumption of normal distribution, non-parametric statistical methods were preferred for between-group comparisons and correlation analyses.

The Mann-Whitney U test was conducted to determine group differences between two independent groups, and effect size calculations utilized the r = |Z|/√N formula. The relationships between categorical variables were analysed using the Pearson Chi-Square test while the strength of the relationship was reported using Cramér’s V coefficient. Correlations between continuous variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient and visualised in the form of a heat map. The Kruskal-Wallis test (H statistic) was used to compare groups in three or more independent groups. Dunn post-hoc multiple comparison test with Bonferroni correction was applied to detect sources of inter-group differences where differences were observed. Groups showing significant differences were indicated in the tables with different superscript letters.

In addition, data were predicted using two different three-stage hierarchical multiple linear regression models to predict the indicators of BMI and the duration of exercise per week. The modelling strategy consisted of three stages, that is, in the first stage Model 1 was adjusted by the age and gender variables. In the second stage, Model 2 additionally included the other outcome variable as a predictor. Subsequently, in the final stage, Model 3 was adjusted by a comprehensive set of control variables including the intake of energy and protein, hydration status, smoking status as well as A-NSKQ scores. Regression analysis also displays the unstandardised coefficients (B), standard errors (SE), standardised beta coefficients (β) and R2 and adjusted R2 values. As part of the model diagnostics, it was checked by Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) that the issue of multicollinearity does not arise, and that all VIF values were below 2.2, indicating no multicollinearity concern. All analyses were considered statistically significant at two-tailed p < 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic and lifestyle profile

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics of the 454 university students (282 females, 62.1%; 172 males,37.9%) who completed the study. The median age was 21.0 years [IQR: 20.0–23.0], and while gender differences in age reached statistical significance, the actual effect size was small (p = 0.019; r = 0.110). Males had significantly higher body weight (median = 80.0 kg) and BMI (median = 24.3 kg/m²) than females (56.5 kg and 20.8 kg/m², respectively; p < 0.001). Furthermore, the prevalence of overweight (32.6%) and obesity (8.7%) was markedly more pronounced among males (p < 0.001). Regarding lifestyle habits, we observed that salt preferences did not differ significantly between genders (p = 0.431). However, striking differences were observed in the other behavioural patterns examined. It was observed that the daily water consumption level, calculated using standard 200 mL units as a reference, was higher in male participants than in female participants (median: 10.0 vs. 8.0 glasses/day; p < 0.001). Furthermore, smoking (68.0%) and the use of dietary supplements (28.5%) were significantly more prevalent in males compared to females (39.0% and 15.2%, respectively; p < 0.001). Among the participants who reported supplement use, the most frequently consumed types included multivitamin complexes (36%), creatine (30.7%) and whey protein (25.3%).

Exercise characteristics and sports nutrition knowledge

Participants’ exercise characteristics and A-NSKQ scores by gender are presented in Table 2. Although both genders reported a median exercise frequency of 3 days per week, males engaged in significantly longer sessions (median: 80 min) and achieved a higher total weekly exercise duration (median: 210 min) than females (60 min and 180 min, respectively; p < 0.001). A-NSKQ total scores were significantly higher in males (median = 11.0) than in females (median = 9.0; p < 0.001). Similarly, A-NSKQ percentage scores were higher in males than females (42.3% vs. 34.6%, p < 0.001). In terms of knowledge categories, low/inadequate sports nutrition knowledge was more prevalent among females (81.2%) than males (65.1%), whereas moderate knowledge was more common among males (29.7% vs. 17.7%; p < 0.001). No participant was classified in the excellent category.

Relationships between study variables

When interactions between variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis, it was found that although many relationships exceeded the threshold for statistical significance, these associations generally remained weak in practical terms. As shown in the correlation heatmap (Fig. 1), the most notable positive correlation was recorded between daily total energy intake and the percentage of energy derived from protein (rho = 0.65; p < 0.001). From an anthropometric perspective, BMI values show a weak but positive correlation with age (rho = 0.12), daily water intake (rho = 0.23) and protein intake (rho = 0.17; p < 0.001). Contrary to expectations, despite its theoretical critical role in weight management, it was found that weekly exercise duration did not establish a significant correlation with BMI. On the other hand, daily water intake has a limited relationship with both weekly exercise duration (rho = 0.11; p < 0.05) and protein consumption (rho = 0.15; p < 0.01). Whilst A-NSKQ scores showed a weak association with age (rho = 0.11; p < 0.05), no meaningful relationship was observed with exercise duration.

Nutritional intake and adequacy by knowledge level

Participants’ nutrient intakes and inadequacy according to A-NSKQ categories are presented in Table 3. No differences were observed across groups for carbohydrate, protein, fat, or fiber intake (all p > 0.05). In contrast, sports nutrition knowledge significantly differentiated the intake of several micronutrients, namely vitamin E (p = 0.005), riboflavin (p = 0.021), iron (p = 0.033), and zinc (p = 0.034). Median intakes of vitamin E, riboflavin, and iron tended to be higher in participants with better sports nutrition knowledge. Although zinc intake also differed significantly across groups, the highest median intake was observed in the moderate knowledge group rather than in the good knowledge group. The proportion of participants below the reference level generally tended to decrease with increasing knowledge category, particularly for vitamin E, riboflavin, and iron.

Multivariable predictors of BMI and exercise

Two separate hierarchical multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to identify the variables influencing BMI and total weekly exercise duration (Table 4). The final regression model designed to predict BMI (Model 3) explained 23.1% of the total variance (adjusted R2 = 0.231, F = 18.021, p ≤ 0.001). In the final stage of the analysis, the inclusion of variables relating to dietary habits, lifestyle factors and knowledge levels significantly strengthened the model’s explanatory power and contributed an additional 6.8% to the total variance (ΔR²= 0.068, Fchange = 7.955, p < 0.001). In this fully adjusted framework, BMI was significantly predicted by gender (β = -0.436, p < 0.001), daily energy intake (β = -0.091, p = 0.001), protein intake expressed as %E (β = 0.091, p = 0.030), daily water intake (β = 0.159, p < 0.001), and cigarette consumption (β = -0.163, p < 0.001). Being female, higher daily energy intake, and greater cigarette consumption were associated with lower BMI, whereas higher protein intake and higher daily water intake were associated with higher BMI. Age, weekly exercise duration and A-NSKQ scores, however, did not emerge as predictors in the final BMI model.

Discussion

Starting higher education is a crucial period during which young adults take their first steps towards independent living and establish their own habits. The dietary and physical activity routines acquired during this process become permanent behaviours that determine health status in later life [61]. However, despite the critical importance of this period, the current literature indicates that young adults’ knowledge of nutrition is generally inadequate. This knowledge gap is closely linked to energy imbalances, micronutrient deficiencies and a tendency towards a sedentary lifestyle [28, 61, 62]. Particularly in physically active individuals, sports nutrition knowledge is expected to play a fundamental role in both enhancing exercise performance and fostering health-protective behaviours [63]. Based on this, the aim of this cross-sectional study was to examine the multidimensional relationships between sports nutrition knowledge, nutritional status, lifestyle factors and BMI specifically among university students who engage in regular exercise. Considering the data obtained, it appears that theoretical knowledge of sports nutrition remains quite limited among this group of active students and is only weakly associated with the intake of a few micronutrients. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that differences in BMI were not explained by nutritional knowledge or duration of exercise but were instead related to a combination of sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle factors.

Sociodemographic profiles and exercise characteristics

The findings confirm the study’s main hypothesis regarding gender differences. Female and male participants differed in anthropometric data, exercise habits, and nutritional awareness. Consistent with existing data in the literature, the body weight, BMI and daily water intake of the men participating in our study were found to be higher than those of the women [47, 49, 61, 64]. Furthermore, it was determined that men engaged in exercise for longer periods; both their A-NSKQ scores and the frequency of nutritional supplement use exceeded those of women. Similar studies conducted specifically in Türkiye also emphasise that the ‘muscular physique’ ideal is increasingly driving the use of supplements among men who actively participate in sport, transforming this into an established behavioural pattern. Taken together, these findings may suggest that the underlying motivation for exercise among men is primarily driven by the desire to increase muscle mass, maximise performance, and address aesthetic concerns, although this was not directly evaluated in the present study [65, 66].

When general lifestyle factors are examined, the finding that the frequency of cigarette use is significantly higher among men is consistent with current national statistics [67, 68]. On the other hand, the fact that a significant proportion of participants (33.5%) prefer salty foods is an issue that warrants attention from a public health perspective. The literature indicates a chronic disconnect between knowledge levels and behaviour regarding the restriction of sodium intake among young adults; it shows that most individuals are unaware of daily legal consumption limits or do not reflect warnings on food labels in their purchasing choices [69, 70]. This emerging socio-demographic picture highlights the need to develop gender-specific public health strategies on issues such as smoking cessation, the informed use of dietary supplements and adequate water intake.

The knowledge–behaviour gap and nutritional adequacy

At the outset of the study, we anticipated that a high level of nutritional knowledge would be positively associated with more optimal dietary profiles. Whilst our analyses partially support this hypothesis, they also highlight a clear disconnect between knowledge and behaviour. The fact that approximately three-quarters of the sample had low or inadequate levels of nutritional literacy is fully consistent with recent literature reporting systematic deficiencies in sports nutrition literacy among university students [28, 32]. One of our notable findings is that high levels of sports nutrition knowledge show a positive association with the consumption of specific micronutrients such as vitamin E, riboflavin, iron and zinc. Conversely, no similar positive impact of this theoretical knowledge was observed on macronutrient distribution or overall energy balance. This finding aligns with the observation by Yanagihara and Narumi-Hyakutake [71] that high nutritional knowledge contributes more to micronutrient diversity rather than optimising total energy distribution. Indeed, the current literature consistently demonstrates that the relationship between nutritional knowledge and diet quality is generally weak (r ≈ 0.20–0.30) and that increased awareness does not always translate directly into behavioural change [72]. These findings support our aim to examine the inconsistency between theoretical knowledge and practical application, which is one of the starting points of our research. Our findings indicate that theoretical knowledge alone is insufficient for managing daily energy and macronutrient intake. In this context, psychosocial, systemic, and environmental factors, such as campus dining options, limited budgets, time constraints, and peer influence, may also play a role in complicating the translation of knowledge into behaviour.

Multivariable predictors of BMI and methodological considerations

Although the research findings indicate a statistical association between sports nutrition knowledge and daily dietary choices, they reveal that the rate at which this knowledge is put into practice remains low. A relative increase in participants’ knowledge was observed with advancing age; however, this theoretical gain did not result in a significant improvement in dietary quality or physical activity levels. This finding, which aligns with other data in the Turkish literature, demonstrates that while education and age factors contribute to raising awareness, they are insufficient on their own to change ingrained habits [65, 72–74].

The link between lifestyle and physiological indicators is far more pronounced. In line with the existing literature [75], our study identified a positive correlation between weekly exercise duration and water intake. This alignment between activity level and water intake suggests that physically active individuals develop a practical awareness of their hydration needs based on their own experiences rather than theoretical knowledge.

According to the results of the multivariate analysis, it was found that the primary factors associated with BMI values are demographic characteristics and lifestyle, rather than theoretical nutritional knowledge. A plausible explanation for this pattern is that nutritional knowledge influences body weight only indirectly, through its translation into everyday food choices, whereas behaviours such as habitual water intake, smoking and energy consumption are involved in energy balance more directly and are therefore more closely reflected in BMI. The regression models established revealed that gender, daily energy intake, protein intake, water intake and smoking were independently associated with BMI. Among these variables, the strongest association with BMI was observed for gender. The higher BMI values observed in male students likely reflect a multifactorial relationship. This difference can be attributed to a combination of factors, including men having a higher lean body mass from an anatomical perspective, their tendency to prefer high-calorie foods more frequently, and dietary routines characteristic of an active male population [76, 77].

The inverse relationship observed between energy intake and BMI in our study may, at first glance, appear biologically contradictory. However, this finding can be explained by the ‘underreporting bias’ frequently encountered in dietary questionnaires and epidemiological studies. Indeed, the existing literature clearly demonstrates that individuals with a high BMI tend to report their daily calorie intake as lower than it is [78]. In contrast, the positive association between water intake and BMI is physiologically consistent. As total body water is directly related to lean body mass, it is a natural consequence that men who exercise regularly and have high muscle mass have a greater need for fluids.

Smoking status was negatively associated with BMI in this cohort. This finding is consistent with literature describing the appetite-suppressing effects of nicotine and irregular eating patterns typical among smokers [79, 80]. However, a recent Turkish study observed higher BMIs among smokers who exhibited lower nutrition knowledge and unhealthier eating attitudes [81]. The inverse relationship observed here should thus be interpreted with caution, considering the complex metabolic and behavioural mechanisms involved. The multivariable model predicting weekly total exercise duration was not significant. This lack of significance suggests that exercise duration in university populations might be shaped more heavily by environmental, psychosocial, and campus-related factors rather than the specific lifestyle variables measured in this study [82, 83].

Limitations

When interpreting the findings, it is important to bear in mind certain methodological limitations inherent to the nature of the study. Firstly, because of the cross-sectional design, the study was limited to reporting existing correlational relationships rather than drawing definitive causal inferences between variables. Accordingly, the mechanistic pathway proposed here should be regarded as a conceptual rationale consistent with our findings rather than an empirically established causal sequence. The fact that our data set relies on self-reporting for dietary intake and physical activity introduces a risk of recall and reporting bias. Although two non-consecutive 24-hour dietary recalls were employed, there remains a possibility that this does not fully reflect individuals’ long-term dietary routines. In the assessment of physical activity, the focus was solely on frequency and duration parameters; the fact that exercise intensity was not measured has left room for subjective interpretation of activity profiles.

Another significant limitation is the reliance solely on BMI values in the anthropometric assessment. Without objective body composition analyses, it should not be overlooked that high muscle mass in this sample group of regular exercisers may have artificially inflated the BMI results. Moreover, it was not recorded whether participants were receiving guidance from a certified dietitian at the time of the study or had done so in the recent past. Given that professional dietary counselling can shape both nutritional literacy and knowledge levels, this uncontrolled variable represents a potential confounding factor in the interpretation of the results. Finally, the participant group, recruited via convenience sampling from a single university in İzmir, carries a risk of selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the participants’ specific academic departments were not recorded. Students in health- or sports-related fields may have higher baseline sports nutrition knowledge, and the absence of this variable limits our ability to account for the influence of formal academic background on the observed scores. Nevertheless, the meticulous application of the face-to-face interview technique and the use of validated scales were adopted as a strategic choice to balance these methodological risks and support data quality.

Conclusion

Overall, the present findings suggest that differences in BMI among physically active university students are shaped more by demographic characteristics and everyday lifestyle habits than by theoretical nutrition knowledge. The fact that gender was the strongest predictor in the regression model points to the importance of adopting gender-sensitive perspectives when designing future public health interventions. That the increase in theoretical nutritional knowledge was reflected only in the consumption of a few micronutrients, with little effect on overall energy balance, indicates that knowledge alone is insufficient to translate into action. Consequently, it is essential that future educational programmes are not limited to the mere transfer of theoretical knowledge but address the subject within a much broader and more practical framework. Concrete steps taken by universities—such as providing professional dietitian support for sports facilities, enriching course content, or improving the quality of menus in canteens— may help healthy living become a permanent culture on campus. Our key recommendation for future research is to include objective body composition measurements alongside participant self-reports, and to prioritise longitudinal research designs to clearly observe the process by which knowledge translates into behaviour.
