The European Resuscitation Council course strategy project – developing courses fit for the future
Authors: Robert Greif, Andrew S Lockey, Carsten Lott, for the ERC Course Strategy Taskforce Collaborators
Published: 2026

The European Resuscitation Council (ERC) has commenced a bold and exciting project to identify evidence to support development for the future of its life support courses. The plan is to undertake comprehensive reviews of current publications concerning key questions for the design of resuscitation courses and build upon the experience from over a quarter of a century of teaching resuscitation within the ERC.

ERC bases its resuscitation guidelines1 upon treatment recommendations produced through a rigorous, and transparent continuous evidence evaluation process by the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR).2 This approach translates clinical science into international lifesaving treatment protocols. The Utstein Formula for Survival reinforces the equitable importance of education alongside medical science and implementation. As such, the approach to life support education also needs to be underpinned by contemporaneous and cutting-edge educational and implementational research.3

A Course Strategy Taskforce has been commissioned by the ERC Board to update the ERC courses based upon such contemporaneous educational research. The specific aims of this ‘Course Strategy’ project are threefold. Firstly, the project will seek to retain the most effective of the current teaching strategies. Secondly, it will identify and incorporate new and innovative developments for future resuscitation courses. Thirdly, it will take into account insight from a recently published large scale multinational survey on ERC courses that included participant and instructor perspectives.4 Once the suite of published reviews has been completed, the ERC Course Strategy Taskforce will aim to publish a scientific statement on education to inform the evolution of existing life support courses as well as future development of new courses.

Basic and advanced ERC accredited life support courses covering adult, paediatric, and newborn resuscitation have been in existence since 2000.5 When these courses were initially conceived, they were ground-breaking in that they utilised simulation-based scenario training for multi-professional groups of learners6, 7 long before this became common practice in other parts of healthcare education. Many aspects of programme delivery have evolved over the years, with a greater emphasis on hands-on practice, self-directed8, 9 and blended learning10 as opposed to lecture-based approaches. Further, ERC courses have developed beyond the European borders reflecting the need for cultural and resource driven adaptations.

The educational theories that underpin the overall approach have been described elsewhere,11 and this reinforces the benefits of the heutagogical (self-determined) foundation and a learner-centred approach to life support education.12, 13, 14 Beneath the surface of these overarching theoretical principles, it remains important to keep up to date with developments in specific teaching and assessment modalities.

Specific topics were defined though group consensus, taking into account current teaching and learning strategies and the needs of learners, instructors, as well as the organisation. These include reviews of simulation-based scenario training (teaching outcomes, instructional design, and efficacy), assessment of learning and evaluation of teaching, as well as feedback and debriefing strategies for ERC life support courses. In addition, there are plans to review the best formats and learning outcomes needed to teach resuscitation knowledge, psychomotor and team collaboration skills. Finally, a review will be performed looking at how to teach different and specific populations, from healthcare professionals to lay people and first responders, but also the faculty development needed for those who teach on the ERC courses.

A writing group consisting of global educational experts was convened for each topic. Where appropriate, systematic reviews and educational recommendations produced by ILCOR’s Education, Implementation and Teams Task Force will be included in these ERC reviews.9 Where gaps in the published evidence are identified, these will be highlighted to prompt future research and, in the meantime, expert consensus will continue to be used.

A standardised template will be utilised to define the research question, structured around the population of interest, the intervention or exposure, a comparison, and the clinical, patient, and educational outcomes for both learners and instructors. The review will be registered at Open Science Framework (OSF, https://osf.io/), A professional information specialist will conduct a search across at least three databases, employing a meticulously developed search strategy focused specifically on resuscitation publications. The exclusion of publications concerning trauma and general medical education was a deliberate decision.

We will follow established procedures for systematic reviews using a software platform (Covidence, Melbourne, Australia) for title, abstract, and full-text screening, which will be conducted independently by two members of the writing group.15 In the event of a conflict, a third reviewer will resolve the issue. These reviews will follow standard methodology, including a risk-of-bias assessment, standardised data extraction, and a meta-analysis or, where this is not appropriate or feasible, a narrative synthesis of the evidence. Review authors will not assess studies they have authored themselves. Artificial intelligence will be employed to assist with the risk-of-bias assessment and the generation of data extraction tables summarising the included studies: subsequently, the human authors will manually review and verify all AI-generated outputs to ensure complete accuracy.

This issue of the journal publishes the ERC Course Strategy Taskforce’s first systematic review of the evidence for teaching resuscitation in groups.[ref when available] The review concluded that smaller instructor-led groups were linked with improved dialogue between group members and instructors, more hands-on training with direct feedback, and better error detection by instructors during psychomotor skill teaching. Very limited direct comparisons of small-group and larger group teaching formats were found and the participants performance directly after a course was often similar despite the size of the group or the teaching format. The review identified a significant heterogeneity of included studies and outcomes which precluded the use of meta-analyses and meant that a narrative synthesis only was feasible. This emphasises the importance of primary research adhering to a standardised set of educational and implementation outcomes as recently defined by ILCOR to allow more direct comparison in future reviews.16, 17 The findings of this review support the current design of ERC courses with group sizes of up to six learners, but highlights the need for further research into retention, clinical transfer, instructor workload, and resource implications of group size on ERC courses.

It has been established that prior participation by members of a cardiac arrest team in accredited resuscitation courses improves patient outcomes.18 It is important therefore that the ERC continues to invest in future proofing its portfolio of courses to further increase survival and improve patient-centred outcomes after cardiac arrest.
