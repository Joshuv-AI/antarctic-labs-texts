Abstract
As researchers increasingly rely on machine learning models and LLMs to annotate unstructured data, such as texts or images, various approaches have been proposed to correct bias in downstream statistical analysis. However, existing methods tend to yield large standard errors and require some error-free human annotation. In this paper, I introduce Surrogate Representation Inference (SRI), which assumes that unstructured data fully mediate the relationship between human annotations and structured variables. The assumption is guaranteed by design provided that human coders rely only on unstructured data for annotation. Under this setting, I propose a neural network architecture that learns a low-dimensional representation of unstructured data such that the surrogate assumption remains to be satisfied. When multiple human annotations are available, SRI can further correct non-differential measurement errors that may exist in human annotations. Focusing on text-as-outcome settings, I formally establish the identification conditions and semiparametric efficient estimation strategies that enable learning and leveraging such a low-dimensional representation. Simulation studies and a real-world application demonstrate that SRI reduces standard errors by over 50% when machine learning prediction accuracy is moderate and provides valid inference even when human annotations contain non-differential measurement errors.



Keywords:
Text-as-Data, Representation Learning, Semiparametric Statistics, Deep Learning, Double Negative Control

1
Introduction

With a wide availability of texts and images, the use of these unstructured data for statistical inference has become increasingly common in many fields, including political science (e.g.
Grimmer et al., 2022
;
Stukal et al., 2022
;
Wratil et al., 2023
), economics (e.g.
Gentzkow et al., 2019
;
Hassan et al., 2019
;
Ash and Hansen, 2023
), and sociology (e.g.
Kozlowski et al., 2019
;
Macanovic, 2022
). While researchers use machine learning and large language models (LLMs) to annotate such data, their predictions contain errors that invalidate the downstream statistical inference.
As a result, various approaches have been proposed to correct the bias in the downstream analysis by combining the human annotations and machine learning or LLM predictions (e.g.
Fong and Tyler, 2021
;
Angelopoulos et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2024
;
Zrnic and Candès, 2024
;
Carlson and Dell, 2025
). However, the existing methodology tends to yield large standard errors, especially when the quality of machine learning predictions is moderate, and assumes human annotations contain no error.

In this paper, I introduce a new methodological framework,
Surrogate Representation Inference
(SRI), which improves efficiency and yields statistically valid estimates even when human annotations contain non-differential measurement errors. SRI improves efficiency based on the assumption that unstructured data work as a surrogate variable, fully mediating the relations between human annotations and structured variables of interest, such as predictors. This assumption is guaranteed by design if human coders rely only on texts and images for the annotation task and do not directly look at other relevant structured variables. Since unstructured data are high-dimensional, I propose using low-dimensional representations that still satisfy the surrogacy assumption. I refer to these as
surrogate representations
. Because surrogate representation can leverage surrogate structure, the proposed SRI methodology can improve efficiency.

To estimate surrogate representation and perform subsequent statistical inference, I introduce a neural network architecture that jointly predicts human annotations and structured variables.
As a concrete example, I demonstrate the proposed SRI methodology in the setting where the text-based variable, such as sentiments or topics, serves as an outcome.
I show that the covariate-adjusted outcome mean (i.e., the average value of the text-based variable for each level of the predictors after adjusting for the set of control variables)
is nonparametrically identified using surrogate representation. This surrogate representation can be estimated with the proposed neural network architecture. Drawing on the literature of semiparametric statistics (e.g.
Tsiatis, 2006
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
;
Kennedy, 2023
), I derive the semiparametric efficient estimation strategies tailored to the proposed neural network architectures and formalize the conditions under which the SRI estimator is valid and efficient.

The SRI framework can further adjust measurement errors in human annotations when multiple human annotations are available. Specifically, I show that when the measurement error in human annotations is
non-differential
(i.e., the measurement error does not depend on the true annotation or the contents of texts), the quantity of interest can be nonparametrically identified and estimated from multiple annotations using the literature of double negative control (e.g.,
Kuroki and Pearl, 2014
;
Tchetgen et al., 2020
;
Zhou and Tchetgen, 2024
). Since perfect human annotations are not required, once researchers can create a solid coding rule, they can then leverage existing crowd-sourcing platforms, such as Amazon Mechanical Turk and CrowdFlower, to generate human-annotated labels with relative ease.

I finally apply the proposed SRI methodology to the existing research on the framing of immigrants in the U.S. Congressional speeches
(
Card et al., 2022
)
. I compare the proposed SRI estimator with the existing bias correction algorithm (
Angelopoulos et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2024
) that leverages both machine learning predictions and human annotations. I find that the direct use of machine learning predictions inflates the partisan difference in the framing of immigrants. Both SRI and the existing bias-correction approach yield consistent point estimates, but because tone classification is challenging, SRI reduces the standard errors by roughly one-third.

In Appendix
S3
, I also validate the performance of the proposed estimator through simulation studies, in which I systematically vary the quality of the machine learning predictions. The findings reveal that SRI outperforms the existing approach across all settings except the case that the machine learning predictions are already almost perfect (above 90% accuracy), with the efficiency gains becoming particularly pronounced when proxy accuracy is moderate (e.g., below 85%). I further find that when human annotations contain classification errors, the existing bias-correction approach, which relies on the assumption of perfect human annotations, suffers from substantial bias and improper confidence interval coverage, but the proposed SRI estimator with multiple human labels gives valid estimates with the smaller bias and root mean square error (RMSE) compared to the existing bias-correction methodology.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
presents a motivating example drawn from the actual published research. Section
3
formalizes the proposed methodology with error-free human annotations. Section
4
then relaxes this assumption and deals with more realistic setting in which humans annotations contain non-differential measurement errors.
Section
5
then applies the proposed method to the existing study introduced in the motivating example. Finally, Section
6
concludes by summarizing the advantages and limitations of SRI and outlining directions for future research.

Related Literature.
There is a growing body of literature that aims to conduct statistical inference with a limited amount of ground-truth labels by leveraging both labeled data and machine learning predictions (e.g.,
Fong and Tyler, 2021
;
Angelopoulos et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2024
;
Zrnic and Candès, 2024
;
Carlson and Dell, 2025
).
Unlike the existing approaches, the proposed SRI framework leverages the assumption that human coders only rely on the text they are annotating, but this assumption can be guaranteed by design and can be used to improve the efficiency.
The SRI framework is also different from the existing literature in that it allows human annotations to contain non-differential measurement error.
The existing literature, implicitly or explicitly, assumes that human annotations are error-free.
The only exception in the literature is
Egami et al., 2024
, which addressed this issue using multiple coders and a quasi-Bayesian approach to model uncertainty in annotations, in a manner akin to multiple imputation. However, that method does not offer formal guarantees of consistent estimation for the target parameter of interest.
I provide the methodology with formal consistency guarantee under the assumption that measurement errors are non-differential, and empirically demonstrate that SRI substantially decreases the bias using only the error-prone human annotations. In addition, I introduce a diagnostic tool that tests the non-differential error assumption using only a minimal number of researcher-generated labels.

The proposed SRI framework also differs from the existing literature on surrogate outcomes in that it considers surrogate variables that are high-dimensional and unstructured. While some recent studies have introduced methods using multiple surrogate outcomes (e.g.,
Price et al., 2018
;
Athey et al., 2019
;
Wang et al., 2020
), their focus remains on low-dimensional, structured surrogate variables. Because SRI uses high-dimensional, unstructured data as surrogates, I propose learning low-dimensional representations that continue to function as surrogates to improve efficiency.

This paper contributes to the literature of double negative control and proximal causal inference (e.g.,
Hu and Schennach, 2008
;
Kuroki and Pearl, 2014
;
Miao et al., 2018
;
Tchetgen et al., 2020
). Double negative control is a widely used technique when control variables are not directly observed, and similar techniques are extended to a variety of settings, including synthetic controls
(
Qiu et al., 2024
)
, hidden treatments
(
Zhou and Tchetgen, 2024
)
, and networks
(
Egami and Tchetgen Tchetgen, 2023
)
.
This paper contributes to the literature by formally deriving the identification and semiparametric estimation strategies in the case of hidden outcomes, which has not been done in the literature to the best of my knowledge.

Finally, this paper has broader implications for the literature on representation learning. Because texts are high-dimensional, the proposed SRI framework employs low-dimensional representations to reduce dimensionality while preserving the surrogate structure. Similar techniques have been used in the literature of statistical inference for unstructured data (e.g.,
Veitch et al., 2020
;
Pryzant et al., 2021
;
Wang and Jordan, 2022
;
Gui and Veitch, 2023
;
Imai and Nakamura, 2024
;
Imai and Nakamura, 2025
), since such data are often high-dimensional and thus require dimensionality reduction to extract only the relevant information, such as confounding variables.

2
Motivating Example: Framing of Immigrants in the U.S. Congress

Statistical inference with unstructured data often faces two fundamental challenges that can significantly affect the validity of research. First, machine learning predictions are subject to bias, and their direct use in downstream statistical inference may yield biased estimates and incorrect standard errors. The magnitude of this bias tends to increase as prediction accuracy declines, but even with modern LLMs, achieving very high accuracy, such as above 90%, remains difficult for many concepts that are not present in the training data
(
Halterman and Keith, 2024
)
. Second, human annotations cannot be assumed as the ground truth, since annotators can make mistakes, especially when coding large volumes of text.

These challenges are clearly illustrated in the analysis of elite discourse on immigration by
Card et al., 2022
. The authors examined how congressional rhetoric on immigration evolved over 140 years and how this evolution intersected with partisan divides in anti-immigration sentiment. Given the central role of elite rhetoric in shaping public opinion, the political rhetoric on immigration has been a central topic in political science and computational social science (e.g.
Abrajano et al., 2017
;
Hajdinjak et al., 2020
;
Simonsen and Widmann, 2025
). To provide a long-term historical perspective,
Card et al., 2022
compiled a dataset of more than 200,000 immigration-related congressional speeches (1880–2021) from the Congressional Record and related archives.

The text-based variable of interest in this application is the tone toward immigrants expressed in congressional speeches.
The outcome was coded into three categories: pro-immigration (valuing immigrants and favoring less-restrictive policies), neutral, and anti-immigration (emphasizing threats or restrictions). To begin their analysis, the authors constructed a set of human annotations. Specifically, they randomly assigned 3,643 immigration-related speech segments to at least two independent coders. Inter-coder reliability was assessed using Krippendorff’s
\alpha
across three historical periods. Because tone classification is inherently complex and non-binary, agreement levels were modest, with an average
\alpha
of approximately 0.5 (see Table
1
, first row).
To aggregate these annotations, the authors employed a Bayesian hierarchical model that accounts for annotator bias and estimates the probability distribution of the underlying “true” labels
(
Luo et al., 2020
)
, and then the label with the highest posterior probability is assigned as the inferred “true” label.

Using the inferred labels, the authors trained a supervised machine learning classifier to categorize each speech segment as pro-immigration, neutral, or anti-immigration. The classifier was built on a pre-trained language model (RoBERTa;
Liu et al., 2019
). To incorporate uncertainty in the inferred labels, they employed a weighted training scheme in which class probabilities served as observation weights. The resulting model achieved an accuracy of about 65% across all periods (see Table
1
, second row). Finally, the predicted labels were regressed on the party affiliation of the speaker (Democrat, Republican, or other), revealing that Democrats consistently framed immigrants more positively than Republicans.

This example demonstrates how both challenges manifest in practice. Although the authors conducted careful robustness checks, relying solely on machine-learning predictions with 65% accuracy may yield biased results and incorrect standard errors. Since only a small portion of the corpus is annotated, using annotations alone would discard most of the data. Recent studies, including
Angelopoulos et al., 2023
,
Egami et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2024
, and
Carlson and Dell, 2025
, address this issue by combining human annotations with machine-learning predictions. However, as
Angelopoulos et al., 2023
and
Egami et al., 2024
acknowledge, these methods often produce large standard errors, particularly when prediction accuracy is moderate.
In this application, for example, while the direct use of machine learning for difference-in-means produce a standard error of 0.003, whereas the bias-correction approach with RoBERTa predictions yields a much larger standard error of 0.020.
This challenge motivates the SRI framework introduced in Section
3
.

Similarly, the modest inter-coder agreement illustrates why treating human annotations as perfect ground truth is problematic. When multiple annotations are available, some papers have proposed inferring true labels using parametric models (e.g.
Dawid and Skene, 1979
;
Raykar et al., 2010
;
Luo et al., 2020
) or majority voting (e.g.,
Whitehill et al., 2009
). However, these methods typically lack theoretical guarantees for downstream analysis or rely on strong but untestable assumptions. I address this issue in Section
4
by introducing not only an estimator that corrects non-differential measurement error but also a procedure to test whether measurement errors are non-differential.

3
The Proposed Methodology with Perfect Annotations

This section outlines the proposed SRI framework. For the sake of simplicity, I begin with the case that human annotations contain no measurement errors, and relax this assumption in the next section. Throughout the paper, I focus on the case in which texts serve as outcomes, motivated by the examples in Section
2
. However, the proposed SRI methodology is broadly applicable to other settings where unstructured data play a role in statistical inference. I proceed by defining the quantities of interest, specifying the necessary assumptions, establishing identification, and discussing estimation strategies.

3.1
Assumptions, quantity of interest, and nonparametric identification

Consider a simple random sample of
N
observations drawn from a population of interest, indexed by
i=1,2,\dots,N
, where for each unit
i
, the observed data consist of
(T_{i},\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})
.
The binary predictor of interest is denoted by
T_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.
The set of control variables and texts are also observed for all units, represented by
\bm{Z}_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
\bm{Y}_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
, respectively, where
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are the support of each variable. Suppose that the analysis focuses on a specific feature of texts (e.g., the tone of the speeches) as outcomes, for which the human annotations are available only for a subset of the data.
Let
L_{i}\in\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
denote the unobserved true concept of interest and let
\tilde{L}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}
represent the human-annotated label, both supported on
\mathcal{L}
.
Human annotated labels
\tilde{L}_{i}
are observed only for units where
S_{i}=1
, with
S_{i}\in\{0,1\}
as the sampling indicator.

The goal is to identify the average value of the text feature
L_{i}
for each level of the predictor
T_{i}
, after adjusting for the set of control variables
\bm{Z}_{i}
. Formally, the target parameter is the covariate-adjusted outcome mean under
T_{i}=t\in\{0,1\}
, which is denoted as

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{E}[L_{i}\mid T_{i}=t,\bm{Z}_{i}]\bigr].

I say that the target parameter is identified when it is uniquely determined by the joint distribution of the observed data.

To identify this parameter of interest, the text feature of interest
L_{i}
is assumed to be a deterministic function of the text
\bm{Y}_{i}
. This assumption rules out cases in which the same text corresponds to multiple true annotations. It is consistent with prior work in the literature on text-based inference (e.g.
Egami et al., 2022
;
Imai and Nakamura, 2024
) and is formalized as follows.

Assumption 1
(True Coding Rule)

There exists a deterministic function
g_{L}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{L}
that maps outcome texts to a set of concepts
L_{i}
, i.e.,

L_{i}=\ g_{L}(\bm{Y}_{i}).

I then assume that human annotations
\tilde{L}_{i}
do not contain classification errors, so that they always capture the true concept of interest
L_{i}
. This is formalized as follows.

Assumption 2
(Perfect Annotations)

The human annotations
\tilde{L}_{i}
always captures the concept of interest
L_{i}
so that

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{i}=L_{i}

for all
i=1,\cdots,N
.

This assumption is often made implicitly in the literature (e.g.,
Fong and Tyler, 2021
;
Angelopoulos et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2024
). Note that this assumption is very restrictive as it does not allow any single mistake by human coders. I relax this assumption in Section
4
.

Under this setup, coders may still infer the information of structured variables (e.g., the party affiliation of a speaker), but such inference must rely solely on the text they observe.

The human coders annotate the part of texts that are randomly sampled from the entire corpus such that

(1)

with
\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=1)>0

Together, these assumptions provide the foundation for the identification strategy. The identification strategy of the SRI framework uses the fact that texts
\bm{Y}_{i}
satisfy the conditional independence
T_{i}\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ \tilde{L}_{i}\mid\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}
. Since the full texts
\bm{Y}_{i}
are inherently high-dimensional and challenging to incorporate directly into statistical inference, I propose the use of a low-dimensional representation that fully mediates the relationship between the predictors of interest
T_{i}
and the human annotations
\tilde{L}_{i}
. I refer to this as
surrogate representation
, a term that reflects its role in satisfying the statistical surrogacy condition
(
Prentice, 1989
;
Athey et al., 2019
)
. This surrogate representation reduces the dimensionality of the text data while retaining the essential information needed to make a statistical inference.

To proceed, I assume the existence of such low-dimensional representations.

Assumption 4
(Sufficiency of Representation for Texts)

There exists a low-dimensional representation
\bm{W}_{i}=\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i})\in\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
that fully explains the relationship between
\{L_{i},T_{i}\}
and outcome
\bm{Y}_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{i}\;\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\;\{L_{i},T_{i}\}\mid\bm{W}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}

(2)

Under Assumptions
1
and
2

2

\bm{W}_{i}=\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i})
fully mediates the relationship between the predictor
T_{i}
and the human annotations
\tilde{L}_{i}
, yielding
T_{i},\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ \tilde{L}_{i}\mid\bm{W}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}
. The independence in Equation (
2
) is also crucial for identification because it ensures that the identification formula does not depend on the specific choice of representation
\bm{W}_{i}
.

Substantively, Assumption
4
states that not every aspect of the text
\bm{Y}_{i}
is simultaneously relevant for the predictor of interest
T_{i}
and the concept of interest
L_{i}
. This assumption is often reasonable, since many components of texts, such as individual writing or speaking style, grammatical habits, or discussion of unrelated topics, are typically extraneous and statistical surrogacy conditions hold even after removing these features. Importantly, Assumption

requires only the existence of such a representation
\bm{W}_{i}
and does not impose uniqueness.

Under this setup, the target parameter can be nonparametrically identified using the surrogate representation

.

(Identification under Perfect Annotations)

Under Assumptions

where

is the latent representation that satisfies
\bm{Y}_{i}\;\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\;\{L_{i},T_{i}\}\mid\bm{W}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}
and
\mathcal{W}
is its support. Importantly, the latent representation
\bm{W}_{i}
needs not to be unique and any latent representation with the same independence constraint leads to the same identification formula.

3.2
Estimation and inference

Given the identification result, I next consider estimation and inference. As derived in the previous section, SRI uses the surrogate representation
\bm{W}_{i}=\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i})
that satisfies

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{i}\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;\{L_{i},T_{i}\}\ \mid\ \bm{W}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}.

(4)

However,
\bm{W}_{i}=\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i})
is not directly observed and it is thus necessary to estimate the surrogate representation that satisfies the independence stated in Equation (
4
).

I propose estimating
\bm{W}_{i}
using a neural network architecture, following a similar approach to DragonNet
(
Shi et al., 2019

. Specifically, the loss function is

is the outcome model given the low-dimensional representation
\bm{W}_{i}=\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i})
and the set of control variables
\bm{Z}_{i}
,
\rho_{t}(\bm{W}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})=\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=t\mid\bm{W}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})
is the surrogacy score model given the low-dimensional representation
\bm{W}_{i}=\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i})
and the set of control variables
\bm{Z}_{i}
,
\pi_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})=\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=t\mid\bm{Z}_{i})
is the propensity score model given the set of control variables
\bm{Z}_{i}
, and
\bar{m}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})=\int\mu(\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})dF(\bm{Y}_{i}\mid\bm{Z}_{i},T_{i}=t)
.

The proof is given in Appendix

Once the efficient influence function is obtained, the asymptotically valid estimator can be constructed. Here, I propose to use a
K

(

;
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
)
. Suppose that

is divisible by

. The entire estimation procedure with
K

1.

Randomly partition the data into
K
folds of equal size. Let

be the indicator of the observation

belonging to the fold
k
.

2.

For each fold
k\in\{1,\cdots,K\}
, use
I(i)\neq c
as training data:

(a)

Estimate the propensity score model
\pi_{t}
using the whole sample with
I(i)\neq c
.

(b)

Split the training data with
S_{i}=0
into two halves, say
I_{-1s}^{(-k)}
and
I_{-2s}^{(-k)}
. Let the remaining training data (with
S_{i}=1
) be
I_{s}^{(k)}
.

(c)

Solve the optimization problem in Equation (
5
) using the data
I_{s}^{(k)}
and
I_{-1s}^{(-k)}
and obtain
\hat{\mu}^{(-k)}
and
\hat{\rho}_{t}^{(-k)}
.

(d)

Regress the obtained

on
T_{i}=t
and
\bm{Z}_{i}
using the data
I_{-2s}^{(-k)}
and obtain
\hat{\bar{m}}^{(-k)}
.

3.

For the level of predictors of interest
t
, solve the estimating equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{I(i)=k}\psi_{t}(T_{i},\tilde{L}_{i},S_{i},\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i};\hat{\bm{f}}^{(-k)},\hat{\rho}_{t}^{(-k)},\hat{\pi}_{t}^{(-k)},\hat{\mu}^{(-k)},\hat{\bar{m}}_{t}^{(-k)},\hat{\Psi}_{t})=0

(7)

and obtain the estimate of the target parameter
\hat{\Psi}_{t}
.

Finally, I derive the asymptotic normality of the proposed estimator. For this, I assume the following regularity conditions.

Assumption 5
(Regularity Conditions)

Let
c_{1}
be a positive constant and
\delta_{n}
be a sequence of positive constants approaching zero as the sample size

increases.

(a)

(Primitive Condition) The labeled outcome is bounded such that
\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\tilde{L}_{i}|^{2}\bigr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq c_{1}
.

(b)

(Positivity) There exists a fixed constant
c_{2}\in(0,1/2]
such that for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(c_{2}\leq\pi_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})\leq 1-c_{2})=1,\quad\mathbb{P}(c_{2}\leq\hat{\pi}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})\leq 1-c_{2})=1.

In addition, the estimated surrogacy score is also almost surely bounded so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{\rho}_{t}(\hat{\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})\leq 1)=1

for all
t\in\{0,1\}
.

(c)

(Nuisance Functions Estimation) Nuisance functions are consistently estimated so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\rho}_{t}(\hat{\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})-{\rho}_{t}({\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq c_{1},

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\rho}_{t}(\hat{\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})-{\rho}_{t}({\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\delta_{n}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\mu}(\hat{\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})-\mu({\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq c_{1},

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\mu}(\hat{\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})-\mu({\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\delta_{n}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\pi}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})-\pi_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq c_{1},

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\pi}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})-\pi_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\delta_{n}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\bar{m}}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})-\bar{m}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq c_{1},

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\bar{m}}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})-\bar{m}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\delta_{n}

for all
t\in\{0,1\}
.

(d)

(Convergence Rate of Cross Product)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\rho}_{t}(\hat{\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})-{\rho}_{t}({\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\mu}(\hat{\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})-\mu({\bm{f}}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq n^{-1/2}\delta_{n}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\pi}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})-\pi_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{\bar{m}}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})-\bar{m}_{t}(\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq n^{-1/2}\delta_{n}

for all
t\in\{0,1\}
.

These are the standard conditions in the literature on semiparametric inference
(
Newey and McFadden, 1994
;
van der Laan, 2010
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2018
)
, but they become difficult to verify when high-dimensional unstructured data are used directly as input. A common strategy is to project texts onto low-dimensional representations via pretrained embeddings, such as Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT;
Devlin et al., 2019
) or ModernBERT
(
Warner et al., 2024
)
. Although these embeddings capture much of the textual content, their statistical properties remain poorly understood, and estimation errors may arise in practice.
If users care about the estimation loss from the embedding, a more principled alternative is to use the internal representation of the generative models, such as LLMs
(
Imai and Nakamura, 2024
)
. When a model can faithfully reconstruct an input
\bm{Y}_{i}
, the internal representation that actually generates the data can be extracted without any estimation error. Thus, if users can extract the internal representations of the generative models, these error‐free latent vectors can then be fed into the standard feed‐forward network with ReLU activations with sufficient depth and width, for which there is a convergence rate guarantee
(
Bauer and Kohler, 2019
;
Schmidt-Hieber, 2020
;
Farrell et al., 2021
)
.

Given the stated regularity condition, the asymptotic normality of the proposed estimator can be derived as follows.

Theorem 2
(Asymptotic Normality)

Under Assumptions

–
5

\hat{\Psi}_{t}
obtained by solving the estimating equations in Equation (

) for

satisfies asymptotic normality:

\frac{\sqrt{n}(\hat{\Psi}_{t}-\Psi_{t})}{\sigma}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

where
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(\psi_{t}(T_{i},\tilde{L}_{i},S_{i},\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})\biggr)^{2}\biggr].

The proof is provided in Appendix

.

4
The Proposed Methodology with Noisy Human Annotations

The methodology described in the previous section enables the estimation under the assumption that human annotations contain no errors (Assumption
2
). Although this assumption is common in the literature, human coders may still make random mistakes even when the coding rule is clearly defined.
Given that some amount of perfectly labeled data is required to obtain the sufficient statistical power, this limitation can be particularly problematic for real-world applications.

In this section, I extend the SRI framework in the previous section to relax the assumption of the perfect human annotations using the recent literature of double negative control (e.g.,
Hu and Schennach, 2008
;
Kuroki and Pearl, 2014
;
Miao et al., 2018
;
Tchetgen et al., 2020
;
Zhou and Tchetgen, 2024
). Specifically, I propose a methodology that requires multiple, but potentially incorrect, human annotations and still performs the efficient statistical inference. As before, I state assumptions, identification, and estimation strategies. I also write about the practical guidance for applied researchers about how to diagnose the required assumptions that are not guaranteed by design.

4.1
Assumptions and nonparametric identification

Consider the same setting as in the previous section, except that the true label
L_{i}\in\mathcal{L}
is assumed to be discrete. For simplicity, let
\mathcal{L}=\{0,\cdots,C\}
with some constant
C<\infty
. Instead of assuming the perfect annotations, suppose that there are
J
coders who independently label texts
\bm{Y}_{i}
. Let
\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}
be a label coded by the coder
j\in\{1,\cdots,J\}
. As before, I use
S_{i}=1
if
\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}
is observed for all coders. Importantly, I allow for the possibility that
\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}\neq L_{i}
for any
j
and
i
, meaning that coders can make mistakes. For simplicity, I assume
J=2
(i.e., two human coders) for the remainder of this section, but the method can be generalized to the case of multiple coders.

The goal is to identify the same target parameter from the previous section using only the human-annotated labels
\tilde{L}_{i}^{(j)}
for
j=\{1,2\}
, which are observed only for the part of the corpus and can be incorrect (i.e.,
\tilde{L}_{i}^{(j)}\neq L_{i}=g_{L}(\bm{Y}_{i})
). As the annotations may contain mistakes, I assume the following for the sake of formalization.

Assumption 6
(Human Annotation Sampling)

Human coders annotate a subset of texts that are randomly sampled from the entire corpus such that

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{i}^{(j)}\;\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\;S_{i}

(8)

for all coders
j
with
\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=1)>0
.

Assumption 7
(Noisy Human Annotations)

Each coder
j
annotates
\bm{Y}_{i}
solely based on the the information within
\bm{Y}_{i}
so that

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{i}^{(j)}\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ \bm{Z}_{i}\ \mid\ \bm{Y}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{L}_{i}^{(j)}\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ T_{i}\ \mid\ \bm{Y}_{i},\ \bm{Z}_{i}

(9)

for all
j\in\{1,\cdots,J\}
.

These assumptions do not substantially increase the practical burden compared to the previous setting. In particular, they can be satisfied if (1) researchers randomly select the texts to be annotated, (2) human coders do not directly observe the predictors of interest, and (3) the structured variable of interest
T_{i}
is not coded by annotators. Unlike the earlier case, the third condition is essential here because annotated variables may otherwise remain correlated even after conditioning on texts. For instance, if coders classify both the speaker’s party affiliation and the tone of a speech, the classification errors in these two variables can be correlated even after conditioning on the text itself.

I then impose restrictions on the human annotations and the unobserved true label. While the human annotations may differ from the true label for some observations, I assume that (1) the mistakes made by each coder is independent and (2) the only way for texts to influence the human coding is through the true label. This is formally stated as follows:

Assumption 8
(Conditional Independence of Human Annotations)

The human-annotated labels
\{\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}:j=1,\cdots J\}
and the true label
L_{i}
satisfy the followings:

(a)

(Independent Coding) For all pair of
j,j^{\prime}
with
j\neq j^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{i}^{(j)}\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ \tilde{L}_{i}^{(j^{\prime})}\mid L_{i}

(10)

(b)

(Exclusion Restriction) For all human coders
j
,

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{i}\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ \tilde{L}_{i}^{(j)}\ \mid\ L_{i}

(11)

Figure

illustrates the required independence assumptions graphically. These two independence constraints in Assumption
8
fundamentally require that errors in observed human annotations be completely non‑differential (i.e., the measurement error does not depend on true annotations
L_{i}
or the contents of texts
\bm{Y}_{i}
). This essentially requires that the errors arise from pure mistakes or inattention rather than systematic biases related to the content of the texts. When mistakes are non‑differential, the coding errors from each coder are independent, satisfying the independent‑coding assumption. The exclusion restriction is also plausible when only the concept of interest in the text influences human annotation, which is reasonable when mistakes are random. Note that Assumption
8
cannot be relaxed by conditioning on covariates
\bm{Z}_{i}
, as they provide no additional information under Assumption
7
.

To better understand how Assumption
8
may be violated, consider the example from Section
2
of classifying the tones of congressional speeches on immigration. Suppose researchers classify speeches as pro‑immigration as long as any part of the speech values immigrants or favors less restrictive policies. However, if coders interpret speeches as pro‑immigration only when they both value immigrants and favor less restrictive policies, then the text content directly influences the pattern of human coders’ annotations without being fully mediated by the researchers’ definition of the label concept, thus violating the exclusion restriction assumption.

In most cases, violations of both assumptions stem from vaguely specified coding rules. When coding guidelines cannot provide specific instructions for classifying each text, coders may attempt to fill these gaps using their own reasoning and procedures. However, these well‑intentioned efforts lead to violations of Assumption
8
. Conversely, when coding rules clearly specify classification criteria for each text type so that texts can be classified almost deterministically, most errors should result from random mistakes rather than systematic biases. Therefore, researchers must craft clear, unambiguous coding guidelines to maintain this assumption. I discuss procedures for testing this assumption and refining coding rules in Section
4.3
.

Lastly, I assume that human labels are accurate and humans agree on their annotations most of the time. This is formalized as follows.

Assumption 9
(Accuracy of Human Annotations)

The human-annotated labels
\{\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}:j=1,\cdots J\}
and the true label
L_{i}
satisfy the followings:

1.

(Accurate Human Labeling) Human coders are more likely to correctly annotate the true label so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(j)}=l\mid L_{i}=l)

\displaystyle>0.5

(12)

for all
l\in\mathcal{L}
and all coders
j
.

2.

(Human Labeling Agreements) Human coders generally agree on their labeling so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(j)}=\tilde{L}_{i}^{(j^{\prime})}=l,T_{i}=t\mid\bm{Z}_{i},S_{i}=1)>0.5

(13)

for all
l\in\mathcal{L}
,
t\in\{0,1\}
and pair of coders
(j,j^{\prime})
with
j\neq j^{\prime}
.

Although these assumptions may appear restrictive, Equation (
13
) involves only the observed quantities and is thus directly testable. The only untestable component is Equation (
12
). This assumption is satisfied as long as humans correctly label more than half of the texts. The assumption does not require perfection and humans can still make mistakes, but it requires that their labels be biased toward the truth, which is critical for correcting errors when combining annotations from multiple human coders.

Under this setup, the distribution of the covariate-adjusted outcome mean can be nonparametrically identified by leveraging two or more human coding as proxies, which can then be used to identify the covariate-adjusted mean of the unobserved true label.

Proposition 2
(Identification without Perfect Human Annotations)

Under Assumptions
1
,
3
,
4
,
6
–
9
, we can uniquely identify the distribution of the covariate-adjusted outcome mean under
T_{i}=t
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Psi_{t,c}:=\int\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=c\mid T_{i}=t,\bm{Z}_{i})dF(\bm{Z}_{i})

from the observed data.

The proof is given in Appendix
S2.4
.

The essence of identification is as follows. Under Assumption
8
, for all
l,m\in\mathcal{L}
and
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=l,\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}=m,T_{i}=t\mid\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z},S_{i}=1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=0}^{C}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=l\mid L_{i}=c)\mathbb{P}(T_{i}=t\mid L_{i}=c,\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z})\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=c\mid\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z})\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}=m\mid L_{i}=c),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=l,\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}=m\mid\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z},S_{i}=1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=0}^{C}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=l\mid L_{i}=c)\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=c\mid\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z})\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}=m\mid L_{i}=c).

These two factorizations construct the system of equations. In this system, the left-hand sides of both equations are observable and the three terms on the right-hand sides
(i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=l\mid L_{i}=c)
,

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}=m\mid L_{i}=c)
, and

\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=c\mid\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z})
)
are shared between equations. Since the number of equations exceeds the number of unknown parameters, the system is usually identifiable only up to scaling and permutation. However, because the coder classification probabilities
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}=l\mid L_{i}=c)
sum to one across all categories (by definition of probability) and attain their maximum at
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i}=c)
(Assumption
9
), all unknown parameters are in fact identified. Once the coder-specific error rates are recovered for all coders, the target quantity can be obtained by another factorization

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=l,\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}=m\mid T_{i}=t,\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z},S_{i}=1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=0}^{C}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=l\mid L_{i}=c)\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=c\mid T_{i}=t,\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z})\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}=m\mid L_{i}=c),

where the only unknown is
\mathbb{P}(L_{i}=c\mid T_{i}=t,\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z})
, which is used to calculate the parameter of interest.

4.2
Estimation and inference

Given the identification result, I next turn to estimation and inference. The main challenge here is that the true label
L_{i}
is not observed, so it cannot be used to learn the low-dimensional surrogate representation in the same way as in the previous section. To address this, I introduce the surrogate outcome

\displaystyle M_{c}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i})

\displaystyle=\biggl(\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=c\}-\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i}\neq c)}{\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i}=c)-\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)}=c\mid L_{i}\neq c)}\biggl)\biggl(\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)}=c\}-\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i}\neq c)}{\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)}=c\mid L_{i}=c)-\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)}=c\mid L_{i}\neq c)}\biggl)

for all levels of categories
c\in\mathcal{L}
.

Crucially, this surrogate outcome is identifiable and can be estimated from the data. Specifically, one first recovers each coder
j
’s classification accuracy,
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i})
, using the system of equations in Proposition
2
. Although the identification formula holds for any value of the predictor
t\in\{0,1\}
, precision improves when estimates are averaged across all levels. Once
\widehat{\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i})}
is obtained, these estimates can be substituted into the formula above to construct
\hat{M}_{c}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i})
.

This surrogate outcome serves as a substitute for the true label and
M_{c}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i})=\mathbbm{1}\{L_{i}=c\}
if both coders classify texts accurately (i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i}=c)=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i}=c)=1
). Formally, it can proven that this surrogate outcome is an unbiased estimate of the true label given either the true label or the texts: i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{c}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i})\mid L_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[M_{c}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})\mid\bm{Y}_{i}]=\mathbbm{1}\{L_{i}=c\}.

(14)

Furthermore, it can be shown that the outcome model predicting this surrogate outcome is mathematically equivalent to the one predicting the true, unobserved label
L_{i}
. See Appendix
S2.5
for the theoretical properties of the surrogate outcome.

Based on this surrogate outcome, I can extend the estimation strategies in the previous section.
Under Assumptions
4
,
7
, and
8
, the low-dimensional representation
\bm{W}_{i}
satisfies the conditional independence

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{i}\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ \{T_{i},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)}\}\mid\bm{W}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i},

(15)

which also implies
\bm{Y}_{i}\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ M_{c}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})\mid\bm{W}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}
. These conditions sufficiently characterize
\bm{W}_{i}
such that both the identification and estimator do not depend on the specific representations. As in the previous case,
\bm{W}_{i}=\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i})
is not observed, so it is necessary estimate a surrogate representation that satisfies the independence condition above. I thus use the independence constraint to characterize the estimation procedure of the surrogate representations.

The estimation procedure is mostly similar to the one described in the previous section. Specifically, once the surrogate outcome
\hat{M_{c}}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})
is estimated, solve the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\arg

\displaystyle\min_{\xi,\tilde{\lambda}_{c},\zeta,\nu_{j}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\biggl\{S_{i}\cdot\sum_{c\in\mathcal{L}}\biggl(\underbrace{\overbrace{\tilde{\mu}_{c}(\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i};\xi),\bm{Z}_{i};\tilde{\lambda}_{c})}^{\text{Outcome Model}}-\hat{M_{c}}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})}_{\text{Outcome Loss for Category }c}\biggr)^{2}

(16)

\displaystyle+\beta\cdot S_{i}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{J}\underbrace{\biggl(\overbrace{\kappa^{(j)}(\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i};\xi),\bm{Z}_{i};\nu_{j})}^{\text{Human Annotation Prediction}}-\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}\biggr)^{2}}_{\text{Human Annotation Prediction Loss for Coder }j}+\gamma\cdot\underbrace{\text{CrossEntropy}\biggl(T_{i},\overbrace{\rho_{t}(\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i};\xi),\bm{Z}_{i};\zeta)}^{\text{Surrogacy Score}}\biggr)}_{\text{Prediction Loss for Predictor $T_{i}$}}\biggr\},

where
\tilde{\mu}_{c}(\cdot;\tilde{\lambda}_{c})
is the outcome model for category
L_{i}=c
with the estimated surrogate outcome
\hat{M_{c}}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})
given
\bm{W}_{i}=\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i};\xi)
parametrized by
\tilde{\lambda}_{c}
,
\kappa^{(j)}(\cdot;\nu_{j})
is the prediction of the human annotation by coder
j
given
\bm{W}_{i}=\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i};\xi)
parametrized by
\nu_{j}
, and
(\beta,\gamma)
are the hyperparameters regulating the influence of all the loss components. Unlike the previous loss function, the human annotation prediction loss needs to be added to satisfy Equation (
15
).

To characterize the behavior of the estimator at the limit, as in the previous section, I first derive its influence function.

Theorem 3
(Influence Function)

The influence function for the distribution of the covariate-adjusted outcome under
T_{i}=t
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{t,c}(T_{i},\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i},S_{i},\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}

While this section has developed a methodology to account for human annotations with non-differential measurement errors, the proposed SRI approach relies on several additional assumptions. Three specific assumptions warrant particular attention: (1) the independent coding (Assumption

To mitigate this limitation, I propose a procedure that uses a limited number of true, gold-standard labels
L_{i}
and permutation tests to evaluate these assumptions. All procedures are illustrated in Figure
4
. The proposed procedure comprises four steps. First, researchers develop their own coding rule and apply it to code a small number of texts themselves. I assume that researchers can generate a tiny set of gold-standard labels that perfectly capture
L_{i}
for these texts. Given that researchers should have a clear understanding of the classification task, it is reasonable to posit that they can produce error-free labels for a small number of observations, such as 100 texts, although scaling this process without permitting errors is challenging. Second, researchers ask multiple human coders to annotate the texts.

Upon obtaining the researcher-annotated gold-standard labels
L_{i}
and the human-annotated labels
\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}
for the tiny subset of observations, researchers can then verify all the problematic assumptions. For Assumption
9
(a) (human label accuracy), researchers can verify each conditional probability and check if the assumptions are valid or not. For Assumption
8
(independent coding and exclusion restriction), I propose to use permutation tests to diagnose the independence constraints. Permutation tests are nonparametric statistical methods that are particularly advantageous and commonly utilized when dealing with a small number of observations, as conventional statistical tests often exhibit insufficient power in such scenarios (e.g.,
Good, 1994
;
Ojala and Garriga, 2009
;
Pesarin and Salmaso, 2010
;
Berrett et al., 2019
). By default, the null hypothesis of the permutation test is independence, and thus I use test of equivalence so that the independence is directly tested (e.g.
Wellek, 2002
;
Hartman and Hidalgo, 2018
). The proposed procedure is based on
Arboretti et al., 2018
, which formalized the permutation test for test of equivalence.

Formally, to directly evaluate the conditional independence specified in Assumption
8
, the objective of the test is to evaluate the following null and alternative hypotheses:

\displaystyle H_{0}:\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}\ \not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}\ \mid\ L_{i}\quad\text{or}\quad\bm{Y}_{i}\ \not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp\ \{\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}\}\ \mid\ L_{i}

\displaystyle H_{1}:\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i}\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ \ \tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}\ \mid\ L_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{Y}_{i}\ \mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}\ \{\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}\}\ \mid\ L_{i}.

To use the permutation test for conditional independence, since
L_{i}
is categorical, I can simply permute the observations within each stratum defined by
L_{i}
to nonparametrically assess conditional independence
(
Berrett et al., 2019
)
. For exclusion restriction, due to the high dimensionality of
\bm{Y}_{i}
, it is necessary to employ a scalar measure of statistical independence, such as the kernel-based measure proposed by
Gretton et al., 2012

1.

2.

For each stratum

(a)

: Compute the test statistic by

\displaystyle T_{l1}=\max\{0,d(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})-\delta\}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{l2}=\max\{0,d(\bm{Y}_{i},\{\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)}\})-\delta\}

•

\displaystyle p=\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbbm{1}\{T^{(b)}\leq T\}}{B+1}

Regarding the implementation of this test procedure, the following two points need to be considered. First, the standard representation of texts
\bm{Y}_{i}
is typically still too high‑dimensional, so the permutation test can be underpowered unless researchers have a large number of true, gold-standard labels. In practice, researchers may therefore benefit from applying dimension‑reduction techniques that preserve most of the variation in the data and then applying the proposed procedure. Second, the testing procedure is sensitive to the choice of equivalence margin: the larger the margin, the more likely researchers are to reject the null hypothesis. If researchers cannot choose the equivalence margin ex ante on the basis of substantive knowledge, they may instead focus on the equivalence interval, the smallest margin that would lead to rejection of the null hypothesis at the pre‑specified significance level, which is insensitive to the researcher’s choice of equivalence range
(
Hartman and Hidalgo, 2018
;
Hartman, 2021

.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, I introduce
Surrogate Representation Inference
(SRI), a design-based framework that addresses the challenges of incorporating high-dimensional unstructured data into statistical inference with a limited number of human annotations that may contain measurement errors. The SRI framework rests on a key design-based assumption: human coders rely only on unstructured data for annotations and do not directly look at the structured variables of interest. Under this assumption, unstructured data fully mediate the structured variables and human annotations, satisfying the statistical surrogacy condition. As unstructured data are high-dimensional, I propose learning their low-dimensional representations such that the surrogacy conditions continue to hold. The paper examines the setting in which a text-based variable functions as an outcome and develops identification conditions, a neural network architecture for representation learning, and semiparametric efficient estimation strategies based on the learned representations. When multiple annotations are available, SRI also corrects for non-differential measurement error in human annotations. The empirical application demonstrates that SRI significantly reduces the standard errors compared to the existing methodology. Systematic simulation studies in Appendix
S3
also confirm that when the accuracy of the machine learning proxy is moderate (e.g. less than 85%), the proposed SRI methodology can reduce the standard error approximately by half. These findings highlight the potential of SRI to enhance the precision and reliability of statistical inference with unstructured data, paving the way for future applications in diverse text or image-based research settings.

While this work has primarily focused on settings where text-based variable serves as an outcome, the proposed SRI procedure can be applied to diverse settings and modalities, such as those where unstructured data serves as control or predictors.
Another exciting direction for future work is the discovery of text features influenced by predictor of interest. Although this paper currently assumes researchers know which features to measure, unstructured data offers the potential to identify these features without prior specification, an advantage particularly useful for open-ended questions. Looking ahead, future research should develop a unified framework that supports data-driven discovery of outcome concepts and perform the valid and efficient statistical inference with minimal amount of labeling. Finally, it would be valuable to explore how LLM‑generated annotations can be incorporated into the SRI methodology. Although the current framework primarily addresses human annotations with non‑differential error, it remains valid when annotations are produced by LLMs, provided that the required assumptions hold. Some assumptions, such as independent coding, may be difficult to satisfy with LLMs because of overlap in their training data. Future work should therefore investigate ways to relax these assumptions or devise prompting strategies that ensure LLM annotations meet the necessary conditions.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}})\biggr)\biggl(S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}})\biggr)^{\top}\biggr]^{-1}

where
O_{i}=\{T_{i},\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Z}_{i},S_{i}\}
is the set of variables required to consider the score,
S(O_{i})
is the score function, and
\Pi(\cdot\mid\cdot)
is the projection operator (note that we can remove

from
O_{i}
when considering the score since it is a deterministic function of
\bm{Y}_{i}
). As
\mathcal{T}_{\text{SRI}}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}}
,

\displaystyle\underbrace{S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{SRI}})}_{\text{Efficient Score for SRI}}=\underbrace{S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}})}_{\text{Efficient Score for PPI}}+\underbrace{\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{SRI}})}_{:=\Delta\ (\text{residual term)}}

where the residual term
\Delta
is orthogonal to the efficient score for PPI. As a result, since
\Delta\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}}
and the efficient score belongs to the orthogonal complement of the tangent space,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{SRI}})\biggr)\biggl(S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{SRI}})\biggr)^{\top}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}})+\Delta\biggr)\biggl(S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}})+\Delta\biggr)^{\top}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}})\biggr)\biggl(S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}})\biggr)^{\top}\biggr]+\mathbb{E}[\Delta\Delta^{\top}]

\displaystyle\succeq\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}})\biggr)\biggl(S(O_{i})-\Pi(S(O_{i})\mid\mathcal{T}_{\text{PPI}})\biggr)^{\top}\biggr]

where the third equality is by the orthogonality of the residual term to the efficient score that lies in the orthocomplement of the tangent space. Therefore, as most of the existing literature on the bias-correction estimator works on the semiparametric model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{PPI}}
, regardless of the form of the estimators, without imposing any additional restrictions, such as parametric models, SRI always has a lower semiparametric efficiency bound and the proposed estimator in Section
3
attains this bound.

From this theoretical analysis, it is expected that when the machine learning predictions already achieve high accuracy, there should not be much efficiency gain in reality. This is because our efficiency gains come from the fact that the machine learning predictions do not necessarily satisfy the independence constraint, but if they are almost accurate, as they almost satisfy the independence constraint, there is no reason to expect that the residual term
\Delta
will become large. I confirm this conjecture in the application section (Section
5
) and the simulation section (Section
S3
).

For this proof, we use the following lemma to deal with the so-called empirical process term. See

Lemma 1
(Lemma 2 of
Kennedy et al., 2020

Let

be a function estimated from a sample
(Z_{n+1},\cdots,Z_{N})

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Z_{i}}

(Z_{n+1},\cdots,Z_{N})

\displaystyle\{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}-\mathcal{P}\}(\hat{f}-f)=O_{p}\biggl(\frac{\lVert\hat{f}-f\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\biggr)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
is
L_{2}
norm and
\mathcal{P}(f)=\mathcal{P}\{f(Z_{i})\}=\int f(z)d\mathcal{P}(z)

Proof.

Let
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
be a probability distribution consistent with the estimated nuisance functions
\hat{\pi}
,
\hat{\mu}
, and
\hat{\bm{f}}
,
\Psi_{t}(\mathcal{P})
be an estimator constructed from
n
i.i.d. sample from
\mathcal{P}

O_{i}
be a specific observed data point indexed by
i

\psi_{t}(O_{i},\mathcal{P})
be an efficient influence function of the observation
O_{i}
under the distribution
\mathcal{P}
.

Now, consider the parametric submodel
\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}=(1-\epsilon)\mathcal{P}+\epsilon\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
. Recall that the von Mises expansion yields

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})=\Psi_{t}(\mathcal{P})+\frac{d\Psi(\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon})}{d\epsilon}\biggl|_{\epsilon=1}(0-1)+R(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n},\mathcal{P})

where

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\biggl(\Psi_{t}(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})-\Psi_{t}(\mathcal{P})\biggr)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{t}(O_{i},\mathcal{P})-H_{1}+H_{2}-\sqrt{n}R(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n},\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle H_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{t}(O_{i},\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}),\quad H_{2}=\sqrt{n}\{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}-\mathcal{P}\}(\hat{\psi}_{t}-\psi_{t})

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}

\displaystyle H_{2}

Hence, by replacing only the original outcome model with

Lemma 3
(Characterization of the observed data influence function)

Any observed influence function
Q
and any full data influence function
Q^{F}
must satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q\mid S_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Q^{F}\mid S_{i}]

(S9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q\mid\bm{Y}_{i},T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Q^{F}\mid\bm{Y}_{i},T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}]

Proof.

This proof goes analogous to that of Lemma 2 in
Zhou and Tchetgen, 2024
. Let
G_{S_{i}}(\cdot)
be a coarsening operator with coarsening variable
S_{i}
, which just means that

\displaystyle G_{S_{i}}(\{\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}\}_{j=1}^{J})=\begin{cases}\{\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\cdots,\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}\}\quad\text{ if }S_{i}=1\\
\emptyset\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and
Q=Q(S_{i},T_{i},\bm{Y}_{i},G_{S_{i}}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i}))
be any observed data influence function. Then,
Q
must satisfy the following
(
Tsiatis, 2006
;
Ichimura and Newey, 2022
)
:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\Psi_{t,c}(\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon})-\Psi_{t,c}(\mathcal{P})}{\epsilon}=\mathbb{E}\biggl[Q\;\mathbb{E}[U^{F}\mid S_{i},\bm{Z}_{i},T_{i},\bm{Y}_{i},G_{S_{i}}(\tilde{L}^{(1)}_{i},\tilde{L}^{(2)}_{i})]\biggr]=\mathbb{E}\biggl[Q\ U^{F}\biggr]

where

is the full data oracle score (see Chapter 7 of
Tsiatis, 2006
for the theoretical details of the observed data score function). Similarly, for the full data influence function, denoted as
Q^{F}
, we also have

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\Psi_{t,c}(\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon})-\Psi_{t,c}(\mathcal{P})}{\epsilon}=\mathbb{E}\biggl[Q^{F}U^{F}\biggr].

Now, by factorization,
U^{F}
(i.e., the full data score we considered in the previous setting) is written as

\displaystyle U^{F}=g_{s}(S_{i})+g_{z}(\bm{Z}_{i})+g_{t}(T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})+g_{y}(\bm{Y}_{i},T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}).

for any

g_{z}\in\Lambda_{Z}^{F}

g_{t}\in\Lambda_{T}^{F}

g_{y}\in\Lambda_{Y}^{F}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{S}^{F}=\biggl\{g_{s}(S_{i})\in\mathcal{H}^{F}:\mathbb{E}[g_{s}(S_{i})]=0\biggr\}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{Z}^{F}=\biggl\{g_{z}(\bm{Z}_{i})\in\mathcal{H}^{F}:\mathbb{E}[g_{z}(\bm{Z}_{i})]=0\biggr\}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{T}^{F}=\biggl\{g_{t}(T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})\in\mathcal{H}^{F}:\mathbb{E}[g_{t}(T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})\mid\bm{Z}_{i}]=0\biggr\}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\bm{Y}}^{F}=\biggl\{g_{y}(\bm{Y}_{i},T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})\in\mathcal{H}^{F}:\mathbb{E}[g_{y}(\bm{Y}_{i},T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i})\mid T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}]=0\biggr\},

and

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\Psi_{t,c}(\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon})-\Psi_{t,c}(\mathcal{P})}{\epsilon}

Thus, to align these two expressions for any

,
g_{s}\in\Lambda_{T}^{F}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q\mid S_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Q^{F}\mid S_{i}],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q\mid\bm{Z}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Q^{F}\mid\bm{Z}_{i}],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q\mid T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Q^{F}\mid T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q\mid\bm{Y}_{i},T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Q^{F}\mid\bm{Y}_{i},T_{i},\bm{Z}_{i}].

Note that the second and third conditions are redundant since the fourth implies the second and the third by the law of iterated expectations.

. Thus, we only need to show that the second term is asymptotically negligible at the same rate. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\mathbb{E}[\hat{M}_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})-M_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})\mid\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i}]\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\biggl\{\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{M}_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})-M_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})\biggr|^{2}\mid\bm{f}(\bm{Y}_{i}),\bm{Z}_{i}\biggr]\biggr\}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{M}_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})-M_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]^{\frac{1}{2}}

where the first inequality is by Jensen’s inequality and the second line is by the law of iterated expectation. We thus only need to bound the
L^{2}
convergence of surrogate outcome. Let

\displaystyle g(X,u,v)=\frac{X-u}{v-u},\quad X\in\{0,1\},\;0\leq u<v\leq 1.

As
u<v
, there always exists a constant
c_{3}>0
such that
v-u\geq c_{3}
. Then, by fixing
X
, the quotient rule yields

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{u}g&=\frac{-(u-v)-(X-u)(-1)}{(u-v)^{2}}=\frac{-(u-v)+(X-u)}{(u-v)^{2}}=\frac{X-v}{(u-v)^{2}}\\
\partial_{v}g&=\frac{-(X-u)}{(u-v)^{2}}=\frac{X-u}{(u-v)^{2}}\end{cases}

Hence, for any
(u,v)
, we have

\displaystyle|\partial_{u}g|=\frac{|X-v|}{(v-u)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{(v-u)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{c_{3}^{2}},\quad|\partial_{v}g|=\frac{|X-u|}{(v-u)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{(v-u)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{c_{3}^{2}}

Let
\gamma(t)=[u_{1},v_{1}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}+t\cdot[u_{2},v_{2}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
and set
h(t)=g(\gamma(t))
. By mean value theorem, there exists
t^{*}\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle g(X,u_{2},v_{2})-g(X,u_{1},v_{1})=h(1)-h(0)=h^{\prime}(t^{*})

Notice that by chain rule,
h^{\prime}(t)=\nabla g(\gamma(t))\cdot([u_{2},v_{2}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}-[u_{1},v_{1}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}})
. Hence, by triangular inequality,

\displaystyle|g(X,u_{2},v_{2})-g(X,u_{1},v_{1})|

\displaystyle\leq|\partial_{u}g(\gamma(t^{*}))|\cdot|u_{2}-u_{1}|+|\partial_{v}g(\gamma(t^{*}))|\cdot|v_{2}-v_{1}|

(S10)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{3}^{2}}\biggl(|u_{1}-u_{2}|+|v_{1}-v_{2}|\biggr).

Also, notice that

\displaystyle|X-u|=\begin{cases}u\quad(x=0)\\
1-u\quad(x=1)\end{cases}\leq 1

and hence

\displaystyle|g(X,u,v)|=\frac{|X-u|}{v-u}\leq\frac{1}{v-u}\leq\frac{1}{c_{3}}.

(S11)

Now, let

\displaystyle p_{c}^{(j)}=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i}\neq c),\quad q_{c}^{(j)}=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i}\neq c),\quad X^{(j)}_{c}=\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}=c\}

for all
j
and
k\in\{1,\cdots,K\}
, and denote
\hat{p}_{c}^{(j)}=\widehat{\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i}\neq c)}
and
\hat{q}_{c}^{(j)}=\widehat{\mathbb{P}(\tilde{L}^{(j)}_{i}=c\mid L_{i}=c)}
as the predicted quantities. Using the derived inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\biggl|\hat{M}_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})-M_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})\biggr|

\displaystyle=\biggl|g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(2)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)-g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},{p}_{c}^{(1)},{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},{p}_{c}^{(2)},{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)\biggr|

\displaystyle=\biggl|g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)\biggl(g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(2)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)-g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},{p}_{c}^{(2)},{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)\biggr)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},{p}_{c}^{(2)},{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)\biggl(g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)-g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},{p}_{c}^{(1)},{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)\biggr)\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\biggl|g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)\biggl(g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(2)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)-g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},{p}_{c}^{(2)},{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)\biggr)\biggr|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\biggl|g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},{p}_{c}^{(2)},{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)\biggl(g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)-g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},{p}_{c}^{(1)},{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)\biggr)\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq|g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)|\biggl|g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(2)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)-g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},{p}_{c}^{(2)},{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)\biggr|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+|g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},{p}_{c}^{(2)},{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)|\biggl|g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)-g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},{p}_{c}^{(1)},{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{3}}\biggl(\biggl|g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(2)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)-g\bigl(X_{c}^{(2)},{p}_{c}^{(2)},{q}_{c}^{(2)}\bigr)\biggr|+\biggl|g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)},\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)-g\bigl(X_{c}^{(1)},{p}_{c}^{(1)},{q}_{c}^{(1)}\bigr)\biggr|\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{3}^{3}}\biggl(|\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)}-{p}_{c}^{(1)}|+|\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}-{q}_{c}^{(1)}|+|\hat{p}_{c}^{(2)}-{p}_{c}^{(2)}|+|\hat{q}_{c}^{(2)}-{q}_{c}^{(2)}|\biggr)

where the first inequality is by triangular inequality, the second is by Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, the third is by Equation (
S11
), and the last is by Equation (

). Now, by Cauchy-Schwartz,

\displaystyle(a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}+a_{4})^{2}=\biggl(\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&a_{4}\end{bmatrix}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}_{:=\bm{a}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&1\end{bmatrix}}_{:=\mathbf{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}}\biggr)^{2}\leq\lVert\bm{a}\rVert^{2}\lVert\mathbf{1}\rVert^{2}=4(a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}+a_{4}^{2})

for any

. Applying this, we obtain

\displaystyle\biggl|\hat{M}_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})-M_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})\biggr|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{c_{3}^{6}}\biggl(|\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)}-{p}_{c}^{(1)}|^{2}+|\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}-{q}_{c}^{(1)}|^{2}+|\hat{p}_{c}^{(2)}-{p}_{c}^{(2)}|^{2}+|\hat{q}_{c}^{(2)}-{q}_{c}^{(2)}|^{2}\biggr).

By taking the expectation, we finally get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl|\hat{M}_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})-M_{c}(\tilde{L}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{L}_{i}^{(2)})\biggr|^{2}\biggr]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{c_{3}^{6}}\biggl\{\mathbb{E}\biggl[|\hat{p}_{c}^{(1)}-{p}_{c}^{(1)}|^{2}\biggr]+\mathbb{E}\biggl[|\hat{q}_{c}^{(1)}-{q}_{c}^{(1)}|^{2}\biggr]+\mathbb{E}\biggl[|\hat{p}_{c}^{(2)}-{p}_{c}^{(2)}|^{2}\biggr]+\mathbb{E}\biggl[|\hat{q}_{c}^{(2)}-{q}_{c}^{(2)}|^{2}\biggr]\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{c_{3}^{6}}\biggl(o_{p}(n^{-1/2})+o_{p}(n^{-1/2})+o_{p}(n^{-1/2})+o_{p}(n^{-1/2})\biggr)=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})

where the last line is by using Assumption

S3.1
Case 1: Perfect Human Annotations

S3.1.1
Simulation setup

I conduct simulation studies to evaluate the empirical performance of the proposed SRI estimator and compare it with existing estimators. Given that it is extremely difficult to generate texts that are influenced by predictors in a synthesized experiment, I instead generate high-dimensional vectors that have similar dimensions to standard sentence embeddings and make them influenced by the predictors in the data-generating process. Specifically, I generate data with
N=20,000
units using the following data-generating process:

\displaystyle T_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)

(S12)

\displaystyle\text{For }j\in[1,\cdots,2048]

\displaystyle\alpha_{1j}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0.0,1.0]

\displaystyle Y_{ij}=\alpha_{1j}T_{i}+\epsilon_{i},\quad\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{i}=\mathbbm{1}\{\sigma(1.0+0.2\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}\bm{Y}_{i})>0.5\}.

I then artificially simulate machine-predicted labels
\hat{L}_{i}
with accuracies ranging from 70% to 90%, randomly changing the value of
\tilde{L}_{i}
according to the selected error percentage (i.e., 10% of
\tilde{L}_{i}
values are randomly changed for
\hat{L}_{i}
if the accuracy of
\hat{L}_{i}
is set to 90%).

To implement the proposed SRI estimator, I use 2-fold cross-fitting with the neural network architecture illustrated in Figure
2
. Specifically, the neural network architecture has two layers with dimensions 100 and 50 leading to the shared layer
\bm{f}(\bm{R}_{i})
. The shared layer is then connected to prediction heads for both the outcome and the predictor of interest, each consisting of two layers with dimensions 50 and 1 (i.e., predicting a scalar at last). I use ReLU activations and solve the optimization problem in Equation
5
with
\alpha=1
using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.00002, a maximum of 200 epochs, and a batch size of 256
(
Kingma and Ba, 2017
)
. I use 20% of the input as validation data and implement early stopping with a patience of 5 epochs to prevent overfitting (i.e., if the loss computed on the validation data does not decrease for five consecutive epochs, we stop training). Once the outcome model and surrogacy score are estimated, their estimates are used to calculate the difference in outcome means using the estimating equation in Equation (
7
).

As in the empirical application in Section
5
, I evaluate the performance of the proposed estimator by comparing it to the existing bias correction techniques
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2023
;
Egami et al., 2024
)
. Note that I omit any control variables
\bm{Z}_{i}
from this simulation for ease of comparison. Because the proposed estimator must estimate the propensity score when

is included, differences in efficiency may arise from this additional estimation step, whereas the alternative methods do not require it

Hirano et al., 2003
)
