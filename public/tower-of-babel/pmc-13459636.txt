Pain in Parkinson’s disease: bridging pathophysiology, clinical features, and personalized management
Authors: Thalis Asimakopoulos, Iliana Psychari, Martina Rekatsina, Zinovia Kefalopoulou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pain is a highly prevalent and disabling non-motor symptom of Parkinson’s disease (PD), yet it remains underrecognized and frequently undertreated in clinical practice. Although long considered a secondary consequence of motor dysfunction, pain in PD is now increasingly understood as a manifestation of disease-related alterations in nociceptive processing. These changes extend beyond dopaminergic deficiency and involve broader neurotransmitter imbalances and impaired descending inhibitory control across different neuroanatomical regions, providing a framework for understanding pain as an intrinsic feature of PD rather than a purely peripheral or motor-related phenomenon. We also summarize the main categories of pain experienced in PD, each with distinct clinical features and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, and link them with current management strategies. A multimodal, mechanism-informed approach that integrates optimization of dopaminergic therapy with non-dopaminergic pharmacological treatments, neuromodulation, rehabilitation, and complementary interventions is key to a personalized treatment plan for pain in patients with PD. Finally, we highlight emerging translational directions, including candidate biomarkers that may help objectify pain and its related dysfunction in the PD population. Focusing on the neurobiological basis of pain in PD, this review aims to support a shift toward mechanism-based assessment and management, with an ultimate goal of improving pain outcomes and quality of life for individuals living with the disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02416-w.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder primarily characterized by motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, and postural instability. However, non-motor symptoms, such as cognitive dysfunction, autonomic disturbances, sleep disorders, and pain, are increasingly recognized as major contributors to the disease burden [1]. Among these, pain is one of the most prevalent yet often underappreciated symptoms, affecting up to 80% of patients with PD and significantly impairing their quality of life [2, 3]. While historically considered a secondary consequence of motor dysfunction, pain in PD is increasingly recognized as a complex phenomenon with mechanisms that extend beyond musculoskeletal strain. Thus, PD patients may experience various types of pain (Fig. 1), not only due to peripheral musculoskeletal abnormalities but also because of complex, multifactorial mechanisms [4] involving dysfunction in the dopaminergic, serotonergic, and noradrenergic pathways [5]. Current research has revealed that pain in PD may involve central sensitization, altered neurotransmitter signaling, and disrupted sensory processing, underscoring the need for a more structured approach to its characterization and treatment [6].

These findings raise critical questions: Is pain an intrinsic feature of PD pathophysiology? How can we refine the classification and assessment of PD-related pain to distinguish it from other comorbid pain conditions? What are the key neurobiological mechanisms—beyond dopaminergic dysfunction—that drive altered pain perception in PD? Furthermore, given the heterogeneity of PD-related pain phenotypes, why do some patients experience severe pain while others remain relatively unaffected? Addressing these questions is crucial to improving the understanding, diagnosis, and management of pain in PD.

This review aims to present a thorough and clinically applicable summary of the most recent findings on PD pain, with an emphasis on its mechanisms, assessment, and treatment. By incorporating current knowledge, it seeks to improve clinical decision-making and patient care by gaining a deeper comprehension of pain pathophysiology and available treatments. Additionally, we highlight unresolved questions and future research directions, including the role of biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets. Given the significant burden of pain in PD, a deeper understanding of its background and management strategies is essential to improving patient outcomes and advancing personalized treatment approaches.

Methods

This narrative review was conducted in accordance with the scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) criteria [7]. A structured literature search was performed in PubMed and supplemented by Google Scholar and Scopus to identify studies examining pain perception in Parkinson’s disease. Predefined search terms included “pain,” “nociception,” “pathophysiology,” and “non-motor symptoms.” Study selection was based on relevance to the mechanistic and clinical aspects of pain in Parkinson’s disease. Priority was given to recent publications, with inclusion of seminal studies where appropriate. Only articles published in English were considered. This review is based exclusively on previously published data and did not require ethical approval.

Types of pain in Parkinson’s disease

Ford and Goetz et al. historically distinguished the following pain categories in PD: musculoskeletal pain, radicular/neuropathic pain, dystonia-related pain, akathitic pain, and central pain [8, 9]. More recent classification frameworks, including the Parkinson’s Disease Pain Classification System (PD-PCS) proposed by Mylius et al., increasingly incorporate mechanism-based phenotyping, grouping PD-related pain syndromes according to nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic mechanisms [4, 10]. A conceptual overview integrating traditional clinical pain syndromes with contemporary mechanism-based frameworks is presented in Fig. 1.

Nociceptive pain

Nociceptive pain arises from activation of peripheral nociceptors by actual or threatened tissue damage and represents the most common pain mechanism in PD [4]. In PD, nociceptive pain predominantly develops secondary to motor abnormalities such as rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability, abnormal muscle contractions, and skeletal deformities, all of which increase mechanical stress on musculoskeletal structures. Visceral nociceptive pain may also occur, manifesting as poorly localized thoracic, abdominal, or pelvic discomfort. Within this mechanism-based framework, the two principal nociceptive pain syndromes in PD are musculoskeletal pain and dystonic pain.

Musculoskeletal pain

Musculoskeletal pain represents the most commonly reported pain subtype in PD, with estimates ranging between 40% and 90% of patients experiencing it during the disease course [11]. This type of pain, which is typically regarded as nociceptive, results from parkinsonian motor abnormalities that cause dysfunction in the bones, muscles, joints, and connective tissues. Recent mechanism-based frameworks, including the updated ICD-11 criteria, further classify musculoskeletal pain in PD as chronic secondary musculoskeletal pain associated with altered biomechanical function, motor fluctuations, and dopaminergic state changes [12]. Increasing the mechanical loading on musculoskeletal structures due to rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability, and tremor frequently causes pain or discomfort in the hips, shoulders, neck, and lower back [13]. Notably, one of the first symptoms to appear is shoulder pain, which can occur before motor manifestations and frequently results in missed or delayed recognition [14]. Long-term pain syndromes are perpetuated by muscle rigidity, which not only modifies movement biomechanics but also maintains aberrant muscle contractions that cause localized myofascial stress and ischemia [15].

This musculoskeletal burden is further exacerbated in PD patients by age-related degenerative changes. Since PD patients are usually elderly, they are more likely to have both sarcopenia and osteoarthritis coexist [16]. Joint strain is exacerbated, and widespread pain is a result of the combination of preexisting degenerative changes and PD-induced rigidity. This combination has been linked to decreased mobility and poorer functional outcomes [13]. Furthermore, musculoskeletal pain may vary in intensity with motor symptoms, particularly during OFF periods of dopaminergic treatment cycles. This makes it challenging to differentiate between central or dystonic pain types without a thorough clinical evaluation [14]. The King’s Parkinson’s Disease Pain Scale (KPPS) includes musculoskeletal pain as a specific domain, reflecting its clinical relevance and frequent occurrence [16].

According to newly available longitudinal data, chronic musculoskeletal pain may be a risk factor for PD in addition to being a symptom of the disease. According to a large 13-year cohort study, people who experience chronic pain at multiple musculoskeletal sites are much more likely to develop PD, and there is a dose-response relationship between the number of painful sites and the incidence of PD [15]. This discovery suggests that prodromal neurodegenerative processes may be influenced by nociceptive pain and the peripheral inflammation it causes. Even though musculoskeletal pain is still underdiagnosed and undertreated in clinical settings, its prevalence in PD patients—often affecting multiple anatomical regions—has been linked to impaired quality of life, disturbed sleep, and a higher risk of using analgesics [13]. All things considered, musculoskeletal pain in PD is a serious clinical issue that calls for specialized diagnostic and treatment approaches that are different from those used for central or neuropathic pain syndromes.

Dystonic pain

Dystonic pain in PD is a distinct and often severe form of pain that arises from involuntary, sustained muscle contractions leading to abnormal postures or repetitive movements. It is commonly experienced in the context of motor fluctuations, particularly during the “OFF” periods when dopaminergic medication levels decline and motor symptoms reemerge. The pain associated with dystonia is typically described as intense, cramping, or pulling, and is most frequently localized to the feet, toes, hands, neck, or jaw, depending on the distribution of the dystonic movement. It may present unilaterally or asymmetrically, often matching the side more affected by parkinsonian symptoms [14, 17]. Although dystonic pain can occur at any disease stage, it is especially prominent in younger-onset PD and in patients with pronounced motor fluctuations, sometimes even preceding visible dystonic postures, which may delay clinical recognition [18].

The temporal correlation between dopaminergic states and dystonic pain is a crucial aspect. Early morning dystonia, which occurs before the first medication dose, or the wearing-OFF phases of medications later in the day are the times when dystonia—and the pain it causes—occurs most frequently. Due to its waxing and waning characteristics, this pain is episodic but extremely predictable, and it often correlates with low levels of plasma levodopa [19]. Though less frequent and poorly understood, some patients also experience “on-dystonia,” a condition in which dystonia paradoxically manifests at the highest dosages of medications. In either scenario, even when motor symptoms are otherwise very well managed, the excruciating dystonic contractions can be extremely incapacitating and emotionally draining, greatly lowering quality of life.

Distinctly localized and temporally associated with the underlying motor phenomenon, dystonic pain differs from musculoskeletal pain, which is typically more diffuse, and posture related. Its primary pathophysiological cause is dysfunction of the basal ganglia, specifically in circuits that mediate inhibitory control of motor output, such as those involving the putamen, globus pallidus, and supplementary motor areas. When this regulatory system becomes disinhibited, aberrant firing patterns emerge, leading to excessive muscle contractions characteristic of dystonia. The resulting pain is often disproportionate to the duration or visibility of the dystonic movement itself and is caused by ischemia, abnormal proprioceptive feedback loops, and mechanical strain [14, 17]. Crucially, this type of pain can arise independently of any peripheral structural abnormalities because it is inherently linked to the neurophysiology of PD. This emphasizes the importance of appropriate classification and clinical awareness.

Neuropathic pain

Neuropathic pain in PD is a distinct clinical condition that is defined by abnormal somatosensory processing resulting from dysfunction or lesions in the somatosensory system [17]. It is now estimated that up to 20–25% of PD patients have neuropathic features, despite their historical underrecognized status. These features can include small fiber neuropathy affecting peripheral nerves or radicular pain resulting from spinal nerve compression [20]. The radicular subtype of neuropathic pain, often described as sharp, burning, or electric shock-like, is typically unilateral and follows dermatomal patterns, frequently affecting the lower back and legs [14]. PD-related rigidity or axial deformity, as well as mechanical spinal degeneration, can both contribute to this condition, which is made worse by postural changes and increases the likelihood of nerve root irritation [21].

A peripheral neuropathic component in PD is increasingly associated with small fiber neuropathy (SFN), which may be present even in de novo or drug-naïve patients [22]. This is characterized by length-dependent symptoms such as burning, tingling, or numbness, typically in the feet, and is often accompanied by reduced intraepidermal nerve fiber density on skin biopsy [20]. Proposed mechanisms include vitamin B12 deficiency, mitochondrial dysfunction, and α-synuclein deposition in peripheral nerves, particularly in patients receiving chronic levodopa therapy, although the etiology is still multifactorial [23]. These patients’ sensory abnormalities can also be identified using the DN4 diagnostic tool, which has demonstrated good discriminatory power for neuropathic pain syndromes in PD, or quantitative sensory testing [24].

From a pathophysiological standpoint, both central and peripheral alterations most likely mediate the experience of neuropathic pain in PD. Peripherally, inflammatory and degenerative processes change nociceptive input and reduce pain thresholds, while centrally, loss of dopaminergic inhibition and aberrant thalamocortical rhythms lead to hyperexcitability in pain-processing regions [18, 19].

Nociplastic pain

In addition to nociceptive and neuropathic pain mechanisms, PD-related pain may also exhibit nociplastic features [4]. According to the IASP, nociplastic pain is defined as “pain that arises from altered nociception despite no clear evidence of actual or threatened tissue damage causing activation of peripheral nociceptors or evidence for disease or lesion of the somatosensory system causing the pain [25, 26].” Rather than reflecting peripheral tissue injury or structural nervous system damage, nociplastic pain is thought to result from altered central nociceptive processing and dysfunctional pain modulation. Importantly, nociplastic mechanisms may coexist with nociceptive or neuropathic pain states and should not be regarded as mutually exclusive categories [26]. Recent mechanism-based classification frameworks increasingly recognize nociplastic mechanisms as contributors to PD-related pain phenotypes [4]. Within this framework, pain syndromes historically described as central pain, as well as restlessness-related pain states may reflect altered central pain processing and central sensitization in PD, characterized by pain hypersensitivity, hyperalgesia, and impaired pain modulation in PD.

Central pain

Central Parkinson’s pain is a unique and complex type of pain that is thought to be caused by PD itself rather than by musculoskeletal disorders, rigidity, or dystonia. According to estimates, 4–10% of patients are affected, but because of its elusive clinical features and diagnostic ambiguity, the true prevalence is probably underestimated [23].

In the clinical context, this pain is commonly characterized as diffuse, aching, burning, cramping, stabbing, or exhibiting a pins-and-needles sensation. It can occur intermittently or persistently and is frequently bilateral, commonly involving the limbs, but also extending to less typical sites such as the face, mouth, chest, abdomen, pelvis, and genitalia [17, 23, 27]. In some patients, the pain is more pronounced on the side where motor symptoms initially emerged, while in others, the distribution bears no apparent relationship to motor asymmetry or disease progression. Crucially, in these cases, no other somatic or neurological cause is typically found.

Motor symptoms and central Parkinson’s pain can fluctuate together, with the former frequently getting worse during OFF periods. Nevertheless, its phenomenology points to a fundamentally different origin, and it is not directly related to rigidity or dystonic posturing. The experience of pain may exhibit a visceral or autonomic character and is frequently described as emotionally distressing or obsessional in quality—features that underscore its divergence from nociceptive or peripheral neuropathic types [17].

The underlying mechanisms of central pain in PD remain incompletely understood and are discussed in detail in the section on pain pathophysiology. Briefly, current hypotheses implicate dysregulation within the basal ganglia–thalamocortical and diencephalo-spinal sensory pathways, as well as involvement of the medial spinoreticulothalamic system and its autonomic projections [23]. Additional speculation suggests that aberrant processing within affective-emotional components of pain perception—potentially involving limbic circuits or dopaminergic autonomic centers—may contribute to the experience of central pain in PD [27].

Central Parkinson’s pain is notable for being fundamentally different from classical central neuropathic pain syndromes, including central post-stroke pain. Classical central pain is caused by focal lesions in the somatosensory system, usually presents as anatomically congruent patterns of sensory deficits, and progresses in time from lesion to symptom. On the other hand, central pain in PD does not require a visible structural lesion, exhibits variable somatotopy, and lacks consistent sensory loss. These distinctions support the use of the term central Parkinson’s pain to reflect its unique clinical and pathophysiological profile [23].

Akathisia and other pain related symptoms

Patients’ overall discomfort in PD is greatly influenced by a few non-primary pain syndromes. Among these, akathisia and restless legs syndrome (RLS) are especially relevant for their subjective intensity and potential to be interpreted as painful.

Akathisia in PD is defined as a persistent inner restlessness that compels the individual to move or change position. In its most extreme manifestations, it can be extremely uncomfortable or even painful, even though it is not strictly classified as a pain syndrome. Patients report an inability to remain seated during meals, drive, or engage in social interactions [17]. It is distinct from other causes of movement such as dyskinesias, anxiety, or urinary urgency [27]. Approximately half of patients with akathisia report symptom fluctuations in line with dopaminergic medication schedules, particularly during OFF periods [17]. In the mesocortical pathway, which starts in the ventral tegmental area and is implicated in the pathophysiology of PD, the disorder has been mechanistically linked to dopaminergic deficiency [27].

RLS is also reported in up to 20% of individuals with PD and may produce discomfort or pain, particularly at night. It is frequently associated with ON–OFF fluctuations in advanced stages of the disease and must be carefully distinguished from night-time akinesia-related pain [28].

These syndromes illustrate how PD-related discomfort may extend beyond classic pain categories, highlighting the complex interplay between sensorimotor symptoms and altered central processing, within a broader mechanism-based framework encompassing nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic pain mechanisms [4].

Mechanisms of pain in Parkinson’s disease

Neuroanatomical and neurophysiological basis of pain processing in Parkinson’s disease

A highly interconnected network of cortical, subcortical, brainstem, and spinal structures make up the neuroanatomical substrates of pain processing [22] (Fig. 2). These areas combine the sensory, affective, and cognitive aspects of pain perception to create what is known as the “pain matrix”, a dispersed system. In PD, numerous components of this network are affected by neurodegenerative changes, contributing to altered pain experiences and reduced pain threshold [1]. At the cellular level, these alterations are driven by maladaptive synaptic plasticity and disrupted intracellular signaling, leading to hyperexcitability within nociceptive circuits [29]; these changes are further amplified by neuroinflammatory processes and α-synuclein–related neuronal dysfunction, collectively promoting persistent pain states (Supplementary Table 1). Understanding the anatomical architecture of the affected structures and functions provides critical insight into the pathophysiology of pain in PD.

The primary somatosensory cortex (S1) and secondary somatosensory cortex (S2) are key components of the sensory-discriminative component of pain perception at the cortical level. While S2 aids in the integration and recognition of pain, S1 encodes the location and intensity of unpleasant stimuli. Both areas are functionally connected to the insula and receive projections from the thalamus, which is necessary for combining visceral and somatic pain signals and creating the subjective perception of pain [30]. Affective-emotional appraisal of pain involves the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), especially its dorsal subdivision [31]. In PD, the ACC exhibits altered activation, indicating dysfunctional top-down modulation [32].

Subcortically, the thalamus serves as the principal relay center for nociceptive information ascending from the spinal cord. It is essential for allocating sensory input and projects to both limbic and somatosensory cortices [33]. The ventral posterolateral (VPL) and mediodorsal (MD) thalamic nuclei have been shown to undergo structural and functional changes in PD, which may compromise the integrity of pain signals and lead to aberrant pain perceptions [34].

The insula and ACC are interconnected with the amygdala, a critical component of the limbic system involved in assigning emotional salience to pain [35]. The thalamus and parabrachial nucleus transmit nociceptive information to the amygdala, which interprets pain’s affective and emotional aspects. Following its transmission from the amygdala to the brainstem and hypothalamus, this information modifies the brain’s autonomic and behavioral reactions [36]. The amygdala’s neurodegeneration in PD may hinder the processing of emotional pain and be a contributing factor to the distress frequently observed in PD-associated pain [37].

Through its descending projections to the medullary rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM) and beyond to the spinal dorsal horn, the periaqueductal gray (PAG), another crucial area in the midbrain, plays a key role in pain modulation [38]. Coordinating behavioral reactions to pain, the PAG interacts with the ACC, amygdala, and hypothalamus, among other forebrain structures.

Nociceptive pathways also provide convergent input to the mesencephalic reticular formation and superior colliculi, which are located next to the PAG [39]. As neurodegeneration spreads through brainstem circuits in PD, these structures—which are involved in orienting and defensive behaviors in response to painful stimuli—may be impacted [40].

Crucially, the functional architecture of the basal ganglia network—specifically the interplay between the striatum, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, and substantia nigra—modulates not only motor control but also sensorimotor gating, including pain signaling [41]. The thalamocortical output is changed when this network is dysregulated in PD, which probably affects how nociceptive input is filtered and prioritized for higher-order processing centers. Neuroimaging studies of PD patients who are in pain have shown abnormal activity in the basal ganglia-thalamocortical loops, highlighting the part that network-level dysfunction plays in the distortion or amplification of pain signals [42]. Through links to the thalamus and brainstem pain circuits, the substantia nigra—in particular, its reticulata division—also aids in the regulation of pain [43]. Neurotransmitter involvement in pain processing in Parkinson’s disease

An intricate interaction between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters operating at several levels of the neuraxis controls how pain is processed (Fig. 2). Aberrant nociceptive signaling is the result of a profound disruption in the neurochemical balance that occurs in PD. In addition to the traditional dopaminergic deficiencies, dysregulated pain modulation is also largely caused by changes in the glutamatergic, serotonergic, noradrenergic, and opioid systems [1]. The clinical heterogeneity of pain phenotypes in PD is exacerbated by these neurochemical abnormalities, which impact not only synaptic transmission in canonical pain pathways but also plasticity mechanisms, receptor density, and descending inhibitory tone [42]. With an emphasis on mechanistic accuracy and integrative neurochemical understanding, this section outlines the pathophysiological contributions of the main neurotransmitter systems implicated in pain related to PD.

Dopamine

Both direct and indirect mechanisms are used by dopaminergic neurotransmission to modulate pain. The regulatory balance of basal ganglia-thalamocortical circuits is altered by nigrostriatal degeneration in PD, impacting the motivational-affective and sensory-discriminative aspects of pain [44] According to functional neuroimaging research, levodopa repletion (ON) is linked to a partial normalization of activation patterns, whereas dopaminergic withdrawal states (OFF) cause hypoactivation in pain-processing areas like the insula and anterior cingulate cortex in PD patients [45]. Dopaminergic modulation of nociceptive thresholds, specifically through D2 receptor signaling within the striatum, which affects both spinal and supraspinal nociceptive relay, is supported by experimental evidence from animal models [43]. Furthermore, dopaminergic depletion may enhance central responsiveness to noxious input by disrupting tonic inhibitory modulation within basal ganglia output pathways, as supported by experimental evidence showing altered nociceptive processing in downstream structures such as the parabrachial nucleus [2].

Notably, the interpretation of isolated dopaminergic effects is complicated by the reciprocal interactions between dopamine and other neurotransmitter systems. Second-order neurotransmission is implicated in dopaminergic pain modulation because dopamine modulates glutamatergic output from the subthalamic nucleus and cortico-striatal terminals [46]. Additionally, there is an inverse relationship between the subjective experience of pain and the density of D2/D3 receptors in the striatum, suggesting a receptor-level basis for individual differences in pain perception [47]. Recent evidence from trigeminovascular models indicates that dopaminergic activation, particularly via D2 receptors, may exert pronociceptive effects by enhancing nociceptive signaling and CGRP release, highlighting a context-dependent role of dopamine pain modulation [48].

Glutamate

Glutamatergic excitotoxicity is a salient feature of PD pathophysiology and a critical contributor to pain amplification [49]. At nociceptive synapses in the cortex, thalamus, and dorsal horn, glutamate is the main excitatory neurotransmitter [50]. Loss of dopaminergic inhibition in PD leads to disinhibition of the subthalamic nucleus and excessive glutamatergic output nuclei, contributing to N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor-mediated excitotoxicity- particularly in the globus pallidus interna and substantia nigra reticulata [51]. This fosters a hyperexcitable state conducive to central sensitization and persistent pain.

Preclinical studies have demonstrated upregulation of NR2B subunit-containing NMDA receptors in the basal ganglia of rodent models of PD, with corresponding increases in glutamate concentrations measured via microdialysis [52, 53]. While NMDA receptor activity contributes to aberrant firing in Parkinsonian basal ganglia circuit, the specific role of N2RB in modulating such activity remains to be clarified [54]. Since metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluRs) mediate synaptic plasticity in pain circuits and may contribute to the long-term potentiation of nociceptive pathways in PD, their role is also being studied [55].

Serotonin (5-HT)

The serotonergic system, arising primarily from the raphe nuclei, orchestrates descending inhibition of spinal nociceptive neurons via 5-HT1A, 5-HT2A, and 5-HT3 receptor subtypes [56]. Degeneration of serotonergic projections in PD affects descending antinociceptive control, especially in the spinal cord’s dorsolateral funiculus. Studies using Positron Emission Tomography (PET) have shown that chronic pain in PD patients is associated with decreased serotonin transporter (SERT) binding, which is linked to elevated pain scores and changed mood regulation [57].

Serotonergic tone is implicated in basal pain thresholds because experimental lesioning of the dorsal raphe in animal models causes mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia [58]. The complexity of serotonergic dysfunction in PD is highlighted by 5-HT’s dual function in regulating both inhibitory and facilitatory pain pathways. When comorbid depression is present, pharmacological interventions utilizing SSRIs and SNRIs have shown modest analgesic effects, indicating that 5-HT-mediated circuits are possibly involved in the affective appraisal of pain [59]. In addition, serotonin exhibits region-specific and bidirectional effects on nociception, as activation of 5-HT3 receptors has been shown to facilitate nociceptive transmission in trigeminal afferents, in contrast to its inhibitory role within descending pain pathways [60].

Noradrenaline (Norepinephrine)

It is known that the locus coeruleus sends noradrenergic projections to the dorsal horn, where they use α2-adrenergic receptors to control nociceptive transmission [61]. A significant decrease in noradrenaline availability results from early locus coeruleus degeneration in PD, impairing inhibitory control while promoting spinal sensitization [62].

In animal models of PD, systemic administration of α2 adrenergic receptor agonists, such as clonidine, reverses behavioral hyperalgesia-likely by restoring descending noradrenergic inhibition [63].

Endogenous opioid system

Multiple receptor subtypes (µ, δ, and κ) found in the spinal cord, PAG, thalamus, basal ganglia, and limbic structures are used by the endogenous opioid system to regulate pain [64]. Receptor density, ligand availability, and receptor coupling efficiency have all been shown to change in PD [65]. In PD patients’ putamen and insula, autoradiographic studies have shown decreased µ-opioid receptor binding. This could be due to either compensatory receptor downregulation or neurodegenerative loss of receptor-expressing neurons [66].

The relevance of opioid mechanisms in Parkinson’s-related pain is partially further highlighted by a recent clinical trial, which showed that naloxone altered pain responses in PD patients [67].

In conclusion, complex neurochemical abnormalities involving multiple neurotransmitter systems in different nervous system regions are the cause of PD-associated pain. These systems affect pain threshold, tolerance, and sensitivity through non-linear interactions. A thorough comprehension of these processes is necessary for the logical creation of multimodal medications that address the affective and neurophysiological aspects of pain in PD.

Central and peripheral mechanisms

Central mechanisms

Central sensitization

In central nociceptive pathways, heightened neuronal excitability is referred to as central sensitization. Increased synaptic potentiation and spontaneous activity in the spinal dorsal horn and ascending pain pathways mediate this in PD [68]. Loss of descending monoaminergic inhibition causes wide dynamic range neurons in laminae I and V to become hyperactive, which promotes allodynia and hyperalgesia, according to preclinical models [69]. Patients with PD who undergo quantitative sensory testing (QST) consistently exhibit lower mechanical and thermal thresholds, particularly when they are not taking medication, which is indicative of central disinhibition [70].

Further evidence that central sensitization goes beyond the spinal level comes from functional imaging studies that show aberrant activation patterns in the thalamus, insula, and ACC in response to painful stimuli [71]. EEG and MEG data also indicate thalamocortical dysrhythmia, characterized by elevated theta and beta oscillations, which may underlie spontaneous and evoked pain in PD [72, 73].

Importantly, dopaminergic modulation of pain circuitry is insufficient to restore normal central gating, especially in the presence of structural changes [71]. Disruption of the descending pain modulatory system—particularly the PAG-RVM-spinal axis—contributes to a failure of endogenous analgesia [74]. Reduced noradrenaline and serotonin availability due to neurodegeneration in the locus coeruleus and raphe nuclei affects inhibition at the spinal and supraspinal levels [75].

Cortical dysregulation

Cortical pain processing in PD is characterized by changes in integration within the default mode and salience networks [76]. Resting-state fMRI studies show disrupted connectivity between the medial prefrontal cortex, ACC, and posterior cingulate cortex, reflecting a reduced capacity for contextual modulation of pain [77]. Despite minimal nociceptive input, this could account for the high affective burden of pain observed in PD [78]. Independent of the degree of motor pain, persistent pain has also been associated with grey matter atrophy in the ACC and insula [79]. These results lend credence to a theory that suggests chronic pain in PD is a result of maladaptive plasticity within cortical hubs [76].

Peripheral mechanisms

Peripheral neuropathy and small fiber involvement

Peripheral mechanisms play a significant role, particularly in neuropathic pain phenotypes. Peripheral nociceptive input in Parkinson’s disease is not limited to somatic structures but may also include visceral afferents, contributing to integrated nociceptive processing and potentially to diffuse or autonomic-like pain phenotypes [22, 67].

Even in those who have never taken drugs, skin biopsy studies have repeatedly shown decreased intraepidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD) in PD. This SFN affects unmyelinated C-fibers and thinly myelinated Aδ-fibers, impairing nociceptive signaling and thermal sensation [80, 81]. Histopathological data indicates that peripheral nerves, particularly autonomic and sensory fibers, are affected by α-synuclein pathology [82]. Furthermore, axonal transport deficiencies and mitochondrial dysfunction are factors in peripheral nerve degeneration [83]. The clinical manifestations, which are frequently mistaken for motor symptoms or musculoskeletal disorders, include burning, tingling, and dysesthetic pain.

Inflammation and peripheral sensitization

Through the release of cytokines and the alteration of ion channel expression, persistent inflammation in peripheral tissues can make nociceptors more sensitive [84, 85]. Mechanical hyperalgesia is often the result of localized inflammatory changes that occur in PD patients with axial rigidity or dystonia. Systemic inflammation is further linked to peripheral sensitization by elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [18].

Additionally, the activation of peripheral glia, especially satellite glia and Schwann cells, increases inflammatory signaling and adds to a feedback loop of nociceptive enhancement [86]. Increased spontaneous firing and resting pain are caused by peripheral terminals expressing more voltage-gated sodium channels (Nav1.7, Nav1.8), which lowers the threshold for action potential generation [87].

Taken together, central sensitization and peripheral neuropathy form an integrated pathophysiological framework for PD-related pain. Each phenotype has a different degree of dominance in these mechanisms, which highlights the necessity of stratified diagnostic methods and customized treatment plans.

Pain perception and sensory abnormalities in Parkinson’s disease

Changes in pain threshold and tolerance

Significant changes in pain processing are seen in patients with PD, which are most frequently characterized by a lower pain threshold and tolerance when compared to healthy controls [88, 89]. These abnormalities are evident across thermal, electrical, and pressure modalities and are independent of motor symptom laterality [90, 91]. A moderate decrease in pain threshold during the unmedicated OFF state was confirmed by a meta-analysis of experimental studies; dopaminergic treatment only partially restores this threshold [88].

Disease progression contributes further to nociceptive dysfunction, with lower thresholds correlating with Hoehn and Yahr stage, motor severity, and depressive symptoms [89]. However, findings are not entirely consistent across all paradigms. According to some experimental conditions, such as stereotactic frame fixation, PD patients report less pain than non-parkinsonian neurosurgical patients, which may indicate hypoalgesia in some situations [92]. Studies on pressure pain threshold have also yielded unfavorable findings, suggesting that sensory dysfunction in PD is varied and may rely on the clinical context as well as the stimulus modality [93].

Experimental evidence: quantitative sensory testing and neuroimaging

Widespread nociceptive abnormalities in PD are revealed by QST, including decreased thresholds to mechanical and thermal stimuli and impaired tolerance across multiple body sites [90, 91]. Early-stage and pain-free patients also exhibit these abnormalities, suggesting that disrupted nociceptive processing may occur before clinical pain manifests [90].

The anterior cingulate and insular cortices produce a decrease in N2/P2 amplitudes in neurophysiological studies employing laser-evoked potentials. This is accompanied by increased subjective pain ratings, which is consistent with hyperalgesia [90].

This dissociation points to an imbalance between medial and lateral pain pathways and provides a mechanistic explanation for central pain in PD. Deficits in the nociceptive flexion reflex (RII) lend credence to the existence of dopamine-regulated spinal hyperexcitability [91, 93].

Functional imaging studies reinforce these findings. According to positron emission tomography, levodopa raises thermal pain thresholds while striatal and prefrontal activity return to normal [88]. Functional MRI shows aberrant connectivity and activation in the thalamus, insula, anterior cingulate cortex, and basal ganglia, which are important nodes in the pain network. Lateralized effects have also been noted; in left-predominant PD, paradoxical hyperalgesia after dopaminergic medication suggests hemisphere asymmetry in dopaminergic modulation of nociception [91].

Mechanistic evidence also implicates neuroinflammatory pathways. Experimental models indicate that pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling upregulation in the periaqueductal gray disrupts GABAergic inhibitory control of descending pathways, thereby increasing pain sensitivity [94].

Differences between PD patients and controls

In comparison to age-matched controls, PD patients consistently exhibit lower pain thresholds in a variety of sensory modalities, with pressure, electrical, and cold stimuli having the strongest effects [88]. Findings for heat stimuli are more variable, with some studies reporting no significant difference [93]. The fact that PD impacts both the affective-motivational and sensory-discriminative aspects of pain is demonstrated by the documented reduction in tolerance to suprathreshold stimuli [89]. These abnormalities are not explained by comorbid pain conditions, as they are also present in patients without chronic pain [90, 91].

Sex-related differences have been reported, with women exhibiting lower thresholds than men across both patient and control groups [89, 95]. The perception of pain is further influenced by psychological comorbidities, with anxiety and depression linked to a reduction in tolerance [89].

Overall, the available evidence supports the concept of intrinsic hypersensitivity to nociceptive input in PD. This dysfunction emerges early, is only partially corrected by dopaminergic treatment, and is shaped by neuroinflammatory mechanisms, mood disturbances, and sex-related factors. As a result, PD patients exhibit a unique pain phenotype that sets them apart from healthy controls and leads to the high rate of chronic pain in this demographic.

Clinical assessment and diagnostic challenges

Tools and scales for pain assessment in PD

Assessing pain in PD is complicated by varied pain phenotypes and subjective experiences. Traditional generic pain scales such as the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and the Brief Pain Inventory are frequently used to quantify overall pain intensity, but they do not account for PD-specific pain types or their underlying mechanisms [18]. Recognizing this gap, the King’s Parkinson’s Disease Pain Scale (KPPS) was developed as the first PD-specific pain assessment tool, enabling evaluation across a range of pain syndromes that are particularly prevalent or unique to PD [95]. The KPPS assesses different pain categories—including musculoskeletal, chronic, fluctuation-related, nocturnal, and radicular/neuropathic pain—by using both intensity and frequency ratings to provide a comprehensive severity score [18, 95].

Other multidimensional PD assessment tools, such as the Nonmotor Symptoms Scale (NMSS) and the Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire (PDQ-39), include pain as a subdomain, reflecting the broader impact of non-motor symptoms on patient quality of life [96, 97]. In addition to PD-specific instruments, several generic and neuropathic pain assessment tools such as the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) short form and the Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4), have also been utilized in PD populations, particularly for the evaluation of pain severity, sensory characteristics, and neuropathic features [98].

However, because they address pain as only one item within a larger scale, these instruments may lack sensitivity for detailed pain phenotyping or tracking pain-related therapeutic responses. Despite these instruments, no universal gold standard exists; thus, clinicians are encouraged to integrate standardized scales with thorough history-taking and physical examination [18]. Recent developments have further emphasized the need for structured, mechanism-based pain assessment in PD. The Parkinson’s Disease Pain Classification System (PD-PCS) has been proposed as a clinically applicable framework to improve pain phenotyping and guide management [4]. In parallel, the ICD-11 chronic pain classification has introduced refined diagnostic criteria for chronic secondary musculoskeletal pain in Parkinson’s disease, emphasizing its association with motor fluctuations, dopaminergic responsiveness, and the need to distinguish Parkinson’s disease-related from non–Parkinson’s disease-related pain [12]. A recent expert consensus highlights the importance of integrating clinical characterization with underlying mechanisms to optimize individualized pain management strategies [99].

Overlapping symptoms with other PD-related conditions

The assessment of pain in PD is complicated by fluctuations in motor symptoms, which can alter both the perception and reporting of pain. It is therefore critical that pain characterization in PD considers not just the presence of pain but also the patient’s current motor status, ongoing therapies such as levodopa or deep brain stimulation (DBS), and associated complications like dyskinesias or OFF-periods [100]. Fluctuations arising from these factors can introduce significant bias in pain reporting and interpretation.

Furthermore, distinguishing pain that is specifically related to PD from that due to unrelated or pre-existing conditions is essential. Evaluating the temporal relationship of pain with PD onset, as well as correlating pain episodes with motor and non-motor fluctuations, helps to establish whether a patient’s pain is a direct manifestation of PD [101].

Certain pain syndromes, such as neuropathic and myofascial pain, can typically be identified using screening tools or basic clinical examination. For example, a recent investigation found that 69% of PD patients experienced pain that intensified during motor OFF periods, while myofascial pain syndrome was present in up to 79% of cases [100]. Myofascial pain is notable for referred pain and the occurrence of secondary hyperalgesia that may be located remotely from the originating muscle, presenting additional diagnostic challenges [102]. Finally, Primary headache disorders, particularly migraine, have been increasingly studied in relation to PD, with some evidence suggesting a higher risk of PD in individuals with prior migraine, although findings remain inconsistent. Overlapping mechanisms-including dopaminergic and serotonergic dysfunction and impaired descending pain inhibition-support shared alterations in central nociceptive processing [103].

Influence of non-motor symptoms on pain perception

Non-motor features such as depression and anxiety play a crucial role in the perception and reporting of pain in PD [1]. Numerous studies highlight the bidirectional relationship between mood disturbances and pain: depression and anxiety can lower pain thresholds, increasing its subjective severity, while chronic pain can exacerbate mood symptoms [1, 37]. Comprehensive assessment should screen for psychiatric comorbidities and consider their treatment as part of overall pain management.

Treatment strategies

The clinical evaluation and management of PD-related pain are equally complicated. To effectively address pain in PD, close cooperation between pain physicians and neurologists who specialize in movement disorders is needed. The presence of non-motor symptoms like anxiety and depression, the stage of the disease, and medication status all affect how pain is perceived in PD. A multimodal approach to treatment is necessary because some types of pain are refractory to dopaminergic therapies. Here we summarize the up-to-date pharmacological, non-pharmacological and complementary therapies for pain in PD. Accurate pain classification between related and unrelated to PD pain, as well as mechanism-based phenotyping for PD-related pain, including nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic components is recommended to facilitate more individualized treatment strategies in PD [10]. Pharmacological approaches

The central pillar of pain management in PD remains the effective use and adjustment of dopaminergic therapies. Levodopa and dopamine agonists are frequently utilized not only to control motor symptoms but also to address musculoskeletal, dystonic, and fluctuation-associated pain syndromes [3]. Improving dopaminergic tone can reduce the pain experienced during “OFF” periods, as well as minimize discomfort arising from rigidity and abnormal postures [101, 104]. However, the response to such therapies may be less consistent in patients suffering from central pain mechanisms, highlighting the need for comprehensive pain phenotyping [105].

For cases where dopaminergic medications alone are insufficient, a variety of non-dopaminergic pharmacological agents may be considered [106]. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and acetaminophen are typically reserved for musculoskeletal or nociceptive pain, but evidence specifically supporting their use in PD is limited [23]. For patients with severe or refractory pain, opioids, such as sustained-release oxycodone-naloxone, can be prescribed with caution; some clinical trials demonstrate improvements in pain intensity and function with these agents [3]. However, it has been reported that high opioid doses in PD patients can increase akinesia [107].

Neuropathic pain syndromes in PD may be treated with gabapentinoids, such as gabapentin and pregabalin, which target abnormal nerve signaling and have shown efficacy in reducing burning, tingling, or shooting sensations [19]. Additionally, antidepressants including tricyclics, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) can be considered, particularly when chronic pain coexists with affective symptoms [23]. These agents exert analgesic effects through modulation of central pain pathways [108].

Botulinum toxin injections are also a recognized option for managing focal pain syndromes in PD, particularly when dystonia or spasticity are present, as evidenced by current literature [109, 110]. Last, cannabinoids have attracted interest for chronic pain and non-motor features in PD, with some studies supporting that medical cannabis might have a place in the therapeutic armamentarium of PD [111, 112].

Non-pharmacological approaches

Non-pharmacological treatments play a crucial complementary role in managing pain in PD, targeting aspects of pain not fully addressed by medication. DBS of the subthalamic nucleus is one of the most effective and studied options, primarily known for its impact on motor symptoms but with increasing recognition of its positive effects on certain pain types, particularly dystonia- or fluctuation-related pain [100, 113–117]. DBS often allows for the reduction of dopaminergic medication, thereby also mitigating pain associated with dyskinesia or medication side effects. These effects are long-lasting, as pain relief has been observed to extend up to eight years [118, 119]. The procedure’s impact on pain likely results from reducing pathologically elevated muscle tone, modifying pain threshold and tolerance, and enhancing motor performance [120]. However, although patients with dystonic and musculoskeletal pain respond well to DBS, the benefit for neuropathic and “pure” central pain remains less certain and may vary between individuals [100]. While zona incerta-specific DBS has been established as a treatment for motor symptoms in PD, recent data indicate that it may also provide pain relief, making it a promising target for addressing non-motor symptoms, as well [121, 122].

In addition, there is growing exploration of advanced neuromodulation techniques, such as spinal cord stimulation (SCS) and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), for chronic pain in PD [106]. SCS has demonstrated significant improvements in pain and certain motor outcomes in patients with PD—including those who have previously undergone DBS—suggesting its potential utility as both a primary bioelectric therapy and as salvage therapy [123]. Early research indicates potential benefits for certain refractory pain types; nonetheless, evidence is preliminary, and further clinical trials are required to support their widespread implementation [120, 124].

Psychological strategies, in particular cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), are also gaining traction for their role in the management of non-motor symptoms in PD [124]. CBT and related approaches help individuals reframe maladaptive thoughts about pain, reduce catastrophizing, and develop more effective coping skills [125, 126]. As pain perception in PD is often influenced by mood and emotion, addressing the psychological dimension is vital for optimizing treatment outcomes.

Complementary and alternative therapies

A number of patients with PD seek additional symptom relief through alternative or complementary therapies. Among these, individualized physical therapy and aerobic exercise stand out as key interventions [127–129]. Physical therapy regimens tailored to the patient’s needs—including stretching, strengthening, resistance exercise, and aerobic activity—have demonstrated benefits in alleviating musculoskeletal pain, enhancing flexibility, and improving postural alignment [128]. These interventions can also address gait abnormalities and functional limitations, which indirectly reduce pain through improved mobility and independence [127, 130].

Acupuncture and massage therapy (MT) are also commonly utilized methods for pain relief in PD and have been reported in small studies and case series to offer reductions in pain severity and improvements in relaxation and well-being [131, 132]. Fortunately, the majority of complementary therapies are safe and well-tolerated, and the present data, yet limited, suggests that these therapies should be included as part of a multimodal approach to pain therapy [106]. Other complementary therapies include hydrotherapy and aquatic physical therapy, tai chi, and music therapy, all of which have shown promising potential for pain relief and improvement of overall well-being in individuals with PD [133, 134]. However, the current evidence base is limited, and data from large, high-quality randomized trials are still lacking to fully support the incorporation of these techniques into clinical practice for PD-related pain.

Finally, dietary modifications and supplementation with antioxidants and vitamins have demonstrated immunomodulatory and neuroprotective effects in PD and other neurodegenerative disorders [85].

Future directions

Biomarkers for pain in Parkinson’s disease

While numerous imaging and fluid biomarkers have been developed for the prediction, prognosis, and diagnosis of PD we still lack pain-specific biomarkers [135–138]. Recent advances in plasma proteomics have demonstrated that a targeted blood panel can predict PD up to seven years before symptom onset, underscoring the growing potential of biological signatures to identify at-risk individuals and guide early intervention strategies [139]. However, these biomarkers are not yet linked to pain phenotypes.

Pain in PD is often a subjective experience, making it particularly challenging to quantify and assess objectively. The development of pain-specific biomarkers for PD patients is therefore a complex endeavor. Nevertheless, the broader field of acute and chronic pain research has evolved rapidly, offering a diverse array of potential biomarkers—from genetic and inflammatory markers to neurotransmitter profiles and biological signatures reflective of responses to analgesic treatment [140]. Understanding the biological underpinnings of pain syndromes might hold the key for individualization and increased efficacy in pain management [141, 142].

Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that certain biological alterations may serve as candidate biomarkers for distinct PD-related pain subtypes. Reduced intraepidermal nerve fiber density, reflecting small-fiber neuropathy, has been consistently demonstrated in PD and is associated with neuropathic pain features such as burning and dysesthesia, even in early or drug-naïve patients [81]. In parallel, the detection of α-synuclein pathology in cutaneous sensory fibers supports the concept of direct peripheral nervous system involvement and may represent a disease-specific marker of neuropathic pain in PD [82]. At the central level, molecular imaging studies provide further candidate biomarkers. Positron emission tomography has revealed reduced serotonin transporter binding in patients with PD and chronic pain, suggesting impaired descending inhibitory control and a potential biomarker for affective and hyperalgesic pain components [56]. Similarly, decreased µ-opioid receptor binding in limbic and basal ganglia regions points toward dysfunction of endogenous analgesic systems and may underlie central Parkinson’s pain phenotypes [65]. Finally, immune-related pathways may intersect with key pathogenic mechanisms in Parkinson’s disease, with potential prognostic value and therapeutic tractability, particularly through microglial and macrophage activation, although their clinical relevance remains to be established [143, 144]. Future research and unresolved questions

There is a pressing need for improved therapeutic options and clinical trial designs addressing pain in PD. Future studies should prioritize pain as a primary endpoint, utilizing refined classification systems and standardized assessment tools to better capture the variability and severity of pain phenotypes in PD. Research into novel treatment modalities—such as targeting neuroinflammatory pathways, non-dopaminergic mechanisms, cannabinoid signaling, and neuromodulation—holds promise for expanding the scope of individualized pain management in the context of the disease.

Last, integrating patient perspectives into research and care models, and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration, will be vital for developing interventions that are both effective and relevant to the lived experience of PD. Multicenter, adequately powered trials and translational studies remain essential for establishing evidence-based guidelines and ultimately improving quality of life for people living with the disease.

Conclusion

Pain remains a prevalent and disabling non-motor symptom in PD, intricately linked to both central and peripheral pathophysiological changes. Although recent advances have deepened our understanding of pain mechanisms and highlighted the heterogeneity of pain phenotypes in PD, management continues to be suboptimal for many patients. To effectively address this challenge, a shift toward individualized, mechanism-based assessment and treatment is essential. Future progress depends on integrating emerging scientific insights with clinical innovation, ultimately aiming to reduce the burden of pain and enhance quality of life for people living with Parkinson’s disease.
