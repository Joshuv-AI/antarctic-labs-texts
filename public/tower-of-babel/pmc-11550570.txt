Invasive mucinous urachal adenocarcinoma: A case report of surgical management and insights from the literature

Introduction

The urachus represents an embryological structure serving as a remanent duct between the umbilicus and bladder anterior dom. The initial development of the bladder takes place at the level of the umbilicus. During the fourth to fifth month of gestation, it descends and attains its position within the pelvis. After that, the urachus undergoes a process called obliteration, where it becomes a fibrous cord known as the median umbilical ligament [1,2].

Urachal adenocarcinoma, constituting less than 1 % of all bladder carcinomas and between 10 % and 35 % of bladder adenocarcinoma cases, is categorized as an aggressive type of bladder malignancy [3]. This cancer is frequently detected in advanced stages with a median age of diagnosis in the patient's sixth decade of life. It contributes to a slightly higher incidence in males with a 1.4–1.6:1 male-to-female ratio with an overall poor prognosis [3,4].

In 1930, Begg initially described this cancer type as the result of malignant conversion of the enteric epithelium within the urachus [5]. UrC mostly manifests as adenocarcinoma in 90 % of cases [6]. No clear risk factors for a malignant transformation of the remanent of urachus have been identified [7].

With the aim of increasing the medical knowledge about UrC entity, owing to its limited literature and their patients are mainly from the male population, here we present a case of a 58-year-old female patient with an invasive mucinous urachal adenocarcinoma that primarily presented with painless hematuria and was treated surgically.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old female patient sought medical attention due to persistent, painless, gross hematuria lasting for two weeks. The patient also reported urinary frequency, urgency, and dysuria, with no history of constitutional symptoms. She has a medical history of diabetes mellitus (DM) and a strong family history of cancer in first-degree relatives (breast cancer, gastric cancer, and bone cancer). Physical examination did not reveal significant findings.

An abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan with intravenous contrast demonstrated an exophytic complex cystic lesion, measuring 8 × 6.5 × 4.8 cm, with solid enhancing soft tissue elements arising from the anterior wall of the urinary bladder dome and extended to the bladder neck. This lesion exhibited intra and extra-vesical components and contained coarse calcifications (Fig. 1).

Cystoscopy revealed multiple smooth-surfaced, solid gray papillary masses involving the dome and anterior wall of the bladder, extending to the neck (Fig. 3). A transurethral resection showed bladder-invasive adenocarcinoma mucinous type with deep muscularis propria invasion (Jewett Marshall B2).

The planned surgical management was radical cystectomy with incontinent ileal conduit, along with hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, anterior vaginal wall excision, and appendectomy. Intraoperatively, there were no adhesions or other organ involvement. Post-surgical pathology confirmed urachal adenocarcinoma mucinous type, with no lymph node metastasis. The neoplastic cells were positive for CK7, CK20, HMWCK, and CDX2, but negative for B-catenin, GATA3, and PAX8.

The patient was advised to undergo regular clinical assessments every week for the first month, then every 2 weeks for the next two months, followed by an annual CT scan as part of the follow-up protocol. No adjuvant chemotherapy was administered, and the patient experienced no complications or recurrence during follow-up.

Discussion

UrC is associated with a poor prognosis primarily attributed to delayed symptom presentation, a heightened risk of metastasis, and its predominant anatomical localization in the dome of the bladder or anterior wall. Glandular cancers of the urachus constitute a notable portion, comprising 20–40 % of bladder adenocarcinomas [8]. Urachal glandular cancers further delineate into cystic and noncystic tumors, with the latter representing the majority at 83 % of urachal adenocarcinomas. Among the non-cystic subtypes, the mucinous variant is the most prevalent (50 %), followed by enteric (24 %), mixed (10 %), not-otherwise-specified (9 %), and signet ring cell types (7 %) [8,9]. There is an overlap in immunophenotyping on many markers among these different subtypes such as expression of CDX2, CK7, CK20, claudin-18, and Reg IV and negativity for p63 and b-catenin. In our case, a urinary bladder mass biopsy revealed invasive adenocarcinoma mucinous type with muscularis propria invasion.

In its early stages, urachal adenocarcinoma is often asymptomatic. Symptoms arise only when a substantial tumor burden has developed, leading to erosion through the bladder wall [10]. UrC primarily presents with hematuria, with subsequent occurrences of suprapubic mass, dysuria, frequency, and lower abdominal pain [11].

The diagnosis is typically established when cystoscopy and transurethral resection of the tumor confirm the presence of adenocarcinoma in the midline bladder, taking a biopsy commonly leads to the diagnosis [10]. Eighty-eight percent of lesions are visually detectable through endoscopy, presenting as protrusions, flat epitheliomas, papillary or polypoid masses, or exhibiting gelatinous fluid or bloody discharge from the urachal orifice. Radiographic imaging, such as CT or MRI, offers crucial supportive information, especially when the mass has stippled calcification. It aids in assessing the disease extent, determining metastatic status, and evaluating the potential for surgical resection [4,10].

In the treatment of urachal adenocarcinoma, partial cystectomy is a viable alternative to radical cystoprostatectomy when the tumor can be resected with adequate margins. The procedure should involve an en bloc resection of the bladder dome, urachal ligament, and umbilicus, preferably using a laparoscopic or robot-assisted technique due to the high implantability of these tumors [16].

A retrospective analysis of 66 cases at the Mayo Clinic from 1951 to 2004 demonstrated varied treatment efficacy. Partial cystectomy was performed in 70 % of cases, complete cystectomy in 21 %, and radiotherapy in 9 %. The overall five-year survival rate was 49 %. Multivariate analysis indicated that a low tumor grade and negative surgical margins were significantly associated with improved survival. In contrast, higher pathologic stage and surgical margin involvement correlated with a poorer prognosis, underscoring the importance of achieving negative margins [16].

While radical cystectomy has been conventional, achieving a cure in 70 % of patients is possible through partial cystectomy with en-bloc urachectomy up to the umbilicus [12]. Our patient presents with muscle-invasive bladder cancer that involves the bladder neck, necessitating the recommended treatment of radical cystectomy for localized, muscle-invasive bladder carcinoma in both sexes [13]. For women, this comprehensive procedure involves cystectomy, bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy, hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and resection of the upper third of the anterior vaginal wall. Commonly referred to as anterior pelvic exenteration, this surgical approach aims to minimize the risk of pelvic recurrence [14]. There is no study showing the survival role of neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy and there is no standard protocol for this treatment, but it can be used in surgically unrespectable patients and patients with recurrent or metastatic disease [8,12].

It was estimated that the 5-year survival rate was almost 45 %. The most important factors influencing the prognosis of UrC are surgical margin status, lymph node, or other metastases at the time of surgery, and proper surgical intervention [12]. Overall survival is strongly impacted by the ability to establish negative margins [10]. During surgery, the discovery of further occult lymph node metastases has been linked to a poor outcome. Since the umbilicus may be implicated in approximately 7 % of patients, removal of the umbilicus together with the bladder and urachal ligament is crucial to managing the tumor [10].

Conclusion

Urachal adenocarcinoma is a rare and aggressive malignancy associated with a poor prognosis due to its propensity for late-stage presentation. It can be primarily diagnosed by cystoscopy. The treatment of urachal adenocarcinoma depends on tumor invasion. The comprehensive surgical approach employed in this case, involving anterior pelvic exenteration, and ileal conduit creation, represents a thorough and effective urachal adenocarcinoma treatment strategy.
