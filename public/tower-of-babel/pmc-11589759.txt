Fostering the waste management with environmental-resource interacting attribute
Authors: Wenbo Huang, Xianlai Zeng, Jinhui Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective waste management is essential for achieving sustainability, yet challenges persist in resource recovery and mitigating environmental impacts. The environmental-resource interacting attribute framework quantifies these difficulties in waste processes, revealing attribute bias and guiding treatment pathway selection. Here we analyze twelve waste categories and reveal significant variability in recyclability and environmental impact. For instance, copper slag demonstrates a range of 25.25-285.46 bit−1 (the unit is the inverse of information entropy), indicating greater recyclability, while downcycling pathways exhibit the highest values (up to 285.46 bit−1), emphasizing resource recovery (up to 77.45° while degree indicates bias). It proposes a classification of waste based on environmental-resource interacting attribute values, prioritizing materials with high recovery potential to prevent irreversible losses. The framework offers insights into waste recyclability and environmental burdens, serving as an alternative tool for advancing waste management towards a circular economy and reducing carbon emissions.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

The 21st century faces the dual challenge of resource depletion and waste explosion, which pose tremendous hurdles to sustainability. As we transition to a low-carbon economy, both opportunities and dilemmas emerge for natural resources and supply chains1. In China alone, 3 to 4 billion tons of industrial waste are produced annually, highlighting the scale of this issue2,3. Technological advancements allow us to view waste as a recyclable or secondary resource, mitigating reliance on primary minerals and easing the pressure on critical metal supplies4. However, these advancements also introduce new management complexities, such as the need for detailed classification and the identification of appropriate recycling pathways for diverse waste types. Compounding these challenges are fluctuations in waste properties, driven by shifts in primary resource mining technology and declining ore grades5. Moreover, controlling secondary pollution during the recovery process remains a critical concern, directly impacting the economic and managerial aspects of resource development from waste. To effectively address these challenges, it is essential to adopt a quantified methodology that evaluates both the environmental and resource attributes of waste and their intricate interactions. By applying this innovative method across diverse waste management pathways, we aim not only to identify the difficulties inherent in waste processing but also to propose strategic solutions that enhance resource recovery and mitigate environmental impact.

Waste contains both valuable and toxic elements6, each of which represents different resource and environmental attributes. These interact in waste management and processing: improving recycling and reusing optimizes resource attributes, reduces primary resource consumption, and mitigates environmental burden7. This decreases waste volume, preventing pollution and reducing the threats to health and the environment. Thus, waste resource attribute optimization helps control environmental attributes, promoting dual protection of resources and the environment. Despite standards from the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of China to manage pollution and promote recycling of solid waste, evaluation indicators pose challenges. Current resource indicators, which emphasize the proportions of recyclable components and recycling efficiency, and environmental evaluations, which focus on waste toxicity and environmental pollution, provide limited guidance to waste management companies8,9. These indicators fall short in helping companies understand the complexities of waste treatment and in selecting the most appropriate treatment pathways. While composition analysis offers detailed insights into the elemental content and phases of waste, it remains a singular evaluation tool. Similarly, life cycle assessments, estimating environmental impacts, rely on established treatment technologies and global averages, failing to quantify varying challenges in processing different wastes along diverse treatment pathways10.

Waste often contains abundant “minor” metals and non-metallic minerals. Their reuse, however, is hindered by their low concentrations11 and disparate structures, properties, and compositions compared to ore12,13. Furthermore, residual elements such as lead, mercury, cadmium, chromium, arsenic, and sulfur pose threats to ecosystems and human health14,15, necessitating sound management to mitigate or eliminate their environmental impacts, particularly if they cannot be economically re-mined. This increases the difficulty of waste treatment and management. Current recycling strategies encompass recovery to obtain equivalent or higher-grade recycled resources (upcycling), including the extraction of precious metals or their compounds16–18, or conversion into lower-grade recycled resources (downcycling), such as road base materials, cement, and ceramics19–21. Quantifying the difficulty of recycling waste demands comprehension of the inherent properties of solid waste and hinges on technical stipulations such as emission standards, recovery rates, and product quality22, even for individual waste. Therefore, an integrated, operational-level accounting approach is instrumental in managing data variability and enables industries to grasp the complexities of different waste-handling methods, thus fostering informed decision-making. In prior research, we introduced the concept and methodology for evaluating recyclability23,24 and removability25 to estimate the resource and environmental attributes of waste from a “quality” perspective. This approach assesses the resource and environmental attributes of solid waste concerning the difficulty of recycling and detoxification. It has been effectively employed for assessing various waste types, including e-waste24,26, industrial waste23, construction waste27, municipal waste28, and plastic waste29. As the study progressed, it became evident that correlating the environmental and resource attributes through their interactions was necessary to holistically evaluate the overarching difficulty of the industrial waste treatment process. Although prior research has defined and quantified the environmental and resource attributes of various wastes from multiple theoretical perspectives (Supplementary Table 1), there is a limited understanding and quantitative assessment of the complexity involved in resource recovery and the mitigation of environmental impacts across various waste treatment pathways. Furthermore, there is a noticeable gap in integrated, systems-based approaches to acknowledge the intricate interconnections between environmental impact reduction and resource recovery.

This study aims to devise an accounting methodology that assesses the treatment difficulty associated with a specific type of waste along diverse treatment paths. We introduce the concept of evaluating the Environmental-Resource Interacting Attribute (ERIA) of waste from the perspective of “difficulty”, which refers to the inherent challenges in resource recovery and environmental impact mitigation. This difficulty arises from factors such as the composition of waste, the presence of hazardous substances, and the complexity of recycling processes. Building on this concept, we develop an ERIA accounting model based on recoverability and removability algorithms, applying it to various waste management pathways to quantify the associated processing difficulty. The model is expressed in polar coordinates (A, θ), where A (bit−1, the inverse of the unit of information entropy) quantifies the overall difficulty, and θ (°, degrees) indicates the bias towards environmental or resource attributes. Given its diverse sources, varied types, and the wide range of industries involved, industrial waste, considered the most challenging yet valuable type of waste, has been chosen. This choice forms a robust foundation for understanding the correlations and variability among the method’s key parameters. In this work, the ERIA method was applied to 52 inorganic elements (compounds) categorized into four groups30 (Supplementary Fig. 1): heavy metals (Pb, Hg, Cd, Cr, As, Ni, Cu, Zn, Ag, V, Mn, Co, Tl, Sb)31, other metals32, hazardous non-metals (S, F, Cl)33, and low-toxicity elements (C, H, O, N, Si, P, K, Na, Ca, Mg). Radioactive elements are excluded from this study. By closely integrating the natural characteristics of waste with specific treatment pathways, this study aims to improve waste management methods. It not only identifies the potential for resource recovery and environmental impact mitigation but also proposes a comprehensive framework for evaluating waste management strategies through the lenses of sustainability and resource efficiency.

To evaluate the ERIA of typical industrial waste, four scenarios were developed, each representing diverse treatment pathways and technological frameworks. These scenarios and their system boundaries incorporate a model of simplified treatment methods, standards for resource recovery, and environmental baseline values, aiming to measure the difficulty associated with resource recovery and mitigation of environmental impacts throughout various waste treatment processes. In this work, we show that copper slag exhibits the highest and most consistent ERIA, indicating its relative ease of treatment. Moreover, downcycling pathways are identified as having the greatest ERIA, with a strong tendency towards resource attributes. In addition, the sensitivity and uncertainty analysis demonstrate that the variability in ERIA is significantly shaped by the statistical entropy (H), recovery grade (D), and removal grade (D’) of waste elements. Further details on the premises of these scenarios are elucidated in the Methods section.

Results

Assessment of ERIA of industrial waste

ERIA was calculated for each waste for four scenarios: elemental recycling, compounds recycling, downcycling, and landfill (Supplementary Fig. 2). We use gold cyanide tailing as an example to illustrate its ERIA estimation in different scenarios (details in Supplementary Tables 2, 14, and 26). A of ERIA of gold cyanide tailing across all the scenarios ranges by 2.75 to 29.56 bit−1, θ ranges from 0 to 84.63° (Supplementary Fig. 3). The case includes 12 elements with the H of 2.10 bit. In the elemental recycling and compound recycling scenarios, the ERIA was calculated for the elemental level. Elements like Pb, Cu, Zn, Ag, Au, and S are considered to be environmentally impactful because their contents surpass their respective crustal abundances. Of these, recovery is possible for Pb, Cu, Ag, Au, and S, as their contents exceed their natural mineral cut-off grade. Therefore, the accounting boundaries of environmental and resource attributes are determined, respectively. Over 50% of the elements in this gold-cyanide tailing have an environmental impact. Except for zinc, all of those elements can be recovered to mitigate their environmental impact, and their ERIA demonstrates positive interaction Consequently, when processed through the elemental (compounds) recycling pathway, the ERIA value of gold cyanide tailing is high (A is 25.24 bit−1 and 29.56 bit−1, respectively), and the bias is an environmental attribute (θ are 29.93° and 42.27°, respectively).

The downcycling of elements to low-applications is referred to as non-functional recycling. An example of this is the use of mineral components (such as Si, Ca, Fe, and Al) in slags for cement production, where ERIA is calculated at the compound level. The process of downcycling solid waste is commonly used in construction materials and is known as solidification. As a result, the ERIA of downcycling is calculated using the physical recovery or removal method. In the case of gold cyanide tailings (see Supplementary Methods 2 and Supplementary Equations 7–17), the mineral components (Si, Ca, Fe, and Al) are calculated for their recyclability, while the compounds (S, Au, Ag, Cu, Pb, and Zn) are calculated for their removability. Consequently, the ERIA of the gold cyanide tailings in the downcycling pathway is (17.85 bit−1, 84.63°).

Although landfill disposal can reduce the impact of solid waste on the surface environment, it may result in the loss of renewable value of resources. In the ERIA calculations, the landfill scenario considers only environmental attributes. To avoid secondary pollution, solid waste needs to be treated through stabilization and solidification before being disposed of in landfills. Therefore, the accounting of ERIA in the landfill scenario is assessed at the compound level using the chemical removal calculation method. In the gold cyanide tailing case, the ERIA in the landfill scenario is (2.27 bit−1, 0°).

Based upon this approach, we aggregated the data on typical waste (Source Data) and illustrated their ERIA for elemental recycling, compounds recycling, downcycling, and landfill scenarios in Fig. 1 (the details of the results are shown in Supplementary Tables 2–30). The results show that in those four scenarios, the range of A falls between 3.79–25.25 bit-1, 4.68–32.29 bit-1, 31.25–285.46 bit-1, and 0–2.90 bit-1, respectively. The range of θ, on the other hand, falls between 0–37.46°, 0–54°, 43.86–77.45°, and 0°. The results indicate that the downcycling scenario has the highest absolute value of ERIA and is biased toward resource attributes. The absolute values of ERIA in the compounds recycling and elements recycling scenarios are similar, but the former is biased toward environmental attributes, while the latter is biased toward balance. In contrast, the ERIA in the landfill scenario is the lowest and has environmental attributes only. The ERIA of different wastes varied significantly across four scenarios. Non-ferrous smelting slag and non-ferrous tailings demonstrated higher ERIA values and more resource-biased attributes in the elements recycling and compounds recycling scenarios. Conversely, in the downcycling scenario, iron tailing and coal fly ash exhibited higher ERIA values with stronger resource attribute bias. In the landfill scenario, non-ferrous smelting slag and non-ferrous tailings showed higher environmental attributes compared to other wastes. This suggests that treatment technologies can significantly reduce the environmental impacts of these wastes, although caution should be exercised to avoid potential secondary pollution.

Sensitivity and uncertainty analysis of ERIA

The accounting boundaries and parameter values of ERIA vary across different scenarios. To evaluate the impact of different parameters on ERIA, sensitivity and uncertainty analyses were performed. Furthermore, the distribution of ERIA of wastes is presented for different scenarios.

The sensitivity of n (number of detected elements), H, D, and D’ to A and θ under the elements recycling, compounds recycling, downcycling, and landfill scenarios is illustrated in Fig. 2a–d. The sensitivity trends of the parameter factors in the four scenarios are different. The scenarios of elements recycling and compounds recycling exhibit the highest sensitivity of D and D’ to A and θ. However, in the downcycling scenario, H and D’ have the highest sensitivity for A and θ. The findings suggest that the total number and quality of recoverable and removable components in wastes significantly impact ERIA.

We also conducted an uncertainty analysis on the ERIA results of wastes using Monte Carlo simulation (104), and the resulting ERIA distributions are presented in Fig. 2e, f. The ERIA of wastes in elements recycling, compounds recycling, downcycling, and landfill scenarios (presented as mean) are (13.35 bit−1, 19.44°), (18.02 bit−1, 38.13°), (66.17 bit−1, 60.16°), and (1.3 bit−1, 0°), respectively. The findings suggest that wastes have the highest ERIA value under the downcycling pathway with resource-biased attributes, making the resource recovery process easier. Conversely, the ERIA value is lower for the elemental or compound recovery pathway, but the environmental attribute is more embodied, leading to a more significant reduction of environmental impact. In contrast, landfill disposal exhibits the lowest ERIA value and is not a favorable option for either resource recovery or environmental impact reduction.

Evaluation of the robustness of the model

The presence of elements in industrial waste is highly variable, and certain elements may be present in trace amounts or complex compounds with other elements. These conditions, combined with limitations in sample handling and analytical methods, can render certain elements undetectable or difficult to measure. Therefore, it is crucial to assess how the number of detected elements (n) affects the robustness of the ERIA model. In this study, crustal conditions are selected for robustness analysis due to a wide variety of elements. Different parameters can be changed by randomly selecting several elements and removing them from the range of accounts. Through increasing the missing proportion of accounting elements, the ratio of D, D’, R, R’, and A change is illustrated in Fig. 3. The result shows that in the scenarios of elements recycling and compounds recycling, as the missing proportion of n increases from 1% to 20%, both D and D’ exhibit a decrease of 2–20%. However, the reduction in A is not more than 7%, and θ drops by no more than 2%. Conversely, in the downcycling scenario, the values of D and D’ exhibited a reduction of over 50% when the percentage of n missing was 5%. This decrease was mainly due to the randomly missing mineral elements. Nonetheless, the change of A and θ is not more than 20%. The n missing rate had a comparable impact on D’ and A in the landfill scenario, resulting in a decrease of 2–19% for both. The findings indicate that the ERIA model’s stability is impacted by the total number of detected elements, particularly in the downcycling scenario. To ensure accurate accounting results, elements in wastes must be sufficiently detected, particularly mineral elements with high content.

ERIA-based waste-pathway classification system

A waste-pathway classification system is developed using a combined system of polar coordinates and quadrants (Fig. 4). The polar coordinate system is utilized to classify various types of waste along specific pathways. The polar coordinate representing the distribution of ERIA is divided into zones based on the results of Monte Carlo simulations. The polar radius direction is used to divide the ERIA into three regions: low, medium, and high, based upon the quarter loci and three-quarters loci obtained from simulations using parameter A. These regions indicate the varying levels of difficulty, moderation, and ease in waste resource recyclability and environmental impact removal. The polar angle direction is used to divide the ERIA into three regions: environmental bias, balance, and resource bias, employing the same method with simulation results of parameter θ as a reference. These regions represent the tendency of waste to achieve resource recycling or environmental impact elimination under a specific management pathway. Two-thirds of the waste falls into the moderate level in terms of ERIA in the case of the elements recycling pathway, showing no bias. Copper slag and gold cyanide tailing take up the high ERIA region, suggesting that they are the most suitable for achieving resource recovery and environmental impact elimination using this approach. In contrast, steel slag and coal gangue are located in the low ERIA region, which is unsuitable for elements recycling pathways. Phosphogypsum is situated in a region characterized by resource bias, indicating that the elements recycling pathway facilitates resource recovery more easily than it eliminates environmental impact. Conversely, coal gangue is situated in a region characterized by environmental bias, demonstrating that elements recycling can mitigate its environmental impact but faces challenges in achieving resource recovery. By applying the same analytical approach, we discovered that copper slag falls within the high ERIA region for all pathways with a balanced bias, rendering it more suitable for upcycling compared to other waste. Coal fly ash, iron tailings, steel slag, and coal gangue are difficult to upcycle as elements or compounds, but they are suitable for downcycling. Using a polar coordinate system, we establish a waste classification method based on ERIA to determine the compatibility between different treatment pathways and waste materials.

Discussion

The role of waste management in achieving the SDGs is primarily related to environmental and resource sustainability. Our results demonstrate the ERIA system can offer a comprehensive assessment of the supply of resources and environmental challenges when waste is used as a secondary resource. This approach optimizes waste utilization and demonstrates the trade-offs between resource use and environmental impact. It also provides useful guidance to industry decision-makers for the development of recycling technology. Finally, policymakers’ decisions on policies that facilitate pollution reduction and dual carbon goals integration also receive useful insights from this approach.

When different waste is considered in four treatment pathways, our ERIA assessment shows that the downcycling pathway enables recovery of resources and elimination of environmental impact. This approach appears to be one of the more feasible treatment pathways for most waste. In the downcycling of waste, recovering high-volume mineral components such as SiO2, CaO, Fe2O3, and Al2O3 to low-value applications as non-functional recycling (e.g., backfilling materials, cement, and soil amendment), with low technical barriers, can significantly reduce industrial waste stocks34. Therefore, downcycling appears to be a more suitable waste reduction approach in the short term based on the quantity and rate of recycling. However, some metals with low content are also transferred to those mineral materials through downcycling permanently dissipated into the environment due to corrosion and wear (involuntary loss) from long-term use35. The severity of this situation has been overlooked in the past due to low levels of elements in the waste and involuntary loss pathways. In the current critical period of China’s transition to new energy sources, there is a significant risk of supply disruptions, primarily for strategic metals and other mineral resources. Waste utilization pathways should be optimized to minimize metal loss and supply chain risks, as well as environmental and climate impacts. According to the 14th Five-Year Plan of the Chinese government’s policy on the utilization of waste includes both large-scale utilizations to reduce the stock and high-value utilization to improve efficiency in utilizing resources. Upcycling, such as extracting low-content metals from secondary resources, is the preferred long-term treatment method for waste to reduce the risk of metal supply shortages.

According to our findings (Fig. 4), the range of waste favorable for upcycling is restricted, and the amount of waste generated during the recycling process is significant. These factors imply that upcycling as a single pathway of waste utilization may be inefficient, both in terms of resource production and economic benefits. Chinese authorities have issued a circular on further promoting the nationwide battle to prevent and control pollution, setting the goal for promoting meticulous management of solid waste. This inspired us to classify waste by ERIA and develop a cascade utilization scheme that ensures effective treatment of waste while minimizing irreversible resource loss. Based on the data from Supplementary Fig. 4, it is a priority to rapidly deplete the stock of iron tailing and three other types of industrial waste with production volumes greater than 50,000 Mt⋅a−1 through downcycling. For copper slag and three other types of industrial waste with high ERIA values in the upcycling process and production volumes less than 12,000 Mt⋅a−1, a stepwise recovery of valuable components is promoted by large-scale downcycling of residual waste after extracting valuable metals. Gold cyanide tailings and other types of waste generated between 10,000 and 50,000 Mt⋅a−1 can be temporarily contained by using tailings ponds or landfills and mined in the future when technology advances, costs are reduced, and risks are under control. It should be noted that solidification and stabilization will further complicate the recycling of these industrial wastes.

ERIA could guide for upgrading recycling technologies, especially upcycling technologies. According to the statistical entropy results obtained through ERIA accounting, certain carrier metals, such as Fe, Al, Cu, Zn, Mn, and Ti, along with precious metals like Au and Ag, have a considerable difference in probability density (Hi) and thus can be effectively recycled36. However, due to their low concentration and similar Hi, most heavy metals and non-metallic hazardous elements are not technically or economically recoverable and, therefore, often dissipate in other flows. The sensitivity analysis shows that D is the key parameter affecting ERIA in the upcycling pathway, which is determined by the quantity and grade of the recovered metal. Within the established range for the ERIA accounting, the number of recycled elements is less than six, indicating that the quality of the recycling element is a more significant factor affecting recyclability. However, the concentrations of metals in waste are typically much smaller than those in raw minerals and are highly dependent on them. At present, waste and other anthropogenic ores are incorporated into primary metal production. This process involves concentrating the target metals through pyrometallurgy, followed by the separation and purification of the target metal via hydrometallurgy. Despite these efforts, technical barriers to metal recycling persist due to the constraints of metallurgical thermodynamics and kinetics. The ERIA assessment’s analysis of H suggests that for the separation of various metals in different wastes, a meticulous evaluation of the trace elements distributed between phases during smelting is required to enhance the opportunities for metal recovery from waste. In addition, most metals demonstrate stability in waste, including oxides, phosphate, apatite, zirconium, and glass phases17, as evidenced by the low D obtained from the ERIA assessment. Certain studies suggest that incorporating an activation process before extraction, aimed at mitigating the binding of mineral phases to metals, can enhance the extractability of metals in waste, particularly those composed of a high proportion of complex, hard-to-dissolve substances37–39. Analyzing from the ERIA perspective, to improve the D of waste through pretreatment offers crucial insights for the metal industry to innovate and design a smelting process compatible with waste.

The State Council’s “Action Plan for Carbon Peaking by 2030” explicitly stipulates that a key facet of China’s carbon peaking strategy is to foster the advancement of a circular economy. This plan advocates for the use of waste as a replacement for raw minerals in the industrial sector, aiming to amplify the multi-purpose utilization rates of mineral resources. When taking into account both the carbon footprint and cumulative energy demand, the production phase imposes the highest environmental impact within the life cycle of the metals industry5,40,41. While recycling and reuse strategies can alleviate this environmental burden42, the ERIA assessment suggests that unless innovative breakthroughs in recycling technology are achieved, waste will persist depending on the energy-intensive and CO2-emitting construction materials manufacturing industry for processing in the near term43–45. This makes achieving circular economy and climate objectives in the metal sector extremely challenging. The findings from the ERIA analysis indicate that to prevent the irreversible dissipation of metals due to downcycling use46, the treatment of waste should progressively transition toward upcycling in the long-term development plan. However, the implementation of feasible, environmentally friendly, innovative upcycling business models or technologies necessitates time and financial investment47. The “Zero-waste City” program facilitates this transition48. We intend to apply the ERIA assessment methodology to the “Zero-waste City” construction in the industrial sector, thereby fostering the reduction of pollution and carbon emissions through a synergizing strategy. Firstly, the outcomes of the ERIA could serve as a useful reference for the categorization of waste. This may involve developing waste management strategies based on category-pathway alignment, establishing tiered recycling routes for solid waste, and prioritizing resource recovery for waste with significant resource attributes. Secondly, ERIA accounting could be employed as a guideline for advancing recycling technologies within the industrial sector. Techniques for directional separation of resource and pollution flows should be designed with consideration of parameters such as H, D, and D’ in the model. Lastly, the findings of ERIA could act as an indicator for determining financial support or subsidy standards for waste recycling projects. Prioritization might be given to upcycling projects that exhibit high resource attributes of waste, with greater subsidies allocated for the sound management of waste with lower environmental attributes, supporting the upgrade of technology and equipment. Overall, with the ERIA estimation method in this study, we hope to provide fresh perspectives to policymakers and industry practitioners.

Apart from the primary assumptions, certain methodological decisions have been made that may impose limitations. Firstly, the model applies to ERIA accounting for metallic and non-metallic inorganic elements in waste that experience valence changes during treatment for recovery or removal, but it does not apply to organic matter. The model excludes organic matter due to its complex chemical transformations, which involve more than valency changes, such as shifts in functional groups and molecular frameworks. This complexity necessitates specific adjustments in the model’s parameters to accurately reflect the transformation challenges of organic materials. Future enhancements will refine these parameters, aiming to incorporate organic matter due to its significance in resource supply and climate impact. Secondly, in the ERIA accounting framework, the accounting boundaries for resource attributes and environmental attributes rely on cut-off grade and crustal abundance, but these measures may prove excessively theoretical for practical implementation. To reduce the limitations of the method, it is necessary to incorporate toxicity, bioaccumulation, persistence, and the behavior of substances across different environmental media into the accounting of environmental attributes. In addition, the model should be expanded to include parameters related to economic, social, and political factors. Thirdly, the treatment pathway is restricted to upcycling, which includes recycling of elements and compounds, downcycling, and landfill disposal. This simplified scenario fails to encompass all technologies employed in waste treatment. Lastly, the concept of environmental attributes encompasses a broader scope than our framework. However, the assessment of environmental attributes in this study does not consider the challenges associated with mitigating the environmental impacts resulting from carbon emissions during waste management. Therefore, to enhance the model’s relevance and effectiveness, it is crucial to refine the scenarios in industrial waste management to mirror treatment technologies and the recycling of elements more accurately. In addition, there is a pressing need to thoroughly analyze the interaction between carbon emissions and environmental mitigation efforts in the context of climate change. These insights should then be integrated into the environmental accounting parameters, ensuring a comprehensive and realistic framework for evaluating environmental attributes and their impacts on sustainable waste management strategies.

Methods

Description of ERIA accounting model

The ERIA was calculated using the Eqs. (1) and (2):

where A is the absolute value of ERIA, representing the difficulty in achieving resource recovery and environmental impact mitigation through different treatment pathways; θ is the degree of ERIA bias towards environmental or resource attributes; R, R’, D, and D’ represent the recyclability, removability, recovery grade, and removal grade of waste, respectively24,25; n is the total number of elements (compounds) in a given waste, and H is its statistical entropy. Recyclability and removability reflect the recycling and removal difficulty of waste, respectively. The definitions and calculations of H, D, D’, R, and R’ are detailed in Supplementary Methods 1 and 2.

An interaction between two factors occurs when the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. This interaction can be either positive or negative, leading to synergistic or antagonistic effects49. In this study, the resource attribute refers to the difficulty of extracting usable components from solid waste through resourcefulness. On the other hand, the environmental attribute represents the challenge of removing environmentally harmful components from solid waste through sound management. When viewed from a material cycle perspective, solid waste used as an alternative resource led to new pollutant emissions in the production process. This, in turn, dilutes the pollutants and further reduces their recoverability and removability. Therefore, the interaction between the environmental and resource attributes exhibits a negative relationship, i.e., A ≤ R + R’, which can be represented by Eqs. (1) and (2).

Scenario and system boundary

To examine the influence of solid waste properties on both resource supply potential and climate impact, four waste utilization and disposal scenarios were established: elemental recycling, compounds recycling, downcycling, and landfill. We defined a system boundary for each scenario, consisting of elements (compounds) that should be considered in terms of their environmental and resource attributes. We also considered the change in the chemical valence of the compounds before and after treatment and disposal of the waste, as well as the values of the parameters in the equations, which are depicted in Fig. 5 and Table 1. The ERIA was reported in terms of ERIA (A, θ) for all scenarios.

The removability and recyclability of organic materials are influenced by intrinsic factors such as chemical bond strength and chemical structure. Therefore, the principles used to calculate their ERIA differ from those used for inorganic materials. If organic materials are degraded or recovered through chemical processes, the ERIA cannot be applied. In this study, we focus on inorganic materials, such as tailings, smelting slag, coal ash, and desulphurization gypsum, which produce more than 10 million tons per year and possess significant resource and environmental attributes. To maintain consistency in our methodology, we exclude organic materials from the ERIA calculation for the waste.

Data sources

The original data were obtained from official statistics such as China Statistical Yearbook2, China Industrial Statistical Yearbook50, China Environmental Statistical Yearbook51, Annual Report on Ecological Environment Statistics, Annual Report on Solid Waste Pollution Prevention and Control in Large and Medium-sized Cities3, official data released by the United States Geological Survey (USGS)52 and other agencies, published papers, and field research. The data and field research results are cited in the text and are detailed in “Source Data”.

Sensitivity and uncertainty analysis

The data varies among the waste. Most information is available for major metals (iron, manganese, aluminum, copper, nickel, zinc, and lead), precious metals (gold, silver, and platinum-group metals), and potentially toxic metals (antimony, cadmium, chromium, mercury, and lead). For other elements, information is limited and can only be obtained or estimated from multiple sources. Here, a Monte Carlo simulation (104 iterations) was conducted to obtain the uncertainty of the results and the sensitivity of the different parameters.
