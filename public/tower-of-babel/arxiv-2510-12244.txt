Bilateral facial reduction: qualification-free subdifferential calculus and exact duality in convex analysis
Matthew S. Scott

Abstract
This paper introduces a geometric framework to extend convex analysis results beyond standard qualification conditions such as intersecting relative interiors of domains. We define the joint facial subspace T T as the span of the face of C − D C-D generated by 0 0 , with its affine translation T a T_{a} containing C ∩ D C\cap D . The intersections C ∩ T a C\cap T_{a} and D ∩ T a D\cap T_{a} are faces of the original sets, establishing T a T_{a} as a bilateral facial reduction with parallels to Borwein-Wolkowicz facial reduction in conic programming. When reducing the domains of two convex functions to their joint facial subspace T a T_{a} , their relative interiors then always intersect, enabling unqualified application of classical theorems via localization. Key generalizations include subdifferential additivity, normal cones of intersections, a subdifferential chain rule, attained infimal convolution for ( f + g ) ∗ , (f+g)^{*}, and an exact Fenchel-Rockafellar dual. We characterize T T via generated faces and computationally through an iterative process converging in at most n n steps in R n \mathbb{R}^{n} . Proofs are self-contained and introduce novel concepts like facial subspaces and nested normals to describe convex boundaries and the lattice of faces.



1
Introduction

In convex analysis, the effective domain of the sum of two convex functions
is the intersection of their effective domains.
Many central results, including the summation rule of subdifferentials,
the convex conjugate of sums of functions, or
the strong duality of the Fenchel-Rockafellar dual, exclude the case
where the domains of the convex functions intersect only on their relative boundaries.
The conditions excluding such cases are called

qualification conditions
, or

constraint qualifications
depending on the context. One such condition, widespread in the seminal
work
[
14
]
by Rockafellar, is that for
two functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
,

\rint(\dom(f))\cap\rint(\dom(g))\neq\emptyset.

For instance, this condition appears in
[
14
, Theorem 23.8]
, which establishes the
summation rule of subdifferentials:

\partial(f+g)(x)=\partial f(x)+\partial g(x).

A second example is the strong duality of the Fenchel-Rockafellar dual:
for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
denoting by
f^{*}
the convex conjugate of
f
,

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)+g(Ax)=-\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}[f^{*}(A^{T}y)+g^{*}(-y)].

In
[
14
, Corollary 31.2.1]
, this is guaranteed to hold
if

\rint(\dom(f))\cap A^{-1}(\rint\dom(g))\neq\emptyset,

where
A^{-1}(\cdot)
is the preimage for the linear map induced by the matrix
A
.
This requirement is often called a
constraint qualification
, and is strikingly similar
to the qualification condition for the subdifferential sum rule.

These and a number of other results share similar conditions. The object of this work is to generalize such results beyond the regime where these conditions hold. Specifically, in
Section

5
we find generalizations for the two aforementioned results,
as well as a few others: the attainment of infimal convolution as the convex conjugate of the sum of two convex functions, the normal cone of the intersection of two convex sets, and a chain rule for the subdifferential. There are many reasons such generalizations are worthwhile, and we list two here.
From a theoretical perspective, extending such results makes the theory more elegant and intuitive.
A second motivation is that such generalizations could
lead to improved methods in optimization, as did facial reduction in conical programming (see
[
3
]
).

Qualification conditions are not merely theoretical artifacts; they
are supported by counter examples, which indicates that these conditions
reflect more or less accurately the true scope of the statements.
We consider one such counter-example for the sum rule of subdifferentials.
Take the function
f(x)=-\sqrt{x}
for
x\geq 0
and
f(x)=\infty
otherwise. This function has effective domain
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Adding it to the indicator function
g(x)=\iota_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}(x)
,
the sum rule states that
\partial(f+g)(0)=\partial f(0)+\partial g(0)=\emptyset+\mathbb{R}_{+}=\emptyset
,
when in fact,
\partial(f+g)(0)=\partial\iota_{0}(0)=\mathbb{R}
.
The relative interiors of the domains are
(-\infty,0)
and
(0,\infty)
,
and do not intersect. The qualification condition does not hold, so there is
no contradiction with
[
14
, Theorem 23.8]
, yet this still shows that the sum rule does not in fact apply to
the sum of any two convex functions.
Thus, an accurate generalization of the sum rule must offer a different
formula for the subdifferential of the sum of two convex functions,
and such a formula should ideally reduce to the classical sum rule when
qualification conditions are satisfied. We provide such a formula in
Theorem

5.1
.

Our approach to relaxing qualification conditions is to focus on
the geometry of the effective domain of the convex functions.
Qualification conditions fail when relative interiors do not
intersect, in which case only the boundaries of the domains meet. We show
that the boundaries of two convex sets always meet in a structured way: each set presents
a convex face to the other, and both of these faces are revealed by the
intersection with a common affine subspace, which we name the
joint facial subspace

T_{a}
. The main property of the
joint facial subspace
is that
given convex sets
C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
T_{a}
defined as in
Definition

4.1
, whenever
C\cap D\neq\emptyset
,

\rint(C\cap T_{a})\cap\rint(D\cap T_{a})\neq\emptyset.

The results which shows this is
Corollary

4.3
.
Therefore, qualification conditions always hold when localizing to the
joint facial subspace. Roughly, we generalize results in convex analysis by
replacing convex functions
f
by their localized version
f+\iota_{T_{a}}
.
The modified results then hold even without qualification conditions. And
when qualification conditions do hold for the original results,
then
T_{a}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see
Proposition

5.1
), and the additional indicator function can be ignored.

For instance, in
Theorem

5.1
we state that

\partial(f+g)(x)=\partial(f+\iota_{T_{a}})(x)+\partial(g+\iota_{T_{a}})(x),

without any condition on
f
and
g
other than they be convex functions.
This modification works for the previously encountered
counter example. In that problem, where
f(x)=-\sqrt{x}
and

g(x)=\iota_{(-\infty,0]}
, the joint facial subspace from

Definition

4.1
is
T_{a}=\{0\}
, and so

\partial(f+\iota_{T_{a}})(x)+\partial(g+\iota_{T_{a}})(x)=\partial\iota_{\{0\}}+\partial\iota_{\{0\}}=\mathbb{R},

which is indeed
\partial(f+g)(x)
. One way to interpret our result is that
there are subgradients that are “hiding” in the faces of the domains of the convex
functions. Intersecting with the joint facial subspace reveals these
hidden gradients.

The central theoretical concept introduced in this work is the
joint facial subspace. We therefore focus much of our efforts in
describing this object. This is the focus of
Section

4
, where we define the joint facial subspace in
Definition

4.1

, provide two characterizations and state core property, that
qualification conditions hold when localizing to it. Given two convex sets

C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the joint facial
subspace is defined from the Minkowski difference
C-D=\{c-d\mid c\in C,d\in D\}
. In the first
characterization,
Theorem

4.1
,
it is made clear how the joint facial subspace relates to
C
and
D

individually. The third characterization is computational in nature,
and describes how the joint facial subspace can be found from
a number of steps that is bounded by the ambient dimension.

Another important contribution of the present work
is in our proof techniques. In
Section

6
, we build a theoretical framework with
nested normals
(
Definition

6.3
) and
facial subspaces
(
Definition

6.2
), two objects which together describe the lattice of faces of a convex set. Their combination forms a dual object which is more descriptive than the standard normal cone (see
Theorem

6.2
). Our theory is therefore at least in part a theory about the boundary of convex sets. From this toolkit, we are able to describe the faces of differences of convex sets
C-D
in
Section

6.2
, from which we derive the various properties of the joint facial subspace in
Section

4
. These objects will be further explored and refined in future work.

The structure of the paper goes as follows. In
Section

2
surveys prior works and
Section

3
introduces some notation.
Section

4
is as described above, with proofs deferred to
Section

7
. In
Section

5
, we provide generalizations of central results in convex analysis, together with their proofs, which use
Corollary

4.3
from
Section

4
. In
Section

6
, we give a motivated introduction to our mathematical framework, introducing key concepts. In
Section

7
, we prove the results in
Section

4
by using tools from
Section

6
.

2
Prior works

The existing research which is the most related to this work is Facial Reduction (FR) in conic programming
[
2
,
1
,
3
]
,
which helped guide the development of the theory in this work.
We briefly describe this method.
Given a feasible cone and affine constraints,
Slater’s condition (that the interior of the feasible cone has at least one
point satisfying the constraints), FR
narrows the ambient space of the problem to the span of a “minimal” face
of the feasible cone. After narrowing the problem via this “preprocessing” step,
the problem is guaranteed to satisfy Slater’s condition.

Our joint facial subspace does something similar, but for
any two intersecting convex sets. We find a “minimal” face of the
difference of the sets,
C-D
(see
Definition

4.1
), and
this yields the joint facial subspace.
The computation of the joint facial subspace can be similarly viewed as
a processing step, after which the problem is reduced to the joint facial
subspace. As specified in
[
3
]
, FR can be applied with finite-step algorithms, with
a number of steps bounded by the ambient dimension.
This is very similar to our second characterization of the joint facial subspace,

Theorem

7.1
.

A result which is related (where the relation is examined in
[
11
]
),
is the Extended Dual System of Ramana, a dual program for semidefinite programming for
which strong duality always holds
[
13
]
. The
corresponding result in our work is the exact Fenchel-Rockafellar dual in
Theorem

5.5
.

From these strong parallels, we conjecture that many aspects of Facial Reduction
and the Extended Dual System can be recovered—and potentially generalized—from results in this work.

Prior efforts to obtain a qualification-free sum
rule for subdifferentials
have developed powerful limiting
approaches that achieve exact characterizations without traditional qualifications.
For instance,
\epsilon
-subdifferentials define approximate
subgradients satisfying the subgradient inequality up to an
\varepsilon
-error, recovering the exact subdifferential as
\varepsilon
approaches zero
[
4
,
12
,
10
]
.
Similarly, fuzzy sum rules approximate the subdifferential by perturbing the
point
x
at which the subdifferential is taken,
deriving exact additivity rules in the limit of vanishing perturbations
[
6
,
5
]
. These methods, leveraging local neighborhood behavior, are
related to ideas in variational analysis, and offer versatile tools for theoretical and computational analysis across general spaces. They can often be implemented with fixed tolerances for practical computation, though the exactness relies on infinite limiting processes to eliminate approximation errors.
Though our approach is tailored to pairs of functions, our formula for the subdifferential
of a sum of convex functions is exact, and can be computed in finite steps.

One theoretical insight that was critical to developing our theory was the
lexicographical
characterization of facial subspaces
from
[
8
]
. This work shows that every face of a convex set can be obtained by iteratively exposing faces. It is a sequel of a preceding result
[
9
]
, which shows a separation theorem for convex sets. Though the lexicographical framework was not flexible enough for our needs, prompting us to develop an independent theory, we did draw inspiration from the results and the proofs. In this paper, we recover
this lexicographical representation, though in different terms, primarily in
Lemma

6.5
. Our proof is direct, and does not make use of a separation theorem.

3
Preliminaries

For two sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
A+B:=\{a+b\mid a\in A,b\in B\}
is the Minkowski sum, with
A-B:=\{a-b\mid a\in A,b\in B\}
,
and for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
A+z:=A+\{z\}
.
For a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote
by
\iota_{S}(x)
the indicator function which is

0
if
x\in S
and
\infty
otherwise.

For a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denote by
\conv(S)
its convex hull. For
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
[y_{1},y_{2}]:=\conv\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
, and
]y_{1},y_{2}[:=\conv\{y_{1},y_{2}\}\setminus\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
.

Intuitively the notion of a
face
of a convex set
C
is a subset of
C
for which there is no line segment in
C
that “goes through”
F
. The following definitions can be found inline in
[
16
, p. 16]
and
[
16
, p.75]
respectively.

Definition 3.1

(Face of a convex set)
.

Let
C
be convex. A subset
F\subseteq C
is defined to be a
face
of
C
when

\forall y,z\in C,(\exists x\in]y,z[\cap F\implies y,z\in F).

Definition 3.2

(Exposed face of a convex set)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex. A subset
F\subseteq C
is defined to be an
exposed face

of
C
when

\exists u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}:F=\argmax_{c\in C}\langle c,u\rangle.

For any set
S\subseteq C
, we denote by
F_{C}(S)
the face of
C

generated
by
S
;
it is the smallest face of
C
that contains
S
(it is also the intersection
of all faces containing
S
, which, it can be shown, is itself a face).

We also recall some objects from convex analysis. Let
\bar{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cap\{-\infty,\infty\}
. For a convex function

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
, the subdifferential is

\partial f(x):=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle v,y-x\rangle\leq f(y)\},

and the convex conjugate is
f^{*}(y):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\langle y,x\rangle-f(x)
.

4
The joint facial subspace

In this section we investigate the
following object.

Definition 4.1

(The joint facial subspace)
.

The
joint facial subspace
of two convex sets
C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

T(C,D):=\Span F_{C-D}(0).

We also define
T_{a}(C,D):=T(C,D)+C\cap D
, the
joint affine facial subspace
.

The Minkowski sum
(+C\cap D)
in the definition of
T_{a}
is in fact
a simple translation. This follows from the next corollary.

Corollary 4.1

(Containment of the intersection)
.

Let
C,D\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex. Then

C\cap D-C\cap D\subseteq T(C,D).

This result follows from
Lemma

6.10
; we defer
the proof
of
Corollary

4.1
to the end of
Section

6.2
.

From
Corollary

4.1
, we see that picking any
x\in C\cap D
, we have
T_{a}=T+x
, independently of the choice of
x
.
Corollary

4.1
also implies that

C\cap D\subseteq T_{a}
. The affine subspace
T_{a}
is therefore the translation of
T
that contains
C\cap D
.

In this section we provide two characterizations of the joint facial subspace.
The next result shows the relation of the joint facial subspace to the individual convex sets, and builds towards the first characterization.

Proposition 4.1

(The joint facial subspace reveals faces)
.

For convex sets
C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
T_{a}=T_{a}(C,D)
,

T_{a}\cap C=F_{C}(C\cap D).

For the first characterization we require a subspace that is related
to
F_{C}(C\cap D)
. This subspace, the
generated
facial subspace
, is central to our theory, as is made clear
in
Section

6
. There, we will re-introduce
this object in a motivated way, in
Definition

6.5
.

Definition 4.2

(Preliminary definition of the generated facial subspace)
.

Given a convex set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
S\subseteq C
non-empty, define the
facial subspace
of
C

generated
by
S\subseteq C
to be

H_{C}(S):=\Span(F_{C}(S)-F_{C}(S)).

The next theorem is our first characterization of the joint facial subspace.

Theorem 4.1

(Characterization through generated faces)
.

The joint facial subspace of
C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

T(C,D)=H_{C}(C\cap D)+H_{D}(C\cap D).

Definition

4.1
and
Theorem

4.1

both specify the joint facial subspace via analytical
formulas. Our second characterization instead specifies the joint facial
subspace as the result of a process with a number of steps bounded
by the ambient dimension, which is more amenable to computation.
This result,
Theorem

7.1
, is best stated in terms of
nested normals

a concept that we introduce in
Definition

6.3
,
and motivate throughout
Section

6
.
For this reason, we defer it to
Section

7.3
. For the moment, we state a direct corollary which does not require this object.

Corollary 4.2

(Iterative bilateral facial reduction)
.

Let
C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex sets and fix any

x\in C\cap D
. Beginning with
T_{0}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

T_{i+1}=T_{i}\cap(N_{C}(x)\cap[-N_{D}(x)]\cap T_{i})^{\perp}.

Let
\ell
be the smallest number such that

T_{\ell+1}=T_{\ell}
. Then:

1.

T_{\ell}=T(C,D)
.

2.

\ell\leq n
.

Now that the joint facial subspace has been thoroughly identified,
we move on to describing its core property.
Recall that for two convex functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
,
a common qualification condition is that for convex sets
C:=\dom(f),D:=\dom(g)
,

\rint(C)\cap\rint(D)\neq\emptyset.

(1)

It was shown in
[
14
, theorem 11.3]

that this notion is equivalent to a
lack of proper separation

of the domains. We define the notions of
separation
and
proper separation
below.

Definition 4.3

(Separation of sets)
.

Two convex sets
C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
are said to be
separated
if there exists some
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\forall c\in C,d\in D,v\cdot c\leq v\cdot d.

(2)

The concept of
proper separation
is slightly stronger.

Definition 4.4

(Proper separation of sets)
.

Sets
C,D\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are said to be
properly separated
when
there exists some
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
Equation

2
holds, and
such that there is some pair
(c,d)\in C\times D
for
which the inequality in
Equation

2
is
strict.

Proper separation excludes the case where the two sets are
merely separated by virtue of being both contained in some affine hyperplane.

We will show that inside the joint facial subspace, we always have a “lack of separation” of the convex sets. Since this regularity condition is stronger than a “lack of proper separation”, it is stronger than
Equation

1
.

Theorem 4.2

(Qualification conditions always holds in the joint facial subspace)
.

For two convex sets
C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
C\cap T_{a}(C,D)
and
D\cap T_{a}(C,D)
are not separated.

The proof
of
Theorem

4.2
is the subject of
Section

7.1
. The next corollary follows immediately from
Theorem

4.2
.

Corollary 4.3

(rint qualification holds in joint facial subspace)
.

For convex sets
C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\rint(C\cap T_{a})\cap\rint(D\cap T_{a})\neq\emptyset.

With
Corollary

4.3

we can use standard results of convex analysis from
[
14
]

by first taking intersections with
T_{a}
. We do this in the proof of each of the
results in
Section

5
.
Also note that if we only had access to weaker results than those available in
[
14
]
,
results that would have the stronger assumption of “no separation of the sets”
instead of “intersection of relative interiors is non-empty”,
the theory in this paper would still hold,
and indeed we would recover the stronger results with the weaker assumption,
that the intersection of relative interiors is non-empty.

5
Main results

In this section we show five generalizations of important results of convex
analysis: additivity of subdifferentials, normal cone of the intersection of two
convex sets, attainment of the infimal
convolution in the conjugate of a sum of two functions, the chain rule of the subdifferential, and the Fenchel-Rockafellar dual. All proofs are deferred to
Section

7
, because they require
the machinery introduced in
Section

6
.

Proposition 5.1

(Recovery of the classical results)
.

In all results that follow, the classical analogue is recovered when
qualification conditions hold. Indeed, given two convex functions

f,g
such that their relative interiors intersect,
we have
\dom(f),\dom(g)\subseteq T_{a}(\dom(f),\dom(g))
, and
therefore
f+\iota_{T_{a}}=f
and
g+\iota_{T_{a}}=g
.

For a proper convex function
f
, the
singular subdifferential

for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as
\partial^{\infty}f(x):=N_{\dom(f)}(x)

when
x\in\dom(f)
, and is empty when
x\notin\dom(f)
(see, e.g.
[
10
,
15
]
). Singular subdifferentials capture the notion of an infinite slope in a particular direction.

Theorem 5.1

(Qualification-free subdifferential additivity)
.

For
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
proper convex,
x\in\dom(f)\cap\dom(g)
, and
T_{a}=T_{a}(\dom(f),\dom(g))
,

\partial(f+g)(x)=\partial(f+\iota_{T_{a}})(x)+\partial(g+\iota_{T_{a}})(x),

\partial^{\infty}(f+g)(x)=\partial^{\infty}(f+\iota_{T_{a}})(x)+\partial^{\infty}(g+\iota_{T_{a}})(x).

P
roof of
Theorem

5.1
.

By

Corollary

4.3

applied to
\dom(f)
and
\dom(g)
,

\displaystyle\rint\dom(f+\iota_{T_{a}})\cap\rint\dom(g+\iota_{T_{a}})

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rint(\dom(f)\cap T_{a})\cap\rint(\dom(g)\cap T_{a})\neq\emptyset.

(4)

Therefore, a qualification condition is satisfied for the functions
f+\iota_{T_{a}}
and
g+\iota_{T_{a}}
, and the first part of the result follows by application of

[
14
, Theorem 23.8]
.

The second part of the result follows from applying
[
14
, Corollary 23.8.1]
to the convex sets
\dom(f)\cap T_{a}
and
\dom(g)\cap T_{a}
, by which

\displaystyle N_{(\dom(f)\cap T_{a})\cap(\dom(g)\cap T_{a})}(x)

\displaystyle=N_{\dom(f)\cap T_{a}}(x)+N_{\dom(g)\cap T_{a}}(x)

\displaystyle=N_{\dom(f+\iota_{T_{a}})}(x)+N_{\dom(g+\iota_{T_{a}})}(x).

And since
N_{(\dom(f)\cap T_{a})\cap(\dom(g)\cap T_{a})}(x)=N_{\dom(f)\cap\dom(g)}(x)=N_{\dom(f+g)}(x)
, the second part
of the result follows.
∎

Theorem 5.2

(Normal cone of the intersection of convex sets)
.

For
C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
convex, and
x\subseteq C\cap D
, for
T_{a}=T_{a}(C,D)
,

N_{C\cap D}(x)=N_{C\cap T_{a}}(x)+N_{D\cap T_{a}}(x).

P
roof of
Theorem

5.2
.

With
Corollary

4.3
we can use

[
14
, Corollary 23.8.1]
,
from which we find that

N_{C\cap D}(x)=N_{(C\cap T_{a})\cap(D\cap T_{a})}(x)=N_{C\cap T_{a}}(x)+N_{D\cap T_{a}}(x).

∎

Recall that for two convex functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
, the infimal convolution is

(f\,\square g)(z):=\inf_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x+y=z}f(x)+g(y).

In the following results, we denote by
f^{*}
the convex conjugate of
f
.

Theorem 5.3

(Attained infimal convolution in the conjugate of a sum)
.

For two proper convex functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
,
and
T_{a}=T_{a}(\dom(f),\dom(g))
,

(f+g)^{*}=(f+\iota_{T_{a}})^{*}\square(g+\iota_{T_{a}})^{*},

where the infimum in the infimal convolution is attained.

P
roof of
Theorem

5.3
.

When
\dom(f)\cap\dom(g)=\emptyset
,
T_{a}=\emptyset
and the equality
holds trivially. Otherwise, by
Corollary

4.3
,
\rint(\dom(f+\iota_{T_{a}}))\cap\rint(\dom(g+\iota_{T_{a}}))\neq\emptyset
, and the result
follows from
[
14
, Theorem 20.1]
.
∎

Theorem 5.4

(Generalized subdifferential chain rule)
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper convex function, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a matrix.
Then for
T=T(\dom(g),\range(A))
,

\partial(g)(Ax)=A^{T}\partial(g+\iota_{T})(Ax).

P
roof of
Theorem

5.4
.

Denote
T_{a}=T_{a}(\dom(g),\range(A))
.
Note that
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(g\circ A)(x)=([g+\iota_{T_{a}}]\circ A)(x),

because
T_{a}\supseteq\dom(g)\cap\range(A)
. So it suffices to calculate the subdifferential of the r.h.s. of the above equation.

If
\dom(g)\cap\range(A)=\emptyset,

then the result holds trivially because
g(Ax)\equiv\infty
, and
(g+\iota_{T_{a}})(Ax)\equiv\infty
as well.
Otherwise, by
Corollary

4.3

\rint\dom(g+\iota_{T_{a}})\cap\range(A)\neq\emptyset
.
Additionally, note that the only face of
\range(A)
is itself, whence by

Proposition

4.1

\range(A)\subseteq T_{a}
. Since
0\in\range(A)
,
this in fact implies that
T_{a}=T
. The statement then follows by applying
[
14
, Theorem 23.9]

to the composition
[g+\iota_{T}]\circ A
.
∎

In the following result, for a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and a set

, we denote by

the preimage of
S
under
the linear map associated with the matrix
A
, making no assumption on the
invertibility of the matrix
A
.

Theorem 5.5

(Exact Fenchel-Rockafellar dual)
.

For

proper, lower semi-continuous convex functions, and let
T_{a}:=T_{a}(A\dom(f),\dom(g))
. Then

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)+g(Ax)=-\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}[(f+\iota_{A^{-1}(T_{a})})^{*}(A^{T}y)+(g+\iota_{T_{a}})^{*}(-y)].

P
roof of
Theorem

5.5
.

In this proof we use the notation of left multiplication of functions
by a matrix

(Af)(u):=\inf\{f(x)\mid Ax=u\}.

Note that
Af+\iota_{T_{a}}\equiv A(f+\iota_{A^{-1}(T_{a})})
.
Indeed, both functions have the domain
(A\dom(f))\cap T_{a}
, where
A\dom(f):=\{Ax\mid x\in\dom(f)\}
, and

\forall y\in A\dom(f)\cap T_{a}
,

\displaystyle(Af+\iota_{T_{a}})(y)

\displaystyle=\inf\{f(x)\mid Ax=y\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{f(x)+\iota_{Ax\in T_{a}}\mid Ax=y\}

\displaystyle=A(f+\iota_{A^{-1}(T_{a})})(y).

Then, employing the change of variable

,

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)+g(Ax)

\displaystyle=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}(Af)(u)+g(u)

\displaystyle=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}(Af+\iota_{T_{a}})(u)+(g+\iota_{T_{a}})(u)

\displaystyle=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}(A(f+\iota_{A^{-1}{T_{a}}}))(u)+(g+\iota_{T_{a}})(u)

\displaystyle=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}(f+\iota_{A^{-1}{T_{a}}})(x)+(g+\iota_{T_{a}})(Ax).

Note that by

Corollary

4.3
,

\exists u\in\rint\dom(Af)\cap\rint\dom(g)
. Note that
\dom(Af)=A\dom(f)
, and by
[

, Theorem 6.6]
,
\rint A\dom(f)=A\rint\dom(f)
. Therefore,

, i.e. there exists an

such that

. With this,
we satisfy a sufficient requirement of
[

, which guarantees strong duality and completes the proof.
∎

6
Nested normals and facial subspaces

Inspired by the lexicographic characterization of convex faces
of
[
8
]
, we develop a framework to describe the lattice of convex
faces at a point
x\in C
. By using this theory, we will then be able to show the results of
Section

4
.

We begin with a characterization of
faces of convex sets. In the next few results, we denote
X
for
a real vector space.

Theorem 6.1

(Facial subspace characterisation of convex faces)
.

Let
C\subseteq X
. A subset
F\subseteq C
is a face of
C
if, and only if
the two following conditions hold.

1.

\aff(F)\cap C=F
,

2.

C\setminus F
is convex.

P
roof of
Theorem

6.1
.

Let
F
be a face. We argue by contradiction that
\aff(F)\cap[C\setminus F]=\emptyset
;
let us assume that there is some

z\in\aff(F)\cap[C\setminus F]
.

If
F
is a singleton, then
\aff(F)=F
, and
\aff(F)\cap[C\setminus F]=\emptyset
.
Otherwise, we may assume that
0\in F\setminus\{z\}
without
loss of generality, because the faces of a convex set are invariant under translation. With this assumption,

\aff(F)=\Span(F)=\cone(F-F)
. Then
z\in\cone(F-F)
,
so there are
y_{1},y_{2}\in F
such that
z=\alpha(y_{1}-y_{2})
for some
\alpha\geq 0
.
We can rule out the possibility that
z\in[y_{1},y_{2}]
,
because by convexity of
F
it would follow that
z\in F
.
The remaining possibility is that
y_{1}\in[y_{2},z[
.
Further, note that
y_{1}\neq y_{2}
, because otherwise
z=0
,
which disagrees with our assumption that
z\in F\setminus\{0\}
.
Therefore,
y_{1}\in]y_{2},z[
. The line segment
[y_{2},z]
has a point
y_{1}\in]y_{2},z[\cap F
. Since
y_{2},z\in C
,
the fact that
F
is a face implies that

z
must then be in
F
, in contradiction
with our definition of
z
. We have therefore shown the claim.

We next argue that
C\setminus F
is convex.
\forall y,z\in C\setminus F
, any
x\in]y,z[
is in
C
by convexity of
C
. Also,
x
is not in
F
because
F
is a face. So any
x\in]y,z[
is also in

C\setminus F
, which means that
C\setminus F
is convex.

We now show the converse: let
F\subseteq C
be such that
C\setminus F
is convex and
\aff(F)\cap C=F
, we show that
F
is a face of
C
.

Take any
y,z\in C
,
x\in F\cap]y,z[
. If
y\in\aff(F)
then
z\in\aff(F)
as well, because
z=\alpha(x-y)+y
for some
\alpha>0
. So either
z,y
are both in
\aff(F)
, or neither are. But
if neither are, then
z,y\in C\setminus\aff(F)
, and by convexity of
C\setminus\aff(F)
,

x\in C\setminus\aff(F)
also, which is a contradiction with
x\in F
. Therefore,
z,y
are both in
F
,
which shows that
F
is a face.
∎

This characterization, surprisingly, appears to be novel to the knowledge of the author. It allows us to shift our focus away from the faces themselves to the comparatively simpler affine subspaces which select them through intersections with the convex set. We call such face-selecting subspaces
affine facial subspaces
.

Definition 6.1

(Affine facial subspace)
.

Let
C\subseteq X
be convex. An affine subspace
L\subseteq V
is an affine facial subspace of
C
if
C\setminus L
is convex.

The next result describes the relationship between affine facial subspaces and faces.

Corollary 6.1

(Facial subspace relation to faces)
.

Let
C\subseteq X
be convex and
S\subseteq C
non-empty.

1.

For every affine facial subspace
L
of
C
, the set
F:=C\cap L
is a face of
C
.

2.

Conversely, for every face
F
of
C
, there exists an affine facial subspace
L
such that
F=L\cap C
(specifically,
L=\text{aff}(F)
).

P
roof of
Corollary

6.1
.

For the first part of the result, we show that if
L
is an affine facial subspace, then
F:=C\cap L
satisfies the conditions of
Theorem

6.1
.

1.

That
\aff(F)\cap C=F
: note that
\aff(F)\subseteq L
because
F\subseteq L
, and so

\aff(F)\cap C\subseteq L\cap C=:F
.
On the other hand,
L\cap C=(L\cap C)\cap C\subseteq\aff(L\cap C)\cap C
, and so

F\subseteq\aff(F)\cap C
.

2.

That
C\setminus F
is convex: note that

C\setminus F=C\setminus[C\cap L]=C\setminus L,

which is convex by definition of
L
as an affine facial subspace.

We show the second part of the result. For
F
a face of
C
, by
Theorem

6.1

F=C\cap\aff(F)
and
C\setminus F
is convex. But then
C\setminus\aff(F)=C\setminus(\aff(F)\cap C)=C\setminus F
, and therefore
C\setminus\aff(F)
is convex, which makes
\aff(F)
an affine facial subspace.
∎

In this work, we focus on “local” affine facial subspaces; the
subset of affine facial subspaces that contain a point
x\in C
. These subsets have some additional nice algebraic
properties. We find it useful to generalize this idea of “locality” to affine facial subspaces that are localized at a set
S\subseteq C
instead of only at points, i.e., we consider all affine subspaces that contain
S
.
To emphasize this connection, we use the word “at” to describe the containment of
S
: ”a facial subspace is at

S
”. The reader should keep in mind the important special case of
S
being a singleton
S=\{x\}
, for
which the results are localized at a point.

Any affine facial subspace
L
at
S
has its affine offset determined by

S
. Recall that
L-L
is simply the affine subspace
L
translated in such a way that it becomes a (non-affine) subspace. With this, it becomes clear that
L=(L-L)+S
.
We can always deduce the correct affine offset from
in
S
. Therefore, we find it convenient to describe the set of affine facial subspaces that contain
S
through their translated, non-affine counterparts.

Definition 6.2

(Facial subspace at S)
.

Let
C\subseteq X
be a convex set and
S\subseteq X
a non-empty subset. A subspace
U\subseteq X
is defined to be a
facial subspace
of
C
at
S
if:

1.

S-S\subseteq U

2.

U+S
is an affine facial subspace of
C
.

The set of all facial subspaces of
C
at
S
is denoted
\mathcal{F}(C;S)
.

When
S
is the singleton
\{x\}
, the requirement that
S-S\subseteq U
is
trivially satisfied. When
S
is not a singleton, this assumption guarantees that
\forall s_{1},s_{2}\in S,U+s_{1}=U+s_{2}
. This implies that
\forall s\in S,U+s=U+S
, and so the Minkowski sum
U+S
is a simple affine offset of
U
, for which
S\subseteq U+S
. In other words, the affine facial subspace
U+S
contains
S
.

Next, we introduce a generalization of the notion of a normal cone.
A normal cone of a convex set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

N_{C}(x):=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\forall y\in C\langle y-x,z\rangle\leq 0\}.

When
C
is a cone and
x=0
, the normal cone
N_{C}(0)=:C^{\circ}

is called the
polar
of
C
. The polar is a dual operation, in the sense that

{C^{\circ}}^{\circ}=\closure(C).

We can see that a normal cone is a dual object which “describes” the original set only up to its closure.

We require a dual object which has sufficient descriptive power to go beyond the closure, encoding information about the boundary.
To this end, we introduce the notion of a
nested normal cone
. Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex and
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a subspace, then
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

N_{C}^{U}(x):=N_{C\cap U}(x)\cap U.

We would also like for our normal cones to located
at a set

S\subseteq C
, similarly to facial subspaces.

N_{C}(S):=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\forall s\in S,c\in C,\langle c-s,v\rangle\leq 0\}.

The hyperplanes corresponding to the vectors in this normal cone are
supporting hyperplanes of
C
that contain
S
. Note also that
N_{C}(S)=\bigcap_{s\in S}N_{C}(s).

The idea of normal cones that are located at sets appears in the earlier work

[
7
]
, where normal cones are at faces of
polytopes, instead at points. The normal cone at a set has an elegant connection to the notion of relative interior of that set: the normal cone at any point in the relative interior
of a convex set
S\subseteq C
will match the normal cone of
S
.

Combining the two generalizations, we get the following.

Definition 6.3

(nested normals)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set, and
S
a
non-empty subset of
C
. Then for a subspace
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

such that
S-S\subseteq U
, and with
C^{\prime}:=C\cap(U+S)
,

N_{C}^{U}(S):=\{v\in U\mid\forall c\in C^{\prime},s\in S,\langle c-s,v\rangle\leq 0\}

is a
nested normal cone
of
C
at
S
,
and we label any
v\in N_{C}^{U}(S)

to be a
nested normal
of
C
at
S
in
U
.

The idea of normal cone defined at a set has an implicit relation with the notion
of relative interior.

Lemma 6.1

(nested normals in the relative interior are representative)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex and
S\subseteq C
a non-empty convex set.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a subspace, and
x\in\rint(S)
.
Then

N_{C}^{U}(S)=N_{C}^{U}(x).

P
roof of
Lemma

6.1
.

That
N_{C}^{U}(S)\subseteq N_{C}^{U}(x)
follows from simple inclusion
x\subseteq S
.

For the containment (
\supseteq
), WLOG let
U=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We can make this assumption because the normal
cones are invariant under the translation
U+S\to U
,
and so we have an equivalent normal cone inside the subspace
U
. Since
U

is a finite dimensional vector space, it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let
v\in N_{C}(x)
. Then
\forall s\in S,\langle v,s\rangle\leq\langle v,x\rangle
because
S\subseteq C
.
We show that this inequality is in fact always an equality.
Arguing by contradiction, assume that there is an
s\in S
such that
\langle v,s\rangle<\langle v,x\rangle
. Then since
x\in\rint(S)
, there is a
\varepsilon>0

such that
(\varepsilon B+x)\cap\aff(S)\subseteq S
for
B
the unit
ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. There is
a
\lambda>0
such that
s^{\prime}:=x+\lambda(s-x)\in\varepsilon B+x
.
Then note that
s^{\prime}\in(\varepsilon B+x)\cap\aff(S)\subseteq S
, and

\langle v,s^{\prime}\rangle=\langle v,x\rangle-\lambda\langle v,s-x\rangle.

The second term is strictly positive, therefore
\langle v,s^{\prime}\rangle>\langle v,x\rangle
.
But this is a contradiction with
v\in N_{C}(x)
.
Therefore, we find that
\forall s\in S,

\langle v,s\rangle=\langle v,x\rangle
.
Then
\forall c\in C,s\in S

\langle c-s,v\rangle=\langle c-x,v\rangle\leq 0
,
and therefore
v\in N_{C}(S)
.
∎

The nested normals
at a set
are orthogonal with that set.

Lemma 6.2

(Matching sets means paired normals)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set, and
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
a subspace. Then for any non-empty
S\subseteq C
such that
S-S\subseteq U
,

N_{C}^{U}(S)\subseteq(S-S)^{\perp}\cap U.

Further, when
S=C\cap(U+S)
, the sets are equal.

P
roof of
Lemma

6.2
.

Let
y\in N_{C}^{U}(S)
. By
Definition

6.3
,
\forall z,x\in S,
since
x\in S
and
z\in C
,
\langle x-z,y\rangle\leq 0,

and since
x\in C
and
z\in S

\langle x-z,y\rangle\geq 0
. Then
y\perp S-S
, which shows the inclusion.

Next we show the containment (
\supseteq
), in the case that
S=C\cap(U+S)
.
Let
y\in(S-S)^{\perp}\cap U
. For any
x\in S,z\in C\cap(U+S)
we need to show that
\langle z-x,y\rangle\leq 0
.
Note that
z\in S
, so
x-z\in S-S
, and by definition of
y
,
\langle z-x,y\rangle=0
, which proves the statement.
∎

As previously discussed, one motivating property of the nested normals
is that they can encode information about the boundary of convex sets.
We make this idea concrete.

The tangent cone of a convex set
C
at
x\in C
is the smallest closed
cone
T
such that
C\subseteq x+T
. We can think of this cone as
a local description of
C
around
x
. The normal cone is the polar of
hthe tangent cone, and therefore “describes” the convex set
C
around

x
to the same extent as the tangent cone. In contrast,
nested normals in facial subspaces of
C
at
x
describe the

cone of feasible directions
.

Definition 6.4

(Cone of feasible directions)
.

For a convex set
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
x\in C
,
we define the
cone of feasible directions
of
C
at
x
to be
D_{C}(x):=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\exists\lambda>0:\lambda v\in C\}
.

The closure of the cone of feasible directions is the tangent cone.
The difference is that the cone of feasible directions captures additional
information about the boundary of
C
around
x
.
The following result shows that any convex cone (including the cone of
feasible directions at any point) can be recovered from
the nested normals in facial subspaces.

Theorem 6.2

(Local polar without closure)
.

Given any convex cone
T
with
0\in T
,

T=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\forall U\in\mathcal{F}(T;0),v\subseteq N_{T}^{U}(0),x\in U\implies\langle x,v\rangle\leq 0\}.

P
roof of
Theorem

6.2
.

Let
T^{\prime}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\forall U\in\mathcal{F}(T;0),v\subseteq N_{T}^{U}(0),x\in U\implies\langle x,v\rangle\leq 0\}
.
By the definition of conditional normals,
T\subseteq T^{\prime}
.
For the containment (
\supseteq
), we argue by contradiction: we
assume that there is some
x\in T^{\prime}\setminus T
.
If
x\in\mathrm{int}(T^{c})
, then
x
is strictly separated from
T
by a supporting hyperplane of
T
(e.g., see
[
14
, Theorem 11.3]
).
Any such hyperplane induces a nested normal of the form
v\in N_{T}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)
,
for which
v^{\perp}
is the hyperplane, and
\langle v,x\rangle>0
. But then
x\notin T^{\prime}
, and so

x\notin\mathrm{int}(T^{c})
. Since we assumed that
x\notin T
, the only possibility is that
x\in\partial T
. Then
x
is
contained in a supporting hyperplane, so there
is some nested normal
v\in N_{T}^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0)
such that
\langle v,x\rangle=0
. Let
T_{1}=T\cap v^{\perp}
, and narrow the ambient space to the subspace
T_{1}
.
Inside
T_{1}
, we repeat the same argument, and keep narrowing the ambient space in this manner. This process must eventually reduce the ambient space to
\Span(x)
, because
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is finite-dimensional. In this one-dimensional space, with the narrowed cone
T_{\ell}=T\cap\Span(x)
, note that
T_{\ell}
is a closed cone. Therefore, either
x\in T_{\ell}
or
x\in\mathrm{int}(T_{\ell}^{c})
, both of which yields a contradiction, from which the result follows.
∎

Another important property of nested normals is that they describe
the containment structure of facial subspaces, which is closely related to the
lattice structure of the faces. We make this precise in the next two results.

Lemma 6.3

(Nested normals induce facial subspaces)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex,
S\subseteq C
non-empty,
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
a subspace, and
v\in N_{C}^{U}(S)
. Let
V\in\mathcal{F}(C;S)
be such that
V\subseteq U
. Then

V\cap v^{\perp}\in\mathcal{F}(C;S).

Lemma 6.4

(Nested normals induce facial subspaces)
.

First, note that if
v=0
, then
V\cap v^{\perp}=V
, and there result holds. We now consider the case where
v\neq 0
.

By
Lemma

6.2
,
v\perp S-S
, and therefore

S-S\subseteq V\cap v^{\perp}
.

We make two observations.

First, since
v\in N_{C}^{U}(S)
and
V\subseteq U
,
\forall y\in C\cap(V+S)
,
\forall s\in S,
we have that
\langle y,v\rangle\leq\langle s,v\rangle
. Second,
this inequality is tight precisely for those values of
y
that are also contained in
v^{\perp}+S
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\{y\in C\cap(V+S)\mid\forall s\in S,\langle y,v\rangle=\langle s,v\rangle\}

(5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((V+S)\cap C)\cap(v^{\perp}+S)=(V\cap v^{\perp}+S)\cap C.

(6)

These two facts imply that

(V\cap v^{\perp}+S)\cap C=\argmax_{y\in C\cap(V+S)}\langle y,v\rangle.

The r.h.s. is an exposed face of the face
C\cap(V+S)
, so it is itself a
face, i.e.,
V\cap v^{\perp}\in\mathcal{F}(C;S)
.

We next show that all facial subspaces are “reached” by nested normals
in facial subspaces.

Lemma 6.5

(Completeness of conditional normal proof composition)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex,
S\subseteq C
non-empty,
and
V,U\in\mathcal{F}(C;S)
such that
U\subseteq V
.
Then there is a sequence
V_{0},\ldots,V_{k}\in\mathcal{F}(C;S)

with
k=\dim(V)-\dim(U)
such that
V_{0}=V
,
V_{k}=U
, and for each

i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
there is a
v_{i}\in N_{C}^{V_{i-1}}(S)\setminus\{0\}
such that
V_{i}=V_{i-1}\cap v_{i}^{\perp}
.

This result is similar to the lexicographic characterization of the faces
of convex sets in
[
8
, Proposition 6]
,
which states that every face can be obtained by a sequence of faces
where each face is an exposed face of the preceding face. Let us state
a technical lemma that we will need for the proof.

Lemma 6.6

(Affine slices preserve facial subspaces)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex and
S\subseteq C
non-empty.
Let
U\in\mathcal{F}(C;S)
, and
V
any subspace such that

S-S\subseteq V
. Then
U\cap V\in\mathcal{F}(C\cap(V+S);S)
.

P
roof of
Lemma

6.6
.

Since
C\setminus(U+S)
is convex,

(V+S)\cap(C\setminus(U+S))
is also convex.
We can write this set as
[(V+S)\cap C]\setminus[(V+S)\cap(U+S)]
,
which is the same as
((V+S)\cap C)\setminus((V\cap U)+S)
, because
S-S\in V\cap U
.
Since this set is convex, and
S-S\subseteq V\cap U
, we find that
V\cap U\in\mathcal{F}((V+S)\cap C;S)
.
∎

P
roof of
Lemma

6.5
.

It suffices to show the following statement: given any
U,V\in\mathcal{F}(C;S)
such that
V\supsetneq U
,
there exists a
v\in N_{C}^{V}(S)\setminus\{0\}
such that

.
If this statement is shown to hold, applying it recursively generates the sequence of facial subspaces

in a finite number of steps.
Note that the dimension of the facial subspaces decreases by one at each iteration while always containing

, which implies that

6.1
Minimal facial subspace

Recall that the smallest face of a convex set
C
containing some
x\in C

, the face of
C

generated
by
x
. It is the only face with
x

in its relative interior. In this section we develop an analogous notion
for facial subspaces, and characterize this facial subspace by using nested normals.

Definition 6.5

(The generated facial subspace)
.

Given a convex set
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
S\subseteq C
non-empty,
we define the
generated facial subspace

H_{C}(S)
of
C
, generated by
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, to be the facial subspace
that is contained in every other facial subspace in
\mathcal{F}(C;S)
.

We show existence and uniqueness for the above definition, starting with a lemma.

Lemma 6.7

(Facial subspaces are closed under intersections)
.

For
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
convex and
S\subseteq C
non-empty, let
U,V\in\mathcal{F}(C;S)
. Then
U\cap V\in\mathcal{F}(C;S)
.

P
roof of
Lemma

6.7
.

The subspace
U\cap V
is such that

(U\cap V+S)\cap C=((U+S)\cap C)\cap((V+S)\cap C).

The r.h.s. is the intersection of two convex faces of
C
. Their intersection
is then also a face (this is immediate from
Definition

3.1
).
By
Theorem

6.1
,
C\setminus[(U\cap V+S)\cap C]

is convex. The convexity of this set, which we can write as
C\setminus((U\cap V)+S)
,
implies that
U\cap V\in\mathcal{F}(C;S)
.
∎

Theorem 6.3

(Existence and uniqueness of the generated facial subspace)
.

The generated facial subspace of
Definition

6.5
exists and is unique.

P
roof of
Theorem

6.3
.

We argue that there exists a facial subspace which contains no other.
Arguing for the existence by contradiction, if every facial subspace of
C
at
S
properly contains some other facial
subspace of
C
at
S
, starting at any facial subspace, we can find an infinite chain of facial subspaces with dimension decreasing by one at each step. This is not possible because the dimension is finite. Therefore this chain of subspaces must terminate at some subspace which contains no other.

We now argue that there is only one subspace that contains no other. Let
V_{1},V_{2}
be two distinct minimal facial subspaces of
C
at
S
. Then by
Lemma

6.7
,
V_{1}\cap V_{2}
is a facial subspace of
C
at
S
properly contained in both
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, which contradicts
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
being minimal.

We have shown the existence and uniqueness of a facial subspace that contains no other.
From any facial subspace, we can start a proper containment chain as in our existence argument. The subspace at the end of this chain is the subspace which contains no other, which concludes the proof.
∎

It only remains to show that
N_{C}^{U}(C\cap D)=N_{C}^{U}(C\cap T_{a})
. By

Corollary

4.3
,
we may pick some
x\in\rint(C\cap T_{a})\cap\rint(D\cap T_{a})
. Then by

Lemma

6.1
,

N_{C}^{U}(C\cap T_{a})=N_{C}^{U}(x)\supseteq N_{C}^{U}(C\cap D).

Then
N_{C}^{U}(C\cap D)=\{0\}
, and so the result follows from
Lemma

6.9
.
∎

P
roof of
Proposition

4.1
.

From

Lemma

7.4
,

H_{C}(C\cap D)=T\cap N_{C}^{T}(C\cap T_{a})^{\perp}
. Since
(C\cap T_{a}-C\cap T_{a})\perp N_{C}^{T}(C\cap T_{a})
by
Lemma

6.2
,
(C\cap T_{a}-C\cap T_{a})\subseteq H_{C}(C\cap D)
, i.e.,

H_{C}(C\cap D)+C\cap T_{a}\supseteq C\cap T_{a}
. But
H_{C}(C\cap D)+C\cap T_{a}=H_{C}(C\cap D)+C\cap D
, therefore

H_{C}(C\cap D)+C\cap D\supseteq C\cap T_{a}.

(14)

Additionally, since we know that
T\in\mathcal{F}(C\cap D;C)
from
Lemma

6.10
, by definition of the generated facial subspace we know that

H_{C}(C\cap D)\subseteq T
. Therefore,

H_{C}(C\cap D)+C\cap D\subseteq T_{a}.

(15)

From
Equation

14
and
Equation

15
it follows that
(H_{C}(C\cap D)+C\cap D)\cap C=C\cap T_{a}
.
Then the statement follows from

Lemma

6.8
.
∎

P
roof of
Theorem

4.1
.

From
Proposition

4.1
, we have that

T\in\mathcal{F}(C;C\cap D)
is a facial subspace of
C
that contains
C\cap D
,
and so by
Definition

6.5
,

H_{C}(C\cap D)\subseteq T
. Therefore,
for
T^{\prime}:=H_{C}(C\cap D)+H_{D}(C\cap D),

T^{\prime}\subseteq T.

For the other containment, we argue by contradiction: let
T^{\prime}\subsetneq T
. Then there is a unit vector
v\in T

with
v\perp T^{\prime}
. Then
v\perp H_{C}(C\cap D)
, which by

Lemma

7.4
,
implies that
v\in N_{C}^{T}(C\cap T)
. By
Lemma

6.2
,

-v\in N_{C}^{T}(C\cap T)
as well.
And since
N_{C}^{T}(C\cap T)\subseteq N_{C}^{T}(C\cap D)
, we find that
v,-v\in N_{C}^{T}(C\cap D)
.
By a similar argument,
v,-v\in N_{D}^{T}(C\cap D)
.
But then
v\in N_{C}^{T}(C\cap D)\cap(-N_{D}^{T}(C\cap D))
, which by
Lemma

6.10
means that
v\in N_{C-D}^{T}(0)
.
But by
Lemma

6.9
,
T
cannot contain such a nested normal because by
Definition

6.6
,
T=H_{C-D}(0)
. Therefore, by contradiction
T=T^{\prime}
.
∎

7.3
Second characterization

We can now state the second characterization in full.

Theorem 7.1

(Iterative bilateral facial reduction)
.

Let
C,D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex sets and

S\subseteq C\cap D
non-empty. Beginning with
T_{0}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

T_{i+1}=T_{i}\cap(N^{T_{i}}_{C}(S)\cap[-N^{T_{i}}_{D}(S)])^{\perp}.

Let
\ell
be the first index for which

T_{\ell+1}=T_{\ell}
. Then:

1.

T_{\ell}=T(C,D)
.

2.

\ell\leq n
.

P
roof of
Theorem

7.1
.

First,
(N^{T_{i}}_{C}(S)\cap[-N^{T_{i}}_{D}(S)])=N^{T_{i}}_{C-D}(S)
by
Lemma

6.10
.
Applying
Lemma

7.3
at each step, we find that
\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,\ell\},\,T_{i}\in\mathcal{F}(C-D;S)
.
Finally, note that whenever
N^{T_{i}}_{C}(S)\cap[-N^{T_{i}}_{D}(S)]\neq\{0\}
,

\dim(T_{i+1})\leq\dim(T_{i})-1
. Therefore, the dimension may be reduced
at most
n
times, which implies that
\ell\leq n
. On the other hand, the fact that
T_{\ell}=T_{\ell+1}

implies that
N^{T_{\ell}}_{C-D}(S)=\{0\}
.
Then from

Lemma

6.9

it follows that
T_{\ell}=H_{C-D}(S)
, which is
T(C,D)
from
Definition

6.6
.
∎
