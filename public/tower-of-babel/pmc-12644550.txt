Variation characteristics of support strength and stability in post-peak large deformation for roadways surrounding rock
Authors: Shengliang Lu, Miao Sun, Shaoqing Niu, Yong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The stability of deep underground roadways is critically challenged by post-peak large deformations of surrounding rock, especially under high in-situ stress conditions. To investigate the evolution characteristics of support strength required to maintain roadway stability throughout the post-peak deformation process, this study integrates physical similarity simulation, numerical simulation, and field monitoring. A novel laboratory testing system with adjustable support force and deformation was developed to simulate varying rock strength conditions. Three typical rock strength scenarios were reproduced using optimized gravel-sand-gypsum material ratios. Numerical simulations were conducted using a fractured rock mass flow model to capture post-peak mechanical behavior. Field tests in a deep coal roadway verified the deformation-support inter-action under long-term loading. The study identifies six distinct categories of support force-displacement relationships, including linear decline, negative exponential decay, and fluctuation-dominated responses. These findings provide a systematic understanding of roadway rock-support interaction mechanisms under large deformation, offering practical guidance for optimizing support design and ensuring long-term roadway stability in deep mining environments.

Introduction

The progressive deepening of modern coal mining operations has resulted in pronounced nonlinear and large deformation behaviors in the surrounding rock of roadways, particularly after the surrounding rock stress exceeds its peak strength1–8. The cumulative internal damage within the rock mass induces structural deterioration and time-dependent variations in mechanical parameters significantly impacting coal mine safety5,9–14. Therefore, studying the balance characteristics between post-peak large deformation surrounding rock and support is of significant theoretical importance and practical value for ensuring the long-term stability of roadways, optimizing support schemes and enhancing safety15–17.

Investigation into the balance characteristics and mechanisms of roadway surrounding rock–support interaction has been an enduring research focus8,17–22. Hoek23 building on the Kastner equation, established the relationship between deformation and bearing capacity during the elastic–plastic phases of surrounding rock deformation, and further proposed calculation formulas for support force and surrounding rock deformation at the elastic–plastic transition point. By integrating the longitudinal displacement curve with the ground reaction curve solution, he developed an analytical algorithm that accurately captured tunnel displacement and support pressure, with results consistent with 3D numerical simulations and field monitoring data24. Zhu21 conducted true triaxial model tests to examine the deformation and failure mechanisms of deep soft rock tunnels during excavation, as well as the stability of surrounding rock following support installation, and classified the deformation process into three stages: preimpact, rapid alteration, and stabilization. Zhou25 developed a mechanical model for tunnels with pre-reinforced rock, deriving equations for internal stress, displacement, and plastic zone radius of surrounding rock. This model clarified the interaction mechanism between support and surrounding rock, refined the 3D construction analysis scheme, and was validated through numerical simulations. Liu13 proposed a novel method to determine the key stability threshold of rock by establishing a coordinated deformation relationship between surrounding rock and support, thereby identifying the optimal timing for support installation. Xu and Xia26 introduced a two-stage method based on the generalized Hoek–Brown strength criterion to characterize yielding support in strain-softening rock masses. Yuan6 investigated a coupled support system comprising bolt–net–cable–grouting, which effectively mitigated axial stress in anchor rods, alleviated stress concentration in the surrounding rock, and reduced both surface and deep displacements. Zhou27 further analyzed the evolution of support load and surrounding rock damage, while subsequent studies28–30 explored load distribution and transfer mechanisms under progressive load-bearing degradation after support yielding. Collectively, these efforts have advanced the understanding of deformation mechanisms, stability criteria, and optimization of support timing. Nevertheless, the influence of support systems on roadway surrounding rock during the post-peak stage, when deformation intensifies—remains insufficiently explored. A unified theoretical framework capturing the support mechanism under such conditions is still lacking. Addressing this gap will not only elucidate the deformation patterns of surrounding rock under different geological and support conditions, but also provide a scientific basis for roadway support design, thereby fostering innovation and development in support technology31.

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the equilibrium characteristics between the surrounding rock and the support system that encompass the entire post-peak deformation process of the surrounding rock of roadway. In this study, by integrating physical similarity simulation, numerical modeling, and field monitoring, it establishes a multi-scale framework linking laboratory and field responses. six distinct categories of support–displacement relationships are proposed and validated through deep roadway tests, offering both theoretical insights and practical guidance for optimizing support design under large deformation conditions.

Physical similarity simulation test

Determination of physical simulation material ratio

To simulate the mechanical behavior of surrounding rock under post-peak large deformation, a series of similar material tests were conducted using mixtures of gravel (3–6 mm), sand (0.25–0.35 mm), gypsum, water, and borax. A total of 13 mixing ratios were tested (Table 1), with compressive strength ranging from 0.13 MPa to 4.72 MPa and densities from 1910 to 2186 kg/m³, effectively covering typical rock mass strength in deep coal formations.

In preparing the similar simulation material, each component is first weighed according to the specified mix ratio. Sand and gypsum are thoroughly mixed, and a thin layer of machine oil is applied to the gravel surface to prevent bonding with the sand, thereby simulating internal cracks within the rock mass. The gravel is then combined with the sand-gypsum mixture. Once evenly mixed, clean water containing borax is gradually added and stirred until the mixture becomes moldable. The mixture is then placed into standard molds and air-dried after shaping. Figure 1a shows the specimen prepared using the specified mixture ratio. Except for the first and second groups, all other groups include at least three specimens, ensuring sufficient sample size and reducing experimental error. Figure 1b, c display the specimen’s cross-section after compression failure, indicating that the gravel particles are appropriately scaled relative to the specimen size and that their surfaces remain largely free of adhering sand and gypsum post-failure.

The failure modes of the rock specimens are shown in Fig. 1d. Two distinct types of failure are observed: tensile failure and shear failure, reflecting variations in brittleness under different mixture ratios. When the compressive-to-tensile strength ratio is high, specimens exhibit the failure pattern, characterized by multiple cracks with failure surfaces nearly parallel to the loading axis—typical of tensile failure in hard rocks. In contrast, a lower compressive-to-tensile strength ratio results in the failure pattern, where the failure surface forms an angle of approximately 60° with the axial direction, indicating compressive-shear failure in line with the Mohr-Coulomb criterion. To clarify the effects of gravel, sand, and gypsum on the strength and density of similar mixture materials, cloud maps were generated to illustrate the relationships between strength, density, and the proportions of gravel and sand. A curve introduced by Chu7 was used to analyze the relationship between gypsum content and material strength. Comparative analysis was conducted to reveal the relative influence and trends of these factors. As shown in Fig. 2a, the cloud map of compressive strength against gravel and sand proportions indicates that the contour lines form angles of approximately 61° and 29° with the gravel and sand axes, respectively. This suggests that gravel has 1.8 times the influence on strength compared to sand (sin61°/sin29°). To achieve the greatest strength gradient, the optimal mass ratio of gravel to sand is 9:5. Overall, increasing the proportion of gravel and sand reduces the compressive strength. The compressive strength of analogous materials tends to decrease with increasing proportions of gravel and sand32, a general pattern consistent with the experimental results observed in this study. Figure 2b illustrates how material density changes with gravel and sand proportions. The density also increases, with gravel having a greater impact. The contour lines form angles of about 56° with the gravel axis and 34° with the sand axis, indicating that gravel’s effect on density is 1.5 times that of sand (sin56°/sin34°). To maximize the density gradient, a gravel-to-sand mass ratio of 3:2 should be maintained, corresponding to line AB in Fig. 2b. This trend aligns with previous findings on the influence of particle gradation on material density. Liu33 noted that increasing the gravel content significantly enhances the bulk density of the material, whereas sand primarily serves a filling function within a certain range, exerting a comparatively weaker effect on density improvement.

To represent different strength levels of the similar material, three typical proportion groups (1.24 MPa, 0.48 MPa, and 0.13 MPa) were selected from the 13 tested. These groups correspond to low, medium, and high compressive strengths, covering the full range of material behavior. This selection ensures that the numerical simulations reflect varying rock conditions and remain consistent with the experimental results.

The curves of compressive strength of specimens with sand and without sand as a function of gypsum ratio are shown in Fig. 3. Both curves show that the strength of compressive gradually increases with the gypsum content, following a parabolic trend. Under the same gypsum proportion, the strength of compressive of the sand-containing specimens is lower. This is attributed to the formation of a stable cementation structure by gypsum during consolidation, which strengthens interparticle bonding and enhances the overall compressive strength34. This phenomenon is consistent with previous research findings32,34,35. Thus, gravel, sand, and gypsum ratio materials were selected as the physical simulation materials for this research, with rock mass compressive strengths chosen as approximately 25 MPa, 10 MPa, and 2.5 MPa, a geometric similarity ratio of 1:20, and a similarity ratio of bulk density of 0.8. Therefore, similar materials with compressive strengths of 1.24 MPa, 0.48 MPa, 0.13 MPa, which were chosen for the test. The experimental findings revealed that the strength of Group 3 was 1.24 MPa, Group 9 was 0.48 MPa, and Group 13 was 0.13 MPa. Their respective ratios were: Gravel 67%, Sand 0%, Gypsum 33%; Gravel 46%, Sand 46%, Gypsum 8%; and Gravel 49%, Sand 49%, Gypsum 2% (Fig. 4).

Development of a test frame with adjustable support force and deformation capacity

In the study of the surrounding rock-support balance in roadways using physical models, one key device is the adjustable test support frame for support force and deformation. Separate frames were designed for circular and rectangular roadway cross-sections. The diameter of the circular roadway model support frame is 20 cm, while the span and height of the rectangular roadway model support frame are both 20 cm, with a frame length of 15 cm. The adjustable range is 50% of the roadway model’s dimensions, and the model’s support design strength ranges from 0 to 1.5 MPa. Under a stress similarity ratio of 1:20, the simulated engineering support strength range is 0 to 30 MPa. The support frame components include: round steel pipes, steel backplates, adjustment screws, pressure sensors, universal joints, and rulers. The frame diagram is shown in Fig. 7. For the support frame, the distance between the round steel pipe and the steel backplate can be controlled by adjusting the screw. This allows for the adjustment of the support frame’s size in roadway support, thereby accommodating tunnel deformation and displacement. The pressure sensors on the frame transmit the support force to the computer.

The support frame was developed to simulate the mechanical interaction between the surrounding rock and roadway support systems under controlled laboratory conditions, following the principles of geometric and mechanical similarity commonly adopted in physical modeling. The frame dimensions (span, height, and adjustable range) were based on the scaled dimensions of typical roadway sections. The design support strength range (0-1.5 MPa) was selected to correspond to the field support strength range of 0–30 MPa under a stress similarity ratio of 1:20, in line with established simulation practices6. The incorporation of pressure sensors and adjustable components was guided by the need to accurately replicate support displacement and loading conditions as observed in real engineering scenarios.

The physical simulation test plan is shown in Table 2, which comprehensively considers the tunnel morphology, rock mass structure layer characteristics, rock mass strength characteristics, and stress field characteristics. The roadway cross-section was designed as both circular and rectangular. The rock mass structure was considered in two types: a single homogeneous thick rock layer and a multi-layer thin rock layer structure. The rock mass strength characteristics were set at 25 MPa, 10 MPa and 2.5 MPa, while the rock mass stress field was chosen as 20 MPa (considering a burial depth of 800 m). The deformation exhibited by the surrounding rock along the roadway surface was controlled by gradually reducing the diameter or span of the support frame during the experiment. The support force required to maintain the balance of the roadway with the change of displacement was measured to obtain the law and characteristics of the surrounding rock-support balance.

Physical simulation process and results

The deformation and failure of the surrounding rock at different stages were recorded to understand the interplay between surrounding rock and support system. The corresponding support force was also measured in both similar and numerical simulation experiments.

The test steps are to first excavate the roadway, then put in the adjustable deformation bracket, and adjust the stress to zero and balance the bracket. After that, the top pressure and side pressure are applied to the model using oil pressure to simulate the original stress field. The experimental procedure begins with the removal of rock to form the roadway, followed by the installation of the adjustable deformation support frame. The stress is then zeroed and balanced. Afterward, oil pressure is applied to the model to simulate the original stress field by applying top and lateral pressures. Once the stress field stabilizes (the oil pressure in the cylinder is maintained, and the original stress field is considered stable once the pressure remains constant), the dimensions of the support frame are gradually reduced. The surface displacement of the roadway and the force of support are recorded once the support force stabilizes. At the same time, a camera is used to capture the structural response and damage evolution of the surrounding rock at the point where the displacement-support force relationship stabilizes. The dimensions of the support frame are then adjusted again, and this process is repeated to obtain the relationship between the surrounding rock deformation and the support force under different surrounding rock deformation levels.

The curve includes support force versus displacement for the roof, floor, left side and right side of the roadway, with green dashed lines representing the trendlines for all four curves. From the graph, it is evident that as the roadway deformation increases, the support force initially decreases gradually. The reduction becomes less pronounced once the decrease reaches a certain point. The rate of decrease in support force varies across the roof, floor and sides. The curves for the roof and floor are similar in shape, with matching volatility characteristics, while the curves for the two sides also exhibit similar patterns with corresponding volatility features. The underlying nature of these curve patterns results from the balanced forces at the top and bottom, as well as the balance of forces on both sides in the design of the support system.The four curves mainly exhibit two forms, which generally show the characteristics of first decreasing and then decaying in a wave shape. The fluctuations for the roof and floor are more significant than those for the sides. The support force on the side walls eventually diminishes to zero, while the roof requires a small residual support force to be maintained. In the post-peak stage, where the surrounding rock deformation exceeds the yielding limit of the support system, a distinct trend emerges: the support force of the roof and floor does not continue to decrease linearly but exhibits strong oscillatory behavior. This reflects repeated local failures and re-engagements of support elements as the rock mass undergoes progressive damage and fracture propagation. Such oscillatory weakening–recovery phenomena are typical in deep soft rock roadways under large deformation conditions and indicate a transitional stage between stable and unstable mechanical response. The sidewalls, in contrast, reach near-zero support force and stabilize, suggesting that post-peak instability is less significant laterally in this scheme. Numerical simulation test

The selection of numerical simulation materials

For numerical simulations in geotechnical engineering, the first consideration is the choice of computational theory, followed by the selection of model materials. Over the past 40 years, numerical analysis methods in rock mechanics have rapidly advanced, and many methods have been introduced. For fractured surrounding rock roadways, which exhibit characteristics of fragmentation and looseness, potential sliding and separation may occur along internal failure surfaces. Therefore, the chosen computational method must be capable of describing these features such as sliding, separation and collapse. Therefore, discrete element software that can describe the rotation and complete separation of blocks with large deformation is selected for analysis and calculation.

The core of numerical simulation material selection lies in the choice of constitutive models. In Discrete Element Method (DEM), there are two main categories of constitutive models: block constitutive models and interface constitutive models. A reasonable constitutive model should reflect the overall behavior of the material’s strength characteristics and deformation features, rather than being specific to a particular specimen.

The post-peak behavior of rock is governed by complex mechanical responses under external loading. As permanent deformation develops, the compressive strength exhibits a gradual reduction, reflecting the progressive degradation of load-bearing capacity. This evolution can be captured by variations in key strength parameters: cohesion decreases steadily due to the breakdown of cementation between mineral grains, while the internal friction angle increases as grain interlocking and sliding become more pronounced. Confining pressure plays a pivotal role by enhancing rock strength and suppressing crack propagation, thereby delaying failure. With increasing confining pressure, the stress–strain curve demonstrates a distinct transition—from brittle elasticity, dominated by sudden crack initiation, to strain softening associated with progressive damage, then to an ideal elastic–plastic stage characterized by stable deformation, and finally to strain hardening as compaction and grain rearrangement further improve resistance. The essence of these characteristics is the evolution of rock from rupture to internal fragmentation and flow during post-peak failure. Therefore, in establishing a model that describes these rock characteristics, the FRMF (FRACTURED ROCKMASS FLOW) model is defined to represent the deformation and strength characteristics that arise due to the flow resulting from fragmentation after peak failure. In this study, the block constitutive model adopts the FRMF model, which is capable of describing the post-peak strength degradation characteristics and the brittle-ductile transition of the rock mass with increasing confining pressure. The joint model utilizes an ideal elastoplastic interface constitutive model.

Field observation of surrounding rock-support balance characteristics

Engineering background

To further verify the applicability and accuracy of the numerical simulation results, field observations were conducted under actual engineering conditions. While the numerical models provide insight into the mechanical responses of surrounding rock-support systems under controlled parameter variations, they cannot fully capture the complexity of in-situ geological environments. Therefore, a series of field monitoring practices were presented to aim at observing the deformation characteristics of surrounding rock and the performance of support structures in real roadway conditions. This actual validation not only complements the simulation findings but also enhances the reliability and engineering relevance of the proposed support strategies. Thus, a roadway with high in-situ stress and relatively fractured rock mass was selected to observe a comprehensive equilibrium pattern between the surrounding rock and support during roadway support. The 502 roadway of Danning Coal Mine is characterized by fractured surrounding rock, significant mining disturbance and large deformation. It meets the basic conditions required for the experimental observation roadway. Therefore, the 502 roadway was selected as the test roadway.

Roadway 502 is the drift between working faces 104 and 105 of the #3 coal seam at the Danning Coal Mine. Three drifts are arranged between the working faces 104 and 105. As the 104 working face was mined, the 501 roadway was lost to the mined-out area. When the 105 working face was mined, the 503 roadway disappeared. The #3 coal seam is a nearly horizontal coal seam with a thickness of approximately 4.5 m. The 502 roadway has an excavation height of 3.7 m and a width of 5.5 m. It serves as the return air roadway for working face 105. To ensure its stability during mining and to maintain the required roadway cross-section for ventilation, the design pier column support to reinforce the roadway’s stability. The engineering in-situ diagram of the pier column support is shown in Fig. 22.

The observation section was located in an area where the workfaces on both sides had already been mined, thus avoiding the influence of mining activity. Pier column support was arranged along the centerline of the 502 roadway, with a center-to-center spacing of 2.0 m between pier columns. The pier columns were installed using templates that abutted the roadway roof and floor. Each pier column had a diameter of 900 mm and the design included a 5 mm steel plate wrapping on the exterior, with an internal mix of concrete and pearl cotton. Nine observation stations were set up at different positions along the roadway. Four pressure boxes are set under each pier column, and steel plates (900 × 900 × 5–10 mm) are placed above and below the pressure boxes to ensure proper alignment between the pressure boxes, pier columns and steel plates.

The mechanical properties of pier column

The mechanical properties of the pier column directly impact the support performance of the roadway. According to the principle of the combined action of surrounding rock and support, the relationship between the load-deformation characteristic curve of the pier column and the surrounding rock deformation characteristics determines the deformation of the roadway cross-section. Compression tests were conducted using a large hydraulic servo pressure testing machine at the Structural Engineering Laboratory of Taiyuan University of Technology. The deformation and load curves of the pier column were obtained from these tests. The pier column and test loading device are shown in Fig. 23.

Fig. 23Pier column sample and loading equipment in test.

Pier column sample and loading equipment in test.

Two specimens (pier columns) were tested in this experiment and the results are shown in Fig. 24. The load-deformation curves of both pier columns are similar in shape. The load initially increases gradually with deformation, followed by fluctuating bearing characteristics. This behavior of the pier columns is attributed to the internal filling material, which consists of concrete and pearl cotton. Concrete exhibits strong compressive properties, while pearl cotton has a low stiffness. The combination of these materials imparts significant deformation adaptability and considerable load-bearing capacity. As the pier column undergoes axial deformation in practical engineering, the surrounding steel skin wrapping deforms to accommodate the compression of the internal material. The internal material continues to deform during compression and its load-bearing capacity fluctuates with the compression. The column maintains a certain load-bearing capacity under large deformation conditions, with the maximum deformation exceeding half of the pier column’s length. The pier column also exhibits time-dependent deformation when the load exceeds its strength limit, a feature known as creep. This creep manifests in two forms: creep and stress relaxation. Owing to the limited service life of the roadway, the cross-sectional shrinkage resulting from creep deformation has a negligible impact on the 502 roadway, which functions as an auxiliary ventilation passage. The relationship between roadway deformation and support force can be determined by controlling the deformation amount during the use of pier column support in the roadway.

The relationship characteristic curve between the pier column support strength (calculated by converting the pier column support force to the average support force per unit top plate area) and the roadway roof and floor convergence was obtained through long-term observation of the data from nine observation stations. The pier columns at the nine observation points exhibited buckling (wrinkling of the steel sheet), indicating that the load-bearing capacity of the pier columns had reached its limit and a rheological phenomenon occurred. This resulted in the feature of the pier columns automatically controlling the roadway roof and floor displacement over time.

The observation results reveal that the load-displacement characteristics of the pier columns at all measurement points are essentially the same. The support strength increases rapidly with the increase in roadway roof and floor convergence, followed by fluctuating changes in support strength as the convergence continues. The support strength exhibits a linear growth pattern during the initial phase of increase. Due to immediate deformation following roadway excavation, the roadway has already deformed when the pier column support is installed, and the pier columns do not provide sufficient support force to the top and bottom immediately after installation. The contact between the roof and floor and the pier column gradually becomes compressed as time progresses, causing the support force to continuously increase. Once the support force exceeds the pier column’s bearing capacity limit, the pier column undergoes sustained deformation and exhibits continuous fluctuating load-bearing characteristics. The support strength of the pier column increases linearly with the initial changes in the roadway floor and roof, does not represent the inherent characteristics of the surrounding rock support force and displacement relationship. Only when the contact between the pier column and the roof and floor reaches equilibrium can the relationship between support force and displacement reflect the support characteristics of the surrounding rock in the roadway. The surrounding rock–support interaction in the roadway is primarily manifested in three types: post-peak horizontal fluctuation, attenuation fluctuation, and increasing fluctuation.

The post-peak stage observed in these field measurements reflects the complex and nonlinear interaction between the surrounding rock and the support system under sustained and large deformations. After the initial peak support strength is reached, the system does not simply exhibit a steady weakening but often exhibits oscillatory or fluctuating behaviors caused by progressive damage, micro-cracking, and local instabilities within the rock mass and support elements. These fluctuations indicate the redistribution of load paths and intermittent engagement of support components as the rock mass undergoes rearrangement and partial failure. The presence of rheological deformation in the pier columns further emphasizes the time-dependent nature of the post-peak response, where creep and gradual yielding influence the stability of the roadway.

Importantly, a composite pier made of concrete and pearl cotton was adopted as a semi-flexible boundary to simulate the progressive resistance behavior of roadway support. This design allows gradual load transfer and better reflects the interaction with surrounding rock compared to rigid supports. To minimize material-induced artifacts such as stratification and compression-related fluctuations, a constant low loading rate was applied, preloading cycles were introduced to stabilize the pier, and the load–deformation data were smoothed to remove high-frequency noise. The observed support response is interpreted as a relative indicator of interaction strength for comparative analysis. In future studies, more homogeneous and instrumented support elements will be considered to enhance simulation accuracy.

The field observations confirm many of the trends and failure modes identified in the controlled experiments and simulations. Specifically, the deformation and failure patterns observed in the surrounding rock, as well as the performance of the support system, align closely with the previous results. These findings not only validate the simulation models but also demonstrate that the laboratory-based results are applicable in real-world scenarios. The consistency between field observations and previous findings reinforces the robustness of the study and provides a practical basis for the further application of similar materials and support systems in future geotechnical projects.

The results of the numerical simulations show strong consistency with the findings from the physical similarity material experiments. Both approaches reveal similar trends in the influence of gravel, sand, and gypsum proportions on the compressive strength and deformation behavior of the material. In particular, the numerical models reproduced the observed failure modes—tensile splitting under high strength ratios and shear failure under low strength ratios—corresponding closely to those captured in the laboratory tests. Additionally, the simulated stress distributions and displacement fields align with the mechanical responses recorded in the experiments, reinforcing the validity of the material constitutive assumptions and boundary conditions used in the simulation. This cross-validation between physical and numerical methods provides robust evidence for the reliability of the modeling framework and supports its application in evaluating the mechanical behavior of surrounding rock-support systems under varying geological conditions.

Discussion

The evolution of internal stress within the surrounding rock is governed by its loading conditions and structural transformation. Stress serves as an indicator of the mechanical state and bearing capacity of the roadway’s surrounding rock, thereby providing an indirect measure of its stability. Distinct stress characteristics are exhibited at different deformation stages. A thorough understanding of the stress evolution throughout these stages is essential for elucidating the underlying mechanisms of roadway stability. Accordingly, the stress evolution characteristics under varying stability conditions are analyzed separately.

The roadway surrounding rock itself is incapable of maintaining stability

Under elastic and elastoplastic states of the surrounding rock, the roadway gradually approaches stability as the surface displacement of the surrounding rock increases. However, in practical engineering, many roadways experience cracking and even fragmentation of the surrounding rock as the plastic zone expands, rendering the rock incapable of maintaining stability autonomously. Such phenomena are attributed to the presence of fractures or weak planes within the rock mass. Consequently, these stress jump points serve as critical indicators for assessing fracture initiation and compaction.

Shear stress above the roof increases gradually from zero with rising displacement, similar to the behavior in the rib shear stress, and shows segmented fluctuations once displacement surpasses a critical value. Conclusion

This study investigates the mechanical behavior of similar simulation materials composed of gravel, sand, gypsum, and water, focusing on how variations in material proportions influence compressive strength, density, and failure modes. Based on systematic physical experiments and numerical simulations, the following conclusions can be drawn:

In the similarity simulation, the optimal material ratio for achieving the highest strength gradient was identified as a gravel-to-sand mass ratio of 9:5. The original rock stress intensity and the strength of surrounding rock are the primary factors determining the surrounding rock-support balance characteristics of the roadway. The relative magnitudes of these two parameters determine the intrinsic stability characteristics of the roadway. The relative magnitudes of these two parameters determine the intrinsic stability characteristics of the roadway. As roadway deformation increases, the support force initially decreases gradually; upon reaching a certain threshold, it begins to decline in a fluctuating manner.

Numerical simulation results indicate that the support strength of both the roadway roof and floor initially decreases continuously with surrounding rock deformation, followed by a stage of fluctuating decline, characterized by pronounced amplitude variations.

Field observations reveal that the load–displacement characteristics of the pier columns at various measurement points are generally consistent, all exhibiting a rapid increase in support strength as the roadway roof and floor converge. Subsequently, the support strength undergoes fluctuating variations as convergence continues, with the initial phase of increase displaying a linear growth trend. The support force required to maintain the stability of the roadway surrounding rock as the displacement of the surrounding rock surface increases can be divided into six categories: linear decreasing, negative exponential, fluctuating decreasing, fluctuating stable, fluctuating increasing and nearly horizontal constant.

The equilibrium law governing the interaction between the roadway surrounding rock and the support exhibits distinct stage-wise characteristics. With the increase in surface displacement of the surrounding rock, the radial stress exhibits a decreasing trend, while the tangential stress shows an increasing trend. Both radial and tangential stresses are symmetrically distributed along the radial direction relative to the line of zero displacement.
