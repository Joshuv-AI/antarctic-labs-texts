Impact of anatomical adaptation in cemented femoral stems on mechanical properties and alignment
Authors: Tomohiro Shimizu, Tetsuro Oue, Tatsuhiro Ishijima, Michihiro Fujiwara, Yoshihiro Hasebe, Keita Uetsuki, Norimasa Iwasaki, Daisuke Takahashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolished tapered femoral stems are widely used in total hip arthroplasty (THA) for their excellent long-term outcomes. However, anatomical mismatches in Asian populations increase the risk of periprosthetic femoral fractures and malalignment. This study evaluated the mechanical properties and radiographic alignment of the VLIAN stem—a cemented, polished, double-tapered stem with a rounded shoulder design developed for Asian femoral anatomy—compared to the Exeter stem. Mechanical testing assessed rotational resistance and stiffness using cobalt-chromium stems cemented into custom jigs and composite femur models. Rotational torque and load-displacement curves were analyzed. For clinical evaluation, 654 Japanese patients undergoing primary cemented THA were retrospectively reviewed: 275 received the Exeter stem (2013–2018) and 379 the VLIAN stem (2018–2023). Radiographs were analyzed for stem alignment, Dorr classification, and femoral neck-shaft angle. Results showed no significant difference in rotational resistance or construct stiffness between designs. Radiographic analysis demonstrated significantly smaller varus angles with the VLIAN stem, particularly in Dorr type C femurs and those with lower neck-shaft angles. These findings suggest that anatomical design modifications, such as a rounded shoulder, improve alignment without compromising mechanical strength. Introduction

Polished tapered femoral stems are widely used in total hip arthroplasty (THA) because of their favorable long-term outcomes, including low revision rates and high survival rates1,2. Compared to matt-finished and cementless stems, polished tapered stems demonstrate significantly lower revision rates and improved patient satisfaction, with excellent functional outcomes2–5. However, these stems are associated with a higher risk of periprosthetic femoral fractures (PFF), particularly when compared with anatomical and straight stems6–8. Among these, the femoral stem of the CPT hip system (Zimmer Biomet Holdings, Indiana, USA) has been identified as having a notably higher risk of PFF, as evidenced by both clinical data and biomechanical studies9–11.

Factors such as stem alignment (e.g., varus or valgus positioning) and anteversion mismatches are critical in influencing the risk of PFF12–14. Appropriate sizing, alignment, and surgical techniques are essential for mitigating these risks. Certain stem designs, including CPT, short cement stems, and single-wedge stems, have been linked to higher fracture rates, emphasizing the importance of meticulous implant selection15–17.

Anatomical differences between Asians and Westerners, such as variations in anteroposterior diameter, femoral neck anteversion, femoral isthmus diameter, and femoral neck length, play a significant role in orthopedic implant design18–21. While the Exeter stem (Stryker, Newbury, UK) offers excellent long-term outcomes with low revision rates and high survivorship22,23, its design, tailored to Western anatomy, may not be ideal for Asian populations. To address this gap, the VLIAN stem (Teijin Nakashima Medical, Okayama, Japan) was developed by incorporating a rounded shoulder design based on the Exeter concept and unique anatomical characteristics of the Japanese femur24,25.

We hypothesized that the VLIAN stem, with a rounded shoulder developed to match Asian femoral morphology, would achieve superior radiographic alignment compared with the Exeter stem without compromising mechanical strength. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of this rounded shoulder design on mechanical properties and radiographic alignment in Japanese patients undergoing cemented THA, through a direct comparison between the VLIAN and Exeter stems to clarify whether such anatomical modifications improve alignment and may help reduce complications such as periprosthetic femoral fractures.

Methods

Study 1 mechanical property comparison

This mechanical study compared the performance of femoral stems with a rounded shoulder (Type A) and without a rounded shoulder (Type B). (Fig. 1A). Both stems were fabricated from a polished cobalt-chromium alloy for cemented implantation, incorporating a double-taper design to enhance primary stability. The narrowest size (45#1, stem length 148 mm, offset 45 mm) was selected for testing, representing the lowest expected rotational resistance.

(A) Stem design A type: Round shoulder type. B type: Non-round shoulder type. (B) Rotational resistance test. The stem is fixed in a jig, pushed in with a force of 600 N, and then rotated once every second. (C) Saw Bone with cement-fixed A type and B type stems. (D) Mechanical test using Saw Bone. Force is applied to the head of the bone at a speed of 10 mm per minute.

For each group, three specimens (n = 3) were tested. The stems were cemented into a custom installation jig (Fig. 1B) using Surgical Simplex cement (Stryker Orthopedics, Mahwah, NJ, USA) and mounted on a mechanical testing machine (Mini Bionix; MTS Japan, Tokyo, Japan). The stem axis was aligned with the machine’s rotational axis. A vertical load of 600 N was applied to the femoral head, and rotational torque was applied at a rate of 1°/s, up to a maximum of 150 Nm, to assess rotational resistance.

Additionally, stems were inserted into a composite saw bone femur model to simulate clinical conditions (Fig. 1C). The medullary cavity was precisely machined to match the stem geometry, and stems were cemented similarly. The assembly was mounted on a servo-hydraulic testing machine (SERVOPULSER, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with a custom fixture. A compressive load was applied to the femoral head at 10 mm/min (Fig. 1D), and construct stiffness was calculated from the load-displacement curve within the 0–500 N range.

Study 2 radiographic outcome comparison

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Approval #021–0152) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Medical records from February 2013 to January 2023 identified 654 patients who underwent primary THA via the posterolateral approach. From 2013 to 2018, the Exeter stem was used for patients with Crowe type I hips (n = 275, mean age 61.1 ± 14.5 years). From 2018 to 2023, the VLIAN stem was used in a comparable population (n = 379, mean age 63.8 ± 13.6 years). All surgeries were performed by experienced hip surgeons with more than 10 years of clinical experience. All procedures were performed via a posterolateral approach with a standardized third-generation cementing technique. The same technique was consistently applied across all cases, including those with Dorr type A femurs26, without variation in indications.

Patient baseline characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, diagnosis, and bone morphology, were reviewed and statistically compared between the two groups to confirm comparability. Standardized anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs were obtained preoperatively and two weeks postoperatively with consistent beam positioning and penetration settings. Radiographic evaluations included Dorr femoral bone classification26, femoral neck-shaft angle measurement, and stem alignment analysis (Supplementary Figure S1)27. Radiographic measurements were independently performed by two orthopedic surgeons (first and second authors). Intra- and inter-observer reliabilities were excellent: for Dorr classification, intra-observer κ = 0.923 and inter-observer κ = 0.913; for angular measurements, intra-observer ICC = 0.875 and inter-observer ICC = 0.821. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistics analyses

Sample size calculations were based on previous studies28, targeting a total sample of 404 hips to achieve 80% power with an alpha of 0.05 and effect size of 0.8. Given subgroup analysis needs, the limitations of power in certain categories (e.g., Dorr type C) were acknowledged. Comparisons of baseline characteristics between the two groups were performed using unpaired t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Comparisons of mechanical performance and radiographic outcomes between the stem types were conducted using unpaired t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and Tukey’s post-hoc tests where appropriate. Analyses were performed using JMP Pro version 17.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Study 1 mechanical testing

In the torsional resistance test, all specimens withstood the maximum applied torque of 150 Nm without visible cracks or deformation in the cement, stem, or jig (Fig. 2A). The mean torsion angle at 150 Nm did not differ significantly between the rounded shoulder (Type A) and non-rounded shoulder (Type B) stems (p = 0.441) (Fig. 2B). This indicates that the rounded shoulder design does not adversely affect rotational resistance.

(A) Results of the torsion test. X-axis torsion angle, Y-axis torque. (B) Comparison of rotation angle at 150 N.m. (C) Load displacement curve. X-axis displacement, Y-axis load. (D) Comparison of stiffness.

The load-displacement curves in the simulated bone model showed a consistent trend for both stem types, with an initial increase followed by a stable slope (Fig. 2C). Rigidity, defined as the slope of the load-displacement curve from 0 to 500 N, also showed no significant difference between Type A and Type B stems (p = 0.790) (Fig. 2D). This confirms that the mechanical stiffness of the construct was not influenced by the shoulder design.

Radiographic comparison of stem alignment revealed that the VLIAN stem achieved significantly smaller varus angles than the Exeter stem across all Dorr classifications (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). Specifically, the VLIAN stem demonstrated better alignment in the more anatomically challenging Dorr type C femurs, where the risk of varus malposition is known to be higher. When alignment was analyzed by Dorr classification, the VLIAN stem showed significantly less valgus alignment in Dorr type A compared to Dorr types B and C (Fig. 3B), suggesting that anatomical variations still influence stem positioning even with design improvements.

(A) Comparisons of the average varus angle between stems according to the shape of the medullary cavity. Data show mean (95% CI). (B) Comparisons of the average varus angle between the shapes of the medullary cavity in each stem. Data show mean (95% CI).

Subgroup analysis according to femoral neck-shaft angle demonstrated consistent differences between the two stem types (Fig. 4A). In femurs with a neck-shaft angle < 125°, the mean varus angle was 0.44° lower in the VLIAN group compared with the Exeter group (95% CI − 0.14 to 1.01, p = 0.133), indicating no significant difference. In the 125–135° group, the mean varus angle was 0.68° lower in the VLIAN group (95% CI 0.40 to 0.96, p < 0.0001), and in the > 135° group, the mean varus angle was 0.50° lower in the VLIAN group (95% CI 0.03 to 0.96, p = 0.037). In subgroup analyses within each stem type (Fig. 4B), the VLIAN stem showed significantly greater varus alignment in femurs with a neck-shaft angle < 125° compared with those with > 135° (mean difference 0.55°, 95% CI 0.02 to 1.08, p = 0.040 by Tukey’s test). Other comparisons among neck-shaft angle subgroups were not significant in the VLIAN group. In the Exeter group, no significant differences were observed among subgroups (< 125° vs. 125–135°, p = 0.999; <125° vs. >135°, p = 0.236; 125–135° vs. >135°, p = 0.062), although there was a trend toward greater varus alignment in femurs with smaller neck-shaft angles.

Overall, the VLIAN stem demonstrated superior radiographic alignment compared to the Exeter stem, particularly in challenging anatomical conditions such as Dorr type C and lower neck-shaft angles, without compromising mechanical performance.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide important insights into the mechanical and clinical performance of femoral stems with a rounded shoulder design specifically tailored for Asian anatomical features. Although polished tapered femoral stems are widely recognized for their excellent long-term results, their design, which is largely based on Western anatomical data, presents a significant risk of malalignment when used in Asians with smaller builds. Mechanical evaluation confirmed that stems with a rounded shoulder design achieved rotational resistance and rigidity comparable to those without this feature, indicating that these anatomical adaptations do not compromise the structural integrity.

Radiographic analysis demonstrated the superiority of the VLIAN stem in achieving proper alignment, particularly in narrow medullary cavities and in patients with smaller neck-shaft angles. Consistent with previous reports, Dorr type A and B femurs generally demonstrated better alignment than type C femurs29. The varus alignment observed in Dorr type C femurs can be attributed to the medial overhang of the greater trochanter, as reported in prior study30. Furthermore, the study confirmed that smaller femoral neck axis angles increased the likelihood of varus insertion, corroborating earlier findings31–34.

However, neutral stem insertion remains a widely accepted goal because no adverse outcomes have been associated with this alignment. Different femoral stem designs, such as the metaphyseal anchoring of short stems, anatomic stems, and straight stems, influence the accuracy of insertion angles and postoperative outcomes in THA35–37. While studies comparing cemented stem insertion alignment across designs are limited, this study demonstrated that improving the shoulder roundness of the stem significantly enhanced insertion alignment. Although these findings were based on immediate postoperative radiographic evaluations, further research is required to assess their long-term clinical implications, including patient satisfaction and functional outcomes.

Limitations

Given the absence of significant changes in mechanical strength, these findings suggest that cemented stems with a rounded shoulder design may be beneficial for femoral anatomy with a higher risk of varus insertion, such as those with Dorr type C or a lower neck-shaft angle. However, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the clinical analysis was focused exclusively on the Japanese population, limiting the generalizability of the results to non-Asian populations with differing femoral anatomies. Second, radiographic outcomes were evaluated only in the immediate postoperative period, leaving long-term clinical implications such as functional outcomes and implant survivorship unexplored. Third, biomechanical testing was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions using a single implant size, which may not fully represent the in vivo conditions or the performance of other sizes in the series. Fourth, the retrospective design also introduces the potential for selection bias and incomplete data. Subtle changes in surgical technique over time cannot be completely excluded. However, all procedures were performed at a single institution by experienced hip surgeons using a consistent posterolateral approach and third-generation cementing technique. Moreover, the improvement in alignment with the VLIAN stem was systematically observed in anatomically challenging cases, such as Dorr type C femurs and those with small neck-shaft angles, suggesting that the primary factor was the difference in stem design rather than temporal changes in operative skill. Fifth, subgroup analysis revealed that while the sample size was adequate overall, the number of Dorr type C cases was smaller, which may limit statistical power in detecting subtle differences within this high-risk group. Finally, patient-reported outcomes, such as satisfaction and pain relief, were not included, limiting the ability of the study to assess the implant’s overall clinical utility.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the mechanical properties and clinical utility of a VLIAN femoral stem designed with a rounded shoulder to suit Asian anatomical characteristics. The VLIAN stem demonstrated superior alignment compared with the Exeter stem, particularly in Dorr type C and among patients with smaller neck-shaft angles, without compromising rotational resistance or rigidity. These results emphasize the importance of tailoring implant designs to population-specific anatomical features to reduce the risk of malalignment. Although immediate postoperative radiographic outcomes are promising, further studies are needed to explore their long-term clinical implications, including functional outcomes and patient satisfaction.
