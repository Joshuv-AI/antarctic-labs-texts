Hilbert space models and Blaschke frames
Connor Evans

Abstract
. For a finite Blaschke product B B and for an isometry V V on an infinite-dimensional separable complex Hilbert space ℋ \mathcal{H} we study a sequence ( b m ) m = 1 ∞ (b_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty} of vectors in ℋ \mathcal{H} , defined by b m = B ⁡ ( V ∗ ) ​ e m b_{m}=B(V^{*})e_{m} , where ( e m ) m = 1 ∞ (e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty} is an orthonormal basis in ℋ \mathcal{H} . We call ( b m ) m = 1 ∞ (b_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty} a Blaschke frame for B B with isometry V V on ℋ \mathcal{H} . We show how instrumental the use of Hilbert space models are in frame theory by completely solving the question of redundancy for a Blaschke frame, that is, what vectors can be removed from the frame ( b m ) m = 1 ∞ (b_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty} such that ( b m ) m ≠ k (b_{m})_{m\neq k} is still a frame? Using the Wold decomposition, we prove that a Blaschke frame can have no redundant vectors (a Riesz basis), have some redundant vectors, or every vector is redundant (a fully insured frame). These unique cases depend on the choice of finite Blaschke product and which isometry one chooses in the construction of a Blaschke frame.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we show that the use of Hilbert space models is invaluable in solving the redundancy problem for a particular family of frames that we call a
Blaschke frame
. Recall, a sequence
(x_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
of elements in an infinite-dimensional separable complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a
frame
for
\mathcal{H}
if there exists constants
A,B>0
such that

A\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lvert\langle f,x_{m}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\rvert^{2}\leq B\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

(1.1)

for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
. Corresponding to a finite Blaschke product
B
of degree
d
and an isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
, we define a
Blaschke frame for B with isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
, or just a
Blaschke frame on
\mathcal{H}
, to be any sequence of vectors
(b_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{H}
expressible in the form

b_{m}=B(V^{*})e_{m}

(1.2)

for
m=1,2,\ldots
and some orthonormal basis
(e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
of
\mathcal{H}
. Since the spectrum of
V^{*}
is contained in the closed unit disc
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and
B
is holomorphic on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
,
B(V^{*}):\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is defined by means of the Riesz-Dunford or the rational functional calculus for operators, Definition
2.2
. The operator
B(V^{*})
is a bounded, surjective operator, Proposition
3.2
. It is required that
B(V^{*})
be surjective. This is due to the fact that every Hilbert space frame is of the form
(Te_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
where
T:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is a bounded, surjective operator
[
11
, Theorem 5.5.4]
.

Frames were first introduced in the seminal paper of Duffin and Schaeffer in
[
12
]
to study non-harmonic Fourier series. Since then, countless subjects have benefitted from frame theory, such as advances in quantum mechanics from Balazs and Teofanov
[
8
]
, multiresolution analysis in Benedetto and Li
[
9
]
, analogue-digital conversion in sampling theory with Eldar
[
13
]
, and in signal processing by Mallat
[
16
]
. The
redundancy problem
for a frame is the reason why frame theory has become increasingly important in recent years, most notably in the development of internet communications and even its use in modelling the brain. For instance, how can we recover an entire data file even if some of the data has become partially corrupted? How can the brain retain memory even though neurons die and regenerate? Loosely, if
(x_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is a frame for a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, then
\overline{\mathrm{Span}}(x_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}=\mathcal{H}
, but deleting a vector
x_{k}
from the frame may still leave us with
\overline{\mathrm{Span}}(x_{m})_{m\neq k}^{\infty}=\mathcal{H}
. When this situation occurs, we call such a vector
x_{k}
a
redundant vector
. A vector that is not redundant is termed an
essential vector
. We state a concrete test to find the redundant and essential vectors of a frame, originally given by Christensen in
[
11
, Theorem 5.4.7]
, which we state for the readers convenience as Theorem
4.6
. We can think of a frame as an over-complete basis with insurance against loss of information.

It will be our main focus to find the redundant vectors for a Blaschke frame, Section
4
. For many frames, this is typically not an easy task. Instead, we circumvent this problem by utilising
Hilbert space models
. Recall that the Schur class on the unit disc,
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{D})
, is the set of holomorphic functions
f:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}|f(z)|\leq 1
, where
\mathbb{D}
is the unit disc in
\mathbb{C}
. We say that a
model
for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{D})
is a pair
(\mathcal{M},u)
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a separable Hilbert space and
u:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
is a mapping such that

1-\overline{f(w)}f(z)=\Big\langle(1-\overline{w}z)u(z),u(w)\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{M}}

for all
w,\,z\in\mathbb{D}
,
[
5
, Definition 2.7]
. Models are extremely useful tools to study function theoretic properties in terms of Hilbert space geometry. For example, there exists a model formula for functions defined on the polydisc,
\mathbb{D}^{d}
, first discovered by Agler in
[
1
]
, from which they can be used to study boundary point singularities and directional derivatives in the work of Agler, Evans, Lykova and Young in
[
2
]
. This was based on earlier investigations by Agler, Tully-Doyle and Young
[
6
]
, in the case of the bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. Models have even been defined for functions on elliptical domains in the paper by Agler, Lykova and Young
[
3
]
, to study operators with numerical range in an ellipse
[
4
]
. A very special domain for which a model formula has also been defined is the
symmetrized bidisc
. This was discussed in the paper by Agler and Young in
[
7
]
. We will require a generalisation of this domain, namely the
symmetrized polydisc
, in Definition
2.4
.

Finite Blaschke products are functions of the form

B(z)=\tau\prod_{i=1}^{d}\dfrac{z+\lambda_{i}}{1+\overline{\lambda_{i}}z}

(1.3)

for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
, where
\tau\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{D}
for
i=1,\ldots,d
. Since finite Blaschke products are in the Schur class on the unit disc and every function
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{D})
has a model
[
5
, Theorem 2.9]
, finite Blaschke products must also have a model.

Lemma 1.1
.

[
5
, Lemma 2.47]

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{D}
and let
B
be a finite Blaschke product on
\mathbb{D}
defined by equation (
1.3
). For
j=1,\ldots,d
, let

E_{j}(z):=\dfrac{(1-\lvert\lambda_{j}\rvert^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{1+\overline{\lambda_{j}}z}\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}\dfrac{z+\lambda_{k}}{1+\overline{\lambda_{k}}z}.

(1.4)

for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. The functions
E_{1},\ldots,E_{d}
are orthonormal in
\mathrm{H}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, the Hardy space on the disc, and, for all
z,w\in\mathbb{D}
,

1-\overline{B(w)}B(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\overline{E_{j}(w)}(1-\overline{w}z)E_{j}(z).

(1.5)

The functions
(E_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
defined by equation (
1.4
) are known as the
Malmquist-Takenaka
functions corresponding to the finite Blaschke product
B
, as seen in Malmquist
[
17
]
and Takenaka
[
20
]
. By equation (
1.5
), the pair
(\mathcal{M},u)
with
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
u(z)=(E_{1}(z),\ldots,E_{d}(z))
, is a model for
B
, that is, for all
z,w\in\mathbb{D}
,

1-\overline{B(w)}B(z)=\Big\langle(1-\overline{w}z)u(z),u(w)\Big\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}.

Note that this formula can be extended to all
z,w\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, since
B
, as well as the Malmquist-Takenaka functions corresponding to
B
, are well-defined on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Exploiting models of finite Blaschke products gives us our two main results, Theorem
4.5
and Theorem
4.7
, which we state next. Although Theorem
4.5
is somewhat simple, its importance cannot be understated.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
(E_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
be the Malmquist-Takenaka functions corresponding to a finite Blaschke product
B
. For every vector
b_{m}
in a Blaschke frame for
B
with isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
for
m=1,2,\ldots
, there exists a positive integer
s
such that

\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=1-\dfrac{1}{\big((s-1)!\big)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Bigg\lvert\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\Bigg\rvert^{2}.

From the above result, the norm of each vector
b_{m}
for
m=1,2,\ldots,
in a Blaschke frame determines whether it is a redundant or an essential vector. We use this to derive a redundancy criterion for any Blaschke frame, which is the second main result we state here. This is Theorem
4.7
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
(E_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
be the Malmquist-Takenaka functions corresponding to a finite Blaschke product
B
. For every vector
b_{k}
in a Blaschke frame for
B
with isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
for
k=1,2,\ldots
, there exists a positive integer
s
such that:

(1)

If
\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}=0
for
j=1,\ldots,d
, then
b_{k}
is essential;

(2)

If
\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\neq 0
for at least one
j=1,\ldots,d
, then
b_{k}
is redundant.

The choice of finite Blaschke product and isometry
V
influences the outcome of the redundancy criterion for a Blaschke frame. This can be summarised in three distinct cases as follows:

(1)

A Blaschke frame for any finite Blaschke product with a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
is a Riesz basis (Proposition
4.8
);

(2)

A Blaschke frame for
z^{d}
with a pure isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
has redundant vectors (Proposition
4.10
);

(3)

If
B
is a finite Blaschke product with at least one non-zero root, then every vector in a Blaschke frame for
B
with a pure isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
is a redundant vector (Theorem
4.11
).

By a pure isometry
V\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, where
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the set of bounded linear operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, we take to mean an isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
such that there is no non-trivial reducing subspace
\mathcal{K}
for
V
such that
V|_{\mathcal{K}}
is unitary
[
14
, Section 3]
. Equivalently, the unitary part in the Wold decomposition, Theorem
4.4
, is trivial. Note that all of the Hilbert spaces considered here will be infinite-dimensional separable and complex, unless explicitly stated.

After giving the gist of the paper with this section, we prove a few results regarding the invertibility of operators and Riesz bases, Lemma

, that is, it is a frame such that the frame bounds

2.
A Riesz basis and dual Riesz basis

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
T
be a bounded invertible operator on
\mathcal{H}
. A
Riesz basis
for
\mathcal{H}
is a sequence of the form
(Te_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
where
(e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}

[
11
, Definition 3.6.1]
. A Riesz basis is indeed a basis and it is also a special type of frame, it is a frame which is
exact
, that is, every vector in the frame is essential, the removal of any element of the frame means we no longer have a frame. From Christensen
[
11
, Theorem 7.11]
, a sequence
(x_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is an exact frame if and only if
(x_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is a Riesz basis. Hence, if
(Te_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}=(x_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is a Riesz basis, then there exist constants
A,B>0
such that equation (
1.1
) is satisfied. Moreover, the largest possible value for the constant
A
and smallest possible value for the constant
B
are

\|T^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}^{-2}~~\text{and}~~\|T\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}^{2}

respectively, see
[
11
, Proposition 3.6.4]
. This is a consequence of the following result.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
15
, Page 30]

Suppose that
T
is a bounded invertible operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that
Te_{m}=x_{m}
for
m=1,2,\ldots
, where
(e_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis. Then

\|T^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{B(H)}}^{-1}\leq\|x_{m}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\|T\|_{\mathcal{B(H)}}~~\text{for all}~m=1,2,\ldots.

(2.1)

For the readers convenience, we describe the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus. Recall that the spectrum of an isometry
V
is a subset of the closed unit disc, that is,
\sigma(V)\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Definition 2.2
.

[
5
, Chapter 1]

Fix an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. For any open set
U\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\sigma(T)\subseteq U
, let
\gamma
be a finite collection of closed rectifiable curves in
U\setminus\sigma(T)
that winds once around each point in the spectrum of
T
and no times around each point in the complement of
U
. If
f\in\mathrm{Hol}(U)
, then the Cauchy integral formula,

f(w)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\dfrac{f(z)}{(z-w)}~dz

whenever
z\in\sigma(T)
. The Riesz-Dunford functional calculus defines
f(T)
for
f\in\mathrm{Hol}(U)
by the substitution
z=T
in this formula, that is to say,

f(T)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}f(z)(z-T)^{-1}~dz.

(2.2)

Note that this definition makes sense, since the assumption
\gamma\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(T)
implies that
w-T
is invertible for all
w\in\gamma
. Moreover, equation (
2.2
) depends neither on the choice of contour nor on the choice of
U
, since by Cauchy’s theorem, for any vectors
u,v
in
\mathcal{H}

\big\langle f(T)u,v\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}f(z)\Big\langle(z-T)^{-1}u,v\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}~dz

is independent of
\gamma
,
[
5
, Chapter 1]
.

Consider the space of all polynomials on the closed unit disc
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, denoted
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
. Note
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset\mathrm{H}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
where
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
(respectively
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
) is the Hardy space of bounded analytic functions on the closed unit disc (respectively open unit disc) equipped with the supremum norm
\|f\|_{\infty}
. For
p(z)=z^{d}+c_{1}z^{d-1}+\ldots+c_{d}
, we refer to the map
J:\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})\rightarrow\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
defined by

Jp(z)=z^{d}\overline{p(1/\overline{z})}

(2.3)

as the
exchange map
. We take this terminology from the exchange matrix, and is meant to imply the “flipping” of the coefficients between
p(z)
and
z^{d}\overline{p(1/\overline{z})}
. Define
\overline{q}(z)=z^{d}\overline{p(1/\overline{z})}
such that
\overline{q}(z)=1+\overline{c_{1}}z+\ldots+\overline{c_{d}}z^{d}
and
q(z)=1+c_{1}z+\ldots+c_{d}z^{d}
. For
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
in
\mathbb{D}
, the polynomials

p(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(z+\lambda_{j})~~~\text{and}~~~\overline{q}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+\overline{\lambda_{j}}z)

(2.4)

for all
z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. We give a short result regarding the exchange map. This grounds our conversation in the importance of the polynomials
p
and
\overline{q}
from equation (
2.4
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
J
be the exchange map on
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
. Then
J^{2}=1_{\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})}
and
\|J\|_{\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})}=1
.

Proof.

J^{2}=1_{\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})}
follows immediately from the definition of the exchange map. As
\mathbb{P}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})\subset\mathrm{H}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
,

\displaystyle\|Jp(z)\|_{\mathrm{H}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})}

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\Big\lvert z^{d}{\overline{p(1/\overline{z})}}\Big\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\Big\lvert{p(1/\overline{z})}\Big\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\Big\lvert\overline{z}^{-d}+c_{1}\overline{z}^{-(d-1)}+\ldots+c_{d}\Big\rvert

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\Big\lvert z^{d}+c_{1}z^{d-1}+\ldots+c_{d}\Big\rvert

\displaystyle=\|p(z)\|_{\mathrm{H}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})}.

∎

Note that the zeroes of the polynomial
p
in equation (
2.4
) are all inside the unit disc. Equivalently, the zeroes of
q
are contained in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. We characterise these observations in following domain, of which we note next.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{D}^{d}
and define
\pi_{d}:\mathbb{D}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}
to be

\pi_{d}(\lambda)=(\pi_{d,1}(\lambda),\ldots,\pi_{d,d}(\lambda)),

where

\pi_{d,k}(\lambda)=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{k}\leq d}\lambda_{j_{1}}\ldots\lambda_{j_{k}}.

(2.5)

The
symmetrized polydisc
, denoted as
\mathbb{G}_{d}
, is a proper subset of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
\mathbb{G}_{d}=\pi_{d}(\mathbb{D}^{d})
, that is,

\mathbb{G}_{d}=\bigg\{(c_{1},\ldots,c_{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:c_{k}=\pi_{d,k}(\lambda)~\text{for}~\lambda\in\mathbb{D}^{d}~\text{and}~k=1,\ldots,d\bigg\}.

Throughout, we set
\pi_{d,0}(\lambda)=1
and we will write
\pi_{d,k}(\lambda)=c_{k}
for
k=0,1,\ldots,d
, suppressing the dependence on
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}^{d}
. Therefore, the polynomial
p
of equation (
2.4
) has all its zeroes inside the unit disc if and only if the coefficients
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{d})
of
p
belong to
\mathbb{G}_{d}
, by equation (
2.5
). Hence all the zeroes of
q
lie strictly outside of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

We now consider invertible operators built from polynomials and the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus. The following theorem, known as
von Neumann’s inequality
, is of great importance to us. Recall that an operator
T
is a contraction on
\mathcal{H}
if
\|T\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}\leq 1
.

Theorem 2.5
.

[
5
, Theorem 1.36]

If
\mathcal{H}
a Hilbert space,
T\in{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}
and
T
is a contraction, then for any polynomial
p
,

\|p(T)\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}\leq\max_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\lvert p(z)\rvert.

Polynomials can be used to construct a very natural, invertible operator, to produce a Riesz basis of a Hilbert space. This operator theoretic viewpoint makes it more amenable to analysis and will be used in later results.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
T
be a contraction on
\mathcal{H}
. The operator

q(T)=1_{\mathcal{H}}+c_{1}T+\ldots+c_{d}T^{d}

is bounded and invertible on
\mathcal{H}
if and only if
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{G}_{d}
. Moreover, the sequence
(q(T)e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is a Riesz basis of
\mathcal{H}
such that

\min_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}|q(z)|\leq\|q(T)e_{m}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\max_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}|q(z)|

(2.6)

for
m=1,2,\ldots
and any orthonormal basis
(e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Let
q
be a polynomial defined on the unit disc such that
q
has no zeroes on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. There exists scalars
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{D}
such that

q(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+\lambda_{j}z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_{k}z^{k}

for all
z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and scalars
c_{1},\ldots c_{d}
in
\mathbb{C}
with
c_{0}:=1
. By the maximum modulus principle, the maximum of
q
is attained on the unit circle and
q^{-1}
is bounded and holomorphic on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

For a contraction
T
on
\mathcal{H}
,
\sigma(T)\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. By von Neumann’s inequality, Theorem
2.5
,
q(T)
is bounded by

\|q(T)\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}\leq\max_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\lvert q(z)\rvert,

which ensures boundedness. Moreover, the operator
q(T)
is invertible, as each bracket
(1+\lambda_{j}T)
for
j=1,\ldots d
in the factorisation of
q(T)
is invertible, since
\|\lambda_{j}T\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}<1
for
j=1,\ldots,d
. By equation (
2.5
), this is equivalent to
(c_{k})_{k=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{G}_{d}
.

Furthermore, for an orthonormal basis
(e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
of
\mathcal{H}
,
(q(T)e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is a Riesz basis for
\mathcal{H}
since
q(T)
is bounded and invertible. Because the sequence
(q(T)e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
produces a Riesz basis of
\mathcal{H}
and is generated by the operator
q(T)
, we have inequality (
2.6
) as an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.1
.∎

Every Riesz basis generates another sequence which itself is also a Riesz basis.

Theorem 2.7
.

[
11
, Theorem 3.6.2]

If
(x_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is a Riesz basis for
\mathcal{H}
, there exists a unique sequence
(y_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{H}
such that

f=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\langle f,y_{m}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}x_{m}

(2.7)

for all

the orthogonal complement of
K
in
\mathcal{H}
, that is,

\mathcal{H}\ominus K=\Big\{x\in\mathcal{H}:\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0~\text{for all}~y\in K\Big\}.

Lemma 4.1
.

[
19
, Lemma 1]

Let
V\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then

\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})=\mathcal{H}\ominus V\mathcal{H}.

We recall the definition of a
unilateral shift
.

Definition 4.2
.

[
14
, Section 1]

Let
V
be an isometry on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. A subspace
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathcal{H}
is called a wandering space for
V
if
V^{p}\mathcal{L}\perp V^{q}\mathcal{L}
for every pair of integers
p,q\geq 0
and
p\neq q
; equivalently,

V^{j}\mathcal{L}\perp\mathcal{L}~~\text{for}~j=1,\ldots.

An isometry
S
on
\mathcal{H}
is called a unilateral shift if there exists a subspace
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathcal{H}
which is wandering for
S
and
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}S^{j}\mathcal{L}
. In this case
\mathcal{L}
is called generating for
S
.

For a unilateral shift
S
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,
\sigma(S)=\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Let
S
be a unilateral shift on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and let
(e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
. For any unilateral shift
S
, there exists an index function
\Lambda:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of natural numbers
1,2,\ldots
, such that

\mathrm{Ker}(S^{*})=\overline{\mathrm{Span}}\{e_{\Lambda(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}.

(4.1)

A simple example to demonstrate this is when the unilateral shift is the right shift on
\mathcal{H}
defined by
Se_{m}=e_{m+1}
. We have
\mathrm{Ker}(S^{*})=\overline{\mathrm{Span}}\{e_{1}\}
and an index function
\Lambda
that satisfies
\Lambda(1)=1
and
\Lambda(n)=0
for
n>1
. Another example involves the multiplication operator
M_{p}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, where
p
is a prime number and is defined by

M_{p}e_{m}=e_{pm},

(4.2)

where
(e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}
. Indeed, the proof that
M_{p}
is a unilateral shift was considered when
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(0,1)
, the space of square integrable functions on the interval
(0,1)
, by Boulton and Lord in
[
10
, Lemma 2]
. In this case,
e_{m}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin(m\pi x)
for all
x\in(0,1)
and
m=1,2,\ldots
. Since
\mathrm{Ker}(M_{p}^{*})=\{e_{n}\}_{n\not\equiv_{p}0}
, the index function
\Lambda
in this case is the function satisfying
\Lambda(n)=n
when
n\not\equiv_{p}0
and zero otherwise. Note that there exists positive integers
n\not\equiv_{p}0
and
s=1,2,\ldots
such that
m=np^{s-1}
for
m=1,2,\ldots
. One can check that the proof found in
[
10
, Lemma 2]
is not limited to the Hilbert space
L^{2}(0,1)
.

This acts as motivation for the next result, where we need the understanding of how a unilateral shift acts on orthonormal basis elements.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
be an orthonormal basis of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and let
S
be a unilateral shift on
\mathcal{H}
with
\mathrm{Ker}(S^{*})=\overline{\mathrm{Span}}\{e_{\Lambda(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
where
\Lambda:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is an index function. There exists a finite sequence of orthonormal basis vectors corresponding to each
e_{m}
, denoted

e_{\Lambda(n)}^{(s-1)},\ldots,e_{\Lambda(n)}^{(0)},

where
e_{\Lambda(n)}^{(s-1)}=e_{m}
and
e_{{\Lambda(n)}}^{(0)}=e_{\Lambda(n)}
for a positive integer
s
such that

S^{*}e_{m}=e_{\Lambda(n)}^{(s-2)},\ldots,~S^{*}e_{\Lambda(n)}^{(1)}=e_{\Lambda(n)}.

(4.3)

That is,
(S^{*})^{k}e_{m}=e_{\Lambda(n)}^{(s-1-k)}
for
k=0,1,\ldots s-1
.

Proof.

Since
S
is a unilateral shift on
\mathcal{H}
, we have
\mathrm{Ker}(S^{*})
is a wandering subspace for
S
, that is,
\mathcal{H}=\oplus_{j=0}^{\infty}S^{j}\mathcal{L}
where
\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{Ker}(S^{*})
. Choose any
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
such that
e_{\Lambda(n_{1})}
and
e_{\Lambda(n_{2})}
belong to
\mathrm{Ker}(S^{*})
. Then

\Big\langle S^{k_{1}}e_{\Lambda(n_{1})},S^{k_{2}}e_{\Lambda(n_{2})}\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0

for
k_{1},k_{2}=0,1,\ldots
such that
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
when
n_{1}=n_{2}
. Moreover, as
S
is an isometry,
\|S^{k}e_{\Lambda(n)}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
for a positive integer
n
and
k=0,1,\ldots
. Therefore, for each
k=0,1,\ldots
each vector in the sequence
(S^{k}e_{\Lambda(n)})_{n\geq 1}
is orthonormal in
\mathcal{H}
. Moreover, when
k\neq 0
, the subspace spanned by
(S^{k}e_{\Lambda(n)})_{n\geq 1}
is orthogonal to
\mathrm{Ker}(S^{*})
. Hence,
\cup_{k=0}^{\infty}(S^{k}e_{\Lambda(n)})_{n\geq 1}=(e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
.

For some
s=1,2,\ldots
and a positive integer
n
, let
e_{m}=S^{s-1}e_{\Lambda(n)}
for an
m=1,2,\ldots
. We can define a finite sequence of orthonormal basis vectors corresponding to
e_{m}
such that

Se_{\Lambda(n)}=e_{\Lambda(n)}^{(1)},~Se^{(1)}_{\Lambda(n)}=e_{\Lambda(n)}^{(2)},\ldots,Se_{\Lambda(n)}^{(s-2)}=e_{m}.

Equivalently, we have that equation (
4.3
) holds, where
e_{\Lambda(n)}^{(s-1)}=e_{m}
and
e_{\Lambda(n)}^{(0)}=e_{\Lambda(n)}
.∎

For a unilateral shift
S
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, we know that
\mathrm{Ker}(S^{*})
is a wandering subspace for S. For a general isometry
V
, we cannot make this claim. Instead, observe that the kernel of an operator
V
in
\mathcal{H}
is always a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. Therefore
\bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}V^{j}\mathcal{H}
is a closed subspace in
\mathcal{H}
. This is the basis for the next theorem, the
Wold decomposition
of an isometry
V
on a Hilbert space,
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
18
, Section 1.3]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

[
19
, Theorem 2]

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
V
be an isometry on
\mathcal{H}
. Then
\mathcal{H}
decomposes uniquely into an orthogonal sum
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that

V\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{1},~~V\mathcal{H}_{2}\subset\mathcal{H}_{2}.

Moreover, the restriction of
V
on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
V|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
, is unitary. The restriction of
V
on
\mathcal{H}_{2}
,
V|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
, is a unilateral shift. In particular, for

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}\ominus V\mathcal{H},

then

\mathcal{H}_{1}=\bigcap_{j=0}^{\infty}V^{j}\mathcal{H},~~\mathcal{H}_{2}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}V^{j}\mathcal{L}.

Note that for the Wold decomposition, we can apply Lemma
4.1
to rewrite the condition
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}\ominus V\mathcal{H}
as
\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})
.

Let
B
be a finite Blaschke product of degree
d
. Since
B\in\mathrm{Hol}(\overline{\mathbb{D}})
, we have that
B
can be extended analytically to an open set
U
including
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
as a subset, such that no poles of
B
lie inside
U
. Hence
B\in\mathrm{Hol}(U)
. Such a choice of open set
U
always exists, as the poles of
B
lie strictly outside
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Thus, a contour of small enough radius can always be chosen to exclude all the poles of
B
. We require such a choice of
U
as
\sigma(V^{*})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

Recall the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus, Definition
2.2
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
V
be an isometry on
\mathcal{H}
. Let
U
be an open set in
\mathbb{C}
such that
\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subset U
where no poles of the function
B
lie inside
U
and let
\gamma
be a closed rectifiable contour in
U\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
which winds once around each point in the spectrum of
\sigma(V^{*})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and no times around each point in the complement of
U
. The Riesz-Dunford functional calculus defines
B(V^{*})
for
B\in\mathrm{Hol}(U)
by

B(V^{*})=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}B(z)(z-V^{*})^{-1}~dz.

(4.4)

In light of this set up, we have the following result regarding the norm of the vectors
b_{m}
of a Blaschke frame for
m=1,2,\ldots
. The use of Hilbert space models, not only simplifies the calculation, but is also necessary to solve the redundancy question for a Blaschke frame for
B
with an isometry
V
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Recall from equation (
1.4
) the Malmquist-Takenaka functions corresponding to a finite Blaschke product
B
, equation (
1.3
), defined by

E_{j}(z):=\dfrac{(1-\lvert\lambda_{j}\rvert^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{1+\overline{\lambda_{j}}z}\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}\dfrac{z+\lambda_{k}}{1+\overline{\lambda_{k}}z},

for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
and
j=1,\ldots,d
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
(E_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
be the Malmquist-Takenaka functions corresponding to a finite Blaschke product
B

b_{m}
in a Blaschke frame for
B
with isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
for
m=1,2,\ldots
, there exists a positive integer

Proof.

By Lemma
1.1
, there exists a model
(\mathbb{C}^{d},u)
for any finite Blaschke product
B

u:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}

1-\overline{B(w)}B(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\overline{E_{j}(w)}(1-\overline{w}z)E_{j}(z)

for all
z,w\in\mathbb{D}
. Note that the model can be extended analytically to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, since the poles of
B
and the Malmquist-Takenaka functions are strictly outside
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. The operator
B(V^{*})
is defined by the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus by equation (
4.4
). A similar argument defines
E_{j}
for each

. Therefore,

1_{\mathcal{H}}-B^{*}(V^{*})B(V^{*})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}E^{*}_{j}(V^{*})(1_{\mathcal{H}}-VV^{*})E_{j}(V^{*}).

By the Wold decomposition,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that
V|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
is unitary on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
V|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
is a unilateral shift on
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Let
V|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}=U
and
V|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}=S
. Then
V
has the matrix representation on
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
given by

V=\begin{bmatrix}U&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}:\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}.

As
U
is unitary on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
S
is a unilateral shift on
\mathcal{H}_{2}
with wandering subspace
\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})
, Theorem
4.4
, we have

1_{\mathcal{H}}-VV^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}1-UU^{*}&0\\
0&1-SS^{*}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&0\\
0&P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}\end{bmatrix},

where
P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}:\mathcal{H}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2}
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{H}_{2}
onto
\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})
. Hence,

1_{\mathcal{H}}-B^{*}(V^{*})B(V^{*})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}E^{*}_{j}(V^{*})\begin{bmatrix}0_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&0\\
0&P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}\end{bmatrix}E_{j}(V^{*}).

(4.5)

Moreover,

\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\Big\langle b_{m},b_{m}\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\Big\langle B(V^{*})e_{m},B(V^{*})e_{m}\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\Big\langle B^{*}(V^{*})B(V^{*})e_{m},e_{m}\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

By equation (
4.5
),

\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\bigg\langle E^{*}_{j}(V^{*})\begin{bmatrix}0_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&0\\
0&P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}\end{bmatrix}E_{j}(V^{*})e_{m},e_{m}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

By the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus, there exist open sets
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
in
\mathbb{C}
, excluding the poles of the function
B
, for which
\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subset U_{1}
,
\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subset U_{2}
and closed, rectifiable contours
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle\bigg\langle E^{*}_{j}(V^{*})\begin{bmatrix}0_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&0\\
0&P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}\end{bmatrix}E_{j}(V^{*})e_{m},e_{m}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\Big)\overline{\Big(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\Big)}\int_{\gamma_{1}}\int_{\gamma_{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\bigg\langle\begin{bmatrix}0_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&0\\
0&P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}\end{bmatrix}E_{j}(z)(z-V^{*})^{-1}e_{m},E_{j}(w)(w-V^{*})^{-1}e_{m}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}~~dz~dw.

\displaystyle=\Big(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\Big)\overline{\Big(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\Big)}\int_{\gamma_{1}}\int_{\gamma_{2}}\bigg(\dfrac{E_{j}(z)}{z}\bigg)\bigg(\overline{\dfrac{E_{j}(w)}{w}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\cdot\bigg\langle\begin{bmatrix}0_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&0\\
0&P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}\end{bmatrix}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{-k}(V^{*})^{k}e_{m},\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}w^{-l}(V^{*})^{l}e_{m}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}~~dz~dw.

Let
e_{m}=f_{m}+g_{m}
, where

and
g_{m}=P_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}e_{m}
such that

is the orthogonal projection from
\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{H}_{j}
for

\begin{bmatrix}0_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&0\\
0&P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}\end{bmatrix}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{-k}(V^{*})^{k}e_{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{-k}P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}(S^{*})^{k}g_{m}.

By the Wold decomposition,
\mathcal{L}=\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})
is a wandering subspace for the unilateral shift
S

4.1

\mathcal{H}_{2}=\oplus_{j=0}^{\infty}V^{j}\mathcal{L}

\mathcal{H}_{2}
gives us

g_{m}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n\geq 1}\big\langle e_{m},S^{l}e_{\Lambda(n)}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}S^{l}e_{\Lambda(n)}.

Thus, for
k=0,1,\ldots
,

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}(S^{*})^{k}g_{m}

\displaystyle=P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}(S^{*})^{k}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n\geq 1}\big\langle e_{m},S^{l}e_{\Lambda(n)}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}S^{l}e_{\Lambda(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n\geq 1}\big\langle e_{m},S^{l}e_{\Lambda(n)}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}S^{l-k}e_{\Lambda(n)}.

For

, we have
P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}S^{l-k}e_{\Lambda(n)}

P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}(S^{*})^{k}g_{m}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\big\langle e_{m},S^{k}e_{\Lambda(n)}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}e_{\Lambda(n)}.

Therefore,

\begin{bmatrix}0_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&0\\
0&P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}\end{bmatrix}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{-k}(V^{*})^{k}e_{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n\geq 1}z^{-k}\big\langle e_{m},S^{k}e_{\Lambda(n)}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}e_{\Lambda(n)}.

(4.6)

By Lemma

\displaystyle\bigg\langle E^{*}_{j}(V^{*})\begin{bmatrix}0_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&0\\

\displaystyle=\Big(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\Big)\overline{\Big(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\Big)}\int_{\gamma_{1}}\int_{\gamma_{2}}\bigg(\dfrac{E_{j}(z)}{z}\bigg)\bigg(\overline{\dfrac{E_{j}(w)}{w}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\cdot\bigg\langle z^{-(s-1)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}w^{-l}(V^{*})^{l}e_{m}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}w^{-l}\begin{bmatrix}(U^{*})^{l}f_{m}\\
(S^{*})^{l}g_{m}\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}w^{-l}\begin{bmatrix}(U^{*})^{l}f_{m}\\

From equation (

\displaystyle\bigg\langle z^{-(s-1)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\

\displaystyle\bigg\langle E^{*}_{j}(V^{*})\begin{bmatrix}0_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}&0\\

\displaystyle=\Big(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\Big)\overline{\Big(\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\Big)}\int_{\gamma_{1}}\int_{\gamma_{2}}\bigg(\dfrac{E_{j}(z)}{z}\bigg)\bigg(\overline{\dfrac{E_{j}(w)}{w}}\bigg)(z\overline{w})^{s-1}~~dz~dw

\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\bigg\langle P_{\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})}E_{j}(V^{*})e_{m},E_{j}(V^{*})e_{m}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=1-\dfrac{1}{\big((s-1)!\big)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Bigg\lvert\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\Bigg\rvert^{2}.

The following test we state is to find the redundant and essential vectors of any frame, which was given by Christensen in
[

]
. This result is of the utmost importance.

Theorem 4.6
.

[
11
, Theorem 5.4.7]

Let
(x_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
be a frame for a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with frame operator
\mathfrak{F}
. The removal of a vector
x_{k}
from the frame
(x_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
leaves either a frame or an incomplete set. More precisely:

(1)

If
\langle x_{k},\mathfrak{F}^{-1}x_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\neq 1
, then
(x_{m})_{m\neq k}^{\infty}
is a frame for
\mathcal{H}
, that is,
x_{k}
is redundant;

(2)

If
\langle x_{k},\mathfrak{F}^{-1}x_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=1
, then
(x_{m})_{m\neq k}^{\infty}
is incomplete, that is,
x_{k}
is essential.

By Theorem
4.5
, the norm of each
b_{m}=B(V^{*})e_{m}
in a Blaschke frame for
m=1,2,\ldots
is a consequence of
e_{m}=S^{s-1}e_{\Lambda(n)}
and
e_{\Lambda(n)}\in\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})
for some positive integer
s
(where
S=V|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
is a unilateral shift by the Wold decomposition and
\Lambda:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is an index function). We completely solve the the problem of redundancy for a Blaschke frame with an isometry in the following result.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
(E_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
be the Malmquist-Takenaka functions corresponding to a finite Blaschke product
B
. For every vector
b_{k}
in a Blaschke frame for
B
with isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
for
k=1,2,\ldots
, there exists a positive integer
s
such that:

(1)

If
\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}=0
for
j=1,\ldots,d
, then
b_{k}
is essential;

(2)

If
\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\neq 0
for at least one
j=1,\ldots,d
, then
b_{k}
is redundant.

Proof.

To prove (1) and show incompleteness of the sequence
(b_{m})_{m\neq k}^{\infty}
, where
b_{m}=B(V^{*})e_{m}
, we need to find when

\langle b_{k},\mathfrak{F}^{-1}b_{k}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=1

where
\mathfrak{F}
is the frame operator for a Blaschke frame, Theorem
4.6
. By Theorem
3.5
,
\mathfrak{F}=1_{\mathcal{H}}
for a Blaschke frame for
B
with isometry
V
. Therefore we need to calculate the norm of the vector
b_{k}
to find the redundancy condition. By Theorem
4.5
, for each
m=1,2,\ldots
, there exists a positive integer
s
such that

\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=1-\dfrac{1}{\big((s-1)!\big)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Bigg\lvert\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\Bigg\rvert^{2}.

Therefore, if
\|b_{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
, the sequence
(b_{m})_{m\neq k}^{\infty}
is incomplete in
\mathcal{H}
. Under this assumption,

\displaystyle\|b_{k}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=1

\displaystyle\iff\dfrac{1}{\big((s-1)!\big)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Bigg\lvert\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\Bigg\rvert^{2}=0

\displaystyle\iff\Bigg\lvert\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\Bigg\rvert^{2}=0~~\text{for}~j=1,\ldots,d

\displaystyle\iff\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}=0~~\text{for}~j=1,\ldots,d.

To prove (2), if
\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\neq 0
for at least one
j=1,\ldots,d

\|b_{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=1-\dfrac{1}{\big((s-1)!\big)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Bigg\lvert\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\Bigg\rvert^{2}\neq 1.

By Theorem
4.6
, the vector
b_{k}
is redundant and
(b_{m})_{m\neq k}
is a frame for
\mathcal{H}
.∎

In Theorem
4.5
, if the isometry
V
is a unitary unitary operator
U
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, then
1_{\mathcal{H}}-UU^{*}=0
and

\|b_{m}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\Big\langle B^{*}(U^{*})B(U^{*})e_{m},e_{m}\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\bigg\langle\Big(1_{\mathcal{H}}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}E^{*}_{j}(U^{*})(1_{\mathcal{H}}-UU^{*})E_{j}(U^{*})\Big)e_{m},e_{m}\bigg\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=1.

Therefore, by the redundancy condition from Christensen, Theorem
4.6
, a Blaschke frame for
B
with a unitary operator
U
is an exact frame, or equivalently, a Riesz basis for a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. In this instance, we have
(b_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}=(B(U^{*})e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
, therefore
B(U^{*})
must be invertible. Indeed, by Lemma
2.10
,
B(V^{*})
is a co-isometry for any isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
. By the model formula for the finite Blaschke product, Lemma
1.1
,

1_{\mathcal{H}}-B^{*}(U^{*})B(U^{*})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}E^{*}_{j}(U^{*})(1-UU^{*})E_{j}(U^{*})=0.

Thus
B^{*}(U^{*})B(U^{*})=1_{\mathcal{H}}
and
B(U^{*})
is also an isometry. Hence
B(U^{*})
is invertible on
\mathcal{H}
and
B^{-1}(U^{*})=B^{*}(U^{*})
, that is,
B(U^{*})
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
. We have therefore proved the following.

Proposition 4.8
.

A Blaschke frame for
B
with a unitary operator
U
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a Riesz basis for
\mathcal{H}
.

For a pure isometry
V
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, we consider the following simple example to illustrate the test for redundancy.

Example 4.9
.

Let
b_{m}
be a vector in a Blaschke frame for
z^{d}
with the right shift operator
S
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then every vector
b_{m}
for
m\leq d
is a redundant vector, that is, the sequence
(b_{k})_{k\neq m}
for
m\geq d+1
is incomplete.

Proof.

For the right shift operator
S
on
\mathcal{H}
, we have

(b_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}=((S^{*})^{d}e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}=(0)_{m=1}^{d}\cup(e_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}.

As
z^{d}
has one zero,
z=0
, of multiplicity
d
, the Malmquist-Takenaka functions
E_{j}
corresponding to
z^{d}
are given by
E_{j}(z)=z^{j-1}
for all
z\in{\overline{\mathbb{D}}}
and
j=1,\ldots,d
. By Theorem
4.7
, for every positive integer
s
such that
s\geq d+1
,

\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}=0

(4.9)

for
j=1,\ldots,d
. Thus every vector
b_{m}=(S^{*})^{d}e_{m}
such that
m\geq d+1
is essential, that is,
(b_{k})_{k\neq m}
is incomplete. Moreover, every vector
b_{m}
such that
m\leq d
is redundant. This makes sense, as the frame sequence
(b_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
is simply the original orthonormal basis union with
d
copies of the zero vector. Therefore we can delete each zero vector at no cost to the frame.∎

Example
4.9
extends beyond just the shift operator. Independent of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and the isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
, if
B(z)=z^{d}
for all
z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, the functions
E_{j}
always satisfy equation (
4.9
) for all positive integers
s\geq d+1
and
j=1,\ldots,d
. Moreover, for all positive integers
s\leq d
,

\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\neq 0

for at least one
j=1,\ldots,d
, as

\dfrac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}E_{j}(0)=(j-1)!

for
j=1,\ldots,d
. Therefore we have proved the following result.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space. The vector
b_{m}=(V^{*})^{d}e_{m}
in a Blaschke frame for
z^{d}
with isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
is redundant if and only if
e_{m}=S^{s-1}e_{\Lambda(n)}
and
s\leq d
, where
S=V|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}
and
e_{\Lambda(n)}\in\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})
.

Observe an implication of Theorem
4.7
. By Proposition
4.8
, a Blaschke frame for
B
with a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
is a Riesz basis. Therefore every vector in a Blaschke frame is essential. Instead, suppose
V
is a pure isometry. If
B=z^{d}
, then Proposition
4.10
tells us the redundancy condition for this Blaschke frame. The only other alternative is if
B
a finite Blaschke product with at least one non-zero root. Our final result gives us conditions for when every vector in a Blaschke frame is redundant. This encourages our use of the following terminology. We say a frame
(x_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
for a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is
fully insured
if any vector
x_{k}
can be deleted from the frame such that
(x_{m})_{m\neq k}^{\infty}
is a frame for
\mathcal{H}
, that is, if every vector
x_{m}
is redundant for
m=1,2,\ldots
.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
B
be a finite Blaschke product with at least one non-zero root. Then a Blaschke frame for
B
with a pure isometry
V
on
\mathcal{H}
is fully insured.

Proof.

If
B
a finite Blaschke product of degree
d
with at least one non-zero root, say
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, we can write

B(z)=z^{k}\dfrac{z+\lambda}{1+\overline{\lambda}z}\widetilde{B}(z)

where
\widetilde{B}
is a finite Blaschke product of degree
d-k-1
with no roots at zero. The first
k+1
Malmquist-Takenaka functions corresponding to
B
satisfy
E_{j}(z)=z^{j-1}
for
j=1,\ldots,k
and

E_{k+1}(z)=\dfrac{z^{k}(1-\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}{1+\overline{\lambda}z}.

By Theorem
4.7
, if there exists a positive integer
s
such that

\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}=0

for
j=1,\ldots,d
, then the vector
b_{m}=B(V^{*})e_{m}
such that
S^{s-1}e_{\Lambda(n)}=e_{m}
and
e_{\Lambda(n)}\in\mathrm{Ker}(V^{*})
is essential for
m=1,2,\ldots
. Note

\dfrac{d^{k}E_{j}(0)}{dz^{k}}=0

for
j=1,\ldots,k
but, for the function
E_{k+1}
,

E_{k+1}(z)=(1-\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2})^{1/2}\big(z^{k}-\overline{\lambda}z^{k+1}+\overline{\lambda}^{2}z^{k+2}-\overline{\lambda}^{3}z^{k+3}+\ldots\big)

and

\dfrac{d^{s-1}E_{k+1}(0)}{dz^{s-1}}\neq 0

for
s\geq k+1
. Hence, there does not exist a positive integer
s
such that all the Malmquist-Takenaka functions satisfy Theorem
4.7
, (1). Instead, for every positive integer
s
, Theorem
4.7
, (2) is satisfied. Thus, for a finite Blaschke product
B
with at least one non-zero root, a Blaschke frame for
B
with a pure isometry
V
is fully insured. ∎
