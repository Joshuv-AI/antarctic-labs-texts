Solvation structure of hydrated electrons overlooked in the degradation of PFAS by UV/sulfite
Authors: Xu Liu, Fang Li, Zhicheng Zhang, Tong Guan, Boxue Pang, Delin Qi, Wei Wang, Yang Wu, Shubo Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The UV/sulfite system is currently regarded as the most effective method for degrading and defluorinating per- and poly-fluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), operating via hydrated electrons (eaq−). Previous studies largely concentrate on PFAS molecular structure, overlooking the critical influence of eaq− microstructure. By integrating ab initio molecular dynamics simulations with experiments, we reveal strong correlations between cavity radius and PFAS removal efficiency (R2 = 0.99) and defluorination reaction (R2 = 0.89). Expanding the cavity radius enhances spin density at its centre (R2 = 0.83), increasing the availability of solvated electrons for interaction with PFAS active sites and facilitating diffusion-controlled electron transfer that drives defluorination. Under alkaline conditions, an increase in cavity radius from 1.52 to 1.82 Å enhances spin-density delocalisation within the eaq− cavity, improving degradation efficiency. These findings are supported by experimental results revealing >97% degradation of hexafluoropropylene oxide dimer acid (or its ammonium salt ‘GenX’) at pH 12. Concentrated eaq− enables cooperative multi-site attack on PFAS molecules, accounting for the enhanced defluorination. Overall, this study reveals an atomic-scale correlation between eaq− microstructure and defluorination dynamics. Beyond conventional approaches of modifying PFAS structures, we propose that regulating the solvation structure of eaq− offers a strategy for efficient degradation.

Introduction

Per- and poly-fluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), characterised by stable carbon–fluorine bonds (bond energy ~115 kcal mol–1) and bioaccumulative features, have emerged as chemicals of global concern, widely referred to as ‘forever chemicals’1,2. Accordingly, researchers have developed replacement compounds to minimise their environmental impact. For instance, hexafluoropropylene oxide dimer acid (HFPO–DA) and its ammonium salt, collectively referred to as ‘GenX chemicals’, have been synthesised as alternatives to perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA). However, these compounds display similarly high environmental persistence, a consequence of fluorine’s elevated electronegativity (4.0) and sp3 hybridisation3,4. Moreover, conventional degradation methods remain largely ineffective, with biological and photolytic processes resulting in low PFAS removal efficiency. Although advanced oxidation processes, employing •OH and SO4•− radicals, can degrade PFAS through oxidative pathways, such as hydrogen abstraction or electron transfer, their application is restricted by small rate constants (k = 0.9–4.4 × 104 M−1 s−1 for SO4•− + perfluorocarboxylic acids) and poor defluorination efficiency5–8. For example, eliminating 52% of PFOA under intense ultraviolet (UV) irradiation requires 12 h, rendering the process impractical for wastewater treatment7. These limitations underscore the urgent need for more efficient PFAS degradation technologies.

In contrast, reductive pathways are possible under strongly reducing conditions, such as hydrated electrons (eaq−), which are generated via advanced reduction processes (ARPs). Previous studies have indicated that eaq− exhibit pronounced reactivity owing to their highly negative reduction potential (E⁰ = −2.9 V vs. SHE) and rapid reaction kinetics (k ≈ 106–1010 M−1 s−1)9,10. Following the pioneering work of Park et al.11, researchers have systematically explored numerous eaq− generation conditions, including chemical precursors (e.g. iodide11, sulphite12 and indole13), optimised synthesis protocols14 and UV irradiation settings15,16, to evaluate their defluorination performance. In particular, recent efforts have revealed that at pH 9–10, eaq− facilitates C–F bond cleavage in GenX through single electron transfer, resulting in 50%–90% defluorination17. However, recent studies on PFAS degradation have primarily focused on how molecular features, such as carbon chain length and functional group identity, affect the reduction efficiency of eaq−18. By contrast, its intrinsic microstructure remains largely unexplored. Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations preliminarily suggest that while excess electrons can promote PFAS degradation19, eaq− delivers greater reductive efficiency20. Simulations have further indicated that eaq− comprises three structural components: an inner cavity, a hydration shell and a diffuse tail21. These features, represented by the radius of gyration (rg), are believed to influence electron transfer. However, the precise role of eaq− microstructure in governing defluorination dynamics at the atomic scale remains poorly understood. Although previous studies have proposed an electron-transfer mechanism for eaq−-driven PFAS degradation22 and indicated that the approach direction of eaq− can affect C–F bond cleavage23, several questions remain unresolved: What is the structure–activity relationship between the transient inner cavity of eaq− and its defluorination reactivity? Additionally, eaq− achieves substantial defluorination under alkaline conditions17, does increasing pH enhance degradation by altering cavity structure? What are the associated microscopic mechanisms? Specifically, how does the cavity size of eaq− vary under acidic and alkaline conditions, and to what extent does such structural contraction or expansion affect the degradation rate constant of GenX molecules? A quantitative analysis of the cavity’s structure–reactivity relationship is therefore essential to elucidate the GenX degradation process24. In parallel, the mechanism by which the ether functional group in GenX governs the selectivity of C–F bond dissociation also remains unresolved25, posing a major challenge for the efficient degradation of ether-containing PFAS using eaq−26.

Building on the above research, this study integrates photochemical techniques with AIMD simulations to establish, a quantitative structure–activity relationship between the cavity size of non-equilibrium eaq− and efficiency of GenX defluorination. The research objectives include the following: (1) to map the spatial distribution of C–F bond dissociation energies (BDEs) in GenX and assess its reactivity with eaq−; (2) to determine how dynamic variations in cavity size influence the electron-domain extent and probability of defluorination; and (3) to elucidate how pH and eaq− concentration affect the atomic-scale cavity structure of eaq−. By analysing the dynamic behaviour of eaq− during GenX reduction and degradation, this study uncovers a previously unexplored but critical link between its internal cavity structure and the defluorination process. The findings offer atomic-scale theoretical insights into PFAS degradation mechanisms and support the design of more effective remediation strategies.

GenX degradation performance in the UV/sulphite system

The UV/sulphite system degrades various PFAS while achieving high defluorination efficiency in solution27. As presented in Fig. 1, GenX degradation improved with increasing solution pH, with higher sulphite dosage further enhancing GenX removal. Specifically, at a sulphite concentration of 20 mM and a solution pH of 1228, GenX was no longer detectable. Meanwhile, the effects of solution pH and sulphite dosage on the defluorination percentage of GenX followed trends similar to those observed for its overall degradation. Specifically, at a sulphite concentration of 20 mM and a solution pH of 12, GenX defluorination reached 64.2%. In contrast, GenX underwent only partial degradation at pH 7 and minimal degradation at pH 2. Moreover, even at a low sulphite dose of 5 mM, GenX removal exceeded 97% at pH 12, highlighting the dominant role of solution pH in governing the GenX degradation process. Compared with the UV/sulphite system, GenX was more resistant to degradation in the UV/persulfate system (Supplementary Fig. 1), with defluorination not exceeding 27% across the pH range of 2–12 and persulfate concentrations of 20 mM. Notably, eaq− species, reactive nucleophiles generated in the UV/sulphite system, are the primary agents driving PFAS degradation and defluorination. Accordingly, perfluoroalkyl ether carboxylic acids, which contain ether oxygen bonds, are particularly susceptible to attack by eaq− in this system17,29–31. Meanwhile, in the UV/persulfate system, SO4•− radicals are the primary species; they predominantly target accessible functional groups such as the carboxylate terminus of PFAS. To identify the dominant reactive species responsible for degradation under alkaline conditions (pH 12), several mechanistic experiments were conducted. In the absence of UV/sulphite, GenX underwent negligible degradation, even after prolonged shaking for 96 h at pH 12 (Supplementary Fig. 2). This indicates that OH− or hydrated OH− alone cannot initiate GenX degradation under these conditions and that the alkaline environment may instead stabilise reactive species such as eaq−. Electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy31 confirmed the presence of eaq− in the alkaline system (Supplementary Information Section 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3). Laser flash photolysis26,32 further detected the transient absorption signal of eaq− in the UV/sulphite system (Supplementary Information Section 2 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Upon GenX addition, the signal decayed more rapidly, indicating that GenX effectively reacts with eaq− under alkaline conditions. To confirm the critical role of eaq−, scavenger experiments were conducted next33. In these experiments, methanol (MeOH)34 and sodium nitrate (NaNO3)35 were employed as characteristic scavengers of oxidative radicals (e.g. •OH) and eaq−, respectively. The results (Supplementary Fig. 5) indicated that methanol (1–10 mM) negligibly affected GenX removal, suggesting that oxidative radicals such as •OH or SO4•− are not the primary reactive species. In contrast, NO3− strongly inhibited degradation in a concentration-dependent manner. Specifically, when the NO3− concentration reached ≥5 mM, the GenX removal efficiency fell sharply from nearly 100% to below 5%. This pronounced suppression confirms that a reducing species efficiently scavenged by NO3− primarily governs the degradation, suggesting that GenX removal proceeds mainly via a reductive pathway.

Hydrated electron solvation structure in GenX defluorination

Notably, the solvation characteristics of eaq− species, which are key nucleophilic agents in the UV/sulphite system, directly govern the defluorination efficiency of PFAS (Fig. 1 and Fig. 2a–c). To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we performed eight randomised NPT ensemble equilibrium simulations of the GenX solvation system, generating eaq− through spin density distribution calculations and the real-time monitoring of spatial configuration evolution during degradation. In parallel, the radius of gyration (rg, Supplementary Information Section 5), a key parameter for validating accurate eaq− generation, was calculated from its spin density. In the explicitly solvated GenX system, the average rg value across the initially neutral defluorination reaction trajectories (Supplementary Fig. 6) was 2.83 Å, consistent with reported values (rg = 2.8 Å)36,37 in the liquid phase. It forms a cavity structure solvated by 5–8 water molecules, with the O–H bonds directed towards the centre37–41. This cavity emerges as the electron occupies a volume-excluded region in water owing to short-range repulsions. Its structure was quantified using radial distribution functions (RDFs, Supplementary Information Section 6). Notably, RDF analysis defines the cavity boundary (r0) as the distance where g(r) = 0, indicating complete water exclusion with no atomic nuclei present. Accordingly, we defined the cavity radius of eaq− (r0) as the radial distance where the Hw g(r) function first dropped to zero (Supplementary Information Section 7). Through eight independent NPT ensemble equilibrium simulations, we statistically examined the cavity radius of eaq− in each trajectory, obtaining values of 1.52, 1.54, 1.57, 1.62, 1.67, 1.72, 1.79 and 1.82 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7). Further, RDF analysis of the first solvation shell of eaq− in the GenX-containing system yielded coordination numbers between 5.58 and 8.01 (eaq−−Hw), corresponding to approximately 5–8 water molecules per electron cavity (Supplementary Table 1). These values are consistent with previously reported stable eaq− configurations42,43. Supplementary Table 1 summarises the coordination numbers of Hw atoms and the corresponding cavity radii across systems. Our AIMD simulations indicate that cavity expansion notably enhances defluorination potential while maintaining stable water coordination. Importantly, a correlation analysis between the transient time required for bond elongation to 2.5 Å and cavity radius revealed that smaller cavities (1.52–1.57 Å) exclusively induced C2–O3 bond cleavage at the carboxyl terminus, whereas larger cavities (1.62–1.82 Å) simultaneously triggered C3–F2/F3 bond defluorination. The former occurred in three trajectories and the latter in five. Notably, as depicted in Fig. 2d, cavity size exhibits a strong negative correlation with bond dissociation dynamics (R2 = 0.99 and 0.89 for C–O and C–F dissociation times), with larger cavities leading to earlier bond cleavage in both cases. This quantitative correlation indicates that cavity expansion facilitates bond dissociation by improving spin density transfer efficiency, ultimately promoting defluorination. Although O3–C3 bond cleavage preference may appear counterintuitive based on BDEs (Fig. 2b; C2–O3: 94.6 kJ mol–1; O3–C3: 81.0 kJ mol–1), Hirshfeld charge analysis (Supplementary Information Section 8 and Supplementary Fig. 8) reveals pronounced charge polarisation within the carboxylate moiety: C3 (+0.381) bears a considerably greater positive charge than C2 (+0.134), while O3 (–0.225) is negatively charged. The resulting enhanced electrostatic stabilisation of the O3–C3 linkage under aqueous, hydrated-electron conditions suppresses its cleavage, rendering the less polarised C2–O3 bond more prone to dissociation.

Further analysis reveals that the eaq− spin density primarily localises at the cavity centre (Supplementary Information Section 9) and also somewhat delocalises towards surrounding water molecules, forming a spherically symmetric distribution with the peak density at the cavity core. Electrons can also diffuse towards the cavity surface, where they attack the terminal carboxyl group of the target pollutant, GenX. In our next analysis, we examined the spin density distribution of eaq− in its neutral state (Supplementary Fig. 9). Across seven independent trajectories, the initial eaq− exhibited maximum spin density values of 0.0222, 0.0223, 0.0260, 0.0286, 0.0291, 0.0290 and 0.0308 e Å−3, respectively. More importantly, a strong positive correlation (R2 = 0.83, Fig. 2e) was observed between the cavity radius of eaq− and its maximum spin density. This trend reflects cavity expansion driven by high central electron density, which increases the spin density peak. The enlarged cavity concurrently enhances electron accessibility and promotes their localisation near the carboxyl terminus of GenX, thereby facilitating defluorination. Further, quantitative analysis revealed strong negative correlations between maximum spin density and the cleavage times of the C–O and C–F bonds (R2 = 0.82 and 0.99, respectively; Fig. 2f). Collectively, these key correlations provide strong evidence for the critical influence of eaq− microstructure on the degradation mechanism.

AIMD-based insights into the dynamics of GenX degradation

AIMD simulations revealed initial distances of 5–8 Å between eaq− and GenX across all trajectories (Supplementary Table 1). Under neutral conditions, all four defluorination trajectories exhibited an identical two-step mechanism involving ether bond cleavage followed by defluorination. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, at the initial stage, eaq−, surrounded by six water molecules, occupies a cavity with a radius of 1.79 Å and is located approximately 7 Å from GenX molecule. Subsequently, the spin density initially confined within the eaq− cavity progressively delocalises onto the carboxylate group (–COO−), α-carbon (C2) and adjacent side chain and ether bond of GenX (Fig. 3b). More importantly, eaq− with a larger cavity radius exhibits higher spin density (0.0308 e Å−3, Fig. 3c), which facilitates electron transfer and thereby accelerates GenX reduction. Further, Hirshfeld charge analysis44 was conducted to quantify charge redistribution during the degradation process (Supplementary Tables 2, 3). As depicted in Fig. 3d, at the initial stage (0 ps), the α-carbon atom C2 carries a positive charge of +0.13, oxygen atom O3 carries a negative charge of −0.13, and fluorine atom at C3 has a charge of −0.07. By 0.25 ps, this charge distribution undergoes a marked shift owing to spin density accumulation in the C2–O3 region. The positive charge on C2 drops to +0.04, while the negative charge on O3 rises to −0.26. This charge redistribution is accompanied by a notable elongation of the C–O bond to 2.21 Å, exceeding its equilibrium length. This suggests that the C–O bond begins to cleave. In this study, bond cleavage is defined as substantial elongation relative to the initial bond length, without the formation of a stable complex. Once the ether bond is weakened, the transferred electrons populate the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of GenX, particularly the C–F σ*-orbital (Fig. 2c), thereby weakening the C3–F bond. At 0.70 ps, the C–F bond extends to 2.10 Å, which is beyond its equilibrium length and indicates imminent cleavage. At this point, O3 carries a charge of −0.20, and the dissociating fluorine atom accumulates a charge of −0.26. Relative to the initial charge of −0.07 on the fluorine atom, this reflects a net transfer of 0.19 electrons during the reductive defluorination process. This quantitative result demonstrates that an eaq− species with a larger cavity radius more effectively promotes electron transfer, thereby facilitating cleavage of both C–O and C–F bonds. This reaction exhibits clear site selectivity. Across all trajectories, electrons from the eaq− species preferentially localise near the carboxylate head group, rendering the adjacent C2 site the primary electron-accepting centre and initiating the cleavage of the C2–O3 bond. The excess electrons are subsequently transferred to the perfluoroalkyl chain, with the defluorination site governed by the electronic structure of the GenX molecule. As illustrated in Fig. 2c, the LUMO of GenX exhibits stronger antibonding character at the C3–F bond, which lies far from the head group, while the antibonding interaction at the C2–F bond is weaker. Furthermore, the –CF3 group at the C2 position introduces steric hindrance. Together, these factors render the C3–F bond more susceptible to reductive cleavage. Therefore, although ether bond cleavage initiates at the C2 site, subsequent defluorination predominantly occurs at the C3 position. Throughout the 3.00 ps simulation, cleavage remains confined to these specific bonds, with no other bond-breaking events detected. The dissociated fluorine atom is stabilised through weak hydrogen bonding with surrounding water molecules, accompanied by minor charge transfer (Fig. 3d).

Three trajectories with contracted eaq− cavities (r0 = 1.57 Å, Fig. 3e) exhibit only ether bond cleavage during the 3.00 ps simulation (Fig. 3f). Interestingly, the reduced spin density at the cavity centre (0.0260 e Å−3, Fig. 3g) substantially limits the electron’s accessibility to reactive sites. Charge distribution analysis indicates that, at the start of the reaction, the C2 site, O3 site and fluorine atom at the C3 position bear charges of +0.13, −0.15 and −0.06, respectively. As the spin density gradually delocalises, the electron transfers to the C2–O3 region at 0.45 ps, causing the C2–O3 bond to elongate considerably, while the charge on O3 remains highly negative (−0.28), suggesting the accumulation of approximately 0.13 additional electrons at this site. This electron transfer directly initiates the reductive degradation of GenX. Further analysis reveals that although the O3 site accumulates a large number of electrons, the electron density transferred to the fluorine atom remains insufficient (Fig. 3h). Specifically, at 0.45 ps, the fluorine atom accumulates only 0.22 electrons, reflecting a net increase of 0.16 electrons relative to the initial state (Fig. 3h). Nonetheless, the spin density is sufficient to drive the defluorination reaction. These findings underscore the critical relationship between the eaq− cavity radius and electron-transfer capability, which ultimately determines whether the system can fulfil the conditions necessary for reductive defluorination.

Further, to elucidate the differences in reactivity between the oxidative and reductive systems, we selected SO4•− as the oxidant for dynamic simulations of GenX degradation (Supplementary Fig. 10). Although the initial separation between SO4•− and GenX was approximately 5 Å, no detectable elongation or cleavage of key bonds, including C3–O and C3–F, was observed in the AIMD simulation at 3.00 ps. Additionally, no substantial charge transfer was detected (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 4). In contrast, the reductive system exhibited much greater degradation efficiency, attributable to the ability of eaq− to delocalise the electron density into the GenX molecule, thereby weakening the highly polar C–F bonds and promoting their dissociation. Conversely, in oxidative systems, the SO4•− radical tends to extract electrons from the GenX molecule. However, in intact PFAS molecules, carbon atoms are already in relatively high oxidation states, whereas fluorine atoms are in their lowest oxidation state. This electronic configuration provides little thermodynamic driving force for further oxidative electron transfer, rendering oxidation-induced bond activation inefficient. These AIMD results align well with experimental observations, revealing substantially higher GenX removal efficiency in the UV/sulphite system (>97%) than in the UV/persulfate system (<35%) under neutral solvation conditions.

Effects of solution pH and hydrated electron concentration

To investigate the atomic mechanisms by which environmental pH and eaq− concentration affect the defluorination efficiency of GenX molecule, trajectory simulations were conducted and three distinct reaction systems were constructed: (1) an acidic system, modelled by introducing H+ ions to create low-pH conditions; (2) an alkaline system, established through the addition of OH− ions to simulate high-pH environments; and (3) a high-concentration eaq− system, represented by a dual-eaq− model to mimic intense reducing conditions. The results indicate that environmental pH substantially influences the cavity size of eaq−. Under acidic conditions, the cavity radius decreases to 1.47 Å (Supplementary Fig. 12), whereas under strongly alkaline conditions, it expands to 1.82 Å (Supplementary Fig. 13), yielding a Δr of 0.35 Å. This pH effect is primarily mediated by H+ ions under acidic conditions. Specifically, an H⁺ ion readily associates with a water molecule to form an H+–OH2 complex. This solvation effect causes the spin density to become strongly localised on the hydrogen atom (0.1686 e Å−3, Supplementary Fig. 14), while the maximum spin density at the centre of the eaq− cavity drops to 0.0001 e Å−3. This strong proton-dominated localisation severely restricts electron transfer to active PFAS sites, thereby completely inhibiting the degradation reaction. Furthermore, the standard reduction potential of hydrogen atoms (E°(H+/H•) = −2.3 V vs. SHE) exceeds that of eaq− (E°(eaq⁻) = −2.9 V vs. SHE)45, indicated that H• exhibits limited reductive activity towards GenX. In contrast, under alkaline conditions, although the eaq− cavity radius increases (r0 = 1.82 Å, Supplementary Fig. 13), electrostatic repulsion from OH− induces slight polarisation in the spin density distribution of eaq−. While this reduces the maximum spin density slightly (0.0278 e Å−3, Supplementary Fig. 16), it renders the electron distribution more diffuse. This subtle electronic redistribution optimises the spatial orientation of the reaction and considerably facilitates spin density diffusion towards the C–F bond region, thereby greatly accelerating defluorination dynamics. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, under alkaline conditions, the delocalised spin density promotes rapid elongation of the C2–O3 ether bond to 2.61 Å within 0.30 ps, accompanied by charge redistribution (C2: 0.07 → O3: −0.30, Supplementary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Table 6). Subsequently, the C3–F bond extends to 2.29 Å at 0.45 ps, with the negative charge on fluorine increasing to −0.32 and that on O3 decreasing to −0.20 (Supplementary Fig. 17). Notably, the charge on F3 rises from −0.09 at 0 ps to −0.32 at 0.45 ps. This finding indicates that an alkaline environment does not considerably enhance electron transfer during diffusion. Instead, this effect arises from localised spin density, which increases the spatial accessibility of eaq− to PFAS molecules, thereby accelerating degradation. The bond cleavage sites remain consistent across pH conditions, indicating that the reaction mechanism remains unchanged. These results explain the experimentally observed improvement in defluorination efficiency at the atomic level: the efficiency gain is directly linked to the pH-dependent expansion of the eaq− cavity. Under strongly alkaline conditions, the cavity radius increases by 19.7%; this structural expansion provides more favourable spatial conditions for spin density transfer, thereby accelerating bond dissociation. This insight offers a clear explanation for the observed defluorination efficiency of over 60% under alkaline conditions (Fig. 1).

Experiments also reveal that increasing the eaq− concentration considerably improves defluorination efficiency. A theoretical model containing two eaq− was constructed, as detailed in Supplementary Section 12. As illustrated in the AIMD simulation outcomes in Fig. 4c, when effective eaq− species interact with GenX in the early stages of the reaction (Supplementary Fig. 18), electrons are more easily delocalised onto the carboxyl and ether bond regions of GenX, thereby promoting C–F bond cleavage. The simulation trajectory further supports this acceleration mechanism: the reaction initiates as early as 0.05 ps, marked by clear elongation of the C2–O3 bond to 2.28 Å. As electrons continue to delocalise towards the GenX molecule, the C3–F bond elongates to 2.09 Å at 0.65 ps, showing signs of cleavage. At this point, the fluorine atom carries a greater negative charge (–0.29 e, Supplementary Fig. 19 and Supplementary Table 7). The spin density then delocalises further towards the C4 region, enhancing electron transfer. At 1.25 ps, the C4–F bond elongates to 2.13 Å, and the fluorine atom carries a charge of −0.33 e. This corresponds to a net gain of 0.25 electrons relative to that at 0 ps. Earlier, at 0.65 ps, the C3–F bond had already undergone notable elongation, with the fluorine atom capturing 0.22 electrons, thereby triggering reductive degradation. Within 3.00 ps, two fluorine species detach from the GenX molecule, confirming that eaq− concentration is a key determinant of defluorination efficiency. This result offers a mechanistic explanation for the experimental observation that, at high concentrations, the system achieves over 60% defluorination efficiency and removes more than 97% of GenX (Fig. 1). In realistic environments, the likelihood of two or more eaq− acting simultaneously on a single PFAS molecule is extremely low. Our simulation represents a limiting-case model designed to account for the experimental. It effectively demonstrates that increasing the sulphite concentration leads to a higher density of reactive eaq− in the local environment around GenX, thereby accelerating degradation and revealing the underlying microscopic mechanism.

Hydrated electron structures under alkaline/acidic conditions

To further validate the proposed pH-dependent regulation of eaq− cavity dimensions, we constructed aqueous systems containing one, two or three OH− or H+ ions per simulation box. Each system was subjected to extensive 8 ns NPT ensemble equilibrium simulations (see Supplementary Information Section 14 for details). The results reveal that OH− notably promotes cavity expansion (Supplementary Fig. 21). Under neutral conditions, the radius of the eaq− cavity is 1.55 Å. Subsequently, the addition of OH− causes gradual expansion. Specifically, the radius increases to 1.57 Å (for OH− distant from eaq−) and 1.62 Å (for OH− near eaq−) with one OH⁻ addition, 1.68 Å with two OH− additions and 1.72 Å with three OH− additions. Conversely, in the presence of H+, the cavity radius shrinks to 1.47 Å (with one H+). When the number of H+ exceeds one, no stable eaq− is observed during the 8 ns NPT simulation. These dynamic simulation results demonstrate that pH regulates eaq− formation: acidic conditions inhibit its generation, whereas alkaline conditions enhance its formation. Moreover, these findings are quantitatively supported, confirming that the internal cavity structure of eaq− plays a decisive role in its reactivity. Spin density analysis at the cavity centre reveals that under acidic conditions (in the presence of H+), spin density is highly concentrated on the hydrogen atom (0.1473 e Å−3), while the adjacent eaq− retains only minimal residual spin density (0.00016 e Å−3, Supplementary Fig. 22). Further, as the H+ concentration increases, spin density fully localises on the proton, thereby impeding eaq− formation. Under alkaline conditions (in the presence of OH−), the maximum spin density within the cavity gradually increases with rising OH− concentration. Specifically, it increases from 0.0219 e Å−3 (for OH− distant from eaq− with one OH−), 0.0264 e Å−3 (for OH− near eaq− with one OH−) to 0.0300 e Å−3 (with two OH−) and then to 0.0320 e Å−3 (with three OH−) (Supplementary Fig. 23). A strong correlation is observed between the eaq− cavity radius and maximum spin density (R2 = 0.98, Supplementary Fig. 24), indicating that spin density increases with cavity expansion. These combined changes enhance the electron-transfer capability of eaq−, which promotes PFAS reduction and degradation.

To elucidate the origin of the increased electron density associated with cavity expansion, we analysed the cavity dimensions of eaq− under four alkaline conditions. We found that the expansion becomes more pronounced as OH− approaches the hydrated electron (Supplementary Fig. 21), indicating that electron density enhancement is closely associated with the local OH− distribution around the cavity. To validate this relationship, we removed all OH− ions from the initial configurations and conducted AIMD simulations. In the absence of OH−, the cavity radii of eaq− contracted to 1.52 Å for distant from eaq− with one OH−, 1.52 Å for near eaq− with one OH⁻, 1.57 Å for two OH− and 1.57 Å for three OH−, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 25). These findings confirm that the expansion observed under alkaline conditions is primarily driven by the presence of OH−. Importantly, the simulation results provide a mechanistic explanation for our experimental observations, showing that OH− modulates the solvation microstructure of eaq− by expanding its cavity and increasing local electron density. This, in turn, improves the reactivity of eaq− and accounts for its continued dominance as the active species responsible for GenX degradation at pH 12.

To confirm the universality of the eaq− degradation mechanism, we further investigated the degradation behaviour of three other GenX analogues: hexafluoropropylene oxide trimer acid (HFPO–TA), hexafluoropropylene oxide tetrameric acid (HFPO–TeA) and perfluoro-2,5-dimethyl-3,6-dioxaheptanoic acid (C7 HFPO–TA) (Supplementary Fig. 26). LUMO analysis reveals that the C–F bonds distal to the carboxyl group exhibit substantially greater electron density in their antibonding orbitals (σ*), accounting for their preferential involvement during defluorination (Supplementary Fig. 27). Experimental studies on degradation in the UV/sulphite system demonstrate that these analogues also achieve higher degradation efficiency under alkaline conditions (Supplementary Information Section 16). Although all three PFAS exhibited degradation efficiencies above 87% at both pH 7 and pH 12 with 20 mM sulphite, the defluorination efficiency was markedly higher at pH 12 than under neutral conditions (Supplementary Fig. 28). Dynamics simulations (Supplementary Figs. 29–31) reveal that these compounds display degradation behaviours closely resembling those of GenX. Importantly, NPT ensemble simulations show that eaq− forms an internal cavity with a radius of 1.77 Å (Supplementary Fig. 32). In parallel, the maximum spatial distribution of spin density ranges from 0.0246 to 0.0263 e Å−3 (Supplementary Fig. 33), and the degradation follows a selective pathway, with preferential cleavage of the C–O bond. Complete defluorination occurs within just 3.00 ps across all observed trajectories (Supplementary Figs. 34–36). In addition to confirming the broad applicability of the hydrated electron–induced degradation mechanism, these findings further demonstrate that the eaq− cavity radius governs PFAS degradation pathways. Hirshfeld charge analysis (Supplementary Figs. 37–39 and Supplementary Tables 8–10) reveals that during reductive defluorination, the dissociating fluorine atom gradually accumulates negative charge as the C–F bond elongates. In the HFPO–TA, HFPO–TeA, and C7 HFPO–TA systems, defluorination occurs when the net negative charge on the fluorine atoms reaches approximately 0.21, 0.19 and 0.22, respectively. These results indicate that charge accumulation is closely linked to the solvation dynamics of eaq−: an eaq− cavity with a radius of 1.77 Å (Supplementary Fig. 32) facilitates electron transfer to the terminal site of PFAS molecules and promotes selective C–F bond cleavage through localised spin-density effects. The process begins with eaq− occupying the C–F antibonding orbital, which increases the spin density at the reactive site. The fluorine atom progressively accumulates negative charge (0.19–0.22), which substantially weakens the C–F bond, as reflected in its elongation beyond 2.0 Å. When the electrostatic repulsion between the accumulated charge and the bonding electrons exceeds the C–F bond energy, the bond dissociates. Across all PFAS systems under investigation, a notably consistent charge-transfer behaviour emerges: when the oxygen atom in the ether bond accumulates a negative charge of 0.10–0.16, the bond elongates substantially, initiating reductive degradation; defluorination occurs only when the negative charge on fluorine atoms surpasses 0.19 (Supplementary Fig. 40).

Discussion

This study integrates experiments and AIMD simulations to establish an atomistic, quantitative connection between eaq− solvation microstructure and PFAS degradation. We demonstrate that, in UV/sulphite systems, the cavity radius and spin density distribution of eaq− are key determinants of PFAS degradation efficiency. Previous studies on eaq− 19–23 have proposed electron-transfer pathways20,22 and directionality effects23; however, the present study builds upon these by quantitatively linking eaq− microstructure to defluorination efficiency, rather than to the intrinsic properties of PFAS. By establishing a quantitative structure–activity relationship between eaq− cavity size and degradation efficiency, we demonstrate that increasing the cavity size promotes spin-density delocalisation, enhances electron accessibility to C–F bonds and lowers their dissociation energy barrier. Building on this mechanistic insight, we propose that precise control of the solvation environment (e.g. pH and reductant concentration) enables optimisation of eaq−, achieving over 97% GenX degradation efficiency under tailored conditions. These findings offer fresh insights into PFAS degradation pathways and support the design of efficient, controllable ARPs. The solvation structure–degradation relationship established here provides a theoretical foundation for developing ARPs and offers strategic guidance for advancing environmental remediation technologies.

Methods

Chemicals

GenX (HFPO–DA, C6HF11O3, 97%) and HFPO–TeA (C12HF23O5, 95%) were purchased from Shanghai Maclean Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HFPO–TA (C9HF17O4, 95%) was purchased from J&K Scientific Ltd. (Beijing, China). C7 HFPO–TA (C7HF13O4, 98%) was purchased from Zhengzhou Anmschem Co., Ltd. (Zhengzhou, China). Potassium peroxydisulfate (K2S2O8, 99.5%) was purchased from Shanghai Guangnuo Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sodium sulphite (or sulfite) anhydrous (Na2SO3, 98%) was obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ultrapure water was produced in a PALL Cascada III water purification instrument (PALL, USA). Other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Experimental details

Photochemical treatment of PFAS were conducted in a photoreactor (YM-GHX-SX, Shanghai Yu Ming Instrument Co. Shanghai, China) equipped with 6 UV lamps (T820W, Foshan Huierkang Technology Co. Guang Dong, China), and the wavelength was 253.7 nm and the emission power of each UV lamp is 20.0 W. The degradation experiments were performed in 35 mL solution containing 0.2 mmol L–1 of PFAS. To explore the effect of sulphite concentration on GenX degradation, the sulphite concentration was adjusted to 5–20 mmol L–1. To explore the effect of solution pH on GenX degradation, the solution pH was adjusted to 2, 7 and 12, in which the solution pH at 12 was reported as the suitable condition for the PFAS degradation in the UV/sulphite system28.

After 2 h photochemical reaction, the PFAS concentrations were determined by a liquid chromatography (HPLC, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a conductivity detector (CDD-10Avp) and a shim-pack sceptre column (4.6 mm × 250 mm i.d., 5 μm). The mobile phase was a NaH2PO4/methanol mixture (v/v, 40:60 for GenX, 29:71 for HFPO–TA, HFPO–TeA and C7 HFPO–TA), the flow rate of mobile phase was 1.0 mL/min and the temperature column was 45 °C. The F– concentrations in water were analysed using an ion chromatography instrument (Dionex Aquion IC, Thermo Scientific USA) equipped with an AS23-HC column (4 × 250 mm analytical columns, Thermo Scientific USA).

Theoretical system setup

In experiments, eaq− typically form in an approximately spherical cavity in an aqueous solution. To model this expected cavity, we initially placed 81 water molecules, one GenX molecule and one chloride ion to a periodic box of dimensions 24 × 24 × 24 Å3. Given the approximate length of 8 Å for the GenX molecule, the size of the simulation box was chosen to effectively avoid artificial interaction between the chemical substance and its periodic image. We then performed equilibration simulations for over 8 ns in the NPT ensemble (constant particle number N, pressure P and temperature T) with a time step of 2 fs. After equilibration, the chloride ion was removed from the system, and an additional negative charge was introduced to generate the desired eaq−. Following this, we conducted an additional 3.00 ps of AIMD simulation on these pre-created cavities with a time step of 1 fs. It is important to note that the chloride ion was added solely to facilitate cavity formation. The resulting cavity was surrounded by five to eight water molecules. In this work, analyses were performed on all trajectories with random and distinct initial conditions.

Computational details

To establish the initial periodic box containing 81 water molecules, one GenX anion, and one chloride ion, we employed the GAFF force field implemented by Gromacs software. All subsequent AIMD simulations were performed using the QUICKSTEP46 module in the CP2K47 software (2023.1 version). The unrestricted Kohn-Sham formalism was utilised to capture the dynamics of the eaq−. All NVT simulations were conducted using a Nosé-Hoover48 chain thermostat at 300 K, and the AIMD equations of motion were propagated with a time step of 1 fs. For the electronic structure calculations, we selected the GTH pseudopotentials49 to describe the atomic core and TZV2P basis set50 for valence electrons. The PBE exchange-correlation functional with self-interaction-correction51,52 and Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction53 were adopted simultaneously to ensure accurate treatment of electronic interactions. In the Gaussian and Plane-Wave (GPW) scheme54 implemented in CP2K, the auxiliary plane–wave basis had its energy cutoff set to 600 Ry, while the reference grid cutoff was set to 60 Ry. The temperature for all simulation processes was maintained at room temperature (300 K). The initial coordinates of the hydrated electrons and contaminants for all chemical dynamics trajectories in this work are provided in Supplementary Data 4–7.
