Interpretable machine learning for cardiogram-based biometrics
Ilija Tanasković*, Ljiljana B. Lazarević, Goran Knežević, Nikola Milosavljević, Olga Dubljević, Bojana Bjegojević, Nadica Miljković

Abstract
This study investigates the role of electrocardiogram (ECG) and impedance cardiogram (ICG) features in biometric identification, emphasizing their discriminative capacity and robustness to emotional variability. A total of 29 features spanning four domains (temporal, amplitude, slope, and morphological) are evaluated using random forest (RF) models combined with multiple interpretability methods. Feature importance shows that both ECG- and ICG-derived features are consistently ranked among the top 10 by Gini importance, permutation importance, and SHAP values, with ECG features, particularly QRS-centric descriptors, occupying the highest positions. In parallel, ICG BCX features contribute complementary, however, with lower cross-method stability. Correlation analysis reveals substantial multicollinearity, where the RF distributes and diminishes importance across highly correlated pairs, confirming reduced independent contributions. Statistical analysis identifies 14 features with significant differences between baseline and anger, without a clear pattern by domain. Feature selection with recursive feature elimination and genetic algorithms converges on a subset (14 features) that attains accuracy within 1% of the full set (99%), improving efficiency in storage and computation. These complementary analyses indicate that the individuality required for reliable identification is primarily encoded in QRS-related ECG features across all four domains. Meanwhile, BCX-derived ICG features contribute mainly through amplitude, providing supportive but less stable discriminatory cues. The confirmed resilience of QRS-centric descriptors to emotional variation, where stable inter-individual differences in the QRS complex could be traced to variations in ventricular mass, conduction pathways, and thoracic geometry, may indicate their central role in future models of cardiogram-based identity.



1
Introduction

Biometrics leverages measurable physiological and behavioral characteristics to establish and verify the individual’s identity
[
1
]
. While traditional biometric modalities, such as fingerprints, iris patterns, and facial recognition, have long dominated the field, recent scientific advances have shifted attention toward biometric indicators that are less vulnerable to theft or forgery, particularly biomedical signals. Among these, the electrocardiogram (ECG) has emerged as a promising modality, due to its inherent liveliness detection capability
[
2
,
3
]
. Additionally, multimodal approaches combining ECG with traditional biometric signals or complementary biosignals have attracted increasing interest
[
3
,
4
]
. For example, the impedance cardiogram (ICG), which captures the changes in thoracic impedance linked to the heart’s mechanical activity
[
5
,
6
]
, could provide complementary information to the ECG for identification purposes
[
7
,
8
]
.

An approach that has revolutionized decision-making processes in biometrics over the past few decades is undoubtedly machine learning (ML)
[
2
,
7
,
3
]
. ML techniques have consistently demonstrated high accuracy in biometric identification tasks, including those relying on cardiogram signals
[
2
,
3
]
. However, a major limitation persists: the limited explainability of ML models
[
9
]
. The contribution of specific features extracted, for example, from ECG and ICG signals, to identity determination remains insufficiently understood. This limited understanding of the discriminative power of individual features restricts insights into the fundamental mechanisms driving the identification process, making an important gap in the current literature.

Nevertheless, cardiogram-based biometrics face several real-world challenges, including inconsistency over time and susceptibility to emotional states
[
3
]
. Because the autonomic nervous system regulates cardiovascular activity, emotional arousal directly modulates cardiac signals
[
10
]
. For example, emotions such as anger can greatly disrupt identification accuracy as shown in our previous work
[
8
]
, whereas others, such as anxiety or stress, exert a relatively small impact
[
11
,
12
]
.

In this study, we move beyond predictive modeling, which has already established the strong biometric potential of cardiogram signals
[
8
,
3
,
2
]
, and instead adopt an exploratory perspective
[
13
,
14
]
. Specifically, we aim to uncover the underlying principles of individuality by examining whether distinguishing features reside in the amplitude, morphology, interval, or rhythm variability of ECG and ICG signals, and how these features are modulated by emotional states.

1.1
Research questions

Our previous studies
[
15
,
8
]
demonstrated that a multimodal approach utilizing fiducial-based features extracted from both ECG and ICG signals achieved the highest biometric identification accuracy, with statistically significant results. These findings, consistent with other predictive modeling efforts in the field
[
3
,
2
]
, reaffirm the strong biometric potential of cardiogram signals. However, the emphasis of these studies has predominantly been on optimizing prediction accuracy, with limited attention to the underlying relationships between variables or the physiological origins underlying biometric identification.

Building on the conceptual distinction between prediction and explanation
[
13
,
14
]
, the present study adopts an exploratory perspective. Rather than focusing solely on whether individuals can be identified, the aim is to elucidate which signal properties of ECG and ICG carry individual-specific information. This includes examining whether inter-individual variability is expressed through the heartbeat amplitude, waveform morphology, and shape, or the variability of heart rhythm across time. Addressing these questions represents an important step toward moving beyond proof-of-concept predictive performance and developing a deeper understanding of the physiological mechanisms that underpin the robustness of cardiogram-based biometrics in applied settings.

Accordingly, the study is guided by the following research questions:

1.

What part of the cardiogram signals contains the essential information for biometric identification?

2.

How does redundancy or correlation among features influence their measured importance in ML models, such as random forests (RF)?

3.

Which features are the most affected by emotional state changes, and do particular feature types (
e.g.
, time, amplitude, slope, or morphology) exhibit consistent sensitivity to emotional variations?

2
Materials and methods

Our publicly available dataset is utilized for the study, comprising ECG and ICG signals recorded from 202 healthy psychology students (37 males, 163 females, and 2 who preferred not to disclose their sex; mean age = 20.18 ± 1.88 years, with 9 NA for age)
[
16
]
. ECG and ICG signals are acquired simultaneously at a sampling frequency of 2000 Hz. ECG signals are recorded using Lead II electrodes placed according to the Einthoven’s triangle, and ICG signals are acquired with four surface electrodes positioned on the subjects’ backs.
[
17
,
15
,
8
]

The recording protocol consists of two distinct parts. The initial group of 59 recordings comprises two segments, each approximately two minutes in duration: relaxation (baseline) and anger induction. The second group (143 recordings) includes three segments: baseline, neutral, and anger. A neutral phase is incorporated to control for confounding variables, such as mental engagement. Both neutral and anger-inducing fantasy materials are pre-tested in a pilot study
[
17
]
. For this study, only the baseline and anger segments are utilized. During the baseline phase, subjects were instructed to sit calmly and relax, and to optionally close their eyes. During the anger-induction phase, participants listened to an audio-guided scenario designed to evoke anger. Detailed descriptions of the recording protocols are available in our previous publications
[
17
,
8
]
.

2.1
Analytical strategy

The visual representation of the proposed methodology is shown in Figure
1
. The pipeline begins with preprocessing to reduce noise and prepare the cardiograms, followed by delineation to identify fiducial points, that is, characteristic landmarks tied to key cardiovascular events. These two steps, adopted from our previous work
[
15
,
8
]
, provide the basis for an extended feature set, enabling a more nuanced analysis. An RF is first trained and evaluated using all 29 features, which serves as the base model and benchmark, with identification accuracy used as the reference metric for all subsequent comparisons.
Several analyses are then conducted in parallel to identify an approach-specific subset of features that are crucial for identification. First, feature importance is estimated with three methods (Gini importance, permutation feature importance, and SHAP values), from which the top 10 features are taken and intersected to produce the first subset, providing a stable consensus ranking. Second, feature selection is performed using recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV) and a genetic algorithm (GA), each producing a data-driven subset without manual thresholds. The intersection of these two subsets defines the second subset, which emphasizes selection stability. Third, correlation analysis is conducted using Pearson and Spearman coefficients to identify clusters of highly correlated features, which are then used to assess cluster-level contribution and to examine how multicollinearity influences importance estimation. Finally, statistical testing across emotional states identifies features that change significantly, and this inference is complemented by training the Random Forest on three subsets: all features, the non-significant set, and the significant set, to compare predictive behavior. The outcomes from these four paths are synthesized to localize where identity-related information resides in ECG and ICG and to characterize how emotional variability shapes those signals. The steps of the proposed pipeline correspond to the subsections in Materials and methods and Results, which is intended to make the manuscript easier to follow.

2.2.1
Preprocessing and delineation

ECG and ICG signals are filtered using the
4^{th}
order Butterworth bandpass filter to reduce both high-frequency and low-frequency noise. The frequency bandwidth is set to 1 Hz – 40 Hz for ECG recordings and 0.5 Hz – 40 Hz for ICG recordings
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
. All filters are applied in a bidirectional manner to compensate for zero-phase distortion. For R peak detection, we employ the modified Pan-Tompkins algorithm
[
24
]
. The position of the peaks is double-checked automatically by verifying the location as a maximum within ± 30 ms of the detected peak, which roughly corresponds to the minimum QRS duration in healthy adults
[
25
]
. The C point is detected as the maximum value of the ICG signal occurring between two consecutive R peaks in the ECG
[
6
,
26
]
. To further reduce noise, ensemble averaging is conducted, which involves averaging 10 consecutive ECG and ICG beats (templates)
[
27
]
. This approach effectively minimizes noise, particularly noise with a mean value of zero (such as white noise), although it comes at the cost of reducing variability within the averaging cohort
[
5
]
. For this process, ECG and ICG templates are created with a duration of 750 ms, comprising 250 ms before the R and C peaks and 500 ms following the peaks for ECG and ICG, respectively
[
5
,
8
]
. A total of 9 averages are extracted per segment (baseline and anger), resulting in 18 samples per individual. The choice of nine averages is made heuristically, as it represents the maximum number of non-overlapping beats (9 averages × 10 beats) that could be extracted from each segment while ensuring equal representation across all subjects. Besides averaging the ensemble, the RR interval and RC interval are averaged on the selected cohort, which are used as features shown in Table
1
. On averaged ECG signals, we also detect the positions of Q and S points, along with T1, T, and T2 points, which represent the start, peak, and the end of the T wave, respectively
[
11
]
. Additionally, from the ICG signals, besides the C point, the B and the X points are identified, corresponding to the opening and closing of the aortic valve
[
6
,
5
]
. These delineation steps are adopted from our previous studies
[
15
,
8
]
.

2.2.2
Fiducial-based feature extraction

The fiducial-based features represent relationships between two or more fiducial points, capturing the connections between key events in the cardiovascular system. Depending on the type of relationship, these features can be broadly categorized into temporal, amplitude, slope, and morphological features
[
27
,
30
,
8
]
. All temporal features, except for RR, are corrected using Bazett’s formula to compensate for potentially high heart rate. A list of the extracted features, along with their signal of origin, domain, description, and references, is provided in Table
1
. In this work, we extend prior feature sets by broadening temporal, amplitude, and morphological descriptors, and by introducing a new slope, also called angular, domain that links changes in amplitude with timing. We also develop additional temporal features that connect ECG and ICG fiducials, denoted as ECG\ICG features. These extensions build on and generalize ideas from Patro et al.
[
30
]
and Antić et al.
[
15
]
, while maintaining compatibility with established measures in the literature.

To understand the distribution of the features listed in Table
1
, we calculate the Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients with the appropriate
p
-value. From this analysis, we identify pairs of features with high correlation, where the absolute value of the correlation coefficient exceeds 0.7. These pairs are represented as an adjacency list, which is then interpreted as a graph structure. In this representation, features are nodes and significant correlations are edges. Groups of nodes, connected in the form of islands, are treated as clusters of highly related features. These clusters represent groups of features that share similar correlation patterns, offering a descriptive view of the structure present in the dataset. It should be noted, however, that correlation does not imply causation
[
34
]
, and these clusters should be interpreted as descriptive structures rather than evidence of direct underlying mechanisms.

Decision-making

, pandas
[

[

, pickle
[

, random
[

, scikit-learn
[

, SciPy
[

, and SHAP
[

]
. In this study, we employ the RF algorithm, which demonstrated superior performance compared to other ML models in our previous research
[
8
]
. Additionally, RF inherently provides a ranked list of feature importance without requiring additional processing steps
[
42
,
43
,
39
,
44
]
, making it a strong candidate for our research, as it directly supports the central aim of identifying which aspects of the ECG and ICG signals carry individual-specific information. It is important to note that hyperparameter tuning is intentionally omitted at all stages of the ML pipeline, as its computational expense is deemed excessive relative to the marginal increase in accuracy observed
[
8
]
. The focus is therefore on using ML as a benchmark to track relative changes and improve interpretability, particularly for understanding the contribution of each cardiogram to biometric identification, so the default values for hyperparameters are employed
[
39
]
. However, thorough hyperparameter tuning was performed in our previous research
[
18
]
, in the present study, both the sample size and the feature space are substantially increased (from 8-12 up to 18 samples per individual and from 17 up to 29 extracted features), which does not guarantee that the previously optimized hyperparameters would yield the best performance under these new conditions. Moreover, prior work has shown that hyperparameter tuning in RF can indeed improve performance, but often at the cost of significant computational demand
[
45
]
.

We assume a closed-world scenario, meaning individuals not registered in the database cannot access the identification system. Consequently, the problem is designed as a multi-class (multinomial) classification task, where each individual represents a distinct class. The dataset is split into training and test sets using a 33% test set ratio with stratification. This ensures that each individual’s information is proportionally represented in both the training and test datasets. RF is evaluated solely on the test set, which does not participate in the training process, using standard classification metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1 score.

2.3.1
Feature importance

Feature importance in this study is evaluated using three complementary metrics: Gini importance (
i.e.
, reduction in impurity), permutation feature importance, and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)
[
42
,
46
,
43
,
41
]
. In the first method, feature importance estimation is embedded in the training process and the importance scores are scaled so that their sum equals 1
[
39
,
43
]
. This approach is computationally efficient and directly integrated into the RF training process, providing an immediate understanding of which features influence the model. In the second approach, the feature importance is evaluated using the permutation feature importance method with
n_repeat
parameter set to 100 on the independent test set. Permutation-based importance is robust to features with numerous categories
[
47
]
and can be computed on an independent test set
[
39
]
, ensuring a more reliable evaluation of feature relevance. On the other hand, this method is computationally more intensive, as it involves shuffling feature values multiple times and re-evaluating the model to estimate importance. As the third method, we compute SHAP values, which attribute each prediction to individual features based on cooperative game theory
[
41
]
. In the multinomial classification, SHAP produces a three-dimensional output where feature contributions are calculated for each sample and for each class, reflecting how much a given feature pushes the prediction toward or away from each possible class. To obtain a global view, these values are averaged per sample and then across all samples within each class, resulting in the mean importance of each feature for that class.

Since each of these methods captures feature importance from a different perspective, they are applied in parallel, consistent with ensemble-based strategies for feature selection
[
48
]
. As feature importance methods do not explicitly eliminate less important features, but rather provide a ranked list, we use thresholding to select only the ten highest-ranked features. The intersection of the sets is then taken, keeping only those features consistently identified among the top ten by all three methods. This strategy improves the stability of the selected features, though it comes at the expense of diversity, as unique features highlighted by a single method are excluded
[
48
]
. The resulting subset is evaluated by retraining the RF model and comparing performance against the base model that included all features. Importantly, these evaluation metrics are used solely for benchmarking the stability and informativeness of the selected feature set, and not as predictive validation in the sense of classification performance.

Assessing feature importance in datasets exhibiting multicollinearity is subject to correlation bias
[
46
]
. This arises when pairs or groups of highly correlated variables influence the importance scores assigned to individual features
[
46
]
. The analysis is conducted using clusters of highly correlated features. The collective influence of each cluster on model accuracy is assessed by randomly shuffling all features within a cluster and monitoring the resulting decrease in classification accuracy on the test set. Moreover, we examine the relationship between the Gini importance scores of individual features within and outside their respective clusters. From each cluster, a representative feature (
i.e.
, a feature that is correlated with all features in a cluster) is selected and individually shuffled to disrupt its association with the target variable. The RF model is retrained, and Gini importance scores are recalculated. The relative changes in importance scores are analyzed for highly correlated feature pairs. In contrast, the distribution of changes across all remaining features is summarized using descriptive statistics (minimum, maximum, median, the
25^{th}
percentile, and the
75^{th}
percentile values).

2.4
Feature selection

In contrast to feature importance methods, which provide a ranked list of features according to their estimated significance, feature selection methods explicitly eliminate less informative features from the dataset. This approach removes the need for additional thresholding strategies to determine a final subset, since the selection process itself yields a reduced feature set along with a direct evaluation of its performance, which we validate on an independent test set. In this study, feature selection is conducted using two methods: recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV) and a genetic algorithm (GA).

RFECV starts with the complete feature set and iteratively removes the least informative features, with the number of eliminated features per step determined by a predefined parameter (commonly one). At each iteration, model performance is evaluated using classification accuracy under cross-validation, and the process continues until the subset that yields the highest predictive performance is identified
[
43
,
39
]
. The minimum number of features is fixed at a predefined parameter of one, and the procedure continues by iteratively discarding the least informative feature until the optimal subset is determined
[
43
,
39
]
.

In contrast, the GA follows a stochastic search strategy inspired by natural selection, where candidate feature subsets evolve toward optimal solutions through iterative refinement. The algorithm begins with an initial population of randomly generated subsets, and over multiple generations, applies crossover to exchange features between subsets and mutation to introduce random variations, thereby preserving diversity and reducing the risk of premature convergence. The fitness of each subset is assessed according to model performance, and the best-performing candidates are retained for subsequent iterations. In this study, the GA is implemented with a population size of 50 and evolved for 50 generations to balance exploration of the feature space with computational cost
[
49
]
. The population size and number of generations are chosen heuristically, as they are often tuned empirically based on the feature set and dataset properties. At the same time, other parameters are kept at predefined values
[
50
,
51
]
. For the GA-based feature selection, we used the implementation available at GitHub
[
52
]
.

As in the case of feature importance, the final set is produced by intersecting the features selected by both methods. The resulting consensus subset is then used to retrain the RF model, and its performance is compared with the results of the base model. Finally, the consensus feature set obtained through feature selection is compared with the stable subset derived from feature importance metrics, allowing us to identify the most consistent discriminators across complementary approaches.

2.5
Emotions and cardiogram-based features

Our previous findings indicate that variability in emotional states challenges the model to generalize effectively
[
8
]
. In this context, generalization refers to the model’s ability to apply patterns learned under one emotional state to another, an ability that tends to deteriorate when affective conditions differ, thereby reducing identification performance.

To examine this effect, we conducted statistical analyses comparing features extracted from baseline and anger segments to identify those significantly affected by emotional changes. Firstly, the Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to each feature within the baseline and anger groups to assess for normal distribution. Depending on the outcome, either a parametric test (independent t-test) or a non-parametric test (Mann-Whitney U test) was employed to evaluate group differences. To control for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes were calculated in parallel: Cohen’s
d
for the independent t-test, and Cliff’s
\delta
for the Mann-Whitney U test, providing an estimate of practical relevance alongside statistical significance, as recommended in
[
53
]
. Cliff’s
\delta
was calculated using the Python implementation available in the
cliffsDelta
repository on GitHub
[
54
]
. Features with adjusted
p
-values below the significance threshold
(p<0.05)
were considered significantly influenced by emotional changes.

Due to multicollinearity, we also examine whether highly correlated pairs of features can yield opposite statistical outcomes, for example, one feature showing a significant baseline–anger difference while its counterpart did not. Identifying such pairs helps pinpoint potential pitfalls because it may reveal a big issue in the development of emotion-agnostic biometric systems.

To further investigate the predictive implications of these findings, we evaluated how emotional variability impacts model performance, specifically identification accuracy. Given that the dataset includes two segments corresponding to different emotional states, the RF model was first trained on one segment using all available features and validated through stratified 4-fold cross-validation. Generalization was then assessed by evaluating on samples from the other (unseen) segment. The results, reported in terms of accuracy and F1 score, served as benchmark values. The model was subsequently retrained using two additional feature sets: (i) features that did not differ significantly across emotional states, and (ii) features that did show significant emotion-related variation. Both subsets underwent the same cross-validation and generalization procedures. These complementary analyses allowed us to determine whether excluding emotion-sensitive features yields more stable identification performance, in comparison to the emotion-sensitive subset.

Discussion

In line with previous studies
[
15
,
8
]
, this study delves deeper into the analysis of the features by employing an extended feature set and a larger number of samples per class than our previous studies
[
15
,
8
]
. The updated feature set incorporates a broader representation of temporal, amplitude, and morphological descriptors compared with prior work. All interval-based features (except for RR interval) are now normalized using Bazett’s correction to account for heart rate variability and thereby reduce the potential confounding effect of emotional state, whereas in the earlier set, only selected intervals were corrected, indicated as int2
[
8
]
. A key enhancement is the inclusion of slope (angular) features, such as QR_slope, RS_slope, TT1_slope, TT2_slope, CB_slope, and CX_slope, which capture the rate of change in waveform segments and thus provide complementary information beyond amplitudes and intervals. Moreover, the new set also extends amplitude and morphological descriptors (
e.g.
, RT_amp and ECGQRScrest), resulting in a richer and more balanced feature space.

4.1
Base RF model evaluation

The RF classifier exhibits excellent performance across all evaluation metrics, consistently achieving values of approximately 99%. However, the performance metrics in this study are approximately 2% higher than those reported in
[
8
]
and around 3% higher than the results from our initial study
[
15
]
. ML optimization is not the primary focus of this study; instead, emphasis is placed on analyzing the relationships and contributions of features to the classification process. The improved identification accuracy of RF in the current study can likely be attributed to the increased number of samples per subject, which is expanded to 18, whereas previous studies included only 8 or 12 samples, depending on the protocol. Additionally, the feature set has been extended from 17 features in previous studies to 29 features in this research, inspired by Patro et al., (2022)
[
30
]
. Furthermore, the neutral segments are disregarded, and only the baseline and anger segments are utilized for feature extraction.

4.2
Interpretation of correlation and clustering patterns

The correlation analysis reveal substantial interdependencies among variables. Given the high similarity between Pearson and Spearman correlation patterns, only the Pearson heatmap is presented in Figure
2
, while the corresponding Spearman heatmap is provided in the Supplementary materials
[
55
]
. Twenty-seven feature pairs exhibit a strong Spearman correlation coefficient
(|\rho|>0.7)
, indicating robust monotonic associations insensitive to outliers. Pearson correlation confirms these findings, with the same 27 pairs showing high linear correlations and an additional four pairs exceeding the 0.7 threshold in absolute value, suggesting that linear relationships predominate, but are complemented by monotonic non-linear patterns. Overall, these results emphasize the redundancy inherent in ECG- and ICG-derived features, reflecting the physiological interconnectedness of cardiac signals. Despite this, multicolinearity does not impair the learning process of the RF model, consistent with prior findings
[
44
]
, although it could challenge other model types that require mitigation strategies. The clusters of highly correlated features were identified by analyzing the pairs of correlated features as an adjacency list, which forms graph islands interpreted as clusters (
4
), that represent distinct feature groups for the assessment of cluster importance.

Based on the results presented in Table
4
, it can be observed that when Variable 1 is permuted, almost all correlated features (except for RC_int) showed an increase in their importance scores. However, the rate of increase does not always align with the strength of the correlation coefficient. This finding could be interpreted as evidence that when RF assesses feature importance using Gini importance, it tends to reduce the importance of highly correlated features
[
46
]
. This behavior is likely to the fact that these features contain similar information. As individual decision trees in the ensemble randomly select among these features, they do so inconsistently, distributing importance more evenly among them. Additionally, when observing the decrease in classification accuracy when shuffling all features from respective clusters, it could be noticed that features around QRS complex (RQ_amp, RT_amp, QR_slope, RS_amp, RS_slope) exhibit the highest drop, as shown in Table
4
, which corroborates the finding shown in Figure
3
, and echoes findings of Pinto & Cardoso (2020)
[
56
]
, who utilized several explainable AI techniques on deep learning models, highlighting the significance of the QRS complex in decision-making. Their study emphasized not only the importance of the R peak, but also the Q and S points as critical landmarks for deriving features, further demonstrating that even state-of-the-art deep learning models rely on the QRS complex as a reference point for locating other waveforms. Similarly, Silva Teodoro et al. (2017)
[
57
]
emphasized the importance of the QRS angle feature, showing that it consistently produced the best results in feature selection processes, supporting the critical role of QRS-related features. On the other hand, features QX_int, SX_int, BX_int, and TX_int exhibit the lowest decrease in accuracy (only 0.33%). This observation is consistent with the intersection of all three feature importance metrics, where electromechanical intervals (defined as the temporal differences between ECG and ICG events, denoted here as ECG\ICG) are absent. Their exclusion from the most influential subset is intriguing, as several of these intervals have physiological interpretations, making them an interesting target for future research.

4.3
Insights from feature importance analysis

Building on the strong classification performance of the RF model, feature contributions were examined using three complementary approaches. Interestingly, in the sorted order, features extracted from ECG occupy slightly higher positions, particularly within the top five, as shown in Figures A2, A3, and A4 in the Supplementary materials
[
55
]
. This outcome is expected, as it is well-established in the literature that ECG alone can serve as a biometric identifier with excellent performance
[
11
,
32
,
27
,
3
]
. Additionally, there is a substantial overlap between feature importance determined by all three metrics (Gini importance, permutation importance, and SHAP value), with 8 features overlapping when comparing the top 10: (RS_amp, ECGQRScrest, QRS_int, RT_amp, TT2_amp, RQ_amp, RS_slope, QR_slope). By intersecting the top 10 features across all three importance metrics, the resulting consensus set consists exclusively of ECG-based features spanning the domains of time, amplitude, slope, and morphology. This convergence highlights the stability of ECG descriptors across methods. At the same time, ICG and combined ECG/ICG features also appear among the top 10 when each method is considered individually, such as CX_amp and CB_slope in Gini importance, CX_amp in permutation importance, and TX_int in SHAP. This reflects a trade-off inherent to the intersection strategy in ensemble feature selection: while it improves stability, it also reduces diversity by excluding features that are method-specific. In this context, the absence of ICG and ECG/ICG features in the final set suggests they may be less stable across methods, although the repeated appearance of CX_amp in two independent metrics indicates that it remains a promising candidate for further consideration. These results further strengthen the claims of previous studies
[
15
,
8
]
by demonstrating the biometric potential of ICG signals, as proposed in
[
7
]
. Moreover, Figure A2 from the Supplementary Materials
[
55
]
highlights the permutation importance, where four features (CX_slope, BX_int, TX_int, and SX_int) exhibit non-positive importance, suggesting that their inclusion could negatively impact the overall accuracy. Training and evaluating the RF model using only the 8 features that consistently ranked in the top 10 across all importance metrics results in performance only about
\sim
2% lower than with the full dataset. Several features, such as RS_amp, QRS_int, ECGQRScrest, RQ_amp, RS_slope, and QR_slope already highlight the central role of the QRS complex. In addition, RT_amp indicates the relative dominance of the R peak compared to the peak of the T wave, while TT2_amp captures information from the downgoing part of the T wave, pointing to repolarization dynamics as a further contributor to individuality. At the same time, the repeated appearance of CX_amp across two methods suggests that differences between the systolic C point and the X point (closure of the aortic valve) also provide discriminatory value. Together with CB_slope, identified by permutation importance, these findings indicate that the BCX complex of the ICG signal contributes to identification, although less prominently than the QRS complex. This raises a practical question of balancing feature set reduction, where eight features represent just 28% of the original set, with the potential gains in storage efficiency and computational cost.

4.4
Evaluation of feature selection strategies

Feature selection using RFECV results in a reduced subset of 15 features while maintaining the evaluation metrics (
\sim 99\%
) compared to the base model. The features selected by RFECV include, QRS_int, RS_amp, RQ_amp, RT_amp, TT1_amp, TT2_amp, QR_slope, RS_slope, ECGQRScrest, CB_amp, CX_amp, CB_slope, CX_slope, RC_int, and RR. In contrast, the GA approach selects 20 features and achieves marginally higher performance, with 9 eliminated features (BT_int, BX_int, CX_slope, QB_int, QX_int, SB_int, ST_int, TT1_slope, TT2_slope) grouping around temporal and slope features. Despite these differences, the two methods converge on 14 common features, shown in Figure
5
, reflecting a strong consistency across distinct selection strategies. Moreover, when comparing these 14 features with the 8 consistently identified across all feature importance metrics, full overlap is observed, further reinforcing their relevance as a robust core set for discrimination. In the final feature selection set, three ICG-derived descriptors are retained: CB_amp, CX_amp, and CB_slope. Their inclusion is noteworthy, as these features originate from the BCX complex of ICG, which reflects systolic flow and the closure of the aortic valve. This suggests that BCX dynamics provide complementary discriminatory information to the dominant QRS-related features from the ECG. However, their relative stability appears lower, as evidenced by their absence in the SHAP-derived subset. This indicates that while the QRS complex carries the most robust identity-specific signatures, ICG-based BCX features contribute additional, though less stable, discriminatory capacity and may enhance multimodal biometric systems when considered alongside ECG. Although the performance gains of RFECV and GA are marginal and should not be overstated, their ability to maintain very high accuracy on substantially reduced feature sets highlights potential benefits for real-world applications where storage and computational efficiency are important considerations.

4.5
Implications of emotion-sensitive cardiogram features

Inter-individual variability in the selected features plausibly arises from stable anatomical, electrophysiological, and hemodynamic differences. QRS-centric ECG features, such as RS_amp, RQ_amp, RS_slope, QR_slope, ECGQRScrest, and QRS_int, primarily capture ventricular depolarization amplitude, synchrony, and conduction velocity. These measures depend on myocardial mass and geometry, Purkinje network distribution, thoracic anatomy (heart position, chest wall thickness, lung volume), and tissue conductivity, which differ among individuals and yield unique waveform morphology
[
58
,
59
]
. Repolarization-related measures, RT_amp, TT1_amp, and TT2_amp, summarize the size and shape of the T wave,
i.e.
, capturing how the ventricles recover after activation. These differences in tissue properties, even at similar heart rates, could result in taller or more asymmetric T waves in some individuals. Also, RR summarizes intrinsic sinus rate and vagal–sympathetic balance, which indirectly modulate morphology through rate-dependent electrical differences
[
5
]
. ICG BCX features, CB_amp, CX_amp, and CB_slope, present the index of ventricular ejection dynamics: B marks aortic valve opening (pre-ejection termination), C the peak aortic flow (dZ/dt max, related to stroke volume/contractility), and X the aortic valve closure
[
5
,
6
,
26
]
. Their amplitude and slope characteristics depend on left-ventricular contractility, ejection velocity, aortic compliance, valve kinematics, and basal thoracic impedance (blood volume, lung inflation, adiposity), all of which vary across people. The RC_int, time difference between peak ECG and ICG value, links electrical activation to peak mechanical output and is shaped by excitation–contraction coupling efficiency and afterload. Taken together, QRS-based features dominate because they are anchored in relatively fixed structural and conduction properties, while BCX features add subject-specific hemodynamic signatures of systolic function and vascular properties, and T-wave metrics contribute individuality through repolarization gradients, yielding a multimodal profile across depolarization, repolarization, and ejection physiology. Finally, small differences in electrode placement can introduce small amplitude and shape changes that are not perfectly reproducible or fully mitigated by standard skin prep and landmarking. In biometric applications, these are treated as nuisance variation, motivating robust acquisition protocols and feature choices.

Our previous study
[
8
]
demonstrated that when subjects experience different emotional states during enrollment and evaluation, the identification accuracy can drop by up to 13% compared to scenarios where the emotional state remains consistent. The present results identify 14 emotion-sensitive features differing significantly between baseline and anger conditions. Notably, the significantly altered features are not specific to the signal; rather, they belong to ECG-only, ICG-only, as well as ECG\ICG features. However, no clear pattern emerges to suggest that a particular type of feature is more susceptible to emotional changes. Interestingly, features extracted from the QRS complex, such as RQ_amp, RS_amp, QR_slope, RS_slope, and ECGQRS_crest, do not exhibit significant changes between baseline and anger. Moreover, it is noteworthy that all these features are ranked among the top 10 most important features by all three methods, as shown in Figure
3
as well as in Figure
5
, indicating that the features most crucial for identity recognition are minimally affected by emotional states. Although no statistical differences were observed in these QRS-related features when comparing the baseline and anger segments, the corresponding effect sizes are negligible. This indicates that the absence of significant change cannot be generalized to the broader population without caution and highlights the need for further investigation in this direction.

To evaluate predictive implications, RF models were trained on three feature sets: all features, only features without significant baseline–anger differences, and only features with significant differences. Using all features, within-segment performance under cross-validation remains high (
\sim 99\%
accuracy/F1), while generalization to the opposite emotional segment drops to
\sim 90–92\%
(Table
6
). Restricting training to emotion-insensitive features maintains strong within-segment scores (98–99%) but reduces cross-segment performance (87–89%), indicating that excluding emotion-sensitive features does not enhance robustness and can reduce predictive capacity. Conversely, models trained solely on emotion-sensitive features perform markedly worse (within the segment 92 - 93%; cross-segments 66 - 72%), confirming that emotion-driven variance does not benefit identification. Despite the Bazett correction, several intervals still differed significantly, suggesting that additional normalization strategies may be beneficial. Notably, beyond the QRS descriptors that remain stable and highly important, features derived from the T wave and from the ICG BCX complex are split between the significant and non-significant sets; this division likely contributes to their lower stability and lesser overall impact on identification compared with QRS-based features. Lastly, based on all the information provided, it can be concluded that not all features contain the same amount of information, which is expected, and the selection of features depends on the specific goal (
e.g.
, biometrics or mitigation of emotional effects).

4.6
Contributions of the study

Taking into consideration all of the data provided in this study, we now revisit the initial research questions and present a summary of the findings in relation to each of them:

1.

The most reliable information comes from features derived from the QRS complex. Across three feature importance methods, QRS features (ECGQRScrest, RQ_amp, RS_amp, QR_slope, RS_slope, QRS_int) rank consistently high. The intersection of the three top 10 lists yields 8 ECG-only features that keep RF performance high with only a small drop versus all 29 features. Feature selection (RFECV, GA) reaches similarly strong results and also retains amplitude and slope BCX features (
e.g.
, CB_amp, CX_amp, CB_slope), indicating that ICG adds complementary but less stable information across feature importance methods. These patterns are physiologically plausible, since QRS amplitude and slope reflect stable differences in ventricular mass, conduction pathways, and thoracic geometry, while BCX amplitudes and slopes reflect inter-individual variation in systolic ejection, aortic compliance, and valve timing.

2.

The RF model inherently adjusts importance scores for correlated features, confirming their reduced individual contributions. The study shows that almost all highly correlated features (except RC_int) experienced an increase in importance scores upon shuffling, but the rate of increase does not always align with their correlation coefficients.

3.

Statistical analysis reveals that 14 features exhibit significant changes when comparing baseline and anger states. Importantly, no clear pattern emerges regarding which type of features is the most affected. However, features derived from the QRS complex, except for QRS_int, remain stable and do not exhibit significant changes. Predictive analyses are not fully consistent with the statistical findings, which is expected given the negligible effect sizes. Using only features without significant differences does not improve generalization; when training and evaluating on segments with different emotional states, accuracy can drop by
\sim 11\%
. In contrast, restricting the model to statistically significant features aligns with the statistical perspective, as cross-emotion accuracy can decline by up to 23%, indicating that these features capture emotion-related variance that does not generalize well for identification.

4.7
Limitations of the study

Here, we recognize the limitations of the proposed study:

1.

In future work, we will evaluate P-wave features using delineators optimized for low-amplitude/abnormal morphologies, such as
[
60
,
61
]
. Given ongoing challenges in P-wave delineation—particularly under pathological conditions, we will consider approaches shown to be robust in recent studies as potential candidates for future experiments.

2.

Although frequency‑domain heart rate variability (HRV) metrics are often used to quantify autonomic nervous system activity and the impact of emotions, our 2-minute segments are too short to capture reliable spectral information. HRV guidelines and recent reviews recommend at least 1–2 min (preferably 5 min) of stable data for the HF and LF bands
[
62
,
63
]
, noting that shorter windows lack prognostic power
[
63
]
. With only
\sim
2 min per emotion, we could not extract multiple independent frequency‑domain samples for ML analysis without violating these methodological recommendations.

3.

The dataset exhibits an unbalanced sex distribution, primarily due to the demographics of the psychology student population recruited for this study, which reflects sex disparity worldwide
[
64
,
65
]
. This imbalance is not intentional and is not addressed in the analysis. Furthermore, sex is not used as a stratification variable, nor are potential differences in identification accuracy between male and female participants investigated.

4.

In this study, temporal features are corrected for heart rate using Bazett’s formula to minimize the influence of emotion-related changes in heart rate. Future research explores alternative normalization techniques, as suggested in earlier work (
e.g.
, Israel et al.,(2005)
[
11
]
), to assess whether different corrections affect model performance.

5.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the most reliable identity signal resides in QRS-centric ECG features across temporal, amplitude, slope, and morphology, while ICG BCX features add complementary but less stable cues. Random forest analyses, correlation structure, and feature selection converge on a compact, high-performing subset, confirming that much of the discriminative content can be retained with fewer features. Emotional variability continues to challenge cross-state generalization, and removing emotion-sensitive variables alone does not yield systematic improvements. Multicollinearity analyses indicate that information is distributed across correlated groups rather than isolated in single predictors, guiding principled reduction strategies. Physiologically, the dominance of QRS amplitude and slope is consistent with stable inter-individual differences in ventricular mass, conduction pathways, and thoracic geometry. Taken together, these findings clarify where identity-related information is localized in cardiograms, centered on QRS features with supportive BCX contributions, and they help connect observable waveforms to stable inter-individual physiological variation.
