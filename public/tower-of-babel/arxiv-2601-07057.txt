Idempotents and Powers of Ideals in Quandle Rings
Valeriy Bardakov, Mohamed Elhamdadi

Abstract
. This article addresses two central problems in the theory of quandle rings. First, motivated by Conjecture 3.10 in Internat. J. Math. 34 (2023), no. 3, Paper No. 2350011: for a semi-latin quandle X X , every nonzero idempotent in the integral quandle ring ℤ ⁡ [ X ] \mathbb{Z}[X] necessarily corresponds to an element of X X , we investigate idempotents in quandle rings of semi-latin quandles. Precisely, we prove that if the ground ring is an integral domain with unity, then the quandle ring of Core( ℤ \mathbb{Z} ) admits only trivial idempotents. Second, powers of augmentation ideals in quandle rings have only been computed in few cases previously. We extend the computations to include dihedral quandles and commutative quandles. Finally, we examine idempotents in quandle rings of 2 2 -almost latin quandles and apply these results to compute the automorphism groups of their integral quandle rings.

[

[

[

. It was proved in

that quandle rings of non-trivial quandles are not power-associative rings and that if the ground ring is Noetherian and the quandle is finite then the quandle ring is both left and right Noetherian. Zero divisors in quandle rings were investigated in
[

]
. An initiation to homological study of quandle rings was given in
[

where a complete characterization of derivations of quandle algebras of dihedral quandles over fields of characteristic zero has been given, and dimension of the Lie algebra of derivations has been investigated. The question of whether a given quandle ring possesses non-trivial idempotents was initiated in
[
BPS1
]
. In
[

a complete characterization of the idempotents of certain quandle coverings was given. In
[

2.
Basics of Quandles and Quandle Rings

We review the basics of quandles needed for this article. All definitions and known results can be found in the book
[
BES
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
quandle

X
is a
set with a binary operation
(x,y)\mapsto x*y

satisfying the following conditions.

(1)
For any
x\in X
,

x*x=x
.

(2)
For any
y\in X
, the right multiplication
R_{y}:x\mapsto x*y
is a bijection.

(3)
For any
x,y,z\in X
, we have

(x*y)*z=(x*z)*(y*z).

Notice that Axioms (2) and (3) can be combined to state that
R_{y}
is an automorphism of the quandle
(X,*)
. The notions of quandle homomorphisms and isomorphisms are natural. The following are some examples of quandles.

•

Any non-empty set
X
with the operation
x*y=x
for any
x,y\in X
is
a quandle called a
trivial
quandle.

•

Any group
G
with the binary operation
x*y=yxy^{-1}
is a quandle called a
conjugation quandle
.

•

The binary operation
x*y=yx^{-1}y
defines a quandle structure on any group
G
called the
Core
quandle of
G
and denoted Core(
G
). If furthermore,
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is the abelian group of integers modulo
n
, where
n
is a positive integer, then the operation is denoted by
x*y=-x+2y
and the quandle is called the
dihedral
quandle and denoted by
R_{n}
.

•

A
generalized Alexander quandle
is defined by a pair
(G,f)
where

G
is a group and
f\in{\rm Aut}(G)
,
and the quandle operation is defined by

x*y=f(xy^{-1})y
.
If
G
is abelian, this is called an
Alexander quandle
.

•

A function
\psi:X\times X\rightarrow A
for an abelian group
A
is
called a
quandle
2
-cocycle

[
CJKLS
]
if it satisfies

\psi(x,y)+\psi(x*y,z)=\psi(x,z)+\psi(x*z,y*z)

and
\psi(x,x)=0
for all
x,y,z\in X
.
For a quandle
2
-cocycle
\psi
the set
X\times A
is a quandle under the operation

(x,a)*(y,b)=(x*y,a+\psi(x,y))
for
x,y\in X
,
a,b\in A
,
and it is called an
abelian extension
of
X
by
A
, see
[
CENS
]
.

Given a quandle
X
, the subgroup
\operatorname{Inn}(X)
, of the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
, is the group generated by the right multiplications
R_{x}
. When the natural action of
\operatorname{Inn}(X)
on
X
is transitive then
X
is said to be a
connected
quandle.

Let
(X,*)
be a quandle and
\mathbbm{k}
be an integral domain (a commutative ring without zero divisors) with unity. Let
\mathbbm{k}[X]
be the set of all formal expressions of the form
\sum_{x\in X}\alpha_{x}e_{x}
, where
\alpha_{x}\in\mathbbm{k}
such that all but finitely many
\alpha_{x}=0
. The set
\mathbbm{k}[X]
is a free
\mathbbm{k}
-module with basis
\{e_{x}\mid x\in X\}
and admits a product given by

\Big(\sum_{x\in X}\alpha_{x}e_{x}\Big)\Big(\sum_{y\in X}\beta_{y}e_{y}\Big)=\sum_{x,y\in X}\alpha_{x}\beta_{y}e_{x*y},

where
x,y\in X
and
\alpha_{x},\beta_{y}\in\mathbbm{k}
. This turns
\mathbbm{k}[X]
into a ring called the
quandle ring
(or the
quandle algebra
) of
X
with coefficients in
\mathbbm{k}
. When
X
is a non-trivial quandle then the quandle ring
\mathbbm{k}[X]
is non-associative ring. There is a natural identification of
X
with a subset of
\mathbbm{k}[X]
via the map sending
x
to
e_{x}
.

The surjective ring homomorphism
\varepsilon:\mathbbm{k}[X]\rightarrow\mathbbm{k}
given by

\varepsilon\Big(\sum_{x\in X}\alpha_{x}e_{x}\Big)=\sum_{x\in X}\alpha_{x}

is called the
augmentation map
. The kernel
\Delta(X)
of
\varepsilon
is a two-sided ideal of
\mathbbm{k}[X]
, called the
augmentation ideal
of
\mathbbm{k}[X]
.

Notation: Through the whole article, it is important to make a distinction between the product in the quandle, denoted by
*
, and the product, denoted by
\cdot
or juxtaposition, in the quandle ring.

One of the interesting problems in quandle rings is the calculation of idempotents, that are element
u\in\mathbbm{k}[X]
such that
u^{2}=u
. Any element of
X
is idempotent called trivial idempotent. A more interesting case of quandle rings is when
\mathbbm{k}=\mathbb{Z}
.
For a full description of idempotents of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
one needs to understand the structure of
X
.
If
X=\coprod_{i}X_{i}
is the disjoint union of quandles, then for the set of idempotents
I(\mathbb{Z}[X])
, the following inclusion holds
I(\mathbb{Z}[X])\supseteq\coprod_{i}I(\mathbb{Z}[X_{i}])
.
Also, if

X=\{{1},{2},\ldots,{m}\}
is the
m
element trivial quandle, then

I(\mathbb{Z}[X])=\{e_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{m}\alpha_{j}(e_{j}-e_{1}),~~\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\}

(see Proposition 13.21 of
[
BES
]
). One of the interesting classes of quandles is the class of
latin
quandles. Recall that a quandle is called
latin
if multiplication on the left by any element is a bijection.
In particular, the dihedral quandles of odd orders are latin. For the class of latin quandles, we have the following well known

Conjecture 2.2
.

(
[
BES
, Conjecture 13.19]
) Let
X
be a finite latin quandle,
u
be a non-zero idempotent in the integral quandle ring
\mathbb{Z}[X]
. Then

(1)

The element
u
is a trivial idempotent;

(2)

\varepsilon(u)=1
.

If we take a field
\mathbbm{k}
instead
\mathbb{Z}
, then this conjecture does not hold. The first item of this conjecture follows from the following proposition. For the second item of this conjecture see Proposition
4.4
.

Proposition 2.3
.

(
[
BES
, Proposition 12.18]
)
Let
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be a finite latin quandle. Then the following assertions hold:

(1)

The element
w=e_{x_{1}}+\cdots+e_{x_{n}}
lies in the center
Z(\mathbbm{k}[X])
.

(2)

If
\mathbbm{k}
is a field with characteristic not dividing
n
, then
\frac{1}{n}w
is idempotent.

For quandle rings which have only trivial idempotents, it is easy to find the automorphism group of the quandle ring.

Proposition 2.4
.

If a quandle
X
has only trivial idempotents, then
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}[X])=\operatorname{Aut}(X).

In the next sections we will consider quandles of special types and study idempotents in their quandle rings.

3.
Infinite Quandles

The question of idempotents in quandle rings can be formulated not only for finite latin quandles but for infinite latin quandles and for semi-latin quandles.

As example, consider the Core(
\mathbb{Z})
in which the operation is defined by the rule
a*b=2b-a
, for all
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
L_{a}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
be the operator multiplication from the left, i.e.
L_{a}(x)=a*x
for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. We see that
L_{2}(x)=2(x-1)
. Hence,
L_{2}
is injective but is not bijective and hence is not invertible. Furthermore,

L_{a}^{2}(x)=4x-3a\not=x
, this means that
L_{a}
is not involutive. On the other hand, if
R_{a}
,
a\in\mathbb{Z}
is the operator multiplication from the right, then
R_{a}
is an automorphism and
R_{a}^{2}=id
.

Next, we want to understand the structure of the augmentation ideal
\Delta(Core(\mathbb{Z}))
. To do so, we will consider
\mathbb{Z}
as multiplicative group with the generator
t
and write

\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,t^{-1},1,t,\ldots\}.

Then the binary operation in
Core(\mathbb{Z})
is defined by the rule
t^{i}*t^{j}=t^{2j-i}
. Further, for simplicity, we will denote the basis elements in quandle ring by
e_{i}
instead
e_{t^{i}}
. Then the product
e_{i}\cdot e_{j}
is given by
e_{i}\cdot e_{j}=e_{2j-i}
.

On the elements of
\mathbb{Z}
we have a linear order:
t^{i}<t^{j}
if
i<j
. Further, for any
t^{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

t^{i}*t^{k}=t^{2k-i}>t^{j}*t^{k}=t^{2k-j}.

On the other side

t^{k}*t^{i}=t^{2i-k}<t^{k}*t^{j}=t^{2j-k}.

Hence, this order is preserved under multiplication on the left and is not preserved under multiplication on the right. The quandle
Core(\mathbb{Z})
is left orderable but not right orderable
[
BPS1
]
(see also
[
BES
, Propositions 11.3 and 11.5]
).

Let us take

u=\sum_{m\leq i\leq M}\alpha_{i}e_{i},~~\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},

where we assume that
\alpha_{m}\alpha_{M}\not=0
. In this case we say that
\alpha_{m}e_{m}=\min(u)
,
\alpha_{M}e_{M}=\max(u)
, and
supp(u)\subseteq[m,M]
.
We want to find idempotents in
\mathbb{Z}[Core(\mathbb{Z})]
. For element
u
we have

u^{2}=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}e_{2j-i}.

Let us extend the linear order from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{Z}[Core(\mathbb{Z})]
by the rule

\alpha e_{a}<\beta e_{b}~\mbox{if and only if}~\alpha\beta\not=0~\mbox{and}~a<b.

The maximal and minimal elements in
u^{2}
are defined by the rules

\min(u^{2})=\max(u)\min(u),~~\max(u^{2})=\min(u)\max(u).

Using this observation we can prove

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\mathbbm{k}
is an integral domain with unity,
then
\mathbbm{k}[Core(\mathbb{Z})]
has only trivial idempotents.

Proof.

Let

u=\alpha_{m}e_{m}+\ldots+\alpha_{M}e_{M},~~\alpha_{m},\alpha_{M}\in\mathbbm{k}\setminus\{0\}

be a non-zero element in
\mathbbm{k}[Core(\mathbb{Z})]
and
\min(u)=\alpha_{m}e_{m}
,
\max(u)=\alpha_{M}e_{M}
.

If
m=M
, then
u=\alpha_{m}e_{m}
is an idempotent if and only if
\alpha_{m}=1
. This idempotent is trivial.

Let
m<M
. Then

\min(u^{2})=\max(u)\min(u)=\alpha_{M}e_{M}\alpha_{m}e_{m}=\alpha_{M}\alpha_{m}e_{2m-M},

\max(u^{2})=\min(u)\max(u)=\alpha_{m}e_{m}\alpha_{M}e_{M}=\alpha_{m}\alpha_{M}e_{2M-m}.

Let us show that the following inequalities hold

\min(u^{2})<\min(u)<\max(u)<\max(u^{2}).

The first inequality means

\alpha_{M}\alpha_{m}e_{2m-M}<\alpha_{m}e_{m}\Leftrightarrow 2m-M<m\Leftrightarrow m<M.

The second equality holds by definition. The third inequality means

\alpha_{M}e_{M}<\alpha_{m}\alpha_{M}e_{2M-m}\Leftrightarrow M<2M-m\Leftrightarrow m<M.

From the proved inequalities follows that
u^{2}\not=u
.
∎

It is easy to check that if
X
is an infinite field, then
Core(X)
with operation
a*b=2b-a
is a latin quandle.

Question 3.2
.

Is it possible to replace
\mathbb{Z}
in Theorem

with an arbitrary ordered (abelian) group

.

Is it true that for any unital ring
\mathbbm{k}
without zero-divisors and an infinite field

What can we say about
\Delta^{2}(Core(\mathbbm{Z}))

E_{1}=e_{1}-e_{0}
lies in
\Delta^{2}(Core(\mathbbm{Z}))

3.1.
Commutator Subalgebra

Let
A=\mathbbm{Z}[Core(\mathbbm{Z})]
and
A^{\prime}
be its commutator subalgebra that is the subalgebra generated by ring commutators
[u,v]=uv-vu
,
u,v\in A
. It is easy to see that
A^{\prime}
is generated by commutators

[e_{i},e_{j}]=e_{i}e_{j}-e_{j}e_{i}=e_{i*j}-e_{j*i}=e_{2j-i}-e_{2i-j}.

Hence
A^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta(Core(\mathbbm{Z}))
. One can naturally ask if the other inclusion is true?

We have the following

Proposition 3.5
.

The following inclusion holds
\mathbbm{Z}[Core(3\mathbbm{Z})]\subseteq A^{\prime}
.

Proof.

As we know,
\Delta(Core(\mathbbm{Z}))
is generated by elements
E_{a}=e_{a}-e_{0}
and
E_{a}=[e_{\frac{a}{3}},e_{2\frac{a}{3}}]
. Hence, if
a
is divisible by
3
, then this element lies in the commutator subalgebra.

4.
Idempotents in Quandle Rings of Dihedral Quandles

Let
X
be a quandle and
\mathbbm{k}[X]
be its quandle ring. To find idempotents in
\mathbbm{k}[X]
we can write

\mathbbm{k}[X]=\mathbbm{k}e_{0}+\Delta_{\mathbbm{k}}(X)

and any element
u
of this algebra has a form

u=\alpha e_{0}+\delta,~~\alpha\in\mathbbm{k},~~e_{0}\in X,~~\delta\in\Delta_{\mathbbm{k}}(X).

Hence,

u^{2}=(\alpha e_{0}+\delta)^{2}=\alpha^{2}e_{0}+\alpha(e_{0}\delta+\delta e_{0})+\delta^{2}.

Since,
\Delta_{\mathbbm{k}}(X)
is a two-sided ideal,
\alpha(e_{0}\delta+\delta e_{0})\in\Delta_{\mathbbm{k}}(X)
, and
u
is an idempotent if and only if

\alpha^{2}=\alpha,~~\alpha(e_{0}\delta+\delta e_{0})+\delta^{2}=\delta.

From the first equality follows that
\alpha=1
, or
\alpha=0
. In the first case, we have
\varepsilon(u)=1
and in the second case
\varepsilon(u)=0
. Hence, if we want to find idempotent
u
such that
\varepsilon(u)=0
we must find element
u
which lies in
\Delta_{\mathbbm{k}}(X)
.

Suppose that
u
is an idempotent in some groupoid. Then it is easy to see that subgroupoid, which is generated by
u
contains only one element. In particular,

u^{n}=(\ldots((uu)u)\ldots u)=u.

In
\mathbbm{Z}[R_{n}]
put

E_{i}=e_{i}-e_{0},~~i=1,2,\ldots,n-1.

These elements form a basis of
\Delta(R_{n})
. The products of these elements are described by the following lemma, whose proof is a straight computation.

Lemma 4.1
.

The following equalities hold

E_{i}E_{j}=E_{2j-i}-E_{n-i}-E_{2j},

where all indices are reduced modulo
n
and we assume
E_{0}=0
. In particular,

E_{i}^{2}=E_{i}-E_{n-i}-E_{2i}.

At first, suppose that
X=R_{3}=\{0,1,2\}
is the 3-element dihedral quandle and
\mathbbm{k}=\mathbb{Z}
. The elements
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
form a basis of
\Delta(R_{3})
.
In the following proposition, we consider powers of augmented ideals
\Delta=\Delta(R_{3})
and denote
\Delta^{k+1}=\Delta^{k}\Delta
,
k=1,2,\ldots
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For the dihedral quandle
R_{3}
, the powers of the augmentation ideal are given by:

\Delta(R_{3})^{2k+1}=\langle 3^{k}E_{1},\;3^{k}E_{2}\rangle,\quad k\geq 0,

and

\Delta(R_{3})^{2\ell}=\langle 3^{\ell-1}(E_{1}+E_{2}),\;3^{\ell}E_{2}\rangle,\quad\ell\geq 1.

Proof.

Since
R_{3}
is a commutative quandle, we see that
\Delta^{2}
is generated by elements
(E_{1})^{2}
,
E_{1}E_{2}
,
(E_{2})^{2}
. We find that

(E_{1})^{2}=E_{1}-2E_{2},~~E_{1}E_{2}=-E_{1}-E_{2},~~(E_{2})^{2}=E_{2}-2E_{1}.

It is easy to see that elements
E_{1}+E_{2}
and
3E_{2}
form a basis of
\Delta^{2}
.

Hence, we have a basis of induction. By induction hypothesis we assume that for the odd powers,
\Delta(R_{3})^{2(k-1)+1}
has a basis:
\{3^{k-1}E_{1},3^{k-1}E_{2}\}
. Then
\Delta(R_{3})^{2k+1}=\Delta(R_{3})^{2(k-1)+1}\cdot\Delta^{2}
is generated by elements

3^{k-1}E_{1}\cdot(E_{1}+E_{2})=-3^{k}E_{2},~~3^{k-1}E_{1}\cdot 3E_{2}=-3^{k}(E_{1}+E_{2}),

3^{k-1}E_{2}\cdot(E_{1}+E_{2})=-3^{k}E_{1},~~3^{k-1}E_{2}\cdot 3E_{2}=3^{k}(-2E_{1}+E_{2}),

and it is easy to check that the set
\{3^{k}E_{1},3^{k}E_{2}\}
form a basis of
\Delta(R_{3})^{2k+1}
.

By induction hypothesis we assume for the even powers,
\Delta(R_{3})^{2(l-1)}
,
l>1
, has a basis:
\{3^{l-2}(E_{1}+E_{2}),3^{l-1}E_{2}\}
. Then
\Delta(R_{3})^{2l}=\Delta(R_{3})^{2(l-1)}\cdot\Delta^{2}
is generated by the elements

3^{l-2}(E_{1}+E_{2})\cdot(E_{1}+E_{2})=-3^{l-1}(E_{1}+E_{2}),~~3^{l-2}(E_{1}+E_{2})\cdot 3E_{2}=-3^{l}E_{1},

3^{l-1}E_{2}\cdot(E_{1}+E_{2})=-3^{l}E_{1},~~3^{l-1}E_{2}\cdot 3E_{2}=3^{l}(-2E_{1}+E_{2}),

and it is easy to check that the set
\{3^{l-1}(E_{1}+E_{2}),3^{l}E_{2}\}
form a basis of
\Delta(R_{3})^{2l}
.

∎

As corollary we get

Corollary 4.3
.

\mathbbm{Z}[R_{3}]
does not contain non-zero idempotent
u
such that
\varepsilon(u)=0
.

On the other hand, it is not difficult to prove the following

Proposition 4.4
.

If
\mathbbm{k}
is a field of
char(\mathbbm{k})\not=3
, then the element

u=-\frac{1}{3}\left(E_{1}+E_{2}\right)

is an idempotent of
\mathbbm{k}[R_{3}]
such that
\varepsilon(u)=0
.

In Corollary 13.42 of
[
BES
]
one can find idempotents in
\mathbbm{k}[R_{2n}]
.
For
\mathbbm{Z}[R_{4}]
we have the following

Proposition 4.5
.

(1)

The quandle ring
\mathbbm{Z}[R_{4}]

does not contain idempotents for which the value of the augmentation
\varepsilon
is equal to
0
.

(2)

The quandle ring
\mathbbm{Z}[R_{4}]

contains the idempotents

e_{0}-2\beta E_{1}+\beta E_{2}-2\beta E_{3},~~e_{0}+\beta_{1}E_{1}+\beta_{2}E_{2}+(1-\beta_{1})E_{3},\;\text{where}\;\beta,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbbm{Z},

for which the value of
\varepsilon
is equal to
1
.

Proof.

For item (1), let

u=\alpha E_{1}+\beta E_{2}+\gamma E_{3}\in\Delta(R_{4}).

This element is a non-zero idempotent if and only if the following system has non-zero integer solutions

\begin{cases}\alpha=\alpha^{2}-\gamma^{2},\\

From the first and the third equations follows

Item (2) follows from

5.
Commutative Quandles, Powers of Augmentation Ideals and Idempotents

A quandle
(X,*)
is said to be commutative, if
x*y=y*x
for all
x,y\in X
. It is easy to see that any commutative quandle is a latin quandle. Notice that this implies also that commutative quandles are faithful meaning that the map
x\mapsto R_{x}
is injective. This is clear since for any
z
,
R_{x}(z)=R_{y}(z)
implies
x*z=y*z
and thus
x=y
.

Examples 5.1
.

(1)

On the set
\mathbb{Z}_{2n+1}
define the following operation,

x*y=(n+1)(x+y),~~x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{2n+1}.

Then
(\mathbb{Z}_{2n+1},*)
is a commutative quandle. We will denote it by
C_{2n+1}
. If
n=1
, then
C_{3}
is the dihedral quandle
R_{3}
since
2y-x=2(x+y)
mod
3
.

(2)

Let
X=\mathbbm{k}
be a unital ring in which
2
is invertible. Then the operation on
\mathbbm{k}
given by:

x*y=\frac{x+y}{2},~~x,y\in\mathbbm{k},

defines a commutative quandle structure on
\mathbbm{k}
.

(3)

The set of dyadic rationals,

\left\{\frac{m}{2^{k}}~|~m\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\right\}

with the operation
a*b=\frac{1}{2}(a+b)
is a commutative quandle.

(4)

As remarked by Luc Ta in his blog
[
Ta2026CommutativeQuandles
]
, a conjugation quandle
Conj(G)
is commutative if and only if the group
G
is trivial, and a core quandle
Core(G)
is commutative if and only if
G
has exponent 3.

From these examples we see that there are many commutative quandles. We can formulate a question on the classification of finite commutative quandles. The first step towards an answer to this question is the following

Proposition 5.2
.

If
(X,*)
is a finite commutative quandle then it contains an odd number of elements.

Proof.

Let assume that the cardinality of
X
is greater than
2
. Now fix an element
x\in X
. Given an element
y\neq x
, by the quandle axiom, there is exists a unique
z\in X
such that
z*y=x
. Now since
x\neq y
and
X
is a commutative (then Latin) quandle, the elements
x,y,z
are pairwise disjoint. Now assume we have a family of elements
x,y_{1},z_{1},\ldots y_{n},z_{n}
such that for all
i
with
1\leq i\leq n
,
z_{i}*y_{i}=x
. Now let
y_{n+1}\not\in\{x,y_{1},z_{1},\ldots y_{n},z_{n}\}
. There exists a unique
z_{n+1}\in X
such that
z_{n+1}*y_{n+1}=x
. Now we
claim
that
z_{n+1}\not\in\{x,y_{1},z_{1},\ldots y_{n},z_{n},y_{n+1}\}.
It is clear that
z_{n+1}\neq y_{n+1}
, otherwise
y_{n+1}=x
and thus contradiction. Now if
z_{n+1}=z_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
then
y_{n+1}=y_{i}
, thus contradiction. Lastly, if
z_{n+1}=y_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
then we get
y_{i}*y_{n+1}=y_{i}*z_{i}=x
giving that
z_{i}=y_{n+1}
, thus contradiction. Thus the cardinality of
X
is an odd number.
∎

From straight calculations, we have the following

Lemma 5.3
.

In a commutative quandle
(X,*)
for any
x,y,z\in X
, it holds that,

(1)

(x*y)*z=(y*x)*z=z*(y*x)=z*(x*y)
, in particular,
(x*y)*x=x*(x*y)=x*(y*x)
;

(2)

R_{x}^{-1}R_{y}R_{x}=R_{y}^{-1}R_{x}R_{y}
, where we consider the actions from the left to the right, i.e.

(a)(R_{x}^{-1}R_{y}R_{x})=(((a)R_{x}^{-1})R_{y})R_{x}
for any

.

(3)

R_{x}^{-1}R_{y}=[R_{y},R_{x}^{-1}]
, where
[a,b]=a^{-1}b^{-1}ab
is the group commutator.

The following properties of commutative quandles are evident.

The commutative quandle

6.
m
-almost Latin Quandles

As we have seen above, if a quandle ring has only trivial idempotents, then the automorphism group of the quandle ring is equal to the automorphism group of the quandle. In this section we consider a quandle ring of some generalizations of latin quandle and find its idempotents and automorphisms.

Almost latin quandles are a generalization of latin quandles. The following definition can be found in
[
PY
]
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer. A quandle
X
is said to be
m
-almost latin
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

The stabilizer
Stab(a)=\{x\in X|\;R_{x}(a)=a\}
has order
m
for each
a\in X
.

(2)

The equation
L_{a}(x)=b
has a unique solution for each
b\in X\setminus Stab(a)
.

Notice that when
m=1
, an
m
-almost latin quandle is simply a latin quandle.

Let us consider some example of 2-almost latin quandle (see
[
BES
, Example 13.17]
).
This quandle
X=\{1,2,\ldots,6\}
is the involutory connected quandle of order 6 given in terms of its right multiplications as follows:

\displaystyle R_{1}=(3\;5)(4\;6),\quad R_{2}=(3\;6)(4\;5),\quad R_{3}=(1\;5)(2\;6),

\displaystyle R_{4}=(1\;6)(2\;5),\quad R_{5}=(1\;3)(2\;4),\quad R_{6}=(1\;4)(2\;3).

It is easy to see that
X
is the disjoin union of trivial subquandles
\{1,2\}
,
\{3,4\}
,
\{5,6\}
, and then
\mathbb{Z}[X]
has non-trivial idempotents

\alpha e_{1}+(1-\alpha)e_{2},~~\beta e_{3}+(1-\beta)e_{4},~~\gamma e_{5}+(1-\gamma)e_{6},~~\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}.

Let us show that any idempotent of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
has a form from this list. More accurately, the following proposition holds.

Proposition 6.2
.

Any idempotent of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
is of one of the following
3
forms:
\alpha e_{1}+(1-\alpha)e_{2}
,
\beta e_{3}+(1-\beta)e_{4}
and
\gamma e_{5}+(1-\gamma)e_{6}
, where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Any element of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
has a form

\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\alpha_{2}e_{2}+\beta_{1}e_{3}+\beta_{2}e_{4}+\gamma_{1}e_{5}+\gamma_{2}e_{6},~~\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},\gamma_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Let
R_{3}=\{\overline{0},\overline{1},\overline{2}\}
be the dihedral 3 element quandle. As we know this quandle is abelian. Also, it is easy to check that the map

f:X\rightarrow R_{3}
, which acts by the rules

f(1)=f(2)=\overline{0},~~f(3)=f(4)=\overline{1},~~f(5)=f(6)=\overline{2},

is a homomorphism of quandles. By linearity this homomorphism can be extended to a homomorphism of quandle rings
F:\mathbb{Z}[X]\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}[R_{3}]
. The kernel of this homomorphism consists of the following elements

\ker(F)=\{\alpha(e_{1}-e_{2})+\beta(e_{3}-e_{4})+\gamma(e_{5}-e_{6})~|~\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Suppose that
u
is a non-zero idempotent in
\mathbb{Z}[X]
. Since under a homomorphism any idempotent goes to an idempotent, then
F(u)
is an idempotent in
\mathbb{Z}[R_{3}]
.
By Proposition 4.3 of
[
BPS1
]

F(u)=0
or
F(u)\in\{e_{\overline{0}},e_{\overline{1}},e_{\overline{2}}\}
is a trivial idempotent.

Suppose at first that
F(u)=0
. It means that
u
lies in the kernel
\ker(F)
. Let

u=\alpha(e_{1}-e_{2})+\beta(e_{3}-e_{4})+\gamma(e_{5}-e_{6})

for some
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then

u^{2}=4\alpha\beta(e_{5}-e_{6})+4\alpha\gamma(e_{3}-e_{4})+4\beta\gamma(e_{1}-e_{2})

and it is easy to see that
u^{2}=u
if and only if
\alpha=\beta=\gamma=0
.

Further, if
F(u)=e_{\overline{j}}
,
j\in\{0,1,2\}
, then

u_{i}=e_{i}+\alpha(e_{1}-e_{2})+\beta(e_{3}-e_{4})+\gamma(e_{5}-e_{6})

for some

and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let us take
i=1
, then

u_{1}^{2}=e_{1}+(\alpha+4\beta\gamma)(e_{1}-e_{2})+2\gamma(1+2\alpha)(e_{3}-e_{4})+2\beta(1+2\alpha)(e_{5}-e_{6})

and the equality
u_{1}^{2}=u_{1}
holds if and only if the following system has integer solutions:

\begin{cases}\alpha=\alpha+4\beta\gamma,\\
\beta=2\gamma(1+2\alpha),\\
\gamma=2\beta(1+2\alpha).\end{cases}

From the first equation follows that
\beta=0
or
\gamma=0
, but from the second and the third equations we get
\beta=\gamma=0
. Hence,
u_{1}=e_{1}+\alpha(e_{1}-e_{2})
is an idempotent in
\mathbb{Z}[1,2]
.

Let us take
i=2
, then

u_{2}^{2}=e_{2}+(\alpha+4\beta\gamma)(e_{1}-e_{2})+2\gamma(-1+2\alpha)(e_{3}-e_{4})+2\beta(-1+2\alpha)(e_{5}-e_{6})

and the equality

\begin{cases}\alpha=\alpha+4\beta\gamma,\\

From the first equation follows that
\beta=0
or

, but from the second and the third we get
\beta=\gamma=0

u_{2}=e_{2}+\alpha(e_{1}-e_{2})
is an idempotent in
\mathbb{Z}[1,2]
.

Instead of considering all other cases:
i\in\{3,4,5,6\}
, we can note that the following permutation of elements of
X
:

\varphi=(135)(246)

is an automorphism of
X
and it induces an automorphism of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
. Hence the elements
\varphi(u_{1})
,
\varphi(u_{2})
have the form of
u_{3}
,
u_{4}
respectively, and the elements
\varphi^{2}(u_{1})
,
\varphi^{2}(u_{2})
have the form of
u_{5}
,
u_{6}
respectively. From this observation follows that any idempotent of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
lies in one of the following rings:
\mathbb{Z}[\{1,2\}]
,
\mathbb{Z}[\{3,4\}]
and
\mathbb{Z}[\{5,6\}]
.
∎

Let us consider automorphisms of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
. It is known (see page 24 of
[
Maq
]
) that the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
of the quandle
X
is the symmetric group on four letters:
\operatorname{Aut}(X)\cong S_{4}
. Thus the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}[X])
of the quandle ring
\mathbb{Z}[X]
should contains
S_{4}
as a subgroup.
On the other hand,
\mathbb{Z}[X]
contains 3 quandle rings of 2-element trivial quandles:
\mathbb{Z}[\{1,2\}]
,
\mathbb{Z}[\{3,4\}]
or
\mathbb{Z}[\{5,6\}]
.

Let us find the group of automorphisms of
\mathbb{Z}[T_{2}]
, there
T_{2}=\{t_{1},t_{2}\}
is the 2-element trivial quandle. Any endomorphism
\varphi
of
\mathbb{Z}[T_{2}]
sends idempotent to idempotent and, hence, has a form

\varphi(t_{1})=\alpha t_{1}+(1-\alpha)t_{2},~~\varphi(t_{2})=\beta t_{1}+(1-\beta)t_{2},~~\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}.

This endomorphism is a bijection if and only if
\beta=\alpha+\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
. Hence, we have the following

Lemma 6.3
.

Any automorphism
\varphi
of
\mathbb{Z}[T_{2}]
has a form

\varphi(t_{1})=\alpha t_{1}+(1-\alpha)t_{2},~~\varphi(t_{2})=(\alpha+\varepsilon)t_{1}+(1-\alpha-\varepsilon)t_{2},~~\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},~~\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}.

Let
\psi
be a ring automorphism of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
. Then for any
1\leq i\leq 6
, the image
\psi(e_{i})
is idempotent and thus it is one of the 3 idempotents
\alpha e_{1}+(1-\alpha)e_{2}
,
\beta e_{3}+(1-\beta)e_{4}
and
\gamma e_{5}+(1-\gamma)e_{6}
, where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus the map
\psi
will permute the three 2-dimensional subspaces
\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle
,
\langle e_{3},e_{4}\rangle
and
\langle e_{5},e_{6}\rangle
of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
. In the matrix form
\psi
will be one of the following
6
block-matrices (corresponding to the
6
permutations of the symmetric group
S_{3}
):

\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}A&0&0\\
0&B&0\\
0&0&C\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}A&0&0\\
0&0&B\\
0&C&0\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}0&0&C\\
0&B&0\\
A&0&0\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}0&B&0\\
A&0&0\\
0&0&C\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}0&B&0\\
0&0&C\\
A&0&0\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}0&0&A\\
B&0&0\\
0&C&0\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix},

where
A,B,C\in GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
correspond to automorphisms of
\mathbb{Z}[\{1,2\}]
,
\mathbb{Z}[\{3,4\}]
and
\mathbb{Z}[\{5,6\}]
.

Here is an explicit example of an automorphism:

\displaystyle\psi(e_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{2},\;\psi(e_{2})=-e_{1}+2e_{2},\;\psi(e_{3})=e_{4}

\displaystyle\psi(e_{4})

\displaystyle=

\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}0&-1&0&0&0&0\\

From this observation and Lemma
6.3
follows the following

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
X_{1}=\{1,2\}
,
X_{2}=\{3,4\}
,
X_{3}=\{5,6\}
be the trivial subquandles of
X
. Then any automorphism
\psi
of
\mathbb{Z}[X]
can be presented in the form
\psi=\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}\varphi_{3}\pi
, where
\varphi_{i}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}[X_{i}])
and

is a permutation of the quandles

.
