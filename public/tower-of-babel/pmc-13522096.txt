Outcomes of 39 Canine Eyes Treated With Combined Endoscopic Cyclophotocoagulation and Ahmed Valve Gonioimplantation for Glaucoma
Authors: Sanskruti S Potnis, Ana Ripolles‐Garcia, Kathryn L Good, Jennifer E Sheahan, Ruby B Goldenberg, Kunbee Michael Chang, Sara M Thomasy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo evaluate the long‐term (> 1 year) outcomes of vision and intraocular pressure (IOP) control in dogs with glaucoma undergoing combined endoscopic cyclophotocoagulation (ECP) and Ahmed valve gonioimplantation (AVG).MethodsMedical records between 2020 and 2025 were reviewed. Outcomes evaluated included IOP control (< 25 mmHg), vision preservation, number of medications, and complications. A successful outcome was defined as intact menace response and controlled IOP < 25 mmHg.ResultsMedian (range) follow‐up time for 39 eyes (32 dogs) was 26 (12–65) months. Among eyes with documented vision status at each postoperative time point, vision was preserved in 72.0% (18/25) at 12 months, 72.2% (13/18) at 18 months, 75% (12/16) at 24 months, 57.1% (4/7) at 30 months, and 69.2% (9/13) at 36 months. Among eyes with IOP recorded at each postoperative time point, IOP was controlled in 96.9% (31/32) at 12 months, 95.7% (22/23) at 18 months, and 100% at 24 months (19/19), 30 months (11/11), and 36 months (9/9). These outcomes were achieved with antiglaucoma medications administered when clinically indicated; however, medication burden decreased from a median of 3 preoperatively to 1 at 36 months postoperatively. Common complications included IOP spikes (n = 18/39 eyes, 46.2%), intraocular fibrin (n = 9/39 eyes, 23.1%), corneal ulceration (n = 9/39 eyes, 23.1%), uveitis (n = 8/39 eyes, 20.5%), and corneal edema (n = 7/39 eyes, 17.9%).ConclusionsThe combined ECP/AVG approach was successful in maintaining vision and controlling IOP for more than 1 year in the patients included. Outcomes were similar between glaucoma types, while preoperative vision status was significantly associated with postoperative visual outcome.

Introduction

Canine glaucoma is a progressive and blinding optic neuropathy characterized by elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) resulting in retinal and optic nerve head degeneration [1, 2]. It is the leading cause of vision loss in veterinary patients and can severely impact quality of life by inducing debilitating pain, blindness, and secondary ocular pathologies [1]. Current treatment strategies for glaucoma involve both medical and surgical approaches aimed at reducing IOP [3]. Medical therapies, including topical prostaglandin analogs, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, and beta‐blockers, are often used as first‐line treatments [1, 2]. However, these therapies are frequently associated with limited long‐term efficacy and challenges with compliance, as frequent topical administration is required lifelong [4]. Surgical options are commonly pursued when medical management fails to achieve sustained IOP control [1].

Surgical approaches focus on reducing aqueous humor production via cycloablation, creating alternative pathways for aqueous humor outflow, or combining both techniques [2, 5]. Transscleral and endoscopic laser cyclophotocoagulation (TSCPC and ECP, respectively) techniques have gained importance for their ability to reduce aqueous humor production by selectively ablating the pigmented ciliary epithelium [1, 2]. At 1‐year postoperatively, reported success rates for traditional TSCPC—utilizing neodymium: yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG) or diode lasers—range from 50% to 53% for vision preservation and from 51% to 92% for IOP control [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Because traditional TSCPC does not allow for direct visualization of the ciliary processes, it requires high energy levels to deliver the energy through the bulbar conjunctiva and sclera with documented complications including immediate IOP spikes, neurotrophic corneal ulceration, severe uveitis, and phthisis bulbi [5, 6, 7, 8]. To mitigate collateral tissue damage, micropulse TSCPC (MP‐TSCPC) delivers laser energy in short, repetitive bursts interspersed with cooling intervals [5, 11]. Reported 1‐year postoperative success rates for MP‐TSCPC range from 28% to 38% for visual retention and 39% to 54% for IOP control, with complications including IOP spikes, uveitis, corneal hypoesthesia resulting in neurotrophic ulceration, and uncontrolled IOP requiring repeat procedures [12, 13, 14]. ECP utilizes an internal approach which offers real‐time visualization of the ciliary processes facilitating titration of energy delivery to minimize off‐target tissue damage and intraocular inflammation [5]. Studies report success rates of 57%–93% for vision preservation and 63%–94% for IOP control [15, 16, 17, 18]. However, endoscopic laser delivery is not without complications which also include IOP spikes, uveitis, corneal ulceration, phthisis bulbi, and retinal detachment [5, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Due to these advantages, clinical studies on ECP have demonstrated greater efficacy in maintaining long‐term IOP control and extending vision preservation over transscleral techniques [17, 18]. Compared to ECP, TSCPC techniques are associated with tissue necrosis, intense inflammatory response, and complications such as corneal hypoesthesia, neurotrophic ulceration, and a greater need for multiple procedures resulting in lower success rates [12, 14]. Reported success rates for ECP at 1 year postoperatively are 85%–94% for IOP control and 63%–93% for vision maintenance [15, 16, 17, 18]. The TSCPC technique has reported success rates of approximately 53% for vision preservation and 60%–92% for IOP control at 1 year postoperatively [7, 8, 9], whereas the MP‐TSCPC technique achieves 43%–50% and 42%–54%, respectively [12].

Gonioimplants create an alternative outflow pathway for aqueous humor and are another common surgical approach for the management of glaucoma, while used alone or in combination with cyclodestructive procedures. The purpose of these filtering devices is to divert the flow of aqueous humor from the anterior chamber to another site, such as the subconjunctival space, and eventually return it to systemic circulation [19, 20]. Silicone Ahmed valve gonioimplants (AVG) have been associated with lower complication rates and improved IOP reduction compared with others, and they are also one of the most commonly used devices in canine glaucoma [19]. The most common long‐term complication after gonioimplantation in dogs is bleb fibrosis, which may develop within weeks to months after surgery and can result in device failure [21, 22]. Ahmed valve gonioimplants can be performed alone or in combination with the aforementioned cyclodestructive techniques [20, 23, 24]. Previous studies have shown that dogs undergoing AVG plus topical antiglaucoma medication only achieved a 90% success rate for both vision preservation and IOP control at 1 year after surgery [20, 25]. When AVG is combined with TSCPC, success rates at 1 year after surgery ranged from 41% to 57% for vision preservation and from 74% to 76% for IOP control [23, 24]. The study by Sapienza and van der Woerdt evaluated patients with primary glaucoma only while Bentley et al. did not specify glaucoma type and employed either diode laser TSCPC or cyclocryotherapy.

Despite the increasing use of ECP, comprehensive evaluations of outcomes in canine patients, particularly those involving concurrent AVG gonioimplantation, remain limited. In a large ECP study, Sosnowik et al. reported outcomes in 389 canine eyes; however, only seven eyes underwent concurrent ECP and AVG surgery, precluding statistical analysis of the combined approach [17]. Another study by Turicea et al. evaluated ECP outcomes in 14 canine eyes following prior AVG failure [18]. Previous studies have advocated staggering the two techniques to minimize premature fibrosis and reduced aqueous outflow through gonioimplants secondary to cyclodestruction‐associated intraocular inflammation [18, 20]. However, the benefits of a combined approach include reduction in cycloablation‐related postoperative IOP spikes by diverting outflow through the valve while also potentially reducing the number of anesthetic events and associated costs to owners [23]. Although valve failure may occur within weeks to months after surgery, photocoagulation techniques may provide sustained IOP control long after valve failure [24]. Multiple studies report outcomes for either ECP or AVG at 1 year postoperatively; however, data beyond this time point are poorly characterized and no long‐term data for the combined approach have been published.

The present study aimed to report the long‐term (> 1 year) outcomes of combined ECP and AVG for IOP control, vision maintenance, and use of antiglaucoma medication requirements in dogs with either primary or secondary glaucoma and to present the postoperative complications. The study seeks to provide further insights into the benefits, complications, and prognostic factors associated with combined surgical intervention.

Materials and Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Guidelines of the Ethical Research in Veterinary Ophthalmology (GERVO). Written informed consent was obtained from dog owners for the procedures and treatment performed, as well as for the potential publication of data and images for investigational purposes. Patient data were anonymized to protect client privacy in compliance with national laws governing the use of data from medical records.

Patient Data Recruitment

Medical records of canine patients that underwent combined ECP and AVG at the Veterinary Eye Clinic between July 2020 and December 2025 were reviewed. Eyes with a minimum postoperative follow‐up time of 12 months were included. Owners were contacted by telephone to obtain additional follow‐up information regarding visual status, the number of ongoing antiglaucoma medications, and whether the patient had been evaluated by another veterinary ophthalmologist from whom additional medical records could be obtained.

All patients underwent a complete ophthalmic examination, including the Schirmer tear test 1, rebound tonometry (iCare TONOVET, Jorgensen Laboratories Inc.), slit lamp biomicroscopy (Kowa SL 17 portable slit lamp, Kowa Company Ltd.), and indirect ophthalmoscopy (Keeler Vantage Plus) with 20 D and 28 D condensing lenses (Volk Optical Inc.). Collected data included age, breed, sex, affected eye or eyes, diagnosis of primary or secondary glaucoma, concurrent systemic disease, gonioscopy findings, preoperative and postoperative IOP, number of medications, visual status, lens status, laser settings, extent of ciliary process treatment, immediate and long‐term complications, additional procedures performed, and follow‐up time. Eyes were classified as having primary glaucoma based on gonioscopy findings, signalment, and the absence of identifiable causes of secondary glaucoma. Eyes were classified as having secondary glaucoma when causes unrelated to goniodysgenesis were identified based on history and ophthalmic examination. Vision was assessed by menace response and/or maze testing. In eyes with refractory glaucoma, particularly those with preserved vision or vision loss within the preceding 24 h, prompt surgical intervention with combined ECP and AVG was recommended.

Prior to each surgical procedure, each patient underwent a complete physical examination, preanesthetic bloodwork including a complete blood count and serum chemistry panel, and urinalysis and/or urine culture when indicated. Gonioscopy was performed in 28 patients. The preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative medication regimen is provided in Table S1.

Surgical Procedure

After induction of anesthesia, each dog was positioned in dorsal recumbency with the head angled away from the eye undergoing surgery. The periocular region and conjunctival sac were prepared with 0.25% povidone iodine and draped in a sterile manner, after which a Barraquer wire speculum was placed. In phakic eyes, phacoemulsification or intracapsular lens extraction was performed, and an intraocular lens (An Vision Inc.) was implanted in the capsular bag when possible. Intracapsular lens extraction was performed only in glaucoma cases secondary to lens luxation when phacoemulsification was not possible. Vitrectomy was performed when indicated according to the surgeon's preference and intraoperative findings.

After lens removal, ECP was performed. Depending on the surgeon's preference, a second clear corneal incision was created to improve visualization and facilitate treatment of multiple quadrants of the ciliary processes. Viscoelastic 1.8% or 2.2% (An Vision Inc.) was used to inflate the ciliary sulcus. An Endo Optiks E2 unit with a curved 19‐gauge Endo Optiks E2 MicroProbe (Beaver Visitec International) was used to photocoagulate the heads of the ciliary processes until tissue blanching and shrinkage were observed. Laser power was titrated to effect between 200 and 800 mW using continuous wave duration. Between 270 and 300 degrees of ciliary processes were photocoagulated, depending on the surgeon's preference, severity of glaucoma, and whether previous ECP had been performed in the eye (n = 2). Irrigation and aspiration were performed to remove the remaining viscoelastic. Moxifloxacin (Fosun Pharmacy), dexamethasone (Somerset Therapeutics), and carbachol (Alcon Laboratories Inc.) were instilled intracamerally. The corneal incision was closed with 8–0 Vicryl suture (Ethicon LLC) in a simple interrupted or reverse saw tooth pattern.

An Ahmed valve, either model VFP7 or VFP8 (Caralife/New World Medical), was placed in a dorsolateral conjunctival pocket at the end of the intraocular procedure. A conjunctival pocket was created 5–8 mm posterior to the limbus using tenotomy or Westcott scissors to dissect the conjunctiva and Tenon's capsule to the level of the sclera. Depending on surgeon preference, topical phenylephrine (Lifestar Pharmaceuticals) or cautery was used for vasoconstriction or cauterization of individual vessels, respectively. Care was taken to avoid interference with the extraocular muscles and vortex veins. The valves were primed with tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) or balanced salt solution and then placed in the conjunctival pocket. Two simple interrupted 8–0 nylon sutures (Ethicon LLC, Guaynabo) were used to secure the valve to the sclera, and the knots were rotated inward to sit within the eyelet. A 22‐gauge needle attached to a 1 mL syringe or a 2.2% viscoelastic syringe was inserted into the anterior chamber 1–2 mm posterior to the limbus to create a tunnel for the valve tube. The valve tube was trimmed to the appropriate length and inserted through the scleral tunnel into the anterior chamber at a length of 3–4 mm, ensuring that it did not contact the iris or cornea. Tenon's capsule and conjunctiva were closed in two layers with 6–0 or 8–0 Vicryl (Ethicon LLC) in a simple continuous pattern. In eyes undergoing repeat surgery, revision strategies varied by the operating surgeon and included valve decapsulation, second AVG gonioimplantation, or a combination of both. A second AVG was placed in a separate quadrant where sufficient conjunctiva was available for closure. Valve decapsulation was performed using blunt and sharp dissection with Stevens tenotomy or Westcott tenotomy scissors to excise a portion of the fibrous capsule sufficient to restore aqueous outflow while preserving valve position.

Following surgery, IOPs were monitored at 1‐h intervals during the immediate postoperative period, with the trends dictating the specific antiglaucoma medication regimen. Patients were hospitalized for the duration of the day to ensure full anesthetic recovery and for hourly IOP monitoring, then were subsequently discharged as outpatients at the conclusion of working hours. Routine follow‐up examinations were performed daily for the first three postoperative days. Subsequent recheck intervals and ongoing medical protocols were dictated by individual patient clinical stability and progression. Follow‐up care focused on monitoring IOP, visual status, anterior chamber inflammation, fibrin formation, and valve function. In cases in which fibrin accumulation was observed around the Ahmed valve tube and was considered likely to interfere with aqueous outflow, intracameral tPA was administered during postoperative re‐examinations to facilitate fibrin dissolution and restore tube patency.

Outcome Definitions

A successful outcome was defined as intact menace response and controlled IOP < 25 mmHg regardless of the need to use antiglaucoma medications. Surgical failure was defined as vision loss or refractory IOP ≥ 25 mmHg persisting for 72 h. Eyes meeting failure criteria were excluded from subsequent time point analyses. Repeat endoscopic photocoagulation of an additional quadrant of ciliary processes or repeat ECP of previously treated processes was recommended for eyes that failed but were considered to retain visual potential. Depending on surgeon preference, the existing Ahmed valve was decapsulated, an additional Ahmed valve was placed in a different location, or both procedures were performed. Eyes with permanent vision loss (cupped optic nerve head, retinal atrophy, buphthalmos) underwent enucleation or ciliary body ablation.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software LLC, GraphPad Prism, Version 10.6.1; 2025) and in R [26] with RStudio [27]. Data were summarized as mean ± SD or median (range), as appropriate, and normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk tests. Longitudinal continuous outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models with random effects to account for intereye correlation within dogs and repeated measurements over time. Post hoc comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni method when appropriate. Time‐to‐event outcomes were summarized with Kaplan–Meier curves and compared with log‐rank tests, and associations with clinical covariates were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models. Categorical proportions were compared using Fisher's exact tests. Total postoperative complication counts were compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum or Kruskal–Wallis tests, and associations with continuous predictors were assessed using Spearman rank correlation. The timing of postoperative complications occurring within 3 months versus more than 3 months after surgery was compared using chi‐squared tests. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Included Cases and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 52 eyes underwent combined ECP and Ahmed valve gonioimplantation. Thirteen of 52 eyes (25%), representing 11 dogs, required an additional rescue glaucoma procedure within 12 months and therefore did not reach the 12 month follow up timepoint required for inclusion in outcome analyses. The mean time to rescue intervention was 3.6 months (SD 3.1; range 0.2–10.7 months) after surgery. Of these early failures, 4 of 13 eyes (30.8%) were classified as secondary glaucoma, attributed to lens induced uveitis in 2 eyes, uveitis in 1 eye, and vitreopathy in 1 eye, while 9 of 13 eyes (69.2%) were classified as primary glaucoma. Breeds represented among early failures included Basset Hound (3 eyes) and Shiba Inu (2 eyes), with one eye each from Labradoodle, Chihuahua, Goldendoodle, French Bulldog, Husky, Shih Tzu, German Wirehaired Pointer, and Terrier.

Thirty‐two dogs (39 eyes) met all predefined inclusion criteria for the study. Primary glaucoma accounted for 13 of 32 dogs (40.6%) and secondary glaucoma for 19 of 32 dogs (59.4%). Causes of secondary glaucoma included lens‐induced uveitis (14 of 19 dogs, 73.7%), primary lens luxation (4 of 19 dogs, 20%), and uveitis (1 of 19 dogs, 5.3%). The mean age at surgery was 8.07 ± 2.86 years. Sex distribution included 3 intact females, 14 spayed females, and 15 neutered males. Nineteen breeds were represented as detailed in Table S2; the most common were Husky (n = 4, 12.5%), Chihuahua (n = 3, 9.4%), Shiba Inu (n = 3, 9.4%), and Shih Tzu (n = 3, 9.4%). Twenty‐five dogs underwent unilateral surgery (78.1%), and 7 dogs underwent simultaneous bilateral surgery (21.9%). Diabetes mellitus was present in 7 dogs (21.9%); other systemic conditions included heart disease (n = 3, 9.4%), systemic hypertension (n = 3, 9.4%), hypothyroidism (n = 3, 9.4%), hyperadrenocorticism (n = 2, 6.2%), chronic kidney disease (n = 2, 6.2%), seizures (n = 1, 3.1%), liver disease (reactive hepatopathy secondary to gastrointestinal disease) (n = 1, 3.1%), and infectious disease (positive titers for Lyme and Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever) (n = 1, 3.1%). Median (range) follow‐up time was 26 (12–65) months. Owners of 5 patients were contacted when an in‐clinic examination was not possible. Four of these 5 patients were reported to be blind, whereas 1 retained vision in the only remaining eye (the contralateral eye had been enucleated). Given that most owner‐reported outcomes reflected complete vision loss, the risk of substantial misclassification was considered lower.

Vision Outcomes and Factors Associated With Postoperative Visual Retention

Postoperative vision was maintained in most eyes throughout follow‐up. Overall, 23 of 39 eyes were visual at baseline (59%; Figure 1A). Eighteen of 25 eyes that had vision evaluated at follow‐up were visual at 12 months (72.0%), 13 of 18 at 18 months (72.2%), 12 of 16 at 24 months (75.0%), 4 of 7 at 30 months (57.1%), and 9 of 13 at 36 months (69.2%). When these proportions were recalculated using all 39 eyes present at baseline as the denominator, visual retention was 46.2% (18/39) at 12 months, 33.3% (13/39) at 18 months, 30.8% (12/39) at 24 months, 10.3% (4/39) at 30 months, and 23.1% (9/39) at 36 months. These baseline denominator‐based proportions should be interpreted with caution, as some dogs were lost to follow‐up, did not have vision status documented in some visits, or missed individual time points but returned for later visits, which is an inherent limitation of the retrospective study design.

Kaplan–Meier analysis of the full cohort demonstrated sustained postoperative visual retention, with the probability of remaining visual declining gradually over time but remaining above 60% through the end of follow‐up (Figure 1B). The steepest decline occurred within the first 30 months after surgery, after which the survival curve plateaued. When stratified by glaucoma type, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no significant difference in postoperative visual retention between eyes with primary and secondary glaucoma (p = 0.4400; Figure 1C). By contrast, preoperative vision status was significantly associated with postoperative visual outcome (p = 0.0362; Figure 1D), with eyes that were visual before surgery showing superior visual retention throughout follow‐up compared with eyes that were blind at presentation. Baseline IOP was not a significant predictor of postoperative blindness in Cox proportional hazards analysis (p = 0.1020; Figure 1E). Similarly, time from diagnosis to surgery was not a significant predictor of postoperative blindness in Cox proportional hazards analysis (p = 0.5935; Figure 1F).

We additionally evaluated whether other variables were associated with postoperative visual outcome. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that postoperative visual retention did not differ significantly according to sex (p = 0.811; Figure S1A), breed type (p = 0.221; Figure S1C), or eye laterality (p = 0.93; Figure S1D). Likewise, age at treatment was not a significant predictor of postoperative blindness in the Cox proportional hazards model (p = 0.8061; Figure S1B).

Although preoperative visual status was associated with a greater likelihood of remaining visual after surgery, eyes that were blind at presentation may still derive benefit from treatment. Preoperative vision status showed clinically meaningful patterns during follow‐up, as 4 of 9 eyes that were blind preoperatively regained vision after treatment. Two eyes were documented as visual at 12 months, and 2 additional eyes were first documented as visual at 24 months. For the latter 2 eyes, the 24‐month visit was the first postoperative vision assessment available, so visual recovery may have occurred earlier but was not documented. Together, these findings indicate that visual improvement remains possible even in eyes that are blind at presentation.

Intraocular Pressure Control After Surgery and Associated Factors

Overall, IOP was substantially lower after surgery than before treatment and remained significantly reduced throughout follow‐up (Figure 2A). Mean ± SD IOP was 41 ± 27 mmHg pre‐surgically, 12.66 ± 6.08 mmHg at 12 months (p < 0.0010 vs. pre‐surgery; 32/39 eyes had IOP follow‐up), 14.39 ± 10.44 mmHg at 18 months (p = 0.0013 vs. pre‐surgery; follow‐up available in 23/39 eyes), 14.16 ± 3.93 mmHg at 24 months (p = 0.0011 vs. pre‐surgery; in 19/39 eyes), 13.36 ± 6.31 mmHg at 30 months (p = 0.0051 vs. pre‐surgery; in 11/39 eyes), and 12.78 ± 5.24 mmHg at 36 months (p = 0.0330 vs. pre‐surgery; in 9/39 eyes), indicating sustained postoperative IOP control (Figure 2A). The proportion of eyes with controlled IOP (IOP ≤ 25 mmHg) increased from 35.9% before surgery (14/39) to 96.9% at 12 months post intervention (31/32) and remained high thereafter, with IOP control documented in 95.7% of eyes at 18 months (22/23) and in 100% of eyes with available follow‐up at 24, 30, and 36 months (19/19, 11/11, and 9/9, respectively). Using the 39 eyes present at baseline as the denominator, these proportions corresponded to 79.5% (31/39), 56.4% (22/39), 48.7% (19/39), 28.2% (11/39), and 23.1% (9/39) at 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 months, respectively. While a number of dogs were lost to follow‐up, as indicated by the fractions showing the number of cases included for each type of measurement for each time point, some dogs missed specific time points but returned for subsequent re‐evaluations.

When stratified by glaucoma type, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no significant difference in postoperative IOP control between eyes with primary and secondary glaucoma (p = 0.7230; Figure 2B). We additionally evaluated whether other variables were associated with postoperative IOP control. Sex was not significantly associated with the probability of maintaining postoperative IOP control (p = 0.874; Figure S2A). Age at treatment was also not a significant predictor of loss of postoperative IOP control in Cox proportional hazards analysis (p = 0.5377; Figure S2B). Size of dog breed was significantly associated with postoperative IOP control (p = 0.0428; Figure S2C), with medium breed dogs showing a lower probability of maintaining IOP control over time than small and large breed dogs. Laterality was not significantly associated with postoperative IOP control (p = 0.886; Figure S2D). Likewise, baseline IOP was not a significant predictor of loss of postoperative IOP control in Cox proportional hazards analysis (p = 0.3421; Figure S2E), and neither was time from diagnosis to surgery (p = 0.5386; Figure S2F).

Postoperative Antiglaucoma Medication Burden

Overall, antiglaucoma medication burden was lower after surgery than before treatment (Figure 3). The median number of antiglaucoma medications was 3 (IQR, 2.5; n = 39) pre‐surgically, 1.5 at 12 months postoperatively (IQR, 2; n = 32; p = 0.0250 vs. pre‐surgery), 2 at 18 months (IQR, 2; n = 24), 2 at 24 months (IQR, 1; n = 21), 2 at 30 months (IQR, 2.25; n = 12), and 1 at 36 months (IQR, 0; n = 13; p = 0.0172 vs. pre‐surgery). Because postoperative antiglaucoma medication burden is inherently linked to the other main outcome measures, particularly IOP control, no additional exploratory analyses were performed for this variable.

Complications

Postoperative complications across all follow‐up time points are shown in Figure 4A. The most common complication was IOP spike (46.2%; 18/39 eyes). Fibrin and corneal ulcer each occurred in 9/39 eyes (23.1%), followed by anterior uveitis beyond 4 weeks postoperatively in 8/39 eyes (20.5%), corneal edema in 7/39 eyes (17.9%), and hyphema in 6/39 eyes (15.4%). Quantitative dry eye disease was observed in 5/39 eyes (12.8%), while retinal detachment occurred in 4 of 39 eyes (10.3%). There were a variety of keratopathies in 5/39 eyes (12.8%) that included pigmentary keratitis (n = 3), corneal lipid dystrophy (n = 1), and corneal mineral degeneration (n = 1). Valve issues unrelated to the development of a fibrous capsule occurred in 3/39 eyes (7.7%) and included replacing sutures that dehisced (n = 1), fixing the positioning of a patent valve after twisting (n = 1), re‐anesthetizing a patient to re‐trim the length of the tube (n = 1). Less frequent complications included valve fibrosis, iris bombe, and hypotony, each affecting 3/39 eyes (7.7%), phthisis bulbi in 2 of 39 eyes (5.1%), and lipoid aqueous, hypopyon, endophthalmitis, and corneal perforation, each affecting 1 of 39 eyes (2.6%). Five eyes with secondary glaucoma underwent salvage procedures due to refractory glaucoma and permanent vision loss: four were enucleated (lens‐induced uveitis, n = 1; anterior lens luxation, n = 1; posterior lens luxation, n = 2) and one underwent ciliary body ablation (lens‐induced uveitis, n = 1). No eyes with primary glaucoma underwent salvage procedures.

IOP spike events—defined as refractory IOP ≥ 25 mmHg for 72 h—were documented during scheduled examinations or during emergency examinations prompted by owner‐observed clinical signs consistent with an IOP spike, varying by individual patient clinical presentation. Among recorded IOP spike events, 8 occurred within 3 months after surgery and 13 occurred more than 3 months after surgery, indicating that IOP spikes were numerically more common later in the postoperative course; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.275; Figure 4B). Corneal ulcers were distributed similarly between the early and late postoperative periods (p = 0.763; Figure 4C). Uveitis‐related complications occurred predominantly within the first 3 months after surgery, with 16 events recorded within 3 months and 6 occurring more than 3 months postoperatively (p = 0.033; Figure 4D). Valve‐related complications were recorded at comparable frequencies within and beyond 3 months after surgery (2 vs. 6 events; p = 0.157; Figure 4E). Keratoconjunctivitis sicca was likewise recorded at comparable frequencies between the early and late postoperative periods (1 vs. 4 events; p = 0.180; Figure 4F). Retinal detachment was also comparably distributed between the two postoperative periods, with 2 events recorded within 3 months after surgery and 2 recorded thereafter (p = 1.000; Figure 4G).

To assess the overall postoperative complication profile, we examined whether complication counts differed according to selected clinical and demographic variables. Eyes with primary and secondary glaucoma had comparable numbers of complications (p = 0.9880; Figure S3A). Among eyes with secondary glaucoma, complication counts were also comparable across the underlying causes, including lens‐induced uveitis, primary lens luxation, and uveitis not associated with lens status (p = 0.9870; Figure S3B). Likewise, complication counts were comparable among intact females, spayed females, and neutered males (p = 0.8370; Figure S3C). Age at treatment was not significantly associated with complication count (R = 0.23, p = 0.1678; Figure S3D), although a weak positive trend was observed, suggesting that older dogs may have tended to experience more complications. Complication counts were also comparable among small, medium, and large breed dogs (p = 0.2780; Figure S3E). Similarly, right and left eyes showed comparable complication counts (p = 0.1700; Figure S3F). Preoperative vision status was likewise not associated with complication count, as eyes that were visual and eyes that were blind before surgery had comparable numbers of complications (p = 0.6830; Figure S3G). Preoperative IOP was not significantly associated with complication count (R = −0.17, p = 0.2905; Figure S3H). Time from diagnosis to surgery was also not significantly associated with complication count (R = −0.23, p = 0.1850; Figure S3I).

Discussion

In this study, combined ECP and AVG resulted in sustained IOP reduction, meaningful long‐term visual retention, and a reduced antiglaucoma medication burden over a median follow‐up period of 26 months. At 12 months, 72% of eyes remained visual and 97% had controlled IOP; at 24 months, 75% of eyes remained visual and 100% maintained IOP control. These outcomes are comparable to previously reported success rates for ECP alone, which range from 63% to 84% for visual retention and 90% for IOP control at 12 months, and from 49% to 84% for visual retention and 95% for IOP control at 24 months [17, 18]. Previous studies advocated for a staged approach to minimize inflammation‐induced valve failure [18, 20]. However, in clinical practice, owners may be reluctant to pursue a second surgical procedure because of financial burden and caregiver exhaustion associated with intensive postoperative management. A combined surgical approach was adopted in the present study with the hope to partially reduce the caregiver burden, number of anesthetic events, and associated costs. To the author's knowledge, this is the first veterinary study to evaluate long‐term outcomes after combined ECP and AVG, and the findings suggest that this procedure may offer outcomes comparable to staged approaches for the management of primary and secondary canine glaucoma.

The postoperative visual outcomes of the present study compared favorably with previously reported results for ECP or AVG performed independently in dogs [17, 18, 20, 25]. Visual retention declined gradually before reaching a plateau, supporting sustained visual preservation with the combined surgical approach. Preoperative vision status was associated with postoperative visual outcome, underscoring the importance of surgical intervention before irreversible retinal and optic nerve damage occurs [1, 5]. Nevertheless, some eyes that were blind at presentation regained vision after surgery, indicating that treatment may still be beneficial in select acutely blind patients. Overall, these findings are consistent with previous reports of ECP alone and compare favorably with TSCPC, which has been associated with lower visual retention rates at 12 months [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. The more favorable visual outcomes observed with ECP likely reflect the advantages of direct visualization and titration of laser energy, which may reduce collateral tissue damage and postoperative uveitis [5].

The combined procedure of the present study provided durable IOP control throughout the follow‐up, with no significant difference between primary and secondary glaucoma. Although eyes with secondary glaucoma showed a numerically greater probability of long‐term IOP control, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it did not reach statistical significance. This observed trend aligns with the findings in Sosnowik et al. in which IOP control rates were significantly higher in eyes with secondary glaucoma associated with cataract formation compared to primary glaucoma [17]. The lack of statistical significance in our cohort may reflect differences in study design, sample size, and case composition, particularly because the cohort in the present study included multiple causes of secondary glaucoma. Cataract development and phacoemulsification may also alter drainage angle and ciliary cleft morphology in ways that influence postoperative aqueous humor dynamics [28, 29].

Westermeyer et al. reported 88% (8/9 eyes) IOP control at 1 year after AVG alone in a small cohort, although long‐term follow‐up was limited [20]. Their surgical protocol included intraoperative mitomycin C and temporalis muscle fascia grafting, which may have improved gonioimplant stability and patency [20]. However, complications were frequent and included conjunctival dehiscence and necrosis (4/9 eyes; 44%), bleb fibrosis (3/9 eyes; 33%), and the need for additional surgical intervention (5/9 eyes; 55%) [20]. This may indicate that the value of combining AVG with ECP lies less in preventing late valve fibrosis than in improving immediate postoperative aqueous outflow and reducing the IOP spikes commonly associated with cyclodestructive procedures [23, 24]. This interpretation is also supported by Sosnowik et al., who noted a trend toward lower early postoperative IOP in eyes undergoing concurrent ECP and AVG, although the sample size was too small for formal statistical analysis [17]. Taken together, these findings suggest that ECP and AVG may provide complementary benefits that support sustained IOP control similar to staged approaches.

A secondary goal of glaucoma surgery is to reduce the long‐term burden of medical therapy. In this study, the median number of antiglaucoma medications decreased postoperatively, with significant reductions documented at 12 and 36 months compared with preoperative values, aligning with previous reports on staged and combined approaches [17, 18, 20]. While some degree of medication is often still required, reducing medication burden is clinically meaningful as it can improve owner compliance, decrease caregiver burden, and minimize ocular surface toxicity and systemic side effects [30]. Therefore, the reduction of medications is an important marker in the success of this combined glaucoma surgical approach.

Complications observed in this study were similar to previous reports in dogs [17, 18]. The most frequent complication was postoperative IOP spikes. Timing of re‐examinations was dictated by patient‐specific clinical parameters. Owners were also instructed to monitor for clinical signs consistent with an IOP spike, such as episcleral injection, corneal edema, and/or pain, and patients would be re‐examined on an emergent basis. While this approach may have facilitated prompt detection of IOP spikes, a study by Sanchez et al. demonstrated that 42% of IOP spikes occur outside of standard clinic hours and can be missed by single daytime measurements [31]. Even with individualized monitoring and client education, it is likely that some IOP spikes went undetected. Fluctuation between postoperative IOP increases and subsequent paracentesis can be more damaging to the optic nerve compared to sustained IOP elevation due to deformation of the lamina cribrosa and reperfusion injury, which is why in the present study this approach was not used despite the presence of documented IOP spikes [32, 33]. Interestingly, IOP spikes in the present study were numerically more common after 3 months postoperatively and it is possible that the concurrent AVG could have mitigated early postoperative spikes seen when ECP or AVG is performed independently [23]. Further studies directly comparing dogs treated with ECP alone versus combined ECP and AVG would help elucidate any differences in the rate of postoperative IOP spikes. The rate of fibrin formation was similar to other ECP studies [18] but lower than in TSCPC studies [23, 24] demonstrating the reduced intraocular trauma caused by ECP compared to TSCPC. Corneal ulceration frequency was also comparable to other ECP studies [17, 18]. While transscleral techniques are frequently linked to high rates of corneal ulcers due to damage to the long ciliary nerves, the intraocular approach of ECP may help reduce the incidence of these severe complications by sparing corneoscleral nerves [5, 14]. The fact that in the present study there were no significant differences in the cause of glaucoma encourages further studies on the application of this combined technique in patients with primary and secondary glaucoma.

The retrospective design of this study introduces several limitations, including non‐standardized postoperative management, incomplete medical records, and missing data related to follow‐up visits occurring outside predefined intervals or undocumented visual status at some ophthalmic re‐evaluations. In addition, requiring a minimum follow‐up period of 12 months introduced selection bias, because eyes with early failure were excluded from the main outcome cohort, potentially leading to overestimation of long‐term success rates. Although no significant differences were identified between primary and secondary glaucoma, the small number of eyes within specific subtypes limited subtype‐specific interpretation, and fewer eyes were available at later time points for very long‐term analyses.

In conclusion, the combined use of ECP and AVG appeared to be an effective management strategy independent of the cause of glaucoma in the dogs included in the present study. It provided reasonable IOP control, preservation of vision, and reduced medication burden within the follow‐up time included, and it had a complication profile comparable to that previously reported for standalone procedures. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow‐up times are warranted to further assess these findings and their application to clinical practice.
