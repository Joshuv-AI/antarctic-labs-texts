A psychophysically-tuned computational model of human primary visual cortex produces geometric optical illusions
Authors: Chrysa Retsa, Ana Hernando Ariza, Nathanael W. Noordanus, Lorenzo Ruffoni, Micah M. Murray, Benedetta Franceschiello
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Geometric optical illusions (GOIs) are mismatches between physical stimuli and perception. GOIs provide an access point to study the interplay between sensation and perception, Yet, there is relatively scant quantitative investigation of the extent to which different GOIs rely on similar or distinct perceptual mechanisms, which themselves are driven by specific physical properties. We addressed this knowledge gap with a combination of psychophysics and computational modelling. First, 30 healthy adults reported quantitatively their perceptual biases with three GOIs, whose physical properties parametrically varied on a trial-by-trial basis. A given physical property, when considered in isolation, had different effects on perceptual biases depending on the GOI (e.g. the spacing of stimuli affected one GOI, but not another). For a given GOI, there were oftentimes interactions between the effects of different physical properties. Next, we used these psychophysical results to tune a computational model of primary visual cortex that combines parameters of orientation selectivity, receptive-field size, intra-cortical connectivity, and long-range interactions. We showed that similar biases generated in-silico mirror those observed in human behavior when receptive field size, bandwidth and shape (rounded or elongated) are tuned, as well as parameters encoding the strength of the long-range intra-regional interactions between receptive fields. Collectively, our results suggest that different physical properties are not operating independently, but rather synergistically, to generate a GOI. Such results provide a roadmap whereby computational modelling, informed by human psychophysics, can reveal likely mechanistic underpinnings of perception.

Introduction

The effortlessness of vision belies its mechanistic complexity as well as its perceptual fragility. Illusions are key phenomena to study vision. They provide a possibility to distinguish between sensation and perception. Illusions also have an ethological significance, as those organisms whose vision is capable of surmounting noise and ambiguity in visual scenes (e.g. camouflage) have a clear evolutionary advantage (Lesher, 1995; Marr, 2010; Ullman, 2007). These phenomena also provide a more accurate depiction of the real world, playing a role in object perception and navigation (among other functions). Illusory figures present a solution to complex perceptual problems (Lesher, 1995; Rock and Anson, 1979). In this paper, we focus our attention on a subset of illusory phenomena called Geometrical-Optical Illusions (GOIs). They were first described in the 19th century by Johann Joseph Oppel (1855) and subsequently studied by other German psychologists, such as Ewald Hering (1861), Rudolph Hermann Lotze (1852) and Karl Zöllner (1860).

GOIs were defined as situations where “there is a mismatch of geometrical properties between the item in the object space (physical source of the stimulus) and its associated percept” (Westheimer, 2008). Fig. 1 presents some classical examples of GOIs: the Hering, the Zöllner, and the Poggendorff illusions. For example, in the Hering illusion the presence of a radial background induces a misperception of the two parallel lines, which appear as bowed towards the outside although they are straight. Optical illusions were first studied from a psychophysical standpoint, leading to qualitative models that first identified the underlying mechanisms in terms of cognitive, retinal, and perceptual processes. Behavioral tests using these illusions have been performed during the 1960s–1980s (Beckett, 1989; Holt-Hansen, 1961; Oyama, 1975; Weintraub and Krantz, 1971), but there are both methodological limitations (e.g. hand-drawings were often used) and limited statistical analysis (i.e. most studies were purely observational and not quantitative). Therefore, understanding the mechanisms giving rise to the bias, i.e. the difference between the geometrical nature of the stimuli and the perceived one (amount of perceived curvature induced by the distance between original and corrected line, divergence and misalignment, respectively, for the GOIs depicted in Fig. 1) can provide insights into core properties of visual perception and may help identify its undergirding neural computations (see e.g. (Howe et al., 2005; Morgan et al., 1990).Fig. 1Geometric Optical illusions (GOIs) investigated in this study. From the neurophysiological standpoint, several mechanisms have been proposed to contribute to illusory perceptual phenomena (Eagleman, 2001), such as lateral interactions between cells responsible for extracting basic features at the intracortical level in visual areas, or feedback and feed-forward mechanisms across hierarchical levels of the visual pathway. Due to competing features of the initial stimulus, the produced neuronal responses are themselves ambiguous in their coding. In the case of illusory contours, where geometrical forms such as lines or figures are perceived – although not physically present, feedback mechanisms have been postulated as – at least partly – responsible (Dura-Bernal et al., 2024; Murray and Herrmann, 2013). Feedback modulations in the case of illusory perceptual phenomena allow for the incorporation or integration of information across longer distances, since higher-level visual cortices contain neurons with large (and bilateral) receptive fields, and therefore the substantiation of Gestalt-like principles of perceptual grouping (Dura-Bernal et al., 2024; Murray and Herrmann, 2013). The specific contributions of these mechanisms to perceptual outcomes remain to be fully characterized.

Nonetheless, these psychophysical and neurophysiological observations have opened up the possibility of also investigating and computationally modeling the neural substrates of such illusory processing by means of the features (contour perception, brightness, depth) – determinants – of the stimuli (Lesher, 1995). Neural-based computational models of visual perception aim to provide mathematical descriptions of such neurophysiological features, and as such serve as proxies for neurobiological mechanisms. A stable prediction of the misperception in GOIs could be explained by a model considering three main neurophysiological features (Franceschiello et al., 2018, 2019): 1) orientation selectivity processes performed by visual areas V1 and V2 (Hubel and Wiesel, 1962), 2) interference between receptive profiles of simple cells in these areas, and 3) long-range connectivity (Bosking, William H., 1997).

In terms of computational modelling, the orientation selectivity process is implemented by applying convolutional filters capable of extracting the most prominent orientation for each small portion of the visual field where the stimulus is represented. This interpretation holds in the case of possible interference between close-by receptive fields. Many models have been proposed for this convolutional operation, among those the Derivative of Gaussians (DoG), introduced by Young (1987) and Daugman (Koenderink and van Doorn, 1990), Gabor filters, introduced by Daugman (1985) and Jones and Palmer (Jones and Palmer, 1987), and cake wavelets (Duits et al., 2007). Gabor filters are particularly interesting as they have been proven to mimic accurately receptive field profiles of simple cells in V1, as recorded neurophysiologically by De Angelis (De Angelis et al., 1995). As for the long-range connectivity (or horizontal connectivity) of the primary visual areas, we refer to the mechanism “connecting” neurons spiking simultaneously while situated at relatively far distances (ca. 300-400 μm) from one another (though still limited to visual cortices, despite the established existence of long-range connections that also extend from visual to frontal cortices, by way of example). Long-range connections enable the formation of contours, surfaces, and more complex objects and they have been mathematically described in terms of mean field equations (Bertalmío et al., 2020; Bertalmío and Cowan, 2009; Bressloff et al., 2001; Schuster and Wagner, 1990), of statistics of the visual inputs (Bednar, 2014; Simoncelli and Olshausen, 2001) or by a geometrical description of the visual areas (Ben-Shahar and Zucker, 2004; Citti and Sarti, 2006; Hoffman, 1980; Petitot, 2002). Among those computational models providing a description for GOIs, some approaches are statistically-based (Fermüller and Malm, 2004) and others are geometrically-based (Ehm and Wackermann, 2012, 2016). The first models, characterizing GOIs while also having a neural-based approach, are those in (Franceschiello, Benedetta, Sarti, Alessandro, Citti, 2017; Franceschiello et al., 2019). These provided a first qualitative reconstruction of the phenomena, as these did not incorporate actual perceptual reports by human observers nor quantitative metrics of bias. By integrating psychophysical data from a relatively large group of observers, we were therefore in a position to determine the contributions of these “neural” components of the in-silico simulations (the parameters of which are proxies for putative neurobiological mechanisms) and thus to provide a quantitative reconstruction of the phenomena by tuning these parameters to match the empirical psychophysical observations.

The specific contributions of the present paper are threefold. Firstly, and to our knowledge for the first time, we introduce a standardized, quantitative measure of perceptual bias for each of the three GOIs: Hering, Zöllner and Poggendorff illusions. Furthermore, these perceptual illusions are analyzed together for the first time, enabling us to determine to what extent biases are related and by extension the degree to which distinct mechanisms likely contribute to each illusion. Second, we tune the computational models used in prior works of our group (Franceschiello et al., 2018, 2019) by using psychophysical data. Specifically, parameters in direct link with biological correlates are fine-tuned to have the computational models reproducing psychophysics. This is achieved by iteratively optimizing the parameters to achieve a replication of human behavior. The third contribution of this work is to derive neural-based conclusions from those neurophysiological-related parameters which permitted the computational model fitting, i.e. a quantitative reproduction of the phenomena, as well as to compute in silico the perceptual bias of new illusions artificially generated starting from the estimates of the biological parameters identified by the model fit.

Materials and methods

Behavioral experiment

Participants and stimuli. Thirty healthy unpaid volunteers (19 females; aged 22–39years; mean ± SD = 28.4 ± 4.3years) provided informed consent to participate in the experiment. All procedures conformed to the 2013 update of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Cantonal Ethics Committee. None of the subjects had current or prior neurological or psychiatric illnesses. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The experiment included stimuli for the three different illusion tasks: Hering, Zöllner and Poggendorff. Visual stimuli were independently varied along 2 aspects of interest (i.e. our independent variables for each condition). For each illusion, nine sets of visual stimuli were presented in different conditions, following a 3 × 3 factorial design.

Hering stimuli. The neutral configuration of Hering stimuli consists of two parallel vertical lines (foreground curves) and a symmetrical arrangement of radial background lines, which meet in the center of the image, located halfway between the two foreground lines. The participants then usually report the two straight, parallel vertical lines as appearing curved (Hering, 1861). In the present experimental set-up, participants had to adjust the vertical lines in order to appear subjectively straight and parallel.

In our experiment, each stimulus is drawn in a rectangular region in the center of the screen with a gap above and below of 5% of the height of the screen (the height of the screen used was 20 cm), such that the vertical extent of the stimulus is the middle 90% of the height of the screen (i.e. 18 cm or 13.5°). The background of the stimulus consists of a number of lines disposed radially, see Fig. 1, top row 1. These lines are evenly spaced along the edges of the central square, as a rule always including the horizontal and diagonal lines and excluding the central vertical line. All stimuli consisted of black lines presented on a white background at 100% contrast.

The nine different stimuli in the Hering condition were obtained by varying independently the number of radial lines and the distance between the two main vertical lines (Fig. 1C, top row). These two variables were chosen because they constitute the main components of the Hering illusion. Each stimulus included either 7, 11 or 15 radial lines (the corresponding values in the Psychopy code for stimuli presentation are 5, 7 and 9) and a distance between the two vertical lines of either 2.4 cm (visual angle: 1.8°), 3.2 cm (2.4°) and 4 cm (3°), (the corresponding values in the code are: 0.06, 0.08, 0.1). The values were fixed in order to have perceivable differences while experiencing the illusory effects. To recover the measure in cm, these values (0.12, 0.16 and 0.2) were multiplied by the height of the screen (i.e. 20 cm).

The two foreground curves were not always drawn straight, but the curves appear smooth in all configurations. The foreground curves can veer towards or away from the center of the image. We define the offset to be the initial horizontal distance (varied for each trial) between one of the straight vertical lines in the neutral configuration and the corresponding foreground curve (see Appendix A for further explanations). We varied the initial value of the offset by discrete steps (up to 4 steps in either direction). When the participant adjusted the offset parameter by pressing the up or down arrow key on the computer keyboard, the value of the offset is adjusted by 1 step. We varied the initial value of the offset in order not to bias the participant towards a stable initial configuration. However, this parameter is not among our main experimental manipulations. The measure of this experiment is the final offset, which corresponds to the distance from the neutral configuration and represents what the participant perceived as straight/parallel, and it is measured in discrete steps. This is the perceived bias.

Hering design. A 3 × 3 factorial design was adopted, based on the number of radial lines (7, 11 and 15), and the distance between vertical lines (2.4 cm, 3.2 cm and 4 cm). Each of the nine experimental conditions was presented 12 times, resulting in 108 trials in total. It should be noted that the initial value of the offset was not specified as an experimental factor. For each condition, we set several initial offset values: 0.107 cm (1 time), −0.053 cm (3 times), 0 cm (4 times), 0.053 cm (3 times) and 0.107 cm (1 time), following a Gaussian distribution with the mean equal to the condition we wanted to test most often. The bias here was defined as the horizontal distance between the original vertical line and the manipulated final curve. An explanation that links the described “distance” and the curvature of the final curve is presented in the Appendix, (A). To calculate the distance, we summed the number of times the up and down arrow keys were pressed, giving it a positive value when the down key was pressed and a negative value when the up key was pressed. Participants had no time constraint and thus could press up and down keys as frequently they deemed necessary. The resulting final offset, encoding the final position of the line according to the participant's perception, was then multiplied by the offset step size, equal to 0.0267 cm in the Hering illusion. This final position was then calculated relative to the initial starting position of the lines as well as relative to the true vertical.

Zöllner stimuli. The neutral configuration of Zöllner stimuli consisted of two vertical lines each intersected by a number of line segments (inducers), see Fig. 1, central row. Although the vertical lines are parallel to each other, the participants perceive them as tilted. That is, they often instead reported them to appear to converge or diverge from each other. In the present experimental set-up, participants adjusted the angle of the vertical lines in order to appear subjectively parallel rather than tilted. In our experimental setup, the inducers were themselves parallel segments spread evenly over the length of the vertical lines. The inducers intersecting the left vertical line were consistently oriented from top left to bottom right and the inducers on the right line are vertically mirrored (i.e. from top right to bottom left). The length of the vertical lines was 80% of the height of the screen (16 cm or visual angle:12.1°). The distance between them was 4 cm (3°) (0.2 of the screen height). The length of the inducers was 2 cm (1.5°) (0.1 of the screen height). All stimuli consisted of black lines presented on a white background at 100% contrast.

The nine different versions of Zöllner stimuli were obtained by varying independently the two following parameters: the number of inducers and the angle of the inducers. In our example, only the angle is manipulated and the number of inducers, which could vary between 8, 9 and 10 inducers, is used as a control. The angle could vary between 40°, 65° and 90° (from vertical). These latter values were fixed in order to have perceptual differences while experiencing the illusory effects. The participants manipulated the angle of the vertical lines (both lines were rotated together in a mirrored way). The offset step size was 0.1°. The vertical lines in the Zöllner illusion were not always drawn parallel; sometimes they were tilted by a small angle, which is considered as the distance from vertical; it is what we refer to as offset, and it varied in discrete steps. We varied the initial offset between −0.6° to 0.6° of visual angle. When the participant adjusted the offset parameter by pressing the up or down arrow key on the keyboard, the value of the offset was adjusted by 1 step. The final offset corresponds to the distance from objective vertical and represents what the participant perceives as parallel. This was their perceived bias.

Zöllner design. A 3 × 3 factorial design was adopted. This was based on the number of inducers (8, 9 and 10) and the angle of the inducers (40°, 65° and 90°). Each of the nine experimental conditions was presented 12 times, resulting in 108 trials in total. The initial value of the offset was not specified as an experimental factor. For each condition, we set several initial offset values: 0.6° (1 time), −0.4° (3 times), 0° (4 times), 0.4° (3 times) and 0.6° (1 time), following a Gaussian distribution with the mean equal to the condition we wanted to test most often. The bias refers to the angle created between the corrected and the parallel lines (see Fig. 1, bottom row, (B)). To calculate that angle, we summed the number of times the up and down arrow keys were pressed, giving it a positive value when the up key was pressed and a negative value when the down key was pressed. The resulting value (final offset) was then multiplied by the angle in degrees corresponding to how much the line moved each time we pressed an arrow, i.e. its offset step size equal to 0.1°.

Poggendorff stimuli. The neutral configuration of Poggendorff stimuli consisted of two collinear segments separated by a central surface. The participants usually reported the two collinear segments as misaligned. In the present experiment, participants adjusted the vertical position of the right line segment in order for the two segments to be subjectively perceived as collinear.

In our experimental setup, the stimuli consisted of a central black rectangle, with a fixed height of 16 cm and a width that varied from trial-to-trial, and two black lines, one on the left side and one on the right of the rectangle. These were displayed on a white background at 100% contrast. The nine different stimuli in the Poggendorff conditions were obtained by varying independently the width of the rectangle and the angle (slope) of the line segments. These two independent variables were chosen as they constitute the main components of the Poggendorff illusion. The width of the rectangle varied between 2, 4 and 6 cm (visual angle:1.5, 3 4.5°), and the angle of the line segments between 30°, 60° and 90° (from the vertical configuration). These values were chosen in order to have a significant difference in the perception of the illusory effects. The two segments always had the same slope. Both line segments extended to 4 cm (3°) horizontally away from the center of the screen. Therefore, their length depended on the width of the rectangle and on the slope. In the neutral position (zero offset), the right and left segments both aligned with the central point of the rectangle, as if to form a single line passing through that point. The left line was always fixed in this position. As in the other illusion tasks, there was an initial offset variable. Specifically, the right line could be offset by a variable amount ranging between −0.04 and 0.04 cm (equivalent to visual angles of ±0.03°). When the participant adjusted the offset parameter by pressing the up or down arrow key (and thus moving the right line segment upwards or downwards), the value of the offset was adjusted by 1 step of 0.02 cm size (i.e. step of 0.015° of visual angle). The measure of this experiment was the final offset, which is the vertical distance from the neutral configuration (alignment of the segments) and represents what the participant perceived as collinear. This is the perceived bias.

Poggendorff design. A 3 × 3 factorial design was adopted. This was based on the width of the rectangle (2, 4 and 6 cm) and angle of the line segments (30°, 60° and 90°). Each of the nine experimental conditions was presented 12 times, resulting in 108 trials in total. The initial value of the offset was not specified as an experimental factor. For each condition, we set several initial offset values: 0.04 cm (1 time), −0.02 cm (3 times), 0 cm (4 times), 0.02 cm (3 times) and 0.04 cm (1 time), following a Gaussian distribution with the mean equal to the condition we wanted to test most often. The bias for Poggendorff was calculated as the distance, computed along the right side of the rectangle, between the right line segment and the actual physically aligned segment. To calculate that distance, we summed the number of times the up and down arrow keys were pressed, giving it a positive value when the up key was pressed and a negative value when the down key was pressed. The resulting value (final offset) was then multiplied by the distance in cm that the line moved each time we pressed an arrow (offset step size equal to 0.02 cm).

Procedure and Experimental design. Participants sat in a darkened, sound-attenuated room (WhisperRoom MDI, 102126E) in front of a laptop screen (MacBook Pro end 2013, 13-inch, resolution: 2560 x 1600) that presented the different stimuli. The distance between participants’ eyes and the screen was kept constant at 75 cm. The experiment consisted of three different blocks, one for each illusion task. Participants performed each illusion task one after the other; the order of which was counterbalanced across participants. Participants took short breaks between the different tasks. Within each block, the nine different sets of stimuli were presented randomly to the participants. Each stimulus remained on the screen for as long as the participant took to respond. Participants then pressed the spacebar in order to proceed to the next trial. Short instructions were presented at the beginning of each of the tasks, followed by a couple of practice trials for the participant to get familiarized with the tasks and stimuli. Stimulus delivery and behavioral response collection were programmed and controlled by PsychoPy v3.0 (Peirce et al., 2019). The code for the experiments is publicly available at the following link: https://gist.github.com/nat-n/879d06244a694d47c4911098aac60656.

Behavioral analysis. IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM Corp. Released, 2017. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) was used for data analysis. First, we tested whether the recorded data were normally distributed using a Shapiro-Wilk test. All data were normally distributed. Hence, a 3 × 3 repeated measures ANOVA (rmANOVA) was used for each illusion task in order to analyze the effects of our experimental manipulations on the participants’ calculated biases. The factors that were used were: the number of radial lines (7, 11, 15) and the distance between the vertical lines (2.4 cm, 3.2 cm, 4 cm) for the Hering stimuli, the number of inducers (8, 9, 10) and angle of inducers (40°, 65°, 90°) for the Zöllner stimuli, and the width of the rectangle (2 cm, 4 cm, 6 cm) and the angle of the line segments (30°, 60°, 90°) for the Poggendorff stimuli.

Computational experiment

In this paragraph we briefly summarize the cortically inspired computational models introduced in (Franceschiello et al., 2018, 2019) from a broad neurobiological standpoint. For a more extended summary, we refer readers to Appendix B and the corresponding articles cited therein. These computational models have proven to be effective in reproducing the qualitative responses of the primary visual cortices in response to geometrical optical illusions. As a first step and from a purely image processing perspective, the model takes into account the orientation selection mechanism (short-range or intracortical connectivity) characterized by Hubel and Wiesel (Hubel and Wiesel, 1962), such that receptive field profiles of simple cells in V1 are modelled as filters. Every point of the visual input is assigned with an orientation output. Contours and more complex visual objects are built through long-range connectivity mechanisms, which connects points of the visual input located at a certain distance. This connectivity is modelled as a metric (i.e. a certain way to measure distance between points) weighted by the orientation output of simple cells. The metric approximates the connectivity patterns between different hyper-columns as some diffusive propagation of single cells’ responses, complementing the intra-cortical connectivity patterns. Illusory curves (such as in the case of the Poggendorff illusion) can also be computed as minimal with respect to the metric induced by the geometry of the primary visual cortex. Concretely, the computational model allows for tuning the features of the receptive fields - namely their shape, bandwidth and size - while at the same time controlling for the strength of the intra-regional long-range connectivity between receptive profiles. To match the behavioral results of the study, such parameters have been iteratively modified and tested to identify those which allow to best replicate the empirical patterns of behavior we obtained.

Numerical Implementation. For the implementation of the mathematical models described above, we use MATLAB ver. R2019a. The initial images used for the psychophysical experiments were extracted and resized manually, to optimize the computation time, which is dependent on the size of the initial image. For the three illusions, the original stimuli were images of size 2880x1800 pixels, first cropped along the horizontal and vertical directions to obtain a square of size 1800x1800 pixels (Hering), a rectangle of 592x1267 pixels (Zöllner) and a rectangle of 770x1540 pixels (Poggendorff). The three images were then scaled by a factor of 4.18, 3.34 and 3.85, respectively, obtaining final images with sizes 430x430 pixels (Hering), 177x379 pixels (Zöllner) and 200x400 pixels (Poggendorff). The resized images corresponding to the stimuli were convolved with a set of Gabor filters (N = 36 orientations for the Hering and Zöllner, N = 32 for the orientation of lines extraction in the Poggendorff illusion and N = 62 for the surface contour orientation extraction).

The number N of orientations stated above was computed as equally spaced steps within the two intervals, the latter being θ∈[0,π) (for the Hering, the Zöllner and the Poggendorff) and θ∈[−π,π) (for the Poggendorff, as the polarity of pi indicates whether or not the receptive field is in our outside the surface; something moot for the Hering and Zöllner stimuli), depending on the features extracted (i.e. lines or contours as detailed above).

The implementation of the Gabor filters has been described by Petkov and Subramanian (Petkov and Subramanian, 2007), setting the required parameter λ=0. According to Daugman (1985), Gabor filters simulate the receptive field profiles of simple cells in V1/V2 responsible for the orientation tuning. The size of the Gabor patch, i.e. the dimensions of the filter windows in pixels, was varied across illusions and conditions by tuning σ. The implementation of Gabor filters in MatLab is available in http://www.cs.rug.nl/∼imaging/spatiotemporal_Gabor_function/GaborKernel3d.m (Petkov and Subramanian, 2007). The Gabor filters’ parameters were fixed for all presentations of the same illusion: γ=0.5; b‾=0.56; bandwidth = 2, σ∈[5.74,12.88] for the Hering; γ=0.5; b‾=0.56; bandwidth = 1, σ∈[5.04,16.24] for the Zöllner; γ=1; b‾=0.56; σ=3 for the orientation of lines extraction in the Poggendorff; and γ=1; b‾=0.56; , bandwidth = 2, σ∈[7,40] for the orientation of contours extraction in the Poggendorff. In the Hering and Zöllner illusion a constant c=0.037,0.028 respectively scales the displacement vector fields u‾ = (x1, x2) once applied to the initial image to reconstruct the percept. In the Poggendorff experiment, the parameter ξ – which modulates the anisotropy between the two direction, ξΔx1 = Δθ, where Δx1, Δθ are the discretization steps along x1 and θ – is chosen based on the entry angle for the transversal line and the width of the central surface. The geodesics are computed through the Sub-Riemannian Fast-Marching, as implemented in (Sanguinetti et al., 2015). The parameter ϵ indicates the Riemannian approximation and in our experiments is set equal 0.1. Bias calculation in the computational results. The computational model, once tuned, was fitted with the outcome of the psychophysical results according to the variable of interest of the three detailed geometrical optical illusions. As our goal was to compare the results obtained in the psychophysical experiments with the resulting biases of the computational protocol, the definitions of bias are the same as those given in the behavioral bias paragraph. However, the way the bias is extracted in the computational case is clearly different, but it has been standardized to reduce any variability induced by a manual extraction of the measures.

Computational bias for the Hering illusion. To recall the definition, the bias is the horizontal distance between the straight vertical lines of the neutral configuration and the curve computed by the model. To calculate it in a standardized way, we extracted the distance between point 1 and 2, see Fig. 1B, which corresponds to the value in pixels of the displacement vector field u‾ = (u1, u2) computed at point 1, multiplied by the constant c = 0.037, see Numerical Explanation section.

Computational bias for the Zöllner illusion. The bias refers to the angle created between the modelled vertical line and the original parallel one. To calculate the angle, we choose two points in the final image: the first one on the bottom of the left perceptual reconstructed line (x1,y1), and the second on the top of the same line (x2,y2), see Fig. 1B. The rotation angle is computed as atan(x2−x1y2−y1). The result is then converted from radians to degrees.

Computational bias for the Poggendorff illusion. The bias for Poggendorff is again calculated as the vertical distance between the reconstructed segment corresponding to the perceptual alignment and the neutral configuration (in which the left and right segments are aligned). The computational bias then results as the difference in pixels between the original y-coordinate of the physically aligned segment and the y-coordinate of the segment obtained through our algorithm (Fig. 1B).

Tuning the computational model through behavioral results

As described in the numerical implementation section, the computational models used to simulate the perceptual behavior of the visual cortex that generates the perception of geometrical optical illusions depend on a series of parameters. Such parameters do have an interpretation from the neurophysiological standpoint and therefore impact the fitting of the computational curves with the behavioral ones. By tuning the computational models, we refer to the practice of iteratively modifying these parameters in a fashion that would lead to optimally fit the obtained computational biases with the behavioral ones. Once the model is fit on behavioral data, we can generate artificially variation of the illusions and computationally compute the bias in silico. We can also estimate and study how biologically based parameters, such as receptive field size, long-range connectivity strength, vary across different conditions, suggesting how our visual cortex may handle different percepts.

Comparison between computational and behavioral biases

The computational results were then compared with the psychophysical ones to tune the model and evaluate its prediction. Different sets of initial parameters for the size of receptive fields (encoded by the σ of the Gabor filter) were tried before choosing those described in the Numerical implementation subsection. The final value of σ was chosen as the instance maximizing the fitting between computational and behavioral curves (minimizing the error) and remained fixed across different conditions of the same illusion. The comparison between computational and behavioral biases of the Zöllner illusions was straightforward, as the compared variables are both in degrees. To ensure the same comparison between the distances extracted in the Hering and Poggendorff illusions, the bias is expressed in percentages; in the Hering illusion as BiasLength_of_the_positive_x_semi_axis∙100% ; in the Poggendorff illusion is computed as BiasTotal_size_rectangle∙100%. In this way, the behavioral measures extracted in cm become comparable with the computational measures in pixels.

Relationships of biases across illusions

To evaluate whether a given physical property impacting perceptual biases for one illusion exerted a similar influence on the perceptual biases for another illusion, we performed a series of correlations. First, we estimated the slope of the line fitting the reported biases for each illusion for each participant and for a given physical property (e.g. line spacing across all numbers of inducing lines in the Hering illusion). Once we estimated these slopes, we then performed a correlation analysis using Pearson's coefficient. When comparing the slopes of the biases as a function of the number of inducing lines in the Hering and Zöllner illusions, we observed no reliable correlation (R(28) = 0.319; p > 0.05). When comparing the slopes of the biases as function of the angle of the lines in the Zöllner and Poggendorff illusions, we observed no reliable correlation (R(28) = 0.017; p > 0.92). Finally, when comparing the slopes of the biases as a function of the spacing of the lines/surface in the Poggendorff and Hering illusions, we observed no reliable correlation (R(28) = 0.177; p > 0.34). Collectively, these analyses provide no indication that a given physical property has similar effects on biases across illusions.

Discussion

Three different GOIs have been studied in the present work: the Hering, the Zöllner and the Poggendorff illusions from psychophysical and computational standpoints. The recorded behavioral biases for the three illusions served as a basis for tuning the parameters of the computational model in order to obtain computational curves replicating numerically the behavioral biases. Furthermore, we empirically generated new, unseen versions of the illusions to test our model and investigate in silico the linear relationship between simple geometrical variation (distance between lines, angle of tilt) and the size of receptive fields. Our results provide a roadmap whereby computational modelling, informed by human psychophysics, can reveal mechanistic underpinnings of perceptual processing of simple visual features.

To the best of our knowledge, this was the first time these three illusions were psychophysically studied together and that a standardized definition of bias was quantified, with up-to-date experimental methods. The determinants of the biases in these three GOIs share an angular component given by the intersection of the foreground line (or surface) with radial lines, segments (inducers) or one crossing line. This angular component seems to reflect the intracortical connectivity of hypercolumns: neurons belonging to the same hypercolumn - and spiking simultaneously - might be a first determinant of misperception (bias). Second, the distance between lines (or the width of the surface) is depicted by the global integration of local selected features, therefore being more representative of the way through which long-range intra-regional connectivity represents visual stimuli. This is supported by the size of the receptive field enhancing and permitting such interactions.

With this in mind, our results indicate that for the Hering illusion, the number of radial lines does influence the perception; more radial lines resulted in larger bias, in agreement with Holt-Hasen, 1961; Holt-Hansen, 1961). A similar effect was observed for the Zöllner illusion: the inducers' angle had an effect on the perceived bias, which increases as the intersect angle decreases, in agreement with observations in Oyama (1973); Oyama (1975). On the other hand, no significant main effect of distance between the two vertical lines was reported in the Hering illusion. Interestingly, an interaction between the two variables indicating that the distance was affecting participants’ performance in the minimum and maximum radial lines conditions, but not the medium one, was observed. Specifically, in the minimum radial lines condition, there is significantly larger bias for the medium and large distance between the vertical lines. By contrast, for the maximum radial lines condition the largest bias is observed when the vertical lines are closer together. These two conditions (minimum and maximum radial lines) look quite different. In the minimum radial lines each individual radial line (and their angle) plays a more significant role in affecting our perception. Also, the more we move further away from the center, the more pronounced the lines angle becomes, possibly contributing to the observed effect. In contrast, at the maximum radial condition individual lines seem far less important and it is instead the overall “texture” that matters. A mechanistic explanation for this could be the continuous interplay between intracortical connectivity and long-range connectivity given a certain visual stimulus and is supported by the fact that the size of receptive fields to fit the behavioral results via the model increases as we increase the number of background lines.

If we consider the perceptually more complex phenomenon of the Poggendorff illusion, its determinants, i.e. the angle of the crossing line and the width of the central surface, both had an influence on the perception of the illusion, in accordance with previous studies (Jones-Buxton and Wall, 2001; Weintraub and Krantz, 1971). In fact, the participants' bias increased as the rectangle's width increased, while the smaller the angle, the larger the bias. This seems to suggest that the presence of a rectangle surface constitutes a much stronger determinant for the formation of long-range connections than two straight lines (Hering illusion). Also, an interaction between the two factors (width and angle) at a behavioral level was observed. It is also worthwhile to observe that in the behavioral results of the three illusions, the observed bias is always significantly different from zero. This would indicate that participants do not have a sharp “non-illusory” effect while tested. Instead, while observers do indeed have the perceptual experience of what is not an illusion, there is nonetheless a slight bias reported. We found no evidence of significant correlations between the sizes of the biases across the three different illusions. This suggests that the described mechanisms (intracortical and long-range connectivity) contribute to a different extent to each of these illusions. The size, shape and bandwidth of the receptive field corresponding to the intra-cortical selected receptive field profile is identified by means of an iterative selection of the parameters that fit the behavioral results. While we did successfully consider the modulation of the long-range connectivity between selected receptive field profiles within each illusion, the correspondence between the in-silico parameters and the neurophysiological mechanisms (which varies across illusions) warrants further investigation. Brain imaging studies would likely help us to further explore the underlying mechanism and the extent to they are separate or overlapping across the three illusions.

The behavioral results served as a basis for tuning the computational models, i.e. iteratively exploring the set of parameters representing the intra-cortical (σ;γ; b‾; bandwidth - Gabor filters parameters) and long-range connectivity (c;ξ) within the model to produce a computational bias matching the recorded behavior. Computational models have the unique value of enabling the in-silico exploration of the boundaries of the neurophysiological mechanisms modelled without the need of running an in vivo experiment to do so. Within the same illusions, all predicted (computational) biases have been computed by varying the size of receptive profiles until fitting and by fixing the constant c. The size of receptive fields mediates for local interaction between simple cells belonging to the same hypercolumn, together with playing the role of a subsequent level of visual cortices (V2), known to have larger receptive fields. A fine tuning of the parameters detailed above helped investigating the contribution of intra-cortical connectivity to the formation of long-range connectivity: the need for large-size (σ) receptive fields has been identified by means of the model, as a function of increased number of features in the visual stimuli, pointing out the likely contribution of higher-level visual processes such as those in V2 and perhaps elsewhere (Murray and Herrmann, 2013). The strength of neuro-geometrical models give a clear and elegant framework to explain the cellular organization and construction of visual percepts, which can be easily applied to other illusions, such as the Kanizsa triangle (Citti and Sarti, 2006) or basic perceptual phenomena (line completion) (Favali et al., 2015).

The computational models replicated perfectly the Zöllner and Hering behavioral results as well as specific conditions of the Poggendorff illusion. Undoubtedly, the current neuro-geometrical models, despite discarding the connectivity time features (i.e. the speed at which such connections take place in the hypercolumnar structure of V1 and V2) provided an excellent match with the neurophysiological mechanisms underpinning the illusions, i.e. strength of the connectivity, size of receptive fields, etc. Its effectiveness in predicting “spatial-related” phenomena such as the Hering and Zöllner illusions, where any effect of distance between the vertical lines seems to play a central role, indicates that the main neural mechanism involved is these illusions is likely the intra-cortical connectivity of V1, which is thought to transpire on a relatively fast timescale, and the support of the long-range connectivity, modelled as size of receptive fields, changes by varying the features present in the image. Furthermore, new generative illusions where the insight on receptive fields was used to feed the model with new visual stimuli validated the linear nature of the perceptual biases for these illusions. As for the Poggendorff illusion, the fitted conditions clearly highlighted an increase in the parameter ξ, which describes the relationship between spatial and orientation components and the strength of the long-range connectivity, i.e. the “strength in tilting” of the curve to point towards the perceptual completion. The more elements there are in the visual stimuli (and the bigger their complexity), the more “activation” is need to complete a percept such as that elicited by the Poggendorff illusion. We think that our computational and behavioral results could be complemented by brain mapping and neuroimaging studies with high temporal resolution to complete the spatio-temporal brain analysis of such phenomena (Biasiucci et al., 2019).

A limitation of the computational modelling implemented here is that it was not possible to generate variability in performance that is similar to the inter-individual variability observed in human participants. This could be artificially generated via Monte-Carlo simulations able to automatically generate a variance around the Gabor filters’ parameters or the long-range connectivity parameters, which has been partially investigated via the iteratively fitting procedure.

To conclude, the present study combining a carefully designed behavioral and computational paradigms highlights the likely neurophysiological mechanisms e.g. intra-cortical connectivity and long-range connectivity in V1/V2 implicated in the Hering, Zöllner and the Poggendorff illusions and provides a roadmap for future studies using psychophysical data to tune and validate computational models.
