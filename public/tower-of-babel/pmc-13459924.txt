Mapping shared and specific cortical after-effects of repetitive TMS on brain function
Authors: Yating Lv, Zijian Feng, Fengmei Fan, Jinhui Wang, Jingsong Wu, Zaixu Cui, Mingrui Xia, Gong-Jun Ji, Xiujuan Geng, Liyang Sai, Qihong Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRepetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is widely used to modulate brain activity and treat neurological and psychiatric disorders, yet how diverse stimulation protocols shape cortical function—and whether they share common principles—remains unclear.MethodsWe combined resting-state fMRI acquired before and after single-session rTMS across nine distinct protocols targeting specific cortical regions to comprehensively map changes in the local amplitude of hemodynamic fluctuations.ResultsWe found that after-effects co-varied across protocols, with similarity patterns constrained by structural and functional connectivity. Strikingly, a shared spatial pattern emerged across all protocols, closely aligned with the brain’s sensory–association hierarchy. Both shared and protocol-specific effects exhibited significant correspondence with the distribution of neurotransmitter systems. Mapping to the Neurosynth cognitive atlas revealed many-to-many correspondences between rTMS effects and cognitive domains, primarily driven by stimulation of parietal and motor regions. These rTMS-induced changes also overlapped with functional abnormalities in major depressive disorder and Parkinson’s disease, highlighting the parietal cortex as a potential transdiagnostic target. Key results were independently replicated in two datasets of multi-session rTMS.ConclusionsThese findings reveal a unified organizational framework for rTMS after-effects, grounded in cortical hierarchy, neurochemistry, and cognitive activations, offering potential guidance for neuromodulations of brain disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04978-7.Keywords: rTMS, Sensory-association, Neurotransmitters, Cognition, Brain disorders

Background

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a powerful non‑invasive technique for brain modulation [1], yet its observed effects exhibit substantial heterogeneity across studies. This variability is frequently attributed to the wide diversity of rTMS protocols, which include distinct targeting sites (e.g., motor and prefrontal cortex) and stimulation protocols (for instance, high‑frequency rTMS and theta burst stimulation (TBS)) [2–4]. While different protocols are known to influence overlapping cognitive or clinical domains [5–15], it remains unclear whether they engage shared or distinct large-scale brain circuits. This is critical as rTMS after-effects propagate through functionally connected networks beyond the stimulation site [16–21], raising a key unresolved issue: does rTMS modulate a common cortical system across protocols, and if so, what organizational principles, such as cortical hierarchy, govern its architecture? Furthermore, given that rTMS acts through neuromodulatory mechanisms [22–25], engages multiple cognitive domains [5–7, 10, 12], and is clinically applied across a range of brain disorders [8, 9, 11, 13–15], the alignment between the common after‑effects observed across distinct rTMS protocols and their neurochemical substrates, cognitive correlates, and disorder‑related functional alterations remains unclear.

To identify both shared and protocol-specific functional effects, we employed the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), a widely recognized, reliable and regionally specific metric for assessing the amplitude of BOLD fluctuations [26, 27]. As a direct measure of local activity amplitude, ALFF is sensitive to both widespread changes common across protocols and focal alterations unique to individual ones. Its utility in capturing rTMS-induced plasticity has been demonstrated in prior studies [28, 29]. More importantly, ALFF is mechanistically informative: its variance has been linked to neurochemical processes, including regional excitatory-inhibitory balance and the function of specific neurotransmitter systems [30, 31]. This neurochemical link is pivotal, as rTMS after-effects are established to arise through the modulation of these neurotransmitter systems [22–25]. Furthermore, ALFF correlates with differences in cognitive performance and is altered in several brain disorders [26], domains that are direct targets of rTMS interventions [32–36]. Consequently, ALFF serves as a convergent biomarker, positioned to bridge rTMS protocol-induced changes in brain activity, their underlying neurochemical substrates, and their behavioral and clinical relevance.

To this end, we integrated pre- and post-rTMS fMRI data from 283 sessions across nine real single-session protocols and three sham conditions in 211 healthy participants. We quantified cortical changes using ALFF to map after-effects induced by each real protocol compared to its matched sham control. We then systematically examined whether the spatial patterns of these changes (1) are organized along the cortical hierarchy [37, 38], (2) correlate with the distributions of key neurotransmitter receptors [39], (3) relate to meta-analytic maps of task-based cognitive activation [40], and (4) associate with functional abnormality observed in brain disorders [41, 42]. By integrating these multiscale associations, our work provides an integrative framework for understanding how rTMS reshapes the human brain.

Methods

Experimental design and datasets

To reveal the cortical profiles in response to rTMS, we curated three datasets including nine single-session real rTMS protocols and three sham rTMS protocols applied to healthy young adults, collected from multiple studies (Fig. 1, Additional file 1: Table S1, and Additional file 2: Supplementary methods [1, 18, 20, 39, 40, 43–48]). Overall, the nine real rTMS protocols included in this study targeted both motor and association cortical regions using a variety of stimulation protocols: continuous TBS (cTBS) applied to the right primary motor cortex (rM1c, identified as the most significantly activated voxel in this region during a left-handed serial reaction time task); cTBS applied to the right supplementary motor area (rSMAc, identified as the most significantly activated voxel in this region during the above-mentioned serial response task); intermittent TBS (iTBS) applied to the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (rDLi, located at the F4 electrode of the 10–20 EEG system); iTBS applied to the left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (lVLi, positioned at MNI coordinates (-46, 21, -5)); high-frequency rTMS applied to the left primary motor cortex (lM1H, identified as the most significantly activated voxel along the left anterior wall of the central sulcus during a right finger-tapping task) [20]; low-frequency rTMS applied to the left primary motor cortex (lM1L, identified as the most significantly activated voxel along the left anterior wall of the central sulcus during a right finger-tapping task) [20]; high-frequency rTMS applied to the right middle frontal gyrus (rMFGH, determined by the peak voxel in this region that connected to the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex) [18]; high-frequency rTMS applied to the right inferior parietal lobule (rIPLH, determined by the peak voxel in this region that connected to the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex) [18]; and high-frequency rTMS consecutively applied to the right middle frontal gyrus and inferior parietal lobule (rDOUBLEH, the two above-mentioned targets) [18]. Each participant underwent one MRI scan before (pre-rTMS) and one scan after rTMS stimulation (post-rTMS).

Dataset 1 includes four real (rM1c, rSMAc, rDLi, lVLi) and one sham (Sham1) interventions with a between-subject design, using a same imaging protocol on a GE Architect 3-Tesla scanner with a 48-channel head coil at the Affiliated Hospital of Hangzhou Normal University. Dataset 2 comprises two real (lM1H, lM1L) and one sham (Sham2) interventions also with a between-subject design, using a same imaging protocol on a GE MR-750 3-Tesla scanner with an 8-channel head coil located at the same hospital. Dataset 3 includes three real (rMFGH, rIPLH, rDOUBLEH) and one sham (Sham3) interventions with a within-subject design, using the same imaging protocol as Dataset 2. Detailed rTMS protocols and fMRI acquisition parameters are provided in Additional file 2: Supplementary methods.

Although minor differences existed in scanner versions and specific fMRI parameters, all protocols were executed in the same room at the same site under an identical experimental design. Post-fMRI session was conducted immediately after the complement of the rTMS intervention, with a time gap (< 30 min; mean ± SD: 7.9 ± 5.9 min) for participant transportation from rTMS room to MR scanner room and getting prepared for fMRI scan. The exact duration between the end of the rTMS session and the beginning of the post-fMRI session was recorded for the six real and two sham protocols included in Datasets 1 and 2 (Additional file 1: Table S1); this timing information was not recorded for Dataset 3. There were no significant differences in duration among the four real and one sham rTMS protocols (F = 0.857, P = 0.330) in Dataset 1, or among the two real and one sham protocols (F = 0.845, P = 0.437) in Dataset 2.

fMRI data processing

We followed the same preprocessing and denoising procedures for each dataset to ensure comparability. Both the pre- and post-rTMS fMRI data were preprocessed using AFNI (https://afni.nimh.nih.gov/) and FMRIB Software Library (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/docs/#/) following the same processing strategy. For each fMRI scan, preprocessing steps included removal of several initial volumes (20 volumes for Dataset 1 and 10 volumes for the other two datasets), slice timing correction, realignment (motion correction), and removal of nuisance covariates including Friston-24 head motion parameters [49], mean signals from the white matter and cerebrospinal fluid, and their first derivatives. Spatial normalization to the International Consortium for Brain Mapping standard template (ICBM152) was performed with a voxel size of 2 × 2 × 2 mm3, using a nonlinear transformation estimated from spatial normalization of the high-resolution T1 image to the same template. The preprocessed fMRI data were then spatially smoothed with a full-width at half-maximum of 6 mm.

ALFF was computed based on the preprocessed fMRI data to quantify the average spectral power within the 0.01–0.08 Hz frequency range, following methodologies from previous studies [27]. An ALFF map was calculated for each of the pre- and post-rTMS fMRI scans. ALFF maps were then parcellated into 100 cortical regions according to the Schaefer atlas [50] and z-scored.

rTMS after-effects construction

rTMS after-effect was assessed by effect size for each protocol at group level. For each participant in each rTMS protocol, an ALFF difference map was calculated as the post-ALFF map minus the pre-ALFF map. Principal component analysis (PCA) on the rTMS after-effect maps

To extract the shared neural response patterns across the nine rTMS protocols, we performed PCA on the nine rTMS after-effect maps. The after-effect data were z-scored for each protocol prior to PCA and the structure of the input matrix was organized as regions × protocols, i.e., 100 × 9. A score map for each principal component (PC) was then derived, representing a summary of cortical responses. Each score map reflects the degree to which a brain region expresses the dominant modulation pattern captured by that PC, thereby indicating its contribution to the shared neural‑response axis. Associated feature loading for each PC was computed to examine how the after-effect induced by each protocol was mapped onto that PC.

Dominance analysis of rTMS after-effects with the spatial landscape of neurotransmitter systems

Dominance analysis [51] is adopted to determine the relative importance (dominance) of each rTMS after-effect (independent variable) to the model fit (adjusted R2) of the multiple linear regression model, with a neurotransmitter as dependent variable. This method evaluates all possible subset models derived from the full set of predictors. For a model with p input variables, the procedure fits the regression on every combination of predictors, resulting in 2p − 1 distinct submodels. The total dominance of a predictor is then calculated as the average increase in adjusted R2 obtained by adding that predictor to each of the submodels in which it was originally absent, averaged across all relevant subsets. A key property of this approach is that the sum of the total dominance values for all predictors equals the total adjusted R2 of the full model, thereby providing an intuitive and additive decomposition of the overall explained variance among predictors. Unlike metrics based solely on regression coefficients or univariate associations, dominance analysis inherently accounts for shared variance and interactions among predictors, offering a more robust measure of each variable’s contribution within the correlated predictor set. Finally, each dominance value was normalized by the total adjusted R2 of the model, which allow for direct comparison of predictor importance both within and across different models.

Neurotransmitter receptor densities were constructed by collating positron emission tomography (PET) images from a total of 19 different neurotransmitter receptors, transporters, and receptor-binding sites from an independent sample with a combined total of 1,238 healthy participants [6]. The neurotransmitter landscapes, were generated across nine different neurotransmitter systems, including dopamine (D1, D2, and DAT), noradrenaline (NET), serotonin (5-HT1A, 5-HT1B, 5-HT2A, 5-HT4, 5-HT6, and 5-HTT), acetylcholine (α4β2, M1, and VAChT), glutamate (mGluR5 and NMDA), GABA (GABAA), histamine (H3), cannabinoid (CB1), and opioid (MOR). All PET neurotransmitter maps were parcellated into the same 100 cortical regions and z-scored.

Spatial permutations test for spatial autocorrelation correction

Spatial autocorrelation, where neighboring data points are not statistically independent, violates the assumptions of many common inferential frameworks [52], such as computing spatial correlation between brain maps in this study. To ensure statistical inferences accurately reflect the underlying data, it is necessary to generate null distributions preserving the spatial autocorrelation of the brain maps. The spatial permutation test, called the spin test [53] (https://brainsmash.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html), generates a null distribution of randomly rotated brain maps that preserve spatial covariance structure of the original map.

To test whether rTMS similarity is greater in structurally connected regions than in unconnected ones, we generated a spatially constrained null model of the structural connectome. This model preserved key topological and spatial properties of the structural connectome [54], including its edge density, edge length distribution, and degree distribution. This edge-swapping procedure was repeated iteratively for 10,000 permutations. To compute a P value, the mean rTMS similarity of unconnected edges was subtracted from the mean rTMS similarity of connected edges, and this difference was compared to a null distribution of differences computed on the 10,000 rewired networks.

To test whether rTMS similarity is greater within the same intrinsic networks than between different intrinsic networks, according to the Yeo seven-network classification [55]. This model permuted the intrinsic network affiliations while preserving spatial autocorrelation [48], repeated iteratively for 10,000 permutations. To compute a P value, the mean rTMS similarity of within network edges was subtracted from the mean rTMS similarity of between network edges, and this difference was compared to a null distribution of differences computed on the 10,000 rewired networks.

To test whether the PC1 of rTMS after-effects was spatially correlated with each of the three gradients previously linked to cortical hierarchy. 10,000 surrogate maps were constructed for the PC1 of rTMS after-effects. For each cortical hierarchy map, its spatial correlation with each surrogate map was calculated. P value for spatial correlation was calculated as the proportion of correlations from the 10,000 surrogate data greater than (for positive correlations) or less than (for negative correlations) the observed real correlation value. Similar spatial correlation analyses were conducted for the PC1 of rTMS after-effects with PET neurotransmitter maps and functional abnormality maps of brain disorders.

Results

rTMS after-effects on local hemodynamic activity co-vary across protocols

For each rTMS protocol, an after-effect map of ALFF, was calculated. Examining the unthresholded effect size maps enabled us to gain a more comprehensive view of the cortical profiles for rTMS after-effects, especially when the sample size is limited [56]. We observed small to medium effect sizes (Cohen’s d no less than 0.2) in more than 30% of cortical regions for all the rTMS protocols (Fig. 2A), and in more than 50% of cortical regions for lM1L (74%), rIPLH (61%), lVLi (57%) and rM1c (56%) protocols.

Stimulation targets are marked with black dots. B rTMS similarity matrix calculated as the Pearson correlation of after-effect patterns across the nine protocols for each pair of brain regions. The similarity matrix was ordered according to the Yeo seven-network classification: frontoparietal, default mode, dorsal attention, limbic, ventral attention, somatomotor and visual. C rTMS similarity is higher when two regions are structurally connected (left) and belong to the same functional network (right). The bars and errorbars indicate mean values and SE

Having mapped rTMS after-effects across the cortex, we next derived rTMS after-effect similarity to test how after-effects co-vary across protocols. The rTMS after-effect similarity was computed as the pairwise regional Pearson’s correlations based on the nine-element vector of after-effects across the nine rTMS protocols (Fig. 2B). A strong negative correlation between two regions indicates that across the nine rTMS protocols, the ALFF after-effects in these two regions consistently change in opposite directions. Based on group-consensus structural and resting-state functional connectivity from the Human Connectome Project [39, 57], we found that cross-rTMS similarity was significantly greater between structurally connected regions than between unconnected regions (Pspin = 0.0450, against 10,000 surrogate structural connectivity matrices; Fig. 2C). Cross-rTMS similarity was also significantly greater within intrinsic functional networks than between networks (Pspin = 0.0040, against 10,000 surrogate functional connectivity matrices; Fig. 2C). Thus, cross-rTMS similarity was bounded by both structural and functional connectivity.

To rule out the potential bias for repeated use of the sham condition maps, we re-conducted the rTMS similarity analysis based on real rTMS after-effects without subtraction of the sham effects. Similar results to those shown in Fig. 2B were observed (Additional file 3: Fig. S1), with a correlation of 0.72 (across N = 100 × 99/2 elements) between the two similarity matrices. This indicates that the strong pattern of inter-protocol similarity reported in Fig. 2B is robust and not an artifact of the experimental design or the sham-correction procedure.

Situating shared rTMS after-effects with cortical hierarchy

PCA was performed to identify one or more PCs that capture the dominant, shared axes of neural response variation induced by different rTMS interventions. The first principal component (PC1) (Fig. 3A-B) exhibited a significantly greater eigenvalue compared to those obtained from spin-permuted surrogate rTMS after-effects (Pspin = 0.0025, 10,000 repetitions), accounting for 33.53% of the total variance. This result suggests the presence of a dominant cross-protocol modulation pattern that captures shared spatial features of cortical responses to rTMS. To examine how the after-effect induced by each protocol mapped onto PC1, we visualized the PC1 loadings for all nine protocols, which revealed a clear bipolar opposition (Fig. 3C). High-frequency rTMS and iTBS targeting the association regions, as well as low-frequency rTMS targeting the dominant sensory regions loaded on the negative side of PC1. In the contrast, cTBS targeting the non-dominant sensory regions loaded on the positive side. It should be noted that the global sign of a PC in PCA is arbitrary, multiplying the entire component by -1 yields a mathematically equivalent solution. However, the relative signs among the loadings within that component are interpretable and stable. Our analysis showed that different rTMS protocols loaded onto PC1 in opposite directions. Crucially, this pattern of relative signs, i.e., which protocols cluster together versus in opposition—is invariant to an overall sign flip.

We next examined whether PC1 score map spatially correlates with the organizing principles of the cortical hierarchy. This investigation was motivated by the observation that rTMS effects are not randomly distributed across the cortex, but instead preferentially engage regions according to hierarchical cortical organization (Fig. 3B). We found that the PC1 score map was significantly correlated with all three cortical hierarchy gradients (all Pspin values < 0.001, 10,000 repetitions; Fig. 3D). These findings indicate that the shared rTMS-induced modulation pattern is embedded within the broader sensory-association cortical axis, spanning functional, structural, and molecular domains.

To evaluate whether rTMS specifically engages this hierarchy, we generated null models/spatial perturbations using the effects (difference between post-ALFF and pre-ALFF) of the three sham conditions and calculated PC1 of the three sets of sham after-effects. We observed non-significant alignment with any of the cortical hierarchies using the PC1 of these three sham after-effects (Additional file 3: Fig. S2; all P values > 0.05). While the sham-based control analysis strengthens our methodological approach, it does not fully address the broader concern that any spatially widespread perturbation pattern may project onto the principal cortical hierarchy.

Although the second, third and fourth principal components also showed significant eigenvalues (all Pspin values < 0.01, 10,000 repetitions), explained 22.51%, 14.63%, and 12.45% of the total variance, separately, score maps of these three principal components were not significantly associated with the abovementioned functional, structural or molecular hierarchies (all Pspin values > 0.05, 10,000 repetitions).

To explicitly test whether the PC1 manifests differently among networks, we first parcellated the cortical surface into Yeo seven-network classification. For each rTMS protocol, we then computed the spatial correlation between its cortical after-effect map and the spatial pattern of PC1 separately within each of these seven networks. We found that the relationship between a protocol’s after-effect and PC1 was highly consistent across all networks for a given protocol, but this relationship varied fundamentally across different protocols. For example, protocols whose whole‑brain after‑effect was negatively correlated with PC1 (e.g., high‑frequency rTMS targeting association regions, low‑frequency rTMS targeting dominant sensory regions) maintained a consistent negative correlation with PC1 in nearly all seven networks. Conversely, protocols that showed a positive or non‑significant whole‑brain correlation (e.g., cTBS targeting non‑dominant sensory regions, high‑frequency rTMS targeting a sensory region) generally did not exhibit significant correlations with PC1 in any of the functional networks (Additional file 3: Fig. S3).

Shared and specific rTMS after-effects correlate with the neurotransmitter landscape

Neurotransmitters shape the way that neuronal circuits respond to neuromodulation [58]. Given that initial studies on rTMS after-effects have demonstrated changes not only in neuronal activity, but also in neurotransmitter levels [59], we were motivated to investigate the dominant rTMS contributions to the neurotransmitter landscape across the cortex.

The neurotransmitter landscapes were represented as cortical distributions of 19 neurotransmitter receptors and transporters across nine different neurotransmitter systems, obtained using PET imaging from more than 1,200 healthy participants [39]. We first tested whether the shared rTMS-effect, i.e., the PC1 score map, would spatially correlate with the neurotransmitter profiles. The shared rTMS effect showed significant associations with 6 out the 19 neurotransmitters (Fig. 4A), including opioid, cannabinoid, serotonin (i.e., 5HT4, 5HT1a and 5HT2a) and noradrenaline transmitters (Pspin < 0.05, 10,000 repetitions). The opioid receptor showed the highest correlation with the PC1 map, which remained significant after FDR correction.

To investigate whether the combined spatial patterns of multiple rTMS protocols collectively explain the cortical distribution of neurotransmitter systems, we fitted a multiple linear regression model to predict each neurotransmitter cortical profile based on the rTMS-induced hemodynamic after-effects. Model significance was assessed against a spin-permuted null model (10,000 repetitions). We found that 13 out of the 19 models were significant (Pspin < 0.05, one-sided), and 11 models remained significant after FDR correction among the 19 models. Notably, the rTMS after-effect maps showed strong spatial correspondence with the cortical distributions of serotonin, dopamine, glutamate, noradrenaline, and opioid systems (Fig. 4B), suggesting that the spatial topographies of multiple rTMS protocols may jointly associate with the organization of neurotransmitter systems. While the shared rTMS effect captured by PC1 exhibited limited though significant associations with selected neurotransmitters, the combined spatial patterns of individual rTMS protocols, as modeled by multiple regression, accounted for broader variance in neurotransmitter distribution, revealing stronger and more spatially extensive relationships.

To determine how individual rTMS protocols specifically contributed to the total fit (adjusted R2) of the statistically significant models, we performed a dominance analysis [60, 61], which assigned a proportion of the final model fit to each independent variable [51], i.e., an rTMS after-effect map in the current study. To make dominance comparable across models, the dominance was normalized by the final fit of each model (Fig. 4C). rTMS at the primary motor cortex, including cTBS, low- and high-frequency rTMS, contributed substantially to the fit with cannabinoid, opioid, dopamine, and mGluR5 receptors, while cTBS at the right supplementary motor area made divergent contribution to the fit with noradrenaline. Compared to other rTMS protocols, the after-effects of iTBS and high-frequency rTMS at the prefrontal cortex showed large contributions to the fit with multiple serotonin as well as dopamine receptors and glutamate distribution (NMDA). Finally, high-frequency rTMS at the inferior parietal lobule contributed specifically to the fit of GABA receptor.

Link shared and specific rTMS after-effects to cognitive functions

rTMS has been widely used as a neuromodulation tool to enhance cognitive performance [3, 34, 62] for healthy and clinical populations. We next examined how shared and specific rTMS after-effect profiles correspond to cognitive processes. Neurosynth [45] was used to derive 123 meta-analytic large-scale task activation maps, which represent regional changes in brain activity elicited by different cognitive processes (Additional file 2: Supplementary methods). We applied partial least squares (PLS) analysis [46] to identify a multivariate mapping between rTMS after-effect profiles and functional activation maps (Additional file 2: Supplementary methods).

The PLS analysis extracted a significant latent variable relating shared rTMS after-effect profiles to functional activation over the cortex (Fig. 5A). The latent variable represents the dominant spatial pattern of rTMS after-effects (rTMS scores) and functional activations (cognitive scores), capturing 56.98% of the covariance between rTMS after-effects and cognitive activations (Pspin < 0.001, 10,000 repetitions). Both the rTMS and cognitive score patterns reveal a sensory-association spatial gradient, separating higher-level association cortices from visual and somatosensory cortices (Fig. 5B and D; both Pspin < 0.001, 10,000 repetitions), and spatially associate with PC1 score map shown in Fig. 3B (both Pspin < 0.01, 10,000 repetitions).

Specific rTMS loading for each protocol was then derived by calculating the spatial correlation between its after-effect map and the PLS-derived scores. Thus, the rTMS loading can be treated as the contribution of each rTMS protocol to the latent variable. Using the same strategy, cognitive loadings were also derived. Interestingly, the rTMS loadings from the PLS analysis with cognition showed positive loadings in the cTBS protocols at the right motor regions and high-frequency rTMS at the left primary motor cortex, as well as negative loadings in other protocols (Fig. 5C). The cognitive processes with large positive loadings were enriched for sensory information processing and perception, such as visual processing, movement and multisensory integration in sensory regions (Fig. 5E). This suggests that after-effects of cTBS at the non-dominant motor regions co-varied with functional activation in sensory regions, consistent with the role of rTMS in motor regions in sensory processing. On the other hand, we found that negative rTMS loadings spatially co-varied with cognitive functions primarily focused on emotional and memory processes. This is in line with the findings that fronto-parietal rTMS enhances higher-level cognitive functions. Collectively, these results demonstrate a many-to-many mapping between cortex-wide rTMS after-effects and functional activations.

Shared and specific rTMS after-effects associate with functional abnormalities of brain disorders

rTMS has been widely adopted as a non-invasive neuromodulation treatment for multiple brain disorders. Characterizing the rTMS protocols that correspond to specific disorders is important for developing new neuromodulation treatments. We related rTMS after-effects on cortical activity to functional abnormality patterns of major depressive disorder and Parkinson’s disease (Fig. 6A). The functional abnormality pattern in major depressive disorder was assessed by alterations in BOLD fluctuation amplitude indexed by ALFF in 709 patients compared to 725 healthy controls [42]. The functional abnormality pattern in Parkinson’s diseases was also derived from BOLD ALFF changes in a large sample of 376 patients compared to 311 healthy controls [41].

The shared rTMS after-effect, represented by PC1 score map, demonstrated significant spatial correlation with cortical abnormalities in major depressive disorder and Parkinson’s disease (Fig. 6B; both Pspin < 0.05 after FDR correction). Functional abnormality patterns of both disorders showed significant correspondence with rTMS after-effect profiles (Pspin < 0.05, 10,000 repetitions, one-sided). We found that after-effects of the high-frequency rTMS at the right inferior parietal lobule, cTBS at the right primary motor cortex, low-frequency rTMS at the left primary motor cortex, and iTBS at the left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex associated with cortical abnormalities of both disorders (Fig. 6C). Moreover, after-effects of the high-frequency rTMS at the right middle frontal gyrus specifically correlated with cortical abnormalities of major depressive disorder, and iTBS at the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex specifically correlated with cortical abnormalities of Parkinson’s disease.

Robustness, sensitivity and replication analyses

To ensure the current findings were not driven by the selection of cortical parcellations, we replicated our main results using a 200-region Schaefer parcellation [50] and a 219-region Lausanne atlas [63] (Fig. 7). We repeated cortical hierarchy association, neurotransmitter contribution, cognitive mapping, and brain disorder correspondence analyses (Fig. 7). All the rTMS after-effects from these two different parcellations were highly similar to the original results. Using both parcellations, the shared rTMS after-effects evaluated by the PC1 scores showed significant spatial correlations with cortical hierarchy. The serotonin and opioid neurotransmitters were consistently and significantly fitted by rTMS after-effects using these two cortical parcellations, with rTMS targeted at motor regions contributed to both serotonin and opioid neurotransmitters, rTMS at the frontal regions specifically contributed to serotonin, while rTMS at the parietal region contributed to opioid. Likewise, a significant many-to-many mapping between Neurosynth cognitive activations and rTMS after-effects from both cortical parcellations were robustly replicated. In addition, rTMS targeting motor and inferior parietal lobule regions specifically contributed to the cortical abnormalities of depression, while rTMS at the frontal-parietal regions contributed to the cortical abnormalities of Parkinson’s disease beyond the traditional motor targets.

We then asked whether any single rTMS protocol disproportionately influenced rTMS after-effects on hemodynamic activity. To test this, we performed a leave-one-protocol-out stability analysis to test the robustness of the main findings in each subset of data (nine distinct data subsets, each created by systematically excluding one of the nine rTMS protocols). All the rTMS after-effects from these nine distinct subsets were highly correlated with the original PC1 scores (Additional file 3: Fig. S4A; all Pearson’s r (98) > 0.89), linear regression model fits to the 19 neurotransmitters (Additional file 3: Fig. S4B; all Pearson’s r (17) > 0.92), the 123 Neurosynth cognitive term loadings (Additional file 3: Fig. S4C; all Pearson’s r (121) > 0.97), and similar models fits to cortical abnormalities of the two brain disorders (all ratio > 0.93; Additional file 3: Fig. S4D), all confirming that the spatial profiles of rTMS after-effects are not driven by a single rTMS protocol.

Although there is non-significant differences in sex (Dataset 1: χ2 = 5.483, P = 0.241; Dataset 2: χ2 = 0.180, P = 0.914; Dataset 3: within-subjects design), given the known relevance of sex to rTMS effects [64, 65], we also stratified the data by sex and repeated the rTMS after‑effect analyses in female and male subgroups. All main findings were well replicated in both subgroups (Additional file 3: Fig. S5).

While single-session paradigms are commonly employed to investigate immediate or short-term after-effects on brain function, multi-session rTMS more closely reflects clinical and therapeutic applications. To enhance the generalizability and translational relevance of our findings derived from single-session rTMS data, we incorporated two independent multi-session rTMS datasets for replication (Additional file 1: Table S2 and Additional file 3: Fig. S6). The replication results (Additional file 3: Figs. S7-S10 and Additional file 2: Supplementary results) validate the robustness of both shared and specific after-effects induced by multi-session rTMS, thereby establishing a mechanistic link between the transient effects of single-session rTMS stimulation and the cumulative impact of multi-session rTMS interventions.

Discussion

In the present study, we characterized the shared and specific acute after-effects on hemodynamic activity over the cortex induced by nine single-session rTMS protocols with varied stimulation types and targeted regions. These rTMS after-effects on cortical fluctuation amplitude, co-varied across protocols. Critically, the principal component of these after-effects revealed a shared spatial organization along multiple hierarchical gradients of the cortex. Moreover, the rTMS induced effects on hemodynamic activity manifested as shared and specific spatial patterns associated with neurotransmitter levels, cognitive specialization, and brain dysfunction. Together, our findings uncovered the shared and specific rTMS after-effects over the cortex, bridge the gap between rTMS after-effect and brain organizational principles, and shed light on the modulatory effect of rTMS on neurotransmitters, cognition, and brain disorders.

Specific and shared after-effects across rTMS protocols

We found protocol-specific after-effects on hemodynamic activity over the cortex induced by varied rTMS protocols. Protocol-dependent rTMS after-effects are consistent with the previous studies showing that neuronal and glial plasticity [66], transcriptomic [67] changes in the cortex following rTMS, depend on the unique circuitry of the cortical region investigated. Moreover, the rTMS after-effects were widespread over the cortex, with 30% to more than 70% of brain regions exhibiting small to medium effect sizes. These findings support the notion that rTMS not only locally affects the targeted region but also induces changes in widespread regions across the brain [67, 68]. As the induced electric field decreases exponentially from the coil surface, the observed changes outside the stimulated region indicate the rTMS modulation effect propagates independently of the induced electric field. It has been speculated that rTMS alters neural excitability within the targeted site of stimulation [69, 70], which drives activity-dependent plasticity in downstream circuits [71] through anatomical connections.

Critically, our results demonstrate that rTMS after-effects exhibited significant covariation across stimulation protocols, with greater similarity in local hemodynamic amplitude modulation observed between structurally and functionally connected brain regions. These findings suggest that rTMS-induced plasticity is not restricted to the stimulated site but rather propagates in a network-dependent manner, governed by the intrinsic architecture of structural and functional connectivity. The observed covariation of after-effects across distinct rTMS protocols implies the existence of a shared network-level mechanism underlying diverse stimulation paradigms. The higher rTMS similarity in connected regions aligns with the network propagation hypothesis [17, 19], which posits that neuromodulatory effects disseminate through pre-existing structural and functional pathways. It should be noted that the covariation of after-effects across protocols could reflect homeostatic plasticity [72], wherein stimulation of one node triggers compensatory plasticity in connected regions to maintain system stability, irrespective of the specific rTMS protocol employed.

The presence of negative covariation across stimulation protocols provides evidence that the nine rTMS protocols engage a system-level dynamic between large-scale brain networks. This aligns with established models of inter-network competition, particularly between the sensory and association networks. rTMS may be systematically biasing this intrinsic competition, protocols that potentiate activity in one network tend to suppress activity in the other, and vice versa. rTMS may act as a perturbation that pushes the whole-brain system towards a state preferentially oriented toward either lower-order sensory processing or for higher-order cognitive/executive processing.

Shared and specific rTMS after-effects aligned with cortical hierarchy

Moreover, we discovered a shared spatial pattern of cortical responses to a broad array of rTMS protocols. Previous reports have linked rTMS after-effects to the brain’s structural connectivity [73], functional connectivity [17, 74], and gene expression [67]. While these reports demonstrate a link between after-effects of a specific rTMS protocol and a particular region or brain circuit, a comprehensive view over the cortex encompassing multiple rTMS protocols remains unknown. Our findings fill this gap, showing significant spatial alignments between the shared cortical activity pattern of the rTMS after-effects and the hierarchical gradients of brain functional, structural as well as molecular profiles across the cortex. The shared cortical activity represents a primary ‘neuromodulation polarity axis’ in the brain’s low‑frequency BOLD responses to rTMS. One pole of this axis aligns with spatial patterns characteristic of increased excitability in association areas or increased inhibition in sensory regions; the opposite pole aligns with patterns characteristic of increased inhibition in non-dominant sensory regions; in line with prior evidence that the fronto-parietal regions and motor regions are located at opposite ends of the cortical hierarchy [37].

Shared and specific rTMS after-effects correlated with neurotransmitters

A major manifestation of rTMS effect lies in modulation of neurotransmitters [59]. Importantly, we uncovered a spatial concordance between rTMS after-effects and cortical profiles of a wide range of neurotransmitters. Our results provide evidence for previously established associations between rTMS after-effects and neurotransmitters. For example, we found that the rTMS protocols targeting the prefrontal cortex associated with spatial distribution of serotonin receptors, consistent with the previous findings that rTMS at the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex affected the serotonin receptors [75, 76]. On the other hand, we additionally revealed new associations between specific rTMS after-effects and neurotransmitters. For instance, we found spatial correspondence that have not been established, including rTMS at the motor regions with the opioid receptor, and rTMS at the prefrontal cortex with the NMDA receptor. The multifaceted results may arise from potential biological mechanisms. Many of the rTMS protocols we curated have varied neurotransmitter targets. For example, rTMS at the prefrontal cortex can alter multiple neurotransmitters, including GABA [77], dopamine [25], and serotonin [76]. On the other hand, even when an rTMS protocol has low effect for a given neurotransmitter, it might get involved in the corresponding neurotransmitter system through complex downstream effects. For instance, the highest alignment with the spatial profiles of noradrenaline transporter was found for the cTBS at the right supplementary motor area. Our findings contribute to associating the large-scale after-effects of multiple rTMS protocols with the landscape of neurotransmitter systems, which will aid in the development of novel protocols for non-invasive modulation of neurotransmitters.

Shared and specific rTMS after-effects related to cognitive functions

As varying rTMS protocols are differentially patterned along the cortical hierarchy, we next asked how rTMS after-effects relate to cognitive processes. Rather than traditional one-to-one association investigations between rTMS modulation and cognitive function, we found a multivariate many-to-many mapping between rTMS after-effects and cognitive activations. Although individual rTMS protocols have been associated with specific functions (for example, high-frequency rTMS at the inferior parietal lobule and associative memory [74]), our findings suggest that the combined spatial distributions of high-frequency rTMS at the right inferior parietal lobule and low-frequency rTMS at the left primary motor cortex co-vary with patterns of cognitive activations related to emotional regulation and memory retrieval. Altogether, these results offer clues about how multiple rTMS protocols collectively influence cognitive functions and present novel hypotheses that future causal studies can test. In addition, we state that these findings will motivate rTMS applications in brain diseases [4, 78]. Individual patients exhibit multidimensional profiles, including diverse aspects of cognitive performance and clinical symptoms [79]. Thus, to design rTMS therapies targeting the cognitive profiles of individual patients, mapping rTMS after-effects to psychological processes will be critical. This mapping between rTMS after-effects and cognition offers a potential biological look-up table, providing multiple candidate targets for therapeutic interventions.

Shared and specific rTMS after-effects associated with functional abnormalities of brain disorders

We found that after-effects of rTMS targeted at the right inferior parietal lobule and the left primary motor cortex were major contributors to the functional abnormalities of major depressive disorder, though rTMS treatment protocols mainly focus on the prefrontal cortex [36, 80]. Our results do not challenge the established role of the prefrontal cortex in depression pathophysiology or the clinical efficacy of prefrontal rTMS. Rather, they suggest a complementary perspective on how network dysfunction in major depressive disorder may be expressed. Motor and parietal cortices serve as core hubs of the sensory and association networks, which exhibit robust and reliable alterations in the major depressive disorder [42, 81]. The rTMS‑induced after‑effect patterns resulting from stimulation of these regions may exhibit higher topographic overlap with the most consistent major depressive disorder-related network abnormalities compared to the more variable patterns elicited by prefrontal stimulation. While the largest rTMS after-effect contributor for Parkinson’s disease were the motor and prefrontal regions, which is as expected and in agreement with current rTMS treatment protocol for this brain disorder [35, 82, 83], the effectiveness of rTMS at the parietal region is non-trivial. Increasing evidence has shown that the parietal regions are promising candidate targets for rTMS [84, 85]. These results represent an initial step toward a correspondence map that relates rTMS interventions to brain disorders, providing potential guidance for developing new treatments for these disorders.

Robustness of the shared and specific rTMS after-effects

The consistency of fMRI-derived functional changes observed following both single-session and multi-session rTMS underscores the robustness of the identified shared and protocol-specific after-effects, and suggests that acute stimulation effects may contribute to the longer-term plastic changes induced by repeated sessions. Although the sample size for the multi-session analysis was limited, the directional concordance with single-session findings reinforces confidence in the reliability of our initial observations. Moreover, the validation analyses based on multi-session rTMS provide empirical support for the potential predictive value of early-session effects in informing long-term neuromodulatory outcomes. Such cross-paradigm validation not only enhances the credibility of our findings but also advances the translational potential of fMRI-based biomarkers as clinical indicators for rTMS treatment planning and monitoring.

Link ALFF findings to network-level framework

ALFF derived from BOLD fMRI signals, is a widely recognized, reliable metric for assessing the amplitude of BOLD fluctuations [26, 86, 87]. It is important to note that while ALFF quantifies regional signal amplitude, it is not a measure isolated from network dynamics. Spontaneous low-frequency fluctuations in one region are intrinsically linked to the activity of functionally connected regions. Consequently, a targeted rTMS intervention which is known to propagate through functional networks, can induce secondary local amplitude changes in distal network nodes. By performing whole-brain voxel-wise ALFF analysis, our approach was expressly designed to capture such distributed patterns of local activity modulation, potentially mapping both the primary stimulation site and the associated downstream (or interconnected) brain regions. The analysis revealed significant ALFF changes across the whole brain, a pattern consistent with the network‑propagation hypothesis of rTMS. The observed ALFF alterations in these distal regions likely reflect changes in local excitability or metabolic demand that occur as a consequence of the propagated influence from the stimulation target via anatomical or functional connections. Thus, ALFF provides a valuable and parsimonious metric for initially mapping the spatial extent of rTMS effects across the brain’s functional landscape.

Limitations and future directions

There are some limitations in the current work. First, although we curate an extensive coverage of rTMS protocols, it should be noted that the included rTMS protocols are not exhaustive. For instance, rTMS targeting the precuneus, which has been demonstrated as a useful therapeutic approach in Alzheimer’s disease patients [62, 88, 89], remains unexplored in our study regarding its after-effects on hemodynamic activity.

Second, the term ‘an rTMS protocol’ refers to a combination of stimulation parameters, including the target site and and the stimulation protocol. Future studies could systematically dissect these factors—for example, by applying multiple protocols to a single target, or by targeting multiple sites with a single protocol—to delineate their distinct neural effects.

Third, the target localization methods varied across the included rTMS protocols. Target identification can be achieved through multiple approaches (structural anatomy, diffusion MRI, or functional data), each with different implications for precision and biological validity. This variability in localization methods might contribute to the observed variance in after-effect among protocols. Future experimental work employing consistent, state-of-the-art, individualized targeting within a single cohort is needed to disambiguate the effects of stimulation site from the effects of localization method.

Fourth, the present study integrated datasets employing distinct sham control conditions (e.g., 180-degree flipped coil, 90-degree coil positioning), which are reported to differ in the degree of incidental auditory and somatosensory stimulation they produce [90]. While the primary analysis calculates after-effects as the difference between real and sham conditions, the heterogeneity in sham experiences could theoretically introduce variability in the estimated baseline ‘sham effect’. Our aggregated analytical approach implicitly assumes that this variability is random with respect to the real rTMS protocols. The use of standardized, active sham methods in future studies would help to better isolate and control for these peripheral sensory confounds.

Fifth, participants in all the datasets curated by the current study are healthy young adults. However, age has a significant effect on rTMS after-effects [33]. Potential avenues for future complementary research include examining whether and how rTMS after-effects vary with healthy development and aging [91].

Sixth, the current focused on group-level effects, which does not account for the well-documented and substantial inter-individual variability in TMS responses [92–94]. Our analysis, by averaging across participants, necessarily obscures these individual response patterns. Consequently, the generalizability of our group after-effect maps to a given individual is uncertain. Future studies with larger cohorts are urgently needed to move beyond group averages and systematically characterize the inter-individual variability.

Seventh, using rodent models [67, 68, 95], rTMS has been shown to induce protocol-, brain region- and cortical layer-dependent changes in neuronal plasticity. Whether rTMS induces layer-specific changes in the human brain remains an open question. Further investigations using ultra-high-filed 7-Tesla MRI with sub-millimeter resolution are warranted to address the potential rTMS after-effects across cortical layers [96].

Eighth, neurotransmitters and cognitive activations data were not acquired from the same participant cohort that underwent rTMS-fMRI. Instead, these maps were obtained from an independent, publicly available datasets. Within-subject fMRI and PET imaging would provide a more direct and powerful approach for establishing such neurochemical links. Nonetheless, comparing intervention-derived brain maps to curated reference maps from independent samples facilitates robust, cross-modal and cross-disciplinary discovery [97].

Finally, another limitation is the correlational nature of the association analyses with neurotransmitters, cognitive activations and brain disorders’ dysfunction, which do not indicate the direction of effect. However, these correlational findings can provide a reference to guide future causal experiments. For example, upon finding that an rTMS after-effect associates with a neurotransmitter not previously known for its involvement in neuromodulation, that rTMS protocol may act as a promising candidate for this neurotransmitter system.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we mapped both shared and specific cortical after-effects induced by nine rTMS protocols with various stimulation types and targeted regions, and validated these findings using two independent multi-session rTMS datasets. Our findings showed that these rTMS after-effects were organized by stimulated activity in locally targeted regions and affected distally connected networks, showing co-varied changes across protocols. Moreover, the shared rTMS after-effects spatially aligned with the cortical hierarchy. Both the shared and specific rTMS after-effects were associated with the brain’s rich neurotransmitter systems, correlated with a cognitive axis spanning emotion to perception, and related to patterns of functional abnormality in brain disorders. Together, these findings reveal a fundamental organizational principle underlying the brain’s hemodynamic responses to diverse rTMS interventions, and establish a reference framework for causally probing human brain function through neuromodulation.
