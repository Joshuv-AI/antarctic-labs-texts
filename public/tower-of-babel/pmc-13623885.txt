Why does scabies persist despite effective treatment? Coordination failure, Nash equilibrium, and replicator dynamics
Authors: Miryam Kerner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionScabies remains a persistent global public health problem despite the availability of effective treatments, including topical permethrin, benzyl benzoate, crotamiton, and systemic ivermectin. This paper develops a formal economic-epidemiological framework using game theory to explain persistence through coordination failure and Nash equilibrium.MethodsHousehold treatment decisions are modeled as a coordination game under imperfect information, treatment costs, and reinfection externalities. The analysis is further extended using an evolutionary replicator dynamics framework to examine how parameter conditions determine equilibrium stability and long-run behavioral outcomes. To illustrate the theoretical framework, I analyzed global data linking scabies disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) to household washing machine coverage.ResultsThe analytical results demonstrate that individually rational behavior can lead to inefficient equilibria, resulting in recurrent infections and elevated societal costs. The empirical analysis reveals a statistically significant negative association between scabies DALYs and household washing machine coverage that is consistent across multiple yearly specifications and stable regional patterns of household washing machine coverage.DiscussionThe empirical findings should be interpreted as correlational and illustrative rather than causal, since the empirical analysis does not control for the broader socioeconomic and institutional determinants of scabies burden. Nevertheless, the results are consistent with the theoretical framework and highlight the potential role of infrastructure-based hygiene interventions, in combination with mass drug administration and health education, in aligning individual incentives with socially optimal outcomes. Sustainable scabies control requires integrated economic, epidemiological, and behavioral strategies.

Introduction

Scabies, caused by the mite Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, is a neglected tropical disease with a substantial global burden (1, 2). Current estimates suggest that approximately 200–206 million individuals are infected at any given time, with more than 600 million new cases occurring annually. The disease contributes to approximately 5 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), reflecting both direct morbidity of very severe itching, especially at night accompanied by a rash, and secondary complications such as bacterial superinfection, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, and rheumatic heart disease (3–5). It often co-occurs with pyoderma and tinea infections, especially in pediatric populations (6). Skin disorders contribute substantially to global morbidity (7).

The epidemiology of scabies varies considerably across countries and regions. Scabies prevalence remains high in low-resource settings (8, 9). In low- and middle-income countries, particularly in tropical settings, the prevalence is often extremely high. In Papua New Guinea and Fiji, community prevalence can exceed 25%−30%, especially among children [(10, 11), based on national survey data in Fiji]. In Indonesia and parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence typically ranges between 10% and 20% (12). In contrast, high-income countries such as the United Kingdom, Germany, and Israel report lower baseline prevalence—generally below 1%−2%—yet have experienced rising incidence and periodic outbreaks in institutional settings such as nursing homes, prisons, and schools (2). In Australia, scabies remains endemic in some Indigenous communities, with prevalence exceeding 20% in certain populations (12).

These patterns indicate that while economic development mitigates prevalence, it does not eliminate disease (12). Structural factors such as overcrowding, access to healthcare, and institutional transmission dynamics remain central (13). Importantly, scabies spreads primarily through prolonged skin-to-skin contact, making the household the key unit of transmission (1).

Effective scabies control requires the simultaneous treatment of all close contacts, including asymptomatic individuals, to prevent ongoing transmission and reinfestation (14, 15). Failure to treat—even by a single individual or through incomplete adherence—can sustain transmission within households and lead to reinfection cycles, as untreated contacts act as a continuing reservoir of infestation. Outbreaks in households and institutional settings further highlight the importance of coordinated treatment strategies to interrupt transmission (12).

From an economic perspective, scabies transmission generates a collective-action problem because individual treatment decisions create positive externalities that extend beyond the treated person. Effective control requires coordinated activity among household members and close contacts, but individuals may underinvest in treatment if they do not fully internalize the benefits conferred on others. This logic is closely related to the broader literature on collective action, public goods, and common-pool resource problems (16–18). The persistence of scabies despite the availability of effective treatments illustrates how individually rational behavior can produce socially inefficient outcomes, especially when treatment decisions are influenced by expectations about others' compliance.

The present framework also relates to the literature on coordination games, strategic complementarities, and health externalities. Treatment decisions depend on beliefs regarding whether others will also participate in control efforts, creating the possibility of multiple equilibria and coordination failure (19–21). Similar mechanisms have been documented in infectious-disease settings, where preventive actions generate spillover effects that individuals may fail to internalize (22–24). For example, Miguel and Kremer (23) found that that treating certain people reduced the risk of illness for others, resulting in positive externalities. This illustrates how individual rational decisions may not result in the socially optimal level of treatment. More broadly, recent evidence emphasizes that scabies transmission is influenced not only by biological factors but also by behavioral, environmental, and socioeconomic conditions, reinforcing the importance of coordinated responses and collective prevention strategies.

This paper argues that the persistence of scabies can be understood as a coordination failure problem. Using a formal game-theoretic framework, I demonstrate that rational individuals may converge to inefficient Nash equilibria characterized by incomplete treatment and ongoing transmission. This framework provides a unified explanation for delayed treatment, partial treatment, recurrent infection, and persistent disease burden despite the availability of therapies.

Beyond the static Nash equilibrium framework, this study extends the analysis with an evolutionary game-theoretic perspective based on replicator dynamics. This extension allows the examination of how treatment behaviors evolve over time in response to differences in expected payoffs and how populations may converge toward efficient or inefficient equilibria under alternative treatment-cost scenarios. In addition, the theoretical analysis is complemented by empirical evidence and robustness assessments based on multiple yearly specifications and regional comparisons of household washing machine coverage. Together, these analyses link the theoretical framework to observed patterns of scabies burden and provide additional insight into the factors associated with persistent transmission.

Theoretical framework

The objective of the theoretical framework is to develop a stylized game-theoretic representation of the coordination problem underlying scabies treatment decisions rather than a comprehensive epidemiological transmission model. The model is intentionally reduced-form and is designed to isolate the strategic interactions that arise when treatment decisions are interdependent. Treatment by one household member reduces the risk of infection for other household members, generating a positive externality analogous to herd protection. Conversely, an untreated household member may remain a source of infestation and reinfect a household member who has already received treatment. Heterogeneous contact networks and detailed transmission dynamics are abstracted in order to focus on the coordination incentives that motivate the subsequent evolutionary analysis.

The parameter c denotes the private treatment cost, whereas L denotes the full expected loss when neither household member treats. If one member treats, an untreated member faces the residual conditional infection probability p(k), where 0 < p(k) < 1, and therefore the reduced expected loss p(k)L.

The inequality p(k)L<L captures herd protection: treatment by one household member removes one potential source of transmission and reduces, without eliminating, the infection risk faced by the untreated member. The term “herd protection” is used because treatment reduces exposure but does not necessarily produce lasting biological immunity.

The untreated member is not fully protected because p(k) remains positive. The parameter p(k) is therefore defined directly as a conditional residual probability; no unused baseline transmission parameterization is imposed.

Conversely, an untreated household member may remain in a reservoir and reinfest a member who has completed treatment. The resulting loss r is cumulative: it encompasses not only reinfestation itself but also repeated medication, additional medical consultations, repeated laundering, productivity losses, persistent symptoms, and delayed household eradication.

The reinfestation loss and the residual infection probability describe related consequences of continued exposure and should not be treated as independent parameters. I therefore write r=ρ(p(k)), ρ′(p(k))>0. The function ρ(·) maps the conditional probability that the untreated household member remains infested into the cumulative expected loss imposed on the treated member. This loss may include repeated medication, additional consultations, repeated laundering, productivity losses, persistent symptoms, and delayed household eradication. No numerical functional form is imposed because these quantities are not estimated in the present study; monotonicity nevertheless prevents r from being selected independently of p(k ).

Under this link, the two conditions defining the coordination regime are c<p(k)L and c+ρ(p(k))>L. Assuming that ρ(·) is strictly increasing, these conditions are equivalent to p(k)>max{cL,ρ-1(L-c)}. The coordination region is non-empty when max{cL,ρ-1(L-c)}<1. Thus, coordination is a conditional regime rather than an automatic feature of the model. A sufficient substantive interpretation is that c<L and that the expected cumulative reinfestation loss at sufficiently high conditional exposure risk can exceed L−c. Simultaneous-treatment recommendations are consistent with this possibility because untreated household contacts can remain continuing sources of reinfestation (14, 15).

Nash equilibrium analysis

Under the coordination restrictions c<p(k)L and c + r > L, the best-response correspondence yields two pure-strategy Nash equilibria.

First, (T, T) is a Nash equilibrium because, when the other household member treats, deviating from treatment to non-treatment changes the payoff from −c to −p(k)L. Since c<p(k)L, it follows that −c > −p(k)L, and treatment is preferred. This equilibrium reflects successful coordination: both members incur the treatment cost, the within-household reservoir is removed, and neither remains exposed to reinfestation by the other.

Second, (N, N) is a Nash equilibrium because, when the other household member does not treat, deviating from non-treatment to treatment changes the payoff from −L to −(c + r). Since c + r > L, it follows that −L > −(c + r), and non-treatment is preferred. This equilibrium reflects coordination failure: although joint treatment would remove the within-household source of reinfestation, neither individual wishes to treat unilaterally.

The two equilibrium exist only in the coordination regime. Expectations then determine which equilibrium is reached, and the unstable interior threshold separating their basins of attraction is derived from Section 2.3. The model does not assume that bistability applies for every possible parameter configuration.

Evolutionary dynamics framework

The evolutionary framework models treatment and non-treatment as strategies whose population frequencies change according to their expected payoffs. The two-player household game provides the micro-foundation, while the replicator dynamics offer a stylized representation of how treatment strategies may spread across a population of households (35). They are not intended to describe behavioral adaptation within a single household.

Let x∈[0, 1] denote the fraction of the population choosing treatment, so that 1−x denotes the fraction choosing non-treatment. The parameters c, L, and p(k) retain their definitions from Section 2.1, and r = ρ(p(k)) is the expected reinfestation loss associated with encountering an untreated individual. Throughout the analysis, r is used as shorthand for this increasing function of the conditional probability of infection; it is not an independently chosen parameter.

d) Parameter regimes

The model generates four logically possible regimes. Three are central to the present analysis; the fourth is stated for completeness.

Coordination and bistability arise when c+r>L and c<p(k)L. Treatment is then disadvantageous when adoption is near zero but advantageous when adoption is near one. Both boundary equilibrium are locally stable, while the interior equilibrium satisfies 0 < x* < 1 and is unstable. It therefore acts as a threshold: initial adoption below x* converges toward no treatment, whereas adoption above x* converges toward full treatment.

Free-riding with a stable interior equilibrium arises when c+r<L and c>p(k)L. Treatment is attractive when few others treat, but non-treatment becomes attractive at high coverage because untreated individuals benefit from herd protection. Both boundary equilibria are unstable, and the interior equilibrium is locally stable.

Treatment is strictly dominant when c+r<L and c<p(k)L, or equivalently c<min{L−r, p(k)L}. The payoff advantage of this treatment is positive at both boundaries and therefore throughout the unit interval. The interior threshold disappears, x = 0 is unstable, and x = 1 is locally stable. A sufficiently large reduction in treatment cost can therefore eliminate the coordination problem rather than merely moving its threshold.

For completeness, non-treatment is strictly dominant when c+r>L and c>p(k)L, or c>max{L−r, p(k)L}. In that case, x = 0 is locally stable and x = 1 is unstable.

Because r = ρ(p(k)), the coordination regime may also be written as p(k)>max{cL,ρ-1(L-c)}. It is non-empty when max{cL,ρ-1(L-c)}<1. A sufficient substantive interpretation is that c<L and that, at sufficiently high conditional exposure risk, the cumulative expected reinfestation loss can exceed L−c. These restrictions make clear that coordination is conditional on the underlying epidemiological and economic environment; it is not imposed by selecting r independently of p(k ).

Discussion of the theoretical model results

The model does not imply that scabies treatment always constitutes a coordination game. Coordination and bistability arise only when joint treatment is individually preferable to free-riding, c<p(k)L, while unilateral treatment is less attractive than mutual non-treatment, c+r>L. In this region, expectations matter because treatment is advantageous only after adoption exceeds the unstable interior threshold.

The condition c+r>L is substantive. It states that when another household member remains untreated, the combined cost of treatment and the expected cumulative consequences of reinfestation exceed the loss associated with remaining untreated. The term r includes not only the biological event of reinfestation but also repeated medication, additional medical consultations, repeated laundering, productivity losses, persistent symptoms, and delayed household eradication. Clinical recommendations that all household members and close contacts be treated simultaneously reflect the importance of these cumulative consequences (14, 15).

Policies may change the type of game. In particular, reducing c below both L−r and p(k)L makes treatment strictly dominant. The intervention then removes the unstable threshold instead of merely lowering it. Conversely, when herd protection makes non-treatment attractive at high coverage while treatment remains attractive at low coverage, the model generates a stable interior free-riding equilibrium.

The principal theoretical conclusion is therefore conditional: reinfestation externalities can generate a coordination trap, but sufficiently strong cost reductions or coordinated delivery mechanisms can eliminate that trap. This distinction sharpens the policy rationale for subsidies, simultaneous household treatment, and mass drug administration.

Limitations of the theoretical framework

The framework is intentionally reduced-form and should be interpreted as a conceptual representation of strategic treatment interdependence rather than as a calibrated epidemiological transmission model. The model links reinfestation loss monotonically to conditional infection risk through r = ρ(p(k)), but it does not impose a specific functional form because these parameters are not estimated in the present study.

The analysis also assumes homogeneous individuals and abstracts from variation in treatment effectiveness, adherence, household size, contact networks, age structure, asymptomatic infestation, access to healthcare, and repeated transmission over time. These factors could alter both the location of the regime boundaries and the dynamics of treatment adoption.

Finally, replicator dynamics describe the evolution of strategy frequencies across a population of households, not learning within a particular household. Future research could embed the strategic mechanism in a richer epidemiological model and estimate the function ρ(·) using household-level longitudinal data.

Despite these limitations, the model identifies transparent conditions under which reinfestation externalities produce coordination, free-riding, or dominant-strategy outcomes and clarifies how policy can move the system between these regimes.

Empirical illustration, public policy, and urban implementation

Empirical evidence underscores the substantial global burden of scabies, particularly in resource-limited and overcrowded settings (1, 29, 30). Fiji provides a notable example, where prevalence has reached approximately 30% of the population. Studies further demonstrate that the Mass Drug Administration (MDA) can reduce prevalence by more than 70%, but without sustained coordination and follow-up interventions, infection rates may rebound over time, highlighting the importance of continuous control efforts (11). These findings illustrate both the potential and the limitations of large-scale control programs and motivate the discussion below.

Empirical illustration

The experience of Fiji illustrates both the effectiveness and the limitations of large-scale scabies control programs. Scabies cases are defined according to the international consensus diagnostic criteria developed by Engelman et al. (31). Population-based studies report prevalence rates ranging from 0.18% to 76.9%, with the highest rates observed in tropical and low-income regions (7). Global estimates suggest that hundreds of millions of individuals are affected annually, with repeated outbreaks exacerbated by overcrowding and inadequate hygiene infrastructure (15). The disease causes intense pruritus, sleep disruption, secondary bacterial infections, and social stigma, collectively contributing to substantial morbidity and economic burden (6, 32).

Fiji provides a particularly informative example of these challenges. In some communities, scabies prevalence has reached approximately 30% of the population. Although MDA programs have reduced prevalence by more than 70%, infection rates may rebound when coordinated treatment efforts are not maintained, underscoring the importance of sustained surveillance, community engagement, and follow-up interventions.

The theoretical analysis implies that persistent low-treatment outcomes depend on the relationship among treatment cost, infection loss, and reinfestation loss rather than on an assumed numerical calibration. Public policy can alter these relationships through subsidies, free topical treatments, or mass drug administration campaigns, thereby promoting the socially optimal full-treatment outcome. Beyond pharmacological interventions, hygiene practices are critical: washing bedding, clothing, and linens at temperatures of at least 60 °C can markedly reduce reinfection. However, in many low-income or rural areas, including parts of Sub-Saharan Africa and India, laundering is done by hand in rivers or other communal water sources, often limiting the effectiveness of this preventive measure.

An urban economics approach can help overcome these structural barriers. Establishing communal washing centers equipped with high-temperature washing machines can reduce logistical constraints and infection risks, particularly in dense urban settlements. These centers not only provide accessible hygiene infrastructure but can also serve as focal points for education and community engagement, reinforcing coordinated treatment behavior. Integrating such infrastructure with MDA programs and targeted health education campaigns aligns individual incentives with social welfare and reduces the likelihood of returning to inefficient, low-treatment equilibria. This evidence suggests that sustainable scabies control requires a multifaceted policy strategy that combines medical, behavioral, and infrastructural interventions.

Public policy implications

The persistence of scabies, as demonstrated in the theoretical framework above, reflects a classic case of coordination failure under positive externalities. These theoretical results have direct implications for public policy.

According to the theoretical model, a coordination trap arises when treatment is unattractive at low adoption even though universal treatment is stable. In that parameter regime, expectations of limited participation can trap populations in a low-treatment equilibrium and sustain scabies transmission.

Public policy can reduce the effective treatment cost through subsidies, free distribution of topical medications, or mass drug administration. Within the coordination regime, reducing treatment cost lowers the unstable adoption threshold; a sufficiently large reduction changes the game more fundamentally by making treatment strictly dominant. The policy objective is therefore not merely to shift behavior within a fixed game, but potentially to eliminate the coordination problem itself. Context-specific hygiene infrastructure and community education can complement this change by reducing the risk of reinfestation and supporting simultaneous treatment.

Subsidization and financial incentives

One of the most direct policy tools is the subsidization of treatment. By reducing the private treatment cost c, subsidies increase the likelihood that the individual treatment condition derived in Section 2.1, c≤p(k)L, is satisfied. Consequently, treatment becomes individually rational for a larger share of the population. Because treatment decisions exhibit strategic complementarities, this increase in individual incentives can shift the population toward the socially optimal full-treatment equilibrium.

In practical terms, subsidies lower the effective treatment cost c, making treatment attractive to more individuals. As treatment adoption increases, coordinated participation becomes more likely, reducing persistent transmission and improving collective welfare.

Empirical evidence from neglected tropical disease (NTD) programs suggests that even small reductions in cost can significantly increase treatment uptake. Importantly, because treatment decisions exhibit strategic complementarities, subsidies can generate multiplier effects: as more individuals choose treatment, the marginal benefit of treatment increases for others, potentially tipping the system toward a full-treatment equilibrium.

However, subsidies alone may be insufficient in high-prevalence settings where expectations of non-compliance remain entrenched.

Mass drug administration (MDA) as a coordination mechanism

Mass drug administration represents a powerful intervention that directly addresses coordination failures. By providing treatment to all individuals within a target population simultaneously, MDA effectively eliminates strategic uncertainty.

In game-theoretic terms, MDA collapses the strategy space: ai=T∀i The expression indicates that all individuals receive treatment simultaneously. This removes uncertainty about whether others will comply, thereby eliminating the coordination problem that sustains transmission. As a result, MDA can move the population directly to the socially optimal outcome in which reinfection risk is minimized.

Evidence from Fiji demonstrates that community-wide MDA can reduce scabies prevalence by more than 70% within a relatively short time frame (11). Nevertheless, sustainability remains a key challenge. Without repeated rounds of MDA or complementary behavioral interventions, populations may revert to inefficient equilibria due to the reintroduction of the disease and declining compliance.

Thus, MDA should be understood not as a one-time solution but as part of a broader dynamic strategy.

Exploratory empirical analysis

The empirical component provides an illustrative country-level assessment of the relationship between household hygiene infrastructure and scabies burden. The unit of observation is the country. The dependent variable is the natural logarithm of the 2021 age-standardized scabies disability-adjusted life-year (DALY) rate per 100,000 population for both sexes. The principal explanatory variable is the percentage of households with a washing machine, measured separately in 2018, 2019, 2020, and 2021. Because the analysis is observational and cross-sectional, the estimated coefficients are interpreted as descriptive conditional associations rather than causal effects.

The DALY data were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 dataset reported by Li et al. (5, 34), from which the 2021 age-standardized scabies DALY rate was extracted. Data on household washing machine coverage were obtained from the Global Data Lab (36). The original Global Data Lab worksheet contained both national and subnational observations. To ensure that each country contributed no more than one observation, I retained only records identified as “National” and “Total.” The resulting national observations were then merged one-to-one with the country-level DALY data. Sample sizes vary across specifications because washing machine coverage is not available in every country in every year.

Separate ordinary least squares regressions were estimated using washing machine coverage from each of the four measurement years (33). In the global sample, all specifications include continent indicators to account for broad regional differences, and heteroskedasticity-robust p-values are reported. A second set of regressions is restricted to countries classified by the Global Data Lab as Asia/Pacific. The four annual specifications are intended as a timing-sensitivity analysis: they examine whether the estimated association depends materially on the year in which washing machine coverage is measured. They should not be interpreted as four independent corroborating tests.

Table 2 reports the results for the global sample with continent controls. The estimated coefficient on washing machine coverage is negative in every specification, ranging from −0.0060 in 2018 to −0.0074 in 2021, and is statistically significant at the 10% level in all four regressions. Because the dependent variable is logarithmic, the estimates imply that a one-percentage-point increase in household washing machine coverage is associated with an approximately 0.6%−0.7% lower scabies DALY rate, conditional on the included continent indicators. The number of countries ranges from 50 to 63, and the R-squared values range from 0.394 to 0.445. These findings indicate a stable negative conditional association, but they do not establish a causal effect of washing machine ownership on scabies burden.

Robustness and interpretation

Country-level DALY estimates were available only for 2021. Accordingly, the outcome is held constant while washing machine coverage is measured from 2018 to 2021. Household appliance ownership generally changes gradually, making earlier measurements potentially informative about the prevailing household infrastructure. The similarity in the signs and magnitudes of the coefficients across the four specifications indicates that the estimated association is not highly sensitive to the measurement year. Nevertheless, the strong persistence of washing machine coverage over time means that these regressions are closely related specifications rather than independent replications.

Table 3 presents the exploratory analysis for the Asia/Pacific sample. The estimated coefficient on washing machine coverage remains negative across all four specifications, ranging from −0.0115 to −0.0139. Each coefficient is statistically significant at the 5% level. In log-linear terms, a one-percentage-point increase in washing machine coverage is associated with an approximately 1.1%−1.4% lower scabies DALY rate in this subsample. The estimates are somewhat larger in absolute value than those obtained from the global sample, although the Asia/Pacific regressions contain only 22–27 countries and do not include additional covariates. The regional results should therefore be regarded as exploratory evidence of heterogeneity, not as an independent causal test or as proof that washing machine ownership itself reduces the scabies burden.

Dataset limitations

The empirical illustration is subject to several important limitations. First, both datasets are aggregated at the country level and cannot capture within-country variation in household conditions, scabies exposure, treatment behavior, or disease burden. Country-level associations must therefore not be interpreted as relationships at the household or individual level. Second, washing machine coverage may serve as a proxy for broader socioeconomic development, including income, housing quality, access to healthcare, sanitation, education, and institutional capacity. Although the global regressions include continent indicators, the analysis does not control comprehensively for these potential confounders. Third, the outcome is observed only for 2021, preventing a longitudinal assessment of changes in scabies burden. Fourth, incomplete availability of national washing machine data produces relatively small and changes samples across specifications, particularly in the Asia/Pacific analysis. Finally, the observational cross-sectional design does not permit causal identification. The empirical results should consequently be viewed as exploratory evidence consistent with the theoretical emphasis on household infrastructure, rather than as a definitive test of the proposed mechanism or a basis for standalone policy conclusions.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive explanation for why scabies persists despite the availability of effective treatments. Theoretical modeling demonstrates that household treatment decisions constitute a coordination game in which individually rational behavior can lead to inefficient equilibria, sustaining infection cycles. To complement the theoretical analysis, the study presents an illustrative cross-country empirical analysis. The empirical results are consistent with the proposed theoretical framework: structural factors such as overcrowding and limited hygiene infrastructure are associated with persistent scabies burden, while greater household washing machine coverage is negatively associated with scabies DALYs. However, these findings should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal, since the empirical analysis does not control for broader socioeconomic and institutional determinants of scabies burden.

The analysis reveals a divergence between private and social incentives: individual treatment costs are low, yet societal costs—including reinfection, healthcare utilization, and productivity loss—are substantial. Public policy interventions, such as subsidized treatments, mass drug administration, health education, and improved urban hygiene infrastructure, can shift populations toward socially optimal equilibria by aligning individual behavior with collective welfare.

Ultimately, scabies should be viewed not only as a medical condition but also as a coordination problem. The principal contribution of this study is the development of a game-theoretic framework for understanding persistent scabies transmission, while the empirical analysis serves as an illustrative complement that is consistent with the proposed mechanism. Sustainable control requires integrating epidemiological insights, behavioral economics, and infrastructure-based interventions, with early, coordinated, and context-sensitive policies being essential to break persistent transmission cycles and reduce the global burden of disease.
