Viscous drag-induced self-regeneration of laser-dissected actin stress fibers: A potential mechanism for mechanical memory
Authors: Kazuaki Nagayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiological tissues are constantly subjected to physiological mechanical stresses that can induce microdamage to cellular components, a process linked to cellular aging and carcinogenesis. Actin stress fibers (SFs) are central to maintaining structural integrity and transducing mechanical cues to intracellular organelles. While SF mechanical properties and disassembly have been extensively studied, the regenerative dynamics following acute physical trauma, specifically complete transection, remain poorly understood. Using a short-pulsed laser nanoscissor system to investigate porcine aortic smooth muscle cells, this study reveals a self-repairing capacity in individual SFs that we characterize as a form of "mechanical memory". Following precise ablation, SFs exhibited immediate viscoelastic contraction, which was well-characterized by a first-order viscoelastic model. The results revealed a significant spatial dependency in repair capabilities: peripheral SFs demonstrated a markedly higher regeneration rate (∼80%) compared to central SFs (∼30%). This difference was closely correlated with the contraction time constant (τ); SFs with a higher τ (indicating prolonged contraction against high viscous resistance) were less conducive to repair. The author propose a mechanochemical repair model where the localized high-strain field generated by viscous friction at the severed ends of SFs serves as a cue for the recruitment of crosslinking proteins. Excessive surrounding resistance dampens the contraction velocity, preventing the formation of a sufficiently intense dynamic strain field. This attenuated mechanical signal appears insufficient to trigger the mechanosensitive recruitment of crosslinking proteins. These findings suggest that SF regeneration is governed by a delicate balance between contractile dynamics, intracellular location, and the surrounding mechanical environment, providing new insights into the biomechanical pathways underlying cellular homeostasis.Keywords: Cell biomechanics, Actin stress fibers, Laser nanodissection, Viscoelastic contraction, Self-repair mechanism, Mechanotransduction

Introduction

The cells in biological tissues are constantly exposed to biochemical and mechanical stresses during life processes. Recent studies have shown that mechanical stress can cause microscopic damage to intracellular components, such as cell membranes and the nuclear membranes.1 The accumulation of such damage has also been suggested to contribute to cellular ageing and carcinogenesis.2,3 In load-bearing environments like bone and vascular tissues, physiological mechanical stresses often result in the accumulation of microdamage. From the perspective of mechanobiology, characterizing the cellular pathways involved in tissue restoration and regeneration is essential for understanding the biomechanical integrity of these systems.

Constituent cells within these tissues and at wound-healing sites possess “actin stress fibers (SFs)”, which are contractile actomyosin bundles that serve as primary mediators of cellular contractility. These structures are analogous to the highly organized actomyosin arrays found in myocytes. Comprising multiple actin filaments cross-linked by specialized proteins,4 SFs generate tensile forces on the order of several tens of nanonewtons (nN) through interactions with non-muscle myosin II.5 The distal ends of SFs are physically anchored to the extracellular matrix via integrin-based focal adhesions, spanning the cytoplasm to maintain intracellular mechanical tension. Consequently, SFs play a pivotal role in maintaining structural integrity, regulating cell migration,6,7 and transducing extracellular mechanical cues to intracellular organelles, including the nucleus.8,9

Historically, investigations into SFs have predominantly focused on their mechanical properties 10, 11, 12 and contractile dynamics 13, 14, 15, 16. Previous studies have extensively documented SF disassembly and structural reorganization in response to cyclic stretching 17, 18, 19 and transient compression.20 For instance, tension relief via compression has been shown to enhance the affinity of actin-cleaving molecules for SFs, thereby facilitating disassembly.21 Furthermore, a reduction in tension diminishes the binding frequency between actin and non-muscle myosin II, rendering SFs more susceptible to detachment.22 In recent years, attention has shifted toward the mechanisms of SF damage. Evidence suggests that intracellular SFs undergo spontaneous cycles of stochastic damage and subsequent repair.23 Moreover, SFs may exhibit a protective response under excessive force, where localized polymerization and elongation serve to shield the structure from catastrophic failure.24 Despite these insights, the behavioral dynamics of SFs following acute physical trauma, specifically complete transection, remain poorly understood. Whether such severed SFs possess the capacity for autonomous repair and structural regeneration remains a critical, yet unresolved, question in cellular mechanobiology. To address these fundamental questions, our group previously developed an experimental platform employing a pulsed laser nanosurgery to dissect selectively individual SFs within living cells. This system has enabled the characterization of SF tension and the underlying mechanisms of intracellular force transmission25

Thus, the present study aims to elucidate the mechanisms governing the regeneration of the dissected SFs. By employing laser ablation to induce precise mechanical failure, we performed a detailed analysis of the subsequent contractile and regenerative dynamics of SFs in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs). Specifically, we hypothesized that the regenerative capacity of SFs are modulated by their immediate contractile behavior following dissection. To test this hypothesis, we systematically investigated the correlations between biomechanical factors of SFs and the success of their regenerative process.

Materials and methods

Preparation of specimens

Porcine aortic VSMCs (PSMC010 [passage 3]; Cosmo Bio) were employed as the experimental model. VSMCs were plated on conventional cell culture dishes (60 mm TC-treated Culture Dish, 430166, Corning Inc., USA) and cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; JRH Bioscience, Lenexa, KS, USA), penicillin (100 units/ml), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Cells were passaged repeatedly at a 1:4 split ratio when they reached approximately 80% confluence. For all experiments, VSMCs (passage 7–10) were seeded onto glass-bottomed dishes (GD-0400; Ina-optica, Osaka, Japan) pre-coated with 0.02% type I collagen (AteloCell I-AC-50; Koken, Tokyo, Japan) and cultured for 48 h to ensure complete cellular spreading.

Laser nano-dissection of SFs

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying stress fiber regeneration, we employed laser nanodissection to selectively ablate individual intracellular SFs in VSMCs and systematically characterized their subsequent contractile and regenerative dynamics.

For fluorescence imaging with laser nanosurgery in living cells, SFs were visualized via GFP-actin expression following previously established protocols.9 Briefly, VSMCs were transfected with a GFP-actin vector (CellLight™ Actin-GFP BacMam 2.0, Invitrogen) and incubated for 48 h to ensure optimal protein expression. During observation, cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment using a stage-top incubator (Tokai Hit, Japan).

The imaging system was integrated with a custom-built laser nanosurgery platform25 equipped with an ultraviolet pulsed laser (λ = 355 nm, pulse width = 400 ps; FTSS355-Q1, CryLaS GmbH, Berlin, Germany), as illustrated in Fig. 1A. In this study, we categorized SFs into two groups based on their subcellular localization (Fig. 1B): Central SFs, which are located on the apical side of the cell and extend along the cell's long axis without contacting the nucleus; and Peripheral SFs, which run along the long axis to form the cell's outer contour. The contractile and regenerative behaviors of these two populations were then systematically compared. The laser was focused onto the target fibers using a 100 × oil-immersion objective lens (NA = 1.4), and a single SF was cut by the laser upon irradiation for 1 s. Although SFs undergo immediate contraction upon laser dissection, a subset of fibers exhibits structural regeneration, eventually restoring their original architecture. Thus, we captured the resultant shortening and regeneration behavior of the dissected SFs (Fig. 1B) using an electron-multiplying CCD camera (C9100-12; Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu City, Japan) for 60 min.

Mechanical analysis of retraction behavior of the dissected SFs

The region of measurement (ROI) segments of the central SFs and peripheral SFs were extracted from the time-lapse images obtained in the previous section using the open-source software ImageJ (Fiji, National Institutes of Health) (Fig. 2A). The initial length L(0) of each SF prior to laser ablation was determined by manual tracing (Fig. 2B). To characterize the fiber morphology, the initial width W(0) was calculated as the mean value from five distinct points within the identified contraction range (indicated by orange arrows, Fig. 2B). Regarding measurement precision, ImageJ facilitates sub-pixel resolution by allowing coordinate specification beyond the physical pixel grid, constrained only by the display resolution. In the present optical setup (1 pixel = 0.128 μm), this yielded a minimum measurement increment ranging from approximately 0.03 to 0.06 μm.

The displacement between the severed ends of SFs, δ(t), was recorded at each time point t to quantify fiber shortening (Fig. 2B). The contraction ratio, S(t), was subsequently defined as the change in the shortening length relative to the initial length: S(t) = δ(t)/L(0). Based on the dynamics of SF shortening (Fig. 2C), we directly determined the viscoelastic properties of SFs in VSMCs. We fitted the curve of S(t) to a first order exponential function S(t)=α(1−exp(−t/τ)), where α and τ represent the saturation contraction rate and the contraction time constant, respectively (Fig. 2D). We assume a simple viscoelastic model where the contraction behavior of SFs is governed by their elastic constant k and the surrounding viscous resistance η (Fig. 2D, right schematic). The contraction time constant τ is thus expressed as τ = η/k.

Analysis of the regeneration of the dissected SFs

To quantitatively evaluate whether severed SFs regenerate into a single continuous fiber, raw fluorescence intensity data proved difficult to use directly due to inherent variability in initial intensity across different experiments and significant fluctuations between frames in time-lapse imaging. Therefore, in this study, we performed background subtraction using ImageJ (Fig. 2E and F), followed by a Tubeness plugin filter to enhance the visibility of the fiber structures (Fig. 2G). Based on these processed images, SFs were classified into two groups: the "repaired" group, characterized by the morphological restoration of a continuous linear structure, and the "non-repaired" group, where regeneration was not observed within 30 min post-SF severing.

To identify the factors governing regeneration of the dissected SFs, we characterized the pre-severing morphology of SFs, including L(0) and width W(0), and intracellular distribution, following previously described methods.12 This distribution was assessed by fiber count and spacing, with the latter calculated by dividing the cell width by the fiber count. These parameters were then evaluated in relation to the regenerative capacity of the fibers to discern any significant morphological predictors for repair.

Data analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were assessed using ANOVA with correction for multiple comparisons, followed by Steel-Dwass multiple comparisons of the means between the two groups, using the MEPHAS statistical analysis program (in Japanese, https://alain003.phs.osaka-u.ac.jp/mephas/index_jp.html). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. In the contraction and regeneration data for SFs, the sample size (n) corresponds to both the number of stress fibers and the number of cells.

Results

VSMCs exhibited mature SFs throughout the cell body and along the periphery, with prominent thickening observed at the focal adhesions where fiber ends anchored to the substrate (Fig. 3). While central SFs typically displayed a linear morphology, peripheral SFs frequently showed a slight inward curvature toward the cell interior. For the SFs targeted by laser dissection, central SFs had a mean length of L(0) = 59.5 ± 13.3 μm and a width of W(0) = 0.72 ± 0.18 μm (mean ± SD, n = 48). In comparison, peripheral SFs shared a similar width of W(0) = 0.53 ± 0.13 μm (n = 42) but were significantly longer at L(0) = 75.8 ± 20.5 μm (n = 42), representing an approximately 25% increase in length compared to central SFs.

In non-repaired cases (B, D), fibers failed to regenerate within the 10-min window and remained discrete strands even after 30 min. The kymographs capture these distinct temporal dynamics of shortening and subsequent structural outcomes.

Upon laser-induced dissection, these SFs exhibited characteristic viscoelastic contraction (Fig. 3, Kymograph). A subset of the fibers displayed regenerative capacity, effectively reconnecting to restore a continuous fibrous architecture (Fig. 3A and C). In instances where regeneration occurred, reconnection was typically finalized within several to 10 min post-severing, resulting in the re-establishment of a singular structural unit (Fig. 3A and C: Repaired). Conversely, certain fibers failed to exhibit regenerative behavior within the 60-min observation window. Specifically, some SFs remained split into two discrete strands even after 30 min (Fig. 3B and D: Non-repaired), and eventually overall cellular contraction and subsequent detachment from the substrate.

Consequently, both central and peripheral SFs were categorized into two distinct groups based on their post-severing dynamics: “a repaired group”, characterized by regenerative reconnection following initial contraction, and “a non-repaired group”, which exhibited no restorative behavior. Quantitative analysis revealed a marked disparity in regenerative potential: peripheral SFs achieved a regeneration rate of 80%, whereas central SFs demonstrated a substantially lower rate of approximately 30% (Fig. 4A). To elucidate the morphological determinants underlying these divergent outcomes, we compared the pre-ablation architecture of central and peripheral SFs. While no significant differences were detected in fiber length or width between the two groups (Fig. 4B and C), distinct variations emerged regarding fiber density (Fig. 4D and E). Specifically, within the central SF population, the repaired group was characterized by a significantly higher number of SFs per cell and a more constrained inter-fiber spacing, indicating a denser overall arrangement (Fig. 4D and E: central SFs).

Comparative analysis of morphological and structural parameters between repaired and non-repaired SFs. Regeneration rate of central and peripheral SFs following laser dissection (A). Morphological and structural parameters were compared, including initial fiber length (B), fiber width (C), the number of SFs per cell (D), and inter-fiber spacing (E). Furthermore, the contraction dynamics of severed SFs were investigated, with a focus on the differences between central and peripheral SFs, as well as between repaired and non-repaired groups (Fig. 5). The magnitude of shortening exhibited substantial variability, ranging from approximately 8 μm to 50 μm. On average, central SFs shortened by 24.3 ± 7.7 μm (n = 48), while peripheral SFs shortened by 28.4 ± 8.3 μm (n = 42). Notably, peripheral SFs, which typically possessed greater initial lengths, showed significantly larger absolute shortening immediately following the laser dissection (Fig. 5A). However, when evaluating the shortening strain S(t), defined as the change in length normalized by the initial length (Section 2.3), no significant difference was observed between the two locations, with both exhibiting a 35–40% reduction (Fig. 5B).

Both repaired and non-repaired SFs displayed characteristic viscoelastic contraction following the laser dissection. Consequently, the shortening behavior up to 300 s post-severing was modeled using Equation S(t) (Fig. 5C), which yielded a high goodness-of-fit for both groups. A comparative analysis of contraction kinetics revealed that the non-repaired group followed a more gradual contraction curve than the repaired group in both central and peripheral regions (Fig. 5D and E). Quantitative assessment of the saturation contraction rate α and the time constant τ indicated that mean values for both parameters tended to be larger in the non-repaired group but no significant difference was observed in α. In contrast, the contraction time constant τ showed a statistically significant increase in the non-repaired group (Fig. 5F and G). These results suggest that the absence of repair is associated with a distinct mechanical relaxation process characterized by prolonged contraction.

Discussion

SFs play a pivotal role in cell morphogenesis, motility, and mechanotransduction. They have been extensively studied from both biochemical and biomechanical perspectives. To elucidate their mechanical and contractile properties, researchers have employed diverse methodologies, including tensile testing of isolated SFs from vascular endothelial and smooth muscle cells10,11 and the evaluation of contractile behavior in SFs exposed via cell membrane permeabilization.20 Furthermore, laser ablation techniques have been utilized to sever SFs within living cells to assess their immediate contractile response.9,15,16 Despite these advancements, the regeneration process of SFs following such physical damage remains largely unexplored. In the present study, we utilized a short-pulsed laser to physically sever both central and peripheral SFs in VSMCs and conducted a comparative analysis of their contraction and subsequent recovery. Our findings revealed a novel phenomenon wherein severed SFs undergo regeneration in parallel with contraction. Based on these observations, we interpret this process as a form of “mechanical memory,” where the dynamics of contraction appear to govern the subsequent regeneration. Here, we discuss the correlation between these contraction dynamics and the subsequent regeneration process.

Upon laser-induced dissection of SFs in VSMCs, an immediate contraction was observed. The time-dependent shortening ratio S(t) was well-characterized by a first-order viscoelastic model (Fig. 5C). While peripheral SFs exhibited significantly greater initial lengths (Fig. 4B) and higher absolute contraction compared to central SFs (Fig. 5A), the mean saturated contraction ratio α ranged from 35% to 40% regardless of the intracellular location (Fig. 5B). This α value is notably higher than those reported for endothelial cells and glioblastoma cells (≈10%),15,16 as well as myoblasts (17%).26 Furthermore, it slightly exceeds the previously reported value for a rat VSMC line.25 These results suggest that the saturated contraction ratio α reflects cell-type-specific differences in the homeostatic tension maintained within SFs.

In the present study, SF contraction was modeled as a viscoelastic behavior, with the contraction time constant, τ, evaluated using Equation S(t) (Section 2.3). The factors governing τ are attributed to the intrinsic viscosity of the SFs and the frictional resistance between the fibers and the surrounding intracellular environment. It has long been established that SFs are interconnected with other cytoskeletal components, such as microtubules and intermediate filaments, via linker proteins.27 Recent studies have further demonstrated that thick peripheral SFs are physically integrated with the surrounding fine actin meshwork.28 Moreover, SFs are anchored to the extracellular matrix (ECM) through focal adhesions, complexes of adhesion proteins, with multiple attachment sites confirmed along the peripheral fibers.29 These molecular-level interconnections and architectural constraints are suggested to collectively dictate the contractile dynamics of SFs following laser ablation.

Following the SF severing, SF regeneration was observed to occur concurrently with contraction within a 10-min period (Fig. 3A and C). Notably, the regeneration rate of peripheral SFs was more than double that of central SFs (Fig. 4A). While the saturation contraction rate α, showed no significant difference between the repaired and non-repaired groups (Fig. 5F), the contraction time constant τ was significantly higher in the non-repaired group across both central and peripheral SFs (Fig. 5G). These results suggest that SFs undergoing prolonged, large-scale contraction are less conducive to regeneration. Specifically, we hypothesize that the continuous exertion of averaged high tensile forces, while simultaneously overcoming viscous resistance from the surrounding intracellular environment during contraction, suppresses the regenerative capacity of the fibers. Consequently, we propose the following mechanisms governing the regeneration of severed SFs based on the observed mechanical dynamics.

SFs are known to undergo continuous, spontaneous cycles of microdamage and repair, and it has been confirmed that zyxin, a protein associated with focal adhesions, accumulates at these microdamage sites.23 Zyxin is localized in response to intracellular strain changes and is thought to function by recruiting α-actinin, an actin-crosslinking molecule, to these sites.23 In the present study, the laser dissection of SFs induced rapid contraction and subsequent sliding motion; during this process, physical interactions with the surrounding environment act as viscous resistance, potentially generating a localized high-strain field at the severed ends of SFs. One possibility is that zyxin accumulates at this high-strain field, potentially recruiting α-actinin to promote actin crosslinking and ultimately facilitating SF regeneration. We interpret this phenomenon as a form of “mechanical memory”; specifically, the localized viscous friction resulting from the contractile movement is not merely a byproduct but is utilized as a vital cue for subsequent fiber regeneration. Conversely, if the initial tension of the SF is excessively high (indicated by a large saturation contraction rate α), it is considered that zyxin accumulation cannot occur rapidly enough, thereby hindering regeneration. Furthermore, when numerous adhesion sites to the substrate exist along the ventral surface of the fiber, the SF undergoes a sliding contraction similar to being dragged across a surface, resulting in an increased contraction time constant, τ. In such cases, the viscous friction exerted across the entire ventral surface of the fiber dampens the contraction velocity of the fibers and averages the internal strain field, preventing the formation of a localized high-strain field at the severed ends. This may delay the recruitment of zyxin and α-actinin, leading to the suppression of SF regeneration. Additionally, a study by Tanner et al.16 using glioblastoma cells reported that the biochemical signaling pathways for contractile force generation differ between central SFs and peripheral SFs. Specifically, while central SF contraction is highly dependent on the Rho-associated signaling pathway, peripheral SF contraction tends to be mediated by intracellular Ca2+. Such differences in the molecular groups involved in contraction and variations in molecular density due to intracellular locations may also influence the capacity of severed SFs to regeneration.

To further elucidate these detailed mechanisms, future studies should focus on the dynamics of zyxin and α-actinin, as well as the localization of focal adhesions. Colombelli et al.30 proposed a comprehensive model for stress fibers (SFs) that incorporates internal viscoelasticity and active contractile properties, as well as viscoelastic interactions with the surrounding environment. While our current study employs a simplified first-order model, further refinement incorporating these complex mechanical properties will be essential to fully elucidate the underlying factors governing SF regeneration. In addition to these crosslinking molecules, the influence of the surrounding actin architecture on SF regeneration process must be considered. In our experiments, we observed instances where mist-like actin structures recruited from the surrounding environment accumulated between the severed and contracting SFs, leading to fiber regeneration. These structures may serve as a molecular source for the reconstruction of thick SFs. Future quantitative investigations utilizing advanced techniques, such as super-resolution microscopy, will be essential to characterize these regenerative processes and their underlying structural dynamics.
