Remco van der Hofstad

Abstract
Percolation is a model for random damage to a network. It is one of the simplest models that displays a phase transition : when the network is severely damaged, it falls apart in many small connected components, while if the damage is light, connectivity is hardly affected. We study the location and nature of the phase transition on random graphs. In particular, we focus on the connectivity structure close to, or below, criticality, where components display intricate scaling behaviour such that a typical connected component has bounded size, while the average and maximal connected component sizes grow like powers of the network size. We review the recent progress that has been made in two important settings: random graphs whose expected adjacency matrix is close to being rank-1 , the most prominent examples being the configuration model and rank-1 inhomogeneous random graphs, and dynamic random graphs , i.e., random graphs that grow with time, such as uniform and preferential attachment models. Remarkably, these two settings behave rather differently. In all cases, the inhomogeneity of the underlying random graph on which we perform percolation is of crucial importance.



1
Introduction.

In percolation, edges and/or vertices are randomly removed from a connected graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
. Often, percolation is studied on infinite connected graphs, such as the hypercubic lattice or infinite trees. See the books by Grimmett
[
38
]
, Bollobás and Riordan
[
22
]
and Kesten
[
49
]
for background. Percolation on infinite graphs is the simplest imaginable model that displays a
phase transition
. Suppose edges in the graph are removed independently with some probability
\pi\in[0,1]
.
1

1

Traditionally,
p
is used as the edge-retention probability. Due to the potential conflicts with other usages of
p
here, we choose for
\pi

We think of the retention probability
\pi
as the proportion of edges that escapes being damaged. When
\pi
is small, the damage is substantial, and the graph falls apart into many small and finite components. In particular, while
G
had an infinite connected component, after percolation, for
\pi
small, no infinite connected component remains. On the other hand, when the damage is very little and
\pi
is close to 1, the graph remains to have an infinite connected component even after the damage is inflicted. By monotonicity in the edge-retention probability, there is a
unique

\pi_{c}
above which infinite structures remain, while below it, no infinite connected component exists. Throughout the remainder of this paper, we will write ‘component’ to mean ‘connected component’.

Motivation for percolation on finite graphs.

Real-world networks are finite, yet very large, graphs. Such networks can be affected by vertices and edges being down or attacked, rendering them out of service. This is sometimes called
attack
or
failure vulnerability
. Such a failure process could arise from vertices or edges randomly failing. Then, the key question is how much connectivity is preserved while vertices or edges are randomly knocked out. We will often assume that this is a truly random process, in the sense that the edges or vertices fail with equal probabilities independently of one another. Such an attack is sometimes called a
random attack
.

Real-world networks are generally
sparse
(see e.g.
[
41
,
44
,
51
]
), meaning that their number of edges grows linearly with the network size. The relevant notion of convergence for sparse graphs is the highly powerful theory of
local convergence

[
6
,
10
,
43
]
, which investigates the structure of finite graphs from the perspective of a
uniformly chosen vertex
called a
root
. The space of locally-finite rooted graphs, apart from isomorphisms, can be equipped with a local topology that turns it into a
Polish space
. Graph sequences converge locally when expectations of bounded and continuous functions of these rooted graphs converge to expectations with respect to a limiting probability distribution on rooted graphs. While this may seem a weak notion, it has proven to be very effective in analysing structural properties of large random graphs. For example, while the maximal component size is not described by the local limit, under one additional assumption, it is
[
42
]
, and we therefore refer to it as ‘almost local’.

Percolation on finite graphs.

Let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be a finite graph. For convenience, we denote the vertex set as
V(G)=[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, while the edge set
E(G)
can be general. Let
\pi
be the edge-retention probability. For
u,v\in[n]
, we write that
u\longleftrightarrow v
when there exists a path of edges that are retained in percolation, and let
\mathscr{C}_{v}^{\pi}=\{u\in[n]\colon u\longleftrightarrow v\}
be the collection of vertices that are connected to
v
in percolation on
G
with percolation parameter
\pi
. Percolation on finite graphs is inherently different from percolation on infinite transitive graphs in that a
unique critical value
, such as present on many infinite transitive graphs, cannot exist. Indeed, let
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
denote the percolation measure on the finite graph
G
. Then, the dependence on the edge-retention probability
\pi
is smooth, and
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(\mathcal{E})
is a polynomial of finite degree for every event
\mathcal{E}
. As a result, there cannot be a sharp phase transition, in the sense that if
\pi
is close to critical and
\delta
very small, then also
\pi+\delta
will be close to critical. Further, on a finite graph, no infinite components can exist. This means that we have to redefine the notion of a critical value.

A
local
way of quantifying the critical behaviour is to take a
uniform
vertex
U
, and to investigate the component
\mathscr{C}_{U}^{\pi}
of that vertex after percolating with percolation parameter
\pi
. When

\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{n}}(|\mathscr{C}_{U}^{\pi_{n}}|\geq k)=0,

the components in the percolated graph are small and we can think of
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
as being subcritical, while when

\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{n}}(|\mathscr{C}_{U}^{\pi_{n}}|\geq k)>0,

the sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is supercritical. Of course, the boundary between these two settings may not be sharp, and are generally referred to as
finite-size corrections
.

Percolation on random graphs.

In this paper, we focus on percolation on random graphs, which poses an additional difficulty due to the fact that the graphs are
random
, and thus we deal with
double randomness
due to percolation and the random graph on which it acts. As a result, we can think of the stochastic models on random graphs as processes in a disordered medium. Especially for percolation, this is somewhat paradoxical, since percolation often provides the random medium on which other processes, in particular random walks, act. For a more extended overview of percolation on random graphs, we refer to
[
44
, Part III]
.

The largest components in percolation on random graphs.

We let
\big(|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}^{\pi}|\big)_{i\geq 1}
denote the ordered collection of components, where we break ties (if they exist) arbitrarily. Thus, in particular,

(1.1)

|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi}|=|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}^{\pi}|=\max_{i\geq 1}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}^{\pi}|

denotes the largest or maximal component size, and we write
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm sec}^{\pi}|=|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}^{\pi}|
for the second largest component size. Note that the vector
\big(|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}^{\pi}|\big)_{i\geq 1}
has a
random
number of coordinates, which equals the number of disjoint components, and it will be convenient to extend this finite vector to an infinite vector by appending infinitely many zeros at the end of it.

In the above, we have defined critical behaviour in terms of the
local structure
of the component of a random vertex after percolation. This does not give us much information though about the largest component of the graph after percolation. The difficulty in dealing with the largest component is that it is an inherently
global
quantity, making it a difficult object to get our hands upon from a local perspective. We now discuss the percolation critical value from this global perspective.

We say that
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
(possibly depending on the network size
n
) is
asymptotically supercritical
when there exists
\varepsilon>0
independent of
n
such that

(1.2)

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{n}}\big(|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi_{n}}|\geq\varepsilon n\big)=1,

and
asymptotically subcritical
otherwise. Thus, for an asymptotically supercritical sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
the maximal component contains, with high probability, a
positive
proportion of the vertices, while
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi_{n}}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
for a subcritical sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. Here
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denotes convergence in probability.

Critical value in large graph limits.

In many cases, we see that

(1.3)

\frac{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi}|}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\theta(\pi),

for some
\theta(\pi)\in[0,1]
. Then, we can define the asymptotical percolation critical value also in terms of
\theta(\pi)
as

(1.4)

\pi_{c}=\inf\{\pi\colon\theta(\pi)>0\}.

When
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\theta(\pi)>0
, the component
\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi}
is often
unique
, and then we called it the
giant
. In words, for
\pi<\pi_{c}
, there is no giant in the percolated graph, while, for
\pi>\pi_{c}
, there exists a giant. In what follows, we will focus on finer properties of
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi}|
for
\pi
close to the critical value
\pi_{c}
.

Critical behaviour and finite-size effects in large graph limits.

While the notion of asymptotically supercritical sequences is useful to determine whether, after percolation, a giant component still exists, it is less useful to describe the critical behaviour for percolation on finite graphs. Indeed, for critical sequences
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, we would expect that the scaling of largest percolation components is quite intricate. A large body of work has recently appeared aimed at identifying the critical behaviour of percolation on various random and non-random finite graphs. Typically, an explicit sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is then chosen, and the behaviour of the largest percolation components determined. In each of these cases, the sequences involved are asymptotically subcritical. From this large body of work, we distill the following general picture of what it means to be barely subcritical, critical and supercritical: We say that a sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is
barely subcritical
when it is asymptotically subcritical, and there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that

(1.5)

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{n}}\big(|\mathscr{C}_{\rm sec}^{\pi_{n}}|/|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi_{n}}|\geq\varepsilon\big)=1,

while we say that a sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is
barely supercritical
when it is asymptotically subcritical and

(1.6)

\frac{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm sec}^{\pi_{n}}|}{|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi_{n}}|}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

Finally, we say that
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is
in the critical window
when it is asymptotically subcritical and there exist
0<a<b<\infty
, such that

(1.7)

\displaystyle 0<\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{n}}\big(|\mathscr{C}_{\rm sec}^{\pi_{n}}|/|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi_{n}}|\in[a,b]\big)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{n}}\big(|\mathscr{C}_{\rm sec}^{\pi_{n}}|/|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi_{n}}|\in[a,b]\big)<1.

A few remarks are in place here. In most cases, much stronger results are being proved in the literature. Often, in the barely subcritical regime, explicit sequences
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are identified such that
|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}^{\pi_{n}}|/a_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}c_{i}>0
for every
i\geq 1
, which certainly implies (
1.5
). In the barely supercritical regime, often explicit sequences
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are identified such that
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi_{n}}|/a_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}c>0
, while
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm sec}^{\pi_{n}}|/a_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
. Again this certainly implies (
1.5
). Finally, in the critical window, often results are proved that there exists a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that
a_{n}^{-1}\big(|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}^{\pi_{n}}|\big)_{i\geq 1}
converges in distribution to a certain (infinite) random vector in a suitable topology. We will see quite a few of such sharper results in what follows.

2
Percolation on rank-1 random graphs.

We start this overview by discussing a class of models that we will call
rank-1 random graphs
. Note that each random graph
G_{n}=([n],E(G_{n}))
of size
n
can equivalently be defined as a collection of random variables
(X_{uv})_{u,v\in[n]}
, where
X_{uv}
denotes the number of edges between vertices
u,v\in[n]
. We assume that we are in the
undirected
setting, for which
X_{uv}=X_{vu}
for every
u,v\in[n]
. We allow for
multi-edges
, in that we allow
X_{uv}\geq 2
so that
X_{uv}
indicates the number of edges between
u,v\in[n]
. Further, we allow for
X_{vv}\geq 1
for all
v\in[n]
, so that the random graph has self-loops. For convenience, we think of
X_{vv}
as denoting
twice
the number of self-loops incident to
v\in[n]
.

To explain the name rank-1 random graphs, we consider the
expected
adjacency matrix, given by
\mathbb{E}[X_{uv}]
, where the expectation is over the randomness in the random graph, and we assume that there exists a sequence
(a_{v})_{v\in[n]}
such that

(2.1)

\mathbb{E}[X_{uv}]\approx a_{u}a_{v}.

Thus, the expected adjacency matrix is close to being of rank 1. We focus on two examples, the
configuration model
in Section
2.1
, and
rank-1 inhomogeneous random graphs
in Section
2.2
.

2.1
Percolation on configuration models.

In this section, we discuss percolation on configuration models. We start by introducing the model in Section
2.1.1
, and discuss the percolation phase transition on it in Section
2.1.2
. In Sections
2.1.3
–
2.1.5
, we describe the main results on the scaling limits of critical components.

2.1.1
The configuration model.

The configuration model is a model in which the degrees of vertices are fixed beforehand, and was introduced by Bollobás in
[
18
]
; see
[
41
,
43
]
for extensive overviews. Fix an integer
n
that denotes the number of vertices in the random graph. Consider a sequence of degrees
\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{v})_{v\in[n]}
.
2
2

2

In many cases, the vertex degrees actually depend on
n
, and it would be more appropriate, but also more cumbersome, to write the weights as
\boldsymbol{d}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=(d_{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{v\in[n]}
. To keep notation simple, we refrain from making the dependence on
n
explicit.
The aim is to construct an undirected multi-graph with
n
vertices, where vertex
v
has degree
d_{v}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
d_{v}\geq 1
for all
v\in[n]
, since vertex
v
is isolated when
d_{v}=0
and
can thus be removed from the graph. We assume that the total degree

(2.2)

\ell_{n}=\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}

is even. To construct our multi-graph, we have
n
separate vertices and, incident to vertex
v
, we have
d_{v}
half-edges. Every half-edge needs to be connected to another half-edge to form an edge, and by forming all edges we build the graph. For this, the half-edges are numbered in an arbitrary order from
1
to
\ell_{n}
. We start by randomly connecting the first half-edge with one of the
\ell_{n}-1
remaining half-edges. Once paired, two half-edges form a single edge of the multi-graph, and the half-edges are removed from the list of unpaired half-edges. Hence, a half-edge can be seen as the left or the right half of an edge. We continue the procedure of randomly choosing and pairing the half-edges until all half-edges are connected, and call the resulting graph the
configuration model with degree sequence
\boldsymbol{d}
, abbreviated as
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
.

Let us continue by explaining why we call the configuration model a rank-1 random graph. Since each half-edge incident to vertex
v
is connected to a half-edge incident to vertex
u
with probability
d_{u}/(\ell_{n}-1)
, we obtain

(2.3)

\mathbb{E}[X_{uv}]=\frac{d_{u}d_{v}}{\ell_{n}-1},

so that (
2.1
) holds
with equality
, for
a_{v}=d_{v}/\sqrt{\ell_{n}-1}
.

Assumptions on the degrees.

To investigate the asymptotic properties of configuration models, we impose
regularity conditions
on the degree sequence
\boldsymbol{d}
. In order to state these assumptions, we introduce some notation. We denote the degree of a uniformly chosen vertex
U
in
[n]
by
D_{n}=d_{U}
. The random variable
D_{n}
has distribution function
F_{n}
given by

(2.4)

F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d_{j}\leq x\}},

which is the
empirical degree distribution.

We assume that the vertex degrees satisfy the following
regularity conditions:

Condition 2.1
(Regularity conditions for vertex degrees).

(a) Weak convergence of vertex weights.

There exists a distribution function
F
such that, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

(2.5)

D_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}D,

where
D_{n}
and
D
have distribution functions
F_{n}
and
F
, respectively. Further, we assume that
F(0)=0
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(D\geq 1)=1
.

(b) Convergence of average vertex degrees.
As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

(2.6)

\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]\rightarrow\mathbb{E}[D]\in(0,\infty),

where
D_{n}
and
D
have distribution functions
F_{n}
and
F
from part (a), respectively.

Having defined the configuration model, we proceed by identifying its
percolation phase transition.

2.1.2
The phase transition of the configuration model.

In this section, we assume that Conditions
2.1
(a)-(b) hold, and that
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
is
supercritical
, in that

(2.7)

\nu_{n}=\frac{\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}(d_{v}-1)}{\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}}\to\nu=\frac{\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[D]}>1.

In our first main result, which is due to Janson
[
46
]
, we identify the percolation phase transition. In its statement, we write
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d};\pi)
for the result of percolation on
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
with edge-retention probability
\pi
:

Theorem 2.2
(Percolation phase transition in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})

[
46
]
).

Suppose that Conditions
2.1
(a)-(b) hold, and consider the random graph
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, letting
n\to\infty
.
Assume that (
2.7
) holds, so that
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
is supercritical.

(a)

For
\pi>1/\nu=\mathbb{E}[D]/\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]
, there exists

\theta(\pi)\in(0,1]
such that
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\theta(\pi)
,while
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm sec}^{\pi}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.

(b)

For
\pi\leq 1/\nu
,
|\mathscr{C}_{\rm max}^{\pi}|/n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.

Theorem
2.2
identifies the percolation critical value as
\pi_{c}=1/\nu=\mathbb{E}[D]/\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)]
, in the sense that any
\pi<\pi_{c}
is asymptotically subcritical, while any
\pi>\pi_{c}
is asymptotically supercritical. In particular,
\pi_{c}=0
when
\mathbb{E}[D^{2}]=\infty
, a property that is sometimes called
instantaneous percolation
or
robustness
. Robustness is desirable, since it implies that whichever the proportion of removed edges, there remains some decent connectivity in the percolated graph.

We continue by investigating the
critical behaviour
of percolation on the configuration model.

2.1.3
Scaling limits for finite third-moment degrees.

To formulate our main convergence result, for
p\geq 1
, define
\ell^{p}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}
to be the set of infinite sequences

\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
with
x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
and
\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{p}<\infty
, and define the

\ell^{2}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}
metric by

(2.8)

d(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\sqrt{\sum_{i\geq 1}(x_{i}-y_{i})^{2}}.

Further, by
\ell^{2}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}\times\mathbbm{N}^{\infty}
, we denote the product topology of
\ell^{2}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}
and
\mathbbm{N}^{\infty}
, where
\mathbbm{N}^{\infty}
denotes the collection of sequences on
\mathbbm{N}
, endowed with the product topology. Define also

(2.9)

\mathbb{U}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}:=\big\{((x_{i},y_{i}))_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\ell^{2}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}\times\mathbbm{N}^{\infty}\colon\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}y_{i}<\infty\text{ and }y_{i}=0\text{ whenever }x_{i}=0\;\forall i\big\},

endowed with the metric

(2.10)

d_{\mathbb{U}}((\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1}),(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})):=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(x_{1i}-x_{2i})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\big|x_{1i}y_{1i}-x_{2i}y_{2i}\big|.

Finally, we introduce
\mathbb{U}^{0}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}\subset\mathbb{U}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}
as

(2.11)

\mathbb{U}^{0}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}:=\big\{((x_{i},y_{i}))_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{U}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}\colon\text{ if }x_{k}=x_{m},k\leq m,\text{ then }y_{k}\geq y_{m}\big\}.

For any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{U}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}
, let
\mathrm{ord}(\mathbf{z})
denote the element of
\mathbb{U}^{0}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}
obtained by suitably ordering the coordinates of
\mathbf{z}
. The
\mathbb{U}^{0}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}
-topology will allow us to state joint convergence of component sizes and surpluses in a strong topology.

Consider bond percolation on
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
with probability
\pi
, yielding
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d};\pi)
. Fix

(2.12)

\pi_{n}=\pi_{n}(\lambda):=\frac{1}{\nu_{n}}\bigg(1+\frac{\lambda}{n^{1/3}}\bigg),

for some
\lambda\in\mathbbm{R}
. Here we recall that
\nu_{n}=\mathbb{E}[D_{n}(D_{n}-1)]/\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]
, as defined in (
2.7
), and thus
\pi_{n}
is close to
\pi_{c}=1/\nu
. The value
\pi_{n}
as in (
2.12
) rather than
\pi_{c}=1/\nu
takes the finite-size corrections of the critical behaviour more closely into account. Note that
\pi_{n}(\lambda)
in (
2.12
) is non-negative for
n
sufficiently large even when
\lambda<0
.

The main result in this section identifies the scaling limit of the ordered components of percolation on
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
with edge-retention probability
\pi_{n}
in (
2.12
). Let us now define the objects whose scaling limit we consider in detail. Recall that
(|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}^{\pi}|)_{j\geq 1}
denotes the ordered component sizes of
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d};\pi)
. Let
\mathrm{SP}(\mathscr{C})
denote the surplus of the component
\mathscr{C}
. The main result of this section is then the following weak convergence result:

Theorem 2.3
(Critical percolation on configuration models with finite third-moment degrees
[
31
]
).

Consider
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
with the degrees satisfying Condition

(a)-(b) and (

) with
\nu>1
, and that
\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{3}]\rightarrow\mathbb{E}[D^{3}]<\infty.
Fix
\pi_{n}=\pi_{n}(\lambda)
as in (

\mathbf{Z}(\lambda)
such that, with respect to the
\mathbb{U}^{0}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}

(2.13)

\mathbf{Z}_{n}(\lambda):=\mathrm{ord}\Big(\big(n^{-2/3}|\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}^{\pi_{n}}|,\mathrm{SP}(\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}^{\pi_{n}})\big)_{j\geq 1}\Big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbf{Z}(\lambda).

Remark 2.4
(Multiple values of
\lambda
, and informal relation to multiplicative coalescent).

The result in Theorem
2.3
can be extended to
joint convergence
for several values of
\lambda
at the same time. This process, with
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
considered as
time
, is a so-called
multiplicative coalescent process

[
4
,
5
]
, where components of masses
x_{i}(t)
and
x_{j}(t)
merge to form a component of mass
x_{i}(t)x_{j}(t)
at rate
x_{i}(t)x_{j}(t)
. An intuitive picture is that as we change the value of the percolation parameter from
\pi_{n}(\lambda)
to
\pi_{n}(\lambda+d\lambda)
, exactly one edge is added to the graph and the two endpoints
u,v
are chosen approximately proportional to the number of half-edges of
u
and
v
that were not retained in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d};\pi_{n}(\lambda))
. Define the
degree deficiency

\mathscr{D}_{i}
of a component
\mathscr{C}_{i}
to be the total number of half-edges in a component that were not retained in percolation. Think of
\mathscr{D}_{i}
as the mass of
\mathscr{C}_{i}
. By the above heuristics,
\mathscr{C}_{i}
and
\mathscr{C}_{j}
merge at rate proportional to
\mathscr{D}_{i}\mathscr{D}_{j}
and create a component of mass
\mathscr{D}_{i}+\mathscr{D}_{j}-2
. The proof with Dhara, van Leeuwaarden and Sen
[
31
]
shows that the degree deficiency of a component is approximately proportional to the component size. Therefore, the component sizes merge
approximately
like the multiplicative coalescent over the critical scaling window.

2.1.4
Percolation on configuration models with infinite third-moment degrees.

In the previous section, we have focused on the configuration model with finite third-moment degrees whose the percolation critical behaviour is in the same universality class as the Erdős-Rényi random graph (recall
[
4
]
). This can be understood by the fact that, denoting the number of vertices found by pairing one half-edge by
d_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}-1
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(d_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}=k)\approx k\mathbb{P}(D_{n}=k)/\mathbb{E}[D_{n}].

Therefore,
{\rm Var}(d_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)})\rightarrow\mathbb{E}[D^{3}]/\mathbb{E}[D]-(\mathbb{E}[D^{2}]/\mathbb{E}[D])^{2}<\infty

when Condition
2.1
holds and
\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{3}]\rightarrow\mathbb{E}[D^{3}]
. Thus, the exploration process has
finite variance
, explaining why its scaling limit involves Brownian motion. In this section, we instead look at the heavy-tailed setting, where Condition
2.1
holds, but
\mathbb{E}[D^{3}]=\infty
, following work with Dhara, van Leeuwaarden and Sen
[
31
]
.

Main results for heavy-tailed configuration models.

Throughout this section, we use the shorthand notation

(2.14a)

\alpha=1/(\tau-1),\qquad\rho=(\tau-2)/(\tau-1),\qquad\eta=(\tau-3)/(\tau-1),

(2.14b)

a_{n}=n^{\alpha}L(n),\qquad b_{n}=n^{\rho}(L(n))^{-1},\qquad c_{n}=n^{\eta}(L(n))^{-2},

where
\tau\in(3,4)
and

is a slowly-varying function. These sequences turn out to describe the sizes of the maximal degrees, the sizes of the largest components, and the width of the scaling window, respectively, when the degree distribution obeys a power-law with exponent
\tau\in(3,4)
with slowly-varying corrections described by

.

Before stating our main result, let us define the necessary conditions on the degrees under which it applies. Assume that there exists
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots)\in\ell^{3}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}\setminus\ell^{2}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}
such that

(2.15)

\frac{d_{i}}{a_{n}}\to\theta_{i}\quad\text{ for all fixed }\quad i\geq 1,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_{n}^{-3}\sum_{i=K+1}^{n}d_{i}^{3}=0,

The first part of (

in (

) describes the order of the largest degrees, while the second part of (
2.15
) shows that the largest degrees provide the main contribution to their third moment. The main result in this section describes the scaling of the largest critical percolation components on the configuration model with heavy-tailed degrees:

Theorem 2.5

Note that

Configuration model with iid degrees.

A natural setting to investigate the role of heavy-tailed degrees is to start with independent and identically distributed (iid) random degrees having a power-law distribution with

Then, we use that

(2.18)

\big(D_{\scriptscriptstyle(n-i+1)}\big)_{i\in[n]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Big([1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle D}]^{-1}(U_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})\big)_{i\in[n]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\big([1-F_{\scriptscriptstyle D}]^{-1}(\Gamma_{i}/\Gamma_{n+1})\big)_{i\in[n]}.

Equation (

between the degree sequences for random graphs of different sizes, which are such that in particular the

are closely related. This mimics the setting in (

to that of

with deterministic degrees.

2.2
Percolation on rank-1 inhomogeneous random graphs.

Having defined the main results for the configuration model, we move on to study rank-1 inhomogeneous random graphs, where the behaviour is remarkably similar.
We start by introducing the model in Section
2.2.1
, and then describe how results need to be changed in the finite-variance degree cases in Section
2.2.2
. We close this section by discussing the surprising results for the case of rank-1 random graphs with infinite-variance degrees and single-edges in Section

2.2.1
Rank-1 inhomogeneous random graphs.

In the
Poissonian random graph
or Norros-Reittu model
[
52
]
, the edge probability of the edge between vertices
u
and
v
, for
u\neq v
, is equal to

(2.22)

p_{uv}=p_{uv}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm(NR)}=1-{\rm exp}\left(-w_{u}w_{v}/\ell_{n}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{v})_{v\in[n]}
are the
vertex weights,
and now
\ell_{n}
is the total weight of all vertices given by
\ell_{n}=\sum_{v\in[n]}w_{v}.

We denote the resulting graph by
{\rm NR}_{n}(\boldsymbol{w})
.
3
3

3

In many cases, the vertex weights actually depend on
n
, and it would again be more appropriate to write the weights as
\boldsymbol{w}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=(w_{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{v\in[n]}
, and we again refrain from making the dependence on
n
explicit.
The Poissonian random graph can be called rank-1, since
p_{uv}\approx w_{u}w_{v}/\ell_{n}
when
w_{u}w_{v}/\ell_{n}
is quite small. The Poissonian random graph
{\rm NR}_{n}(\boldsymbol{w})
is close to many other inhomogeneous random graph models, such as the
random graph with given prescribed degrees
or Chung-Lu model
{\rm CL}_{n}(\boldsymbol{w})

[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, where instead

p_{uv}=p_{uv}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm(CL)}=\min(w_{u}w_{v}/\ell_{n},1).
A further adaptation is the so-called
generalised random graph model

{\rm GRG}_{n}(\boldsymbol{w})

[
23
]
, in which
p_{uv}=p_{uv}^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm(GRG)}=\frac{w_{u}w_{v}}{\ell_{n}+w_{u}w_{v}}.
See Janson
[
47
]
or
[
41
, Chapter 6]
for conditions under which these random graphs are
asymptotically equivalent
, meaning that all events have equal asymptotic probabilities.

For
{\rm NR}_{n}(\boldsymbol{w})
, the vertex weight
w_{v}
, for
v\in[n]
, is closely related to the
expected degree
of vertex
v
. This can be seen by noting that, with
d_{v}
denoting the degree of vertex

, and

the indicator that the edge

is present in

\mathbb{E}[d_{v}]=\sum_{u\in[n]\colon u\neq v}\mathbb{E}[X_{uv}]\approx\sum_{u\in[n]}p_{uv}\approx\sum_{u\in[n]}\frac{w_{u}w_{v}}{\ell_{n}}=w_{v},

when approximating the sum over
u\in[n]
such that

. While this is not exact, for large

is close to

, which gives a natural interpretation for the vertex weights.

An alternative way of constructing

is to independently consider

edges between any pair of vertices
u,v\in[n]
, and then take

for all
u\neq v
, and
X_{vv}=0
for all
v\in[n]
. This has the advantage that
\sum_{u\in[n]}X_{uv}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(w_{v})
, and

is obtained from the multi-graph with

collapsing
multi-edges and
removing
self-loops.

2.3
Proofs for rank-1 random graphs.

In this section, we give an informal overview of the proofs of our results. Except for Theorems
2.8
and
2.9
, we focus on the configuration model
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, the proofs for
{\rm NR}_{n}(\boldsymbol{w})
are similar in spirit.

The local limit, and the critical value.

It is well-known that the configuration model, assuming Condition
2.1
, converges locally in probability to a branching process having root offspring
D
, and offspring distribution
D^{\star}-1
for every other vertex. Here
D^{\star}
is the size-biased distribution of
D
, given by

(2.30)

\mathbb{P}(D^{\star}=k)=\frac{k\mathbb{P}(D=k)}{\mathbb{E}[D]}.

This is a so-called
unimodular
branching process, and since only the root has a different offspring distribution, the percolation critical value is equal to
\pi_{c}=1/\mathbb{E}[D^{\star}-1]=1/\nu.
This suggests, as in Theorem
2.2
, that there is a giant for
\pi>1/\nu
, while there is no giant when
\pi\leq 1/\nu
. We next explain how such results can be proved.

The percolation phase transition: Janson’s construction.

A beautiful proof of the percolation phase transition in Theorem
2.2
relies on the realisation by Janson
[
46
]
that percolation on
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
can be described in terms of another configuration model. On the Erdős-Rényi random graph with edge probability
\lambda/n
, when we perform percolation with parameter
\pi
, we obtain another Erdős-Rényi random graph, now with parameter
\pi\lambda/n
. When the Erdős-Rényi random graph is supercritical in that
\lambda>1
, we thus obtain that
\pi_{c}=1/\lambda
. Janson’s construction extends this observation to the configuration model, for which it is a little more involved.

Fix a degree distribution
\boldsymbol{d}
. For
v\in[n]
, let
d_{v}(\pi)={\sf Bin}(d_{v},\sqrt{\pi})
, where
\big({\sf Bin}(d_{v},\sqrt{\pi})\big)_{v\in[n]}
are independent binomial random variables.
Further, let

(2.31)

N^{+}=N^{+}(\pi)=\sum_{v\in[n]}\big[d_{v}-{\sf Bin}(d_{v},\sqrt{\pi})\big],

and let
N=N(\pi)=n+N^{+}
. For
v\in[N]\setminus[n]
, set
d_{v}(\pi)=1
and color these vertices
red
. This has the following interpretation: We split a vertex of degree
d_{v}
into a single vertex of degree
d_{v}(\pi)={\sf Bin}(d_{v},\sqrt{\pi})
, and
d_{v}-d_{v}(\pi)
red vertices of degree 1. This is called the
explosion
of vertex
v
. We can think of the red vertices as being
artificial
, and they will later need to be removed. After this red-vertex removal, an edge is retained when both of its half-edges are retained, which occurs with probability
\sqrt{\pi}\times\sqrt{\pi}=\pi
, as it should be. Since all edge-retention decisions are made independently, this is thus equivalent to percolation on
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
. Let
{\rm CM}_{N}(\boldsymbol{d}(\pi))
denote the configuration model with vertex set
[N]
and degree distribution
\boldsymbol{d}(\pi)=(d_{v}(\pi))_{v\in[N]}
. Then, Janson’s construction states that percolation on the configuration model
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d};\pi)
has the same distribution as
{\rm CM}_{N}(\boldsymbol{d}(\pi))
followed by the removal of the
N-n=N^{+}
red vertices with degree 1. Here, we refer to the removal of a vertex of degree 1 as the act of removal of the vertex from the vertex set, together with the removal of its (single) edge from the edge set.

Janson’s construction couples the configuration model and percolation on it on the same probability space. Since the removal of the red degree-1 vertices, and the edges incident to them, does not change the fact whether there exists a giant or not, the phase transition is thus the same as for
{\rm CM}_{N}(\boldsymbol{d}(\pi))
. Let
D_{n}(\pi)
denote
d_{U}(\pi)
, where
U\in[N]
is chosen uniformly at random. Then, note that
\sum_{v\in[N]}d_{v}(\pi)=\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}
since no half-edge is removed, while
\sum_{v\in[N]}d_{v}(\pi)(d_{v}(\pi)-1)=\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}(\pi)(d_{v}(\pi)-1)
, where
d_{v}(\pi)\sim{\sf Bin}(\ell_{n},\sqrt{\pi})
are independent binomial variables. It comes as no surprise that
\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}(\pi)(d_{v}(\pi)-1)
is highly concentrated around its mean
\pi\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}(d_{v}-1)
(where we use that
\mathbb{E}[{\sf Bin}(m,p)({\sf Bin}(m,p)-1)]=p^{2}m(m-1)
). Thus,
\nu_{n}(\pi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\pi\nu
, and a giant can be expected to exist precisely when
\pi\nu>1
. The beauty of Janson’s construction is that this result can be read off from the classical giant component result for the configuration model; see
[
44
, Part III]
for a discussion and references.

Cluster scaling for critical configuration models with finite second-moment degrees.

The proofs of Theorems

follow by again using Janson’s construction. These proofs, alike the proof of Theorem

The second key step is to show that the percolated degree sequence in Janson’s construction satisfies the conditions posed in the theorem for the critical configuration model. The final and third step is to describe the effect of removing the red vertices. In the remainder of this section, we will discuss how to analyse the scaling limit of the largest components for critical configuration models, i.e., without percolation. For Theorem
2.3
, this means that we assume that

\nu_{n}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[D_{n}(D_{n}-1)]}{\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]}=1+\lambda n^{-1/3}+o(n^{-1/3}),

while for Theorem
2.5
,
n^{1/3}
is replaced by

We continue by describing the scaling limits in Theorems

The scaling limit for finite third-moment degrees.

Let us continue by defining the necessary objects so as to be able to describe the scaling limit of
\mathbf{Z}_{n}(\lambda)
. Define the Brownian motion with negative parabolic drift by

(2.33)

S^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}}{\mu}B(t)+\lambda t-\frac{\kappa t^{2}}{2\mu^{3}},

where
\mathbf{B}=\big(B(t)\big)_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion, and
\mu>0
,
\kappa>0
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
are constants to be determined later on. Define the reflected version of
\mathbf{S}^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa}=\big(S^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa}(t)\big)_{t\geq 0}
as

(2.34)

\mathrm{refl}(S^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa})(t)=S^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa}(t)-\min_{0\leq s\leq t}S^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa}(s).

In terms of the above, define
\gamma_{j}^{\lambda}
to be the ordered excursions of the inhomogeneous Brownian motion
\mathbf{S}^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa}
with

(2.35)

\mu=\mathbb{E}[D(\pi_{c})],\qquad\kappa=\mathbb{E}[D(\pi_{c})^{3}]\mathbb{E}[D(\pi_{c})]-\mathbb{E}[D(\pi_{c})^{2}]^{2},\quad\beta=1/\mathbb{E}[D(\pi_{c})],

where
\pi_{c}=1/\nu
is the asymptotic critical value as defined in Theorem
2.2
. Denote the
j^{\rm th}
largest component of
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d};\pi_{n}(\lambda))
by
\mathscr{C}_{\scriptscriptstyle(j)}(\lambda)
. We will also identify the scaling limit of the
surplus edges
of the large critical components. For this, define the counting process of marks
\mathbf{N}^{\lambda}=(N^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa}(s))_{s\geq 0}
to be a process that has intensity
\beta\mathrm{refl}(S^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa})(s)
at time
s
conditional on
(\mathrm{refl}(S^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa})(u))_{u\leq s}
, so that

(2.36)

N^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa}(s)-\int\limits_{0}^{s}\beta\mathrm{refl}(S^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa})(u)du

is a martingale (see Aldous
[
4
]
).

For an excursion
\gamma
starting at time

and ending at time
r(\gamma)

be its length, and
N(\gamma)=N^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa}(\gamma)
denote the number of marks in the interval

Then, the scaling limit in Theorem

\mathbf{Z}(\lambda)=\mathrm{ord}\Big(\big(\big(\big|\gamma_{j}^{\lambda}\big|,N(\gamma_{j}^{\lambda})\big)\big)_{j\geq 1}\Big)

The scaling limit for infinite third-moment degrees.

Consider a decreasing sequence
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots)\in\ell^{3}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}\setminus\ell^{2}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}
. Denote
\mathcal{I}_{i}(s):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\xi_{i}\leq s\}}
where
\xi_{i}\sim\mathrm{Exp}\Big(\frac{\theta_{i}}{\mu\sqrt{\nu}}\Big)
independently, and
\mathrm{Exp}(r)
denotes the exponential distribution with rate
r
. Let
\mu=\mathbb{E}[D]
. Consider the process

(2.37)

S^{\lambda}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\theta_{i}}{\sqrt{\nu}}\left(\mathcal{I}_{i}(t)-\frac{\theta_{i}}{\mu\sqrt{\nu}}t\right)+\lambda t,

for some
\lambda\in\mathbbm{R},
and define the reflected version of
S^{\lambda}(t)
as in (
2.34
). Processes of the form (
2.37
) were termed
thinned
Lévy processes in work with Bhamidi and van Leeuwaarden
[
16
]
, since the summands are thinned versions of Poisson processes. Indeed, we can interpret
\mathcal{I}_{i}(t)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{N_{i}(t)\geq 1\}}
, where
(N_{i}(t))_{i\geq 1}
are independent Poisson processes of rate
\theta_{i}/(\mu\sqrt{\nu})
, so that

(2.38)

L^{\lambda}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\theta_{i}}{\sqrt{\nu}}\left(N_{i}(t)-\frac{\theta_{i}}{\mu\sqrt{\nu}}t\right)+\lambda t

is a Lévy process. Obviously,
S^{\lambda}(t)\leq L^{\lambda}(t)
. In what follows, we will explain that the indicator processes
(\mathcal{I}_{i}(t))_{i\geq 1}
describe whether the vertex with the
i^{\rm th}
largest degree has been found by the exploration or not, and then
\theta_{i}\mathcal{I}_{i}(t)/\sqrt{\nu}
describes the jump made by the exploration process when
i
has been found. Clearly, in the graph context, any vertex can only been found
once
for the first time, which explains why the process
(\mathcal{I}_{i}(t))_{i\geq 1}
is relevant rather than the Poisson process
(N_{i}(t))_{i\geq 1}
. Thus, the fact that we deal with a thinned Lévy process is directly related to a
depletion-of-points effect
present in exploring random graphs.

Let us continue to describe the necessary notion for the scaling limits of component sizes for critical percolation on
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
in the infinite third-moment degree setting. For any function
f\in\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)
, define
\underline{f}(x)=\inf_{y\leq x}f(y)
.
\mathbb{D}_{+}[0,\infty)
is the subset of
\mathbb{D}[0,\infty)
consisting of functions with positive jumps only. Note that
\underline{f}
is continuous when
f\in\mathbb{D}_{+}[0,\infty)
. An
excursion
of a function
f\in\mathbb{D}_{+}[0,T]
is an interval
(l,r)
such that

(2.39)

\displaystyle\min\{f(l-),f(l)\}

\displaystyle=\underline{f}(l)=\underline{f}(r)=\min\{f(r-),f(r)\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x)>\underline{f}(r),\ \forall x\in(l,r)\subset[0,T].

Excursions of a function
f\in\mathbb{D}_{+}[0,\infty)
are defined similarly. We use
\gamma
to denote an excursion, as well as the length of the excursion
\gamma
, to simplify notation.
Then, as in (
2.36
), we define the counting process
\mathbf{N}
to be the Poisson process that has intensity
\mathrm{refl}(S^{\lambda})(t)
at time
t
conditional on
(S^{\lambda}(u))_{u\leq t}
.
We let
N(\gamma)
denote the number of marks in the interval
\gamma
.

In terms of these quantities, the scaling limit
\mathbf{Z}(\lambda)
in Theorem
2.5
is given by

\mathbf{Z}(\lambda):=\mathrm{ord}(\nu^{1/2}\gamma_{i}(\lambda),N(\gamma_{i}(\lambda)))_{i\geq 1}
,
where
\gamma_{i}(\lambda)
is the
i^{\rm th}
largest excursion of
S_{\infty}^{\lambda}
.

The scaling limit for infinite second-moment degrees.

Consider a decreasing sequence
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots)\in\ell^{2}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}\setminus\ell^{1}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}

\mathcal{I}_{i}(s):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\xi_{i}\leq s\}},
where
\xi_{i}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\theta_{i}/\mu)
independently, and consider the process

(2.40)

S^{\lambda}(t)=\frac{\lambda\mu}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\theta_{i}\mathcal{I}_{i}(t)-t,

for some
\lambda,\mu>0
, and define the reflected version
\mathrm{refl}(S^{\lambda})(t)
of
S^{\lambda}(t)
as in (
2.34
). It turns out that, for any
\lambda>0

We again let
N_{i}(\lambda)

\mathbf{N}^{\lambda}

largest excursion of
\mathbf{S}^{\lambda}

Then the scaling limit of critical component sizes in the case where

in Theorem
2.6

Exploration of components.

The fact that the scaling limits in Theorems
2.3
,
2.5
and
2.6
all have a similar shape is due to the fact that they all arise through an analysis of
exploration processes
of the components in the configuration model. We focus our explanation on Theorems
2.3
and
2.5
.

Our
breadth-first
exploration algorithm for the components in
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
carries along vertices that can be alive, active, exploring and killed, and half-edges that can be alive, active or killed. We sequentially explore the graph as follows:

(S0)

At stage
i=0
, all the vertices and the half-edges are
alive
but none of them are
active
. Also, there are no
exploring
vertices.

(S1)

At each stage
i
, if there is no active half-edge at stage
i
, choose a vertex
v
proportional to its degree among the alive (not yet killed) vertices, and declare all its half-edges to be
active
and declare
v
to be
exploring
. If there is an active vertex but no exploring vertex, then declare the
smallest
vertex to be exploring.

(S2)

At each stage
i
, take an active half-edge
e
of an exploring vertex
v
and pair it uniformly to another alive half-edge
f
. Kill
e,f
. If
f
is incident to a vertex
v^{\prime}
that has not been discovered before, then declare all the half-edges incident to
v^{\prime}
active (if any), except
f
.
If
\mathrm{degree}(v^{\prime})=1
(i.e. the only half-edge incident to
v^{\prime}
is
f
) then kill
v^{\prime}
. Otherwise, declare
v^{\prime}
to be active and larger than all other vertices that are alive. After killing
e
, if
v
does not have another active half-edge, then kill
v
also.

(S3)

Repeat from (S1) at stage
i+1
if not all half-edges are already killed.

Define the
exploration process
by

(2.41)

S_{n}(0)=0,\quad S_{n}(l)=S_{n}(l-1)+d_{(l)}J_{l}-2,

where
J_{l}
is the indicator that a new vertex is discovered at time
l
and
d_{(l)}
is the degree of the new vertex chosen at time
l
when
J_{l}=1
. Let
\mathscr{C}_{k}
be the
k^{\rm th}
component explored by the above exploration process and define
\tau_{k}=\inf\big\{i\colon S_{n}(i)=-2k\big\}.
Then
\mathscr{C}_{k}
is discovered between the times
\tau_{k-1}+1
and
\tau_{k}
, and
\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1}-1
gives the total number of edges in
\mathscr{C}_{k}
. Since our critical components have small surplus, also
|\mathscr{C}_{k}|
is close to
\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1}-1
. This explains how
component sizes
are related to
excursion lengths
of appropriate stochastic processes.

Scaling limit of the exploration process for finite third-moment degrees.

When
\mathbb{E}[D_{n}^{3}]\rightarrow\mathbb{E}[D^{3}]<\infty
, as in Theorem
2.3
,
\mathbb{E}[d_{(l)}]\approx\mathbb{E}[D^{\star}]=2
, so that the expected jump sizes in (
2.41
) have almost zero mean. Further, as explained at the start of Section
2.1.4
, for any fixed
l
,
{\rm Var}(d_{\scriptscriptstyle(l)})\rightarrow\mathbb{E}[D^{3}]/\mathbb{E}[D]-(\mathbb{E}[D^{2}]/\mathbb{E}[D])^{2}<\infty.
Thus, we are dealing with a stochastic process that has almost zero mean, and finite variance, so that it will not come as a surprise that the scaling limit involves
Brownian motion
. Let us now relate this in more detail to the process in (
2.33
). We claim that

(2.42)

\Big(n^{-1/3}S_{n}\big(\lceil tn^{2/3}\rceil\big)\Big)_{t\geq 0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}(S^{\lambda}_{\mu,\kappa}(t))_{t\geq 0}.

The fact that time is rescaled by
n^{2/3}
is indicative of the fact that component sizes are of order
n^{2/3}
, while the rescaling by
n^{1/3}=\sqrt{n^{2/3}}
corresponds to diffusive rescaling. Results of Aldous
[
4
]
prove that (
2.42
) implies the scaling limit of component sizes in Theorem
2.3
, while the surplus follows by tracking the number of
l
for which
J_{l}=0
, which correspond to edges that are connected to active half-edges, and thus count surplus edges.

Let us next explain how (
2.42
) arise. The linear
\lambda t
term in (
2.41
) arises since the mean of our process is
not quite zero
, but is by (
2.32
) equal to
\nu_{n}-1=\lambda n^{-1/3}+o(n^{-1/3})
. Thus, in
\lceil tn^{2/3}\rceil
steps, the total drift is close to
\lambda n^{-1/3}\lceil tn^{2/3}\rceil\approx\lambda tn^{1/3}
, which after rescaling by
n^{-1/3}
as in (
2.42
) gives rise to the linear drift term
\lambda t
in (
2.41
).

We next explain the occurrence of the negative parabolic drift in (
2.41
), which is more delicate. While it is true that
\mathbb{E}[d_{(l)}-2]\approx\lambda n^{-1/3}+o(n^{-1/3})
for
small

l
, we actually need to know what
\mathbb{E}[d_{(l)}-2]
is for
l
of the order
n^{2/3}
, for which it turns out that
\mathbb{E}[d_{(l)}-2]
becomes smaller. This is because larger vertices are more likely to be chosen early, due to the fact that vertices are chosen according to their degree. In order to describe this effect, given a sequence of weights
\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{v})_{v\in[n]}
, the
size-biased reordering
of
[n]
is the random vector of vertices
(v_{i})_{i\in[n]}
such that

(2.43)

\mathbb{P}(v_{i}=j\mid v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1})=\frac{d_{j}}{\sum_{\ell\not\in\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}}d_{\ell}}.

It turns out that the sequence
(d_{(l)}J_{l})_{l\geq 1}
, removing the zeros for which
J_{l}=0
(i.e., the degrees of vertices that are actually new) have
exactly
the same distribution as
(d_{v_{l}})_{l\geq 1}
. The key analytical result is then that

(2.44)

\mathbb{E}[d_{(\lceil tn^{2/3}\rceil)}-2]\approx n^{-1/3}\Big(\lambda-\frac{t(\sigma_{3}-2\sigma_{2})}{\mu^{2}}\Big),\quad\text{ so that }\quad\sum_{l=1}^{\lceil tn^{2/3}\rceil}\mathbb{E}[d_{(l)}-2]\approx n^{1/3}\Big(\lambda t-\frac{t^{2}(\sigma_{3}-2\sigma_{2})}{2\mu^{2}}\Big).

This explains how the negative quadratic drift in (
2.33
) arises. The scaling limit in (
2.42
) follows by applying a Martingale Central Limit Theorem.

Scaling limit of the exploration process for infinite third-moment degrees.

We next extend this analysis to the case where
\tau\in(3,4)
as in Theorem
2.5
, for which we conveniently rewrite (
2.41
). Call a vertex
discovered
if it is either active or killed. Let
\mathscr{V}_{l}
denote the set of vertices discovered up to time
l

\mathcal{I}_{i}^{n}(l):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\in\mathscr{V}_{l}\}}

(2.45)

S_{n}(l)=\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{i}\mathcal{I}_{i}^{n}(l)-2l=\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{i}\left(\mathcal{I}_{i}^{n}(l)-\frac{d_{i}}{\ell_{n}}l\right)+\left(\nu_{n}-1\right)l.

Recall from (
2.42
) that the exploration process runs on scale
n^{1/3}
when
\mathbb{E}[D^{3}]<\infty
. For
\tau\in(3,4)

a_{n}

) to (

), we can expect that the linear term
\lambda t
in (

i

i
in (

\Big(a_{n}^{-1}S_{n}\big(\lceil tb_{n}\rceil\big)\Big)_{t\geq 0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}(S^{\lambda}(t))_{t\geq 0},

with

Cluster scaling for critical configuration models with infinite second-moment degrees.

The proof for
\tau\in(2,3)
in Theorem

in
[

proceeds in a similar way as above, with one major difference, since the limiting process turns out to have

\mathbb{E}\big[|S^{\lambda}(t)-S^{\lambda}(u)|\big]\leq\frac{\lambda\mu}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\theta_{i}{\mathrm{e}}^{-\theta_{i}u}(1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-\theta_{i}(t-u)})+|t-u|\leq(\lambda\mu+1)|t-u|.

Tiny giant in single-edge scale-free Poissonian random graph.

To define the limiting variables in Theorem

which belongs to a general class of models studied by Durrett and Kesten in

. For this, we let vertex

(\lambda_{uv})
many edges with

(2.48)

\lambda_{uv}:=\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Theta_{u}(x)\Theta_{v}(x)\mathrm{d}x,\quad\text{where}\quad\Theta_{v}(x):=1-{\mathrm{e}}^{-c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}\theta_{v}x^{-\alpha}}.

For
i\geq 1
, let
\mathscr{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}^{\infty}(\lambda)

The following result implies that the limiting object is well-defined for

Proposition 2.10
(Phase transition for the limiting model
[
34
]
).

For
\lambda\leq\lambda_{c}
,
(\mathscr{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}^{\infty}(\lambda))_{i\geq 1}
is in
\ell^{2}_{\mathrel{\text{\rotatebox[origin]{270.0}{$\vrule height=4.09723pt,width=0.0pt\shortrightarrow$}}}}
almost surely, while for
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
,
\mathscr{G}_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\lambda)
is connected almost surely, in particular
\mathscr{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}^{\infty}(\lambda)=\infty
and
\mathscr{W}_{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}^{\infty}(\lambda)=0
almost surely.

The crux to the proof of Theorem
2.8
in
[
12
]
is that asymptotically there are Poisson(
\lambda_{uv}
) many

paths between ‘macro-hubs’
u
and
v

in

.
The integral in (

to

.
These two-step paths between hubs form the backbone of the largest components. By Proposition

, the connectivity structure of these two-step connections undergoes a phase transition, and the resulting components on

. This explains the critical phase for

The proof for

is more delicate and technical. We only sketch the highlights. Fix a parameter

, and let

N_{n}(a)=\lfloor an^{(3-\tau)/2}\rfloor,\qquad\text{and}\qquad N_{n}=N_{n}(1).

Observe from (

\pi_{n}\asymp\lambda/N_{n}\asymp a\lambda/N_{n}(a)
. Since
w_{v}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}(n/v)^{\alpha}
with

as defined in (

), for

,

(2.50)

w_{\lceil N_{n}u\rceil}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}u^{-\alpha}\Big(\frac{n}{N_{n}}\Big)^{\alpha}=c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}u^{-\alpha}\big(n^{(\tau-1)/2}\big)^{\alpha}\asymp\sqrt{n}c_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}u^{-\alpha},

and thus
[N_{n}(a)]
consists of vertices with weight at least of order
\sqrt{n}a^{-\alpha}
. The key conceptual step is that, for large enough
a
, a giant component emerges inside
[N_{n}(a)]
that forms the core connectivity structure of the tiny giant component in the whole graph. In turn, this graph is an inhomogeneous random graph, for which the critical value can be determined exactly, as we explain in more detail now.

To this end, consider the percolated graph
\mathrm{NR}_{n}(\boldsymbol{w},\pi_{n})
, restricted to
[N_{n}(a)]
, and denote this subgraph by
\mathscr{G}_{\scriptscriptstyle N_{n}(a)}
. Then,
\mathscr{G}_{\scriptscriptstyle N_{n}(a)}
is distributed as an inhomogeneous random graph that is
sparse
in that the number of edges grows linearly in the number of vertices in the graph. Thus, the emergence of the giant component within
\mathscr{G}_{\scriptscriptstyle N_{n}(a)}
can be studied using the general setting of inhomogeneous random graphs developed by Bollobás, Janson and Riordan in
[
20
]
.
In particular, the results of
[
20
]
gives a critical value
{\lambda_{c}(a)}
, such that, for
\lambda>\lambda_{c}(a)
, a unique giant with concentrated size exists inside
[N_{n}(a)]
, that is
stable
to the addition of a small proportion of edges. The stability result is crucial to understand the perturbation on this giant after adding all the edges due to connections outside
[N_{n}(a)]
.

It turns out that
\lim_{a\to\infty}{\lambda_{c}(a)}=\lambda_{c}
, where
\lambda_{c}
is given by (
2.25
). The connection between
{\lambda_{c}(a)}
and
\lambda_{c}
is remarkable given their vastly different descriptions, and is proved by an explicit computation. The convergence of
{\lambda_{c}(a)}
is also a key conceptual step, since it shows that, whenever
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
, one can choose
a
to be large enough to make a giant appear inside
[N_{n}(a)]
. Finally, the asymptotics for the giant inside
\mathscr{G}_{\scriptscriptstyle N_{n}(a)}
are given by properties of certain multi-type branching processes that depend sensitively on
a
. Since
a>0
is arbitrary, we need to take the limit of
a\rightarrow\infty
, and show that the size of the tiny giant in
[n]
converges. For this, we identify the primary contributions to the size of the giant in the whole graph using the giant inside
[N_{n}(a)]
, for
a
large enough, which consist of the vertices at
finite
distance from
[N_{n}(a)]
in the percolated graph. To explain that these are of order
\sqrt{n}
, we note that a vertex of weight
w_{v}\gg 1
has degree roughly
w_{v}\pi_{n}
in the percolated graph. Thus, the
neighbourhood
of the giant in
[N_{n}(a)]
in the percolated graph
\mathrm{NR}_{n}(\boldsymbol{w},\pi_{n})
has order

(2.51)

\sum_{v=1}^{N_{n}(a)}w_{v}\pi_{n}=\Theta(1)n^{\alpha}\sum_{v=1}^{N_{n}(a)}v^{-\alpha}n^{-(\tau-3)/2}=\Theta(1)n^{\alpha}N_{n}^{1-\alpha}n^{-(\tau-3)/2}=\Theta(\sqrt{n}),

by (
2.24
) and since
N_{n}=\Theta(n^{(3-\tau)/2})
by (
2.49
). This explains why the size of the tiny giant is of order
\sqrt{n}
. Its concentration and uniqueness follow from the fact that the giant in
[N_{n}(a)]
is concentrated and unique, as proved in
[
20
]
.

3
Dynamic random graphs.

In this section, we discuss recent progress on percolation on various
dynamic
random graphs models. Such models can be seen as random graph models that
grow
in time, so that the graph sequence
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
forms a
stochastic process
. This is not the case for, e.g.,
{\rm CM}_{n}(\boldsymbol{d})
, for which the graphs of size
n
and
n+1
are unrelated. It turns out that the percolation phase transition is remarkably different for such dynamic random graphs.

We study preferential and uniform attachment random graphs in Section
3.1
, and related dynamic random graphs (such as dynamic inhomogeneous random graphs) in Section
3.2
.

3.1
Percolation on preferential and uniform attachment random graphs.

3.1.1
Preferential and uniform attachment models.

Fix an integer parameter
m\geq 2
, weight parameters
a\in[0,1]
,
\delta>-m/a
, and consider an attachment function
f\colon\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

(3.1)

f(x)=ax+\delta,\qquad x\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Let
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of networks grown using preferential attachment dynamics and number of incoming edges
m
. Thus, having constructed the network
G_{n}
and writing
\deg(v,n)
for the degree of an existing vertex
v
in
G_{n}
, a new vertex
n+1
enters the system with
m
edges which it uses to connect to the existing network, via sequentially connecting each edge to an existing vertex
v\in G_{n}
in the network with probability approximately proportional to
f(\deg(v,n))
. In more detail, we start from any graph on
2
vertices and finitely many connections between them such that at least one of the initial vertices has degree at most
m
. Let
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
denote the degrees of vertices
1
and
2
in the initial graph. Without loss of generality, consider
d_{2}\leq m
. For every new vertex
v
joining the graph and
j=1,2,\ldots,m
, the attachment probabilities are given by

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}\Big(v\overset{j}{\rightsquigarrow}u\mid{\rm PA}^{(d)}_{v,j-1}(m,\delta)\Big)=\frac{d_{u}(v,j-1)+\delta}{c_{v,j}^{(d)}}\hskip 42.67912pt\text{for}~u<v~,

where
v\overset{j}{\rightsquigarrow}u
denotes that vertex
v
connects to
u
with its
j^{\rm th}
edge,
{\rm PA}^{(d)}_{v,j}({m},\delta)
denotes the graph on
v
vertices, with the
v^{\rm th}
vertex having
j
out-edges, and
d_{u}(v,j)
denotes the degree of vertex
u
in
{\rm PA}^{(d)}_{v,j}({m},\delta)
. We identify
{\rm PA}^{(d)}_{v+1,0}({m},\delta)
with
{\rm PA}^{(d)}_{v,m}({m},\delta)
. The normalizing constant
c_{v,j}^{(d)}
in (
3.2
) equals

(3.3)

c_{v,j}^{(d)}:=ad_{[2]}+2\delta+(2am+\delta)(v-3)+a(j-1),

where
d_{[2]}=d_{1}+d_{2}
. We denote the above model as
{\rm PA}^{(d)}_{v}({m},\delta)
, which is equivalent to
{\rm PA}_{v}^{(m,\delta)}(d)
as defined in
[
41
]
when we set
a=1
. The case where
a=0
corresponds to the
uniform attachment
case. The main quantity of interest is percolation on the constructed network. Fix a parameter
\pi\in(0,1)
, and let
G^{\pi}_{n}
denote the network obtained from
G_{n}
by independently retaining each edge with probability
\pi
and deleting it with probability
1-\pi
.

3.1.2
The phase transition.

We first describe the phase transition, by identifying for which percolation parameter
\pi\in[0,1]
there exists a giant component, and for which there does not:

Theorem 3.1
(Critical percolation threshold for
{\rm PA}
models
[
39
]
).

Fix
a=1
and
m\geq 2
. For any
\pi\in[0,1]
, let
\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{\max}(n)
and
\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{\rm sec}(n)
denote the maximal and second largest component of
G_{n}^{\pi}
.
Then

(3.4)

\frac{|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{\max}(n)|}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\theta(\pi)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{\rm sec}(n)|}{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}0.

Furthermore, define
\pi_{c}=0
for
\delta\in(-m,0]
, and

(3.5)

\pi_{c}=\frac{\delta}{2\big(m(m+\delta)+\sqrt{m(m-1)(m+\delta)(m+1+\delta)}\big)}\qquad\text{for }\delta>0.

Then

\theta(\pi)>0
for
\pi>\pi_{c}
, whereas
\theta(\pi)=0
for
\pi\leq\pi_{c}
.

It turns out that
1/\pi_{c}
denotes the exponential growth of the generations of the local limit of the preferential attachment model, which is the Pólya point tree (PPT)
[
11
,
36
]
. Thus, for
\pi<\pi_{c}
, the exponential growth is removed, and the percolated PPT dies out almost surely, while for
\pi>\pi_{c}
, it continues to grow exponentially with positive probability. This explains the relevance of
\pi_{c}
in (
3.5
). Bollobás and Riordan
[
21
]
, by adapting argument for the
uniformly grown random graph

[
19
]
, have shown that, for the uniform attachment case for which
a=0
and
\delta=1
, the critical percolation threshold becomes
\pi_{c}=1/[2\big(m+\sqrt{m(m-1)}\big)]
.

3.1.3
Subcritical percolation on the uniform attachment model.

Here we restrict to the uniform attachment case, for which
a=0
, and
m=2
. Write
\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{v}(n)
for the component of vertex
v
in
G^{\pi}_{n}
. Define

(3.6)

\pi_{c}=\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{4}=\frac{1}{2(2+\sqrt{2})}.

Further, for
\pi<\pi_{c}
, define

(3.7)

\alpha(\pi):=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\sqrt{8\pi^{2}-8\pi+1}\right).

We now state the main results in
[
8
]
:

Theorem 3.2
(Convergence of maximal components
[
8
]
).

Fix
a=0
and
m=2
. Let
\pi<\pi_{c}
.

(a)

For any fixed
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
n^{-\alpha(\pi)}|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{i}(\pi)|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\zeta_{i}
as
n\to\infty
, where
\zeta_{i}>0
almost surely.

(b)

n^{-\alpha(\pi)}|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{\max}(n)|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\max_{i\geq 1}\zeta_{i}
as
n\to\infty
. Further, the maximal component is ‘weakly persistent’ in the sense that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K(\varepsilon)
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{\max}(n)=\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{i}(n)\text{ for some }i>K(\varepsilon)\text{ for infinitely many }n\right)<\varepsilon.

3.2
Percolation on related dynamic random graphs.

In Section
3.1
, we have discussed the phase transition on preferential and uniform attachment models, and the subcritical phase on uniform attachment models. We believe that similar results hold for various related dynamic random graph models. Mörters and Schleicher
[
50
]
investigate a class of dynamic inhomogeneous random graphs, in which an edge is independently present between vertices
u
and
v
with probability
p_{uv}=\beta(u\wedge v)^{\gamma-1}(u\vee v)^{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in[0,1]
. Such models are called
inhomogeneous random graphs of preferential attachment type
, since also for preferential attachment models, the probability that an edge is present between vertices
u
and
v
is of order
p_{uv}
for
\gamma=(m+\delta)/(2m+\delta)
and an appropriate
\beta>0
(see
[
43
, Chapter 8]
). By an analysis as in
[
30
]
, it can be shown that
\beta_{c}=(\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{\gamma}{2})\wedge 0
. Thus,
\beta_{c}>0
when
\gamma<\tfrac{1}{2}
, which corresponds to
\delta>0
. Then, Mörters and Schleicher
[
50
]
show that the maximal component
\mathscr{C}_{\max}(n)
satisfies that
\log{|\mathscr{C}_{\max}(n)|}/\log{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tfrac{1}{2}-\sqrt{(\tfrac{1}{2}-\gamma)^{2}-\beta(1-2\gamma)}
.

The phase transition results in Theorem
3.1

[
39
]
apply to uniform, as well as preferential, attachment models, and it can be expected that the subcritical results extend to preferential attachment models with major technical adjustments. This is ongoing. A related problem is the so-called
Bernoulli
preferential attachment model, studied by Dereich and Mörters in
[
29
,
30
]
. Also in this model, vertices appear dynamically, and, conditionally on the graph
G_{n}
at time
n
, vertex
n+1
connects to vertex
v\in[n]

independently
with probability
f({\rm in-deg}(v,n))/n
, where
{\rm in-deg}(v,n)
denotes the in-degree of vertex
v
at time
n
, i.e., the number of edges connected to
v
in the time period
v+1,\ldots,n
, and
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
is an appropriate function. Dereich and Mörters
[
30
]
determine when this model has a giant component Remarkably, Eckhoff, Mörters and Ortgiese
[
35
]
show that for super-critical
f
that are close to critical, the giant is small, in that the proportion of vertices in it is approximately
{\mathrm{e}}^{-c/\sqrt{\rho-\rho_{c}}}
, where
\rho_{c}
corresponds to the critical edge density, and
\rho>\rho_{c}
is a near-critical edge density. The results also apply to near-critical supercritical percolation on such supercritical graphs. This ‘small’ giant component corresponds to an
infinite-order phase transition
that was already found by Riordan
[
53
]
, building on
[
19
,
21
]
, in related settings, including the
m
-out graph, but not yet for preferential attachment models with fixed out-degrees.

3.3
Proofs for percolation on dynamic random graphs.

The phase transition.

The proof for the critical percolation threshold in
[
39
]
consists of three main steps. First, we show that preferential attachment graphs are so-called
large-set expanders
, meaning that even large sets have many edges pointing out of them. This enables us to verify the conditions posed by Alimohammadi, Borgs, and Saberi
[
7
]
. Under their conditions, the proportion of vertices in the largest component in a random graph sequence converges to the survival probability of percolation on the local limit. In particular, the critical percolation threshold for both the graph and its local limit are identical.
Second, we identify
1/\pi_{c}
as the spectral radius of the mean offspring operator of the Pólya point tree (PPT), which is the local limit of preferential attachment models.
Lastly, we prove that the critical percolation threshold for the PPT is the inverse of the spectral radius of the mean offspring operator. For positive
\delta
, we use sub-martingales to prove sub-criticality and apply spine decomposition theory to demonstrate super-criticality, completing the third step of the proof. For negative
\delta
, we show that the percolated PPT can be stochastically lower bounded by a supercritical branching process, for all
\pi>0
.

Dynamics of components in Theorem
3.2
.

Again we fix
a=0
and
m=2
. It will be convenient to use a continuous-time construction
(G^{\pi}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Let
(|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|)_{t\geq 0}
denote the size process of the component containing vertex one.
For continuous time
t\geq 0
, write the collection of components in
G^{\pi}_{t}
as

(3.8)

\mathbf{C}(t)=\left\{\mathscr{C}\subseteq G^{\pi}_{t}\colon\mathscr{C}\text{ component}\right\}.

Denote the size of the random graph at time
t
by
N(t)
. It is not hard to see that
t\mapsto N(t)
is a
Yule process
, i.e., a pure-birth process that grows by 1 at rate
N(t)
. A Yule process satisfies that
N(t){\mathrm{e}}^{-t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}E
, which is an exponential random variable with parameter 1. Thus,
N(t)
grows exponentially.

Note that the dynamics of the component
\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)
are as follows:

(a)

A new vertex enters the system with two edges and then merges another component
\mathscr{C}\in\mathbf{C}(t)
with
\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)
. Thus,
\mathscr{C}\neq\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)
merges with
\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)
leading to the transition
|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|\leadsto|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|+|\mathscr{C}|+1
at rate
2\pi^{2}|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)||\mathscr{C}|/N(t).

(b)

A new vertex enters the system with at least one edge and all its edges connect to
\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)
leading to the transition
|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|\leadsto|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|+1
, which occurs at rate
\pi^{2}|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|^{2}/N(t)+2\pi(1-\pi)|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|.

(c)

A new vertex enters with no edges connected to it; this leads to no change in the size of
\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)
.

Denote the generator of the associated continuous-time Markov process by
\mathcal{L}
, and, for
k\geq 2
, define the
k^{\rm th}
susceptibility in continuous time as

(3.9)

S^{\pi}_{k}(t):=\frac{1}{N(t)}\sum_{\mathscr{C}\in\mathbf{C}(t)}|\mathscr{C}|^{k}=\frac{1}{N(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{N(t)}|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{i}(t)|^{k-1},\quad t\geq 0.

Then

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathscr{C}\in\mathbf{C}(t)\colon\mathscr{C}\neq\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)}\frac{2\pi^{2}}{N(t)}|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)||\mathscr{C}|(|\mathscr{C}|+1)+\left(\pi^{2}\frac{|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|^{2}}{N(t)}+2\pi(1-\pi)|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|\right)

\displaystyle=2\pi^{2}|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|\left(S^{\pi}_{2}(t)-\frac{|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|^{2}}{N(t)}\right)+2\pi^{2}|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|\left(1-\frac{|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|}{N(t)}\right)+\left(\pi^{2}\frac{|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|^{2}}{N(t)}+2\pi(1-\pi)|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(2\pi^{2}S^{\pi}_{2}(t)+2\pi\right)|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|.

As a result, for
t\geq 0
, the process

(3.11)

M(t)=|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}[2\pi^{2}S^{\pi}_{2}(u)+2\pi]~\mathrm{d}u\right)

is a non-negative super-martingale, and thus converges almost surely to a finite random variable
M(\infty)
. In order to gain insight into the asymptotics of
|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|
, we need to understand the asymptotics of
t\mapsto S^{\pi}_{2}(t)
, which we explain next.

Susceptibilities.

Define the
k^{\rm th}
susceptibility functions in discrete time by

(3.12)

s^{\pi}_{k}(n)=s_{k}(n)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\mathscr{C}\subseteq\mathbf{C}(n)}|\mathscr{C}|^{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{v}(n)|^{k-1}.

Note that the discrete and continuous susceptibilities are related through
s^{\pi}_{k}(N(t))=S^{\pi}_{k}(t)
. From the above,
s^{\pi}_{2}(n)
can be viewed as the expected component size of a uniformly chosen vertex in
G^{\pi}_{n}
given
G^{\pi}_{n}
. Although this is a ‘local’ quantity, our proofs will show that this quantity is also key to the asymptotics of ‘global’ quantities like (maximal) component sizes.

Theorem 3.3
(Convergence of susceptibility
[
8
]
).

Fix
a=0
and
m=2
. For any
\pi<\pi_{c}
,
s^{\pi}_{2}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)
, where

(3.13)

s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty):=\frac{(1-4\pi)-\sqrt{8\pi^{2}-8\pi+1}}{4\pi^{2}}.

We explain the proof of Theorem
3.3
below. What is remarkable, is that the susceptibility
s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)
in (
3.13
) remains bounded
for all

\pi<\pi_{c}
, with a finite limit as
\pi\nearrow\pi_{c}
. This is unlike related percolation settings, where the expected component size blows up as
\pi\nearrow\pi_{c}
, and as such is indicative of a different percolation universality class.

Convergence of components in Theorem
3.2
: martingales.

Recall (
3.11
). Since
s^{\pi}_{2}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)
, and
S^{\pi}_{k}(t)=s^{\pi}_{k}(N(t))
with
N(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty
, it follows that also
S^{\pi}_{k}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)
. Thus, almost surely,

\int_{0}^{t}[2\pi^{2}S^{\pi}_{2}(u)+2\pi]~\mathrm{d}u=t(2\pi^{2}s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)+2\pi)+o(t)=t\alpha(\pi)+o(t),

by (
3.13
) and (
3.7
). As a result,
|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|={\mathrm{e}}^{t\alpha(\pi)+o(t)}
. Together with
|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|
in continuous time being equal to
|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(N(t))|
in discrete time, and the fact that
N(t)={\mathrm{e}}^{t+o(t)}
, this explain why
|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(n)|=n^{\alpha(p)+o(1)}
. To improve this to almost sure convergence of
n\rightarrow|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(n)|n^{-\alpha(p)}
, we improve the results to show that
n^{\gamma}(s^{\pi}_{2}(n)-s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
for some small
\gamma>0
, so that
\int_{0}^{t}(S^{\pi}_{2}(u)-s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty))~\mathrm{d}u
converges almost surely as well. To prove that
\zeta_{1}>0
almost surely, instead, we consider a martingale
M^{\prime}(t)=|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{1}(t)|^{-1}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}[2\pi^{2}S^{\pi}_{2}(u)+2\pi+R(u)]~\mathrm{d}u\right)
, for some appropriate error term
u\mapsto R(u)
. Since the non-negative martingale
M^{\prime}(t)
converges almost surely,
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(M^{\prime}(\infty)=\infty)=0
, from which it follows that
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(M(\infty)=0)=0
.

Convergence of susceptibilities in Theorem
3.3
: stochastic approximations.

The evolution of the process
(s^{\pi}_{2}(n))_{n\geq 1}
in discrete time can be written as

(3.14)

s^{\pi}_{2}(n+1)-s^{\pi}_{2}(n)=\frac{1}{n+1}\left[F(s^{\pi}_{2}(n))+R_{n}+\xi_{n+1}\right],\qquad n\geq 1,

where the function
F
equals
F(s)=2\pi^{2}s^{2}+(4\pi-1)s+1
, the error term
R_{n}
satisfies
|R_{n}|\leq Ks^{\pi}_{4}(n)/n
for a positive constant
K
, and the martingale differences
(\xi_{n+1})_{n\geq 1}
given by
\xi_{n+1}=(n+1)\big(s^{\pi}_{2}(n+1)-\mathbb{E}[s^{\pi}_{2}(n+1)\,|\,G^{\pi}_{n}]\big)
satisfy
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{n+1}^{2}\,|\,G^{\pi}_{n}]\leq K(s^{\pi}_{3}(n))^{2}
. The stochastic recursion in (
3.14
) follows by an analysis similar to the one in (
3.10
), applied to the sum of squares of component sizes rather than that of a single vertex. For this, we add vertices one by one, and inspect the evolution of the sum of squares of the components. When two edges are added, they merge components
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
to become a component of size
|\mathscr{C}|+|\mathscr{C}^{\prime}|+1
with probability
2|\mathscr{C}||\mathscr{C}^{\prime}|/n^{2}
. Summing out over all pairs of components
\mathscr{C},\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
explains why the evolution is
quadratic
. The precise shape of
s\mapsto F(s)
follows by carefully analysing all terms.

To analyse the asymptotics of the stochastic evolution of
s^{\pi}_{2}(n)
, we note that we can write

F(s)=b(s-\lambda_{1})(s-\lambda_{2}),

where
b=2\pi^{2}
,
\lambda_{1}=[1-4\pi-\sqrt{8\pi^{2}-8\pi+1}]/[4\pi^{2}]=s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)
, and
\lambda_{2}=[1-4\pi+\sqrt{8\pi^{2}-8\pi+1}]/[4\pi^{2}]>\lambda_{1}.
The values
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
correspond to the
fixed points
of the dynamics in (
3.14
). Since
F^{\prime}(s)=2bs-b(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})
, we have that
F^{\prime}(\lambda_{1})<0
and
F^{\prime}(\lambda_{2})>0
, so that
\lambda_{1}
is the stable fixed point, while
\lambda_{2}
is the unstable one. As such, it comes as no surprise that the dynamics drives
s^{\pi}_{2}(n)
to the stable fixed point
\lambda_{1}=s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty),
which explains Theorem
3.3
. The proof follows by a careful analysis of the evolution, using as a starting point that
s^{\pi}_{2}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)
by a local convergence argument, combined with a uniform integrability argument in
[
43
, Chapter 8]
. This argument identifies
s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)
also as the expected component size of the root of percolation on the uniform attachment version of the Pólya point tree, which is the local limit of our model. Since
s^{\pi}_{2}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)
, for every
\eta>0
,
n\mapsto s^{\pi}_{2}(n)
must enter the
\eta
neighbourhood of
s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)
infinitely often. Each time, the evolution has a strictly positive probability of never leaving this
\eta
neighbourhood, so eventually it has to succeed.

An essential ingredient in the proof is a preliminary estimate that shows that
|\mathscr{C}^{\pi}_{\max}(n)|/\sqrt{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
. Such a bound is crucial, since it shows that the process
n\mapsto s^{\pi}_{2}(n)
makes small steps, so that it cannot jump far when it is close to
s^{\pi}_{2}(\infty)
.

4
Concluding comments.

In this paper, we discussed recent progress on percolation on random graphs, showing that there are many universality classes. We focussed on the
sizes
of the components, thus ignoring their metric structure. Much progress has been made on the metric structure of critical rank-1 random graphs. See
[
1
,
2
,
9
,
14
,
17
,
24
,
28
,
13
,
37
]
and the references therein. We again refer to
[
44
, Part III]
, as well as
[
3
]
, for extensive overviews.

Many problems remain, and I expect them to continue attracting attention in the coming years. Of course, the story of near-critical
dynamic
random graphs is far from complete, and we do not really understand why they behave so differently compared to the rank-1 settings. So far,
spatial
random graphs at criticality have not yet attracted much attention, in part because the method of choice in spatial mean-field-like systems, the lace expansion (see e.g.,
[
40
]
for an extensive introduction), is quite technical, and would need considerable adaptation in order to be able to deal with the significant
inhomogeneity
and
dependence
present in most spatial random graphs.
