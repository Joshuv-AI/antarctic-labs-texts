Abstract
. Let K K be a number field and f 1 , … , f s ∈ K ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x n ] f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}] forms of odd degrees. In 1957, Birch proved that if n n is sufficiently large then the forms always have a nontrivial zero in K n K^{n} . Apart from some small degrees, the number of variables required was so large that it has been described as ”not even astronomical”. We prove that for any fixed degree, n n may be taken as a polynomial in s s . We deduce this from a stronger result – the Zariski closure of the set of rational zeros has codimension bounded by a polynomial in s s . When K K is totally imaginary, our results hold for forms of any (possibly even) degrees.



1.
Introduction

Let
K
be a number field and let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be forms (i.e. homogeneous polynomials) of
odd
degrees
\leq d
in
n
variables. We say that the system
\underline{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})

has a solution
if there exists
0\neq x\in K^{n}
which satisfies
f_{i}(x)=0
for all
1\leq i\leq s
. In
[
2
]
, Birch proved that if
n\geq N(d,s)
then any such
\underline{f}
has a solution. The proof relied on an elegant but highly iterative inductive argument which yielded extremely weak bounds.

We slightly abuse notation by now writing
N(d,s)
for the
minimal
integer for which Birch’s theorem holds. Much work has been done on improving the bounds for
N(d,s)
. We mention just a few landmark results for
K=\mathbb{Q}
. The most well-understood case is that of a single cubic form. In a remarkable series of papers, Davenport first proved that
N(3,1)\leq 32

[
7
]
, then reduced this to 29
[
8
]
and finally to 16
[
9
]
. This last record held for over forty years, until it was shattered by Heath-Brown
[
13
]
who showed that 14 variables are sufficient. On the other hand, it is known that at least 10 variables are required.

For systems of cubics, Schmidt proved
[
22
]
that
N(3,s)\leq(10s)^{5}
. The dependence on
s
was subsequently improved to quartic by Dietmann
[
10
]
. For systems of quintic forms, the best bound available is due to Wooley
[
25
]
and is approximately
c^{s^{6}}
for a very large constant
c
. For systems of forms of larger odd degrees, the only explicit bounds available are of recursive type and are also due to Wooley
[
26
]
. They are so large that Wooley describes them as ”not even astronomical”.

Our main result is that
N(d,s)
is bounded by a polynomial in
s
, for
all

d
. In fact, we prove substantially more. Writing
Z=(f_{1}=\ldots=f_{s}=0)\subset\mathbf{A}^{n}
, we have:

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
d
there exist constants
A=A_{\ref{thm:main-num}}(d),B=B_{\ref{thm:main-num}}(d)
such that
\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}\overline{Z(K)}<As^{B}
, where the closure is taken in the Zariski topology. In particular,
N(d,s)\leq As^{B}
.

In our discussion so far, the assumption that the
f_{i}
have odd degrees was a natural way to avoid positive definite forms such as
x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{n}^{2}
over
\mathbb{Q}
. When
K
is totally imaginary (for example
K=\mathbb{Q}[i]
) this is no longer an issue. In this setting, we will see that theorem
1.1
applies for even degrees as well. Actually, it will hold for a much larger class of fields.

Definition 1.2
.

K
is called a
Brauer field
if for any
d\geq 1
there exists an integer
\phi=\phi_{d}
such that
a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\ldots+a_{\phi}x_{\phi}^{d}
has a solution, for any
a_{1},\ldots,a_{\phi}\in K
.

Birch proved that
p
-adic fields are Brauer
[
4
]
and Peck proved this for totally imaginary number fields
[
21
]
. See
[
1
]
for many more examples of Brauer fields. Brauer famously proved
[
5
]
that for every Brauer field
K
there exists
n_{0}(d,s)
such that any collection of forms
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degrees
\leq d
with
n\geq n_{0}
has a solution. As in Birch’s theorem, the proof was based on a highly iterative induction and yielded very weak bounds for
n_{0}(d,s)
. This remained the state of affairs for many years until Leep and Schmidt
[
19
]
introduced an efficient modification of Brauer’s inductive argument, obtaining a value of
n_{0}(d,s)
that was polynomial in the parameters
s,\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{d}
. Wooley refined their inductive scheme to yield a further improvement in
[
27
]
. For Brauer fields we obtain an improvement analogous to theorem
1.1
.

Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any
d
there exist constants
A=A_{\ref{thm:main-Brauer}}(d),B=B_{\ref{thm:main-Brauer}}(d)
such that the following holds. If
K
is a Brauer field of characteristic zero and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are forms of degree
\leq d
then

\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}\overline{Z(K)}\leq A(s+\phi_{2}+\ldots+\phi_{d})^{B}.

In particular, by a bound from
[
27
]
, if
K
is a totally imaginary number field we get

\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}\overline{Z(K)}\leq A(s+e^{2d+1})^{B}\leq A^{\prime}s^{B}.

Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
will follow from relative regularization combined with the next two results (we define all of the relevant notions in the next section).

Theorem 1.4
.

For any
d
there exist constants
C=C_{\ref{thm:dense}}(d),D=D_{\ref{thm:dense}}(d)
such that the following holds. If
K
is a Brauer field of characteristic zero and
\mathcal{F}
is absolutely
(C,D,\phi_{2}+\ldots+\phi_{d})
-strong then the
K
-points of
Z(\mathcal{F})
are Zariski dense. The same conclusion holds when
K
is a number field,
\mathcal{F}
is composed of forms of odd degrees and is absolutely
(C,D,1)
-strong.

It is instructive to compare this with results of a somewhat similar spirit which are obtained via the Hardy-Littlewood circle method. For systems of sufficiently nonsingular forms, asymptotic formulas were obtained for the number of integer solutions in
[
3
,
24
,
6
,
12
]
. Schmidt
[
23
]
used this to deduce lower bounds for the asymptotic point count of integer solutions for general systems of equations, but with very weak dependence on
d,s
. Our hypothesis on
\mathcal{F}
is substantially more flexible than the one required in the cited results, and we will see in proposition
2.4
how this flexibility allows us to obtain such dramatic improvements for the bounds of theorems
1.1
and
1.3
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Given any
C,D,d
there exist constants
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}_{\ref{thm:reg}}(d,A,B),D^{\prime}=D^{\prime}_{\ref{thm:reg}}(d,A,B)
such that the following holds. If
K
is a field of characteristic zero and
\mathcal{F}
is a tower of forms in
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
(1,\ldots,d)
which is
(C^{\prime},D^{\prime},r)
-strong then it is absolutely
(C,D,r)
-strong.

In the next section we quickly deduce theorems
1.1
and
1.3
from these two results. After that we collect some geometric properties of regular towers and prove theorem
1.4
. Then we turn to theorem
1.5
, the technical crux of the paper.

2.
Deduction of theorems
1.1
and
1.3

In this section we assume that theorems
1.4
and
1.5
hold. We begin with the definition of strength, introduced by Schmidt
[
23
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

The
strength
of a form
f\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is

\textnormal{str}(f)=\inf\{r:f=g_{1}h_{1}+\ldots+g_{r}h_{r}\},

where
g_{i},h_{i}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are forms of lower degree. Note that linear forms have infinite strength. More generally, the strength of a collection of forms
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
of the same degree is

\textnormal{str}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})=\min\{\textnormal{str}(a_{1}f_{1}+\ldots+a_{s}f_{s}):a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\in K\textnormal{ not all zero}\}.

The
absolute strength
of
f
, denoted
\overline{\textnormal{str}}(f)
, is its strength in
\overline{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, where
\overline{K}
is an algebraic closure of
K
.

The above definition generalizes to any standard graded algebra
R=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/I
, where
I
is a homogeneous ideal. This generalization was first explored in
[
16
]
, where it was called relative rank.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a form
f\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree
d
, its
relative strength
is

\textnormal{str}_{I}(f)=\inf\{r:f=g_{1}h_{1}+\ldots+g_{r}h_{r}\mod I\},

where
g_{i},h_{i}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are forms of lower degree. When
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are forms of the same degree their collective relative strength is

\textnormal{str}_{I}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})=\min\{\textnormal{str}(a_{1}f_{1}+\ldots+a_{s}f_{s}):a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\in K\textnormal{ not all zero}\}.

In
[
23
,
20
]
, it is shown that good properties hold for collections of forms which have strength larger than some polynomial expression in the number of forms involved and an error parameter. For relative strength we will work with a similar hypothesis, described in the following definition.

Definition 2.3
.

(1)

A
tower

\mathcal{F}
of degree
\underline{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{h})
in
R
is a collection of forms
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{i})_{i\in[h]}
where each
\mathcal{F}_{i}=(f_{i,j})_{j\in[s_{i}]}
is a tuple of homogeneous elements of
R
of degree
d_{i}.
For
i\in[h]
we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{<i}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\leq i}
the tower obtained by keeping only those forms with
i^{\prime}<i
or
i^{\prime}\leq i,
respectively.

(2)

We say that the tower
\mathcal{F}
is
(A,B,r)
-
strong
if for each
i\in[h]
we have

\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}_{<i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})>A(s_{i}+\ldots+s_{h}+r)^{B},

where
\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}_{<i}}
denotes the strength in the quotient
R/(\mathcal{F}_{<i})
. It is
absolutely

(A,B,r)
-strong if
\mathcal{F}\otimes\overline{K}
is
(A,B,r)
-strong.

A key step in various applications of strength (e.g.
[
23
,
1
]
) is a regularization process where an arbitrary collection of forms is replaced by a high strength collection which has some desirable property. This regularization process is extremely inefficient, leading to bounds of highly recursive type in any result where it is used. In
[
16
]
it was shown that relative strength retains many of the good properties of strength, while also naturally lending itself to an
efficient
regularization process, allowing one to replace an arbitrary initial collection of forms
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
with a strong tower of size
polynomial
in
s.

Proposition 2.4
.

Given any
C,D,d
there exist constants
E=E(C,D,d),F=F(C,D,d)
such that the following holds. For any homogeneous
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
of positive degrees
\leq d
there exists a
(C,D,r)
-strong tower
\mathcal{F}
of degree
(1,\ldots,d)
and size
s_{1}+\ldots+s_{h}\leq E(s+r)^{F}
such that the ideals satisfy the containment
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})\subset(\mathcal{F}).
Moreover, if the
f_{i}
’s all have odd degrees then we can choose
\mathcal{F}
to be composed only of homogeneous elements of odd degree.

Proof.

We begin with an initial tower
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}
obtained by placing
f_{i}
in the
\deg(f_{i})
layer. To each layer
i\in[d]
we assign a number
n_{i}
by a formula we’ll soon describe. Now we regularize: If for each
i\in[d]
we have
\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}_{<i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})>Cn_{i}^{D}
then we’re done.

Otherwise, there exists some
i
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s_{i}}\in K
not all zero with

(1)

\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}a_{j}f_{i,j}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}p_{k}q_{k}\mod(\mathcal{F}_{<i}),

where
t\leq Cn_{i}^{D}.
Assume without loss of generality that
a_{1}\neq 0
and replace
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}
by the tower
\mathcal{F}^{(k+1)}
obtained by deleting
f_{i,1}
and by adding each
p_{k}
to the layer of degree
\deg(p_{k})<i.
This process must eventually terminate, yielding
\mathcal{F}^{(k_{0})}=\mathcal{F}^{(\infty)}
which is
(C,D)
-strong and
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})\subset(\mathcal{F}).
Now we give the formula for
n_{i}
in terms of
m_{i},
the original number of forms in each degree,

n_{d}=m_{d}\ ,\ n_{i}=m_{i}+Cn_{i+1}^{D+1}.

Note that
|n_{i}|\geq\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\geq i}^{(k)}.
This can be seen by downward induction on
i
, where the base case
i=d
is clear. Assuming it holds for
i+1,
we get that each of the
n_{i+1}
forms appearing in degree
i+1
and above can contribute at most
Cn_{i+1}^{D}
forms of degree
i,
proving the claim for degree
i.
It’s easy to see that all of the
n_{i}
are polynomial in
s
and so
s_{1}+\ldots+s_{h}\leq n_{1}\leq Es^{F}
as desired. For the statement about odd degrees, note that in equation (
1
) either
p_{k}
or
q_{k}
must have odd degree, so assume without loss of generality that
p_{k}
has odd degree.
∎

Proof of theorems
1.1
and
1.3
.

We first prove theorem
1.3
. Let
C,D
be the constants of theorem
1.4
and let
C^{\prime},D^{\prime}
be the corresponding constants of theorem
1.5
. Apply proposition
2.4
to replace
\underline{f}
by a
(C^{\prime},D^{\prime},\phi_{2}+\ldots+\phi_{d})
-strong tower
\mathcal{F}
of size at most
E(s+\phi_{2}+\ldots+\phi_{d})^{F}.
By theorem
1.5
,
\mathcal{F}
is absolutely
(C,D,\phi_{2}+\ldots+\phi_{d})
-strong. Let
Z=(f_{1}=\ldots=f_{s}=0)
and
Z^{\prime}=(\mathcal{F}=0).
By theorem
1.4
,
\overline{Z^{\prime}(K)}=Z^{\prime}
, which implies

\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}\overline{Z(K)}\leq\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}\overline{Z^{\prime}(K)}=\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}\overline{Z^{\prime}}\leq E(s+\phi_{2}+\ldots+\phi_{d})^{F}.

This proves theorem
1.3
(with
A=E,B=F
).
The proof of theorem
1.1
is identical, except that proposition
2.4
is applied for odd degrees.
∎

3.
Preliminaries

In this section we collect various results required for the proof of theorem
1.4
.

3.1.
Relative strength and singularities

In
[
3
]
, Birch introduced the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

The
Birch rank
of a form
f
is
\textnormal{Brk}(f)=\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}(x:\nabla f(x)=0).
More generally, the Birch rank of a collection of forms
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
is

\textnormal{Brk}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})=\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}(x:\nabla f_{1}(x),\ldots,\nabla f_{s}(x)\textnormal{ are linearly dependent}).

Birch rank and absolute strength are closely related by the following result.

Lemma 3.2

(Kazhdan-Lampert-Polishchuk
[
14
]
)
.

\frac{\textnormal{Brk}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})}{2}\leq\overline{\textnormal{str}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})\leq(d-1)(\textnormal{Brk}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})+s-1)

A corresponding result holds for relative strength. Before stating it, we note some simple consequences of the definition of strong towers.

Claim 3.3
.

(1)

Given
A,B
there exist
C=C(A,B),D=D(A,B)
such that if
\mathcal{F}
is
(C,D,r)
-strong then it is
(A(r+s_{h})^{B},B,A(r+s_{h})^{B})
-strong.

(2)

If
\mathcal{F}
is
(A,B,r+|\mathcal{G}|)
-strong and
\mathcal{G}\mod(\mathcal{F})
is
(A,B,r)
-strong then the tower
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})
is
(A,B,r)
-strong (the notation indicates that the layers of
\mathcal{G}
are placed above those of
\mathcal{F}
).

Proof.

The second statement follows immediately from the definitions. For the first statement, since

\displaystyle A(r+s_{h})^{B}(s_{i}+\ldots+s_{h}+A(r+s_{h})^{B})^{B}

\displaystyle\leq A(r+s_{h})^{B}(2A(s_{i}+\ldots+s_{h}+r)^{B})^{B}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{B}A^{B+1}(s_{i}+\ldots+s_{h}+r)^{B^{2}+B},

we can take
C=2^{B}A^{B+1}
and
D=B^{2}+B
.
∎

Relative strength and singularities are closely related as follows.

Lemma 3.4

(Lampert
[
17
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is absolutely
(A,B,r)
-strong then

\textnormal{codim}_{Z(\mathcal{F}_{<h})}Z(\mathcal{F}_{<h})\cap S(\mathcal{F})>r,

where
S(\mathcal{F})=(x:\nabla f_{i,j}\textnormal{ are linearly dependent})
.

As a consequence, we have:

Lemma 3.5
.

There exist
C,D
such that if
\mathcal{F}
is absolutely
(C,D,1)
-strong then
Z(\mathcal{F})
is a complete intersection and
(\mathcal{F})
is prime.

The proof of the above lemma relies on a standard criterion for primeness of ideals (see
[
11
, Theorem 18.15 and Proposition 18.13]
).

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose that
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
are polynomials which cut out a complete intersection
Z=(f_{1}=\ldots=f_{s}=0)\subset\mathbf{A}^{n}
and let
R=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
be its coordinate ring. If
\textnormal{codim}_{Z}Z\cap S(\underline{f})>1
then
R
is a direct product of domains.

Corollary 3.7
.

Under the hypotheses of the previous lemma, if
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
are homogeneous, then
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
is prime.

Proof.

By the lemma,
R
is a direct product of domains. Since it is also a graded ring over a field, it must be a domain.
∎

Proof of lemma
3.5
.

The proof is by induction on
h
, where the base case
h=0
is trivial. Suppose the statement holds for towers of height
<h
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a tower of height
h
. By claim
3.3
, we may assume that
\mathcal{F}
is
(A,B,s_{h}+1)
-regular, after perhaps increasing
C,D
.

We first prove that
Z(\mathcal{F})
is a complete intersection.
Suppose, to get a contradiction, that
X\subseteq Z(\mathcal{F})
is an irreducible component of codimension
<s.
Then
X\subseteq S
. Writing
Z=Z(\mathcal{F})
and
Z_{<h}=Z(\mathcal{F}_{<h})
, We obtain the contradiction

s_{h}+1<\textnormal{codim}_{Z_{<h}}S\cap Z_{<h}\leq\textnormal{codim}_{Z_{<h}}X<s_{h}.

Now we move on to primeness of
(\mathcal{F})
. By lemma
3.4
we have

\textnormal{codim}_{Z}Z\cap S=\textnormal{codim}_{Z_{<h}}Z\cap S-s_{h}\geq\textnormal{codim}_{Z_{<h}}Z_{<h}\cap S-s_{h}>1.

By corollary
3.7
,
(\mathcal{F})
is indeed prime.
∎

3.2.
Taylor expansion and restriction to subspaces

We now recall the multi-homogeneous Taylor expansion for forms, which will be used to investigate their restriction to subspaces.

Definition 3.8
.

The
Taylor expansion
of a degree
d
form
f
is given by

f(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{m})=\sum_{|e|=d}f^{e}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}),

where each
f^{e}
is multi-homogeneous of multi-degree
e
.

Lemma 3.9
.

The terms of the Taylor expansion satisfy

f^{e}(x,\ldots,x)=\binom{d}{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}}f(x).

Proof.

Compute the coefficient of
t_{1}^{e_{1}}\ldots t_{m}^{e_{m}}
in the expression
f(t_{1}x+\ldots+t_{m}x)
in two different ways. On the one hand,
f(t_{1}x+\ldots+t_{m}x)=(t_{1}+\ldots+t_{m})^{d}f(x)
so this coefficient is
\binom{d}{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}}f(x)
. On the other hand,

f(t_{1}x+\ldots+t_{m}x)=\sum_{|\alpha|=d}f^{\alpha}(t_{1}x,\ldots,t_{m}x)=\sum_{|\alpha|=d}t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots t_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}f^{\alpha}(x,\ldots,x),

so this coefficient equals
f^{e}(x,\ldots,x)
.
∎

Definition 3.10
.

Let
V=K^{n}
and
\mathcal{F}
a tower in
K[V]
. For any positive integer
m
we have a tower
T_{m}(\mathcal{F})
in
K[V^{m}]
where the elements of
(T_{m}(\mathcal{F}))_{i}
are given by the terms of the Taylor expansion of the elements of
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Note that the forms in
T_{m}(\mathcal{F})_{i}
have the same degree as those in
\mathcal{F}_{i}
.

Lemma 3.11
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is
(Am^{dB},B,r)
-strong then
T_{m}(\mathcal{F})
is
(A,B,r)
-strong.

Proof.

The number of forms in
(T_{m}(\mathcal{F}))_{i}
is
\leq m^{d}s_{i}
, so it’s enough to show that the relative strength is at least that of
\mathcal{F}
. If
\mathcal{F}_{i}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
let
g=\sum_{j\in[s],|e|=d_{i}}a_{j,e}f^{e}_{j}
be a nontrivial linear combination with
\textnormal{str}_{T_{m}(\mathcal{F})_{<i}}(g)=r.
For
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})\neq 0,
consider the diagonal embedding given by
x\mapsto(\lambda_{1}x,\ldots,\lambda_{m}x).
By lemma
3.9
we have
f^{e}(\lambda_{1}x,\ldots,\lambda_{m}x)=\binom{d}{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}}\lambda^{e}f(x)
and thus plugging this in to the relative rank of
g
gives us

\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}_{<i}}\left(\sum_{j}f_{j}(x)\sum_{e}a_{j,e}\binom{d}{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}}\lambda^{e}\right)\leq r.

Our assumption that
g
is a nontrivial linear combination implies that for some
j
not all
a_{j,e}
are zero. Therefore, there exists a choice of
\lambda
such that
\sum_{e}a_{j,e}\binom{d}{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}}\lambda^{e}
is not zero, which implies that
\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}_{<i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq r
as desired.
∎

For convenience, we write
Z_{m}(\mathcal{F})
for
Z(T_{m}(\mathcal{F}))
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
m\geq s_{h}+2,
and suppose
\mathcal{F}
is absolutely
(Cm^{dD},D,1)
-strong. Then the variety

X=\left((a,\underline{v})\in\mathbf{A}^{m}\times V^{m}:\underline{v}\in Z_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h}),\ a_{1}v_{1}+\ldots+a_{m}v_{m}\in Z(\mathcal{F}_{h})\right)

is an irreducible complete intersection of codimension
|T_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})|+s_{h}
.

Proof.

By the previous lemma,
T_{m}(\mathcal{F})
is absolutely
(C,D,1)
-strong. For fixed
0\neq a\in\mathbf{A}^{m}
, note that the
s_{h}
forms
\mathcal{F}_{h}(a\cdot\underline{v})
are a linearly independent subset of
T_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{h})
. Thus, the equations for the fiber

are absolutely
(C,D,1)
-strong and hence by lemma

this fiber is an irreducible complete intersection. The remaining fiber over zero has codimension
|T_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})|+m\geq|T_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})|+s_{h}
, so that
X
is indeed a complete intersection of the claimed codimension.

Now we prove that
X
is irreducible. Let By corollary
3.7
, this will follow once we show that
\textnormal{codim}X\cap S>|T_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})|+s_{h}+1
, where
S
is the set where the differentials of the defining equations for
X
are linearly depdendent. The fiber over zero is
Z_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h}))
and has codimension
m+|T_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})|>|T_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})|+s_{h}+1
, since
Z_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})
is a complete intersection by lemma
3.5
. For any
a\neq 0
, the fiber
X(a)\cap S(a)
is contained in
Z_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})\cap S(T_{m}(\mathcal{F}))
. By claim

, for
C(\underline{d}),D(\underline{d})
sufficiently large we have that
T_{m}(\mathcal{F})
is absolutely
(A,B,m+s_{h}+1)
-strong and hence
\textnormal{codim}Z_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})\cap S(T_{m}(\mathcal{F}))>|T_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})|+m+s_{h}+1
. Taking the union over the fibers

, we get
\textnormal{codim}X\cap S>|T_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})|+s_{h}+1
, completing the proof.
∎

We also need the fact that a generic tuple of forms has large absolute strength.

Lemma 3.13
.

For an open dense set of tuples of forms
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
of degree
d
in
m\geq d
variables we have
\overline{\textnormal{str}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})>\frac{m-s}{d2^{d}}.

Proof.

This is an effective version of
[
15
, Proposition 2.7]
. Let
r=\frac{m-s}{d2^{d}}
. First, note that the set of
f
with absolute strength
\leq r
is parametrized by
r
pairs of forms
g_{i},h_{i}
of degrees
a_{i},b_{i}<d
such that
a_{i}+b_{i}=d
. For a fixed sequence
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}<d
, the dimension of this parameter space is

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left[\binom{m+a_{i}-1}{a_{i}}+\binom{m+b_{i}-1}{b_{i}}\right]\leq r\left[\binom{m+d-2}{d-1}+m\right].

For fixed
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\in P^{s-1}
, the codimension of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
such that
a_{1}f_{1}+\ldots+a_{s}f_{s}
has absolute strength
\leq r
is thus
\geq\binom{m+d-1}{d}-r(\binom{m+d-2}{d-1}+m).
By a union bound, the codimension of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
with
\overline{\textnormal{str}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})\leq r
is at least

\displaystyle\binom{m+d-1}{d}-r\left(\binom{m+d-2}{d-1}+m\right)-(s-1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m^{d}}{d!}-2r\frac{(m+d-2)^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}-(s-1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m^{d}}{d!}-r\frac{2^{d}m^{d-1}}{(d-1)!}-(s-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{sm^{d-1}}{d!}-(s-1)\geq 1,

as desired.
∎

4.
Proof of theorem

The proof of theorem

is by induction on

the number of layers in
\mathcal{F}.

4.1.
Base case
h=1

For Brauer fields, we rely on the following result from
[

]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exist constants

such that if

is a Brauer field,

all have degree

\textnormal{Brk}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})>A(s+\phi_{2}+\ldots+\phi_{d})^{B}

Corollary 4.2
.

Theorem
1.4
holds over Brauer fields for towers of height one.

Proof of corollary

.

This is an immediate consequence of the above proposition and lemma

.
∎

For number fields, we begin with the following well-known fact.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are forms of odd degrees and

is an irreducible complete intersection, then the real points are Zariski dense in

Proof.

The Zariski closure

has dimension at least

.
∎

Our main tool is a result of Skinner on weak approximation. First we recall the definition.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
K
be a number field and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
forms. We say that

if

and the diagonal embedding

has dense image, where the product is over the completion of

at all places

.

Now we can state Skinner’s result
[

, Corollary 1]
.

Proposition 4.5

(Skinner)
.

Suppose that
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
all have degree

and that

\textnormal{Brk}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})>s(s+1)(d-1)2^{d-1}.

Suppose furthermore that

-point for every place

. Then weak approximation holds for

.

.

Theorem
1.4
holds over number fields for towers of height one.

Proof of corollary
4.6
.

Choosing

sufficiently large in the hypothesis of theorem

, lemma
3.2
guarantees that

and also that

is an irreducible complete intersection by lemma

. Now, by lemma

, it is enough to prove that weak approximation holds for

, this will follow once we show that the

-points of

are Zariski dense for every place

.

For finite places, Zariski-density of the

for Brauer fields, together with the fact that

for any finite extension of

(see

).

∎

4.2.
The inductive step

Now that we have established theorem
1.4
for towers of height one, we move on to the inductive step. We will use the following standard fact.

Lemma 4.7
.

If
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are forms which comprise a regular sequence then the map
\underline{f}:\overline{K}^{n}\to\overline{K}^{s}
is surjective.

Proof.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
be scalars and consider the forms
g_{i}=f_{i}-a_{i}z^{d_{i}},
where
z
is a new variable and
d_{i}
is the degree of
f_{i}
. Then
z,g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}
is a regular sequence of forms, hence a regular sequence in any order, so
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s},z
is also a regular sequence. In particular,
z
doesn’t vanish identically on
Z(g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}).
If
(x,z)
is a point with
g_{i}(x,z)=0
and
z\neq 0
then we have
f_{i}(x/z)=a_{i}
as desired.
∎

The inductive step relies on the following proposition.

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose
m\geq\max(d,s_{h}+2)
,
\mathcal{F}
is absolutely
(Am^{dB},B,1)
-strong and
U\subseteq Z(\mathcal{F})
is a nonempty open subset. Then there is a dense open
W\subseteq Z_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})
such that, for all
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})\in W
, the subspace

satisfies

(1)

\overline{\textnormal{str}}(\mathcal{F}_{h}\restriction_{V_{0}})>\frac{m-s_{h}}{d2^{d}}
and

(2)

Z(\mathcal{F}_{h})\cap U\cap V_{0}\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

By lemmas
3.11
and
3.5
,
Z_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})
is irreducible. Thus, it suffices to verify that each of the conditions holds separately on a nonempty open set. By possibly shrinking
U
, we may assume that
U=(x\in Z(\mathcal{F}):g(x)\neq 0)
for some polynomial

. The variety

X=\left((a,\underline{v})\in\mathbf{A}^{m}\times V^{m}:\underline{v}\in Z_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h}),\ a_{1}v_{1}+\ldots+a_{m}v_{m}\in Z(\mathcal{F}_{h})\right)

is irreducible by lemma

. Note also that

surjects onto

by projection on

and that the polynomial

does not vanish identically on

, hence it cuts down the dimension. It follows that for a dense open set of
\underline{v}\in Z_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h}),

doesn’t vanish identically on the fiber

which is exactly the second condition.

For the first condition, use the fact that
T_{m}(\mathcal{F})
is a regular sequence by lemmas
3.11
and
3.5
. By lemma
4.7
this implies that the corresponding polynomial map is surjective. In particular,
T_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{h})\restriction_{Z_{m}(\mathcal{F}_{<h})}
is surjective. By lemma

, this implies that the first condition holds on a dense open subset of

.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

Let

and suppose that theorem

.

Proof.

Let

be such that theorem

holds with these constants for all towers of degree

and height

. Apply the previous proposition with either

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=d2^{d}(A(s_{h}+\phi_{2}+\ldots+\phi_{d})^{B}+s_{h})\textnormal{ or}

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=d2^{d}(As_{h}^{B}+s_{h}).

By claim

, there exist

such that if

is absolutely

-strong then it is absolutely

-strong. The tower

has degree

and is

∎

5.
Properties of multi-linear forms

We now get to work in earnest, building towards our main technical result – theorem
1.5
. We begin by recalling the standard correspondence between polynomials and multi-linear forms.

Definition 5.1
.

For a homogeneous polynomial
f
of degree
d
, its
polarization
is the multi-linear form
\tilde{f}:V^{d}\to K
given by
\tilde{f}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})=f^{(1,\ldots,1)}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})
.

Note that this correspondence preserves strength.

Lemma 5.2
.

We have

2^{-d}\cdot\textnormal{str}(\tilde{f})\leq\textnormal{str}(f)\leq\textnormal{str}(\tilde{f}).

Proof.

Suppose first that
\textnormal{str}(\tilde{f})=r
and write
\tilde{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}q_{i}
, where
p_{i},q_{i}
are forms of degree
<d
on
V^{d}
. Plugging in
h_{1}=\ldots=h_{d}=x
, we get

d!f(x)=\tilde{f}(x,\ldots,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}(x,\ldots,x)q_{i}(x,\ldots,x),

where the equality on the left follows from lemma
3.9
. This implies

\textnormal{str}(f)=\textnormal{str}(d!f)\leq r=\textnormal{str}(\tilde{f}),

proving the inequality on the right hand side.

Now suppose
\textnormal{str}(f)=r
and write
f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}q_{i}
, where
p_{i},q_{i}
are forms of positive degrees
d_{i},d-d_{i}
. Comparing the corresponding terms of the Taylor expansion of
f(h_{1}+\ldots+h_{d})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}(h_{1}+\ldots+h_{d})\cdot q_{i}(h_{1}+\ldots+h_{d})
, we get

\tilde{f}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{J\in\binom{[d]}{d_{i}}}\tilde{p}_{i}((h_{j})_{j\in J})\cdot\tilde{q}_{i}((h_{j})_{j\in[d]\setminus J}).

This proves the inequality
\textnormal{str}(\tilde{f})\leq 2^{d}\cdot\textnormal{str}(f)
.
∎

Applying the correspondence of definition
5.1
to a tower of homogeneous polynomials gives rise to a multi-linear tower.

Definition 5.3
.

A
multi-linear tower

\mathcal{F}
on
V^{[d]}
is a tower where for each
i\in[h]
there is some
I_{i}\subset[d]
such that
(f_{i,j})_{j\in[s_{h}]}
are all multi-linear forms on
V^{I_{i}}
. For a tower
\mathcal{F}
with
d=\max_{i}d_{i}
, its polarization
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
is a multi-linear tower on
V^{[d]}
. For every
i\in[h]
and
I\in\binom{[d]}{d_{i}}
we get a layer
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i}^{I}
whose forms are
\tilde{f}_{i,j}^{I}:V^{I}\to K
. The layers of
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
are ordered consecutively in the different
i
and arbitrarily in the
I
. Given
i,I
we write
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{<(i,I)}
for the tower consisting of
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i^{\prime}}^{I^{\prime}}
for
i^{\prime}<i
or
i^{\prime}=i
and
I^{\prime}\neq I
.

The correspondence for towers also preserves relative strength.

Lemma 5.4
.

For every
i\in[h]
and
I\in\binom{[d]}{d_{i}}
we have

2^{-d}\cdot\textnormal{str}_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{<(i,I)}}(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i}^{I})\leq\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}_{<i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq\textnormal{str}_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{<(i,I)}}(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{i}^{I}).

Proof.

The proof of the left inequality is identical to lemma
5.2
.

For the right inequality, suppose that
f=\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}\sum_{I\in\binom{[d]}{d_{i}}}a_{j}^{I}\tilde{f}^{I}_{i,j}
is a nontrivial linear combination with
\textnormal{str}_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{<(i,I)}}(f)=r
. Then there exists some
I_{0}\in\binom{[d]}{d_{i}}
such that
a_{j}^{I_{0}}
are not all zero. Plugging in
h_{\ell}=x
for
\ell\in I_{0}
and
h_{\ell}=0
for
\ell\notin I_{0}
, we get
\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}_{<i}}(d_{i}!\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}a_{j}^{I_{0}}f_{i,j})\leq r
, which proves the claim.
∎

Due to this equivalence between relative rank for the original tower and for its polarization, it is sufficient to prove theorem
1.5
for multi-linear towers.

Corollary 5.5
.

Suppose that theorem
1.5
holds for multi-linear towers. Then it holds for all towers.

Proof.

Apply the previous lemma to both
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}\otimes_{K}\overline{K}
.
∎

In light of the above, we henceforth restrict our attention to multi-linear towers. In this setting there is a very simple geometric condition which ensures density of rational points.

Proposition 5.6
.

If
K
is an infinite field and
\underline{F}=(F^{I}_{i})
is a collection of multi-linear forms on
V^{[d]}
which cut out an irreducible complete intersection, then their rational zeros are Zariski dense.

Proof.

By induction on
d.
The base case
d=1
is immediate. For the inductive step, we need to show that
Y=\overline{Z(K)}\subset Z
has full dimension. Consider the projection of
Z
on
V^{[d-1]}
, denoted
Z^{\prime}
. This is the image of an irreducible set, hence is irreducible itself. The set
Z^{\prime}
is the zero locus of
(F^{I}_{i})_{I\subset[d-1]}
, so it is also a complete intersection. By inductive hypothesis,
Z^{\prime}(K)
is dense in
Z^{\prime}
. For each point
x\in Z^{\prime}(K),
the fiber
Y(x)
is a closed subset of the linear subspace
Z(x)\subset V_{d}
which contains all the
K
-points, so
Y(x)=Z(x).
This implies that
\dim Y\geq\dim Z^{\prime}+\dim V_{d}-r=\dim Z
, where
r
is the number of equations involving the
d
-th coordinate.

∎

For multi-linear forms, strength admits a natural refinement to partition rank.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
F:V^{[d]}\to K
be a multi-linear form. We say that
F
has partition rank one if
F(x)=G(x_{I})\cdot H(x_{I^{c}})
for a proper nonempty subset
I\subset[d]
and
G,H
multi-linear forms. The
partition rank
of
F
is

\textnormal{prk}(F)=\min\{r:F=F_{1}+\ldots+F_{r}\},

where each
F_{i}:V^{[d]}\to K
is multi-linear of partition rank one. If
I\subset[d]
,
F:V^{I}\to K
is multi-linear and
G_{i}:V^{I_{i}}\to K
are multi-linear then the
relative partition rank
of
F
on
\underline{G}
is

\textnormal{prk}_{\underline{G}}(F)=\min\{\textnormal{prk}(F+H)|H:V^{I}\to K\textnormal{ is multi-linear and }H\in(\underline{G})\}

In a similar way, Birch rank may be refined to geometric rank.

Definition 5.8
.

The
geometric rank
of
F:V^{I}\to K
is

\textnormal{grk}(F)=\textnormal{codim}(x:F(x_{I\setminus\{i_{0}\}},\cdot)\equiv 0),

where
i_{0}\in I
is any element. Note that this quantity does not depend on the choice of
i_{0}.

The
relative geometric rank
of
F
on
\underline{G}
is

\textnormal{grk}_{\underline{G}}(F)=\min_{i_{0}\in I}\textnormal{codim}_{Z(\underline{G})}(x\in Z(\underline{G}):F(x_{I\setminus\{i_{0}\}},\cdot),\underline{G}^{i_{0}}(x_{[d]\setminus\{i_{0}\}},\cdot)\textnormal{ are linearly dependent}),

where
\underline{G}^{i_{0}}
are the multi-linear forms in
\underline{G}
which involve
i_{0}
.

Using these definitions, we can refine the definitions of strong/regular towers to the multi-linear setting in the obvious way. As in the non-relative setting, low relative partition rank implies low relative geometric rank:

Claim 5.9
.

We have
\textnormal{grk}_{\underline{G}}(F)\leq\overline{\textnormal{prk}}_{\underline{G}}(F).

Proof.

If
F=\sum_{i=1}^{r}P_{i}Q_{i}\mod\underline{G}
then
F(x,\cdot)\in\textnormal{span}(G(x,\cdot))
whenever
P_{i}(x)=0
.
∎

The main result we will use is:

Proposition 5.10
.

[
17
, Theorem 3.5]

Given
A,B
, there exist
C,D
such that if
\mathcal{F}
is a multi-linear tower which is absolutely
(C,D,r)
-strong (in the sense of partition rank) then it is
(A,B,r)
-regular (in the sense of geometric rank).

A key role will also be played by the regularity of towers obtained by fixing subsets of the coordinates.

Definition 5.11
.

Suppose
\mathcal{F}
is a multi-linear tower on
V^{[d]}
and
I\subset[d]
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}^{I}
the multi-linear tower on

consisting of layers which only involve coordinates from

. Given

\mathcal{F}(x)
for the multi-linear tower on

obtained by fixing the

x
in layers which involve some coordinate outside
I
. In other words, these are the equations for the fiber of the projection map
Z(\mathcal{F})\to Z(\mathcal{F}^{I})
over

.

Lemma 5.12
.

Given
A,B
, there exist
C,D
such that if
\mathcal{F}
is absolutely
(C,D,r)
-strong then for
x\in Z(\mathcal{F}^{I})
outside a set of codimension
\geq r

\mathcal{F}(x)
is absolutely
(A,B,r)
-strong.

This is proved by induction on

where the base case

is trivial. Suppose the statement holds for height
<h
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a multi-linear tower of height

on
V^{[d]}
. Note that
\mathcal{F}_{<h}
is absolutely
(C,D,r+s_{h})
-strong whenever
\mathcal{F}
is
(C,D,r)
-strong.

There are two cases to handle. The first case is when

. In this case
\mathcal{F}(x)=\mathcal{F}_{<h}(x)
for each

\mathcal{F}(x)
is
(A,B,r)
-regular for

\textnormal{codim}_{Z(\mathcal{F}^{I})}E\cap Z(\mathcal{F}^{I})\geq\dim Z(\mathcal{F}^{I})-\dim E=\dim Z(\mathcal{F}_{<h}^{I})-s_{h}-\dim E\geq r,

where we used the fact that

The second case is when

. In this case,

. By inductive hypothesis,
\mathcal{F}_{<h}(x)
is absolutely

outside a set of codimension

, so we need to show that
\overline{\textnormal{str}}_{\mathcal{F}_{<h}(x)}(\mathcal{F}_{h}(x))>A(s_{h}+r)^{B}

outside a set of codimension

. Suppose, without loss of generality, that

and let

For any fixed

(2)

\alpha(s+r)^{\beta}<\textnormal{codim}_{Z(\mathcal{F}_{<h})}S\leq\textnormal{codim}_{Z(\mathcal{F}_{I})}E_{\eta}+\eta,

where the inequality on the left follows from proposition

and the one on the right is a general property for dimension of fibers.
By claim

the set of

with

is contained in

so it’s enough to show that

\geq r
. By inequality (

.
∎

We often rely on a stronger version of lemma

, namely that
\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}(\underline{y})
is regular for generic choices of
\underline{y}\in Z(\mathcal{F}_{I})^{m}

and define forms

F_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1})=F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
and
F_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1})=F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1},x_{d+1})

coordinates and

coordinates, respectively, which is an example of

cloning. Alternatively, we can set

for

and
U_{d}=V_{d}^{2}
and then define

by
F_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}(i))
. These are both multi-linear in the
[d]
coordinates, which is an example of
internal
cloning. We now give the general definition.

Definition 5.13
.

Given a multi-linear form

and a subset

, we can produce

multi-linear forms

by

. When the

are considered as multi-linear forms on different subsets of

of degree
m
over
I
. When they are all considered as multi-linear forms on
[d]
, we call this
internal cloning
of degree
m
over
I
. These operations naturally extend to multi-linear towers. External cloning produces a tower with at most
m
times as many layers and internal cloning produces a tower with the same number of layers.

These cloning operations preserve regularity.

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a multi-linear tower on
V^{[d]}
and let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
be obtained by external, internal cloning of degree
m
over
I
, respectively.

(1)

If
\mathcal{F}
is
(A,B,r)
-strong then so is
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{F}
is
(Am^{B},B,r)
-strong then
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
is
(A,B,r)
-strong.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{i})_{i\in[h]}
then
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{F}_{i,j})_{i\in[h],j\in[r]}
with the layers arranged in lexicographic order. The first claim follows from
\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{<(i,j)}}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i,j})\geq\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}_{<i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})
for all

. Indeed, suppose that

for a nontrivial linear combination of the forms in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i,j}

and

for all

yields

\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}_{<i}}(\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}_{<i}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{s_{i}}a_{k}F_{i,k}(x,y)\right)\leq t\leq\textnormal{str}_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{<(i,j)}}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i,j}).

The proof of the second claim is almost identical.
∎

Lemma 6.1

Suppose that
