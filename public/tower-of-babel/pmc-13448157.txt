Elbow Metastasis From Primary Lung Adenocarcinoma: A Case Report
Authors: Liliana Fernandez-Trujillo, Ciro D. Ibarra-Enriquez, Carlos A. García, Luz F. Sua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer is a leading cause of cancer‐related death worldwide, with bone metastasis frequently seen in advanced stages and often located in the axial skeleton or proximal long bones. Metastasis to the elbow is extremely rare and poses diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Case PresentationWe report the case of a 39‐year‐old male with a history of smoking and recent occupational exposure, presenting with an 8‐month history of progressive pain, swelling, and limited mobility in the left elbow. Imaging revealed an osteolytic lesion in the distal humerus, prompting further evaluation. A biopsy confirmed metastatic adenocarcinoma from a primary lung tumor. Following diagnosis, the patient underwent a targeted treatment regimen, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and localized radiotherapy for elbow metastasis. After 2 years of treatment, the patient achieved complete oncological remission.ConclusionThis case underscores the potential for positive outcomes in rare presentations of metastatic lung cancer to atypical sites such as the distal humerus. Although bone metastasis in lung cancer usually signals poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options, a personalized, multimodal treatment approach can substantially improve outcomes, even in advanced‐stage lung cancer with unusual metastasis.

1. Introduction

Tumors around the elbow joint are extremely rare [1], representing less than 1% of all primary bone tumors [2]. Pain is the most common symptom in these cases, and when there is a mass in the bone or soft tissues, it is crucial to rule out differential diagnoses such as inflammatory processes, infections, trauma, neoplasms, and rare anatomical variants such as the os supratrochleare anterius and the patella cubiti that should be considered in the differential diagnosis of elbow pathology when interpreting radiological studies [3–5]. It is also important not to dismiss the possibility of bone metastasis originating from another type of primary tumor, including lung tumors [1].

Bone metastases in lung cancer (LC) patients occur in 30%–40% of cases [6]. These metastases are generally more commonly located in the axial skeleton, such as the spine, although they can also appear in sites like the pelvis and extremities, such as the femur, with few reports of metastasis in the elbow joint [7]. In this case, we describe a patient with a mass found in the left elbow, in the distal humerus, which was a metastasis of primary lung origin.

2. Case Presentation

This case involves a 39‐year‐old male patient with a history of hypertension, a 15‐year history of smoking half a pack per day (7.5 pack‐years) until four years ago, and 25 years working in construction. His family history includes a mother with hypertension and a father with oxygen‐dependent chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) due to smoking, with no family history of cancer. The patient presented with an 8‐month history of moderate pain in the left elbow, with progressive swelling and limited mobility, and no history of trauma. No other relevant medical history was recorded.

Initially, a radiograph of the arm was performed, showing no abnormalities. A soft tissue ultrasound followed, revealing mild edema at the insertion of the lateral epicondyle extensor tendons and fluid in the elbow joint cavity, without other significant findings. The patient was started on anti‐inflammatory treatment, which provided little relief, leading him to seek further consultation. A magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the elbow showed a 5.5 × 4.8 cm irregular, osteolytic, expansile mass surrounding the bone almost circumferentially, infiltrating the triceps and brachial muscles, and involving the radial nerve, highly suggestive of a neoplastic lesion (Figure 1). Based on these findings, the patient was referred to our institution for evaluation by orthopedic oncology.

On admission, the patient was clinically stable, with normal vital signs, a blood pressure of 119/95 mmHg, heart rate of 75 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 16 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 95% on room air. He presented with swelling and pain in the distal third of the left arm, with no other significant physical examination findings. Analgesic treatment was initiated, and a biopsy of the left arm mass was planned due to suspicion of sarcoma. Simultaneously, staging studies were requested due to suspected malignancy, including a computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest, which showed a suspicious primary lesion in the left upper lung lobe, consisting of a 1.9 × 1.5 cm spiculated nodule and an additional 0.4‐cm nodule in the lower lingula segment, without lymphadenopathy, associated with centrilobular emphysema with diffuse and symmetrical involvement of both lung fields, likely related to hypersensitivity pneumonitis (Figure 2).

Pulmonary function tests were performed, including a 6‐min walk test on room air fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) of 21%, where the patient walked 615 m (92% of the predicted value of 664 m), ending with a peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 94%, heart rate of 181 bpm (74% of maximum heart rate), and no reported dyspnea or fatigue. Spirometry showed a forced vital capacity (FVC) of 5.42 L (108% of the predicted value), a forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) of 3.28 L (80% of the predicted value), and an FEV1/FVC ratio of 61%, indicating a mild obstructive pattern, with no bronchodilator response and a mildly reduced diffusion capacity. Alpha‐1 antitrypsin levels were measured and found to be normal.

Subsequently, the biopsy report from the left elbow confirmed the bone lesion to be a lytic tumor in the distal humerus, corresponding to a metastasis from a primary lung adenocarcinoma. This finding, along with the characteristics of the suspicious lung nodule, led to the decision to perform a wedge resection via left thoracoscopy to excise the lesion.

A wedge resection was performed without complications, revealing a nearly 3‐cm mass in the left upper lobe, with no other lesions in the cavity. Pathology revealed a moderately differentiated cribriform variant of primary lung adenocarcinoma, Grade 3, with lymphovascular invasion present and negative pleural and parenchymal resection margins (Figure 3). Immunohistochemistry showed high programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) expression (TPS 80%), with no mutations detected in epidermal growth factor (EGFR), echinoderm microtubule‐associated protein‐like 4 anaplastic lymphoma kinase (EML4‐ALK), or proto‐oncogene receptor tyrosine kinase (ROS1) genes. It also showed nuclear positivity for thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) and granular cytoplasmic expression for napsin A in malignant cells (Figure 4).

Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with metastatic adenocarcinoma, cT1bNxM1b, clinical stage IVa. The patient was functional, with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0. Treatment was initiated with chemotherapy plus immunotherapy (carboplatin, pemetrexed, and pembrolizumab) along with a session of radiotherapy for the management of the left elbow metastasis. The patient responded very well clinically, and after 4 months, his treatment was adjusted to maintenance therapy with pemetrexed and pembrolizumab. The patient continued this regimen, and a year later, chest CT and MRI of the left upper limb showed a complete response. Follow‐up with a positron emission tomography (PET) scan at 2 years confirmed a complete metabolic response.

The patient remains on maintenance with pemetrexed and pembrolizumab; however, he experienced a skin toxicity episode related to chemotherapy, later diagnosed as morphea, which was treated with clobetasol, with no other treatment‐related toxicities. Due to his positive response, treatment was discontinued 2 years after initiation, with regular follow‐ups. Three years from treatment initiation, sustained complete metabolic response was confirmed on chest CT and PET scan (Figure 5). A baseline PET/CT was not obtained prior to treatment initiation; posttreatment metabolic response was assessed by PET/CT at two and 3 years, with chest CT and left upper limb MRI used for earlier interval monitoring. The patient remains asymptomatic and under follow‐up.

3. Discussion

LC is the leading cause of cancer‐related death worldwide, with a low long‐term survival rate, particularly in advanced stages. Metastasis is associated with higher mortality and recurrence rates in this disease, influenced by multiple factors such as epithelial transition, vascular remodeling, and colonization [8]. LC can metastasize to any organ system [9], with the most common sites, in descending order, being the nervous system, bone, liver, respiratory system, and adrenal glands [10].

Bone metastases represent the third most affected system after the lungs and liver as metastatic sites for any primary tumor. The primary tumors that most commonly metastasize to the lungs include breast, prostate, thyroid, lung, and kidney cancers. Specifically, 30%–40% of LC patients develop bone metastases [6], representing the second most common metastatic site [11]. It is noteworthy that multiple metastatic lesions are more common than solitary lesions [12], and patients with bone metastases have very poor survival rates [10].

The axial skeleton is the main site for bone metastasis, accounting for approximately 70% of cases, particularly the thoracolumbar spine, which may be linked to the higher bone marrow activity and the paravertebral venous plexus [13]. Metastases to the appendicular skeleton are less common, occurring in about 10% of cases. In the long bones, the primary sites of metastasis are the proximal femur and proximal humerus, which are prone to pathological fractures [14]. Bone metastases rarely affect the distal portions of the appendicular skeleton, below the elbows and knees, representing only 0.1% of all bone metastases [15]. Atypical manifestations of LC including metastases to unusual anatomical sites carry inherent diagnostic complexity that may delay diagnosis and treatment initiation, as has been documented in recent case literature [16].

Few cases of bone metastasis in the elbow joint have been reported, as seen in our patient. This joint comprises three bones: first, the distal humerus, which forms two articulations—the trochlea and the capitulum; second, the proximal ulna, which contains two articulations—the greater and lesser sigmoid notches; and third, the proximal radius. These bones are connected by a set of ligaments that provide stability to the joint [17].

The humerus is the second most common site for bone metastasis after the femur, with the proximal region most frequently affected. Unlike typical humeral fractures, metastatic fractures often require surgical intervention, as they are unlikely to heal with conservative treatment and can significantly impact the patient’s independence and quality of life (QOL). Managing metastasis in the distal humerus is more complex, often necessitating elbow joint–sacrificing procedures, such as resection and total elbow endoprosthesis reconstruction [6].

To date, only a limited number of cases of humeral metastasis from primary LC have been documented in the literature. Reported cases encompass both the proximal and, more rarely, the distal humerus, with presentations ranging from pathological fractures to osteolytic lesions identified incidentally or during staging workup [18–21]. These cases underscore the importance of a high index of suspicion for metastatic disease when evaluating atypical bone lesions in patients with a pulmonary history or suspicious lung imaging findings, given that conventional radiographs may appear deceptively normal in early stages, as occurred in our patient.

Additionally, rare anatomical variants and morphological anomalies of the elbow such as the os supratrochleare anterius, an accessory ossicle located anterior to the trochlea that may present as a painful or incidental finding and can be misidentified as a loose body, osteochondral fragment, or neoplastic lesion on imaging, and the patella cubiti, a sesamoid‐like ossification within the triceps tendon whose variable morphology and inconsistent radiological appearance pose significant diagnostic challenges, should be considered in the differential diagnosis of elbow pathology, particularly when interpreting radiological studies in the absence of a clearly defined primary tumor [3–5].

The proximal humerus is a frequent site for bone metastasis, typically appearing in advanced cancer stages. Pathological fractures here often take a long time to heal, with up to 50% failing to heal entirely, resulting in severe pain, limb dysfunction, and a major impact on patients’ QOL. As proximal humerus metastasis is currently incurable and survival times are limited, treatment should focus on maximizing QOL for affected patients [22].

Our patient’s outcome is notably more favorable than what is typically observed in cases of bone metastases from LC. Generally, LC with bone metastasis is associated with poor prognosis, high recurrence, and low survival rates, given the aggressive nature of the disease and the challenges in managing metastases, especially in weight‐bearing bones. Bone metastases from LC commonly result in substantial pain, reduced mobility, and diminished QOL. However, our patient responded exceptionally well to a combination of chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted radiotherapy, achieving complete metabolic remission within 2 years. It should be noted that tumor mutational burden (TMB) quantification and microsatellite instability (MSI) testing were not performed in this case, as the molecular panel employed (Idylla GeneFusion Assay) does not assess these biomarkers; this represents a limitation of the molecular characterization and may be relevant for the selection of immunotherapy in future similar cases.

4. Conclusion

Our case highlights the rare presentation of lung adenocarcinoma metastasis to the elbow, an atypical site for bone metastases. Our patient demonstrated an impressive response to a comprehensive treatment approach with a complete metabolic remission. This outcome underlines the importance of a personalized, multimodal treatment strategy in managing metastatic LC, especially in cases where the metastasis is detected in unusual sites like the distal appendicular skeleton. While bone metastasis from LC typically signals a poor prognosis and reduced functional outcomes, this case exemplifies how targeted therapies can meaningfully extend survival and enhance QOL, even in advanced disease stages.
