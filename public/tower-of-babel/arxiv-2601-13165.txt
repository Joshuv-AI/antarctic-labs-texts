Optimistic Imprecise Shortest Watchtower in 1.5D and 2.5D
Bradley McCoy, Binhai Zhu

Abstract
A 1.5D imprecise terrain is an x x -monotone polyline with fixed x x -coordinates, the y y -coordinate of each vertex is not fixed but is constrained to be in a given vertical interval. A 2.5D imprecise terrain is a triangulation with fixed x x and y y -coordinates, but the z z -coordinate of each vertex is constrained to a given vertical interval. Given an imprecise terrain with n n intervals, the optimistic shortest watchtower problem asks for a terrain T T realized by a precise point in each vertical interval such that the height of the shortest vertical line segment whose lower endpoint lies on T T and upper endpoint sees the entire terrain is minimized. In this paper, we present a linear time algorithm to solve the 1.5D optimistic shortest watchtower problem exactly. For the discrete version of the 2.5D case (where the watchtower must be placed on a vertex of T T ), and we give an additive approximation scheme running in O ⁡ ( O ​ P ​ T ε ​ n 3 ) O(\frac{{OPT}}{\varepsilon}n^{3}) time, achieving a solution within an additive error of ε \varepsilon from the optimal solution value O ​ P ​ T {OPT} .

Introduction

Placing watchtowers on polyhedral terrains is a fundamental problem in computational geometry.
In practice, terrains are often constructed by surveying aircraft, which record distances to the ground.
Since these measurements inevitably contain errors, the resulting
data typically specifies not precise heights but intervals of possible elevations.
This motivates the imprecise watchtower problem, a natural and practical extension of its classical counterpart.

Imprecise terrains were introduced by Gray and Evans
[
5
]
, who
showed that computing an optimistic shortest path on a 2.5D imprecise terrain is NP-hard. Since then, the problem of finding realizations of imprecise terrains with desirable properties has developed into an active research area.

For a given imprecise terrain and geometric problem, one can ask
for a realization that yields the best (optimistic) possible outcome, or one that yields the worst (pessimistic) outcome.
Examples include approximation algorithms for minimizing turning angles in 1.5D in
[
7
]
,
minimizing the number of extrema in 2.5D
[
6
]
, and water flow on 2.5D terrains
[
4
]
. More recently, Lubiw and Stround
[
12
]
studied algorithms for optimizing coplanar features, minimizing surface area, and minimizing maximum steepness.

For precise terrains, guarding with a single shortest watchtower has a rich history. In 1988, Sharir
[
15
]
gave an
O(n\log^{2}(n))
time algorithm for guarding a 2.5D terrain with
n
vertices, later
improved to
O(n\log n)
by Zhu
[
17
]
.
A shortest watchtower for a precise 1.5D terrain can be computed in
linear time using the algorithm by Lee and Preparata
[
11
]
.
There are two natural versions of the watchtower problem: the discrete version, where the watchtower base must be on a vertex of the terrain, and the continuous version, where it may be placed anywhere on the terrain. The results mentioned above are for the continuous version.
Several other variations of guarding a precise terrain have also been studied

[
2
,
3
,
16
,
1
,
14
,
10
]
, and more recently, maximizing visibility on a 1.5D terrain with imprecise viewpoints was considered in
[
9
]
.

For imprecise watchtowers, McCoy, Zhu, and Dutt
[
13
]
gave an additive PTAS for the optimistic 1.5D imprecise shortest watchtower problem.
In this paper, we strengthen that result by presenting an exact linear time algorithm for the 1.5D case.
We then extend our result to the discrete version of the problem in 2.5D, for which we give an additive approximation scheme running in
O(\frac{{OPT}}{\varepsilon}n^{3})
time, where
{OPT}
is the optimal solution value of the problem.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
provides necessary definitions. Section
3
presents our linear time algorithm for the 1.5D case. Section
4
gives details for the 2.5D case. We conclude in Section
5
with a discussion of related open problems.

3
Algorithm for the 1.5D Case

In this section, we present an
O(n)
time algorithm to compute an optimistic shortest watchtower on an imprecise 1.5D terrain. We show that any realization can be transformed into one of
O(n)
canonical realizations without increasing the height of the watchtower.
We first consider the discrete version, where the base of the watchtower is restricted to a vertex of the terrain, and then extend the argument to the continuous version, where the base may lie anywhere on an edge. The following lemmas apply to both cases.

Lemma 1
(Raise Wings)

Let
W
be a watchtower for a realization

be the realization obtained from

by raising
v_{1}
and
v_{n}
to the tops of their respective intervals. Then

is also a watchtower for
T^{\prime}
.

Proof

Let
w
denote the top of the watchtower. Consider the quadrilateral
D
formed by
w,v_{2},v_{1},
and
t_{1}
. Since
w
guards the edge
v_{1}v_{2}
, the interior angle at
v_{2}
is at most
180^{\circ}
. An example is shown in Figure
2
. Hence, the quadrilateral
D
is convex, and the watchtower also sees the edge
t_{1}v_{2}
. Thus,
W
is a valid watchtower for
T^{\prime}
. The argument is symmetric for raising
v_{n}
to
t_{n}
.
∎

Let

and

be two vertices of a realization with

. Consider any watchtower whose

Lemma 2
(Shorten)

For fixed
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
as described above. Locally shortening the subpath
v_{i}\rightarrow v_{j}

does not increase the height of the watchtower.

Proof

Since the endpoints
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
are fixed, shortening the path moves at least one inner vertex. Let

denote such a vertex. The slope of the edge
v_{k-1}v_{k+1}
lies between the slopes of
v_{k-1}v_{k}
and
v_{k}v_{k+1}
. When we shorten the path, the slopes of
v_{k-1}v_{k}
and
v_{k}v_{k+1}
approach that of
v_{k-1}v_{k+1}
(Figure
3
).

Thus, if the edges
v_{k-1}v_{k}
and
v_{k}v_{k+1}
are visible from the watchtower before shortening, they remain visible after the path is shortened. By repeatedly applying this local shortening operation, any path with fixed endpoints can be transformed into a shortest path without increasing the watchtower height.
∎

The top of an optimal watchtower is determined by a vertex on

We now show that the edges (or edge) whose extensions determine the top of an optimal watchtower

Lemma 3
(Top of tower determined by
\pi
)

Let
\pi
be a shortest path
\pi:t_{1}\rightarrow t_{n}
with non-zero watchtower height.
Let

be any realization with optimal (shortest) watchtower height, and let

and
e_{j}

denote the edges whose extensions intersect at the top of the watchtower.
Then
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
are edges of
\pi
.

Proof

Since
\pi
has non-zero watchtower height, it is not a straight line, and the realization cannot be shifted vertically.
Suppose

is an optimal realization, with

lying to the left of the watchtower and
e_{j}
to the right.

Let
e_{i}
be determined by intervals
\ell_{\alpha}
and
\ell_{\beta}
.
Then

makes a left turn at
\ell_{\alpha}
and a right turn at
\ell_{\beta}
(or

is a subedge of a straight line from
\ell_{\alpha}
to
\ell_{\beta}
).
Since

minimizes the watchtower height,

must have positive slope, with
\ell_{\alpha}

\ell_{\beta}
minimized.

Similarly, let

be determined by intervals
\ell_{\gamma}
and
\ell_{\delta}
.
Then
T
makes a right turn at
\ell_{\gamma}
and a left turn at
\ell_{\delta}
(or
e_{j}
is a subedge of a straight line from
\ell_{\gamma}
to
\ell_{\delta}

In this case,

has negative slope, with
\ell_{\gamma}

\ell_{\delta}

Thus,

is an edge from the top of an interval to the bottom of another with positive slope,

e_{j}
is an edge running from the bottom of an interval to the top of another with negative slope.
Since

\ell_{\beta}
and
\ell_{\gamma}

∎

The case where the top of the watchtower is determined by a single edge

3

Algorithm for the Discrete Version in 2.5D

In this section, we consider the 2.5D case. In this model, we start with an underlying planar triangulation of
n
vertices
p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{n}
, with
p_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
; then each
\triangle p_{i}p_{j}p_{k}
in
the plane is lifted so that each
p_{i}
can possibly be within a vertical interval
I_{i}=((x_{i},y_{i},b_{i}),(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i}))
. We then use the 3D vertex (point)
q_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})
to represent a geometric realization of
p_{i}
, with
b_{i}\leq z_{i}\leq t_{i}
. Note that
\triangle p_{i}p_{j}p_{k}
and
\triangle q_{i}q_{j}q_{k}
are topologically equivalent. We call the lifted triangulation
T
over 3D points
Q=\{q_{i}|i\in[n]\}
an
imprecise terrain
over
Q
, keeping in mind that the
z
-coordinate of
q_{i}
is a variable.

The optimistic shortest watchtower problem in 2.5D is then defined as
computing a shortest watchtower
|uv|
such that
u
is on
T
and
v
can see all points on
T
, among all possible realizations of
T
.

The problem is certainly harder than the 1.5D counterpart, one reason is probably that in 2.5D computing the shortest path between two vertices
q_{i}
and
q_{\ell}
on
T
is already NP-hard
[
5
]
. So, we need to first consider a more fundamental question:
is there an optimistic watchtower on
T
of height zero? We call this problem the
zero-watchtower problem
on
T
.

It is easy to see that the zero-watchtower solution, if exists, must occur at some vertex
q_{i}
, i.e.,
u=v=q_{\ell}
.

Lemma 7

If
q_{\ell}=(x_{\ell},y_{\ell},z_{\ell})
is a solution of the zero-watchtower problem on
T
, then
q^{\prime}_{\ell}=(x_{\ell},y_{\ell},t_{\ell})
is also a solution.

Proof

If
q_{\ell}
(
\neq q^{\prime}_{\ell}
) is a solution then for all other triangular face
F_{j}
’s in
T
not adjacent to
q_{\ell}
,
q_{\ell}
must be on
F_{j}
or above the half-plane determined by
F_{j}
— otherwise,
q_{\ell}
cannot guard some face
F_{j}
and cannot be a solution. Fixing all these face
F_{j}
’s, we then lift
q_{\ell}
vertically to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
. Clearly,
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
is above all
F_{j}
’s. The remaining faces are all adjacent to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
(before the lift-up, adjacent to
q_{\ell}
), hence are all guarded by
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
.
∎

Given an edge
e_{k}=p_{i}p_{j}
on
T
, define
H(p_{i}p_{j})
or
H(e_{k})
as the vertical half-plane bounded above by the line through
e_{k}=p_{i}p_{j}
. We have the following lemma.

Lemma 8

Fixing
q^{\prime}_{\ell}=(x_{\ell},y_{\ell},t_{\ell})
, there is no solution for the zero-watchtower problem at
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
if and only if (1) there is a vertex
p_{k}\in T
whose highest realization
p^{\prime}_{k}

cannot see
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
regardless of the realization of other vertices; or (2) there is an edge
p_{c}p_{d}\in T
whose lowest realization
p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}
is not visible to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
, regardless of the realization of other vertices.

Proof

Note that in case (2), when
p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}
is not visible to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
, one of the two faces
adjacent to
p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}
is also not visible to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
.

We proceed to prove this “if and only if” relation. The “only if” part is easy to see. We focus on proving the “if” part, i.e., assuming that there is such a
p^{\prime}_{k}
or
p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}
satisfying the condition in Lemma
8
. For Case (1), when
p^{\prime}_{k}
and
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
are not visible to each other, there must be an edge
p_{i}p_{j}
such that the segment
p^{\prime}_{k}q^{\prime}_{\ell}
intersects
the vertical half-plane
H(p_{i}p_{j})
; in fact, the segment would even intersect
H(p^{-}_{i}p^{-}_{j})
. See Figure
7(a)
for an example.
As a matter of fact,
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
cannot be a zero-watchtower solution. At this point, suppose there is no vertex
p_{k}
satisfying the condition in Case (1). Now consider Case (2), assuming there is an edge
p_{c}p_{d}\in T
whose lowest realization
p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}
is not visible to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
and one of the two faces adjacent to it, say
\triangle p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}p_{f}
, is not visible to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
(but the three vertices
p^{-}_{c},p^{-}_{d}
and
p_{f}
are all visible to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
). In this case, if we lower
p_{f}
to its lowest realization
p^{-}_{f}
and the edge
p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}
(and the corresponding face
\triangle p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}p^{-}_{f}
) are still not visible to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
, then certainly
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
cannot be a zero-watchtower solution. See Figure
7(b)
for an example.
∎

The proof of Lemma
8
is constructive and it implies an algorithm to test if
q^{\prime}_{\ell}

is a solution for the zero-watchtower problem.

Theorem 4.1

The zero-watchtower problem on
T
can be decided in
O(n^{3})
time.

Proof

With the above lemma, we obtain an algorithm as follows. We start with the highest realization of
all vertices in
T
and then focus on
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
. If
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
can see all the edges of the
current realization of
T
, then we are done. If not, following Lemma
8
, we have the following two cases.

In Case (1), if there is a
p^{\prime}_{k}
(the highest realization of
p_{k}
) such that
p^{\prime}_{k}
and
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
cannot see each other, i.e., they intersect some
H(p_{i}p_{j})
. Then we label
p^{\prime}_{k}
as a candidate vertex,
and identify all the edges of
T
, say
e_{k,1},e_{k,2},...,e_{k,s_{k}}
, whose vertical half-planes
H(e_{k,1}),H(e_{k,2}),\cdots,H(e_{k,s_{k}})
intersect the line segment
p^{\prime}_{k}q^{\prime}_{\ell}
. (If there are multiple such candidate vertices, we process the farthest one from
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
first.) We then lower the realizations
of these
s_{k}
edges by translating them vertically down to a position such that
p^{\prime}_{k}
and
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
are barely visible to each other (i.e., they just touch the line segment
p^{\prime}_{k}q^{\prime}_{\ell}
). If it is impossible to make
p^{\prime}_{k}
and
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
visible by these downward translations, then we conclude that there is no solution for the zero-watchtower problem at
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
.
Otherwise, we repeat the above process until all candidate vertex
p^{\prime}_{k}
’s are now visible to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
but
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
still cannot cover the current realization of
T
, then we continue with the next step.

By now in Case (2), suppose there is an edge
p_{c}p_{d}
whose lowest realization
p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}

cannot be seen from
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
. As discussed earlier, one of the two faces adjacent to

p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}
, say
\triangle p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}p_{f}
, is also not visible to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
. We then lower
p_{f}
to its lowest realization
p^{-}_{f}
. If
\triangle p^{-}_{c}p^{-}_{d}p^{-}_{f}
is visible to

q^{\prime}_{\ell}
then we repeat this process for Case (2) to find and process some other edges not visible to
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
; otherwise, we report that
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
cannot be a solution for the zero-watchtower problem.

Note that in the whole process for Case (1) and Case (2), except at the beginning step, we only lower vertices and edges to their lowest realization, hence there is no conflict. Each step of Case (1) and Case (2) obviously takes
O(n)
time, and in each case we could have
O(n)
steps (e.g., in Case (1) we have
O(n)
candidate vertex
p^{\prime}_{k}
’s). Clearly, if there is a solution when
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
fixed, we would spend
O(n^{2})
time to return a solution.

Since we have to try
O(n)
vertex
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
’s, the whole algorithm takes
O(n^{3})
time to either return a solution, or report that no solution exists for the zero-watchtower problem.
∎

Theorem
4.1
also implies that we could obtain an additive approximation scheme for the discrete shortest watchtower problem on
T
where the base must be at a vertex of
T
. The algorithm first decides if
a zero-solution exists; if so then we can solve the problem in
O(n^{3})
time. Otherwise,
at each candidate base vertex
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
we check the point
q^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}
which is
\varepsilon
distance above
q^{\prime}_{\ell}
vertically. If
q^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}
can see every edge of a realization of
T
then
return the vertical segment
q^{\prime}_{\ell}q^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}
as the approximate solution. Otherwise, update
q^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}\leftarrow q^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}\oplus\varepsilon
and repeat the algorithm in Theorem
4.1
(where
v\oplus\varepsilon
is the operation to move
v
by a vertical distance
\varepsilon
). If the optimal solution value is
{OPT}
, then we can certainly find a solution with solution value at most
{OPT}+\varepsilon
.

Corollary 1

An additive approximation scheme for the discrete shortest watchtower on an imprecise terrain
T
with
n
vertices
can be computed in
O(\frac{{OPT}}{\varepsilon}n^{3})
time, and with a solution value at most
{OPT}+\varepsilon
, where
{OPT}
is the optimal solution value of the problem.

5
Concluding Remarks

In this paper we solve the optimistic shortest watchtower problem optimally (in linear time) for the 1.5D case. For the discrete version in 2.5D we give an additive approximation scheme which runs in
O(\frac{{OPT}}{\varepsilon}n^{3})
time, where
n
is the number of imprecise vertical intervals and
{OPT}
is the optimal solution value. A natural question is to determine the computational complexity of the 2.5D problem. Given that finding shortest paths is hard in this setting, we conjecture that the 2.5D problem is intractable. As a further evidence for our conjecture, we comment that for the discrete version in 2.5D, our approximation scheme is not a PTAS, as
{OPT}
is not a part of the input.
Hence, even designing a PTAS for the discrete version is itself an interesting open problem.
