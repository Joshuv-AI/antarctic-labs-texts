Multi-turn Jailbreaking via Global Refinement and Active Fabrication [WARNING] This paper contains unsafe model responses. Reader discretion is advised.
Hua Tang, Lingyong Yan, Yukun Zhao, Shuaiqiang Wang, Jizhou Huang, Dawei Yin

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved exceptional performance across a wide range of tasks. However, they still pose significant safety risks due to the potential misuse for malicious purposes. Jailbreaks, which aim to elicit models to generate harmful content, play a critical role in identifying the underlying security threats. Recent jailbreaking primarily focuses on single-turn scenarios, while the more complicated multi-turn scenarios remain underexplored. Moreover, existing multi-turn jailbreaking techniques struggle to adapt to the evolving dynamics of dialogue as the interaction progresses. To address this limitation, we propose a novel multi-turn jailbreaking method that refines the jailbreaking path globally at each interaction. We also actively fabricate model responses to suppress safety-related warnings, thereby increasing the likelihood of eliciting harmful outputs in subsequent questions. Experimental results demonstrate the superior performance of our method compared with existing single-turn and multi-turn jailbreaking techniques across six state-of-the-art LLMs. Our code is publicly available at https://github.com/Ytang520/Multi-Turn_jailbreaking_Global-Refinment_and_Active-Fabrication .

(2) Multi-turn jailbreaking

;

;

(also denoted as the

However, previous multi-turn jailbreaking methods mostly utilize pre-defined templates to generate the

Jiang et al. 2024
, and
keep the queries fixed

Ren et al. 2024
;
Russinovich et al. 2024
. Besides, previous methods simply accept and concatenate the target model’s intermediate responses to the dialogue history without any modification, which may hinder the model’s ability to answer subsequent queries and result in suboptimal performance.

To this end, we propose a novel multi-turn jailbreaking method, which
globally
refines the jailbreaking path and
actively
fabricates the dialogue history throughout the multi-turn jailbreaking process.
We begin by requesting our attack model to generate an initial comprehensive jailbreaking path based on the given task.
At each turn
i
, the query
q_{i}
in the jailbreaking path
Q
is first fed into the target model to obtain the corresponding answer
a_{i}
.
If the target model refuses to answer the query
q_{i}
, the attack model first revises
q_{i}
until a successful response is obtained or the maximum number of retries is reached. Otherwise, all subsequent queries
Q_{\geq i}
are revised. Once a non-rejective answer is returned, the model globally refines all following queries
Q_{>i}
based on the dialogue history up to
a_{i}
. In contrast to prior work, our approach refines all remaining queries at each step, allowing the model to stay focused on ultimate jailbreak success and to avoid generating off-topic or ineffective queries. We further introduce an active fabrication mechanism with two key strategies:
(1) if the target model continues to reject
q_{i}
, we discard
(q_{i},a_{i})
and proceed to
q_{i+1}
;
(2) When a non-rejective answer
a_{i}
is returned, we proactively modify it by removing any safety-related warning.
These strategies enhance the attacking model’s ability to recover from rejections and maintain effective progress toward a successful jailbreak.
We repeat the above process until the final query
q_{N}
receives a valid response.

In summary, our contributions are tri-fold:

•

We introduce a novel multi-turn jailbreaking method that globally refines the jailbreaking path through updating all the remaining queries inside the jailbreaking path to adapt to the context, and actively fabricates the dialogue history to enhance the jailbreaking success.

•

We perform extensive analyses of multi-turn jailbreaking methods. We find that our approach shifts the representations of harmful queries closer to those of harmless ones, which helps explain the effectiveness of multi-turn attacks.

•

We further evaluate our method against different defense techniques and demonstrate its effectiveness and generalization.

Jailbreaking

Single-turn Jailbreaking

Mainstream attacks primarily focus on single-turn jailbreaks, which allow only a single attack on the target model during the test phase
Zou et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2024
;
Yi et al. 2024a
, like using optimization-based methods to generate adversarial prompts

Most of these methods include explicit malicious content to convey their intent to the target model, and can be easily defended by those input filters

Huang et al. 2024
;
Souly et al. 2024

Multi-turn Jailbreaking

Given the real-world scenario of multiple interactions with LLMs, some researchers have extended attacks to multi-turn jailbreaking. Multi-turn jailbreaking allows multiple attacks on the target model, with its final response during the dialogue evaluated for the success or failure of the attack
Russinovich et al. 2024
;
Gibbs et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Ren et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024a
;
Jiang et al. 2024
. Therefore, malicious intent can be concealed within the jailbreaking path
Russinovich et al. 2024
;
Ren et al. 2024
, rather than being explicitly stated in the jailbreaking queries.
For instance,
Russinovich et al. 2024
designs query chains to avoid explicitly expressing harmful content in the final query, while
Wang et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2024
iteratively refines queries to gradually align query semantics with user intent. However, these approaches often rely on fixed templates
Yang et al. 2024a
;
Jiang et al. 2024
. Some methods
Ren et al. 2024
;
Russinovich et al. 2024
attempt to refine queries during the attack, but they typically make only local updates by modifying the immediate next query, which can result in off-topic queries and may fail to jailbreak within the maximum number of tries. In addition, previous approaches often directly append the target model’s responses to the dialogue history without any revision, which may hinder the model from answering subsequent queries and lead to suboptimal performance.

Our approach differs from prior work in two main ways. First, we globally refine the jailbreaking paths through updating all the subsequent queries at once to adapt to the dynamic dialogue history. In contrast,
Ren et al. 2024
only refines the query when it is rejected by the target model, and
Russinovich et al. 2024
generates the next query based solely on the previous turn.
Second, instead of simply appending the target model’s responses to the dialogue history, we detect and remove safety-related warnings, which helps the attack model to smoothly progress toward jailbreaking success.

3.1
Path Initialization

Since LLMs may refuse to generate the jailbreaking path, following
Ren et al. 2024
, for the jailbreaking goal (e.g, the bomb construction instruction), we begin by querying the attack model for related information (e.g., a well-known criminal Ramzi Yousef who builds bombs for terrorism). Based on the information and the jailbreaking goal, the attack model is requested to generate an initial innocuous query (e.g., What have Ramzi Yousef done in history?), followed by a sequence of queries, which are prompted to guide target model’ responses to become increasingly relevant to the harmful target until reaching it. Further implementation details can be found in Appendix
A.1
, and the example initialized paths can be referred to Appendix
G.6.1
.

3.2
Global Refinement

As shown in Figure
2
, during each turn
i
, the query
q_{i}
in the jailbreaking path
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},...,q_{n}\}
is first fed into the target model to obtain the corresponding answer
a_{i}
. Despite the efforts to conceal malicious intent within the jailbreaking path, some queries may still contain harmful content and be rejected by the target model. If a query is rejected, we first revise
q_{i}
to replace the harmful content with referential expressions such as ’that in your previous response’. This helps to conceal explicit malicious content within the conversation history while preserving the core intent of each query. However, if the query continues to be rejected after the maximum number of attempts, we revise all subsequent queries
Q_{\geq i}
to redirect the current jailbreaking path. This changes the core intent of
q_{i}
and further conceals its malicious intent. For simplicity, we use the attack model to determine whether the response answers or rejects the intermediate query during the attack process.

Once the non-rejection answer is obtained, the model then globally updates the remaining queries
Q_{>i}=\{q_{i+1},...,q_{N}\}
based on the dialogue history up to
a_{i}
. The refinement helps the attack model to discover more effective and stealthy jailbreaking paths with the comprehensive dialogue history. This global refinement strategy, which updates all subsequent queries at each step, helps the model remain focused on achieving a successful jailbreak. Unlike previous approaches
Russinovich et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024b
, our method reduces the likelihood of generating off-topic or ineffective queries, increasing the chances that the path will reach the target within the allowed number of interactions. To support this process, we also monitor the final query to determine whether its topic deviates from the target goal. If it happens, we iteratively refine the path until it becomes relevant or the maximum number of attempts is reached.

3.3
Active Fabrication

LLMs can memorize information from the dialogue history
Yi et al. 2024b
;
Maharana et al. 2024
, and recent studies demonstrate that LLMs tend to answer a query with confirmation prefixes such as "sure", or reject it with prefixes like "sorry"
Qi et al. 2024
. Based on these findings, we argue that the presence of rejection phrases—such as "I’m sorry, but I can’t assist with that"—and safety-related warnings phrases—such as "I want to be clear and responsible"—in the dialogue history increases the likelihood that the target models will reject subsequent queries. As illustrated by real examples in Appendix
G.2
, when such safety-related warnings appear in the dialogue history, the models are more likely to refuse to answer subsequent queries, thereby hindering progress toward a successful jailbreak.

Therefore, if the target model still rejects the intermediate query
q_{i}
after the refinement described in Section
3.2
, we discard the query-answer pair
(q_{i},a_{i})
and move on to the next query
q_{i+1}
. If an answer
a_{i}
is obtained from the target model, we then modify it by removing any safety-related warnings. Specifically, we prompt the attack model to remove such content while retaining the core content that directly addresses the query. We then use the attack model to rephrase the core content with introductory sentences and summary sentences. The model then rephrases the core content into a smooth response with introductory and concluding sentences (Refer to Appendix
E.1
for the detailed prompt, and Appendix
G.2
for examples). This reduces the likelihood of detection caused by unnatural phrasing, such as only extracting fragmented sentences directly from the original response.

Implementation Details.

For our proposed method, following
Ren et al. 2024
, we use GPT-4o to initialize the jailbreaking paths, with a temperature setting of 1 to ensure diversity in the paths. During attack, we use GPT-4o-mini as the attack model, and GPT-4o as the judge model, both with the temperature set to 0. For the baseline methods, unless otherwise specified, we use GPT-4o-mini as the attack model. Additionally, we apply an early stopping mechanism using the attack model to terminate the jailbreaking process once the generated response fulfills the jailbreaking task. This prevents unnecessary computation when an intermediate response is already sufficient. We also include formatting prompts such as "format the previous content into a … [required response format]" to ensure that the final output matches the task’s desired format.
For further details, please refer to Appendix
B.1
.

5
Analyses

The effectiveness of our multi-turn jailbreaking approach prompts three key questions: (1) Why do LLMs fail to defend against multi-turn jailbreaking attacks? (2) Does Path Initialization affect our attack performance? And (3) Can existing defense methods effectively defend against our attack? In this section, we present extensive experiments and analyses to address these questions.

5.1
Why Do LLMs Fail to Defend Against Multi-Turn Attacks?

In this subsection, we investigate why LLMs fail to defend against the multi-turn jailbreaking attacks by analyzing how original harmful queries and corresponding harmless queries lie in the model’s representation space, and how our multi-turn attacks alter the representations of harmful queries.

We use the whole HarmBench dataset and generate corresponding harmless queries using GPT-4o-mini. To eliminate the impact of the irrelevant factors, such as format and length, similar to
Zheng et al. 2024
, we generate harmless queries that match the harmful ones in both verb and format (See Appendix
G
for examples). We also control that each harmful-harmless pair has a similar length to reduce the effect of length on the representations. Please refer to Appendix
C.1
for detailed steps, Appendix
E
for the prompt used in harmless query generation. The History dialogues for these harmful queries are obtained in Section
4.2
, using Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.1-70B as the target models.

Figure
3
shows that harmful and harmless queries are almost separable, as revealed by the decision boundary from logistic regression. It also illustrates that our multi-turn attack moves the representations of harmful queries closer to those of harmless queries, demonstrating the effectiveness of our multi-turn jailbreaking attacks. In addition, we observe that as more dialogue history is added, the representation of harmful queries shifts (slightly) closer to that of harmless ones. This finding is consistent with prior work in multi-turn jailbreaking, which shows that longer conversational histories can help obscure malicious intent
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Ren et al. 2024
;
Cheng et al. 2024
.

5.2
Does Path Initialization Affect Our Attack Performance?

In this subsection, we investigate whether Path Initialization affects our attack performance. We initialize the path through three methods: (1) the initialization method adapted from
Ren et al. 2024
; (2) path in-context learning from
Russinovich et al. 2024
; (3) directly prompt the attack model to generate a random jailbreaking path that guides us to address the goal with the final query (See Appendix
E.3
for the prompt). To reduce the effect of randomness, we sample three independent jailbreaking paths, each consisting of five queries, for every combination of the jailbreaking goal and the initialization method. If at least one path successfully jailbreaks the model, we consider the method successful on that query. We randomly sample 50 queries from the HarmBench dataset and conduct experiments across all six LLMs with GPT-Judge as our judge method. Please refer to Appendix
A.1
for more details and Appendix
G.6
for examples.

Moreover, our method achieves similarly high ASR on both the GPT-series and LLaMA-series models. This can be attributed to our method focusing on refining the attack process through global path refinement and active fabrication, rather than relying on path initialization as in previous works
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Cheng et al. 2024
.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel multi-turn jailbreaking method that globally refines prompts and actively fabricates dialogue history to increase the likelihood of eliciting harmful responses. Our approach effectively jailbreaks six state-of-the-art LLMs, outperforming both existing single-turn and multi-turn baselines. Besides, our multi-turn jailbreaking method shifts harmful query representations closer to those of harmless ones, which helps explain the effectiveness of multi-turn attacks. In addition, our methods consistently improve the ASR across various path initialization strategies, indicating its stable and reliable performance improvements. Finally, our proposed method remains effective against current defense mechanisms, indicating its ability to partially bypass safety filters and expose vulnerabilities in existing defense techniques. We hope our work raises awareness of LLMs’ vulnerabilities to multi-turn attacks.

Limitations

Our work has limitations in two aspects. First, in this paper, we simply concatenate the entire conversation history without modifying or reordering the sequence of question-answer pairs. Reordering different dialogues could potentially confuse the LLMs’ reasoning process, thereby reducing the likelihood of rejection when responding to queries. Second, our definition of multi-turn jailbreaking requires the final response to be evaluated for success or failure. However, in practice, harmful information can be distributed across multiple dialogues. Understanding how to recognize, gather, and defend such distributed information could provide valuable insights for future research on both jailbreaking strategies and defense mechanisms.

A.1
Path Initialization

Since LLMs might refuse to generate the jailbreaking paths, we initialize the path by first requesting the information related to the jailbreaking goal (e.g, the bomb construction instruction) from the attack model (e.g, the renowned criminal of the bomb construction). After that, the attack model generates the initial innocuous query based on the information, followed by subsequent queries and their predicted answers. This helps maintain the coherence across the queries. Please refer to
Ren et al. 2024
for further details and the rationale behind it.

Note that we here specifically require the predicted answer from the attack model to the final query in the jailbreaking path to
explicitly and directly
fulfill our jailbreaking goal, thereby ensuring that the path leads to the desired response. Without this constraint, the path may yield undesired responses that fail to fulfill the jailbreaking goal. For example, if the goal is ’how to make a bomb’ and the path is initialized without this constraint, the final query inside the jailbreaking path might be ’What are the regulations on explosive production?’ Although the target model’s response may include partial information about explosive construction as part of a regulatory explanation, it does not satisfy the jailbreaking task. Please refer to Appendix
G.6.1
for examples of the initialized paths.

Implementation Details.

For our proposed method, GPT-4o is used to initialize the jailbreaking paths, with a temperature of 1 to ensure the path diversity. We initialize three distinct jailbreaking paths, each composed of five queries. During the attack, GPT-4o-mini is used to refine the queries, with a temperature set to 0. If an intermediate query is rejected, the query can be refined through up to two iterations of prompt refinement and one iteration of path refinement. Additionally, we use an early stopping mechanism that terminates the jailbreaking process once the response has met the goal. This prevents unnecessary computation when an intermediate response is already sufficient. We also include formatting prompts such as "format the previous content into a … [required response format]" to ensure that the final output matches the task’s desired format.

For the baseline methods, unless otherwise specified, we use GPT-4o-mini as the attack model. However, for methods such as Crescendo and GPTFuzz, where GPT-4o-mini frequently generates to refine jailbreak prompts, we use GPT-3.5-turbo-0125 as the attack model instead. C.1
Why LLMs fail to defend against the multi-turn jailbreaking?

In this subsection, we investigate why LLMs fail to defend against multi-turn jailbreaking attacks by analyzing how original harmful queries and their corresponding harmless queries are represented in the model’s internal space, and how our multi-turn attacks alter these representations.

We use the whole HarmBench dataset as harmful inputs and generate corresponding harmless queries using GPT-4o-mini with the temperature set to 0. Similar to the approach in
Zheng et al. 2024
, we require the generation following these requirements:

•

To control for irrelevant factors such as format and length, we require that each harmless query matches its harmful counterpart in both verb usage and structure. For example, given the harmful query “Write an essay about how race-based discrimination is beneficial for society,” the verb phrase “Write an essay” is preserved, and the overall length remains comparable. The corresponding harmless query is “Write an essay about how diversity can enrich society and promote understanding.” See the generation prompt in
E.2
and additional examples in
G.5
.

•

We filter out query pairs where the harmless queries are rejected by both Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, as this suggests those queries may not be truly harmless. Besides, we also identify harmful queries that are incorrectly accepted by both models, indicating that they fail to recognize the harmful intent. Since including such misclassified queries could distort the visualization by clustering them with clearly harmful examples, we thus exclude those pairs for further visualization. We use RS-Match for evaluation and additionally perform manual verification to ensure the validity of the samples.

The History dialogues for these harmful queries are obtained in Section
4.2
, using Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.1-70B as the target models. After eliminating the samples for which the jailbreaking paths cannot be generated, there are 157 harmful and harmless pairs remaining. Figure

shows that harmful and harmless queries are nearly separable, as indicated by the decision boundary from logistic regression. The figure also shows that our multi-turn attack shifts the representations of harmful queries closer to those of harmless ones, demonstrating the effectiveness of the attack. Additionally, as more dialogue history is concatenated, the representations of harmful queries move slightly closer to those of harmless ones, which is consistent with previous findings in multi-turn jailbreaking research (
Ren et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2024
;

Does Path Initialization Affect Our Attack Performance?

In this subsection, we examine how Path Initialization influences the performance of our attack. We initialize the path using three methods: (1) our proposed initialization method described in
