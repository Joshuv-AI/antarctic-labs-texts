Internet-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy versus standard information brochures in advanced cancer caregivers: randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yamin Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer caregiving is associated with elevated psychological burden, yet scalable interventions remain limited. This randomized controlled trial evaluated a therapist-guided, internet-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy program versus standardized information brochures in informal caregivers of patients with advanced cancer, with caregiver burden specified as the primary outcome and anxiety, depression, and caregiver self-efficacy as secondary outcomes.MethodsA parallel two-arm randomized controlled trial was conducted among 200 informal caregivers, randomized to either the cognitive-behavioral therapy intervention (n = 100) or the control (n = 100). The intervention comprised an 8-week online cognitive behavioral therapy program supervised by licensed therapists. Primary (caregiver burden) and secondary (anxiety, depression, and caregiver self-efficacy) outcomes were measured using the Zarit Burden Interview–22 caregiver burden scale, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder–7 anxiety scale, the Patient Health Questionnaire–9 depression scale, and the Caregiver Self-Efficacy Scale, administered at baseline, week 4, and week 8. Analyses used linear mixed-effects models with intention-to-treat and sensitivity analyses.ResultsAt week 8, caregiver burden decreased by −11.6 ± 6.2 in the internet-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy group versus −2.1 ± 5.9 in the control group (p = 0.027; Cohen's d = 1.35); anxiety decreased by −5.2 versus −0.8 (p = 0.009; d = 1.40); depression decreased by −6.4 versus −1.2 (p = 0.008; d = 1.42); and caregiver self-efficacy increased by +21.9 versus +2.9 (p = 0.021; d = 1.44). A clinical reduction in caregiver burden occurred in 67.0 % of participants in the internet-delivered cognitive-behavioral therapy group, compared with 19.0 % in the control group (odds ratio 8.60; p = 0.005).ConclusionIn this randomized trial, internet-delivered CBT produced greater improvements than information brochures in caregiver burden (primary outcome) and in anxiety, depression, and self-efficacy over 8 weeks.

Introduction

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is a structured, evidence-based psychotherapeutic modality that aims to modify maladaptive cognitive processes, emotional regulation, and behavior patterns through guided interventions such as cognitive restructuring, exposure tasks, and skill training (Kim et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024). Online CBT, internet-based CBT (iCBT), or online counseling uses digital means to deliver interventions remotely through structured modules, therapist messaging, and multimedia (Duan et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025). This format is more accessible, particularly to people with low mobility or time limits, and is effective in treating mental health disorders such as depression, anxiety, and insomnia (Derry-Vick et al., 2025; Semple et al., 2024). In the caregiver support, a multidimensional burden is conceptualized as caregiver burden, which is defined as the strain of informal care providers (including emotional exhaustion, physical fatigue, financial burden, and role overload) and measures the strain through validated instruments like the Zarit Burden Interview and Caregiver Strain Index (Gao et al., 2022).

Online CBT applications in oncology have shifted from mainly patient-focused interventions to modulators of psychosocial distress, fatigue, and sleep dysfunction across different cancer stages (Greeley et al., 2025; Lewis-Smith et al., 2025). Meta-analytic data support the elements of the theory, suggesting that iCBT may result in average to significant patient anxiety, depression, and fatigue reduction, and exact positive changes in the quality of life in both early and late-stage cancer populations (Sears et al., 2025). Among them, patients with palliative care or already advanced symptoms stand out as a vulnerable, maybe a critical group that may need constant physical, emotional, and spiritual assistance. Under these conditions, caregiver burden builds up due to a long period of participating in care coordination, anticipatory grief, and uncertainty-based decision-making.

Family caregiving is a universal phenomenon, but caregivers' cultural contexts vary depending on the context. The joint responsibility for caring for their spouses or adult children in China is also based on family obligation norms rooted in Confucian traditions (Hosseini Koukamari et al., 2025). These cultural judgments may constrain outward assistance-seeking behavior, augment emotional self-expulsion, and yield greater levels of suppressed psychological burden. For example, González et al. (2024) reported that among all family caregivers they sampled, more than 80 % were female offspring, and in most cases, focused more on patient needs than on patient mental health, even when distress scores were also at a clinical level. Nevertheless, these results need to be widely validated not only in localized studies but also across various caregiving models.

Even though most existing studies have investigated psychosocial interventions for caregivers, these studies are not rigorously designed, including randomization, longitudinal follow-up, and standard measures of outcome (Yan et al., 2025). Meta-analyses indicate anxious and depressive symptoms slightly but significantly decrease in caregivers using digital instruments; however, there is not enough evidence specifically examining structured CBT protocols as viable measures of dealing with the specific stressors of caregiving in advanced cancer (Lu et al., 2024). Specifically, few trials contrast general psychoeducational platforms with specific CBT interventions that include validated cognitive reframing or behavioral activation elements.

Some recent research in Chinese settings has provided encouraging yet initial results. Bauman et al. (2025) found psychological preparedness and improved communication following a CBT-style psychoeducational intervention, but the study lacked validation of outcomes using psychometric measures. Equally, Shaffer et al. (2024) noted lower emotional strain and insomnia severity in caregivers in an online CBT-I nine-week intervention group, but noted that the lack of a control group and limited sample diversity limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, the very notion of tailoring is not consistently implemented; not many interventions will include culturally specific modules, adaptive pacing, or linguistic responsiveness based on the role of a caregiver (Benson et al., 2024).

Shen et al. (2025) conducted a meta-analysis of 12 internet-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy programs for cancer caregiving and reported modest reductions in anxiety and depression with inconsistent effects on burden; however, inference was limited by heterogeneous comparators and outcome definitions. Applebaum et al. (2022) ran a web-based randomized controlled trial contrasting CBT with Emotion Regulation Therapy and observed stronger short-term reductions in anxiety and depression for CBT; the four-week follow-up curtailed conclusions on resilience and maintenance. Bodschwinna et al. (2022) analyzed therapist-supported versus self-guided formats and found higher module completion and better emotion regulation with guided delivery; uneven therapist exposure introduced delivery bias. Wrede et al. (2023) compared module components and identified behavioral activation and problem-solving as the most consistent drivers of mood improvement; caregiver-specific endpoints were underrepresented. Across these core studies, the signal favors therapist-guided, structured CBT, yet standardization of comparators, module composition, and caregiver-focused outcomes remains insufficient to attribute effects to specific therapeutic ingredients.

Jacobs et al. (2025) evaluated a self-guided digital intervention in a randomized design and found improvements in caregiver burden, PTSD symptoms, and quality of life at 3 months; limited sample diversity and the absence of long-term follow-up constrained generalizability. Zhai et al. (2023) reviewed 22 digital caregiver interventions and noted gains in at least two domains, most often depression and self-efficacy, while inconsistent engagement reporting impeded mechanism testing. Li et al. (2022) pooled outcomes across 16 studies and linked therapist contact to stronger retention and moderate reductions in burden and depression; uncontrolled confounding reduced certainty about the contact effect. Cote-Allard et al. (2023) used machine-learning models to predict dropout and showed that high early-session intensity reliably forecast full completion; causal links between engagement and symptom change were not established. Hong et al. (2023) synthesized predictors of adherence and highlighted the value of guided pacing and weekly support; however, heterogeneous adherence definitions limited cross-study comparability. These gaps motivate the present randomized trial, which uses standardized caregiver outcomes and a defined control.

The control group comprised standardized information materials that summarize evidence-based resources for family caregivers (education on symptoms, communication, and available services) without any cognitive or behavioral therapy techniques or therapist guidance. This usual-care comparator reflects routine psychosocial oncology practice, preserves ethical access to support resources, and controls for attention and expectancy while avoiding contamination by cognitive-behavioral therapy–specific mechanisms (cognitive restructuring, behavioral activation, or skills rehearsal). Selecting an information-only control enhances internal validity for estimating the added effect of therapist-guided, internet-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy on caregiver burden (primary outcome) and on anxiety, depression, and caregiver self-efficacy (secondary outcomes). It provides a pragmatic benchmark for implementation contexts where structured psychotherapy is not routinely offered.

Objectives

This study aims to: (i) ascertain how an eight-week online CBT program could play a crucial role in reducing the burden of caregivers among family members of end-stage cancer patients (ii) assess its effect on the secondary outcomes of anxiety and depression (iii) measure engagement procedures, including module completion and session attendance to identify caregiver behavior patterns that forecast psychological progress.

Methods

Study design

A randomized, controlled, two-armed parallel-group trial was adopted, comparing an online CBT platform with a control (digital caregiving information). The allocation ratio was 1:1. There were outcome assessments at week 8, week 4, and baseline. The design ensured fidelity, engagement tracking, and focused repeated outcome measurement. The control group accessed an information brochure hosted on the study portal, comprising 6–8 pages each, brief text pages (≈500–700 words), and two 3–5-minute explainer videos. Content covered symptom management, communication with clinicians, stress management, and service navigation; materials provided hyperlinks to reputable public resources. No cognitive behavioral therapy techniques or therapist guidance were included, and access frequency matched the intervention schedule (weekly releases) to control for attention. The control group reflects usual-care practice in psychosocial oncology, providing education without psychotherapy content. This comparator controls for information exposure and researcher attention while avoiding contamination with cognitive or behavioral therapy mechanisms, enabling estimation of the added effect of internet-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy.

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Jiangxi Manufacturing Vocational and Technical College (Approval No. AD2024-0157), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed digital consent was obtained from all participants after a comprehensive explanation of the study aims, procedures, risks, benefits, and data privacy measures.

Study setting

The experiment took place remotely in a secure online environment hosted on university servers. The participants were able to access the intervention and take the tests using web-enabled devices, thereby improving geographic and time flexibility. Technical infrastructure facilitated consistent delivery in various internet bandwidths. The trial was not preregistered in a public registry. A prespecified protocol covering objectives, eligibility, outcomes, and the statistical analysis plan was time-stamped and archived on the institutional repository before enrollment (archived March 1, 2025).

Participants

Recruitment

Participants were recruited from March to July 2025 via oncology outpatient clinics at three tertiary hospitals in urban China (see Fig. 1). Oncology nurses identified eligible caregivers through medical records and directly approached them during routine patient visits to briefly introduce the study. Interested caregivers provided their contact details, which were forwarded to research personnel. Eligibility screening was conducted remotely via telephone, including digital literacy assessment and psychiatric screening, followed by electronic informed consent. During the recruitment window, caregivers assessed for eligibility n = 412; excluded n = 212 [not meeting inclusion criteria n = 140; declined n = 56; other reasons n = 16]; eligible and consented n = 200; completed baseline and were randomized n = 200. Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were clearly operationalized as follows: (i) age ≥ 18 years; (ii) identified as the primary informal caregiver of a patient diagnosed with histologically confirmed stage III or IV solid tumor; (iii) providing active care for ≥3 consecutive months; (iv) Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI-22) score ≥ 21 indicating moderate-to-severe burden; (v) regular access to and familiarity with smartphone or computer-based internet use; (vi) proficiency in Mandarin or English sufficient for digital content comprehension. Exclusion criteria included: (i) current psychiatric hospitalization or severe mental illness diagnosis identified through Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) (Sheehan et al., 1998), specifically excluding active psychosis, bipolar disorder, or major depression; (ii) receipt of formal CBT or structured psychotherapy within past 12 months, confirmed by the patient/medical history; (iii) cognitive impairment that is measured based on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (Nasreddine et al., 2005) with a score lower than 24; (iv) unstable internet, which is characterized by internet speeds that are continuously <2 Mbps or ≥3 internet disconnections per week during initial screening; (v) inability or reluctance to give informed consent.

Power analysis

The calculation of the sample size was made upon the identification of a clinically significant difference of 6 points on the Zarit burden Interview (ZBI-22), standard deviation of 12, 80 % statistical power, and a significance level (α) of 0.05. The value obtained was 86 participants per group. Based on the expected attrition rate (15 %), the ultimate recruitment objective was 200 caregivers, providing sufficient power to obtain primary outcomes and scheduled subgroup analyses.

Randomization and bias minimization

Stratification was done by recruiting sites and by digital literacy level, measured with the 21-item Digital Health Literacy Instrument (DHLI) scale, a validated measure with a publicly available, culturally tailored version used in the Chinese population (van der Vaart and Drossaert, 2017). After stratification, members of different strata were randomly allocated to the control and CBT intervention groups at a 1:1 ratio, using computer-generated random sequences. Randomization was performed with a computer-generated sequence (using the reproducible Mersenne Twister algorithm) and permuted blocks of variable sizes (4 and 6), stratified by site and the digital level of health literacy. The allocation was hidden through sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by a third-party administrator; the envelopes were not opened until the baseline assessment was administered, so that foreknowledge of assignment did not occur.

Standardized staff training and written recruitment specifications were used to minimize selection bias. Measurement bias was also minimised using validated Mandarin-language tools, electronically administered and automatically graded. Regular weekly reminders, proactive phone follow-up when assessments are missed, and a flexible schedule were among the mitigation measures to reduce attrition bias. The risk of contamination was mitigated by providing clear instructions for sharing intervention credentials and by conducting frequent audits to identify anomalies in user access logs or IP addresses.

Intervention

Control condition

The members of the control group were provided with conventional caregiving education materials in PDF format on a digital platform, sent to them by email once a week over 8 weeks. The weekly packages had three elements: (i) caregiving self-help instructions which included stress management, communication and practical caregiving skills (ii) information brochures of advanced cancer symptom management, based on the guidelines published by the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO); and (iii) services to local and online caregiver support services, including helplines, hospital-based guidance services and community assistance programs. The automated delivery was performed every Monday at 9:00 am via a secure email scheduling system on an institutional server. To receive the content, delivery reports were automatically tracked, and undelivered mail was automatically forwarded to the research personnel, who made calls within 24 h of receipt to resolve technical issues and confirm the problem.

Online CBT intervention

The internet-based CBT intervention was implemented through an interactive web-based system developed and delivered by Beijing MindWell Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The platform consisted of eight weekly modules, each lasting around 60 min. Such modules covered (i) psychoeducation of caregiver stress; (ii) cognitive restructuring skills; (iii) behavioral activation Skills; (iv) emotion regulation skills; (v) mindfulness-based relaxation skills; (vi) training adaptive coping skills; (vii) anticipatory grief skills; (viii) caregiving self-efficacy skills. The material was delivered in brief instructional videos (8–10 min per module), interactive quizzes (5–7 questions per module), reflective journaling tasks, and downloadable PDF worksheets. Each medical worker had a certified clinical psychologist who received CBT training and provided individualized asynchronous feedback within 48 h of module completion. The input was provided through the platform's secure messaging application, which was typically 150–200 words in length and aimed at clarifying the content, promoting adherence, strengthening learned skills, and offering personalized coping techniques. Mobile and desktop devices were also compatible and required a standard web browser (Chrome, Safari, Firefox) and ≥2 Mbps of internet speed to play the videos stably. Technical support was also available through a 24-hour online chat support room, manned by the Beijing Mindwell Technology team.

Monitoring and fidelity controls

Participants' attendance was under strict observation, as real-time platform analytics recorded module completion rates, session progress, and therapist-message interactions. SMS and email notices and prompts were automatically sent to participants 24 h before the relevant weekly session, and again if no log-in was observed within 48 h after the session. Follow-up telephone calls were made to research staff to evaluate potential issues after 72 h without logging in and to encourage their participation. The measure of adherence was the completion of ≥6 modules (≥75 %) of a course, which was authenticated by platform logs. The definition of dropout included missing two consecutive modules without having any contact with the platform, regardless of multiple attempts to contact the platform. Adverse events such as psychological disturbance or suicidality had been actively observed through weekly online questionnaires (GAD-7 based on (Spitzer et al., 2006a, Spitzer et al., 2006b) and PHQ-9 based on (Kroenke et al., 2001)) incorporated in every module. The alerts created by scores of severe anxiety (≥15 on GAD-7) or severe depression (≥20 on PHQ-9) automatically activated an alert to the clinical team, initiating a telephone communication by the allotted psychologist and a follow-up device to the clinical follow-up in case of necessity. Therapist messages and therapy module delivery were biweekly reviewed by a senior psychologist at a University, who reviewed 10 % of random therapist interactions and rated message content adherence; deviations were discussed at supervisor meetings.

Instruments

Primary outcome

The main outcome was caregiver burden, appraised using the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI-22), a core, validated, publicly available instrument created by Zarit et al. (1980). The ZBI-22 has 22 questions, with answers measured on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = “Never” to 4 = “Nearly Always”). The maximum score is 88, with higher scores indicating a greater caregiver burden. Perceived strain in role and emotional areas is reflected in caregiver burden as measured by the Zarit Burden Interview-22.

Examples of items include: “Do you feel stressed taking care of your relative and attempting to fulfill other duties to your family or employment? Do you think that you have suffered in terms of health due to being involved with your relative? There are set cutoffs that indicate mild (0−20), moderate (21–40), and severe burden (41–88). The Chinese version shows high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.91) and high test-retest reliability (r = 0.89), as demonstrated in Chinese caregiver populations (Cheah et al., 2012; Tang et al., 2017).

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes were anxiety, depression, and caregiving self-efficacy: Anxiety was assessed by the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7), which is publicly available, and has 7 items by Spitzer et al., 2006a, Spitzer et al., 2006b. The items are assessed on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “Not at all,” 3 = “Nearly every day”), and total scores range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety. Scores ≥10 are evidence of extreme anxiety. The Chinese version of GAD-7 showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.93) and test-retest reliability (r = 0.86). Depression was measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a 9-item scale developed by Kroenke et al. (2001).

Responses use a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “Not at all” to 3 = “Nearly every day”), giving total scores ranging from 0 to 27, with higher scores reflecting more severe depression. Scores ≥10 indicate clinical depression. The Chinese PHQ-9 demonstrates robust internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.89) and high test-retest reliability (r = 0.85).

Caregiving self-efficacy was measured using the Caregiver Self-Efficacy Scale (CSES), a publicly available, validated 15-item instrument developed by Steffen et al. (2002). Items are rated on a 10-point Likert scale (1 = “Not confident at all” to 10 = “Extremely confident”), yielding total scores ranging from 15 to 150, with higher scores indicating greater caregiving self-efficacy. The CSES Chinese version has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.94) and satisfactory test-retest reliability (r = 0.87).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). For each continuous outcome, a linear mixed-effects model estimated group (internet-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy vs control), time (baseline, week 4, week 8; categorical), and the group × time interaction, with the baseline value of the outcome included as a covariate. Site (three hospitals) was included as a fixed effect. Random effects comprised a subject-specific intercept; a random slope for time was added when models converged without a singular fit. Models were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom; residual diagnostics (QQ plots and standardized residuals vs. fitted) informed model adequacy. Marginal means and contrasts at weeks 4 and 8 were estimated with 95 % confidence intervals; standardized effect sizes (Cohen's d) were computed from model-based marginal means using pooled baseline SD. A responder analysis for clinical burden reduction (≥1-point decrease on ZBI-22) used logistic regression with group as the predictor and baseline burden as the covariate to obtain odds ratios and 95 % CIs. Pre-specified subgroup and moderation analyses were conducted based on digital literacy level, caregiver relationship, and baseline burden severity, using interaction terms in regression models. Sensitivity analyses employed multiple imputation for missing data and compared intention-to-treat versus per-protocol datasets. A two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 was applied for all statistical tests.

A significantly higher proportion of participants in the intervention group achieved clinically significant improvement in caregiver burden by week 8 (see Fig. 2C). In the control group, 19.0 % (n = 19) met the threshold of ≥6-point reduction on the Zarit Burden Interview compared to 67.0 % (n = 67) in the CBT intervention group. The odds ratio for achieving this improvement was 8.60 (95 % CI: 4.60, 16.08), with a statistically significant group difference (p = 0.005), indicating differential therapeutic responsiveness.

Depression outcomes assessed with the PHQ-9 showed reductions in the CBT group during the intervention period (see Fig. 3B). Subgroup and moderation analyses

Outcomes stratified by digital literacy level at Week 8 showed a greater benefit with higher digital skill (see Fig. 4A). Zarit Burden Inventory scores improved by −7.3, −8.9, and −12.2 in low, medium, and high literacy groups, respectively (F(2,194) = 5.62, p = 0.013). GAD-7 reductions were −3.4, −4.5, and −6.3 (F(2,194) = 4.78, p = 0.021), while PHQ-9 dropped by −4.0, −5.1, and −7.2 (F(2,194) = 5.09, p = 0.018). Discussion

The study shows that internet-based cognitive-behavioral therapy with a therapist led to changes in both the psychological and caregiving spheres, with between-group differences favoring the intervention over the information-only control. Initially, the demographic and clinical characteristics of both the intervention and control groups were similar, providing an equal basis for comparison. The CBT group showed improvement in caregiver burden, anxiety, and depressive symptoms over the eight weeks, and improvement in caregiving self-efficacy over the study period as compared to the control group. These gains were particularly significant among caregivers who were more digitally literate and those with a worse baseline burden. In such a way, the paper demonstrates the therapeutic value of CBT in the management of the psychological burden related to cancer caregiving and the ability of caregivers to better cope with their duties and become more self-confident. These trends align with an intervention-specific effect of therapist-facilitated cognitive-behavioral therapy on appraisal, coping efficacy, and mood regulation, in addition to informational support (Akdemir et al., 2024; Akkol-Solakoglu et al., 2021; Carr et al., 2023; Ebrahimi et al., 2025; Fossey et al., 2021; Gu et al., 2025).

The CBT intervention used in the study resulted in a decrease in anxiety and depression levels, with the intervention group decreasing on the GAD-7 as well as on the PHQ-9 at Week 8. The results align with Applebaum et al. (2022), who found an improvement in the level of anxiety and depression after web-based CBT, indicating more significant symptom reductions and better adherence than Emotion Regulation Therapy did in their study. As well, Shen et al. (2025) discovered that CBT interventions produced moderate effect sizes on anxiety and depression, with an equal number of studies also reporting a reduction in caregiver burden. In comparison with the control, the study showed greater improvements, especially in depression, which may have been facilitated by the synchronous mode of delivery and the explicit focus on caregivers' roles. Such enhancements observed could be sustained as therapeutic congruence between the cognitive restructuring factors of CBT and the prospective stressors of oncology caregiving, thereby boosting emotional regulation and daily coping strategies. The trend resonates with a process in which cognitive reappraisal and behavioral activation lessen perseverative worry and activity loss, thereby helping regulate emotions and build caregiving confidence, as well as providing informational support (Kudrick et al., 2023; Lisowska et al., 2023; Monroy González et al., 2024; Montalescot et al., 2024; Mooney et al., 2023; Mosher et al., 2024).

The results showed that the therapist-led internet-based cognitive behavioral therapy group had reduced depression and anxiety as compared to the control group. Similar studies also show identical tendencies, albeit with lower magnitudes. Mirhosseini et al. (2025) described a 6-week web-based CBT intervention that reported a reduction in depressive symptoms and a moderate decrease in burden, with an 84 % rate of program completion, attributed to the program's flexibility and perceived support. Considering these findings, the positive change observed in this study might be attributed to caregiver-specific content and its concomitant delivery, which increased engagement and cognitive reappraisal. Behavioral activation with customized stress reframing could have allowed a more successful break in the maladaptive thought cycles during high levels of chronic emotional load, which resulted in symptom improvement during eight weeks. The general trend is consistent with a system in which structured therapist feedback and module pacing enhance reappraisal and activity scheduling, converting platform use into quantifiable alterations in mood states rather than merely providing informational support (Raj et al., 2022; Reuther et al., 2025; Supiano et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2025).

The research found that caregivers' burden decreased and self-efficacy improved by Week 8 among CBT participants. This is directionally in line with previous digital CBT assessments, including Bodschwinna et al. (2022), who reported a reduction in distress, with user-assisted therapy formats with more modules than unassisted ones. The emotional coping and confidence of caregivers were also improved. Equally, Jacobs et al. (2025) reported declines in ZBI and PHQ-9 over a 3-month trial, with more caregivers reporting high confidence. In comparison, these results show that adaptive-scheduled and proactive engagement protocols for targeted CBT modules can help facilitate more profound skill development. The focus on the self-efficacy strategy's objectives of setting goals, mastery logging, and reframing scenarios probably led to the observed psychological relief and functional resilience, which aligns with recalibration of perceived control in emotionally complex situations. One possible hypothesis is that repeatedly working on mastery through systematic practice advanced self-efficacy, which subsequently lowered perceived burden by enhancing the assessment of caregiving requirements and more effective coping patterns.

The study found improvement in anxiety and depression by Week 8 of the therapist-referral internet-based cognitive behavioral therapy group, with previous mental health improvements of caregivers subjected to digital cognitive behavioral therapy being caregiver-focused. Shaffer et al. (2024) found that a 9-week internet-based CBT for insomnia (CBT—I) could enhance sleep quality and reduce emotional strain, and that higher baseline symptom severity was associated with greater user engagement. Similarly, Lu et al. (2024) summarized results of 12 randomized controlled trials based on web-based CBT and showed depressive and anxiety reduction in all formats of delivery. Such results suggest cognitive behavioral therapy as an option to change cognitive-emotional loops that stand behind the psychological distress. The modules focused on worry scheduling, behavioral activation, and cognitive restructuring could have helped alleviate symptoms, in combination with sleep hygiene and stress-de-escalation practices consistent with circadian and emotion regulation. This pattern is consistent with a mechanism in which therapist feedback and paced module practice reinforce reappraisal and activity scheduling, leading to a decrease in rumination and physiological arousal beyond the support of information.

Theoretical and practical implications

This study aligns with the cognitive-behavioral model, which holds that distress in caregivers can be reduced by addressing maladaptive cognitions and increasing coping efficacy. The results in practice justify the incorporation of structured, digitally delivered cognitive-behavioral therapy into caregiver support programs, especially in the oncology care environment. Modules guided by therapists potentially reduce burden, anxiety, and depression. They can enhance self-efficacy, offering a feasible, resource-efficient intervention for health systems with limited access to in-person mental health services and for non-urban populations with low access to such services.

Limitations and future research

Limitations include the use of self-reported instruments, which are prone to bias despite validation; a short follow-up period, which limits generalizability regarding durability; and the trial's lack of prospective registration. To strengthen subsequent research, objective indicators, such as platform usage logs and optional wearable sleep metrics, will be incorporated to corroborate self-reports, and follow-up will be extended to 6 months to assess maintenance of effects. A protocol specifying objectives, eligibility, outcomes, and the analysis plan was time-stamped and archived before enrollment.

Conclusion

In this randomized comparison with an information-only control, therapist-guided, internet-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy was associated with greater improvement in caregiver burden (primary outcome), anxiety, depression, and caregiving self-efficacy over eight weeks. Effects were consistent across stratified analyses and robust to intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses, indicating that a structured, therapist-supported digital cognitive-behavioral therapy program improved key psychological outcomes for family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer during the trial period.
