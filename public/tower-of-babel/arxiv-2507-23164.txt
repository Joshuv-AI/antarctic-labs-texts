A remark on equivariant Riemannian isometric embeddings preserving symmetries
Dmitri Burago Email: dyb6@psu.edu and Hongda Qiu Email: hjq5042@psu.edu

Abstract
This remark pertains to isometric embeddings endowed with certain geometric properties. We study two embeddings problems for the universal cover M M of an n n -dimensional Riemannian torus ( 𝕋 n , g ) (\mathbb{T}^{n},g) . The first concerns the existence of an isometric embedding of M M into a bounded subset of some Euclidean space ℝ D 1 \mathbb{R}^{D_{1}} , and the second one seeks an isometric embdding of M M that is equivariant with respect to the deck transformation group of covering map. By using a known trick in a novel way, our idea yields results with D 1 = N + 2 ​ n D_{1}=N+2n and D 2 = N + n D_{2}=N+n , where N N is the Nash dimension of 𝕋 n \mathbb{T}^{n} . However, we doubt whether these bounds are optimal.

The term “equivariant isometric embedding” was introduced in a number of works. Some progress has been made in special cases. Regarding the existence problem, there are many positive results based on Mostow’s theorem
[
6

that a compact manifold acted upon by a compact Lie group can be embedded into some Euclidean space. For instance, Moore

These works on specific examples, especially

M

Question
: Given a smooth manifold
M
acted upon by a group
G
, does there exist a
G

M
into some
\mathbb{R}^{D}
?

This question can be asked for various classes of
M
, such as tori, surfaces of higher genus, manifolds acted upon by a non-compact group and so on. Ideally, we hope that the dimension
D
equals the
Nash dimension
of
M
, which is defined as the smallest value
N_{Nash}
such that
M
equipped with any Riemannian metric can be isometrically embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{N_{Nash}}
. However, this is not always possible, and the discussion on this question can be categorized into four paradigms as follows.

1.

The group
G
cannot act on Euclidean spaces. This situation is sometimes referred to as a “non-existence theorem”. For instance, there are non-existence theorems for manifolds acted upon by a group having exponential growth. Manifolds with sectional curvatures bounded above by a negative constant are a good resource since their fundamental groups are hyperbolic (for a proof of this statement, see
[
9
, Theorem 8.4.28, p. 295]
).

2.

The group
G
is compact and can act on Euclidean spaces, though the bound for
D
depends on both
M
and
G

[
8
, Section 6]
.

3.

The group
G
is compact and can act on Euclidean spaces, and the bound for
D
depends only on
M
. There are such results for some special cases such as compact symmetric spaces
[
10
]
and Hermitian symmetric spaces
[
11
]
.

4.

The group
G
is non-compact and can act on Euclidean spaces. Little is explored about this case, and the precise bounds for
D

It is known that the only discrete groups that can act properly by isometires on Euclidean space are the Bieberbach groups. When
G
is assumed to be discrete and non-compact, it is essentially equivalent to consider finite extensions of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, bringing our attention to the case where
M
is the universal cover of a Riemannian torus, and
G
is the deck transformation group
\mathbb{Z}^{n}

Let

2
Proofs

We aim to construct isometric embeddings as mentioned in Problems 1 and 2. Both constructions begin in a similar manner, as follows.

We choose cyclic coordinates on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
such that
\mathbb{T}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. The lifted metric
\tilde{g}
then agrees with
g
on
M
acted upon by translations by integer vectors.

We start by decomposing
\tilde{g}
into the sum of a Riemannian metric
Q_{1}
and a small constant metric
Q_{2}
on
M
. To do so, we may take, for instance,
Q_{2}=c\mathop{\Sigma}_{i=1}^{n}dx_{i}^{2}
and choose
c
positive and small enough so that
Q_{1}=\tilde{g}-Q_{2}
remains positive definite on the bounded set
[0,1]^{n}
. Since
\tilde{g}
is periodic, such a choice of
c
ensures that
Q_{1}
is also positive definite on the entire manifold
M
.

By the construction above, both
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are smooth and define Riemannian metrics on
M
. It follows that
(M,\tilde{g})
is isometric to an
n
-dimensional submanifold of the product manifold
(M,Q_{1})\times(M,Q_{2})
via the diagonal map
x\mapsto(x,x)
.

Given
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
as metrics on
M
, we construct our embeddings as follows. We first immerse
(M,Q_{1})
into the torus
(\mathbb{T}^{n},Q_{1})
which is itself isometrically embedded into some Euclidean space via Nash embedding. Next, we isometrically embed
(M,Q_{2})
into a Euclidean space—specifically, into a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
for Problem 1 and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for Problem 2. We then pair the immersion of
(M,Q_{1})
and the embedding of
(M,Q_{2})
to obtain an isometric embedding, and show that this embedding resolves Problems 1 and 2.

Let us formalize the discussion above as the following two propositions.

Proposition 1
.

(M,Q_{1})
can be isometrically immersed into a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by some map
\Phi
.

Proof
.

First, the covering map from
(M,\tilde{g})
to
(\mathbb{T}^{n},g)
provides a natural immersion
\phi:M\to\mathbb{T}^{n},(x_{1},...,x_{n})\mapsto(\{x_{1}\},...,\{x_{n}\})
where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part function. Thus,
\phi
is an isometric immersion from
(M,Q_{1})
to
(\mathbb{T}^{n},Q_{1})
.
Next, the desired immersion is constructed as the composition of the covering map and the Nash embedding of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
into
\mathbb{R}^{N}
; that is,
\Phi=u\circ\phi:(M,Q_{1})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Proposition 2
.

(M,Q_{2})
can be isometrically embedded into a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
by some map
\Psi
.

Proof
.

Since
Q_{2}
is a constant metric,
(M,Q_{2})
is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The real line
\mathbb{R}^{1}
can be isometrically embedded into a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
via a map
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for instance, as a spiral curve bounded by an annulus (Figure
2
).

Since
M\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{1}\times...\times\mathbb{R}^{1}
, we can define
\Psi:(M,Q_{2})\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
as follows:

(x_{1},...,x_{n})\mapsto(\psi(x_{1}),...,\psi(x_{n})).

(1)

This map isometrically embeds
(M,Q_{2})
into a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

With
\Phi:(M,Q_{1})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
from Proposition
1
and
\Psi:(M,Q_{2})\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
from Proposition
2
, we are prepared to prove our results.

Theorem 1
(Isometric embedding into a bounded domain).

There exists an isometric embedding
E:(M,\tilde{g})\to\mathbb{R}^{N+2n}
such that
E(M)
is in a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N+2n}
.

Proof
.

Using our discussion above, we can isometrically embed
(M,\tilde{g})
into
(M,Q_{1})\times(M,Q_{2})
via the diagonal map
i:(M,\tilde{g})\to(M,Q_{1})\times(M,Q_{2}),x\mapsto(x,x)
. Therefore, we define

E=(\Phi,\Psi)\circ i:(M,\tilde{g})\to\mathbb{R}^{N+2n},x\mapsto(\Phi(x),\Psi(x)).

(2)

Since
\Psi
is an embedding (hence injective),
E
is an injective immersion. To show that
E
is an embedding, it suffices to show that it is proper, i.e, the preimage of every compact subset is compact.

3

3

In general, an injective immersion need not to be an embedding. The correct statement is that a proper injective immersion is exactly an embedding
[
14
, Proposition 4.22, p. 87]
. For an example that an injective immersion fails to be an embedding, see Example 4.19 (p. 86) of the same reference.

By our construction, for any compact set
K
in
E(M)
, its preimage is completely determined by its projection
K^{\prime}
onto the
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
factor; specifically,
E^{-1}(K)=\Psi^{-1}(K^{\prime})
. Since
K^{\prime}
is compact, it follows that
E^{-1}(K)
is also compact.

Since both
\Phi,\Psi
are Riemannian isometries onto their images,
E
is an isometric embedding. Therefore, the image
E(M)=\Phi(M)\times\Psi(M)
is bounded, which concludes the proof.

Next, we turn to the construction of an equivariant isometric embedding of
M
. Following Remark
1.1
, we carry out this construction in higher generality by considering
M
as an
n
-manifold acted upon by a Bieberbach group
B
. Let
\Gamma
denote the
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-factor of
B
. The quotient
M/\Gamma
is naturally a compact manifold. Consequently, Proposition
1
remains valid—if we replace
\mathbb{T}^{n}
with
M/\Gamma
and the covering map with the quotient map
M\to M/\Gamma
in its proof, the conclusion continues to hold. By pairing the immersion
\Phi:(M,Q_{1})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
from Proposition
1
with the isometry between
(M,Q_{2})
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2
(Equivariant isometric embedding).

There exists an isometric embedding
F:(M,\tilde{g})\to\mathbb{R}^{N+n}
such that any group action
d\in B
extends onto the whole
\mathbb{R}^{N+n}
properly.

Proof
.

Let
e
denote the isometry between
(M,Q_{2})
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define

F=(\Phi,e)\circ i:M\to\mathbb{R}^{N+n},x\mapsto(\Phi(x),e(x)),

(3)

where
i
is the diagonal map
i:(M,\tilde{g})\to(M,Q_{1})\times(M,Q_{2}),x\mapsto(x,x)
. Since
e
is injective by construction,
F
is an injective immersion. Following a similar step as at the beginning of the proof of Theorem
1
,
F
is an isometric embedding.

To show equivariantness, it suffices to extend any group action
d\in B
properly to an isometry
\tilde{d}
acting on the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{N+n}
such that for any
x\in M
,

\tilde{d}\cdot F(x)=F(d\cdot x).

(4)

The image
F(M)
is the product of an
n
-torus embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and an
n
-dimensional Euclidean space. One can think of the
n
-torus lying in the first
N
coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{N+n}
. Furthermore, for any group action
d
, the isometry
e
naturally induces a group action on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined by
d_{e}\cdot e(x)=e(d\cdot x),x\in M
.

Based on the discussion above, we define
\tilde{d}
as the group acting trivially on the first
N
coordinates and by
d_{e}
on the remaining
n
coordinates; that is,

\tilde{d}:\mathbb{R}^{N+n}\to\mathbb{R}^{N+n},(y_{1},...,y_{N+n})\mapsto(y_{1},...,y_{N},d_{e}(y_{N+1},...,y_{N+n})).

(5)

It follows that
\tilde{d}
is an isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{N+n}
satisfying the identity
4
. Thus the proof is concluded.

3
Discussion

We have a feeling that both problems admit solutions with
D_{1},D_{2}
smaller than those we have constructed. In particular, it is possible that in each case, it suffices to take
D=N
, the Nash dimension of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Beyond the
n
-torus, these related questions can be asked for any compact manifold.

Smoothness is yet another sensitive aspect for further study. In this work, we stay with the
C^{3}
category and define the Nash dimension without imposing any requirement on smoothness. It is known, however, that the Nash embedding theorem applies only to the
C^{k}
case with
k\geq 3
, and there are many negative results concerning isometric
C^{2}
-embeddings for specific manifolds (see, for instance,
[
2
, 1.3.12, p. 14 and Appendix 3, p. 41]
). Regarding equivariant isometric embeddings, one possibly can obtain a very different story in the
C^{2}
case.

There is also a hope for developing theories of equivariant isometric embeddings into non-Euclidean spaces. This is inspired by results concerning the existence of equivariant embeddings into more general spaces such as complex spaces, Bergman spaces
[
15
]
, Hilbert spaces
[
8
]
, metrizable
G
-spaces
[
16
]
and so on.
