Transcriptome and co-expression network analysis of the chick blastoderm during egg storage prioritize CTGF as a candidate associated with the dormant state
Authors: Jinping Cai, Guangjie Li, Liubin Yang, Mingzhe Wang, Li Rong, Yunxia Zhao, Xiaomei Wang, Shijun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Embryonic diapause is a reversible developmental arrest that enables embryos to survive unfavorable conditions. In poultry production, fertilized eggs are routinely stored at sub-incubation temperatures before setting, during which the chick blastoderm remains at an early stage and developmental progression is effectively restrained. However, the transcriptional basis underlying entry into and exit from this storage-induced dormant state remains poorly understood. Here, we performed RNA-seq on chick blastoderms collected at 3 stages, including freshly laid, cold-stored, and 4 h after rewarming. Differential expression analyses identified flip differentially expressed genes (flip DEGs) that changed in opposite directions during entry into and exit from storage, suggesting a reversible transcriptional program. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified the module most strongly associated with the dormant state. By intersecting module genes with flip DEGs and applying an integrated ranking framework, we prioritized connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) as a candidate associated with the dormant state. CTGF overexpression in chick embryonic fibroblasts reduced proliferation, increased the G0/G1 fraction, and decreased apoptosis, consistent with dormancy-like cellular features. RNA-seq of CTGF-overexpressing fibroblasts, followed by Hallmark gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), showed enhanced unfolded protein response and protein secretion programs together with reduced inflammatory, interferon, and p53/apoptosis-related signaling. These CTGF-associated transcriptional changes partially overlapped with storage-associated transcriptional features in blastoderms. Collectively, our findings characterize a reversible transcriptional program associated with storage-induced dormant state and nominate CTGF-associated remodeling and downstream transcriptomic features as candidate molecular features of embryo adaptation to egg storage.

Introduction

In birds, oviposition and the onset of sustained incubation are often temporally separated. During this pre-incubation interval, early embryos remain at sub-incubation temperatures and developmental progression is effectively restrained. In commercial poultry production, this feature is operationalized by routine egg storage before setting to synchronize hatchery operations (Fasenko, 2007). Although embryonic development can resume after rewarming, storage duration and conditions can markedly affect hatchability and chick quality (Reijrink et al., 2010; Kouame et al., 2021). Thus, the chick blastoderm during egg storage provides a biologically relevant and production-relevant model for studying reversible developmental arrest (Pokhrel et al., 2021).

Embryonic diapause is classically defined as a reversible developmental arrest in which growth-related activity is restrained while viability and competence for developmental resumption are preserved (Renfree and Shaw, 2000; Özgüldez and Bulut-Karslioğlu, 2024). Previous studies have described storage-associated changes in blastoderm morphology and apoptosis and have implicated specific signaling pathways in the establishment of temperature-induced arrest (Hamidu et al., 2011; Pokhrel et al., 2018; Ren et al., 2023). More broadly, work across diapause and diapause-like systems suggests that arrest is not simply a passive shutdown, but a coordinated state involving growth restraint together with remodeling that supports survival and homeostasis (Hussein et al., 2020; Fenelon, 2024). However, a system-level view of how the chick blastoderm is transcriptionally remodeled during entry into and exit from this reversible storage-associated arrest remains limited, and candidates linked to the dormant state have not been well defined.

Connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) is a matricellular factor downstream of TGF-β signaling with established roles in cell adhesion, survival, and growth control (Grotendorst, 1997; Holbourn et al., 2008). These properties make CTGF a biologically plausible candidate in the context of storage-associated dormant state, a condition in which growth restraint must be coupled with preservation of cellular integrity and viability. In the present study, we used RNA-seq of freshly laid, cold-stored, and rewarmed chick blastoderms to investigate transcriptional changes associated with storage-induced developmental restraint. We further integrated differential expression and co-expression network analyses to identify candidate genes associated with the dormant state and performed gain-of-function experiments in chick embryonic fibroblasts and in ovo to evaluate CTGF-associated cellular and transcriptional features.

Materials and methods

Study design

Fertilized eggs were obtained from an F2 resource population of Turpan gamecock × White Leghorn maintained at the Huazhong Agricultural University experimental chicken farm. This flock was used solely as an available source of fertilized eggs that allowed immediate post-oviposition sampling; the present study was not designed to assess breed or genetic background effects. Hens were artificially inseminated according to the routine management procedures of the experimental farm. Eggs were collected from multiple hens, and each egg contributed one blastoderm sample.

Newly laid eggs were collected within 15 min after oviposition and assigned to one of 3 groups: Normal (N), Dormant (D), and Rewarmed 4 h (R4h). For the Normal group, blastoderms were collected from freshly laid eggs. For the Dormant group, eggs were transferred to a 17°C egg storage room maintained at 70% relative humidity and stored for 3 days. For the R4h group, a separate batch of eggs subjected to the same 3-day storage conditions was then transferred to an incubator (model YFDF-19200 single-stage egg incubator, Beijing Yunfeng Limin Livestock Equipment Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and rewarmed at 38°C and 65% relative humidity for 4 h before blastoderm collection.

In addition to these 3 main groups, a supplementary R40min group was included to assess the immediate early response to rewarming. Eggs in this group underwent the same 3-day storage treatment as those in the R4h group and were then transferred to the same incubator under the same rewarming conditions, but blastoderms were collected after 40 min of rewarming instead of 4 h.

For RNA-seq, 5 biologically independent blastoderms were analyzed in each group. To obtain these samples, approximately 10 to 15 fertilized eggs were collected for each treatment group.

Blastoderm staging and sampling

At each sampling time point (N, D, R40min, and R4h), the blunt end of the egg was carefully opened to expose the blastoderm region. Blastoderms were staged according to morphological criteria (Eyal-Giladi and Kochav, 1976). Each blastoderm was gently dissected using fine forceps and briefly rinsed in ice-cold PBS (catalog no. C10010500BT, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA) to remove residual yolk. For RNA-seq, 5 biological replicates were collected per group, with each replicate derived from an independent egg. Samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after dissection and stored at -80°C until RNA extraction.

RNA-seq data processing and differential expression analysis

Raw paired-end sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using FastQC (v0.12.1). Clean reads that passed quality control were then aligned to the chicken (Gallus gallus) reference genome (GRCg6a; GCF_000002315.5) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) (Kim et al., 2019). The resulting SAM files were converted to BAM format, sorted, and indexed using SAMtools (v1.21) (Li et al., 2009). Transcript quantification was performed using StringTie (v3.0.0) (Pertea et al., 2015). Differential expression analysis was performed in R (v4.4.3) using the DESeq2 package (v1.46.0) (Love et al., 2014). P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as genes with padj < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1.

Definition of flip DEGs

Genes that were differentially expressed in both the Dormant vs. Normal and R4h vs. Dormant comparisons, but showed opposite directions of change, were defined as “flip differentially expressed genes” (flip DEGs). Genes that were significantly downregulated in Dormant vs. Normal (log2FC_DvsN ≤ –1, padj < 0.05) and significantly upregulated in R4h vs. Dormant (log2FC_R4hvsD ≥ 1, padj < 0.05) were classified as “Down→Up” flip DEGs. Conversely, genes that were significantly upregulated in Dormant vs. Normal (log2FC_DvsN ≥ 1, padj < 0.05) and significantly downregulated in R4h vs. Dormant (log2FC_R4hvsD ≤ −1, padj < 0.05) were classified as “Up→Down” flip DEGs.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) (Ashburner et al., 2000) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (Kanehisa and Goto, 2000) enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.14.6) (Yu et al., 2012). Bar plots and bubble plots were generated using clusterProfiler and ggplot2 (v3.5.1).

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis was performed using the WGCNA package (v1.73) (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008) on variance-stabilized transformation (VST)-transformed expression matrices to identify co-expression modules associated with the dormant state. Genes were filtered by median absolute deviation (MAD), and the top 70% most variable genes for network analysis. Signed networks were constructed using the blockwiseModules function with minModuleSize = 100 and mergeCutHeight = 0.22.

Module eigengenes (MEs) were correlated with group traits (N, D, and R4h) to identify modules strongly associated with the dormant state. For each gene, module membership (MM), defined as the correlation between gene expression and the corresponding module eigengene, and gene significance (GS) were calculated. Eigengene-based connectivity (kME) was used as the measure of intramodular connectivity.

Candidate gene prioritization

To prioritize candidate genes associated with the dormant state, we constructed a composite scoring framework that integrated multiple features relevant to downstream candidate selection, because no single metric was sufficient for candidate prioritization in this context. 5 indices were included: module membership in the brown module (kME_brown), the absolute log2 fold change in Dormant vs. Normal (|log2FC_DvsN|), the absolute log2 fold change in R4h vs. Dormant (|log2FC_R4hvsD|), a significance index (SIG), and a low-expression penalty term (LOW). SIG was defined as the larger of the 2 −log10-transformed adjusted p-values from the 2 differential comparisons. LOW was derived from the mean expression level, with larger values assigned to genes with lower expression. To place all indices on a comparable scale and reduce the influence of extreme values, each index was transformed into a robust Z-score using the median and median absolute deviation (MAD) across all genes. The transformed indices were denoted as z_kME, z_LFC1, z_LFC2, z_SIG, and z_LOW. The final raw score was calculated as follows: score_raw = 0.40 × z_kME + 0.22 × z_LFC1 + 0.22 × z_LFC2 + 0.26 × z_SIG − 0.40 × z_LOW

Genes were ranked according to score_raw. To assess ranking robustness, the raw scheme was compared with 3 alternative schemes: an equal-weight scheme (equal), a scheme omitting the significance term (noSIG), and a network-focused scheme based primarily on module connectivity (netonly). Rank concordance across all candidates, overlap among top-ranked candidates, and the ranks of selected genes are summarized in Supplementary Tables S9-S11, and the full rankings are provided in Supplementary Table S12.

Plasmid construction

The full-length coding sequence of chicken CTGF was amplified from pooled blastoderm cDNA and cloned into pcDNA3.1(+) (laboratory stock) under the control of the CMV promoter to generate the overexpression plasmid OE-CTGF. The cDNA template was used solely for amplification of the CTGF coding sequence for plasmid construction. The empty vector was used as a negative control (NC). All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Isolation and culture of chicken embryonic fibroblasts

Chicken embryonic fibroblasts (CEFs) were isolated from embryos at embryonic day 10-11 (E10-E11; E1 = the first day of incubation). Embryos were collected under sterile conditions, and the head, limbs, viscera, and tail were removed. The remaining tissues were minced and digested with Trypsin-EDTA (catalog no. 25200-056, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA) for 8 min to obtain CEFs. Cells were cultured in DMEM (catalog no. C11995500BT, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; catalog no. AUS-01S-02-S, Cellbox Bioproducts, Hong Kong, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (catalog no. 15070063, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA) at 39°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

CEF transfection

CEFs were transfected with either the OE-CTGF plasmid or the empty vector control (NC) using Lipofectamine 3000 (catalog no. L3000015, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA) when cells reached 70% to 80% confluence, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

CCK-8 assay for proliferation of transfected CEFs

To evaluate the effect of CTGF overexpression on CEFs proliferation, CEFs were seeded into 96-well plates and transfected as described above. At 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-transfection, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (catalog no. C0042, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added to each well containing 100 μL of culture medium, and the plates were incubated at 39°C for 1 to 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a multimode microplate reader (Synergy H1, Agilent BioTek, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA).

Cell cycle analysis of transfected CEFs

Cell-cycle distribution was analyzed using a Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis Kit (catalog no. C1052, Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). CEFs transfected as described above were harvested at 24 h post-transfection, washed with PBS, and fixed in ice-cold 70% ethanol at 4°C overnight. After fixation, the cells were washed and stained with RNase A and propidium iodide (PI) in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. DNA content was analyzed by flow cytometry using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA).

Apoptosis analysis of transfected CEFs

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (catalog no. KTA0006, Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). CEFs transfected as described above were harvested at 48 h post-transfection, washed with PBS, and resuspended in binding buffer, followed by staining with Annexin V-FITC and PI according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were analyzed by flow cytometry using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA) within 1 h.

In ovo overexpression of CTGF

To evaluate the effect of CTGF overexpression on early embryonic development in vivo, an independent in ovo overexpression experiment was performed. Fertilized eggs were pre-incubated at 38°C and 65% relative humidity for 24 h. A small window was opened in the eggshell, and lipid-based transfection complexes containing either the OE-CTGF plasmid or the empty vector control were injected into the subgerminal cavity under a stereomicroscope (model 305, Carl Zeiss Microscopy, LLC, White Plains, NY). After injection, the shell window was sealed with PARAFILM® M sealing film (catalog no. P7668-1EA, Heathrow Scientific, LLC, Vernon Hills, IL), and the eggs were returned to the incubator for an additional 48 h at 38°C and 65% relative humidity. Embryonic development was then evaluated by morphological examination. Blastoderm tissues were collected for RT-qPCR analysis to confirm CTGF overexpression. A total of 15 fertilized eggs were used in each group.

RNA-seq of OE-CTGF- and NC-transfected CEFs

To dissect the downstream transcriptional network associated with CTGF overexpression, RNA-seq was performed on CEFs transfected with either the OE-CTGF plasmid or the empty vector control (NC). Library preparation, sequencing, and data preprocessing followed the procedures described above. Differential expression analysis of OE-CTGF vs. NC was carried out with DESeq2 using the same thresholds as for embryo samples (padj < 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1). GO enrichment analysis was performed separately for upregulated and downregulated DEGs as described above. To evaluate pathway-level transcriptional changes, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (Subramanian et al., 2005) was performed using the fgsea package (v1.32.4) with Hallmark gene sets from MsigDB (Liberzon et al., 2015). Hallmark gene sets corresponding to Gallus gallus were obtained using the msigdbr package and mapped to chicken gene symbols. Pathways with adjusted p-values (padj) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from blastoderm tissues and CEFs using TRIzol reagent (catalog no. 15596026, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA). cDNA was synthesized using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Perfect Real Time) (catalog no. RR036A, Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 real-time PCR system (Roche Diagnostics Corp., Indianapolis, IN) using NovoStart® SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (catalog no. E096, Novoprotein Scientific Inc., Suzhou, China). Primers were designed using Primer-BLAST, and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S8. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM from at least 3 independent biological replicates, unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2) and R (v4.4.3). Comparisons between 2 groups were performed using an unpaired, 2-tailed Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test. The statistical tests used, sample sizes, and significance annotations are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Transcriptomic remodeling of the chick blastoderm during low-temperature egg storage and rewarming

To characterize transcriptomic remodeling during low-temperature egg storage and subsequent rewarming, we performed RNA-seq on blastoderms collected from freshly laid eggs (Normal, N), eggs stored at 17°C for 3 d (Dormant, D), and stored eggs rewarmed at 38°C for 4 h (R4h) (Fig. 1A). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear separation of Normal, Dormant, and R4h samples, indicating substantial transcriptomic divergence among the 3 conditions (Fig. 1B). To assess the early transcriptional response to rewarming, we additionally profiled a supplementary R40min group generated under the same storage and rewarming conditions as the R4h group but sampled after only 40 min of rewarming. R40min samples largely overlapped with the Dormant cluster in PCA (Supplementary Fig. S1b). Consistent with this pattern, differential expression analysis identified substantially fewer DEGs in R40min vs. Dormant (724 DEGs) than in R4h vs. Dormant (1,955 DEGs) (Supplementary Fig. S1c). Together, these results suggest that transcriptomic divergence from the Dormant state remains limited at 40 min of rewarming and is more apparent after 4 h. We therefore focused subsequent analyses on the R4h group as a more established rewarming state.

Volcano plots revealed extensive transcriptional changes during both entry into storage (Dormant vs. Normal; Fig. 1D) and rewarming (R4h vs. Dormant; Fig. 1E). In the Dormant vs. Normal comparison, 2,521 genes were upregulated and 1,077 were downregulated, whereas in the R4h vs. Dormant comparison, 694 genes were upregulated and 1,261 were downregulated. The opposite overall direction of change between these 2 comparisons suggested that some genes may be regulated in opposite directions during storage and rewarming. We therefore next asked whether a reversible transcriptional program could be defined by genes showing such flip patterns across the 2 transitions.

Flip DEGs reveal reversible transcriptional programs during storage and rewarming

A total of 758 DEGs were shared between the Dormant vs. Normal and R4h vs. Dormant comparisons (Fig. 2A). Among these shared DEGs, 646 exhibited opposite-direction changes across the 2 transitions and were therefore defined as flip differentially expressed genes (flip DEGs), including 574 Up→Down flip DEGs (upregulated in Dormant vs. Normal and downregulated in R4h vs. Dormant) and 72 Down→Up flip DEGs (downregulated in Dormant vs. Normal and upregulated in R4h vs. Dormant) (Fig. 2B). The remaining shared DEGs did not meet the flip criterion and were not included in this category. Functional enrichment analyses indicated distinct biological themes for the 2 classes of flip DEGs. Up→Down flip DEGs were primarily enriched for processes related to tissue organization, cell-cell interaction, and signaling, including cell-cell adhesion, epithelial organization, receptor-linked signaling, and vesicle trafficking (Fig. 2D). In contrast, Down→Up flip DEGs were enriched mainly for biosynthetic, metabolic, and membrane-related processes, including nucleotide and lipid metabolism, membrane organization, and redox-related functions (Fig. 2E). GO and KEGG enrichment results for all up- and downregulated DEGs in each contrast are provided in Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3 and Table S7.

Together, these findings suggest that a subset of genes undergoes coordinated opposite-direction regulation during storage and rewarming, thereby defining a reversible transcriptional program and providing a basis for subsequent network-based candidate prioritization.

Co-expression network analysis identifies a dormant-state-associated module and prioritizes CTGF as a candidate associated with the dormant state

To identify candidate genes associated with the reversible transcriptional program underlying the dormant state, we next performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) on blastoderm transcriptomes. One sample (H2) was identified as an outlier and excluded from network construction; module-trait relationships were largely preserved before and after exclusion (Supplementary Fig. S4a, b). Using a soft-thresholding power of 8 (Supplementary Fig. S4c), we identified 14 co-expression modules.

Among these modules, the brown module showed the strongest positive association with the Dormant group, with an eigengene correlation of r = 0.88 and an FDR of 0.000545 (Fig. 3A). Consistently, brown module eigengene values were consistently higher in Dormant than in Normal or R4h (Fig. 3B), and gene significance for the Dormant group was strongly correlated with module membership within this module (Spearman r = 0.81; Supplementary Fig. S4d). To refine the candidate set for prioritization, we intersected brown module genes with flip DEGs and obtained 398 overlapping genes (Fig. 3C). These genes were ranked using a composite score that integrated network connectivity and expression-based evidence, as described in Materials and Methods. The overall ranking pattern was broadly preserved across alternative scoring schemes, and CTGF remained among the top-ranked candidates across alternative scoring schemes (Supplementary Tables S9-S12). Among the top-ranked genes with annotated symbols, CTGF was prioritized for functional follow-up because it combined a high composite rank, a clear dormant-enriched expression pattern, and reported links to extracellular signaling, cell adhesion, and growth control (Grotendorst, 1997; Holbourn et al., 2008) (Fig. 3D, E). RT-qPCR further confirmed that CTGF expression was higher in the Dormant group than in the Normal and R4h groups, consistent with the RNA-seq data (Fig. 3E / Fig. 2C). Together, these analyses prioritize CTGF as a candidate associated with the dormant state for subsequent functional evaluation.

CTGF overexpression is associated with dormancy-like cellular features

To assess whether CTGF overexpression was associated with cellular and developmental features consistent with a dormant-like state, we next performed gain-of-function experiments in vitro and in ovo. To establish an in vitro model, chick embryonic fibroblasts (CEFs) were transfected with a CTGF overexpression plasmid. We next examined the effect of CTGF overexpression on cell proliferation and cell-cycle distribution. CCK-8 assays showed that OE-CTGF cells proliferated more slowly than control cells (Fig. 4B). Flow-cytometric analysis further showed that OE-CTGF increased the proportion of cells in G0/G1, indicating a shift toward G0/G1 accumulation (Fig. 4C). Because reduced proliferation can reflect either increased cell death or cell-cycle restraint, we also quantified apoptosis. CTGF overexpression did not increase apoptosis; instead, the apoptotic fraction was significantly lower in OE-CTGF cells than in controls (Fig. 4D). Consistently, RT-qPCR showed reduced expression of multiple apoptosis- and stress-related genes following CTGF overexpression (Fig. 4F).

To explore whether CTGF overexpression might also be associated with altered embryonic development in vivo, the OE-CTGF construct was delivered in ovo. CTGF-injected embryos showed a trend toward delayed development relative to controls, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4E). RT-qPCR confirmed increased CTGF expression in injected embryos (Supplementary Fig. S5a). Together, these findings suggest that CTGF overexpression is associated with dormancy-like cellular features in CEFs, with limited in ovo support in the form of a trend toward delayed embryonic development.

CTGF overexpression reshapes downstream pathways and shares transcriptional features with the dormant state

To determine whether CTGF overexpression was accompanied by broader transcriptional remodeling, we performed RNA-seq on OE-CTGF and empty-vector control CEFs. PCA showed clear separation between the 2 groups, consistent with distinct global expression profiles following CTGF overexpression (Supplementary Fig. S6a). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis highlighted distinct functional themes among these DEGs. Downregulated genes were enriched mainly for immune/inflammatory and cell death-related processes, with additional enrichment of migration- and adhesion-related categories (Supplementary Fig. S6c, d). In contrast, upregulated genes were enriched for cytoskeletal organization, secretion- and protein-localization-related processes, and selected cell-cycle-associated categories (Supplementary Fig. S6e, f).

Hallmark GSEA further supported this pattern. The most prominent positively enriched signatures included Unfolded Protein Response and Protein Secretion, whereas the most prominent negatively enriched signatures included Interferon Gamma Response, TNFA signaling via NF-κB, p53 pathway, and apoptosis (Fig. 5C). Additional Hallmark gene sets are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

To assess the relationship between the CTGF-induced in vitro program and the in vivo dormant-state transcriptome, we compared OE-CTGF vs. control CEFs with Dormant vs. Normal embryos. A total of 442 DEGs were shared between the 2 datasets (Supplementary Fig. S6b). Gene-level expression changes showed a positive correlation between OE-CTGF CEFs and dormant embryos, whereas the correlation was negative relative to the transcriptional changes observed during rewarming (Fig. 5B). Consistently, hierarchical clustering of the shared DEGs showed broadly similar directions of regulation across the in vitro and in vivo comparisons (Fig. 5D). Together, these results suggest that CTGF overexpression induces a transcriptional program that partially overlaps with, and is directionally consistent with, key features of the dormant-state transcriptome.

Discussion

In commercial poultry production, fertilized eggs are commonly stored at sub-incubation temperatures before setting, and prolonged storage can compromise hatchability and chick quality (Fasenko, 2007; Reijrink et al., 2010). Although this process is routine, the molecular basis by which the chick blastoderm enters and exits this reversible storage-induced dormant state remains incompletely understood (Pokhrel et al., 2021; Brady et al., 2022). In the present study, we defined a reversible transcriptional program across freshly laid, cold-stored, and rewarmed blastoderms and prioritized CTGF as a candidate associated with the dormant state. Functional analyses further showed that CTGF overexpression was associated with cellular features and transcriptomic changes consistent with aspects of a dormant state.

The flip DEG patterns suggest that storage-induced dormant state is accompanied by a coordinated and reversible transcriptional program rather than representing only a passive reduction in activity (Ren et al., 2023; Fenelon, 2024). Genes downregulated during entry into dormancy and restored after rewarming were mainly related to biosynthetic and growth-supporting functions, suggesting a reversible suppression of anabolic activity under unfavorable conditions (He et al., 2019). In contrast, genes elevated in the dormant state were enriched in signaling, adhesion, epithelial organization, and homeostatic processes. Together, these findings suggest that the dormant blastoderm restrains growth-related activity while maintaining viability for developmental resumption (Ko et al., 2017; Pokhrel et al., 2022).

CTGF was prioritized because several independent signals converged on this gene. It displayed a clear flip pattern, was strongly associated with the Dormant-related co-expression module, and has been implicated in extracellular signaling, cell adhesion, survival, and growth control (Chen et al., 2004; Shi-Wen et al., 2008). Functionally, CTGF overexpression reduced proliferation, increased G0/G1 accumulation, and decreased apoptosis. These changes are more consistent with a reversible quiescent or dormant state than with irreversible senescence or overt cell death (Coller et al., 2006; Fujimaki and Yao, 2020). Thus, our data support CTGF as a biologically plausible candidate associated with a storage-related dormant state. The directionally consistent, although non-significant, developmental delay observed in ovo is compatible with this interpretation.

The Hallmark GSEA further provides a useful framework for interpreting this phenotype. The dominant pattern can be summarized by 2 linked features: increased ER/secretory and proteostasis-related activity, and reduced inflammatory and apoptotic responsiveness. This combination is consistent with an adaptive protective state in which protein homeostasis is reinforced while stress-induced damage and cell loss are limited (Tabas and Ron, 2011; Walter and Ron, 2011). In addition, prior work has linked UPR signaling to growth restraint through PERK-dependent control of cell-cycle progression, providing a plausible mechanistic connection between proteostasis remodeling and reduced proliferation (Brewer and Diehl, 2000). Although several proliferation-related gene sets, including G2/M checkpoint, Mitotic Spindle, MYC targets, mTORC1 signaling, and Glycolysis, were enriched, these signatures should not be interpreted as evidence of enhanced cell proliferation. In this context, they more likely reflect checkpoint engagement, altered biosynthetic demand, or perturbed cell-cycle progression, which is more consistent with the functional data than with a simple proliferative response (Kastan and Bartek, 2004).

The partial concordance between the OE-CTGF transcriptome in vitro and the Dormant vs. Normal program in vivo supports the utility of the cell-based model as a simplified system for probing CTGF-associated features of the dormant state. However, this correspondence is necessarily incomplete, because CEFs cannot reproduce the cellular heterogeneity or spatial organization of the intact blastoderm (Pampaloni et al., 2007; Duval et al., 2017). Additional studies, particularly loss-of-function experiments and finer temporal sampling during rewarming, will be needed to determine which components of the dormant program are directly CTGF-dependent.

From an applied perspective, egg storage is routine but may compromise subsequent developmental performance (Fasenko, 2007; Goliomytis et al., 2015). Our findings identify CTGF-associated transcriptomic features, especially ER/secretory remodeling together with attenuation of inflammatory and p53/apoptosis-related stress pathways, as candidate molecular features of embryo adaptation to storage. These observations provide a basis for future work aimed at evaluating embryo status under different storage conditions and identifying molecular features associated with post-storage developmental competence.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study characterizes the transcriptional landscape of the storage-induced dormant state in chick blastoderms and defines a reversible program marked by flip DEGs. Integrative transcriptomic and co-expression network analyses prioritized CTGF as a candidate associated with the dormant state. Functionally, CTGF overexpression in CEFs was associated with several dormancy-like features, including reduced proliferation, increased G0/G1 occupancy, and decreased apoptotic propensity. At the pathway level, CTGF overexpression was associated with enhanced unfolded protein response and protein secretion programs together with reduced inflammatory and p53-associated apoptotic signaling, consistent with a protective, low-proliferation-like cellular state. These findings provide a molecular framework for understanding blastoderm remodeling during egg storage and nominate CTGF-associated remodeling patterns and downstream transcriptomic changes as candidate molecular features for future studies of embryo adaptation to storage and post-storage developmental competence.
