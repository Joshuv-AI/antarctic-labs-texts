The Revealed Preference Theory of Aggregate Object Allocations
Umutcan Salman

Abstract
I develop a revealed preference framework to test whether an aggregate allocation of indivisible objects satisfies Pareto efficiency and individual rationality (PI) without observing individual preferences. Exploiting the type-based preferences of 6 , I derive necessary and sufficient conditions for PI-rationalizability. I show that an allocation is PI-rationalizable if and only if its allocation graph is acyclic. Next, I analyse non-PI-rationalizable allocations. First, I study the three respective problems: removal of a minimum size of subset of individuals/types/objects to restore PI-rationalizability. I prove that these three problems are NP-complete. Then, I provide an alternative goodness-of-fit measure, namely Critical Exchange Index (CEI). The CEI assess the highest portion of individuals who can involve exchanging their final objects to reach PI. This measure shows the extent of inefficiencies. The results yield the first complete revealed preference analysis for Pareto efficiency and individual rationality in matching markets and provide an implementable tool for empirical applications. JEL codes: C78, D11 Keywords: Aggregate object allocation, Pareto efficiency, individual rationality, revealed preferences, computational complexity

Introduction

In many real-life allocation problems, a central authority assigns objects to individuals in a centralized manner, for instance, the allocation of social housing to families or the assignment of employees to public institutions.

Yet, after these initial assignments, subsequent reallocations often take place in a decentralized fashion. Individuals may directly swap their objects through mutual agreements, or a sequence of indirect trades may occur, where one individual relinquishes an object that is taken up by another, and so on. These exchanges emerge precisely because preferences evolve after the centralized allocation, and individuals seek outcomes better aligned with their updated preferences. Moreover, the central authority, after the initial allocation, does not observe individuals’ updated preferences and therefore cannot directly assess whether the observed reallocation after exchanges is Pareto efficient and individually rational.
2
2

2

An allocation is Pareto efficient if no agent can be made better off without making at least one other agent worse off through a reallocation of objects.
An allocation is individually rational if every agent receives an object that is at least as good as their initial endowment.
This raises an important policy question: should the authority invest in centralizing the reallocation or exchange process, by implementing a formal matching mechanism, or are decentralized exchanges already delivering efficient outcomes? This question cannot be answered directly without knowledge of individuals’ evolved preferences. The central authority may observe their initial preferences, collected before assignments take place. However, exchanges arise precisely because individuals hold different preferences afterwards, which are no longer observed.

I propose a revealed preference framework to address this question. My approach uses only the initial assignments and the observed exchanges to test whether the resulting allocation could have been generated by a Pareto efficient and individually rational outcome for some preference profile. Therefore the central planner asks the following revealed preference question:

Does there exist a preference profile of individuals such that the observed allocation is both Pareto efficient and individually rational?

If the answer is no for this question, the authority gains empirical justification for considering a centralized reallocation procedure. If not, the evidence suggests that the current decentralized process may already be achieving efficient outcomes, sparing the cost of mechanism redesign.

Without imposing additional structure on the problem, the question of whether a given reallocation is Pareto efficient and individually rational (PI
6
6

6

I jointly refer to Pareto efficiency and individual rationality as
PI
.
) becomes essentially meaningless: for any reallocation, the answer would always be “yes”. To see why, note that for any reallocation one can always construct a hypothetical preference profile, that is, a complete ordering of all objects for each individual, such that the reallocation is PI. In particular, imagine that in this constructed preference profile, each individual ranks the object they receive in the reallocation as his most preferred one. Under this artificial preference profile, the allocation is trivially both Pareto efficient and individually rational. This means that, without further assumptions, every reallocation can be ”rationalized” as PI, making the concept uninformative for empirical or theoretical testing.

To generate testable restrictions or conditions that could potentially be rejected by data, I adopt the type-based approach of
6
. In this framework, as a researcher or as a central planner, I partition individuals into types based on observable characteristics (such as age, occupation, or education). I then assume that all individuals within the same type are identical in their preferences over objects. This homogeneity assumption is admittedly strong: it rules out any unobserved preference heterogeneity within types. However, it is precisely this restriction that gives the analysis empirical content. Without such a structure, the revealed preference framework becomes trivial, as any observed allocation pattern could be explained by some contrived set of individual preferences, leaving no scope for falsification.

The contributions of this paper are twofold. First, I construct from aggregate allocation data

By the type-based preferences structure, these preferences extend to all agents of the same type. I show that the observed allocation is PI-rationalizable, meaning that there exists a preference profile of types where the observed allocation is PI,
if and only if

Second, I turn to allocations that are not PI-rationalizable and study how far they are from satisfying PI, in the spirit of the goodness-of-fit literature in revealed preference theory. In that literature, indices such as Afriat’s critical cost efficiency index (
1
), the Varian index (
17

10

7

3

2

Motivated by this “minimal intervention” perspective, I first introduce three deletion-based goodness-of-fit problems for aggregate allocations: the minimum number of individuals to remove (MIR), the minimum number of types to remove (MTR) and, the minimum number of objects to remove (MHR) in order to restore PI-rationalizability. These are natural analogues, in the allocation environment, of the Houtman–Maks index, which looks for the largest rationalizable subset of observations. I show that MIR, MTR, and MHR are all NP-complete (Proposition
3
), echoing complexity results for classical goodness-of-fit indices in revealed-preference tests, and implying that there is no polynomial-time algorithm that can compute these deletion-based measures efficiently in general.

Given this computational intractability, I then propose an alternative, exchange-based goodness-of-fit measure that is tailored to the aggregate allocation setting. For each type, I use the observed transition from initial endowments to the final allocation to construct a directed graph that partially orders objects according to revealed improvements: when an individual of type t moves from object h to
h^{\prime}
, this reveals that
h^{\prime}
is strictly preferred to h. On the basis of this revealed preference information, I compute the maximum number of individuals who could engage in additional exchanges of their final objects in order to reach a PI allocation, and hence a PI-rationalizable aggregate allocation. Formally, I employ the ReCycle algorithm developed in
13
to identify the largest fraction of individuals who can be involved in such welfare-improving exchange cycles while respecting PI. This leads to the
Critical Exchange Index (CEI)
, which I define as the fraction of individuals who must change their final object in this maximal PI-preserving improvement: a CEI close to zero indicates that the observed allocation is “close” to PI-rationalizable, whereas a CEI close to one signals substantial inefficiency. In line with the NP-completeness result of
12
, the problem of finding a minimum number of individuals who can exchange their objects while maintaining PI is itself NP-complete, so I do not pursue a goodness-of-fit measure based directly on that minimum; instead, I rely on the tractable, maximum-improvement–based CEI as my primary distance-to-rationalizability index.

Related literature.

Since the pioneering work of
8
, matching theory has grown into a rich and influential field. Over the past six decades, much of the progress has been on the normative side, designing algorithms to solve real-life allocation problems in ways that satisfy desirable properties. Two of the most celebrated mechanisms, the Top Trading Cycles (TTC) introduced by
14
and the Deferred Acceptance Algorithm (DAA) of
8
, are now standard tools in practical markets: they allocate students to schools, match medical graduates to hospitals, allocate social houses to low income families, and even facilitate kidney exchanges. In all these cases, the primary focus has been on how to design the market, assuming preferences are observed and using them to obtain an allocation in line with the desirable properties with an algorithm.

While the design side of the field has developed extensively, the positive side has received much less attention. Key questions in the context of object reallocation remain largely unanswered. For instance:
What can one say about the observed outcomes? Is the observed allocation Pareto efficient? Could it result from individually rational behavior?
In settings where
n
individuals each begin with one object and exchanges are allowed, these questions are both natural and important. Without answers, empirical work faces a fundamental obstacle. More clearly, before attempting to estimate utility parameters or other structural features of preferences (identification), one must first establish that the observed outcome is consistent with basic normative properties (characterization) such as Pareto efficiency and individual rationality.

The distinction between characterization and identification is crucial. Characterization provides testable conditions, necessary and sufficient restrictions, that observed data must satisfy to be consistent with equilibrium concepts such as Pareto efficiency and individual rationality. Identification, by contrast, presupposes such consistency and focuses on recovering the underlying structural features of preferences. In this sense, characterization is not only conceptually different but also logically prior to identification. There is little value in estimating preference parameters if the observed allocation cannot satisfy Pareto efficiency and individual rationality under any possible preference profile. Although such characterization steps are standard in other areas of revealed preference analysis, they remain largely unexplored in the study of object reallocations.

A small but growing literature applies revealed preference analysis to matching markets.
6
pioneered this strand by developing a revealed preference framework for stability, the central equilibrium concept in two-sided matching markets.
8
8

8

A matching is stable if there is no pair of agents
(i,j)
such that both
i
and
j
prefer each other to their respective matches.
They introduced the concept of aggregate marriage data and provided graph-theoretic restrictions under which such data can be rationalized as stable. Building on this,
4
offered a simpler characterization of stable aggregate marriages and extended the analysis to non-rationalizable cases. By establishing a connection between matroid theory and non-rationalizable marriages, they designed a greedy algorithm that identifies the minimal number of marriages that must be removed to restore rationalizability.

In aggregate object allocations or one-sided matching markets,
16

6

This paper builds on this emerging positive strand of matching literature. I offer a new characterization result for PI-rationalizability in object reallocations. To the best of my knowledge, this study is the first one that performs a revealed preference analysis for Pareto efficiency and individual rationality in object reallocation environments.

Restoring PI-rationalizability

Theorem
2
provides a test for PI when the preferences are not observed. However, the result of the test is either yes or no. So, one cannot know the extent of violation of PI when the result is no. One can simply remove one individual from the market with his object and the result of the test turns out to be a yes. Or it might not be sufficient to remove the majority of agents or objects to satisfy PI-rationalizability. In that case, the observed allocation is far from being rationalizable. Revealing the preferences of types

For each type
t
, I construct a directed graph
G_{t}=(V_{t},E_{t})
that summarizes the revealed information about the preferences of individuals in

. The vertex set

coincides with the set of objects. There is a directed edge from

to

if and only if there exists an individual

such that
\omega(i)=h
and

. By assumption, such a switch is voluntary and therefore reveals that, for individual

,
h^{\prime}
is strictly preferred to
h
. Since I assume that individuals belonging to the same type are similar and hence have identical preferences, it follows that, for all individuals in type
t
, the final object
h^{\prime}
is strictly preferred to the initial object
h
. In particular, every directed edge in
G_{t}
is oriented from a less preferred object to a more preferred one.

The graph
G_{t}
, however, is not necessarily acyclic. Two different individuals of the same type may exhibit opposite movements: for example, one individual may switch from
h^{\prime}
to
h
, while another starts with
h
and ends with
h^{\prime}
, thereby generating a cycle
h\to h^{\prime}\to h
. More generally, there may exist a sequence of individuals
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in I^{t}
and objects
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}
such that each individual’s final object is the initial object of the next one in the sequence, and the last individual’s final object coincides with the initial object of the first. This gives rise to a directed cycle

h_{1}\to h_{2}\to\cdots\to h_{k}\to h_{1}

in
G_{t}
. Along such a cycle, each object is both revealed preferred to and revealed worse than another object, so these comparisons do not yield a clear strict ranking. I therefore treat the information contained in directed cycles as ambiguous. Accordingly, when constructing the revealed preference relation below, I ignore the changes that occur within directed cycles.

For each type
t
, I then define a (revealed) partial order on objects induced by
G_{t}
. Denote this relation by
\succ_{t}
. For
h,h^{\prime}\in V_{t}
, I write
h^{\prime}\succ_{t}h
if and only if (i) there exists a directed path from

to
h^{\prime}
in

and (ii) there exists no directed path from
h^{\prime}
to
h
in
G_{t}
. In words,
h^{\prime}
is revealed strictly preferred to

can be reached from
h

h
cannot be reached from
h^{\prime}
. By construction,

is irreflexive and transitive, and hence a strict partial order. This construction effectively filters out all directed cycles in

and retains exactly those comparisons that are compatible with acyclicity.

From revealed preferences to exchanges

My goal is to identify, starting from a given allocation
x
, the largest possible set of individuals who can exchange their final objects so that the resulting allocation is Pareto efficient and individually rational (PI) relative to
x
, based on the revealed preference profile
(\succ_{t})_{t\in T}
. To this end, I apply the ReCycle algorithm proposed by
13
.

In place of a full individual-level preference profile, I feed the algorithm the revealed partial order
\succ_{t}
for each type
t\in T
. Since all individuals within a type share the same preferences, the relation
\succ_{t}
can be interpreted as the common strict preference relation of all agents in
I^{t}
, restricted to the part that is revealed by the observed switches. In place of the original endowments, I take the allocation
x
as the baseline and use the final objects assigned in
x
as the endowments in the ReCycle algorithm.

Let

denote the allocation produced by the ReCycle algorithm when starting from
x
and using the revealed relations
(\succ_{t})_{t\in T}
. Given the preference profile
(\succ_{t})_{t\in T}
and initial endowments
x
, by construction of the algorithm,

is PI and maximizes the number of individuals who obtain an object that is strictly preferred to their objects in
x
.

A goodness-of-fit measure

I now define a simple goodness-of-fit measure for allocations that are not PI-rationalizable. I call it the
Critical Exchange Index
(CEI). For an allocation
x
, the CEI is given by

CEI(x)\;=\;\frac{\sum_{i\in I}\mathbbm{1}\big[x(i)\neq y(i)\big]}{|I|},

where
y
is the PI allocation obtained from
x
via the ReCycle algorithm and
\mathbbm{1}[\cdot]
is the indicator function. By definition,
CEI(x)\in[0,1]
. If
x
is already PI-rationalizable with respect to
(\succ_{t})_{t\in T}
and ReCycle returns
y=x
, then
CEI(x)=0
.

The index
CEI(x)
measures the (maximal) fraction of individuals whose assigned object changes along the transition from
x
to the PI allocation
y
, given the revealed preference profile
(\succ_{t})_{t\in T}
. Thus, for a non–PI-rationalizable allocation
x
,
CEI(x)
can be interpreted as the largest share of the population that is involved in exchanges in order to reach a PI allocation consistent with
(\succ_{t})_{t\in T}
. A higher value of
CEI(x)
indicates that
x
is “farther” from being PI-rationalizable, in the sense that a larger fraction of individuals can change their objects to restore individual rationality and Pareto efficiency.

An alternative approach would be to look for the smallest fraction of individuals who need to exchange their objects in order to obtain a PI-rationalizable allocation. However, the problem of identifying a minimum-size subset of individuals whose objects must change so that the resulting allocation is Pareto efficient and individually rational is NP-complete (see
12
). This computational intractability motivates the use of ReCycle and the CEI as a tractable, yet informative, goodness-of-fit measure.

5
Conclusion

I have developed a revealed-preference framework for testing Pareto efficiency and individual rationality (PI) in aggregate object allocations without observing individual preferences. Building on a type-based preference structure, I derived necessary and sufficient conditions for PI-rationalizability and expressed them in graph-theoretic terms.

A characterization emerges: an allocation is PI-rationalizable if and only if its aggregate allocation graph is acyclic. This result provides the first complete revealed-preference test for PI allocations in matching markets, delivering both a purely theoretical characterization and an implementable procedure for empirical applications.

I then studied cases in which allocations are not PI-rationalizable. A natural approach is to investigate the minimal number of individuals, objects, or types that must be removed in order to restore rationalizability. I showed that all three associated problems—MIR, MHR, and MTR—are NP-complete. Thus, unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
, there is no polynomial-time algorithm to solve any of these Houtman–Maks–style goodness-of-fit problems in my allocation environment. This computational hardness motivates the search for alternative, tractable measures of the extent of non-rationalizability.

In response, I introduced an alternative goodness-of-fit measure, the
Critical Exchange Index
(CEI). For each type, I extract the maximal set of revealed preference comparisons that is compatible with acyclicity, thereby ruling out cycles within types. Using the resulting type-level revealed preference profile, I define an index that computes the largest fraction of agents who can be involved in exchanges to reach a PI allocation consistent with the revealed preferences. A value of
CEI(x)
close to one indicates that the observed allocation
x
is far from being PI-rationalizable, in the sense that a large share of agents can strictly improve through exchanges; conversely, values close to zero suggest that
x
is close to PI-rationalizability. This index thus provides a transparent diagnostic that can inform policymakers about the potential gains from introducing or strengthening a centralized allocation mechanism to facilitate exchanges among agents.

As future work, I plan to illustrate the theoretical findings of this paper using UK social housing data.

v_{j}\in\tilde{V}
there will be no within vertex cycle because I remove
i\in I

,

. For every

and

, there will be no between vertices cycle because I remove
i\in I
such that
\tau(i)=t_{k}^{2}
,
x(i)=\omega(i)=h_{v_{k}}
. For every
v_{j}\in V\setminus\tilde{V}
, there will be no cycle related to
v_{j}
, since I remove
i\in I
such that
\tau(i)=t_{j}^{2}
,
x(i)=\omega(i)=h_{v_{j}}
. There is only one possibility to observe a cycle in the aggregate allocation graph: a cycle between two vertices in
\tilde{V}
. This implies that there is an edge between these two vertices. Hence, it contradicts with the fact that
\tilde{V}
is an independent set.

For the reverse, let

. I need to show that there exists an independent set

Hence, for every vertex
