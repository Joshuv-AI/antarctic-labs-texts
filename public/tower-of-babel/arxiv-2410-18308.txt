Exact schedulability test for sporadic mixed-criticality real-time systems using antichains and oracles
Picard, Simon, Paolillo, Antonio, Geeraerts, Gilles, Goossens, Joël

Abstract
This work addresses the problem of exact schedulability assessment in uniprocessor mixed-criticality real-time systems with sporadic task sets. We model the problem by means of a finite automaton that has to be explored in order to check for schedulability. To mitigate the state explosion problem, we provide a generic algorithm which is parameterised by several techniques called oracles and simulation relations. These techniques leverage results from the scheduling literature as “plug-ins” that make the algorithm more efficient in practice. Our approach achieves up to a 99.998% reduction in the search space required for exact schedulability testing, making it practical for a range of task sets, up to 8 tasks or maximum periods of 350. This method enables to challenge the pessimism of an existing schedulability test and to derive a new dynamic-priority scheduler, demonstrating its good performance. This is the full version of an RTNS 2024 paper [ PPGG24a ] .

Introduction

The industry push for integrating systems of varying criticality levels onto a single platform has driven the real-time community to develop the mixed-criticality system model, hence producing a large body of research results for systems designed to degrade gracefully
[
BD22
]
.
The dual-criticality model
enables the concurrent execution of high-criticality tasks (
\mathsf{HI}
) alongside low-criticality tasks (
\mathsf{LO}
).
The
\mathsf{HI}
tasks are certified with two estimates of their worst-case execution time
— an optimistic estimate and a pessimistic upper bound —
whereas the
\mathsf{LO}
tasks are less critical and can be safely disabled should a
\mathsf{HI}
task exceed its optimistic estimate.

Many results about this model have been produced,
addressing
variations of the model
[
EY14a
,
Bar16b
]

varying CPU speeds
[
SGY22
,
SVG+21
]
,
multi-core and parallel systems
[
LB12
,
Bar16a
]
,
and applications in industrial settings
[
ENNT18
,
LBL19
,
PRD+17
]
.
Despite this progress, however, one fundamental problem remains open:
given a generic scheduling algorithm,
how to assess the schedulability of a mixed-criticality task set?
Agrawal and Baruah proved that determining the schedulability of
independent dual-criticality periodic or sporadic implicit-deadline
tasks is NP-hard in the strong
sense
[
AB18
]
, hinting that
the schedulability assessment of a given scheduling algorithm might be
hard as well. Indeed, to the best of our knowledge, no
exact — necessary
and
sufficient — schedulability test has
been produced to date for a general scheduling algorithm, even for a
uniprocessor platform.

The quest for schedulability tests has produced
two main lines of research. On the one hand, the real-time community
has focussed on efficient tests for specific schedulers, but they are
in many cases not exact. For example, EDF-VD
[
BBD+11a
]
, a
notable scheduling algorithm for mixed-criticality systems only
provides a
sufficient
and notoriously pessimistic test. On the
other hand, inspired by the formal methods community, several works
have proposed to model the possible behaviours of the system by means
of an
automaton

[
BC07
]
whose states correspond to
all the possible states of the system. In this case, looking for
potential deadline misses can be done by analysing all the states of
the automaton. While such approaches are guaranteed to provide an
exact test
[
AK18
,
Abd20
]
, they suffer
from the state explosion problem making them impractical for
realistic systems.

In this work, we seek to reconcile both approaches. While our
work is rooted in the automaton-based approach, we show how results
and knowledge from the real-time community can be exploited to make
the traversal of automata states more tractable. We
believe that such hybrid techniques are very rare in the literature,
with some notable exceptions like the works of Asyaban

[
AK18
]
(which addresses mixed criticality in the FTP
case) and Ranjha
[
RNN22
]
. However, those
papers develop
ad hoc
techniques (in the cases of FTP and FJP
scheduling), while we strive for more
generality.

Our contributions are thus as follows. (1) We provide an automaton
model for systems of dual-criticality sporadic tasks on a uniprocessor
platform where the scheduler is left as a parameter. Hence, (2) we
obtain an
exact
schedulability test for any given
scheduling algorithm that is deterministic and
memoryless.
(3) This test consists in exploring the states of the automaton and is
parameterised by optimisations to leverage knowledge built in the
real-time community. More precisely, sufficient and necessary tests
can be exploited as
oracles
that tell the algorithm to avoid
exploring some states. We also exploit a
simulation relation

between states to further prune the state space in the spirit of the
antichain approach of the formal method community

[
WDHR06
,
DR10
,
DR09
,
GGL13
]
. (4) We show how
to exploit this generic framework in our setting of mixed criticality
by defining proper oracles and a simulation relation. On this basis,
we evaluate empirically our approach. (5) Experiments on task sets
generated randomly according to the model specification show promising
results: the efficiency of our approach enables to reduce the state
search space by up to 99.998%.
The genericity of our approach also allows us (6) to evaluate the
EDF-VD scheduling algorithm and its associated test, showing that
the test is pessimistic
w.r.t. its actual scheduling capabilities.
(7) For illustration, we study and present a new dynamic-priority scheduler, LWLF.
We demonstrate its excellent performance, thanks to its anticipation of mode change impact when operating in
\mathsf{LO}
mode.

Problem definition

We consider a mixed-criticality sporadic task set
\tau=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{n}\}
with two levels of criticality, also referred to as a dual-criticality
[
BD22
]
, to be scheduled on a uniprocessor platform.
A dual-criticality sporadic task

\tau_{i}=\langle\langle C_{i}(\mathsf{LO}),C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})\rangle,D_{i},T_{i},L_{i}\rangle

is characterised by a
minimum
interarrival time

T_{i}>0
, a
relative deadline

D_{i}>0
, a
criticality
level

L_{i}\in\{\mathsf{LO},\mathsf{HI}\}
with
\mathsf{HI}>\mathsf{LO}
and the
worst-case execution time
(WCET) tuple
\langle C_{i}(\mathsf{LO}),C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})\rangle
.
Tasks will never execute for more than
C_{i}(L_{i})
.
Time is assumed to be discrete, ergo
\forall i:T_{i},D_{i},C_{i}(\mathsf{LO}),C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
.
I.e., all timing parameters are strictly-positive integers.
It is assumed that
C_{i}(\mathsf{LO})\leq C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})
when
L_{i}=\mathsf{HI}
and
C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})=C_{i}(\mathsf{LO})
when
L_{i}=\mathsf{LO}
.
A dual-criticality sporadic task
\tau_{i}
releases an infinite number of jobs, with each job release being separated by at least
T_{i}
units of time.
The absolute deadline of jobs are set
D_{i}
units of time after their release, and jobs must signal completion before their absolute deadline.
We assume jobs are independent, as formulated by Vestal
[
Ves07
]
.
At each clock-tick, the executing job can signal its completion.
If a job did not signal completion after exhausting its
C_{i}(\mathsf{LO})
, then a mode change is triggered from
\mathsf{LO}{}
to
\mathsf{HI}
:
jobs of
\mathsf{LO}{}
tasks are discarded
and
\mathsf{LO}{}
tasks are not allowed to release jobs any more;
active jobs of
\mathsf{HI}{}
tasks receive an additional budget of
C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})-C_{i}(\mathsf{LO})
, and future jobs of
\mathsf{HI}{}
tasks will receive a budget of
C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})
.
As a running example in the work,
we define the task set
\tau^{a}=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\}

with
\tau_{1}=\langle\langle 1,2\rangle,2,2,\mathsf{HI}\rangle

and
\tau_{2}=\langle\langle 1,1\rangle,2,2,\mathsf{LO}\rangle
.

It is imperative,
when validating a dual-criticality task sets,
to first perform
“due diligence”

by ensuring the corresponding two following single-criticality task sets are schedulable:

(i)
the task set comprising only
\mathsf{HI}
tasks, where

\forall\tau_{i}:C_{i}=C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})
,
and
(ii)
the task set including both
\mathsf{HI}
and
\mathsf{LO}
tasks, where

\forall\tau_{i}:C_{i}=C_{i}(\mathsf{LO})
.

We aim to establish an exact schedulability test (necessary and sufficient) for any dual-criticality sporadic task set

\tau
that tells us whether the set is schedulable
— i.e., no job released by the tasks misses a deadline —
with a given deterministic and preemptive scheduling algorithm with dynamic priorities.
We support both
implicit
(
\forall\tau_{i}:D_{i}=T_{i}
) and
constrained
(
\forall\tau_{i}:D_{i}\leq T_{i}
) deadlines.
The studied problem is difficult, notably due to two sources of non-determinism, the first relating to
the sporadic model
(we do not know when jobs are released) and the second to
mode change
(we do not know when it will occur).

Notations.

Assuming
\alpha,\beta\in\{\mathsf{LO},\mathsf{HI}\}
:
the
\alpha
-utilisation of the task
\tau_{i}\in\tau:U^{\alpha}(\tau_{i})=C_{i}(\alpha)/T_{i}
,
the
\alpha
-utilisation of the task set
\tau:U^{\alpha}(\tau)=\sum_{\tau_{i}\mid L_{i}\geq\alpha}U^{\alpha}(\tau_{i})
,
the
\alpha
-utilisation of the tasks of criticality
\beta
in the task set
\tau:U^{\alpha}_{\beta}(\tau)=\sum_{\tau_{i}\mid L_{i}=\beta}U^{\alpha}(\tau_{i})
,
and the average utilisation of the task set
\tau:U^{\mathsf{avg}}(\tau)=\frac{U^{\mathsf{LO}}(\tau)+U^{\mathsf{HI}}(\tau)}{2}
.

3
Automaton based semantic

To obtain a formal definition of the semantics of the system and of the
schedulability problem, we develop an automaton-based formalism inspired from Baker and
Cirinei
[
BC07
]
.
An automaton is a graph whose nodes are called
states
and whose
(directed) edges are called
transitions
.
States model the states of the system.
Changes from a state to another, triggered by a task, a job, or the scheduler,
are modelled by the transitions.
Hence, each path in the automaton is a possible execution of the system, and we will thus
look for executions that reach states where deadlines are missed (the so-called

failure states
) to check whether the system is schedulable or not.
We use the following formal definition of automaton:

Definition 1
.

An
automaton
is a tuple
A=\langle V,E,v_{0},F\rangle
where

V
is a set of states,

E\subseteq V\times V
is a set of transitions between states,

v_{0}\in V
is the initial state
and
F\subseteq V
is a set of failure states.
An automaton is finite iff
V
is a finite set.

The problem we consider on automata is the problem of
safety
w.r.t. a designated set of

failure
states
F
that need to be avoided.
A
path
in a finite automaton
A=\langle V,E,v_{0},F\rangle
is a finite sequence
of states
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}
such that
\forall\,1\leq j<k:(v_{j},v_{j+1})\in E
.
For a subset of states
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
, if there exists a path
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}
in

A
such that
v_{k}\in V^{\prime}
, we say that
v_{1}
can reach
V^{\prime}
.
We denote the set of states that can be reached from a state
v\in V
by
\mathsf{Reach}\left(v\right)
.
Then, the
safety problem
asks, given an automaton
A
, whether the initial state

v_{0}

cannot
reach the set of failure states
F
— i.e., there is no path from
v_{0}

to any state
v\in F
in the automaton, denoted by
\mathsf{Reach}\left(v_{0}\right)\cap F=\emptyset
.
If this is the case, we say that
A
is
safe
, otherwise (when
v_{0}
can reach
F
)
we say it is
unsafe
.

Let
\tau=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{n}\}
be a task set as defined in
Section 2
.
We model the behaviour of
\tau

by means of an automaton
A
, and we reduce the schedulability problem of

\tau
to an instance of the safety problem in
A
.
A state captures the following
dynamic (or “run-time”) information about each task
\tau_{i}
:

(i)
the
earliest next arrival time

\mathsf{nat}(\tau_{i})
relative to the current instant, and

(ii)
the
worst-case remaining execution time

\mathsf{rct}(\tau_{i})
of the current job of
\tau_{i}
for the current level of criticality.
In addition, we need to remember the current global
criticality level
(or mode)

\mathsf{cri}\in\{\mathsf{LO},\mathsf{HI}\}
of the system.
Hence, each system state will be a tuple of the form
\langle\mathsf{rct}_{S},\mathsf{nat}_{S},\mathsf{cri}_{S}\rangle
:

Definition 2

(System states)
.

Let
\tau=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{n}\}
be a mixed-criticality sporadic task system.
Let
T_{\mathsf{max}}=\mathsf{max}_{i}T_{i}
and
C_{\mathsf{max}}=\mathsf{max}_{i}C_{i}(L_{i})
.
A
system state
of
\tau
is a tuple
S=\langle\mathsf{rct}_{S},\mathsf{nat}_{S},\mathsf{cri}_{S}\rangle
where:

\mathsf{nat}_{S}:\tau\mapsto\{0,1,\ldots,T_{\mathsf{max}}\}
associates each task
\tau_{i}
to the minimal delay

\mathsf{nat}_{S}(\tau_{i})
that must elapse before the next job of the task can be released;

\mathsf{rct}_{S}:\tau\mapsto\{0,1,\ldots,C_{\mathsf{max}}\}
associates each task
\tau_{i}
to its maximal
remaining execution time
\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})
for the current criticality of the system; and
\mathsf{cri}_{S}\in\{\mathsf{LO},\mathsf{HI}\}
is the current
criticality of the system.
We denote by
\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)
the set of all system states of
\tau
.

Intuitively, each state contains the current run-time information of the systems at a particular instant,
i.e., how far we are in the execution of the active jobs (with the
\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau)
values)
and how close we are to the next releases of jobs (with the
\mathsf{nat}_{S}(\tau)
values).
From these definitions, we derive other useful definitions in the following.
Notice that the symbols
\wedge
,
\vee
and
\neg
respectively denote the
conjunction
,
inclusive disjunction
and
negation
logical operators.

Definition 3

(Time to deadline)
.

Let
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{nat}_{S}(\tau_{i})-(T_{i}-D_{i})
be the
time to deadline
,
the time remaining before the absolute deadline of the last submitted job
[
BC07
]

of
\tau_{i}\in\tau
in state
S
.
Note that when deadlines are implicit, we have
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{nat}_{S}(\tau_{i})
.

Definition 4

(Active tasks)
.

A task
\tau_{i}
is
active
in a state
S
iff it currently has a
job that is not completed in
S
.
The set of active tasks in
S
is
\mathsf{Active}\left(S\right)=\{\tau_{i}\mid\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})>0\}
.

Definition 5

(Eligible task)
.

A task
\tau_{i}
is
eligible
in the state
S=\langle\mathsf{rct}_{S},\mathsf{nat}_{S},\mathsf{cri}_{S}\rangle
iff it can release a
job in this state
— i.e., the task does not currently have an active job,
the last job was submitted at least
T_{i}
time units ago
and its criticality is greater than or equal to the state’s.
The set of eligible tasks in
S
is:

\mathsf{Eligible}\left(S\right)=\{\tau_{i}\mid\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{nat}_{S}(\tau_{i})=0\wedge L_{i}\geq\mathsf{cri}_{S}\}
.

Definition 6

(Implicitly completed task)
.

A task
\tau_{i}
is
implicitly completed
in the state
S=\langle\mathsf{rct}_{S},\mathsf{nat}_{S},\mathsf{cri}_{S}\rangle
iff its
latest job has been executed for its maximal execution time.
The set of implicitly completed tasks in
S
is

\mathsf{Completed}\left(S\right)=\{\tau_{i}\mid\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})=0\wedge C_{i}(\mathsf{cri}_{S})=C_{i}(L_{i})\}
.

Note that
C_{i}(\mathsf{cri}_{S})=C_{i}(L_{i})
represents the condition “its latest job has been
executed for its maximal execution time”.
The condition
\mathsf{cri}_{S}=L_{i}
is not appropriate because it is possible for a
\mathsf{HI}
-critical
job to have the same execution time in
\mathsf{LO}
and
\mathsf{HI}
.
Therefore, when
C_{i}(\mathsf{LO})=C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})
,

\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})=0
and
\mathsf{cri}_{S}=\mathsf{LO}

then
\tau_{i}
is implicitly completed because

C_{i}(\mathsf{cri}_{S})=C_{i}(\mathsf{LO})=C_{i}(L_{i})=C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})
even if
\mathsf{cri}_{S}=\mathsf{LO}\neq L_{i}=\mathsf{HI}
.

Definition 7

(Deadline-miss state)
.

A state
S
is a
deadline-miss state
if at least one task’s job reached its
deadline without executing all its maximal execution time.
The set of deadline-miss states on
\tau
is

\mathsf{DeadlineMiss}(\tau)=\{S\mid\exists\tau_{i}\in\tau:\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})>0\wedge\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})\leq 0\}
.

Definition 8

(Scheduler)
.

A uniprocessor
memoryless scheduler
for
\tau
is a function
\mathsf{sch}:\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)\mapsto\tau\cup\{\bot\}
s.t.
\mathsf{sch}(S)\in\mathsf{Active}\left(S\right)
or
\mathsf{sch}(S)=\bot
when no task is to be scheduled.
Moreover, we say that the scheduler
\mathsf{sch}
is
deterministic
, iff for all
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)
s.t.

\mathsf{Active}\left(S_{1}\right)=\mathsf{Active}\left(S_{2}\right)
and
\mathsf{cri}_{S_{1}}=\mathsf{cri}_{S_{2}}
, for all
\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(S_{1}\right)
:
\mathsf{nat}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{nat}_{S_{2}}(\tau_{i})\wedge\mathsf{rct}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{rct}_{S_{2}}(\tau_{i})
implies
\mathsf{sch}(S_{1})=\mathsf{sch}(S_{2})
.

A
memoryless scheduler
is a scheduler that makes decisions based only on the current state, and not on the history of previous states.
The
deterministic
property means that
the scheduler always takes the same decision given the same criticality level and the same active task characteristics.
It implies that the decisions of a deterministic scheduler do not involve
any randomness in the job selection and are unaffected by the inactive tasks.
In this work, we will only consider deterministic memoryless schedulers.
As an example, we define the EDF-VD scheduler
[
BBD+11a
]
within our framework:

Definition 9

(EDF-VD scheduler)
.

With

\lambda=\frac{U_{\mathsf{HI}}^{\mathsf{LO}}(\tau)}{1-U_{\mathsf{LO}}^{\mathsf{LO}}(\tau)}

a discount
factor, let
\mathsf{ttvd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
be the time remaining before the
virtual deadline of the last submitted job of
\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(S\right)
in state
S

defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{ttvd}_{S}(\tau_{i})=

\displaystyle\mathsf{nat}_{S}(\tau_{i})-(T_{i}-D_{i}\cdot\lambda)

if

L_{i}=\mathsf{HI}

\displaystyle\mathsf{ttvd}_{S}(\tau_{i})=

\displaystyle\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})

otherwise.

Further, we let
\mathsf{min_{d}}(S)
be the task
\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(S\right)
which has the minimal
deadline in
S
.
That is,
\tau_{i}
is s.t. for all
\tau_{k}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(S\right)\setminus\{\tau_{i}\}
:

\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{k})>\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
or
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{k})=\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})\wedge k>i
.
We define
\mathsf{min_{vd}}(S)
similarly, using virtual deadlines instead of deadlines
(substituting
\mathsf{ttvd}_{S}
for
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}
in the definition).
Then, for all states
S
, we let:
\mathsf{sch}_{\text{EDF-VD}}(S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bot

if

\mathsf{Active}\left(S\right)=\emptyset

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{min_{d}}(S)

else if

\mathsf{cri}_{S}=\mathsf{HI}\vee U_{\mathsf{LO}}^{\mathsf{LO}}(\tau)+U_{\mathsf{HI}}^{\mathsf{HI}}(\tau)\leq 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{min_{vd}}(S)

otherwise.

In the two first cases, the scheduler is behaving exactly as EDF.
The original work of EDF-VD
[
BBD+11a
]
assumes implicit deadlines (i.e.,
\forall\tau_{i}:D_{i}=T_{i})
.
Notice we cannot have
\lambda>1

as it would mean
U^{\mathsf{LO}}>1
,
the task set not being schedulable in
\mathsf{LO}
mode, contradicting the requisites outlined in Section 2 (
“due diligence”
).

Thanks to these notions, we can define the transitions of the automaton.
Those transitions must embed all the modifications happening to the system state within one clock-tick.
Each of those modifications can be seen as an intermediary transition between two system states, and they happen in the following order:
(1)
Release transitions
model the release of jobs by sporadic tasks at a given instant in time,
(2)
Run transitions
model the elapsing of one time unit, and running the job selected by the scheduler, if any
and (3)
Signal transitions
model a job signalling, or not, completion, and a potential resulting mode change.

In the automaton, an
actual transition
will exist only if those three intermediary transitions happen one after the other.
We now formally define how each kind of intermediary transitions alter the state.
We start by defining
release transitions
.
Let
S
be a state in
\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)
.
Intuitively, when the system is in state
S
,
a task
\tau_{i}
releasing a new job has the effect to update
S
by
setting
\mathsf{nat}(\tau_{i})
to
T_{i}
and
\mathsf{rct}(\tau_{i})
to
C_{i}(\mathsf{cri}_{S})
.
Formally:

Definition 10

(Release transition)
.

Let
S=\langle\mathsf{rct}_{S},\mathsf{nat}_{S},\mathsf{cri}_{S}\rangle\in\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)
be a system state and
\tau^{+}\subseteq\mathsf{Eligible}\left(S\right)
be a set of tasks that are eligible to release a new job in the system.
Then, we say that
S^{+}=\langle\mathsf{rct}_{S^{+}},\mathsf{nat}_{S^{+}},\mathsf{cri}_{S^{+}}\rangle
is a
\tau^{+}
-release
successor
of
S
, denoted
S\xrightarrow{\tau^{+}}_{\mathsf{rl}}S^{+}
iff:

1.

\forall\tau_{i}\in\tau^{+}:\mathsf{nat}_{S^{+}}(\tau_{i})=T_{i}
and

\mathsf{rct}_{S^{+}}(\tau_{i})=C_{i}(\mathsf{cri}_{S})

2.

\forall\tau_{i}\notin\tau^{+}:\mathsf{nat}_{S^{+}}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{nat}_{S}(\tau_{i})
and

\mathsf{rct}_{S^{+}}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})

3.

\mathsf{cri}_{S^{+}}=\mathsf{cri}_{S}
.

Notice that we allow
\tau^{+}=\emptyset
,
that is, no task releases a new job in the system.
Also note that no time elapsed in that transition, so no
\mathsf{nat}(\tau_{i})
nor
\mathsf{rct}(\tau_{i})
must be updated.
Furthermore, observe that changing the definition with
\tau^{+}=\mathsf{Eligible}\left(S\right)
would lead to consider a periodic task model (without offset),
as all tasks that could release a job would then have to release it immediately.
Next, we move to
run transitions
.
Let
S
be a state in
\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)
, and let
\mathsf{run}
be the scheduling decision to apply, i.e., either
\mathsf{run}\in\tau
and
\mathsf{run}
must be executed or
\mathsf{run}=\bot
and the processor remains idle.
Then, letting one time unit elapse from
S
under the
\mathsf{run}
scheduling decision
leads to decrementing the
\mathsf{rct}
of the task
\mathsf{run}
(and only this task) if
\mathsf{run}\in\tau
, and to
decrementing the
\mathsf{nat}
of all tasks.
Formally:

Definition 11

(Run transition)
.

Let
S=\langle\mathsf{rct}_{S},\mathsf{nat}_{S},\mathsf{cri}_{S}\rangle\in\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)
be a system state and
\mathsf{run}\in\tau\cup\{\bot\}
be a task to be executed or
\bot
if no task is to be executed.
Then, we say that

4
An efficient algorithm for safety with simulation relation and oracles

In this section, we present an efficient algorithm to check for safety in an automaton,
which is based on two hypothesis:

(i)
we can query an
oracle
which can sometimes tell us whether a given state can
or cannot reach the set of failure states
F
;

(ii)
we have at our disposal a
simulation relation
between states. Intuitively,
when
v^{\prime}
simulates
v
, it means that all paths that can occur from
v

can be witnessed by a path from
v^{\prime}
. In particular, when
v
can reach the
failure states, then
v^{\prime}
can reach them too.

Both the oracle and the simulation relation can improve the performance of an algorithm answering the
safety problem.
Whenever the oracle guarantees that
v
cannot reach the failure states, there is no need
to compute the paths starting in
v
(and it is safe to do so, as no path to the failure
states will be dropped).
When the oracle says that a state
v
(that is reachable from
v_{0}
) can reach the failure
states
F
, we can immediately conclude that
F
is reachable from
v_{0}
as well, hence
that the automaton is
unsafe
.
Finally, when two reachable states
v
and
v^{\prime}
are found s.t.
v^{\prime}
simulates
v
, only
the paths starting from
v^{\prime}
need to be computed.

We show in
Section 5
how such an oracle and such a simulation
relation can be derived in our mixed criticality setting, but the generality of the
concepts we explain here make them applicable to other scheduling problems.
We formalise the notion of oracle:

Definition 14

(Safe and unsafe states)
.

Let
A=\langle V,E,v_{0},F\rangle
be a finite automaton.
Then, a state
v
is
safe
iff there is no path starting in
v
that reaches
F
,
i.e.,
\mathsf{Reach}\left(v\right)\cap F=\emptyset
.

A state
v
that is not safe (i.e., there is a path from
v
that reaches
F
)
is called unsafe.

In order to formalise the oracle, we assume that we have at our disposal a set
Safe

containing only safe states and a set
Unsafe
containing only unsafe states.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
F\subseteq\text{{Unsafe}}
.
Observe that we can have
\text{{Safe}}=\emptyset
and
\text{{Unsafe}}=F
, so we do not request that

\text{{Safe}}\cup\text{{Unsafe}}=V
.
However, the bigger those sets, the more efficient our algorithm will potentially be.
Next, we define the notion of
simulation relation
.

Definition 15

(Simulation relation)
.

Let
A=\langle V,E,v_{0},F\rangle
be a finite automaton.
A relation
\preccurlyeq\subseteq V\times V
is a
simulation relation
iff it
respects the following properties:

1.

\preccurlyeq
is a preorder, i.e., it is reflexive and transitive.

2.

for all states
v_{1}
and
v_{1}^{\prime}
s.t.
v_{1}\preccurlyeq v_{1}^{\prime}
, for all
v_{2}

s.t.
(v_{1},v_{2})\in E
, there exists
v_{2}^{\prime}\in V
s.t.:
(i)
(v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime})\in E
;
(ii) and
v_{2}\preccurlyeq v_{2}^{\prime}
.

3.

for all states
v
and
v^{\prime}
s.t.
v\preccurlyeq v^{\prime}
:
v\in F
implies that

v^{\prime}\in F
.

Whenever
v_{1}\preccurlyeq v_{1}^{\prime}
, we say that
v_{1}^{\prime}

simulates

v_{1}
.
Thus, the definition says that, for every move
(v_{1},v_{2})
that can be performed from

v_{1}
, there is a matching move
(v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime})
from
v_{1}^{\prime}
.
Here, ‘matching’ means that the state
v_{2}^{\prime}
simulates
v_{2}

(
v_{2}\preccurlyeq v_{2}^{\prime}
) and that
v_{2}^{\prime}
is a failure state if
v_{2}
is.
From this definition, we establish the following:

Proposition 1
.

Let
A=\langle V,E,v_{0},F\rangle
be a finite automaton.
For all pairs of states
v
and
v^{\prime}
s.t.
v\preccurlyeq v^{\prime}
, the following holds:
(1) if
v
is unsafe, then
v^{\prime}
is unsafe too;
(2) if
v^{\prime}
is safe, then
v
is safe too.

Proof.

First, let us assume
v
is unsafe.
From
Definition 14
, it means that there exists a path

v,v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}
s.t.
v_{k}\in F
.
By applying inductively
Definition 15
, we can build a path

v^{\prime},v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime}\ldots,v_{k}^{\prime}
s.t.
v_{i}\preccurlyeq v_{i}^{\prime}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
By
Definition 15
again, and since
v_{k}\in F
, we conclude that
v_{k}^{\prime}\in F
too.
Hence,
v^{\prime}
is unsafe.
This proves point 1.
Point 2 is simply its contrapositive.
∎

This observation prompts for the definition of the set of ‘all states that are simulated
by/simulate a given state
v
’.
Given a state
v
and a simulation relation
\preccurlyeq
, we let the

upward-closure
of
v
be the set of all states that simulate
v
, i.e.,
\uparrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(v\right)=\{v^{\prime}\mid v\preccurlyeq v^{\prime}\}
; and its
downward-closure
be
\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(v\right)=\{v^{\prime}\mid v^{\prime}\preccurlyeq v\}
(the set of all states that are simulated by
v
).
The upward and downward-closures are also defined on a set of states, for which it becomes the union of the respective closures of all the states within the set.
Finally, given a set of states
Y\subseteq V
, we let
\mathsf{max}^{\preccurlyeq}\!\left(Y\right)=\{v\in S\mid\nexists v^{\prime}\in S:v\preccurlyeq v^{\prime}\}
be the set of
maximal
states of
S
w.r.t. the simulation

\preccurlyeq
, i.e., those states in
S
that are not simulated by any other state in
S
.

Let us now explain how these notions (sets of safe and unsafe states, and simulation
relation) can be exploited in a generic algorithm to solve the safety problem in a finite
automaton.
First, let us recall the general strategy of
breadth first search
to solve safety in
an automaton.
It can be formalised as computing two sequences of sets
\left(R_{i}\right)_{i\geq 0}
and

\left(N_{i}\right)_{i\geq 0}
s.t., for all
i\geq 0
,
R_{i}
and
N_{i}
are the sets of all
states that can be reached from
v_{0}
in at most
i
steps and exactly
i
steps,
respectively.
We denote by

\mathsf{Succ}\left(Y\right)=\{v^{\prime}|\exists(v,v^{\prime})\in E\,\forall v\in Y\}

the set of all successors of all states in
Y\subseteq V
,
and we have

N_{0}=\{v_{0}\}
,
\forall i\geq 0:N_{i+1}=\mathsf{Succ}\left(N_{i}\right)\setminus R_{i}
,

R_{0}=\{v_{0}\}
, and
\forall i\geq 0:R_{i+1}=R_{i}\cup N_{i+1}
.
Then, an algorithm to decide reachability consists in computing
N_{1},R_{1},N_{2},R_{2},\ldots N_{j},R_{j},\ldots
until:

(i
) either
N_{j}\cap F\neq\emptyset
, in which case
F
is reachable from
v_{0}
; or

(ii
)
R_{j}=R_{j+1}
, in which case
R_{j}
contains all the reachable states and we know
that
F
is not reachable (otherwise, we would have already returned ‘fail’).
In order to improve this algorithm, we compute the sequences

\left(\widetilde{R}_{i}\right)_{i\geq 0}
and
\left(\widetilde{N}_{i}\right)_{i\geq 0}

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}_{0}=\widetilde{R}_{0}=

\displaystyle\{v_{0}\}\setminus\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\text{{Safe}}\right)

\displaystyle\forall i\geq 0:\widetilde{N}_{i+1}=

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}^{\preccurlyeq}\!\left(\mathsf{Succ}\left(\widetilde{N}_{i}\right)\setminus\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{i}\cup\text{{Safe}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\forall i\geq 0:\widetilde{R}_{i+1}=

\displaystyle\mathsf{max}^{\preccurlyeq}\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{i}\cup\widetilde{N}_{i+1}\right).

Intuitively, these sequences contain less elements than the original ones, but still retain enough information to solve the safety problem. Let us explain why. Assume the algorithm has computed set
\widetilde{R}_{i}
and
\widetilde{N}_{i}
,
and let us consider how it computes
\widetilde{N}_{i+1}
. First, the algorithm computes the successors
of
\widetilde{N}_{i}

\mathsf{Succ}\left(\widetilde{N}_{i}\right)\setminus\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{i}\cup\text{{Safe}}\right)

of
\widetilde{N}_{i}

\widetilde{R}_{i}
or from
Safe
.
Finally, it keeps only the maximal elements from this resulting set.

To understand why these optimisations are correct, assume there is a state
v\in\mathsf{Succ}\left(\widetilde{N}_{i}\right)
which
is also simulated by an element of
Safe
(hence
v\in\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\text{{Safe}}\right)
).
With our optimisations,
v
is not in
\widetilde{N}_{i+1}
, and its successors will
not be computed at the next step.
However, this is not a problem, because
v
is simulated by a safe state, hence it is

safe too
by
Proposition 1
.
Similarly, if
v\in\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{i}\right)
, it means we have already computed all the necessary successors at a previous step, keeping only those that are potentially unsafe.
So, it is correct to avoid computing the successors of
v
, since we are looking for paths
that lead to
unsafe
states.
Now, assume that there is
v\in\mathsf{Succ}\left(\widetilde{N}_{i}\right)\setminus\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{i}\cup\text{{Safe}}\right)
which is not
\preccurlyeq

This implies that there is
v^{\prime}\in\widetilde{N}_{i+1}
that simulates
v
.
There are two possibilities. Either
v
is
safe
, and we do not need to compute its
successors.
Or
v
is
unsafe
, but then, so is
v^{\prime}
.
So, again, we keep in
\widetilde{N}_{i+1}
enough information to discover a path to the failure
states. With these definitions, all sets
\widetilde{R}_{i}
and
\widetilde{N}_{i}
are
\preccurlyeq
-antichains of

\preccurlyeq
-maximal states
(i.e., sets of states which are all incomparable and maximal
w.r.t.
\preccurlyeq
) that retain enough information to find paths to
F
.

Antichain breadth first search with safe and unsafe states optimisation

Proposition 2
.

On all automata
A
,

terminates and returns ‘
Fail
’
iff

is unsafe.

For the sake of readability, we present here a proof sketch. The
complete proof is in Appendix
A

Proof Sketch.

We first establish termination by showing that all states computed in some
\widetilde{R}_{i}
are
reachable states, i.e.,
\widetilde{R}_{i}\subseteq\mathsf{Reach}\left(A\right)
for all
i\geq 0
.
We then observe that the sets
\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{i}\right)
keep growing, i.e.,
\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{0}\right)\subseteq\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{1}\right)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{i}\right)\subseteq\cdots
.
However, since
\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{i}\right)\subseteq\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\mathsf{Reach}\left(A\right)\right)
for all
i\geq 0
, and since

\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\mathsf{Reach}\left(A\right)\right)
is a finite set, this sequence must eventually stabilise, i.e.,
\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{\ell}\right)=\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{\ell+1}\right)
for some
\ell
.
From this, we can conclude that
\widetilde{N}_{\ell+1}=\emptyset
and the algorithm
terminates.

Next, we prove soundness, i.e., when the algorithm returns ‘
Fail
’, we have indeed

\mathsf{Reach}\left(A\right)\cap F\neq\emptyset
.
This stems again from the fact that
\widetilde{N}_{i}\subseteq\mathsf{Reach}\left(A\right)
for all
i\geq 0
.
Hence, when we return ‘
Fail
’, we have indeed found a reachable state that is
unsafe.

Finally, we establish completeness, i.e., when
\mathsf{Reach}\left(A\right)\cap F\neq\emptyset
, the
algorithm returns ‘
Fail
’.
To obtain this result, we consider one path
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k},v_{k+1}
s.t.,
v_{k+1}\in\uparrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\text{{Unsafe}}\right)
, and
v_{i}\not\in\uparrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\text{{Unsafe}}\right)
for all
0\leq i\leq k
.
That is, the path reaches
\uparrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\text{{Unsafe}}\right)
in its last state only.
We show that the prefix
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
can be ‘found’ in the sequence

\widetilde{R}_{0},\ldots,\widetilde{R}_{k}
in the following sense:
0\leq i\leq k
:

v_{i}\in\downarrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\widetilde{R}_{i}\right)
.
From that, we conclude that
\widetilde{N}_{k+1}\cap\uparrow^{\preccurlyeq}\!\!\left(\text{{Unsafe}}\right)\neq\emptyset
, and that

\widetilde{N}_{k+1}
will be computed by the algorithm, hence returning ‘
Fail
’.
∎

5
A simulation relation and oracles for dual-criticality scheduling

In order to leverage the optimised algorithm of
Section 4
on the
automaton defined in
Section 3
(to model the behaviour of dual-criticality
task set),
we define in this Section a suitable simulation relation and
ad hoc
oracles.
In
Section 6
, we will show that these optimisations are efficient in practice to reduce
the size of the state space that needs to be explored.

5.1
Idle tasks simulation relation

We first define a simulation relation
\preccurlyeq_{idle}
, called the
idle tasks
simulation relation
that can be computed efficiently by inspecting the values

\mathsf{nat}
,
\mathsf{rct}
and
\mathsf{cri}
stored in the states.

Definition 16
.

Let
\tau
be a set of dual-criticality sporadic tasks.
Then, the
idle tasks preorder

\preccurlyeq_{idle}\subseteq\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)\times\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)
is s.t. for all
S_{1},S_{2}:S_{1}\preccurlyeq_{idle}S_{2}
iff:
(i)
\mathsf{cri}_{S_{2}}=\mathsf{cri}_{S_{1}}
; (ii)
\mathsf{rct}_{S_{2}}=\mathsf{rct}_{S_{1}}
;
(iii) for all
\tau_{i}
s.t.
\mathsf{rct}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})=0:\mathsf{nat}_{S_{2}}(\tau_{i})\leq\mathsf{nat}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})
; and (iv) for all
\tau_{i}
s.t.
\mathsf{rct}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})>0:\mathsf{nat}_{S_{2}}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{nat}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})
.

This relation is transitive and reflexive, so it is indeed a preorder.
The relation also defines a partial order on
\mathsf{Active}\left(S\right)
, because it is antisymmetric.
Note that
S_{1}\preccurlyeq_{idle}S_{2}
implies that
\mathsf{Active}\left(S_{2}\right)=\mathsf{Active}\left(S_{1}\right)
since
\mathsf{rct}_{S_{2}}=\mathsf{rct}_{S_{1}}
.
We show that this preorder is indeed a simulation relation for a
deterministic scheduler:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\tau
be a dual-criticality sporadic task system and
\mathsf{sch}
a deterministic
uniprocessor scheduler for
\tau
.
Then
\preccurlyeq_{idle}
is a simulation relation for
A(\tau,\mathsf{sch})
.

For the sake of readability we present here a proof sketch. The
complete proof can be found in Appendix
B
.

Proof Sketch.

S_{1},S^{-}_{1}
and
S_{2}
be three states in
\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)
s.t.
(S_{1},S^{-}_{1})\in E
and
S_{1}\preccurlyeq_{idle}S_{2}

S^{-}_{2}\in\mathsf{States}\left(\tau\right)

S^{-}_{1}\preccurlyeq_{idle}S^{-}_{2}

Let

5.2
Safe state oracles

We present a safe state oracle, which is a sufficient schedulability condition depending on the state of the system.

Oracle 1

(
\mathsf{HI}
idle point)
.

\{S\mid\mathsf{cri}_{S}=\mathsf{HI}\wedge\mathsf{Active}\left(S\right)=\emptyset\}\subseteq\text{{Safe}}
.

Proof.

As per the
“due diligence”
outlined in
Section 2
, the task set comprising only
\mathsf{HI}
tasks, where

\forall\tau_{i}:C_{i}=C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})
, must be schedulable. Hence, as of reaching an idle point in
\mathsf{HI}
mode, no more deadline misses are possible.
∎

5.3
Unsafe state oracles

In this section, we present several
necessary
conditions depending on the states of the system
and provide their equivalent formulation in terms of unsafe oracles. Note that the oracles presented below are scheduler agnostic.

Definition 17

(Laxity)
.

The
laxity
of an active task
\tau_{i}
in the state
S
is:
\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})-\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})
.

Lemma 1
.

For feasibility it is
necessary
to have

\forall\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(\tau\right):\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{i})\geq 0
.

Proof.

It is obvious that if
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})<\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})
, then even if the processor is given to the job immediately until its deadline, we will miss its deadline.
∎

Oracle 2

(Negative laxity)
.

\{S\mid\exists\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(\tau\right):\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{i})<0\}\subseteq\text{{Unsafe}}
.

Proof.

It holds trivially from
Lemma 1
that any state
S
violating the necessary condition will lead to a deadline miss.
∎

In
\mathsf{LO}
mode, we introduce a
stronger
condition that anticipates an imminent mode change.
The
worst-laxity
of a
\mathsf{LO}
task is simply its laxity.
However, for a
\mathsf{HI}
task, we incorporate its “bonus” execution time (
C_{i}(\mathsf{HI})-C_{i}(\mathsf{LO})
) that would be incurred if it does not explicitly signal completion, eventually causing a mode change.

Definition 18

(Worst-laxity)
.

The
worst-laxity
of an active task
\tau_{i}
in state
S
is:
\mathsf{worstLaxity}_{S}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{i})-(C_{i}(L_{i})-C_{i}(\mathsf{cri}_{S}))
.

Lemma 2
.

\forall\tau_{i}
, it is
necessary
to have

\mathsf{worstLaxity}_{S}(\tau_{i})\geq 0\text{.}

Proof.

In
\mathsf{HI}
mode the laxity is equivalent to the notion of worst-laxity, the property follows from
Lemma 1
. In
\mathsf{LO}
mode we have to distinguish between
\mathsf{LO}
and
\mathsf{HI}
tasks.
For a
\mathsf{LO}
task it is necessary to have non negative laxity. For a
\mathsf{HI}
task if this condition is not satisfied and if a mode change occurs
simultaneously
, by definition the remaining computation time of the active job of
\tau_{i}
is larger than the time we have before reaching the deadline. Therefore, we will inevitably miss the task deadline, which proves the property.
∎

Oracle 3

(Negative worst-laxity)
.

\{S\mid\exists\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(\tau\right):\mathsf{worstLaxity}_{S}(\tau_{i})<0\}\subseteq\text{{Unsafe}}
.

Proof.

It holds trivially from
Lemma 2
that any state
S
violating the necessary condition will lead to a deadline miss.
∎

For the next oracle, we propose to adapt the reasoning of demand bound functions (
\mathsf{dbf}
)
— as leveraged for EDF and its variations in many prior works
[
BRH90
]
—
to formulate a demand function related to the state of the system
— i.e., the
\mathsf{cri}
,
\mathsf{rct}
and
\mathsf{nat}
information.
Instead of defining, per-task
\tau_{i}
, a demand function between two
absolute
instants
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
,
we define a demand function
relative
to the current state,
with a single parameter
t

which is a time in the future
relative
to the “current time” (or current instant) in
S
.

We need to separate concerns between current jobs
— that can be disabled (
\mathsf{LO}
case) or extended (
\mathsf{HI}
case) —
and future jobs.
We define the number of future jobs of a task,
i.e., the number of jobs the task can release until this future instant.

Definition 19

(Number of future jobs of a task until a future instant)
.

the
maximum number of jobs
that
\tau_{i}
can release
strictly
after the current instant in
S
and before a future instant
t
,
and that have a deadline no later than
t
, assuming
S
is in mode
\alpha
,
is:

\displaystyle\mathsf{nj}_{S}^{\alpha}(\tau_{i},t)=

\displaystyle 0

if

L_{i}<\alpha

\displaystyle\mathsf{nj}_{S}^{\alpha}(\tau_{i},t)=

\displaystyle\biggl\lfloor\frac{\mathsf{max}\{t-\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}),0\}}{T_{i}}\biggr\rfloor

otherwise.

The first case represents the fact that no job can be released by a
\mathsf{LO}
task when assuming
\mathsf{HI}
mode.
The second case is the amount of periods that fits within the interval from the deadline of the current job and
t
.
Based on that, we can define the demand function.

Definition 20

(Demand function of a task in a state)
.

The
demand function
of a task
\tau_{i}
in state
S
in mode
\alpha
is a lower bound on the maximum amount of computation time required by jobs of
\tau_{i}

between the current instant in
S
and a future instant
t
,
only for jobs of
\tau_{i}
having deadlines before
t
,
assuming
S
is in mode
\alpha

or that the mode
\alpha
will be reached at the next possible instant.
Formally, with
\alpha\in\{\mathsf{LO}{},\mathsf{HI}{}\}
:
\mathsf{df}_{S}^{\alpha}(\tau_{i},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

if

t<\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})\vee L_{i}<\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{nj}_{S}^{\alpha}(\tau_{i},t)\cdot C_{i}(\alpha)

else if

\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})=0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\mathsf{nj}_{S}^{\alpha}(\tau_{i},t)\cdot C_{i}(\alpha)+C_{i}(\alpha)\\
-C_{i}(\mathsf{cri}_{S})+\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})\end{multlined}

otherwise.

No work must be accounted in the demand
\mathsf{df}_{S}^{\alpha}(\tau_{i},t)

if
t
is before the deadline of
\tau_{i}
or if the criticality of
\tau_{i}
is below the considered mode
\alpha
.
Otherwise, if
\tau_{i}
is idle, we only consider the demand of
future
jobs,
and do not account for the “bonus” execution time (
C_{i}(\mathsf{HI}{})-C_{i}(\mathsf{LO}{})
)
a running job of a
\mathsf{HI}{}
task receives if a mode switch occurs.
This is accounted in the third case, together with the remaining time (
\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})
) of the current job.
Notice that
\mathsf{nj}_{S}^{\mathsf{cri}_{S}}(\tau_{i},\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))=0
, so
\mathsf{df}_{S}^{\mathsf{cri}_{S}}(\tau_{i},\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))=\mathsf{rct}_{S}(\tau_{i})
.
We can now define the global demand bound.

Definition 21

(Demand bound function in a state)
.

The global
demand bound function
is the total maximum amount of computation time required
between the current instant in
S
and a future instant
t

by jobs of the task set
\tau
,
only for jobs having deadlines before
t
, assuming mode
\alpha
.
Formally:
\mathsf{dbf}_{S}^{\alpha}(t)=\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\tau}\mathsf{df}_{S}^{\alpha}(\tau_{i},t)
.

The definitions of
\mathsf{nj}
,
\mathsf{df}
and
\mathsf{dbf}
uses the mode parameter
\alpha
.
This allows us to consider the current mode when we set
\alpha=\mathsf{cri}_{S}

and to anticipate the demand if a mode switch occurs when we set
\alpha=\mathsf{HI}{}
.

Lemma 3
.

For feasibility it is
necessary
to have

\forall\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(\tau\right):\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})\geq\mathsf{dbf}_{S}^{\mathsf{cri}_{S}}(\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))
.

Oracle 4

(Over demand)
.

\{S\mid\exists\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(\tau\right):\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})<\mathsf{dbf}_{S}^{\mathsf{cri}_{S}}(\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))\}\subseteq\text{{Unsafe}}
.

Proof.

For any
\tau_{k}\in\tau
,

\mathsf{nj}_{S}^{\mathsf{cri}_{S}}(\tau_{k},\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))
is the highest possible
\ell
such that

\mathsf{nat}_{S}(\tau_{k})+D_{k}+(\ell-1)\cdot T_{k}\leq\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
,
showing it is the highest possible number of jobs released by
\tau_{k}
between the current instant in
S
and the next deadline of
\tau_{i}

having a deadline before
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
.
Note that
\mathsf{df}_{S}^{\mathsf{cri}_{S}}(\tau_{k},\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))
represents the total amount of execution time required by
\tau_{k}
including its potentially active job and all its future jobs until
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
, assuming no mode change.
Consequently,
\mathsf{dbf}_{S}^{\mathsf{cri}_{S}}(\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))
is the amount of computation time required by all jobs of the system with deadline
\leq\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
.
This quantity corresponds to the work that must be scheduled before
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
without mode switch,
so it is necessary, in a uniprocessor system, that this quantity does not exceed
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
.
∎

The above result does not anticipate a mode switch (i.e.,
\alpha=\mathsf{cri}_{S}
),
it gives the necessary condition foreseeing that no mode switch occurs.
Using a similar construct but setting
\alpha=\mathsf{HI}{}
,
a necessary condition that foresees a mode switch can be derived as follows.

Lemma 4
.

For feasibility it is
necessary
to have

\forall\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(\tau\right):\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})\geq\mathsf{dbf}_{S}^{\mathsf{HI}{}}(\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))
.

This necessary condition guarantees that all
\mathsf{HI}
-tasks in
S
will have enough time to execute all the
\mathsf{rct}
of both their current and future jobs, with their deadline prior to those of any active tasks’ jobs,
under the assumption that no job explicitly signals completion, eventually triggering a mode switch at the next possible instant.
Note that this necessary condition optimistically assumes that no future computing time would be wastefully given to a
\mathsf{LO}{}
task until
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
.
In practice, should a
\mathsf{LO}
task be scheduled, then
\mathsf{dbf}_{S}^{\mathsf{HI}{}}(\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))
would remain constant, whereas
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
will decrease, potentially violating this necessary condition.

Oracle 5

(
\mathsf{HI}{}
over demand)
.

\{S\mid\exists\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(\tau\right):\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})<\mathsf{dbf}_{S}^{\mathsf{HI}{}}(\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))\}\subseteq\text{{Unsafe}}
.

Proof.

If
\mathsf{cri}_{S}=\mathsf{HI}
, then the
Lemma 4
is equivalent to
Lemma 3
, and the proof provided for
Oracle 4
holds.
If
\mathsf{cri}_{S}=\mathsf{LO}
,
we consider two cases, one where a mode switch, triggered by a
\mathsf{HI}
-task
\tau_{k}\in\{\tau\mid L_{k}=\mathsf{HI}\}
, can occur before
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
, and the other where it may not.
For a (current or future) job of
\tau_{k}
to trigger a mode switch, it is sufficient to have
\mathsf{rct}(\tau_{k})>0\wedge\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})\geq\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{k})
or
\mathsf{rct}(\tau_{k})=0\wedge\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})\geq\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{k})+T_{k}
.
In such cases, we can anticipate the mode switch and enforce

\forall\tau_{i}\in\mathsf{Active}\left(\tau\right):\mathsf{ttd}_{S^{\prime}}(\tau_{i})\geq\mathsf{dbf}_{S^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{cri}_{S^{\prime}}}(\mathsf{ttd}_{S^{\prime}}(\tau_{i}))

as a necessary condition as per
Lemma 3
, with
S^{\prime}=\mathsf{critUp}_{S}\left(\bot\right)
.
Indeed be noted that
\mathsf{critUp}_{S}\left(\bot\right)
merely increases the
\mathsf{rct}
of the active
\mathsf{HI}
-jobs and discards the
\mathsf{LO}
tasks.
Therefore, if anything, this anticipation is more optimistic since it forestalls the wasteful scheduling of
\mathsf{LO}
-jobs.
Furthermore, given
\mathsf{cri}_{S^{\prime}}
=
\mathsf{HI}
, we can express
\mathsf{dbf}_{S^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{cri}_{S^{\prime}}}(\mathsf{ttd}_{S^{\prime}}(\tau_{i}))
as
\mathsf{dbf}_{S^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{HI}{}}(\mathsf{ttd}_{S^{\prime}}(\tau_{i}))
,
so if this inequality does not hold,
S^{\prime}
is deemed unsafe and so is
S
as
S^{\prime}
is more optimistic.
If a mode switch occurrence cannot be guaranteed before
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})
, i.e., when
\mathsf{rct}(\tau_{k})>0\wedge\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})<\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{k})
or
\mathsf{rct}(\tau_{k})=0\wedge\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})<\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{k})+T_{k}
, then
\mathsf{df}_{S}^{\mathsf{HI}}(\tau_{k},\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))=0
per definition.
Moreover, for
\mathsf{LO}
-tasks
\tau_{j}\in\{\tau\mid L_{j}=\mathsf{LO}\}
,
\mathsf{df}_{S}^{\mathsf{HI}}(\tau_{j},\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))=0
, and
\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i})\geq 0=\mathsf{dbf}_{S}^{\mathsf{HI}{}}(\mathsf{ttd}_{S}(\tau_{i}))
per definitions. Thus, under these circumstances, this oracle’s inequality never holds and
S
is never deemed unsafe by it.

∎

We can define a
stronger
condition that generalises the negative laxity one. Indeed, the minimal laxity must be larger than
0
(or null), the sum of the
two
minimal laxities must be larger than
1
(or
1
), etc.

Definition 22

(Sum minimal laxities)
.

In this definition, we assume that the laxities of active tasks are arranged in ascending order:
\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{1})\leq\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{2})\leq\cdots\leq\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{k})
. We define
\ell_{k}
as the sum of the
k
minimal laxities:
\ell_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{i})
.

Lemma 5
.

For the feasibility of
n
sporadic tasks upon uniprocessor it is
necessary
to have

\forall 1\leq k\leq n:\ell_{k}>k-2
.

Proof.

By induction.

Base step
:
When
k=1
, according to Lemma
1
, it is necessary that the smallest task laxity (
\ell_{1}
) to be non negative.

Inductive step
:
We will show that the property also holds for
n=k+1
.
By contradiction: we assume that
\ell_{k}=k-2+\epsilon>k-2
(
\epsilon>0
, induction hypothesis), and
\ell_{k+1}\leq k-1
and the system is feasible.
Notice that
\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{i})
is an integer
\forall\tau_{i}\in\tau
, as we are considering discrete time systems (i.e.,
T_{i}
,
D_{i}
, etc. are all integers).

\displaystyle\ell_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\ell_{k}+\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{k+1})\leq k-1

by definition of
\ell_{k+1}
and assumption

\displaystyle=k-2+\epsilon+\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{k+1})\leq k-1

induction hypothesis

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\epsilon+\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{k+1})\leq 1

rearranging terms

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathsf{laxity}_{S}(\tau_{k+1})<1

To demonstrate the benefits of the search space reduction and the accuracy of the exact test, we developed a C++ tool
[

\displaystyle\mathsf{sch}_{\text{LWLF}}(S)=

EDF-VD’s actual schedulability significantly outperforms its sufficient test, underscoring the test’s pessimism.
Notice, however, that this exact test — even with antichains and oracles — can be a time-intensive process, whereas the EDF-VD’s sufficient test is computed in polynomial time.
For large values of

One can always first run a sufficient test, and if negative, try the exact analysis.

LWLF brings further schedulability significant improvements over EDF-VD.
We derived LWLF after observing the strong impact of the worst laxity oracle

Related Work

There is extensive related research on real-time scheduling.
Below, we categorize and summarize the most relevant prior work to ours.

Scheduling tests.

Utilisation based conditions were produced, both sufficient
[
BBD+11a
]
and necessary
[
SLB15
]
, the latter also adapting them for multiprocessor.
Demand-bound function sufficient schedulability tests have also been produced, for dual-criticality
[
EY12
,
CLLX14
]
,
then extended to any number of criticalities
[
GE17
]
and to cope with deadline reduction on mode change
[
EY14b
]
.

Formal verification for mixed-criticality scheduling.

Burns and Jones used time bands and rely-guarantee conditions
[
JB20
,
BJ22
]
.
Inspired by Hoare’s logic, they proposed a framework to formally specifying the temporal behaviour of schedulers,
hence allowing the verification of schedulers and their code generation.
Abdeddaïm
[
Abd20
]
provided exact schedulability tests for FJP schedulers
for a more modular mixed criticality model, where only specified subsets of
\mathsf{LO}
(sporadic) tasks are discarded in
\mathsf{HI}
mode depending on the subset of
\mathsf{HI}
tasks exceeding their
\mathsf{LO}
budget.

Using automata to check schedulability.

Already discussed in our introduction (see
Section 1
).

Antichains.

The
antichain technique
has been introduced by De Wulf
et al.

[
WDHR06
]
to improve the practical performances of costly algorithms on automata.
These ideas have later been applied to produce a series of efficient algorithms for automata
[
DR10
,
ACH+10
]
, model-checking
[
DR09
]
and games
[
RCDH07
,
FJR11
,
GGNS18
]
.

Schedule-Abstraction Graphs (SAG).

Another reachability-based response-time analysis was developed for an exact test on sets of non-preemptive jobs with release jitter and variable execution time with a FJP scheduler upon a uniprocessor
[
NB17
]
, then upon multiprocessor
[
NNB18
]
, with preemption
[
GVN24
]
, and several kinds of inter-job constraints
[
NNB19
,
NNNB20
,
NMiIN22
]
.
Applying partial-order reduction rules into SAG mitigated
combinatorial explosion
[
RNN22
,
RGNN23
]
.
Notice a sequence of jobs
— the expected input of SAG methods —

cannot support
the sporadic task model.
By definition, the set of jobs is not known in advance,
which is not the case in systems
that are periodic or with release jitters.

8
Conclusion

We have developed a generic framework for exact schedulability assessment in uniprocessor mixed-criticality systems,
by reducing it to the safety problem in an automaton.
We combined insights from the formal verification community
— like antichains, simulation relations and the ACBFS algorithm —
and from the real-time research
— like safe and unsafe oracles —
to make the approach more practical.
We demonstrated in simulations that those allow to reduce the search space by up to 99.998%,
enabling the evaluation of existing and new scheduling algorithms, EDF-VD and LWLF,
and comparing it to a prior sufficient test
[
BBD+11a
]
.

Future work.

Although the scalability of our approach remain limited (up to 8 tasks),
our algorithm is generic,
allowing one to incorporate a range of other optimisations,
and can be applied to other scheduling problems.
Our work can be extended to consider
more realistic
mixed criticality models
[
BD22
]
,
e.g., regarding
\mathsf{LO}
-criticality tasks that should not be abandoned callously.
We then need to modify the completion transition, in order to
let
\mathsf{LO}
-tasks run until completion
or adjust the period and deadline of certain
\mathsf{LO}
-tasks.
A
multi-processor
version of the model is already supported in prior work for sequential jobs
[
LGG11
,
GGL13
]
, we would have to combine it with the extensions proposed in this work.
In addition, the reduction can be adapted to support other platforms and task models
such as modelling
varying CPU speed
and
job-level parallelism
(e.g., the gang model),
by reducing the
\mathsf{rct}
values according to the CPU speed and the number of CPU cores assigned to the job.
The
\mathsf{sch}(S)
function would return the selected CPU speed factor together with how many CPUs are assigned to each job.
A power function could then be used to label the clock-tick transitions with numeric values,
hinting shortest path exploration (instead of breadth-first search) to minimize power consumption.
To account for
preemption delays
, the model could force idle CPU time by forcing
\mathsf{run}=\bot
for a given number of run transitions when the scheduler is switching to a different task across calls.

\mathsf{Completed}\left(S^{\prime}_{2}\right)=\mathsf{Completed}\left(S^{\prime}_{1}\right)
.
Notice how the three outcomes for
S_{1}^{-}
depends only on
\mathsf{rct}_{S_{1}^{\prime}},\mathsf{Completed}\left(S^{\prime}_{1}\right),\theta
and
\mathsf{sch}(S_{1}^{+})
.
As
\theta
is fixed, and as we established that
\mathsf{sch}(S_{1}^{+})=\mathsf{sch}(S_{2}^{+})
and since
S_{1}^{\prime}\preccurlyeq_{idle}S_{2}^{\prime}
, we have
\mathsf{rct}_{S_{1}^{\prime}}=\mathsf{rct}_{S_{2}^{\prime}}
, then the outcome of the signal transition for
S_{2}^{\prime}
will be the same as for
S_{1}^{\prime}
.
Hence,
S_{1}^{-}\preccurlyeq_{idle}S_{2}^{-}
for every case.

To conclude the proof, it remains to prove that, if
S_{1}\preccurlyeq_{idle}S_{2}
and
S_{1}\in\mathsf{DeadlineMiss}(\tau)
then
S_{2}\in\mathsf{DeadlineMiss}(\tau)
also.
Let
\tau_{i}
such that
\mathsf{rct}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})>0\wedge\mathsf{ttd}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})\leq 0
.
Since
S_{1}\preccurlyeq_{idle}S_{2}
:
\mathsf{rct}_{S_{2}}(\tau_{i})=\mathsf{rct}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})
and
\mathsf{nat}_{S_{2}}(\tau_{i})\leq\mathsf{nat}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})
, thus
\mathsf{ttd}_{S_{2}}(\tau_{i})\leq\mathsf{ttd}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})
.
Hence
\mathsf{rct}_{S_{2}}(\tau_{i})>0\wedge\mathsf{ttd}_{S_{2}}(\tau_{i})\leq\mathsf{ttd}_{S_{1}}(\tau_{i})\leq 0
and therefore
S_{2}\in\mathsf{DeadlineMiss}(\tau)
.

∎
