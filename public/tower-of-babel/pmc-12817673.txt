Uncovering metabolic shifts: an ¹H-NMR study on the dual influence of HIV/TB coinfection and ART
Authors: Steven Patrick Ahumada Prado, Shayne Mason, Robin Wood, Laneke Luies
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and tuberculosis (TB) coinfection pose a substantial global health burden, with each infection amplifying the immune and metabolic impacts of the other. Investigating the compounded metabolic disruptions in HIV/TB coinfection and how these are modulated by antiretroviral therapy (ART) offers information into disease mechanisms and therapeutic strategies.MethodsThis study used untargeted proton nuclear magnetic resonance metabolomics to characterize the urine samples from three participant groups: healthy controls, treatment-naive HIV/TB coinfected individuals, and ART-treated HIV/TB coinfected individuals.ResultsSignificant metabolite shifts were observed across gut microbiota, energy, amino acid, and carbohydrate metabolism. Metabolomic profiles of HIV/TB coinfection suggested mitochondrial perturbation, markers of insulin resistance, and microbial dysbiosis. ART was associated with partial restoration of mitochondrial function, stabilization of gut microbiota, and reduced oxidative stress. However, glucose dysregulation appeared to persist. Key metabolites, including phenylacetylglycine and trans-aconitic acid, emerged as potential differential markers for coinfection and treatment response.ConclusionART modulates metabolic pathways disrupted by HIV/TB coinfection, showing partial improvements but leaving glucose metabolism dysregulated. These findings highlight the potential of targeted metabolic monitoring to optimize coinfection treatment strategies. Further investigations are needed in larger cohorts to validate these results and better understand coinfection pathophysiology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12088-7.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB), a deadly infectious disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb), is the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent worldwide, reclaiming its status after being briefly surpassed by COVID-19 over the past three years. This airborne pathogen typically infects the lungs but can spread to other organs in more severe cases [1]. A healthy immune system, primarily through the function of macrophages and T helper cells, can generally control TB, either eradicating the bacteria or forcing it into a latent, non-infectious state [2]. Globally, TB remains widespread, with an estimated 25% of the population thought to have latent TB [1].

Infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) significantly increases susceptibility to TB by compromising the ability of the immune system to contain the infection. HIV specifically targets and depletes CD4+ T cells, crucial for mounting an effective immune response [3], leaving individuals vulnerable to opportunistic infections, including TB. This heightened risk of TB persists even in patients receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART), and it remains approximately four times higher than in those without HIV [4]. In 2023, 662,000 of the 8,200,000 new TB cases occurred in HIV-positive individuals [1], highlighting the substantial public health burden posed by coinfection. Sub-Saharan Africa, particularly South Africa, bears the brunt of this dual epidemic due to the region’s high prevalence of HIV [3].

HIV treatment via ART aims to suppress the viral load to undetectable levels, employing a combination of drugs that target various stages of the HIV life cycle. ART improves immune function, thereby indirectly reducing the likelihood of TB progressing from latent to active disease [5]. However, diagnosing TB in HIV-positive individuals is challenging due to immune suppression, which can lead to atypical radiographic presentations of TB, such as the absence of the usual upper lobe infiltrates and a higher incidence of extra-pulmonary manifestations [6, 7]. Sadly, nearly all HIV-positive people with TB will die without proper treatment. Furthermore, traditional diagnostic tests, including the tuberculin skin test and interferon-gamma release assays, can yield false-negative results in HIV-infected individuals due to anergy. Globally, in 2023, 80% of all notified TB patients had a documented HIV result, and only 56% of those known to have HIV were receiving ART [1].

The metabolic interactions between HIV and TB in coinfected individuals present an emerging field of study, with significant implications for understanding disease progression and developing novel therapeutic approaches. Metabolomics, the comprehensive study of metabolites within biological systems [8], offers a promising approach to elucidate these complex metabolic interactions. Metabolomics helps elucidate the biochemical pathways disrupted by infection and treatment [9]. Metabolomics has illuminated complex metabolic shifts occurring in HIV/TB coinfection, particularly affecting lipid and protein metabolism, energy metabolism, the gut microbiome, immune modulation, systemic inflammation, and oxidative stress [10]. A 2024 study using an untargeted GC-MS metabolomics approach in HIV/TB coinfected individuals identified disruptions in lipid and carbohydrate metabolism, shifts in energy-related metabolites like citric acid, and alterations in the kynurenine pathway, a key player in immune response [11]. These findings highlight the adaptive metabolic changes driven by coinfection, notably increased fatty acid oxidation and amino acid catabolism that reflect elevated energy demands and immune dysregulation. The metabolic influence of TB within coinfection is further characterized by elevated medium-chain acylcarnitines and disturbances in amino acid ratios, such as increased branched-chain amino acids, suggesting a shift towards diabetes-like metabolic profiles [12]. Additionally, coinfection exacerbates TB-driven lipid and protein catabolism, oxidative stress, and microbial translocation. While ART reverses some disruptions, studies indicate that residual effects persist, with ART-associated mitochondrial dysfunction and gut health impacts evident even when viral suppression is achieved [13]. These findings underscore the importance of further research into the metabolic consequences of ART in coinfected patients to refine therapeutic approaches. Despite the widespread use of ART in managing HIV, the broader metabolic effects of ART in HIV/TB-coinfected individuals remain poorly understood [14]. In particular, knowledge gaps exist regarding drug interactions, optimal timing of treatment initiation for coinfected individuals, and the long-term metabolic impacts and potential side effects of ART.

To this end, this proof-of-concept study aimed to address gaps in understanding the complex metabolic interactions in HIV/TB coinfection, specifically focusing on the combined impact of both infections on metabolic pathways beyond the influence of HIV or TB alone. Additionally, we sought to explore how ART modifies these metabolic changes in coinfected individuals. To achieve these aims, we employed an untargeted proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H-NMR) urinary metabolomics approach, selecting urine as the primary biospecimen due to its non-invasive collection, availability in larger volumes, and rich composition of small-molecule metabolites. Unlike blood, urine contains fewer confounding proteins and lipids, facilitating reproducible profiling and reflecting a broad spectrum of systemic metabolic changes, making it particularly well-suited for untargeted NMR studies. By delineating the biochemical impact of both coinfection and ART, this study aims to provide information that could inform optimized therapeutic strategies and improve clinical management for HIV/TB patients.

Methods

Sample origin and selection criteria

Urine samples were collected using standardized protocols by the South African Tuberculosis Vaccine Initiative and the Desmond Tutu HIV Centre at the University of Cape Town [15]. These samples were de-identified before being transported to the Biomedical and Molecular Metabolism Research Group at the North-West University, North West, South Africa, where they were stored at -80 °C until further analysis.

Male and female participants (aged 18–69 years) were recruited from the Masiphumelele and Ocean View informal settlements in Cape Town, South Africa. Inclusion criteria required the absence of concurrent diseases, medication use, pregnancy, or lactation, ensuring a homogeneous study population with minimal confounding factors or variability in M.tb strains. All samples were collected at baseline, i.e., at the time of TB diagnosis and before initiation of TB treatment, where applicable. Written informed consent for HIV testing was obtained from all participants, with HIV status confirmed by serum sample testing. No retrospective HIV testing was performed.

Additional clinical information, including family TB history and current TB-related symptoms, was recorded to support TB status determination. Active pulmonary TB was diagnosed using the GeneXpert test, following South African and World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines. Participants requiring healthcare services were referred accordingly.

Participants (n = 59) were stratified into three groups based on confirmed HIV and TB status and ART use: healthy, asymptomatic controls (HIV-/TB-/Tx-; n = 32), treatment-naïve coinfected patients (HIV+/TB+/Tx-; n = 9), and coinfected patients receiving ART (HIV+/TB+/Tx+; n = 18). All ART-treated participants received a fixed-dose combination regimen of tenofovir (300 mg), emtricitabine (200 mg), and efavirenz (600 mg). Further demographic details of the participants are provided in Table 1.

All samples were collected with the necessary ethical approvals and participant consent. According to standard clinic procedures, routine CD4+ counts and viral load measurements were not performed at the time of HIV diagnosis, in line with WHO recommendations that diagnosis should be based on two consecutive positive HIV tests rather than initial immune or viral load assessments. Interferon-gamma release assays were also not routinely performed. Consequently, the control group may have included individuals with latent or subclinical TB.

Reagents and chemicals

All reagents were sourced from Merck. A 1.5 M 1H-NMR urine buffer solution was prepared by dissolving 20.4 g of potassium phosphate monobasic (KH2PO4) in 80 mL of deuterium oxide (D2O). To this, 100 mg of trimethylsilyl-2,2,3,3-tetradeuteropropionic acid (TSP), used as the quantitative internal standard, was added. Additionally, 13 mg of sodium azide (NaN3) was dissolved in 10 mL of D2O and introduced to the solution to inhibit bacterial growth. The solution was sonicated to ensure thorough mixing and the pH was adjusted to 7.4 using potassium hydroxide (KOH) pellets. The solution was then transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask and brought to volume with D2O.

Sample preparation and 1H-NMR analysis

Prior to analysis, all urine samples were thawed to room temperature. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling 30 µL from each sample. The QC samples were treated identically to the experimental samples. Samples were analyzed as described by Isaiah et al. [52]. Briefly, a 1 mL aliquot of each sample was centrifuged at 12,000 x g for 5 min to remove particulate matter. From the resulting supernatant, 540 µL was collected and mixed with 60 µL of the NMR urine buffer solution. After thorough vortexing to ensure homogeneity, the mixture was centrifuged again at 12,000 x g for 5 min, and a final 540 µL of supernatant was transferred to a 5 mm NMR glass tube and capped for analysis. Samples were loaded in a randomized sequence, with QC samples interspersed periodically, onto the autosampler of a Bruker Avance III HD 500 MHz NMR spectrometer equipped with a triple-resonance inverse (TXI) 1H (15N,13C) probe head and x, y, and z gradient coils. The 1H spectra were recorded with a spectral width of 12,000 Hz, 128 transients, and 32 K data points, with the sample temperature maintained at 300 K. The H2O resonance was pre-saturated using single-frequency irradiation (NOESY) with a relaxation delay of 4 s and an excitation pulse of 8 µsec. Automated sample shimming relied on the deuterium signal, and resonance line widths for TSP and metabolites were kept below 1 Hz.

Data pre-processing and statistical analysis

Data processing was done using Bruker Topspin (version 3.6.4) for Fourier transformation and automated baseline and phase correction. The TSP peak was calibrated to 0.00 ppm. Spectral resolution quality was checked manually by measuring the TSP peak at half-height in width (< 1 Hz). The efficiency of the water resonance peak suppression at approximately 4.72 ppm was verified manually. The creatinine levels across the groups were checked and no significant differences (p-value ≥ 0.05) were detected (i.e., the disease(s) and/or treatment did not affect creatinine excretion); hence, creatinine was deemed suitable for normalization purposes. Normalization was carried out relative to the creatinine peak signal at approximately 4.05 ppm to account for the dilution difference in the urine samples. Further data pre-processing was done using Bruker AMIX software, version 3.9.14.

The 1H-NMR spectral data were binned at set intervals of 0.02 ppm. The generated data matrix contained bins as columns and samples as rows. Data cleaning included the identification of bin regions free of peaks — blank data areas across all groups. The limit of detection (LOD) was then calculated by averaging all intensities in blank bins (those without discernible peaks) and adding 9.9 times the standard deviation of these bins (LOD=Mean of Blank + 9.9 × SD of Blank) [64]. All integral bin values below the LOD (i.e., noise) were zeroed in the spectral data matrix. Thereafter, the bin dataset was subjected to 50% zero filtering/missing value filtering and any remaining missing values were imputed using a fifth of the minimum value across the dataset. The analytical quality was monitored by evaluating the coefficient of variation (CV) across the QC samples. Any bins exhibiting a QC CV ≥ 30% were removed to ensure data reliability.

The bin dataset was imported into MetaboAnalyst 6.0, a web-based platform for high-throughput untargeted metabolomics, and was further normalized by log transformation and Pareto scaling to adjust for skewed distributions, enabling more reliable comparisons between groups. Statistically significant bins were selected based on the absolute fold change (FC) with a threshold of ≥ 2.0 [65]. Metabolites in these statistically significant bins were identified using a pure chemical compound spectral library (both 1D and 2D confirmation) and were quantified relative to creatinine (µmol/mmol creatinine). All subsequent statistical analyses were done on quantified significant metabolites.

To visualize and compare the groups, principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were performed on the significant metabolites to assess potential separations within batches and group interactions [41]. These metabolites also underwent further univariate analyses, including analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess differences between all groups [66] and the calculation of Glass’s d-values. Results

Fold change (FC) with a threshold of ≥ 2.0 was used to identify significant bins. Thereafter, multivariate analyses (PCA and PLS-DA) were done on the identified and quantified metabolites to assess group interactions. PCA showed no clear differentiation between the three cohorts (Fig. S1a). Similarly, PLS-DA revealed no significant separation among the groups (Fig. S1b), and a permutation test had a p-value of 0.05 (Fig. S1c), indicating that the data was overfitted, and poor cross-validation R2 and Q2 values (Fig. S1d). Based on these findings, the multivariate model was deemed unsuitable for this cohort, likely due to the small sample sizes. Consequently, univariate analyses were prioritized to identify significant metabolites by using additional criteria.

For the univariate analyses, three statistical tests, each with strict cutoff criteria, were applied. The identified and quantified metabolites were considered significant if they met at least one of the three following criteria: an ANOVA false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p-value < 0.05, an absolute FC ≥ 2.0, or a Glass’s effect size d-value ≥ 0.5 (moderate effect). The ANOVA (Fig. 1a) showed variance within the metabolic profiles across all three groups, highlighting four statistically significant metabolites. An FC comparison between treatment-naïve coinfected patients (HIV+/TB+/Tx-) and healthy controls (HIV-/TB-/Tx-) revealed eight metabolites (Fig. 1b). Similarly, an FC comparison between HIV+/TB+/Tx- and coinfected patients on ART (HIV+/TB+/Tx+) identified eight significant metabolites (Fig. 1c). Lastly, Glass’s effect size analysis identified three metabolites with significant differences across groups. Using these criteria, a total of 13 significant differential metabolites were selected (when adjusting for duplicates in the list) for further interpretation. These significant metabolites are listed in Table 2, segregated by their biological classification. Corresponding box-and-whisker plots are provided in Fig. S2.

Discussion

Given the small and diverse sample groups — varying in disease severity, ART duration, and lacking extensive clinical indicators — the identified metabolites (Table 2; Fig. 2) are presented as exploratory differential markers. This proof-of-concept analysis provides information on the metabolic impacts of HIV/TB coinfection and ART, with differential metabolite selection based on statistical significance in at least one of three metrics: ANOVA, FC, or Glass’s effect size

Urinary metabolomic comparisons between healthy controls and treatment-naïve coinfected patients highlight the compounded metabolic effects of dual infection, providing a snapshot of the general response to HIV and TB as a combined pathology. Key metabolites such as citric acid, ethanol, mannose, and hippuric acid showed univariate statistical significance thresholds in at least two univariate metrics, reflecting the disruption of energy pathways, carbohydrate processing, and gut microbiota. To gain further insight into how ART modulates these metabolic disruptions, the metabolomic profiles of treatment-naïve and ART-treated coinfected patients were compared. This comparison offers a clearer understanding of how ART impacts the metabolic shifts driven by coinfection, providing a basis for discussing how ART influences these key disrupted pathways. Gut microbiota

HIV/TB coinfection was associated with alterations in gut microbiota-related metabolites, specifically hippuric acid, 2-furorylglycine, phenylacetylglycine, dimethylsulfone (DMSO2), and ethanol. Consistent with prior urine-based omics studies in TB, urinary metabolite signatures can discriminate active TB from controls, underscoring the value of non-invasive readouts for host-microbiome interactions [16, 17]. These metabolites, often reflective of microbial byproducts entering systemic circulation, may indicate gut barrier dysfunction, a hallmark of immune-compromised individuals, such as those with HIV infection [18]. Hippuric acid, a product of microbial aromatic metabolism, decreased in the coinfected treatment-naïve patients in comparison to healthy controls, potentially reflecting dysbiosis and reduced metabolic output of gut microbiota. Dysbiosis can impair detoxification and microbial metabolism [19], highlighting the impact of HIV/TB coinfections on gut microbiota dynamics and underscoring the broader systemic health implications. 2-Furorylglycine, a microbial metabolite derived from phenylalanine and tyrosine degradation [20], was also significantly decreased in the coinfected treatment-naïve group compared to healthy controls. This marked reduction likely reflects microbial dysbiosis exacerbated by dual infection; possibly driven by heightened immune activation and gut barrier dysfunction. Additionally, as an end-product of phenylalanine metabolism, increased levels of phenylacetylglycine in the coinfected treatment-naïve patients may be consistent with increased utilization of phenylalanine due to heightened immune activity [21]; both HIV and TB place substantial demands on the immune system, altering amino acid processing. It implies that the liver compensates for increased immunological demands by intensifying phenylalanine conjugation, resulting in elevated phenylacetylglycine production. Consequently, the preferential diversion of phenylalanine into phenylacetylglycine, rather than 2-furoylglycine, in coinfected treatment-naïve can be attributed to dysbiosis-induced impairment of microbial pathways and upregulated hepatic conjugation in response to increased immunometabolic demands.

DMSO2, a metabolite derived from the oxidation of dimethyl sulfoxide, is linked to microbial sulfur metabolism [22, 23]. Its reduction in coinfected treatment-naïve patients may reflect changes in microbial sulfur metabolism or altered detoxification processes, potentially influenced by oxidative stress and inflammation associated with dual infection. This interplay between sulfur metabolism and gut modulation could influence systemic immune responses, contributing to the heightened inflammation typically observed in HIV/TB coinfection [11]. HIV depletes CD4+ T cells, specifically in the gut-associated lymphoid tissue, compromising the gut mucosal barrier. This fosters a dysbiotic environment that often favors sulphur-reducing bacteria. Alongside ongoing inflammatory processes, these bacteria can further modulate sulphur-containing metabolites such as DMSO2.

Initially, elevated ethanol levels in the coinfected treatment-naïve patients were linked to microbial fermentation [24], particularly in the context of gut dysbiosis seen in chronic infections [25]. However, closer examination suggests that such high ethanol concentrations are unlikely to be solely of microbial origin and are more plausibly due to either recent alcohol consumption by participants or contamination from ethanol-based cleaning agents.

ART was associated with changes in several gut microbiota-derived metabolites, which may reflect partial recovery of gut barrier function and modulating microbial metabolism. Hippuric acid showed a slight improvement under ART but did not reach healthy levels, suggesting residual liver stress [26] and incomplete microbiome recovery. 2-Furorylglycine remained unchanged, indicating that ART does not significantly alter its microbial precursors or that persistent (nonetheless reduced) inflammation and incomplete immune reconstitution limit its metabolic recovery. ART further exacerbated phenylacetylglycine levels, likely due to persistent microbial shifts favoring phenylalanine metabolism [27, 28]. As the immune system recovers under ART, the gut environment changes in a way that allows bacterial communities reliant on phenylalanine to thrive. This reshaping, along with altered nutrient flows and host enzymatic activity, drive up phenylalanine turnover and lead to increased production of its downstream byproducts, including phenylacetylglycine. DMSO2 further declined under ART, possibly due to reduced sulphur-metabolizing bacteria as gut inflammation somewhat decreases. In untreated HIV/TB coinfection, the cycle of gut barrier dysfunction and inflammation is exacerbated. However, ART partially restores CD4+ T cell counts, stabilizing the gut epithelium integrity, which bolsters mucosal defenses and reduces systemic and local immune activation [29]. Thus, the production and turnover of sulfur-containing metabolites like DMSO2 diminish under the effects of ART. Ethanol levels showed improved gut barrier integrity and reduced microbial fermentation, though not fully normalized, possibly reflecting behavioral and metabolic changes. Overall, while ART partially mitigates gut dysbiosis and inflammation, it does not fully restore microbial metabolism, suggesting the need for additional interventions targeting both microbiome and liver health.

Amino acid metabolism

Decreased dimethylglycine levels in the coinfected treatment-naïve patients indicate a compensatory shift in one-carbon metabolism. As a methyl donor, dimethylglycine supports methylation processes which are often upregulated during immune activation [30, 31], reflecting an adaptive response to oxidative stress. This decrease may suggest impairments in the pathway, possibly due to deficiencies in essential cofactors such as folate or vitamin B12, which are common in individuals with HIV or TB [7, 32, 33]. Such deficiencies may lead to dysregulation and cellular damage associated with HIV/TB coinfection [34], as the methylation pathway is crucial for maintaining cellular integrity [35]. Perturbations of amino-acid and one-carbon linked pathways have been repeatedly reported in TB across both untargeted and targeted platforms, supporting this interpretation [36, 37].

Dimethylglycine levels normalized with ART, suggesting reduced methylation demand [38] and altered liver metabolism. While ART lowers inflammation, it does not fully restore methylation balance or cellular repair.

Energy metabolism

HIV/TB coinfection significantly impacted energy metabolism, as reflected in the urinary levels of trans-aconitic acid, citric acid, trigonelline, and 1-methylnicotinamide. Both citric acid and trans-aconitic acid, key intermediates in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, were altered in the coinfected group. Increased trans-aconitic acid in coinfected treatment-naïve patients may reflect mitochondrial stress or dysfunction [39], potentially driven by the heightened energy demands of dual infection. Mitochondrial dysfunction is associated with both HIV [40] and TB [41] and is further supported by the decreased citric acid in these patients, which suggests impaired TCA cycle activity. This could reflect mitochondrial stress, where the energy demands exceed the capacity for efficient energy production, especially in the context of immune activation.

The reduction in trigonelline levels in the coinfected treatment-naïve group likely reflects increased niacin utilization for NAD+ synthesis in response to oxidative stress from both TB and HIV [42]. TB activity is accompanied by increased tryptophan catabolism, and the kynurenine/tryptophan ratio has diagnostic value, including HIV/TB coinfection [43, 44]. These features normalized with effective treatment in several cohorts. Concurrently, elevated 1-methylnicotinamide, a nicotinamide N-methyltransferase product involved in cellular redox regulations [45, 46], indicates heightened NAD+ turnover to counteract infection-induced oxidative stress and inflammation [47], likely representing a compensatory response to mitigate cellular damage.

ART seemed to partially restore mitochondrial function, as reflected by trans-aconitic acid levels returning to baseline. Similarly, 1-methylnicotinamide normalization suggests reduced oxidative stress and inflammation, lowering the demand for NAD+ metabolism [48]. However, persistent metabolic disruptions remain. Citric acid levels declined further with ART, likely due to nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor-induced mitochondrial toxicity, as reported in previous studies [49], impairing the TCA cycle via inhibition of mitochondrial DNA polymerase gamma. Trigonelline levels also continued to decrease, reflecting incomplete recovery of NAD+ metabolism. Since trigonelline is a precursor to NAD+, its reduction alongside citric acid suggests that while ART alleviates some mitochondrial stress, it does not fully restore TCA cycle function or NAD+ homeostasis [40]. ART mitigates oxidative stress and partially restores mitochondrial function, evidenced by near-baseline levels of trans-aconitic acid and 1-methylnicotinamide. Persistent NRTI-induced toxicity and incomplete decrease of inflammation, continue to impair the energy metabolism pathways manifested in the further reduction of citric acid and trigonelline. This underscores ART’s partial metabolic recovery without fully reestablishing TCA cycle function or NAD+ homeostasis.

Carbohydrate metabolism

Sucrose levels were elevated in treatment-naïve coinfected patients compared to healthy controls, potentially reflecting the impact of HIV/TB coinfection on carbohydrate metabolism. Under normal conditions, sucrose, which is naturally found in fruits and vegetables, is metabolized into glucose and fructose [50], which are crucial for energy production. The immune response to HIV/TB coinfection triggers increased cytokine production [51], known to interfere with insulin signaling pathways and alter glucose utilization [52] to meet the elevated energy demands from immune activation. The higher urinary sucrose levels in treatment-naïve coinfected individuals may indicate more complex metabolic and physiological disruptions. Additionally, elevated urinary sucrose levels have been observed in tuberculous meningitis (TBM) cases [52, 53], which may be linked to more severe immune responses or organ-specific effects, such as renal dysfunction or impaired tubular reabsorption — since the kidneys are the most likely source of human sucrose clearance.

Furthermore, the observed decrease in glucose levels among coinfected treatment-naïve patients further indicates a dysregulation of carbohydrate metabolism, potentially reflecting impaired insulin sensitivity [54], a known consequence of chronic infections [55]. Low glucose levels may also trigger counter-regulatory hormones, further exacerbating insulin resistance [56]. Given the broader metabolic disruptions observed, including mitochondrial dysfunction, these findings reinforce the impact of HIV/TB coinfection on energy homeostasis and glucose regulation [57]. Mannose, essential for protein glycosylation, supports immune cell signaling and pathogen recognition [58]. Its elevation in coinfected treatment-naïve patients likely indicates increased glycosylation to support immune activation in response to dual infection, which is consistent with studies showing upregulated glycosylation under infectious stress [59].

ART-associated disruptions in carbohydrate metabolism are apparent, with elevated sucrose levels, contributing to hyperglycemia and inflammation. Although protease inhibitors, which are strongly linked to insulin resistance, were not included in the ART regimens, these treatments may still affect lipid and glucose metabolism [60, 61], as well as cytokine regulation. Similarly, glucose levels decreased further with ART, indicating lingering glucose metabolism disruptions linked to chronic inflammation which possibly contributes to insulin resistance [62]. On the other hand, mannose levels partially recovered with ART, suggesting partial normalization of glycosylation with immune activation stabilization [63]. This reduction may also reflect improved liver function and metabolic processing.

Limitations

While this study reveals significant metabolic alterations in HIV/TB coinfection, several factors limit the generalizability of the results. The small sample size and exploratory nature reduce our ability to control for potential confounders and preclude broad clinical application. As a proof-of-concept study, the primary aim was to identify preliminary metabolic shifts associated with HIV/TB coinfection and ART; however, the absence of detailed clinical data, such as CD4+ cell counts or viral load, limits interpretation of immune function and metabolic response. This study also did not assess low-abundance urinary immune activation markers (e.g., MCP-1, RANTES, IP-10, IL-8, B2M) since untargeted 1H-NMR metabolomics is not optimized for protein or cytokine detection. Future targeted analyses using immunoassay or mass spectrometry could explore correlations between such markers and the metabolic alterations described here, offering complementary insight into systemic immune activation and renal inflammation. To strengthen future work, larger cohorts, integration of clinical and immunological metrics, and longitudinal sampling will be essential for establishing causative links and validating these preliminary findings. Such approaches would help clarify the complex metabolic interplay between HIV and TB and guide more targeted therapeutic interventions.

Conclusion

This proof-of-concept study provides a comprehensive analysis of the metabolic impacts of HIV/TB coinfection and ART, revealing distinct alterations across energy, carbohydrate, amino acid, and gut microbiota-associated pathways. Using untargeted ¹H-NMR metabolomics, the study identified significant shifts in metabolites that highlight the compounded immune and metabolic demands imposed by coinfection, as well as ART’s modulating effects. The metabolic changes associated with coinfection are consistent with increased mitochondrial stress, markers of insulin resistance, and shifts in microbial-associated metabolites, with ART being associated with partial recovery of mitochondrial function, microbial balance, and immune-regulated metabolic markers. However, ART does not fully resolve all metabolic disturbances, such as incomplete recovery of TCA cycle function and NAD+ metabolism. Additionally, continued disruptions in carbohydrate metabolism, such as altered sucrose and glucose levels, contribute to hyperglycemia and inflammation. These findings suggest that while ART improves certain aspects of metabolic health, further interventions targeting both the microbiome and liver health are necessary to fully restore metabolic homeostasis. These findings lay a foundation for future studies that can validate these differential metabolic markers and expand our understanding of HIV/TB coinfection, ultimately contributing to more precise and effective interventions for managing this complex dual pathology.
