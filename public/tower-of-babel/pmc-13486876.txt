Integrating environmental, economic, and technological assessments for modified biochar materials in complex contaminated soil remediation
Authors: Lu Yu, Yuhang Zu, Tianyou Li, Yufeng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLife cycle assessment (LCA) and life cycle cost (LCC) analysis were integrated to evaluate four modified biochars (acid/base-modified, magnetically modified, nano-modified, and organically modified) for complex, contaminated soil remediation. LCA ranked modified biochars in term of environmental impacts as follows: organic > nano > acid/base > magnetic. LCC analysis ranked biochars in terms of total costs as nano > organic > magnetic > acid/base. In physicochemical improvement, biochars ranked as magnetic > nano > organic > acid/base. Comprehensive assessment recommends acid/base-modified biochar for agricultural pollution, magnetically modified biochar for industrial sites (due to high adsorption and recyclability), organically modified biochar for domestic waste dumping areas, while nano-modified biochar is not primarily recommended owing to high cost and performance variability. The findings support decision-making by balancing environmental friendliness, cost effectiveness, and material properties across different contamination scenarios.Keywords: modified biochar materials, soil of multi-contaminated sites, life cycle assessment, LCA, life cycle cost analysis, LCC analysis, physicochemical characteristics, environmental-economic-technological assessments

Introduction

Due to rapid industrialization and urbanization, environmental problems have become increasingly prominent, which, in turn, has hindered the economic development in developing countries. Compared with air and water pollutions, soil pollution is more difficult to detect and characterized by accumulation and irreversibility.1 According to data from the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of the People’s Republic of China (2014),2 the polluted area has reached 16.1% of the total soil area. Among the polluted sites, the minor, mild, moderate, and severe pollution locations were reported to account for 11.2%, 2.3%, 1.5%, and 1.1% of the total areas, respectively. It highlights the severity of historical soil pollution problems. In the past decade, the Chinese government’s soil environmental management strategy has shifted from merely investigating pollution to comprehensive risk control. The current policy focus and evaluation indicators have shifted to “safe utilization rate.” The national trend of worsening soil pollution has been initially curbed. As of now, the safe utilization rate of polluted farmland nationwide has reached 92% (2024).3 Excess heavy metal and organic pollutants in soil have endangered local ecosystems and human health.4,5 Although macro risks have been basically controlled, soil remediation and long-term risk management in historical industrial areas and densely farmed regions remain major practical challenges currently being faced.

Biochar, a product of the pyrolysis of organic materials (e.g., plant straw, livestock excrement, dry branches, and fallen leaves) under completely or partially anaerobic conditions,6,7 was distinguished from charcoal by its use as a soil amendment.8 Due to its large specific surface area, abundant functional groups, and loose tubular structure, biochar was reported to effectively adsorb pollutants in soil environments.9 However, previous studies have focused more on solving pollution problems associated with individual pollutants rather than mixtures of multiple pollutants. Similarly, more studies have reported the higher efficacy of biochar in reducing heavy metal content in soils10,11 than in degrading organic pollutants12; mixtures of organic-inorganic contaminants have been considered even less.

To improve the remediation of soil environments by biochar, some modification techniques have emerged. Generally speaking, modified biochar materials mainly include acid/base-modified biochar,13 magnetically modified biochar,14 nano-modified biochar,15 and organically modified biochar,16 etc., according to the type and use of modified reagents. The application of acid/base-modified biochar offers a low-cost means to regulate soil pH, which subsequently affects π-π electron interactions.17 Magnetically modified biochar offers significant advantages for environmental remediation. It not only provides binding sites for heavy metal stabilization and acts as a reducing agent to degrade organic pollutants but also enables highly efficient separation, extraction, and reuse from treated media.14,18,19,20 Nano-modified biochar can significantly enhance adsorption capacity due to its increased specific surface area, which supports simultaneous cation exchange, π-π binding, and complexation.15 However, this synthesis process is associated with several disadvantages, including complex operations, high energy consumption, and a long production cycle.21 The surface functional groups of organically modified biochar can form dual coordination complexes with heavy metals, thereby reducing the concentration of bioavailable heavy metals in soil.22 Furthermore, such biochar has been shown to effectively remove organic pollutants via partitioning and hydrophobic interactions.23 However, studies indicate that secondary pollution resulting from the organic modification process may be inevitable.24

On the basis of the discussion of the advantages and disadvantages of various modified biochar materials for remediating complex contaminated soils (Table 1), we have developed a relatively clear understanding of their characteristics and mechanisms of action. However, systematic assessments of their overall effectiveness and impacts—considering technological, economic, and environmental perspectives—are still lacking. This study fills this research gap by providing an integrated environment-economy-technology evaluation. Therefore, it is essential to conduct comprehensive techno-economic-environmental evaluations to formulate targeted implementation strategies tailored to specific soil contamination types.

This study, through a systematic literature review (2017–2023), summarizes the merits and demerits of four typical modified biochar materials—acid/base-modified biochar, magnetically modified biochar, nano-modified biochar, and organically modified biochar—for the remediation of complex contaminated soils. Subsequently, the environmental and economic impacts of the production stage (biomass pretreatment, pyrolysis process, and material modification steps) were evaluated using life cycle assessment (LCA) and life cycle cost (LCC) analysis. In parallel, the physicochemical characteristics (including physical adsorption capacity and chemical active groups) of each modified biochar were fully investigated to reflect incremental technical performance. Multivariate statistical methods, including Pearson’s correlation analysis and comprehensive assessment, were adopted to support decision-making for different pollution scenarios.

Research framework and methods

Research strategy and process

In order to achieve the purpose of remediating the compound contaminated soil, we paid attention to biochar, the most commonly used soil pollution remediation agent, and adopted the evaluation method of environment, economy, and technology to conduct multi-dimensional research and evaluate the remediation effect.

Through literature research and soil investigation of contaminated sites, four kinds of biochar-modified materials with application value were selected as research targets: acid/base-modified biochar, magnetically modified biochar, nano-modified biochar, and organically modified biochar. To study the contribution of the above modified biochars in treating complex contaminated soils, studies from 2017 to 2023 were systematically reviewed with the following keywords: “acid/base modified” (or “acid base modified” or “acid modified” or “acidic modified” or “acidic” or “base modified” or “base ion modified” or “base” or “alkaline”), “magnetic modified” or “nano-modified” (or “nanomodified” or “nano modified”), “organic modified” (or “thiourea modified” or “chitosan modified” or “thiol modified” or “mercaptan”), “biochar,” “contaminated soil” or “polluted soil” or “soil pollution” or “heavy metal contaminated soil” or “organic pollutant” (or “organic contaminant”) or “composite contaminated soil” or “organic and inorganic compound polluted soil,” and “soil remediation” or “soil restoration” or “soil pollution control” in Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus. After referring to thousands of studies, we identified a total of 226 studies closely related to our goals (Table S1), covering 7 cases involving acid/base-modified biochar, 50 cases involving magnetically modified biochar, 40 cases involving nano-modified biochar, and 156 cases involving organically modified biochar (some papers introduced multiple modified biochar materials, Figure S1). From north to south, the soil survey sites of contaminated sites included Daxing District of Beijing (39.75°, 116.38°), Jiaojiang District (28.53°, 121.37°), and Wenling City (28.47°, 121.36°), Zhejiang Province, Qingyuan City (23.55°, 113.00°), Guangdong Province, etc.

The environmental and economic impacts of technological processes for producing modified biochar materials were analyzed and evaluated by LCA combined with LCC analysis. In addition, physical adsorption capacity and chemical active groups of each modified biochar material from the above 188 cases were also fully investigated, reflecting incremental physicochemical remediation performance. In detail, pore volume and specific surface area indicating physical adsorption properties, as well as carbonyl groups and carboxyl groups indicating chemical functional groups, were considered main physicochemical indexes reflecting the improvements in dissolving pollutants achieved by the modification process.

This research aimed at optimizing biochar materials for remediating the complex contaminated soils through a comprehensive analysis of the environmental and economic impacts, as well as incremental calculation of the physicochemical characteristics (Figure 1).

LCA method

Regardless of the modification method, the LCA in this study was defined with a cradle-to-gate system boundary. The ecological assessment of materials under practical application scenarios, as well as the disposal or end-of-life stage after material use, were not considered in this study. This was because the present work focused primarily on the comparative evaluation of the production stage (including feedstock pretreatment, pyrolysis, and modification) of different modified biochars, rather than on their full life cycle performance in practical remediation applications. LCA was applied to comprehensively evaluate the environmental impact by using the CML2001 method59 in GaBi software 2020. The environmental impacts mainly included acidification potential (AP), eutrophication potential (EP), global warming potential (GWP), human toxicity potential (HTP), freshwater aquatic ecotoxicity potential (FAETP), and terrestrial ecotoxicity potential (TETP).60 To quantify the above environmental impact indicators, this study mainly employed “equivalent factor method,” which took main factors of the project, such as SO2, as the benchmark.

The data for acid/base-modified biochar were collected from 7 relevant published studies (e.g., Xie et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022).27,61 For magnetically modified biochar, 51 papers (e.g., Li et al., 2023; Chandra et al., 2023)62,63 were considered; for nano-modified biochar, 41 papers (e.g., Chen et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023)64,65 were considered; and for organically modified biochar, 158 papers (e.g., Li et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023)66,67 were considered. These papers provided the preparation parameters, reagent dosage, and energy consumption data required for the LCA and LCC analysis. Among these, 29 papers involved more than one type of modified biochar. Overall, a total of 226 studies were included in the analysis, with the detailed literature inventory provided in Table S1.

LCC method

The economic impacts of biochar modification methods were analyzed using LCC,68,69 which was part of the life cycle framework to quantify the total product cost. The unit price of raw materials and energy cost were obtained according to investigation and research aimed at handling 1 kg biomass for biochar preparation. The service life was assumed to be 1 year. The cost of equipment depreciation, regular maintenance, and equipment updating was negligible when converted to a functional unit. Physicochemical characteristic analysis and standardized calculations

The changes in physicochemical characteristics were represented by pore volume or specific surface area as the physical index and by carbonyl or carboxyl functional groups (–OH or –COOH) as the chemical index; these parameters were recorded when they increased in the modified biochar compared with the pristine biochar. Second, these changes were calculated separately, and a corresponding score was obtained. The detailed calculation method is as follows: average rate of increase in the pore volume was calculated as the increase in pore volume in all modified biochars divided by the number of modified biochar cases based on statistics; growth ratio of specific surface area in the modified biochar was calculated as the number of growth rates in specific surface area exceeding the average growth rate divided by the total amount of modified biochar cases; and increase in the proportion of carbonyl or carboxyl functional groups (%) was calculated as the amount of biochar with increased carbonyl functional groups divided by the total amount of modified biochars. Finally, the physicochemical functional groups were calculated using the normalization method, and the rates of increase in pore volume and specific surface area of the modified biochar and the rates of increase in the carbonyl and carboxyl groups of the modified biochar were normalized. Data processing and statistics

Pearson’s correlation analysis of environmental factors, economic factors, and standardized values of physicochemical characteristics was carried out in IBM Statistics SPSS (version 22).70 The contributions of environmental impacts, economic impacts, and incremental changes in physicochemical characteristics of the four modified biochars were comprehensively evaluated for application to different pollution scenarios, which is summarized in a conceptual diagram. Other data analyses and charting were performed in Origin 2021 and Excel 2021. All analyses, including Pearson’s correlation analysis, data normalization, basic plotting, and chord diagram generation, were performed exclusively by following the standard functions of the software used (SPSS, Excel, Origin, and GaBi).

Results

General methods for preparing modified biochar materials

The four biochar modification materials with application value in remediation of contaminated soil environments were acid/base-modified biochar, magnetically modified biochar, nano-modified biochar, and organically modified biochar (as described in “research strategy and process”). Acid/base-modified biochar is generally treated by H2O2 or NaOH solution, and then modified by HNO3 or HNO3/H2SO4 mixture for several hours. Specific modification conditions vary across the cited studies and are detailed in the corresponding references listed in Table 1.25,71

The preparation technology for magnetically modified biochar is generally classified into two categories: (1) pyrolysis before magnetizing the biochar; and (2) pyrolysis after magnetizing the biomass. For either of the above methods, the detailed procedure is described as follows: a certain amount of K2FeO4 is added to a beaker with DI water under stirring to dissolve it (K2FeO4:DI water ratio, 2 g: 50 mL). Less than five times the amount of K2FeO4 biochar raw material is poured into the above K2FeO4 solution, and the solution is mechanically stirred for 10 h. The mixed solution is then placed in a water bath and continuously stirred at 80°C to obtain a mash. Subsequently, it is transferred to an oven and dried at 100°C for 12 h. The obtain solid mixture is crushed and passed through a 20-mesh sieve and pyrolyzed in a tube furnace at a designated temperature (600°C, 700°C, and 800°C) for 2 h, with a heating rate of 5°C min−1 in N2 atmosphere. After the tube furnace is cooled to room temperature, the black solid material is collected, ground, and washed three times with DI water. Finally, the samples are dried in a vacuum oven at 80°C for 24 h (Table 1).14,18

Nano-modified biochar is prepared as follows: a certified biochar made of 80% coniferous and 20% deciduous wood chip pyrolyzed at 700 °C for 36 h and an air-stable nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVI) product are sieved to <0.5-mm particle size. For the activation of Fe0 nanoparticles, vigorous mixing is conducted in (1) deionized (DI) water and (2) 0.02 M ammonium oxalate solution, both in a 1:4 solid/liquid ratio. Mixing of biochar (BC) in DI water is performed for uniformity. The BC-nZVI composite was prepared by the addition of BC powder to the nZVI-water suspension (1:1 w/w) during mixing. The BC particles are occasionally covered or filled with nZVI particles. A fresh suspension of each sorbent (biochar + nZVI) is prepared immediately before its application to the soil (Table 1).15,44,45

Organic modification method for producing innovative biochar materials is relatively complex and diverse. The main reagents applied in organic modification methods are mercaptan, alcohol amine, thiourea, chitosan, etc.24,34,45,53,54,55,56,57 In terms of the thiol-modified biochar material, a simple and environment-friendly method for preparing this kind of material by ball milling is as follows: 3 g of biochar samples is mixed with 120 mL solution of water, ethanol, and 3-mercaptopropyltrimethoxysilane (3-MPTS, added into the agate jars dropwise). 300 g agate balls are added to the mixture, and then, the agate jars are placed in a ball mill machine and operated at 300 rpm for 12 h, with rotation direction altered every 6 h. After the ball milling, the resultant product is washed with ethanol and DI water for 3 times, freeze-dried for 48 h, and labeled as thiol-modified biochar (Table 1).55,56

LCA results of modified biochar materials

The LCA results indicated that the characteristic AP values for the organically modified and nano-modified biochars were 7.86E−02 kg SO2 eq and 4.59E−02 kg SO2 eq, respectively, which are much higher than those of the other two modified biochar materials (Figure S2). The EP values of organically modified and nano-modified biochars were 7.27E−03 kg PO43− eq and 4.15E−03 kg PO43− eq, respectively, while the characteristic value of the magnetically modified biochar was 5.02E−05 kg PO43− eq (Figure S2). The FAETP value for magnetic modification was the lowest, at 4.27E−04 kg 1,4-DCB eq. Among the four modified biochar materials, the organically modified and nano-modified biochar types had higher characteristic values of GWP, at 2.77E+01 kg CO2 eq and 1.61E+01 kg CO2 eq, respectively, while magnetically modified biochar had the lowest GWP value, at 1.91E−01 kg CO2 eq. The organically modified biochar had the highest characteristic values of HTP and TETP, which were 1.25E+00 kg 1,4-DCB eq and 1.92E−02 kg 1,4-DCB eq, respectively. In addition, the normalized LCA results of both radar diagram and stack diagram indicated that the values for the above six environmental indicators of the organically modified biochar were much larger than those of the other three modified biochar materials, while those of the magnetically modified biochar were the smallest (Figure 2). Among them, GWP, HTP, and EP contributed to more proportions of environmental impact LCA scores, while TETP index has the lowest LCA score (Figure 2B).

LCC analysis results of modified biochar materials

The total costs of each functional unit for producing acid/base-modified, magnetically modified, nano-modified, and organically modified biochars were 2.57 USD, 7.22 USD, 7.82 USD, and 7.28 USD, respectively (Figure 3). Among them, the highest production cost was for nano-modified biochar, followed by organically modified biochar and magnetically modified biochar, while acid/base-modified biochar had the lowest production cost. The modification cost for nano-modified biochar production was the highest, accounting for 87% of the total cost, which directly increased the overall cost. The highest biomass pretreatment cost was for the acid/base-modified biochar. Generally, the total cost of producing modified biochars was in the order of nano-modified biochar > organically modified biochar > magnetically modified biochar > acid/base-modified biochar. The cost ranking presented herein (e.g., nano-modified biochar being less favorable) reflects only production-stage economics and should not be directly interpreted as a conclusion about remediation cost effectiveness. Future research that integrates remediation efficiency indicators into a full cradle-to-grave assessment is encouraged.

Comparatively, the positive relationship between pretreatment cost and physical properties indicated that the pretreatment process was more beneficial for improving the physical properties of biochar materials, such as pore structure and specific surface area (Figure 4).

Chord diagram results of modified biochar materials

The chord diagram clearly presents the relationships among the environmental factors, economic factors, and indexes representing effects on the physicochemical technical characteristics of the four modified biochar materials (Figure 5). The improvement of physical properties of biochar materials was more correlated with acid/base-modified and nano-modified biochar types (Figure 5). For instance, the increase in specific surface area was the largest in the acid/base-modified biochar, while the increase in pore volume was the largest in the nano-modified biochar (Table 3). The improvement of chemical functional groups of biochar materials was more strongly correlated with the magnetically modified and organically modified biochar types (Figure 5). The increase in carbonyl functional groups was the largest in the magnetically modified biochar, and the increase in carboxyl functional groups was largest in the organically modified biochar, as supported by the data in Table 3. The overall increase in physicochemical technical characteristics acting on complex pollutants was in the following order: magnetically modified biochar > nano-modified biochar > acid/base-modified biochar > organically modified biochar.

Economic cost indexes: total cost, electric power cost, hydraulic cost, pretreatment cost, pyrolysis cost, and modification cost. Physicochemical technical indexes: Carbonyl, carbonyl functional group; Carboxyl, carbonyl functional group; specific surface area, pore volume, and pore adsorption capacity.

Similar to the results from LCC analysis (Figure 3), the size of each investment was in the following order: total cost > modification cost > electric power cost > pyrolysis cost > pretreatment cost > hydraulic cost (Figure 5). Among the four modified biochar materials, the acid/base-modified biochar had the least total cost, mainly because the most expensive part—the modification cost—was the lowest in the economic cost of acid/base-modified biochar. The economic input for nano-modified biochar and organically modified biochar ranked first and second, respectively, in each part of the spending unit, much higher than those of the other two modified biochar materials (Figure 5). The environmental impacts of production processes of the four modified biochar materials were in the order of organically modified biochar > nano-modified biochar > acid/base-modified biochar > magnetically modified biochar.

Overall, the magnetic modification method for modifying biochar was mostly associated with physicochemical technical indexes, while nano-modified biochar required the most economic investment, especially for the modification cost and electric power cost. Moreover, it could add to the load of environmental pollutants, which was second only to that of organically modified biochar modification work. Organic modification processes produced large amounts of complex pollutants and had the greatest impacts on the environment (Figure 5).

Discussion

Selection of different modified biochar materials for different polluted soil types

Soil pollution can be divided into those from industrial sources, agricultural sources, and domestic sources according to the pollution source types.72 Modified biochar materials have been considered effective in remediating contaminated soils. However, it has not been known exactly what kind of modified biochar materials should be used for contaminated soils from different sources. In other words, what type of modified biochar material should be used for a specific pollution source soil? Therefore, it is necessary to study the exact mechanisms of biochar materials acting on polluted soils.

Acid/base-modified biochar could reduce the concentration of heavy metals and organic pollutants in soils because it has π-π electron groups, which help pristine biochar better fix or degrade complex contaminants or allow synergistic REDOX reactions to assist electron transport to dissolve or passivate contaminants. Moreover, it could adjust soil pH to enhance soil microbial activity, benefit rhizosphere environment, and promote plant growth.40 Furthermore, acid/base modification process hardly introduces harmful substances that could cause secondary pollution,73,74 which is environmentally friendly and ecologically harmonious. Therefore, acid/base-modified biochar is more suitable for remediating agricultural-source-polluted soils, which have strict environmental quality requirements and residents’ life and health needs.

Magnetically modified biochars could effectively degrade soil heavy metals and organic pollutants75,76 as they showed a large increase in specific surface area and carboxyl and hydroxyl functional groups. Additionally, they have the potential to be recycled77 because of their strong cation exchange abilities and reducibility78 (Figure 6; Table 3), potentially allowing for heavy metal adsorption and organic pollutant degradation. However, a risk of soil secondary pollution exists if the magnetically modified biochar material is not handled properly after soil remediation, because metal ions or their oxides (zero-valent iron, ferroferric oxide, etc.) are added in the material preparation stages. Considering its high efficiency for pollution remediation and multifunctional group effect, it is more likely to be designed to remediate industrial contaminated soil or heavily contaminated soil.

Possible application scenarios of biochar materials for remediation of compound contaminated soil

The contaminated soils were classified as agricultural source-, industrial source-, and domestic source-polluted soils according to the source of pollution. Photos of polluted soils were obtained from our team’s field research. The physical adsorption characteristics and chemical active functional group were regarded as technical evaluation indices for biochar materials’ remediation effects. Besides, LCA and LCC analysis were carried out for environmental-economic evaluation to better provide specific application of biochar materials for different contaminated soils from the comprehensive perspective of technology, environment, and economy.

Organically modified biochar had more organic functional groups (Table 3) that could react with organic pollutants via redox reactions. Additionally, it could form double coordinated compounds with heavy metals. Because soils polluted from domestic sources are rich in organic matter, organically modified biochar is quite suitable for remediating these types of polluted soils, which are enriched in organic and degradable components.

Nano-modified biochar had advantages of non-toxicity and environmental friendliness.79 However, pollutant deposition on the surfaces of material was improved due to increased adsorption sites from the modified biochars. Although this kind of materials has more active surface adsorption sites, it is generally not a good option for soil remediation because of its high price.80 On the other hand, the recycling and reuse potential of nano-modified biochar material is not high because they can agglomerate easily after being applied to the soil.21 The tradeoff between the remediation efficiency of a material and its cost should be considered during the application (Figure 6).

In general, magnetically modified biochar was used for soil polluted from industrial sources, acid/base-modified biochar was used for soil polluted from agricultural sources, and organically modified biochar was used for the remediation of soil polluted from domestic sources. It is worth mentioning that more research should be focused on the applications of magnetically modified biochar because it can be easily separated and recycled and shows potential for broad applications in different soil environments.

Decision-making for complex contaminated soil remediation plans based on technological, environmental, and economic considerations

The improvements in the above modified biochars based on physical and chemical indicators were in the order of magnetically modified biochar > nano-modified biochar > acid/based-modified biochar > organically modified biochar (Table 3). A chord diagram based on a correlation analysis of standardized data also indicated that the magnetically modified biochar was most closely associated with the technological indexes (Figure 5). In summary, the technical evaluation confirms that magnetically modified biochar possesses enhanced physicochemical properties, such as a higher specific surface area, increased adsorption pore volume, and a greater abundance of organic functional groups. These advantages make it the preferred option for soil remediation.

Furthermore, we systematically evaluated the environmental effects and economic costs of different biochar materials using LCA and LCC analysis. The LCA results revealed that all four modified biochars imposed certain environmental impacts, primarily generated during the stages of pretreatment, pyrolysis, modification, and material application. Notably, the pretreatment stage consumed substantial electricity and water for biomass disposal, which also contributed significantly to the overall economic costs (Figure 3). Moreover, the consumption of these resources indirectly exacerbated environmental impacts, as the use of electricity and other inputs generates additional pollutant loads.81,82 Concurrently, pyrolysis directly produced CO2, SO2, and NOx, exacerbating atmospheric pollution and the greenhouse effect.53,83,84 These pollutants caused acid deposition through oxidation and hydrolysis, which, in turn, contaminated soils via surface runoff, posing a further risk to the soil environment.85

The primary contributor to the overall environmental hazards was the modification process (Figures 2 and S2). The increase in FAETP and HTP indicators from the LCA can be attributed to the potential accumulation of harmful substances from metallic and reducing agents used in modification, which poses risks to soil and aquatic ecosystems.86,87 It should be noted that external modifiers such as metals and organics may further decompose into toxic substances—including dioxins and metallic oxides—during reaction. These can be emitted into the atmosphere, transferred to soil and water via precipitation, and then freely migrate and transform in the environment, eventually entering humans through the food chain and threatening health.86 The described processes raised multiple toxicity indicators, including HTP, FAETP, and TETP (Figures 2 and S2). In comparison with other modification types, organic modification resulted in the highest environmental pollutant load, which can be explained by the addition of organic substances leading to substantial greenhouse gas emissions and potential toxic by-products if modifiers do not fully react (Figure 5).

The environmental performance of acid/base- and nano-modified and organically modified biochar revealed a parallel trend in their impact assessment. In this common profile, the TETP consistently formed the lowest point across the spectrum of indicators, even though the absolute magnitudes varied significantly. However, this common trend was broken by the magnetically modified biochar, which displayed a uniquely high TETP value that drastically altered its environmental indicator pattern. We posit that the root cause lies in its modification process; the addition of iron/ferric ions introduces and concentrates metals, while co-used reducing agents like sodium borohydride can persist as residues. The typically microscale or nanoscale size of the magnetic biochar further enhances the environmental mobility and bioavailability of these substances, collectively leading to the elevated terrestrial ecotoxicity. The elevated TETP in magnetically modified biochar is, therefore, explained by a combination of its metallic composition, chemical residues, and its dynamic behavior in the environment, including particle agglomeration. These factors collectively pose a greater potential risk to terrestrial ecosystems compared with other modification methods.

Among the economic factors, modification cost was the primary contributor to the total production cost of modified biochars, with the exception of acid/base-modified biochars (Figure 5). In the latter case, the modification cost showed the strongest correlation with total cost (Figures 4 and 5). The significant correlation between HTP and pyrolysis cost can be attributed to the release of harmful substances (e.g., SO2 and NOX) during pyrolysis, which eventually enter the human body through biogeochemical cycles (Figure 4). It should be noted, however, that the emission levels are lower than those from conventional combustion processes.88

Organically modified biochar exhibited the highest environmental pollutant load (Figure 2), while also incurring relatively high costs compared with other modified biochar materials (Figure 3; Table 2). Furthermore, correlation coefficients—represented by the thickness of the connecting lines in Figure 5 (e.g., R2)—between either environmental or economic indicators and organic modified biochar were substantially stronger than those observed for other modification methods. This phenomenon can be attributed to the introduction of organic functional groups such as carbonyl and carboxyl groups (Table 3), which raised both environmental load and economic cost, particularly during the production stage.

Although these functional groups aid in the degradation of organic pollutants, excess organic compounds may volatilize into the atmosphere, deposit into soils via aerial transport, or settle onto surrounding soil surfaces during the production of organically modified biochar. Once air, water, or soil becomes contaminated with these organic substances, a majority of environmental indicators are likely to be significantly impacted. Moreover, the total cost of the organic modification process is further driven by the procurement and incorporation of additional organic reagents.

In comparison, the pyrolysis cost constituted the largest share for acid/base-modified biochar, while the modification process accounted for the highest cost—91% of the total—in magnetically modified biochar. This suggests that the use of expensive modifiers is a major cost driver, highlighting the need to develop more economical alternatives to reduce overall expenses and expand application potential. As for nano-modified and organically modified biochars, the distribution of cost items showed little variation, yet both types exhibited higher total costs than acid/base-modified and magnetically modified biochars (Figure 3).

Therefore, there is a clear need to enhance technological efficiency while reducing modification costs to improve economic viability. Based on a comprehensive evaluation of physicochemical properties, economic cost, and environmental impact, magnetically modified biochar emerges as the most promising option. It requires moderate economic investment and exerts a far lower environmental impact than other modified biochars. Furthermore, it exhibits significantly enhanced physicochemical properties pertinent to soil remediation, coupled with dual capability for physical adsorption and chemical reduction.

Limitations of the study

First, the LCA and LCC analyses were defined with a cradle-to-gate system boundary, focusing exclusively on the production stage (feedstock pretreatment, pyrolysis, and modification) of modified biochars. The subsequent application phase, including pollutant removal efficiency, material aging, potential secondary release during field use, and end-of-life disposal or recycling, was not included. This may underestimate the full environmental and economic burdens or benefits associated with each modification route in real remediation scenarios. Second, the data used for LCA and LCC analysis were derived from published literature spanning 2017–2023, which inevitably involves variations in experimental conditions, feedstock types, and modification protocols across studies. Such heterogeneity might have introduced uncertainties into the comparative rankings, despite our efforts to standardize the functional unit and calculation procedures. Third, the physicochemical assessment focused only on pore volume, specific surface area, and carbonyl/carboxyl groups, while other potentially relevant properties (e.g., surface charge and long-term stability) and quantitative evaluation of secondary pollution risks were not addressed. Future studies are encouraged to adopt a full cradle-to-grave LCA framework, incorporate site-specific remediation efficiency data, and conduct long-term field trials to validate the feasibility and safety of these modified biochars under diverse real-world conditions.

Method details

Research strategy and literature review

A systematic literature review was conducted to identify studies on modified biochar materials for complex contaminated soil remediation. The literature search was performed in Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus, covering publications from 2017 to 2023. The following keywords were used: “acid/base modified” (or “acid base modified”, “acid modified”, “acidic modified”, “base modified”, “alkaline”), “magnetic modified”, “nano-modified” (or “nanomodified”, “nano modified”), “organic modified” (or “thiourea modified”, “chitosan modified”, “thiol modified”, “mercaptan”), combined with “biochar”, “contaminated soil” (or “polluted soil”, “soil pollution”, “heavy metal contaminated soil”, “organic pollutant”, “composite contaminated soil”), and “soil remediation” (or “soil restoration”, “soil pollution control”). Patents and standards were excluded from the search.

After screening, a total of 226 studies closely related to the research objectives were identified, covering 7 cases of acid/base-modified biochar, 51 cases of magnetically modified biochar, 41 cases of nano-modified biochar, and 158 cases of organically modified biochar (some papers covered multiple modified biochar types). Soil survey sites included Daxing District of Beijing (39.75°, 116.38°), Jiaojiang District (28.53°, 121.37°) and Wenling City (28.47°, 121.36°) of Taizhou City, Zhejiang Province, Qingyuan City (23.55°, 113.00°), Guangdong Province, etc.

Life cycle assessment (LCA)

LCA was conducted using the CML2001 method in GaBi software 2020 (Sphera Solutions GmbH). The system boundary was defined as cradle-to-gate, covering the production stage of modified biochars including feedstock pretreatment, pyrolysis, and modification processes. The ecological assessment of materials under practical application scenarios, as well as the disposal or end-of-life stage after material use, was not considered in this study, as the work focused primarily on comparative evaluation of the production stage of different modified biochars.

The environmental impact categories assessed included acidification potential (AP), eutrophication potential (EP), global warming potential (GWP), human toxicity potential (HTP), freshwater aquatic ecotoxicity potential (FAETP), and terrestrial ecotoxicity potential (TETP). The equivalent factor method was employed to quantify environmental impact indicators, using reference substances (e.g., SO2 for AP) as benchmarks.

Data for each modified biochar type were collected from the identified published studies: 7 relevant published studies (e.g., Xie et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022).27,61 For magnetically modified biochar, 51 papers were used (e.g., Li et al., 2023; Chandra et al., 2023)62,63; for nano-modified biochar, 41 papers (e.g., Chen et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023)64,65; and for organically modified biochar, 158 papers (e.g., Li et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2023).66,67 These studies provided preparation parameters, reagent dosage, and energy consumption data required for LCA and LCC analysis. Among these, multiple papers involved more than one type of modified biochar.

Life cycle cost (LCC) analysis

LCC analysis was used to quantify the economic impacts of different biochar modification methods within a life cycle framework, with total product cost determined for the treatment of 1 kg of biomass. A service life of 1 year was assumed, and equipment depreciation, regular maintenance, and equipment updating costs were considered negligible after conversion to the functional unit. Because no transition cost was involved in this technological research, the economic dimension was represented by the operating cost index.

Physicochemical characteristics analysis

Changes in physicochemical characteristics were evaluated using pore volume and specific surface area as physical indicators, and carbonyl and carboxyl functional groups (−OH and −COOH) as chemical indicators. These parameters were recorded when they increased in modified biochar relative to pristine biochar.

The changes were calculated as follows:

Average rate of increase in the pore volume = the increase in pore volume in all modified biochar/number of modified biochar cases based on statistics.

Growth ratio of specific surface area in the modified biochar = the number of growth rates in specific surface area exceeding the average growth rate/total amount of modified biochar cases.

Increase in the proportion of carbonyl or carboxyl functional groups (%) = the amount of biochar with increased carbonyl functional groups/the total amount of modified biochars.

Finally, the physicochemical functional groups were calculated by the normalization method, and the rates of increase in pore volume and specific surface area of the modified biochar and the rates of increase in the carbonyl and carboxyl groups of the modified biochar were normalized.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical software and tools

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics. Data visualization and additional analyses were conducted using Origin 2021 and Microsoft Excel 2021. LCA calculations were performed using GaBi 2020.

Pearson correlation analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships among environmental impact factors (AP, EP, FAETP, GWP, HTP, TETP), economic cost indicators (pretreatment cost, pyrolysis cost, modification cost, total cost), and standardized physicochemical characteristics (pore volume, specific surface area, carbonyl groups, carboxyl groups). Correlation coefficients (R) and p-values were calculated, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Data normalization

Standardized scores for physicochemical characteristics were calculated using the normalization method described in the method details section. All normalized values were used for subsequent Pearson correlation analysis and chord diagram generation.

Data visualization

Chord diagrams were generated using Origin 2021 to visualize the relationships among environmental factors, economic factors, and physicochemical technical indexes of the four modified biochar materials. Radar diagrams and stack diagrams for LCA results were also created using Origin 2021. All analyses, including Pearson correlation analysis, data normalization, basic plotting, and chord diagram generation, were performed exclusively by following the standard functions of the software used (SPSS, Excel, Origin, and GaBi).
