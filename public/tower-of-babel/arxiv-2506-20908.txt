Smoothness Meets Autobidding: Tight Price of Anarchy Bounds for Simultaneous First-Price Auctions
Riccardo Colini-Baldeschi, Sophie Klumper, Twan Kroll, Stefano Leonardi, Guido Schäfer, Artem Tsikiridis

Abstract
Online advertising systems have recently transitioned to autobidding, enabling advertisers to delegate bidding decisions to automated agents. Each advertiser directs their agent to optimize a valuation-dependent objective function subject to return-on-investment (ROI) or budget constraints . Given their relevance, there has been a surge in literature studying the liquid welfare price of anarchy (POA) of fundamental auction formats in autobidding, among which simultaneous first-price auctions (FPA) . These models capture a large range of heterogeneous agent behaviors, requiring advanced proofs to derive tight POA bounds. Recently, Deng et al. (NeurIPS 2024) showed that the POA of FPA for mixed autobidders (i.e., value and utility maximizers) under ROI is 2.18 for additive valuations. We extend the smoothness framework of Syrgkanis and Tardos (STOC 2013) to autobidding. A key contribution in our Extension Theorem is a new technique to balance smoothness parameters across heterogeneous agent types. Determining the best POA bound then reduces to solving a POA-revealing mathematical program. Our approach has three main strengths: (1) Simplicity: We prove smoothness for single-item FPA only. The results for simultaneous FPA follow via our Extension Theorem. For example, by showing smoothness for value and utility maximizers, we obtain the tight POA of 2.18 2.18 for mixed autobidding. (2) Extendibility: Our Extension Theorem adapts to simultaneous FPA with reserve prices and agents with fractionally subadditive valuations and heterogeneous payment sensitivities and target ROI parameters. We establish the first (mostly) tight POA bounds for several models beyond the current autobidding state of the art. (3) Generality: Our Extension Theorem bounds the POA of coarse correlated equilibria (CCE) , which are especially relevant as they naturally arise when agents employ regret-minimizing algorithms. Building on Kolumbus and Nisan (WWW 2022), we show theoretically that CCE arising from hybrid regret-minimizing agents exhibit properties that keep their POA low.



1
Introduction

Over the past few decades, the online advertising landscape has undergone a significant transformation with the emergence of autobidding. This paradigm shift enables advertisers to delegate complex bidding strategies to automated agents that consider various factors such as ad performance, campaign constraints, and market dynamics. As a result, advertisers can efficiently manage their campaigns and maximize their return on investment.
Autobidding has become increasingly important in the online advertising ecosystem, with a growing number of platforms offering this functionality and the vast majority of advertisers adopting it. According to recent estimates, more than 80% of online advertising traffic is now managed through autobidding agents (
Deng et al. 2024
). The widespread adoption of autobidding has far-reaching implications for the online advertising industry, influencing the behavior of advertisers, publishers, and ad exchanges alike.

In the autobidding world, agents are primarily concerned with reporting high-level constraints for their campaigns, including budget and return-on-investment (ROI) constraints. The budget constraint specifies the maximum payment an agent is willing to make for a given campaign, while the ROI constraint determines the maximum amount an agent is willing to pay for a conversion or impression. This constraint is distinct from the traditional notion of an agents’s valuation, which represents the true value they place on an item. In contrast, the ROI constraint reflects the agent’s budgetary limitations and their desire to achieve a positive return on their investment.

Beyond constraints, agents may also be of different types with respect to the objective they want to maximizes. The two most prominent types that have been considered in the autobidding literature are value maximizer and utility maximizers. Value maximizers focus on maximizing the value of the outcome (e.g., high-value conversions), while controlling costs through budget and ROI constraints.
On the other hand, utility maximizers want to maximize the difference between the value obtained and price paid. These objectives sit at the extremes of a spectrum that defines the agents’ trade-off between value and payment. Therefore, we also allow agents to express their objectives continuously along this spectrum, enabling them to choose an objective that sits anywhere between value-maximization and utility-maximization.

In addition to the shift in bidding strategies, the online advertising industry has also seen a significant transition in auction formats in recent years, particularly with the move from second-price to first-price auctions (
FPA
). This shift has been especially pronounced in display ad markets, culminating in the adoption of first-price auctions by Google Ad Exchange in September 2019 (
Paes Leme et al. 2020
). The combination of first-price auctions and the increasing reliance on autobidding raises essential questions about the efficiency and performance of
FPA
within this new autobidding framework.

To analyze the efficiency of
FPA
in the autobidding world, we consider the Price of Anarchy (
POA
) initially introduced by
Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou 1999
. Given a valuation and equilibrium class, the
POA
measures the ratio of the optimal liquid welfare against the liquid welfare achieved in the worst equilibrium. This metric provides a quantitative assessment of the efficiency loss due to selfish behavior in auctions.
The notion of liquid welfare was first introduced by
Dobzinski and Leme 2014
. In the context of mechanism design, liquid welfare serves as a widely accepted efficiency metric that extends social welfare to scenarios where players are subject to budget constraints. Specifically, a player’s liquid welfare is defined as the minimum value between the utility they derive from an outcome and their available budget. This metric provides a more nuanced understanding of a player’s ability to achieve high values, taking into account their limited financial resources.
Encoding the notion of budget in its definition, the optimal liquid welfare represents a meaningful benchmark for measuring the social value of an auction allocation. It acknowledges that players with limited budgets cannot be expected to pay for outcomes that yield extremely high values. By considering the interplay between a player’s budget and the value they receive, liquid welfare offers a more realistic assessment of the social welfare achieved by a mechanism.

The
POA
of
FPA
in the autobidding world was recently studied by
Deng et al. 2024
, both for the model with only value maximizers and the
mixed model
comprising both value and utility maximizers. Surprisingly, they established a tight bound of 2.18 on the
POA
for mixed Nash equilibria (MNE) in the mixed-agent model. Their proof employs advanced techniques and leverages deep structural insights into mixed Nash equilibria to achieve this result.

A powerful general technique for bounding the
POA
of games is the
smoothness technique
. Originally introduced by
Roughgarden 2015
for strategic games and later extended by
Syrgkanis and Tardos 2013
to composable auctions and mechanism design, its strength lies in its
Extension Theorems
. In the context of composable mechanisms
Syrgkanis and Tardos 2013
, these theorems allow simple
POA
proofs for pure Nash equilibria in a ‘base mechanism’ (e.g., a single-item first-price auction) to extend to more complex compositions (e.g., simultaneous or sequential auctions) and broader equilibrium concepts (e.g., mixed and correlated equilibria).
The combination of being easily applicable and providing strong general bounds makes it particularly appealing. As a result, the smoothness technique has been successfully applied in various settings. However, its application to the autobidding world remains elusive.

1.1
Our Contributions

We derive a smoothness framework to extend the study of the price of anarchy of simultaneous first-price auctions in the autobidding world in various ways:

1.

We consider the general
hybrid agent model
in which agents have different
types
depending on their payment sensitivity and ROI constraint. This includes the previously studied mixed agent model as a special case, in which all agents are either value or utility maximizers.

2.

We significantly broaden the class of valuation functions considered in autobidding by studying fractionally subadditive (or equivalently,
XOS
) valuations (see
Syrgkanis and Tardos 2013
). In particular, monotone submodular functions form an important special case within this class. To the best of our knowledge, all previous work on autobidding has solely focused on additive valuation functions.

3.

We extend our POA bounds to more general equilibrium concepts, including correlated (CE) and coarse correlated equilibria (CCE). The latter is particularly relevant in the context of autobidding, as it naturally emerges when agents use regret-minimizing algorithms.

4.

We extend our results to simultaneous first-price auctions with reserve prices. This is particularly relevant when the seller can leverage machine-learned advice to approximate buyers’ valuations (see also the framework by
Balseiro et al. 2021b
). Using these value signals as reserve prices leads to an improved bound on the POA.

Our Smoothness Framework.

We derive our results for the aforementioned extensions through a unifying framework, adapting the smoothness framework of
Syrgkanis and Tardos 2013
for utility maximizers to our setting in a non-trivial way. However, unlike the original framework, a key technical challenge in our case is the incorporation of different agent types.
To the best of our knowledge, prior works leveraging the smoothness technique assume agents are ‘alike’ in their strategic behavior.
Consequently, the
(\lambda,\mu)
-smoothness parameters established for the base mechanism can be directly lifted to the composed mechanism.
In contrast, our setting allows for different agent types, each with its own smoothness parameters
(\lambda_{t},\mu_{t})
that must be integrated.
A crucial element of our approach is introducing
calibration vectors
that enable us to balance the different smoothness parameters. An upper bound on the POA is then determined by a
POA
-revealing mathematical program
(\text{POA-RMP})
. While solving this program presents its own analytical challenges, we are able to find feasible (sometimes optimal) solutions and bound the objective value of the
POA-RMP
, thereby deriving bounds on the POA.

Once the above machinery is established, we can focus on proving the smoothness of a single-item first-price auction with reserve price for the different agent types.
Similar to the original smoothness approach, our Extension Theorem then allows us to derive (sometimes tight) bounds on the POA of coarse correlated equilibria for simultaneous single-item first-price auctions with reserve prices and fractionally subadditive valuation functions.

1.2
Related Work

Auction design for auto-bidding.

Motivated by the rise of autobidding in online advertising, recent research has focused on auction design tailored for value-maximizing agents in an autobidding environment.
Aggarwal et al. 2019
analyze optimal bidding strategies in truthful auctions, establish the existence of equilibrium, and derive welfare price of anarchy (PoA) bounds.
Deng et al. 2021
and
Balseiro et al. 2021c
explore how mechanisms with budget boosts and reserve prices can enhance welfare efficiency guarantees when leveraging machine-learned predictions of buyers’ values.
Balseiro et al. 2021b
;
Balseiro et al. 2022
characterize revenue-optimal auctions under various informational settings, while
Aggarwal et al. 2025b
focus on finding optimal bidding strategies in a multi-platform auction setting under budget and ROI constraints. Further,
Mehta 2022
and
Liaw et al. 2023
investigate how randomized and non-truthful mechanisms can improve welfare efficiency, particularly in auctions with two agents. Notably,
Liaw et al. 2023
show that when restricting to equilibria with deterministic bids, the PoA of the first-price auction is 2. Expanding on this,
Deng et al. 2024
analyze more general equilibria with randomized bidding strategies, demonstrating that the PoA remains 2 for value-maximizing agents but declines to approximately
2.18
when considering both value-maximizing and utility-maximizing agents. Our work extends these results to broader auction formats and coarse correlated equilibria (
CCE
). For further details, we refer the interested reader to the survey of
Aggarwal et al. 2024a
.

Price of Anarchy of First-Price Auction.

Under the standard model of quasilinear utilities, the inefficiency of the first-price auction has been considered in the economics literature since the seminal work of
Vickrey 1961
. Naturally, due to its simplicity, it has also been well-studied for simultaneous simple auctions. The
POA
of
CCE
was shown by
Syrgkanis and Tardos 2013
to be at most
\frac{e}{e-1}
for simultaneous auctions with XOS valuations. This bound was shown to be tight even for a single auction by
Syrgkanis 2014
and for
MNE
in simultaneous auctions with submodular valuations by
Christodoulou et al. 2016
. Beyond the smoothness framework,
Feldman et al. 2020
showed a
POA
upper bound of
2
for simultaneous auctions with subadditive valuations, while
Feldman et al. 2016
have considered
CE
and
CCE
and their properties. More recently,
Jin and Lu 2023
showed, that the
Bayesian

POA
for the single-item first-price auction is exactly
\frac{e^{2}}{e^{2}-1}
, a breakthrough result.

CCE and regret minimization.

Kolumbus and Nisan 2022a
investigate a setting where autobidding agents employ regret-minimizing algorithms in repeated auctions for utility-maximizing agents. Their findings show that in first-price auction dynamics, when agents use mean-based regret-minimizing algorithms
Braverman et al. 2018
, convergence to co-undominated equilibria—a refinement of CCE. Our work extends these insights to settings involving both value-maximizing and utility-maximizing agents. Finally, regret minimization has also been considered in the context of autobidding by other works (see e.g.,
Aggarwal et al. 2025a
;
Lucier et al. 2024
;
Balseiro and Gur 2019
).

Preliminaries

We consider the setting in which
n\geq 2
agents simultaneously participate in
m\geq 1
single-item auctions.
We use
N=[n]
and
M=[m]
to denote the set agents and auctions, respectively, and
\mathcal{M}_{j}
to denote the mechanism that is used to sell the item of auction
j\in M
.
Each agent
i\in N
submits to each auction
j\in M
a non-negative bid
b_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
The corresponding bid profile of agent
i
is denoted by
\bm{b}_{i}=(b_{ij})_{j\in M}
.
Let
D_{i}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
be the set of all bid profiles of agent
i
.
The aggregated bid profile of all agents is denoted by
\bm{b}=(\bm{b}_{i})_{i\in N}\in D
, where
D=\times_{i\in N}D_{i}
.

First-Price Auctions with Reserve Prices.

We solely focus on first-price auctions with reserve prices.
So, given reserve prices
\bm{r}=(r_{j})_{j\in M}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
, each mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{j}
implements a first-price auction with reserve price
r_{j}
, denoted by
\textit{FPA}(r_{j})
: Given the bid profile
\bm{b}_{j}=(b_{ij})_{i\in N}
submitted to auction
j
,
\textit{FPA}(r_{j})
only considers the bids that are at least as large as the reserve price
r_{j}
, i.e.,
b_{ij}\geq r_{j}
.
Among these bids, and using a fixed tie-breaking rule,
\mathcal{M}_{j}
allocates the item to the highest bidder, also referred to as the
actual winner
and denoted by
\textsf{aw}(j)\in\arg\max_{i\in N:b_{ij}\geq r_{j}}b_{ij}
.
The price of the actual winner is equal to their bid
b_{\textsf{aw}(j)j}
.
Note that the item can remain unassigned if
b_{ij}<r_{j}
for all
i\in N
.

Let
\bm{x}_{j}(\bm{b})=(x_{ij}(\bm{b}))_{i\in N}
and
\bm{p}_{j}(\bm{b})=(p_{ij}(\bm{b}))_{i\in N}
refer to the respective allocation and payments of
\textit{FPA}(r_{j})
, i.e., for
i=\textsf{aw}(j)
we have that
x_{ij}(\bm{b})=1
and
p_{ij}(\bm{b})=b_{ij}
, and for
i\neq\textsf{aw}(j)
we have that
x_{ij}(\bm{b})=0
and
p_{ij}(\bm{b})=0
.
Note that both
\bm{x}_{j}
and
\bm{p}_{j}
only depend on the input profile
\bm{b}_{j}
; we sometime only use
\bm{b}_{j}
as the argument for notational convenience.

Our global mechanism implements the above mechanisms with reserve prices
\bm{r}=(r_{j})_{j\in M}
simultaneously.
That is, given a bid profile
\bm{b}
, the outcome
\mathcal{M}(\bm{r},\bm{b})=(\bm{x}(\bm{b}),\bm{p}(\bm{b}))
is determined by the allocation
\bm{x}(\bm{b})=(\bm{x}_{j}(\bm{b}))_{j\in M}
and the payments
\bm{p}(\bm{b})=(\bm{p}_{j}(\bm{b}))_{j\in M}
obtained by running
m
first-price auctions with reserve prices
\bm{r}
simultaneously.
We define
p_{i}(\bm{b})=\sum_{j\in M}p_{ij}(\bm{b})
as the total payment of an agent
i
over all
m
auctions and
P(\bm{b})=\sum_{i\in N}p_{i}(\bm{b})
as the sum of all payments.
Further, we define
\bm{x}_{i}(\bm{b})=(x_{ij}(\bm{b}))_{j\in M}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m}
to denote the allocation of an agent
i\in N
and
\bm{X}
to denote the set of feasible allocations, i.e.,
\bm{X}=\{\bm{x}(\bm{b})=(\bm{x}_{i}(\bm{b}))_{i\in N}\mid\sum_{i\in N}x_{ij}(\bm{b})\leq 1,\forall j\in M\}
.
We slightly overload notation and use
\bm{x}_{i}(\bm{b})
also to refer to the set of items assigned to an agent
i
, i.e.,
\bm{x}_{i}(\bm{b})=\{j\in M\,\mid\,x_{ij}(\bm{b})=1\}\subseteq M
. Additionally, we sometimes omit the argument
\bm{b}
when it is irrelevant or clear from context.

For ease of notation, we use
\textit{FPA}(m,\bm{r})
and
\textit{FPA}(m)
, respectively, to refer to
m
simultaneous first-price auctions with reserve prices
\bm{r}
and without reserve prices.
We use
\textit{FPA}(r)
to indicate that we consider a single-item first-price auction with reserve price
r
.
If
m=1
, we drop the auction index
j=1
from all our notation.

Valuation Functions.

Each agent
i\in N
has a valuation function
v_{i}:2^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
over the subsets of the auctioned items, where
v_{i}(S)
is the value that agent
i
obtains when receiving the items in
S\subseteq M
. We assume w.l.o.g. that
v_{i}(\emptyset)=0
. Also, we assume that
v_{i}
is
monotone
, i.e.,
v_{i}(S)\leq v_{i}(T)
for all
S\subseteq T\subseteq M
.
We use
\mathcal{V}_{i}
to denote the class of valuation functions of agent
i
and let
\mathcal{V}=\times_{i\in N}\mathcal{V}_{i}
be the set of all valuation functions of the agents.
We use
\bm{v}=(v_{i})_{i\in N}\in\mathcal{V}
to refer to the profile of valuation functions of the agents.
We consider different classes of valuation functions:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
v_{i}:2^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a valuation function.

•

v_{i}
is
additive
if there exist additive valuations
(v_{ij})_{j\in M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
such that for every subset
S\subseteq M
, it holds that
v_{i}(S)=\sum_{j\in S}v_{ij}
.

•

v_{i}
is
submodular
if
v_{i}(S\cup\{j\})-v_{i}(S)\geq v_{i}(T\cup\{j\})-v_{i}(T)
for all
S\subseteq T\subseteq M
.

•

v_{i}
is
fractionally subadditive
(or,
XOS
), if there exists a class
\mathcal{L}_{i}=\{(v^{\ell}_{ij})_{j\in M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}\}
of additive valuations such that for every subset
S\subseteq M
, it holds that
v_{i}(S)=\max_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}\sum_{j\in S}v^{\ell}_{ij}
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}}
,
\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{sub}}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{xos}}
refer to the set of additive, submodular and fractionally subadditive (XOS) valuation functions, respectively.

Randomized Bid Profiles.

Let
\pi
be a probability distribution over the set of bid profiles in
D
.
Throughout the paper, we use
\bm{B}\sim\pi
to denote a random bid profile that is drawn from
\pi
. For ease of notation, we often omit the explicit reference to
\pi
and identify
\bm{B}
with
\pi
.
Further, we define
\Delta_{i}
as the space of randomized bid profiles of an agent
i
and use
\bm{b}\in D
to refer to a deterministic (or pure) bid profile.
For a randomized bid profile
\bm{B}
, we use
f_{\bm{B}}
and
F_{\bm{B}}
to refer to the probability density function (PDF) and cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
\bm{B}
, respectively.
The support of
\bm{B}
, denoted by
\mathrm{supp}(\bm{B})
, refers to the set of bid profiles that have positive density under
\bm{B}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{supp}(\bm{B})=\{\bm{b}\in D\,\mid\,f_{\bm{B}}(\bm{b})>0\}
.
If
\mathrm{supp}(\bm{B})=\{\bm{b}\}
then
\bm{B}
chooses
\bm{b}
deterministically; we write
\bm{B}=\bm{b}
to indicate this.
We use
\mathrm{supp}_{i}(\bm{B})
to refer to the set of bid profiles
\bm{b}_{i}
of agent
i
that have positive density under
\bm{B}
.
The
marginal

\bm{B}_{-i}
of
\bm{B}
is defined by the following PDF:

\forall\bm{b}_{-i}\in D_{-i}:\qquad f_{\bm{B}_{-i}}(\bm{b}_{-i})=\int_{D_{i}}f_{\bm{B}}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})d\bm{b}_{i}.

Given a bid profile
\bm{b}^{\prime}_{i}
of agent
i
, we denote by
(\bm{b}^{\prime}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})
the random bid profile that we obtain from
\bm{B}
when agent
i
bids
\bm{b}^{\prime}_{i}
deterministically and the other agents bid according to the marginal
\bm{B}_{-i}
.

Hybrid Agent Model and Return-On-Investment.

Each agent
i\in N
is associated with a
gain function

g_{i}:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that
i
wants to maximize; for each bid profile
\bm{b}
,
g_{i}
is defined as:

g_{i}(\bm{b})=v_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}(\bm{b}))-\sigma_{i}\cdot p_{i}(\bm{b}).

(1)

Here,
\sigma_{i}\in[0,1]
is the
payment sensitivity
(or,
sensitivity
) parameter of agent
i
. Intuitively, a larger sensitivity
\sigma_{i}
indicates that agent
i
is more sensitive towards the payments (in the sense of being negatively affected). In particular, agent
i
is a value maximizer if
\sigma_{i}=0
, and a utility maximizer if
\sigma_{i}=1
. Our model thus allows us to capture a large spectrum of agents’ sensitivities, ranging from value maximizers to utility maximizers.
This general model is also termed the
hybrid agent model
(see, e.g.,
Balseiro et al. 2021a
,
Aggarwal et al. 2024b
).
Most models studied in the literature consider a mix of value and utility maximiers only, i.e.,
\sigma_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for all
i\in N
; we refer to this special case as the
mixed agent model
.

Each agent
i
has a
return-on-investment (ROI)
constraint that must be satisfied (see
Aggarwal et al. 2024b
).
Given a random bid profile
\bm{B}
, the ROI constraint of an agent
i
enforces that the expected total payment of
i
is at most a factor
\tau_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
of their expected valuation for the received items:

\mathbb{E}\left[p_{i}(\bm{B})\right]\leq\tau_{i}\cdot\mathbb{E}[v_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}(\bm{B}))].

(2)

Here,
\tau_{i}

.
We define
\mathcal{R}_{i}
as the set of random bid profiles
\bm{B}
that satisfy the ROI constraint (

.
Note that for a deterministic bid profile

, the ROI constraint (

p_{i}(\bm{b})\leq\tau_{i}v_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}(\bm{b}))
.

We assume w.l.o.g. that
\tau_{i}\sigma_{i}\leq 1
for all agents
i\in N
.
Basically, if an agent with sensitivity
\sigma_{i}>0
has a target parameter
\tau_{i}>1/\sigma_{i}
, then
i
prefers to withdraw whenever they are asked to pay more than
1/\sigma_{i}
their valuation.
In other words, there are no equilibria in which the expected payment of agent
i

1/\sigma_{i}
times their expected value, as agent
i
would have a negative expected gain and be better off by bidding 0 deterministically in each auction.
In essence, this has the same effect as capping the target parameter at
1/\sigma_{i}
. Note that this argument applies only to agents with sensitivity
\sigma_{i}>0
.
In particular, the target parameter
\tau_{i}
of each value maximizer
i
with
\sigma_{i}=0
remains unconstrained as
\tau_{i}\sigma_{i}\leq 1
is always satisfied.

Equilibrium Notions.

The objective of each agent
i

\bm{B}_{i}
that maximizes their gain, subject to their ROI constraint and the strategy
\bm{B}_{-i}
of the other agents. That is, each agent
i
solves the following optimization problem, which is often defined as the
agent’s optimization problem

\max_{\bm{B}_{i}}\ \mathbb{E}[g_{i}(\bm{B}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})]\quad\text{subject to \quad$(\bm{B}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})\in\mathcal{R}_{i}$.}

The resulting bid profile
\bm{B}
can be interpreted as an equilibrium, when an important refinement is made to the equilibrium notions as defined in Appendix
A
. That is, for each agent
i
only deviations that satisfy the ROI constraint are considered.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\bm{B}
be a random bid profile satisfying the ROI constraint of each agent, i.e.,

\bm{B}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
for all

.

•

\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(\bm{B})]\geq\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(\bm{B}^{\prime}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})]\qquad\forall(\bm{B}^{\prime}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})\in{\mathcal{R}_{i}}.

(3)

•

\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(\bm{B})]\geq\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(h(\bm{B}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i})]\qquad\forall(h(\bm{B}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i})\in{\mathcal{R}_{i}}.

(4)

•

\bm{B}=\prod_{i\in[n]}\bm{B}_{i}
and for every agent

\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(\bm{B})]\geq\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(\bm{B}^{\prime}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})]\qquad\forall(\bm{B}^{\prime}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})\in{\mathcal{R}_{i}}.

(5)

Note that for a correlated equilibrium

Liquid Price of Anarchy.

We use
liquid welfare
as the social welfare objective (see, e.g.,
Aggarwal et al. 2024b
), which is also the standard benchmark in the autobidding literature.
Intuitively, the liquid welfare measures the maximum amount of payments one can extract from the agents.
Given an allocation
\bm{x}
, the liquid welfare of agent
i
with
\sigma_{i}>0
is
\min(v_{i}(\bm{x}_{i})/\sigma_{i},\tau_{i}v_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}))
, where the first term reflects that the gain
g_{i}
of agent
i
always remains non-negative and the second term is due to the ROI constraint.
Note that because
\tau_{i}\sigma_{i}\leq 1
(as discussed above), the liquid welfare of
i
is
\tau_{i}v_{i}(\bm{x}_{i})
.
The liquid welfare of agent
i
with
\sigma_{i}=0
is
\tau_{i}v_{i}(\bm{x}_{i})
, i.e., due to the ROI constraint only.
As a consequence, the liquid welfare of an agent evaluates to
\tau_{i}v_{i}(\bm{x}_{i})
, independently of their type.
So given a feasible allocation
\bm{x}\in\bm{X}
, the liquid welfare of
\bm{x}
is defined as:

\textit{LW}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i\in N}\tau_{i}v_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}).

We use
\bm{\tau}\bm{v}
to refer to the vector of
\tau_{i}
-adjusted agent valuations, i.e.,
\bm{\tau}\bm{v}=(\tau_{i}v_{i})_{i\in N}
.
An optimal allocation
\bm{x}^{*}=\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\tau}\bm{v})\in\bm{X}
maximizes the liquid welfare over all feasible allocations. We use
\textit{OPT}(\bm{\tau}\bm{v})=\textit{LW}(\bm{x}^{*})
to denote the maximum liquid welfare with respect to
\bm{\tau}\bm{v}
.

For additive valuation functions
\bm{v}=(v_{ij})_{i\in N,j\in M}
, we say that an agent
i\in N
is the
rightful winner
of auction
j\in M
if
i
is an agent with maximum
\tau_{i}
-adjusted valuation. We denote this agent by
\textsf{rw}(j)\in\arg\max_{i\in N}\tau_{i}v_{ij}
and in case of ties, we let
\textsf{rw}(j)
denote the winner of auction
j
in the considered optimal allocation.
Thus, in the additive case, the optimal liquid welfare is
\textit{OPT}(\bm{\tau}\bm{v})=\sum_{j\in M}\tau_{\textsf{rw}(j)}v_{\textsf{rw}(j)j}
.

The
price of anarchy
with respect to a given class of valuation functions and an equilibrium notion is defined as the worst-case ratio of the optimal liquid welfare over the expected liquid welfare of any equilibrium. More formally, given a class of valuation functions
\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{val}}
with
\textsc{val}\in\{\textsc{add},\textsc{sub},\textsc{xos}\}
, let
I=(N,M,\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau},\bm{v})\in\mathcal{I}_{\textsc{val}}
denote an instance with
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{val}}
.
Then, given an equilibrium notion
\textit{EQ}\in\{\textit{PNE},\textit{MNE},\textit{CE},\textit{CCE}\}
, we define the
price of anarchy
of
EQ
with respect to
\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{val}}
as:

\textit{EQ}\text{-}\textit{POA}(\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{val}})=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{I}_{\textsc{val}}}\sup_{\bm{B}\in\textit{EQ}(I)}\frac{\sum_{i\in N}\tau_{i}v_{i}(x^{*}_{i}(\bm{\tau}\bm{v}))}{\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i\in N}\tau_{i}v_{i}(x_{i}(\bm{B}))]}.

Reserve Prices.

In auctions with reserve prices, an item might not be sold simply because the reserve price is too
high.
As in previous works (
Balseiro et al. 2021a
,
Deng et al. 2024
), we therefore make the assumption that for additive valuations the reserve price
r_{j}
is at most the
\tau_{\textsf{rw}(j)}
-adjusted value of the rightful winner
\textsf{rw}(j)
for each auction
j\in M
, i.e.,
\tau_{\textsf{rw}(j)}v_{\textsf{rw}(j)j}
.
We introduce a parameter
\eta_{j}
that measures the relative gap between
r_{j}
and
\tau_{\textsf{rw}(j)}v_{\textsf{rw}(j)j}
. More formally, we define
\eta_{j}\in[0,1)
such that
r_{j}=\eta_{j}\tau_{\textsf{rw}(j)}v_{\textsf{rw}(j)j}
and define
\eta=\min_{j\in N}\eta_{j}
as the smallest relative gap.
Note that the price of anarchy could be unbounded if
\eta=1
, as it is not guarantee that the optimal liquid welfare is achieved in each equilibrium. Specifically, consider a single auction with
r=\tau_{\textsf{rw}}v_{\textsf{rw}}
. Suppose that agent
rw
is a utility maximizer and the only agent that can meet the reserve price. Then
rw
deterministically bidding 0 is a
MNE
with a liquid welfare of
0
, while the optimal liquid welfare is
\tau_{\textsf{rw}}v_{\textsf{rw}}>0
. This would lead to an unbounded
POA
.
We therefore only consider reserve prices
\bm{r}
that satisfy
\eta_{j}\in[0,1)
for each auction
j\in M
and call such call reserve prices
feasible
.
For fractionally subadditive valuations
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{xos}}
, we say that reserve prices are feasible if, for all agents
i\in N
, they are feasible for the
additive representatives

(v^{*}_{ij})_{j\in M}
with respect to their optimal allocation
\bm{x}^{*}_{i}
(see Section
3.2
for more details).
As observed by
Balseiro et al. 2021b
, a reserve price
r_{j}
might be interpreted as a prediction of the
\tau_{\textsf{rw}(j)}
-adjusted value of the rightful winner.
In this context,
\eta
is an indicator of the quality of the prediction, and as
\eta
increases so does the quality of the prediction.

Uniform Target Parameter.

All previous works studying the mixed agent model assume that the ROI constraint is imposed on the value maximizers only, while the utility maximizers remain unconstrained. As argued in
Deng et al. 2024
, this is equivalent to assuming a uniform ROI target parameter of
\tau_{i}=1
for all agents
i\in N
.
Without loss of generality, we can extend the assumption of a uniform target parameter to our hybrid agent model.
To see this, consider an instance
I=(N,M,\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau},\bm{v})
with arbitrary valuations, sensitivities and target parameters.
As mentioned earlier, we can assume w.l.o.g. that
\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}\leq 1
for every agent
i\in N
.
Now consider the instance
I^{\prime}=(N,M,\bm{\sigma}^{\prime},\bm{\tau}^{\prime},\bm{v}^{\prime})
that is just a scaled version of
I
with
\bm{v}^{\prime}=\bm{\tau}\bm{v}
,
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}=\bm{\tau}\bm{\sigma}
and
\bm{\tau}^{\prime}=\bm{1}
.
Note that
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\leq 1
for each agent
i\in N
.
For the instance
I^{\prime}
, the gain
g_{i}^{\prime}
of an agent
i
for a bid profile
\bm{b}
is
g^{\prime}_{i}(\bm{b})=v^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i}(\bm{b}))-\sigma^{\prime}_{i}p_{i}(\bm{b})=\tau_{i}v_{i}(x_{i}(\bm{b}))-\tau_{i}\sigma_{i}p_{i}(\bm{b})=\tau_{i}g_{i}(\bm{b})
, which is just the gain function for the instance
I
scaled by the target parameter of agent
i
.
And for the instance
I^{\prime}
, the ROI constraint of agent
i
is
p_{i}(\bm{b})\leq\tau_{i}^{\prime}v^{\prime}_{i}(x_{i}(\bm{b}))=\tau_{i}v_{i}(x_{i}(\bm{b}))
, which coincides with the ROI constraint for the instance
I
.
Therefore, when considering the instance
I^{\prime}
, agents still want to maximize
g_{i}(\bm{b})
and their bidding space remains unchanged compared to
I
. And so, the set of equilibria for the instances
I
and
I^{\prime}
coincide. Additionally, note that the liquid welfare also coincides, as for the instance
I^{\prime}
the liquid welfare of an agent
i
given a bid profile
\bm{b}
is
\tau_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}(\bm{b}))=\tau_{i}v_{i}(x_{i}(\bm{b}))
, which is equal to the liquid welfare of agent
i
for the instance
I
.

In the subsequent sections, we will therefore consider a uniform target parameter
\tau_{i}=1
for all agents
i\in N
.
In this case, agents will behave differently depending on their sensitivity parameter. Depending on the sensitivity parameter
\sigma_{i}
of an agent
i
, we define the
type
of agent
i
.
The set of different agent types is defined as
T=\{t\,\mid\,\text{$\exists i\in N$ with $\sigma_{i}=t$}\}
.
We use
N_{t}\subseteq N
to refer to the subset of agents having type
t
, i.e.,
N_{t}=\{i\in N\,\mid\,\sigma_{i}=t\}
.
Also, given a type
t\in T
, we use
\sigma_{t}
to refer to the sensitivity of type
t
for clarity.

The Lambert
W
Function.

In order to derive bounds on the POA analytically, we use the Lambert
W
function, which is the multi-valued inverse of
f(z)=ze^{z}
.
We exclusively use the primary real branch of the
W
function, also called the
principal branch
and commonly denoted by
W_{0}
, that we define below. We refer the interested reader to
Corless et al. 1996
.

Definition 2.3
.

The principal branch of the Lambert
W
function is the real function
W_{0}:[-\frac{1}{e},\infty)\rightarrow[-1,\infty)
such that
z=W_{0}(z)e^{W_{0}(z)}
.

Fact 2.1
.

For every
z>-\frac{1}{e}
, the derivative of
W_{0}(z)
is
\frac{W_{0}(z)}{z(1+W_{0}(z))}
.

Smoothness and the Extension Theorem

We introduce our smoothness framework for simultaneous first-price auctions with reserve prices under the hybrid agent model.
The advantage of the smoothness framework is that we can focus on proving smoothness for a single auction only.
Our Extension Theorem then enables us to derive bounds on the POA for simultaneous auctions with fractionally subadditive valuation functions.
In contrast to the original framework by
Syrgkanis and Tardos 2013
, the technical challenge is to incorporate different agent types.

We first need to introduce the notion of ROI-restricted bid profiles.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\bm{B}^{\prime}_{i}
be a random bid profile of agent
i
over
D_{i}
. We say that
\bm{B}^{\prime}_{i}
is
ROI-restricted
if for each
\bm{b}_{-i}\in D_{-i}
, it holds that:

\mathbb{E}[p_{i}(\bm{B}^{\prime}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})]\leq\mathbb{E}[v_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}(\bm{B}^{\prime}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i}))].

We can now introduce our smoothness notion.

Definition 3.2

(Smoothness)
.

Consider a first-price auction
\textit{FPA}(r)
with a feasible reserve price.
Let the rightful winner
i
be of type
t\in T
.
Then,
\textit{FPA}(r)
is
(\lambda_{t},\mu_{t})
-smooth for type
t
with
\lambda_{t},\mu_{t}>0
, if there exists a random bid
B^{\prime}_{i}=B^{\prime}_{i}(\bm{v})
such that
B^{\prime}_{i}
is ROI-restricted, and
for every bid profile
\bm{b}
such that
\bm{x}(\bm{b})\neq\bm{0}
it holds that:

\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}\left(B^{\prime}_{i},\bm{b}_{-{i}}\right)\right]\geq\lambda_{t}v_{i}-\mu_{t}p_{\textsf{aw}(\bm{b})}(\bm{b}).

(6)

We remark that, crucially, the random deviation
B^{\prime}_{i}
of the rightful winner
i
may depend on the valuations
\bm{v}
but
not
on the bid profile
\bm{b}
.
Note that (
6
) needs to hold only for bid profiles
\bm{b}
under which the item is sold; this condition is redundant if the reserve price is
r=0
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}\left(B^{\prime}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\theta_{i}}^{\gamma v_{i}}(v_{i}-\sigma p_{i}(z,\bm{b}_{-i}))f(z)dz=\int_{\theta_{i}}^{\gamma v_{i}}(v_{i}-\sigma z)f(z)dz

Proof

Lemma 4.3

Proof.

4.2
Improving Inefficiency Via Reserve Prices

In this section, we show that our smoothness framework allows us to capture how inefficiency improves in simultaneous first-price auctions with feasible reserve prices. To that end, and similarly to the existing literature (see, e.g.,
Balseiro et al. 2021a
), we present the POA upper bounds as functions of the parameter
\eta
.
Recall that
\eta
measures the minimum relative gap between the reserve price and the value of the rightful winner of an auction. Naturally, our results show that the bound on the POA improves as
\eta
increases.
We only focus on additive valuations in this section, as all correlated equilibria are well-supported in this case, which we later proof in Section
5
.
Additionally, for reasons of analytical tractability, we focus on two specific compositions of agent types.
First, we consider the mixed agent model. Recall that in this case agents are either value maximizers, i.e., agents of type
t
with
\sigma_{t}=0
, or utility maximizers, i.e., agents of type
t
with
\sigma_{t}=1
.
This generalizes a result of
Deng et al. 2024
(Theorem 3) to well-supported coarse correlated equilibria.
In the second setting, we consider the scenario of homogeneous agents, i.e., all agents share one common type
t
.

As there are only two agent types in the mixed agent model, we naturally define
\mu_{t}
for each agent type.

Definition 4.2
.

Corollary 4.2

\bar{\bm{\mu}}(\eta)
is a feasible solution of

.

.
We also define

with

as defined in (

The following lemma will be useful when proving our bound on the POA for the mixed agent model.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\zeta=2-\eta+W_{0}\left(-\frac{(1-\eta)^{2}}{e^{2-\eta}}\right)
. Let
T
be a set of different agent types containing value and utility maximizers, i.e.,

. Then, the following properties hold:

(i)

\min_{t\in T}\bar{\lambda}_{t}(\eta)\geq\frac{1-(1-\eta)e^{-\zeta}}{\zeta}.

The proof of Lemma

can be found in Appendix

We can now prove Theorem

Theorem 4.3
.

Consider the class of simultaneous first-price auctions with feasible reserve prices with additive valuations
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}}
in the mixed agent model, i.e.,
|T|=2
and for all

\sigma_{t}\in\{0,1\}
. Let:

. Furthermore,

with

Proof.

Proof of Theorem
4.3

Let
\zeta=2-\eta+W_{0}\left(-\frac{(1-\eta)^{2}}{e^{2-\eta}}\right)
and define
\bar{\bm{\mu}}=\bar{\bm{\mu}}(\eta)

and use

\displaystyle\min\left\{\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T\end{subarray}}\bar{\lambda}_{t},\left(\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T\end{subarray}}\frac{\bar{\mu}_{t}}{\bar{\lambda}_{t}}+\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T\end{subarray}}\frac{1-\sigma_{t}}{\bar{\lambda}_{t}}\right)^{-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\textit{POA}\textit{-}\textit{CCE}\left(\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}}\right)

\textit{CCE}\textit{-}\textit{POA}(\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}})\leq P_{\sigma}(\eta)

Proof

\min\left\{\lambda,\frac{\lambda}{\mu+1-\sigma}\right\}.

5
Well-Supported Equilibria and Lower Bounds

When deriving bounds on the POA for simultaneous first-price auctions with feasible reserve prices, we crucially only consider equilibria that are well-supported.
Recall that an equilibrium
\bm{B}
is well-supported if for each auction
j\in M
the item is always sold, i.e., for each auction
j\in M
it holds that
\mathbb{P}[x_{j}(\bm{B})\neq\bm{0}]=1
.
Even though we consider feasible reserve prices, not all equilibria are necessarily well-supported; this depends on the equilibrium notion and the type of valuation functions at hand.
In this section, we first consider additive valuations and show that, up to correlated equilibria, all equilibria are well-supported.
We then give three lower bounds on the POA for specific settings with additive valuations.
We conclude this section by showing that for submodular valuations, there exist equilibria that are not well-supported. In the homogeneous case of utility maximizers, this leads to an unbounded POA.

5.1
Additive Valuations

In the case of additive valuations, it intuitively does not make sense for the rightful winner
i
of an auction
j
to bid below the reserve price
r_{j}
. As we consider feasible reserve prices, agent
i
could potentially achieve some positive gain from winning auction
j
, unless some other agent wins the item at a price of
v_{ij}
.
We use this intuition in the proof below and show that, up to CE, all equilibria are well-supported.

Lemma 5.1
.

Consider the class of simultaneous first-price auctions with additive valuations
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}}
and feasible reserve prices.
Then, every
CE
is well-supported.

Proof.

Proof
For contradiction, suppose that
\bm{B}
is a CE that is not well-supported, i.e., there exists an auction
j\in M
such that

\mathbb{P}[x_{j}(\bm{B})\neq\bm{0}]<1.

(31)

Let agent
i\in N
be the rightful winner of auction
j
, i.e.,
i=\textsf{rw}(j)
.
We define the deviation of agent
i
as follows. For all auctions
k\neq j
, agent
i
follows the recommendation. However, for auction
j
, agent
i
follows the recommendation only if the recommendation is to bid above or equal to the reserve price
r_{j}
. Otherwise, agent
i
ignores the recommendation and bids exactly the reserve price
r_{j}
for auction
j
. More formally, for any
\bm{b}_{i}
in the support of
\bm{B}
, we define

h(\bm{b}_{i})=\bm{b}^{\prime}_{i}=(b_{i1},\dots,b_{i(j-1)},\max\{b_{ij},r_{j}\},b_{i(j+1)},\dots,b_{im}).

(32)

Note that by (
31
), there exist recommendation(s)
\bm{b}_{i}
in the support of
\bm{B}
that recommend agent
i
to bid below the reserve price
r_{j}
for auction
j
.

We first show that the deviation as defined in (
32
) satisfies the ROI constraint for agent
i
.
The conditional PDF of
\bm{B}_{-i}
given
\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}
is denoted as
f_{\bm{B}_{-i}\mid\bm{B}_{i}}(\bm{b}_{-i}\mid\bm{b}_{i})
.
Note that
\bm{B}\in{\mathcal{R}_{i}}
as
\bm{B}
is a correlated equilibrium, i.e., the ROI constraint is satisfied for agent
i
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\ k\in M}p_{ik}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})\,\middle|\,\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}\right]\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\ k\in M}v_{ik}(x_{ik}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i}))\,\middle|\,\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}\right]\right].

(33)

Recall that the outer expectation is for
\bm{B}_{i}
and the inner expectation is for
\bm{B}_{-i}
.
The ROI constraint for the deviation of agent
i
is:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\sum_{\ k\in M}\!p_{ik}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i})\,\middle|\,\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}\right]\right]\!\!\leq\!\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\sum_{\ k\in M}\!v_{ik}(x_{ik}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i}))\,\middle|\,\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}\right]\right].

(34)

Note that for auctions
k\neq j
, agent
i
always follows the recommendation, so the terms for these auctions
k
on the left- and right-hand side of equations (
33
) and (
34
) coincide.
Agent
i
also follows the recommendation for auction
j
if
b_{ij}\geq r_{j}
, so in this case the terms also coincide.
Finally, consider recommendations for auction
j
with
b_{ij}<r_{j}
. In this case the terms for auction
j
on the left- and right-hand side of equation (
33
) are equal to 0, as agent
i
never wins auction
j
when bidding below the reserve price
r_{j}
. For the deviation, the terms on the left- and right-hand side in (
34
) satisfy:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[p_{ij}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i})\mid\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}\wedge b_{ij}<r_{j}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[v_{ij}(x_{ij}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i}))\mid\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}\wedge b_{ij}<r_{j}\right]\right],

as
r_{j}<v_{ij}
(reserve prices are feasible) and agent
i
deviates to the reserve price
r_{j}
for auction
j
if
b_{ij}<r_{j}
.
Combining this with the reasoning above, it follows that the deviation of agent
i
satisfies the ROI constraint in (
34
), i.e.,
(h(\bm{B}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i})\in{\mathcal{R}_{i}}
.

We now show that agent
i
is strictly better off with this deviation. First, note that we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[

\displaystyle g_{i}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})\mid\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}]=\int g_{i}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})\cdot f_{\bm{B}_{-i}\mid\bm{B}_{i}}(\bm{b}_{-i}\mid\bm{b}_{i})d\bm{b}_{-i}

\displaystyle=\int\left[\sum_{\ k\in M;k\neq j}v_{ik}x_{ik}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})-\sigma_{i}p_{ik}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})\right]\cdot f_{\bm{B}_{-i}\mid\bm{B}_{i}}(\bm{b}_{-i}\mid\bm{b}_{i})d\bm{b}_{-i}

\displaystyle+\int\left[v_{ij}x_{ij}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})-\sigma_{i}p_{ij}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})\right]\cdot f_{\bm{B}_{-i}\mid\bm{B}_{i}}(\bm{b}_{-i}\mid\bm{b}_{i})d\bm{b}_{-i}.

Next we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[

\displaystyle g_{i}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i})\mid\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}]=\int g_{i}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{b}_{-i})\cdot f_{\bm{B}_{-i}\mid\bm{B}_{i}}(\bm{b}_{-i}\mid\bm{b}_{i})d\bm{b}_{-i}

\displaystyle=\int\left[\sum_{\ k\in M;k\neq j}v_{ik}x_{ik}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})-\sigma_{i}p_{ik}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})\right]\cdot f_{\bm{B}_{-i}\mid\bm{B}_{i}}(\bm{b}_{-i}\mid\bm{b}_{i})d\bm{b}_{-i}

\displaystyle+\int\left[v_{ij}x_{ij}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{b}_{-i})-\sigma_{i}p_{ij}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{b}_{-i})\right]\cdot f_{\bm{B}_{-i}\mid\bm{B}_{i}}(\bm{b}_{-i}\mid\bm{b}_{i})d\bm{b}_{-i},

where the second equality follows by definition of the deviation.
Subtracting the former conditional expectation from the latter leads to:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[

\displaystyle g_{i}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i})\mid\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}]-\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})\mid\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\Big[v_{ij}x_{ij}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{b}_{-i})-\sigma_{i}p_{ij}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{b}_{-i})-v_{ij}x_{ij}(\bm{b})+\sigma_{i}p_{ij}(\bm{b})\Big]

\displaystyle\cdot f_{\bm{B}_{-i}\mid\bm{B}_{i}}(\bm{b}_{-i}\mid\bm{b}_{i})d\bm{b}_{-i}.

(35)

Note that the expression in brackets in (

) evaluates to 0 for
\bm{b}_{i}
with
b_{ij}\geq r_{j}
, as in that case
b^{\prime}_{ij}=b_{ij}
.
Otherwise, if
b_{ij}<r_{j}
, then agent
i
never wins auction

under
\bm{b}_{i}
and if agent
i
wins auction
j
under
(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{b}_{-i})
, the expression evaluates to:

v_{ij}x_{ij}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{b}_{-i})-\sigma_{i}p_{ij}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{b}_{-i})=v_{ij}-\sigma_{i}r_{j}>0,

as
b^{\prime}_{ij}=r_{j}<v_{ij}
(reserve prices are feasible) and
\sigma_{i}\leq 1
.
Furthermore, it follows from (

) that there is some positive probability that agent
i
wins auction

in this case.
This leads to:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(h(\bm{B}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(h(\bm{b}_{i}),\bm{B}_{-i})\mid\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}]\right]

\displaystyle>\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(\bm{b}_{i},\bm{B}_{-i})\mid\bm{B}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}]\right]=\mathbb{E}[g_{i}(\bm{B})],

contradicting that

is a CE.

It is no longer true that all equilibria are well-supported beyond correlated equilibria.
Since we consider feasible reserve prices, this phenomenon is not due to reserve prices exceeding the agents’ willingness to pay.
Inspired by
Syrgkanis 2014
, we show that already for a single first-price auction, there exist CCE for which the item is not always sold.
Even though the rightful winner
i
can ensure that the item is always sold, agent
i
might have to pay more in some cases or even not win the item when deviating from the joint probability distribution, causing the gain of agent
i
to not strictly improve.

Lemma 5.2
.

Consider the class of simultaneous first-price auctions with additive valuations
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}}
and feasible reserve prices.
Then, there exist
CCE
that are not well-supported.

Proof.

Proof
Consider a single first-price auction with a feasible reserve price
r
among 2 agents with values
v_{1}=1
and
v_{2}=0
. Let
\sigma_{1}=1
. The type of agent 2 can be arbitrary.
Assume that ties are broken in favor of agent 1 and note that
r<1
, as the reserve price is feasible.
Let
X
be a random variable with the following CDF and PDF:

F_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{e}\cdot\frac{1-r}{1-x}\quad\text{and}\quad f_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{e}\cdot\frac{1-r}{(1-x)^{2}}\quad\text{with}\quad x\in\bigg[0,\frac{e-1+r}{e}\bigg].

Note that the domain is well-defined as
0\leq x<1
, and it is easy to verify that
F_{X}(\cdot)
is non-negative and increasing over the domain and
F_{X}((e-1+r)/e)=1
.
Now consider the randomized bid profile
\bm{B}
for which the agents always bid identically according to
X
, i.e., draw some value
x
from
X
and let both agents bid
x
.
We prove that
\bm{B}
is a CCE.

Since ties are broken in favor of agent 1, the expected gain of agent
2
under
\bm{B}
is
0
and agent 2 satisfies the ROI constraint. Moreover, as
v_{2}=0
, no unilateral deviation of agent 2 leads to a positive expected gain, and so agent 2 satisfies the equilibrium condition.
Note that agent 1 satisfies the ROI constraint for
\bm{B}
as
x\leq(e-1+r)/e<1=v_{1}
, so agent 1 never bids above
v_{1}
.
As agent 1 does not win when bidding below the reserve price, the expected gain of agent
1
is:

\EX[g_{1}(\bm{B})]=\int_{r}^{\frac{e-1+r}{e}}(1-x)f_{X}(x)dx=\frac{1-r}{e}\bigg[-\ln(1-x)\bigg]_{r}^{\frac{e-1+r}{e}}=\frac{1-r}{e}.

First, consider any deterministic unilateral deviation
b^{\prime}_{1}
of agent 1.
If
b^{\prime}_{1}\in[0,r)
, then agent 1 will never win the auction and always have a gain of
0
.
Secondly, consider a bid
b^{\prime}_{1}\in[r,\frac{e-1+r}{e}]
. Let
B_{2}=\bm{B}_{-1}
, then:

\EX[g_{1}(b^{\prime}_{1},B_{2})]=(1-b^{\prime}_{1})F_{X}(b^{\prime}_{1})=(1-b^{\prime}_{1})\frac{1-r}{e(1-b^{\prime}_{1})}=\frac{1-r}{e},

where the first equality holds as agent
1
wins and pays
b^{\prime}_{1}
if agent
2
bids at most
b^{\prime}_{1}
. Finally, if
b^{\prime}_{1}>\frac{e-1+r}{e}
, agent 1 always wins the auction but this leads to an expected gain of
1-b^{\prime}_{1}
that is strictly smaller than
\frac{1-r}{e}
. Therefore, we can conclude that for any randomized unilateral deviation
Y
with PDF
f_{Y}
of agent 1 satisfying the ROI constraint, it holds that:

\EX[g_{1}(Y,B_{2})]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\EX[g_{1}(y,B_{2})]\cdot f_{Y}(y)dy\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-r}{e}f_{Y}(y)dy=\frac{1-r}{e}.

Therefore, agent 1 also satisfies the equilibrium condition, proving that
\bm{B}
is a CCE that is not well-supported.
Note that this holds for any feasible reserve price
r
. Additionally, for any reserve price
r>0
, the CCE
\bm{B}
is not well-supported as in this case the probability that the item is not sold is greater than 0.

Additionally, note that this can be extended to hold for any simultaneous first-price auction, i.e.,
\textit{FPA}(m,\bm{r})
with
m>1
, simply by considering
\bm{B}
for an auction
j\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
and letting the randomized bid profiles of the auctions
i\neq j
be independent of
\bm{B}
.
∎

As the item is not always sold under the CCE
\bm{B}
in the proof of Lemma
5.2
, a lower bound on the POA can be implied.
This results is stated below in Theorem
5.1
and shows that, for the homogeneous case of utility maximizers, the
CCE
-
POA
of simultaneous first-price auctions
with
feasible reserve prices is not better than that of simultaneous first-price auctions
without
reserve prices.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider the class of simultaneous first-price auctions with additive valuations
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}}
and feasible reserve prices.
Then, it holds that:

\textit{CCE}\text{-}\textit{POA}(\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}})\geq\frac{e}{e-1}.

Proof.

Proof
Consider the
\textit{FPA}(r)
among two agents with feasible reserve price and the CCE
\bm{B}
as in the proof of Lemma
5.2
.
It is easy to see that the optimal liquid welfare in this instance is
1=v_{\textsf{rw}}
.
The expected liquid welfare of
\bm{B}
is
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i\in N}v_{i}(x_{i}(\bm{B}))]=\mathbb{P}[X\geq r]=\frac{e-1}{e}
.
Therefore,
\textit{CCE}\text{-}\textit{POA}\geq\frac{e}{e-1}
, concluding the proof.
∎

However, as shown in Theorem
4.4
, the presence of feasible reserve prices does improve the POA when only considering well-supported CCE. The following result shows that this bound on the POA is even tight for all
\eta\in(0,1)
in the case of homogeneous utility maximizers.

Theorem 5.2
.

Consider the class of simultaneous first-price auctions with additive valuations
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}}
and feasible reserve prices among a set of
N
homogeneous agents with
\sigma_{i}\geq\frac{e-1}{e-\eta}

.
Then, for well-supported
CCE
, it holds that:

\textit{CCE}\text{-}\textit{POA}(\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}})\geq\frac{e}{e-1+\eta}.

Proof.

Proof
Consider a first-price auction with reserve price
r
among
n=2
agents of the same type, i.e.,
\sigma:=\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
.

\bm{v}=(1,\eta)

1
for every bid profile

b>\eta

2
for
\bm{b}=(\eta,\eta)

X
be a random variable with the following CDF and PDF:

F_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{e}\cdot\frac{1-\sigma\eta}{1-\sigma x}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ f_{X}(x)=\frac{\sigma}{e}\cdot\frac{1-\sigma\eta}{(1-\sigma x)^{2}}\ \ \text{ with }\ \ x\in\left[\eta,\frac{e-1+\sigma\eta}{e\sigma}\right].

Note that the domain is well-defined as
x\geq 0
, and it is easy to verify that
F_{X}(\cdot)
is non-negative and increasing over the domain and
F_{X}((e-1+\sigma\eta)/e\sigma)=1
.
Now consider the randomized bid profile
\bm{B}
in which the agents always bid identically according to
X
, i.e., draw some value
x
from
X
and let both agents bid
x
.
We prove that
\bm{B}
is a CCE.

First, we verify that
\bm{B}
satisfies the ROI constraints of the two agents. Note that this is trivially true for agent 2 as, by the tie-breaking rule, agent
2
only wins the item for the bid profile
(\eta,\eta)
and
\eta=v_{2}
.
Note that
\bm{B}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}

\EX[p_{1}(\bm{B})]\leq\frac{e-1+\sigma\eta}{e\sigma}\cdot\EX[x_{1}(\bm{B})]\leq\EX[x_{1}(\bm{B})]=\EX[v_{1}(x_{1}(\bm{B}))],

where the first inequality holds as agent 1 never bids above
\frac{e-1+\sigma\eta}{e\sigma}

Secondly, we show that no agent can improve by unilaterally deviating.
First, note that under
\bm{B}
agent 2 only wins when bidding bid
\eta
, and that the gain of agents 2 is non-negative in this case, i.e.,
v_{2}-\sigma\eta=\eta(1-\sigma)\geq 0
.
We consider deterministic deviations of agent 2.
Agent 2 would never win when bidding

.
If agent 2 wins when bidding
b_{2}>\eta

And if agents 2 wins when bidding
b_{2}=\eta
, their ROI constraint is tight.

As ties are broken in favor of agent
1
for every bid profile
\bm{b}=(b,b)
with
b>\eta
, the expected gain of agent
1
under
\bm{B}
is:

\displaystyle\EX[g_{1}(\bm{B})]

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta}^{\frac{e-1+\sigma\eta}{e\sigma}}(1-\sigma x)f_{X}(x)dx=\int_{\eta}^{\frac{e-1+\sigma\eta}{e\sigma}}\frac{\sigma}{e}\frac{1-\sigma\eta}{1-\sigma x}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\sigma\eta}{e}\bigg[-\ln(1-\sigma x)\bigg]_{\eta}^{\frac{e-1+\sigma\eta}{e\sigma}}=\frac{1-\sigma\eta}{e}.

Consider a deterministic deviation
b_{1}^{\prime}
of agent
1
.
Clearly,
b_{1}^{\prime}\leq\eta
leads to an expected gain of
\EX[g_{1}(b^{\prime}_{1},X)]=0
, as agent
2
always wins the item at
(\eta,\eta)
.
Moreover, note that bidding
b_{1}^{\prime}>\frac{e-1+\sigma\eta}{e\sigma}

Therefore, we can conclude that for any randomized unilateral deviation
Y

\bm{B}

Theorem 5.3
.

Consider the class of simultaneous first-price auctions with additive valuations
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}}
and feasible reserve prices among value maximizers. Then, for
\eta\in[0,1)

\textit{MNE}\text{-}\textit{POA}(\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{add}})\geq 2-\eta.

Proof
Consider a
\textit{FPA}(2,\bm{r})

i
and
j

\bm{b}

.

\bm{b}

\bm{b}

v_{2j}

\bm{b}

\bm{b}

, and is equal to
2-\eta_{i}=2-\eta
.

\bm{b}
is equal to
v_{1i}+v_{1j}=1
.

MNE
-

of this instance is to
2-\eta
, concluding the proof.
∎

5.2
Submodular Valuations

If we go beyond additive valuations and consider simultaneous first-price auctions with submodular valuations and feasible reserve prices, then there already exist MNE that are not well-supported, as we show in the lemma below.
Using this result, we show that omitting the precondition of equilibria being well-supported results in an unbounded
MNE
-
POA
in the presence of utility maximizers.

Lemma 5.3
.

Consider the class of simultaneous first-price auctions with submodular valuations
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{sub}}
and feasible reserve prices. Then, there exist
MNE
that are not well-supported.

Proof.

Proof
Consider a
\textit{FPA}(2,\bm{r})
among two agents with feasible reserve prices.
Let
\sigma_{1}=1
for agent 1 and for agent 2, let
\sigma_{2}\leq 1
be arbitrary.
Consider the submodular valuations with
v_{1}(\{i\})=\varepsilon
,
v_{1}(\{j\})=1
and
v_{1}(\{i,j\})=1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
Let the values of agent 2 be
v_{2}(\{i\})=v_{2}(\{j\})=v_{2}(\{i,j\})=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
.
Now consider the feasible reserve prices
r_{i}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
and
r_{j}=1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
The bid profile
\bm{b}
with
\bm{b}_{1}=(\frac{\varepsilon}{2},0)
and
\bm{b}_{2}=(0,0)
is a MNE.
First, note that under
\bm{b}
the ROI constraints are satisfied for both agents.
Also note that agent 2 cannot win anything without violating their ROI constraint, as, for both items, the reserve price exceeds their value.
Agent 1 only wins item
i
under
\bm{b}
and thus has a gain of
g_{1}(\bm{b})=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
Note that for any
\bm{b}^{1}
with
b^{1}_{i}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
and
b^{1}_{j}<1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
,
\bm{b}^{2}
with
b^{2}_{i}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
and
b^{2}_{j}\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
,
\bm{b}^{3}
with
b^{3}_{i}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
