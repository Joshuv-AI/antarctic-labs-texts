Spatial Heterogeneity in Heat‐Related Mortality in the Valencian Region: Implications for Climate Adaptation Beyond Administrative Boundaries
Authors: Laura Paredes‐Fortuny, Coral Salvador, Ana M Vicedo‐Cabrera, Carmen Iñiguez, Samira Khodayar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe intensification of extreme heat events in the Mediterranean basin poses growing threats to public health, particularly in climate‐vulnerable regions like the Valencian Region (eastern Spain). Despite the implementation of provincial and municipal heat prevention plans, their efficacy remains unevaluated, and most studies have focused on provincial capitals, potentially overlooking subregional variability. This study addresses these gaps by quantifying moderate and extreme heat‐attributable mortality across 28 thermoclimatic areas (TAs)—a climatic subdivision of the Valencian Region— from 1975 to 2019, using a two‐stage time‐series analysis of high‐resolution temperature and mortality data. Results reveal a distinct coastal–inland gradient, with significantly higher mortality impacts in inland TAs compared to coastal provincial capitals. Areas most impacted by extreme heat are concentrated in interior regions, while coastal capitals exhibit lower risks. These findings underscore the limitations of relying on administrative boundaries for public health surveillance and adaptation planning. The study highlights the value of climatically coherent zones, such as TAs, for improving the spatial resolution of early warning systems and tailoring heat prevention strategies. This scalable methodological framework provides critical insights for enhancing the design of heat adaptation plans not only in Spain but also in other Mediterranean and climate‐sensitive regions.Keywords: heat‐related mortality

Introduction

The Mediterranean region is one of the most sensitive areas to climate change, with warming occurring 20% faster than the global average—a trend expected to intensify in the coming decades (IPCC, 2023; MedECC, 2020). This accelerated temperature increase poses serious threats to both environmental systems and human health (Anzidei et al., 2024; IPCC, 2023). Numerous studies have highlighted the health risks associated with extreme heat, including elevated mortality and morbidity (Demoury et al., 2022; Ingole et al., 2022; Vicedo‐Cabrera et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023). Recent estimates suggest that between 2000 and 2019, approximately 20,000 excess deaths per year in European cities were attributable to heat (Masselot et al., 2023), underscoring the urgent need to strengthen adaptation strategies (Gibb et al., 2024; IPCC, 2023).

In Spain—situated on the western Mediterranean coast—extreme heat events, particularly heatwaves (HWs), have become more frequent and intense (Castillo‐Mateo et al., 2023; Khodayar Pardo & Paredes‐Fortuny, 2024). From 1951 to 2019, the country experienced an average of 2.5 HWs annually, increasing by 0.3 per decade (Paredes‐Fortuny & Khodayar, 2023). In 2022, Spain reported the third‐highest heat‐attributable mortality rate in Europe (Ballester et al., 2023), and between 1991 and 2018, anthropogenic climate change was responsible for 30% of heat‐related deaths (Vicedo‐Cabrera et al., 2021). Projections indicate that without effective adaptation, the annual death toll could reach 12,900 in the second half of this century (Díaz et al., 2019). A recent study covering 12 Spanish provincial capitals found the highest increase in mortality risk associated with HWs in Valencia (8%), followed by Bilbao and Badajoz (6%), Barcelona (4%), and Madrid (1%) (Paredes‐Fortuny et al., 2024).

Valencia, the capital of both its province and the broader Valencian Region (VR, NUTS 2 level), lies along the Mediterranean coast. The region's climate is characterized by hot, dry summers and low precipitation (Río, Herrero, Fraile, & Penas, 2011, Río, Herrero, Pinto‐Gomes, & Penas, 2011). Key meteorological variables—temperature, humidity, and wind—are known to influence heat‐related health outcomes (Mavrogianni et al., 2022). Coastal areas in the VR typically experience milder temperatures and higher humidity than inland zones (AEMET, 2023), suggesting that the heat impacts in the interior may differ significantly from those in coastal capitals. Therefore, relying solely on provincial capitals may not adequately capture the spatial variability of heat‐related health risks across the region. This spatial mismatch provides the conceptual foundation for adopting thermoclimatic areas (TAs) —zones defined by climatic homogeneity rather than administrative boundaries. By grouping municipalities with similar temperature profiles, the TA framework allows the heat‐mortality relationship to be assessed within more coherent climatic units, improving both epidemiological precision and policy relevance.

Despite this knowledge, most of the existing studies on heat‐related mortality in the VR have largely focused on provincial capitals (Achebak et al., 2019, 2024; Díaz et al., 2018; Martínez‐Solanas & Basagaña, 2019; Navas‐Martín et al., 2022; Paredes‐Fortuny et al., 2024) or aggregated provincial data (Achebak et al., 2018; Gallo et al., 2024; Lloyd et al., 2024; Martínez‐Solanas & Basagaña, 2019). Some studies analyzed the intra‐regional heat‐related mortality in Spain (Carmona et al., 2017; López‐Bueno et al., 2024), but still intra‐regional disparities remain poorly understood, limiting the effectiveness of public health surveillance and adaptation strategies.

Following the 2003 European HW crisis, Spain implemented a national HPP in 2004, which remains in force during the summer months. The HPP triggers heat alerts based on consecutive days exceeding temperature thresholds (Ministerio de Sanidad, 2025). Until 2023, heat alerts were triggered uniformly by provinces and were derived from provincial capital data. More recently, the national HPP has been updated to a climate‐coherent framework, although in some areas of the VR, it remains based on data from provincial capitals. Martínez‐Solanas and Basagaña (2019) evaluated the plan's effectiveness and found that, among the VR provinces, only Castellón showed a reduction in extreme heat‐related mortality post‐implementation.

At the regional level, the VR also has its own HPP, activated during the summer months at the municipal level (LAU 2). This plan uses local temperature percentiles (exceeding the 95th percentile on consecutive days) to trigger warnings. However, no formal evaluation of its performance exists, nor any comparison with the national system, raising concerns about its adequacy and consistency.

Addressing these uncertainties requires a comprehensive assessment of heat‐related mortality across the VR using a spatial framework that goes beyond administrative divisions. Accordingly, this study evaluates the extent to which provincial capitals can serve as reliable proxies for province‐wide heat‐related mortality patterns and identifies high‐risk areas through a high‐resolution, climate‐informed analysis. Using a small‐area study design, we estimated heat‐attributable mortality across the VR from 1975 to 2019. Daily temperature data (5‐km resolution) were linked to municipal mortality records, and results were aggregated by thermoclimatic areas TAs that allow for improved statistical power while maintaining climatic specificity.

By comparing TA‐based mortality estimates with provincial‐level figures, this work provides a nuanced understanding of the spatial distribution of heat‐related mortality across the VR. The study offers critical insights into whether current adaptation measures, which rely heavily on capital‐centric data, sufficiently capture regional vulnerability. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive assessment of heat‐mortality at a fine spatial scale in the VR. The methodological approach can also be extended to other regions of Spain lacking localized mortality assessments, supporting more tailored and equitable adaptation planning.

Data and Methodology

Data Collection

Daily all‐cause mortality records of each municipality of the VR were obtained from the Instituto Nacional de Estadística from 1975 to 2019 (INE, Spanish Statistics Institute). The mortality data set included all 542 municipalities of the VR.

Daily maximum 2‐m temperature (T max) was obtained from the data set ROCIO_IBEB from Agencia Estatal de Meteorología (AEMET, Spanish MetService). Temperature data consists of a 5‐km observational gridded data set covering Peninsular Spain and the Balearic Islands. The grid is obtained from the interpolation of the data records from 1800 thermometric stations with a climatology based on the historical analysis of HIRLAM, a numerical prediction model. Further information regarding the methodology applied to obtain this data set can be found in Amblar‐Francés et al., 2020.

Study Area

The study area is the VR (Figure 1), situated on the eastern Mediterranean coast of Spain. It is characterized by hot, dry summers (mean summer temperature: 22.4°C; mean summer precipitation: 23.6 mm; AEMET Climate Atlas (2022)). To enhance the epidemiological relevance and spatial precision of the analysis, we used TAs (CEAM, 2017) as the unit of aggregation. The TA framework was introduced in 2017 to refine the regional heat prevention plan (HPP). Based on the climatic patterns of the VR, the 542 municipalities of the VR were grouped into 28 TAs.

Top: May‐September 1975–2019 mean daily maximum temperature in Spain (left color scale). Bottom: Thermoclimatic areas classifications of the Valencian Region (right color scale).

TAs are clusters of municipalities sharing similar summer temperature profiles (both maximum and minimum), thereby offering two main advantages for the analysis: (a) Climatic Homogeneity: Unlike provinces, which may encompass diverse climate zones, TAs are internally consistent in terms of temperature, a critical determinant of heat‐related mortality. (b) Statistical Robustness: TAs are smaller than provinces but large enough to ensure sufficient population size for stable mortality estimates. This intermediate scale avoids the instability seen in municipal‐level analyses outside of major cities.

The TA framework was originally developed by the Mediterranean Center for Environmental Studies (CEAM, 2017) to inform improvements to the regional HPP in 2017.

The VR has approximately 5 million inhabitants (INE, 2021), with population density varying significantly across TAs (see Figure S1a in Supporting Information S1). The most populated is TA 14, which includes the city of Valencia, the regional and provincial capital. This is followed by TA 28, encompassing Alicante, another provincial capital. Several inland TAs (e.g., TAs 1, 2, 6, 8, 10, 11, and 9) and others, such as TAs 16 and 24, concentrate a larger share of the elderly population (>65 years), a key demographic group for heat vulnerability (Figure S1b in Supporting Information S1).

Spatial Framework: Thermoclimatic Areas

A two‐stage time‐series analytical framework was applied:

Stage One: Municipality‐level associations between daily maximum temperature and all‐cause mortality were estimated using distributed lag non‐linear models (DLNMs), which account for delayed and non‐linear temperature effects.

This approach allowed for quantifying moderate and extreme heat‐attributable mortality, and for detecting spatial patterns in vulnerability with a resolution finer than traditional administrative boundaries.

Heat and Extreme Heat Days

We analyzed the extended summer (from May to September) that encompasses the period when the HPP is active in the VR. Previous studies have shown that, in this area, the warm season is progressively extending with extreme heat episodes outside the summer months (Paredes‐Fortuny & Khodayar, 2023). We defined Heat Days (HDs) for each TA in accordance with the heat definition of Vicedo‐Cabrera et al. (2021), widely used in this context. We averaged the time series of daily maximum temperature across the grid points of the data set of each TAj during the study period (Tmaxj) and calculated the percentile 95 of each averaged series during the extended summers within the study period (P95j). The value of Tmaxj associated with the lowest mortality risk was also estimated separately for each TA (MMTj) through our statistical analysis. Accordingly, for each TAj, those days with Tmaxj between MMTj and P95j were classified as HDs days while days with Tmax above P95 were classified as EHDs. The EHD definition coincides with the threshold to define heatwaves in Paredes‐Fortuny and Khodayar (2023).

Statistical Analysis

Two‐Stage Time Series Analysis

We performed a small‐area analysis (Gasparrini, 2022) that allowed us to estimate TA‐specific temperature‐mortality risks using data disaggregated by municipality (Figure 1). We conducted a two‐stage time series analysis design to assess the association between heat and mortality using conditional quasi‐Poisson regression and distributed lag non‐linear models. This analysis was done with the R‐packages gnm (Turner & Firth, 2007) and dlnm (Gasparrini, 2011) and mixmeta (Sera et al., 2019). We considered the extended summers (May to September) from 1975 to 2019.

The cut‐off lag at 0, distignuishes between the immediate heat effects, such as heat strokes or severe dehydration and the short delayed effects. In the second stage, we used a random effects meta‐analytical model to combine the set of TA‐specific coefficients and obtain a pooled heat‐mortality risk curve for the whole region and the best linear unbiased predictions (BLUP) for each TA. The predicted pooled curve was centered on the pooled MMT, and the TA‐specific curves were centered on the corresponding TA‐specific MMT.

We employed the Cochran Q‐test to test the non‐heterogeneity hypothesis in the meta‐regression model and the Higgins I2 statistic (Higgins & Thompson, 2002) to assess the heterogeneity of the meta‐regression model. We used the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Wald test to test different meta‐regression models (Table 1 of the Supporting Information S1). According to these tests, we selected the meta‐regression model with the average temperature and temperature range predictors.

Mortality Attribution to Daily Maximum Temperature

We computed the attributable mortality (AN) to moderate and extreme heat (ANHD and ANEHD, respectively) on each TA using a forward approach. ANHD and ANEHD are the sum of heat‐related mortalities on HDs (Tmax above MMT) and on EHDs (Tmax above 95th percentile) in the corresponding TA, respectively. We calculated the confidence intervals at 95% (CI) of the AN by Monte Carlo simulations (n = 1,000) of –previously obtained—reduced coefficients, assuming a normal multivariate distribution. We computed ANHD and ANEHD of the VR as the sum of the values of ANHD and ANEHD obtained on each TA, respectively. Then we computed the corresponding fractions (AFHD and AFEHD) over the sum of observed deaths during the extended summers in each TA, and in the VR (Gasparrini & Leone, 2014).

We assessed how the results change if we use data aggregated by provinces instead of TA by performing an analogous analysis at the provincial scale. We aggregated the mortality data by province (Valencia, Castellón, and Alicante), computed the HD and EHD by province, and then we fit the data from each province to a model. Then, on the second stage, we used a meta‐analytical model to combine the set of province‐specific coefficients and obtain a pooled heat‐mortality risk curve for the whole region and the best linear unbiased predictions (BLUP) for each province. Then we computed the ANHD and ANEHD for the VR, and the AFHD and AFEHD for each province.

Temperature Attributable Mortality

In the VR, the annual mean moderate heat‐related mortality (i.e., temperature‐related mortality on HDs, ANHD) was 959 [CI 628, 1,266], corresponding to an AFHD of 6.9% [CI 4.5, 9.1]. Annual extreme heat‐related mortality (i.e., temperature‐related mortality on EHDs, ANEHD) was substantially lower, with 39 [CI 26, 52] deaths on average during the extended summer, corresponding to an AFEHD of 0.28% [CI 0.18, 0.37].

As for the RR at P95, there was a coast‐interior decreasing gradient in attributable mortality, with lower impacts in TA by the sea. For each TA, the AFHD (Figure 4 left) ranged from 6.22% [CI 4.22, 8.03] in the TA14, where there is Valencia city, up to 8.99% [CI 5.87, 11.66] in the TA10 (corresponding to the regional subdivision of El Rincón de Ademuz). The AFEHD (Figure 4 right) ranged from 0.21% [CI 0.14, 0.27] in the TA14 (Valencia) up to 0.46% [CI 0.30, 0.59] in the TA11(corresponding to the regional subdivision of La Serrania).

For moderate and extreme heat, the attributed fraction tended to be lower in the TA with the provincial capitals (i.e., TA14 for Valencia, TA27 for Alicante, and TA5 for Castellón) than in their surrounding TAs (Table 1). In the case of Valencia, the AFHD was 6.22% [CI 4.16, 8.03] in the TA of the capital (TA14), while the average in its surrounding TAs, excluding the TA of Castellón (TA5), was 7.45% [CI 4.65, 9.95]. For extreme heat, the minimum AF observed (Figure 4 right panel) is an AFEHD of 0.21% [CI 0.14, 0.27] in the TA14 (the capital) and an average AFEHD of 0.35% [CI 0.23, 0.47] in the surrounding TAs. Similarly, in the case of the TA27 (Alicante), the AFHD was of 6.66% [CI 4.32, 8.72] (AFEHD was of 0.27% [CI 0.17, 0.35]), smaller than on the neighboring TAs where the AFHD was of 7.72% [CI 4.79, 10.26] (AFEHD was of 0.36% [CI 0.22, 0.47]). In the case of the TA5 (Castellón), the difference with its surrounding TAs was smaller. We found an AFHD of 7.00% [CI 4.4, 9.23] (AFEHD of 0.32% [CI 0.20, 0.41]) for the TA5 (Castellón) and an AFHD of 7.26% [CI 4.35, 9.87] (AFEHD of 0.34% [CI 0.21, 0.46]) in its surroundings.

If we aggregate data by province instead of municipal data aggregated by TA, the annual mean ANHD and ANEHD found for the VR is 887 [CI 627, 1,139] and 135 [CI 108, 162], respectively. The corresponding AFHD and AFEHD were, respectively (Table 1), 5.66% [CI 3.84, 7.35] and 0.86% [CI 0.68, 1.05] in the province of Valencia, 7.39% [CI 5.44, 9.19] and 1.13% [CI 0.93, 1.33] in Alicante province, and 7.16% [CI 4.99, 9.18] and 1.03% [CI 0.82, 1.25] in Castellón province.

Discussion

This study demonstrates significant spatial heterogeneity in heat‐attributable mortality across thermoclimatic areas (TAs) in the Valencian Region, emphasizing the inadequacy of administrative boundaries as proxies for climatic and epidemiological risk.

A pronounced coastal–inland gradient was observed, with inland TAs bearing a disproportionately higher mortality burden compared to coastal areas. This spatial pattern is consistent with regional climatic dynamics, whereby proximity to the Mediterranean Sea modulates temperature extremes, potentially mitigating health impacts in coastal zones. Other factors, such as the proportion of older adults, which is a risk factor (Arsad et al., 2022; Kollanus et al., 2021; McKenna et al., 2023) and is lower in the TAs of the provincial capitals, or different socioeconomic conditions or access to healthcare across TAs may be contributing factors to this coastal‐interior pattern.

The highest extreme‐heat‐attributable mortality fractions, exceeding 0.38% of all summer deaths, were concentrated in interior areas such as La Serranía, Alt Maestrat, and Canal de Navarrés. Although coastal TAs generally displayed lower risk, exceptions like La Safor and La Marina Alta highlight that proximity to the sea may not be universally protective. These findings challenge assumptions underlying existing heat prevention plans, which are often calibrated using data from coastal capitals (Ministerio de Sanidad, 2025).

The analysis further revealed substantial intra‐provincial variability. In all three provinces, heat‐related mortality in the TA corresponding to the provincial capital was consistently lower than in adjacent TAs. This discrepancy likely reflects both climatic differences and demographic or socioeconomic factors, such as age structure and infrastructure resilience. Consequently, generalizing findings from provincial capitals to broader provincial scales introduces systematic bias and could be underestimating the true health burden of heat, particularly in Valencia and Alicante.

Interestingly, while moderate heat (temperature below the percentile 95) contributed more substantially to overall mortality due to its higher frequency, the spatial patterns of extreme heat impacts were more sharply delineated. Moreover, we found that aggregation at the provincial level artificially inflated extreme heat mortality fractions, while moderating those of less intense heat. Provincial aggregation could be inflating the extreme heat mortality fraction because the number of extreme heat days per summer in the TAs of the provincial capitals is generally lower than in their surroundings within the same province. Therefore, the resulting provincial‐average number of extreme heat days per summer is higher than the corresponding number of the TA of the provincial capital, which in turn results in higher attributable mortalities to extreme heat days. However, other factors may be contributing to non‐linear averaging effects that lead to an inflated heat mortality, and this pattern should be investigated in future research.

These findings underscore the sensitivity of mortality attribution to the choice of spatial unit, reaffirming the epidemiological advantages of using climatologically defined zones.

In terms of public health policy, the regional heat prevention plan (HPP), which operates at a fine municipal scale, may partially capture the spatial heterogeneity observed in this study. However, its reliance on temperature anomalies exceeding 7°C limits its responsiveness to isolated extreme heat events. Our results show that even single‐day extremes can drive mortality fractions between 0.21% and 0.46%, levels not adequately addressed by current alert thresholds. Thereafter, the regional HPP may benefit from issuing warnings for isolated extreme heat at a lower temperature threshold, such as the 95th percentile, which in this work is shown to address a relevant heat‐related mortality fraction.

At the national level, the HPP is based on climatically coherent areas that are smaller than provinces but still larger than the TAs analyzed in this work. Furthermore, it remains dependent on temperature data from provincial capitals in some areas. Given the distinct thermal profiles and mortality risks in non‐coastal areas, this approach could risk overlooking the most vulnerable populations. Therefore, the national HPP would benefit from incorporating the TA division or at least using local temperature data, which is not available in some VR areas. The discrepancy between our results and previous estimates, for example, Gasparrini et al. (2015) reporting only 0.78% heat‐related mortality in Valencia city, likely arises from methodological differences, including seasonal focus and spatial resolution. Our summer‐oriented, TA‐based analysis better captures seasonal mortality peaks. Boned Fustel et al. (2021) analyzed the heat‐mortality during the heatwave of July 2017 in the VR, showing a differential excess death mortality in Valencia city and the rest of Valencia province. They report a higher mortality rate in Valencia city, probably related to the Urban Heat Island. In contrast with our work, they compare Valencia city and the rest of the province, while we compare the TA of Valencia city with the rest of the province.

This study presents the first fine‐scale, region‐wide assessment of heat‐related mortality in the Valencian Region using a robust two‐stage modeling framework. By leveraging thermoclimatic rather than administrative boundaries, we provide a scalable model for identifying risk patterns and informing targeted interventions. Nonetheless, limitations include the exclusion of humidity in TA definitions and the use of a single model across a multi‐decade period. In addition, this study was not able to include relative humidity and air pollution as covariates in the models due to a lack of data availability. The role of the relative humidity as a confounder in heat‐related effects is still an open discussion (Schulte et al., 2021). Other studies examined the effect of including relative humidity on heat‐mortality and found that mortality would remain unchanged in Valencia (Ballester et al., 1997) and England and Wales (Hajat et al., 2007). In the case of air pollution, other studies on the confounding effect of air pollution found modest (Anderson & Bell, 2009; O’Neil, 2003) or no modifying effect (Zanobetti & Schwartz, 2008) on mortality. Another relevant aspect is the demographic and adaptive changes that have taken place from 1975 to 2019, which may have affected the temperature‐mortality relationship and have not been considered in this study. Ordanovich et al. (2023) reported evidence of increased adaptation to extreme heat in Spain over recent decades, with a decrease in the extreme‐heat attributable mortality risk from 0.8% to 0.5% since 1,988–1,999. These findings suggest that future studies should consider temporal changes in the mortality risk to better capture evolving vulnerability and adaptation. Future work should also examine temporal shifts in minimum mortality temperatures and incorporate additional exposure metrics, such as nocturnal temperatures and wet‐bulb indices.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive assessment of heat‐attributable mortality at a fine spatial scale across the Valencian Region, revealing pronounced differences in risk across thermoclimatic areas. In particular, provincial capitals—especially Valencia and Alicante‐‐ are not representative of the heat‐related mortality experienced in their broader provinces. Inland areas are consistently more affected, and even some coastal zones demonstrate elevated vulnerability. Together, these findings emphasize substantial intra‐provincial variability that is not noticed when analyses rely solely on provincial administrative units. Thereafter, this study highlights the need to move beyond administrative boundaries when designing adaptation strategies. Aggregating data by climatologically coherent zones enhances the precision of risk estimates and supports more equitable public health responses. Consequently, the current structure of national and regional heat prevention plans may fail to address the full extent of population vulnerability, especially in non‐coastal areas.

Importantly, the methodological framework presented here is readily transferable to other Spanish and Mediterranean regions and climate‐vulnerable settings. Future research should further refine spatial risk estimation by incorporating additional climatic variables and exploring the impacts of temporal changes in vulnerability. As global temperatures continue to rise, advancing geographically nuanced adaptation strategies will be critical for protecting public health in the Mediterranean and beyond.

The authors thank the Spanish Meteorological Service (AEMet) for the data provided for this work the data set ROCIO_IBEB of daily maximum and minimum temperature. The data sets are available at https://www.aemet.es/en/serviciosclimaticos/cambio_climat/datos_diarios?w=2. The authors thank the Spanish Statistics Institute (Instituto Nacional de Estadística (INE), retrieved 1 September 2025) for providing the mortality data. This data set consisted of anonymised individual records, which were subsequently aggregated to create daily time series.
