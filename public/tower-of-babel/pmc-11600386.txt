Prognostic Factors Associated With Increased Mortality in Pediatric Veno‐Occlusive Disease Following Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation
Authors: Irina Zaidman, Natalie Barsoum, Ehud Even‐Or, Miriam Daher, Adi Avniel Aran, Polina Stepensky, Aharon Gefen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHepatic veno‐occlusive disease (VOD) is a life‐threatening complication of hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) and is categorized as a transplant‐related, systemic endothelial disease. Severe VOD can lead to multi‐organ dysfunction (MOF) and is associated with a high mortality rate.ObjectiveTo evaluate the incidence of VOD in children after HCT and analyze the outcomes and risk factors associated with increased mortality.Study DesignA retrospective cohort study of 1243 children with malignant and non‐malignant diseases who underwent HCT at two large pediatric centers over 20 years.ResultsOne hundred one patients (8%) developed VOD post HCT. Most patients developed VOD post allogeneic HCT (76%) versus autologous (24%). The incidence of VOD was twice as high in children with malignant diseases compared to non‐malignant (68% vs. 32%). A much higher incidence of VOD occurred in patients after a busulfan‐based regimen versus total body irradiation‐based and treosulfan‐based, 73%, 18%, and 1%, respectively. The 100‐day survival rate of HCT patients with VOD was 69%. The overall survival rate of the entire group was 50%, showing improvement over the span of the study years, from 40% between 2000 and 2009 to 63% between 2010 and 2021 (p = 0.022). Factors associated with increased mortality included infections before transplant (p = 0.013), conditioning regimen (p = 0.01), abnormal liver function (p = 0.019), presence of ascites (p = 0.008), MOF (p < 0.001), and the need for admission to a pediatric intensive care unit (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in survival rates between children treated with defibrotide alone or with those treated with defibrotide and steroids (61% and 65%, respectively; p = 0.685).ConclusionsSevere VOD in pediatric patients following HCT remains a life‐threatening complication with a high mortality rate. Early diagnosis and treatment with defibrotide are critical for managing this condition. In our cohort, the addition of steroids to defibrotide was not associated with improved outcomes.Keywords: children, hematopoietic cell transplantation, hepatic veno‐occlusive disease/sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, malignant and nonmalignant diseases, survival

Introduction

Hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) has become a standard treatment for various malignant and non‐malignant diseases among the pediatric population.

Intensive chemotherapeutic regimens used for induction in HCT patients are associated with hepatic Veno‐Occlusive Disease (VOD), also known as Sinusoidal Obstruction Syndrome (SOS). The diagnosis of VOD is primarily clinical. In the past, Hepatic VOD has been diagnosed according to the clinical criteria of Baltimore and Modified Seattle, which include fluid retention, weight gain, ascites, upper right quadrant abdominal pain, hepatomegaly, and hyperbilirubinemia within 21 days post‐BMT [1, 2, 3]. Despite the afore‐mentioned criteria, 29% of the described cases in the pediatric population lacked hyperbilirubinemia at the time of diagnosis, and 20% had late‐onset VOD, leading to under‐diagnosis and eventually a lower survival rate [4, 5]. Consequently, the European Society of Bone Marrow Transplant (EBMT) has established new clinical diagnostic criteria for VOD that are quantitative bilirubin and post‐BMT period independent. According to the EBMT, the new diagnostic criteria require the presence of at least two of the following five criteria [5]:

Unexplained refractory thrombocytopenia.

Unexplained refractory thrombocytopenia.

Unexplained weight gain in 3 consecutive days despite aggressive diuresis or >5% increase in weight.

The incidence of VOD reported in various studies ranges from 1% to 28% in the pediatric population, with mortality rates as high as 50%–80% [6]. Close follow‐up, high suspicion, and early diagnosis and treatment are vital to reduce death rates [1, 7].

The pathophysiology of VOD/SOS involves the loss of the sinusoidal endothelium, leading to cell sloughing and disruption of the sinusoidal barrier. The primary insult triggers a thrombo‐fibrinolytic cascade due to the extravasation of leukocytes and red blood cells, which ultimately obstructs sinusoidal flow and microcirculation. This process results in hepatocyte necrosis and portal hypertension, which can progress to multi‐organ failure (MOF) [6].

Risk factors for developing VOD post‐HCT have been described in literature and were categorized into three main groups: Transplant‐related factors, patient and disease‐related, and hepatic‐related [6, 8]. Richardson et al. identified several negative predictors of survival in the pediatric population: age >18 years, allogeneic HCT, normal portal flow, and encephalopathy [9]. In addition, Luft described hyperbilirubinemia as an independent predictor of overall mortality in adults post allogeneic HCT, showing that maximum bilirubin levels (days 0–28) were associated with increased risk of overall mortality and non‐relapse mortality in patients without SOS/VOD [10, 11]. There has been limited research on prognostic factors for mortality in pediatric patients with VOD following HCT.

In the past, the management of VOD/SOS was primarily supportive, including fluid restriction, diuretics, and cardio‐respiratory support. Anticoagulation regimens have also been used in the treatment of VOD, but their use has been discontinued due to serious complications. Later, defibrotide, a mixture of oligonucleotides extracted from the mucosa of swine's intestine that prevents thrombosis and protects the endothelium—was proven effective. It has led to lower mortality rates and complete resolution of the disease in up to 84% of cases, becoming the treatment of choice for managing severe VOD/SOS. [1, 12–15]. Defibrotide has been approved in Europe and the United States for use in the adult and pediatric populations. However, mild‐to‐moderate cases still may be treated supportively [16, 17, 18].

In this study, we present a large retrospective analysis of pediatric patients who underwent HCT in two tertiary care centers. The main aims of this study were to evaluate the outcomes and risk factors for mortality in pediatric patients with VOD following HCT, to assess the impact of adding steroids to defibrotide in the treatment of VOD, and to analyze the incidence and characteristics of VOD in a large cohort of pediatric patients post‐HCT.

Material and Methods

Study Population

We performed a retrospective study, analyzing the incidence, characteristics, and outcome of VOD following pediatric HCT in two large tertiary centers in Israel: Hadassah Medical Center and Rambam Medical Center, between January 2000 and November 2021. The study included 115 patients younger than 18 years old who underwent HCT and were diagnosed with VOD post‐HCT. We excluded from this study patients with primary VOD or patients who developed VOD prior to HCT. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards in both centers.

Definitions

Patients were stratified according to the underlying disease into two main groups: malignant diseases and non‐malignant disorders.

Malignant diseases included: leukemia (ALL, AML/MDS, CML, and JMML), lymphoma (Hodgkin/Non‐Hodgkin lymphoma), and solid tumors (Ewing sarcoma and neuroblastoma).

Non‐malignant diseases were divided to three groups: Primary immunodeficiencies (SCID, Omenn syndrome, HLH, severe congenital neutropenia, other primary immunodeficiencies), metabolic disorders (Osteopetrosis, Hurler syndrome, Metachromatic leukodystrophy), and non‐malignant hematological disorders (Thalassemia major, Sickle cell anemia, bone marrow failure and others).

The donors were grouped according to the following parameters: related/unrelated and matched/mismatched/haploidentical donors.

Conditioning regimens for induction therapy prior to HCT were stratified to TBI‐based, busulfan‐based, treosulfan‐based, and other conditioning regimens.

Patients who underwent HCT prior to 2017 were diagnosed according to the Baltimore or modified Seattle criteria [2, 6]. After the introduction of the new criteria in 2017, VOD was defined according to the EBMT's new diagnostic criteria [5, 8, 14].

We defined the day of diagnosis of VOD as the time of appearance of suggestive signs and symptoms, starting from the day of transplant and further on.

All patients diagnosed with VOD received supportive treatment. For the investigation of outcomes, we defined two target groups: one group received defibrotide as the sole treatment, while the other group received a combination of steroids and defibrotide as the definitive treatment. We considered mechanical ventilation, inotropic support, and dialysis as intensive treatments, with the need for more than one indicating (MOF).

Patients with renal function tests showing values twice or more than baseline were considered to have acute renal failure. Those requiring respiratory ventilation (invasive or non‐invasive) were classified as having acute respiratory failure. Patients with abnormal liver function tests (twice or more than baseline) were considered to have liver failure. Patients with two or more organ failures due to VOD were classified as having MOF and, consequently, severe VOD.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0. Descriptive statistics are given as medians and means (M), with standard deviations (SD) and 95% Confidence interval (95% CI), or as frequency with percentage and 95% CI, according to the type of variable. The association between different categorical factors was assessed using the chi‐square test and 95% CI for difference between the groups was calculated, when applicable. Differences between study groups regarding independent population characteristics or individualized systemic complications, as well as quantitative variables, were analyzed using the t test. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients and Transplants Characteristics

Out of 1243 children who underwent HCT in two large hospitals in Israel from January 2000 to November 2021, 115 (9%, 95% CI: 8%–11%) developed VOD. After the exclusion of 14 patients from analysis (due to either primary cause of VOD or age over 18 years), 101 (8%, 95% CI: 7%–10%) children who developed VOD post‐HCT were included in the current study, as described in Figure 1.

Overview of the study group.

The median age at HCT was 5 years (range 0.2–18), M = 6.9, 95% CI: 5.7–8.1. The median time of developing VOD post‐BMT was day 10 (range 0–41 days; M = 12, 95% CI: 10–13). Most patients (76%) developed VOD post allogeneic HCT versus autologous (24%) [95% CI: 68%–85% and 16%–33%, respectively; p < 0.001]. The incidence of VOD was twice as frequent in children with malignant diseases compared to non‐malignant (68% vs. 32%) [95% CI: 59%–77% and 23%–41%, respectively; p = 0.008]. A significantly higher incidence of VOD was observed in patients who received a busulfan‐based regimen compared to those who received TBI‐based or treosulfan‐based regimens, with rates of 73%, 18%, and 1%, respectively [95% CI: 63%–80%, 10%–25%, and 0%–3%, respectively; p < 0.001]. Regarding the long‐term survival of patients who developed VOD following HCT, the overall survival (OS) of the whole group was 50% (95% CI: 41%–60%); 51 of 101 children survived. An improvement in survival was seen over the years; from the year 2000 to 2009 the OS rate in our study was 40%, in comparison to 63% between the years 2010 and 2021 (95% CI: 4%–43%, p = 0.022), as shown in Table 2.

Impact of Patient and Transplant Characteristics on Mortality

We identified several factors associated with increased mortality in our cohort. There was a statistically significant difference in mortality rates between patients who suffered from a bacterial infection before HCT compared to those who were infection‐free prior to the transplant (63% vs. 38%; 95% CI: 5%–45%, p = 0.013). The conditioning regimen was also a significant risk factor affecting mortality. Although the highest incidence of VOD in the current study was observed in patients with a busulfan‐based protocol (72% of VOD cases; Table 1), the mortality rate was significantly higher after TBI‐conditioning (83% vs. 41%; 95% CI: 5%–49%, p = 0.016). There was a trend suggesting the impact of the underlying disease on survival, with hematological diseases being a good prognostic factor (p = 0.053). Other patient, donor, and transplant‐related factors were not significantly associated with mortality in our study group. Treatment of VOD and Effect on Mortality

As aforementioned, all patients received supportive treatment including fluid restriction and diuretics. Patients with severe VOD received additional definitive treatment, either with defibrotide alone (53 patients, 61%), or with combination of defibrotide and steroids (34 patients, 39%; Figure 2). There was no statistically significant difference in survival rates between the two treatment groups (61% and 65%, respectively; 95% CI: –17% to 25%, p = 0.685; Table 4).

Impact of MOF on Mortality

Among the 101 patients diagnosed with VOD over a 21‐year period, 17 (17%) patients suffered from one organ failure throughout the hospitalization; 45 patients (45%) had MOF involving two or more systems; 38 patients had no organ failure (Figure 3A; one child was excluded from analysis due to missing data).

The OS of the 45 patients with VOD and MOF was only 20% in comparison to 76% OS of 55 patients with VOD but no MOF according to our definition (95% CI: 36%–76%, p < 0.001, see Table 4).

Out of 45 patients with MOF, 24% suffered from respiratory and hepato‐renal failure. The second highest prevalence (20%) were patients with the combination of cardiopulmonary and hepato‐renal failure. Out of 45 patients with MOF, 30 patients were admitted to the PICU. The remaining 15 children continued treatment in the BMT ward (Figure 3B).

Discussion

Hepatic VOD is a common, potentially fatal complication of HCT. The average incidence of VOD in the pediatric population is 20% but up to 60% in specific conditions, whereas 30%–60% may progress to MOF with a death rate as high as 50%–80% [5, 6]. Known risk factors for developing VOD include: underlying disease (e.g., Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, Osteopetrosis, and hemoglobinopathies), pre‐existing liver disease, pre‐transplant and transplant regimens: gemtuzumab‐ and inotuzumab‐ ozogamicin, myeloablative conditioning (including busulfan or total body irradiation) [1, 2, 8, 19].

We conducted a retrospective study of 1243 children who underwent HCT at two large pediatric centers over 21 years. In our cohort, the incidence of VOD was 8%, with a mortality rate of 50%. This mortality rate is lower compared to the ranges reported in most studies (10%–60% incidence and 50%–80% mortality) [5, 6]. Backer et al. described in a retrospective study an incidence of 18% in pediatric patients and mortality of 40% [6]. Cheuk et al. held a retrospective pediatric cohort study with an incidence of 13.2% and a mortality rate of 37% [2]. A large study by Faraci et al. which included 5072 transplants in 4021 pediatric patients, found a low incidence rate of VOD (2%) and a low mortality rate of 16% [14]. The authors suggest possible explanations for this low incidence, including: the retrospective use of EBMT criteria (with possible omission of mild or moderate cases), improvement of supportive care, use of less toxic drugs in the conditioning regimens (e.g., treosulfan and fludarabine), and defibrotide prophylaxis in patients at risk.

In our cohort, the incidence of VOD was twice as frequent in children with malignant diseases compared to non‐malignant (68% vs. 32%, respectively). Most patients (76%) developed VOD post‐allogeneic HCT versus autologous (24%). A much higher incidence of VOD occurred in patients after a busulfan‐based regimen versus TBI‐based and treosulfan‐based, 73%, 18%, and 1%, respectively. In a retrospective study by Lee et al., the incidence of VOD was high. A total of 53%, half of the 372 children in this cohort developed VOD. The high incidence of VOD in this study group may be attributed to the large proportion of patients with malignant diseases (72%) compared to those with non‐malignant conditions. The incidence of VOD was higher in busulfan‐based conditioning regimen compared to TBI‐based regimens (21% vs. 9%, respectively) [3].

The 100‐day survival rate of HCT patients with VOD was 69%. The OS rate of the entire cohort from 2000 to 2021 was 50%. A statistically significant improvement in OS was seen over the years, 40% between 2000–2009 and 63% in the years 2010–2021 (p = 0.022).

We analyzed predictor factors for mortality in our pediatric study group and found that previous infection was a statistically significant predictor. Among patients with serious infections before HCT, 62% died from VOD, compared to 38% in infection‐free patients. Another significant prognostic factor was the conditioning protocol. The overall mortality rate was significantly higher in the group receiving TBI‐conditioning, potentially due to relapse mortality in patients with ALL, which is not directly associated with VOD mortality. We found that abnormal liver function, presence of ascites, MOF, and need for PICU admission (characteristics of severe VOD) were significantly associated with mortality. Unlike our study, Cheuk et al. found that abnormal liver function was not associated with higher mortality. In their study, the type of transplant and donor type were statistically significant predictors of death, with matched unrelated or haploidentical transplants showing a higher risk compared to autologous or matched sibling transplants. We did not find a statistical correlation between donor type or transplant type and mortality rate. Additional mortality predictors identified in their study included hepatic and cutaneous GVHD, maximal weight gain >9%, pleural effusion, intensive care unit admission (similar to our findings), high peak bilirubin, and an increase in bilirubin levels per day within the first week [2].

However, we found that younger donors were associated with a lower risk of mortality (p = 0.005).

In our study, clinical suspicion based on the aforementioned risk factors was key for diagnosing VOD. We found that higher peak bilirubin levels were associated with increased mortality. Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference in survival rates based on maximum bilirubin levels, with a p value of <0.001. Among the 101 patients, the median maximum bilirubin level was 3.80 mg/dL, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 1.75–9.85 mg/dL. Ascites is a clinical manifestation of moderate to severe VOD, indicating a higher risk of mortality. In our cohort, ascites were associated with higher mortality rates among post‐transplant VOD patients, with mortality rates of 75% in those with ascites compared to 45% in those without.

Ingviya et al. described morbidity and mortality predictors in 518 pediatric patients with ascites. VOD was one of the etiologies associated with increased length of hospitalization and the highest mortality rate among pediatric patients with ascites (39%) [20]. Severe cases of VOD often presented with MOF and required PICU admission.

In our cohort, 30 patients were admitted to the PICU for supportive care. During their PICU stay, the majority experienced Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome and required invasive mechanical ventilation. Some patients also suffered from hepato‐renal failure associated with abdominal compartment syndrome, acute renal failure, and required renal replacement therapy. Additionally, several patients developed septic shock and required inotropic support. Many developed fulminant liver failure secondary to VOD, resulting in hepatic encephalopathy. Among these 30 PICU patients, the mortality rate was as high as 90%, indicating that PICU admission in VOD patients is a strong predictor of mortality. Our findings are consistent with the study by Cheuk et al. [2].

According to Corbacioglu et al., the 100‐day survival rate for patients with severe VOD and MOF treated with defibrotide was 50%, compared to 62% in patients without MOF [21]. In our study, the OS rate for patients with MOF was significantly lower, at only 20%, compared to 76% in children without MOF.

As mentioned, 45% of our VOD patients experienced MOF, with the majority suffering from respiratory and liver failure. MOF was a statistically significant predictor of mortality in our cohort, with an overall mortality rate of 80%. All patients who developed symptoms of VOD in our study were treated supportively. Children with moderate/severe VOD received definitive treatment with either defibrotide (53 patients), or a combination of defibrotide and steroids (34 patients). There was no statistically significant difference in survival rate between the two treatment groups. The addition of steroids to defibrotide did not improve the outcome of children with VOD.

Few studies have examined the effects of adding steroids to VOD management. One study involving nine patients found a 50% complete recovery rate from VOD in children treated with steroids [22]. Another study demonstrated that combining high‐dose steroids with defibrotide resulted in a survival rate approaching 75% and a complete recovery rate of 60% [23].

In our larger retrospective cohort, comparing the two treatment groups (defibrotide alone and defibrotide plus high‐dose steroids) differed from the findings of these two studies and did not show a difference in recovery or mortality [22, 23].

Our study has several limitations. The retrospective nature introduces biases inherent to this study design. Given that our cohort spans over 20 years, diagnostic criteria for pediatric VOD have changed over time, potentially leading to inconsistencies in definitions across the years. Additionally, the role of defibrotide prophylaxis, especially in children, remains unresolved, with conflicting results from two phase 3 studies [24, 25]. We did not have data on the prophylactic use of defibrotide, which could theoretically influence outcomes.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated an 8% prevalence of VOD in pediatric patients following HCT, with a 69% survival rate at 100 days and a 50% long‐term survival rate. Our findings underscore that previous infections and factors consistent with VOD severity are significantly associated with poorer outcomes. Early diagnosis and treatment with defibrotide are crucial for managing this complication. In our cohort, the addition of steroids to defibrotide was not associated with improved outcomes in children with VOD. Although survival rates may be improving over time, further research is needed to identify patients at increased risk for VOD, potentially using techniques such as Elastography for early diagnosis [26] and to refine prophylaxis and treatment strategies.
