Prevalence, Clinical Severity, and Outcomes of Extensively Drug-Resistant and Pan-Drug-Resistant Gram-Negative Infections in a Tertiary Intensive Care Unit: A Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Elif Kerimoglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtensively drug-resistant (XDR) and pan-drug-resistant (PDR) gram-negative bacteria are increasingly encountered in intensive care units (ICUs) and present a serious therapeutic challenge. However, whether resistance phenotype independently drives clinical outcomes remains unclear. This study investigated the prevalence of XDR and PDR gram-negative bacteria in a tertiary ICU and their associations with clinical course, mortality, and ventilator weaning failure.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed adult patients (>18 years) admitted to a tertiary general ICU and classified them as XDR, PDR, or non-XDR/PDR based on antimicrobial susceptibility results. We recorded demographic data, comorbidities, GCS scores, organ support requirements, and ICU and hospital lengths of stay. Disease severity was quantified using the APACHE II score. Predictors of mortality and weaning failure were identified by univariate and multivariate logistic regression.ResultsXDR and PDR gram-negative infections were identified in 12.53% and 6.04% of patients, respectively. Of 463 patients, those with XDR and PDR infections had longer ICU and hospital stays, higher rates of sedation and vasopressor use, higher APACHE II scores, lower GCS scores, hypoalbuminemia, and higher mortality compared with non-XDR/PDR patients. Despite this, resistance phenotype did not independently predict mortality or weaning failure in multivariate analysis. Mortality was independently associated with higher APACHE II score, lower GCS, vasopressor and sedation requirements, elevated CRP, and low albumin. Weaning failure was independently predicted by shorter ICU stay and elevated CRP.ConclusionXDR and PDR infections were associated with a more severe clinical course; however, in multivariate analysis, mortality was more strongly associated with physiological deterioration than with resistance phenotype itself.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, XDR and PDR gram-negative bacteria, intensive care unit, mortality

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global crisis that poses a critical threat to health systems, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs), where multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) jeopardize patient survival. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recently recognized the emergence of MDR bacterial species as a critical public health issue.1 In 2019, AMR was reported to have directly caused 1.27 million deaths worldwide and to have been associated with 4.95 million deaths.2

MDROs are defined as resistant to at least three antimicrobial classes, while extensively drug-resistant (XDR) isolates remain susceptible to only one or two categories and pan-drug-resistant (PDR) strains are resistant to all available classes.3 Recent studies have proposed a clinical term, “difficult-to-treat resistance (DTR)”, for gram-negative bacteria. This term denotes resistance that restricts therapy with all first-line antibiotics; in other words, resistance to all β-lactams (including carbapenems and β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, except for novel combinations) and fluoroquinolones.4,5 With this definition, first-line agents of lower toxicity are distinguished from agents with higher toxicity and lower efficacy, such as aminoglycosides, polymyxins, and tigecycline.5

Gram-negative bacteria — including Enterobacteriaceae species (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., Enterobacter spp., and Proteus spp.) and non-lactose fermenters (Pseudomonas spp. and Acinetobacter spp). — are considered the principal agents of MDR bacterial infections.4,5 These pathogens have become leading causes of community-acquired and healthcare-associated infections. The spread of MDR, XDR, and PDR bacteria has been driven by the misuse and overuse of antibiotics, lack of clean water and sanitation, increasing complexity of medical care, and inadequate infection prevention and control practices.4 At the molecular level, resistance mechanisms identified in Enterobacteriaceae include extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) and AmpC β-lactamases; carbapenemases (serine carbapenemases such as Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase [KPC], imipenem-hydrolyzing β-lactamase [IMI], and OXA-48 groups); and zinc-dependent metallo-β-lactamases such as New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM), Verona integron-encoded metallo-β-lactamase (VIM), and imipenemase (IMP). Non-carbapenemase mechanisms include ESBL overproduction, porin mutations and loss, and upregulation of efflux pumps.6 Pseudomonas aeruginosa resistance arises from the overexpression of chromosomal AmpC-type cephalosporinase (PDC), porin mutations that reduce permeability (eg, OprD), and the presence of multiple efflux pumps that export several antibiotics (eg, MexAB-OprM). P. aeruginosa can also acquire resistance mechanisms via plasmids, including β-lactamase genes (carbapenemase genes such as blaVIM and blaKPC) and determinants of quinolone resistance.7–9 In Acinetobacter baumannii, AMR is the result of coexisting multiple mechanisms, including porin mutations, numerous β-lactamases, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, and efflux pump production.10 Although these mechanisms often act in concert, the most alarming developments contributing to increased carbapenem resistance have been the acquisition of OXA-type carbapenemases (eg, OXA-23) and metallo-β-lactamases (eg, IMP, NDM, and VIM).10

While research into AMR mechanisms continues, resistance to last-resort antibiotics, such as polymyxins, and even to new β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations has now been reported.11 This situation has prompted the search for new options in the treatment of XDR and PDR infections; clinicians frequently resort to salvage therapies whose dosing and routes of administration are debated, and that often involve combination therapy or use of antibiotics outside approved indications.12,13 Concurrently, the geographic spread of PDR gram-negative bacteria and the mortality associated with PDR infections have been examined, with a concerning increase reported in infections caused by PDR A. baumannii.14 Recent surveillance data continue to document rising XDR and PDR rates among gram-negative ICU pathogens across Europe and the Eastern Mediterranean region, yet whether resistance phenotype itself, as opposed to the severity of the underlying critical illness, independently drives mortality remains insufficiently characterized, particularly outside high-income settings.

Although the WHO and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) emphasize the threat that antibiotic resistance poses to health systems, up-to-date data on the prevalence and trends of these resistance patterns specifically within intensive care settings remain limited.15–17 In particular, regional data for tertiary-level ICUs are underrepresented in the literature. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies from Türkiye to simultaneously evaluate XDR and PDR prevalence alongside mortality and ventilator-weaning outcomes, while additionally incorporating reserve-agent (colistin and ceftazidime–avibactam) susceptibility patterns and a clinically oriented redefinition of PDR based on reserve-agent resistance.

This study aimed to determine the prevalence of XDR and PDR bacteria isolated from patients admitted to a tertiary-level ICU, to assess the distribution of these microorganism types, and to identify clinical and epidemiological factors that might be associated with resistance patterns. In addition, we aimed to examine the clinical burden imposed by infections caused by XDR and PDR pathogens in the ICU — including associations with mortality and prolonged length of hospital stay. The results are intended to provide a local and current situational analysis of the resistance burden in the ICU and to contribute to infection control strategies and antibiotic management policies.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted in the tertiary general ICU of Ordu State Hospital. Data from 463 patients followed up between 1 August 2024 and 1 November 2025 were evaluated retrospectively. The study was approved by the Ordu University Non-Interventional Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Approval date: 12.12.2025, decision no: 2025/432) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The ethics committee waived the requirement for individual informed consent because of the retrospective design of the study. Patients aged ≥18 years were included in the study. For cases with multiple XDR or PDR infections, only the first infection episode was included in the risk-factor analyses. Patients with missing data in their files were excluded from the study. Cases in which XDR or PDR growth was detected in culture, but the growth was judged to represent colonization rather than an etiologic agent, were not included in the dataset. The diagnosis of infection versus colonization was made by an infectious disease specialist based on clinical findings and culture results.

The study comprised three groups: patients with isolates showing XDR (XDR group), patients with isolates showing PDR (PDR group), and patients without XDR or PDR growth (non-XDR/PDR group). Because the primary aim of the study was to evaluate isolates with advanced resistance patterns (XDR and PDR), MDR isolates were not analyzed as a separate group. MDR isolates were evaluated within the non-XDR/PDR group. This approach was adopted because the primary objective of the study was to characterize the clinical burden of advanced resistance phenotypes (XDR/PDR) rather than to perform a comprehensive comparison across all resistance tiers. We acknowledge that retaining MDR isolates within the comparator group may have introduced heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses excluding MDR isolates were not performed, as the dataset does not separately code MDR status within the non-XDR/PDR group.

Definitions

Resistance definitions followed the internationally accepted framework proposed by Magiorakos et al (2012), as adopted by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).3

PDR: In addition to the Magiorakos classification, the definition of PDR used in this study was compatible with a “clinical PDR” approach, which reflects isolates for which no therapeutic options remain in clinical practice. At our center, reserve antibiotics are not included in the routine antibiogram panel and are tested only when clinically indicated.3,18,19 This reflex-testing approach was adopted because reserve agents are reserved for confirmed or strongly suspected highly resistant infections in routine clinical practice at our institution, consistent with WHO AWaRe stewardship principles; testing all isolates against reserve agents irrespective of initial susceptibility would not reflect real-world diagnostic practice and was therefore not performed.

XDR: It was defined as a lack of susceptibility to all agents except for ≤2 antimicrobial categories.

Data were obtained by review of the hospital information management system and patient medical records. For each patient, the following parameters were recorded: age; sex; hospital length of stay; ICU length of stay; the unit from which they were admitted to the ICU; APACHE II and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores calculated at ICU admission; and neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin, fibrinogen, D-dimer, B-type natriuretic peptide, lactate, creatinine, aspartate aminotransferase, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) values on the day of admission.

During the ICU stay, the need for invasive mechanical ventilation, sedation/analgesia, and vasopressors was recorded. Comorbidities were recorded as respiratory (asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and interstitial lung disease), cardiovascular-metabolic (hypertension and diabetes mellitus), and malignancy (solid organ and hematologic). Reasons for ICU admission were recorded as sepsis-septic shock, respiratory failure, acute kidney injury, acute cerebrovascular event, cardiac arrest, and trauma.

ICU mortality was defined as the primary endpoint. Weaning failure was evaluated as a secondary endpoint, and independent risk factors affecting weaning failure were assessed by logistic regression analysis. Weaning failure was defined as the need for re-intubation or re-institution of invasive mechanical ventilation within 48 h after discontinuation of invasive mechanical ventilation.

Microbiological Investigation and Treatment

Results of all clinician-requested samples, including blood, catheter, sputum, tracheal aspirate, and urine and tissue cultures, were reviewed. For patients with XDR or PDR isolates, organism species, the type of culture in which growth was detected, and susceptibility/resistance to colistin and ceftazidime–avibactam were recorded. Isolate identification from culture specimens was performed using an automated bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility system.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing criteria in effect at the time the isolates were obtained. Within the routine susceptibility panel, the following agents were evaluated: ciprofloxacin, ampicillin–sulbactam, piperacillin–tazobactam, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, ceftazidime, cefepime, imipenem, meropenem, amikacin, and gentamicin. Colistin and ceftazidime–avibactam (C/A) were not included in the routine antibiogram panel at our center; they were tested only for isolates that the microbiology laboratory classified as XDR or PDR based on the initial antibiogram results. In brief, all isolates first underwent testing against the routine panel. Isolates meeting Magiorakos XDR or PDR criteria based on this initial panel were then reflexively tested for colistin and ceftazidime–avibactam susceptibility. Antibiotic therapies were planned and implemented in accordance with the antimicrobial stewardship program at our center and were administered with the recommendation and approval of an infectious disease specialist.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 23 and R version 4.4.1. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Associations between categorical variables were examined using Fisher’s Exact Test with Monte Carlo correction and Pearson’s chi-square test; pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni-corrected Z tests. For comparisons of non-normally distributed variables across three or more groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, and pairwise comparisons were performed using the Dunn test. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent risk factors affecting mortality and weaning failure. Analysis results were presented as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum–maximum) for quantitative variables. A p-value of <0.05 was accepted as statistically significant in all analyses. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis; no imputation was performed.

For the mortality model, candidate predictors were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature; multiple-response variables (comorbidity, treatment received, and susceptibility/resistance) were structurally excluded, as such variables cannot be entered as single coded predictors. For the weaning-failure model, candidate predictors were first screened by univariate logistic regression using a retention threshold of p < 0.10, with resistance phenotype forced into the final model regardless of univariate significance, as the primary exposure of interest for this study. Multicollinearity among candidate predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with VIF > 5 considered indicative of problematic collinearity. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, in addition to the Cox & Snell and Nagelkerke R2 values reported for each model. Interaction terms were not examined because of the limited number of patients with XDR/PDR phenotypes and the risk of model overfitting.

Results

No significant differences were detected between groups with respect to age and sex distribution (p = 0.161 and p = 0.632, respectively). When admission diagnoses were evaluated, respiratory failure was the most frequent reason for ICU admission across all groups; however, there was no statistically significant association between admission diagnosis and resistance type (p = 0.064). Cardiovascular–metabolic diseases were the most common comorbidity across groups (p = 0.001). The total length of hospitalization and ICU length of stay differed significantly between groups. Both hospital and ICU length of stay were significantly longer in the XDR and PDR groups compared with the non-XDR/PDR group (both p < 0.001).

The unit from which patients were admitted to the ICU differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001). The proportion of patients admitted from the emergency department was lower in the XDR and PDR groups (43.1% and 46.4%, respectively) than in the non-XDR/PDR group (69%). Conversely, patients admitted from level-2 and level-3 ICUs had a higher likelihood of being PDR compared with the other groups. In the XDR group, the proportion of patients who were alive at ICU discharge was 48.3%, and 51.7% of the patients were deceased. In the PDR group, these rates were 35.7% and 64.3%, respectively, while in the non-XDR/PDR group, these rates were 71.1% and 28.9%, respectively. Accordingly, ICU mortality was more likely in the XDR and PDR groups than in the non-XDR/PDR group (p < 0.001). APACHE II scores were significantly higher in the XDR and PDR groups (p = 0.002). GCS scores were significantly higher in the non-XDR/PDR group (p < 0.001). The need for sedation and vasopressor support during ICU stay was significantly higher in the XDR and PDR groups compared with the non-XDR/PDR group (both p < 0.001).

Albumin and ALT were the only laboratory parameters that differed between groups (p < 0.05). The median albumin values were 29.15, 28.25, and 32.4 g/L in the XDR, PDR, and non-XDR/PDR groups, respectively, and the intergroup differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001). The median ALT values were 33.2, 21.45, and 31.2 U/L in the XDR, PDR, and non-XDR/PDR groups, respectively. A statistically significant difference in ALT levels was detected between the PDR and non-XDR/PDR groups (p = 0.040).

Across the entire cohort, XDR and PDR growth were detected in 58 (12.53%) and 28 patients (6.04%), respectively. When the PDR definition was restricted to require resistance to reserve agents (colistin and ceftazidime–avibactam), the PDR prevalence in our unit decreased from 6.04% to 4.1% (n = 19/463; K. pneumoniae n = 8, A. baumannii n = 8, P. aeruginosa n = 2, C. lapagei n = 1). Under that narrower definition, XDR prevalence increased to 14.47% (n = 67/463).

When XDR and PDR groups were examined, K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii were frequently observed in both groups. The distribution of species between the XDR and PDR groups did not differ significantly (p = 0.185). No statistically significant association was observed between resistance phenotype and type of culture sample (p = 0.143). In susceptibility testing for colistin and ceftazidime–avibactam, susceptibility to colistin was largely preserved among XDR isolates (84.5%), whereas susceptibility to ceftazidime–avibactam was limited (27.6%). No susceptibility to the tested antibiotics, including colistin and ceftazidime–avibactam, was observed among PDR isolates. Resistance profiles to these antibiotics differed significantly between the XDR and PDR groups (p < 0.001) (Table 1 and Table 2).

No effect of age or sex on mortality was detected. In the univariate analysis, mortality in the non-XDR/PDR group was lower compared with the XDR group (OR = 0.380; p = 0.001). AMR was not identified as an independent determinant of mortality in the multivariate regression analysis. Compared with admissions from the emergency department, admissions from the inpatient ward and levels-1, −2, and −3 ICUs were associated with 2.187- (p = 0.020), 4.725- (p < 0.001), 7.833- (p < 0.001), and 7.460-fold (p < 0.001) increase in mortality risk, respectively. Patients admitted with the diagnosis of cardiac arrest had a higher mortality risk than those presenting with sepsis/septic shock (OR = 5.532; p = 0.001). ICU length of stay was associated with mortality in the univariate analysis but lost statistical significance in the multivariate model (p = 0.025 and p = 0.143, respectively).

In both univariate and multivariate analyses, requirements for sedation and vasopressors were factors that significantly increased mortality risk (both p < 0.001 for sedation; p < 0.001 and p = 0.003 for vasopressors, respectively). An increase in APACHE II score was associated with increased mortality risk (p = 0.001), whereas an increase in GCS was associated with survival (p = 0.001). Among laboratory parameters, higher serum albumin at ICU admission was associated with survival (univariate p < 0.001; multivariate p = 0.024), while higher CRP was associated with mortality (univariate p < 0.001; multivariate p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the frequency, potential sources, and clinical outcomes of XDR and PDR infections — entities increasingly reported in ICUs in recent years and often classified as difficult-to-treat. In our combined patient group, 18% of patients were infected with XDR or PDR organisms. This subgroup was characterized by higher APACHE II scores, lower GCS scores, and a higher prevalence of cardiovascular-metabolic comorbidities at admission. The absence of differences in age and sex among the resistance groups distinguishes our findings from several previous reports.20–22

Some studies have reported that a history of hypertension and diabetes is associated with increased risk of XDR A. baumannii infection in critically ill patients.23–25 Hypertension may cause endothelial dysfunction and may particularly increase risk in the cerebrovascular disease subgroup via mechanisms such as hypoperfusion and reduced autoregulatory capacity.24,26 Previous studies have shown that diabetic patients exhibit impaired humoral and cellular immune responses, resulting in decreased cytokine production and weakened immune cell function. This immune dysregulation contributes to increased susceptibility to infection and may facilitate proliferation of drug-resistant bacteria.27 To validate these findings, further studies examining the relationship between glycemic and blood pressure control and XDR/PDR infections are needed. In a multicenter case-control study by Nasser et al28 conducted in burn ICUs, the MDR group was found to exhibit lower baseline GCS and higher APACHE II scores compared with the non-MDR group. Although our comparison group included non-XDR/PDR patients who may also have harbored MDR isolates, both studies consistently demonstrate a clinical pattern wherein resistant infections are associated with greater disease severity. This finding suggests that, independently of the antimicrobial resistance spectrum, resistant pathogens tend to arise in patient populations with advanced clinical frailty and high disease severity. A central conceptual contribution of the present study is the explicit dissociation between microbiological resistance phenotype and physiological severity as drivers of mortality: although XDR and PDR infections were consistently associated with a more severe clinical presentation, it was the degree of physiological deterioration, rather than the resistance phenotype itself, that independently predicted death. This distinction has direct implications for how clinicians and infection-control teams prioritize risk in critically ill patients with resistant gram-negative infections.

When the units from which patients were admitted were examined, the higher proportion of admissions from higher-level ICUs and from the palliative unit in the group that developed PDR infections reflects the consequences of prolonged exposure to healthcare services and intense antibiotic pressure. Cohorting patients and assigning dedicated staff to each cohort may reduce transmission.29 In a center without cohorting, a study investigating variables in wards to understand carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae (CPE) strains identified colonization pressure, antibiotic consumption, and caregiver-to-patient ratio as key drivers of in-hospital transmission, and recommended assigning dedicated healthcare workers to units with high colonization pressure.30

AMR is influenced by geographic region. In a single-center study from Saudi Arabia, Almakrami et al31 reported that 10% of isolates were XDR and 6% were PDR. In a single-center study of 1058 patients in İstanbul, Uluç et al32 reported AMR prevalences of 7.66% for XDR (n = 81) and 1.23% for PDR (n = 13). In our study group, a total of 86 patients had XDR or PDR isolates (XDR n = 58, 12.53%; PDR n = 28, 6.04%). However, at our hospital, PDR classification is based on the routine antibiogram panel; agents in the WHO AWaRe “Reserve” group (at our center: colistin and ceftazidime–avibactam) are evaluated after the routine panel.18 This methodological approach should be taken into account when interpreting PDR prevalence, because it might lead to the classification of isolates that are susceptible to reserve agents as PDR. Indeed, when the definition of PDR is narrowed to require resistance to the reserve agents (colistin and ceftazidime–avibactam), the PDR prevalence in our unit decreases from 6.04% to 4.1%, while XDR prevalence rises to 14.47%.

The relatively higher proportion of A. baumannii in the XDR group supports this pathogen’s propensity for extensive drug resistance.33 The genomic surveillance protocol initiated by the ECDC for carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAb) across EU/EEA countries and the Balkans — which includes Turkey — highlights that this pathogen is an important regional problem.17

The predominance of K. pneumoniae in the PDR group supports the notion that carbapenemase and other resistance mechanisms can accumulate more readily in this species.34,35 The presence of rare pathogens such as C. youngae and C. lapagei indicates that, under heavy antibiotic pressure in the ICU, atypical organisms can become clinically relevant.36,37 When AMR groups were analyzed, although K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii were commonly observed in the XDR and PDR groups, the species distribution did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.185). In addition, no statistically significant relationship was observed between resistance phenotype and the type of culture sample. These results might be attributed to the limited sample size of the present study.

When susceptibility to reserve antibiotics was examined, colistin susceptibility was largely preserved among XDR isolates (84.5%), whereas susceptibility to ceftazidime–avibactam was limited (27.6%), suggesting that the XDR group has a clinically heterogeneous structure. Resistance to both agents among PDR isolates differed significantly from the XDR group (p < 0.001). Hoşbul et al38 reported ceftazidime–avibactam and colistin susceptibility rates of 92.7% and 48% (72/150), respectively, while Akkaya et al39 reported high resistance to colistin (37%) and ceftazidime–avibactam (45%) in an ICU setting. Pallett et al40 reported increasing colistin resistance and >67% resistance rates to ceftazidime–avibactam among XDR gram-negative cases in Ukraine. These findings underscore the importance of antimicrobial stewardship programs that use current surveillance data and regional resistance patterns to guide empirical therapy in patients suspected of having XDR or PDR infections.

Regarding reasons for admission, respiratory failure was the most frequent cause across the cohort (49.7%). The higher proportion of respiratory failure in the XDR group (62.1%) suggested that AMR infections in ICU practice are closely linked with ventilator-associated complications. This finding was consistent with reports emphasizing the association of XDR A. baumannii with ventilator-associated pneumonia.23 Although tracheal aspirate was the most commonly positive sample type (53.4%), the absence of a significant difference across culture types suggests that infections may be more related to invasive procedures, mechanical ventilation, and patient characteristics than to a single clinical syndrome. Indeed, prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation has been identified as an independent risk factor for XDR A. baumannii infection in the same study.23 XDR and PDR patients had longer hospital and ICU stays. They also required sedation and vasopressors more often than non-XDR/PDR patients. Together, these findings point to a more fragile patient population needing advanced hemodynamic and organ support. However, this study cannot establish the direction of this relationship. The retrospective design does not let us determine whether prolonged critical illness led to resistant infection, whether resistant infection prolonged the clinical course, or whether both reflect a shared underlying driver of illness severity.

In our cohort, 157 of 463 patients (33.9%) died during the ICU stay. Similar rates have previously been reported for tertiary general ICUs.41 Although the AMR phenotype appeared associated with mortality in univariate analyses, the association lost significance in multivariable logistic regression. This finding suggested that mortality may be driven more by disease severity, inflammatory burden, and physiological frailty than by antimicrobial resistance per se. Indeed, the requirements for sedation and vasopressors emerged as the strongest independent variables associated with mortality. Across the cohort, higher APACHE II scores and higher CRP levels were associated with increased mortality risk, whereas higher GCS scores and higher albumin levels were protective. These results support that ICU mortality is more prominently determined by the patient’s inflammatory burden, hemodynamic instability, and organ dysfunction than by the resistance profile of the infecting organism.42,43 Although the relationship between the presence of XDR/MDR pathogens and mortality is debated in the literature, prognostic value has been attributed to CRP, CRP/albumin ratio, APACHE II, and GCS.23,28,32,41–43

Among the 202 patients who required mechanical ventilation, the mean duration on the ventilator was 9.8 days. Weaning failure was observed in 143 of these patients (70.8%). In multivariate binary logistic regression, AMR phenotype, sex, and need for sedation were not independently associated with weaning failure. In this study, weaning failure was independently associated with vasopressor requirement, shorter ICU length of stay, and higher CRP levels. APACHE II score showed a borderline independent association. The finding that vasopressor requirement more than quadrupled the risk of weaning failure makes biological sense. Hemodynamic instability requiring vasopressor support reflects ongoing cardiovascular compromise. This directly impairs respiratory muscle perfusion and reduces the physiological reserve needed for successful extubation.

The link between shorter ICU stay and higher weaning failure needs careful interpretation. It is not simply a protective effect of prolonged stay. Successful weaning requires clinical stabilization and respiratory rehabilitation. Patients who fail an early weaning attempt may be re-intubated and either die or remain ventilated for a shorter period before reaching one of these endpoints. Patients who succeed, by definition, survive long enough to accumulate a longer stay. This raises the possibility of survivorship bias and reverse causality. Rather than a longer stay protecting against failure, early failure and mortality may themselves shorten the observed ICU stay in patients who fail. Some degree of time-dependent bias is also hard to rule out. This study models weaning failure as a fixed binary outcome rather than a time-to-event process, so a patient’s risk of failure and risk of death are not independent of how much time they have already spent in the ICU. Future studies using competing-risks survival analysis, where death is modeled as a competing event for weaning failure over time, would separate these effects more rigorously than the logistic regression approach used here.

The independent association of CRP with weaning failure also supports a role for ongoing inflammatory burden. Persistent inflammation likely impairs respiratory muscle function and delays clinical stabilization. Inflammatory markers have been shown to influence clinical stabilization and weaning success in ICU patients.44

These findings have several practical implications for ICU practice. First, XDR isolates showed limited susceptibility to ceftazidime–avibactam (27.6%). This argues against relying on international treatment algorithms alone. Empirical antibiotic selection should instead be guided by local, institution-specific resistance surveillance. Second, transfer from higher-level ICUs or the palliative unit was linked to PDR acquisition. This supports reinforced cohorting of patients with known resistant isolates and dedicated staffing in units with the highest colonization pressure. Third, physiological severity, not resistance phenotype, was the dominant independent driver of both mortality and weaning failure. Stewardship and infection-control programs may therefore benefit from combining physiological severity scores like APACHE II and GCS with microbiological surveillance when prioritizing resources. Finally, these results support the value of periodic, institution-specific antibiograms that include reserve-agent susceptibility. This is especially important in resource-limited ICU settings where molecular diagnostics remain unavailable.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Perspectives

The retrospective and single-center design of this study limits causal inference. The relatively small number of XDR and PDR isolates, which is the main focus of the study, reduced the statistical power of subgroup analyses. This limitation warrants caution when interpreting associations between resistance phenotypes and clinical outcomes. MDR isolates were not analyzed as a separate group because the study primarily focused on advanced AMR types (XDR/PDR) that are associated with more limited therapeutic options in ICU practice. Microbiological analyses relied on phenotypic susceptibility testing alone. Molecular confirmation of carbapenemase genes and other resistance mechanisms is not routinely available at our institution. Retrospective molecular testing of archived isolates was also not feasible for this study. The Introduction discusses carbapenemase- and efflux-pump-mediated mechanisms in detail, so this absence of molecular data limits the mechanistic depth of our findings. Future prospective studies should incorporate molecular epidemiology, for example PCR-based detection of blaKPC, blaNDM, blaOXA-48, and blaVIM genes. This would link phenotypic resistance patterns to their underlying genetic determinants and strengthen regional surveillance efforts. MDR isolates were also kept within the non-XDR/PDR comparator group rather than analyzed separately. This may have diluted the differences between groups. It could partly explain why AMR phenotype did not emerge as an independent predictor of mortality or weaning failure. The current dataset does not include a separate flag for MDR status within the non-XDR/PDR group, so a sensitivity analysis excluding these isolates was not possible. Future studies should record MDR status alongside XDR/PDR classification from the outset to allow this comparison. Laboratory parameters evaluated in the study consisted of single measurements taken on the day of ICU admission. The absence of serial measurements limited the ability to assess the relationship between biomarker trajectories and clinical outcomes. Although DTR was discussed as a clinically relevant resistance framework, DTR status was not formally classified or analyzed in the present cohort.

The main strength of this study is its relatively large single-center cohort with detailed clinical, physiological, and microbiological data. This allowed multivariate assessment of both mortality and weaning failure, an outcome rarely examined in this context. Both models showed adequate calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow p = 0.880 and p = 0.472) and good to excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.912 and 0.754). By examining antimicrobial resistance and its clinical consequences in a tertiary ICU, this study also contributes to United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 3, Good Health and Well-Being, specifically the target on strengthening capacity for early warning and management of health risks such as antimicrobial resistance. Future multicenter, prospective studies should incorporate molecular resistance profiling, DTR classification, and competing-risks time-to-event modeling of weaning outcomes to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusions

This study found that prolonged hospital and ICU stay, higher APACHE II score, lower GCS, hypoalbuminemia, and the need for sedation and vasopressors were all significantly associated with XDR and PDR gram-negative infections. XDR and PDR infections were linked to a more severe clinical course. However, resistance phenotype did not emerge as an independent predictor of mortality or weaning failure in multivariate analysis. This finding should be interpreted with caution. The number of PDR cases was limited, and MDR isolates were included within the comparator group rather than analyzed separately. Both factors may have reduced our ability to detect an independent effect of resistance phenotype. Larger, multicenter cohorts are needed to confirm these findings.

Early risk assessment at ICU admission may still help identify patients at risk of developing XDR or PDR infection. New scoring approaches based on clinical parameters could support this goal. Reduced susceptibility to reserve antibiotics in our cohort also points to a clear need. Infection control strategies should be strengthened and guided by local resistance patterns.
