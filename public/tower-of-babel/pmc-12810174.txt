Evaluating the Impact of Acupressure and Xylitol Solutions on Reducing Pain Perception in Children Undergoing Local Anesthesia Administration
Authors: Lumbini Pathivada, Debalina Baidya, Nishita Garg, Krishna M Karthik, Sayani Roy, Pallavi Bhyri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis randomized, parallel-group study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of acupressure using the Aculief device compared to xylitol solution in minimizing pain perception during the delivery of local anesthesia (LA) in patients aged 6–9 years.MethodsFifty-four children were randomly allocated to either the Aculief group (n = 27) or the xylitol group (n = 27). In the Aculief group, the device was applied to the LI4 acupoint for 3 minutes before giving LA. In the xylitol group, participants received 2 mL of a 30% xylitol solution applied to the side of the tongue over a 2-minute period. Pain perception was recorded utilizing the Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale, while pulse rate and SpO2 were measured both before and after the treatment.ResultsWithin the Aculief group, significant differences were observed in mean pulse rate, respiratory rate, and pain scores. In the xylitol group, only pain scores demonstrated a significant difference. Intergroup analysis revealed significant differences in mean pulse rate, SpO2, and pain scores, but not in respiratory rate.ConclusionThese results suggest that acupressure using the Aculief device may be more effective than xylitol solution in managing pain and physiological responses during LA administration in pediatric dental patients.How to cite this articlePathivada L, Baidya D, Garg N, et al. Introduction

The management of pain while administering local anesthesia (LA) in pediatric patients is a critical aspect of dental care. Effective pain management strategies can significantly improve the overall experience and outcomes of children undergoing dental procedures. Researchers have investigated various pharmacological and nonpharmacological approaches to lessen the pain and anxiety related to LA injections in children.

Several techniques have been employed to mitigate discomfort during anesthetic administration. These include the topical application of anesthetic material,1 increasing the temperature of anesthetic drugs,2 diminish the rate of injection, employing diversion techniques, and utilizing other counter-stimulation strategies,3 as well as buffering anesthetic drugs.4 Additionally, devices such as the computer-controlled local anesthesia delivery system (CCLAD), Wand,5 and comfort control syringe (CCS)6 have been developed to monitor the discharge and speed of injections, thereby aiming to minimize discomfort. Furthermore, devices such as Vibraject7 and Dental Vibe8 employ vibrational oscillations to stimulate adjacent tissues, serving as counter-stimulations. Despite their advanced nature, these techniques are expensive, and the intricate design of the equipment may worsen a child's behavior.

Research has shown the effectiveness of sweet-tasting solutions in reducing pain during various medical procedures in neonates and infants, including immunization, catheterization, venipuncture, and cold pressor tests.9,10 A review by Harrison et al. verified that sucrose is effective in managing procedural pain in infants and newborns.11 Analgesia induced by a sweet taste acts as a natural pain reliever. When sweet substances are kept in the mouth, they trigger alterations in the brain's endogenous opioid activity and enhance its positive emotional state. Increased opioid effects and intensified emotional conditions may reduce the sensation of pain.12,13 While sucrose solutions have traditionally been used, recent studies have explored xylitol-based solutions as an alternative for pain and anxiety reduction in children undergoing medical procedures.14

Acupressure, a noninvasive technique that stimulates acupuncture points through gentle pressure, has been effectively used to address dental and preoperative anxiety with minimal adverse effects. In the discipline of dentistry, acupressure is employed to mitigate pain, alleviate stress, address temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders,15 and manage the gag reflex16 during dental procedures. The Aculief acupressure device exerts pressure on the LI4 acupuncture point, offering both pain-relieving and anxiety-reducing benefits.17,18

Evaluating the effectiveness of these methods can provide valuable information for healthcare professionals aiming to enhance pediatric patients' experiences during LA administration. However, research comparing the usefulness of acupressure and sweet-tasting solutions as pain management techniques in children is insufficient. The aim of this research was to assess and contrast the potency of acupressure and sweet-tasting solutions in alleviating pain perception during the administration of LA in children undergoing dental procedures.

Materials and Methods

This was a randomized, parallel-group study conducted in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, after obtaining ethical clearance from the institutional review board (Ref No. RCDSR/IEC/MDS/2024/5-02). The methodology was prepared according to CONSORT guidelines. The sample size (n) was derived by using the Epi Info software v 3.0, Atlanta, Georgia (USA), with an α error of 0.05 and a power of 95%. Fifty-four children, aged between 6 and 9 years, who required pulpectomy or extraction with Frankl's Behavior Rating Scale 3 or 4 and having no prior experience with LA, were included in the study. Children with allergies to LA, as well as patients presenting with pustules, vesicles, erythema, exposed wounds, or inflammation in the vicinity of the LI4 acupressure point, in addition to those diagnosed with Raynaud's disease, urticaria, or peripheral vascular disease, were excluded from the study. Selected children were randomly assigned to one of the two groups:

The randomization process was performed by the child selecting one of the two colored paper chits from a nontransparent bag, denoting whether Aculief was to be performed or xylitol solution method, i.e., by the simple random sampling technique.

Due to the nature of the interventions, full blinding of participants and intervention administrators was not possible. However, outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation when collecting physiological and subjective measures. The data analyst was also blinded to group assignments until after the analysis was completed.

For group I (n = 27), the LI4 acupoint was identified on the hand in the space between the thumb and index finger, specifically in the hollow where these digits separate. To pinpoint the LI4 location (Fig. 1), participants were asked to press their thumb and index finger together. The acupoint was then determined to be at the apex of the muscle protrusion between the first and second metacarpal bones. An Aculief acupressure device (Fig. 2) was applied to the LI4 point for 3 minutes, after which LA was administered after application of anesthetic gel [Lignocaine Hydrochloride Gel IP 2% w/w (Lignomed Gel, Medioint Lifescience Pvt. Ltd., Dhar, Madhya Pradesh, India)].

LI4 acupoint

Aculief device

For the second group, comprising 27 participants, sweet solutions were freshly made each day. To create the 30% solutions, 30 gm of xylitol powder was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water. Participants in the xylitol group received 2 mL of this 30% xylitol solution, which was applied repeatedly to the lateral surface of their tongue over 2 minutes. This administration was performed using a needle-free syringe (Fig. 3). Subsequently, after drying the mucosa at the injection site, a topical anesthetic was applied, followed by LA administration.

Application of xylitol solution

For both groups, for procedures involving the mandible, an inferior alveolar nerve block was utilized, while maxillary procedures employed a supraperiosteal injection. All study participants received LA using 2% lignocaine with 1:2,00,000 adrenaline (LOX 2%, Neon Laboratories Ltd., Mumbai, India) and a 26-gauge needle (UNOLOK, Hindustan Syringes and Medical Devices Ltd., Faridabad, India).

Pain perception was evaluated for each patient using The Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale (Fig. 4). Pulse rate and SpO2 using a pulse oximeter were recorded for two different time frames: Time frame 1—Pretreatment; Time frame 2—Posttreatment.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical software SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA) was utilized for data analysis, with a significance threshold established at p < 0.05. To evaluate the mean and standard deviation of the respective groups, descriptive statistics were conducted. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to determine the normality of the data. Inferential statistics to find out the difference between the groups was done by an independent t-test, and within-group comparison was done by a paired t-test. Data presentation was done using bar graphs.

Results

Table 1 illustrates the comparison of pain perception between the Aculief and xylitol groups during LA administration, using the Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale, pulse rate, and SpO2. The data is presented for two time periods: pretreatment and posttreatment. Within the Aculief group, significant differences were observed in mean pulse rate, respiratory rate, and Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale scores, as determined by the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. However, SpO2 showed no significant change in this group (p > 0.05). For the xylitol group, only the Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale demonstrated a significant difference (p < 0.05), while mean pulse rate, respiratory rate, and SpO2 remained statistically unchanged (p > 0.05). Intergroup analysis using the Mann–Whitney U test revealed significant differences in mean pulse rate, SpO2, and Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale scores (p < 0.05), but no statistical significance was found for respiratory rate (p > 0.05). These results suggest that Aculief and xylitol may have different effects on pain perception and physiological responses during LA administration.

Discussion

Pediatric dentists are continually seeking methods to administer LA without causing pain. This is because children who feel discomfort during their initial dental appointment tend to be less cooperative and are less inclined to return for future procedures compared to adults. The psychological impact of early dental experiences can have long-lasting effects on a child's attitude toward oral health care. Consequently, pediatric dentists are exploring various innovative techniques and technologies to make the administration of LA more comfortable and less intimidating for young patients. These efforts aim not only to reduce immediate discomfort but also to foster positive associations with dental visits, potentially improving long-term oral health outcomes for children.

The combination of innovative approaches—needle-free technology, temperature-adjusted anesthetics, enhanced behavioral management, and welcoming office designs—is contributing significantly to a more positive and less stressful dental experience for patients of all ages, but particularly for children. By addressing both the physical and psychological aspects of dental anxiety, these advancements are helping to foster a new generation of patients who view dental visits with less apprehension and more confidence. This holistic approach to patient care is not only improving immediate experiences but also has the potential to reduce dental anxiety and avoidance behaviors in the long term, ultimately leading to better oral health outcomes for the population at large.

Previous research has demonstrated that nonpharmacological interventions, such as acupressure and sweet solution, can mitigate pain perception in various medical procedures, including venipuncture, dental treatments, and minor surgeries.18 Acupressure, an ancient Chinese healing technique, involves applying pressure to specific points on the body to stimulate natural pain-relieving responses. When used in clinical settings, it has been shown to reduce patient anxiety and pain perception during invasive procedures.19 Xylitol, a natural sugar alcohol, has gained attention for its potential analgesic properties, especially in dental applications.20 Its ability to reduce oral bacteria and promote saliva production may contribute to decreased sensitivity and improved overall oral health.21

This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of acupressure using the Aculief device and xylitol solution in minimizing pain perception during LA administration in children aged 6–9 years. The results indicated that both methods had some impact on pain perception, as measured by the Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale. However, the Aculief group showed more significant changes across multiple parameters, including pulse rate and respiratory rate, in addition to pain scores.

This research concentrated on children, as many struggle with the discomfort associated with LA. The study included participants aged 6–9 years. Children younger than 6 years may not have reached the cognitive development necessary to accurately interpret the pain scale and identify their pain experiences. Furthermore, younger children tend to report higher levels of pain intensity compared to older children.22

The research findings demonstrated that the group receiving acupressure at the key point experienced significantly less discomfort during LA injection compared to the xylitol group. Activating the LI4 acupoint alleviates pain in the orofacial region, leading to reduced discomfort when inserting needles.23 Furthermore, this decrease in pain can help lower anxiety in children during dental treatments. The mechanism for pain reduction may include stimulation of specific acupoints that may trigger the release of endogenous opioids and neurotransmitters and potential modulation of the autonomic nervous system, reducing stress and anxiety responses.

Supporting our findings, Gurharikar et al.24 demonstrated that using beads for acupressure during inferior alveolar nerve block injections significantly reduced injection pain. This observation aligns with our results and has been corroborated by Avisa et al.17 Similarly, Sisodia et al.25 found that acupressure enhances children's cooperation in dental procedures, which is consistent with our study's outcomes. Furthermore, Müller et al.26 reported that acupuncture notably decreases acute dental pain both during and after operations, as well as enhances LA effectiveness, further validating our results.

In the study conducted by Pushpasanthy et al.,27 it was noted that the palatal injection, typically regarded as more painful than other methods, was rendered painless with the use of the Aculief device. Additionally, they discovered that the Aculief significantly alleviates pain during the inferior alveolar nerve block procedure. When assessing the duration needed for pain reduction, they observed that applying the Aculief technique for just 1 minute only diminished the pain associated with needle insertion. When the Aculief device was applied for 3 minutes, it eliminated the discomfort associated with administering LA. Consequently, in this study, the device was utilized for a duration of 3 minutes.

A 2022 investigation by Usichenko et al.28 employed a bilateral acupuncture method at the LI4 point using fixed indwelling needles to evaluate the efficacy of LI4 stimulation. Their findings revealed that patients undergoing dental procedures experienced lower heart rates following LI4 stimulation compared to treatments without acupuncture. In a comparable manner, the current research observed a notable reduction in heart rate when utilizing the Aculief device at the LI4 point.

Many research studies suggest that the pleasurable reaction to sweet substances, such as sucrose, enhances the release of natural opioids in the body.29–32 Consumption of sugary foods activates the production of endogenous opioids in our bodies. These naturally occurring substances are responsible for creating sensations of pleasure and well-being when we indulge in sweet flavors. Many scientific studies have shown this connection between sweet flavors and the release of these natural opioids. In our research, we opted for a 30% xylitol solution as a sweetening agent, despite the extensive study of sucrose solutions. Xylitol's primary benefit over sucrose is its role as a cariostatic agent.21 Numerous researchers have suggested that sweet flavors can alleviate pain perception. For instance, Ghaderi found that giving children a sweet sucrose solution before dental injections reduced their pain and discomfort levels.33 Janiani and Gurunathan34 identified a sweet honey solution as effective in lessening pain during inferior alveolar nerve block and infiltration anesthesia. Davangere Padmanabh et al.20 confirmed the efficacy of a sweet xylitol solution in reducing injection discomfort when administering local anesthetics for dental treatments.

This study addresses the critical need for effective, nonpharmacological methods to reduce pain and anxiety in children during dental procedures. By investigating acupressure and sweet solutions, the research expands pain management options for dental professionals. The findings could improve children's dental experience, reducing fear and potentially leading to better oral health outcomes through increased willingness to attend treatments. The study's focus on noninvasive techniques makes it valuable for practical application in clinics, offering a cost-effective alternative to traditional approaches. The research contributes to evidence on complementary medicine in healthcare, bridging Eastern practices with Western dentistry. Additionally, it establishes groundwork for future research in pediatric pain management and exploration of nonpharmacological approaches during dental procedures.

The study's limited sample size may impact the generalizability of its findings to broader populations. Additionally, the study may not provide a comprehensive comparison of these techniques with all available pain management options in pediatric dentistry. Consequently, further research involving larger sample sizes and comparisons with other pain management techniques is recommended to validate these findings and establish best practices for minimizing pain and anxiety during dental procedures in children.

Future research should undertake comprehensive investigations into the physiological mechanisms underlying the analgesic effects of acupressure and sweet solutions. It is essential to examine how individual factors, such as anxiety levels, prior dental experiences, and cultural backgrounds, influence the efficacy of these pain management techniques. Additionally, the effectiveness of these methods should be explored in the context of various dental procedures beyond the administration of LA.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study compared the effectiveness of acupressure using the Aculief device and xylitol solution in minimizing pain perception during LA administration in children aged 6–9 years. The results indicate that both methods had some impact on pain perception, as measured by the Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale. However, the Aculief group showed more significant changes across multiple parameters, including pulse rate and respiratory rate, in addition to pain scores. The xylitol group demonstrated significant changes only in pain scores. Intergroup analysis revealed significant differences between the two methods in terms of mean pulse rate, SpO2, and pain scores, suggesting that the Aculief device may be more effective overall in managing pain and physiological responses during LA administration. However, the respiratory rate showed no significant difference between the groups. These findings suggest that acupressure using the Aculief device could be a promising nonpharmacological approach for pain management in pediatric dental patients undergoing LA. The xylitol solution, while showing some effectiveness in pain reduction, may not be as comprehensive in its impact on physiological parameters.
