Application of exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells in osteoarthritis
Authors: Qianying Feng, Ruixue Liu, Shitian Feng, Dan Li, Sen Wang, Huigen Feng, Junzheng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis is a chronic disabling disease characterized by progressive degeneration of articular cartilage. The condition is characterized by an imbalance in the inflammatory response, the degradation of the extracellular matrix, the apoptosis of chondrocytes, and the disorder of the immune microenvironment. The present clinical efficacy of the treatment is unsatisfactory due to the limited self-healing ability of the articular cartilage. In recent years, there has been a growing body of research focusing on the therapeutic potential of exosomes (Exos) derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). The findings of these studies have demonstrated the significant potential of MSC-derived exosomes as a promising cell-free therapeutic strategy. This article provides a synopsis of the research progress and mechanisms of mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) from diverse tissue sources in the treatment of osteoarthritis. Furthermore, the application of MSC-Exos in the treatment of osteoarthritis were also discussed. The review will provide a valuable reference for future research directions in this field.Keywords: osteoarthritis, mesenchymal stem cells, exosomes, application, mechanisms

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is one type of the most common chronic diseases, characterized by the degeneration of articular cartilage, synovial inflammation, and the remodeling of the subchondral bone. Patients with OA typically experience severe pain and limited joint mobility [1, 2]. Statistical data demonstrated a significant association between the incidence rate of OA and the risk of disability [2, 3]. Recent epidemiological surveys indicate a marked increase in OA prevalence, with the number of affected individuals rising exponentially from 20,342,000 in 1990 to 41,468,000 in 2019. Additionally, the global age-standardized disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) rate for OA increased from 220.7 per 100,000 population in 1990 to 228.0 per 100,000 in 2019. These findings underscore the substantial economic and societal burden imposed by the growing prevalence and associated disability of OA [4, 5]. The pathogenesis of OA is influenced by a number of factors, including dysregulated inflammatory responses, degradation of the extracellular matrix within chondrocytes, chondrocyte apoptosis, and aberrant activation of related signaling pathways [6, 7]. The limited intrinsic regenerative capacity of articular cartilage presents a significant obstacle in OA management, and therapeutic approaches remain insufficient for the complete repair of degenerative cartilage or subchondral bone lesions [8, 9]. MSCs have the capacity to secrete a variety of growth factors, thereby regulating the microenvironment of target tissues and facilitating subsequent cartilage regeneration. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) possess the ability to secrete various growth factors, thereby modulating the microenvironment of target tissues and promoting cartilage regeneration. Accumulating evidence supports the therapeutic potential of MSCs in OA treatment [10–17]. This regenerative process involves cellular proliferation, differentiation, and synthesis of the appropriate extracellular matrix components [18, 19].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are a heterogeneous population of small vesicles and nanoparticles, with diameters ranging from 10 nm to 5000 nm, which are secreted by a variety of cell types [20]. Over the past two decades, research on EVs has advanced considerably, revealing their broad applicability across numerous pathological conditions [21–23]. EVs are commonly classified into three main subtypes based on their size and biogenetic origin: exosomes (Exos), shed microvesicles (SMVs), and apoptotic bodies (ABs) [24]. Notably, Exos characterized as small vesicles with diameters ranging from 30–150 nm [25–27]. Exos have been demonstrated to exhibit diverse of therapeutic effects and play critical roles in regulating various physiological processes [28–30]. This functionality is attributed to their capacity to mediate intercellular communication by transporting functional biomolecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites [31, 32]. Specifically, mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSCs-Exos) have demonstrated multiple biological activities, including enhancing cell viability, promoting cell differentiation and cell migration, modulating immune responses, and exerting anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects [33–35]. Collectively, these biological properties demonstrate the considerable therapeutic potential of MSCs-Exos in in the treatment of diverse diseases, including osteoarthritis (OA) [36, 37]. In this review, a comprehensive examination of the existing literature was performed using the Web of Science, PubMed, ScienceDirect, and EURO PMC databases, covering the period from 2016 to 2025. The search strategy employed a combination of the keywords, including ‘MSCs’, ‘exosomes’, ‘osteoporosis’, ‘application’ and ‘clinical trial’ by using Boolean operators (AND, OR) to refine the results. The inclusion criteria encompassed studies investigating the use of MSC-Exos as a therapeutic intervention, including MSC-Exos from different tissue sources, for the treatment of OA animal models. Eligible reports were required to present at least one type of outcome measure, including histological score, degree of cartilage degeneration, synovitis scoring, immunohistochemical markers, histopathological evaluation, and inflammatory cytokine levels. Studies were excluded if they were duplicate publications, lacked accessible full texts, were non-original research, or did not report relevant outcome measures. This review aims to summarize the therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms of MSC-Exos in OA. Furthermore, it addresses the current limitations associated with their application and proposes potential strategies to overcome these challenges. The objective of this review is to provide a foundation for research and clinical utilization of MSC-Exos in OA treatment.

Characteristics and isolation of MSCs-Exos

The formation of exosomes (Exos) is a complex biological process. Firstly, endosome formation is triggered by membrane invagination. Subsequently, these endosomes undergo a secondary invagination, resulting in the generation of the intracellular multivesicular bodies (MVBs). The MVBs then fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing the intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) contained within them into the extracellular environment via exocytosis, thereby producing exosomes [38]. Exos typically range in diameter from 30 to 150 nm and comprise a diverse array of nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids. It has demonstrated that Exos play a critical role in mediating intercellular communication in both physiological and pathological processes [39, 40]. Accumulated research has demonstrated that Exos derived from different tissue sources harbor distinct genetic contents and perform varied biological functions. Exos obtained from MSCs can exhibit therapeutic properties analogous to those of the stem cells themselves [41, 42]. Concurrently, MSC-Exos obtained through in vitro isolation possess advantages such as sustained biological activity, ease of storage, and lack of immunogenicity [43]. Additionally, MSC-Exos can be delivered directly to the pathological sites via targeted modification or local injection, thereby enhancing their therapeutic efficacy [44]. Analogous to stem cell therapy, MSC-Exos have been demonstrated to modulate responses, facilitate tumor invasion and metastasis, and play a significant role in tissue repair and the maintenance of homeostasis [45, 46, 47]. Additional research has provided further evidence that MSC-Exos can overcome several limitations associated with stem cell therapies, including a reduced risk of tumorigenesis and the elimination of the need for prolonged stable storage and transportation [48]. In addition, studies have shown that MSC-Exos possess the ability to deliver various cytokines and signaling molecules to specific cells or tissues. These biological functions and characteristics of MSC-Exos play a crucial role in enhancing their therapeutic efficacy in the prevention and treatment of OA [49, 50].

In recent years, the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) has published the third edition of the International Guidelines for Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV2023), titled “Minimum Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles: From Basic to Advanced Approaches.” This document provides standardized definitions and protocols concerning the isolation, characterization, functional analysis, and in vivo investigation of extracellular vesicles [51]. The commonly recognized biomarkers for Exos, including the members of the tetraspanin family (CD9, CD63, CD81), proteins associated with multivesicular body formation (Alix, TSG101), heat shock proteins (HSP70, HSP90); and proteins involved in membrane transport and microdomain formation (Flotillin-1, Flotillin-2, Clathrin) [52, 53]. In accordance with the established guidelines of the ISEV, Exos identification typically requires the satisfaction of the following conditions: (1) morphological characterization, primarily conducted via transmission electron microscopy (TEM); (2) determination of particle size and concentration, commonly assessed through nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA); (3) detection of exosomal biomarkers, generally performed using western blotting to confirm the presence of at least three positive markers alongside at least one negative marker [51]. In recent years, flow cytometry has emerged as a pivotal technique for exosome identification due to its rapidity, high-throughput capacity, and ability to concurrently analyze particle size and surface marker expression [54].

Given that Exos measure between 40 and 100 nm in size, the isolation of MSCs-Exos is of critical importance, as it directly influences their properties and subsequent clinical applications [55, 56]. Numerous techniques have been established for the isolation of MSCs-Exos, including ultracentrifugation, ultrafiltration, size exclusion chromatography, polymer precipitation, immunoaffinity approaches, commercially available reagent kits, and microfluidic chip technologies [57–60]. Each of these isolation methods presents unique advantages and limitations, thereby playing a significant role in the isolation of MSCs-Exos (Table 1).

Applications of MSCs-Exos in OA

MSCs are a type of multipotent stem cells that share all the commonalities of stem cells, specifically the abilities of self-renewal and multipotent differentiation [61]. Accumulating evidence suggests that MSCs possess the capacity to differentiate into various cell lineages, including chondrocytes, adipocytes, and osteoblasts [62]. Furthermore, clinical trials have established the safety and efficacy of MSCs as a viable option for cell-based therapies across a spectrum of diseases [63]. MSCs can be isolated from diverse tissue sources such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, dental pulp, periodontal ligament, dermis, and synovial membrane. This diversity of sources has contributed to a substantial reservoir of MSCs, thereby increasing the availability of MSC-Exos. Notably, Exos derived from various MSC origins have demonstrated considerable therapeutic potential in the treatment of OA [64].

Bone marrow MSC-derived Exos and OA

Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) represent the earliest identified subset of mesenchymal stem cells. These cells have been successfully isolated from various tissues, including the sternum, vertebral body, iliac crest, and femoral shaft [65]. Extensive research has demonstrated that BMSCs exhibit robust capacities for self-renewal and proliferative capacities, making them suitable candidates for autologous, allogeneic, and xenogeneic transplantation in the treatment of various pathological conditions [66]. In addition, evidence suggests that autologous BMSCs represent the safest and efficacious therapeutic option for OA, while allogeneic BMSCs shown considerable efficacy in mitigating clinical pain and improving joint function in OA patients [67]. Among the various bioactive factors secreted by BMSCs, derived from these cells have emerged as particularly promising agents for promoting cartilage regeneration. Significant advancements have been achieved in osteoarthritis treatment through both in vitro cellular studies and in vivo animal models. For example, research has shown demonstrated that BMSC-Exos can induce the polarization of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype in osteoarthritic conditions. Furthermore, data has shown that this process can result in a substantial decrease in the levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and TNF-α) and a significant increase in the release of anti-inflammatory cytokines in OA [68]. Huajun Ling, et al. demonstrated that BMSC-Exos facilitate the restoration of cartilage morphology in joint inflammation sites, and promote the expression of type II collagen fiber alpha 1. In addition, the study demonstrated that BMSC-Exos could inhibit matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13), thereby activating cartilage protective mechanisms and delaying the onset and progression of OA [69]. Shao L et al. reported that BMSCs-Exos that had been pretreated with parathyroid hormone (1–34) exhibited a significantly enhanced capacity to promote chondrocyte migration and proliferation, consequently delaying the progression of OA. This effect is mediated through the upregulation of let-7a-5p by parathyroid hormone, which attenuates matrix metalloproteinase-driven extracellular matrix degradation via the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway [70]. Furthermore, evidence has demonstrated that combining BMSCs-Exos with traditional Chinese medicine yields beneficial therapeutic outcomes in OA treatment. For example, Zhang Y, et al. incorporated puerarin, a monomer derived from traditional Chinese medicine, into BMSCs-Exos. Their results demonstrated that puerarin-modified BMSCs-Exos could stimulate promote chondrocyte proliferation and migration while mitigating the production of inflammatory factors through modulation of inflammatory signaling pathways [71]. In a comparable study, Li Wei et al. administered a combination of BMSCs-Exos and ligustrazine in a rabbit model of knee OA. The data demonstrated that this combined treatment effectively reduced inflammatory molecule levels and facilitated tissue repair in OA-affected joints [72].

Adipose MSCs derived-Exos and OA

Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) represent a distinct category of multipotent stem cells that is isolated from adipose tissue. These cells are notable for their abundance and possess several advantageous characteristics, including multipotent differentiation capacity, self-renewal capability, and immunomodulatory functions [73]. Extensive evidence from animal models and clinical investigations have demonstrated that the efficacy of combining ADSC-derived exosomes (ADSC-Exos) with tissue engineering approaches to facilitate the repair of musculoskeletal disorders [74–76]. Specifically, ADSCs-Exos have been shown to alleviate pain symptoms and promote cartilage regeneration and repair. Moreover, ADSCs-Exos derived from subcutaneous adipose tissue exhibit enhanced osteogenic potential and stemness properties. These beneficial attributes underscore the significant therapeutic potential of ADSCs in the treatment of OA [77, 78]. For example, Zhaowei Yin, et al. conducted a study to investigate the effects of ADSCs-Exos in OA. The results of the study demonstrated that miR-99b-3p, encapsulated with in ADSCs-Exos, targeted to ADAMTS4 and downregulated its the expression, facilitating the upregulation of cartilage-associated proteins. Additionally, the use of hyaluronic acid hydrogel particles (HMPS) as a delivery vehicle demonstrated no cytotoxicity and effectively prolonged the retention time of ADSCs-Exos, ensuring sustained inhibition of extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation [79]. Furthermore, research by Wang et al. indicated that ADSCs-Exos have been shown to significantly enhance the proliferation and migratory capacity of chondrocytes. Their results also suggest that ADSCs-Exos can restore the balance between anabolic and catabolic processes in chondrocytes under inflammatory conditions, thereby promoting chondrocyte regeneration in an osteoarthritis rat model induced by medial collateral ligament transection combined with meniscal injury [80]. Notably, the data demonstrated that ADSCs cultured under a hypoxic condition (1% O2) with a medium containing 10% FBS have been observed to produce higher quality Exos. This finding implies that the cellular microenvironment, including variables such as oxygen tension and medium composition, may critically influence Exos production by modulating the physiological state of the cells. Further evidence demonstrated that hypoxia-induced ADSC-Exos could promote the synthesis of cartilage matrix, inhibit the mRNA expression of inflammation-related genes (CEBPβ, COX2, IL6, and TNFα) and catabolic genes (MMP13 and ADAMT5). exert protective effects against osteoarthritis by delivering microRNAs that inhibit the secretion of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Collectively, these findings suggest a potential pathogenic mechanism and therapeutic avenue for osteoarthritis involving hypoxia-induced ADSC-Exos [81]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. demonstrated that human ADSCs-Exos obtained through 3D microfiber culture exhibited a pronounced capacity to mitigate IL-1β-induced suppression of proliferation and migration in chondrocytes compared to those derived from conventional two-dimensional (2D) culture systems. These findings indicate that ADSC-Exos, when modified through diverse advanced technological approaches, exhibit significant potential in influencing OA [82].

Umbilical cord MSCs-derived Exos and OA

Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSCs) are a type of multipotent stem cells derived from umbilical cord tissue, distinguished by their capabilities for self-renewal, low immunogenicity, and sustained proliferative capacity [83]. Recent studies have demonstrated that hUCMSCs possess the ability to differentiate into osteoblasts when cultured on porous scaffolds, indicating their potential as a novel therapeutic strategy for OA [84]. The therapeutic potential of hUCMSCs-Exos has been validated in various animal models of OA. where they have been shown to significantly attenuate cytokine-induced inflammation (notably IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α), and to effectively enhance cartilage matrix synthesis and tissue regeneration through modulation of chondrocyte metabolic pathways [85–88]. Hao Zhou, et al. demonstrated that targeting the METTL3 gene in hUCMSC-Exos via miR-1208 led to a reduction in the secretion of inflammatory factors. Furthermore, their study revealed that administration of hUCMSC-Exos enhanced the expression levels of type II collagen and glycosaminoglycans. Additionally, this treatment was found to inhibit the expression of ADAMTS5 and MMP13, thereby preventing the progression of OA [89]. Yajie Qi, et al. employed flow cytometry and multi-lineage differentiation assays to show that hUCMSC-Exos promote the migration and proliferation of rat mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) within an inflammatory environment induced by interleukin-1β (IL-1β), suggesting their potential to facilitate the repair of osteoarthritic chondrocytes [90]. Ling-Qiang Chen, et al. investigated the impact of hUCMSC-derived exosomal miR-199a-3p on OA. Their findings indicated a significant reduction in inflammatory cytokine expression and improvement in articular cartilage tissue following treatment with hUCMSC-Exos. The underlying mechanism is proposed to involve modulation of the MAPK4/NF-κB signaling pathway [91].

Other types of MSCs-derived Exos and OA

Evidence demonstrated that MSC-Exos originating from the synovial, embryonic, and dental tissues, have also been employed in OA treatment. Notably, synovial mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (SMSC-Exos) demonstrate inherent targeting capabilities and exhibit high biocompatibility. These Exos facilitate cartilage synthesis and metabolism while exerting anti-inflammatory effects through the upregulation of miR-429, thereby contributing positively to osteoarthritis therapy [92]. Additionally, SMSC-Exos have been shown to deliver miR-485-3p, which targeted neuropilin-1 (NRP1), leading to inhibition of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway and reversal of IL-1β-induced cartilage dysfunction [93]. Furthermore, SMSC-Exos interleukin-17 (IL-17) via MATN3, preventing extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and autophagy impairments [94]. Exos secreted by embryonic mesenchymal stem cells (ESC-Exos) have been reported to enhance the proliferation of articular chondrocytes and promote type II collagen synthesis. These vesicles also exhibit the capacity to migrate to injury sites, facilitating the organized regeneration of cartilage and subchondral bone, ultimately resulting in repaired cartilage that closely resembles healthy tissue [95–97]. Oral mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (OMSC-Exos) possess notable immunomodulatory and angiogenic properties. Specifically, they promote macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype through the delivery of molecules such as microRNA-125a-3p, which enhances the secretion of anti-inflammatory and angiogenic factors, thereby modulating inflammation and supporting bone tissue repair [98, 99]. Furthermore, under hypoxic conditions, an increase in glycosaminoglycan content within chondrocytes has been observed, which further optimizes the microenvironment for tissue repair [100].

In summary, the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-Exos is markedly dependent on the tissue source of the parent cells. BMSC-Exos are enriched with osteogenic microRNAs, such as miR-26a and miR-196a, and have been shown to possess substantial bone regenerative potential. Nonetheless, the procurement of BMSCs involves invasive procedures, and their proliferative capacity diminishes with donor age. ADSC-Exos exhibit enhanced immunomodulatory effects relative to BMSC-Exos, primarily through the enhanced secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines. however, they demonstrate comparatively lower osteogenic potential in direct comparative analyses. UCMSCs represent a promising alternative due to their neonatal origin, which circumvents age-associated functional decline. Additionally, UCMSC-Exos exhibit high proliferative capacity and consistent batch-to-batch quality. Despite these advantages, the allogeneic nature of UCMSC-Exos raises concerns regarding potential immunogenicity. SMSC-Exos possess notably strong targeting capabilities attributable to their inherent affinity for joint cartilage, although they represent the least abundant MSC source (Fig. 1).

A comparative analysis of the application performance of various types of MSC-Exos in the treatment of OA. The evaluation encompasses multiple dimensions, including the source abundance, restoration potential, safety, advancements in clinical research, and targeting capability

A more promising approach for future clinical translation involves developing a source selection strategy tailored to specific therapeutic requirements, rather than seeking a single optimal source. BMSC-Exos continue to be the preferred option for initiating evidence-based early-phase clinical trials. In scenarios demanding large-scale production, ADSC-Exos have demonstrated a distinct advantage. For allogeneic general treatments, hUCMSC-Exos have shown to exhibit the most favorable safety profile. When precise intra-articular delivery is required, SMSC-Exos are regarded as the optimal choice. In the future, it is anticipated that engineering approaches integrating the strengths of various sources-such as conferring the targeting capabilities of SMSC-Exos onto ADSC-Exos-will overcome the limitations inherent to single-source therapies, thereby advancing the efficient, safe, and precise treatment of OA using MSC-derived Exos.

Potential mechanism of MSCs-Exos in the treatment of OA

MSCs-Exos identified as pivotal agents in the therapeutic management of OA through their delivery of diverse bioactive molecules, including miRNAs, proteins, and lipids. The principal mechanisms by which MSC-Exos exert their therapeutic effects in OA encompass the suppression of inflammatory responses, mitigation of joint inflammation-induced tissue damage, enhancement of cartilage repair, preservation of cellular homeostasis, and modulation of the immune microenvironment. It has been demonstrated that MSC-Exos can attenuate inflammatory processes by downregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), thereby reducing joint inflammation and synovial hyperplasia. Additionally, MSCs-Exos facilitate cartilage regeneration by upregulating anabolic markers, including type II collagen (Col2) and aggrecan, while concurrently suppressing catabolic enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP13) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 5 (ADAMTS5). This dual regulatory action contributes to the reversal of ECM degradation and promotes cartilage restoration. In the context of immune modulation, MSC-Exos have been shown to induce macrophage polarization towards the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype and inhibit aberrant T cell activation, thereby fostering an immune milieu favorable to tissue repair. Moreover, MSCs-Exos confer protection to chondrocytes against apoptosis and senescence by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inhibiting oxidative stress-related signaling pathways. Collectively, these multifaceted effects of MSC-Exos contribute to the restoration of joint architecture, symptom alleviation, and deceleration of osteoarthritic progression (Fig. 2).

Inhibit the inflammatory response and mitigate damage caused by joint inflammation

The progression of OA has been associated with sustained chronic inflammation within the joint cavity [101]. A key contributor to cartilage dysfunction and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation is the overexpression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, notably interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [102, 103]. These cytokines have been shown to directly activate synovial cells, prompting the secretion of inflammatory mediators and triggering the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling pathway in chondrocytes [104]. This activation results in the upregulation of the expression of ECM-degrading enzymes, such as MMP3 and MMP13, thereby establishing a self-sustaining inflammatory cycle [105]. Investigations into MSC-Exos have identified distinct effector molecules-including miR-1246, miR-21-5p, and miR-9-5p-yet their overall impact converges on the downregulation of pro-inflammatory factors and matrix metalloproteinases, resulting in attenuated cartilage inflammation and matrix degradation [106]. Central to these therapeutic effects is the modulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. For example, Wang et al. demonstrated that treatment of OA with isolated from BMMSC-Exos reduced the expression of MMP3 and AKT1 while increasing PTEN expression, culminating in decreased IL-1β production, reversal of IL-1β-induced chondrocyte apoptosis, and the promotion of a microenvironment conducive to cartilage repair [107]. Similarly, Mianehsz et al. reported that MSC-derived exosomes regulate inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1 by inhibiting IL-1β and TNF-α release through NF-κB pathway [108]. Furthermore, specific miRNAs carried by BMMSC-Exos have been implicated in mitigating inflammation in OA by NF-κB pathway. Xu et al. identified miR-326 as targeting HDAC3 and STAT1 [109], while Tao et al. found that miR-361-5p comparable effects by inhibiting DDX20 [110]. Both miRNAs ultimately suppress pyroptosis via the NF-κB pathway. Additionally, Exos derived from adipose tissue, synovium, and umbilical cord sources have demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties in OA treatment through analogous mechanisms. Collectively, these findings underscore the NF-κB signaling pathway as a pivotal regulatory hub in the pathogenesis and therapeutic modulation of OA [111–114] (Fig. 3).

The therapeutic application of MSC-Exos in OA is primarily mediated through the modulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. MSC-Exos have been shown to activate specific miRNAs and proteins to inhibit the expression of NF-κB. Consequently, this downregulation leads to a decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1, and IL-1β, and inhibits pyroptosis. These effects collectively contribute to the attenuation of cartilage damage and extracellular matrix degradation

Promote chondrocyte repair and maintain cellular homeostasis

Under normal conditions, the synthesis and degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM)-which is secreted by chondrocytes (such as type II collagen and proteoglycans)- maintain a dynamic equilibrium. Degradation of cartilage tissue is a process that, if accelerated beyond a certain threshold and unable to be replenished in a timely manner, can cause structural damage and functional decline. This phenomenon can consequently result in a loss of the tissue’s ability to self-healing [115]. MSC-Exos have been demonstrated to deliver active substances with anti-apoptotic effects (such as miR-21, miR-26b-5p), to inhibit the activity of key apoptotic proteins such as caspase-3, to reduce programmed cell death of chondrocytes, and to enhance the repair ability of chondrocytes. This approach is designed to target the disruption of cellular homeostasis in the pathogenesis of OA, thereby helping to maintain the survival rate and functional activity of chondrocytes. Recent research has indicated that an augmentation in the expression level of miR16 has the capacity to inhibit the expression of SMAD7 in chondrocytes induced by IL-1β. This process has been demonstrated to mediate the anti-degradation and pro-proliferation function of MEG3 [116]. As demonstrated by Liu et al., the miRNA KLF3-AS1, derived from hMSCs, has been shown to upregulate Col2al and aggrecan, whilst concomitantly inhibiting the degradation of MMP13 and RUNX2. Research has demonstrated that this process can promote the proliferation and migration of chondrocytes [117]. MSC-Exos have been demonstrated to promote cartilage repair through the PI3K/Akt pathway. In contrast, hUCMSC-Exos can deliver miRNAs and associated proteins, thereby inducing the polarization of M2 macrophages, inhibiting cartilage-degrading inflammation triggered by ACLT, and preventing further damage to cartilage [118]. Studies have demonstrated that microRNA-93-5p has the potential to regulate autophagy and apoptosis in cartilage cells treated with IL-1β [119]. The present study demonstrates that the inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation in chondrocytes induced by 1 L-1β by exosome-derived miRNA-127-3p indicates that microRNA-127-3p targets CDH11 and reversibly converts the inhibitory effect of Exos on this pathway [120]. Meanwhile, the over-expression of microRNA-92a-3p by MSC-Exos has been shown to inhibit matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) expression and induce collagen II expression in chondrocytes by targeting WNT5A through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [121] (Fig. 4). Although the above studies have revealed the multiple roles of MSC-Exos in regulating cartilage metabolism, their mechanism research has focused on individual molecules or pathways. Furthermore, the experiments are based on in vitro models or animal experiments, and there is still a lack of sufficient human clinical research. Future studies should place more emphasis on multi-omics integrated analysis and large-sample clinical validation to clarify the true potential and limitations of MSCs-Exos in the treatment of OA.

MSC-Exos have been shown to activate the PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways, thereby modulating the expression of miRNAs and key transcription factors. This regulatory cascade enhances chondrocyte proliferation and inhibits cartilage degradation, ultimately supporting cartilage repair and homeostasis

Remodeling immune microenvironment

Evidence has demonstrated that the pathological process of OA is associated with imbalances in the immune system of the joints [122]. Proliferation of T cells and B cells in synovial tissue is abnormal, and macrophages polarize towards the pro-inflammatory M1 type, releasing a large amount of pro-inflammatory factors (e.g. IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6). Concurrently, immune molecules (e.g. HMGB1, TLR4) activate downstream inflammatory pathways, further exacerbating cartilage damage [123, 124]. It has been demonstrated that MSC-Exos exhibit immunomodulatory properties, which have the capacity to inhibit the excessive activation of immune cells including T cells, B cells, and macrophages. Further evidence has been demonstrated that MSC-Exos reduce the impact of autoimmune reactions on joint tissues, and to promote the polarization of M2 macrophages [125, 126]. For example, Yong Meng et al. discovered that the Exo-mediated PVT1 can regulate the HMGB1/TLR4 pathway through the miR-93-5p, thereby inhibiting the progression of OA [127]. Hou Jingyu, et al. have established through rigorous research that MSC-Exos play a crucial role in regulating immune cell function and immune molecules in the treatment of OA. In macrophage regulation, MSCs-Exos have been observed to interact with the chemokine CCL2, thereby impeding its biological activity and preventing the accumulation of macrophages. Furthermore, MSC-Exos can also regulate the polarity transition of macrophages thereby inhibiting the abnormal proliferation of T cells and inducing the regulatory mode of T cells (Tregs). This ultimately leads to immune regulatory effects [128].

Antioxidant effect and reduction of oxidative stress damage

Oxidative stress has been identified as a significant trigger for cartilage damage in OA [129]. Intra-articular inflammation and ischemia have been shown to result in the production of significant quantities of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can exceed the antioxidant capacity of chondrocytes and induce oxidative stress damage [130]. ROS directly damages the DNA and protein structures of chondrocytes, and activate pro-apoptotic pathways such as MAPK, leading to the chondrocyte apoptosis in these cells [131]. Oxidative stress also inhibits the synthesis of type II collagen and aggrecan in chondrocytes, while accelerating ECM degradation, particularly in the late- stage of OA [132]. Notably, oxidative stress and inflammation act synergistically at in this phase, worsening cartilage degeneration. Under hypoxic conditions, MSCs-Exos may enhance their therapeutic effect on OA. Studies show MSCs-Exos increase mRNA expression of type II collagen and aggrecan, and significantly inhibit chondrocyte apoptosis in chondrocytes under hypoxia. This process also augments the ability of AT MSCs to promote cartilage formation [133]. Furthermore, Jinyun Zhao et al. have demonstrated that intervention of Exos secretion from ADMSCs under hypoxic conditions can effectively inhibit the degradation of articular cartilage. The results of surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SR-FTIR) demonstrated that the loss rate of proteoglycans in cartilage was found to be reduced. The process of optimization was applied to the content and distribution of collagen and proteoglycans in cartilage, with the objective of achieving the desired outcome [134].

Application challenges of MSCs-Exos in OA

OA, a prevalent degenerative joint disease, is primarily treated with strategies aimed at alleviating pain and improving functionality. However, it should be noted that these treatments are unable to reverse the underlying cartilage damage. In recent years, MSCs-Exos therapy has emerged as a promising cell-free treatment for OA, owing to their distinctive biological characteristics. The advantages of these nanomedicines include their nanoscale size, low immunogenicity, high stability, and targeted delivery potential. These properties have opened up new avenues for the treatment of OA. However, the utilization of MSCs-Exos in the treatment of OA is hindered by several challenges.

The source and microenvironment of MSCs-Exos

The effectiveness and safety of Exos therapy for OA depend on the origin of the Exos. The source of these particles determines their ability to treat OA, while the microenvironment influences how efficiently they function. If the donor has underlying diseases, the quantity, quality and biological activity of the secreted Exos may be compromised, thereby directly weakening the therapeutic effect. Therefore, the health status of the donor and the microenvironment in which they are located are key factors in determining the safety and efficacy of using Exos as a treatment option. For example, evidence has demonstrated that Exos derived from chondrocytes of OA patients have the capacity to promote the production of IL-1β in macrophages, reduce autophagy, and enhance the activation of inflammasomes. This exacerbates the progression of OA [135]. If Exos are isolated from inflamed synovial fluid obtained from OA patients and subsequent application to patients with this condition, pro-inflammatory characteristics will be exhibited. Technological solutions, such as precise isolation of specific subgroups and standardization of production processes, could facilitate the practical application of exosome therapy in OA treatment [136].

Isolation and identification of MSCs-Exos

Several methodologies have been currently developed for the isolation of MSC-Exos. These include ultracentrifugation, ultrafiltration, size-exclusion chromatography, polymer precipitation, immunoaffinity, commercial reagent kits, and microfluidic chip technology. However, these isolation methods still present significant challenges in terms of yields, purity and integrity of Exos, and economic cost. Furthermore, heterogeneity exists in the Exos obtained by different isolation methods, including differences in surface markers and in the composition of the contents. Although the ISEV continuously updates the MISEV guidelines to standardize the identification of Exos, issues remain regarding insufficient sensitivity and lack of functional validation in Exos identification techniques. NTA is currently the most widely used tool for analyzing the size of Exos, but it can only identify particles between 30 and 200 nm in size and cannot determine their composition. Western blotting is a commonly used method for identifying biomarkers of Exos, but it can only prove the presence of the proteins and cannot confirm whether they are present on the Exos. Specifically, current Exos identification techniques can only reveal their physical form and cannot prove whether they still have biological functions. Countries around the world still lack a comprehensive regulatory system for the isolation and identification of MSC-Exos. At present, several drug regulatory authorities in the United States, the European Union, and China, require that the production process (including the isolation steps) must undergo strict process validation. The method used to isolate Exos must comply with GMP standards. The “Technical Guidelines for Pharmaceutical Research and Evaluation of In Vitro Gene Modification Systems (Trial)” and the “Technical Guidelines for Pharmaceutical Research and Evaluation of Immune Cell Therapy Products” both released by the China National Medical Products Administration in 2022, stipulate that the source of the donor cells for Exos must be verified and that impurities must be strictly controlled during the isolation process. However, it should be noted that these policies have limited general application. These above issues urgently require relevant researchers to develop and promote high-throughput, high-purity, and high-integrity Exo isolation and Exo identification technologies. Furthermore, authoritative departments are recommended to establish relevant laws and regulations to enhance the safety, effectiveness and controllable quality of Exos.

Large scale industrial production of MSCs-Exos

Current Exos research on focuses on animals, and the short-term administration of a small number of Exos can achieve experimental goals. However, when considering practical treatment, high yield and high-quality Exos are required. Therefore, the industrial large-scale production of Exos is a key prerequisite for their clinical translation [137]. At present, the large-scale and sustained industrial production capacity of therapeutic-grade Exos is limited and far from meeting clinical needs [138]. Additionally, there is a lack of unified quality standards in the production process [139]. It is therefore essential to establish standardized detection methods for selecting Exos origin cell types and culture conditions [140]. Furthermore, regulatory agencies and Exos manufacturers are obligated to establish and improve mechanisms for the safety, effectiveness, manufacturing, and quality control of Exos products [141].

Safety and targeted delivery of MSCs-Exos

Further research is required to validate the adverse reactions of Exos treatment in OA patients, using large animal models and clinical trials [142]. Exos have demonstrated their efficacy in targeted drug delivery for the treatment of OA, owing to their ability to home to the site of injury and to penetrate biological barriers with ease [143, 144]. The targeted delivery of cytokines and proteins is achieved through this process. Given the limitations of targeted delivery, in addition to the hydrogel carrier, the modification of the membrane protein of the Exos by genetic engineering (e.g. the display of short peptides targeting cartilage or inflammatory sites) represents a highly advanced technology for achieving accurate delivery. This suggests that the exosome can accurately identify the pathological sites of patients with OA.

In-depth and systematic mechanism clarification

MSCs-Exos have demonstrated great potential in the treatment of OA by regulating the osteogenic balance, immune microenvironment, and angiogenesis. However, existing research has predominantly focused on a single signaling pathway, ignoring the fact that exosomes carry hundreds of bioactive molecules, including proteins, RNAs, and lipids. The synergistic regulatory mechanisms of these bioactive molecules remain to be elucidated. Furthermore, the pathogenesis of OA is characterized by a complex interplay among osteoblasts, osteoclasts, bone cells, immune cells, and vascular endothelial cells. Existing research often focuses on a single dimension, and there are few studies that can systematically demonstrate how MSC-Exos simultaneously and harmoniously regulate the complex network of osteogenesis-osteoclasts-immune-angiogenesis. Furthermore, there are limited studies that address the limitations of animal models and the spatiotemporal fate of MSCs-Exos within the animal body. It is recommended that future mechanism research combines single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, high-resolution imaging, and gene editing tools to further elucidate the aforementioned issues.

Large scale standardized clinical research

To date, MSC-Exos have demonstrated considerable potential for application in the treatment of OA. The existing evidence mainly comes from cell experiments and animal model studies; it remains to be ascertained whether these results can be extrapolated to human patients. The present study is deficient in the area of systematic dose-response and pharmacokinetic relationships of MSCs-Exos treatment in OA. Furthermore, there is an absence of long-term safety data, including whether MSCs-Exos induces immune responses and whether it may cause non-targeted gene regulation. Therefore, the execution of multicenter, large-scale, standardized clinical trials is imperative to evaluate the safety and preliminary efficacy of MSC-Exos in OA patients. Concurrently, the establishment of a standardized production regulatory system, in conjunction with the development of specialized preclinical and clinical guidance principles for the characteristics of MSCs-Exos is conducive to promoting the rapid transformation of MSCs-Exos therapy for OA from basic to clinical use.

Conclusion

Research on MSC-Exos encounters several challenges when moving from the laboratory to clinical application. To address these, a range of improvements is suggested, including establishing a large-scale and standardized industrial production system for Exos; improving targeted delivery efficiency and retention time within joints; optimizing regulatory standards and therapeutic dosages; and elucidating the mechanisms by which MSC-Exos treat OA. Furthermore, collaboration with fields like biomaterials technology should be intensified to develop more effective and precisely targeted MSC-Exos. Establishing quality control measures and regulatory guidelines throughout the research process is also essential to facilitate the shift from basic research to clinical use. Overcoming these challenges will provide OA patients with safer and more effective MSCs-Exos therapies.
