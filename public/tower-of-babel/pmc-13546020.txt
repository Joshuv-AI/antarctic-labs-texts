Nanoparticle-Based Drug Delivery Systems for Periodontitis: Antibacterial, Immunomodulatory, and Regenerative Strategies
Authors: Liyan Wang, Yanwei Chen, Lu Li, Deshi Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, and has become a serious public health issue. Antibacterial treatment and mechanical debridement are the main treatment strategies for periodontitis. However, side effects and bacterial resistance might lead to treatment failure. Nanotechnology systems have new opportunities for the management of periodontitis. With benefits including superior targeting and fewer side effects, drug delivery systems constructed with nanoparticles (NPs) may provide local, delayed, and regulated drug release. When combined with immunomodulatory therapy, tissue regeneration, and antibacterial therapy, a high drug loading capacity of individual medications or therapeutic combinations is made possible by the large surface area to volume ratio of nanoparticles, providing synergistic beneficial effects. This paper reviews the progress in the research and application of nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems in the treatment of periodontitis. We focused on the pathophysiology of periodontitis, introduced the rational design of nano-drug delivery systems, and concentrated on the local approaches to treatment for periodontitis. Furthermore, the future challenges and research prospects for nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems in the treatment of periodontitis are also covered in this paper.

Introduction

Dental plaque generation has been related to the chronic inflammatory condition known as periodontitis. It usually starts with bleeding and swelling of the gums and develops into permanent loss of periodontal attachment and alveolar bone resorption, which may lead to tooth disability, loss, masticatory dysfunction, and deteriorating nutritional status. Several kinds of systemic illnesses, such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and Alzheimer’s disease, are associated with an increased risk of getting periodontitis.1–7 Periodontitis is affecting 20% to 50% of people around the world, with the incidence in developing countries ranging from 8% to 46%.8 In China, Severe periodontitis has been increasingly prevalent over the past few decades. Elderly people are more likely to suffer from periodontitis, and aging may be a major contributor to this growing trend.9 Periodontitis has thus come to be a serious public health issue.

The recommended fundamental therapies for periodontitis include root planning and mechanical subgingival scaling.10 However, regular treatment alone frequently has a limited effect since bacterial endotoxins may have penetrated the root surface.11 The positive effects of therapy can be improved through non-surgical adjuvant medication treatments such as systemic prescription antibiotics, systemic host modulators, and local administration of medications. Despite the widespread use of these treatment strategies, conventional treatments have a number of drawbacks, including abnormal tooth architecture, calculus composition, and equipment restrictions. Liver first-pass metabolism lowers local drug exposure at inflamed sites, and circulating drug components may trigger widespread systemic side effects. Local drug delivery to periodontal lesions is adopted to resolve these shortcomings.12 Unfortunately, saliva and gingival crevicular fluid continuously flush the pocket space, quickly eliminating deposited therapeutics. This leads to rapid drug clearance from the body and challenges to keep the drug concentration in the therapeutic window.13 Drug stability is further hampered by the pathological environment of periodontitis, which is marked by continuous microbial colonization and dynamic alterations under masticatory pressure and inflammatory stimuli. In addition to generating toxicity, drug resistance, and other adverse effects, repeated antibiotic administrations result in poor patient compliance.14 Drug delivery systems (DDSs) provide feasible strategies to address these limitations by realizing controllable, long-lasting local drug release. It has been discovered in recent years that nanotechnology systems have new opportunities for the management of periodontitis. Since they have a nanoscale size (1–100 nm), which facilitates cellular absorption, intracellular transport, and tissue penetration, they can reach deep into periodontal pockets and biofilms.15 Targeted distribution to inflammatory areas or immune cells is made feasible by surface functionalization.16

With benefits including superior targeting and fewer side effects, drug delivery systems constructed with nanoparticles (NPs) may provide local, delayed, and regulated drug release, each of which is useful for preserving therapeutic drug concentrations. Nanoparticles, for instance, can precisely deliver antibiotics to the targeted areas. Because of their high charge density, they are able to interact with the surface of negatively charged bacteria more effectively, increasing antibacterial activity. Furthermore, adjustable surface charge and mucoadhesive characteristics extend retention within narrow periodontal pockets, while material engineering enables sustained, lesion-triggered drug release to maintain effective concentrations.17 Additionally, when combined with immunomodulatory therapy, tissue regeneration, and antibacterial therapy, a high drug loading capacity of individual medications or therapeutic combinations is made possible by the large surface area to volume ratio of nanoparticles, providing synergistic beneficial effects.18

Although nanoparticle-based delivery carriers display favorable therapeutic performance in various in vitro cell tests and preclinical animal models, multiple practical bottlenecks restrict their clinical transformation. The core unresolved translational hurdles cover long-term biosafety risks and potential organ accumulation of nanomaterials, unstable in vivo biodegradation behaviors, insufficient batch-to-batch reproducibility during mass fabrication, strict medical device regulatory standards, and high production costs that limit large-scale clinical manufacturability.

Based on these, the following search terms were looked up through Web of Science, PubMed, and Google Scholar for this study: “nanoparticle” OR “nanocarrier” AND “periodontitis” OR “periodontal disease” AND “drug delivery” OR “local delivery.” Only English-language articles published between 2015 and 2025 were included in the search. We began by focusing on the pathophysiology of periodontitis. Next, we introduced the logical design of a nanoparticle-based drug delivery system and concentrated on the local approaches to treatment for periodontitis. The future challenges and research prospects for nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems in the treatment of periodontitis are also covered in this paper.

Pathogenesis and Therapeutic Targets of Periodontitis

The periodontal area is an extremely dynamic environment that is constantly changing due to microbial activity, mechanical stress, and tissue regeneration. The periodontal pocket is home to over 500 species of bacteria, including Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria and some fungi.19 Protective immunological responses mediated by humoral antibodies and cells are commonly triggered by interactions between host cells and pathogens,20 eliminating harmful stimuli and keeping the equilibrium of tissue remodeling. This condition is known as “dynamic equilibrium”.21 Immune cells such as macrophages are critical to the immune system. Different stimuli enable macrophages to polarize and generate distinct phenotypes, like M1 and M2, both of which are involved in modulating immunological responses.22

A regulated immune-inflammatory network is formed by the oral bacteria, different host cells, the ECM, and cytokines to maintain periodontal hemostasis.23 However, when the host immune response is insufficient or out of control, hyper-reactive or hypo-reactive inflammatory pathways are activated, leading to the disruption of the oral bacterial homeostasis: a large number of neutrophils accumulate, dendritic cells, macrophages and lymphocytes are activated, leading to a series of damages such as ECM degradation, production of various cytotoxic substances (eg, reactive oxygen species(ROS)), perforation of the alveolar process, and osteocyte resorption. In response to continuous microbiota stimulation, epithelial cells release a variety of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1α (IL-1α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). These cytokines draw and aggregate neutrophils in the junctional epithelial cells.24 When neutrophils are recruited and activated excessively, the inflammatory response is amplified, and tissue damage in periodontitis is worsened.25 Pro-inflammatory cytokines significantly promote the chemotaxis of neutrophils. For instance, people with periodontitis have higher levels of Interleukin-36γ (IL-36γ), a recently identified member of the interleukin-36 (IL-36) family, which has been found to accelerate bone resorption and stimulate neutrophil chemotaxis,26 leading to DNA and mitochondrial damage in fibroblasts.27 Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg) and its metabolites trigger M1 polarization in early periodontitis via the Toll-like Receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway. This leads to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α.28 Anti-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-13 (IL-13) and interleukin-4 (IL-4) induce macrophages to differentiate into the M2 phenotype as the disease progresses, consequently enabling tissue regeneration through growth factors and anti-inflammatory mediators.29 In inflammatory conditions, Exosomal miR-143-3p from periodontal ligament stem cells promotes M1 polarization through the phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase/protein kinase B/NF-κB (PI3K/Akt/NF-κB) signaling pathway.30 Pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-6, Interleukin-12 (IL-12), and TNF-α are released by M1 macrophages, which also express CD86 abundantly.31 TNF-α stimulates M1 to generate IL-1β, which encourages osteoclast differentiation and activation.32

In periodontal tissues, T cells are the primary cells that regulate adaptive immunity. Naive CD4+ T cells can develop into T helper 1 (Th1), Th2 (Th2), Th17 (Th17), and Treg subsets. They produce anti-inflammatory cytokines like Interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-35 (IL-35), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) to regulate immunosuppression. Additionally, Tregs may inhibit bone resorption and osteoclast differentiation, maintaining the dynamic balance of alveolar bone.33 Important mediators in the processes of osteoclastogenesis and inflammation involve Th1 and Th17 cells, which exhibit substantial infiltration in the early stages of periodontitis. As the disease progresses, the number of Th2 cells and Tregs increases in periodontal tissues. Periodontitis gingival tissues include more memory T cells (CD4+CD69+CD103) that produce interferon-γ or IL-17.34 In the meantime, Tregs turn unstable under inflammatory conditions, with increased IL17A expression, decreased Foxp3 expression, and loss of anti-osteoclastic effects.33 During inflammation, their numbers rise dramatically, activating other immune cells and generating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-22 (IL-22), interleukin-23 (IL-23), IL-6, and IL-17. By increasing matrix metalloproteinase (MMPs), receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL), and prostaglandin E2 receptor (PGE2), IL-17 is directly involved in the breakdown of periodontal connective tissue and alveolar bone.35

Nanoparticle-Based Drug Delivery Systems for Periodontitis

Excessive host immune responses and dysregulation of bacteria in the dental plaque biofilm disturb the internal environmental balance of the body.36 Periodontal tissue integrity deteriorates when alveolar bone resorption and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation outweigh the production of new bone and matrix.37 These imbalances in periodontal homeostasis are crucial to the progression of periodontitis, which also determines the severity and progression of the disease.38,39 Controlling inflammation, promoting tissue regeneration and repair, and rebuilding the physiological composition and role of periodontal tissues are the ultimate aims of periodontitis treatments.

Since nanoparticle-mediated formulations provide target specificity and repair damaged gingival tissue, they have been demonstrated to be advantageous in treating periodontitis. Nano-formulations typically possess particles that range in size from 1 to 1000 nm. As drug delivery systems, they have controllable size, shape, and surface charge, which facilitate their penetration into periodontal pockets, plaque biofilms, and bacterial cell membranes.40–43 Treatment for periodontitis can have enhanced antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects because nano-formulations have high activity and a large specific surface area, which allows them to interact with inflammatory mediators and periodontal pathogens and load antibacterial medications efficiently.44

Currently, the main strategies for treating periodontitis with nanoparticles are not only antibacterial therapy alone, but also combined with immune regulation and periodontal tissue regeneration. The nano-biomaterials commonly used in nanoparticle delivery systems for treating periodontitis can be categorized according to their chemical makeup as organic or inorganic. Natural and synthetic polymers, like liposomes, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), dendrimers, etc.45 These polymers are mostly made of carbon and hydrogen atoms and have a high biocompatibility.8 Metals, oxides, and structural compounds make up inorganic nanobiomaterials, which are comparatively simple to synthesize and modify, such as metal oxides and silica. Organic Nanoparticles

Polymeric Nanoparticles

Polymeric nanoparticles have several benefits, including high hydrophilicity, lipophilicity, biodegradability, and biocompatibility. Their outstanding surface-to-volume ratio and small size make them suitable for a variety of dental applications. Simple atomic/molecular-scale alterations that they provide enhance solubility, encourage therapeutic benefits, and quicken the pace of dispersion. Nanoparticles interact closely with microbial membranes as effective ionizing agents and drug delivery systems for tackling tooth issues. Osorio46 prepared Doxycycline nanoparticles (Dox-NPs) using the polymerization/precipitation method. These nanoparticles are composed of covalently linked methacrylic acid, ethylene glycol dimethacrylate, 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate, and silicon dioxide NPs. Dox was previously doped onto these nanoparticles. Human periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) were either cultivated in osteogenic media or treated with IL-1β, with or without NPs. The findings demonstrated that the osteogenic media significantly boosted the expression of the ALP and RUNX2 genes in PDLSCs (ALP: P < 0.001; RUNX2: P = 0.005), and Dox-NPs further increased the expression of the ALP gene in PDLSCs grown in the osteogenic medium. In addition, when the cells were under inflammatory attack, Dox-NPs inhibited the upregulation of IL-1β. Resveratrol (RSV) is a naturally occurring substance. According to reports, the clinic application was limited by the low bioavailability of RSV. Peng47 developed a multifunctional nanocarrier named RSV@DTPF. In an aqueous environment, DTPF self-assembles to form a core-shell structure. The shell is formed by the hydrophilic polyethylene glycol (PEG) ends, protecting phagocytosis, whereas the hydrophobic RSV is surrounded by the lipid distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine (DSPE) ends that comprise the core. Experiments conducted in vitro revealed that in an inflammatory environment stimulated by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), the nanoparticles can effectively reestablish the M1/M2 ratio and eliminate ROS. In a rat model with ovariectomized (OVX) periodontal defects, RSV@DTPF showed the potential to promote osteogenesis. Inhibiting the activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-kB), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) can prevent osteoclastogenesis, oxidative stress, and periodontal tissue destruction. Similar results were observed in the research of Huang, who used RSV and 20(S)-protopanaxadiol (PPD) self-assembled via a phenolic resin reaction to form RSV@PPD nanoparticles.48

Excessive ROS and inflammatory mediator accumulation in the periodontal region is a hallmark of periodontitis. Ming49 designed a sericin-based multifunctional nanoplatform (SeCuSrHA@EGCG NPs) loaded with natural phytochemical compounds named epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG). According to in vitro and in vivo studies, Se-CuSrHA@EGCG releases EGCG in the periodontitis environment to scavenge excessive ROS, protect cells from oxidative stress damage, control the immune microenvironment, suppress M1-type macrophages, lower pro-inflammatory factors, promote M2-type macrophage polarization, and increase anti-inflammatory factors.

Chitosan-Based Nanoparticles

Chitosan (CS), a natural linear semi-crystalline polymer, is extracted from crustacean and fungal cell walls. Due to its superior biocompatibility, biodegradability, and adhesiveness and desorption capabilities, it can be utilized as a drug carrier. CS, which is abundant in amino groups, possesses the ability to combat various kinds of bacteria, viruses, parasites, and fungi.50 Its antioxidant properties may promote the restoration of epithelial attachment, and its antibacterial activity may promote directed tissue regeneration. In periodontal tissue engineering, it may also be employed as a sophisticated scaffold.51

Liao52 developed a porous mesoporous hydroxyapatite/chitosan (mHA/CS) composite. They found that the mHA/CS particles had an antibacterial effect on Pg and Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn) and had a diameter of 2µm. In addition to promoting the development of cementum-like tissue and suppressing the growth of periodontal infections, 20 μg/mL rhAm could elevate the expression of genes and proteins linked to osteogenesis and ALP activity.

Bone marrow stem cells (BMSCs) in the field of periodontal tissue regeneration have drawn a lot of interest since they can be identified, grown in vitro, and have differentiation capabilities.53,54 Polylactic acid nanofibers containing chitosan nanoparticles (CS/PLA nanofibers) can be used as a nano-delivery system to enhance ECM mineralization and promote bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs) osteogenic differentiation.55 The average diameter of nanofibers is 200 nm. CS nanoparticles have an average diameter of around 50 nm. Through adding CS nanoparticles, the fiber composite’s stress, strain at break, and Young’s modulus are increased, allowing it to resist masticatory forces without sacrificing regeneration space. The adjusted mechanical parameters of the scaffold completely match the stress threshold generated by daily mastication of human periodontal tissue (the local pressure of daily masticatory force in the periodontal area is about 0.1–0.3MPa,56 and the Young’s modulus of the modified composite nanofiber can reach 2–3MPa, which can fully maintain the structural integrity of the scaffold and the predetermined regeneration space under continuous clinical masticatory pressure without collapse and deformation. Additionally, by increasing the expression of osteogenic marker genes such as osteopontin (OPN), collagen I (COL-1), osteocalcin (OCn), and bone sialoprotein (BSP).

Nanomicelles

A common natural polyphenol named chlorogenic acid (CGA) has potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Emerging evidence suggests its potential benefits in alleviating periodontal tissue inflammation. Nevertheless, its clinical use is hampered by low bioavailability and systemic side effects from systemic exposure. Li57 developed CGA-loaded PLGA nanomicelles modified with polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) (CGA-PLGA@PVP) to accomplish localized sustained release of CGA. CGA-PLGA@PVP nanomicelles reduced the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, extended CGA release, and effectively scavenged ROS in LPS-stimulated murine macrophage cells (RAW264.7 cells). Additionally, CGA-PLGA@PVP nanomicelles injected locally maintained drug concentrations in periodontal tissues for more than 24 hours, restricting bone loss in mice, suppressing alveolar bone resorption, significantly reducing inflammatory responses, and delaying the development of periodontitis.

Vesicles

The nanoscale hollow spheres known as polymer vesicles (PVs) have hydrophilic cores and hydrophobic membranes, enabling co-loading of hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs. In oral applications, PVs exhibit mucoadhesion via electrostatic interactions with the gingival epithelium, whereas their vesicular structure and nanoscale size facilitate cellular internalization.58 Li designed a bifunctional PV (PV/Ag@IL-4).59 IL-4 was encapsulated in the hydrophilic core during self-assembly utilizing polystyrene-block-polyacrylic acid, an amphiphilic block copolymer (PS-b-PAA) into vesicles. Carboxyl groups on the vesicle exterior electrostatically adsorbed silver ions. PV/Ag@IL-4 showed excellent biocompatibility. In vitro experiments revealed that macrophages secreted considerably less M1-related cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and more M2-related cytokines (IL-10, ARG1) compared to other groups. Supernatants from macrophages treated with PV/Ag@IL-4 promoted osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, demonstrating osteoimmunomodulatory effects that prevent inflammatory alveolar bone resorption.

Gingival mesenchymal stem cells (GMSCs) generate extracellular vesicles (EVs), which are used in conventional cell-based therapies (GMSC-EVs), that mimic parental cell therapeutic effects but suffer from rapid diffusion away from delivery sites and short half-lives. Zarubova60 engineered a dual-delivery system that uses polymeric microparticles that release antibiotics to prevent the growth of biofilms. GMSC-EVs were rendered immobile on microparticle surfaces via MMP-sensitive linkers, enabling targeted release at inflamed sites for localized immunomodulation. Yu designed a customizable, multifunctional 3D-printed module using myeloid-derived suppressor cell vesicles (MDSCs-MVs) and PDLSCs loaded into glycidyl methacrylate-modified poly-L-lysine (EPL-GMA) (EPL-GMA/PDLSCs/MDSCs-MVs, EPM) to repair bone defects triggered by periodontitis. In vitro, EPM exhibited inherent antibacterial properties against periodontal pathogens. In a rat model of periodontal bone defects, EPM scaffolds demonstrated excellent in vivo anti-inflammatory and bone regenerative effects.61

Niosomes are multilamellar vesicles that have an aqueous core surrounded by amphiphilic and non-ionic surfactants, which are capable of loading both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. Mohammadi et al prepared clindamycin-loaded niosomes having an average particle size of 243.03 nm. These niosomes were further formulated into a thermosensitive gel. The clindamycin-loaded niosome gel showed similar effectiveness to positive controls against oral pathogens, including Candida albicans and Staphylococcus aureus (cheese isolate).62

Liposomes

Vesicles composed of one or more lipid bilayers are called liposomes, which primarily consist of phospholipids, cholesterol, and water. These bilayered phospholipid membrane structures were initially identified as “liposomes” by Bangham and Horne in 1964.63 Shi64 developed a liposomal system loaded with resveratrol (Lipo-RSV) for periodontitis treatment. The formulation demonstrated excellent biocompatibility and reprogrammed the M1 phenotype of inflammatory macrophages to M2. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a 14% increase in the M2-like subset (F4/80⁺CD206⁺) and a 8% decrease in the M1-like subset (F4/80⁺CD86⁺) following Lipo-RSV treatment. Lari and associates employed self-assembled CXCR3 antagonist nanoparticles (AMG-487 NPs) made of cholesterol and palmitic acid to prevent osteoclastogenesis.65 Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining results demonstrated that AMG-487-loaded NPs liposome treatment reduced osteoclastogenesis. One week following administration, micro-CT reconstruction analysis verified that the level of bone loss had decreased by 27.8%.

Dendrimers

A dendrimer was made up of three components: intermediate repeating units, terminal groups, and a core, exhibiting high geometric symmetry.66 Due to their hyperbranched structure (hydrophobic core and hydrophilic exterior), dendrimers serve as unique drug delivery carriers, enabling sustained drug release at specific sites to achieve therapeutic effects.67

Polyamidoamine (PAMAM) is one of the cationic dendrimers,68 which is a spherical, hyperbranched, extremely symmetric nanomacromolecule.

It exhibits biodegradability, biocompatibility, low immunogenicityand good water solubility, biodegradability, and biocompatibility. Huang69 used cationic PAMAM dendrimers (PAMAM-G3) to coat selenium-doped hydroxyapatite nanoparticles (SeHANs) to create cationic nanoparticles G3@SeHANs. In vitro, G3@SeHANs reduced inflammation associated with periodontitis by scavenging cfDNA. Compared to bare SeHANs, G3@SeHANs showed a stronger ability to inhibit TLR activation and scavenge anionic TLR agonists like DNA. G3@SeHANs and PAMAM-G3 in RAW 264.7 cells produced by patients with periodontitis significantly decreased the elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-6. In a ligature-induced periodontitis (LIP) mouse model, G3@SeHANs decreased inflammatory bone loss. Wang developed the DSPE-PEG-PAMAM/ALA/Mino dual-pH and enzyme-responsive nano-delivery system for three-mode synergistic therapy.70 PAMAM is used to induce drug release and is sensitive to pH. Under the serious conditions of periodontitis, DSPE-PEG-PAMAM/ALA/Mino can intelligently release ALA and Mino. DSPE-PEG-PAMAM/ALA/Mino suppresses the inflammatory process by reducing the excessive production of ROS and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). Mechanistic investigations have also shown that DSPE-PEG-PAMAM/ALA/Mino increases OCn and ALP mRNA expression, encouraging BMMSCs to differentiate into osteoblasts.

Subsequent research unexpectedly showed that decreased osteoclast activity and bone loss accompanied the relief of local periodontitis in mice. Under diabetic conditions, the inflammatory microenvironment and dental plaque are both acidic (pH 4.5–6.5).71 Numerous studies have shown that PAMAM exhibits structural loosening under weakly acidic conditions and has pH sensitivity.72 Wang70 developed DPPLM nanoparticles, a unique dual-responsive nanocarrier system that responds to both pH and lipase, which is composed of two components: Minocycline hydrochloride (Mino), a broad-spectrum antibiotic used in therapeutic settings, is contained in a hydrophilic dendrimer shell (PAMAM), and a-lipoic acid-loaded hydrophobic lipid core (DSPE-PEG). When compared to the untreated group in a diabetic rat model of periodontitis, an average of 0.357 mm was decreased for the distance between the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) and alveolar bone crest (ABC) (CEJ-ABC) on the palatal side of the maxillary first molar in the DPPLM group, suggesting its inhibitory effect on periodontal destruction and DPPLM has a beneficial effect on postponing alveolar bone loss.

Carbon Dots

Carbon dots (Cd) are a new and important member of the carbon family. They have minimal toxicity, good water solubility, biocompatibility, and environmental friendliness. Additionally, Cd exhibits fluorescence, small size, and molecular targeting ability, making it a novel nanomaterial with broad application prospects.73,74 Liu found that the acetyl-L-cysteine-derived carbonized polymer dots prepared by this team can co-localize with mitochondria and specifically concentrate in periodontal tissues, promoting their antioxidant therapeutic effects.75

Melatonin (MT) has potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory qualities.76 Due to its limited water solubility and poor bioavailability, the therapeutic effect of MT is still unsatisfactory. Xin prepared melatonin carbon dots (MT-Cds) with good water solubility. In vitro experiments, compared with MT, MT-Cds are capable of maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, regulating intracellular ROS, and preventing the synthesis of inflammatory mediators. The mechanism may be that MT-Cds scavenges ROS by regulating the nuclear factor erythroid-related factor 2/heme oxygenase-1 (NRF2/HO-1) pathway, which prevents cell damage and inflammatory factor production. Furthermore, an investigation using a mouse model of periodontitis revealed that MT-Cds considerably decreased inflammation and osteoclast activation, delayed the deterioration of alveolar bone, and contributed to the regeneration of damaged tissues.77

Six-Dimensional Analysis of Organic Nanoparticles for Periodontitis Therapy

Inorganic Nanoparticles

Glass Nanoparticles

Bioactive glass (BG) is an inorganic substance that adheres strongly to cells and protein molecules and has a significant specific surface area. It promotes the surface deposition of bone-like apatite. in a bodily fluid environment and has good biocompatibility.105

Hu developed composite icariin-loaded BGNs (IR-BGNs) with tissue regeneration, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory properties through loading icariin onto bioactive glass nanoparticles (BGNs).106 The diameter of the particles was measured to be between 20 and 60 nm. Cell experiments showed that IR-BGNs were non-cytotoxic. At 8 and 24 hours, the IR-BGN group had significantly higher RAW-264.7 cell migration rates than the control group. Anti-inflammatory effects of IR-BGNs on RAW264.7 cells were better than those of using irisin and BGNs alone. IR-BGNs may affect macrophage polarization and the release of growth factors, significantly reducing the in-vitro induced immune response of macrophages. In comparison to the negative control, human periodontal ligament cells (hPDLCs) induced by IR-BGNs expressed more markers related to osteogenesis, ALP activity, and mineralization ability. Yang prepared a mesoporous bioactive glass loaded with Quercetin (Quercetin/MBG). This mesoporous nano-drug delivery system altered the bone immune microenvironment by reprogramming macrophages via the miR-21a-5p/PDCD4/NF-κB pathway and restoring the osteogenic/angiogenic potential of PDLSCs.107

Metallic Nanoparticles

The abuse of antibiotics to treat periodontitis has drawn more attention due to the issue of bacterial resistance. Metallic nanoparticles have become an ideal choice for inhibiting the growth of various microorganisms due to their great antibacterial properties and huge specific surface area.

Compared with antibiotics, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) have special physical characteristics, including producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), directly interacting with the phospholipid bilayer, and the ability to bind to cytoplasmic proteins, which results in significant antibacterial action.108 Zhang studied the potential of AuNPs to encourage PDLCs to differentiate into osteoblasts.109 Chloroauric acid was the raw ingredient used in the chemical reduction process to produce AuNPs. The surface of AuNPs was modified by L/d-cystine. L-Cys-AuNPs increased the expression of osteogenic genes, such as RUNX2, OCn, ALP, and COL-1, and stimulated the development of human PDLCs. A significant amount of newly generated alveolar bone and periodontal ligament was observed in a rat model of periodontal deficiency treated with L-Cys-AuNPs. Metallic nanoclusters are materials having antibacterial properties that have a precise and simple structural composition and an extremely small size (~2 nm). Using a simple one-pot approach, researchers attempted to develop ultra-small gold nanoclusters (AuNCs), an ultra-small structure composed of 18 sulfate ligands and 25 gold atoms.110 AuNCs are approximately spherical, uniformly distributed, well-dispersed, and have a particle size between 1.5 and 4.0 nm. The findings demonstrated that AuNCs considerably inhibited the growth of Fn and damaged the integrity of the cell wall at a concentration of 0.2 mM. Treatment with AuNCs seriously harmed the cell membrane and may have prevented the growth of bacteria by depolarizing the membrane. After culturing with AuNCs, the membrane potential decreased, and the red/green fluorescence ratio decreased by around 50%, indicating that AuNCs treatment can disrupt the biofilm induced by Fn and alleviate periodontitis and bone loss.

AgNPs can enter the interior of bacteria and continuously dissolve and release silver ions.111,112 Rams113 experimentally confirmed that silver diamine fluoride (SDF) has in vitro antibacterial activity against possible pathogens in severe periodontal lesions, can inhibit the pathogens of the red-orange complex in subgingival biofilms, and selectively retains streptococci associated with periodontal health, suggesting potential new applications of SDF in the management of human periodontal infections.

Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) have been thoroughly studied since they have their remarkable antifungal, antibacterial, and photoelectrochemical characteristics.114 Drug carriers were chosen using polycaprolactone (PCL) nanofibers to load zinc oxide nanoparticles and oxytetracycline hydrochloride (PCL-OTCz).115 Actinomyces aggregans, Prevotella intermedia, Treponema, and Pg were the four Gram-negative anaerobic bacterial species chosen for mixed culture. Compared with oxytetracycline hydrochloride and zinc oxide nanoparticles, PCL-OTCz has stronger antibacterial activity.

Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) have good biodegradability and an inherent superparamagnetic effect. They have immunomodulatory qualities, can encourage the osteogenic development of certain mesenchymal stem cells, and show promise as biocompatible nanomaterials for PDLSC modification. Xia116 used SPIONs to modify human periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSC-SPIONs). PDLSCs treated with 250 μg·mL−1 ferulic acid glyceride-like SPIONs demonstrated greater osteogenic differentiation ability and better cell viability when compared to unmodified PDLSCs. Magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (MNPs) are biocompatible and exhibit minimal cytotoxicity. MNPs can be fixed near the target, penetrate the biofilm, destroy the EPS, and ultimately eradicate the biofilm by concentrating the magnetic field. Tong117 prepared Fe3O4 magnetic nanoparticles loaded with minocycline (FPM NPs) by a co-precipitation method, which have good biocompatibility and strong anti-biofilm activity. By targeting and delivering antibiotics into the biofilm to kill bacteria, sub-periodontal injection of FPM nanoparticles in conjunction with a magnetic field can successfully transfer magnetic nano-drugs to the deep part of the periodontal pocket to alleviate periodontal inflammation.

Photothermal Nanoparticles

PTT converts light energy into thermal energy using photothermal agents. When exposed to near-infrared (NIR) laser radiation, producing local hyperthermia (usually >55°C) to disrupt the integrity of pathogens and kill bacteria.118 Compared with traditional antibiotic therapy, PTT is non-invasive, controllable, has broad-spectrum antibacterial ability, and strong penetration into periodontal tissues. Moreover, it is less likely to cause drug resistance in bacteria that cause periodontitis, which has attracted extensive attention from researchers.119,120 Lin synthesized an NIR-responsive photothermal material, Au nanobipyramids (Au NBPs).121 The surface of Au NBPs was modified using mesoporous silica (mSiO2) loaded with minocycline. A hybrid hydrogel (gel MA-AuNBPs@SiO2) was designed by combining Au NBPs@SiO2 with gelatin methacrylate. Under NIR irradiation, the antibacterial rate of gel MA-Au NBPs@SiO2 against Pg increases to 66.7% with increasing NIR irradiation power when compared to non-NIR irradiation.

With the continuous exploration of PTT in antibacterial applications, some emerging problems limit the potential of PTT in periodontal therapy. First, the short-term antibacterial effect of PTT cannot fully combat unique periodontal pathogens. Deep-seated pathogens in periodontal pockets that are not eliminated face the risk of reactivation and proliferation. Second, the antibacterial temperature of PTT. High temperatures may cause tissue damage, while reducing the temperature to a safe range may hinder antibacterial activity. Studies have found that mild PTT at an appropriate temperature (about 40–45°C), supplemented with additional persistent antibacterial components to ensure antibacterial performance, is expected to be an effective and safe periodontal antibacterial method. Li118 constructed a multifunctional photothermal nanoparticle (BR@C3G-Cu) by using free bilirubin as a raw material and applying a copper-ion-coordinated cyanidin-3-O-glucoside metal-polyphenol network (MPN) to bilirubin nanoparticles. Under safe and mild conditions, the synergistic effect of metal-dependent functions and the photothermal conversion properties of MPN helps to effectively and persistently eliminate periodontal pathogens, satisfying the various antibacterial needs of periodontitis. In addition, BR@C3G-Cu nanoparticles assisted by mild hyperthermia can change the polarization balance of macrophages by scavenging ROS and blocking the cGAS-STING pathway, thereby improving the periodontal pro-inflammatory bone immune microenvironment and reducing the osteogenic damage of periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs). In a mouse periodontitis model, BR@C3G-Cu nanoparticles exposed to NIR radiation can inhibit alveolar bone resorption and promote periodontal tissue remodeling.

PDT in the treatment of periodontitis uses a photosensitizer that generates ROS under light stimulation, disrupting the fragile lipid membrane and causing rapid oxidation of lipids in bacteria.122,123 Because of its high singlet oxygen output, great tissue penetration, and superior biocompatibility, chlorin e6 (Ce6) is frequently utilized in PDT treatment.124,125 Since the photosensitizer Ce6 is highly hydrophobic and cannot be effectively absorbed by periodontal pathogens, by promoting the interaction between Ce6 and the negatively charged cell wall, penetration, and cell membrane, Li developed a nanosystem to increase its bacterial adsorption and solubility.126 Ce6 was initially hydrophilically modified by combining it with the cationic cell-penetrating peptide TAT peptide (TAT-Ce6). After that, TAT-Ce6 was loaded with tinidazole (TDZ) to create self-assembling nanoparticles (TAT-Ce6/TDZ NPs). TAT-Ce6/TDZ NPs have a more compact internal structure and a regular spherical form, according to TEM pictures. The positively charged TAT peptide’s surface dispersion is confirmed by TAT-Ce6/TDZ NPs, which have a zeta potential of about + 40.1 mV and a particle size of around 146.2 nm. Following one hour of TAT-Ce6 therapy, Pg has a zeta potential that rises from −11.2 to +5.86 mV. Through the TAT peptide, TAT-Ce6 significantly improves the penetration of bacterial cell membranes. TAT-Ce6 and TAT-Ce6/TDZ NPs exhibit more antibacterial activity because they are more effectively absorbed by plaque biofilms through the TAT peptide.

Traditional PDT can produce a lot of ROS to destroy infections. However, the increase in ROS during antibacterial treatment may exert oxidative stress in periodontal pockets and cause irreparable damage to surrounding tissues. As a two-dimensional material, black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNs) have a folded layered structure where each phosphorus atom is covalently bound to three neighboring phosphorus atoms in the phosphorus monolayer. Nanoscale black phosphorus films exhibit a double-layer structure folded along the Z-axis in contrast to typical 2D nanomaterials. This folded structure gives BPNs a higher specific surface area, enabling them to effectively act as antioxidants to eliminate ROS. Li127 used BPNs, which can scavenge ROS to produce an antimicrobial effect while removing the adverse effects of ROS-based PDT. After BPN treatment, the levels of TNF-α, TGF-β, Arg-1, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-β mRNA expression were significantly reduced, confirming the in vitro anti-inflammatory effect of this nanomaterial. In addition, recurrent attacks caused by immune system disorders can also damage surrounding tissues.

Mesoporous Material Nanoparticles

Currently, mesoporous calcium silicate nanoparticles (MCSN) and Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN) are two popular inorganic nanocarrier systems.128,129 Tan130 designed a drug carrier system of resveratrol-grafted mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN-RSV). The induction of MSN can eliminate the inherent cytotoxicity of respiratory syncytial virus to RAW 264.7 and improve the bioavailability of RSV. RSV and MSN can hinder the polarization of M1 and activate the M2 phenotype by positively regulating macrophage polarization, thus improving the regulatory function of the drug carrier. It can also improve diabetic periodontitis (DP) related to the regulation of macrophage polarization. The mechanism might be connected to the SIRT1/AMPK and NF-κB signaling pathways. Although it has no discernible impact on blood-glucose-related markers, local treatment of MSN-RSV as an adjuvant can also considerably lower insulin resistance (IR) in diabetic rats to a considerable degree. Since Au nanoparticles (Au NPs) require a smaller size and a high catalytic rate. Deng produced a carbon monoxide (CO)-enhanced multi-enzyme synergistic hybrid nano-platform (MSN-Au@CO) by coating ultra-small Au NPs in the form of multi-nanoenzymes on MSN and loading manganese carbonyl (MnCO). By catalyzing the oxidation of glucose into gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), Au NPs’ glucose oxidase-like activity kills bacteria by converting H2O2 into hydroxyl radicals (·OH) via peroxidase-like activity. In LPS-challenged macrophages, CO generated by the H2O2 reaction and Au NPs shows a synergistic anti-inflammatory effect. The underlying process may involve inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway to lower the inflammatory response and stimulating NRF2 to lower ROS.131

Hydroxyapatite-Based Nanocarriers

As the main component of dental tissue and bone mineral, nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA) is characterized by its compatibility with the physiological environment. This non-toxic material, which is highly compatible with dental/bone tissue, is frequently utilized as an ingredient of fillers and supplements for bone repair. Wang132 developed an nHA in which a polycaprolactone-co-lactide (PCLLA) elastomer matrix included hydroxyapatite particles that make up traditional hydroxyapatite bone powder. Hydrogen bonds are formed by terminal hydroxyl groups on PCLLA arms and many surface hydroxyl groups on HA, which help to create a cross-linked network. In vitro, PCLLA-NanoHA increased macrophage polarization to the M2 phenotype. In vivo, PCLLA-NanoHA promoted alveolar bone repair like that of standard HA bone powder. In diabetic mice, PCLLA-NanoHA performed better at promoting bone tissue repair than the widely used HA bone powder. PCLLA-NanoHA regulates the M2 polarization of macrophages via activating the PI3K/AKT and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR) signaling pathways. As a result, a favorable local immunological milieu is created for bone regeneration and repair.

Six-Dimensional Analysis of Nanocomposites for Periodontitis Therapy

Nanocomposites

Nanozymes

With the latest advancements in nanomaterials, nanozymes have shown promising potential in metabolic regulation as enzyme mimics with adjustable catalytic activity. Nanoparticles doped with cerium (Ce), iron (Fe), platinum (Pt), manganese (Mn), and their oxides have been shown to play beneficial roles in infections, bacterial metabolic disorders, tumors, and neurodegenerative diseases because their metal active centers can mimic the redox processes catalyzed by natural enzymes.152 The primary biological mechanism by which activated macrophages eliminate phagocytosed microbes is the generation of ROS.177 However, too much ROS may cause macrophages to shift to the M1 phenotype, which would worsen inflammation.178 Cerium oxide (CeO2) is a nanozyme with catalase-like and superoxide dismutase-like properties that can efficiently scavenge H2O2 and O2•− by changing between Ce3⁺ (oxidized) and Ce3⁺ (reduced) forms.179 The CeO2 nanoparticles synthesized by Yu151 have activities similar to CAT and SOD and the capacity to scavenge •OH, which, in the inflammatory microenvironment, can scavenge ROS. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments revealed that CeO2 nanoparticles can reduce excessive ROS caused by LPS. By blocking the MAPK-NF-κB signaling pathway, CeO2 nanoparticles can also reduce inflammatory factors. Additionally, CeO2 nanoparticles have been shown to drastically reduce osteoclast activity, alveolar bone resorption, and the inflammatory response in a rat periodontitis model, all of which aid in the healing of injured tissues.

A mitochondrial complex biomimetic nanozyme (MCBN) composed of MnO2 polycrystalline particles coated with TPP, PEG, and bovine serum albumin (BSA) was developed by Ren.152 It has a nano-scale size and the biological characteristics of natural complexes. The prepared MCBN can target the mitochondrial complex of inflammatory macrophages, absorb excess electrons in the electron transport chain (ETC), and convert the electrons to decompose H2O2. By transforming existing free radicals, reducing the burst of ROS and ATP, and inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway and the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, MCBN efficiently suppresses the activation of M1 macrophages and the release of inflammatory factors. It also demonstrated markedly decreased alveolar bone loss and excellent inflammatory control in a ligature-induced periodontitis mouse model.

Nanofibers

Novel antibacterial nanomaterials, like nanofibers, have been included in combined treatment approaches for antibacterial action and periodontal tissue regeneration in recent years. Zinc ions and 2-methylimidazole make up Zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8). ZIF-8 produces antibiotic properties by releasing Zn2⁺ and 2-methylimidazole in the acidic microenvironment of bacterial infections. Sun et al developed a multifunctional delivery system (PPZF JNF) that contained FK506 and ZIF-8 nanoparticles encapsulated in nanofibers.153 The nanofibers were fabricated via electrospinning using PCL and PVP as raw materials. After PPZF JNF treatment, Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli levels were significantly reduced. In BMSCs, osteogenic gene expression levels (RUNX2, ALP, and OCN) were increased. The CEJ-ABC distance in periodontitis-affected rats decreased by 21.8%. Additionally, bone volume/total volume and trabecular number increased by 25.7% and 34.5%, respectively, while trabecular separation decreased by 0.16-fold.

Electrospinning is a versatile method for producing tissue engineering scaffolds, producing nanofibers that mimic the ECM with excellent biocompatibility. He154 used surface-directed epitaxial crystallization to prepare Electrospun nanofibers made of strongly oriented, periodic lamellar poly(ε-caprolactone). In vitro studies, PLN was able to precisely regulate macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. PLN-polarized macrophages markedly increased BMSC migration and osteogenic differentiation. The activation of Yes-associated protein (YAP), which in turn relied on the capacity of YAP to directly oppose the NF-κB signaling pathway and bind IKK-α, was necessary for the topographic signals regulating macrophage polarization. Subsequently, the same research team created TS@BMP-2, a hierarchically constructed nanofiber scaffold with a core containing bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) and a nanoimprinted sheath. A BMP-2/PVA core and a PCL sheath were coaxially electrospun to generate the scaffold, which was then surface-directed epitaxially crystallized to produce cyclic nanolamellar relief on the sheath. In vivo experiments showed that the TS@BMP-2 treatment group significantly alleviated inflammatory conditions in periodontal tissues of C57/BL mice with periodontitis, demonstrating how local cues can effectively reduce inflammation. At four weeks, micro-CT images showed that the TS@BMP-2 group had higher collagen content and denser periodontal ligament structures than the control group. Furthermore, the TS@BMP-2 group had fewer osteoclasts, which improved the conditions for periodontal bone repair.155

Nanoparticle Gels

Hydrogel systems with antibacterial and bone immunomodulatory effects represent a potential approach to the regeneration of periodontal tissue. They avoid the disadvantages of applying drugs directly, such as short half-lives180 and overlapping side-effects.181 Wang158 developed PF-127@DOX/ALG-MS@PA, a thermosensitive composite hydrogel composed of alginate microspheres and Pluronic F-127 (PF-127). The composite hydrogel can be injected locally because it has high gelling capabilities at body temperature and excellent dimensional flexibility in the liquid state. In an inflammatory environment, the composite hydrogel stimulates MC3T3-E1 cell osteogenesis and induces RAW264.7 cells to transform from the M1 to the M2 phenotype. According to in vivo studies, the composite hydrogel stimulates the growth of new bone in an SD rat model of periodontitis. The thermosensitive composite hydrogel PF-127@DOX/ALG-MS@PA, fabricated from Pluronic F-127 and alginate microspheres, enables temporally programmed dual-drug delivery. Doxycycline (DOX) undergoes rapid burst release within 0–24 h to eradicate pathogenic bacteria and suppress acute inflammation; alginate microspheres sustain drug release from day 3 to day 14 for immunomodulatory effects; starting from week 3, proanthocyanidins (PA) exert long-term pro-angiogenic effects to facilitate alveolar bone regeneration.

Wang159 coupled a C10 fatty acid with a recently created peptide KR8 (KRIWQRIK), a newly synthesized peptide, with a C10 fatty acid to construct C10-KR8@ZIF-8 (a zeolite imidazole framework-8 loaded with C10-KR8) nanoparticles, and developed an injectable gelatin-methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel incorporating C10-KR8@ZIF-8 nanoparticles for the continuous release of zinc and the C10-KR8 peptide. The pH-responsive hydrogel has a strong capacity for bone repair through the synergistic action of fostering osteogenesis in diseased circumstances and creating a dynamic immunomodulatory environment. It promotes the osteogenic differentiation of BMMSCs by enhancing the nuclear localization of YAP and activating the focal adhesion kinase/phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/AKT (FAK/PI3K/AKT) pathway.

Patients with DP face challenges in achieving complete periodontal tissue regeneration due to excessive oxidative reactions, immunoregulatory imbalance, and compromised mesenchymal stem cell performance. Fang163 used BSA nanoparticles to develop a polyphenol-mediated redox-active alginate/gelatin hydrogel known as BNP-PEDOT-PSF-AG hydrogel. It couples gas signals and endogenous electrical signals using an H2S sustained-release device and a conductive poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-assembled PDA-mediated silk microfiber network (PEDOT-PSF). The hydrogel has strong conductivity due to the poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-assembled PDA-mediated silk microfibers, which allow it to boost calcium ion influx, facilitate cell alignment, and transfer endogenous bioelectricity. Furthermore, the synergistic action of hydrogen sulfide gas-bioelectricity coupling stimulates bone production by controlling macrophage polarization through lipid metabolism regulation and increasing the autophagy of periodontal ligament stem cells.

Nanocomposite Membranes

Guided Bone Regeneration (GBR) mediated by barrier membranes has been recognized as an effective approach for repairing several kinds of bone defects, such as the alveolar bone.182 Membranes provide room for the formation of new bone by acting as physical barriers that stop soft tissue from developing into bone deficiencies. Most clinical barrier membranes, however, currently available (eg, Bio-Gide collagen membrane), only serve as space-makers and cannot effectively promote bone tissue regeneration.183 Liu167 used polylactic acid (PLA) and hydroxyapatite nanowires (HAP NWs) composites loaded with manganese dioxide nanosheets (MnO2 NSs) to create a barrier membrane for GBR. The HP@Mn membrane features asymmetric component distribution (PLA on one side and HAP@MnO2 on the other). Leveraging the catalytic activity of MnO2 nanozymes, these membranes efficiently decompose H2O2 into O2. Experiments conducted in vitro showed that HP@Mn membranes prevented H2O2-induced macrophage apoptosis, reduced intracellular oxidative stress, downregulated overexpression of inflammation-related genes/proteins, and suppressed M1 macrophage polarization. Nano-hydroxyapatite endowed osteogenic differentiation potential, while MnO2 NSs exerted a cooperative impact. Additionally, the barrier function played by PLA inhibited excessive proliferation of periodontal epithelial cells, establishing an environment that promotes the regeneration of periodontal tissue.

Other Treatment Strategies in Periodontal NDDS

TLR4, a key receptor for recognizing bacterial endotoxins in innate immunity, is essential for triggering immunological reactions during periodontitis.184 Through TLR4, innate immune cells identify endotoxins produced by initiate inflammatory signaling pathways and bacteria in the microenvironment. Furthermore, by attaching to endotoxins on the bacterial surface via TLR4, they can recognize and collect bacteria.185 Thus, TLR4 in immune-activated lesions represents a novel target for periodontitis treatment. Deng168 created a nanoplatform that expresses TLR4 and has biomimetic cell membrane characteristics. RAW264.7 cells with high TLR4 expression were created. via genetic engineering, and their cell membranes were extracted. After that, the functionalized membranes were manually extruded to cover the surface of minocycline-loaded silica nanospheres (SNs), forming the nanodelivery system MSNCs. MSNCs demonstrated excellent targeting ability in in vitro experiments toward E. coli and effectively released antibiotics to kill bacteria. Moreover, they neutralized endotoxins in the microenvironment and prevented macrophage inflammatory activation. MSNCs significantly reduced periodontal tissue inflammation and alveolar bone loss in a ligature-induced periodontitis mouse model, suggesting their promise as a novel approach for immunomodulation and targeted antibacterial therapy. In periodontitis, macrophage recruitment reduces the expression of microRNA-126 (miR-126). Cell Membrane-Camouflaged Nanodecoys (CNDs) are emerging delivery platforms with inherent biocompatibility. Lin169 developed multifunctional CNDs with targeted microbial toxicity and immunomodulatory capabilities for periodontitis treatment. A cascade catalytic system that combines hollow manganese dioxide (hMnO2) and L-amino acid oxidase (LAAO) was constructed. Mutual enhancement of catalytic efficiency accelerated O2 production in hypoxic microenvironments, disrupting metabolic processes of periodontal pathogens. Additionally, LAAO-hMnO2 CNDs scavenged overexpressed ROS, rebalanced the inflammatory microenvironment, and promoted M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization. Nanodecoys triggered nuclear translocation of NRF2 to reduce oxidative stress responses and rewire macrophage polarization, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation. In vivo, alveolar bone regeneration was significantly enhanced following treatment.

Key Challenges in Clinical Translation

In order to achieve the objectives of reducing inflammation and encouraging tissue regeneration, nanotherapy has become a viable approach in the last ten years to modify the periodontal microenvironment and reestablish immunological balance. Although many nanotherapeutic agents have demonstrated immunomodulatory, antibacterial, and regenerating qualities in animal models and in vitro, unfortunately, most current research remains in the preclinical stage. The use of nanoparticles to treat periodontitis has been the focus of several clinical studies. Only less than 20 clinical trials of nano-formulations for periodontitis treatment can be retrieved on ClinicalTrials.gov (Supplementary Material Table S1), such as “Perforated Collagen Membrane With Nano-hydroxyapatite for Intrabony Defects”, “Evaluation of Nano Bioactive Glass Combined With i - PRF Scaffold in Vital Pulp Treatments”, “Locally Delivered Whey Protein Nanoparticles Gel in Stage II Grade B Periodontitis”, “Antimicrobial Photodynamic Therapy With ICG-Loaded Nanoparticles for Periodontitis Management”, “Efficacy of Local Dox Gel Delivery Versus Dox Nanoparticles Prepared Gel in The Treatment of Stage II Periodontitis”, “Clinical and Biochemical Effect of Atorvastatin in Nano Particles Gel in Periodontitis Treatment”, and “Efficacy of Nano - Curcumin and Nano-Hyaluronic Acid as an Adjunct to Scaling and Root Planing in the Treatment of Periodontitis”. With these clinical trials, only two of them have publicly uploaded clinical results. This suggests that the shift from academic achievement to clinical application presents some difficulties.

First, the drug release concentration needs to be higher than the minimum inhibitory concentration in order to prevent bacterial growth utilizing nanoparticles or drug-loaded nanoparticles. To guarantee antibacterial efficacy during treatment, drug release rate and concentration must be controlled via nanomaterials. Despite the fact that several studies have evaluated the dispersing effect of nanoparticles on biofilms, clinical trials have not confirmed this effect.18

Second, the biosafety, toxicity, and long-term in vivo behavior of nanomaterials are crucial. When nanomaterials are applied to periodontal tissues, they must adhere to biosafety regulations to guarantee that they do not damage healthy tissue structures. Nanoparticles must have strong bioabsorbable qualities to be used in periodontal tissue regeneration. Patients may require a second procedure to remove any leftover material if the substance itself does not break down, which prolongs their suffering and recuperation period. To systematically assess chronic risks induced by nanoparticle accumulation in vital organs, multiple imaging techniques have been established to monitor long-term excretion and visceral retention of nanocarriers for chronic toxicity evaluation. Optical imaging represented by the IVIS small-animal in vivo fluorescence system utilizes NIR luminescent nanoparticles to dynamically track particle retention and clearance over long experimental windows.186 X-ray-based modalities including CT and SPECT enable quantitative tracing of radiolabeled nanocarriers; for instance, radionuclides such as Cu can be incorporated into gold nanoparticles for long-term biodistribution testing and semi-quantitative measurement derived from imaging signals.187 Supplementary monitoring tools include MRI, MPI and XPCS. In line with the Technical Guidelines for Nonclinical Safety Evaluation of Nanomedicines (Trial) issued by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), researchers are recommended to label nanoparticles with fluorescent or radioactive tracers, and utilize platforms such as IVIS and SPECT for in vivo tracking and semi-quantitative analysis of imaging readouts. Despite advances in monitoring methodologies, long-term toxicological profiles of most nanocarriers remain unclear. Therefore, it is necessary to study the long-term absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion behavior of NPs in the human body and conduct a thorough investigation of the potential toxicity of nanoparticles. This may be achieved by incorporating natural elements and optimizing degradation pathways. Understanding the mechanism of action of NPs and improving therapies will be made easier with the development of reliable strategies to monitor their distribution and assess their impact on the body.

Third, tissue regeneration, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial defense are all part of the cascade process that treats periodontitis. One-stage intervention could interfere with other stages. This complexity requires nano-therapy to have spatio-temporal and stage-specific regulation, including the late-stage resolution of tissue regeneration and inflammation, the mid-term recruitment of stem/progenitor cells, and the early removal of pathogens and necrotic tissues.188 For example, excessive host inflammation is linked to the degradation of periodontal tissue. By offering potent medication components, NPs can reduce this persistent inflammation and encourage tissue healing. A mild inflammatory reaction can encourage neovascularization, boost the local immune system, aid wound healing, and remove waste from the injured area.189 However, both the absence and excess of inflammation can have adverse effects on tissues.190 Therefore, creating and sustaining an ideal “ideal” level in the tissue environment to successfully fend off foreign invasion without seriously harming normal tissue structures is a challenge. The current targeting ability of nano-therapy is limited, and active targeting is the key to improving the specificity of nano-formulations. Additionally, it is necessary to maintain the pharmacological activity of nanoparticles after binding to the target. Therefore, enhanced specificity for the immune cell subsets t and distinct microenvironment in periodontal lesions is required, and this can be achieved by stimulus-responsive design and ligand-receptor engineering.191

In addition, issues such as large-scale production processes, control of key design features, and cost optimization also need to be resolved.8 Standardized processes for large-scale mass production have been established. For organic nanoparticles, continuous microfluidic synthesis platforms enable mass production at the scale of hundreds of liters by precisely controlling parameters such as two-phase flow rates, reagent ratios, and temperature within microchannels. This approach restricts batch-to-batch variations in particle size distribution, drug loading, and surface potential to within 5%, significantly outperforming traditional batch stirring methods, which typically exhibit variability exceeding 20%.192,193 For inorganic nanoparticles, continuous hydrothermal synthesis reactor systems ensure high batch consistency by programmatically regulating heating rates, pressure, and reaction duration.194,195 Furthermore, real-time quality control is achieved through online detection technologies, such as dynamic light scattering (DLS) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), which screen and eliminate unqualified components. This integration greatly reduces unit production costs while ensuring batch uniformity.196,197 Currently, several medical nanomaterials have achieved mass production through these processes and have entered the stage of medical device registration and application.198,199

Conclusion and Future Directions

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammation triggered by subgingival pathogenic bacteria, progressively destroying periodontal supporting tissues and contributing to multiple systemic complications. Nanoparticle-based delivery systems enable localized controlled drug release, deep biofilm penetration, and targeted antibacterial action, while simultaneously modulating oxidative stress and macrophage polarization to promote periodontal tissue regeneration. These delivery systems address core limitations of conventional therapies, such as insufficient local drug concentrations, brief retention within periodontal pockets, systemic toxic reactions and emerging bacterial resistance, and they possess great translational potential for minimally invasive, targeted periodontal treatment.

However, multiple critical bottlenecks still impede their widespread clinical translation, including unclear long-term biosafety risks, variable biodegradation behaviors in acidic periodontal microenvironments, inconsistent batch-to-batch reproducibility during mass production, limited human clinical validation data, strict medical device regulatory restrictions and high manufacturing costs. The lack of quantitative evidence on periodontal retention, biofilm infiltration, and translational maturity for most nanocarrier categories represents a critical gap in current research.

Despite these challenges, we remain optimistic about the clinical prospects of periodontal nanomedicine. With sustained cross-disciplinary research continuously optimizing carrier design, biosafety, and scalable manufacturing processes, safe and effective nanoformulations are expected to become widely available in the future, transforming the treatment of chronic periodontal inflammation and offering new diagnostic and therapeutic hope for patients with periodontitis and related systemic inflammatory diseases.
