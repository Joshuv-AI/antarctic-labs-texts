Particle swarm optimization of synergetic controller and sliding-mode extreme seeking controller for wind power generation system
Authors: Abdelfatah Khatir, Abdelhak Dida, Badreddine Babes, Fahad Albalawi, Yayehyirad Ayalew Awoke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This article proposes a novel robust control strategy for Doubly Fed Induction Generator (DFIG)-based wind turbine systems by integrating Synergetic Control (SC) with Sliding Mode Extremum Seeking Control (SMESC) to enhance performance and reliability. The proposed SC–SMESC scheme replaces conventional Field-Oriented Control (FOC) with PI regulators by providing a compact and systematic formulation that ensures accurate control under both steady state and dynamic operating conditions. The architecture adopts a dual-loop design: a SC-based inner loop for high-precision active and reactive power regulation, and a SMESC-based outer loop for maximum power point tracking (MPPT). To further enhance adaptability, controller parameters are optimally tuned using a Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithm. Simulation results show that the optimized SC-SMESC strategy improves MPPT efficiency by 2%, reduces steady-state error by 77.52%, decreases settling time by 96.15%, and lowers the total harmonic distortion (THD) of stator currents to 0.63% compared to the conventional FOC-PI method. Moreover, the proposed approach exhibits strong robustness against parameter variations and external disturbances, confirming its effectiveness for high-performance wind energy conversion systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23291-6.

Introduction

The DFIG-based WTS is an electromechanical system characterized by strong nonlinearities, coupled dynamics, and intermittent, uncertain inputs. The aerodynamic behavior of the WTS is a major source of pronounced nonlinearities and uncertainties1. The goals of the WTS governor are contingent upon the turbine’s operating region, which is determined by the wind intensity2. To preserve the structural integrity of the WTS, it is advisable to limit power output and rotor velocity when wind speeds exceed the rated value. Conversely, at low wind speeds, maximizing the mechanical power collected by the turbine blades is becomes a primary objective.

Thus, the power control, actuation, and overall control objectives of the WTS are completely pure nonlinear. The wind turbine (WT) and generator are the primary parts of any WTS. They are linked by a gearbox that allows torque and rotational speed to be exchanged via a common shaft. The WT applies a motoring torque to the shaft, while the shaft velocity is governed by the generator model through an electromagnetic torque that balances the mechanical system. Figure 1 illustrates the exchange of torque and rotor velocity at the gearbox of the WTS. Additionally, a two-level, three-phase power inverter employing pulse-width modulation (PWM) is utilized to supply the rotor terminals of the DFIG.

Fig. 1Main stages and working principle of the suggested WTS equipped with DFIG.

Main stages and working principle of the suggested WTS equipped with DFIG.

The DFIG is often used as a standard for assessing the effectiveness of nonlinear control schemes since it is an induction machine with intrinsically nonlinear behavior. Due to its intermittent and time-varying input, a DFIG-based WTS must operate over a wide range of operating conditions rather than at a single operating point.

Additionally, the DFIG runs far from its normal operating point during grid voltage sags brought on by faults or load disturbances. The grid voltage may decrease sharply under such circumstances, causing sudden dynamics in the rotor and stator currents and a non-constant stator flux3. This compromises the core principle of DFIG vector control, preventing the decoupling of active and reactive power during transients in stator flux or voltage. Vector control techniques commonly employ PI regulators to manage the active and reactive power components of the electrical machine. The key problem consists in adjusting the PI controller parameters to ensure optimal performance around a designated operating point, while preserving system stability and robustness under different operating scenarios. Furthermore, the intrinsic nonlinearity of the system makes it tough to find a constant set of PI parameters capable of maintaining a stable and optimal response when the operating speed fluctuates. Moreover, utilizing a fixed set of PI parameters may not only degrade performance but can also lead to instability in critical or worst-case operating conditions4.

In the past twenty years, much research has concentrated on enhancing the static and dynamic performance of WTSs based on DFIG technology. To address issues of instability and limited robustness in DFIG-based systems, numerous advanced control strategies have been suggested over recent years. The most widely adopted methodologies include nonlinear control methods such as gain schedule controller5,6, sliding mode controller (SMC)7,8, multi-variables LQG controller9, feedback linearization controller10,11, nonlinear adaptive controller12, model reference adaptive controller13, robust H-Infinity controller14,15, predictive direct power controller16,17, intelligent fuzzy controller3, neural network controller18, and neuro-fuzzy controller19.

The SMC approach has proven highly effective in vibration suppression and in managing parameter uncertainties, making it particularly suitable for mitigating mechanical and electrical oscillations in DFIG-based WECSs. Its robustness against parameter uncertainties and external disturbances aligns closely with the control requirements for stabilizing such systems under variable operating conditions20,21.

Related works in other domains demonstrate the potential of SC techniques. For example, the work cited in reference22 illustrates the effectiveness of SC method in managing structural uncertainties and external disturbances, a challenge similar to grid disturbances and mechanical stress in wind turbines.

While advanced controllers such as SMC and SC have proven robust in wind energy systems, the specific integration of SC with SMESC remains underexplored and represents a significant gap in the literature.

To date, no comprehensive study has investigated a unified control framework that leverages the complementary strengths of SC and SMESC while simultaneously addressing the following critical aspects:

Consequently, the effectiveness, robustness, and potential performance benefits of a PSO-optimized SC-SMESC controller for DFIG-based wind turbines lack comprehensive verification, thereby presenting a clear opportunity for new impactful research in this field.

In the current work, we suggest two enhanced control methods for monitoring WTSs. The first one is a robust SC approach23–25, intended to regulate the rotor speed along with the stator active and reactive power of the DFIG. The second one is the Sliding-Mode Extreme Seeking Control (SMESC) approach26,27, which is employed for Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT). To enhance overall system performance, a computational optimization technique, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), is applied to identify the best constant parameters for both the SC and SMESC strategies28,29. Performance evaluation is conducted using standard indices like Integral Time Absolute Error (ITAE), Integral Absolute Error (IAE), and Integral Square Error (ISE), to verify the control objectives are satisfied.

Therefore, the suggested control technique allows precise tracking of the MPP without needing any previous information of the WTS. It also provides exact and stable convergence of the measured stator active and reactive power to their respective reference values.

In this study, we attempt to create a robust SC strategy for the DFIG based WTS, working in conjunction with a SMESC based MPPT regulator. Furthermore, we compare the performances of these two PSO-optimized control methods with that of the conventional Direct Field-Oriented Control (FOC) method, which uses PI controllers for DFIG power regulation and a Standard Hill Climb Search (SHCS) algorithm for MPPT control. Direct FOC of the DFIG based WTS

In DFIGs, the direct FOC strategy enables precise and decoupled control of the instantaneous stator active and reactive power. Figure 2 displays the schematic representation of the system that has to be regulated by taking into account Eqs. (7) and (8).

Three important steps must be considered when designing the FOC strategy for the DFIG. In the first step, the converter’s model can be simplified to a unity gain, as its dynamics are typically quite rapid. In the second step, the control signal will be modified to include the perturbation terms ν2 and ν3, which stand for the remaining parts of the decoupling terms. In the last step, the control system will reject the constant perturbation term ν4. As a result, the DFIG’s closed-loop control is constructed, as explained in Fig. 3, where d-axis controller Rd and q-axis controller Rq are responsible for regulating the stator active and reactive power.

Implementation of the PSO algorithm

The PSO algorithm is employed to optimally tune the SC and SMESC parameters, more specifically by identifying the best values of speed Vi and position Xi. Through an iterative search process, PSO explores the solution space and progressively converges toward the optimal set of parameters39,40.

Initialization

A swarm of particles is initialized in an s-dimensional problem space. Each particle is assigned a random initial position 10− 1≤ Xi ≤ 10 and a random initial speed 10− 2 ≤ Vi ≤ 2, where i = 1, 2,…, N. Here, N denotes the number of particles within the swarm.

Fitness function definition

To evaluate the performance of each candidate solution, a specific fitness function f(Xi, Vi) is defined. In this study, the fitness function is formulated based on the minimization of the Integral of Time-weighted Absolute Error (ITAE) criterion for SC and SMESC, and is expressed by Eq. (30):30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Minimize\:\left(ITAE\right)=Minimize\:\left({\int\:}_{0}^{t}t.\sum\:_{i=1}^{6}\left|{\Delta\:}{e}_{i}\right|dt\right),\:i=1,\:2,\dots\:,\:{N}_{max}$$\end{document} where ei represents the tracking error of the controlled system, t denotes the total evaluation time, and Nmax is the maximum number of iterations.

Particle evaluation

The current position Xi of each particle is evaluated against its personal best Xi,best using the fitness value. If Xi provides a superior fitness than Xi,best, then the personal best is updated by Eq. (31):31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{V}_{i}\left(k+1\right)=w{V}_{i}\left(k\right)+{c}_{1}{\phi\:}_{1}\left(k\right)\left|{X}_{i,best}\left(k\right)-{X}_{i}\left(k\right)\right|+{c}_{2}{\phi\:}_{2}\left(k\right)\left|{X}_{g,best}\left(k\right)-{X}_{i}\left(k\right)\right|$$\end{document} where w is the inertia weight, c1 and c2 are cognitive and social acceleration coefficients, respectively. φ1 and φ2 are variables generated from a uniform distribution in the interval (0, 1). This step ensures that each particle retains the best solution it has found so far during the optimization process.

Once Xi_best is updated, it is then compared with the global best position Xg_best, using the fitness criterion. The speed controllers (either PI or SC) respond to this disturbance by adjusting the generator’s electromagnetic torque. These two simulation tests are presented in Figs. 32 and 33, respectively. Test-6: robustness to modeling inaccuracies and measuring perturbation

Robustness tests are evaluated by perturbing system model parameters to assess control strategy performance under modeling inaccuracies. Since the PI controller is designed using TFs with fixed system parameters, its performance tends to degrade when those parameters deviate from their nominal values. However, in practical systems, these parameters are prone to deviations due to several physical phenomena such as magnetic saturation in inductors, thermal effects in resistors, and other environmental factors. Moreover, the identification of these parameters is inherently uncertain, as it depends on the accuracy of the identification techniques and the precision of the measurement devices. To assess the robustness of the suggested controllers under such faulty working conditions, specific simulation tests have been conducted and are presented in Figs. 34 and 35, and 36.

The deviation in electrical parameters of the DFIG results in a significant steady-state error in the stator power responses when using the FOC strategy with a PI controller, as depicted in Fig. 34. In contrast, the SC approach handles parameter variances well. It performs excellently in both transient and steady-state scenarios. Figure 35 displays a short impulsive oscillation at 1.8 s under machine inductance variances with SC strategy, but the PI controller exhibits significantly poorer performance with a noticeable steady-state error. Under mechanical parameter variations, the SC strategy maintains the same dynamic response as observed under nominal conditions. In contrast, with PI control strategy, the system exhibits a longer response time, accompanied by a pronounced overshoot and extended damping time. Additionally, the SC approach demonstrates effective disturbance rejection with minimal overshoot when rotor speed sensing noise is introduced at 3 s, as shown in Fig. 36, whereas the PI controller exhibits a less robust response.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated a novel, robust control scheme for DFIG-based WECSs. To address the limitations of conventional methods, a dual-loop strategy was proposed: an inner current control loop using a SC method and an outer MPPT loop employing a SMESC method. The parameters for both controllers were optimally tuned using a PSO algorithm to maximize performance. Comprehensive simulations under a wide range of operating conditions demonstrate the superiority of the proposed PSO-optimized SC-SMESC framework. The key quantitative achievements include:

Enhanced dynamic response: The PSO-optimized SC achieved a settling time of 0.0005 s for stator active power, representing a 96.15% improvement over the conventional FOC-PI controller. Furthermore, the steady-state error was reduced to 2%, a significant leap from the 15% error observed with FOC-PI control.Superior MPPT efficiency and stability: The SMESC strategy demonstrated exceptional tracking accuracy, maintaining a power coefficient (Cp) of 0.49 with minimal oscillation (± 0.01). This resulted in an outstanding average efficiency of 98% in the MPPT system, outperforming the conventional SHCS method (96%).Exceptional robustness to parameter variations: Under a + 25% variation in stator and rotor resistances, the proposed control scheme maintained stable operation with a minimal performance degradation. The power fluctuation remained below 2%, whereas the conventional FOC-PI strategy exhibited unstable operation with power oscillations exceeding 7%.Improved power quality: The proposed strategy also enhanced the quality of the power delivered to the grid by reducing the stator current THD to 0.63%, less than half the 1.32% THD produced by the SHCS method.
