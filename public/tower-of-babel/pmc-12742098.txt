Examining the associations between the food environment and dietary intake in British Columbia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bill Zhao, Tamara R Cohen, Jason Sutherland, Rafael Meza, Parveen Bhatti, Rachel A Murphy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo characterise neighbourhood food environments in British Columbia (BC) and determine whether food environment characteristics are associated with fruit and vegetable (FV) intake.DesignA cross-sectional study using geospatial linkage of food environment measures within 1 km residential buffers, analysed with mixed-effects modelsSettingUrban neighbourhoods in BC, Canada.ParticipantsApproximately 25 000 adults aged 35–69 years from the BC Generations Project cohort.Outcome measuresFV intake as a continuous variable (servings/day) and as a binary measure (<5 or ≥5 servings/day).ResultsApproximately 50% of participants lived in neighbourhoods without chain grocery stores, fast-food outlets or convenience stores within walking distance. Neighbourhoods in the highest density category for fast-food outlets were associated with lower odds of consuming ≥5 servings of FV per day (OR=0.89, 95% CI 0.80 to 0.98). Associations between chain grocery stores, convenience stores and FV intake were attenuated after adjusting for neighbourhood characteristics including walkability, and material and social deprivation.ConclusionsThe findings suggest limited neighbourhood access to retail food outlets across urban areas in BC. Participants living in neighbourhoods with greater density of fast-food restaurants were less likely to consume >5 servings of FV per day. Further studies are needed to better understand the null findings and additional factors that may be associated with dietary intake.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, NUTRITION & DIETETICS, Cross-Sectional Studies, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Food Insecurity

Introduction

Poor diet quality is a leading cause of death and disability in high-income countries, including Canada.1 Most Canadians consume low-quality diets that do not meet guidelines.2 There is little evidence of improvement in diet quality over the past decade,2 and there is widening of inequities in diet quality by household income and education.3

In 2016, Health Canada introduced the Healthy Eating Strategy aiming to improve the diet quality of Canadians.4 A central part of the strategy is improving the food environment to ensure healthy choices are easy, accessible and affordable. The food environment encompasses the number and location of food outlets, often conceptualised as ‘food deserts’ and ‘food swamps’. The term food desert describes a geographical area with poor access to healthy and affordable foods.5 Food deserts typically exist in socioeconomically disadvantaged urban areas which may contribute to socioeconomic gradients in diet and diet-related health outcomes, although food deserts may also be present in rural areas where long distances are needed to access healthy food.6 A food swamp is a geographical area which has adequate access to healthier, affordable food with an abundance of food outlets that sell less healthy food and beverages such as convenience stores or fast-food restaurants.6

Despite the growing interest in the food environment, there are large gaps in evidence that make it unclear how to focus public health efforts. For instance, there is uncertainty about the existence of food deserts in Canada, and its potential association with dietary intake. This uncertainty also extends to other aspects of the food environment, such as the presence of fast-food outlets or convenience stores. A study in Montreal, Quebec, noted a lack of food deserts.7 Subsequently, a literature review highlighted evidence of inequities in food access in the SA but concluded studies in other high-income countries (including Canada) were limited and inconclusive.8 Studies of the food environment in Canada have largely focused on a single city/small region, limiting applicability to other areas, and many are dated,79,12 and may not reflect contemporary retail food environments.

Neighbourhood-level factors encompass the population composition and the built environment such as roads, parks and buildings. A study across multiple countries, including Canada, found that living in socioeconomically advantaged neighbourhoods was associated with greater fruit and vegetable (FV) intake.13 Low socioeconomic status (SES) neighbourhoods typically have fewer healthy food outlets and more stores offering unhealthy foods.14 In addition, unhealthy foods are usually less expensive than healthy foods.14 The structural hypothesis posits that the physical surroundings such as institutions and social norms of a neighbourhood can influence residents’ health behaviours.15 Additionally, the stress theory suggests that low SES neighbourhoods create stressful environments that may contribute to adopting unhealthy behaviours.15 However, few studies have considered neighbourhood-level factors in the context of associations between the food environment and dietary intake.16 17 To meet these research gaps, this study aimed to (1) Characterise the neighbourhood food environment in British Columbia using data from participants in a large cohort study and (2) determine if characteristics of the food environment where participants live were associated with FV consumption.

Methods

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. This cross-sectional study is a secondary analysis of existing data from the British Columbia Generations Project (BCGP).18 Between 2009 and 2016, n=29 850 adults aged 35–69 were recruited from across BC. Most participants were from the Greater Vancouver area (Vancouver, Richmond, Coquitlam and Burnaby) and Vancouver Island (Victoria and Nanaimo). At baseline, participants completed a detailed questionnaire providing information on personal demographics (eg, household income, biological sex, ethnicity), health history (eg, pre-existing conditions) and health behaviours (eg, alcohol consumption, physical activity level, FV intake, sleep). Participants were asked to self-report measures of height and weight on the baseline questionnaire. A subset of participants also visited assessment centres where height and weight were measured by trained study staff. The dependent variable for this study was usual daily FV consumption. Participants were asked ‘In a typical day, how many total servings of fruit (not including fruit juice) do you eat?’ and ‘In a typical day, how many total servings of vegetables do you eat?’. One serving was defined as 1/2 cup or 125 mL of fresh, frozen, canned or cooked leafy vegetables. The number of servings of fruits and vegetables was then summed and explored as continuous servings and dichotomised as <5 servings or >5 servings, in line with previous research and public health recommendations.19 20

Food environment

The Canadian Food Environment Dataset (Can-FED) provides a detailed, nationwide examination of the retail food environment in Canada, leveraging data from the Statistics Canada Business Register from 2018.21 This dataset originated from mandatory tax data collected by the Canada Revenue Agency. Can-FED data calculated density measures for food environments at the level of dissemination areas (DAs); a small relatively stable geographic unit with an average population of 400–700 persons. Can-FED measures were calculated within a 1 km network buffer around the population-weighted centroid of DAs. We used the general-use Can-FED dataset focusing on three categorical absolute density measures and two categorical relative density measures. The absolute density measures included fast food stores, convenience stores and chain grocery stores calculated by dividing the count of a given outlet by the area in square kilometres. Large chain grocery stores account for more than 80% of Canadian grocery market share, with the five largest cooperations controlling 76% of the sales.22 The two relative density measures were developed by the Can-FED investigators: the modified Retail Food Environment Index (mRFEI) and the fast-food restaurant mix (Rmix). mRFEI reflects the ratio of stores that are more likely to sell healthy food including FV (ie, chain grocery stores, grocery stores and FV markets) relative to chain supermarkets, grocery stores, FV markets, fast-food outlets, convenience stores and convenience stores at a gas station.23 The classification of the accessibility of foods within stores aligns with evidence on shelf space of ‘healthy’ or ‘unhealthy’ foods in different types of food stores.24 Rmix reflects the proportion of fast-food outlets relative to the total of fast-food and full-service restaurants. Each variable was classified into ordinally ranked categories: class 0, class 1, class 2, class 3 and class 4, with 0 indicating no access to the outlet type and class 4 representing the highest density. For absolute density measures (chain grocery store, fast food outlet and convenience stores), classes 3 and 4 were combined due to low cell counts. The Can-FED food environment measures served as the independent variables of analysis.

Built environment

The Canadian Urban Environmental Health Research Consortium (CANUE) generates standardised, analysis-ready geospatial environmental measures for every postal code in Canada.25 Variables of interest in this study were the Material and Social Deprivation Index (MSDI), neighbourhood walkability and gentrification from the year 2016. The MSDI, generated at the DA level, reflects material and social dimensions of deprivation within communities using Canadian census data.26 27 The MSDI has been used extensively to monitor social inequalities in health and has demonstrated construct validity. Material deprivation was determined by the proportion of individuals lacking a high school diploma, the employment-to-population ratio and the mean income in the area.26 27 Social deprivation reflects the percentage of people living alone, the incidence of marital separation and the percentage of single-parent families.26 27 Both measures were scored from 1 to 100, with 1 indicating lowest deprivation. Walkability measures, derived from factors such as land-use mix and street connectivity, were calculated using 1 km buffers centred on the midpoint of each DA. Measures were then summarised using z-scores. Gentrification, a measure of changes in demographic and economic landscapes in a neighbourhood, was assessed using the GENUINE tool.28 This tool captured shifts in income, housing occupancy, educational attainment and demographic trends from 2006 to 2016 to identify gentrification among Canadian metropolitan areas. Participants in the BCGP were linked with CANUE and Can-FED using six-digit postal codes for their primary residence provided at baseline.

Covariates

Age, sex, ethnicity, education, marital status and behaviours such as physical activity, smoking status and alcohol consumption were identified as possible confounders based on prior studies and directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). Confounders were categorised according to established thresholds such as body mass index (BMI), or from prior studies in the BCGP and/or CanPath cohorts.16 17 29 Effect modification between food environment measures and age, sex, education level and income was considered too.

Study sample

Of the 29 580 participants enrolled in BCGP, 29 570 completed the BCGP baseline questionnaires. Of the 29 570 participants, 1179 were excluded due to missing postal code information. Five duplicates were also removed, leaving a total of 28 386 participants. Of the 28 386 participants, food environment data were missing for 2792 participants, leaving 25 594 participants with complete information on food environment for aim 1. For aim 2, 598 participants were missing FV intake data, leaving a total of 24 996 participants for analysis (online supplemental figure 1).

Statistical analysis

If physical activity from the long form IPAQ was not available, data from the short form IPAQ were used. When physical measurements of height and weight were not available, self-reported height and weight were used to calculate BMI. Otherwise, missing data were imputed using the ‘Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations’ package in R using predictive mean matching, logistic regression and proportional odds logistic regression. Age and sex were correlated with missing data and included as auxiliary variables. In total, 20 sets of imputations were performed, with 10 iterations each to reach convergence. Imputed data were pooled using Rubin’s rule.

Descriptive statistics and cross-tabulations of measures of the food environment were performed. Linear mixed-effects models and generalised logistic mixed-effects models were used to investigate continuous and dichotomous consumption of FV, with nesting at the forward sortation area included as a random intercept. Each food environment measure was modelled as an independent variable in separate models. Covariates were selected based on DAG informed selection and incorporated into the model using a forward stepwise approach to check for robustness. Each model’s Akaike information criterion (AIC) was compared with that of the previous model as a new variable was added. If the new model with the additional covariate exhibited a lower AIC, it was retained. This process continued until all covariates of interest were assessed. Model assumptions were verified using residual plots. Model 1 included adjustments for sex, age, income, marital status, education level, ethnicity, physical activity level, alcohol consumption, smoking status, BMI and urban/rural residential area. Model 2 additionally adjusted for neighbourhood variables: urban/rural residential area, walkability, material deprivation and social deprivation, to understand the effect on risk estimates between the food environment and FV intake. All analyses were conducted in R.

Results

On average, participants were 55.8 years of age. Most participants (69%) were female and Caucasian (86.1%). Approximately 53.1% of the participants earned an annual income of >US$75 000 and 42.9% had a bachelor’s degree or higher. Most participants (39.9%) had a BMI <25 kg/m2, and a substantial proportion (96.3%) lived in urban areas (table 1). Discussion

Within this population of nearly 25 000 adults, a large proportion (48.3%) did not have access to any chain supermarkets, grocery stores or fruits and vegetable markets (mRFEI) within 10–15 min walk and 75.8% lacked access to a chain grocery store in their neighbourhood. Although our study population was generally affluent, the geographic areas studied included neighbourhoods with high material and social deprivation, and thus the implications of limited neighbourhood access to grocery stores may be broader than findings in our study population indicate. There was also evidence of food environments that may be more favourable for healthier dietary intake, with nearly 40% of participants living in neighbourhoods with no convenience stores or fast-food outlets. The prevalence of areas without access to a grocery but high access to fast-food outlets and convenience stores was minimal.

The proportion of healthy food outlets (mRFEI) and density of chain grocery stores was not significantly associated with FV consumption. Literature on access to grocery stores and FV intake is mixed. For instance, a study in the USA reported that in urban areas, access to supermarkets was positively associated (OR=0.95, 95% CI 0.92 to 0.99) with FV consumption,30 although access was measured as proximity to grocery stores rather than density as in our study. In contrast, a study in Quebec found no associations between grocery store proximity or density and FV consumption.31 Pessoa et al found that higher FV intake was associated with living in neighbourhoods with a higher density of healthy food outlets.32 However, the study by Pessoa et al was conducted in Brazil, where the types of food outlets and food systems differ from those in Canada. Furthermore, the definition of ‘healthy food outlets’ encompassed various types of outlets, including ‘open-air markets’ and ‘restaurants with nutritious food’, which differed from the measures used in this study.32 It is important to consider differences in contextual setting and study design when examining findings across food environment studies.

The lack of associations found in this study may reflect that physical proximity to grocery stores is just one of many factors that affect FV consumption. For instance, Aggarwal et al reported that participants travelled further distances to shop at stores outside of their neighbourhood environment if the cost of food was lower.33 Similarly, other studies found that food prices may be more associated with FV consumption than physical proximity.34 Studies from the USA further suggested that increasing geographic access to FV by adding grocery stores in food deserts did not result in changes in FV consumption for residents.34

Our findings suggest that participants living in neighbourhoods with greater fast-food outlets were less likely to meet FV recommendations. However, the difference in FV consumption was minimal. In Hong Kong, researchers found that the highest quartile of fast-food density was associated with lower fruit consumption and a trend for decreasing fruit consumption with increasing density of fast-food outlets.35

Given that convenience stores seldom sell FV,24 we expected convenience stores may influence dietary intake through greater access to energy dense, nutrient poor foods which might displace FV in the diet. However, statistically significant associations between convenience store density measures and FV consumption were attenuated with adjustment for neighbourhood characteristics. In contrast, a study in New Zealand found that individuals living in areas with the shortest travel time to convenience stores had lower FV consumption (OR=0.75, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.93).36 A higher proportion of fast-food restaurants and convenience stores relative to grocery stores was associated with lower FV consumption in a population of Hong Kong.35 A study in Quebec reported that people living in neighbourhoods with the highest density of convenience stores had greater FV consumption,31 although they attributed the finding to residual confounding.

In this study, we did not observe many significant associations. While the null findings might indicate a weak or absent association, it was also possible that small effects were not detectable, and these smaller associations could still have public health relevance given the population-level impact of dietary behaviours. The null findings might also have suggested that food environment measures themselves were not fully able to capture the complexity of food access. Recent global evidence shows that physical access alone might not lead to improvements in dietary quality without simultaneously addressing food affordability and purchasing power. The traditional ‘food desert’ framing had been critiqued for oversimplifying complex social, economic and environmental processes.37 38 Some scholars advocate for the inclusion of affordability and pricing as stronger determinants of dietary behaviour than distance alone.37 38 Global comparative data further demonstrate that the cost and sustainability profile of foods varies widely across regions, and that affordability may interact with nutritional quality and environmental dimensions.39 Additionally, the growing influence of digital food delivery services should be considered, as these platforms are increasingly becoming more embedded in our daily lives. Future studies incorporating more multidimensional factors on dietary behaviours are warranted. Public policies aimed at improving access to healthy foods may need to incorporate complementary strategies such as enhancing affordability, expanding online delivery access and addressing broader socioeconomic determinants in addition to maintaining the physical presence of grocery stores.

The use of data from the BCGP which spans major urban cities and rural regions in BC is a strength and fills a gap in evidence on the retail food environment in the most populous areas in BC. Previous studies conducted in select cities in Canada and the USA—where varying demographics, bylaws and possibly unique food environments limit the applicability of findings to BC. Although the data was collected some ten years prior, this predated the rise in online grocery delivery and food delivery service40 that may blur boundaries of the food environment. Our results may serve as a ‘baseline’ to understand the effect of changes in food purchasing behaviours on food environment and dietary intake. Another strength of this study is the use of Can-FED, which provides comprehensive and standardised metrics of geographic access to different types of food outlets. A significant challenge in food environment research is the variability and inconsistent quality of the measurement of the food environment. For example, some studies measured public transportation and walking distance to stores10 while others assessed distance using pathways over straight-line distances41 or population-weighted distances.41 Studies also measured the number and type of food outlets in different manners such as through phone book listings42 or commercial databases.9 These differing methods limit comparability across studies. Can-FED is a substantial step forward in addressing these issues by providing standardised nationwide measures. Can-FED also enables a more nuanced exploration of the food environment through the use of multiple measures beyond grocery stores and fast-food restaurants which predominate the literature.6

This study also has limitations. The food environment was defined using DA boundaries based on the postal code of residence, which is an inherent limitation of buffer-based measures. People may perceive their neighbourhood differently and spend time and purchase food outside their residential neighbourhood (eg, at work and leisure). It is thus possible the associations we observed were attenuated towards the null without consideration of perceived food environments and food environments beyond participants’ homes. Recent literature has suggested a need for multidimensional and mixed-method approaches that integrate objective spatial data with additional socioeconomic factors like food affordability, food insecurity and other qualitative perspectives to develop a more comprehensive understanding of food access and equity.43 44 The majority of BCGP participants were White and lived in urban areas, which limits statistical power to analyse rural food environments or detect subgroup-specific associations such as by ethnicity. BCGP participants were volunteers, which introduces the risk of participation bias and limits generalisability. BCGP participants tend to be higher income, more educated, older and generally healthier (eg, lower prevalence of smoking) than the general population in BC.45 46 The null association for grocery stores may partly reflect a ceiling effect, as this predominantly high-SES, urban cohort may already experience uniformly high access to food retailers. However, it is notable that even in this typically higher SES study population, participants who lived in neighbourhoods with greater density of fast-food restaurants were less likely to meet FV recommendations. Still, future research should aim to include greater representation of low-income, rural, minority and Indigenous peoples in Canada, to enhance generalisability and inform equitable policies. Potential disconnects in the timing of data should also be acknowledged. Can-FED measures were derived from 2018 Business Registry data,21 CANUE variables were derived from the 2016 Census, while BCGP participants were recruited between 2009 and 2016, which could bias the results towards the null.18 However, since food and neighbourhood environments evolve slowly, any impact on associations was likely negligible. Consumption of FV was self-reported which is prone to underreporting.47 Although FV intake was collected with a short screener in the BCGP, findings for consumption of FV servings within comparable sex and age categories are comparable to the Canadian Community Health Survey (CCHS)-Nutrition. The CCHS-Nutrition used 24-hour dietary recall data which is considered to have less estimate error than screeners, suggesting construct validity for the measure in BCGP.48 BCGP did not collect dietary information beyond FV consumption which limits the ability to assess associations between the food environment and diet quality more broadly. Residential self-selection may also be a source of bias, as individuals with healthier behaviours may choose to live in neighbourhoods with more favourable food environments. Given the inherent limitations of a cross-sectional design, reverse causation cannot be ruled out.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that many urban neighbourhoods in BC have limited access to grocery stores. Conversely, areas with a high density of fast-food outlets and convenience stores are rare. Living in neighbourhoods with a higher density of fast-food outlets was moderately associated with lower FV consumption in this population. Overall, the findings may suggest that the retail food environment alone may play a limited role in promoting FV intake. Further studies are needed to understand the null findings, particularly whether the higher SES population in BCGP resulted in smaller effect estimates for the food environment on dietary intake. Studies should also incorporate additional factors such as food affordability, digital foodscapes, and examine the influence of non-residential food environment exposures such as where people work or spend leisure time.
