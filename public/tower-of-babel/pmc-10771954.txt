Severity of COVID-19 Pneumonia in Kidney Transplant Recipients According to SARS-CoV-2 Vaccination
Authors: Seunghyeok Choi, Hanbi Lee, Sang Hun Eum, Ji-Won Min, Hye Eun Yoon, Chul Woo Yang, Byung Ha Chung
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe reviewed 24 kidney transplantat recipients (KTRs) who had radiologically confirmed coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pneumonia. Enrolled KTRs were divided into a severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)-vaccination (+) group (n = 18) and a vaccination (-) group (n = 6). Clinical outcomes of the two groups including death, pulmonary outcome, and renal outcome were compared. COVID-19 pneumonia was worse in vaccination (-) KTRs. Two out of six vaccination (-) KTRs needed continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and mechanical ventilator (MV) and expired. In contrast, only one KTR expired and required CRRT and MV out of 18 vaccination (+) KTRs. Our results suggest that SARS-CoV-2 vaccination attenuates severity of COVID-19 pneumonia in KTRs.Keywords: Kidney transplant recipient, COVID-19 pneumonia, SARS-CoV-2 vaccine

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) spreads rapidly to most of the world. Kidney transplant recipients (KTRs) are at high risk of infections given their restricted immunity [1]. The transplant recipients present higher incidence of COVID-19 than the general population [2]. The higher rates of mortality ranging from 30% to 50% and severity in KTRs have been reported [3,4,5]. Atypical presentation and more rapid clinical progression in immunosuppressed KTRs lead to late presentation and potentially worse outcomes [3,6]. It is clear that KTRs are more vulnerable to COVID-19 than the general population.

The American Society of Transplantation and the American Society of Transplant Surgeons have distributed statements on severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) vaccination for organ transplantation candidates [7]. In spite of low seropositivity after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination, cellular response following vaccination is present in majority of KTRs [8,9,10]. To protect KTRs from COVID-19 infection, SARS-CoV-2 vaccination is recommended, including subsequent doses to promote immune response against SARS-CoV-2 to improve clinical outcomes [7,9,11]. Although SARS-CoV-2 vaccination is advised for KTRs, the effectiveness of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination in mitigating COVID-19 pneumonia among KTRs remains obscure.

Based on the above background, this study aimed to investigate whether SARS-CoV-2 vaccination could have favorable effects on the severity and clinical outcomes of COVID-19 pneumonia in KTRs. For this, we retrospectively evaluated KTRs with COVID-19 pneumonia who were hospitalized in Seoul St. Mary’s hospital from March 2022 to January 2023. We compared outcomes of COVID-19 pneumonia in KTRs and analyzed the role of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination in attenuating fatality of COVID-19 pneumonia.

We enrolled 24 patients who visited Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital with COVID-19 pneumonia confirmed by polymerase chain reaction or rapid antigen test of nasal swab with typical radiographic findings on chest computed tomography from March 2022 to January 2023 [12]. All patients were managed under protocol with glucocorticoid (methylprednisolone or dexamethasone) and empirical antibiotics. Antiviral therapy was given in accordance with the current guideline [13], although its usage was infrequent due to concerns regarding potential drug interaction with immunosuppressants. We divided patients by vaccination status and compared clinical outcomes.

The primary endpoint of this study was treatment failure defined as death. We also analyzed secondary endpoints including acute kidney injury (AKI) defined by kidney disease improving global outcomes (KDIGO) guideline [14], requirement of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), respiratory failure demanding mechanical ventilator (MV).

Mean age of all patients was 61.0 ± 9.5 year. Seventeen patients were males and seven patients were females. Three patients had deceased donor kidney transplantation and 21 patients had living donor kidney transplantation (LDKT). No patients had underlying lung disease. Patients were admitted 18.8 ± 7.1 days after confirmed COVID-19 pneumonia and hospitalized for 11.0 ± 7.1 days. Eighteen patients were vaccinated more than 2 times with BNT162b2 (Pfizer/BioNTech, United States) or AZD1222 (AstraZeneca/University of Oxford, United Kingdom), while six patients were not vaccinated. In vaccinated patients, interval from vaccination to COVID-19 was 88.3 ± 56.4 days. All patients were prescribed of immunosuppressant agents including calcineurin inhibitors (CNI, tacrolimus or cyclosporine). Twenty of twenty-four patients were taking mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and 22 out of 24 patients were prescribed for glucocorticoid. MMF was discontinued for all KTRs on admission. For KTRs who progressed to a life-threatening state, CNI was also discontinued. All patients were treated with glucocorticoid (dexamethasone 6 mg/day or methylprednisolone 1 mg/kg/day) for several days. Glucocorticoid were then tapered off after a relief of symptoms. Empirical antibiotics were used for all patients to take care of suspected secondary opportunistic infection.

Twenty-one out of twenty-four patients recovered and 3 of 24 died. Three patients who could not recover within 60 days from COVID-19 pneumonia showed renal failure and respiratory failure requiring CRRT and MV. Mean age of expired patients was 71.0 ± 8.2 years, while that of survivors was 59.5 ± 8.9 years (P-value = 0.047). All patients who expired by COVID-19 pneumonia within 60 days were recipients of LDKT.

Vaccinated patients recovered more than unvaccinated patients (mortality rate: 5.5% vs. 33.3%, odds ratio [OR] = 8.5, P-value = 0.081, Table 1). Younger patients under 65-year-old showed favorable outcome (mortality rate: 5.5% vs. 33.3%, OR = 8.5, OR = 8.5, P-value = 0.081, Table 1). Although vaccinated patients were younger than unvaccinated patients (58.0 ± 8.2 years vs. 69.8 ± 7.7 years, P-value = 0.005), among older patients over 65-year-old, only unvaccinated patients expired (0% vs. 100%, Table 2).

We defined respiratory failure by requirement of MV. All patients who required MV were recipients of LDKT. Older patients over 65-year-old showed adverse outcome than younger patients (requirement of MV: 5.5% vs. 33.3%, OR = 8.5, OR = 8.5, P-value = 0.081, Table 1). Vaccinated patients showed better outcome than unvaccinated patients (requirement of MV: 5.5% vs. 33.3%, OR = 8.5, OR = 8.5, P-value = 0.081, Table 1). Two of six patients who were unvaccinated needed MV. One of 18 patients who were vaccinated needed MV (Table 1). Among older patients over 65-year-old, only unvaccinated patients required MV (0% vs. 100%, Table 2).

To distinguish AKI, we followed KDIGO criteria [14] for increased serum creatinine. We also included the need for CRRT for those who showed hemodynamically instabilities. Following the KDIGO criteria [14], 2 of 18 patients who were vaccinated showed stage 3 AKI, while 2 of 18 patients showed stage 1 AKI. Two of six patients who were unvaccinated showed stage 3 AKI. However, only one patient among vaccinated KTRs required CRRT, while two of six patients who were unvaccinated needed CRRT (5.5% vs. 33.3%, OR = 8.5, P-value = 0.081, Table 3). All three patients who needed CRRT were recipients of LDKT. Two of eighteen patients who were under 65 years old showed stage 3 AKI, while 2 of 18 patients showed stage 1 AKI. Two of six patients who were over 65 years old showed stage 3 AKI. While only one patient under 65 years old needed CRRT, 2 patients over 65 years old needed CRRT (5.5% vs. 33.3%, OR = 8.5, P-value = 0.081, Table 3). Among older patients over 65 years old, only unvaccinated patients required CRRT (0% vs. 100%, Table 2).

In this study, we evaluated the effectiveness of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination in reducing severity of COVID-19 pneumonia in KTRs. KTRs suffering from COVID-19 pneumonia who required CRRT or MV were unvaccinated or elderly. SARS-CoV-2 vaccination reduced the requirement of CRRT or MV in KTRs, followed by successful recovery from COVID-19 pneumonia. For KTRs who were more than 65-year-old, vaccination was effective in attenuating severity of COVID-19 pneumonia.

Twenty-four KTRs who had radiologically confirmed COVID-19 pneumonia were reviewed and their clinical outcomes were analyzed. Regardless of comorbidities, unvaccinated or aged KTRs were at risk of severe COVID-19 pneumonia. Two out of 6 unvaccinated KTRs and 1 out of 18 vaccinated KTR expired. Briefly, 80-year-old male, 196 months post-transplant, and 69-year-old female, 346 months post-transplant, unvaccinated KTRs with diabetes and hypertension and 64-year-old male KTR, 17 months post-transplant, with coronary artery disease and diabetes who received the BNT162b2 (Pfizer/BioNTech, United States) three times required CRRT and MV and finally expired, despite aggressive treatment including glucocorticoid, antibiotics, and cessation of immunosuppressant.

We found that KTRs who were older than 65 years were at higher risks of AKI and respiratory failure requiring CRRT and MV due to COVID-19 pneumonia with a fatal outcome, compared to younger KTRs. We observed mortality rate of 5% in younger KTRs and 33% in older KTRs. Although AKI developed similarly in vaccinated and unvaccinated KTRs (22.2% vs. 33.3%, OR = 1.75), the demand of CRRT was lower in vaccinated KTRs (5.5% vs. 33.3%, OR = 8.5). Patients who required MV were also less in vaccinated KTRs (5.5% vs. 33.3%, OR = 8.5). Vaccination could reduce the requirement of CRRT and MV in KTRs, especially in older patients.

Although clinical trials for SARS-CoV-2 vaccine show safety and efficacy with vaccine on market showing successful results in trials, there are concerns about its safety and insufficient effect for KTRs [8,15]. The theoretical concerns and reports that vaccination might induce allograft rejection or development of de novo donor specific antibody exist [16–19]. Also, the course of COVID-19 was similar in vaccinated and unvaccinated recipients and Vaccination did not reduce symptoms [8,20]. Protection of vaccination against COVID-19 and safety related to rejection in KTRs remains obscure.

The most interesting finding of this study was the role of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination in attenuating fatal outcome of COVID-19 pneumonia. Vaccination, notwithstanding the type of vaccine, can diminish death from COVID-19 pneumonia and renal or respiratory failure. There were concerns about vaccination for KTRs regarding vaccine safety and efficacy. However, we revealed that vaccination could reduce fatal outcomes of complications after COVID-19. This confidently suggests that vaccination is necessary for KTRs who are under permanent immunosuppressive therapy. Nonetheless, the small number of enrolled patients and the lack of data for incidence of COVID-19 in KTRs were limitations of this study.
