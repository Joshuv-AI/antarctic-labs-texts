Hospice referral patterns in India during COVID-19: A retrospective study on advanced cancer patients
Authors: Megha Reddy, Seema Rao, Sangeetha N.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ContextDespite, its benefits, palliative care, especially in oncology, remains underutilized globally. The COVID-19 pandemic posed several challenges that may have further exacerbated the timing of referrals and access to care.ObjectiveThis descriptive study examines hospice referral patterns and patient characteristics among advanced cancer patients admitted to an inpatient hospice center in urban India during the COVID-19 pandemic, including demographics, commonly reported cancer types, referral sources, and the timing from referral and admission to death.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted using admission records from January to April 2021 (second wave), September to December 2021 (intermediate period), and January to April 2022 (third wave) of the pandemic. Descriptive and quantitative analyses were performed to analyze patient characteristics and the timing of admission and referral to death.ResultsThe study included 740 new admissions of advanced cancer patients to the hospice across the three pandemic periods. Carcinoma breast was the most common cancer type referred to the hospice with doctors being the primary referral source. The median time from admission to death remained consistent across the three waves, and was less than 30 days. This suggests late transition to inpatient hospice care in the final weeks of life. No significant differences were observed in referral timing by age group or socioeconomic status.ConclusionAdvanced cancer patients in our study sample were consistently referred late to hospice care, irrespective of age or socioeconomic status. This highlights the urgent need for improved and timely referral systems, especially given that advanced cancer patients comprise the majority of cancer patients in India. Addressing gaps in access to care and awareness of the benefits offered by end-of-life care, particularly for younger patients and men, is crucial for ensuring equitable and cost-efficient palliative care delivery.

Introduction

Palliative care is a branch of medicine that focuses on alleviating the suffering of patients with life-limiting or debilitating diseases. It aims to reduce their symptom burden, address their stress and emotional needs, and help patients make decisions regarding their care and treatment. It also extends beyond the patient and looks at the quality of life of the caregiver, by helping them understand care goals and treatment decisions, providing coping strategies through the emotional trauma/pain, and with the dying/bereavement process [1]. Hospice care represents a form of end-of-life palliative care delivered to patients with terminal illness, typically when life-prolonging or curative treatment has ceased and the focus shifts entirely to comfort and quality of life [2].

Referral of advanced cancer patients to a hospice forms a significant component of receiving end-of-life care that assures comfort in the dying process for the patient and the caregiver. This referral system is formed when a health worker seeks care for a patient outside of the acute care facility due to changes in the care goals, insufficient resources, and the milieu of the hospital available for the patient [3]. While the referral or integration of palliative care in oncology has been evidenced to be beneficial and improve patient outcomes, palliative care has not gained much traction in terms of usage and referral of patients. As per the World Health Organisation, globally only 14% of those who require palliative care receive it [4]. This low number also reflects the situation of palliative care in India, which has been around for 20 years [5]. In India, the usage and infrastructure for palliative care stays minimal, with less than 2% of the population having access to it [5, 6]. The COVID-19 pandemic has added further gaps and difficulties to access and treatment in palliative care systems. Consequences of isolation were exacerbated in patients and close ones alike with attenders being denied access to visit hospitalised patients or bury their loved ones with due dignity - all of which changed the patterns of grief experienced during the pandemic [7]. The pandemic also strained palliative healthcare resources with more pain medication in demand [8] and a surge of patients in need of palliative care [9, 10]. Moreover, the healthcare workforce also struggled with the consequences of financial uncertainties and isolation and were all of a sudden required to adopt new behaviours to communicate and treat patients during the COVID-19 pandemic. Lastly, there was a strong shift of healthcare being delivered through telehealth, which created a barrier to palliative care access for homes that lacked internet connectivity or other technological resources - creating wider gaps in care, especially in low to middle-income countries [11].

There is a current dearth of literature on the referral patterns of advanced cancer patients to hospice care centres in India. It is important and necessary to study the referral patterns to be sure that hospice care is accessible to all regardless of their socioeconomic or educational level, in addition to other factors. It is also important to get a glance at whether hospitals and health care centres are referring patients at the right time and integrating hospice care into their practice for advanced cancer patients, who comprise nearly 75% of all cancer cases in India [12].

This research aims to study the characteristics of cancer patients referred to an inpatient hospice centre located in urban India during the COVID-19 pandemic. This study looks at the demographics of cancer patients admitted during the second wave, third wave, and intermediate period (between the second and third wave) of the COVID-19 pandemic, the most common types of cancer reported across the three periods, and the median time of admission and referral to death of the cancer patients. In this study, the term ‘hospice care’ refers to inpatient end-of-life palliative care provided at a dedicated hospice facility.

Methodology

This study is a retrospective study where data from admission records were collected for the following months - January to April 2021 to signify the second wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, September to December 2021 to signify the intermediate period between the second and third wave of the pandemic, and January- April 2022 to signify the third wave of the pandemic. Admissions to the hospice care center during these periods were primarily due to progression of advanced cancer and not due to COVID-19–related illness. The underlying cancer diagnosis at the time of admission was considered the primary cause of death. Descriptive analysis was used to analyse the sociodemographic characteristics of the patients, the most common types of cancers reported, and the most common referral sources of the patients admitted to the hospice. Using the data, the study reports on median time of admission to death and median time of referral to death of the patients across the three periods of the COVID-19 pandemic. Analysis was also done based on the age and socioeconomic condition of the patient to see if there was any difference in referral source and time to death i.e., to see if there were any disadvantages posed in terms of referral by sociodemographic characteristics of the patients.

A total of 740 admissions were analyzed with 224 new admissions seen during the second wave, 260 new admissions during the intermediate period between the second and third wave, and 256 during the third wave of the COVID-19 pandemic. Independent t-test was used to find a difference in median time of admission and referral to death among low-income and middle-income socioeconomic groups and between those aged below 60 and those aged 60 and above.

Study setting

This study was conducted at Karunashraya, a nationally recognized nonprofit 73-bed hospice in Bangalore, India, which has provided free palliative care to advanced-stage cancer patients for over 30 years. Karunashraya serves patients from various socioeconomic backgrounds, primarily low- and middle-income groups, through in-patient and home care services. Supported by the Indian Cancer Society and Rotary Bangalore Indiranagar, the center is staffed by a multidisciplinary team delivering palliative care aligned with international standards.

Based on the socio-economic status, admissions were fairly evenly distributed between low- and middle-income groups across all three periods of the pandemic (Table 1). Admissions from high-income groups remained consistently low across all three periods.

Types of cancer

Across the second wave (Jan–Apr 2021), intermediate period (Sep–Dec 2021), and third wave (Jan–Apr 2022), the most commonly reported cancers comprised 109 cases of carcinoma breast, 60 cases of carcinoma lung, 58 cases of carcinoma buccal mucosa, 53 cases of carcinoma cervix, and 35 cases of carcinoma ovary (Table 1).

Cancer profiles differed modestly by socioeconomic group. Among low-income group patients, carcinoma breast (n = 55) was most frequently reported, followed by carcinoma buccal mucosa (n = 45) and carcinoma cervix (n = 41). In contrast, among middle-income patients, carcinoma breast also predominated (n = 56), with carcinoma lung (n = 29) and carcinoma ovary (n = 20) emerging as the next most common cancer types. Patients in the high-income group were excluded from this stratified analysis due to the small sample size.

Similar types of cancers were reported among patients aged below 60 and 60 and above across all three periods of the COVID-19 pandemic namely, breast, buccal mucosa, cervical, ovarian, and lung carcinomas.

Referral source

Across all three waves of the COVID-19 pandemic, doctors were the most common source of referral (31.3%), with the majority of referrals originating from physicians at super-specialty hospitals, specifically government or community-run facilities (religious or non-profit). In the second wave, doctors accounted for 25.8% of referrals, which increased to 32.6% during the intermediate period and further to 34.7% in the third wave.

Word-of-mouth referrals were notably common during the second wave (16.5%) and the intermediate period (12.8%), highlighting the significance of personal networks. Additionally, referrals through friends were also common, comprising 15.2% and 18.7% of referrals in the second and third waves of the pandemic respectively. Relatives of former patients also played a crucial role, particularly during the intermediate period and the third wave, where referrals were reported at 19.4% and 18.3%, respectively (Fig. 1).

Moreover, referral patterns were similar across socioeconomic and age groups, with doctors being the primary referral source. Less common sources of referrals included the internet, media, former patients, and the hospice home-care team.

The median time from admission to death among all cancer patients was similar across the second wave (10 days), intermediate period (9 days), and third wave (8 days) of the COVID-19 pandemic indicating late referral of patients to the hospice centre across the three periods of the pandemic (Table 2). The median time from admission to death was also similar between low-income and middle-income groups indicating no difference in referral time between the two socioeconomic categories across the three periods of the pandemic (p > 0.05). The median time from admission to death was also the same among those aged below 60 and 60 and above across all three periods of the pandemic (p > 0.05). This similarity indicates late referral of younger and older cancer patients to the cancer hospice.

Discussion

This study found that advanced cancer patients were consistently referred late (less than 30 days from admission to death) to hospice care during the COVID-19 pandemic, with median times from admission to death remaining under 30 days across all three pandemic periods. Median admission-to-death times were 10 days, 9 days, and 8 days, during the second, intermediate, and third waves of the COVID-19 pandemic respectively, indicating limited opportunity for patients to benefit fully from inpatient hospice care services. Importantly, the median time from referral to death mirrored the median time from admission to death, with no observed differences by age group or socioeconomic status, suggesting that late referral was pervasive across patient subgroups. As this study specifically examined referral to a dedicated inpatient hospice facility, these findings reflect delayed transition to end-of-life hospice care rather than the timing of earlier specialist palliative care involvement, which was not assessed.

Delayed transition to hospice care may adversely affect patients’ quality of life in the final days and limit opportunities for comprehensive symptom control and end-of-life decision-making [13]. Evidence from prior studies suggests that earlier integration of specialist palliative care—often several months before death—maximizes benefits such as symptom management, psychosocial support, and advance care planning [14]. However, this is far from what is practised in the real world [15], where the very short hospice stays observed in this study suggest that transitions to comfort-focused end-of-life care are occurring very late in the disease trajectory. While this study does not capture the timing of earlier palliative care involvement, the findings may indicate gaps in timely referral pathways between oncology services and hospice programs.

In terms of referral sources, doctors were the primary source of referral to hospice care across all three periods of the COVID-19 pandemic, followed by referrals through word of mouth, friends, and relatives of former patients. This concentration of referrals through clinicians and informal social networks underscores the central role of physicians and family members in facilitating access to hospice care. These findings point to the need for targeted policies and training programs that improve physician awareness of timely hospice referral, while also equipping families with information about the benefits of early specialist palliative care engagement and appropriate transition to hospice care when indicated. Such approaches may also help reduce unnecessary or non-beneficial healthcare utilization and associated costs [16]. In addition, engagement of external stakeholders such as the media and the internet—could further support public awareness and normalize early palliative care and end-of-life care referral [17].

In terms of patient demographics, patients referred to the hospice were predominantly older, with most aged above 60 years, which might indicate that younger patients may have a stronger hope for a cure and stay in treatment [18]. High-income patients accounted for a very small proportion of hospice admissions (approximately 2%), a pattern consistent with prior literature [19]. This may reflect a preference among higher-income patients for prolonged or more intensive treatment pathways and a greater likelihood of hospital-based deaths [20]. Over half of the hospice admissions were female, which aligns with evidence suggesting that men are more likely to receive aggressive, non-beneficial end-of-life care, including ICU admission and chemotherapy near death, and may therefore experience delayed referral to hospice services [21].

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the absence of baseline data prevents us from determining whether late referrals during the COVID-19 pandemic reflect new trends or pre-existing issues. Further research is necessary to evaluate referral trends over a longer time frame and to assess how these trends were prior to the pandemic and how they have evolved post-pandemic. Second, the reliance on existing records may introduce biases related to incomplete or inconsistent documentation. Third, the pandemic may have influenced referral patterns in ways that are difficult to quantify, such as changes in healthcare access and resource allocation. Fourth, this study did not assess whether patients had received specialist palliative care consultation prior to hospice referral. As such, conclusions regarding the broader integration of palliative care into oncology practice should be interpreted cautiously. Future research examining referral patterns before, during, and after the pandemic would help clarify these dynamics and inform strategies to promote earlier integration of palliative care.

Conclusion

Across the second, intermediate, and third waves of the COVID-19 pandemic, no significant differences were observed in referral sources or in median time from referral to death and admission to death. Despite this stability, advanced cancer patients were consistently referred late to hospice care, irrespective of age or socioeconomic status. These findings underscore the need for more timely and routine integration of palliative care into cancer care pathways, particularly given that physicians were the primary gatekeepers for referral. Strengthening referral pathways and promoting earlier engagement with specialist palliative care services may help ensure smoother transitions to hospice care and more equitable, patient-centered, and cost-effective end-of-life care in India.
