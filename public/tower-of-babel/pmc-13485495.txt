High-grade urothelial carcinoma of the bladder in a 3-year-old boy: Complete resection and 18-year disease-free follow-up
Authors: Anneliese M Fisher, Mukund G Dole, J Todd Boyd, Donald H Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-grade urothelial carcinoma of the bladder is exceptionally rare in children and optimal management remained undefined. We report a three-year-old boy who presented with painless gross hematuria and was found to have a papillary bladder mass. Complete transurethral resection of the bladder tumor with random bladder biopsies demonstrated high-grade urothelial carcinoma without carcinoma in situ, and metastatic evaluation was negative. Intravesical therapy was deferred and the patient underwent surveillance with cystoscopy, urine cytology, and ultrasonography. Eighteen years after diagnosis, he remains disease-free and asymptomatic. This case provides the longest reported disease-free follow-up in pediatric high-grade urothelial carcinoma.Keywords: Pediatric urothelial carcinoma, High-grade urothelial carcinoma, Bladder neoplasms, Hematuria, Long-term follow-up

Introduction

Urothelial carcinoma (UC) of the bladder is very rare in the pediatric population. The incidence is estimated between 0.03% to 0.4% in patients under 20 years of age.1 The most common histological type is transitional cell carcinoma (TCC), with most being of low malignant potential, low-grade, and non-invasive. Infiltrating or high-grade cancers are extremely rare.1 The most common presenting symptom is painless gross hematuria.2, 3, 4 Due to the rarity of the disease, there are limited data to guide management, surveillance strategies, or use of intravesical therapies.5

Because of the rarity of the disease, management and surveillance recommendations for pediatric patients are largely extrapolated from adult experience, and long-term outcome data remain limited. We report a case of high-grade urothelial carcinoma of the bladder diagnosed in a 3-year-old boy treated with TURBT who remained disease free 18 years after diagnosis. To our knowledge, this represents one of the youngest reported patients with high-grade disease and the longest disease-free follow-up reported to date.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 3-year-old Caucasian boy presented with several weeks of painless gross hematuria. He had no family history of urothelial malignancy, no known carcinogenic exposures, and no significant past medical history. Urinalysis was positive for blood and urine culture was negative. Renal and bladder ultrasonography demonstrated a papillary mass arising from the posterior bladder wall (Fig. 1). The patient subsequently underwent cystoscopy with complete transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT). Random bladder biopsies were obtained to assess for occult carcinoma in situ and were negative. Histopathologic examination was positive on immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining for high-grade urothelial carcinoma with marked nuclear atypia and nucleomegaly (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The diagnosis was confirmed independently by pathologists at two institutions.

Following diagnosis, metastatic evaluation was performed with magnetic resonance imaging of the abdomen and pelvis and chest radiography, both of which demonstrated no evidence of metastatic disease.

Management options were discussed with the family. Given the absence of carcinoma in situ, complete endoscopic resection, concerns regarding morbidity associated with intravesical therapy in a young child, a surveillance-based strategy was selected. The patient underwent urinalysis, urine cytology, and renal/bladder ultrasonography every three months and cystoscopy every six months. During six years of surveillance, there was no evidence of recurrent disease. The patient was subsequently lost to follow-up.

Eighteen years after his initial diagnosis, at age 21 years, a telehealth visit was done. The patient reported no episodes of gross hematuria, urinary tract infections, or voiding complaints. Computed tomography demonstrated no evidence of recurrent or metastatic disease. He remained asymptomatic with preserved bladder function and urinalysis was negative for blood.

Discussion

Urothelial carcinoma of the bladder is rare in children and adolescents, with high-grade lesions representing only a small fraction of reported cases.1,2 Most pediatric urothelial tumors are low grade and exhibit indolent behavior, resulting in favorable outcomes following endoscopic management.6 In contrast, the optimal management of high-grade disease remains uncertain because of the scarcity of reported cases. Only two other three-year-old children with high-grade urothelial carcinoma have been reported, both with short follow-up of one to two years compared to our patient follow-up of 18 years.7,8

The rarity of pediatric high-grade urothelial carcinoma has limited the development of evidence-based management strategies. Adult guidelines often recommend intravesical therapy and intensive cystoscopic surveillance for high-risk disease.1,5 However, the applicability of these strategies to young children is unclear. Intravesical therapy may present technical challenges and potential morbidity in pediatric patients, while the biological behavior of pediatric urothelial tumors may differ from that of adult tumors.

In the present case, complete endoscopic resection with fulguration of the base of the tumor was the primary treatment modality. Random bladder biopsies were negative for carcinoma in situ and staging studies demonstrated only localized disease. Based on these findings, combined with the lack of evidence-based protocols for our patient at the age of three years, surveillance without intravesical therapy was chosen in a shared-decision making process. The patient remained disease-free throughout childhood and demonstrated no evidence of recurrence eighteen years after diagnosis.

This prolonged disease-free interval contributes to the limited literature describing the long-term clinical course of pediatric high-grade urothelial carcinoma. Although a single case cannot establish treatment recommendations, it suggests that selected pediatric patients with completely resected, localized high-grade disease may experience excellent long-term outcomes with individualized surveillance strategies.

In addition, this case highlights the importance of careful pathological confirmation, evaluation for carcinoma in situ, and long-term follow-up. The absence of recurrence over nearly two decades suggests that pediatric high-grade urothelial carcinoma may not uniformly exhibit the aggressive clinical course commonly associated with high-grade urothelial malignancies in adults.

Conclusion

We report a 3-year-old boy with high-grade urothelial carcinoma of the bladder treated with complete transurethral resection and surveillance alone who remains disease-free eighteen years after diagnosis. This case represents one of the youngest reported patients with high-grade disease and provides the longest reported disease-free follow-up to date. Although management should remain individualized, this case demonstrates that durable disease-free survival is possible following complete endoscopic resection. Because next-generation sequencing (NGS) could not be performed on the tissue blocks or urine samples due to the prolonged elapsed time since treatment, the duration of surveillance into adulthood remains important in this patient.
