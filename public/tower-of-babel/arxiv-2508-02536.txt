Yuqi Xue, Jian Huang

Abstract
. The energy efficiency of neural processing units (NPU) is playing a critical role in developing sustainable data centers. Our study with different generations of NPU chips reveals that 30%–72% of their energy consumption is contributed by static power dissipation, due to the lack of power management support in modern NPU chips. In this paper, we present ReGate, which enables fine-grained power-gating of each hardware component in NPU chips with hardware/software co-design. Unlike conventional power-gating techniques for generic processors, enabling power-gating in NPUs faces unique challenges due to the fundamental difference in hardware architecture and program execution model. To address these challenges, we carefully investigate the power-gating opportunities in each component of NPU chips and decide the best-fit power management scheme (i.e., hardware- vs. software-managed power gating). Specifically, for systolic arrays (SAs) that have deterministic execution patterns, ReGate enables cycle-level power gating at the granularity of processing elements (PEs) following the inherent dataflow execution in SAs. For inter-chip interconnect (ICI) and HBM controllers that have long idle intervals, ReGate employs a lightweight hardware-based idle-detection mechanism. For vector units and SRAM whose idle periods vary significantly depending on workload patterns, ReGate extends the NPU ISA and allows software like compilers to manage the power gating. With implementation on a production-level NPU simulator, we show that ReGate can reduce the energy consumption of NPU chips by up to 32.8% (15.5% on average), with negligible impact on AI workload performance. The hardware implementation of power-gating logic introduces less than 3.3% overhead in NPU chips.



1.
Introduction

Hardware accelerators for AI workloads, such as neural processing units (NPUs), are pervasive in modern cloud platforms
(
Google 2022
;
AWS 2023
;
Kennedy 2024
)
.
For instance, Google has produced seven generations of TPUs (a typical example of NPU chips) for its cloud in the past six years to accommodate the growing AI demands
(
RUN:AI 2022
;
Jouppi et al. 2023
;
Robinson 2024
)
.
This surge in AI has become the major source of the increasing energy consumption of data centers, leading to a significant increase in data center greenhouse gas emissions over the past five years
(
reu 2025
;
tim 2025
;
wsj 2024
;
bas 2024
;
goo 2024
)
.

The energy efficiency of NPU chips is playing a critical role in the sustainable development of data centers. Unfortunately, NPU chips today suffer from severe energy inefficiency.
Taking the typical TPU architecture as an example, we conduct a characterization study of its energy efficiency across different generations of chips by running various ML workloads, including large language models (LLMs), recommendation models, and stable diffusion models. We report the static and dynamic energy consumption of core hardware components of the chips in
\lx@sectionsign
3
, including systolic arrays (SAs), vector units (VUs), on-chip SRAM, HBM controller & PHY (physical layer), inter-chip interconnect (ICI) controller & PHY, and others.

Our study reveals that despite the energy efficiency improvements of newer NPU generations, there still exists a significant energy waste. Given a typical 60% utilization (duty cycle) for the NPU chips
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
and a datacenter power usage efficiency (PUE) of 1.1
(
Google 2025
)
,
17%–32% of energy is wasted due to chip idleness (e.g., while the chip is powered on but not running any jobs).
When the chips are busy, 30%–72% of energy comes from static power dissipation, due to the lack of system and architectural support for flexible power management in NPU chips. As most machine learning (ML) workloads are either compute-, memory-, or network-bound, they cannot always fully utilize all components on an NPU chip.
Those underutilized components dissipate a significant amount of static power, as the current NPU design cannot dynamically turn them off or put them into low-power mode (i.e., power gating).

Since static power dissipation will be (relatively) higher as the feature size continues to shrink in the future, supporting flexible power management is highly desirable for a sustainable NPU chip design.
A promising approach is to reduce their static power consumption by power-gating idle hardware components at fine granularity. However, given the following unique challenges and opportunities, enabling power-gating in NPU chips is not straightforward.

First, the NPU architecture has unique components such as large systolic arrays.
These large components will have a much longer power-gate and wake-up latency, rendering the conventional simple idle-detection or no-access policies (developed for generic CPU processors and GPU architectures)
(
Abdel-Majeed et al. 2013
;
Flautner et al. 2002
)
less effective. The new components require new techniques for power gating.

Second, the NPU architecture adopts an in-order pipeline without dynamic scheduling to simplify hardware design, and
it relies on the compiler to exploit the instruction-level parallelism (ILP) of ML programs. This renders many sophisticated power-gating techniques on CPUs/GPUs infeasible, such as using branch prediction and cache hit/miss as hints to decide when to power on/off the ALUs and caches
(
Hu et al. 2004
;
Sadrosadati et al. 2019
;
Ravi and Lipasti 2017
)
, and changing the instruction scheduling policy to create longer idle periods of ALUs for creating more power-gating opportunities
(
Abdel-Majeed et al. 2013
;
Kumar et al. 2014
;
Aghilinasab et al. 2016
)
.

Third, NPUs are domain-specific accelerators for ML programs, which are known to be deterministic and predictable.
This creates unique opportunities for software to manage the power states of individual components on an NPU chip in an accurate manner.

We present ReGate, a hardware-software co-design approach that enables fine-grained power gating of each component in an NPU chip. To identify the power-gating opportunities in NPU chips,
we first analyze the temporal and spatial utilization patterns of each component of an NPU chip, based on which we decide its best-fit power management scheme (i.e., hardware- vs. software-managed power gating).
We list our key insights and contributions as follows.

Hardware-managed power gating
is the most effective approach for the SA, the ICI, and the HBM controllers. The SA can be both temporally and spatially underutilized, and a naïve idle-detection mechanism that controls the entire SA is insufficient for exploiting the idleness of the processing elements (PEs) in the SA. And, we also cannot simply add an idle-detection state machine to each PE due to high hardware cost.
Our key insight is that the SA has a deterministic systolic dataflow pattern across its PEs.
We can follow the dataflow to propagate the power-on/off signal through the PEs in the same way as the input vector propagates.
This enables cycle-level power gating for each PE with minimal hardware overhead.

The ICI is only active in collective operators (e.g., AllReduce, AllToAll, ReduceScatter, and AllGather), and it can be completely power-gated in other operators that do not perform inter-chip communications.
As an operator is typically long enough (at least a few
\mu
s), it suffices to employ an idle-detection mechanism for power gating the ICI controller & PHY. Similarly, HBM is usually accessed at large granularity. For ML workloads that favor a large tile size for improved arithmetic intensity, they introduce long idle intervals, offering the opportunity to power off the DMA engine and allow the HBM controller to enter low-power auto-refresh mode.

Software-managed power gating
is the most effective solution for the VU and the SRAM.
VU idleness is mostly due to waiting for other components (e.g., SA, HBM, and ICI), and the exact idle periods vary significantly depending on the workload patterns and compiler optimizations (e.g., operator fusion and instruction scheduling).
Therefore, instead of relying on hardware-based idle-detection mechanism, we leverage the compiler to decide when to power on/off the VU based on the distance between two VU instructions. This is feasible, as NPUs usually employ a statically scheduled VLIW ISA and rely on the compiler to perform instruction scheduling.

The SRAM capacity utilization varies for different tensor operators as they have different tile size demands. Ideally, we can power gate the unused SRAM capacity during the execution of the entire operator
(i.e., with the gated-Vdd approach
(
Aich et al. 2024
;
Powell et al. 2000
)
) and do not need to retain any data in the SRAM cells.
However, this is impossible without software control since the hardware has no hints about the tile size or the data layout in the SRAM.
A possible hardware-only solution, originally designed for power gating the CPU cache, is to apply the idle-detection mechanism to each 4KB SRAM segment
1

and put idle partitions into a low-power mode
(
Flautner et al. 2002

. The low-power mode retains data in the SRAM cells, but it still consumes larger leakage power than a complete power-off.
To maximize the energy savings, we leverage the ML compiler to decide how much of the SRAM capacity should be power-gated for an operator.

ISA extension for flexible power management.

To support software-managed power gating, we extend the ISA of NPUs to expose the power management functions to the software, such as power gating an execution unit or a portion of SRAM. For each component, we provide new instructions to switch between three power states: on, auto, and off. The auto state is enabled by default, in which the hardware transparently manages power gating. The on/off states override the hardware policies and enable the software (e.g., ML compiler) to customize the power gating policy.

We synthesize the proposed power gating hardware with Cadence Genus and place & route the design with Cadence Encounter.
The hardware implementation of power-gating logic introduces less than 3.3% hardware cost in NPU chips.
We also verified the functionality of
ReGate
with FPGA.
We examine the energy/power savings and the performance overhead of ReGate with a production-level NPU simulator.
We show that ReGate can reduce the energy consumption of ML workloads by 8.5%–32.8% (15.5% on average) compared to no power gating, while the performance degradation is less than 0.5%.
When deployed at scale, this further reduces the operational carbon emissions of the NPU fleet by 31.1%–62.9%.
We list our contributions as follows:

•

To our best knowledge, we are the first to quantify the power-gating opportunities across all major components in NPU chips.

•

We enable architectural support for power gating each component on an NPU chip, and identify the best-fit power-gating mechanism (hardware-based or software-based).

•

We extend the ISA of NPU chips to enable software support for managing NPU chip power gating at fine granularity based on AI workload characteristics.

•

We evaluate ReGate with a production-level NPU simulator, showing that ReGate can significantly improve the energy efficiency of NPU chips with minimal performance overhead.

•

We verify the RTL implementation of the power-gating logic on FPGA. We also synthesize and place & route the design with a 7nm PDK, showing that ReGate introduces little hardware cost.

System Architecture of NPUs

Neural processing units (NPUs) are specialized accelerators for machine learning (ML) workloads.
A typical example is Google TPU
(

2.2.
Power Gating Basics

Power gating is a technique to reduce the leakage current flowing through a circuit block when the circuit is not being used.
In general, there are two types of power gating techniques.

The first type completely powers off the circuit block and does not retain any data if the circuit contains flip-flops or SRAM cells. A typical implementation is Gated-Vdd
(
Powell et al. 2000
)
. It is implemented by adding a header transistor between the supply voltage (Vdd) and the circuit block or a footer transistor between the circuit block and the ground (GND).
This technique is commonly employed for power-gating execution units, which do not need to retain data
(
Abdel-Majeed et al. 2013
)
.

The second type puts the circuit block into sleep (drowsy) mode by applying a reduced Vdd.
The sleep mode consumes more leakage power than a power-off, but it allows the circuit to retain data during sleep mode.
When the data needs to be accessed, the circuit block must first switch back to active mode.
This technique is commonly employed in CPU/GPU caches that need to retain data
(
Flautner et al. 2002
)
.

2.3.
Power Gating Challenges in NPUs

Since powering on/off the circuit takes extra latency and consumes dynamic power, the idle period must be larger than the
break-even time
(BET) to avoid negative impact on performance and energy
(
Abdel-Majeed et al. 2013
)
.
Therefore, having long idle periods and accurately detecting idleness are critical to the effectiveness of power gating.
Traditional CPU/GPU architectures tackled these challenges by predicting the idle periods using hints such as branch prediction results and cache hit/miss. Prior works also proposed creating longer idle periods by instruction scheduling
(
Abdel-Majeed et al. 2013
)
, exploiting the unused cache capacity by periodically putting all cachelines into sleep mode and waking up a cacheline upon access
(
Flautner et al. 2002
)
, and dynamically resizing the cache to power off the unused portion without retaining data
(
Powell et al. 2000
)
.

As the NPU has a different architecture than CPUs and GPUs, it faces unique challenges for power gating.
First, its SA is much larger than the INT/FLOAT ALUs in CPUs/GPUs.
It takes much longer to power off/on the SA, resulting in a large BET that conventional idle-detection policies cannot easily exploit.
Also, the SA consists of many processing elements (PEs) that can be idle at different cycles, necessitating a more granular power-gating mechanism.
Second, the SRAM is used as a software-managed scratchpad memory rather than a hardware-managed cache.
We cannot easily resize the SRAM transparently without semantic knowledge from the upper-level software.
Third, the NPU core uses an in-order pipeline and a statically scheduled VLIW ISA to simplify hardware design, relying on the compiler to perform instruction scheduling to exploit ILP.
We cannot directly apply conventional idle period prediction and instruction scheduling techniques in NPUs.

Systolic array.

The SAs contribute 8%–14% (10.4% on average) of static energy.

Figure

4

We do not show LLM training due to space limitations. It has a similar pattern to LLM prefill (both consist of compute-intensive LLM layers with large sequence lengths).
The SA temporal utilization is high for LLM training, LLM prefill, and stable diffusion, as they are compute-bound and involve intensive matrix multiplications (MatMuls) and convolutions (Convs).
The utilization is low for LLM decode and DLRM inferences for two reasons.
First, these workloads are memory- or network-bound.
Second, due to the auto-regressive nature of LLM decode, the embedding tensors are typically too small to amortize the systolic array warm-up latency, so the MatMul operators may be mapped to the VU.
The fundamental cause of SA underutilization is the diverse resource demands of workloads due to their intrinsic ML model architectures, which cannot be easily addressed by software optimizations like operator fusion and reordering (this observation holds for other components as well, as discussed below).
Ideally, by power gating SAs at component granularity, we can save up to 100% static SA energy for non-compute-bound workloads and up to 34% for SA-intensive ones, accounting for 0.8%–10.1% of total energy.

We further analyze the SA spatial utilization in

We focus on LLM prefill and stable diffusion, as they have high temporal SA utilization.

In contrast, stable diffusion models cannot utilize all PEs.

Ideally, power gating VUs can save up to 4.0% of total energy.

On-chip SRAM.

The SRAM accounts for 15.4%–24.4% (20.9% on average) of static energy.
The SRAM has high temporal utilization, as it is used by both SAs/VUs during computation and the DMA engine when moving data from/to the off-chip HBM.
The spatial utilization (i.e., the SRAM capacity usage) is determined by the SRAM demands of tensor operators.
We quantify the demand using the minimum tile size that maximizes the on-chip data reuse. For streaming operators (e.g., elementwise) whose data reuse is not affected by tile size, we use the minimum tile size that hides the HBM latency. We fuse as many consecutive operators as possible when they are small enough to fit entirely into the 128MB SRAM.

Figure

7

Compute-intensive workloads like LLM training, LLM prefill, and stable diffusion typically need large tile sizes to achieve high arithmetic intensity.
For memory- or ICI-bound workloads like LLM decode and DLRM, since they intrinsically have low arithmetic intensity, a larger tile size will not help further improve the on-chip data reuse. For example, the SRAM demand does not exceed 8MB for DLRM, meaning that we can save at least 10.9% of total energy by power gating the unused capacity.

Other components.

Peripheral components such as chip management, control logic, PCIe controllers, and miscellaneous datapaths account for 39.1%–45.8% of static energy.
As they are critical to the regular operation of the chip, we do not aim to power-gate them.

4.
ReGate Design and Implementation

We design ReGate with the following goals:

•

Fine-grained power mode control:
To maximize static power saving, we need to enable the flexibility to partially power-gate a component, such as a portion of an SA or the SRAM.

•

Precise power gating decision:
We need to accurately capture the idle periods to decide the best timing for power gating.

•

Minimal overhead:
We should minimize the performance overhead for ML workloads and the hardware cost of ReGate.

•

Flexibility:

We should provide developers with the flexibility to choose between HW- and SW-managed power gating.

ReGate first enables hardware support for power-gating each component on an NPU chip.
By default, ReGate supports hardware-managed power-gating policies (
\lx@sectionsign
4.1
).
To fully exploit the predictability of ML workloads to maximize the benefits of power gating and minimize performance overhead, ReGate extends the NPU ISA to expose power management functions to the software (
\lx@sectionsign
4.2
).
The compiler can perform static analysis on the NPU program to precisely capture the idle periods of execution units and the SRAM capacity demand for each tensor operator. It inserts power management instructions into the NPU program to implement software-defined power gating policies (
\lx@sectionsign
4.3
).

or
K
is smaller than SA width, some rows/columns of PEs will be idle for the entire operator.
In this case, we should completely power off unused rows/columns.
When
M
is smaller than SA width, all PEs still need to hold the weights. However, as the input data flows through the SA diagonally, in each cycle, a PE is active only when the input data passes through.
The entire PE, except the weight register, can be power-gated otherwise.

To realize the above power-gating strategy, a naïve way is to employ an idle-detection mechanism for each PE.
However, this will incur significant performance overhead for the first case in
Figure

10
, as each PE will suffer at least a cycle of wake-up delay, doubling the execution time when
M
is smaller than SA width.
Even worse, this cannot support the second and third cases in
Figure

10
, as the compiler will pad zero weight values to fit SA width, and the hardware is unaware whether
N
or
K
is smaller than SA width.
Simply adding zero-weight detection in each PE is still insufficient since the PE may still need to pass input data and partial sum to other PEs and cannot be completely power-gated.
Since the PE idleness can change in every cycle, it is also impractical for the software to control the power modes of all PEs in every cycle.
To address these challenges, we design a row/column-wise power-gating mechanism based on non-zero weight detection and leverage the diagonal dataflow to enable cycle-level PE power-gating.

PE architecture for power gating.

We define three power modes for a PE:
ON
,
W_on
, and
OFF
.

Figure

11
shows the PE architecture.
By default, the PE is
OFF
and completely power-gated.
The PE receives four power-gating control signals.
row_on
/
col_on
specifies which rows/columns of PEs should be active. When both signals are high, the PE is put into
W_on
mode, in which only the weight register (
W
) is powered on.
The two
PE_on
signals will be asserted by the left/top PEs as the input data arrives. When one of them is high, the PE will turn into
ON
mode, waking up all registers and the ALU.

Row/column-wise power gating.

To exploit the
N
/
K
underutilization, we enable row/column-wise power gating by detecting zero weight values, as shown in
Figure

12
.
As weight values are being pushed into the SA row by row, we detect non-zero values to generate the column and row bitmaps (
col_nz
and
row_nz
).
The bitmaps indicate which row or column contains at least one non-zero weight value.
As the input data flows diagonally in the SA, a row/column can be put into OFF mode if (1) it contains only zero weights and (2) all rows/columns to its top/right contain only zero weights.
This condition is detected by performing a prefix sum on the
col_nz
and
row_nz
bitmaps.
For example, if
col_nz=4’b0100
, after the prefix sum, the
col_on
bitmap will be
4’b1100
: column 0 is ON despite its zero weights, as it needs to pass input data to column 1.

Figure 13.
Dataflow of power gating systolic array when the
M
dimension is underutilized.

Dataflow of a diagonally power-gated SA.

Figure

13
shows how the PEs will be power-gated and woken up following the diagonal dataflow, which exploits the
M
underutilization.
The SA maintains a queue per row to stage the input data.
When the data arrives at the front of the queue, the
PE_on
signal to the first PE will be asserted.
Then, the input data can flow into the
I
register.
As the computation starts on the first PE, the
PE_on
signals will be propagated diagonally to wake up other PEs in advance.
Once the queue is empty, the
PE_on
signal will be de-asserted for the first PE in the row, and this inactive signal will propagate to put PEs into
W_on
mode.
By overlapping the computation and the PE wake-up delay, we can reduce the exposed SA wake-up delay to the single-PE wake-up delay.
The
PE_on
signal only adds one extra bit of wire between neighboring PEs compared to the original 16-bit input or 32-bit partial sum. It incurs little area overhead and does not introduce timing issues (see our synthesized results in
§
4.4
).

Vector unit.

The NPU architecture leverages a statically scheduled VLIW ISA to simplify hardware design
(
Norrie et al. 2021
)
.
This implies that the exact idle periods of a VU depend on workload characteristics and compiler optimizations (e.g., operator fusion, instruction scheduling).
As there is no dynamic instruction scheduling in the hardware, it will require significant engineering effort and hardware overhead to support an accurate idle period predictor in the hardware.
ReGate uses a simple idle-detection state machine to power-gate a VU during its idle periods with the best effort.
To minimize the performance impact, we set the idle-detection threshold to at least 8 cycles to avoid blocking the SA execution, as each VU can process
8\times
output elements from an SA in each cycle.
We leave it to the software to enable precise power-gating for the VUs (see
\lx@sectionsign
4.3
).

Segment-wise power-gated SRAM.

ReGate divides the SRAM into equally sized segments and enables per-segment power-gating.
By default, the segment size is the same as the vector register size (4KB in our NPU).
Each segment has three power states:
ON
,
SLEEP
, and
OFF
.
The
OFF
mode leverages Gated-Vdd
(
Aich et al. 2024
)
to minimize leakage power without retaining data.
However, as the SRAM is used as a software-managed scratchpad memory, it is impossible for the hardware to detect which segment is never used and contains no useful data.
Hence, ReGate also supports a
SLEEP
mode, which switches the supply voltage of the SRAM cells to a lower value to reduce leakage power while retaining data
(
Flautner et al. 2002
)
.

Most operators in an ML model follow a tile-by-tile computation pattern, and previous tiles will not be accessed again in the near future.
In the worst case, a VU can consume 8
\times
128
\times
3
\times
fp32=12KB per cycle, so it will take 1,365 cycles for 8 VUs to consume all data in a 128MB SRAM.
Since most operators typically have a higher compute intensity, most segments will be idle for a long period, implying that a simple power-gating policy that periodically puts unused segments into sleep can be employed to exploit the idle periods.

ReGate
employs this simple idle-detection policy for hardware-managed SRAM power-gating.
We leave it to the software to exploit the
OFF
mode, as the compiler has precise SRAM allocation information to fully power off unused segments (see
\lx@sectionsign
4.3
).

HBM/ICI controller & PHY.

The HBM is accessed by the DMA engine, and DMA requests in NPUs typically have large sizes.
This is especially true for compute-intensive workloads, which have high HBM idleness and favor a large tile size to improve arithmetic intensity.
Hence, an idle-detection policy is sufficient, as the wake-up delay can be amortized by the long DMA latency.
During the idle period, ReGate powers off the DMA engine (if it is doing RDMA over ICI) and configures the HBM controller into low-power auto-refresh mode.
Similarly, idle-detection is sufficient for the ICI controller & PHY, as they are only used in collectives and have long idle periods.

Power state management in NPU core pipeline.

In
ReGate
, a power-gated component is handled as a structural hazard when an instruction needs to stall until it wakes up.
Taking the Google TPU design as an example, the NPU core pipeline manages structural hazards between different VLIW instruction bundles with standard hold conditions on the instructions
(
Norrie et al. 2021
)
: an instruction cannot be dispatched unless all required components are ready. In
ReGate
, a power-gated component will be set as “not ready” to prevent any operations from being dispatched to it. An operation will trigger a wake-up signal to the corresponding component. The wake-up signal has no effect if the component is already powered on. Otherwise, it will wake up the component and set the ready bit.
As each component has its own ready bit, different components can be powered on/off independently without causing synchronization issues.
By leveraging the existing dependency tracking mechanism,
ReGate
minimizes intrusive modifications to the NPU pipeline.

ISA Extension for NPU Power Management

To support software-managed power gating, ReGate defines three power modes for each component:
on
,
auto
, and
off
.
For SRAM, it also defines a
sleep

The
auto

These power modes offer the flexibility to choose between HW- and SW-managed power gating.
As the HW-based approaches work immediately without software support, this helps maintain compatibility with legacy programs, and serves as a transitional solution while new SW-based power optimizations are being developed for evolving workloads.

We introduce a new
setpm
(set power mode) instruction to configure a component into the
auto
/
on
/
off
/
sleep
mode.

Figure

14
shows its semantics.

setpm
is encoded in the miscellaneous (misc.) slot in a VLIW instruction bundle.
Since components have different power-gating granularity, we define three variants of
setpm
.

The first variant is used for SRAM and is indicated by setting the
fu_type
field to
sram
.
It takes two scalar register operands that specify the start and end addresses of a contiguous region in the SRAM.
This enables the software to “shrink” the SRAM capacity with a single instruction based on the ML workload demand.

The second and third variants are used for other components.
As there are multiple SAs/VUs, we use a bitmap (
fu_id
) to indicate which SA/VU should be affected by a
setpm
.
The bitmap can be either from a scalar register or an immediate field.
For example,
setpm 0b1011,vu,off
will power-gate VU 0, 1, and 3.
Using a bitmap requires only a few extra bits than specifying the functional unit index, which is negligible compared to the entire VLIW instruction (322 bits on TPUv2
(
Norrie et al. 2021
)
).
The bitmap allows a single
setpm
to control multiple functional units.
This reduces the number of
setpm
instructions, which is desirable for two reasons.
First,
setpm
instructions need to be fetched and decoded, which consumes dynamic energy or affects performance.
Second, since we can only issue one
setpm
per cycle (with one misc. slot), configuring multiple functional units in parallel reduces the wake-up delay.

4.3.
Software-Managed NPU Power Gating

Figure 15.
Example code snippet with

To implement software-managed power gating, ReGate extends the ML compiler to analyze the idle periods of each component and instrument an NPU program with
setpm
instructions.
As discussed, SAs, HBM, and ICI should use
auto
mode.
Hence, we focus on the software-managed power gating support for VUs and SRAM.

ML compiler framework.

Modern ML compilers, such as XLA
(
Google 2023
)
, PyTorch (via torch.compile)
(
Ansel et al. 2024
)
, and TensorRT
(
NVIDIA [n. d.]
)
, typically assume a static computation graph with known tensor shapes and bounded loops.
This practice enables aggressive compiler optimizations that significantly improve performance and is widely adopted in production.
Workloads with dynamic behaviors still consist of static subgraphs.
For example, in a conditional branch, each branch (typically a subset of layers or modules in the model) may be treated as a static subgraph; variable input tensor shapes (e.g., batch sizes or sequence lengths) can be bucketized, and a static graph is compiled for each bucket.
Static graphs enable accurate component idleness analysis (i.e., no prediction errors in theory) at compilation time, making software-managed power gating practical and effective.

The static graph is lowered by the compiler frontend into an optimized intermediate representation (IR) and passed to the device-specific compiler backend to generate the assembly code.
The backend can be connected to different frontends by taking their IRs (e.g., XLA HLO
(
Authors 2024
)
or TorchInductor IR
(
Ansel et al. 2024
)
) as inputs.

ReGate
integrates the component idleness analysis pass and the
setpm
instrumentation pass into the backend after the instruction scheduling pass and the SRAM memory allocation pass.

Component idleness analysis.

To determine when to power-gate a VU and which portion of SRAM can be power-gated, the ML compiler needs to extract the idle intervals of VUs and the lifetime of each buffer allocated in the SRAM.
It calculates the distances in cycles between consecutive instructions in the same VU slot.
If there exists a DMA operation between two VU instructions, their distance is set to infinity, as the DMA will be at least the HBM latency (a few hundred nanoseconds), which is much longer than the BET of VU.
For SRAM, ReGate uses the output of the SRAM allocation pass, which includes the lifetime (start/end instruction index), start address, and size of each allocated buffer.
Based on this, ReGate derives the idle intervals of each 4KB segment.

BET-based power-gating policy.

ReGate uses the break-even time (BET)
(
Abdel-Majeed et al. 2013
)
and the power-on/off delay of each component to decide whether it should be power-gated in an idle interval.
If the idle interval is longer than BET and
2\times
the power-on/off delay, ReGate will insert
setpm
instructions at the start and end of the interval.
Otherwise, an idle interval longer than BET means the dynamic energy spent on powering on/off the component will outweigh the energy saved by power gating. An interval longer than
2\times
the power-on/off delay means a potential performance overhead.
In these two cases, we should not power gate the component.
The exact value of BET and power-on/off delay depends on the hardware circuit implementation and is provided by the chip manufacturer (see
§
4.4
for more details in our hardware synthesis results).
The ML compiler takes the BET values as configurable compiler flags.

Figure

15
shows an example code snippet from a MatMul operator.
The VUs post-process SA outputs and are only active for 2 cycles out of each 16-cycle period.
ReGate maximizes the power-gated cycles of VUs (10 cycles in the example) by inserting
setpm
at the most appropriate timings.
In contrast, the HW-based policy will waste a few cycles to observe idleness before gating the VUs.

Simulator methodology.

We implement ReGate with a production-level NPU simulator based on the TPU architecture.
It takes the ML model graph and simulates the execution of each component (e.g., SA, VU, SRAM, HBM, ICI) in each tensor operator at cycle level.

The simulator frontend applies common ML compiler optimizations used in production
(
Google 2023
;
Chen et al. 2018
)
, such as tiling, operator fusion, and operator reordering.
For each operator, it generates tile-level events, including computation (e.g., pushing/popping a tile from/to an SA, performing computation of a tile on a VU), SRAM access (e.g., reading/writing a tile), and ICI/DMA operations.
The simulator backend executes the events and reports statistics (e.g., execution time, memory traffic, FLOPs utilization) on each component.

We model the per-component power based on McPAT
(
Li et al. 2009
;
Zhai et al. 2021
)
and NeuroMeter
(
Tang et al. 2021
)
.
We first model the area of each component based on microarchitectural parameters (e.g., SA dimensions and SRAM capacity) and feature size.
Then, we model the static/dynamic power based on chip area, frequency, and technology node-specific parameters, including voltage levels and channel lengths.
We combine this power model and the results from the performance simulator to quantify the static/dynamic energy consumption for each component.
To implement ReGate, we modify the backend to support idle-detection-based power-gating and account for the wake-up delays.
We also modify the frontend to implement the component idleness analysis pass and add power management events to simulate the effects of
setpm
instructions. The idleness analysis and
setpm
instrumentation passes have a linear time complexity with respect to the number of instructions. They do not incur a noticeable increase in compilation time compared to other compiler passes
(
Developers 2025
)
. The code size inflation due to
setpm
is negligible since it does not occur frequently compared to other operations (see
Figure

19
), and most of them can fit into empty misc. slots.

5.
Discussion

Generalizability of
ReGate
.

Most AI chips today employ matrix units (e.g., systolic arrays), vector units, on-chip scratchpad SRAM, off-chip DRAM, and inter-chip links
(
NVIDIA 2022
;
AWS 2025
;
Jouppi et al. 2023
;
Coburn et al. 2025
;
Knowles 2021
;
Kennedy 2024
)
.
They may also employ specialized functional units for structured sparsity (e.g., sparse tensor cores in GPUs
(
Mishra et al. 2021
)
) or specific operators (e.g., embedding cores in TPUs
(
Jouppi et al. 2023
)
).
For dataflow architectures built of massive interconnected cores
(
Coburn et al. 2025
;
Knowles 2021
;
Kennedy 2024
)
, each core still consists of those components.
As the underutilization of each component is due to intrinsic characteristics of ML models (see
§
3
), there will be abundant opportunities for power gating in these AI chips.

ReGate
offers a systematic solution to power gate those components.
The
setpm
instruction can be extended to support specialized functional units, and it can also be implemented with non-VLIW ISAs.

Security implications.

Power gating amplifies the contrast in the power consumption between idle and active states.
This may potentially increase the risk of power-based side-channel attacks as the power usage pattern is highly correlated with component utilization
(
Kalyanapu et al. 2025
)
.

ReGate
allows the software to override the default hardware-managed power gating policies and disable power gating when processing sensitive data, which helps mitigate such attacks.
To completely obfuscate power usage patterns, we can further incorporate software-defined dynamic power management techniques like DVFS and clock gating.
We wish to explore this as future work.

ReGate-Base

ReGate-Full

First, instead of relying on imprecise idle period detection,

ReGate-Base
and
ReGate-HW

ReGate-Full
powers off unused SRAM segments with compiler knowledge,
ReGate-Base
and
ReGate-HW
must conservatively place unused SRAM cells into sleep mode, which still consumes substantial leakage power. This enables
ReGate-Full
to deliver 35.5% more SRAM energy savings than
ReGate-Base
and
ReGate-HW
.

ReGate-Full
achieves near-ideal energy saving (within 0.40% of
Ideal
).
This negligible gap is primarily due to spatial underutilization of VUs and the energy cost of power state transitions.

6.3.
Performance Impact

Figure

18
shows how different power gating designs impact execution performance.

ReGate-Base
introduces up to 4.6% overhead, primarily caused by its power gating policy’s inability to hide VU and SA wake-up delays.
SA delays are long for
ReGate-Base
as it needs to power on the entire SA before execution.

ReGate-HW
allows execution to start after powering on the first PE and overlaps execution with the wake-up of the remaining PEs.
This drastically reduces exposed SA wake-up delay, slashing performance overhead to under 0.6% on average.
Note that this performance advantage diminishes on memory- or ICI-bound workloads with low temporal SA utilization like LLM Decode and DLRM Inference, as SA wake-up delay is amortized by long SA idle periods.

ReGate-Full
leverages the software to wake up the SRAM/VU in advance.
It incurs negligible performance overhead (up to 0.44%).

Figure

19
quantifies the number of executed
setpm
instructions per 1K cycles for
ReGate-Full
.
For VUs, the compiler will not insert
setpm
when the idle interval is shorter than the 32-cycle BET (see
§
4.3
), implying that the maximum number of
setpm
s per 1K cycles must be less than
1000/32\approx 31
. The measured numbers are much lower than this (less than 20 on average), as many VU idle intervals are larger than the BET, and we only need a single
setpm
invocation to control all VUs.
For SRAM, the number of
setpm
s is negligible.
This is because most SRAM idle intervals come from the unused capacity, which is determined by the tile size demands of operators (see
§
7
).
The
setpm
only needs to be called infrequently when the tile size changes (e.g., between operator boundaries or different parts in a fused operator).
The
setpm
s incur negligible performance overhead as the wake-up delays can overlap with other instructions.
The performance and energy overheads of
setpm
s are greatly outweighed by the net energy savings.

6.5.
Sensitivity Analysis

The effect of power gating is affected by various factors, including the threshold voltage (Vth) and the SRAM data retention voltage (DRV), as well as design trade-offs between wake-up delay and leakage reduction.
The voltage parameters are determined by various factors, including feature size, transistor type, and circuit design choices.
For example, for the logic, an aggressive threshold voltage can reduce leakage power in low-power modes, but it may incur longer wake-up delays.
For the SRAM, smaller feature sizes lead to (relatively) higher DRV due to increased sensitivity to manufacturing variations
(
Raza et al. 2025
)
, making the sleep mode more leaky relative to the OFF mode. Techniques such as assist circuitry have been proposed to reduce leakage, but they come at the cost of additional area or dynamic power
(
Chang et al. 2020
)
.
This section evaluates
ReGate
under different voltage parameters and wake-up delays, as well as different NPU generations with various architectural parameters.

Varying voltage parameters.

To study the benefits of
ReGate
under different voltage parameters, in
Figure

21
, we vary the ratio between the leakage power during OFF/sleep mode and ON mode for the logic and SRAM.
The energy savings decrease as the OFF/sleep mode leakage power becomes relatively larger.
Nevertheless, even under the highest leakage power setting,
ReGate-Full
outperforms other designs and achieves 4.6%–16.4% energy savings.

Varying power-gate & wake-up delays.

Figure

22
shows the effect of different power-on/off delays on ReGate’s energy-saving benefits and performance overheads. In general, a longer delay slightly decreases the energy savings of ReGate as it increases the break-even time, resulting in fewer power-gating opportunities. It also leads to higher performance overhead in
ReGate-Base
and
ReGate-HW
, but not
ReGate-Full
, which leverages precise idle period information from the compiler to avoid power-gating a component if the idle period is too short to hide the power-on/off delay.

Varying NPU generations.

Figure

23
compares the benefits of ReGate on different NPU generations, including a projected future chip, NPU-E (see
Table

2
).
ReGate achieves substantial energy saving on all chips.
Compared to NPU-D, LLM training and prefill, the static energy savings on NPU-E are relatively less since they are compute-intensive and have high dynamic power consumption.
For LLM decode, DLRM, and SD, the energy savings on NPU-E is higher than NPU-D as NPU-E has a larger SRAM (256MB vs. 128MB) and SAs (256
\times
256 vs. 128
\times
128).
The larger units not only consume more static power but also suffer from lower utilization, so the energy savings are more obvious.
Due to the diverse resource demands of ML models, underutilization will remain a problem for future NPUs: ML workloads will still be compute/memory/ICI-bound while underutilizing the non-bottleneck resources.
Power gating alleviates the energy inefficiency due to this fundamental mismatch between workload demand and hardware resources.

Figure 24.
Operation carbon reduction of power gating.

Figure 25.
Overall carbon emission of ML workloads over a 10-year period with different device lifespans, assuming the energy efficiency will improve each year by the ratio of NPU-D over NPU-C. We use the embodied carbon for TPUv4/v5p
(
Schneider et al. 2025
)
. The optimal lifetime with the lowest carbon is highlighted with red bars and stars. For example, for Llama3.1-405B Decode, the optimal lifespan is 4 years (i.e., we should retire old chips and upgrade to a newer generation every 4 years) without power gating (

). With ReGate, we can extend the lifespan to 5 years.

6.6.
Carbon Efficiency Improvements

The total carbon emission of an NPU chip is the sum of embodied carbon (emission during manufacturing) and operational carbon (emission due to electricity consumption at runtime).
ReGate’s energy savings directly translate into the reduced operational carbon, as shown in
Figure

24
, assuming a carbon intensity of 0.0624 kgCO
2
e/kWh
(
Google 2024
)
and a 60% datacenter utilization
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
.
With power gating, ReGate reduces the operational carbon by 31.1%–62.9%.
These numbers are much higher than the energy savings in
Figure

17
, because static power dominates the energy consumption of idle chips, making ReGate’s benefits more obvious.

We study the impact of embodied carbon in
Figure

25
.
If a data center upgrades its NPU fleet too frequently, the embodied carbon will dominate since new NPUs must be acquired frequently.
Reusing old chips will help reduce such costs.
However, since old chips typically have worse energy efficiency than newer chips, their operational carbon emissions will be greater, so there will be no incentive to continue reusing old chips once the operational carbon overhead starts to outweigh the embodied carbon savings.
Exploiting this trade-off leads to an optimal lifespan that achieves the best carbon efficiency, as shown by the red bars and stars in
Figure

25
.

The benefits of reducing embodied carbon become more evident when operational carbon represents a smaller portion of total emissions.
Without power gating, the optimal device lifespan is 4–8 years (red bars in
Figure

25
). ReGate extends this range to 5–9 years (red stars in
Figure

25
).
ReGate demonstrates the effectiveness of power gating in improving the carbon efficiency of NPU chips, paving the way for future sustainable AI chip designs.

7.
Related Work

Architectural support for power management.

Power gating techniques have been widely deployed in generic processors (e.g., CPUs/GPUs). Commercial processors support power-saving modes at core-level (e.g., Intel C-states
(
Corporation 2020
)
). At sub-core granularity, for caches, prior works employ dynamic cache resizing, power-gating unused cachelines
(
Powell et al. 2000
)
, or putting cachelines in drowsy mode
(
Flautner et al. 2002
)
. For execution units, prior works have focused on heuristic- and ML-based idle period detection
(
Hu et al. 2004
;
Sadrosadati et al. 2019
;
Ravi and Lipasti 2017
)
and creating longer idle periods via instruction reordering
(
Kumar et al. 2014
;
Aghilinasab et al. 2016
)
. PowerChop
(
Laurenzano et al. 2016
)
leverages HW/SW co-design to power-gate non-critical components based on application semantics. It is tailored for the CPU architecture and cannot be applied to NPUs.
There are also prior studies on compiler-directed power optimizations for CPUs
(
Giraldo et al. 2016
;
Roy et al. 2009
)
. They focused on instruction reordering to create longer idle periods and inserting power management directives based on runtime profiling of component idleness.

ReGate
thoroughly investigates how to support power gating on NPU chips and overcomes the unique architectural obstacles of power gating NPUs. It proposes a practical and effective solution and exploits the predictability of ML workloads to enable precise software-managed power gating.

Prior works have also investigated power management techniques for AI chips.
Most of them focused on dynamic power, such as dynamic voltage-frequency scaling (DVFS) and clock gating.
They leveraged the power profiles of each DNN layer to select the optimal voltage-frequency setting within the power budget
(
Agrawal et al. 2021
)
and regulate the peak current using software-controlled pipeline stall signals
(
Kar et al. 2024
)
.
The Qualcomm Hexagon processor
(
Kalyanam et al. 2021
)
adjusts the instruction-issue rate of each thread to throttle its power based on thread priorities.
DVFS has also been exploited to adjust the power budget based on input query patterns and latency SLO for ML inference applications
(
Tambe et al. 2023
)
.

ReGate
focuses on static power and is orthogonal to these techniques.
UPTPU
(
Pandey et al. 2021
)
exploits spatial underutilization for power-gating systolic arrays, but it relies on non-volatile memory (i.e., STT-MRAM) for zero-weight detection and power-gating the weight register, which introduces manufacturing complexity and cost.

ReGate
provides a systematic and practical solution for power-gating all components on an NPU chip.

System support for power management.

A few studies to improve the energy efficiency of AI systems have been proposed.

\mu
-Serve
(
Qiu et al. 2024
)
, Envpipe
(
Choi et al. 2023
)
, and Perseus
(
Chung et al. 2024
)
leverage DVFS to optimize energy consumption for ML training and inference.
Power-aware data center scheduling techniques
(
Stojkovic et al. 2025a
;
Stojkovic et al. 2025b
)
route inference requests to evenly distribute cooling and power demands across aisles. ReGate optimizes the energy consumption of individual NPU chips. It can be deployed in tandem with systems-level solutions for further energy efficiency improvement.

Carbon-aware AI systems.

The carbon efficiency of AI is receiving increasing attention in the community.
Prior works analyzed carbon footprints of computer systems
(
Gupta et al. 2021
;
Schneider et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2023a
)
. LLMCarbon
(
Faiz et al. 2024
)
and ACT
(
Gupta et al. 2022
)
simulate emissions for different LLMs and AI chips. GreenSKU
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
combines energy-efficient CPUs with reused components to build greener cloud servers. Google investigated the impact of model architecture, training practices, and infrastructure on DNN training emissions
(
Patterson et al. 2021
;
Patterson et al. 2022
)
. EcoServe
(
Li et al. 2025
)
proposes carbon-aware resource management for LLM serving.
ReGate contributes by developing architectural solutions to improve the energy efficiency of NPU chips, reducing operational carbon emissions.

8.
Conclusion

We reveal significant power gating opportunities on NPU chips.
We present ReGate, which enables power gating in NPU chips.
ReGate supports both HW- and SW-based power gating and enables best-fit decisions via the extended NPU ISA.
ReGate improves the energy efficiency of NPUs with negligible performance overhead.
