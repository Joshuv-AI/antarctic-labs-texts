Histopathologic observations in a coccidiosis model of Eimeria tenella
Authors: Zhixin Sun, Linlin Chen, Mengyu Lai, Xixi Zhang, Jie Li, Zhiping Li, Dongming Yang, Mengyang Zhao, Dongdong Wang, Pei Wen, Fengting Gou, Yuexin Dai, Yilan Ji, Wen Li, Deming Zhao, Xianyong Liu, Lifeng Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpecies of the genus Eimeria cause coccidiosis in chickens, resulting in a huge burden to the poultry industry worldwide. Eimeria tenella is one of the most prevalent chicken coccidia in China, and E. tenella infection causes hemorrhagic cecitis.MethodsUsing an established model of coccidiosis in chickens combined with necropsy, imaging of pathological tissue sections, and other techniques, we evaluated the gross and microscopic lesions of cecal tissue within 15 days after inoculation with sporulated oocysts and described the endogenetic developmental process and relationship between E. tenella infection and enteritis development in chickens.ResultsWe observed three generations of merogony and gamogony in E. tenella. We observed gross lesions in the cecum from 84 hpi (hours post inoculation) and microscopic lesions from 60 hpi. The lesions in the cecum mainly exhibited hemorrhagic enteritis. Their severity increased with the onset of the second generation of merogony. The lesions began to alleviate by the end of the endogenous stages of E. tenella.ConclusionWe show, for the first time, the complete observation of a series of changes in enteritis caused by 5 × 103 E. tenella oocysts. This study provides reference materials for E. tenella research and pathological diagnosis.Keywords: cecitis, chicken, Eimeria tenella, gamonts, histopathology, meronts

INTRODUCTION

Eimeria species are members of phylum Apicomplexa. More than 1200 Eimeria parasites have been reported in different hosts, and 7 species, namely Eimeria tenella, Eimeria maxima, Eimeria acervulina, Eimeria brunetti, Eimeria mitis, Eimeria necatrix, and Eimeria praecox, 1 are restricted to chickens and parasitize the digestive tract, 2 resulting in coccidiosis and major impacts on the global broiler breeding industry. In 2020, Blake et al proposes new 3 species of chicken Eimeria coccidia. 3 As of 2016, the global economic burden caused by chicken coccidiosis was predicted at 10.4 billion pounds. 4

In 1891, Railliet and Lucet first described what was then named Coccidium tenellum in the ceca of chickens, the name of which was quickly changed to E. tenella in 1913. 5 Since these initial descriptions, E. tenella has become the most studied chicken coccidium 6 and is the most prevalent Eimeria species in Anhui Province 7 and the second most prevalent species in Hubei and Henan provinces 8 of China.

The life cycle of E. tenella consists of separate exogenous and endogenous stages. The endogenous stage is subdivided into merogony (schizogony) and gamogony. 9 , 10 , 11 Merogony is the stage of asexual multiplication that occurs before sexual reproduction. During this stage, the parasite nucleus replicates at a high rate, forming cells with many nuclei. Asexual multiplication of the Houghton strain of E. tenella (one of the defined reference strains of E. tenella used for research) consists of three rounds of merogony, resulting in generations of schizonts 12 containing different numbers of merozoites. Asexual multiplication is followed by sexual development. Sexual reproduction can be divided into three events: gametocytogenesis, gametogenesis, and fertilization of macrogametes by microgametes. Mature microgametocytes produce ~97 microgametes to fertilize a macrogamete, resulting in a zygote. 13 The zygote produces a protective wall, forming an oocyst, and is released from the host. 1 , 14 , 15 In 1929, the complete life cycle of E. tenella was reported using hand‐drawn images. 5

Since the initial characterization of E. tenella life cycle, pathological and morphological analyses have been used to confirm and approximately identify the presence of Eimeria species throughout the specific parts of chicken intestine. Pathological manifestations of E. tenella infection include severe hemorrhagic cecitis. Edema and mucosal thickening are often observed, and an inflammatory cellular infiltrate, primarily consisting of monocytes and granulocytes, is visible in the crypts and lamina propria. 16 Despite these early findings, little is known regarding the morphology of E. tenella development during infection in vivo. Understanding these morphological distinctions may aid in rapid diagnosis and treatment.

Here, we studied the development of lesions within 15 days of E. tenella infection in chickens and documented in detail cecal lesions that occur at 5 × 103 oocyst infection. Using microscopy, we illustrated and described the different endophytic developmental stages of coccidia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental model

E. tenella–sporulated oocysts were provided by the National Animal Protozoa Laboratory and College of Veterinary Medicine, China Agricultural University, Beijing, China. Seven‐day‐old broiler chickens (Beijing Arbor Acres Poultry Breeding, Beijing, China) were randomly divided into two groups. For E. tenella infection, each chicken was orally administered 5 × 103 E. tenella–sporulated oocysts. Control chickens were administered phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). All chickens were reared in a coccidia‐free facility and were allowed unlimited access to food and water without anticoccidial drugs and antibiotics. One control group chicken and three coccidia‐infected chickens were killed at 24, 48, 60, 72, 84, 96, 108, 120, 132, 144, 156, 168, 192, 216, 240, 264, 288, 312, 336, and 360 h post inoculation (hpi) for histological analyses.

Necropsy and histopathology

Gross lesions of the intestine were imaged and scored using a previously reported method. 17 Immediately after euthanasia, the distal intestine, one cecum, and the rectum were collected. All samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin wax, and sectioned (4 μm). Tissue sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Leica, Germany). Tissue sections obtained before 156 hpi were also stained with periodic acid–Schiff (PAS, Servicebio, Wuhan) and toluidine blue (Solarbio Life Science, Beijing). Another cecum was opened to obtain materials to prepare smears of the cecal contents, which were stained with Giemsa stain (Solarbio Life Science). Sections and smears were examined under a microscope (Olympus DP72), and digital images were obtained using an Olympus BX51 microscope. A microscope lesion score (measured on a four‐point scale) was used to evaluate E. tenella infection severity in the digestive tract. 18 Coccidia counts were measured at 400× magnification.

Transmission electron microscopy

Infected cecum tissue was cut into small pieces and fixed in 2.5% electron microscopy–grade glutaraldehyde (Solarbio Life Science) and then in 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for 2 h. Fixed tissue pieces were dehydrated and embedded in resin. Ultrathin sections were mounted onto copper grids and stained with 4% uranyl acetate and lead citrate and observed using a transmission electron microscopy (JEM‐1230, Japan).

RESULTS

Merogony of E. tenella in chicken cecum

First‐generation merogony

Immature first‐generation meronts (M1) were initially observed at 48 hpi at the bottom of the glands. Sporozoites became round in parasitophorous vacuoles (PV), forming a nearly circular meront. Subsequently, the meront became irregular (Figure 1A, panels a and b), and the nuclei began to divide, increasing in size and expanding the host cell. Deeply stained granules embedded in a less deeply stained matrix were found in PVs (Figure 1A, panel c), with sizes of (33.2–21.0) × (28.6–18.5) μm (n = 6). Mature first‐generation merozoites (m1) became short and rod shaped and were relatively closely distributed in PVs (Figure 1A, panel d; Figure 1C, panel a; and Figure 1D, panel b). The small size made it difficult to observe the internal structures of m1 under the magnification of an optical microscope (order of magnitude of ~1 μm).

Second‐generation merogony

The development of second‐generation meronts (M2) was similar to that of M1, except that host cells were invaded by mature m1. From 60 hpi onward, immature M2 were observed under a microscope (Figure 1A, panel e; Figure 1B, panel a; Figure 1C, panel a; and Figure 1D, panel a). The parasitized epithelial cells migrated through the basal membrane and invaded the lamina propria (Figure 1A, panel f), and meronts were typically located at the base of the villi, usually in groups. The number of M2 nuclei, as well as the size, then began to increase sharply, ultimately reaching nuclei numbers and sizes larger than those observed for mature M1 (Figure 1A, panel g; Figure 1B, panel b; Figure 1C, panel c; and Figure 1D, panel c). Mature M2 were typified by slender, tightly packed merozoites with sizes of (45.2–36.3) × (41.7–28.0) μm (n = 20) and were observable from 84 hpi onward. Mature second‐generation merozoites (m2) were the largest of the three generations of merozoites. These merozoites demonstrated a strip‐like shape, with a sharp‐pointed extremity and a thicker rounded extremity, and were usually grouped in several groups tightly arranged in parallel with M2 with a size of (17.5–14.7) × (2.4–1.3) μm (n = 20). Immature M2 nuclei were stained purple after Giemsa staining (Figure 1C, panel c). Mature M2 contained numerous m2 and appeared darker after Giemsa staining (Figure 1C, panel d). Mature m2 exhibited obvious PAS positivity (Figure 1B, panel c). A single m2 was visible on the cecal smear, where the blunt end stained purple and the more pointed end stained blue after Giemsa staining (Figure 1C, panel d).

Third‐generation merogony

Development of third‐generation meronts (M3) was similar to that of M2 except that the development of M3 was completed in the epithelium of the cecal mucosa (Figure 1A, panels i–k). Immature M3 generally had more prominent nuclei in the center and some developing third‐generation merozoites (m3). As M3 matured, the nuclei were often difficult to observe (maturation was reached at 108 hpi; Figure 1A, panel l; Figure 1B, panel d; and Figure 1D, panel d). M3 were smaller and measured only (14.2–11.4) × (13.3–9.0) μm (n = 20). M3 contained fewer m3, and mature m3 were similar in shape to m2, with a sharp‐pointed extremity and a thicker rounded extremity measuring only (9.4–5.7) × (2.4–1.2) μm (n = 20). m3 were usually arranged in parallel on the side farther from the cecal lumen.

Sexual development stages of E. tenella in chicken cecum

Gamonts

The sex of early gamonts could not be identified, and a round, uniformly dense mass was visible in the PV (Figure 2A, panel e). During development, most gamonts developed into macrogametes, and a small number developed into microgametocytes.

Microgametes

Microgamonts were morphologically indistinguishable from macrogamonts (Figure 2A, panels a and b, and Figure 2B, panel a). Development of microgametes began at ~120 hpi in enterocytes. Mature microgamonts were approximately (21.4–12.6) × (16.0–8.5) μm (n = 20) and were surrounded by dozens of microgametes (Figure 2A, panels c and d; Figure 2B panel b; Figure 2C, panel a; and Figure 2D, panels a and b). There were significantly fewer microgamonts than macrogametes. We performed statistical analyses on data from 20 high‐magnification fields of view, and the sex ratio (number of male gametocytes: number of female gametocytes) was ~0.096. The microgametes appeared purple under H&E staining, PAS staining, and Giemsa staining. The residue in the middle was PAS positive and blue as observed after Giemsa staining. Mature microgametes left the microgamonts to fertilize macrogametes (Figure 2A, panel d).

Macrogametes

Macrogametes were usually observed in enterocytes (Figure 2A, panels f and g; Figure 2C, panels b and c; and Figure 2D, panel c) and were present in the PVs of cytoplasm. As they developed, they pushed the nucleus flat to one side of the cell. The diameter of the mature macrogametes was (19.6–16.1) × (17.6–12.4) μm (n = 20). The microscope showed coarse, round, irregularly arranged particles in macrogametes. Some of these particles were pink, whereas others were orange. These particles formed the wall‐forming body (WFB). The WFB edges in the gamete were set and fused to form the oocyst wall.

Oocysts

Oocysts were also found in enterocytes and started to appear at 132 hpi (Figure 2A, panel h; Figure 2B, panel d; Figure 2C, panel d; and Figure 2D, panel d). These oocysts had an eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and a well‐developed wall. The eosinophilic granules contained starch granules, so PAS staining was strongly positive. The size of the oocysts observed in smears was (23.1–18.3) × (18.5–15.0) μm (n = 20), whereas that observed in tissue sections was (20.9–16.1) × (15.9–12.3) μm (n = 20).

Transmission electron microscopy

Tissue samples were observed at 48, 108, and 144 hpi. Cecal tissue from all examined infected chickens showed evidence of infection. Merozoites were found within meronts in epithelial cells (Figure 3). Mature M1 were observed in samples at 48 hpi (Figure 3A,B). Mature M1 were located in the cecal epithelium, causing deformation of the epithelium and pushing the nucleus to one side, flattening it. A centrally located parasite nucleus with high electron density was observed in the PV surrounded by many short, rod‐shaped m1 (Figure 3A). A nearly circular nucleus was observed in m1, and some scattered amylopectin granules with high electron density were identified around the nucleus (Figure 3B). E. tenella undergoing M2 could be observed in samples at 108 hpi (Figure 3C,D). Compared with M1, M2 were significantly larger, and more m2 were packed in the PV, making parasite nuclei invisible in some cases and part of the m2 aligned in parallel (Figure 3C). Mature m2 were banana shaped, with a slightly pointed end and a slightly rounded end. Mature m2 were longer than m1, whereas the widths were similar between the two. A nearly circular nucleus was observed in the center of m2. Amylopectin granules were observed around the nucleus (Figure 3D). Macrogametes and oocysts were observed in tissue samples at 144 hpi (Figure 3E,F), which were also localized in the PVs of intestinal epithelial cells. In macrogametes, in addition to amylopectin granules, some circular, electron‐dense structures were observed, which were the WFB (Figure 3E). In oocysts, the WFB disappeared, but the oocyst wall with high electron density could be observed (Figure 3F).

Gross autopsy findings

Autopsy revealed no obvious gross lesions in the intestinal tract and intestinal lumen over time in the control group. In infected chickens, gross pathological changes were observed from 84 hpi onward. From 144 hpi, cecum bleeding was markedly increased. After 168 hpi, the cecum core appeared, and it did not disappear until 288 hpi. It was not until 360 hpi that the gross score returned to 0, indicating that cecal damage did not fully recover until the end of the experiment (Figure 4). Because severity grade scores are neither continuous nor normally distributed, using median assessment may cover a low incidence of events, and simply using the average to evaluate the situation makes it difficult to detect data dispersion. 19 Therefore, all gross lesion severity score data in the form of heat maps are shown in the top right of each image in Figure 4.

Histopathologic alterations

All control group chickens exhibited no pathological tissue change, and no parasites were found during examination under a microscope. In the infected group, E. tenella was more likely to parasitize the middle and distal segments of the cecum, consistent with findings from previous studies.

No obvious histopathologic changes were observed until 60 hpi. A small number of parasites developed in intestinal epithelial cells after 48 hpi. At 60 and 72 hpi, only a small number of inflammatory cells could be found in the cecum (Table 1). Infiltrating inflammatory cells were predominantly heterophilic granulocytes.

Serious injury of the mucosa gradually led to severe hemorrhagic enteritis (Figure 5). At 96 hpi, this inflammatory reaction extended multifocally and spread from the mucosal epithelium to the serosal surface. Histopathologic observations included diffusely infiltrating marked mixed inflammatory cells, including eosinophils and lymphocytes. As the parasites developed, a large area of the cecal mucosa was destroyed, a large number of densely distributed M2 were visible in the submucosa, and a large number of M3 and gametophytes were observed in the mucosal epithelium. The tips of the villi were mostly broken off, resulting in truncation and fusion of villi; villi blunting was also observed.

Morphological changes in chicken ceca experimentally infected with Eimeria tenella as observed under low magnification. Tissue sections were taken from the middle part of the cecum, placed mucosa side up, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The scale bar applies to all images. hpi, hours post inoculation.

Starting at 144 hpi, the condition no longer worsened. Severe bleeding gradually declined; however, the wall of the cecum was still significantly thickened, numerous inflammatory cells were still visible, and fibroblasts still proliferated and repaired the intestine. At this time, many parasites and shedding cell fragments accumulated in the crypt and the mucosal layer, and a small number of parasites may have also been observed under the ruptured mucosal muscle layer. Cecal epithelial cell proliferation and repair and a significant increase in the number of goblet cells were observed in later stages of the experiment. Parasites were visible in tissue slices and smears in the cecum until the end of the experiment, and the damage was not fully recovered. The histological lesions exhibited proliferative cecitis.

Some endogenous stages and histopathologic lesions were found in the posterior small intestine and rectum. M1, M2, macrogametes, and oocysts were found in tissue sections of the rectum. M2, microgamonts, macrogametes, and oocysts were found in tissue sections of the small intestine. The presence of parasites in these positions was less frequent than that in the cecum. From 48 hpi onward, parasites were visible in the rectum in most chickens and were observed in the small intestine in only a small number of chickens. The small number of E. tenella in the posterior small intestine caused only slight histopathologic changes. Mild inflammatory responses were observed in the rectum compared to the inflammatory responses observed in the cecum. However, this mild inflammation and subsequent minor injuries should not be ignored.

DISCUSSION

Different generations of meronts have different sizes and shapes and appear in different parts of the cecum at different times. However, as the speed of development is not completely uniform, different‐generation meronts can be observed simultaneously. 20 M1 and M2 developed in cells of the crypts, whereas M3 and gamonts were located either in the crypts or in the surface epithelium. 21 Immature meronts of each generation were very similar in appearance. Therefore, the number of generations was determined by carefully assessing histologic sections. H&E, PAS, and toluidine blue staining are all staining techniques commonly used to visualize parasites in tissue sections. H&E staining is the most commonly used technique for pathologic diagnosis of coccidiosis. Klimes et al. 20 suggested that sex differentiation could be performed using the results of PAS staining of m3. This view was challenged by D. J. P. Ferguson (unpublished observations). The m3 observed in this paper did not show differences in color, so using PAS staining for sex identification of merozoites does not appear to be reliable. Toluidine blue staining provides a clear view of the internal structures of the parasite, such as the WFB in macrogamonts. 22 Giemsa staining helps to demonstrate the structure of merozoites, which tend to cluster together in tissue sections and are difficult to observe in detail. 23 , 24

McDonald and Shirley 21 observed large ((27.8 ± 5.7) × (19.7 ± 3.6) μm), mature M1 first at 48 hpi and then abundantly at 60 hpi. It has also been reported in the literature that immature M1 can be observed at 38 hpi, where refractile particles are visible. 25 This experiment was not designed with a time point of sampling between 24 and 48 hpi, so M1 were observed as early as 48 hpi. From 60 hpi, the second generation of trophozoites was observed. At 84 hpi, there were a few mature M2, and by 96 hpi, the M2 were mostly mature. Mature M2 were (40.6 ± 5.2) × (28.0 ± 3.6) μm in size. As in previous reports, PVs are more likely to be distributed in epithelial cells on the side near the intestinal lumen, crowding the nucleus down. 25 M3 were not observed until 108 hpi. Sexual stages of the Houghton strain were initially observed in abundance from 108 and 120 hpi. E. tenella at different life cycle stages was observed in this experiment at essentially the same times as noted in the literature. Unfortunately, we did not observe sporozoites in this experiment; to observe sporozoites, a higher (e.g., 3 × 107 sporulated oocysts per chicken) infective dose is required, and sporozoites are most readily observed in large numbers at 12 hpi. 25

We observed that more macrogamonts were present than microgamonts, as reported in the literature. West et al. calculated the number of macrogamonts to be about 20 times that of microgametocytes, 13 similar to what we observed. The size of the large gametes reported in the literature is 16.0 × 9.0 μm, 26 which was similar to our measurements. The location of the host cells for E. tenella indicated most host‐parasitized cells were enterocytes. Some E. tenella can break through the muscularis mucosa and invade the submucosa, 27 a process that may potentially be assisted by mast cells. 28 We observed enterocyte‐parasitizing M2 at the lamina propria and submucosa at 84 hpi.

The dose of 5 × 103 sporulated oocysts per chicken caused decreases in weight gain but was not fatal. 12 Previous studies were also conducted using this infectious dose. In particular, this dose was used for increasing coccidian oocysts and was shown to be a relatively safe dose for animals. 29 Therefore, this dose was selected to study the developmental status and pathological changes in E. tenella in the present study. In this experiment, we observed the death of one animal, so we consider that this infection dose is also not absolutely safe for 7‐day‐old chickens.

The types of histopathologic changes that we observed were essentially consistent with those observed by others. 30 , 31 , 32 We observed a pronounced thickening of the cecum wall, in which thickening occurred in both the mucosal and muscular layers. Inoculation of 1‐day‐old chicks with 100 or 1000 sporulated oocysts and 2‐ to 3‐week‐old chicks with 1.5 × 105 sporulated oocysts both resulted in significant thickening of the intestinal wall. 33 , 34 Lesions began to appear 3 days after infection, the top regions of the villi were visible, the lesions were most severe at 6–8 days after infection, the intestinal mucosa was shed, the intrinsic layer was exposed, repair began 8 days after infection, and most of the injuries were covered with a layer of nascent mucosal epithelium on about the 14th day. 35 Both the time of worsening of the tissue lesions and the time of the sudden increase in the number of coccidian occurred simultaneously. From 144 hpi, the oocysts were expelled from the chickens, the amount of E. tenella decreased, and the damage of the cecum gradually recovered. Host cells of meronts and gamonts were shown to exhibit significant nucleus enlargement, 36 similar to what we observed in the present study.

Because E. tenella also has multiple stages of endogenous development in the posterior small intestine and rectum, we showed again that E. tenella can complete endogenous development in sites other than the cecum, 37 Support for this was also found in the literature. Position specificity is due to the interaction between microneme proteins (MICs) and their receptors on the surface of target host cells. Among them, the protein EtMIC3 binds mainly to the cecum, and a small part binds to the posterior part of the intestine and even less to the middle of the intestine. 38 , 39

CONCLUSIONS

Despite minimal difficulty in clinically confirming the diagnosis of coccidiosis caused by E. tenella infection (through unique lesion sites and observation of mucosal scrapings), more detailed morphological data on the field of scientific research is needed. The coccidiosis model we constructed can help researchers observe the characteristics of the lesions at different periods in depth and provide assistance in drug and vaccine discovery and development for coccidiosis. We look forward to more studies on the morphology of chicken coccidia and other parasites. Histopathologic diagnosis, as an auxiliary means of molecular diagnosis, plays an important role in poultry farming and research on avian diseases.
