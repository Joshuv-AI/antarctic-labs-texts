Confined-deconfined interface tension and latent heat in OPEN SU ⁡ ( N CLOSE ) \operatorname{SU}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right) gauge theory Preprint: HIP-2025-21/TH
Tobias Rindlisbacher, Kari Rummukainen, Ahmed Salami

Abstract
We present high-precision lattice results for the confined-deconfined interface tension and the latent heat of pure S ​ U ​ ( N ) SU(N) gauge theories up to N = 10 N=10 and investigate their asymptotic N N -dependency. For both quantities we observe the leading N 2 N^{2} behaviour and subleading corrections, with the result for the interface tension σ / T c 3 = 0.0182 ​ ( 7 ) ​ N 2 − 0.194 ​ ( 15 ) \sigma/T_{c}^{3}=0.0182(7)N^{2}-0.194(15) and for the latent heat L / T c 4 = 0.360 ​ ( 6 ) ​ N 2 − 1.88 ​ ( 17 ) L/T_{c}^{4}=0.360(6)N^{2}-1.88(17) . We use the mixed phase ensemble method - where the system is constrained so that half of the volume is in the confined phase and the other half in the deconfined phase - and the interface tension is obtained by measuring the capillary wave fluctuation spectra of the interfaces between the two phases. The method bypasses supercritical slowing down from which other methods for determining the interface tension suffer, and as a by-product produces accurate estimates of the critical inverse gauge coupling as a function of the inverse temperature. We use the latter to determine the lattice beta function values, required to compute the latent heat from the discontinuity in the average plaquette action across the confined-deconfined transition.



I
Introduction

Extensive work using lattice simulation methods has unambiguously shown that non-Abelian gauge field theories have a phase transition from low temperature and density confined phase to high temperature or density deconfined phase, provided the number of fermionic degrees of freedom is not too large (for an overview, see ref.
Aarts et al. 2023
). Pure SU(
N
) gauge theories at large
N
are a particularly interesting case for studying thermodynamics in strongly coupled theories. The transition is of second order at
N=2
and first order at
N\geq 3
, becoming increasingly strong as
N
grows. The absence of fermions means that it is possible to obtain precise results with moderate computational effort. At large
N
this enables studying
the approach to the ’t Hooft limit
’t Hooft 1974
and testing duality relations
Witten 1998
;
Ares et al. 2022
.

The latent heat and the interface tension are the most important quantities characterizing a first order phase transition, since they determine to a large extent the degree of supercooling (or -heating) and the energy release in the phase transition. In the context of cosmology, phase transitions in dark sector SU(
N
) extensions of the Standard Model have been analyzed in light of gravitational wave production
Huang et al. 2021
;
Sanchez-Garitaonandia and van de
Vis 2024
;
Ares et al. 2020
;
Bigazzi et al. 2021
;
Morgante et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2022
.

In this article we report high-precision results for the latent heat and the interface tension of the deconfinement phase transition in SU(
N
) gauge theories at
N=4,5,8
and
10
. We unambiguously observe the leading
O(N^{2})
behavior and
O(1)
subleading terms, as expected in the ’t Hooft limit. At the same time, we obtain the critical lattice couplings as functions of the imaginary time extent (
N_{t}
) of the lattice, and determine the lattice
\beta
-functions in the relevant coupling constant range.

While the large-
N
equation of state
Panero 2009
, critical temperature
T_{c}

Lucini et al. 2012
, and the latent heat
Lucini et al. 2005
have been studied extensively, the interface tension remains poorly determined
Lucini et al. 2005
. The results in this work represent a significant increase in precision compared with the existing literature.
Preliminary results were published in ref.
Salami et al. 2025
.

We use the
mixed phase ensemble
method, where the order parameter, in our case the Polyakov line, is constrained so that half of the lattice is in the confined phase and the other half is in the deconfined phase. With periodic boundary conditions there will be two phase interfaces spanning the system. This method enables us to obtain a precise value of the critical coupling, the interface tension and the latent heat.

With the mixed phase ensemble the interface tension can be measured using
capillary wave theory
(CWT)
Buff et al. 1965
;
Rowlinson and Widom 1989
, which states that the magnitude of long-wavelength transverse fluctuations of the interface are inversely proportional to the interface tension.
It has been applied in fluid dynamics to measure the interface tension both in experiments
Rowlinson and Widom 1989
;
Penfold 2001
and in atomistic simulations
Chacón and Tarazona 2003
;
Senapati and Berkowitz 2001
.
To our knowledge the interface fluctuations were first used to measure the interface tension in a lattice field theory context by Moore and Turok almost 30 years ago
Moore and Turok 1997
, but it appears to have been used since much less than order parameter distribution (histogram) methods
Binder 1981
. The advantage of the mixed phase method is that it does not suffer from supercritical slowing down, as described in Section
II
, enabling the use of large volume lattices. We note that essentially similar fluctuation physics underlies the Lüscher term in QCD Wilson loops
Luscher 1981
.

The structure of this paper is the following: in Sec.
II
we describe the implementation of mixed phase simulations and the measurement of the critical couplings. In Sec.
III
we discuss the capillary wave method
for the interface tension measurement, and in Sec.
IV
the lattice
\beta
-function and the latent heat. We conclude in Sec.
V
.

II
Mixed phase lattice runs

II.1
Lattice setup

We consider SU(
N
) gauge field theory on a 4-dimensional Euclidean lattice with periodic boundary conditions and
N_{x}\times N_{y}\times N_{z}\times N_{t}
lattice sites, where
N_{t}
is the number of lattice sites to imaginary time direction.
We use the standard Wilson single-plaquette action
Wilson 1974
,

S=-\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{x;\mu<\nu}\mathrm{Re}\tr\bigl(\big.U_{\mu\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\big.\bigr)\ ,

(1)

with the inverse lattice gauge coupling
\beta=2N/g^{2}
and the plaquette variables being defined by

U_{\mu\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=U_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)U_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+\hat{\mu}}}\right)U^{\dagger}_{\mu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+\smash{\hat{\nu}}}}\right)U^{\dagger}_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\ .

(2)

The temperature
T
and the lattice spacing
a
are connected through the relation

T=1/(N_{t}\,a)
.
As usual, with fixed
N_{t}
the temperature is controlled by varying the inverse lattice coupling
\beta
, through the inverse lattice beta function
\mathrm{d}\log a/\mathrm{d}\beta
. The order parameter for the transition
is the volume average of the Polyakov line,

P_{L}=\frac{1}{V_{s}}\sum_{\bar{x}}\underbrace{\tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U_{t}(\bar{x},0)U_{t}(\bar{x},1)\ldots U_{t}(\bar{x},N_{t}-1)}}\right)}_{P_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{x}}}\right)}\,,

(3)

where
\bar{x}
is a spatial coordinate vector,
V_{s}=N_{x}N_{y}N_{z}
the number of spatial lattice sites, and
P_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{x}}}\right)
the local Polyakov line observable. In the confined (low-temperature) phase the Polyakov line expectation value vanishes, whereas there are
N
degenerate deconfined (high-temperature) phases:

\langle P_{L}\rangle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\mbox{confined}\\
c\,e^{i2\pi n/N},&\mbox{deconfined}\end{array}}}\right.

(4)

where
n=0,\ldots,(N-1)
and
0<c\in\mathbb{R}
is a function of
N
,
N_{t}
, and
\beta
.

We restrict the imaginary time
N_{t}\geq 5
in order to avoid the unphysical bulk phase
Lucini et al. 2005
. Indeed, it turns out that the lattice effects are still too large at
N_{t}=5
for it to be useful for continuum limit extrapolation.

The update algorithm is a combination of a heat bath
Cabibbo and Marinari 1982
;
Kennedy and Pendleton 1985
sweep followed by four overrelaxation
Brown and Woch 1987
;
de Forcrand and Jahn 2005
update sweeps. We use SU(2) subgroup updates for both heat bath and overrelaxation.
1

1

We did not observe significant benefit from the full SU(
N
) overrelaxation by de Forcrand and Jahn
de Forcrand and Jahn 2005
, presumably due to its algorithmic complexity on GPUs.

The simulation program uses the
hila
lattice field theory programming framework
HIL
, allowing the same program to be used on different computing platforms, including GPUs.

II.2
Order parameter probability distribution

Let us consider a first-order phase transition with two distinct phases. By definition, at the critical point the two phases have equal probabilities in the large-volume limit. A schematic order parameter distribution is shown in Fig.
1
: the two high probability peaks in the distribution correspond to the order parameter values characteristic for the two distinct bulk phases, which are bridged by a mixed phase region with suppressed probability.

P_{\text{max}}
P_{\text{min}}

Figure 1:
Schematic real part of the Polyakov line
\mathrm{Re\,}P_{L}
distribution at
\beta_{c}
(dashed curve), with restriction to the central region (red lines).

In Fig.
2
we show a configuration on a periodic volume near the half-way point between the peaks. The two bulk phase domains each occupy half of the volume and are separated by phase interfaces. Due to the interface tension, the area of the interfaces is minimized. With the geometry shown in Fig.
2
, the two interfaces settle in planes perpendicular to the
\hat{z}
-direction. In a sufficiently large volume the central part of the probability distribution becomes flat and the volume fractions occupied by the two phases can therefore change within some range without changing the total free energy. Within these bounds, the two phase interfaces can therefore be considered independent, since they can move freely with respect to each other.

Figure 2:
Two-phase configuration with two planar interfaces corresponding to the central region of
\mathrm{Re\,}P_{L}
distribution in Fig.
1
.

In SU(
N
) gauge theory there is one confined phase and
N
degenerate deconfined phases, all of which have equal probability at the critical point in a sufficiently large periodic volume. Thus, the system is
N
times more likely to be deconfined than confined at the critical point. However, in what follows, we restrict the state of the system so that only the confined and the
n=0
deconfined phase (see Eq.(
4
)) appear. In this case, the expectation value of the Polyakov line is real, and in the following we use the real part of the Polyakov line,
\mathrm{Re\,}P_{L}
, as our order parameter. Now, the confined phase with
\langle\mathrm{Re\,}P_{L}\rangle=0
and the deconfined phase
\langle\mathrm{Re\,}P_{L}\rangle>0
have equal probability at the critical point.

We note that the slightly skewed shape of the probability peaks in Fig.
1
is caused by bubble-like mixed phase configurations; see ref.
Moore et al. 2001
for a more detailed discussion and
Rummukainen et al. 2025
for an application in SU(8) gauge theory. These configurations are important for measuring the critical bubble nucleation rate.

The suppressed probability of the mixed states is due to the interface tension
\sigma
:

\frac{P_{\textrm{min}}}{P_{\textrm{max}}}\approx\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{A\,\sigma}{T_{c}}+\text{finite-{V} corr.}}}\right)\ ,

(5)

where
A
is the total area of the interfaces and
P_{\textrm{max}}
and
P_{\textrm{min}}
are the maximum and minimum values of the probability distribution of the order parameter, see Fig.
1
. This property is often used to measure the interface tension in lattice simulations. If the interface tension is large, the probability of the mixed phase may be very strongly suppressed, possibly by tens of orders of magnitude. In these cases, standard Monte Carlo sampling will not visit the mixed states. The probability barrier can be overcome with various modified sampling methods: multicanonical
Berg and Neuhaus 1992
, Wang-Landau
Wang and Landau 2001
, density of states
Bennett et al. 2024
, or applications of Jarzynski’s theorem
Caselle et al. 2016
. Multicanonical methods have been used very successfully to study phase transitions in electroweak-like theories on the lattice, see for example
Kajantie et al. 1996
. However, while modified sampling methods help to overcome the dominant part of the probability suppression,
these methods still suffer from a milder form of supercritical slowing down as the volume becomes large: there is a condensation barrier for the initial formation of the mixed phase bubbles
Nußbaumer et al. 2008
;
Hällfors and Rummukainen 2025
.

We note here that the interface tension can also be measured from the components of the energy-momentum tensor, as was done in early work in SU(3) gauge theory
Kajantie et al. 1990
. However, the energy-momentum tensor on the lattice is an ultraviolet divergent quantity and its non-perturbative renormalization for the interface tension has not been attempted.

II.3
Constrained mixed phase and the critical coupling

Figure 3:
Restricted average real part of the Polyakov line
\mathrm{Re\,}P_{L}

6\times 50^{2}\times 160

The mixed phase method
Moore and Turok 1997
;
Moore and Rummukainen 2001
avoids the supercritical slowing down by sampling only a part of the order parameter distribution, a narrow range around the center of the distribution, as shown in Fig.
1
. This is achieved by initiating the simulation with a suitable mixed-phase configuration and then rejecting updates that would move the value of the average Polyakov line beyond the boundaries of the admissible range. The outcome is that the system remains in the mixed phase, as shown schematically in Fig.
2
. With a sufficiently elongated lattice in the
z
direction, the interfaces minimize their area by spanning across the (
x,y
) plane and are well separated.

We prepare the initial configuration using simulations with the inverse lattice gauge coupling
\beta
slightly above and below the pseudo-critical value
\beta_{c}
on the two sides of the lattice given by
z<N_{z}/2
and
z\geq N_{z}/2
, respectively. This automatically gives rise to a two-phase configuration. We also ensure that the deconfined phase Polyakov line expectation value is along the positive real axis; because of the very large metastability, the expectation value remains real over any realistic simulation time, justifying the use of the real part of the Polyakov line as the order parameter. After the initial configuration has been generated, we set
\beta
to
\beta_{c}
and impose the above mentioned restriction on the value of the average Polyakov line, that

\mathrm{Re\,}P_{L}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(1/2-c_{w})\,P_{0},(1/2+c_{w})\,P_{0}}}\right]\ ,

(6)

where
P_{0}=\lvert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle P_{L}}}\right\rangle_{\text{decon.}}\rvert
and the relative interval width,
c_{w}
, is set to a small %-value. If the range is too narrow, the update rejection rate grows too large, and the simulations are inefficient. If the range is too wide, the simulation might encounter meta-stabilities, because the system starts to experience the tails of the two peaks in the probability distribution for
\mathrm{Re\,}P_{L}
sketched in Fig.
1
and will be attracted by the boundaries of the Polyakov loop range.

At the critical value of the inverse gauge coupling
\beta
, both bulk phases have equal probability weight, or equivalently, equal free energy density. This implies that in the mixed phase setup, the volume fractions of the two phases can vary (within the constraint given by (
6
)) without changing the probability, resulting in a flat probability distribution of the restricted order parameter.
Even a small deviation of
\beta
away from
\beta_{c}
is enough to change the probability of the bulk phases and to tilt the distribution. An example of this is shown in Fig.
3

for SU(8) on a lattice of size
N_{x}\times N_{y}\times N_{z}\times N_{t}=50^{2}\times 160\times 6
. We use this property to accurately determine
\beta_{c}
.
The final value of
\beta_{c}
is obtained by reweighting and error analysis is done using the jackknife method.

We note that this method of determining
\beta_{c}
has exponential finite volume effects, expected to vanish as
\propto\exp(-L/\xi)
, where
\xi
is some correlation length of the system and
L
the linear size. This is due to the exponentially suppressed finite volume effects in bulk phase probabilities.
As an example, Fig.
4
shows the results of the determination of
\beta_{c}
in SU(8) gauge theory on
N_{t}=6
lattices. No systematics in finite volume behavior is observed.
Thus, in each case, we determine
\beta_{c}
from the largest volume used for each
N
and
N_{t}
. All volumes satisfy the inequalities
N_{t}\leq 10N_{x,y}\ll N_{z}
, with the exception of the
N_{t}=10
cases, which have been added to improve the accuracy of the lattice beta function required to determine the latent heat in Sec.
IV
, and the single SU(16),
N_{t}=6
lattice. This point was included as a consistency check, and it is not used in our main analysis. The simulation volumes and
\beta_{c}
values are reported in Table
1
.

III
Interface tension

Because the lattices are elongated in the
z
direction, the two interfaces between the confined and the deconfined phase will always span across the
(x,y)
-plane. Let us consider the idealized case of an infinitely thin interface of size
L^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions. Assuming there are no overhangs, the interface is described by the height function
z(x,y)
. The energy of the interface is proportional to the area:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sigma\int_{0}^{L}\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,\sqrt{1+|\nabla z|^{2}}

(7)

where the interface tension
\sigma
restricts the thermal fluctuations of
z(x,y)
. In long wavelengths the spectrum of
z(x,y)
fluctuations
approach the
capillary wave
limit
Buff et al. 1965
;
Rowlinson and Widom 1989
, where the amplitude of the fluctuations is much smaller than the wavelength. In this limit Eq. (
7
) can be linearized, and the components of the 2-dimensional Fourier transform of
z(x,y)
obey

\langle|\hat{z}(k)|^{2}\rangle=\frac{T}{\sigma L^{2}k^{2}}=\frac{T}{4\pi^{2}\sigma(n_{x}^{2}+n_{y}^{2})}\penalty\ ,

(8)

where
k=2\pi n/L
is a 2-dimensional wave vector along the
(x,y)
-plane, and the expectation value is over the ensemble of configurations. For the derivation and corrections to Eq. (
8
) see Appendix
A
. It turns out that due to the corrections to the capillary wave picture, the limit
k\rightarrow 0
has to be taken in Eq. (
8
). Indeed, the value of the interface tension itself is
k
-dependent, and only the limit
k\rightarrow 0
gives us a uniquely defined result. In a realistic field theory the interface is not infinitely thin; however, in the limit of long wavelengths (
k\rightarrow 0
) the capillary wave picture becomes valid.

Thus, in the constrained mixed phase ensemble the interface tension can be obtained by (a) locating the interface surface,
z(x,y)
, for each configuration (for periodic lattices, the two interface surfaces
z_{i}(x,y)
,
i=1,2
), (b) calculating the Fourier transform
\hat{z}(k)
and (c) extrapolating the result with Eq. (
8
) to
k\rightarrow 0
. In field theory simulations this method has been used to measure
\sigma
in the SU(2)-Higgs model
Moore and Turok 1997
;
Moore and Rummukainen 2001
and, with some modifications, in atomistic condensed matter simulations (see e.g. ref.
Chacón and Tarazona 2003
).

We note here that the capillary wave theory, Eq. (
8
), implies logarithmic broadening of the averaged interface width
Rowlinson and Widom 1989
:

\langle w^{2}\rangle=\frac{T}{2\pi\sigma}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L\,\mu}}\right)

(9)

where
\mu
is an ultraviolet cutoff scale, and

w^{2}=\int_{A}(z(x,y)-\bar{z})^{2}\textrm{\penalty\ with\penalty\ }\bar{z}=\int_{A}z(x,y),

(10)

where we have denoted
\int_{A}=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y
. This property has been used to analyze interface tension, both in experiments and in simulations
Buff et al. 1965
;
Rowlinson and Widom 1989
;
Penfold 2001
;
Senapati and Berkowitz 2001
.

Returning to the SU(
N
) gauge theory, we define the local position of the interface
z(x,y)
, often called the
Gibbs surface
, from the real part of the Polyakov line field,
\mathrm{Re\,}P_{L}(x,y,z)
:
for each configuration and for each
(x,y)
we search for two values (for two interfaces) of
z
where
\mathrm{Re\,}P_{L}(x,y,z)
crosses a threshold value of
P_{0}/2
, where
P_{0}=\lvert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle P_{L}}}\right\rangle_{\text{decon.}}\rvert
as in (
6
). The precise value of the threshold is not important, but choosing a value near
P_{0}/2
reduces the effect of bulk fluctuations.

The bare
P_{L}(x,y,z)
is a very noisy observable and its measurement-by-measurement fluctuations can be much larger than the difference between confined and deconfined phase averages. Thus, as such
P_{L}(x,y,z)
cannot be used to locate the interface. A simple way to reduce the noise is to perform recursive smearing,

P_{L}^{(n+1)}(\bar{x})=\sum_{\bar{y}}S(\bar{x},\bar{y})P_{L}^{(n)}(\bar{y})\ ,

(11a)

with
P_{L}^{(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{x}}}\right)=P_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bar{x}}}\right)
and smearing kernel

S(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\frac{1}{1+6\,\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}+\rho\sum_{i=1}^{3}(\delta_{\bar{x}+\hat{i},\bar{y}}+\delta_{\bar{x}-\hat{i},\bar{y}})}}\right)\ .

(11b)

Following ref.
Moore and Turok 1997
, we implement on top of
n_{s}
steps of isotropic smearing with Eqn. (
11
), also additional smearing in
z
-direction only, by applying the smearing kernel

S_{z}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\frac{1}{1+2\rho}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\delta_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}+\rho\,(\delta_{\bar{x}+\hat{e}_{z},\bar{y}}+\delta_{\bar{x}-\hat{e}_{z},\bar{y}})}}\right]

(12)

n_{s,z}
times.

The smearing rapidly removes short-wavelength fluctuations. After
n_{s}
isotropic smearing steps the 3-dimensional momentum space Polyakov line is approximately

P_{L}^{(n_{s})}(\bar{q})\approx P_{L}(\bar{q})\,e^{-\alpha_{\rho}n_{s}\,a^{2}q^{2}}\ ,

(13)

with
\alpha_{\rho}=\rho/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+6\,\rho}}\right)
. For a precise analysis of the smearing see Appendix
B
.
The smearing efficiently reduces the amplitude of fluctuations with wavelength shorter than
\sim a\sqrt{\alpha_{\rho}n_{s}}
.
Too few smearing steps do not reduce the order parameter fluctuations sufficiently, leading to spurious dislocations in the measurements of the interface location, as illustrated in Fig.
5

n_{s}
in the range 5 …160, with
\rho=0.9
, and the surface location
z(x,y)
is measured with several smearing levels. The
z
-direction smearing turns out to be less significant, and we keep a fixed value

III.1
Long-wavelength extrapolation

We extrapolate the non-kernel-corrected measurements of
k^{2}\,\langle|\hat{z}(k)|^{2}\rangle
to
k^{2}\rightarrow 0
by fitting the function

f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=c_{1}\exp(c_{2}\,x+c_{3}\,x^{2}),

(15)

to the data
\{(k^{2}/T_{c}^{2},\small\langle|\hat{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k}}\right)|^{2}\small\rangle\,k^{2}\,T_{c}^{2}\,N_{x,y}^{2})\,|\,k^{2}/T_{c}^{2}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt$\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$<$}}6\}
, where each data point is weighted by the inverse of its squared error. The restriction
x\equiv k^{2}/T_{c}^{2}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt$\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$<$}}6
implies interface fluctuation wavelengths
\lambda\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\lower 4.0pt\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt$\sim$}\hss}\raise 1.0pt\hbox{$>$}}2.5/T_{c}
.
For the kernel-corrected measurements, we substitute
\small\langle|\hat{z}|^{2}\small\rangle
with
\small\langle|\hat{z}_{\text{corr.}}|^{2}\small\rangle
from Eq. (
14
).

The exponential form of the fit function is motivated by the fact that the non-kernel-corrected measurements decrease exponentially as functions of
k
at high smearing levels, which was also used in ref.
Moore and Turok 1997
. With the added subleading
\mathcal{O}(x^{2})
correction in the exponential it allows for good fits in a wider fit range.

The fits to kernel-corrected and non-kernel-corrected data give compatible results, but our final results are presented with the kernel correction applied, since it generally allows for a better quality of the fits. Examples for SU(4) and SU(10) at
N_{t}=7

of Appendix

\delta f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i,j}\sigma^{ij}\frac{\partial f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}{\partial c_{i}}\,\frac{\partial f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}{\partial c_{j}}}\ ,

with

IV
Latent heat

The latent heat can be calculated from the discontinuity of the derivative of the free energy density

L=\Delta\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}T}\frac{F}{V}=\frac{T_{c}^{2}}{V}\Delta\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}T}\ln Z

(22)

where
\Delta
is the discontinuity at
T=T_{c}
. On the lattice this can be expressed in terms of the
discontinuity of the plaquette expectation value
\Delta\langle U_{\Box}\rangle
at
\beta=\beta_{c}
:

\frac{L}{T_{c}^{4}}=-6\,N_{t}^{4}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\beta}{\mathrm{d}\ln a}}{\Delta\langle U_{\Box}\rangle}.

(23)

IV.1
Lattice beta-function

The lattice
\beta
-function
{\mathrm{d}\beta}/{\mathrm{d}\ln a}
is often evaluated using string tension or gradient flow for scale setting. However, since we have accurate measurements of
\beta_{c}
as functions of
N_{t}=1/(aT_{c})
, we use
T_{c}
as the fixed physical scale.

Because the data is discrete, we interpolate the measurements to continuous
N_{t}=1/(aT_{c})
to evaluate the derivative, using a generalization of the fit ansatz from
Lucini et al. 2005
to fit the data for all
N
simultaneously:

\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\,T_{c}}}\right)=-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{t}}}\right)=\\
f\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=-\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{t,0}}}\right)-\frac{12\pi^{2}}{11}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x-x_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)}}\right)\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{ord}}}\sum_{j=0}^{n_{c,\text{ord}}}c_{i,j}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)}}}\right)^{i}\frac{1}{N^{j}}\ ,

(24)

where
x=\beta/N^{2}
,
x_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)=\beta_{c}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{t,0},N}}\right)/N^{2}
, and the expansion orders are set to
n_{\text{ord}}=4
,
n_{c,\text{ord}}=2
.
We use
N_{t,0}=7
, which is approximately in the middle of the
N_{t}
domain for which we have data, but the final results are not sensitive to this choice. For this choice of
N_{t,0}
the function
x_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
in the range
N\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[4,10}}\right]
is well described by the following interpolation to our data:

x_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)=0.71329806(1)+0.00198546(4)/N\\
-0.58432233(22)/N^{2}+0.42305467(43)/N^{3}\ .

(25)

It turns out that the derivative
\mathrm{d}\beta/\mathrm{d}\ln a
is sensitive to the details of the parametrization of the fit function at the ends of the interpolated range. This is especially significant at
N_{t}=8
, corresponding to our smallest production lattice spacing, which has an effect on the continuum extrapolation. To stabilize the fits we added a set of relatively small volume simulations at
N_{t}=10
for all
N
. This gives us lower-precision results for
\beta_{c}
at
N_{t}=10
, as shown in Table
1
. Nevertheless, these values are sufficient to anchor the value of the derivative at
N_{t}=8
, and we do not evaluate the derivatives at
N_{t}=10
. The parameters given in Eq. (
24
) include the
N_{t}=10
results.

Since the measurement errors are in
x=\beta/N^{2}
instead of
\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{t}}}\right)
, we initially perform an unweighted fit and then convert the
x
errors into effective errors for the corresponding values of
\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)}}\right)
, using the local derivatives of the fitted function. The effective errors for
\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)}}\right)
are then used to iteratively perform weighted fits, until the fit result and the effective errors it produces for the values of
\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)}}\right)
stop changing. The final fit obtained with this procedure yields
\chi^{2}/\text{\#dof}=0.3
.

for

,

are listed in Table

V
Conclusions

We have measured the latent heat and the interface tension in the confinement-deconfinement phase transition in SU(
N
) gauge theory with
N=4,5,8
and
10
. We extracted the large-
N
limit of both of these quantities,
with the result
\sigma/T_{c}^{3}=0.0182(7)N^{2}-0.194(15)
for the interface tension and
L/T_{c}^{4}=0.360(6)N^{2}-1.88(17)
for the latent heat, valid for
N\geq 4
. These represent significant increase in the precision of the results, and indeed, in previous results the
N^{2}
behaviour of the latent heat could not be determined unambiguously
Lucini et al. 2005
.

We have also determined the lattice
\beta
-function
in SU(
N
) theory using the critical temperature to set the scale, and obtained the critical coupling for
N_{t}=6
,
7
,
8
and
10
.
We used the mixed phase ensemble together with the capillary wave theory to determine the critical coupling and the interface tension. The method avoids the supercritical slowing down which affects the widely used histogram method.

Appendix A
Surface tension from the capillary wave theory

In this appendix we derive the equation (
8
) relating surface tension
\sigma
and the amplitude of the fluctuations, and discuss subleading corrections. We mostly follow the discussion in ref.
Moore and Turok 1997
.

Let us assume we have a thin surface of size
L^{2}
aligned along
(x,y)
-plane and let
z(x,y)
denote the surface height. We also assume periodic boundary conditions to
x
and
y
-directions.
At finite temperature
T
the surface fluctuations are described by the partition function

Z=\int\prod_{x,y}\mathrm{d}z(x,y)\,e^{-H/T}\,,

(28)

where the surface energy is surface tension
\sigma
times the area:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sigma\int_{0}^{L}\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,\sqrt{1+|\nabla z|^{2}}=\sigma\int_{0}^{L}\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla z|^{2}-\frac{1}{8}|\nabla z|^{4}+\ldots}}\right].

(29)

This defines two-dimensional field theory, where higher derivative terms are unrenormalizable. Thus, at long distances, far from the UV cutoff, their effect vanishes and we are left with a massless free theory, capillary wave theory (CWT)
Rowlinson and Widom 1989
. Truncating Eq. (
29
) at second order derivative, the partition function can be solved with a Fourier transform to momentum space:

\displaystyle\hat{z}(n_{x},n_{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L^{2}}\int_{0}^{L}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,z(x,y)e^{2\pi i(n_{x}x+n_{y}y)/L}

(30)

\displaystyle z(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{x},n_{y}}\hat{z}(n_{x},n_{y})e^{-2\pi i(n_{x}x+n_{y}y)/L}\,.

(31)

The partition function then factorizes into a product of zero-centered, one-dimensional Gaussian integrals:

Z\propto\prod_{n_{x},n_{y}}\int\mathrm{d}{\hat{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n_{x},n_{y}}}\right)}\,\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{4\,\pi^{2}\,n^{2}\,\sigma\,|\hat{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n_{x},n_{y}}}\right)|^{2}}{2\,T}}}\right)\ ,

(32)

revealing that

\langle|\hat{z}(n_{x},n_{y})|^{2}\rangle=\frac{T}{4\,\pi^{2}\,n^{2}\,\sigma}\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ n^{2}\equiv n_{x}^{2}+n_{y}^{2}\neq 0\ .

(33)

Due to the non-renormalizable operators Eq. (
33
) becomes exact only in the limit

n^{2}\rightarrow 0
, with corrections expected to vanish as
O(n^{2}/L^{2})
. The negative sign of the first correction term in Eq. (
29
) implies that the energy of the large
n
(large
k
) modes at a given amplitude is reduced. Hence the amplitude of the surface fluctuations of these modes can be expected to be larger than given by Eq. (
33
). This is consistent with the linear growth of the kernel corrected
n^{2}\langle|\hat{z}(n)|^{2}\rangle
measurements when plotted against
k^{2}/T^{2}
as in Fig.
6
.

In realistic field theory simulations there are also fluctuations of the order parameter in the bulk phases. These are not directly associated with the phase interface, but bulk fluctuations near the interface will give rise to deviations in the measurements of the interface location
z(x,y)
. These are not described by the partition function in Eq. (
28
). The length scale of the bulk fluctuations is the bulk correlation length
\xi\ll L
.
Let us consider long-wavelength modes of
\hat{z}(n)
, with wavelength
\lambda=2\pi/|k|=L/|n|\gg\xi
.
With a surface of area
L^{2}
, we can estimate that there are
n_{f}=L^{2}/\xi^{2}
fluctuation domains which overlap with the surface. Assuming the fluctuation is approximately constant within the domain, the integral (
30
) over the area within one domain gives contribution
\propto\xi^{2}/L^{2}
.
Each domain gives a random deviation to integral (
30
) to positive or negative direction, so that the total effect is
\propto\sqrt{n_{f}}\xi^{2}/L^{2}
.
Thus, we can estimate that the overall effect of the bulk fluctuations on long-wavelength modes is

n^{2}\langle|\hat{z}(n)|^{2}\rangle_{\text{bulk fluct.}}\propto\frac{n^{2}\xi^{2}}{L^{2}},

(34)

again vanishing linearly in
n^{2}/L^{2}\sim k^{2}
. The magnitude of this effect is reduced rapidly as the smearing level
n_{s}
is increased, proportional to
\exp[-2\pi\alpha_{\rho}n_{s}a^{2}/\xi^{2}]
, see Eq. (
13
).

Appendix B
Smearing kernel correction

The effect of the smearing on the expectation values of the Fourier mode amplitudes of interface fluctuations can be calculated semi-analytically. Let

A\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,z}}\right)=\begin{cases}A_{0}\quad\text{if}&z_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)\leq z<z_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)\\
0\quad\text{else}&\\
\end{cases}\ ,

(35)

describe the shape of the volume occupied by the deconfined phase (cf. Fig.
2
) with
z_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)
,
i=1,2
being the parametrizations of the two interface surfaces. We neglect bulk fluctuations, so that
A_{0}=\lvert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle P_{L}}}\right\rangle_{\text{deconf.}}\rvert
is constant. We now Fourier transform in
z
:

\hat{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,k_{z}}}\right)=\frac{i\,A_{0}}{2\,\pi\,k_{z}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{i\,k_{z}z_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)}-e^{i\,k_{z}z_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)}}}\right)\ .

(36)

Since we neglect bulk fluctuations and the two phases occupy about the same space in z-direction in our setup, the small-
k_{z}
modes should dominate in (
36
). If we assume that the
z_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)
,
i=1,2
are well separated, so that the amplitudes of their fluctuations as functions of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)
are small relative to the wavelengths of the relevant
k_{z}
-modes, i.e.

z_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)=z_{0,i}+\Delta z_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)\quad\text{with}\quad\lvert\Delta z_{i}\,k_{z}\rvert\ll 1\quad\forall\,i=1,2\quad,

(37)

we can write

\hat{A}(x,y,k_{z})=\frac{A_{0}}{2\,\pi}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{e^{i\,k_{z}z_{0,1}}-e^{i\,k_{z}z_{0,2}}}{i\,k_{z}}-e^{i\,k_{z}z_{0,1}}\,\Delta z_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)+e^{i\,k_{z}z_{0,2}}\,\Delta z_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)\,+\,\mathcal{O}\bigl(\big.k_{z}\,\Delta z_{i}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)\big.\bigr)}}\right)\ .

(38)

Taking now the Fourier transform of
\tilde{A}(x,y,k_{z})
with respect to
x
and
y
, using (
38
), we find for
k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}>0
, i.e. the non-zero modes, that

\hat{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}}}\right)=\frac{A_{0}}{2\,\pi}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-e^{i\,k_{z}z_{0,1}}\,\Delta\hat{z}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)+e^{i\,k_{z}z_{0,2}}\,\Delta\hat{z}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\,+\,\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{z}\,\Delta z_{i}^{2}}}\right)}}\right)\ ,

(39)

with
\Delta\hat{z}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)
,
i=1,2
being the Fourier transforms of the
\Delta z_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)
,
i=1,2
. Since the differences
z_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)-\Delta z_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)=z_{0,i}
,
i=1,2
are constant, the Fourier transforms
\Delta\hat{z}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)
,
i=1,2
coincide for
k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}>0
with the Fourier transforms
\hat{z}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)
,
i=1,2
of the interface surface parameterizations
z_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)
,
i=1,2
and we therefore find that if (
37
) is valid, we have

\hat{z}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\propto\hat{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}}}\right)\quad,\quad i=1,2\quad.

(40)

Now, because our ”local order parameter” for deconfinement, i.e. the local Polyakov loop field
P_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,z}}\right)
from Eq. (
3
), suffers from severe UV noise, Eq. (
35
) with
A_{0}
constant is not a good approximation for
P_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,z}}\right)
itself. However, with increasing number
n_{s}
of smearing steps performed on
P_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,z}}\right)
with the kernel Eq. (
11b
), the resulting smoothed Polyakov loop field,
P^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n_{s}}}\right)}_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,z}}\right)
(cf. Fig.
5
), can be better and better represented by a correspondingly smoothed version of Eq. (
35
). In Fourier space the latter is simply given by

\hat{A}^{(n_{s})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}}}\right)=\hat{S}^{n_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}}}\right)\,\hat{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}}}\right)\ ,

(41)

with

\hat{S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}}}\right)=\frac{1+2\,\rho\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x}}}\right)+\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{y}}}\right)+\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{z}}}\right)}}\right)}{1+6\,\rho}=1-\rho\,\frac{4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sin^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x}/2}}\right)+\sin^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{y}/2}}\right)+\sin^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{z}/2}}\right)}}\right)}{1+6\rho}

(42)

being the Fourier transform of the smearing kernel from Eq. (
11b
).
In the limit of small
k_{z}
Eq. (
40
) then suggests that

\hat{z}_{i}^{(n_{s})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)=\hat{S}^{n_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}\approx 0}}\right)\,\hat{z}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\quad,\quad i=1,2\quad.

(43)

Assuming that Eq. (
41
) is indeed a good approximation for the Fourier transform of the smoothed Polyakov loop field
P^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n_{s}}}\right)}_{L}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,z}}\right)
, then Eq. (
43
) suggests that

\big\langle\lvert\hat{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\rvert^{2}\big\rangle=\frac{1}{\hat{S}^{2\,n_{s}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}\approx 0}}\right)}\,\big\langle\lvert\hat{z}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n_{s}}}\right)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\rvert^{2}\big\rangle\

(44)

can be used as a
kernel-corrected
estimator for the interface surface fluctuation spectrum, which for sufficiently large
n_{s}
becomes independent of the exact value of
n_{s}
.

Note that as long as
\rho<1
the Fourier transformed smearing kernel
\hat{S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}}}\right)
can be written as:

\hat{S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}}}\right)=\exp\bigl(\big.\log\bigl(\big.\hat{S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z}}}\right)\big.\bigr)\big.\bigr)=\exp\Bigl(\Big.-\alpha_{\rho}\sum\limits_{\mathclap{\hskip 8.19447pti\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x,y,z}}\right\}}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\,\sin\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{i}/2}}\right)}}\right)^{2}\Big.\Bigr)+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\big.\alpha_{\rho}^{2}\big.\bigr)\ ,

(45)

with
\alpha_{\rho}=\rho/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+6\,\rho}}\right)
as in (
13
).

Appendix C
Surface fluctuation spectrum without surface detection

Consider again Eq. (
35
) from the previous section. Its partial Fourier transform in
z
, given in Eq. (
36
), can also be written as

\hat{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,k_{z}}}\right)=\frac{A_{0}}{\pi\,k_{z}}\,\sin\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)-z_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)}}\right)/2}}\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{i\,k_{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)+z_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)}}\right)/2}\ .

(46)

We can then define:

h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right):=\arg\bigl(\big.\hat{A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y,k_{z}}}\right)\big.\bigr)/k_{z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(z_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)+z_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)}}\right)/2\ ,

(47)

where
k_{z}
should be set to the lowest non-zero value. By taking the Fourier transform of
h\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)
, we find that its absolute value squared satisfies:

2\,\lvert\hat{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\rvert^{2}=\frac{\lvert\hat{z}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\rvert^{2}+\lvert\hat{z}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\rvert^{2}}{2}+\mathrm{Re}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\hat{z}^{*}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\hat{z}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)}}\right)\ ,

(48)

where the last term should vanish in ensemble averages, provided that
z_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)
and
z_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,y}}\right)
fluctuate independently.
One could therefore use

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\lvert\hat{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\rvert^{2}}}\right\rangle=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\lvert\hat{z}_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\rvert^{2}}}\right\rangle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\lvert\hat{z}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\rvert^{2}}}\right\rangle}{2}=\big\langle 2\,\lvert\hat{h}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{x},k_{y}}}\right)\rvert^{2}\big\rangle

(49)

as estimator for the surface fluctuation spectrum. The advantage of this approach is that one does not have to actually locate the phase boundaries. However, smearing of the local Polyakov loop field would still be required to approach a situation where the system can be described by Eq. (
35
).
