Observation of Kondo cloud-coupling in a mirror-symmetric carbon nanotube array-molybdenum structure
Authors: Zheng Wei, Zhisheng Peng, Yu-Hao Wan, Zhongpu Wang, Ziyi Han, Xiaoxu Zhao, Enzheng Shi, Yong Jun Li, Weiguo Chu, Jian Zhang, Liu Qian, Jin Zhang, Qing-Feng Sun, Lianfeng Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnetic impurities in a non-magnetic metal are screened by conduction electrons, forming a spin cloud known as a Kondo cloud. Experimental detection of this Kondo screening cloud in dilute magnetic alloys remains challenging. Here we show evidence of Kondo clouds and their coupling in devices composed of mirror-symmetric carbon nanotube arrays separated by a molybdenum strip. The Kondo effect in these devices depends sensitively on the width of the molybdenum strip. For widths of 0.3-1.2 micrometers and 1.5-3.0 micrometers, the Kondo temperature forms two plateaus at about 107.8 kelvin and 15.8 kelvin, respectively. These results are explained by the formation of Kondo clouds and by intra-edge and inter-edge coupling within molybdenum. Calculations based on a two-spin model reproduce the two plateaus and their crossover. These findings may open opportunities for applications in spintronics and quantum computing.

Introduction

In strongly correlated electronic materials, the synergistic effect of multiple factors such as Coulomb interaction1–3, spin-orbit coupling4,5, and spin correlation6,7 enables the system to exhibit rich quantum many-body phenomena and correlated ground states. In nonmagnetic metals with dilute magnetic impurities, the electrical resistance decreases, reaches a minimum, increases logarithmically, and saturates when the temperature is lowered, which is known as the Kondo effect8–11, originating from exchange interactions between impurity spins and conduction electrons. The Kondo effect has played a central role in understanding correlated ground states of electronic systems and strongly correlated electron transport, including Kondo condensation12, Kondo lattices13,14, heavy-fermion systems15,16, and unconventional superconductivity17.

Below a characteristic temperature, the Kondo temperature, the impurity spin will couple with nearby itinerant electrons to form a Kondo cloud due to the creation of a many-body singlet state, as shown in Fig.1a18,19. The length of the Kondo cloud is usually estimated with ξK = ℏvF/(kBTK), where ℏ is the reduced Planck constant, vF is the Fermi velocity, kB is the Boltzmann constant, and TK is the Kondo temperature19. In dilute magnetic alloys, the typical Kondo length is about 0.8 μm when values of vF = 105 m s−1 and TK = 1 K are used, which is about several thousands of lattice spacings and much larger than the typical size of electron clouds in atoms (~1 Å). Meanwhile, experimental studies to look for the Kondo screening cloud have been difficult, and different results have been obtained. For example, scanning tunneling microscopy experiments have found that the Kondo signatures can be observed within distance several nanometers near the magnetic impurity, which vanish rapidly with increasing distance20–25. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements of a dilute alloy of CuFe with Fe concentration in the range of 500 to 5000 ppm were interpreted to indicate the absence of this cloud26. One reason for the NMR experiment is the unusual largeness of the Kondo cloud length, which is much larger than the inter-impurity separation of 6 to 13 lattice spacing. This suggests that each Kondo impurity has many other impurities inside its screening cloud26. To overcome this difficulty, by using Kondo impurity in a quantum dot27–31, Borzenets et al. have found evidence of Kondo cloud recently by Fabry-Perot oscillations coupling to the dot with gate tunable length of several micrometers19. However, experimental detection of Kondo screening clouds in dilute magnetic metals remains challenging. Here, we show evidence of the Kondo cloud and its couplings in mirror-symmetric CNT arrays separated with a Mo strip. In these devices, the magnetic moments are from the open ends of CNTs35–40. And the width of the Mo strip is the spacing between the magnetic impurities, which can be precisely controlled. When the Mo width exceeds 1.5 μm, the Kondo clouds along the same edge of the Mo strip are coupled into a Kondo cloud region with a Kondo temperature plateau of 15.8 K, which is named intra-edge coupling of Kondo clouds. When the Mo width is in the range of 0.3 to 1.2 μm, the two Kondo cloud regions overlap with each other to form inter-edge coupling of Kondo clouds with a Kondo temperature plateau of 107.8 K. Based on a model of two representative spins, one on each edge of the Mo strip, coupled through the Mo, numerical renormalization group (NRG) calculations reproduce the two Kondotemperature plateaus and their crossover.

Results and discussion

Mirror-symmetric CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices

The devices of mirror-symmetric CNT arrays and the Mo strip are fabricated as schematically shown in Fig.1b. Here, the aligned CNT arrays used in this work are obtained from a CNT film on a SiO2/Si substrate, using a soft-lock drawing method as reported recently41 (the top image of Fig. 1b). Then, a layer of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) is spin-coated, and the device is patterned with the electron beam lithography (EBL) technique. After development, a section of the aligned CNT array can be exposed, which is etched off with argon plasma treatment (the middle panel in Fig. 1b). Because these two aligned CNT arrays are fabricated from the same CNT array by etching a section of CNTs, they are mirror-symmetric. Finally, a Mo strip is deposited with a Lesker Lab-18 magnetron sputter, and the devices are obtained with a standard lift-off. When measuring, a current is applied through the two outer Mo electrodes, and a voltage difference is obtained through the inner Mo electrodes as in a four-terminal method (the bottom panel of Fig. 1b). More details of the fabrication of the CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices are provided in the “Method” section. Figure 1c shows two images of the cross sections at different positions of the device using a high resolution transmission electron microscope. The lower image of Fig. 1c represents that the Mo strip is composed of Mo nanoparticles. The upper image of Fig. 1c shows that there are five individual CNTs in the CNT bundle, suggesting that the CNT array is composed of CNT bundles rather than individual CNTs. It should be pointed out that the number of individual CNTs varies from bundle to bundle, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. In this work, the magnetic moment at the end of the CNT bundles is regarded as the sum of those at the open ends of individual CNTs (the bottom enlarged inset figure in Fig. 1b).

Figure 1d shows three schematic diagrams illustrating the proposed working mechanisms of pure Mo strips and the CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices in this work. For pure Mo strips (the top panel of Fig. 1d), which are fabricated with the same conditions and procedures for reference, their resistance shows typical metallic behavior as reported recently42. When the width of the Mo strip is larger than 1.5 μm (the middle panel of Fig. 1d), the Kondo clouds from CNT bundles are coupled to form a Kondo cloud region, which is an intra-edge coupling along the same edge of the Mo strip. When the width of the Mo strip is smaller than 1.2 μm, there is an overlap between the two Kondo cloud regions along the two edges of the Mo strip, which is inter-edge coupling of Kondo clouds (the bottom panel of Fig. 1d).

The above R–T results indicate the existence of the Kondo effect in CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices, which depends sensitively on the width of the Mo strip. Meanwhile, an interesting characteristic of the Kondo temperature, a key parameter of the Kondo effect, can be seen from Fig. 2d. There are ten data points of Kondo temperature and their corresponding R–T curves are shown in Fig. 2c and in Supplementary Fig. S2. In Fig. 2d, the Kondo temperatures for Mo width in the range of 0.3 to 1.2 μm are similar, with an average value of 107.8 ± 2.7 K; while for Mo width in the range of 1.5 to 3.0 μm, their Kondo temperatures have a much smaller average value of about 15.8 ± 2.0 K. These results indicate that the Kondo temperature remains almost the same when the width is smaller (larger) than 1.2 μm (1.5 μm) (Fig. 2d), which results in two plateaus of Kondo temperature. Meanwhile, there is an abrupt change of Kondo temperature when the width of the Mo strip is in the range of 1.2 to 1.5 μm.

Furthermore, we observed that Tk1 forms a plateau under strong coupling, then drops with the tuning of Ieff and eventually converges to Tk0, an abrupt transition that is in excellent agreement with the experimental results. In the experiments, the ratio of two Kondo temperatures is approximately 6.8, whereas the theoretical calculation yields only a value (Tk1/Tk0) of about 1.7, which may be attributed to the high-spin effects present in real carbon nanotubes35,37 (more details are provided in the “Method” section).

Based on the transport data, the critical length scale for the high-Tk to low-Tk transition lies between 1.2 and 1.5 μm, corresponding to the overlap of the Kondo clouds at the opposite edges of the Mo strip. This scale is much larger than the spacing between neighboring CNTs along the same edge (50–60 nm). Therefore, in addition to the inter-edge coupling of Kondo clouds (bottom panel of Fig. 1d), from a theoretical and numerical comparison perspective, there must also be intra-edge coupling along the same edge (middle panel of Fig. 1d). From an experimental perspective, a CNTs-Mo-CNTs device with a markedly reduced CNT bundle density was fabricated (see Supplementary Fig. S3). The mean inter-bundle spacing is ~5 μm, substantially exceeding the characteristic coupling length scale. Physically, this corresponds to the regime of individual Kondo clouds. The R–T curve exhibits characteristic Kondo behavior, showing a resistance minimum followed by a resistance upturn as the temperature decreases. The fitted Kondo temperature is 8.0 K (see Supplementary Fig. S3). This value is lower than the TK (15.8 K) observed for the same Mo strip width (2 μm) at a CNT density of 16–20 bundles/μm, confirming that intra-edge coupling indeed plays a fundamental role in determining the absolute value of TK. However, the variation in CNT density is not the main focus of this work. The central point is that, under the premise of mirror-symmetric magnetic moments, tuning the width of the Mo strip drives a crossover from intra-edge to inter-edge coupling of the Kondo clouds.

It should be noted that the Kondo cloud region, which is formed through inter-edge coupling, can be modified with the width of the Mo strips. This results in different R–T curves as shown in the left and middle panel of Fig. 2c and in Supplementary Fig. S2. For example, the resistance at 2 K is 3.32, 2.32, and 1.40 times as much as that at 400 K when the Mo width is 0.3 and, 0.5, and 0.8 μm, respectively. For Mo with a width of 1.2 μm, a resistance minimum is observed at 344.2 K, and the resistance at 2 K is 1.18 times as much as that at 400 K. Despite the differences in these R–T curves, they have a common parameter of Kondo temperature about 107.8 ± 2.7 K. The underlying mechanism of this observation is due to the fact that they originate from inter-edge coupling of Kondo clouds, as illustrated in the bottom panel of Fig. 1d.

In contrast, when the Mo width is in the range of 1.5 to 3.0 μm, there is no overlap between the two Kondo cloud regions in the Mo strip, which is intra-edge coupling of Kondo clouds. And these R–T curves share a common parameter of Kondo temperature about 15.8 K. From the intra-edge of the coupling of Kondo clouds, the size of Kondo clouds can be estimated. Here, the width of the Mo strip plays a key role because it can be easily controlled in CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices. Since the overlapping between the two Kondo cloud regions along each edge of the Mo strip occurs between the Mo width of 1.2 to 1.5 μm, this suggests that the size of the Kondo clouds along the direction of Mo width is in the range of 0.6 to 0.75 μm. This value is comparable to the theoretical Kondo length ξK of 0.4 to 0.5 μm, when the average Fermi velocity vF = 9.2 × 105 m s−1 in Mo metal49 and Kondo temperature of 15.8 ± 2.0 K are used.

When Mo-CNTs devices are fabricated, so that only one edge of the Mo strip has a transverse and aligned CNT array, inter-edge coupling of Kondo clouds should disappear. In Fig. 3c, a schematic diagram, an SEM image, and an AFM image are shown for such a CNTs-Mo device. Figure 3d represents five sets of the experimental R–T data and their corresponding theoretical fittings for Mo widths of 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, and 2.0 μm, respectively. It can be seen clearly that their resistance decreases when the temperature is lowered from 400 K. Resistance minimums can be observed at temperatures of 201.3, 186.7, 176.6, 68.3, and 54.1 K for Mo widths of 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, and 2.0 μm, respectively. Meanwhile, Kondo temperatures of 15.4, 17.4, 18.2, 18.8, and 14.3 K have been obtained through theoretical fittings. The average value of these Kondo temperatures is 16.9 ± 3.0 K, as shown in Fig. 3e, which has similar values to that of 15.8 ± 2.0 K in Fig. 2d. These results indicate that the intra-edge coupling of Kondo clouds in CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices (Fig. 2d) can also be observed in CNTs-Mo devices. Since the Mo widths of 0.3 and 0.5 μm are smaller than the size of Kondo cloud region induced by intra-edge coupling (in the range of 0.6 to 0.75 μm), this suggests that the shapes of Kondo clouds can be varied with the Mo width, which results in different R–T curves (the left two panels of Fig. 3d). When the Mo widths are larger than the size of Kondo cloud region induced by intra-edge coupling (0.8, 1.0, and 2.0 μm in Fig. 3d), their R–T curves also show dependence on the Mo width. The mechanism of this observation is schematically shown in the inset of Fig. 3e, where the total resistance can be regarded as a parallel connection of two resistors: intra-edge coupling of Kondo clouds and pure Mo. It should be noted that there is no correlation between the Kondo temperature and the temperature of the resistance minimum, both for CNTs-Mo-CNTs (Fig. 2) and CNTs-Mo (Fig. 3) devices as shown in Supplementary Table 2. In both kinds of devices, the temperature of resistance minimum decreases when the Mo width increases. However, the Kondo temperature shows quite different characteristics: two plateaus have been observed for the ten devices in Fig. 2 and only one plateau for the five devices in Fig. 3. The vales of these plateaus correspond to 107.8 ± 2.7 K, 15.8 ± 2.0 K, and 16.9 ± 3.0 K, respectively. The mechanisms for these plateaus are attributed to inter-edge coupling and intra-edge coupling of Kondo clouds.

The nontrivial dependence of the observed R–T curves on the Mo width is attributed to two kinds of Kondo clouds coupling. Meanwhile, other effects such as Mo strip size quantization, weak localization, or disorder-induced scattering can be excluded due to the following reasons: first, for pure Mo strips, typical metallic behavior can be observed42. Second, the control experiments shown in Fig. 3a, b indicate that inter-edge coupling results in a higher Kondo temperature, while intra-edge coupling results in a lower Kondo temperature. Third, when the CNTs in CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices are etched off by argon plasma treatment, their R–T curves show typical metallic behavior and lie on top of each other as shown in Fig. 3f. This indicate that the Kondo effect and corresponding Kondo clouds originate from magnetic moments and spins at the open ends of CNTs.

Kondo hard-core in intra-edge coupling of Kondo clouds

These observations indicate that the characteristic size of the Kondo cloud plays a central role in the transport behavior of the CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices. Here, the concept of a Kondo hard-core is introduced to describe the size of the Kondo cloud region in intra-edge coupling, and its size dependence on temperature can be obtained.

For the CNTs-Mo device (the middle schematic of Fig. 4a, b), the CNT array induces a Kondo cloud at the Mo-CNTs interface. Due to the enhanced scattering from the Kondo cloud, the resistivity within a characteristic width W0 increases to ρKondo cloud. The region beyond this width (W - W0) remains unaffected and retains the bulk resistivity ρMo. Notably, as shown in the middle panel of Fig. 1d, the conductance of the CNTs-Mo-CNTs device can be regarded as a parallel combination of three conductors: two Kondo-cloud-region conductors arising from intra-edge coupling and one pure Mo conductor. Since the two CNT arrays are mirror-symmetric, it is reasonable to assume that the two Kondo-cloud conductors are identical.

To quantify this effect, the Kondo hard-core width (W0) is defined using an equivalent circuit model (the right schematic of Fig. 4a, b). The Kondo cloud region (W0 with resistivity ρKondo cloud) is treated as electrically equivalent to a parallel composite bilayer: a perfectly insulating “hard-core” region of width WK (where ρKondo hard-core → ∞) and a conducting region of width W0-WK with the original bulk resistivity ρMo. Under this equivalence, identical resistances are obtained for the Mo strips in the middle and right schematic of Fig. 4b. Consequently, WK is taken to represent the effective reduction in the conducting channel width caused by Kondo scattering. Crucially, this effective width WK is not merely a theoretical construct; rather, it is a robust and experimentally observable quantity.

Quantitatively, the equivalent width of the pure Mo can be described with W0 (RMo/RK), where RK and RMo represent the resistance of the Kondo cloud region and pure Mo strips per micrometer, respectively. This implies that the remaining red region in the right schematic of Fig. 4a, with width WK = W0-W0(RMo/RK), exhibits zero conductance (ρKondo hard-core → ∞). Physically, this width (WK) of the Kondo hard-core represents the effective width within which the Kondo effect suppresses the conductance to zero, while the conductance outside this region remains unchanged.

The black line in Fig. 4c represents the conductance of pure Mo strips with different widths; while the red line is a linear fit of four data points for CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices in Fig. 2d, in which the Mo width is 1.5, 1.8, 2.0, and 3.0 µm, respectively. The red line in Fig. 4c can also be represented with a parallel connection of two conductors: 1/R = W0/RK + (W-W0)/RMo = W0(1/RK − 1/RMo) + W/RMo. The intercept of the red line with the Mo width axis can be represented with W0 (1- RMo/RK). This width is exactly what we define as the width of the Kondo hard-core, as shown in Fig. 4a. Moreover, the fact that the red curve is parallel to the black one indicates that, at 7.0 K, the width of the Kondo hard-core remains constant.

The red data in Fig. 4d shows the width WK of Kondo hard-core versus temperature in the range of 2 to 400 K for those CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices with Mo widths of 1.5, 1.8, 2.0, and 3.0 µm. And the blue data in Fig. 4d correspond to those of the CNTs-Mo devices shown in Fig. 3 with Mo widths of 0.8, 1.0, and 2.0 µm, respectively. It can be seen that the blue and red curves are almost the same. And the widths WK of the Kondo hard-core remain constant with values of about 0.36 µm at the temperature range of 2 to 62.4 K. Since RMo remains constant in this temperature range (Fig. 3f), this suggests that the size of the Kondo cloud (W0) and the resistance of the Kondo cloud region per micrometer (RK) also do not vary in the temperature range of 2 to 62.4 K. At the temperature of 400 K, the Kondo hard-core width WK of Kondo clouds almost becomes zero, indicating W0 (1 − RMo/RK) = 0.

It should be noted that there are some previous reports about the local magnetic moments at the open ends of CNTs35–40, and chemical adsorption or termination may introduce variations of the moment size50,51. In this work, the existence of magnetic moments in the open ends of CNTs is further confirmed through significantly different R–T curves among pure Mo strips and CNTs-Mo-CNTs and CNTs-Mo devices. Meanwhile, the magnetoresistance (MR) for CNTs-Mo-CNTs and CNTs-Mo devices has also been measured. Compared to the parabolic, positive and isotropic MRs of pure Mo strips42, the MRs of CNTs-Mo-CNTs and CNTs-Mo devices are negative and anisotropic, which further confirms the existence of the Kondo effect, magnetic moments, and correlations in these devices52,53. More details are provided in Supplementary Figs. S9 and S10 in Supplementary Notes 7 and 8.

In summary, by studying the R–T curves of CNTs-Mo-CNTs and CNTs-Mo devices, their nontrivial dependence on the Mo widths has been observed. These experimental results provide evidence of Kondo screening clouds in Mo strips. Meanwhile, intra-edge and inter-edge coupling of Kondo clouds have been found, in which the Kondo temperature forms two plateaus with values of about 107.8 and 15.8 K, respectively. A theoretical model and NRG calculations were carried out to support these observations. For intra-edge coupling of Kondo clouds, the concept of Kondo hard-core has been proposed, and its width dependence on temperature has been successfully obtained. These results of Kondo clouds and their two kinds of couplings have potential applications in spintronics and quantum information.

Methods

Preparation of the aligned CNT array

The CNTs used in this work are grown by floating catalytic chemical vapor deposition, and a CNT film is obtained when the deposition time is about 1 h54. After purification, a CNT film is transferred onto a silicon wafer (5 × 5 mm2) and dried with a nitrogen gun. In order to obtain an aligned CNT array from the random CNT network film, a soft-lock drawing method is adopted41.

Fabrication of CNT-Mo-CNTs and CNTs-Mo devices

CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices with CNT arrays at both edges of the Mo strip: based on the position and orientation of the CNT array, Mo strips and the electrodes are patterned using poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA, ~600 nm thick) and electron-beam lithography (Vistec EBPG 5000plus ES). Typical SEM images of a random CNT film and the aligned CNT arrays at the edges of the film are shown in Supplementary Fig. S11 in Supplementary Note 9. The devices are developed with 1:3 MIBK: IPA solution. After argon plasma treatment, a Mo strip is deposited using magnetron sputtering (Lesker Lab 18) under ultra-high vacuum conditions at 10−5 torr and a power of 300 W. The fabrication process is finished with a lift-off in acetone at 60 °C. The thickness of the Mo strip is usually checked with atomic force microscopy (AFM). The topographic image was acquired using AFM (Bruker, Multimode 8-HR) operated in tapping mode.

CNTs-Mo devices with a CNT array at only one edge of the Mo strip: the processes are similar to those mentioned above for CNTs-Mo-CNTs devices with CNT arrays at both edges of the Mo strip. When the lift-off in acetone, the samples are treated ultrasonically for about 3 min. Parts of the CNT film are usually lifted off after this treatment. Thus, the aligned CNT array connected to the CNT film will also be lifted off, making CNTs- Mo devices with only one CNT array at the other edge of the Mo strip.

Device measurement

Electrical characterizations of CNTs-Mo-CNTs and CNTs-Mo devices at room temperature are performed using an Agilent B1500A Semiconductor Device Analyzer and a probe station. The magnetoresistance measurements are performed in a physical properties measurement system equipped with a magnetic field in the range of 0 to 7 tesla and temperature in the range of 2 K to 400 K. The transport characteristics were measured utilizing the standard lock-in method with an AC current of 10 μA.

Cross-sectional TEM sample preparation and characterization

To investigate the integrity of CNTs on both sides of the Mo strip and ensure that there are no CNTs beneath the Mo strip, we selected different regions of the device to prepare a transmission electron microscopy (TEM) sample. The cross-sectional sample was then prepared via focused-ion-beam (FIB) processing (Nova200 NanoLab EFI) using Ga⁺ ions, and subsequently analyzed with TEM (JEM-F200) at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV.

The theoretical model and numerical renormalization group calculation

We employed a two-coupled-spin Kondo model combined with the numerical renormalization group method to qualitatively describe the Kondo physics arising from magnetic moments at the symmetric CNT ends on both sides of the Mo strip. In this framework, the two localized spins are coupled to conduction electrons through Kondo exchange interactions and mutually interact through an effective exchange coupling that includes both the RKKY interaction and an additional exchange contribution associated with the polycrystalline Mo strip. By analyzing the temperature-dependent entropy and magnetic moment, we identify the crossover from a ferromagnetically coupled triplet-screening regime to a regime where the two spins are nearly independently screened. This qualitative picture captures the experimentally observed plateau-like behavior and abrupt decrease of the Kondo temperature with increasing Mo strip width. Details of the model parameters, numerical implementation, and quantitative comparison are provided in the Supplementary Notes 5 and 6.
