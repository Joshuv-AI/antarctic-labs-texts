Benchtop NMR as a clinical tool for acute respiratory illness: metabolic signatures associated with disease severity

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute respiratory failure remains a major challenge in critical care, and early identification of patients at risk of clinical deterioration is essential. Metabolomics provides a systems-level characterization of disease severity but its clinical implementation is limited by the need for high-resolution Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) infrastructure. Benchtop NMR offers a compact and potentially scalable alternative. This study aimed to identify serum metabolic signatures associated with respiratory severity and evaluate agreement between benchtop and high-resolution NMR platforms.MethodsSerum samples from COVID-19 patients with acute respiratory failure were classified by respiratory severity (mild, moderate, severe, and very severe). Metabolomic profiling was performed using 500 MHz high-resolution and 80 MHz benchtop NMR systems. Multivariate analyses were used to identify severity-associated metabolic signatures, assess cross-platform agreement, and evaluate associations with clinical variables.ResultsBoth platforms identified consistent metabolic alterations across severity groups. Supervised models achieved accurate discrimination between hospitalized and non-hospitalized patients and between moderate and very severe cases. Twenty-one metabolites, including glutamine, citrate, lactate, phenylalanine, myo-inositol, glycerol, and trimethylamine N-oxide, contributed to group separation. Altered pathways included carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid turnover, lipid metabolism, and tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates, consistent with hypoxia and immune activation. Several metabolites correlated with inflammatory markers, immune cell counts, and oxygenation parameters. Importantly, benchtop NMR reproduced the key metabolic patterns observed with high-resolution NMR.ConclusionsSerum NMR metabolomics identifies metabolic signatures associated with respiratory severity. Background

Respiratory failure is a major challenge in critical care medicine and a leading cause of intensive care unit (ICUs) admissions worldwide. During large-scale outbreaks of viral respiratory infections, such as SARS-CoV and H1N1 influenza, healthcare systems have repeatedly faced situations of extreme stress, with emergency departments rapidly overwhelmed and ICUs reaching saturation [1, 2]. These episodes, further amplified during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, have highlighted the urgent need for reliable strategies to rapidly identify patients at high risk of clinical deterioration who may require advanced respiratory support [3, 4]. Despite significant progress in ventilatory management and critical care protocols, early risk stratification at presentation remains an unmet need, particularly in the context of dynamic epidemics and emerging respiratory pathogens.

Although mechanical ventilation is a life-saving intervention, approximately 10–20% of hospitalized patients with acute respiratory infections ultimately require ventilatory support [5, 6]. However, its use is associated with important risks and resource implications, including high ICU costs [7] and ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI), which may worsen pulmonary damage [8, 9]. Therefore, predicting which patients will progress toward severe respiratory impairment early in the disease course is essential to optimize triage decisions, improve resource allocation, and guide timely therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, biomarker-based stratification could be particularly valuable in future pandemics and in settings with limited access to high-complexity care.

Traditional clinical and laboratory markers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), ferritin, D-dimer, and lymphocyte counts, provide useful but incomplete information about impending respiratory failure, and their predictive value is limited across different respiratory pathogens [10, 11]. Multi-omics approaches have expanded our understanding of disease biology; however, many remain difficult to implement in real-time within hospital workflows due to their technical complexity, turnaround time, or cost [12]. In this context, metabolomics has emerged as a promising approach for capturing early biochemical disturbances associated with infection, inflammation, and organ dysfunction in critically ill patients. By providing a direct molecular fingerprint of systemic metabolism, metabolomics integrates host response, immunometabolic alterations, and tissue injury signals in a manner that is both biologically informative and clinically actionable [13].

Several metabolomic studies performed during the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated profound impairments in amino acid metabolism, mitochondrial function, lipid remodeling, redox balance, and markers of immune activation, all of which correlated with clinical severity, respiratory deterioration, and outcomes [14–17]. Importantly, these studies revealed changes that were conserved across independent cohorts and different analytical platforms, supporting the robustness of metabolomic signatures as biomarkers of acute respiratory failure. Beyond COVID-19, similar metabolic disturbances have been described in septic shock, influenza, and other critical care conditions [18, 19], suggesting that metabolomics may identify fundamental pathways of respiratory decompensation across diverse etiologies.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy is particularly well-suited for clinical metabolomics because its high reproducibility, minimal sample preparation, quantitative performance, and non-destructive nature [20]. However, the widespread clinical implementation of high-resolution NMR has been hindered by its high acquisition and maintenance costs and the need for specialized infrastructure and personnel. Recent developments in low-field or benchtop NMR (bNMR) spectrometers offer attractive alternatives. These instruments are substantially more affordable, easier to operate, and increasingly robust for quantitative metabolomic studies [21]. Their portability and rapid turnaround times create a unique opportunity to bring metabolic profiling closer to the bedside, enabling real-time triage in emergency departments or resource-limited environments, and potentially strengthening preparedness for future pandemics or mass-casualty respiratory outbreaks.

In this context, the present study leverages the unprecedented clinical scenario of the COVID-19 pandemic as a model to explore the potential of serum NMR-based metabolomics—using both high-resolution and benchtop platforms—to identify metabolic signatures associated with respiratory failure. Specifically, we aimed to (i) characterize metabolic alterations linked to varying degrees of respiratory support requirements, (ii) evaluate the prognostic value of these metabolic markers for predicting the need for mechanical ventilation, (iii) explore the underlying pathways involved in respiratory deterioration, and (iv) assess the feasibility and performance of a bNMR system for rapid clinical stratification. While grounded in COVID-19 data, our objective was to generate insights and methodological approaches broadly applicable to other forms of acute respiratory failure and future respiratory pandemics.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included adult patients (≥ 18 years) who presented to the Emergency Departments of two tertiary hospitals in Madrid, Spain (Fundación Jiménez Díaz and Getafe University Hospital) between February 26th and April 24th, 2020, during the first epidemic wave of SARS-CoV-2. Patients were eligible if they presented with respiratory symptoms and had a confirmed positive SARS-CoV-2 quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) from a nasopharyngeal swab, or if they presented with mild respiratory symptoms consistent with COVID-19 and were discharged without the need for oxygen therapy. Given the epidemiological context of the first wave, these non-hospitalized symptomatic patients were considered to have mild COVID-19 despite the lack of microbiological confirmation.

Patients with a history of immunosuppression (including active chemotherapy, organ transplantation, or chronic immunosuppressive therapy) were excluded. No other exclusion criteria were applied to the study. The study cohort comprised 45 hospitalized patients and 83 outpatients with mild disease.

Hospitalized patients were admitted due to dyspnea and/or arterial oxygen saturation < 90% on room air. They were stratified into three severity groups based on the initial level of respiratory support required during hospitalization: (i) moderate cases, requiring low-flow oxygen therapy (nasal prongs or face mask); (ii) severe cases, requiring advanced non-invasive respiratory support (high-flow nasal cannula, non-rebreathing mask, CPAP, or non-invasive mechanical ventilation); and (iii) very severe cases, requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) was defined according to the Berlin criteria [22].

Sample collection and processing

Blood samples were obtained within 24 h of presentation to the Emergency Department. Blood samples were collected in BD Vacutainer tubes. After collection, the sample was left at room temperature for 30 min to allow clotting. The clot was removed by centrifugation at 1,500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant, which was serum, was immediately transferred into 2 ml Eppendorf tubes and stored at −80 °C. Serum was used because, during the COVID-19 pandemic, it represented the most feasible biological matrix for routinely collect, processing, and storage in a high-throughput clinical setting.

Prior to NMR acquisition, serum samples underwent high-speed centrifugal filtration using Amicon Ultra-0.5 3 kDa molecular-weight cut-off filters (15,000 × g, 4 °C). This step efficiently removes macromolecules such as proteins and lipoproteins, which can generate broad resonances or baseline distortions and interfere with the detection of low-molecular-weight metabolites [23]. Approximately 300 µL of filtered serum was collected and mixed with 300 µL of deuterated water containing 1 mM trimethylsilylpropionic acid (TSP), which was used as an internal chemical shift reference and quantification standard. The final 600 µL mixture was transferred into NMR tubes for subsequent high-resolution and benchtop NMR measurements.

HR-NMR spectroscopy

High resolution 1H-NMR (HR-NMR) spectra were acquired on a Bruker Avance III HD 500 MHz spectrometer (CIC biomaGUNE, Donostia–San Sebastián, Spain) using 5 mm NMR tubes and a standardized 1D NOESY-presaturation pulse sequence. The acquisition parameters were selected to ensure high spectral quality and robust metabolite quantification, while maintaining a total acquisition time comparable to that of the benchtop NMR experiments. Spectra were recorded at 25 °C with a 12 ppm spectral width, 4 s acquisition time, 100 ms mixing time, and a recycle delay of 4.5 s, resulting in a total acquisition time of 13 min. Each spectrum was collected with 256 scans, zero-filled and processed with an exponential line broadening of 0.3 Hz prior to Fourier transformation.

For metabolite identification and confirmation of peak assignments, two-dimensional NMR experiments, including COSY, TOCSY, and HSQC, were performed on a subset of samples using standard Bruker pulse programs. The detailed acquisition parameters for the 2D experiments are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

bNMR spectroscopy

Benchtop 1H-NMR (bNMR) spectra were acquired using a Magritek Spinsolve 80 MHz ULTRA spectrometer (1.4 T; Complutense University of Madrid, Spain; Supplementary Fig. 1). Samples were measured at 25 °C in standard 5-mm NMR tubes using a 1D WET SUP pulse sequence to ensure efficient water suppression, which is particularly relevant in low-field bNMR systems. For each sample, 128 scans were collected with an acquisition time of 3.2 s and repetition time of 15 s. Under these acquisition conditions, the total measurement time per sample was approximately 32 min. The acquisition parameters were selected as a practical compromise to ensure sufficient spectral quality while accounting for the intrinsic sensitivity and hardware constraints of the benchtop system. Automated tuning and shimming routines were performed before each experiment to optimize the spectral quality.

Processing of NMR spectra

All NMR spectra were processed using MestReNova software (v.15; Mestrelab Research, Santiago de Compostela, Spain) following standardized procedures [23–25] applied uniformly across all samples. Manual phase correction was performed first, followed by automated baseline correction using the Whittaker smoother algorithm, which provides robust baseline flattening for metabolomic applications [26].

Chemical shifts were referenced to the trimethylsilylpropionic acid (TSP) peak at 0.00 ppm. The TSP resonance was excluded from the multivariate analyses. Different water suppression techniques were used for each platform (NOESY-presat in HR-NMR and WET in benchtop NMR), which may lead to differences in residual water tails and baseline behavior. To minimize variability associated with water suppression, the residual water resonance region (4.26–5.61 ppm for HR-NMR; 4.51–5.04 ppm for bNMR) was excluded from the analysis. The excluded water region was defined individually for each platform based on the residual water linewidth, baseline distortions, and presence of sidebands, ensuring complete removal of water-related artifacts while avoiding overlap with relevant metabolite resonances.

For the HR-NMR spectra, binning was applied at δ = 0.001 ppm to reduce the data dimensionality and compensate for minor chemical shift variations across the samples [27]. Due to the lower spectral resolution and broader peak profiles in benchtop NMR, binning was not applied, as it could lead to further signal overlap and loss of information. Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) values for benchtop NMR spectra were calculated after phase and baseline correction as the ratio between signal intensity and the standard deviation of the noise measured in signal-free spectral regions. Due to the lower spectral resolution and increased signal overlap in benchtop NMR, peak intensities were estimated using the deconvolution module implemented in MestReNova. In this approach, the signal contribution was derived from the integrated area of deconvoluted peaks, providing a more robust estimation of signal magnitude for partially overlapping resonances. A minimum threshold of SNR > 10 was applied as a criterion for reliable quantification, and low-intensity or near-noise signals were excluded from further analysis.

Chemical shift variability across samples was minimized through standardized sample preparation, temperature control, and referencing to TSP. In addition, spectra were aligned using the automatic alignment tool in MestReNova based on selected reference signals, ensuring consistent peak positioning across samples. All spectra were normalized to the total spectral area (excluding the removed regions) to account for differences in the sample concentration [28].

The resulting processed datasets were exported for statistical analysis, ensuring consistency and comparability between the high-resolution and benchtop NMR platforms.

Statistical metabolomic analysis

Prior to statistical analysis, all spectra were mean-centered and Pareto-scaled to reduce the dominance of high-intensity metabolites while preserving the overall variance structure [29]. Multivariate analyses were performed using MetaboAnalyst v6.0. Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) [30] was applied to explore global clustering patterns and identify sources of variance without using the group labels. In all models, the Hotelling’s T2 regions shown as ellipses in the corresponding score plots represent the 95% confidence interval.

Supervised partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) [31] was performed to discriminate between the predefined clinical groups, with two components applied for each instrument. fivefold cross-validation parameters (accuracy, R2, and Q2) were used to assess the model performance. To assess the risk of overfitting in the supervised models, permutation testing (n = 100) was performed on the OPLS models using MetaboAnalyst. Class labels were randomly reassigned, and the resulting R2Y and Q2 values were compared with those of the original model. Models were considered statistically valid when the permutation p-value was ≤ 0.05. Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores [32] were calculated to identify discriminant spectral regions, with VIP > 2 considered significant.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, together with the area under the curve (AUC) [33], sensitivity, and specificity, were generated using MetaboAnalyst to evaluate the classification performance of the multivariate models. ROC curves were generated using Monte Carlo cross-validation (MCCV) with balanced sub-sampling as implemented in MetaboAnalyst. In each iteration, two thirds of the data were used for training and one third for independent validation. The number of retained components in PLS-DA was determined automatically based on the proportion of explained variance and the cross-validation metrics.

Statistical analyses were performed independently for the high-resolution and benchtop NMR datasets using the same procedures.

Metabolite assignment and quantification

Metabolite assignment and quantification were performed following a standardized workflow previously validated in our NMR-based metabolomics studies on infectious diseases [34–38]. Metabolite identification in the 1D 1H-NMR spectra was performed using Chenomx NMR Suite 9.0 (Chenomx Inc., Edmonton, Canada) [39], supported by reference spectra from the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB 5.0) [40]. The assignments were based on chemical shift positions, multiplicity patterns, and comparisons with spectral libraries, considering the results obtained from both spectrometers. To confirm metabolite identities, two-dimensional NMR experiments (COSY, TOCSY, and HSQC) were performed on a representative subset of samples using standard Bruker pulse programs in HR-NMR. These 2D datasets enabled the verification of spin–spin couplings and carbon–proton correlations, ensuring the accurate identification of overlapping or ambiguous resonances. No 2D experiments were performed on the bNMR spectrometer.

Quantification was carried out using a targeted fitting approach. For each metabolite, one or more characteristic resonances were selected based on specificity and minimal spectral overlap. When multiple peaks contributed to the same metabolite, they were simultaneously fitted within the deconvolution model to estimate a single metabolite-level signal. Metabolite quantification was based on relative signal intensities derived from the integrated areas of the selected resonances. No correction for the number of contributing protons was applied, and therefore results are expressed as relative rather than absolute concentrations. Although TSP was used as an internal chemical shift reference, absolute metabolite concentrations were not calculated because metabolite-specific correction factors were not applied.

A total of 21 metabolites were reliably assigned and quantified as relative signal intensities in the samples. Subsequent univariate statistical comparisons between clinical groups were performed using Student’s t-test (unequal variances) and a significance threshold of p < 0.05 [41]. P-values obtained from univariate analyses were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure, which accounts for dependency between variables. Adjusted p-values (q-values) were calculated, and a threshold of q < 0.05 was used to define statistical significance.

Metabolomics pathway analysis

Pathway analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst v6.0, following a combined approach that integrated pathway enrichment analysis and pathway topology assessment [42–44]. A list of 21 metabolites that showed statistically significant differences between the clinical groups was used as input. Metabolites that could not be confidently assigned were excluded from the analyses.

For pathway enrichment, we employed the GlobalTest algorithm [45, 46], which evaluates whether sets of functionally related metabolites show coordinated changes and is particularly suitable for datasets with small sample sizes. Pathway topology analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) human metabolic pathway library, with node importance computed using betweenness centrality to estimate pathway impact.

Pathways were considered significantly altered when they met the following criteria: (i) FDR-adjusted p-values < 0.05, and (ii) pathway impact > 0 [47]. Raw p-values are shown in figures for reference only. Enrichment p-values were adjusted internally within the GlobalTest framework, which accounts for the correlations among metabolites. Only pathways that fulfilled both criteria were retained for interpretation.

Correlation analyses

To investigate the relationship between metabolic alterations and clinical parameters, correlation analyses were performed using both multivariate PLS-DA latent variables and the intensities or concentrations of individual metabolites. Prior to the analysis, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Pearson´s correlation coefficients were used for normally distributed variables, whereas Spearman´s rank correlations were applied for non-normally distributed variables.

Correlations were computed between (i) the first latent variable from the PLS-DA model (PLS1), which captures the major axis of metabolomic variation associated with clinical severity, and (ii) individual metabolite levels (absolute concentrations for HR-NMR and relative intensities for bNMR), and the clinical parameters of interest, including lymphocyte and monocyte counts, oxygenation indices, and inflammatory markers. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was used. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure. Statistical significance was defined as a q-value threshold of < 0.05.

Additional correlation analyses were performed to assess the association between key discriminant metabolites and demographic variables (age and sex).

All statistical analyses were performed in R (v.4.2.2; R Core Team, 2022) using the base “stats” package [48].

Characteristics of patients by severity

Abbreviations: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001

Significant differences in demographic characteristics were observed across severity groups. Patients with mild disease were significantly younger than hospitalized patients, whereas those in the moderate and severe groups were the oldest (p < 0.001). The proportion of female patients was highest in the mild group (77.1%) and decreased progressively with increasing disease severity, reaching 20.0% in patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (p < 0.001).

Clinical outcomes differed markedly according to disease severity. Mortality occurred exclusively in the very severe group, where 7 out of 25 patients (28.0%) died, while no deaths were observed among patients with mild, moderate, or severe disease (p = 0.031). The incidence of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) increased significantly with severity, affecting 6.7% of patients receiving low-flow oxygen, 33.3% of those on high-flow oxygen, and all patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (100%; p < 0.001). Pneumonia and bacteremia were also significantly more frequent in the very severe group compared with the other groups (p = 0.036 and p = 0.001, respectively).

Laboratory parameters reflected increasing systemic inflammation with disease severity. C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations were significantly higher in patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation compared with those receiving low- or high-flow oxygen therapy (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed between groups for leukocyte count, hemoglobin, platelet count, glucose, creatinine, bilirubin, international normalized ratio (INR), or procalcitonin levels.

Gasometric analysis revealed significant differences in arterial pH across severity groups (p = 0.013), whereas arterial oxygen tension (PaO₂), carbon dioxide tension (PaCO₂), bicarbonate concentration, and lactate levels did not differ significantly. As expected, impairment of oxygenation was most pronounced in the very severe group, as reflected by reduced PaO₂/FiO₂ (PaFi) ratios in patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation.

Global metabolomic signatures across severity groups

The metabolomic dataset comprised serum samples from patients across four COVID-19 severity categories: mild (n = 83), moderate (n = 15), severe (n = 6), and very severe disease (n = 25), classified according to clinical severity and respiratory support requirements. One sample from the very severe group was excluded from further analysis due to pronounced ethanol contamination detected in the NMR spectrum, which was considered non-biological and likely related to external contamination during sample handling. Consequently, metabolomic analyses were performed on a final cohort of 24 very severe patients.

Unsupervised PCA revealed clear clustering according to clinical severity for both the high-resolution and benchtop NMR datasets. In HR-NMR (Fig. 1A), PC1 (4.3% of variance) separated mild cases from hospitalized patients, while PC2 (1.4%) further discriminated between the moderate, severe, and very severe groups. Although the explained variance of the first two components was modest, as expected in high-dimensional metabolomic datasets, the group-wise separation was visually consistent and reproducible. An alternative stratification based on hospital classification (outpatients, ICU, and non-ICU patients) showed consistent group separation in both HR-NMR and benchtop datasets. Notably, a small subset of samples exhibited discordance between clinical classification and metabolomic clustering. Specifically, some ICU patients without mechanical ventilation clustered closer to less severe groups, while a few non-ICU patients requiring ventilatory support were positioned within more severe metabolic profiles (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Supervised PLS-DA provided robust group discrimination for both NMR platforms. In HR-NMR (Fig. 1C), the first latent variable clearly separated all four severity categories, with cross-validation showing high predictive performance (accuracy = 99.2%, R2 = 0.999 and Q2 = 0.857). The benchtop model (Fig. 1D) yielded a similar pattern (accuracy = 85.9%, R2 = 0.999 and Q2 = 0.758). The agreement between HR-NMR and benchtop NMR should be interpreted at the level of global metabolic patterns rather than strict metabolite-level correspondence.

To further evaluate the discriminative capacity of the metabolomic profiles within hospitalized patients, an additional analysis was performed including only moderate, severe, and very severe cases. In this subset, PLS-DA models maintained a clear separation between severity groups in both HR-NMR and bNMR datasets (Supplementary Fig. 3). In HR-NMR, the first component accounted for the main separation between groups, explaining 5.4% of the variance, with high classification performance (accuracy = 97.78%, R2 = 0.991, Q2 = 0.745). Similarly, in the bNMR dataset, the first component captured group separation (3.2% of the variance), with an accuracy of 80.17% (R2 = 0.949, Q2 = 0.260).

VIP analysis identified 160 significant spectral regions in the HR-NMR dataset, corresponding to 21 metabolites spanning amino acid metabolism (alanine, glutamine, leucine, isoleucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, proline and valine), energy metabolism (glucose, lactate and citrate), lipid-related metabolites (acetate, acetone, glycerol, isobutyrate, 2-hydroxybutyrate and 2-hydroxyisovalerate), osmolytes (myo-inositol), and gut-derived molecules (trimethylamine-N-oxide and tyramine). Predictive stratification models

To explore the clinical utility of the metabolomic signature, we evaluated whether the serum 1H-NMR profiles could discriminate clinically relevant patient categories. Two complementary stratification tasks were assessed: (i) hospitalized (including very severe, moderate, and severe cases) versus mild non-hospitalized patients, and (ii) very severe versus moderate cases, representing low versus high respiratory support requirements.

Using HR-NMR, PLS-DA models achieved complete separation between hospitalized and mild non-hospitalized patients (Fig. 4A). Cross-validation confirmed excellent model performance (accuracy = 100%, R2 = 0.999, Q2 = 0.896), and ROC analysis showed near-perfect diagnostic ability (AUC = 0.999; Table 2). Perturbations in phenylalanine metabolism (impact = 0.50) and phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis (impact = 0.357) were also consistently detected, reflecting changes in aromatic amino acid pathways closely associated with immune activation and inflammatory responses.

Alterations in glycerolipid metabolism (impact = 0.237), which were largely influenced by differences in glycerol concentrations, were among the most prominent findings (p = 1.48 × 10−15 (FDR: 5.17 × 10−15) and 4.15 × 10−14 (FDR: 2.32 × 10−13)), suggesting shifts in lipid mobilization and membrane remodeling with increasing disease severity. Additional pathways with strong significance included inositol phosphate metabolism driven by myo-inositol (impact = 0.129; p = 9.75 × 10⁻13 (FDR: 3.90 × 10–12)) and cysteine and methionine metabolism (impact = 0.104), both of which showed consistent perturbation between groups.

Pathways associated with nitrogen balance and mitochondrial function were also identified. Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism (impact = 0.113) and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (impact = 0.090) were significantly affected, reflecting decreased citrate and glutamine levels (p = 0.028 (FDR: 0.036) and 3.21 × 10−15 (FDR: 3.00 × 10−14), respectively). Despite lower impact values (< 0.1), additional pathways, including arginine and proline metabolism, galactose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and pyruvate metabolism, were significantly enriched, highlighting a coordinated disruption of central carbon metabolism.

Finally, significant alterations were detected in glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (impact = 0.080), one-carbon pool by folate metabolism (impact = 0.051), and tyrosine metabolism (impact = 0.025). Taken together, these findings indicate that increased respiratory severity is associated with widespread shifts in carbohydrate utilization, amino acid catabolism, and energy/redox pathways.

Integrative metabolite–clinical correlations

To link metabolomic alterations with clinical severity markers, we performed correlation analyses integrating both multivariate and univariate metabolite information with key physiological and inflammatory parameters. Two complementary approaches were used.

First, we examined the association between the first latent variable of the PLS-DA model (PLS1), which represents the dominant metabolic gradient separating severity groups, and the clinical parameters. PLS1 scores were significant positive correlated with lymphocyte (r = 0.54, p = 1.4 × 10−4, q = 3.85 × 10−4) and monocyte (r = 0.63, p = 4.3 × 10−6, q = 2.4 × 10−5) counts and negatively correlated with arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2; r = –0.40, p = 0.009, q = 1.65 × 10−2). These findings indicate that the global metabolic signature captured by PLS1 is closely associated with immune activation and impairment of oxygenation.

Second, we evaluated the correlations between individual metabolite concentrations and the same clinical variables to identify the specific biochemical drivers of these relationships. Several metabolites were strongly associated with immune cell counts. Glutamine exhibited marked negative correlations with both lymphocytes (r = –0.73, p = 1.6 × 10−9, q = 6.9 × 10−9) and monocytes (r = –0.76, p = 1.0 × 10−10, q = 7.2 × 10−10), and a positive correlation with C-reactive protein (CRP; r = 0.74, p = 8.4 × 10−3, q = 2.3 × 10−2). Citrate levels were also inversely correlated with monocyte counts (r = –0.73, p = 1.9 × 10−9, q = 6.9 × 10−9).

Several additional metabolites showed strong negative correlations with monocyte concentration, including myo-inositol (r = –0.77, p = 5.8 × 10−11, q = 7.2 × 10−10), trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) (r = –0.77, p = 7.7 × 10−11, q = 7.2 × 10−10), proline (r = –0.73, p = 1.6 × 10−9, q = 6.9 × 10−9), and phenylalanine (r = –0.72, p = 4.7 × 10−9, q = 1.5 × 10−8). These associations reinforce the involvement of amino acid metabolism, membrane turnover, and gut-associated metabolites in the metabolic response to respiratory deterioration.

No additional clinical parameters were significantly correlated with the metabolomic data. Overall, these integrative analyses highlight that both global metabolic patterns and specific metabolite alterations are strongly linked to immune dysregulation and oxygenation status in patients with different degrees of respiratory compromise.

Discussion

In this study, we performed a comprehensive serum metabolomic analysis of patients with varying degrees of respiratory compromise using HR-NMR and bNMR spectroscopy. We demonstrated that metabolic alterations associated with clinical severity can be robustly captured using both platforms, despite their markedly different magnetic fields. The agreement observed between platforms primarily reflects consistency in multivariate patterns and metabolic trends, rather than exact metabolite-level quantification. This distinction is particularly relevant given the lower spectral resolution of benchtop NMR, which limits the unambiguous identification of certain metabolites. The strong agreement between HR-NMR and bNMR highlights the feasibility of low-field bNMR as an accessible and scalable technology for metabolic phenotyping during acute respiratory illnesses and future infectious outbreaks.

Metabolic signatures and pathophysiological interpretation

The metabolic signatures identified in our cohort align closely with previously described biochemical disturbances in severe COVID-19, including alterations in amino acid metabolism, mitochondrial energy pathways, inflammation-associated metabolites, and endothelial activation [12, 14, 15, 17, 49]. Elevated levels of phenylalanine, TMAO, and formate, together with reductions in glutamine, citrate, and several amino acids, point to profound metabolic reprogramming driven by systemic inflammation, hypoxia, and immune cell activation. In particular, glutamine depletion and reduced TCA cycle intermediates are consistent with prior observations linking metabolic exhaustion to lymphopenia and impaired immune responses [50, 51].

Mitochondrial dysfunction, reflected by altered citrate and pyruvate metabolism, has been reported in critically ill patients [52, 53]. Disruption of aromatic amino acid pathways, such as phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolism, has been associated with cytokine storms, endothelial injury, and altered NO synthesis [49, 54]. Likewise, increased levels of TMAO, an established marker of endothelial dysfunction, have been described in severe COVID-19 and correlate with vascular inflammation [55].

Overall, the pathway analysis performed in our study highlighted coordinated disturbances across carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid turnover, glycerolipid metabolism, and redox-related pathways. These alterations reflect the combined effects of hypoxia, mitochondrial stress, inflammation, and immune activation, all of which are hallmarks of severe respiratory illnesses.

Clinical relevance and stratification potential

The central objective of this study was to assess whether metabolomic profiling could discriminate between clinically relevant severity categories. Both HR-NMR and bNMR demonstrated excellent performance in distinguishing hospitalized patients from non-hospitalized patients and separating very severe cases from moderate cases. Importantly, the metabolomic models retained their discriminative capacity even when restricting the analysis to hospitalized patients, indicating that the observed separation is not solely driven by differences between mild and severe cases but reflects progressive metabolic changes associated with increasing respiratory support requirements. The relatively low variance explained by individual multivariate components is consistent with the complexity and heterogeneity of clinical metabolomics data and does not preclude robust group discrimination. Similar findings have been reported in large multi-omics studies linking metabolic phenotypes to disease progression and clinical deterioration in COVID-19 [12, 16, 56].

Interestingly, a small number of cases showed discrepancies between clinical classification and metabolomic clustering. These discordant samples may reflect differences between administrative or treatment-based classification (e.g., ICU admission) and underlying physiological severity. In particular, some ICU patients without mechanical ventilation displayed metabolic profiles closer to less severe groups, whereas a subset of non-ICU patients requiring ventilatory support exhibited profiles associated with higher severity. These findings suggest that metabolomic profiling may capture aspects of disease severity not fully reflected by categorical clinical classification, supporting its potential role as a complementary tool in early risk stratification. However, these observations are exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts, and no formal conclusions regarding improved predictive performance can be drawn from the present study.

Unlike conventional biomarkers such as CRP, ferritin, or D-dimer, which often lack predictive granularity for impending respiratory failure [10, 11], metabolomics provides an integrated snapshot of the host metabolic state. This offers the potential to identify high-risk patients earlier in their trajectory, thereby improving triage and decision-making during epidemics and overwhelming emergency departments.

bNMR instruments offer additional advantages. They are low-cost, cryogen-free, require minimal operator expertise, and can deliver results rapidly in hospital settings that lack specialized infrastructure. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of low-field bNMR for near-patient applications in critical hospital environments, enabling rapid metabolomic screening in hospital laboratories, emergency departments, and potentially in intensive care units, even if not directly at the bedside [23, 25, 34, 57, 58]. However, the lower spectral resolution of benchtop NMR limits the ability to resolve certain metabolites with overlapping signals, such as citrate, myo-inositol, and TMAO, which could not be unambiguously distinguished at 80 MHz. This represents an inherent limitation of low-field NMR that should be considered in clinical applications.

Relations to clinical biomarkers and immune parameters

The correlation analysis further supports the biological relevance of the observed metabolic alterations. The primary PLS-DA latent variable, which summarizes the main metabolic gradient, correlated strongly with lymphocyte and monocyte counts and inversely with arterial oxygenation. These associations mirror known patterns of immune dysregulation and respiratory compromise in severe disease.

Specific metabolites, including glutamine, citrate, proline, myo-inositol, TMAO, and phenylalanine, showed strong correlations with immune cell counts and CRP. Similar relationships have been documented in studies linking metabolic stress to immune exhaustion, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired oxidative metabolism in ARDS and other critical illnesses [55, 59–63]. These findings highlight the potential of NMR-derived metabolic biomarkers as interpretable markers of disease biology.

Broader implications beyond COVID-19

Although this analysis focused on COVID-19, many of the identified pathways, TCA cycle disruption, amino acid imbalance, lipid mobilization, and mitochondrial stress, are core features of acute respiratory failure regardless of etiology. Prior metabolomic studies on influenza pneumonia, bacterial pneumonia, and non-infectious ARDS have described similar disturbances [5, 18, 36, 38, 64, 65]. Thus, the signatures identified here may be generalized to the broader context of respiratory insufficiency, potentially informing metabolic phenotyping in future respiratory epidemics.

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of this study includes the parallel use of two independent NMR platforms, the convergence of results across multivariate, univariate, and pathway analyses, and the integrative assessment of clinical–metabolite correlations. The ability of a low-field bNMR instrument to reproduce key metabolic signatures identified by HR-NMR is a notable finding with direct translational relevance to clinical practice.

Nonetheless, this study has some limitations. The cohort reflects the early pandemic period, with heterogeneity in diagnostic and treatment practices across different sites. Group sizes were unbalanced, a common limitation in COVID-19 studies that increases the risk of overfitting despite cross-validation and permutation testing. While permutation testing supported the predictive validity of the models, the lower statistical significance of R2Y in some bNMR analyses suggests an increased susceptibility to overfitting, likely related to the reduced spectral resolution and signal-to-noise ratio of low-field systems. ROC/AUC values were derived from model outputs using the MetaboAnalyst platform and may therefore represent optimistic estimates of performance, as they were not based on independent or out-of-fold predictions. Additionally, correlations between clinical variables and PLS components should be interpreted with caution, as these components are influenced by class labels and may reflect supervised model structure.

Some metabolites showed statistically significant correlations with age and sex; however, these associations were generally moderate in magnitude, explaining only a limited proportion of the observed variance. This suggests that demographic factors may contribute partially to the metabolic profiles but do not fully account for the discrimination between severity groups. Nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly given the imbalance in baseline characteristics across groups. Future studies including larger and more balanced cohorts will be necessary to perform fully adjusted multivariable analyses and validate these findings.

Direct quantitative comparison between HR-NMR and benchtop NMR remains challenging due to differences in spectral resolution, signal overlap, and preprocessing approaches. As a result, metabolite concentrations are not directly comparable across platforms. Furthermore, metabolite quantification was based on relative signal intensities rather than absolute concentrations. This is particularly relevant for benchtop NMR, where baseline distortions, signal overlaps, and water-suppression artifacts may affect peak integration more strongly than in high-resolution spectra. Future studies specifically designed for cross-platform standardization and quantitative agreement analysis, such as Bland–Altman approaches, would be required to fully assess method concordance.

This study was performed using serum samples due to the practical constraints at the time of sample collection. Serum was also preferred because it avoids spectral contributions from anticoagulants (e.g., EDTA or citrate), which may interfere with NMR measurements, particularly in benchtop systems. However, serum preparation requires coagulation, which may induce metabolic changes related to platelet activation and ongoing cellular metabolism, such as increased lactate, decreased glucose, and variations in certain amino acids. In contrast, plasma prevents clot formation but may still exhibit metabolic alterations due to residual cellular metabolism prior to processing. Future studies including both serum and plasma samples could facilitate comparisons across studies and further evaluate matrix-related differences.

An additional limitation is that two-dimensional NMR experiments were only performed on the high-resolution platform and not on the benchtop system. This reflects the current technical constraints of low-field NMR, where 2D acquisitions are limited by sensitivity and require prohibitively long acquisition times. Consequently, metabolite assignments in the benchtop spectra relied on 1D spectral features supported by prior validation using HR-NMR. However, this approach is consistent with the intended clinical application of benchtop NMR, where rapid 1D-based acquisition and analysis are essential for practical implementation.

Future directions

Future studies should aim to validate these findings in diverse populations and other causes of respiratory failure. The acquisition time required for benchtop NMR (~ 32 min per sample) reflects the need for signal averaging in low-field systems and may limit immediate bedside implementation. However, ongoing developments in hardware and acquisition strategies may enable faster measurements, improving the feasibility of near-patient applications. The development of simplified metabolite panels or automated interpretation algorithms could support bedside implementation. Integrating metabolomics with clinical parameters, radiology, and machine learning approaches may enhance predictive accuracy and support real-time clinical decision-making in emergency and intensive care settings.

Conclusions

Serum NMR metabolomics identifies robust metabolic signatures associated with respiratory severity and demonstrates strong agreement between high-resolution and benchtop NMR spectroscopy. These findings support the feasibility of low-field bNMR as a scalable, low-maintenance technology for patient stratification in acute respiratory illnesses. The identified biological pathways extend beyond COVID-19 and may represent generalizable metabolic responses to severe respiratory compromise.
