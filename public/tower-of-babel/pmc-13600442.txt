Serotype-specific Immunity and Association With Pneumococcal Carriage Following 3+1, 3+0, and 2+1 Schedules of 10-valent Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccine
Authors: Darren Suryawijaya Ong, Zheng Quan Toh, Beth Temple, Cattram Duong Nguyen, Thanh V Phan, Vo Thi Trang Dai, Anne Balloch, Rachel Ann Higgins, Belinda Daniela Ortika, Jemima Beissbarth, Kathryn Bright, Kien Vientrung, Leena Spry, Monica Larissa Nation, Nhung Vu Pham Hong, Nguyen Trong Toan, Pham Thi Hoan, Yin Bun Cheung, Heidi Smith-Vaughan, Catherine Satzke, Tran Ngoc Huu, Kim Mulholland, Paul V Licciardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCorrelates of protection for pneumococcal carriage, a precursor for invasive pneumococcal disease, are not well-defined. The relationship between memory B cells (Bmem) with carriage remains unclear. We assessed serotype-specific Bmem, IgG, and opsonophagocytic (OPA) responses following different infant schedules of 10-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV10, Synflorix) and their association with pneumococcal carriage.MethodsWe analyzed secondary outcome data from a phase II/III randomized controlled trial in Vietnam (trial registration NCT01953510). Infants received PCV10 in a 3 + 1 (2–3–4–9 months), 3 + 0 (2–3–4 months), or 2 + 1 (2–4–9.5 months) schedule. Serotype-specific Bmem, IgG, and OPA were measured at 10 months (all) and 18 months (Bmem and IgG only). Carriage was assessed from nasopharyngeal swabs at 12–18–24 months with additional carriage episodes identified by seroincidence. Associations between immunological parameters at 10 months and subsequent carriage were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsAt 10 months, Bmem levels were similar across vaccine arms, but IgG and OPA were higher in the 3 + 1/2 + 1 than 3 + 0 arm. At 18 months, Bmem and IgG waned for most serotypes in all vaccine arms. There were minimal associations between the immunological parameters and subsequent carriage. Only IgG was negatively associated with serotype 19A carriage, when defined as carriage and/or seroincidence ≥2-fold (odds ratio: 0.31, 95% CI: .10–.94, P = .038), and carriage and/or seroincidence of any increase (odds ratio: 0.13, 95% CI: .04–.40, P < .001).ConclusionsWe found no substantive evidence linking Bmem, IgG, and OPA with pneumococcal carriage. Larger studies are needed to identify associations between different immunological parameters and carriage.

Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV) have substantially reduced vaccine-type pneumococcal carriage and invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) [1]. Between 2000 and 2030, an estimated 697 000 pneumococcal deaths, 46 million disability-adjusted life years and 131 million cases will be averted in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to 13-valent PCV [2]. PCV manufacturers recommend a 3 + 1 schedule (3-dose primary series with a booster dose) of 10- or 13-valent PCV (PCV10 [Synflorix, GSK, and PNEUMOSIL, Serum Institute of India] or PCV13 [Prev[e]nar, Pfizer]), while the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a 3-dose schedule of either vaccine given either as a 3 + 0 schedule (3-dose primary series with no booster dose) or 2 + 1 schedule (2-dose primary series with a booster dose) [3]. Higher valency PCVs, such as PCV14/PCV15/PCV20, have been licensed and are used in some countries [4]. Recent recommendations in September 2025 by the WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) also include a 1 + 1 schedule for settings with established herd immunity [3]. While long-term, high-coverage PCV programs are essential for herd immunity, this remains challenging in most LMICs. Moreover, in LMICs where high coverage has been achieved, the higher force of infection relative to high-income countries has resulted in lower levels of herd protection [5, 6].

Long-term protection against carriage and disease relies on protective serum antibody levels and immunological memory [7]. Regulatory agencies consider serotype-specific IgG concentrations and opsonophagocytic (OPA) titers to be the gold standard for licensure of new PCV products. These immunogenicity measures are also used to demonstrate non-inferiority between different vaccine formulations and schedules. An aggregated serotype-specific IgG antibody concentration of 0.35 μg/mL was established as a correlate of protection against IPD [8]. However, later studies have suggested that this threshold varies by individual serotypes [9, 10]. Moreover, this threshold does not accurately reflect protection against carriage [10]. Serum antibody levels typically peak 1-month post-vaccination and wane substantially thereafter. Despite antibody waning as seen in several studies [11, 12], protection against IPD and carriage, a precursor for IPD, is maintained. Protective thresholds for carriage have not been established and are likely to vary between serotypes [10].

Memory B cells (Bmem), induced post-PCV vaccination, are thought to be a novel marker of long-term protection against carriage [7]. Although adult challenge studies have demonstrated the protective role of Bmem against carriage [13], its role in young children remains unclear due to differences in both epidemiological burden and immunological maturity. To our knowledge, only 3 studies have explored the relationship between Bmem and antibody responses in infants. The first study administered a PCV10/PCV13 booster to infants who received a 2-dose primary series and found a positive correlation at 1-month post-booster, but mixed results at pre- and 12 months post-booster [14]. The second study administered PCV10/PCV13 to infants in a 1 + 1 or 0 + 1 schedule and found that Bmem did not wane as rapidly as IgG by 24 months of age [15]. The third study administered a PCV10/PCV13 booster to infants who received a 3-dose primary series and found higher serotype-specific Bmem to shared serotypes and serotypes 6A and 19A [16]. The relationship between different immunological parameters such as Bmem, IgG, and OPA following different PCV schedules and their association with carriage remains unclear.

We previously conducted 2 randomized controlled trials (RCT) in Vietnam: (1) VPT1 compared different PCV10 schedules and a PCV13 schedule (2 + 1) and (2) VPT2 compared reduced-dose PCV10 and PCV13 schedules (1 + 1 vs 0 + 1). We have reported serotype-specific antibody and carriage outcomes from both VPT1 [11, 17–19] and VPT2 [12, 15], and Bmem responses from VPT2 [15]. Bmem data from VPT1 have only been reported for the control group who received PCV10 at 18 months of age [20]. Here, we report serotype-specific Bmem responses in Vietnamese children following 3 + 1, 3 + 0, and 2 + 1 schedules of PCV10 as part of a secondary analysis of VPT1. This study extends findings from other papers [10], by exploring the relationship between Bmem/antibody and carriage as part of a RCT. We then applied multivariable regression analyses to determine the relationship between Bmem, IgG, and OPA responses with subsequent pneumococcal carriage in the second year of life.

METHODS

Study Design

VPT1 was a single-blind, open-label, phase II/III, non-inferiority RCT evaluating 6 different infant PCV schedules (trial registration NCT01953510) [21]. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Northern Territory Department of Health and Menzies School of Health Research (ethics approval number 09/19) and the Vietnam Ministry of Health Ethics Committee. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Pasteur Institute of Ho Chi Minh City.

Participants

Infants aged between 2 months and 2 months plus 2 weeks, with no significant maternal or perinatal history, and born at 36 weeks of gestation or more, were recruited in commune health centers in districts 4 and 7 of Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. Written informed consent was provided by a parent or legal guardian. Full details of the eligibility criteria and study setting have been published previously [21].

Study Procedures

A total of 1201 participants were randomly assigned to 6 infant vaccination schedules, as previously described [21]. This analysis included infants who received PCV10 in a 3 + 1 (2, 3, 4, and 9 months), 3 + 0 (2, 3, and 4 months), or 2 + 1 (2, 4, and 9.5 months) schedule (Figure 1). Blood samples for this analysis were collected at 10 and 18 months and nasopharyngeal swabs at 12, 18, and 24 months of age. At 18 months, only 50 participants in each arm provided a blood sample due to ethical considerations [21]. IgG and nasopharyngeal carriage was measured for all trial participants, while Bmem and OPA were measured in a subset of 50 and 100 participants per arm, respectively (Supplementary Appendix A).

Serotype-specific Immunological Assays

Blood samples were analyzed for immunological markers. Pneumococcal serotype-specific Bmem levels were measured for serotypes 1, 5, 6B, 14, 18C, 19A, and 23F using an ELISpot assay as previously described (Supplementary Appendix B) [22]. Results were reported as antibody secreting cells (ASC) per million cultured peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC).

Serotype-specific IgG concentrations were measured for all PCV13 serotypes using a modified WHO ELISA as previously described [23]. Results were reported as micrograms per milliliter.

Serotype-specific opsonophagocytosis was measured for all PCV13 serotypes using a multiplexed OPA method as previously described [24]. Results were reported as opsonic indexes (OI) and were only measured at 10 months. Here, we report IgG and OPA results for the 7 serotypes with Bmem data.

Measurement of Nasopharyngeal Carriage

Nasopharyngeal swabs were analyzed for pneumococcal carriage as previously described [17]. Briefly, samples from 9 and 12 months were analyzed using conventional culture methods and presumptive isolates were identified based on α-hemolysis, colony morphology, and optochin susceptibility. Samples from 18 and 24 months underwent latex agglutination and Quellung serotyping [25, 26], and isolates were analyzed by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction prior to molecular serotyping by microarray [27, 28]. Carriage data at 9 months was used as an adjustment, as this was the final time point preceding 10 months, while data at 12, 18, and 24 months were used as outcomes in the regression analyses.

Statistical Analysis

Stata/SE 18.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.5.0 were used for statistical analyses. Outcome data were reported by vaccine arm (3 + 1, 3 + 0, and 2 + 1) at each time point (Bmem and IgG: 10 and 18 months; OI: 10 months; carriage: 12, 18, and 24 months). To describe the distributions of serotype-specific Bmem, IgG, and OI levels at each time point, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were calculated for each vaccine arm. For the comparison of Bmem between time points, we used multilevel negative binomial models to handle overdispersion and excess zero counts. For the comparison of IgG between time points, multilevel linear mixed models were used (Supplementary Appendix C). We calculated the proportion of participants at 18 months who had a detectable level of Bmem or IgG ≥0.35 µg/mL and performed Firth's penalized maximum likelihood regression between vaccine arms. Using Firth's method to estimate logistic regression models prevents bias arising from rare events [29]. To identify associations between serotype-specific Bmem, IgG, and OI levels at 10 months and serotype-specific nasopharyngeal carriage at 18 or 24 months, we performed multivariable regression adjusted for vaccine arm and serotype-specific carriage status at 9 months of age. As carriage was rare in this data, we also used Firth's method. Carriage status was limited to the most frequently carried serotypes with Bmem data available (6B, 19A, and 23F). As there were few serotype-specific carriers detected by nasopharyngeal carriage, we used serology to identify additional carriers. We repeated the regression analyses using a combined outcome of nasopharyngeal carriage and/or a ≥4-fold, ≥2-fold or any increase in serotype-specific IgG concentration from 10 to 18 months (seroincidence) [30]. Carriage regressions were complete case analyses, only conducted in participants with carriage and immunological data. Odds ratios are described as a fold change in the odds of carriage for every standard deviation (SD) increase in Bmem, IgG, or OI. All other analyses are described in Supplementary Appendix C.

RESULTS

A total of 1424 infants were screened between 30 September 2013 and 9 January 2015, with 1201 infants enrolled (Figure 2). A total of 152, 149, and 250 infants were allocated to the 3 + 1, 3 + 0, and 2 + 1 PCV10 schedules, respectively, while the remaining 650 infants were allocated to other vaccine arms and were not included in this analysis. Participant characteristics at baseline and at 10 months of age were similar across arms, except sex (Supplementary Table 1). We observed some differences in participant characteristics between the full cohort and Bmem and OPA subsets (Supplementary Table 1). Participant retention by 10 months was 95% (n = 144/152), 91% (n = 135/149), and 94% (n = 235/250) for the 3 + 1, 3 + 0, and 2 + 1 arms, respectively; all attrition was due to participant withdrawal.

Comparison of Immunological Outcomes at 10 and 18 Months

When assessing the degree of waning between 10 and 18 months, Bmem levels decreased for all serotypes across the 3 arms, except serotype 18C in the 3 + 1 and 2 + 1 arms, with reductions of 60–88% (Figure 3 and Supplementary Table 2). IgG concentrations also decreased, except serotype 19A in the 3 + 0 arm, with reductions of 3–88% (Supplementary Table 2). When comparing vaccine arms at each time point, serotype-specific Bmem levels were similar across arms at both 10 and 18 months (Figure 4 and Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 3). At 18 months, serotypes 6B, 14, and 19A had a median Bmem level of 0 across all vaccine arms (Supplementary Table 2). Serotype-specific IgG concentrations were generally highest in the 3 + 1 arm, followed by the 2 + 1 and 3 + 0 arms, at 10 months (Figure 4 and Supplementary Table 3), and had a similar pattern at 18 months as expected albeit with fewer differences between arms (Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 3). Serotype-specific OI also had a similar pattern at 10 months (Figure 4 and Supplementary Table 3).

Proportion of Participants With Detectable Bmem Levels or IgG Above Immunogenicity Threshold at 18 Months

Bmem was detected in 31–71%, 35–55%, and 27–56% of participants in the 3 + 1, 3 + 0, and 2 + 1 arms, respectively (Figure 5 and Supplementary Table 4). When comparing vaccine arms, the proportions were higher in 3 + 1 than 2 + 1 for serotypes 1 (P = .019) and 23F (P = .033) and 3 + 1 than 3 + 0 for serotype 5 (P = .030). IgG was ≥0.35 µg/mL in 75–96%, 35–98%, and 74–98% of participants in the 3 + 1, 3 + 0, and 2 + 1 arms, respectively (Figure 5 and Supplementary Table 4). When comparing vaccine arms, the proportions were higher in 3 + 1 than 3 + 0 for serotypes 1 (P = .002), 5 (P = .030), 18C (P < .001), and 23F (P = .006) and 2 + 1 than 3 + 0 for serotypes 1 (P = .002) and 18C (P < .001).

Associations Between Immunological Outcomes and Pneumococcal Carriage

There were no differences in pneumococcal carriage prevalence between vaccine arms. At 18 and 24 months, most pneumococcal carriers (n = 68/86, 79%) for the serotypes tested were non-carriers at the preceding time point. There were no associations between Bmem, IgG, and OI levels at 10 months and subsequent carriage at 18 or 24 months (Table 1). Similarly, there were no associations when participants with a seroincidence of ≥4-fold increase were included (Table 1). Using a seroincidence threshold of ≥2-fold or any increase, we observed a 69% (P = .038) and 87% (P < .001) decrease in the odds of carrying serotype 19A, respectively, for every SD increase in IgG. Conversely, for Bmem, we observed a 65% (P = .037) increase in the odds of carrying serotype 6B with every SD increase in Bmem using the “any increase” seroincidence threshold. No associations were observed when stratified by vaccine arm (Supplementary Table 5) or when nasopharyngeal carriage at the 12-month time point was added (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). We observed similar results when the analysis was stratified by time point (Supplementary Table 8). Across serotypes 6B, 19A, and 23F, the ROC curves and corresponding AUCs (range: 0.27–0.67) demonstrated poor discriminatory ability of all 3 immunological parameters in predicting subsequent carriage (Supplementary Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

Different PCV schedules are used globally due to varied disease epidemiology and logistical considerations. Consequently, it is important to understand the duration of protection afforded by different schedules. We described the immunogenicity measured by serotype-specific Bmem, IgG, and OPA responses induced by current PCV10 infant schedules (3 + 1, 3 + 0, and 2 + 1) and their relationship with pneumococcal carriage in the second year of life.

We observed higher IgG concentrations and OIs in the 3 + 1 and 2 + 1 than 3 + 0 arm, as expected, with no differences between the 3 + 1 and 2 + 1 arms when comparing the proportion of participants with IgG ≥0.35 µg/mL (immunogenicity threshold) at 18 months. This reinforces the primary immunogenicity endpoint of our main trial, where the 2-dose primary series was noninferior to a 3-dose primary series [11]. This current study is strengthened with the addition of Bmem responses. The role of serotype-specific Bmem responses in protection against carriage following PCV use in children remains an important question. A meta-analysis of PCV trials found that post-vaccination antibody levels were similar between 2- and 3-dose primary vaccination series (both with and without an additional booster dose), except for serotypes 6B and 23F [31]. However, the clinical relevance of these immunological differences is unclear. In our study, post-vaccine serotype-specific Bmem levels at 10 months of age were similar across all vaccine arms. This is despite the absence of a booster dose in the 3 + 0 arm, and Bmem levels in this arm being measured 4 months after the final dose of the primary series. This likely reflects the longer persistence of Bmem compared with antibodies. While several studies have assessed Bmem responses with individual PCV schedules, none have directly compared Bmem levels across current 3- or 4-dose infant PCV schedules. A study from the UK found increased Bmem levels following a PCV13 but not PCV10 2 + 1 booster dose at 12 months of age, compared with pre-booster levels [14]. In the USA, Bmem levels were similar post-dose 2 and 3, but elevated post-booster dose, in infants that received a PCV13 3 + 1 schedule [32]. Children that received a 3 + 0 infant schedule of PCV7/13 had elevated Bmem after a PCV13 booster at 3.5 years of age, which was the only study we identified that examined long-term Bmem responses beyond 12 months post-booster [33].

Between 10 and 18 months, both Bmem and IgG waned for most serotypes in all vaccine arms. While IgG concentrations tend to peak 1 month following PCV administration [11], Bmem levels peak by 7 days post-PCV with 1 + 1 and 0 + 1 PCV schedules [15]. Our current finding that Bmem did not wane as rapidly as IgG by 18 months of age is consistent with our previous findings [15]. The main limitation of using blood to measure Bmem is that nasopharyngeal carriage is a mucosal phenomenon, so it is unclear how serological levels accurately reflect mucosal levels. However, serological testing is the best approximate as it is challenging to measure mucosal Bmem levels in young children [34].

Only 2 other studies have measured B cells at 7 days post-vaccination. One study observed no differences in the frequency of serotype-specific splenic marginal zone B cells at 7 days or 1 month post-second dose of PCV7 or 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine [35]. Another study found that serotype 4, 14, and 23F-specific Bmem increased at days 6–7 post-first and second doses of PCV7 [36]. Our current study did not measure Bmem at 7 days post-vaccination due to ethical considerations, which might have impacted our ability to measure the peak responses and true waning effect. Nevertheless, we were still able to detect serotype-specific Bmem responses 1-month post-vaccination with further waning by 18 months of age. This is consistent with our previous study showing a detectable increase in Bmem 1-month post-vaccination [22].

Pneumococcal carriage is a precursor for IPD, but correlates of protection for carriage are not well-defined. While studies have tried to establish these correlates [10], most have focused on antibodies, and none have explored Bmem responses. Compared with other studies, our trial was conducted in a PCV-naive setting, thereby allowing us to measure associations between immunological parameters and carriage. Based on earlier findings from the trial, either a 3 + 1, 3 + 0, or 2 + 1 PCV10 schedule was similarly effective in reducing vaccine-type carriage during the first 2 years of life [17], consistent with the similar Bmem responses observed at 10 months of age, whereas differences in IgG and OI were observed between either the 3 + 1/2 + 1 and 3 + 0 schedules. Our current analysis found minimal associations between immunological parameters at 10 months and subsequent carriage up to 24 months of age. This is likely due to the low incidence of serotype-specific carriage detected in this study (11–14% for vaccine-type at 12–24 months in unvaccinated controls) [17]. Other studies have mixed findings regarding the association between IgG or OI and carriage [37–40]. In an adult challenge model of participants who were previously colonized with pneumococcus, polysaccharide-specific Bmem levels at the time of pneumococcal exposure, but not IgG levels, were associated with protection against carriage [34]. However, immunity generated through natural exposure could differ from immunization with PCV [41].

Nasopharyngeal carriage was only measured at 6 monthly intervals and it is possible that some carriers were missed. To improve the sensitivity of the carriage analysis, we incorporated seroincidence to identify additional carriers that were not identified using swabs. We found that an increase in serotype 19A-specific IgG decreased, while serotype 6B-specific Bmem increased, the odds of carriage. We note that serotype 6B-specific antibody responses increased over time following vaccination, rather than the waning seen with other serotypes, so this may have impacted our results using the less stringent “any increase” seroincidence definition. Furthermore, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small number of carriers in our study and the potential for misclassification when using the seroincidence definition, especially the “any increase” definition. A separate publication of ours showed that PCV10 induces an IgG response to serotype 19A, a non-PCV10 serotype [11], which has the potential to impact carriage. In our current analysis, we demonstrated that Bmem, IgG, and OI were minimally associated with carriage reduction. While we incorporated seroincidence to identify additional carriers, the natural waning of IgG over time meant that we may have still missed some carriage episodes. As previously discussed, measuring mucosal Bmem responses may be more accurate but is challenging in young children. Furthermore, measuring carriage at 6 monthly intervals may have missed the time points when immunity most strongly influenced carriage. To mitigate this, we incorporated analysis of seroincidence to identify potentially missed carriage episodes. Given the low carriage prevalence, even in a PCV-naive setting, and the limited number of nasopharyngeal swab collection time points, larger studies are needed to identify any associations between immunological parameters and carriage to better predict vaccine protection.

A major strength of this study is that it is one of the few studies to analyze Bmem responses as part of a large RCT in a PCV-naive population. This maximized our ability to assess immunological associations with carriage. However, a key limitation was that compared with IgG, Bmem and OPA were measured in a smaller proportion of participants. Secondly, we did not measure Bmem at 7 days post-vaccination, which may have limited the potential to identify any associations with carriage acquisition. This is planned for our other trial of reduced-dose PCV schedules in Vietnam [15, 42], where we have previously reported Bmem levels at 7 days post-vaccination. Thirdly, different methods were used to measure carriage at 9/12 months and 18/24 months, but the same methodology was used across all vaccine arms at each time point so any differences in assay sensitivity would be applied uniformly across the arms [17]. Fourthly, carriage analysis was limited due to few nasopharyngeal carriers detected and seroincidence could not be measured between 18 and 24 months of age as we did not measure immune outcomes at 24 months. Lastly, our findings among Vietnamese children may not be generalizable to settings with substantially different pneumococcal carriage prevalence.

CONCLUSION

This study describes serotype-specific Bmem responses following 3 + 1, 3 + 0, and 2 + 1 infant schedules of PCV10 in a PCV-naive setting. We observed differences in serotype-specific antibody responses, but Bmem levels were similar across vaccine arms. The small number of carriage episodes limited our ability to identify meaningful associations with immunological parameters, warranting larger studies to disentangle the complex relationships between different immune markers and carriage. Identifying potential correlates of protection and their associations with carriage is crucial to improve PCV programs. This can enhance the evaluation of PCVs to maximize protection of children from pneumococcal disease.
