Post-translational modifications in metabolic reprogramming: implications for metabolic therapy and immunotherapy in cancer
Authors: Si-Jie Chen, Shi-Wei Yue, Yun-Pu Zhang, Hui-Fang Liang, Hai-Xin Wu, Xiao-Ping Chen, Bi-Xiang Zhang, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer metabolism is characterized by profound reprogramming, yet the mechanisms enabling rapid and precise adaptation remain incompletely understood. This review establishes post-translational modifications (PTMs) as the central processing unit of oncogenic metabolic reprogramming. PTMs execute a conserved three-tiered regulatory logic: they interpret oncogenic and environmental signals, command metabolic flux, and cement malignant phenotypes through epigenetic and feedback mechanisms. We systematically demonstrate how this PTM-driven logic governs key pillars of cancer metabolism—glucose, lipid, amino acid, and nucleotide utilization—and extends its command to critical cell fate execution programs, including mitochondrial dynamics, autophagy, and ferroptosis. Furthermore, we delineate how PTMs act as master regulators of immunometabolic reprogramming within the tumor microenvironment (TME), directly linking tumor metabolism to T cell exhaustion, myeloid cell polarization, and immune evasion. By integrating recent advances on the determinants and crosstalk of PTM networks, we describe how metabolic plasticity and heterogeneity are encoded at the PTM level, with metabolic gradients shaping distinct “PTM geographies” within tumors. Finally, we translate these insights into clinical prospects, highlighting PTM-based biomarkers, PTM-targeted drugs and emerging therapeutic strategies, including targeted protein degradation, PTM-targeted vaccines and dietary interventions. Deciphering this PTM-encoded program reveals a new landscape of therapeutic vulnerabilities, shifting the paradigm toward rationally targeting the fundamental computational logic that sustains tumors.Subject terms: Cancer metabolism, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Cancer is increasingly regarded as a disease of metabolic disorder. To support sustained proliferation, survival under stress, and metastasis, tumor cells undergo profound metabolic reprogramming—a hallmark capability driven by the cooption of core biochemical pathways. For decades, research has focused on transcriptional master regulators such as MYC and oncogenic driver mutations to explain this rewiring.1 However, transcriptional changes are relatively slow, and a significant subset of tumors lacks clear canonical drivers.2 This presents a central paradox: how do cancer cells achieve the rapid, reversible, and exquisitely precise metabolic adaptations required to thrive in a dynamically hostile microenvironment characterized by fluctuating nutrient availability, therapeutic assault, and immune pressure? The answer likely lies not in genetic hardwiring alone but in a more agile, pervasive layer of cellular regulation that can instantly interpret environmental cues and execute metabolic commands.

This agile regulatory layer is epitomized by post-translational modifications (PTMs). The covalent attachment of chemical groups to proteins—such as phosphate or acetyl groups—provides a swift and tunable mechanism to alter protein activity, stability, localization, and interactions.3,4 This regulatory versatility is now being mapped at scale by global profiling studies, which powerfully illustrate the scope and functional centrality of PTM-driven metabolic control in tumor growth and the tumor microenvironment (TME). For instance, integrative lactylome and proteome analyses of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) have identified thousands of lysine lactylation (Kla) sites, with this metabolite-derived modification preferentially targeting enzymes across core metabolic pathways, including the TCA cycle and carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolism.5 Critically, specific lactylation events on histones (e.g., H3K56la) and glycolytic enzymes (e.g., ALDOA) have been shown to be critical for maintaining the stem-like properties of liver cancer stem cells.6 These findings underscore that PTMs constitute a pervasive functional code, translating metabolic states into precise regulatory commands. However, most research to date has focused on individual PTM types or sites. A critical and unresolved question is how diverse PTMs function not as isolated events but as an integrated regulatory system to orchestrate the full spectrum of metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion.

To address this gap, this review presents a unifying framework. We propose that PTMs act as the central processing unit of oncogenic metabolic reprogramming, operating through a conserved, three-tiered logic: sensing oncogenic and environmental signals, executing precise metabolic flux decisions, and consolidating malignant phenotypes through epigenetic and signaling feedback. Beyond optimizing bioenergetics and biosynthesis, this PTM-centric system directly couples metabolic activity to cell fate decisions and actively orchestrates the immunometabolic landscape within the TME to promote immune evasion. To build this case, we first outline the core PTM machinery and its metabolic connections (Overview of post-translational modifications). We then systematically dissect how PTMs command glucose, lipid, amino acid, and nucleotide metabolism and further extend their command to critical cell fate execution programs (PTMs and tumor metabolic reprogramming). Section “PTMs of immunometabolic reprogramming in the tumor microenvironment” details how PTMs direct the metabolic reprogramming of immune populations, establishing a direct link between tumor metabolism and immune dysfunction. Delving into the principles enabling this precision, the section “Determinants and dynamics of PTM function in cancer metabolism” explores the multilayered determinants of PTM function and their intricate crosstalk. Finally, section “Clinical implications and therapeutic prospects of PTMs in cancer metabolism” translates this foundational understanding into the clinical realm, reviewing PTM-based biomarkers, emerging therapeutic strategies that target the network’s unique vulnerabilities, and future directions toward “network therapeutics”.

By integrating PTM biology, cancer metabolism, and immunometabolism, this review not only synthesizes a rapidly expanding body of work but also establishes PTMs as the mechanistic linchpin connecting nutrient utilization to malignant progression and immune escape. Ultimately, deciphering this PTM-encoded program reveals novel therapeutic vulnerabilities for disrupting the very metabolic circuitry that sustains tumors.

Overview of post-translational modifications

PTMs refer to the chemical modifications made to proteins during or after their synthesis, which include the addition of functional groups or other molecules to the protein structure. PTMs act as “master switches” that regulate protein activity, stability, localization, and interactions within the cell.

A brief review of the history of post-translational modifications research

The study of PTMs has undergone a remarkable evolution, driven by sequential technological breakthroughs and conceptual shifts (Fig. 1). The journey began in 1906 with the identification of phosphorylation, the first known PTM.7 Its profound regulatory significance, however, was not established until the 1950s through the groundbreaking work of Edmond H. Fischer and Edwin G. Krebs, who deciphered its role as a central switch in cellular signaling and metabolism—a contribution awarded the Nobel Prize in 1992.8–12

Timeline of key discoveries in PTMs from 1906 to 2025. This timeline traces the historical evolution of PTM discovery, beginning with phosphorylation (1906, Nobel Prize 1992). Key milestones include histone acetylation (1963), ubiquitin (1975, Nobel Prize 2004), and the mass spectrometry-driven discoveries of the 1980s, such as S-palmitoylation, S-prenylation, N-myristoylation, N-glycosylation, and O-GlcNAcylation. The 1990s introduced ubiquitin-like modifiers (neddylation, sumoylation, UFMylation). The 21st century has seen an explosion of metabolite-derived acylations, including crotonylation, succinylation, lactylation (2019), and most recently vitcylation (2025). This timeline underscores the growing recognition of PTMs as a dynamic, metabolically integrated regulatory system

The 1960–1970s marked the expansion of the PTM landscape beyond phosphorylation. Acetylation was reported on histones in 1963, linking it to chromatin regulation. A pivotal advance came in 1975 with the discovery of ubiquitin,13 followed by the demonstration of histone ubiquitination in 1977, unveiling a dedicated pathway for protein degradation.14 This work contributed to the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2004.

Technological leaps in the 1980s—especially the advent of mass spectrometry—unleashed a wave of PTM discovery. Within a single decade, the lipid anchors S-palmitoylation (1979)15,16 and S-prenylation (fungi 197817; mammals 198818) were defined, while the glyco-ensemble expanded to include N-myristoylation (1982),19 N-glycosylation and O-GalNAcylation (1983),20 and nutrient-responsive O-GlcNAcylation (1984),21 collectively revealing how chemical tags dictate protein localization, stability and function. The 1990s further revealed the complexity of PTM systems with the discovery of ubiquitin-like modifiers (e.g., NEDD8,22–24 SUMO,25,26 and UFM127), highlighting the sophistication of modification-based signaling networks.

Phosphorylation

Phosphorylation is the quintessential energy-sensing PTM. Kinases and phosphatases act as the writer and eraser enzymes, respectively, transferring or removing a phosphate group from ATP to specific serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues. Crucially, the availability of the donor molecule ATP is a direct readout of the cell’s energetic flux through glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation. This creates a direct feedback loop: metabolic activity regulates ATP levels, which in turn modulates global kinase/phosphatase equilibria and downstream signaling cascades (e.g., AMPK, AKT). In cancer, oncogenic kinases often hijack this system to enforce a pro-growth metabolic program, such as constitutive AKT phosphorylation promoting glucose uptake and glycolytic flux.

Methylation

Methylation involves adding a methyl group (-CH₃) to specific lysine or arginine residues on proteins. Methyltransferases, such as EZH2 and the PRMT family, act as “writers”, utilizing S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) as the universal methyl donor to install single or multiple methyl groups on these residues. Demethylases such as LSD1 and the JmjC family remove these marks, ensuring reversibility. “Reader” proteins containing domains such as Tudor or Chromo interpret these methylation signals. SAM synthesis depends on the essential amino acids methionine and ATP and is regulated by folate and vitamin B12, tightly coupling the cellular methylation state to nutritional status.

Lysine acylations

Lysine acylations are a functionally diverse family in which various acyl groups are attached to Lys residues, including acetylation, lactylation, succinylation, and crotonylation. These modifications serve as direct molecular conduits, translating metabolic intermediate abundance into regulatory instructions for protein function and chromatin dynamics.

Protein acetylation, catalyzed by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and lysine acetyltransferases (KATs), such as the p300/CBP,42 GNAT,43,44 and MYST families,45,46 transfers an acetyl group from acetyl-coenzyme A to target lysine. This neutralization of the positive charge of lysine can alter protein conformation, disrupt electrostatic interactions, and modulate activity, stability, and protein‒protein interactions. The reverse reaction is catalyzed by lysine deacetylases (KDACs), comprising zinc-dependent HDACs47 and NAD+-dependent sirtuins (SIRT1-7).48 The signal is interpreted by “reader” proteins containing bromodomains,49 YEATS domains,50 and PHD finger domains.51 As acetyl-CoA integrates flux from glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and the TCA cycle, acetylation acts as a pervasive cellular rheostat linking metabolism to the protein state.52,53

Lactylation, the addition of a lactyl group to lysine, has emerged as a direct molecular bridge connecting high glycolytic activity to the malignant phenotype of tumors. Its establishment can proceed via two potential biochemical routes. The lactyl-CoA pathway involves the activation of lactate to lactyl-CoA by enzymes such as acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family member 2 (ACSS2)54 and nuclear GTPSCS,55 which can then be utilized by certain KATs (e.g., KAT2A,54,56 KAT557) as donor substrates. Alternatively, the lactyl-adenylate (lactyl-AMP) pathway employs alanyl-tRNA synthetases (AARS1/AARS2) to generate lactyl-AMP from lactate for direct transfer to proteins.58,59 The modification is removed primarily by classical deacetylases (HDACs57,60 and SIRTs61,62), and its functional readout involves specific “reader” proteins, such as DPF2 for histone lactylation.63 The physiological relevance of lactyl-CoA as a primary donor remains controversial. Given that intracellular lactate concentrations in cancer cells can reach 10–30 mM and that the Kd values for lactate binding to human AARS1 range from 2 to 35 μM—comparable to those of alanine, the primary substrate of AARS1—some researchers argue that AARS1 is likely to play a significant role in mediating lactylation in vivo.64

Beyond acetylation and lactylation, a diverse array of short-chain lysine acylations (e.g., propionylation, butyrylation, succinylation, crotonylation, glutarylation) significantly expands the regulatory lexicon. These modifications are defined by the transfer of distinct acyl groups from their corresponding acyl-CoA donors. Intriguingly, they often co-opt the enzymatic machinery used for acetylation; broad-specificity acyltransferases such as p300/CBP65–67 and HAT68,69 can utilize various acyl-CoAs, while sirtuins function as the major “erasers”.70,71 The most defining feature of this group lies in the direct and diverse metabolic origins of their donor CoA thioesters, which are intermediates or derivatives of major pathways, including the TCA cycle (succinyl-CoA, glutaryl-CoA), fatty acid metabolism (butyryl-CoA), and amino acid catabolism.

Consequently, fluctuations in the cellular levels of these metabolites are directly translated into changes in the global protein acylation landscape. This mechanism allows mitochondrial metabolic flux or dysregulated nutrient catabolism to directly impinge on nuclear epigenetics and cytoplasmic signaling networks, positioning these acylations as fundamental sensors of metabolic network activity.

Glycosylation

Glycosylation, the attachment of complex carbohydrate chains (glycans) to proteins, is one of the most abundant and functionally consequential PTMs. It governs protein folding, stability, and trafficking and—most critically—mediates the vast majority of specific molecular recognition events at the cell surface and in the extracellular space.72 Based on the linkage between the sugar and the protein, glycosylation is primarily categorized into N-linked glycosylation, O-linked glycosylation, and distinct O-GlcNAcylation.

Classical N-glycosylation (asparagine-linked) and O-glycosylation (serine/threonine-linked) are complex, hierarchical processes occurring within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus.73 These modifications utilize activated nucleotide sugars (e.g., UDP-glucose, GDP-fucose, CMP-sialic acid) as donors, which are synthesized through dedicated pathways branching from core metabolism, including glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and amino acid metabolism. A series of highly specific glycosyltransferases and glycosidases sculpt these glycan structures, creating an immense repertoire of “glyco-codes”.74,75 The functional impact of these glycans is profound. They are indispensable for the proper folding and quality control of secreted and membrane proteins.

In stark contrast to the complex, static glycans of the secretory pathway, O-GlcNAcylation involves the dynamic cycling of a single N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) moiety on serine and threonine residues of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins. This modification is regulated by just two enzymes: O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT), which installs the mark, and O-GlcNAcase (OGA), which removes it.76 The donor substrate, UDP-GlcNAc, is synthesized via the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP), which integrates inputs from glucose (providing glucosamine-6-phosphate), glutamine (providing the amine group) and acetyl-CoA.77 This unique position makes O-GlcNAcylation a master intracellular nutrient and stress sensor. Fluctuations in the levels of glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, and nucleotides directly influence UDP-GlcNAc pools and consequently global O-GlcNAcylation levels.

Lipid-related protein modification

Protein lipidation involves covalent attachment of lipid moieties, primarily directing proteins to membranes and regulating signaling complex assembly. Based on their dynamics and permanence, lipid modifications are broadly categorized into constitutive and reversible types, each playing nonredundant roles in cellular physiology and cancer biology.78

S-prenylation, including farnesylation (C15) and geranylgeranylation (C20), involves the irreversible attachment of isoprenoid lipids to cysteine residues within a C-terminal CaaX motif.79 Catalyzed by farnesyltransferase (FTase) or geranylgeranytransferase (GGTase), this modification is critical for the membrane targeting and function of over 100 proteins, most notably the RAS superfamily of small GTPases such as Kirsten Rat Sarcoma Viral Oncogene Homolog (KRAS).80 The prenyl donor farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) or geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) is synthesized via the mevalonate pathway, directly linking protein prenylation to cellular cholesterol and isoprenoid biosynthesis.

N-myristoylation is a cotranslational, typically irreversible modification where myristic acid (C14:0) is attached via an amide bond to an N-terminal glycine by N-myristoyltransferases (NMT1/2).81 This modification facilitates the stable membrane association of numerous signaling proteins, including Src family kinases and apoptotic regulators.

In contrast to the static nature of prenylation and myristoylation, S-palmitoylation (S-acylation) is a highly dynamic and reversible process. It involves the attachment of long-chain fatty acids, predominantly palmitate (C16:0), to cysteine residues via a thioester bond. This cycle is governed by opposing enzymes: the ZDHHC family of palmitoyl acyltransferases (23 members in humans) act as “writers”, while acyl-protein thioesterases (APTs) and members of the ABHD family serve as “erasers”.82 This reversibility allows S-palmitoylation to function as a rapid molecular switch, regulating protein membrane microdomain partitioning (e.g., lipid rafts), intracellular trafficking, stability, and protein‒protein interactions.83

Ubiquitination and ubiquitin-like modifications

Ubiquitination and its related ubiquitin-like (UBL) modifications constitute a sophisticated regulatory system that governs virtually every aspect of cellular activity, most notably protein degradation and signal transduction. This system employs a conserved enzymatic cascade (E1 activating, E2 conjugating, E3 ligating enzymes) to covalently attach small protein modifiers—ubiquitin or UBLs such as NEDD8 and SUMO—to target proteins. The specificity and outcome of modification are determined by the combinatorial action of hundreds of E3 ligases and the topology of the modifier chain.84

Ubiquitin has eight potential attachment sites—seven lysine residues (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, K63) and an N-terminal methionine (M1)—allowing for the formation of different types of ubiquitin chains. These chains can be classified into monoubiquitination, multimonoubiquitination, and polyubiquitination, each with distinct cellular functions, often referred to as the “ubiquitin code”.85,86 For instance, K48 chains signal proteasomal degradation,87 K63 chains are involved in signaling,88 DNA repair,89 and immune responses,90 and K29 chains regulate stress responses and the cell cycle.91 The human genome contains only two E1 enzymes—UBA1 and UBA6—each with distinct target specificities.92 This “ubiquitin code” is dynamically written by E3 ligases and erased by a family of deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs), allowing rapid cellular adaptation. In the context of metabolism, the ubiquitin‒proteasome system is a crucial post-translational regulator that rapidly adjusts the abundance of rate-limiting metabolic enzymes, transporters, and transcription factors (e.g., HIF-1α, SREBPs) in response to nutrient and oxygen availability.

UBL modifications share the enzymatic logic of ubiquitination but execute more specialized functions. Neddylation involves the attachment of NEDD8 to substrate proteins. NEDD8 is activated by the E1 enzyme (NAE),93 transferred to E2 enzymes (e.g., UBE2M94 or UBE2F95), and conjugated to target proteins by E3 ligases (e.g., RBX1/296). This process is reversible, with the COP9 signalosome removing NEDD8 from substrates.97 The conjugation of NEDD8 primarily activates Cullin-RING E3 ligases, the largest family of ubiquitin ligases.98 By neddylation of the Cullin scaffold, the CRL complex is allosterically activated, thereby promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of its diverse substrates, which include numerous cell cycle regulators and tumor suppressors. This position positions neddylation as a master switch for global protein turnover. SUMOylation is catalyzed by a series of enzymes, including the E1 enzyme (composed of SAE1/AOS199 and SAE2/UBA2100), E2 enzyme Ubc9,101 and E3 ligases such as RanBP2 and PIAS,102 and is reversed by SUMO-specific proteases (SENPs).103 SUMO-1 typically modifies substrates as monomers, while SUMO-2/3 can form poly-SUMO chains, increasing the functional diversity of SUMOylation.104 SUMOylation often acts as a molecular counterweight to ubiquitination, stabilizing proteins or altering their interaction networks in response to stress.105

Core regulatory logic and network properties of PTMs

The diverse array of PTMs constitutes a sophisticated and integrative regulatory language governing cellular processes. These modifications operate through conserved principles that enable precise control over protein function. First, PTMs are inherently dynamic and reversible, maintained in a state of flux by opposing “writer” and “eraser” enzymes, allowing cells to respond rapidly to internal and external cues. Second, their installation is relatively specific, governed by enzyme-substrate recognition, consensus sequences, and cellular compartmentalization, ensuring that signals are translated into accurate functional outcomes. Most critically for cancer metabolism, PTMs serve as direct molecular sensors and effectors of the cellular metabolic state. The availability of their donor substrates—such as ATP for phosphorylation, acetyl-CoA for acetylation, SAM for methylation, and UDP-GlcNAc for O-GlcNAcylation—is directly coupled to the flux through core metabolic pathways, including glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism. Consequently, fluctuations in nutrient availability, energy charge, and redox balance are instantly reflected in the global PTM landscape, making PTMs a central processor that interprets metabolic status into regulatory commands. Finally, PTMs do not function in isolation but engage in extensive crosstalk, forming a complex, information-rich network. Sequential, competitive and cooperative interactions between different modifications on a single protein or within a functional complex create a layered PTM code, which exponentially expands regulatory capacity and specificity. This interconnected network allows for the integration of disparate signals—from growth factors and stressors to metabolic intermediates—into coherent cellular responses. It is this very property of PTMs as context-dependent, metabolically fueled, and networked regulators that underpins their pivotal role in oncogenic metabolic reprogramming, a theme that will be extensively explored in the following sections.

The technological landscape and challenges in post-translational modifications research

Research on PTMs heavily relies on the advancement of detection and characterization technologies. The primary challenges in PTM investigation stem from three inherent biological characteristics: First, the low stoichiometry and high dynamics, where modified proteins often constitute a minute fraction of the proteome and change rapidly with cellular cues. Second, the remarkable complexity and diversity, with over 400 known types of PTMs and the existence of intricate “modification codes”. Third, there is spatial heterogeneity and context dependency, where PTM patterns vary dramatically across cell types and tissue microenvironments. Overcoming these hurdles requires a sophisticated and evolving technological arsenal.

Established technological arsenal and its strategic applications

Conventional strategies form a tripartite pipeline: specific enrichment, sensitive detection, and precise site mapping. For enrichment, immunoprecipitation with modification-specific antibodies remains a cornerstone for isolating low-abundance PTM-bearing proteins from complex mixtures, albeit limited by antibody availability and epitope occlusion. Detection and validation often rely on targeted methods such as Western blotting and the more sensitive proximity ligation assay, which provide crucial spatial context but offer low throughput. At the heart of discovery and systems-level analysis lies mass spectrometry (MS). As a hypothesis-free platform, MS enables high-throughput identification and site-specific mapping of PTMs and is indispensable for unraveling their complexity. However, traditional bottom-up MS workflows struggle with the dynamic range required to capture low-stoichiometry modifications alongside abundant proteins and often involve multistep sample preparation that can compromise quantitative accuracy and sample integrity.

Emerging frontiers and integrative approaches

To address the limitations of bulk analysis and capture PTM heterogeneity, the field is rapidly advancing toward single-cell resolution and integrated functional analysis. Cutting-edge platforms now merge miniaturized sample processing with ultrasensitive MS. A paradigm is the integrated Chip-Tip workflow, which consolidates single-cell lysis, cleanup, and digestion on a microfluidic device, directly coupled to high-speed LC‒MS/MS.106 This “enrichment-free” approach minimizes sample loss and enables large-scale profiling. Beyond discovery, new strategies quantify specific PTM modules while linking them to cell phenotype. The ‘sc-hPTM’ strategy exemplifies this for histone modifications. It bypasses extraction by using in situ chemical labeling (propionylation) to quantify multiple histone acetylation marks via MS. By coupling this with index sorting, it directly correlates histone modification states with cell cycle phase or other parameters in the same cell, placing the ‘histone code’ into a precise cellular context.107 Genetic code expansion technology enables the site-specific incorporation of unnatural amino acids bearing PTM mimics (e.g., acetyl-lysine analogs) into proteins in living cells.108 This allows causal study of a PTM’s function without perturbing the native enzymatic machinery. Beyond these platforms, biophysical methods offer distinct advantages—thermal shift assays track PTM-mediated stability changes,109 and nanopore technology achieves label-free, single-molecule detection.110

In summary, PTMs constitute a versatile and metabolically informed regulatory language that is fundamental to cellular decision-making. Their dynamic, specific, and interconnected nature allows them to function as a central processing unit, integrating diverse signals—especially metabolic cues—into precise functional outputs. The following sections will delve into how cancer cells exploit this PTM logic to drive the pathological reprogramming of core metabolic pathways, thereby sustaining proliferation, evading immune surveillance, and establishing resilient malignant states.

PTMs and tumor metabolic reprogramming

The reprogramming of metabolism in cancer is executed through a coherent regulatory logic, which can be understood as a three-tiered control system, with PTMs operating at each level. First, PTMs are the primary means by which cancer cells interpret and integrate upstream oncogenic and environmental signals to initiate metabolic changes. Second, PTMs provide direct, rapid, and reversible control over the activity, stability, and localization of metabolic enzymes, precisely steering nutrient fluxes toward biosynthetic and energetic needs. Third, PTMs, particularly those derived from metabolites themselves, cement the reprogrammed state by altering the epigenetic landscape and stabilizing oncogenic transcription factors, creating feedback loops that ensure phenotypic persistence. This section systematically explores this PTM-centric framework across the core pillars of cancer metabolism: glucose utilization, lipid dynamics, amino acid management, and nucleotide provision. We further demonstrate how this same principle extends to metabolic control of cell fate, quality control, and immune modulation. Viewing cancer metabolism through PTMs reveals the profound interconnectivity and plasticity that define this hallmark of cancer.

Carbon allocation: PTMs balance glycolytic flux, biosynthetic diversions, and mitochondrial engagement

The fate of glucose-derived carbon—whether destined for rapid ATP generation, channeled into biosynthesis, or committed to mitochondrial oxidation—is dynamically controlled by PTMs at key metabolic branchpoints. This regulatory network ensures that carbon flux is precisely aligned with the biosynthetic, energetic, and redox demands of proliferating cancer cells.

Guiding carbon into biosynthesis

Upon entry into the cell, a critical branchpoint decision involves diverting glucose-6-phosphate into the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) for NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate production. This diversion is robustly governed by PTMs on the rate-limiting enzyme glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD). Lactylation exerts a context-dependent dual effect: while direct lactylation of G6PD suppresses its activity, lactylation of the scaffold protein DCBLD1 stabilizes G6PD and promotes PPP flux, highlighting how the same modification can have opposing outcomes through different targets.111,112 Methylation by PRMT6 and its upstream phosphoinositide 3-kinase enhancer-activating AKT (PIKE-A)–signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling further enhance G6PD activity and PPP flux to support tumor growth and redox homeostasis.113,114 Conversely, the ubiquitin‒proteasome system acts as a critical brake, with the E3 ligase TRIM21 targeting G6PD for degradation to restrict PPP activity.115 Notably, this degradation is itself inhibited by AKT-mediated phosphorylation of TRIM21, and AKT signaling can be indirectly activated by PPP products.116 This creates a hierarchical PTM crosstalk that reinforces glycolytic addiction under sustained oncogenic signaling. Thus, PTMs on G6PD integrate multiple signals to precisely calibrate the flux into this crucial biosynthetic and redox-buffering pathway.

Diverting flux from mitochondria to secure glycolytic primacy

To fully commit to aerobic glycolysis, cancer cells establish a multilayered post-translational blockade to prevent mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation. The principal switch is the phosphorylation and inhibition of the mitochondrial gatekeeper pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDHC) by pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDHK).117 Recent studies have found that acetylation of the PDHX subunit by p300 disrupts PDHC assembly.118 Spatial control of this process is epitomized by phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1). A phosphorylation toggle—ERK-mediated S203 phosphorylation opposed by PTEN—determines whether PGK1 can move to mitochondria, where it phosphorylates and activates PDHK1, thereby bottling pyruvate inside the cytosol for lactogenesis.119,120 This toggle is further refined by additional PTMs: PRMT1-catalyzed R206 methylation enhances ERK phosphorylation efficiency,121 O-GlcNAcylation at T255 boosts PGK1 activity and accelerates its translocation,122 and hypoxia-induced loss of T255 crotonylation strengthens the PGK1-PDHK1 interaction.123 Thus, PGK1 acts as a PTM-integration node, converging multiple signals to divert glucose flux away from the TCA cycle. Upstream, the E3 ligase Smurf1-mediated neddylation of 3-phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase 1 ignites AKT signaling,124 which then phosphorylates malic enzyme 2 (ME2), preventing its mitochondrial import and repurposing it as a cytoplasmic scaffold for glycolytic enzymes.125 Collectively, these converging PTMs hijack pyruvate from the TCA cycle and redirect glucose flux toward the Warburg phenotype.

Fine-tuning the glycolytic gatekeeper

Pyruvate Kinase M2 (PKM2) sits at metabolic crossroads, acting as both a catalytic gatekeeper and a nuclear signaling hub. Its activity and oligomeric state are dictated by a combinatorial PTM code: O-GlcNAcylation promotes the formation of low-activity dimers and nuclear translocation, a switch antagonized by ULK1-dependent phosphorylation126; deacetylation by HDAC8 inhibits its activity127; UBA2/UBC9-dependent SUMOylation drives dimerization and nuclear translocation128–130; and hypoxia-induced citrullination by PADI1/3 leads to constitutive activation and uncontrolled glycolysis.131 Arginine methylation provides context-dependent fine-tuning: under glucose sufficiency, PRMT1 promotes glycolysis by regulating the PKM2/PKM1 ratio,132 whereas hypoxia inhibits PRMT6, activating the CRAF-ERK-PKM2 axis to enhance glycolytic flux.133 The ubiquitin system adds another layer of control, with deubiquitinases such as OTUB2 preserving PKM2 activity to sustain flux.134 Beyond serving as a PTM target, PKM2 itself acts as a histidine kinase to phosphorylate phosphatase phosphoglycerate mutase (PGAM1), creating a feedforward loop to reinforce glycolysis.135 This PTM-based rheostat principle extends to the entire glycolytic cascade. Enolase 1 (ENO1) is independently activated by PRMT5/6-mediated methylation, SENP1-induced deSUMOylation, and OGT-catalyzed O-GlcNAcylation, increasing throughput.114,122,136–138 Similarly, succinylation enhances the activity of both lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) and PGK1, coupling glycolysis to tumor invasion.139–142 Ubiquitin-dependent regulation operates at multiple steps: USP15 stabilizes the hexokinase HKDC1143; USP4 protects the terminal step by stabilizing LDHA144; Parkin-dependent ubiquitination suppresses GAPDH phosphorylation and activity145; and SENP1-mediated deSUMOylation of HK2 enhances its mitochondrial binding, reprogramming metabolism toward the Warburg effect.146 The phosphatase PHLPP2 is ubiquitinated by TRIM46, leading to activation of the AKT/HK2 axis and accelerated glucose uptake.147 Notably, PTMs can confer noncanonical, “moonlighting” functions. For example, mitochondrial release of HK2 upon glycolytic activation enables its recruitment of USP11 to stabilize the stemness marker CD133, directly linking glycolytic enzyme dynamics to the maintenance of cancer stemness.148 In summary, a dense network of PTMs acts as a master control panel, precisely tuning the activity, localization, and even nonmetabolic functions of glycolytic enzymes to meet the dynamic demands of tumor cells.

Hierarchical control by upstream oncogenic pathways

Upstream oncogenic signaling pathways exert hierarchical control over metabolic reprogramming, largely executed by the ubiquitin‒proteasome system. A prevalent strategy is the targeted elimination of metabolic suppressors. For instance, oncogenic KRASG12D promotes the expression of the E2 enzyme UBE2T, which enhances p53 ubiquitination, thereby disrupting the p53-G6PD interaction and subsequently increasing PPP flux.149 Similarly, the E3 ligase TRIM33 drives glycolysis by facilitating K48-linked polyubiquitination and degradation of the tumor suppressor p53.150 Conversely, oncogenic signaling stabilizes key proglycolytic transcription factors. This is achieved either by competitive inhibition of their E3 ligases or active deubiquitination. KIF20A overexpression competitively inhibits FBXW7-mediated degradation of MYC,151 while the deubiquitinase USP11, facilitated by DLGAP5, stabilizes MYC, reinforcing its-driven glycolytic transcription.152 Similarly, ERK1/2-mediated phosphorylation stabilizes the transcription factor SIX1 by preventing its ubiquitin-mediated degradation, leading to the transcriptional upregulation of glycolytic genes.153 Hypoxia signaling is entrenched by stabilizing HIF-1α: a p-MYH9/USP22 axis mediates its deubiquitination,154 while downregulation of OTUB2 and upregulation of UBE2S promote degradation of the E3 ligase VHL, preventing HIF-1α turnover.155,156 Collectively, this layered UPS-dependent architecture ensures that glycolytic flux is irreversibly wired into the oncogenic program.

Phenotype consolidation: PTMs translate metabolic state into malignant cell fate

Beyond the immediate rerouting of carbon fluxes, a profound consequence of metabolic reprogramming is the establishment of self-reinforcing circuits that lock cells into aggressive, treatment-resistant states. This phenotype consolidation is mediated by PTMs that directly bridge metabolic activity to the control of epigenetics, genome integrity and cellular differentiation, thereby transforming transient metabolic adaptations into enduring malignant traits.

Reprogramming the epigenome via metabolic messengers

Metabolic intermediates from glycolytic flux act as epigenetic messengers, their covalent attachment to chromatin directly reshaping the transcriptional landscape and establishing self-reinforcing circuits. Lactylation is a prime example, functioning as an epigenetic “memory module” for high glycolytic activity. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), H3K18la upregulates the mitotic kinases TTK and BUB1B, which subsequently phosphorylate and hyperactivate LDHA, creating a feed-forward loop that regenerates lactate and reinforces the lactylation signal.157 Beyond histones, lactylation of the transcription factor NUSAP1 drives its binding to the LDHA promoter, further entrenching this phenotype.158 Remarkably, this metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk can traverse cellular compartments. In the tumor microenvironment, peritumoral cells secrete GDF15, upregulating tumor cell glycolysis. The resulting lactate is imported back into peri-tumoral cells, inducing H4K8la and further amplifying GDF15 expression, establishing a paracrine circuit.159 In parallel, reinforcing mechanisms exist: lactylation of ATP-binding cassette subfamily F member 1 initiates a KDM3A–HIF-1α cascade that stabilizes glycolysis and invasion genes,160 while other metabolite-driven acylations, such as KAT2A-mediated succinylation of H3K79 promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) via 14-3-3ζ upregulation, are interwoven into this network.161 Together, these interlocking pathways form an integrated network in which metabolic flux directly instructs gene expression, locking cells into a resilient, malignant state.

Coupling metabolic flux to genome integrity and cell division

PTMs provide a direct molecular interface that couples the metabolic state to the machinery of genome integrity and cell division, enabling cancer cells to proliferate under genotoxic stress. Lactylation exemplifies this coupling: under stress, it enhances the function of key DNA repair proteins (MRE11, NBS1, XRCC1), promoting efficient repair and cell cycle progression.57,162–164 It also targets RCC2, a protein critical for mitotic fidelity, via KAT2A-mediated lactylation.165 This principle extends to other metabolite-driven PTMs. In HCC, the succinyltransferase KAT2A catalyzes succinylation of the splicing factor SRSF11, thereby enhancing homologous recombination efficiency for DNA repair.166 Through these pathways, metabolic signals not only fuel the energetic demands of DNA repair and division but also directly modulate the executive machinery itself.

Orchestrating phenotypic plasticity through nutrient and stress sensors

Nutrient- and stress-sensitive PTM systems act as central interpreters of cellular status, actively driving transitions toward stem-like and plastic states. The O-GlcNAcylation system is a prime nutrient sensor. It stabilizes master regulators such as c-Myc and SIX1, aligning transcription with nutrient supply.167,168 Its own activity is precisely tuned by PTMs: while FBXO31-mediated ubiquitination of OGT suppresses tumorigenesis,169 CARM1-dependent methylation of OGT recruits USP9X to remove ubiquitin marks, enhancing OGT stability and promoting global O-GlcNAcylation and glycolysis in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).170 Furthermore, O-GlcNAcylation integrates genotoxic stress: disruption of the AMPK–GFAT1 axis elevates UDP-GlcNAc, leading to hyper-O-GlcNAcylation of CHK2 and KAT5. This stabilizes both proteins and activates a TWIST1-dependent EMT program.171–173 Energy-sensing kinases enact complementary, context-dependent decisions. Under acute stress, AMPK phosphorylates metabolic regulators (e.g., HDAC8/10) to rapidly mobilize survival pathways such as gluconeogenesis and the PPP.174,175 Conversely, in sustained favorable conditions, HDAC11 epigenetically suppresses the LKB1-AMPK axis, enforcing a persistent glycolytic phenotype and maintaining stem-like traits.176 Together, these sensor systems translate fluctuating metabolic and stress signals into coherent, self-reinforcing transcriptional and metabolic programs, conferring the phenotypic plasticity essential for tumor dissemination, therapy resistance, and survival.

Bridging metabolism and immunity: PTMs as direct effectors of immune evasion

PTMs function as direct molecular bridges linking core glucose metabolism to immunosuppressive outcomes in the TME.

Orchestrating immune checkpoint expression

PTMs directly govern the abundance of pivotal immune checkpoint molecules, creating a direct causal link from metabolic activity to immune inhibition. Lactylation of histones emerges as a dominant pathway in this process. For instance, PRMT3-catalyzed methylation of PDHK1 raises lactate, which deposits H3K18la and lifts death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) transcription177; parallel H3K18la-activated POM121-MYC signaling and a STAT5-driven H4K5la burst achieve the same immune-evasive output,178,179 illustrating that diverse histone lactylation events converge on a common PD-L1 checkpoint. Beyond PD-L1, H3K18la also mediates the upregulation of the immune checkpoint molecule B7-H3, an action that attenuates CD8 + T cell infiltration and cytotoxicity.180 In parallel, the nutrient-sensitive O-GlcNAcylation system stabilizes PD-L1 through complementary mechanisms: O-GlcNAcylation of ENO1 prevents PD-L1 ubiquitin-mediated degradation,137 while O-GlcNAcylation of the acetyltransferase EP300 enhances its transcriptional activation, further promoting PD-L1 expression.181,182 Thus, diverse PTM-driven pathways establish a mechanistic bridge from glycolytic flux to checkpoint expression.

Repurposing metabolic enzymes as direct signal transducers

Beyond their catalytic roles, metabolic enzymes can be transformed by PTMs into direct signal transducers that activate immunosuppressive pathways. A paradigm is HK2. Upon phosphorylation and mitochondrial translocation, HK2 acquires a noncanonical function: it directly phosphorylates IκBα, leading to the activation of the transcription factor NF-κB pathway and subsequent promotion of PD-L1 expression.183–185 This exemplifies how a PTM can fundamentally repurpose a metabolic enzyme, converting it from a facilitator of glycolysis into an active instigator of immune-inhibitory signaling.

Reprogramming the immunological microenvironment

The influence of metabolism-driven PTMs extends beyond the tumor cell boundary to systemically reshape the entire immunological landscape. Metabolism-induced modifications can drive the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, as exemplified by H3K9la-induced interleukin (IL-11) secretion186 and GFPT2-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of the transcription factor YBX1, which promotes IL-18 secretion.187 Systemically, metabolic changes within the tumor are broadcast via PTMs on circulating proteins. A striking example is apolipoprotein C2 lactylation, which induces extracellular lipolysis and promotes the accumulation of regulatory T cells (Tregs).188 Collectively, these mechanisms demonstrate how PTMs propagate metabolic information to reprogramme the multicellular composition and functional state of the TME, solidifying immune evasion.

In summary, the study of PTMs in glucose metabolism reveals a regulatory landscape of immense depth and connectivity. It is a system where modifications do not act as isolated on/off switches but as an integrated code. This code allows the cancer cell to make coordinated decisions: allocating resources with precision, adapting to adversity with resilience, and engaging its environment with immunosuppressive force. The following sections will explore how this same PTM-driven logic governs lipid and amino acid metabolism, further solidifying the concept of PTMs as the central processors of oncogenic metabolic reprogramming.

Lipid metabolic rewiring: PTMs dictate lipid fate and survival decisions

Reprogramming of lipid metabolism is a strategic imperative for cancer cells, enabling rapid membrane biogenesis, energy production, and stress resilience. This process is governed by a hierarchical control system where PTMs serve as the fundamental executors. PTMs first decode cellular signals to initiate transcriptional programs, then directly command the enzymatic machinery to control metabolic flux, and ultimately, lock these metabolic states into stable, protumorigenic phenotypes. Signal interpretation: PTMs set the metabolic direction

The fundamental choice between lipid synthesis and catabolism is dictated by oncogenic cues and nutrient availability, primarily mediated by two interconnected transcriptional axes: the SREBP family and the PPAR family. PTMs serve as the molecular arbiters of this decision, directly activating anabolic or catabolic transcriptional programmes by sensing and interpreting the cellular state.

Transducing progrowth and nutrient signals into lipogenic commands

When nutrients are abundant and growth signals are active, cancer cells commit to anabolic synthesis through a series of PTM-mediated commitments. The canonical PI3K-AKT-mTORC1 axis triggers a decisive switch: AKT-mediated phosphorylation of insulin-induced gene protein 1/2 at the ER releases the SREBP complex, unleashing a transcriptional program for fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis.189,190 Complementary mitogenic cues, such as those activating the phosphokinase JNK, further drive cholesterol biosynthesis through phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of SREBP2.191 These phosphorylation events ensure that growth signals are directly translated into a lipogenic transcriptional output. Beyond classical signaling, PTMs also directly interpret nutrient sufficiency to enforce a pervasive anabolic state. A key mechanism involves the nutrient-sensitive O-GlcNAcylation of serine/arginine-rich protein-specific kinase 2 (SRPK2). Promoted by glucose availability, this modification facilitates SRPK2 nuclear translocation, activating the pro-lipogenic mTORC1-S6K1-SRPK2 axis.192,193 In parallel, the O-GlcNAcylation of ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY)—enhanced by PI3Kβ-dependent phosphorylation of OGT194—directly channels cytosolic acetyl-CoA toward fatty acid synthesis.195 These coordinated modifications ensure that from signal transduction to substrate provision, the cellular response to nutrient abundance is comprehensive and unequivocal.

Transcriptional master switches

The commitment to a specific metabolic fate is further cemented through the exquisite, context-dependent control of transcription factor stability. The lipogenic regulator SREBP1 exemplifies this precision, with its stability being finely tuned by competing PTMs: it is maintained by the deubiquitinase USP11 in one context but targeted for degradation by the E3 ligase TRIM21 in another.196,197 Similarly, the functional outcome of PPARγ ubiquitination is not predetermined but is dictated by the specific modifying enzyme. Ubiquitination by Von Hippel‒Lindau (VHL) inhibits PPARγ activity and lipid accumulation,198 whereas its stabilization by the deubiquitinase USP22 drives de novo lipogenesis.199 This layer of regulation allows the cell to override the initial transcriptional command based on evolving environmental or cellular needs, adding a crucial dimension of plasticity to the metabolic program.

Flux execution: PTMs direct synthesis, oxidation, and spatial logistics

Once the transcriptional direction is set, PTMs take direct command of the metabolic enzymes themselves. They fine-tune activity, stability, and—critically—the spatial organization of the lipid metabolic machinery to precisely execute and optimize flux.

Tuning the activity and stability of metabolic enzymes

The stability of rate-limiting enzymes is a primary control point. Fatty acid synthase (FASN) abundance, for instance, is oppositely regulated by site-specific palmitoylation: ZDHHC20-mediated palmitoylation at Cys1471/1881 prevents its degradation and promotes lipogenesis,200,201 while ZDHHC21-dependent palmitoylation at Cys1317 reduces its stability.202 This demonstrates how the same PTM type can produce opposing functional outcomes depending on the exact residue modified. This principle extends across the synthetic network. The deubiquitinase eIF3f deubiquitinates and stabilizes long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase 4 (ACSL4), while loss of the E3 ligase SIAH1 prevents FASN from degradation; together, these enzymes fuel fatty acid synthesis and tumor invasion.203,204 The control can be remarkably sophisticated, involving hierarchical PTM cascades. For example, neddylated PTEN can migrate to the nucleus to dephosphorylate and stabilize FASN, relaying a high-nutrient signal directly to the synthetic machinery.205 This intricate, site-specific regulation underscores the precision with which PTMs govern lipid metabolic flux, enabling nuanced and context-dependent control over tumor survival and growth.

Organizing the spatial logistics of lipid metabolism

The efficiency of lipid metabolism hinges on the precise spatial organization of enzymes and transporters across cellular compartments. PTMs act as molecular ZIP codes to ensure correct localization. At the plasma membrane, ZDHHC9-mediated palmitoylation of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) secures its membrane retention, maintaining glucose uptake.206 At organelle interfaces, phosphorylation of phosphofructokinase PFKL and perilipin-2 strengthens the physical and functional coupling between lipid droplets and mitochondria, optimizing fatty acid oxidation.207 At the site of signal initiation, RNF5-mediated ubiquitination of SREBP cleavage-activating protein (SCAP) in the endoplasmic reticulum attenuates SREBP activation, constituting an organelle-localized PTM circuit that fine-tunes the lipogenic signal at its source.208 Spatial regulation also governs metabolic pathway engagement. Reduced K63-linked ubiquitination of enoyl-CoA hydratase 1 facilitates its mitochondrial import to accelerate β-oxidation.209 Even transcriptional effectors are spatially guided, as ZDHHC6-mediated palmitoylation of PPARγ facilitates its nuclear import to ensure efficient execution of the lipogenic program.210 Thus, by directing proteins to their functional compartments, PTMs establish the essential spatial architecture that underpins efficient and adaptable lipid metabolism in cancer cells.

Phenotypic enforcement: from acute adaptation to lasting malignancy

The goal of metabolic rewiring is to confer survival advantages that are consolidated into stable, aggressive traits. PTMs are pivotal in this transformation, enabling rapid adaptation to stress while also installing self-reinforcing programs that cement the malignant state.

Orchestrating acute adaptive rewiring under stress

To survive immediate threats, cancer cells employ PTMs as dynamic switches for rapid metabolic adaptation. Under glucose deprivation, activated AMPK suppresses MDM2-mediated ubiquitination of PPARα, stabilizing it to promote fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and maintain energy homeostasis.211 When confronted with ferroptotic pressure, the S100P-P62 axis directs K63-ubiquitinated acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 to lysosomal degradation, rapidly halting the synthesis of pro-ferroptotic polyunsaturated fatty acids.212 Phosphorylation also underlies adaptive remodeling, as the lipid kinase PIKfyve plays an important role in lysosomal lipid recycling.213,214 Recent studies have reported that its inhibition triggers KRAS-MAPK-mediated phosphorylation of SREBP1, reprogramming lipid synthesis to preserve membrane homeostasis.215 Extending this paradigm, O-GlcNAcylation provides an equally swift level: in normal hepatocytes, glucose deprivation induces PERK-driven FBP1 S170 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, where FBP1 binds PPARα to silence β-oxidation; however, in HCC, O-GlcNAcylation at S124 blocks S170 phosphorylation, thereby enhancing β-oxidation to fuel tumor growth.216 Together, these examples illustrate how PTMs confer greater metabolic plasticity to cancer cells.

Enforcing metabolic memory through epigenetic lock-in

Beyond transient adaptation, PTMs translate metabolic activity into self-reinforcing epigenetic programmes, conferring a form of metabolic memory. Under nutrient-rich conditions, phosphorylated SREBP1 recruits the acetyltransferase KAT2A/GCN5 to acetylate histone H2A, directly imprinting a pro-lipogenic state onto chromatin to sustain anabolic transcription.217 Conversely, under therapeutic stress, a phosphorylation-acetylation switch occurs: JNK-activated c-Jun recruits CBP/p300 to acetylate H3K27 at the CPT1A promoter, creating a stable epigenetic environment that enhances FAO and drives tamoxifen resistance.218 Notably, FAO itself can be epigenetically reinforced, as FAO-derived acetyl-CoA fuels H3K27 acetylation at stemness and metastasis genes, stabilizing an invasive phenotype.219 This exemplifies a powerful feed-forward loop where metabolism fuels PTMs that cement the very metabolic state that produced them.

Consolidating phenotypes by stabilizing oncogenic drivers

The stabilization of malignant phenotypes is ultimately cemented by PTMs that directly enhance the persistence of key oncoproteins, often through antagonistic relationships between modifications. A recurring theme is the antagonism between palmitoylation and ubiquitination. ZDHHC23-dependent palmitoylation of the E3 ligase PHF2 triggers its degradation, thereby preventing the turnover of its target SREBP1c and sustaining long-term lipogenesis.220 A more direct link between core lipid metabolism and oncogenesis is established when FASN-dependent palmitoylation of mutant p53 by ZDHHC3/17 shields it from ubiquitin-mediated degradation, inextricably coupling high lipogenic flux to the stabilization of a potent oncogenic driver.221 Furthermore, under fatty acid oxidation–derived palmitoyl-CoA accumulation, ZDHHC5-mediated transcription factor CLOCK palmitoylation prevents its degradation, reinforcing circadian-driven metabolic rhythms that favor tumor persistence.222 Through these mechanisms, PTMs ensure that the malignant state, once acquired, becomes deeply entrenched and self-sustaining.

In summary, PTMs in lipid metabolism form a multilayered, self-referential control system. They function as signal interpreters, flux executors, and phenotypic enforcers. The extensive crosstalk between different PTMs—such as the antagonistic relationship between palmitoylation and ubiquitination or hierarchical phosphorylation-acetylation cascades—creates a robust and highly plastic regulatory network. This allows cancer cells to not only respond to immediate demands but also embed past metabolic experiences into their present identity, supporting survival, proliferation, and metastasis in the face of fluctuating environments and therapeutic challenges.

Amino acid metabolic rewiring: PTMs fuel anabolism, redox balance, and survival signaling

Amino acid metabolism is a cornerstone of cancer cell biology, providing essential building blocks for macromolecule synthesis, maintaining redox homeostasis, and fueling energy production. Beyond these foundational roles, a more profound paradigm is emerging that amino acids are not merely metabolic intermediates but also precursors for novel PTMs that directly translate nutrient availability into cellular instruction. This is exemplified by two unique modifications—hypusination and serotonylation. Hypusination is a distinctive PTM that directly links polyamine metabolism with the control of protein synthesis and beyond. It involves the covalent modification of the translation factor eukaryotic translation initiation factor 5 A (eIF5A), a two-step process catalyzed by deoxyhypusine synthase (DHPS) and deoxyhypusine hydroxylase (DOHH) that uses spermidine (derived from methionine and ornithine) as its substrate.223,224 Serotonylation represents a direct link from tryptophan metabolism to signaling and epigenetics, catalyzed by tissue transglutaminase 2 (TGM2) to conjugate serotonin to histones and small GTPases.225,226 These mechanisms illustrate that the metabolic and signaling functions of amino acids are inextricably linked. The broader PTM network—encompassing these unique modifications alongside classical ones—enables cancer cells to dynamically allocate amino acid resources to meet three fundamental demands: sustaining rapid anabolism, managing oxidative stress, and integrating environmental cues to cement protumorigenic phenotypes (Table 4 and Fig. 4).

PTM-mediated rewiring of tumor amino acid metabolism

Sustaining anabolism and nitrogen economy

To support unchecked proliferation, cancer cells must ensure a constant supply of amino acids and their derivatives. PTMs directly orchestrate this by activating biosynthetic pathways, removing metabolic checkpoints, and creating self-reinforcing anabolic loops.

Activating biosynthetic pathways

PTMs directly enhance the activity of rate-limiting enzymes in amino acid synthesis. For instance, PRMT1-mediated methylation activates phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), the gateway enzyme of the serine synthesis pathway, to promote serine production.227 Similarly, PKCε-mediated phosphorylation enhances the activity of glutaminase C to drive glutamine catabolism.228 Fine-tuning of mitochondrial glutamine entry is further achieved through SIRT3- and SIRT5-dependent deacylation of glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), modulating TCA cycle input and energy yield.229,230 These modifications ensure that biosynthetic flux is maximized under permissive conditions.

Releasing metabolic brakes and maintaining homeostasis

Cancer cells employ PTMs to bypass endogenous checkpoints that restrict anabolism. In esophageal carcinoma, the RNA-binding protein RBM4 promotes kinase LKB1 ubiquitination and degradation, which suppresses AMPK signaling and consequently unleashes glutamine catabolism, allowing cells to escape senescence.231 To prevent overactivation and maintain homeostasis, rheostatic mechanisms exist, such as FBXO7-driven ubiquitination of PRMT1, which limits excessive serine biosynthesis to balance growth with redox needs.227,232 This braking strategy exemplifies how PTMs enable a controlled anabolic state suitable for survival and proliferation.

Building interlocked anabolic networks

PTMs enable the integration of different metabolic pathways to amplify biosynthetic output. PRMT1-mediated methylation of glycolytic enzymes PFKFB3 and PKM2 redirects glycolytic carbon toward the serine synthesis pathway. This serine, in turn, fuels a palmitoylation-dependent circuit involving PHGDH and FASN, creating a self-reinforcing serine–lipid anabolic loop that drives fatty acid synthesis and chemotherapy resistance.233 The unique PTM hypusination of eIF5A, fuelled by polyamine biosynthesis, is itself promoted by aspartate signaling to facilitate lung metastasis.234 This regulatory principle also extends to inhibitory modifications, as seen when succinate-induced succinylation of serine hydroxymethyltransferase 2 suppresses de novo purine synthesis, thereby enforcing a dependency on the salvage pathway to maintain anabolic flux.235 Together, these circuits ensure that anabolism, once initiated, becomes self-sustaining.

Maintaining redox homeostasis

The high metabolic rate of tumors generates substantial oxidative stress. PTMs reconfigure amino acid metabolism to prioritize the production of reducing equivalents, building a robust, inducible antioxidant defense system.

Channeling flux toward reductive synthesis

A key strategy involves diverting amino acid carbon into NADPH-producing pathways. In KRAS-driven PDAC, O-GlcNAcylation of malate dehydrogenase 1 (MDH1) activates the glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase 1 (GOT1)–MDH1–ME1 shuttle. This directs glutamine-derived carbon toward cytoplasmic NADPH production instead of OXPHOS.236 This is complemented by loss of SIRT5, which enhances GOT1 acetylation and activity, further reinforcing this redox-supporting circuit.237 Thus, PTMs directly rewire central carbon flux to bolster the reducing power of the cell.

Stress-responsive metabolic switches

PTM cascades are deployed as rapid response systems to acute stress. Under oxidative stress, MAPK-mediated phosphorylation of succinate-CoA ligase ADP-forming subunit beta (SUCLA2) triggers its dissociation from glutaminase, enabling subsequent succinylation and activation of glutaminase. This phosphorylation-succinylation cascade enhances glutaminolysis and NADPH generation to directly counteract ROS.238 The functional outcome of such PTM-mediated redox regulation is exquisitely context dependent. In one context, GCDH, a key enzyme converting glutaconyl-CoA to crotonyl-CoA in the lysine degradation pathway, promotes the crotonylation of glycolytic and PPP enzymes (PGD, TKT, ALDOC), inhibiting their activity and limiting tumor growth by enhancing oxidative stress.239 In a contrasting scenario in melanoma, loss of GCDH leads to the accumulation of glutaryl-CoA, which drives glutarylation and stabilization of the transcription factor NRF2, triggering an apoptotic response.240 This duality demonstrates how the same metabolic node can dictate opposing cell fates through differential PTM signaling.

Fortifying one-carbon and stress resistance pathways

PTMs further fortify antioxidant defense by integrating amino acid metabolism with stress resistance pathways. In HCC, hyperglycolytic stress induces IGF2BP3 lactylation, which enhances serine-derived antioxidant defense, conferring resistance to the targeted therapy lenvatinib.241 Under glucose deprivation, acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1)-dependent ME2 acetylation routes glutamine flux toward lactate production and protein lactylation, enhancing DNA repair and chemoresistance in ovarian cancer.162–164,242 In bladder cancer, TRIM25-mediated ubiquitination of MAT2A, a central enzyme in methionine metabolism, creates a direct link between metabolic and epigenetic regulation that contributes to chemoresistance.243 These examples underscore how PTMs tailor antioxidants and repair capacities to specific therapeutic and metabolic challenges.

Integrating signaling and locking in malignant states

Amino acid metabolism is deeply intertwined with oncogenic signaling, epigenetic regulation, and immune surveillance. PTMs serve as the critical interface, translating metabolic flux into long-term transcriptional programs and adaptive phenotypes.

Coupling metabolism to oncogenic signaling

PTMs directly couple amino acid availability to the activation of major oncogenic pathways. Energy stress sensed by the LKB1-AMPK axis leads to phosphorylation- and ubiquitination-coupled degradation of the transcription factor PROX1, suppressing the branched-chain amino acid (BCAA)-mTOR axis. Conversely, in LKB1-deficient tumors, AMPK inactivation prevents PROX1 degradation, allowing excessive BCAA accumulation to activate mTOR and promote tumor growth.244 This rewiring is reinforced in HCC, where DLAT (a PDH subunit) acetylates the leucine hydrolase AUH at K109, inhibiting leucine decomposition and leading to leucine accumulation that activates the mTOR axis.245 Oncogenic signaling can also establish self-reinforcing loops; the MYC oncoprotein drives the expression of enzymes for hypusination, which in turn promotes the efficient translation of MYC mRNA itself, creating a powerful positive feedback loop.246–248 Similarly, YAP/TAZ transcriptionally activates polyamine synthesis and then exploits the hypusination-LSD1 axis to epigenetically silence tumor suppressor genes, forming a proliferation-promoting closed loop.249 Collectively, these mechanisms illustrate how PTMs function as dynamic couplers, transforming simple amino acid abundance into potent, self-sustaining oncogenic signals.

Enforcing phenotypes through epigenetic metabolites

Amino acid-derived metabolites are key substrates and cofactors for chromatin-modifying enzymes, allowing metabolism to directly sculpt the epigenome. Glutamine-derived α-ketoglutarate is an essential cofactor for demethylases such as KDM6A. Its flux influences histone methylation (e.g., H3K27me3) to either reinforce glycolytic/mTOR circuits or shift toward energy conservation in tumors.250–252 BCAA-derived α-KG similarly enhances H3K27 demethylation, promoting glycolytic flux and resistance to EGFR inhibitors in NSCLC.253 Beyond serving as cofactors, metabolites can directly rewire enzyme function, as seen under hypoxia, where acetylation repurposes GDH1 from an α-KG-producing enzyme to an α-KG-consuming component of the HIF-1α stabilization complex.254 Tryptophan-derived modification serotonylation exemplifies a direct link to epigenetics. Histone serotonylation (e.g., at H3Q5ser) functions as a potent activating mark that recruits readers such as WDR5 to drive oncogene expression such as MYC.255–257 Mechanistically, H3Q5ser synergizes with the canonical activation signal H3K4me3: it not only enhances transcription factor II D complex binding but also stabilizes the H3K4me3 mark itself by promoting TBP-associated factor 3 recognition and inhibiting demethylases.258,259 This intricate cooperation with the core transcription machinery ensures that metabolic signals are robustly and precisely translated into malignant gene expression programs.

Directly modulating immune surveillance and therapeutic resistance

Amino acid metabolism intersects directly with anti-tumor immunity and therapy response. In PDAC, KAT2A-mediated acetylation of glutaminase 2 promotes YAP1 glutamylation and subsequent PD-L1 induction under hypoxia, facilitating immune escape.260 In bladder cancer, asparagine binding facilitates retinoic acid-inducible gene I ubiquitination and degradation, suppressing interferon signaling.261 Serotonylation also upregulates PD-L1 and activates Ras homolog family member A to initiate mechanotransduction through the ROCK/YAP axis.262,263 Notably, targeting such pathways can be complex; inhibiting the serotonin transporter may paradoxically enhance tryptophan catabolism, amplifying the production of both serotonin and oncogenic kynurenine, thus sustaining the tumor-promoting circuit.264 Furthermore, hypusination contributes to therapeutic resistance by facilitating mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming and maintaining MYC protein biosynthesis.247,265 Through these diverse mechanisms, PTMs ensure that the metabolic state of the cancer cell directly dictates its ability to evade immune attack and resist treatment.

In summary, PTMs govern amino acid metabolism in cancer with remarkable precision and breadth. They function as allocators of biochemical resources, architects of stress resilience, and integrators of disparate biological programs—from translation control via hypusination to epigenetic regulation via serotonylation. This intricate PTM network allows cancer cells to translate the simple availability of building blocks into a complex, coherent, and durable malignant identity, presenting both a formidable challenge and a landscape of promising therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Nucleotide metabolic rewiring: PTMs fuel proliferation and immune evasion

Nucleotide metabolism, while fundamental for DNA replication and RNA transcription in proliferating cells, is coopted by cancers to serve broader purposes. PTMs drive nucleotide overproduction by activating salvage pathway enzymes and stabilizing the de novo synthesis machinery (Fig. 4). In ALK-positive lung cancer, phosphorylation of guanylate kinase 1 (GUK1) not only enhances the conversion of GMP to GDP but also unleashes an unexpected signaling function: the resulting GDP acts as a signaling molecule to transactivate the Ras-MAPK pathway. This creates a feedforward loop that directly couples nucleotide salvage to oncogenic proliferation signals.266 A complementary strategy involves protein stabilization. The ERK-USP9X axis stabilizes thymidine kinase 1 (TK1), thereby boosting dTMP synthesis while concurrently enhancing glycolytic flux.267 Furthermore, PTMs can hijack circadian machinery for metabolic gain. Phosphorylation exports CLOCK to the cytoplasm, where it acetylates and stabilizes ribose-phosphate pyrophosphokinase 1/2, boosting de novo nucleotide synthesis.268 Thus, through diverse mechanisms, PTMs lock the nucleotide synthesis machinery into a state of hyperactivation, directly supporting the proliferative engine of cancer.

Concurrently, PTMs repurpose nucleotide-metabolizing enzymes to deplete immunostimulatory molecules or accumulate immunosuppressive oncometabolites, erecting a metabolic barrier against innate immune surveillance. Under hypoxic conditions, phosphorylation reprograms phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1 (PCK1) to consume GTP. By depleting this essential nucleotide, the cell blunts cGAS-STING signaling and subsequent interferon production, thereby crippling a major arm of the innate immune response.269 Similarly, IKKβ-mediated phosphorylation of adenylosuccinate lyase drives the accumulation of fumarate, an oncometabolite that disrupts STING activation and inhibits innate immune responses.270 These mechanisms illustrate a strategic diversion: PTMs co-opt metabolic enzymes not only for production but also for consumption and interference, actively dismantling the host’s immune alarm system.

In summary, PTMs govern nucleotide metabolism in cancer with remarkable duality. They drive anabolic overproduction to fuel proliferation while simultaneously orchestrating catalytic sabotage to promote immune evasion. This dual role elevates nucleotide metabolism from a mere housekeeping function to an active participant in tumor survival and defense, highlighting PTMs as the master switches that toggle between these two critical oncogenic programs.

PTM-driven metabolic reprogramming: from bioenergetic remodeling to gatekeeping cell fate

The relentless survival of cancer cells hinges not only on fueling growth but, more critically, on their ability to dynamically sense and neutralize existential metabolic threats. This section explores how PTMs, through the dynamic reprogramming of core metabolic and organellar functions, enable tumor cells to navigate a spectrum of challenges—from maintaining bioenergetic and biosynthetic homeostasis under nutrient constraints to actively modulating the threshold of diverse cell death pathways. By examining PTM regulation of mitochondrial plasticity, autophagic recycling, oxidative stress responses, ferroptosis, and the emerging pathway of cuproptosis, we delineate a coherent framework in which PTMs act as the central processing units, continuously rewriting metabolic flux to sustain survival in the face of evolving threats (Table 5).

PTM-mediated mechanisms in the metabolic control of quality control and cellular fate

Mitochondrial remodeling: PTMs reconfigure the powerhouse for survival and defense

The mitochondrion is not a static powerhouse but a dynamic signaling hub whose function is reprogrammed by PTMs to serve dual roles: meeting bioenergetic demands and preemptively configuring defenses against death signals. PTMs achieve this through internal fine-tuning and external signal integration.

Reprogramming energetic and biosynthetic output

PTMs directly tune the balance between energy production and biosynthetic support by modifying key enzymes. In HCC, PRMT1-mediated methylation of ME2 stabilizes the enzyme, promoting the TCA cycle and generating NADPH for redox balance and anabolism.271 Conversely, the same PTM type can suppress OXPHOS to favor invasion, as observed when PRMT7-dependent methylation of the mitochondrial ribosomal protein MRPS23 triggers its degradation.272 Beyond methylation, palmitoylation acts as a potent activator; in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), ZDHHC21-catalyzed palmitoylation of mitochondrial adenylate kinase 2 activates the enzyme, promoting OXPHOS and driving the expansion of leukemia stem cells.273

Controlling organelle quality and biogenesis

Mitochondrial integrity is maintained through PTM-regulated quality control. In breast cancer, a PRMT1-dependent pathway regulates DEAD-box helicase 3 X-linked (DDX3), stimulating both mitophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis to renew the functional organelle pool and facilitate metastasis.274 Oncogenic signaling can directly hijack biogenesis: in AML, the FLT3-SUCLG1 axis reduces succinylation of the mitochondrial RNA polymerase POLRMT, enhancing mitochondrial gene expression and biogenesis to support tumor growth.275

Responding to external signals

Mitochondria utilize PTMs to interpret environmental cues and establish self-reinforcing, resilient states. The demethylase KDM3A acts as an oxygen sensor, and its activity regulates the monomethylation of the master regulator PGC-1α, adapting mitochondrial biogenesis to hypoxia.276 Under glutamine deprivation, SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of ME2 and ZDHHC18-mediated palmitoylation of MDH2 enhance mitochondrial respiration to sustain proliferation.277,278 A striking example of a locked-in phenotype is the OXCT1-SUCLA2 axis in HCC. Here, SUCLA2 phosphorylation initiates a cascade leading to the succinylation and activation of 3-oxoacid CoA-transferase 1 (OXCT1) and LACTB, forming a powerful positive feedback loop that boosts ketolysis and respiratory capacity to accelerate tumor growth.279,280

Directing cell fate decisions at the immune interface

The role of mitochondrial PTMs extends to directly arbitrate immunogenic cell death decisions. PJA1-mediated ubiquitination of the mitochondrial phosphatase PGAM5 inhibits DRP1-dependent mitochondrial fission and subsequent GSDME-mediated pyroptosis.281 By suppressing this proinflammatory death pathway, PTMs actively disarm a potential trigger for antitumor immunity, showcasing how organelle-centric PTM regulation is coopted for immune evasion.

In summary, PTMs serve as central regulators that dynamically reconfigure mitochondrial function beyond bioenergetics. By fine-tuning metabolic output, controlling organelle integrity, and integrating stress signals, PTMs transform the mitochondrion into an adaptive command center that supports survival, directs cell fate, and facilitates immune evasion.

Autophagic resupply: PTMs command nutrient salvage under stress

When the nutrient supply is limited, autophagy becomes a critical lifeline. PTMs act as the central command, directly controlling the autophagic machinery to initiate flux, supply building blocks, and selectively recycle cargo, thereby buffering cells against metabolic stress.

Metabolite-triggered Initiation of flux

Autophagic initiation is hardwired to metabolic sensors via PTMs. A key circuit links glycolytic flux to autophagy: nutrient deprivation triggers ULK1-mediated phosphorylation of LDHA, increasing lactate production. This lactate then promotes KAT5/TIP60-mediated lactylation of vacuolar protein sorting 34, directly activating autophagosome formation and promoting tumor progression.282 Beyond direct modification, lactate can also stimulate autophagy epigenetically via H3K18 lactylation-induced expression of Rubicon-like autophagy enhancer (RUBCNL), driving bevacizumab resistance in colorectal cancer.283 Acetylation dynamics provide another switch; inhibition of ACSS2 triggers its nuclear translocation to activate the SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of autophagy-related protein ATG5/ATG7, promoting autophagy.284,285

Logistics of membrane expansion

PTMs ensure the supply of essential lipids for autophagosome membrane expansion. Given the demand for fatty acids,286 the recruitment of USP2 by Valosin-Containing Protein to stabilize FASN becomes a critical PTM-driven mechanism for fueling autophagy in osteosarcoma.287 Direct modification of the core autophagic machinery also occurs, as ZDHHC7-mediated S-palmitoylation of ATG16L1 enables membrane-anchored protein LC3 lipidation, a prerequisite for autophagosome formation.288

Cargo selection and pathway integration

PTMs govern the selective degradation of cargo, integrating autophagy with other stress responses. The ubiquitin system plays a pivotal role: USP13-mediated deubiquitination of the transcription factor NRF2 activates the NRF2-p62-KEAP1 axis, promoting a switch from ferroptosis to autophagy in KRAS-mutant lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), enabling adaptation to oxidative stress.289 Metabolite-sensitive PTMs also direct this process; under lipid deprivation in HCC, adenylosuccinate lyase-generated fumarate inhibits KDM8, leading to increased Beclin1 methylation and subsequent autophagy induction, linking amino acid metabolism to autophagic cargo handling.290

Collectively, these examples underscore how precisely timed and metabolically responsive PTMs serve as the molecular clutch that couples autophagic initiation, membrane expansion and cargo selection to cellular nutrient status, thereby reaffirming post-translational modification as the central rheostat governing autophagy-driven nutrient salvage and stress adaptation.

Gatekeeping oxidative stress and ferroptosis: a multilayered PTM network

When stress escalates beyond the buffering capacity of organelle adaptation and recycling, cells activate defined death programs. PTMs serve as the ultimate gatekeepers, calibrating the sensitivity of these programs with exquisite precision.

Mastering the antioxidant tone via the KEAP1-NRF2 axis

The primary antioxidant response is governed by PTMs that control the stability of its master regulator, NRF2, primarily by modulating its interaction with the repressor KEAP1. Proteins such as WNK1,291 aPKCι292 and DDRGK1293 promote NRF2 stability by competing for KEAP1 binding, shielding it from ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Context is provided by E3 ligases: TRIM25 enhances NRF2 activity,294,295 while TRIM22 destabilizes NRF2 to promote ROS-driven cell death.296 This axis is integrated with metabolism. For instance, xanthine oxidoreductase interacts with USP15 to promote KEAP1 deubiquitination, suppress NRF2 and block liver cancer stem cell propagation.297 Furthermore, under metabolic stress, a feedforward loop is engaged. SQSTM1/p62-dependent feedback coactivates AMPK and NRF2, creating an integrated circuit that supports cancer cell viability.298 Thus, the antioxidant response is dynamically calibrated by the cell’s overall metabolic and energetic status.

Fine-tuning the core ferroptosis defense machinery

PTMs directly control the stability and activity of the key anti-ferroptosis enzymes GPX4 and FSP1. GPX4 is stabilized by OTUB1-mediated deubiquitination299 and PRMT5-dependent arginine methylation,300 while its activity is inhibited by site-specific palmitoylation mediated by ZDHHC20 and ZDHHC8.301,302 FSP1 activity is modulated by HDAC1 lactylation303 and supported by AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of aldehyde dehydrogenase 7A1.304 Beyond direct modification, PTMs reinforce the defensive infrastructure, such as interleukin-1β-induced acetylation of nicotinamide nucleotide transhydrogenase (NNT) to maintain iron-sulfur clusters and NADPH production,305 or NAA10-mediated lactylation of RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 to promote glutathione biosynthesis.306

Governing metabolic supply lines and execution

PTMs dictate the availability of pro- and anti-ferroptotic substrates. A major regulatory node is the cystine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11, whose stability is controlled by stabilizing deubiquitination (OTUB1, OTUD5)307,308 or degradative ubiquitination (SOCS2, TRIM34)309,310 and whose function is enhanced by O-GlcNAcylation in response to ROS.311,312 On the pro-death side, the lipid-metabolizing enzyme ACSL4 is regulated by TRIM21 and USP15,313 and its degradation is promoted by ferroptosis-induced SUMO2-K11 lactylation.314 PTMs also regulate auxiliary detoxification systems; for example, SMURF2-dependent degradation of glutathione S-transferase, and FBXL12-mediated degradation of aldehyde dehydrogenase 7A1, both sensitize cells to ferroptosis in GPX4-independent manners.315,316 Furthermore, exosomal circ_0006896 impairs HDAC1 activity to decrease histone H3 acetylation, thereby suppressing the transcription of arachidonic acid metabolism genes and limiting lipid peroxidation substrates.317 Iron homeostasis—central to ferroptosis execution—is critically tuned by O-GlcNAcylation of the transferrin receptor, regulating its stability and iron uptake,318 by DTX2-mediated degradation of nuclear receptor coactivator 4 to inhibit ferritinophagy and prevent iron overload,319 and by REST/LRSAM1-mediated ubiquitination of SLC40A1, a protein that inhibits ferroptosis by exporting ferrous ions.320 Thus, PTMs act as master tuners at every step—from iron import and storage to the final amplification of death signals—creating a highly responsive and integrable control system for ferroptosis.

In summary, PTMs constitute a multilayered gatekeeping system that dynamically sets the threshold between survival and ferroptotic death. By integrating stress signals and precisely tuning every node of the antioxidant and iron-metabolic network, they enable cancer cells to adaptively resist lethal stress. This pervasive regulatory logic highlights PTM networks as critical targets for overcoming therapy-resistant malignancies.

Navigating novel metabolic threats: PTMs as arbiters of cuproptosis

The identification of cuproptosis—copper-induced mitochondrial cell death—has revealed a novel metabolic cell death pathway.321 Emerging evidence suggests that PTMs may directly influence cellular sensitivity to this metabolic insult, highlighting their potential as pivotal regulators at this emerging junction of metabolism and cell fate.

Elevated copper levels can directly instigate pro-death PTMs. In gastric cancer, copper promotes the AARS1/AARS2-catalyzed lactylation of the RNA methyltransferase METTL16 at K229, which drives the execution of cuproptosis—antagonized by SIRT2-mediated deacetylation of METTL16.322 Conversely, cancer cells exploit PTMs to rewire metabolism and evade this novel threat. A key resistance mechanism is orchestrated by HIF-1α, which upregulates PDK1/3. These kinases phosphorylate the TCA cycle component DLAT at S100, promoting its ubiquitin-mediated degradation. By actively dismantling this target of copper, PTMs lead to resistance to cuproptosis under hypoxic conditions.323 This illustrates a recurring theme: When confronted with a metabolic death threat, cancer cells use PTMs to actively dismantle the very metabolic pathway targeted, opting for survival.

From the adaptive remodeling of mitochondria and autophagy to the precise calibration of antioxidant defenses, ferroptosis thresholds, and emerging threats such as cuproptosis, PTMs constitute the central command system through which cancer cells navigate multilayered metabolic stress. They function as programmable molecular switches, enabling minute-by-minute rerouting of metabolic flux—powering rapid adaptation for growth while instantly shutting down death pathways under threat. This capacity for dynamic compilation and execution of the metabolic network elevates PTMs beyond mere signal transduction, establishing them as the fundamental coding language that links metabolic state to cell-fate decisions. Targeting this PTM-gated survival network offers a novel therapeutic paradigm to disrupt the most fundamental adaptive defenses of cancer cells.

Antitumor immune responses are determined not only by antigen recognition but also by the metabolic landscape of the TME. Nutrient scarcity and metabolic waste products create a hostile setting that actively suppresses effector immune functions. At the heart of this immunometabolic regulation are PTMs, which serve as dynamic molecular interpreters, translating environmental cues into precise metabolic programmes within immune cells. Metabolic checkpoints in T cell fate determination: from activation to exhaustion

The functional plasticity of T cells—ranging from potent effector to long-lived memory and dysfunctional exhausted states—is underpinned by distinct metabolic pathways. PTMs act as the definitive metabolic checkpoints that meticulously govern these fate decisions, installing specific modification programs to ignite anabolism, enforce oxidative fitness, or impose metabolic paralysis.

Effector T cells: fueling proliferation and cytotoxicity

Upon activation, effector T cells (Teff) undergo profound metabolic reprogramming toward aerobic glycolysis to fuel rapid cloning and cytotoxic function. A sophisticated network of PTMs fine-tunes this metabolic engine. Glycolytic enzymes themselves are primary targets; for instance, serotonylation and acetylation of GAPDH enhance its activity to promote glycolysis and robust T cell activation.324,325 This regulatory principle can be co-opted in peripheral T cell lymphoma, where an oncogene VAV1-MYO1F fusion drives α-TAT1-mediated acetylation of GAPDH to sustain malignant proliferation.324 Beyond metabolic enzymes, PTMs orchestrate metabolism through upstream signaling hubs. SUMOylation of PDPK1 at K299 promotes its autophosphorylation and subsequent activation of mTORC1-driven glycolysis, sustaining CD4 + T cell homeostatic proliferation.326 To consolidate the effector state, PTMs facilitate essential metabolic-epigenetic coupling. The provision of acetyl-CoA for histone acetylation, primarily mediated by ACLY under nutrient-replete conditions, supports the epigenetic landscape necessary for robust interferon-γ production in CD8+ Teff.327 Demonstrating metabolic flexibility, T cells under glucose limitation shift toward an ACSS2-mediated acetate utilization pathway to maintain nuclear acetyl-CoA pools and effector gene expression, highlighting how PTM-regulated metabolic adaptation underpins transcriptional fidelity.328 Furthermore, PTMs integrate diverse signals to fine-tune the Teff response by modulating key checkpoints. Under oxidative stress, SENP7-mediated deSUMOylation of the tumor suppressor PTEN leads to its degradation, sustaining the PI3K-AKT axis and reinforcing metabolic capacity.329 Lipid metabolism is similarly tuned; SENP2-dependent deSUMOylation of PPARγ promotes lipid synthesis in CD8 + T cells, ensuring membrane biogenesis during clonal expansion.330 Collectively, these layered controls ensure that Teff cells are metabolically primed for proliferation and function.

Memory T cells: engineering longevity and metabolic fitness

In stark metabolic contrast, memory T cells (Tm) rely on oxidative metabolism to support long-term persistence and rapid recall. PTMs cement this state through metabolic-epigenetic circuits. A key pathway involves diverting mitochondrial acetyl-CoA into ketogenesis. The resulting β-hydroxybutyrate induces histone H3K9 β-hydroxybutyrylation (H3K9bhb), an epigenetic mark that activates PCK1 expression to promote gluconeogenic flux essential for Tm formation and maintenance.331,332 Mitochondrial metabolism-driven histone lactylation (H3K9la) further serves as an epigenetic signature distinguishing memory from effector CD8 + T cells.333 To sustain OXPHOS, PTMs actively optimize mitochondrial fitness. The SENP1-SIRT3 axis is pivotal: under glucose limitation, SENP1-mediated deSUMOylation activates the mitochondrial deacetylase SIRT3, enhancing OXPHOS efficiency and promoting mitochondrial fusion. This PTM-driven remodeling creates a stable mitochondrial network that forms the bioenergetic cornerstone for Tm longevity.334,335 Beyond core bioenergetics, PTMs integrate alternative nutrient cues to enforce stem-like transcriptional programmes. Mannose metabolism fuels O-GlcNAcylation by OGT, leading to the stabilization of β-catenin and its target transcription factor 7, a master regulator critical for sustaining the Tm pool and antitumor responses.336 Thus, PTMs meticulously engineer the metabolic and functional fitness of the memory compartment through epigenetic locking, mitochondrial optimization, and stemness programming.

T cell exhaustion: metabolic lockdown and epigenetic reinforcement in the TME

Persistent antigen exposure in the TME drives CD8 + T cells into exhaustion (TEX), a state of dysfunction actively reinforced by inhibitory PTMs. A pivotal intrinsic mechanism is the establishment of an epigenetic barrier. TEX cells shift from acetate to citrate metabolism, downregulating ACSS2 while maintaining ACLY activity. This promotes citrate-derived histone acetylation at TEX genes while reducing acetate-dependent acetylation at effector/memory genes, epigenetically suppressing the functional transcriptional program.337 Concurrently, PTM-dependent signaling circuits are activated to suppress pro-metabolic pathways. The E3 ubiquitin ligase ZFP91 restricts mTORC1-driven glycolysis by ubiquitinating protein phosphatase 2A.338 Similarly, chromobox protein homolog 4 promotes the SUMOylation of the transcription factors SP1/KLF3, increasing the expression of aldolase B to inhibit glycolysis via AKT suppression, creating a metabolic brake.339 Metabolites in the TME can directly impair function: glutarate inhibits α-KG-dependent demethylases and promotes PDH glutarylation, disrupting energy production,340 while SIRT7 deficiency, by failing to desuccinylate BCAA catabolic enzymes, drives exhaustion.341 Finally, PTMs cement the exhausted phenotype by stabilizing surface inhibitory receptors. Palmitoylation of TIM-3 by ZDHHC9 antagonizes its ubiquitination and degradation, increasing surface stability and exacerbating exhaustion.342 Collectively, these intrinsic PTM mechanisms orchestrate a comprehensive “metabolic lockdown” that defines TEX.

Extrinsic attack: tumor microenvironment-driven PTM sabotage of T cell fitness

Beyond intrinsic adaptation, the TME actively assaults T cells through direct, PTM-mediated sabotage. Tumors deploy a multipronged strategy to systemically undermine T cell metabolism.

Nutrient deprivation

They create a nutrient-poor milieu that starves T cells of PTM substrates. Malignant ascites inhibit glucose uptake and N-linked glycosylation, suppressing mitochondrial metabolism.343 Methionine scarcity disrupts the methionine cycle, reducing histone methylation and impairing survival-critical STAT5 signaling.243,344 Additionally, tumors deplete microenvironmental uridine, thereby limiting N-glycosylation of CD45—the transmembrane tyrosine phosphatase indispensable for CD8⁺ T-cell development and activation.345

Intercellular “Trojan Horse” transfer

In a direct assault, tumor cells transfer dysfunctional mitochondria pre-equipped with the deubiquitinase USP30 to T cells.346 Upon transfer, USP30 inhibits PARKIN-mediated mitophagy, imposing defective mitochondrial quality control and suppressing antitumor activity.347

Soluble mediator barrage

Tumors broadcast metabolites and vesicles that rewrite the T cell PTM landscape. Lactate induces histone H3K18 lactylation to sustain exhaustion and sensitize cells to death.348,349 The inhibitory neurotransmitter 4-Ac-GABA suppresses CD8⁺ T cell activity via GABA receptor-mediated inhibition of AKT1 phosphorylation.350 Tumor exosomes carrying the E3 ligase β-TrCP induce YAP1 ubiquitination and degradation, disrupting amino acid metabolism.351 Additionally, tumor-secreted factors such as FGF21 hyperactivate the AKT-mTORC1-SREBP1 axis in CD8⁺ T cells, driving aberrant cholesterol biosynthesis and promoting exhaustion.352 Similarly, tumor-derived lipids—such as lanosterol and palmitic acids—disrupt T-cell lipid metabolism, leading to an imbalance in lipid-associated PTMs within T cells. For instance, they impair KRAS prenylation and promote pathological STAT3 palmitoylation, further driving dysfunction.353,354 Collectively, these extrinsic PTM-driven attacks—from resource starvation to direct molecular hijacking—constitute a powerful offensive that actively dismantles T cell metabolic fitness, enforcing a state of irreversible dysfunction and presenting a formidable barrier to immunotherapy.

Myeloid and other immune cells: metabolic polarization and crosstalk

This section expands the view beyond T cells to myeloid cells and regulatory lymphocytes, detailing how PTMs guide their protumor polarization and mediate a complex metabolic dialog within the TME.

Tumor-associated myeloid cells: drivers of immunosuppression

Tumor-associated myeloid cells, including macrophages and neutrophils, are pivotal architects of the immunosuppressive TME. Current research indicates that their protumor functions are often associated with PTM-driven metabolic reprogramming and effector modulation.

PTMs ignite and sustain the specific metabolic pathways that underpin immunosuppressive functions. In PDAC, the long noncoding RNA SNHG17 enhances the phosphorylation of PGK1, promoting aerobic glycolysis and facilitating the polarization of macrophages toward M2-like tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) polarization.355 This glycolytic shift is further reinforced by glutamine metabolism promoting eIF5A hypusination-mediated translation of HIF-1α, solidifying the immunosuppressive metabolic configuration.356 Critically, the metabolites generated from these pathways directly modify chromatin to cement the phenotype. Glycolysis-driven histone lactylation in neutrophils and macrophages enhances the expression of canonical immunosuppressive mediators such as arginase 1 and IL-10.357,358 In HCC, a glycolytic/H3K18 lactylation axis similarly reinforces M2 polarization.359 Conversely, certain metabolites actively suppress immunostimulatory functions: α-ketoglutarate activates the OXGR1/MAPK pathway to inhibit major histocompatibility complex class II antigen presentation,360 while succinate accumulates and inhibits histone demethylases, leading to increased ARG1 expression and enhanced immunosuppressive function.361

Beyond intrinsic reprogramming, PTMs directly hijack the effector mechanisms of myeloid cells. In neuroendocrine prostate cancer, serotonin promotes histone serotonylation and citrullination in neutrophils, inducing the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps that facilitate liver metastasis.31 PTMs also govern myeloid cell recruitment. In HCC, PGAM5 deficiency promotes TRIM28-dependent ubiquitination of DRP1. The resulting inhibition of mitochondrial fission reduces chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2 secretion, thereby attenuating macrophage recruitment to the tumor site.362 Collectively, these findings illustrate that PTMs coordinately reshape both the metabolic identity and the effector functions of tumor-associated myeloid cells, thereby consolidating their protumorigenic role within the TME.

Progression of myeloid-tumor metabolic crosstalk fuels

Tumor-associated myeloid cells engage in active, PTM-mediated metabolic crosstalk with cancer cells, a dialog that fuels tumor progression and therapy resistance through two principal channels: fueling anabolic programs and installing defensive shields.

Myeloid cells deploy PTMs to directly activate and stabilize the core anabolic machinery within tumor cells. TAMs stabilize the master transcriptional regulator HIF-1α in HCC cells via USP22-mediated deubiquitination, driving glycolytic metabolism and tumor progression.363 They also secrete the metabolic enzyme PKM2, which phosphorylates aldolase A in cancer cells to enhance glycolytic flux and metastatic spread.364 Similarly, in gastric cancer, M2-polarized TAMs suppress β-TRCP-mediated ubiquitination of δ-catenin, resulting in sustained activation of the β-catenin pathway and enhanced aerobic glycolysis in tumor cells.365

Beyond fueling proliferative programmes, myeloid-derived signals employ PTMs to install robust defense mechanisms in cancer cells, conferring resistance to both targeted therapies and immune attack. TAM-secreted L-carnitine activates carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A) in liver cancer stem cells. This activation subsequently inhibits F-box and WD repeat domain-containing 7 (FBXW7)-mediated ubiquitination of the oncoprotein c-Myc, bolstering cellular antioxidant capacity and establishing a ferroptosis-resistant niche.366 Furthermore, macrophage-derived itaconate is imported by tumor cells and alkylates PD-L1 at Cys272, preventing its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby maintaining surface expression and conferring immunotherapy resistance.367 Myeloid-derived metabolites can also drive epigenetic reprogramming; for example, TAM-derived lactate induces EP300-mediated histone lactylation in tumor cells, upregulating the “don’t eat me” signal CD47 to promote immune evasion.368 This crosstalk is not unidirectional. Tumor cells can influence myeloid phenotypes through PTMs, as seen with tumor-derived microparticles that reprogram macrophages via succinylation-dependent metabolic rewiring, promoting a proinflammatory M1 phenotype.369 This bidirectional exchange underscores that PTMs serve as the central language of cellular communication within the TME, coscripting outcomes of growth, evasion, and therapeutic response.

Regulatory T cells: PTM-enforced metabolic programming for immune suppression

Regulatory T cells (Tregs) maintain immunosuppressive microenvironments through a specialized metabolic program favoring oxidative phosphorylation while actively suppressing glycolysis. PTMs enforce this identity through dual strategies: actively restraining pro-effector pathways and proactively adapting to the TME.

A key mechanism is the restraint of glycolysis. The deubiquitinase USP47 inhibits the ubiquitination of the m⁶A reader YTHDF1, which suppresses the translation of the glycolytic master regulator c-Myc, thereby preserving a low-glycolysis state essential for suppressive capacity.370 Complementing this, T cell receptor signaling induces ZFP91-dependent ubiquitination of Bcl-2-interacting protein 1 to activate autophagy, a process essential for maintaining Treg metabolic homeostasis by supporting mitochondrial quality and OXPHOS function.371 Beyond suppression, nutrient-sensing PTMs drive transcriptional reprogramming for adaptation. Enhanced glucose uptake via glucose transporter GLUT3 promotes O-GlcNAcylation of key cellular factors. This widespread modification drives a tumor-specific transcriptional program that enhances the fitness, stability, and suppressive potency of tumor-infiltrating Tregs.372 Thus, PTMs function not only as brakes on inappropriate metabolism but also as dynamic interpreters of the nutrient landscape, actively tailoring the Treg state for optimal function within tumors.

In summary, PTMs constitute the fundamental mechanism that rewires the metabolism of diverse immune cells within the TME. They govern the functional fate of myeloid cells and Tregs, mediate intricate and often destructive metabolic crosstalk between immune and tumor cells, and collectively enforce a state of adaptive immunosuppression. Understanding this multilayered PTM network is paramount for developing novel immunotherapies aimed at metabolically “rearming” the antitumor immune response.

Determinants and dynamics of PTM function in cancer metabolism

The preceding sections systematically delineate how PTMs act as central processors driving tumor metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion. However, a pivotal and complex question remains: What determines whether a specific PTM exerts an oncogenic or tumor-suppressive function? This functional plasticity is not stochastic but is governed by the precise integration of multilayered determinants. Moreover, PTMs do not operate in isolation; they form a dense, interactive network, and their functions are further sculpted by the spatial heterogeneity of the TME. This chapter explores the core principles that dictate PTM functional output, the crosstalk within the PTM network, and how metabolic gradients confer a spatial dimension to PTM activity, thereby unifying the regulatory logic of cancer metabolism.

Multilayered determinants of PTM functional output

The functional outcome of a PTM is dynamically specified by a confluence of molecular and contextual factors, which together explain the paradoxical roles that individual modifications can play across different biological settings.

Chemical nature and site specificity

The functional impact is first defined by the chemistry of the modifying group and its precise location on the target protein. Acetylation neutralizes a lysine’s positive charge to alter protein conformation and interactions (e.g., disrupting PDH complex assembly118), whereas bulkier, charged modifications such as succinylation can more dramatically remodel protein structure (e.g., succinylation of LDHA disrupts its binding to p62 from degradation139). Critically, the same PTM can exert opposing effects depending on the exact residue modified. Lactylation of the scaffold DCBLD1 stabilizes G6PD and promotes flux, while lactylation of G6PD itself suppresses its activity.111,112 Similarly, site-specific palmitoylation either stabilizes or destabilizes FASN.200–202 The ubiquitin code further exemplifies this principle, where chain topology (e.g., K48-linked for degradation vs. K63-linked for signaling) dictates opposing cellular fates.373 Thus, the initial functional instruction is encoded in the chemical identity and exact address of the modification.

Cellular and cancer-type context

The preexisting molecular landscape of a cell dictates how a PTM is interpreted. In ALK-positive lung cancer, phosphorylation of guanylate kinase 1 leads to GDP accumulation that transactivates the Ras-MAPK pathway, directly coupling nucleotide metabolism to proliferation—an outcome contingent on this specific oncogenic driver.266 The stability of master regulators such as SREBP1 and PPARγ is also contextually tuned by competing PTMs, such as stabilization by USP11 versus degradation by TRIM21 or inhibition by VHL versus activation by USP22.196–199 This demonstrates how identical regulatory nodes are wired into distinct circuitries depending on the cellular context.

Metabolic environment and donor availability

Metabolite-derived PTMs serve as direct sensors of local nutrient and metabolic states. Hypoxia-driven lactate accumulation promotes histone lactylation (e.g., H3K18la) to upregulate immune checkpoints,177,180 while acetyl-CoA abundance in nutrient-replete zones fuels pro-anabolic acetylation. Conversely, nutrient or energetic stress rewires PTM networks through altered metabolite availability: glucose deprivation activates the AMPK-PPARα axis to promote catabolism,211 whereas glutamine deprivation triggers SIRT5-mediated ME2 desuccinylation to sustain respiration.278 Notably, NAD⁺ metabolic reprogramming activates the NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase SIRT1, which promotes PD-L1 nuclear translocation by deacetylating H3K27ac.374 In this way, PTMs act as real-time metabolic rheostats, translating fluctuations in metabolite pools into immediate regulatory commands.

The “Writer-Eraser-Reader” machinery

The expression and activity of PTM enzymes define a cell’s regulatory capacity. Oncogenic phenotypes are often reinforced by upregulated deubiquitinases that stabilize key metabolic enzymes, such as OTUB2, preserving PKM2.134 Conversely, the stability of the “writers” themselves is fine-tuned by PTMs—for example, OGT stability is controlled by opposing ubiquitination and methylation events.169,170 The presence of specific “readers” (e.g., DPF2 for lactylation63) is ultimately required to translate the mark into a functional output. This enzymatic triad establishes the cell’s capacity to write, erase, and interpret the PTM code, setting the bounds of possible regulatory outcomes.

Integration into protein interaction networks

PTMs exert their function primarily by rewiring protein‒protein interactions. Mitochondrial translocation of phosphorylated hexokinase 2 enables its noncanonical kinase activity toward IκBα, activating the NF-κB pathway and PD-L1 expression.183–185 Lactylation of DNA repair proteins enhances complex assembly, promoting survival.57,163 Modifications can also repurpose enzyme function, as observed when hypoxic acetylation converts GDH1 from an α-KG producer to a component of the HIF-1α stabilization complex.254 Phosphorylated eIF3f enhances the interaction between eIF3f and ACSL4,204 while succinylation of PPP2R1A at K541 disrupts its interaction with PCK1.375 Thus, the cellular interactome acts as the final processor that interprets and executes PTM-encoded signals.

The crosstalk of PTM networks: a nexus for signal integration

PTMs do not function in isolation. They engage in intricate crosstalk, forming a dense network that integrates signals from metabolism, stress, and growth pathways into coherent cellular responses. This crosstalk manifests in several archetypal modes—hierarchical, competitive, and cooperative—each enabling distinct forms of signal processing and functional output.

Hierarchical crosstalk

In hierarchical crosstalk, one PTM directly enables or precludes the installation of another, creating deterministic sequences that relay and amplify signals. The phosphorylation-acetylation axis is paradigmatic: phosphorylated SREBF1 recruits KAT2A to acetylate histone H2A, directly imprinting a pro-lipogenic state onto chromatin.217 This logic extends to phosphorylation-ubiquitination crosstalk. AKT-mediated phosphorylation of TRIM21 inhibits TRIM21-driven ubiquitination of G6PD, creating a feedforward loop that controls PPP flux.115,116 Permissive control, a more hierarchical form of interaction, is evident when AMPK phosphorylation of SUCLA2 is a prerequisite for its activating succinylation, linking TCA flux to energy status.238 Conversely, PTMs can also function in an inhibitory cascade. A notable example is the FBXO7-mediated ubiquitination of PRMT1 at K37, which suppresses PRMT1-dependent methylation and activation of PHGDH—the rate-limiting enzyme of serine synthesis.232

Competitive crosstalk

Competitive crosstalk occurs when different PTMs vie for the same or adjacent residues, leading to antagonism and binary functional switches. This antagonism is exploited when FASN-dependent palmitoylation of mutant p53 shields it from degradation.221 Similarly, metabolite-derived acylations can compete, leading to a functional switch. This competitive paradigm is vividly illustrated by the direct antagonism between lactylation and acetylation at lysine 40 of α-tubulin. Here, the glycolytic byproduct lactate serves as the donor substrate for a noncanonical activity of HDAC6—traditionally a deacetylase—which installs lactyl marks in a manner that is dependent on concentration and its deacetylase activity.376 Similarly, Zong et al. reported that lactylation of p53 at K120/K139 prevents its activating acetylation, impairing p53 function.58 Thus, competitive crosstalk allows the dominant metabolic or signaling context to dynamically rewrite the functional identity of a protein.

Cooperative crosstalk

Multiple PTMs can act synergistically on the same protein or within a complex to produce distinct functional outcomes. Histone modifications exemplify this combinatorial code: H3Q5ser not only enhances transcription factor recruitment but also stabilizes the adjacent H3K4me3 mark by inhibiting demethylases, leading to robust oncogene activation.258,259 On metabolic enzymes, sequential modifications fine-tune activity—PRMT1-mediated methylation of PGK1 at R206 enhances its subsequent phosphorylation at S203, collaboratively boosting glycolytic flux.121 This combinatorial logic exponentially expands the regulatory vocabulary, allowing precise control over complex processes such as transcription and metabolism.

Metabolic-signaling integration via networked crosstalk

PTM crosstalk networks fundamentally hardwire the metabolic state into cellular programs. The nutrient-sensitive O-GlcNAcylation system exemplifies this: high nutrient flux stabilizes c-Myc, driving metabolic gene expression167,168 and generating metabolites that fuel other PTMs, creating self-reinforcing circuits. Similarly, PCK1 deficiency inactivates AMPK, elevating UDP-GlcNAc and driving hyper-O-GlcNAcylation. This single perturbation, through modification of CHK2 and KAT5, simultaneously hijacks cell cycle control and epigenetic programming to drive malignancy.171–173 This integrative logic extends to the direct coupling of metabolism and immunity via hierarchical PTM cascades. A paradigm is the CPT1A-MAVS axis. The lipid metabolic enzyme CPT1A recruits ZDHHC4 to palmitoylate MAVS. This palmitoylation acts as a master switch, reprogramming MAVS ubiquitination from a degradative (K48-linked) to an activating (K63-linked) pattern, thereby converting cellular lipid metabolism into potent type I interferon signaling.373 These examples demonstrate that PTM crosstalk networks are not merely passive interaction maps but are active, signal-processing circuits. They enable metabolic enzymes to function as direct commanders of signaling cascades and allow nutrient availability to dictate transcriptional and immune outcomes, epitomizing the role of PTMs as the central processing unit of cellular integration.

Metabolic gradients and spatial heterogeneity: the geography of PTMs

Tumors are not homogeneous masses; instead, they contain steep gradients of nutrients, oxygen, and metabolic waste. These dynamic metabolite landscapes act as active spatial encoders rather than passive scenery. At subcellular, cellular, and tissue scales, they sculpt the distribution, abundance, and functional output of PTMs, thereby driving intratumoral functional heterogeneity and therapeutic resistance.

Metabolic gradients as spatial encoders of PTMs

Metabolite gradients within the TME establish a fundamental spatial logic for PTM regulation. In oxygen- and nutrient-rich perivascular zones, high ATP and acetyl-CoA levels support active kinase signaling and extensive acetylation, driving anabolic and invasive programs—exemplified by AKT-phosphorylated SREBP activation for lipogenesis189,190 and FAO-derived acetyl-CoA fuels H3K27 acetylation at stemness and metastasis genes.219 Conversely, the hypoxic and acidic core is characterized by lactate accumulation, which drives histone and protein lactylation (e.g., H3K18la), a modification consistently linked to immune modulation and cellular plasticity.6,177,180 At nutrient-deprived interfaces, a high AMP/ATP ratio activates AMPK-mediated phosphorylation, promoting catabolic adaptation, such as FAO.211 Critically, these PTM programmes actively construct spatially organized functional compartments. The functional impact of this PTM geography is vividly illustrated in human cancers. For instance, integrated multiomics at single-cell resolution in HCC has identified three spatially organized functional archetypes: metabolic, stem-cell-like, and inflammatory. Notably, the metabolic prototype induces TREM2⁺ TAMs polarization by activating phosphorylation signaling pathways through local metabolic regulation, forming an immunosuppressive niche.377 Moreover, PTM landscapes can directly define the functional identity of cells within these compartments. Spatial phosphoproteomics in HBV + HCC has demonstrated that macrophage subtypes, defined solely by phosphorylation signatures, exhibit distinct functional capacities and are directly linked to differential responses to immunotherapy.378

Organelle-specific metabolic compartments specialize PTM actions

Spatial regulation extends into the cell, where metabolite availability and enzyme localization create specialized PTM environments within organelles. The nucleus is not merely a passive recipient of metabolic signals but an active compartment where metabolic enzymes can be repurposed as transcriptional coregulators. A paradigm is the glycolytic enzyme PKM2. Specific PTMs, such as O-GlcNAcylation and SUMOylation, promote its nuclear translocation.128–130,379,380 Mitochondria, as central metabolic hubs, utilize local concentrations of TCA cycle intermediates and NAD+ to dynamically regulate enzyme activity through site-specific acylation and deacylation; for instance, SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of ME2 under glutamine deprivation sustains oxidative respiration,278 while succinylation activates the OXCT1-LACTB axis to boost ketolysis.279,280 In the cytoplasm and at the plasma membrane, nutrient-sensitive O-GlcNAcylation and phosphorylation integrate signaling, while dynamic S-palmitoylation directly controls the membrane localization and activity of metabolic transporters, such as the palmitoylation of GLUT1 by ZDHHC9 for glucose uptake.206 These organelle-specific PTM programs ensure that metabolic signals are processed and executed within the correct functional context.

Integrated spatial heterogeneity drives fate determination and therapeutic challenges

The TME exhibits profound gradients in oxygen, nutrients, and metabolic waste. These spatial variations are likely translated into distinct “PTM geographies”—regional patterns of protein modifications shaped by local metabolite and modifier availability. The convergence of tissue-scale and organelle-scale PTM heterogeneity locks cells into distinct states, such as proliferation and metastasis in perivascular zones and stem-like and metabolic plasticity in hypoxic cores.171–173 This spatial logic creates formidable therapeutic barriers, as agents targeting a single PTM enzyme often fail against cells in dissimilar metabolic compartments. More complexly, tumor cells can also mobilize metabolic compensation mechanisms to adapt to the treatment environment. Overcoming this requires spatially informed strategies: combinations cotargeting different niches; microenvironment normalization to reduce PTM disparity; organelle-targeted drug delivery; and dynamic PTM biomarkers to map heterogeneity and guide treatment.

Collectively, these insights cement the overarching thesis: PTMs constitute the central processing unit of oncogenic metabolic reprogramming. They are the fundamental mechanism through which cancer cells interpret environmental cues, execute metabolic flux decisions, consolidate malignant phenotypes, and orchestrate immune evasion. Viewing cancer metabolism through this lens of context-dependent, networked, and spatially resolved PTM regulation not only unifies disparate observations but also reveals the profound interconnectivity and adaptability that define this hallmark of cancer. This refined understanding highlights promising yet complex avenues for therapeutic intervention, aiming to disrupt the very computational logic that governs tumor survival and progression.

Clinical implications and therapeutic prospects of PTMs in cancer metabolism

The PTM regulatory network of tumor metabolism, given its core biological functions and high plasticity, has become a critical interface connecting fundamental discoveries to clinical translation. From an interventional logic perspective, targeting this network follows a multilayered strategic framework. The most direct approach, molecular targeting, aims to inhibit PTM pathway components or eliminate the modified proteins themselves. Metabolic intervention strategies seek to indirectly reshape the PTM landscape by modulating the availability of key metabolite cofactors or donors. A more advanced frontier, spatiotemporal precision intervention, leverages unique features of the TME for conditional activation or targeted delivery, striving to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity. Currently, clinical translation primarily focuses on the first two strategies and has achieved substantial progress in biomarker development, targeted drugs, and dietary therapies. PTMs as diagnostic, prognostic and predictive biomarkers

By analyzing tumor-specific PTM signatures, it is possible to refine disease classification, predict clinical outcomes, and forecast responses to therapy, thereby enabling personalized treatment strategies.

PTM profiles serve as potent diagnostic and prognostic indicators. For instance, specific histone lactylation signatures are not only drivers of distant metastasis but also shape an immunosuppressive TME, correlating strongly with poor prognosis in cancers such as HCC.381 Similarly, elevated expression of modifying enzymes such as HDAC7 in small cell lung cancer is linked to disease progression and predicts enhanced sensitivity to the exportin-1 inhibitor selinexor, highlighting its dual role as a prognostic marker and a predictor of drug response.382 The integration of multiomics approaches with machine learning is now propelling this field forward. A study systematically performed a panoramic profiling of 20 types of PTMs and integrated ten machine learning algorithms, identifying the key gene B4GALT2 as a core regulatory factor driving immune escape in LUAD and offering a translatable tool for individualized management.383 Crucially, PTMs function as robust predictive biomarkers for therapeutic vulnerability and resistance. They can identify specific pharmacological dependencies; for example, N-glycosylation has been identified as a genetic and therapeutic vulnerability in CALR-mutant malignancies.384 Beyond prediction, PTM dynamics offer a means to monitor disease progression. Evolving lactylation signatures, for example, have been shown to track the progression from liver fibrosis to HCC, providing a window into disease evolution.385 Collectively, these advances underscore the translational potential of PTM-based biomarkers in guiding clinical decision-making, from initial diagnosis and risk stratification to the selection and monitoring of tailored therapies.

It is crucial to acknowledge the translational hurdles: the low stoichiometry and dynamic nature of PTMs demand highly sensitive detection, while tumor heterogeneity complicates the establishment of standardized profiles. Prospective, large-scale clinical validation is essential to confirm their predictive value.

PTMs as direct therapeutic targets: molecular targeting strategies

The direct targeting of proteins that install, remove, or interpret PTMs represents the cornerstone of translating PTM biology into medicine. This field has undergone a radical evolution: from the pharmacological inhibition of enzymatic activity to the hijacking of endogenous degradation machinery for target elimination and now toward the programmable, context-aware editing of PTM states themselves.

Pharmacological inhibition: the cornerstone of precision oncology

Drugs targeting kinases, histone deacetylases (HDACs), and the ubiquitin‒proteasome system represent the most mature and successful class of PTM-directed therapies, each validated through profound clinical benefits. Despite their success, PTM enzyme inhibitors face inherent challenges. Achieving target specificity within large enzyme families is difficult, risking off-target toxicity. Cancer cells exploit network plasticity to activate compensatory pathways, driving resistance. Moreover, key PTM regulators, such as specific E3 ligases, remain “undrugged” due to pharmacological challenges.

Kinase inhibitors

Kinase inhibitors exemplify the therapeutic principle of intercepting a genetically defined, driver phosphorylation signal to exert profound control over oncogenic programmes. Their efficacy hinges on targeting nonredundant, genetically defined dependency nodes—kinases that become true “Achilles’ heels” due to driver mutations, as exemplified by imatinib in BCR-ABL + CML386 and osimertinib in EGFR-mutant NSCLC.387,388 Recent breakthroughs, such as the approval of KRAS G12C inhibitors, have conquered historically “undruggable” targets.389 The mechanistic understanding of these agents has evolved beyond simple catalytic blockades. Certain inhibitors can induce a degradation-prone conformation in their target, effectively hijacking endogenous ubiquitin‒proteasome or autophagic machinery to achieve a dual strike of activity inhibition and protein elimination, offering a novel strategy to combat resistance.390

However, robustness and plasticity of the network often lead to resistance through compensatory pathway activation or metabolic rewiring. For instance, resistance to EGFR inhibitors in lung cancer can be driven by upregulation of dipeptidyl peptidase 4, which enhances fatty acid uptake and antioxidant defenses.391 This intrinsic link between oncogenic signaling and metabolic adaptation has spurred efforts to directly target “metabolic kinases”. The translation of metabolic kinase inhibitors faces unique hurdles: drugging kinases involved in essential housekeeping metabolism is challenging, and metabolic networks exhibit robust compensatory capacity. While no broad-spectrum anticancer drug approved solely for this purpose currently exists, promising candidates are in development.392 The case of ACLY inhibitors illustrates both their potential and complexity. The novel ACLY inhibitor EVT0185, recently identified for its efficacy in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis-driven HCC, exemplifies a “network-targeting” strategy. Unlike the approved ACLY inhibitor bempedoic acid (which activates AMPK and induces metabolic compensation), its active metabolites concurrently inhibit ACLY and its key compensatory enzymes (ACC1/2, ACSS2, and AMPKβ1).393 This system-level, network-targeting design demonstrates a blueprint for overcoming metabolic plasticity, pointing the way toward more durable kinase-directed therapies.

HDAC inhibitors

Targeting deacetylases present a distinct therapeutic paradigm. Unlike kinase inhibitors that often target genetically defined drivers, HDAC inhibitors intervene at the dynamic interface between cellular metabolism and epigenetic regulation, where histone acetylation serves as a ubiquitous physiological code. This broad action limits their efficacy in solid tumors and causes dose-limiting toxicities such as myelosuppression. This limitation has driven the pursuit of isoform-selective inhibition to improve the therapeutic index. For example, the HDAC I/IIb dual inhibitor purinostat mesylate, the HDAC3-specific compound KH16 (Yanostat) and the HDAC6-selective inhibitor ricolinostat (ACY-1215) show promising efficacy and safety.394–396 Notably, HDAC6, primarily a cytoplasmic isoform, recognizes and deacetylates nonhistone substrates such as α-tubulin and HSP90. Beyond its classical roles, HDAC6 directly regulates glycolytic flux—for instance, by deacetylating and activating transaldolase 1,397 illustrating how an isoform-restricted PTM–metabolic axis can be exploited to reshape tumor cell metabolism without broadly perturbing nuclear epigenetic circuits.

The quest for specificity is driving innovation beyond catalytic inhibition. Novel regulatory mechanisms are informing drug design; for instance, endogenous inositol phosphates, NADPH and even metabolic enzymes such as phosphofructokinase, liver type (PFKL)—which translocates to the nucleus to act as a natural HDAC inhibitor—can dramatically influence inhibitor potency and selectivity.398–400 Metabolic priming strategies show great promise for combination therapy. Metabolic priming strategies have powerful combinatorial potential. The small molecule ISX9, blocking inhibition of the nuclear pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (nPDC), increases nuclear acetyl-CoA and histone acetylation. When combined with an HDAC inhibitor, it not only delays tumor progression but also sustainably depletes therapy-persistent tumor-initiating cells,401 demonstrating potent synergy between metabolic and epigenetic interventions. To overcome limited single-agent efficacy, mechanism-driven combinations are being explored. Regimens such as “CAP” (HDACi + anti-angiogenic + anti-PD-1) aim to remodel the tumor immune microenvironment.402 The development of multipharmacology agents (e.g., dual JAK/HDAC403 or triple JAK/BRD4/HDAC inhibitors404) represents another innovative tactic. Future success will hinge on precision patient stratification using predictive biomarkers (e.g., HDAC6 expression405) and the exploitation of noncanonical HDAC functions, such as HDAC6’s role as a valine sensor regulating DNA repair.406 These insights position HDAC-targeted therapy not as a standalone intervention but as a central component in the broader toolkit of PTM-based metabolic reprogramming, paving the way for truly context-dependent and combinatorial precision oncology.

Inhibitors of the UPS and other emerging modifications

The ubiquitin‒proteasome system (UPS) is a rich source of therapeutic targets. Proteasome inhibitors such as bortezomib and carfilzomib, which induce catastrophic proteotoxic stress, are cornerstones in the treatment of multiple myeloma.407 Beyond the proteasome, components of the ubiquitination cascade are being targeted. The NEDD8-activating enzyme (NAE) inhibitor pevonedistat, which blocks the activity of Cullin-RING ligases (CRLs), received the FDA Breakthrough Therapy designation for high-risk myelodysplastic syndromes.408 The revolutionary Proteolysis-Targeting Chimera (PROTAC) technology, which will be discussed in the next section, represents a paradigm shift by hijacking the UPS for targeted protein degradation.

Beyond phosphorylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination, other PTM pathways are yielding promising targets. Methylation inhibitors are gaining traction. EZH1/2 inhibitors, which target the histone methyltransferase responsible for H3K27me3, are approved for specific lymphomas.409 PRMT5 inhibitors are being developed with a synthetic lethality strategy for tumors with MTAP gene deletion, moving toward biomarker-driven precision therapy.410,411 Recent studies have revealed an inhibitor targeting NSD2—the enzyme that catalyzes H3K36me2—and this discovery offers new hope for treating lung and pancreatic cancers.412 Lipidation inhibitors are also emerging. The farnesyltransferase inhibitor tipifarnib, originally developed for HRAS-driven cancers, has been found to inhibit the Rho/ROCK pathway, offering a new angle to combat resistance in NSCLC.413 Inhibition of N-myristoylation, catalyzed by N-myristoyltransferase 1 (NMT1), has shown antitumor effects, with the repurposed anti-allergy drug desloratadine demonstrating NMT1 inhibitory activity in tumor models.414,415 Targeting aberrant glycosylation represents another frontier. Tumor cell surface hypersialylation, which promotes immune evasion, positions sialyltransferase (ST) inhibitors as a promising strategy. However, their clinical translation is hampered by severe off-target toxicity due to a lack of tumor selectivity and the “whack-a-mole” effect of compensatory upregulation within the redundant ST family. Despite these challenges, novel approaches such as prodrug strategies and the development of potent, broad-spectrum oral inhibitors such as Y-320 are emerging to overcome these barriers.416,417

Paradigm shift: from inhibition to contextual precision with chemically induced proximity

The most transformative advance in targeting PTMs is the paradigm shift toward chemically induced proximity. These strategies move beyond simple enzyme inhibition to achieve programmable, event-driven control over protein fate and modification states by recruiting specific enzymatic activities directly to target proteins. This “recruit-and-modify” principle is best exemplified by PROTACs, which use a chemical linker to simultaneously engage a target protein and an E3 ubiquitin ligase, inducing polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This catalytic, “event-driven” mechanism offers key advantages over traditional inhibitors, including substoichiometric dosing and the ability to target previously “undruggable” proteins. Clinically, PROTACs recruiting CRBN or VHL ligases have advanced rapidly, with agents such as the AR degrader ARV-110 and the ER degrader ARV-471 reaching late-phase trials.418 The scope of targets is expanding to include drivers such as KRAS G12D and STAT3,419 while innovations such as dual-targeting PROTACs (e.g., degrading both EGFR and PARP) and bifunctional molecules that both degrade and inhibit (e.g., PKMYT1-targeting D16-M1P2) continue to emerge.420,421

Inspired by the PROTACs paradigm, the Targeted Chimera (TAC) toolkit has diversified into a platform for precision PTM editing. Beyond degradation, this growing family includes DUBTACs422,423 (which recruit deubiquitinases to stabilize targets), PHICs/PHORCs424,425 (to edit phosphorylation), AceTAGs426 and OGTACs427 (which install acetylation or O-GlcNAcylation), and DGlyTACs428 (which remove N-glycans). This evolution marks a conceptual leap from merely eliminating proteins to actively rewriting their PTM codes. The foundational power of PROTACs is further demonstrated by their application against PTM-modifying enzymes themselves, offering a potent strategy to reshape the entire cellular PTM landscape. A prime example is HDAC8-directed PROTACs, which overcome tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance in clear cell renal cell carcinoma.429 This development prompts a critical question for therapeutic design: when is it more advantageous to degrade a target versus precisely editing its PTM state? The answer is not universal but is dictated by the specific biological and pathological context of the target protein.

The convergence of PTM biology with proximity-induction technology is now enabling an even more sophisticated paradigm: degraders whose action is conditional on the PTM state or metabolic context of the target. This “functional-state-aware” approach aims to maximize therapeutic precision by selectively eliminating pathologically activated protein pools while sparing their physiological counterparts. Emerging evidence confirms that PROTAC efficacy can be dictated by the PTM status of its target. For instance, a PROTAC against the DNA damage kinase ATR selectively degrades its phosphorylated (active) form—a crucial feature given that active ATR also localizes to mitochondria to support metabolic homeostasis.430,431 A PTM-selective ATR degrader could thus precisely dismantle the active, metabolic pool that fuels tumor adaptation. Similarly, the challenge and potential of this approach are powerfully illustrated in targeting the oncogenic kinase FLT3-ITD. FLT3-ITD exists in a highly phosphorylated, underglycosylated state localized to the ER, which is its key form in metabolic reprogramming and stress-response networks.432 A breakthrough came with the development of an FLT3-ITD isoform-selective PROTAC that preferentially degrades this pathogenic ER-associated, hyperphosphorylated pool.433 This demonstrates that beyond target engagement, the subcellular localization and PTM status of a protein are decisive parameters for degradation efficacy. It provides a blueprint for designing the next generation of degraders that can precisely dismantle the most disease-relevant forms of a target within its complex biological context. To enhance tumor selectivity, “smart” PROTACs are being designed to be activated by the unique metabolic environment of cancer cells. One strategy involves masking the PROTACs with an O-GlcNAc sugar group. This inert “prodrug” is selectively cleaved by the overactive enzyme OGA, which is often upregulated in tumors, leading to localized activation and degradation exclusively within the cancer cell.434 A complementary approach exploits the high glucose avidity of tumors by designing glycosylated PROTAC prodrugs that are selectively taken up via overexpressed GLUTs on cancer cells.435 Similarly, hypoxia-activated PROTACs exploiting high reductases and cathepsin-activated “click” PROTACs exploiting overexpressed proteases have achieved precise degradation of “reader” proteins such as BRD4 in multiple tumor models.436,437 These frontiers aim to integrate the spatial and metabolic determinants of PTM function directly into therapeutic design.

However, these technologies confront distinct barriers.438 The high molecular weight of bifunctional molecules often compromises pharmacokinetics. Their catalytic mechanism risks a “hook effect” at high concentrations, reducing efficacy. Achieving absolute selectivity is difficult, with off-target degradation a concern. For PTM-editing TACs, precise control over modification stoichiometry adds another layer of complexity.

Immunological reprogramming: PTMs as direct antigens and immunomodulatory targets

Capitalizing on the precision of targeted degradation, immunotherapy is advancing a frontier strategy: directly harnessing PTMs as antigens for immune recognition. This paradigm transforms the tumor’s pathogenic PTM signature into a targetable vulnerability. PTM-directed cancer vaccines exemplify this approach, exploiting tumor-specific neoantigens defined by aberrant glycosylation,439,440 pathological phosphorylation,441,442 or citrullination.443–445 These modification-dependent epitopes bypass central tolerance, eliciting potent and tumor-specific immune responses. Clinical translation is progressing, as evidenced by the glycosylation-targeting vaccine adagloxad simolenin (Globo-H-MUC1), which enhanced antibody responses and progression-free survival in a phase II breast cancer trial,446 prompting its ongoing phase III evaluation (GLORIA study). Innovative delivery platforms, such as the engineered NDV-GT vector that induces immunogenic αGal epitopes on tumors, have shown preliminary safety and antitumor activity in clinical studies.447 PTMs can also be harnessed to generate unique neoantigens that enhance immune recognition of cancer cells. Covalent KRAS-G12C inhibitors such as ARS1620 alkylate mutant Cys, generating MHC-I-presented peptides that elicit T-cell responses and offer readily targetable immunotherapy hits.448

Beyond serving as neoantigens, glycosylation critically regulates the stability and function of immune checkpoints such as PD-L1/PD-1 and B7-H4, promoting immune evasion and limiting the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockers (ICBs). Specifically, N-glycosylation at all four PD-L1 sites (N35, N192, N200, and N219) impairs the ability of both anti-PD-L1 and anti-PD-1 antibodies to block PD-L1/PD-1 interactions.449,450 Targeting these PTM epitopes with specific monoclonal antibodies offers a promising passive immunotherapeutic strategy. One example is the monoclonal antibody Ab-82, which selectively recognizes B7-H3 glycosylated at sites N91/309 and N104/322. This antibody reprograms the immune microenvironment by driving B7-H3 internalization and triggering a potent cytotoxic T-lymphocyte response.451 However, a fundamental challenge for high-affinity antibodies is “on-target, off-cancer” toxicity—recognition of the same antigen expressed at low physiological levels in healthy tissues. This has spurred the development of next-generation glycan-dependent T-cell recruiters. These molecules employ a multivalent, density-dependent binding mechanism that simultaneously engages tumor-enriched glycans such as β1,6-GlcNAc-branched N-glycans and Tn/sTn antigens, achieving selective killing of cancer cells.452

This approach faces unique hurdles. The immunogenicity of PTM-neoantigens varies greatly with HLA haplotype and antigen processing.453 The immunosuppressive TME can simultaneously inhibit the very immune responses these therapies seek to elicit. Antibody strategies are limited to cell-surface PTMs, and antigen loss under therapeutic pressure remains a risk for resistance.

Modulating the PTM landscape: dietary metabolic interventions

Emerging as a novel adjunctive strategy, dietary interventions aim to modulate the tumor metabolic milieu, thereby indirectly reshaping the global PTM network to exert antitumor effects. This approach exploits the fact that many PTMs are directly dependent on metabolite co-factors or donor substrates whose availability can be influenced by nutrient intake.

The therapeutic logic is based on modulating specific PTM donor substrates. Macronutrient manipulation, such as a ketogenic diet, elevates β-hydroxybutyrate to induce protein β-hydroxybutyrylation, suppressing glycolytic pathways in preclinical models.454 Conversely, high-palmitate diets can promote tumor progression by enhancing ZDHHC15-mediated palmitoylation of oncogenic signaling.455 Precision targeting extends to micronutrients and amino acids. Vitamin C induces “vitcylation” of STAT1, enhancing its antitumor activity,41 while valine restriction activates HDAC6-mediated TET2 deacetylation to promote DNA demethylation.406 Gut microbiota-derived short-chain fatty acids from fiber-rich diets serve dual roles as HDAC inhibitors and direct donors for histone butyrylation.456,457 A more sophisticated strategy involves the precision supplementation of specific metabolic precursors to directly rewire the PTM landscape. Supplementation with α-ketoglutarate or succinate promotes the succinylation of PD-L1, leading to PD-L1 degradation and thereby enhancing T cell-mediated tumor elimination in preclinical models.458 This approach demonstrates the feasibility of using “metabolic supplements” to activate specific enzymatic PTMs for dismantling immunosuppressive signals. Complementing this, ex vivo metabolic conditioning in adoptive cell therapy represents an active form of intervention. By shifting T cells toward oxidative phosphorylation, this process triggers histone acetylation-mediated epigenetic reprogramming, endowing T cells with a superior memory-like phenotype and in vivo persistence.459 Dietary strategies show great potential in sensitizing tumors to established therapies. Lysine restriction synergizes with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in glioma models through immune microenvironment reprogramming.460 Similarly, vitamin E succinate enhances the immunotherapy response by promoting FTO ubiquitination and degradation.461 Serine/glycine restriction, while triggering adaptive lactylation, creates nucleotide synthesis vulnerabilities that could potentiate chemotherapy.462

However, transforming dietary strategies into reliable therapies faces fundamental barriers. The effects are systemic and lack tumor specificity, with risks of paradoxically fueling tumor growth. For instance, fasting-induced fatty acids may activate pro-growth AMPK-MNK-eIF4E signaling in some contexts.463 Metabolic networks exhibit robust compensation, limiting durability. Patient compliance and monitoring present additional practical hurdles. Future applications require precision nutrition approaches guided by tumor-specific metabolic and PTM profiling. The goal is to identify and target the specific metabolite dependencies that maintain oncogenic PTM networks in individual tumors, rationally combining dietary regimens with matched pharmacological agents to block adaptive responses and maximize therapeutic efficacy.

The clinical translation of PTM biology rests on two established pillars: precision PTM-based biomarkers for patient stratification and multilayered therapeutic strategies including direct molecular targeting and systemic metabolic interventions. While these approaches show promise, they face challenges of selectivity, resistance due to metabolic plasticity and practical implementation hurdles. Progress will require evolving from broad inhibition toward spatiotemporally precise interventions, such as tumor-activated prodrugs and organelle-targeted delivery, and ultimately computationally guided network-rewiring strategies. This shift from target elimination to cellular fate reprogramming represents the next frontier for achieving durable and selective control over cancer progression.

Conclusion and future perspectives

This review establishes that PTMs are the central processing units of oncogenic metabolic reprogramming. They function as a universal molecular language, translating upstream oncogenic signals and microenvironmental cues into precise metabolic commands across all core pathways—glucose, lipid, amino acid, and nucleotide metabolism. Beyond flux control, PTMs directly couple metabolic activity to cell fate determination, locking in aggressive phenotypes through metabolite-derived epigenetic remodeling. Furthermore, they extend their reach into the TME, reprogramming immune cell metabolism to enforce immunosuppression. This intricate control is defined by a multitiered logic and is exquisitely context dependent, governed by site specificity, metabolic cross-talk, and spatial heterogeneity. Viewing cancer metabolism through the lens of PTM networks thus provides a unifying, systems-level paradigm that explains its hallmark plasticity and resilience. Looking forward, the field is poised to evolve from mapping static PTM–metabolite associations to dynamically decoding their causal logic and therapeutic potential. Technologically, the development of single-cell, multi-PTM profiling and high-spatiotemporal resolution imaging will be crucial to capture the dynamic interplay of modifications within metabolic microcompartments and across tumor heterogeneities. Theoretically, a deeper understanding is needed. We must move beyond cataloging interactions to computationally model the PTM network’s emergent properties, such as robustness, switching thresholds, and compensatory pathways, to predict vulnerabilities.

Translationally, the future lies in developing context-smart interventions that overcome the limitations of broad enzymatic inhibition. This includes 1) precision targeting via synthetic lethal approaches or agents activated by tumor-specific metabolic conditions (e.g., hypoxia, acidity); 2) advanced PTM editing using next-generation chimera technologies to actively rewrite specific oncogenic PTM marks (e.g., installing tumor-suppressive modifications or removing oncogenic ones) with high spatial precision; and 3) rational combination therapies designed based on network models to simultaneously target a primary oncogenic PTM pathway and its predicted compensatory route, thereby blocking metabolic escape. Finally, the exploration of circulating PTM signatures as dynamic, noninvasive biomarkers holds promise for early detection, patient stratification, and monitoring therapeutic efficacy.

In conclusion, PTMs constitute the fundamental operating system of cancer metabolism. The coming era will challenge researchers to transition from observers to programmers—to not only understand this complex code but also to learn how to precisely rewrite it, thereby transforming the cancer cell’s greatest adaptive strength into its most critical therapeutic vulnerability.
