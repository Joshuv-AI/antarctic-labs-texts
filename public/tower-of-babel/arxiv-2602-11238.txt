Abstract
. The exponential growth of scientific literature has driven the evolution of Automatic Survey Generation (ASG) from simple pipelines to multi-agent frameworks and commercial Deep Research agents. However, current ASG evaluation methods rely on generic metrics and are heavily biased toward Computer Science (CS), failing to assess whether ASG methods adhere to the distinct standards of various academic disciplines. Consequently, researchers, especially those outside CS, lack clear guidance on using ASG systems to yield high-quality surveys compliant with specific discipline standards. To bridge this gap, we introduce SurveyLens, the first discipline-aware benchmark evaluating ASG methods across diverse research disciplines. We construct SurveyLens-1k, a curated dataset of 1,000 high-quality human-written surveys spanning 10 disciplines. Subsequently, we propose a dual-lens evaluation framework: (1) Discipline-Aware Rubric Evaluation, which utilizes LLMs with human preference-aligned weights to assess adherence to domain-specific writing standards; and (2) Canonical Alignment Evaluation to rigorously measure content coverage and synthesis quality against human-written survey papers. We conduct extensive experiments by evaluating 11 state-of-the-art ASG methods on SurveyLens, including Vanilla LLMs, ASG systems, and Deep Research agents. Our analysis reveals the distinct strengths and weaknesses of each paradigm across fields, providing essential guidance for selecting tools tailored to specific disciplinary requirements.

1.
Introduction

The exponential growth of scientific literature has outpaced researchers’ capacity to manually track developments across fields. In response, Automatic Survey Generation (ASG) systems leverage Generative AI, including Large Language Models (LLMs), Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
, to curate relevant literature, synthesize diverse findings, and produce survey-style narratives. Existing ASG systems have evolved from pipeline-based approaches that streamline discrete literature review tasks
(
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Liang et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
to human-in-the-loop paradigms enabling iterative refinement
(
Wen et al. 2025
;
Chao et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
, and finally to multi-agent systems that simulate collaborative survey workflows
(
Liu et al. 2025
;
Shi et al. 2025
)
, and commercial products such as Gemini Deep Research and Qwen Deep Research.

Despite their advancement, current assessment methods for ASG systems remain inadequate, particularly in assessing their cross-disciplinary capabilities. Traditional evaluation
(
Kasanishi et al. 2023
)
based on semantic metrics (
i.e.
, ROUGE
(
Lin 2004
)
and BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
) fails to assess the structural diversity of survey writing, while recent approaches
(
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
based on LLM-as-a-judge
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
utilize generic, domain-agnostic criteria, leading to the lack of interpretability and disciplinary grounding. More importantly, existing benchmarks
(
Kasanishi et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Yan et al. 2025
;
Shi et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Su et al. 2026
)
are predominantly restricted to the field of Computer Science (CS), likely due to data accessibility and the background of the researchers; they fail to verify whether the generated survey papers adhere to the intellectual traditions of other fields. Meanwhile, existing discipline-aware LLM benchmarks suffer from a fundamental capability mismatch: while datasets like MMLU and SciBench evaluate atomic domain knowledge through Question Answering (QA) tasks, they cannot assess the complex planning and coherent narrative construction required for systematic reviews. Consequently, researchers, especially those outside CS, lack clear guidance on which systems yield high-quality surveys compliant with their specific discipline standards.

To bridge this gap, we introduce SurveyLens, the first discipline-aware benchmark designed to evaluate ASG methods across diverse research disciplines. Specifically, we first construct SurveyLens-1k, a comprehensive dataset with 1,000 high-quality human-written survey papers spanning 10 distinct research disciplines, capturing the structural, content, and reference variances inherent to each discipline. Based on SurveyLens-1k, we establish a holistic evaluation framework to assess the adherence of ASG methods to specific disciplinary norms through two primary lenses:

Discipline-aware Rubric Evaluation
evaluates adherence to specific disciplinary norms via an LLM-as-a-judge paradigm. It adopts a bottom-up approach to distill field-specific rubrics directly from the SurveyLens-1k corpus. Furthermore, to guide the LLM judge in capturing distinct disciplinary priorities (
e.g.
, Medicine prioritizing evidence hierarchy vs. Computer Science emphasizing benchmarking), we incorporate a Preference-Aligned Weighting mechanism based on the Bradley-Terry model. This calibrates the judge’s scoring criteria against simulated expert preferences, ensuring the evaluation rigorously emulates the reasoning process of a domain expert.

Canonical Alignment Evaluation
quantitatively measures factual grounding and content coverage against canonical human-written surveys. To address the inability of traditional metrics to detect redundancy in long-text generation, we introduce a dual-metric framework comprising Redundancy-Aware Matching Score (RAMS) and Thresholded Average Maximum Similarity (TAMS). RAMS leverages one-to-one Hungarian matching to explicitly penalize repetition, while TAMS measures the semantic quality upper bound.
Together, they rigorously assess whether the output achieves substantive synthesis while complying with the distinct stylistic conventions of various academic fields.

We utilize SurveyLens to benchmark three distinct paradigms for ASG: Vanilla LLMs, Specialized ASG systems, and Deep Research Agents. Specifically, we employ each ASG method to generate 100 surveys based on topics extracted from SurveyLens-1k, with 10 samples sampled from each discipline. We then rigorously assess these outputs using our proposed framework, employing Gemini-3-Pro as the judge for discipline-aware rubric evaluation and calculating the RAMS and TAMS metrics for canonical alignment evaluation, comparing with 100 human-written survey papers on the same topics.

Our analysis reveals critical insights into the current state of ASG. Notably, we identify a trade-off between structural rigor and content synthesis: specialized ASG systems excel at maintaining the strict organizational standards required for STEM fields, whereas Deep Research Agents produce richer narratives but often lack academic structural precision. Furthermore, we observe significant performance disparities across domains, where vanilla LLMs surprisingly outperform specialized systems in the humanities by leveraging rhetorical flexibility, yet struggle with the technical verification required in hard sciences. These findings guide researchers in selecting tools tailored to their disciplinary needs, prioritizing agents for narrative-heavy reviews and specialized pipelines for structured technical surveys. Finally, validation confirms that SurveyLens aligns highly with human expert judgment.

Related Work

2.1.
Automatic Survey Generation

Automatic Survey Generation (ASG) is the task of producing a comprehensive survey paper
\mathcal{S}
, given a research topic
t
. The development of ASG systems has been driven by advances in generative AI, such as LLMs, RAG pipelines, and autonomous agents. As one of the earliest ASG approaches, AutoSurvey
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
employs a sequential pipeline comprising retrieval, outlining, drafting by specialized LLMs, and iterative refinement.
Building upon this direction, subsequent ASG systems introduced memory mechanisms to enhance structural planning and information extraction
(
Yan et al. 2025
;
Liang et al. 2025
;
Wu et al. 2025
)
. Leveraging the improved long-context understanding of LLMs, later research transcended these pipeline frameworks and shifted toward iterative long-context processing paradigms
(
Wang et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
for survey generation. Subsequent work improves ASG reasoning and controllability through multi-agent frameworks with verification-based roles
(
Shi et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Nguye et al. 2025
)
or human-in-the-loop interventions during generation
(
Chao et al. 2025
;
Wen et al. 2025
)
. Besides, commercial tools
(
OpenAI 2025
;
Gemini 2025
;
Qwen 2025
)
further integrate strong reasoning with real-time environmental access.

Despite these advances, existing ASG methods are primarily evaluated on Computer Science corpora, implicitly assuming consistent survey conventions across disciplines. Furthermore, significant variations in datasets, human involvement, and evaluation criteria also hinder reliable comparisons and cross-disciplinary generalization assessments.

2.2.
Assessment of ASG

The assessment of ASG has evolved from early n-gram metrics (ROUGE)
(
Kasanishi et al. 2023
)
to semantic approaches, yet most still assume a unified quality standard across disciplines. For instance, to optimize evaluation methods, AutoSurvey
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
established a multi-dimensional content quality framework and popularized the “LLM-as-a-Judge” paradigm in ASG assessment. To refine citation evaluation, SurveyX
(
Liang et al. 2025
)
extended beyond basic precision/recall metrics to measure reference relevance.
Some approaches also address the improvement of structural quality. Their approaches have advanced from Tree Edit Distance (e.g., HiCaD
(
Zhu et al. 2023
)
) to hybrid methods combining semantic overlap with LLM-based logical assessment
(
Lai et al. 2024
;
Bao et al. 2025
;
Su et al. 2026
)
. However, the lack of standardized protocols across different systems makes systematic comparisons challenging. As for ASG-specific benchmarks, early efforts were limited to being small-scale and CS-centric (e.g., LLMxMapReduce-v2
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
and SurveyForge
(
Yan et al. 2025
)
). Subsequently, SurGE
(
Su et al. 2026
)
and SurveyBench
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
introduced quality control via rigorous data filtering, while SurveyGen
(
Bao et al. 2025
)
, SurveyEval
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
, and SGSimEval
(
Guo et al. 2026
)
extended coverage to interdisciplinary fields.

To summarize, early assessments prioritized grounding but remained small-scale and CS-focused. While recent benchmarks improved coverage and scalability, their reliance on LLM-based judgments overlooks critical structural and semantic features. Furthermore, most existing metrics apply generic criteria, ignoring discipline-specific survey styles, such as evidence hierarchy in medicine versus chronological flow in history. These limitations necessitate a unified multi-disciplinary benchmark with discipline-aware evaluation criteria, which this study aims to address.

3.2.
Structured Survey Representation Generation

For generated surveys and the human-written surveys, MinerU
(
Wang et al. 2024c
)
is used to process the PDFs into structured Markdown format. To ensure the construction of a high-quality ground truth dataset, we implement a multi-stage data cleaning and verification framework consisting of three sequential steps.

Step 1: Rule-based Filtering.
In the initial stage, we apply heuristic rules to establish a preliminary document structure. Specifically, we identify a rough section hierarchy based on font size metadata provided by MinerU. Textual content is then mapped to the corresponding section headings, during which we filter out anomalies by discarding sections with extreme length deviations. For reference parsing, we design rule-based heuristics to handle the significant variability in citation formats across disciplines. All textual data undergoes a cleaning process to remove redundant whitespace, extraneous line breaks, and special characters.

Step 2: LLM-assisted Refinement.
Building upon the rule-based output, we leverage Large Language Models to refine the extracted information. For structural organization, an LLM-based alignment process standardizes the nesting levels of the preliminary section hierarchy. For references, the LLM complements heuristic rules in a hybrid parsing pipeline, enabling accurate extraction of reference titles even when formats deviate from common conventions.

Step 3: Human Verification.
In the final stage, human annotators manually verify the processed data to establish definitive ground truth. This includes validating the document outline structure and ensuring the accuracy of extracted references. This verification step guarantees the reliability and quality of the resulting dataset.

Following extraction, we represent each survey paper as a
Structured Survey Representation
(SSR), formally defined as a triplet:

(1)

\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{O},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})

where
\mathcal{O}
denotes the hierarchical
outline
(i.e., nested section titles),
\mathcal{C}
represents the
content
(including textual paragraphs, figures, tables, formulas, and in-text citations), and
\mathcal{R}
is the
reference list
(containing bibliographic metadata such as titles and publication venues). This decomposition facilitates fine-grained, multi-dimensional evaluation of generated surveys.

3.3.
Discipline-aware Survey Evaluation

Evaluating automatically generated surveys requires assessing whether the writing follows disciplinary conventions; in light of this, we propose a discipline-aware evaluation framework centered on
discipline-aware rubrics evaluation
. This component employs discipline-specific rubrics derived from expert surveys, using aspect weights learned from human preferences. The framework is applied across all three parts of our Structured Survey Representation
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{O},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
. To complement this reference-free assessment, we further incorporate
Canonical Alignment Evaluation
that measures semantic correspondence with human-authored surveys, providing auxiliary verification of content coverage and factual grounding.

preference-aligned weights

Discipline-Aware Rubric Generation
While existing evaluation methods have established criteria for assessing the outline, content, and references of survey papers, they are generally designed to be universal across all disciplines. Consequently, these frameworks overlook the distinct stylistic nuances and evaluation standards unique to specific research fields. To address this, we synthesize aspects and their descriptions from current evaluation methods, then leverage high-quality, human-written survey papers in SurveyLens-1k to calibrate them into discipline-aware aspects and further generate concrete, field-adapted scoring rubrics.

Specifically, for each component
c\in\{\mathcal{O},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R}\}
, we define aspects
\mathcal{A}_{c}
and construct the discipline-aware assessment rubrics via a two-stage process:

(2)

a\in\mathcal{A}_{c}\;\xrightarrow[\text{per survey}]{\text{expand}}\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{d,c,a}\;\xrightarrow[\text{per discipline}]{\text{merge}}\mathcal{K}_{d,c,a},

where
\mathcal{A}_{c}
is the set of aspects synthesized from existing assessments, as shown in Table
2
; the expand stage instantiates each aspect for human-written surveys in discipline
d
, while the merge stage consolidates items to extract shared disciplinary assessment aspects. Prompts used to generate the discipline-aware rubrics are shown in Appendix
6
.

Preference-Aligned Weighting
After we got the discipline-aware survey assessment rubrics for each research discipline, a remaining challenge is how to aggregate per-aspect scores into a final quality measure. Simplistic equal weighting implicitly assumes that all aspects contribute uniformly to quality, failing to reflect the nuanced reality of academic survey assessment. Instead, we employ the Bradley-Terry model to learn preference-aligned weights from expert judgment that better capture the diverse evaluation standards across different disciplines. Since obtaining large-scale human preference labels is cost-prohibitive, we utilize a strong LLM (
i.e.
, Gemini-3 Pro) as a proxy for expert judgment. Specifically, we conduct pairwise comparisons on 100 human-written surveys across 10 disciplines. To mitigate position bias
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, we perform double round-robin comparisons with randomized position swapping. Formally, given normalized aspect scores
\bar{x}_{i,k}=x_{i,k}/x_{\max}\in[0,1]
for survey
s_{i}
and the resulting set of pairwise preferences
\mathcal{P}={(s_{i},s_{j}):s_{i}\succ s_{j}}
, we optimize the weights to align with these expert-simulated judgments.

We estimate weights
\mathbf{w}
by maximizing:

(3)

\hat{\mathbf{w}}=\arg\max_{\mathbf{w}\geq\mathbf{0}}\sum_{(s_{i},s_{j})\in\mathcal{P}}\log\sigma\bigl(\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\bigr)-\alpha\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function. After optimization, weights are normalized to
\tilde{w}_{k}=\hat{w}_{k}/\sum_{j}\hat{w}_{j}
so that
\sum_{k}\tilde{w}_{k}=1
. For each component
c\in\{\mathcal{O},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R}\}
, the final compliance score
\mathcal{S}^{\textsc{rub}}_{f,c}(s)=5\cdot\sum_{k}\tilde{w}_{c,k}\cdot\bar{x}_{c,k}\in[1,5]
reflects weighted adherence to expert-preferred quality criteria for that component.

In practice, the per-aspect scores
x_{i,k}
are obtained via LLM-based evaluation. Given the field-specific rubric
\mathcal{K}_{f,c,a}
and the corresponding survey component, we prompt an LLM judge to assess compliance on a 5-point scale (
x\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
). To ensure reliability, we adopt a structured output format requiring the judge to provide a rationale before assigning each score. Detailed scoring example could be found in Appendix
C
.

3.3.2.
Canonical Alignment Evaluation (c2 in Figure
3
)

As a complementary to the reference-free assessment, we also introduce alignment assessment metrics that measure semantic correspondence and content coverage between generated surveys and human-written survey papers.

Conventional reference-based metrics such as ROUGE score and embedding-based F1 have two limitations: (1) they rely on many-to-many matching that ignores redundancy in generated content, and (2) they apply uniform thresholds that do not account for component-specific similarity distributions
(
Bao et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2025
)
. To overcome these issues, we propose Redundancy-Aware Matching Score (RAMS) and thresholded Average Maximum Similarity (TAMS), applied across all three components of our Structured Survey Representation.

Instead of taking the full component as the input, we split the components into entries for fine-grained comparison. For each component-topic pair
(c,t)
, we define the generated entry set
\mathcal{S}_{c,t}
and ground-truth entry set
\mathcal{G}_{c,t}
as:

(4)

\mathcal{S}_{c,t}=\{s_{c,t}^{(1)},s_{c,t}^{(2)},\dots,s_{c,t}^{(m_{c,t})}\},\quad\mathcal{G}_{c,t}=\{g_{c,t}^{(1)},g_{c,t}^{(2)},\dots,g_{c,t}^{(n_{c,t})}\},

where
m_{c,t}
and
n_{c,t}
denote the number of entries for the generated and ground-truth human-written surveys in SurveyLens-1k, respectively. Table
3
summarizes the entry types for each component.

Redundancy-Aware Matching Score (RAMS)
measures the
overall coverage and faithfulness
while strictly penalizing repetition. Unlike standard F1 scores which allow a single ground truth entry to validate multiple generated entries (many-to-many matching), RAMS enforces a strict
one-to-one mapping
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the optimal set of one-to-one matched pairs
(s,g)
between
\mathcal{S}_{c,t}
and
\mathcal{G}_{c,t}
determined by the Hungarian algorithm. To penalize repetitive hallucinations, we introduce a redundancy weight
\omega(s)
for each generated entry based on its maximum internal similarity to other generated entries. The Precision (
P_{\text{RAMS}}
) and Recall (
R_{\text{RAMS}}
) are formulated as:

(5)

\displaystyle P_{\text{RAMS}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{c,t}|}\sum_{(s,g)\in\mathcal{M}}\omega(s)\cdot\mathbb{I}[\sigma(s,g)\geq\tau_{c}],

\displaystyle R_{\text{RAMS}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{c,t}|}\sum_{(s,g)\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{I}[\sigma(s,g)\geq\tau_{c}],

where
\omega(s)=\exp\bigl(-\lambda\cdot\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{c,t}\setminus\{s\}}\sigma(s,s^{\prime})\bigr)
is the redundancy penalty, and
\mathbb{I}[\cdot]
is the indicator function. The final RAMS is the harmonic mean of
P_{\text{RAMS}}
and
R_{\text{RAMS}}
. Detailed implementation is provided in Appendix
B
.

Thresholded Average Maximum Similarity (TAMS)
measures the
best semantic match
between the generated survey papers and the human-authored survey papers. Inspired by recall-oriented metrics in information retrieval, TAMS is calculated as:

(6)

\mathrm{TAMS}_{c}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{c}|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{c}}\max\!\Bigl\{0,\;\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{c}}\sigma(s,g)-\tau_{c}\Bigr\}.

Unlike standard precision metrics that utilize a binary hit-or-miss logic (i.e.,
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum\mathbb{I}[\max\sigma>\tau]
), TAMS quantifies the
degree
of semantic alignment. By incorporating the similarity margin
\sigma(s,g)-\tau_{c}
, TAMS rewards high-fidelity reproductions while discounting weak matches that barely surpass the relevance threshold. This continuous scoring mechanism provides a more granular assessment of content quality, effectively distinguishing between deep semantic understanding and superficial topical overlap.

4.2.1.
Discipline-Aware Rubric Evaluation Result

Based on discipline-aware rubrics, we calculate scores for each ASG method at the discipline level, component level (outline, content, reference), and fine-grained aspect level, respectively.

Finding 1:

Deep Research Agents consistently achieve the highest performance across all disciplines, while Vanilla LLMs rank the lowest.

As shown in Table
4
, Deep Research Agents consistently exceed the discipline-wise average across all ten fields, while vanilla LLMs consistently underperform. Notably, Gemini Deep Research dominates in 8 out of 10 disciplines, achieving the highest scores. Among ASG systems, SurveyForge demonstrates competitive performance, securing second-best results in multiple domains (Engineering: 3.74, Medicine: 3.84). The performance gap between vanilla LLMs and specialized systems is substantial: The average ASG system score (3.02) exceeds vanilla LLMs (2.43) by 0.59 points, while Deep Research Agents (3.41) outperform vanilla LLMs by 0.98 points. These results suggest that while task-specific architectural design remains valuable, the combination of strong base models with advanced reasoning capabilities may be more critical for survey generation.

Finding 2:

Data source quality is a decisive factor; systems using curated academic databases significantly outperform those relying on other sources.

Within ASG systems, we observe substantial performance variance correlated with data source quality. AutoSurvey, SurveyForge, and LLMxMapReduce-V2 consistently outperform their counterparts, while InteractiveSurvey and SurveyX show moderate performance, and SciSage and AutoSurvey2 lag behind. Detailed analysis reveals a pattern: top-performing systems leverage curated academic databases (e.g., research paper database in
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
) with rigorous filtering mechanisms (e.g, Scholar Navigation Agent in
(
Yan et al. 2025
)
), whereas underperforming systems rely on noisier web-crawled data or less selective retrieval pipelines. This finding underscores that for knowledge-intensive tasks like survey generation, the purity and reliability of source documents may matter as much as sophisticated generation architectures.

Finding 3:

Academic ASG systems excel at structural organization while Deep Research Agents dominate content synthesis, revealing a fundamental skeleton-versus-flesh trade-off.

As shown in Figure
4
and Figure
5
, component-level analysis reveals a pronounced trade-off between structural rigor and content richness. Academic ASG systems excel at outline generation: SurveyForge achieves outline scores approaching 4.0, surpassing even Qwen Deep Research. In contrast, Deep Research Agents demonstrate superior content synthesis, with content scores exceeding 4.0, while their outline scores remain comparatively modest. This divergence reflects fundamental architectural differences. ASG systems typically employ specialized planning mechanisms with heuristics optimized for academic hierarchical structures, producing outlines that closely adhere to scholarly conventions. Conversely, Deep Research Agents leverage extensive pretrained knowledge and extended context windows to generate fluent, information-dense prose, but lack domain-specific structural constraints, often producing generic, blog-like organizations. Notably, reference quality remains the weakest component across nearly all systems, indicating that citation generation persists as a shared challenge requiring further research attention.

Finding 4:

Different ASG paradigms exhibit distinct cross-disciplinary performance patterns: ASG systems favor STEM fields, vanilla LLMs perform relatively better in humanities and social sciences, while Deep Research Agents achieve balanced performance across all disciplines.

Our analysis reveals systematic performance contrasts.
ASG systems
(e.g., AutoSurvey, SurveyForge) show a strong STEM bias, scoring highest in CS (3.64) and Engineering (3.61) but struggling in social sciences like Psychology (2.99). This likely stems from arXiv-heavy corpus and rigid hierarchical structures ill-suited for theoretical frameworks. Conversely,
vanilla LLMs
like Qwen-Max perform better in Education (2.67) and Sociology (2.58) than in STEM fields, as their fluent, descriptive outputs lack technical density. Finally,
Deep Research Agents
(e.g., Gemini Deep Research) achieve remarkable stability across all fields (3.43–3.95), thanks to dynamic agentic workflows that adapt to domain-specific structures and combine fluency with rigorous retrieval.

5.
Conclusions and Future Work

We introduce SurveyLens, the first discipline-aware benchmark for Automatic Survey Generation (ASG), anchored by SurveyLens-1k, a dataset comprising 1,000 high-quality human-written surveys across 10 disciplines. Then, we establish a holistic evaluation framework with discipline-aware assessment rubrics with canonical alignment metrics to rigorously assess adherence to specific disciplinary norms. Benchmarking 11 state-of-the-art ASG methods reveals critical performance disparities, offering essential guidance for selecting tools tailored to specific academic requirements.

Our future work will investigate leveraging these discipline-aware metrics as optimization objectives for model alignment, enabling ASG systems to dynamically adapt to distinct disciplinary norms.

Algorithm Details

Algorithm 1

Calculation of RAMS

1:

Input:
Generated entries
\{s^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, ground-truth entries
\{g^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, threshold
\tau
, penalty
\lambda

2:

Output:

\mathrm{RAMS}\in[0,1]

3:

Compute similarity matrix:
\mathbf{S}[i,j]\leftarrow\max(0,\sigma(s^{(i)},g^{(j)})-\tau)

4:

Find optimal matching:
\pi^{*}\leftarrow\textsc{Hungarian}(\mathbf{S})

5:

Compute redundancy weights:

6:

w_{i}\leftarrow\exp\!\Big(-\lambda\cdot\max_{k\neq i}\sigma(s^{(i)},s^{(k)})\Big)

7:

\mathrm{P}\leftarrow\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(i,j)\in\pi^{*}}w_{i}\cdot\mathbb{I}[\sigma(s^{(i)},g^{(j)})\geq\tau]

8:

\mathrm{R}\leftarrow\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(i,j)\in\pi^{*}}\mathbb{I}[\sigma(s^{(i)},g^{(j)})\geq\tau]

9:

return

\mathrm{RAMS}\leftarrow 2\mathrm{PR}/(\mathrm{P}+\mathrm{R})

Appendix D
Construction Details of SurveyLens-1k

To ensure a robust and fair evaluation across diverse academic fields, we constructed
SurveyLens-1k
. This dataset comprises
1,000 high-quality survey papers
, evenly distributed across
10 disciplines
(100 papers per field). This section details our data collection, filtering, and verification pipeline.

We used the Semantic Scholar (S2) API
2
2

2

https://api.semanticscholar.org
to obtain a comprehensive collection of survey papers published and corresponding metadata between 2020 and 2025. To guarantee domain diversity, we targeted ten representative disciplines: Biology, Business, Computer Science, Education, Engineering, Environmental Science, Medicine, Physics, Psychology, and Sociology.

To balance efficiency and accuracy, we restrict API queries to the "Review" document type for each discipline. Subsequently, we employ a hybrid filtering strategy to identify genuine surveys: papers with standard keywords in their titles (e.g., "survey," "review") are automatically accepted, while ambiguous entries undergo LLM-based binary classification using their titles and abstracts.

Following the initial retrieval and filtering process, we obtained an Initial Survey Pool consisting of approximately 3,000 papers. To ensure the inclusion of high-quality and influential works, we employed a citation-based selection strategy. Specifically, we ranked the papers within each discipline in descending order based on their citation counts and influential citation metrics. From this ranked list, we selected the top 110 papers per discipline. This procedure resulted in a refined set of Candidate Surveys containing roughly 1,100 papers, which serves as the foundation for our subsequent analysis.

To ensure the integrity and accessibility of the dataset, we implemented a rigorous two-step verification process on the candidate surveys. First, we employed an LLM-based verification to definitively validate the paper type, filtering out any remaining non-survey articles that passed the initial screening. Subsequently, we conducted a human verification phase focused on full-text acquisition. In this step, we manually inspected the candidates to ensure PDF accessibility, discarding entries with broken links, paywalls that could not be bypassed, or corrupted files. This process yielded a finalized collection of 1,000 high-quality survey papers, constituting the SurveyLens-1k Dataset. Through this process, we obtain a finalized dataset of 1,000 high-quality survey papers.

Future research directions

Conclusion

Critical analysis of the field’s strengths and weaknesses

Identification of research gaps

Conclusion summarizing key findings

References section with all cited sources
