A 140 line MATLAB code for topology optimization problems with probabilistic parameters
Andrian Uihlein, Ole Sigmund, Michael Stingl

Abstract
We present an efficient 140 line MATLAB code for topology optimization problems that include probabilistic parameters. It is built from the top99neo code by Ferrari and Sigmund and incorporates a stochastic sample-based approach. Old gradient samples are adaptively recombined during the optimization process to obtain a gradient approximation with vanishing approximation error. The method’s performance is thoroughly analyzed for several numerical examples. While we focus on applications in which stochastic parameters describe local material failure, we also present extensions of the code to other settings, such as uncertain load positions or dynamic forces of unknown frequency. The complete code is included in the Appendix and can be downloaded from www.topopt.dtu.dk.

Introduction

Topology optimization is a highly active field of research in optimization and engineering alike. Thus, it is hardly surprising that an overwhelming amount of free as well as commercial software for efficient topology optimization is available, e.g.,
Wang et al. (2003)
;
Andreassen and
Andreasen (2014)
;
Talischi et al. (2012)
;
Suresh (2010)
;
Challis (2009)
;
Xia and Breitkopf (2015)
;
Liu et al. (2005)
;
Sanders et al. (2018)
;
Allaire and Pantz (2006)
;
Sokół (2010)
;
Amir et al. (2013)
;
Liu and Tovar (2014)
. The field was given an extensive review in
Wang et al. (2021)
. The most popular implementations for linear elasticity in MATLAB are the famous
top99

Sigmund (2001)
and its descendants
top88

Andreassen et al. (2010)
and
top99neo

Ferrari and Sigmund (2020)
, which we build upon in this contribution.

With such a repertoire of strong numerical tools, the complexity of optimization tasks has grown rapidly over time. In recent years, more and more focus has been placed on topology optimization under uncertainty, i.e., optimization problems in which some of the involved parameters are unknown and need to be modeled probabilistically. Such problems arise naturally in many applications, for example when the forces applied to the structure are unknown
Liu and Gea (2018)
;
Nishioka and Kanno (2023)
;
Maute (2014)
. Likewise, stochastic models can be used to describe local material impurities
De et al. (2022)
;
Long et al. (2018)
;
Greifenstein and
Stingl (2020)
;
Jansen et al. (2013)
;
Zhou and Fleury (2016)
or geometry imperfections
De et al. (2020)
;
Krüger and Kriegesmann (2024)
;
Komini et al. (2023)
;
Schevenels et al. (2011)
of the structure, as they may appear in the context of additive manufacturing.

In the literature, many different schemes have been proposed to deal with model uncertainties of different kinds. For example, a natural approach is to perform a worst-case optimization. Here, the main challenge is to design schemes that efficiently find (or approximate) this worst-case, e.g.,
Brittain et al. (2011)
;
Gao and Liu (2021)
;
Kharmanda et al. (2004)
;
Lombardi and Haftka (1998)
;
Zhou and Fleury (2016)
. Alternatively, it is common to consider a combination of average and standard deviation over all possibilities, since there exist powerful tools from statistics for this setting
Gao et al. (2022)
;
Krüger and Kriegesmann (2024)
;
Tootkaboni et al. (2012)
;
Schevenels et al. (2011)
. Lastly, there are several fully stochastic optimization approaches, in which stochasticity is dealt with by different sampling techniques
De et al. (2020)
;
De et al. (2021)
;
De et al. (2022)
;
Campi and Garatti (2010)
. Note that it is also possible to use stochastic optimization schemes to solve deterministic problems, as it is often observed that noisy gradient steps can help the optimizer escape poor local minima in the objective function
Kleinberg
et al. (2018)
. In this work, however, we focus on optimization problems in which stochasticity is part of the model itself and not (artificially) added by the optimization algorithm.

The method proposed in this contribution follows this sample-based philosophy and is closely related to the famous stochastic gradient scheme
Robbins and Monro (1951)
. To be precise, we adapt the techniques proposed in
Pflug et al. (2020)
. It is important to note that this method stores sampled gradient values to adaptively recombine old and new information for the current gradient approximation. It can be shown that the associated approximation error vanishes over the course of iterations
Grieshammer et al. (2023)
, resulting in an optimization scheme that aims to minimize the number of times we need to solve the system state equation during the optimization process.

Our goal is to provide an easy-to-use educational tool for students and newcomers to the field. For maximum accessibility, the implementation is designed and presented as an add-on to the original 88/99 line codes. Therefore, large parts of the code are identical to
top99neo
and we use similar notation as in
Andreassen et al. (2010)
;
Sigmund (2001)
;
Ferrari and Sigmund (2020)
. Ideally, users which are familiar with these codes will be able to use
topS140
without difficulty.

2
Problem formulation

We consider a two-dimensional rectangular design domain
\Omega
and associated discretization
\Omega_{h}
. Specifically,
\Omega_{h}
consists of
m
square finite elements
\Omega_{e}
,
{e=1,\ldots,m}
. We denote by
{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{e}\in[0,1]}
the physical material density on
\Omega_{e}
and collect all information in the physical design vector
{\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in[0,1]^{m}}
. To be precise,
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
is obtained from the pseudo-density vector
{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{m}}
(design vector) by a two-step procedure: First, given a filter radius
{r_{\text{min}}>0}
, we apply the linear filter

\tilde{x}_{e}:=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}H_{e,i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{e,i}},

(1)

where

H_{e,i}:=\max\big\{0,r_{\text{min}}-\dist(\Omega_{i},\Omega_{e})\big\}.

Afterwards, we project each element via a relaxed Heavyside function (see
Wang et al. (2010)
)

\mathcal{H}(\tilde{x}_{e},\eta,\beta):=\frac{\tanh(\beta\eta)+\tanh\big(\beta(\tilde{x}_{e}-\eta)\big)}{\tanh(\beta\eta)+\tanh\big(\beta(1-\eta)\big)}.

(2)

Lastly, we define
{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{P}_{1}\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}}
as disjoint sets of active design variables, passive void elements (
x_{e}=0
) and passive solid elements (
x_{e}=1
), respectively.

Based on the entries of
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
, the stiffness matrix
\mathbf{K}
is constructed using a SIMP interpolation
Bendsøe and Sigmund (1999)

E(\hat{x}_{e}):=E_{\text{min}}+\hat{x}_{e}^{p}(E_{0}-E_{\text{min}}),

(3)

where
E_{\text{min}}
and
E_{0}
are the Young’s moduli of void and solid. Furthermore, we allow the stiffness matrix to depend on a random parameter
{\xi\in\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Xi}}}
, which is distributed according to a probability measure
\mu_{\xi}
. For example, in
Section

5.1
, we model local material failure by punching a hole in the design, i.e., locally reducing the value of
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
at a small subdomain. In this setup, we may use
{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
to describe the (random) position of this damage region and
\mu_{\xi}
to model the probability of the damage being located at a specific point, e.g., a uniform distribution
{\mu_{\xi}\equiv|\Xi|^{-1}}
if each possible position has the same probability.

Likewise, we allow the load vector
\mathbf{F}
to depend on a second random parameter
{\psi\in\Psi\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\Psi}}}
with probability distribution
\mu_{\psi}
, which may be used to model unknown positions, directions or magnitudes of the applied forces.

The corresponding displacement
\mathbf{U}_{\xi,\psi}
is obtained by solving the state equation

\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi)\mathbf{U}_{\xi,\psi}=\mathbf{F}(\psi).

(4)

Lastly, we define the physical volume

V(\hat{\mathbf{x}}):=\sum_{e=1}^{m}|\Omega_{e}|\hat{x}_{e},

compliance

c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi):=\mathbf{F}(\psi)^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{\xi,\psi},

and expected compliance

\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\psi}\left[c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)\right]:=\int_{\Xi\times\Psi}\!c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)\,\mathrm{d}(\mu_{\xi}\times\mu_{\psi})(\xi,\psi).

Here, we use
{\,\mathrm{d}(\mu_{\xi}\times\mu_{\psi})}
to denote the product measure of the individual probability distributions. For example, if
\mu_{\xi}
and
\mu_{\psi}
are independent and have probability densities
\rho_{\xi}
and
\rho_{\psi}
, the expected compliance can be calculated as

\int_{\Xi}\int_{\Psi}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)\rho_{\xi}(\xi)\rho_{\psi}(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Now, given a volume fraction
{f\in(0,1)}
, we consider the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{A}}\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{A}|}}\quad&\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\psi}\left[c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)\right],\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&V(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\leq f|\Omega_{h}|.\end{split}

(5)

3
Method

3.1
Optimality criterion method

It is straightforward to calculate that the gradients of compliance and volume constraint are given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)

\displaystyle=-\big(\mathbf{U}_{\xi,\psi}^{\top}\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}\mathbf{K}(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)\mathbf{U}_{\xi,\psi}\big)\odot\chi_{\mathcal{A}},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}V(\hat{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\chi_{\mathcal{A}},

where
\odot
indicates element-wise multiplication and
{\chi_{\mathcal{A}}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}
is the indicator vector

(\chi_{\mathcal{A}})_{e}=\begin{cases}1,&e\in\mathcal{A},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus, we have

\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\beta\frac{1-\tanh\big(\beta(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\eta)\big)^{2}}{\tanh(\beta\eta)+\tanh\big(\beta(1-\eta)\big)}

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\odot\big(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)\big),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}V(\hat{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\mathcal{H}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\odot\big(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}V(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\big).

Due to the regularity of all involved functions, the full objective function gradient can be calculated as

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\psi}\left[c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\psi}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)\right].

(6)

Assuming that this gradient information is available in each iteration, problem (
5
) can be solved using the optimality criterion method (OCM,
Bendsøe (1995)
;
Sigmund (2001)
). For this purpose, in iteration
{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, define

\mathcal{F}_{k,e}:=x_{k,e}\sqrt{-\frac{\partial_{e}\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\psi}\left[c(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\xi,\psi)\right]}{\tilde{\lambda}_{k}\partial_{e}V(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k})}}.

(7)

Here,
\tilde{\lambda}_{k}
is an approximation to the true Lagrange multiplier
\lambda_{k}^{\ast}
of the volume constraint and is obtained by a bisection subroutine for the equation

V\big(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}(\tilde{\lambda})\big)-f|\Omega_{h}|=0,

where
{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}(\tilde{\lambda})}
is calculated via the element-wise design update given by

x_{k+1,e}=\min\big\{1,\max\{0,\mathcal{F}_{k,e}\}\big\}.

(8)

For improved stability of the method, it is common to define a move limit
\Delta_{\text{move}}\in(0,1)
and replace (
8
) with

x_{k+1,e}=\min\big\{\delta_{+},\max\{\delta_{-},\mathcal{F}_{k,e}\}\big\},

where

\displaystyle\delta_{+}

\displaystyle:=\min\{1,x_{k,e}+\Delta_{\text{move}}\},

\displaystyle\delta_{-}

\displaystyle:=\max\{0,x_{k,e}-\Delta_{\text{move}}\}.

3.2
Stochastic approximations

Note that the OCM update
\mathcal{F}_{k}
in (
7
) requires calculation of
{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\psi}\left[c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)\right]}
. In practice, this step is exceptionally time consuming, as evaluating the integral over
{\Xi\times\Psi}
requires us to solve the state equation (
4
) for a large number of random parameters
{(\xi,\psi)}
.

In order to reduce this computational effort, we approximate the true gradient using the stochastic sample-based approach proposed in
Pflug et al. (2020)
;
Grieshammer et al. (2023)
. For this purpose, let
{(\xi_{k},\psi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\Xi\times\Psi}
be random samples, which we assume to be independent and identically distributed according to
{\mu_{\xi}\times\mu_{\psi}}
. Then, in each iteration, we only calculate one gradient sample

g_{k}:=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\xi_{k},\psi_{k}).

In contrast to other stochastic approximation techniques, these samples are not discarded after the iteration. Instead, all available gradient samples are combined to form an approximation
G_{k}
to the current gradient

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\psi}\left[c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)\right]\approx G_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}g_{i}.

The coefficients
{\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
, called
integration weights
, can be efficiently calculated on-the-fly, as they are based on a constant nearest neighbor surrogate model. Due to the continuity of
{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\xi,\psi)}
, we know that the difference in gradient values

\big\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\xi_{1},\psi_{1})-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\xi_{2},\psi_{2})\big\|

is small, as long as the distance between
(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\xi_{1},\psi_{1})
and
(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\xi_{2},\psi_{2})
is small. In order to quantify the distance between two sample points, we choose a norm
{\|\cdot\|_{\ast}}
on the product space
{[0,1]^{m}\times\Xi\times\Psi}
. Now, unknown gradient values at the current iterate are simply approximated by the closest sample in memory:

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\xi,\psi)\approx g_{j_{k,\xi,\psi}},

where

j_{k,\xi,\psi}\in\argmin_{j=1,\ldots,k}\big\|(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\xi,\psi)-(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},\xi_{j},\psi_{j})\big\|_{\ast}

(9)

denotes the index of the closest sample point to
{(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\xi,\psi)}
with respect to
{\|\cdot\|_{\ast}}
. It is important to note that, since all appearing spaces are of finite dimension, all possible choices of
{\|\cdot\|_{\ast}}
are equivalent. However, the practical performance of the method does of course vary. Furthermore, some specific choices simplify the weight calculation process, see, e.g., (
11
).

Since the constructed nearest neighbor model is piecewise constant, the integration yields

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi,\psi}\big[

\displaystyle c(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\xi,\psi)\big]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Xi\times\Psi}\!\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}c(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k},\xi,\psi)\,\mathrm{d}(\mu_{\xi}\times\mu_{\psi})(\xi,\psi)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{\Xi\times\Psi}\!g_{j_{k,\xi,\psi}}\,\mathrm{d}(\mu_{\xi}\times\mu_{\psi})(\xi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{i}\cdot(\mu_{\xi}\times\mu_{\psi})\big[\mathcal{V}_{i,k}\big],

with

\mathcal{V}_{i,k}:=\left\{(\xi,\psi)\in\Xi\times\Psi\,:\,j_{k,\xi,\psi}=i\right\}

denoting the sets on which the gradient in iteration
k
is approximated by sample
i
. Note that, by construction the sets
(\mathcal{V}_{i,k})_{i=1,\ldots,k}
form a partition of
{\Xi\times\Psi}
for all
{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. An illustration is provided in
Figure

and

\mathcal{V}_{i,k}
, i.e.,

\alpha_{i}=(\mu_{\xi}\times\mu_{\psi})\big[\mathcal{V}_{i,k}\big].

Again, assuming that
\mu_{\xi}
and
\mu_{\psi}
are independent and have probability densities
\rho_{\xi}
and
\rho_{\psi}
, this simplifies to

\alpha_{i}=\iint_{\mathcal{V}_{i,k}}\rho_{\xi}(\xi)\rho_{\psi}(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

3.2.1
Sample management

As the method aggregates more and more gradient samples during the optimization process, both the computational cost of calculating the integration weights as well as the memory required to store the samples increases. To counteract this problem, we may limit the maximum number of stored gradient samples beforehand. If this limit is reached and a new sample is collected, we simply remove all information related to the sample point associated with the smallest integration weight, as it can be considered to be the least valuable information.

Consider the famous MBB beam setup, i.e., a rectangular design domain of height

7
Conclusion

This paper presents a simple yet efficient MATLAB code for topology optimization with uncertain parameters. The code is constructed as an optional add-on to the most recent descendant of the famous 88 line code,
top99neo

Ferrari and Sigmund (2020)
. The proposed implementation does not represent cutting-edge research, nor is it intended as hyper-optimized code for high performance computing. It is published to provide an accessible tool for students, newcomers and other non-specialists, that is easy to modify and can be run on basically any hardware. Nonetheless, our experiments show that the method is capable of solving numerically expensive probabilistic optimization problems in short computing time.

We have given detailed motivation and theoretical background for each new step in the code. Additionally, we have provided extensive numerical results concerning several key aspects of the method, like the impact of different random sample sequences or adjustments of the stochastic model in case of symmetry. Moreover, several generalizations have been discussed. Specifically, we have considered tinkering with the sampling strategy, adapting a model reduction and incorporating mini-batch techniques.

MATLAB codes are given in the Appendix and are available at
www.topopt.dtu.dk
as well as
Uihlein et al. (2025)
. They use the
stenglib
package for fast sparse matrix operations, which can be downloaded from
https://github.com/stefanengblom/stenglib
.
