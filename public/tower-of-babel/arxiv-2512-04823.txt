Constrained Control of PDE Traffic Flow via Spatial Control Barrier Functions
Brian Block, Stephanie Stockar

Abstract
In this paper, a constrained control approach to variable speed limit (VSL) control for macroscopic partial differential equation (PDE) traffic models is developed. Control Lyapunov function (CLF) theory for ordinary differential equations (ODEs) is extended to account for spatially and temporally varying states and control inputs. The stabilizing CLF is then unified with safety constraints through the introduction of spatially varying control barrier functions (sCBFs). These methods are applied to in-domain VSL control of the Lighthill–Whitham–Richards (LWR) model to regulate traffic density to a desired profile while ensuring the density remains below prescribed limits enforced by the sCBF. Results show that incorporating constrained control minimally affects the stabilizing control input while successfully maintaining the density within the defined safe set.



I
INTRODUCTION

In traffic systems management, reducing congestion and regulating the system toward a desired traffic density profile has been the focus of numerous control strategies in literature
[
6
,
23
,
24
]
. However, it is equally critical to ensure that the traffic state remains within safe or acceptable bounds
[
20
,
17
]
. This includes preventing density from exceeding a prescribed maximum and avoiding excessively high or low velocities. Accordingly, constraint handling must be incorporated directly into traffic control strategies.

Traffic is generally modeled via partial differential equations (PDE) that describe the density and flow of vehicles through the system
[
20
]
. For in-domain control of PDE-based traffic models, variable speed limit (VSL) control is frequently used to modify the speed limit and, therefore, influence vehicle flow
[
8
,
15
,
11
,
6
,
7
]
. In many approaches, the PDE is discretized into a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs), and the controller is designed on the discrete model. For example,
[
8
]
develops a discrete feedback controller to mitigate bottlenecks, while
[
15
]
formulates a model predictive controller to reduce congestion. Alternatively, the controller can be designed directly in the PDE domain
[
11
,
6
,
7
]
. In
[
11
]
, a feedback law is constructed to regulate the LWR model to a desired density profile, and
[
6
]
develops an optimal controller based on a linear quadratic regulator to achieve the same objective. This approach is further used in
[
7
]
to generate an implementable discretized rule-based controller. However, these methods primarily focus on regulation of the density to a desired profile and do not explicitly enforce state constraints or guarantee that the system remains within a prescribed operating range.

The requirement to keep a system within a designated set is typically formalized through the forward invariance of that set
[
4
,
5
,
3
]
, which is achieved through the use of control barrier functions (CBFs). The use of CBFs for safety-critical control emerged from their unification with stabilizing control Lyapunov function (CLF) theory
[
21
,
3
]
. For systems described by ODEs, particularly in automotive control applications, CBFs have been applied to ensure safe operation of adaptive cruise controllers
[
4
,
5
,
22
]
. In
[
4
]
, CLFs and CBFs are unified under a quadratic programming (QP) optimization problem that enforces both desired vehicle speed and force at the wheel constraints. This methodology is extended in
[
5
]
to incorporate zeroing and reciprocal barrier functions, and subsequently applied to adaptive cruise control and lane-keeping. Higher-order CBFs are used in
[
22
]
to address similar cruise control problems. An event-triggered control strategy is proposed in
[
19
]
to address feasibility issues in safe connected and automated vehicle (CAV) control.

While the application of barrier functions to ODE systems is well established, their use in PDE systems remains underexplored. Barrier functionals have been used, for example, in
[
1
,
2
]
to estimate outputs of PDE systems. However, the application focused on verification of constraint satisfaction rather than control design. In
[
16
]
, the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation is discretized and a CBF-based controller is applied to the resulting ODE approximation rather than directly to the PDE. A boundary feedback controller is developed in
[
10
]
to guarantee a lower bound on the density of a compressible flow, though the combined use of CLFs and CBFs was not investigated. In
[
12
,
13
]
, the boundary control of a one-dimensional Stefan PDE for the melting of a material was obtained using CBFs and event-triggered control. This approach, though, used a finite-dimensional approximation of the continuous control input.

This paper presents a unified control framework that introduces spatially and temporally varying CBFs for in-domain control of PDEs and provides a novel formulation enabling safety-critical constraint enforcement directly at the PDE level. Furthermore, a CLF-based controller is unified with the proposed CBF-based controller within a quadratic programming framework to simultaneously achieve regulation to a desired profile and enforcement of state constraints in infinite-dimensional systems.

The paper is structured as follows. First, in
Section

II
the traffic PDE is described and the control design for VSLs is introduced. Then in
Section

III
, CLF theory is extended for use in PDE systems and in
Section

IV
CBF theory is extended to a spatially varying control input and combined into a quadratic programming problem. Finally, in
Section

V
the developed controllers are validated on a case study.

II
MODEL DESCRIPTION

II-A

Lighthill-Whitham-Richards Model

The LWR model
[
14
,
18
]
is a first-order, hyperbolic conservation equation given by

\frac{\partial\rho(z,t)}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial q(\rho(z,t))}{\partial z}=0

(1)

where
\rho(z,t)
is the traffic density, representing the number of vehicles occupying a road length,
q(z,t)
is the traffic flow rate, and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the space and time variables, respectively. The closure of the problem is achieved through the adoption of a fundamental flow diagram that relates density with flow. The fundamental diagram used in this paper is the triangular model
[
9
]
, shown in
Fig.

1
and described by

q(\rho(z,t))=\begin{cases}V_{\mathrm{max}}\rho(z,t),&\forall\rho(z,t)\leq\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\\
w(\rho_{\mathrm{max}}+\rho(z,t)),&\forall\rho(z,t)>\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\end{cases}

(2)

where
V_{\mathrm{max}}
is the maximum velocity,
w
is the congested speed,
\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}
is the critical density, and
\rho_{\mathrm{max}}
is the maximum density of the road. The congested speed
w
is given as

w=\frac{V_{\mathrm{max}}\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{max}}-\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}}

(3)

Fig. 1
:
Triangular fundamental diagram.

II-B

Variable Speed Limits

The variable speed limit actuation is included in the governing equations by modifying the fundamental diagram in
Eq.

2
as

q(\rho(z,t),b(z,t))=\begin{cases}b(z,t)V_{\mathrm{max}}\rho(z,t),&\forall\rho\leq\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\\
b(z,t)w(\rho_{\mathrm{max}}+\rho(z,t)),&\forall\rho>\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\end{cases}

(4)

where
b(z,t)
is the VSL ratio. A value of
b(z,t)>1
increases the slope of both the free flow and congested regime in
Fig.

1
, while a value of
b(z,t)<1
will decrease it. This, in turn affects the maximum possible flow
q_{\mathrm{max}}
, which occurs at the critical density. For simplicity, for the rest of the paper
(z,t)
is omitted, except where it is needed for emphasis.

II-C

Linearization of LWR Model

The linearized LWR model is derived considering the nominal equilibrium density
\rho_{0}
and nominal maximum velocity ratio
b_{0}

[
6
]
such that the variables can be written as

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}+\Delta\rho

(5)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=b_{0}+\Delta b

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}v_{\mathrm{max}}((\rho_{0}+\Delta\rho)(b_{0}+\Delta b)),&\rho\leq\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\\
w((\rho_{\mathrm{max}}-\rho_{0}-\Delta\rho)(b_{0}+\Delta b)),&\rho>\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\end{cases}

The LWR model
Eq.

1
can then be written in standard, linear hyperbolic PDE form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}(z,t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}\frac{\partial x}{\partial z}(z,t)+\mathcal{M}x(z,t)+Bu(z,t)

(6)

\displaystyle y(z,t)

\displaystyle=Cx(z,t)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-V_{\mathrm{max}}b_{0},&\forall\rho\leq\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\\
wb_{0},&\forall\rho>\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\end{cases}

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-V_{\mathrm{max}}\rho_{0},&\forall\rho\leq\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\\
-w(\rho_{\mathrm{max}}-\rho_{0}),&\forall\rho>\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\end{cases}

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=I

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\Delta\rho,\quad u=\frac{\partial\Delta b}{\partial z}

The control input
u=\frac{\partial\Delta b}{\partial z}
is the change in the variable speed limit over the length of road.

III
STABILIZING CONTROL OF TRAFFIC MODELS

III-A

Control Lyapunov Theory for ODEs

To motivate the description of safety for a system, first it is necessary to discuss the family of stabilizing controllers. Consider the system

\frac{dx}{dt}=f(x)+g(x)u

(8)

where
f(x)
and
g(x)
can be either nonlinear or linear functions of the state
x
. If the goal is to stabilize the system
Eq.

8
to a point
x^{*}=0
or equivalently
x(t)\rightarrow 0
, this is equivalent to finding a feedback control law that drives a positive definite function,
V:D\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, to zero
[
3
]
. That is, if

\exists\,u=k(x)\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\dot{V}(x,k(x))\leq-\gamma(V(x))

(9)

where

\dot{V}(x,k(x))=L_{f}V(x)+L_{g}V(x)k(x)

(10)

then the system
Eq.

8
is stabilizable to
V(x^{*})=0
, meaning
x^{*}=0
. In
Eq.

9
,
\gamma
is a class-
\mathcal{K}
function defined such that
\gamma(0)=0
and that
\gamma
is strictly increasing. The function
V
is known as a Lyapunov function, and it can be found that when
V\leq-\gamma(V)
the system
Eq.

8
is stable under the control law
u=k(x)

[
4
]
.

The above definitions lead to the description of a control Lyapunov function (CLF) which is a function
V
that stabilizes a system without the need to actually construct the feedback controller
u
. All that needs to be done is to find a controller that obeys the inequality in
Eq.

9
. This definition can be written as

\inf_{u\in U}[L_{f}V(x)+L_{g}V(x)]\leq-\gamma(V(x))

(11)

which allows the set of all stabilizing controllers to be defined as

K_{\mathrm{clf}}(x):=\{u\in U:L_{f}V(x)+L_{g}V(x)u\leq-\gamma(V(x))\}

(12)

The constraint defined in
Eq.

12
is affine in the control input
u
meaning that it can be used for the formulation of optimization based controllers.

III-B

Application of CLF Theory to Traffic PDEs

For the LWR model, the state is now a function of both space
z
and time
t
, so
Eq.

8
becomes

\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}(z,t)=f(x(z,t))+g(x(z,t))u

(13)

where

\displaystyle f(x(z,t))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}\frac{\partial x}{\partial z}(z,t)+\mathcal{M}x(z,t)

(14)

\displaystyle g(x(z,t))

\displaystyle=B

An admissible CLF is chosen as the quadratic deviation from a desired profile such as

V(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}(\Delta\rho-\Delta\rho_{\mathrm{des}})^{2}\>dz

(15)

where
\Delta\rho_{\mathrm{des}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the desired density profile. A change of variables is introduced by defining the error system as

e=\Delta\rho-\Delta\rho_{\mathrm{des}}

(16)

meaning

\displaystyle\frac{\partial e}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=f(e)+g(e)u

(17)

\displaystyle f(e)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}\frac{\partial e}{\partial z}(z,t)+\mathcal{M}e(z,t)

\displaystyle g(e)

\displaystyle=B

The CLF defined in
Eq.

15
then becomes

V(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}e^{2}\>dz

(18)

The dynamics of the CLF are defined as

\displaystyle\dot{V}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}e\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}e\>dz

(19)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}e(f(e)+g(e)u)\>dz

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}ef(e)\>dz+\int_{0}^{L}eg(e)u\>dz

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}e\frac{\partial e}{\partial z}\>dz+B\int_{0}^{L}eu\>dz

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{2}\bigg|_{z=0}^{z=L}+B\int_{0}^{L}eu\>dz

Using this definition of a CLF, the following quadratic programming controller is considered

\displaystyle u(z,t)=\argmin_{(u,\delta)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(m+1)}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u^{T}Hu+p\delta^{2}

(20)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle L_{f}V+L_{g}Vu\leq-\gamma(V)+\delta

where
H
is any positive definite matrix,
\delta
is a relaxation variable that ensures
Eq.

20
is solvable, and
p>0
penalizes the use of
\delta
.

IV
SAFE CONTROL OF TRAFFIC MODELS

IV-A

Control Barrier Function Theory for ODEs

Safety, or safe control, of a system can be thought of as enforcing invariance of a set whereby the system does not leave a safe set of operating points, rather than driving a system to a setpoint as in stability
[
4
,
5
,
3
]
. The safe set of interest can be defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\{x\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}:h(x)\geq 0\}

(21)

\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\{x\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}:h(x)=0\}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Int}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\{x\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}:h(x)>0\}

where
\mathcal{C}
is known as the safe set and
h:D\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable function. This leads to the definition of safety based on forward invariance of the set
\mathcal{C}
:
The set
\mathcal{C}
is forward invariant if for every
x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
,
x(t)\in\mathcal{C}
for
x(0)=x_{0}
and all
t\in\mathcal{I}(x_{0})
. The system
Eq.

8
is safe with respect to the set
\mathcal{C}
if the set
\mathcal{C}
is forward invariant

[
3
]
.

The continuously differentiable function
h
is a control barrier function (CBF) if there exists an extended class
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
function
\alpha
such that for
Eq.

8

\sup_{u\in U}[L_{f}h(x)+L_{g}h(x)u]\geq-\alpha(h(x))

(22)

for all
x\in D
. An extended class-
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
function is a function
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that is strictly increasing with
\alpha(0)=0
and is defined on the entire real line
\mathbb{R}=(-\infty,\infty)
.

Thus, the set of all control values that render
\mathcal{C}
safe is

K_{\mathrm{cbf}}=\{u\in U:L_{f}h(x)+L_{g}h(x)u+\alpha(h(x))\geq 0\}

(23)

The main result from
[
3
]
is that if
h
is a control barrier function on
D
and
\frac{\partial h}{\partial z}\neq 0
for all
x\in\partial\mathcal{C}
, then any Lipschitz controller
u(x)\in K_{\mathrm{cbf}}(x)
for
Eq.

8
renders the set
\mathcal{C}
safe and the set
\mathcal{C}
is asymptotically stable in
D
. An additional benefit of this design is robustness to modeling errors. Although such errors might force trajectories to leave the set
\mathcal{C}
, the asymptotic stability of
\mathcal{C}
under any controller in
K_{\mathrm{cbf}}
ensures return to the safe set
\mathcal{C}

[
3
]
.

IV-B

Application of CBF Theory to Traffic PDEs

For PDE control, the safe set
\mathcal{C}
and CBF becomes spatially varying as well as temporally varying as the state is both time and space dependent. The set
\mathcal{C}
is now defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\{x\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}:h(x)\geq 0\}

(24)

\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\{x\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}:h(x)=0\}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Int}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\{x\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}:h(x)>0\}

An admissible spatially varying CBF (sCBF) is considered here

h(x):=\lambda(z,t)-x(z,t)\geq 0

(25)

where
\lambda(z,t)
is a desired upper bound on traffic density. The conditions in
Eq.

23
are incorporated into the optimization problem
Eq.

20
to yield a stabilizing and safe controller as

\displaystyle u(z,t)=\argmin_{(u,\delta)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(m+1)}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u^{T}Hu+p\delta^{2}

(26)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle L_{f}V+L_{g}Vu\leq-\gamma(V)+\delta

\displaystyle L_{f}h+L_{g}hu\leq-\alpha(h)

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{min}}\leq u\leq u_{\mathrm{max}}

where
u_{\mathrm{min}}
and
u_{\mathrm{max}}
are the lower and upper bounds on the control input, respectively.

VI
CONCLUSION

In this paper, the theory of control Lyapunov functions and control barrier functions was extended to in-domain control of traffic PDE models. First, a stabilizing controller was designed based on control Lyapunov theory. Then, control barrier function theory was extended to spatially defined functions, enabling the enforcement of safety constraints in PDE-based formulations. A unified optimization framework incorporating both CLF and sCBF constraints was then formulated. The approaches developed were applied to variable speed limit control of the LWR traffic model. Future work will focus on developing sCBFs that also depend on the control input and vehicle speed, as well as applying the constrained control method to optimal controller designs.
