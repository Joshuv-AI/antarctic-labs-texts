Nocardia vinacea infection with septic articular involvement and tenosynovitis
Authors: Axel Tabary, Oana Dumitrescu, Elisabeth Hodille, Claire Triffault Fillit, Cyrille B. Confavreux, Emmanuel Massy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nocardiosis, typically affecting immunocompromised patients, can manifest in rare forms like hand tenosynovitis. We report a 91-year-old man with Nocardia vinacea, confirmed by genetic sequencing after negative cultures, likely caused by a gardening injury. The diagnosis was made early, in less than a week, thanks to molecular biology techniques performed directly on the specimen (synovial biopsy), drastically shortening the time needed to introduce antibiotic therapy adapted to Nocardiosis, as the Nocardia culture grew in around a month. This case emphasizes the need for early PCR testing and tailored diagnostic approaches.

Nocardia is a bacterium responsible for nocardiosis, a rare opportunistic infection. The most common route of contamination is through inhalation, leading to pulmonary infections, particularly in patients with depressed cell-mediated immunity. These infections can occasionally lead to disseminated nocardiosis via hematogenous spread. Direct inoculation or soil contamination are less common routes of infection, and typically affect immunocompetent hosts presenting with primary soft tissue nocardiosis.

Here, we report a case of primary hand tenosynovitis, an uncommon presentation of nocardiosis, caused by Nocardia vinacea, a rare species. The authors affirm that all individuals identifiable in this manuscript have given their consent for publication.

A 91-year-old man was admitted to the rheumatology department with a progressively worsening inflammatory swelling of his left hand. He denied fever, chills, recent local trauma, or involvement of other joints. His medical history included marginal zone lymphoma treated with Rituximab five years earlier (currently in remission), stage 4 chronic kidney disease, and poorly controlled gout characterized by frequent polyarticular flares. The patient lived alone in a rural area and enjoyed gardening as a hobby. He had no pets and no known animal contacts. Eight weeks before admission, his primary care physician had prescribed colchicine and corticosteroids for a presumed gout flare, but his symptoms did not improve. Four weeks prior to admission, he received a local corticosteroid injection into the extensor digitorum communis (EDC) tendons, which provided almost complete relief for two weeks before the symptoms recurred. Upon physical examination, the patient had tender, erythematous, dorsal swelling of the left hand extending proximally to the wrist and forearm. Passive and active flexion of the last four digits were limited. No other joints were affected, and he had no extra-articular complaints. Further questioning revealed that the patient had injured his dorsal left hand on a rusty pipe while gardening two years before symptom onset, resulting in a 5 cm soil-contaminated wound (scar visible in Fig. 1a). Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 14,400/µL with neutrophil predominance (13,400/µL), lymphopenia (0.4 mg/L), elevated C-reactive protein (51.5 mg/L), and elevated serum uric acid levels (8.58 mg/dL). Ultrasound of the hand and wrist revealed significant tenosynovitis of the EDC tendons with a positive doppler signal. A CT scan of the left upper extremity (see Fig. 1b) showed marked infiltration centered around the synovial sheath of the EDC tendons, with air bubbles adjacent to the third digit tendon, the distal radioulnar articulation, and the interosseous membrane of the forearm. There was diffuse subcutaneous infiltration without fluid collection and para-articular calcifications suggesting tophi, with nearby joint erosions.

Needle aspiration of the tenosynovitis yielded no synovial fluid, prompting an ultrasound-guided biopsy of the synovium for bacteriological, mycological, mycobacteriological, and histological analysis. Histological examination revealed fibrous synovium with a large ulceration containing fibrin and degraded neutrophils. Microscopic exams were negative, and routine bacterial and fungal cultures remained sterile. On the fourth day, panbacterial PCR based on amplification/sequencing of the 16 S rRNA gene and PCR based on amplification/sequencing of the Actinomycetes hsp65 gene revealed the presence of DNA matching Nocardia vinacea on the sample. Among various synovial culture conditions, a liquid mycobacterial-specific medium (Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tube, MGIT, Coletsos) grew gram-positive, acid-fast, filamentous bacilli after 26 days. The antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed by microdilution using ready-to-use Sensititre™ Myco RAPMYCOI AST plates (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Table 1). The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) were then interpreted in accordance with Clinical Laboratory Standard Institute (CLSI) guidelines [1]. An automated broth microdilution (BMD) system (Sensititre, ThermoFisher) was performed to determine the MIC value of the isolated N. vinacea strain (Table 1). Blood cultures performed concomitantly with BacT/Alert Virtuo (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France) aerobic and anaerobic bottles remained sterile.

An empiric regimen of Ceftriaxone (2 g/day) and Linezolid (600 mg twice daily) was initiated after the identification of Nocardia. Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and amikacin were avoided because of the patient severe chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate between 15 and 30 ml/min). Swelling in the hand improved rapidly with treatment. The patient was discharged, with same drug regimen, and a brain MRI and PET scan were planned to assess for possible disseminated nocardiosis. Unfortunately, the patient returned to the emergency department two weeks later with a fever and passed away shortly thereafter due to a septic, previously undiagnosed condition.

Nocardia species are gram-positive, filamentous, aerobic bacteria found ubiquitously in soil and decomposing organic matter. There are currently over 120 recognized species, with more than 50 known to cause human infections [2]. Septic arthritis due to Nocardia spp is rare and has been reported mostly in isolated case reports or small case series [3–6]. A literature review from 2022 identified 37 cases of nocardial septic arthritis, 32 of them involving native joints [7]. This observation is consistent with the recent findings of Khodadadi et al. (2024), who identified 9 cases of musculoskeletal infections caused by diverse Nocardia species [8]. These studies highlight the broad spectrum of Nocardia species associated with joint infections, predominantly N. asteroides, N. farcinica, N. brasiliensis and N. nova. Nocardia vinacea is a recently identified species, first isolated from soil in 2001 by a Japanese team [9]. To date, only five cases of human infection with Nocardia vinacea have been reported between 2009 and 2014 [10–14]. In all these cases, the infection was the result of direct inoculation into the skin. Two patients had undergone renal transplants, one was being treated for polymyositis, and the other were two elderly men considered immunocompetent. In 4 out of 5 cases, the upper limb (hand, forearm, or arm) was involved, and none of the patients presented with disseminated infection. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of septic articular involvement and tenosynovitis caused by Nocardia vinacea. We suspect that the infection originated from an untreated soil-contaminated wound on the patient’s dorsal hand sustained while gardening. The infection progressed slowly, spreading up the forearm and involving the EDC tendons.

Routine microbiological tests and standard cultures were inconclusive, and the diagnosis was made using 16 S rRNA, hsp65 gene sequencing directly on the samples. However, culture of the samples and isolation of the bacterial strain remain essential for AST. Cultivation of the patient’s strain was made possible thanks to the medium normally used for mycobacterial research, MGIT medium, which is incubated over a long period (maximum 56 days). The laboratory diagnosis of Nocardia species can be mistaken for Mycobacterium, as they not only grow in specific media for mycobacteria, but also form partially acid-fast beaded branching filaments, similar to those formed by rapidly growing mycobacteria [15]. Therefore molecular methods and gene sequencing are often necessary to identify precisely these agents. The clinical course suggests in our case a disseminated form; however, we did not have time to perform imaging to assess the extent of the infection. On the other hand, the anterior infiltration and the initially subtle progression could raise the possibility of a mycetoma [16].

Soft tissue infections and arthritis affecting the upper limbs with an insidious course and negative routine cultures should prompt suspicion of Nocardia infections among other rare conditions. A history of soil-contaminated wounds, along with factors like immunosuppression or advanced age, may increase the likelihood of infection. When Nocardia is suspected, it is important to request Nocardia-specific PCR and appropriate culture media.
