Peripheral NMDAR subunits as predictors of working memory improvement in schizophrenia
Authors: Xiangqin Qin, Wenpeng Hou, Zhen Mao, Hang Li, Qi Wang, Yushen Ding, Lige Ge, Fuchun Zhou, Qijing Bo, Xianbin Li, Chuanyue Wang, Fang Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive dysfunction is a defining characteristic impairing social functioning in schizophrenia (SZ). N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) hypofunction may underlie these impairments. This study explored the association between peripheral blood levels of the NMDAR subunits NR1 and NR2 and cognitive improvement in SZ, evaluating their potential as biomarkers for the efficacy of cognitive intervention. This secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial included 60 clinically stable SZ patients and 30 healthy controls (HCs). Patients received five-day transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) during cognitive tasks across three groups: the active dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), the active posterior parietal cortex (PPC), and the sham stimulation group. Cognition was evaluated, and blood samples were collected at baseline, week 1, and week 2. Baseline blood samples were also obtained from HCs. NR1 and NR2 concentrations were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). We found that baseline NR1 concentration was significantly lower in the patient group than in HCs, with no NR2 differences observed. Compared to the other two groups, the active PPC group demonstrated significant working memory improvements. In the active PPC group, baseline NR1 and NR2 concentrations were negatively correlated with working memory improvements at week 1. Moreover, changes in NR1 at weeks 1 and 2, and NR2 at week 1, were positively associated with working memory improvements at week 1 in the active PPC group. Peripheral NR1 and NR2 levels may serve as biomarkers for predicting cognitive improvement in SZ, supporting the role of NMDAR dysfunction in SZ-related cognitive deficits.Subject terms: Schizophrenia, Biomarkers, Psychosis

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) is characterized by pronounced cognitive impairment, which serves as a key determinant of functional decline and social disability1,2. Among the affected domains, working memory dysfunction is a core feature with a substantial impact on patients’ everyday functioning1. Notably, cognitive impairment affects over 80% of patients with SZ3 and is challenging to remediate4. Existing interventions have yielded limited and slow improvements5–7 with inconsistent results largely attributable to patient heterogeneity8. This situation highlights the need for biomarkers to guide precise interventions for cognitive deficits in SZ.

A core pathological mechanism underlying cognitive deficits in SZ involves N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) dysfunction9,10. The hypomethylation and reduced expression of NMDAR subunit genes have been linked to cognitive deterioration11,12. Animal studies have demonstrated that NMDAR antagonists impair cognitive function and gamma oscillations13, a phenomenon that has been corroborated by clinical trials showing that enhancing NMDAR signaling may improve cognition in patients with SZ14–16. Although NMDAR abnormalities are of central importance in SZ-related cognitive deficits, NMDAR-based biomarkers for monitoring and predicting cognitive function remain scarce2,17.

NMDAR consists of several subunits, including NR1, NR2, and NR318. It assembles into a tetramer structure consisting of two mandatory NR1 subunits and two other subunits, which are typically NR2 subunits that bind glutamate, or less frequently, NR3 subunits that bind glycine18,19. The NR1 subunit is pivotal and governs receptor trafficking and synaptic anchoring20. The NR2 subunit regulates NMDAR function and is vital for cognition, whereas the NR3 subunit, which is mainly active during development, is expressed at low levels in adult cortical tissue21. NMDAR activation requires dual ligand binding: the binding of glycine/D-serine to NR1 and that of glutamate to NR2 subunits22,23. Central NR levels can reflect NMDAR status directly and specifically and may interact with peripheral NR levels through immune inflammation and blood–brain barrier permeabilization24. Beyond central neurons, NMDARs are also expressed in peripheral blood cells, such as lymphocytes and platelets25, which themselves contain functional NR1 and NR2 subunits involved in calcium signaling and immune regulation26–28. In addition, neuron-derived extracellular vesicles (NDEVs) released from the brain can cross the blood–brain barrier and carry synaptic proteins, including NMDAR components29,30. These NDEVs preserve molecular signatures of the central nervous system and have been proposed as “liquid biopsies” for brain disorders. Together, peripheral expression and brain-derived vesicular transport provide a biological rationale for using peripheral NR1/NR2 as accessible indicators of central NMDAR function.

Few studies have explored peripheral NR1 and NR2 levels as biomarkers for SZ. One study reported that peripheral NR1 and NR2 levels in patients with early psychosis were reduced relative to those in siblings and healthy controls (HCs), suggesting NR2 as a potential plasma biomarker for recognizing psychosis31. However, no studies on the monitoring or predictive role of NR in cognitive improvement in SZ have been reported yet. Importantly, peripheral NR1 and NR2 levels may reflect central NMDAR function and individual differences in NMDAR-related cognitive plasticity, making them plausible candidates as predictive biomarkers for cognitive improvement. To date, no studies have directly examined the predictive role of peripheral NR1/NR2 in cognitive enhancement following interventions in SZ. This gap motivates the current investigation.

Building on our previous clinical trial32, which demonstrated that transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) targeting the posterior parietal cortex (PPC) was superior to targeting the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) or to sham stimulation in improving working memory in SZ, we reasoned that the response to tDCS might be related to NMDAR function. We therefore hypothesized that peripheral levels of NMDAR subunits (NR1/NR2) are altered in SZ and could serve as biomarkers to predict cognitive improvement following intervention. Leveraging the data and biosamples from our prior trial and incorporating an additional group of healthy controls (HCs), this study aims to: 1) compare peripheral NR levels between patients with SZ and HCs, and 2) evaluate the potential of NR levels to monitor and predict tDCS-induced cognitive improvement in SZ.

Methods

Participants

A total of 60 clinically stable patients with SZ and 30 age- and sex-matched HCs constituted the study sample. SZ diagnosis was established in accordance with DSM-5, with the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview used for determination. Eligible patients were between 18 and 50 years old, right-handed, with a minimum of eight years of formal education, an intelligence quotient (IQ) above 70, and spatial span test T-scores ranging from 30 to 50. Patients were clinically stable, with their antipsychotic treatment regimen (including type and dosage) remaining unchanged throughout the study. HCs were required to be aged 18–50 years and right-handed, with at least eight years of education and an IQ exceeding 70. Participant details and exclusion criteria have been outlined in our previous publication32. Every participant furnished written informed consent prior to commencing their participation. The Ethics Committee of Beijing Anding Hospital granted approval (No. 2020-70) for this double-blind, three-arm randomized trial, which was also registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2000038961).

Using a block randomization procedure, patients were evenly assigned (1:1:1) to one of three groups: the active DLPFC, active PPC, and sham stimulation groups. High-definition tDCS synchronized N-back task was conducted for five consecutive days. Clinical symptoms and cognition were assessed, and electroencephalograms and blood samples were collected from patients at baseline, week 1, and week 2. Baseline blood samples were also obtained from the HCs. Data collection for the same assessment at each time point was scheduled within the same time frame, ensuring that the time interval did not exceed 2 h to control for potential confounding factors. Changes in spatial span test scores from baseline to week 1 were the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes included changes in spatial span test scores from baseline to week 2, along with changes in delay – estimation and change detection task scores from baseline to weeks 1 and 2.

Detection of NR1 and NR2

For the patient group, peripheral blood was collected in EDTA-containing tubes at baseline, week 1, and week 2. Baseline samples were collected prior to the tDCS intervention, while week 1 and week 2 samples were collected following completion of the 5-day tDCS intervention. A solitary blood draw was executed for the HC group. Upon collection, the samples were left stationary for half an hour before being centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min. The separated supernatant was then harvested, aliquoted into cryovials, and preserved at −80 °C. NR1 and NR2 levels were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits from My BioSource (catalog numbers MBS724343 and MBS751425, respectively). The intra-assay coefficient of variation was below 10%, the inter-assay coefficient of variation was below 12%, and spike recoveries ranged from 94% to 103%. Personnel involved in sample handling and measurement were blinded to the group assignments of the subjects.

Cognitive and clinical assessments

Neurocognitive function was assessed by using the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB), and standardized scores from each domain were used in the statistical analyses. Specifically, (1) processing speed was evaluated through the Trail Making Test: Part A, symbol coding, and animal naming tasks; (2) attention/vigilance was measured by the Continuous Performance Test-Identical Pairs; (3) working memory was tested with the Spatial Span Test; (4) verbal learning was examined by using the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised; (5) visual learning was measured with the Brief Visuospatial Memory Test-Revised; and (6) reasoning and problem-solving was evaluated with the Mazes subtest33. Besides using the Spatial Span Test to evaluate working memory, this study also employed the color delay – estimation task to assess working memory capacity and accuracy34. Additionally, the change detection task was employed to evaluate participants’ working memory capacity and filtering efficiency. Filtering efficiency indexes the ability to control interference in working memory. A lower filtering efficiency value signifies a stronger ability to suppress interference in working memory35,36 (supplementary material). A short version of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale was employed to determine participants’ IQ37, and the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)38 was utilized to assess the severity of the participants’ symptoms. In addition, to provide a more comprehensive description of symptomatology, we calculated scores of the five-factor model of PANSS (positive, negative, disorganized/concrete, excited, and depressed factors)39,40 for the patient group at baseline, week 1, and week 2 (Supplementary Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was executed by using SPSS 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, the USA), and data plotting was performed with the help of MATLAB R2020b (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, the USA). The normality of the data was examined by means of histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. First, independent samples t-tests and chi-square tests were used to compare the baseline features between patients with SZ and the HCs. Next, a linear mixed model was utilized to evaluate the effects of tDCS intervention on NR concentrations. Spearman correlation analyses were then conducted (1) to explore the association between baseline NR concentrations and baseline cognitive function in the patient group; (2) to explore correlations between baseline NR concentrations and working memory alterations; and (3) to investigate correlations between NR change values and working memory alterations. Our previous analysis found that the active PPC group showed cognitive improvements in four indicators compared with the other two groups. These improvements included changes in spatial span test scores from baseline to week 1, delay – estimation task capacity from baseline to weeks 1 and 2, and filtering efficiency for four targets in the change detection task from baseline to week 132. Therefore, the cognitive change–related analysis in this study focused on these four indicators. These analyses aim to evaluate whether baseline NR levels could monitor and predict therapeutic efficacy. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Results

Comparison of NR levels between the patient and control groups

No significant differences were detected between the two groups with respect to gender, age, IQ, smoking, and alcohol consumption (Table 1). Given that the patient group had a longer duration of education than the HCs (t = −2.549, p < 0.05), educational duration was included as a covariate in the comparison of NR1 and NR2 levels between the two groups. Through analysis of covariance, a significant decrease in baseline NR1 levels was detected in the SZ group relative to the HCs (F = 11.993, p < 0.01; Fig. 1A, Cohen’s d = 0.8), and no statistically discernible difference was observed in NR2 concentrations (F = 0.012, p > 0.05; Fig. 1A).

Correlation between baseline NR and cognition/cognitive changes

In the entire patient group, baseline NR2 exhibited a significant positive correlation with visual learning (r = 0.284, p < 0.05; Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 1B), and no significant associations were observed between baseline NR and other cognitive domains. In the active PPC group, baseline NR1 and NR2 were significantly and positively associated with changes in filtering efficiency for four targets at week 1 relative to those at baseline (NR1: r = 0.680, p < 0.01; NR2: r = 0.510, p < 0.05; Table 2; Fig. 1C). Nevertheless, no significant correlations were identified with changes in other working memory tasks. Moreover, the predictive effect of NR1 remained significant after FDR correction. Neither the active DLPFC group nor the sham stimulation group demonstrated significant associations with changes in any working memory measures. In addition, we examined correlations between PANSS five-factor scores and NR levels, as well as working memory performance, at both baseline and change levels in the overall patient group and within each stimulation subgroup. No statistically significant associations were observed.

Correlation between NR changes and working memory alterations

In the active PPC group, a significant negative correlation was found between the changes in NR and alterations in the filtering efficiency for four targets at week 1 relative to baseline (Table 4; Fig. 1D). Specifically, NR1 changes at weeks 1 (r = −0.549, p < 0.05) and 2 (r = −0.603, p < 0.05) and NR2 changes at week 1 (r = −0.535, p < 0.05) were negatively associated with changes in filtering efficiency for four targets at week 1 relative to baseline. Significant correlations were not observed in the active DLPFC and sham stimulation groups. None of the correlations remained significant after FDR correction.

Discussion

Our study investigated peripheral blood NR1 and NR2 levels in clinically stable patients with SZ before and after tDCS intervention and their association with working memory improvements. We found lower baseline NR1 levels in patients with SZ than in HCs. In the active PPC group, wherein substantial improvements in working memory were noted, baseline NR levels negatively predicted working memory improvement. Furthermore, changes in NR levels were positively correlated with working memory enhancements.

We compared peripheral blood NR levels between patients with SZ and HCs, finding a significant decrease in NR1 levels in the SZ group. Previous research has shown that plasma NR concentrations were significantly lower in patients with SZ spectrum disorders than in HCs31, echoing our results. The diminished expression of NMDAR subunits is fundamental to the pathophysiology of SZ, as validated and replicated in postmortem and animal studies41–44. NR1 is the pivotal subunit of NMDAR and is essential for its primary functions. Our study further highlighted its critical function in SZ and underscored its potential as a reliable biomarker for SZ.

Our work demonstrated that within the active PPC group, baseline NR levels inversely predicted working memory improvement, with lower baseline NR concentrations associated with greater cognitive gains following tDCS intervention. Baseline NR could indicate NMDAR dysfunction severity, with lower levels suggesting more pronounced impairment45,46. Consequently, patients with low NR levels may possess higher potential for cognitive improvement, as tDCS could facilitate recovery by modulating NMDAR function45,47. This pattern is paralleled by pharmacologic studies showing that abnormal peripheral levels of D-serine or D-cycloserine, which bind to the NR1 subunit, are strongly associated with cognitive improvement in patients with SZ15,48,49. We also considered statistical explanations like regression to the mean. However, our patient group was selected for mild to moderate — rather than extreme — cognitive impairment, reducing the likelihood of regression artifacts. Moreover, the effect was specific to the active PPC group, which suggests a biological rather than purely statistical mechanism. Collectively, these results support the potential of peripheral NR levels as a stratification biomarker to identify patients with more severe NMDAR hypofunction who may derive particular benefit from plasticity-enhancing interventions like tDCS. Our work reinforces the role of NMDAR function as a predictor of cognitive improvement in SZ.

Our study found that although tDCS did not improve NR levels in patients with SZ, changes in NR levels were positively correlated with improvements in working memory. This finding mirrors the results of our previous study, which showed that in the active PPC group, the mismatch negativity (MMN) amplitude improved, and changes in MMN-related indices were positively associated with working memory enhancement32. Given that MMN serves as an electrophysiological marker of NMDAR function50, this parallel pattern suggests that biomarkers based on NMDAR function may serve as effective indicators for evaluating the efficacy of cognitive improvement in patients with SZ. Broad lines of indirect evidence further corroborate this finding. Past studies revealed that increased peripheral D-serine concentrations were closely linked to improved neuroplasticity and cognitive function in patients with SZ51,52. For example, among patients with SZ who were engaged in cognitive training, an increase in D-serine levels was positively correlated with enhancements in overall cognitive function and verbal learning abilities53. The absence of tDCS-induced changes in NR levels observed in our study may stem from the multifactorial biological mechanisms governing peripheral NR dynamics. Such mechanisms include receptor endocytosis/recycling, intracellular signaling cascades, molecular regulation of receptors, and neuroplasticity. These intricate processes may not be fully captured through peripheral NR quantification alone21,54,55. Furthermore, while tDCS elicits cognitive benefits in a short time, its effects on blood-based biomarker profiles likely exhibit a temporal lag56.

Our findings suggest that baseline NR levels predict tDCS-induced working memory improvement in a target-specific manner. The association was confined to the PPC group, consistent with the PPC’s central role in storage capacity and internal attention, which are more directly tied to working memory than DLPFC activity57,58. NMDAR-mediated persistent activity and frontoparietal synchrony, particularly involving NR2B subunits, are critical for maintaining memory representations59,60. Task-related PPC modulation may therefore more effectively translate systemic NMDAR status, indexed by peripheral NR1/NR2 levels, into measurable cognitive gains. In contrast, the lack of associations in the DLPFC group may be explained by lower and more variable electric field strength in this region, especially in psychotic populations61,62, and by evidence that PPC stimulation, particularly under higher load, more consistently enhances working memory than DLPFC stimulation63,64.

As far as we are aware, this study represents the first attempt to investigate the role of NR in cognitive intervention for clinically stable patients with SZ. Several limitations should be noted. First, the relatively small sample size, divided across three groups, reduced the statistical power for within-group correlation analyses. In the active PPC group, only the baseline NR1–outcome association remained significant after FDR correction, whereas other associations did not, suggesting that these exploratory findings may be subject to Type I errors and overfitting. Replication in larger, preregistered cohorts is therefore warranted. Second, we focused exclusively on working memory, as it was the only domain to show improvement in our previous research. Third, HCs were sampled only once at baseline, which enabled baseline group comparisons but limited the ability to characterize the natural temporal trajectory of NR1/NR2. Future longitudinal studies involving both patients and controls are needed to clarify these dynamics. Finally, cognitive function was not assessed in HCs, preventing evaluation of whether the NR–cognition association is specific to SZ. This limitation restricts the generalizability of NR as a biomarker, and future work should include cognitive assessments in both patients and controls to establish the specificity and broader applicability of NR–cognition associations.

In conclusion, this study is the first to demonstrate that both baseline levels and changes of peripheral NMDAR subunits NR1 and NR2 are associated with working memory improvement in SZ. While overall NR1/NR2 levels did not significantly change across the intervention period, their baseline concentrations and individual changes were linked to subsequent cognitive gains, highlighting their potential for guiding personalized interventions. These findings reinforce the critical role of NMDAR dysfunction in SZ-related cognitive deficits and provide a novel avenue for developing peripheral biomarkers to predict cognitive outcomes.
