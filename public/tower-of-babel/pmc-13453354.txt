The Role of Serum Albumin as a Prognostic Marker in Guillain-Barré Syndrome: A Systematic Review
Authors: Vivien Puspitasari, Yang Yang Endro Arjuna, Alexander Erick Purnomo, Yusak Mangara Tua Siahaan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGuillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an immune-mediated neuropathy typically triggered by infection. Prognosis is primarily based on clinical features and established scoring systems. Serum albumin, a biomarker reduced in systemic inflammation, has demonstrated prognostic value in various conditions. This study aims to investigate the correlation between serum albumin levels and clinical outcomes in patients with GBS. Following PRISMA guidelines, a systematic review was conducted using PubMed, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, and EuropePMC, covering all records up to April 2025. The search strategy included terms such as “Guillain-Barré Syndrome”, “Albumin”, and “Outcome” along with relevant MeSH terms. Studies eligible for inclusion were cohort studies and randomized controlled trials (RCT) evaluating the association between serum albumin levels and GBS. Primary outcome was GBS disability scores (GBS-DS), with secondary outcomes including Medical Research Council (MRC) scores and the need for mechanical ventilation (MV). Risk of bias was assessed using Risk of Bias of Non-Randomized Study of Exposure (ROBINS-E). Fifteen studies including 1,441 patients met the criteria for inclusion. Lower albumin levels at both admission and follow-up were strongly associated with higher GBS scores, lower MRC scores, and the necessity for mechanical ventilation. Conversely, elevated albumin levels correlated with better clinical outcomes. According to the ROBINS-E assessment, four studies were rated as having a “some concerns” risk of bias, while the remaining eleven studies were considered low risk. Serum albumin concentration is a useful prognostic indicator in patients with GBS, with lower levels significantly associated with poorer clinical outcomes.Keywords: guillain-barré syndrome, albumin, outcome

Introduction

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an immune-mediated, post-infectious neuropathy that presents with progressively worsening, symmetrical, flaccid weakness, typically beginning in the lower limbs and ascending upward. It may be accompanied by motor dysfunction, with or without sensory involvement. As one of the most thoroughly studied neuroinflammatory disorders, GBS offers important insights into peripheral nerve inflammation and is often recognizable through clinical assessment.1,2 Recent epidemiological trends indicate a steady global increase in the incidence of GBS. In 2019, the average global prevalence was reported at 1.9 per 100,000 individuals, with a higher incidence observed in males. The total number of cases rose substantially from 90,249 in 1990 to 150,095 in 2019.3–5 Regions contributing most significantly to this increase include South Asia, high-income North America, and East Asia. Current estimates suggest approximately 100,000 new GBS cases occur annually worldwide.3–5 Approximately 20% of individuals with GBS experience lasting morbidity, and the mortality rate can reach 13%.3–5 Prognostic assessment largely relies on clinical features and scoring systems. Factors such as cranial nerve involvement, autonomic instability, low MRC scores, and elevated GBS-DS scores at admission are associated with poorer outcomes. However, there is limited research investigating the use of blood biomarkers as predictors of GBS prognosis.6–8 Serum albumin plays a crucial role in maintaining osmotic pressure and mediating the transport of various endogenous and exogenous substances.9 Its levels frequently decline during significant inflammatory states.10 The use of serum albumin as a prognostic marker has been explored in several clinical areas, including cardiovascular and respiratory diseases.11,12

Despite advances in the clinical characterization and supportive care of GBS, early and reliable prognostic indicators remain limited. While existing prognostic models largely rely on clinical scoring systems such as the Medical Research Council (MRC) sum score and GBS Disability Scale (GBS-DS), these tools may not fully capture the underlying pathophysiological dynamics, especially in the early phase of disease.13,14 Blood-based biomarkers, particularly those reflecting systemic inflammation and nutritional status, offer a promising adjunct to clinical evaluation. Serum albumin, a negative acute-phase reactant, is known to decrease in systemic inflammatory states and has shown prognostic relevance in conditions such as sepsis, stroke, and chronic pulmonary disease. However, its predictive value in the context of GBS has not been systematically reviewed.15,16

Given the growing number of observational studies reporting associations between hypoalbuminemia and adverse GBS outcomes, such as prolonged disability, need for mechanical ventilation, and poor response to therapy, there is a pressing need to synthesize this emerging evidence. This systematic review aims to evaluate the prognostic utility of serum albumin in GBS and assess its potential role as a routine biomarker in clinical decision-making.

Materials and Methods

The systematic review was performed and documented in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses) guidelines.17 Additionally, the review protocol was listed in the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews, assigned registration number CRD420251073687.

Search Strategies and Study Selection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using the following databases: PubMed, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, and Europe PMC. Articles examining the association between serum albumin levels and Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) were identified from inception through April 2025. The search strategy incorporated the following terms: “Guillain Barré Syndrome,” “Albumin,” and “Outcome,” combined using Boolean operators “AND” and “OR.” A detailed list of search keywords is presented in Table 1. The inclusion criteria were limited to cohort studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that investigated the relationship between serum albumin and GBS. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: non-English language, case reports, case control studies, case series, animal studies, cadaveric studies, abstract-only publications, or conference proceedings. Article screening was initially performed based on titles and abstracts by three independent reviewers (Vivien Puspitasari, Yang Yang Endro Arjuna, Alexander Erick Purnomo). Full texts of potentially eligible studies were then independently assessed by the same reviewers. Discrepancies or uncertainties were resolved through discussion with a neurology expert (Yusak Mangara Tua Siahaan) until a consensus was reached.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

The articles selected based on the inclusion criteria are organized into two main sections for data extraction: (1) Characteristics of Included Studies and (2) Correlation of serum albumin and clinical scores. The extracted data includes variables such as Study ID, Study Design, Country, Sample Size, Age, Time from Symptom Onset to Hospital Admission, Length of Hospital Stay, Presence of Diarrhea and Upper Respiratory Tract Infection (URTI), and Cranial Nerve Involvement (CNI) at admission. Additional detailed data extraction covers variables including Electrophysiological Subtype, Initial Albumin Level, Follow-up Duration, GBS and MRC Score, Use of Mechanical Ventilation at Admission and Post Treatment, Measured Outcomes,Definitions of Outcomes, and the Conclusions or Key Findings from each study. The Cochrane’s Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Exposure (ROBINS-E) tool is used to assess the risk of bias in included studies. It evaluates seven domains: 1) Bias due to confounding, 2) Bias arising from the measurement of the exposure, 3) Bias in the selection of participants into the study (or into the analysis), 4) Bias due to post-exposure interventions 5) Bias due to missing data, 6) Bias arising from the measurement of the outcome, 7) Bias in the selection of the reported result. Each domain is assessed and categorized as having a “low risk of bias”, “some concerns”, “high risk of bias”, or “very high risk of bias”.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome assessed the correlation between serum albumin levels and functional outcomes in Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) patients, using the Guillain-Barré Syndrome Disability Score (GBS-DS/GBS Score) and the Hughes Functional Grading Scale/Hughes Disability Score (HFGS/HDS) as evaluation tools. The secondary outcome investigated the association between serum albumin and the Medical Research Council (MRC) score, and the requirement for mechanical ventilation. Although this review aimed to explore the prognostic role of serum albumin in Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS), a meta-analysis was not conducted due to variability in definitions and measurement of outcomes (eg, GBS Disability Score, MRC sum score, HDS, and need for mechanical ventilation), timing of serum albumin assessment (eg, at admission, post-treatment, or follow-up), and cut-off values used to define hypoalbuminemia.

Results

Search Results and Study Selection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across four databases: ScienceDirect, EuropePMC, PubMed, and Google Scholar, identifying a total of 1,441 records (ScienceDirect: n = 632; EuropePMC: n = 250; PubMed: n = 72; Google Scholar: n = 487). After removal of 599 duplicate records, 842 unique articles were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 60 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. During this stage, 45 articles were excluded for reasons including duplicate publication (n = 19), irrelevant outcome measures (n = 18), commentary or letter format (n = 4), abstract-only availability (n = 3), and inaccessible full text (n = 1). Ultimately, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis.15,16,18–28 No reports were excluded due to retrieval issues at any stage. Characteristics of Included Studies

The characteristics of included studies is shown in Table 2. A total of 15 studies encompassing 1,779 patients were included in this systematic review, with sample sizes ranging from 32 to 222 participants. The studies spanned diverse geographical regions, including China (n=7), Pakistan (n=2), India (n=2), Turkey (n=2), Netherlands (n=1), and Mexico (n=1). Most were retrospective cohort designs (n=11), with four studies employing a prospective cohort design. Patient ages varied widely, covering both adult and pediatric populations. Adult cohorts generally had mean ages between 35 and 58 years, while pediatric focused studies reported means as low as 5 years. The time from onset to admission was reported in 7 studies and ranged from 3.5 to 14.5 days, with shorter delays noted in patients requiring mechanical ventilation. Hospital stay was inconsistently reported but ranged from around 11 to 28 days, with more severe cases staying longer. Several studies reported antecedent infections, with diarrhea and upper respiratory infections are the most reported antecedent infections, depending on the cohort and geographic region. Few studies mentioned cranial nerve involvement, with some reporting rates over 90%. Most studies had a male predominant population, consistent with GBS epidemiology.

Prognostic Relevance of Serum Albumin and Functional Outcomes in GBS

The prognostic relevance of serum albumin and functional outcomes in GBS is shown in Table 3. Across the 15 included studies, serum albumin was assessed as a potential prognostic biomarker in patients with GBS, with most studies measuring levels at admission or within 24 hours of hospital arrival. The population sizes ranged widely, and most studies followed patients for outcomes at 3 to 6 months. Outcomes were commonly defined by functional scales such as the GBS-DS, HFGS/HDS, MRC score, and the need for MV. A poor outcome was most typically defined as the inability to walk independently (eg, GBS-DS >2 or HDS ≥3). In this study notably, Yao et al (2023) reported that 51.03% of patients had poor outcomes (GBS ≥3) at 6 months. Study conducted by Di et al (2023) similarly found 17.7% with GBS scores >3, linking higher scores with worse prognosis in GBS.18,24 Electrophysiological subtypes, including AIDP, AMAN, AMSAN, and MFS, were reported in ten studies. These subtypes allowed stratified analysis, with demyelinating and axonal forms being most frequent. Several studies observed that axonal subtypes were associated with worse clinical outcomes, but serum albumin remained a consistent independent predictor regardless of subtype. Timing of serum albumin measurement varied slightly but was typically performed at admission or within 24 hours post-admission, with several studies such as those by Xie et al (2024) and Fokkink et al (2016) explicitly reporting pre-treatment values to avoid confounding.16,27 Most studies showed that lower serum albumin levels were significantly associated with poor outcomes. For instance, Xie et al (2024) reported lower mean serum albumin in the mechanically ventilated group (34.13 ± 5.90 g/L) compared to non-ventilated patients (38.52 ± 8.91 g/L). Similarly, Tiwari et al (2022) found that patients with poor outcomes had lower serum albumin levels (35.9 ± 3.4 g/L) than those with good outcomes (38.4 ± 3.1 g/L).15,31 Ethemoglu et al (2018) showed that both adult and pediatric patients with higher HDS scores (≥3) had lower serum albumin compared to those with milder disability.23 Furthermore, studies that reported MRC scores (eg, Badshah et al, 2018; Xue et al, 2023) consistently found lower muscle strength in patients with hypoalbuminemia, supporting the link between nutritional-inflammatory status and neuromuscular function.20,21

Correlation Between Serum Albumin Levels, Clinical Scores, and Outcomes in GBS

The correlation between serum albumin levels, clinical scores, and outcomes in GBS is shown in Table 4. Serum albumin levels at admission or during early hospitalization consistently showed a strong association with disease severity, functional outcomes, and need for mechanical ventilation in GBS patients. Several studies stratified patients into low and normal albumin groups, using thresholds ranging from 34.5 to 40 g/L. Notably, Yao et al (2023) demonstrated that patients with low albumin (<40 g/L) had significantly higher GBS Disability Scores (2.6 ± 1.6 vs 1.7 ± 1.2) and lower serum albumin concentrations (35.4 ± 3.3 g/L vs 40.5 ± 3.6 g/L). These patients also had a higher rate of mechanical ventilation (MV) (19.12% vs 6.35%), suggesting a clear link between hypoalbuminemia and respiratory failure.32

Consistent findings were reported in Xue et al (2023), where poor outcome patients had significantly lower serum albumin levels (34.90 g/L) and lower MRC scores (23.5 vs 54.0). Fookink et al (2016) extended this observation by correlating pre-treatment albumin levels with both short and long-term outcomes, revealing that patients in the low albumin group had a significantly higher MV rate (36.4% pre-treatment, 54.7% post-treatment) and worse GBS-DS scores. Similarly, Badshah et al (2018) reported that patients with low albumin (<3.5g/dL) had poorer 6-month outcomes (73.1% poor vs 43.2%) and lower MRC scores at every time point, reinforcing albumin’s prognostic role.16,20,21

Several studies supported the predictive power of serum albumin for poor functional recovery. For instance, Tiwari et al (2022) showed that patients with poor outcomes had lower albumin levels (33.5 ± 3.4 g/L vs 36.2 ± 2.4 g/L), while Ethemoglu et al (2018) demonstrated that discharge HDS scores were inversely associated with serum albumin—patients with worse disability (HDS ≥3) had significantly lower albumin (3.15±0.49 g/dL).15,23

In contrast, a few studies observed no significant association between albumin and need for MV. However, these studies still reported slightly lower albumin in MV groups, suggesting a possible trend even if not statistically significant.15,29,30 Conversely, Xie et al (2024) identified a significant drop in serum albumin in MV patients (30.12 ± 5.34 g/L vs 35.28 ± 5.19 g/L), linking hepatic protein synthesis impairment with respiratory deterioration.

Risk of Bias

The result of ROBINS-E assessment is shown in Figure 2. Risk of bias was evaluated for all included studies using the ROBINS-E tool, which assesses bias across seven domains related to exposure and outcome. Overall, the quality of the studies was high. Out of 15 studies, 11 were judged to have a low risk of bias across all domains. Minor concerns were noted in four studies. Di et al (2023) and Luo et al (2019) showed some concerns in domain D4 (bias due to post-exposure interventions), while Xie et al (2024) had some concerns in D3 (selection bias),Mendez et al (2024) also presented minor concerns in D5 (missing data). Despite these isolated issues, no study was rated as high risk in any domain, and the overall risk of bias was acceptable for inclusion in quantitative and qualitative synthesis. These findings enhance the credibility of our review by confirming that the majority of studies were methodologically sound, particularly in how they measured exposure (serum albumin), selected participants, and assessed outcomes.

Discussion

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is a potentially life-threatening immune-mediated peripheral neuropathy, frequently precipitated by a preceding infection.33 Although treatments such as plasmapheresis and IVIG are generally effective, both morbidity and mortality rates remain significant.24 Consequently, accurate early prognostication is critical during the initial stages of the disease to facilitate timely therapeutic interventions and improve clinical outcomes. Recently, serum albumin levels have been investigated as a potential prognostic marker in various conditions, including cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, and may hold similar promise in the context of GBS.11,12

Our study confirms a higher prevalence of GBS in males, aligning with previous studies.34,35 While the exact mechanisms are not fully understood, it is hypothesized that sex hormones especially testosterone, may influence immune regulation differently than estrogen, potentially affecting vulnerability to autoimmune diseases like GBS.36 Genetic factors associated with sex chromosomes may also play a role in the observed differences in incidence and disease course.37 Multiple studies have demonstrated that prior infections, including diarrhea and upper respiratory tract infections, serve as important risk factors for GBS. This is supported by a large-scale, population-based epidemiological study in Korea, which found that 72% of individuals diagnosed with GBS had experienced an infection beforehand.38 The underlying mechanism is thought to involve molecular mimicry between surface lipo-oligosaccharides (LOS) and host peripheral nerve gangliosides, leading to the formation of cross-reactive antibodies that cause damage to the axoglial structures.1 While post-infectious events are a common trigger, other identified causes of GBS include vaccination, surgical interventions, and organ transplantation.1

Our findings demonstrate a clear relationship between serum albumin levels, often measured at the time of admission, and the severity of Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS), as reflected by GBS scores. Yao et al (2023) observed significantly higher GBS scores in patients with hypoalbuminemia (2.6 ± 1.6) compared to those with normal albumin levels (1.7 ± 1.2), with corresponding serum albumin values of 35.4 ± 3.3 g/L and 40.5 ± 3.6 g/L, respectively.18 Similarly, Xue et al (2023) reported that patients with unfavorable outcomes had markedly lower albumin concentrations [34.90 g/L (32.13–41.78)] than those with favorable outcomes [42.93 g/L (38.10–45.80)].20 All 10 studies comparing serum albumin and GBS scores reported consistent findings. Reduced albumin levels were also linked to poor prognosis in 73.1% and 54.5% of cases, as reported by Badshah et al (2018) and Di et al (2023), respectively.21,24 A parallel trend was noted in GBS outcomes assessed by the Medical Research Council (MRC) score. Lower serum albumin levels were consistently associated with lower MRC scores, indicating poorer outcomes, whereas higher albumin levels correlated with higher MRC scores, suggesting better recovery.21,24 Hussain et al (2021) reported significantly lower MRC scores in the low albumin group compared to the high albumin group (35.55 ± 12.14 vs 45.32 ± 11.17).22 This trend was consistent across all reviewed studies. Similarly, the need for mechanical ventilation (MV) was higher among patients with lower albumin levels. Fokkink et al (2016) found MV was required in 54.7% of the low albumin group versus only 5.2% of the high albumin group. Although three of eight studies also reported higher MV use in the low albumin group, the differences were not statistically significant.16 Overall, these findings indicate a strong association between serum albumin levels, MRC and GBS scores, and MV use. Lower albumin correlates with higher GBS scores, lower MRC scores, and increased MV use, while higher albumin is associated with more favourable clinical outcomes.

Serum albumin, a major circulating protein synthesized in the liver, plays a crucial role in maintaining colloid osmotic pressure and transporting hormones, fatty acids, and drugs. During systemic inflammation, hepatic albumin synthesis declines, capillary permeability increases, and the redistribution of albumin to interstitial spaces occurs, resulting in hypoalbuminemia.39,40 In the context of Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS), this phenomenon may be particularly relevant. GBS is characterized by intense peripheral nerve inflammation, which not only contributes to axonal damage but also enhances vascular leakage at the nerve roots. Such inflammation-driven extravasation may lead to lower serum albumin levels, especially in severe cases.39 Beyond being a marker of inflammation, albumin may directly influence disease progression. Its antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties suggest a protective role in maintaining endothelial integrity and reducing oxidative stress. Hypoalbuminemia may thus reflect both the intensity of immune-mediated nerve damage and impaired systemic resilience, which could explain its association with more severe disability and longer recovery times. Multiple studies support this relationship. For instance, Badshah et al (2018) demonstrated that patients with low albumin levels had significantly higher disability at 6 months post plasmapheresis, while Khichar et al (2019) found a negative correlation between baseline albumin and Hughes disability scores.21,41

Moreover, Fokkink et al (2017) highlighted that albumin levels measured both before and after IVIG treatment were significantly associated with mechanical ventilation needs and delayed functional recovery in GBS.16 The reduction in albumin over time was particularly pronounced in patients with poor outcomes, suggesting its potential utility as a dynamic marker.39 In adult GBS cohorts, Ethemoglu et al (2018) further reinforced this finding by identifying lower albumin levels as an independent predictor of poor prognosis at hospital discharge.23 Comparable patterns have been observed in other inflammatory neurological disorders, including neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD).42 Decreased serum albumin levels are linked to increase neurological severity, and patients with lower albumin levels also tend to exhibit greater disability in myasthenia gravis (MG).43

This study is subject to several limitations. First, a small number of included studies applied varying definitions for favourable versus unfavourable outcomes according to the GBS disability score, which may contribute to inconsistencies in outcome classification. Second, there was a lack of standardization in the albumin cutoff values used across studies, potentially impacting the comparability of findings. Third, the majority of the included studies were retrospective cohort studies, which may introduce selection bias and limit causal inference. Fourth, some studies did not report exact albumin levels and instead categorized participants into low or normal albumin groups without providing specific numerical values, limiting the precision of the data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, current evidence suggests that serum albumin levels, assessed at admission and during the course of treatment, are predictive of clinical prognosis in GBS. Decreased albumin levels are linked to greater disease severity, as reflected by elevated GBS disability scores, lower MRC scores, and increased dependence on mechanical ventilation. In contrast, higher serum albumin levels appear to be associated with improved clinical outcomes. While this study offers valuable findings, its noted limitations highlight the necessity for further large-scale studies to substantiate and expand upon the current evidence.
