Eco-friendly fabrication of flag blue flower-derived CQD-doped chitosan films with tunable optical and photoluminescent properties
Authors: Hawkar A Mohammed, Dara M Aziz, Shujahadeen B Aziz, Muaffaq M Nofal, Govar H Hamasalih, Ismael I Hussein, Kawan F Kayani, Pshko A Mohammed, Sambasivam Sangaraju
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the influence of carbon quantum dots (CQDs) synthesized from flag blue (FB) flower dyes on the optical band gap reduction of chitosan (CS) biopolymer. The resulting CQDs were characterized using fluorescence spectroscopy, XPS, XRD, HR-TEM, FTIR, and UV–Vis spectroscopy. Fluorescence measurements revealed a pronounced emission peak at 415 nm when excited at 330 nm, with a quantum yield of 7.41%. The HR-TEM and XRD analyses confirm the successful formation of uniformly dispersed, nanosized CQDs. XPS analysis was conducted to determine the elemental composition and surface functionalities of the CQDs. The characteristic absorption peak of the FB dye vanished in the CQDs spectrum, indicating successful carbonization and transformation of the dye molecules into carbonaceous nanostructures. The interactions of CQDs with the functional groups of the CS host polymer were examined through FTIR spectroscopy. Shifts and intensity changes in the FTIR bands of the CS polymer upon incorporation of CQDs demonstrated the formation of strong intermolecular interactions between chitosan and CQDs derived from FB dye. UV–Vis spectroscopy revealed that the optical properties of the films could be tailored by adjusting the CQDs concentration. Essential optical parameters—absorption edge, refractive index (n), dielectric loss (εi), energy gap (Eg), Urbach energy (Eu), extinction coefficient (k), plasma energy (Ψ), Fermi energy (Ef), and Penn energy (Ep)—were determined for both pure CS and composite films. The doped CS films exhibited a reduced optical band gap of 2.55 eV, indicating enhanced optoelectronic performance. Introduction

Due to their unique structural and functional properties, polymers have become better alternatives to traditional materials in several fields, including building, industrial production, and biomedical engineering. Their capacity to merge lightweight characteristics with durability, flexibility, and cost-efficiency has markedly enhanced performance and fostered advancement across several technical fields1. Similarly, owing to their cost-effectiveness, low density, structural diversity, and extensive availability, polymers have attained considerable significance in advanced optical technologies. Their distinctive optical and mechanical characteristics make them appropriate for use in contemporary optical storage systems, waveguides, and optical fibers2. Improving their applications requires the development of their optical and mechanical properties. Several investigations provide that the optical and mechanical properties of polymer van be improved by adding filers to the polymer composite. These rapidly developing materials exhibit exceptional linear and nonlinear optical properties3.

This research examined chitosan biopolymer (CS), a recently commercialized material. CS serves as a viable host polymer for composite fabrication owing to its unique properties4. It contains glucosamine and N-acetyl glucosamine interconnected by β−1,4 glycosidic linkages5. This natural polymer is environmentally degradable, biocompatible, and non-toxic6. The largest portion of commercial chitosan is generated by alkaline N-deacetylation of crustacean chitin. Chitosan is soluble in dilute organic acids such as acetic and formic acid owing to the presence of amino groups7. A major amino group and both primary and secondary hydroxyl groups are among the several reactive groups found in CS. These functional groups allow for a variety of modifications that produce polymers with distinct characteristics and actions. By promoting the development of particles inside the polymer matrix, these functional groups influence the structural arrangement of the polymer composite by facilitating hydrogen bond formation8,9. CS and its composites find utility across a range of fields such as healthcare, the food industry, energy sectors, agriculture, wastewater treatment, and environmental applications, due to their biodegradable, biocompatible, and non-toxic properties10,11.

Numerous investigations have shown substantial optical, and mechanical characteristic of CS-based composites after incorporation with many fillers. For example, Taspika et al. (2020) previously examined how TiO2/Ag particles affect chitosan film characteristics. Solution casting produced pure chitosan and TiO2/Ag-chitosan hybrid films. Particle agglomeration in the chitosan polymer causes a red-shifted absorption band in the TiO2/Ag-chitosan film’s UV–vis spectra. TiO2/Ag lowers chitosan’s band-gap energy from 5.2 to 4.7 eV12. Hussein et al. (2021) used nanocomposites film with TiO2 nanoparticles, through UV-vis studies investigate a lower band gap and Urbach energy13. Mawlod et al. (2025) prepared chitosan (CS) films doped with oleander dye. They found the direct energy gap of pure CS to be 5.45 eV, which reduced to 2.05 eV in the doped films. This work also confirmed that incorporating the dye enhances the thermal stability of CS14.

In this work, CQDs were hydrothermally synthesized via a bottom-up approach using the natural dye extracted from the blue flag (Iris germanica) flower (FBD), which is rich in carbon-based phytochemicals. The resulting FBD was subsequently incorporated into chitosan (CS) for composite formation. The bottom-up synthesis method utilizes tiny molecules of organic material as carbon precursors to generate sp² carbon domains via techniques like hydrothermal treatment and pyrolysis, allowing for accurate control of particle size and morphology. CQDs represent a distinct category of carbon-based nanomaterials that have garnered significant interest due to their adjustable optical properties, excellent photostability, robust fluorescence, biocompatibility, and minimal cytotoxicity. These characteristics have enabled their use in various fields, such as optoelectronics, bioimaging, photocatalysis, and biomedicine. The discovery of CQDs is attributed to Professor Scrivens and colleagues in 2004, during the purification of carbon nanotubes, marking the onset of extensive research into their synthesis and functional applications15,16. On CQDs, zero-dimensional nanoparticles, particularly nanospheres, are frequently observed with a diameter of less than 10 nm. The composition is primarily carbon, with minor quantities of hydrogen, nitrogen, and oxygen present on the surface. The CQDs contain a graphitic nucleus with sp2 and sp3 hybridization carbon, which is encased in amorphous carbon and exhibits exceptional fluorescence17,18, biocompatibility and functional stability19,20. CQDs are non-toxic, water-soluble, and readily synthesized21. CQDs may be made from food, plants, candle soot, garbage, coal, and chemicals17,22. Natural dyes are organic compounds derived from plant sources such as leaves, roots, bark, flowers, and fruits, and are valued for being renewable, biodegradable, and eco-friendly materials suitable for modern technological applications23. Among these, the flag blue (FB) flower is particularly rich in anthocyanins and flavonoids—water-soluble, pH-sensitive pigments known for their optical activity, chemical stability, and biocompatibility24,25. The focus of our research is to transform waste materials into valuable resources a concept often described as turning ‘trash into treasure’ in materials science. In this context, large quantities of discarded flowers can be repurposed as sustainable raw materials for developing polymer-based composite technologies. This approach not only minimizes environmental waste but also supports the creation of cost-effective and eco-friendly functional materials. In the current project high quality carbon quantum dots (CQDs) has been synthesized from the dyes of waste FB. This research establish that alongside chemical precursors used in the synthesis of CQDs; natural dyes of waste flowers could be considered as an alternative for chemical precursors which are free of cost and environmentally friendly materials. FTIR and XPS study shows that CQDs derived from FBD are enriched with hydroxyl (OH), C-N and carbonyl (C = O) functional groups, and the carbon dot derived from this dye have good fluorescence properties. This method is an effective alternative to traditional ceramic fillers and it is compatible with other dye doped CS composites and the films show strong absorption in UV to visible region.

Experimental section

Flower and polymer materials

Flag blue flowers (Iris germanica), used as the natural precursor for CQD synthesis, were collected locally inside the University of Sulaimani gardens, washed thoroughly, and air-dried at room temperature. Chitosan (medium molecular weight, degree of deacetylation ~ 80–85%) was obtained from a commercial supplier. Analytical grade glacial acetic acid was used to prepare the solvent for dissolving chitosan. Distilled water (DW) was employed throughout the extraction, synthesis, and film preparation processes. All chemicals and reagents, of analytical grade, were used without further purification.

Extraction of natural dye and synthesis of CQDs

The dried flag blue flowers were first ground into a fine powder. An amount of 8 g of the powder was dispersed in 800 mL of distilled water and subjected to heating at 60 °C for 5 h with continuous stirring. The mixture was filtered to remove coarse plant residues, and the filtrate was concentrated by boiling to ~ 200 mL. The concentrated extract was then transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and hydrothermally treated at 200 °C for 8 h. After cooling to room temperature, the reaction mixture was filtered to remove insoluble residues. To ensure purity and homogeneity, the aqueous suspension of carbon quantum dots (CQDs) was further passed through a 2 μm microfilter, yielding a stable colloidal solution of CQDs (Fig. 1).

Extraction of FB Natural dye and synthesis of CQDs using hydrothermal approach.

Preparation of chitosan and CQD–chitosan composite films

Pristine chitosan films were fabricated by the solvent casting technique. Briefly, 0.7 g of chitosan powder was dissolved in 50 mL of 1% (v/v) acetic acid solution under magnetic stirring until a clear solution was obtained. For composite films, the aqueous CQD solution was added dropwise to the chitosan solution under vigorous magnetic stirring at 600 rpm for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion. Two formulations were prepared by adding 15 mL and 30 mL of CQD solution, respectively, to 0.7 g of dissolved chitosan. The resulting homogeneous mixtures were cast onto clean glass Petri dishes and allowed to dry slowly at ambient temperature until transparent, flexible films were obtained. The dried films were carefully peeled off and stored in desiccators for further characterization. The samples were labeled as CSFBCDs0, CSFBCDs1, and CSFBCDs2, where CSFBCDs0 is the pure CS film, and CSFBCDs1 and CSFBCDs2 were incorporated with 15 mL and 30 mL of CQDs, respectively, with average thicknesses of 0.055 mm, 0.090 mm, and 0.092 mm (Fig. 2a). The macroscopic optical characteristics of the as-prepared films were further examined under both visible and ultraviolet (UV) illumination to demonstrate their uniformity and photoreactive nature. As shown in Fig. 2b, under normal visible light, the chitosan–CQD composite films appeared smooth, homogeneous, and slightly amber in color, confirming the absence of surface defects or agglomerated particles. Upon exposure to 365 nm UV light (Fig. 2c), the films exhibited an intense cyan–blue luminescence distributed uniformly across the entire area, verifying the successful incorporation and even dispersion of fluorescent CQDs within the polymeric matrix, so this bright emission indicates strong confinement and efficient excitation–emission behavior of CQDs in the chitosan network, which prevents aggregation-induced quenching.

Characterization

The functional groups present in the CQDs and CS biopolymer composites were examined using an FTIR spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS10) operating within a wavenumber range of 600–4000 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 2 cm⁻¹. UV–Vis absorption spectra were recorded using a Jasco V-570 UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer to evaluate the optical properties of both CQDs and the prepared biocomposite films. The crystallinity of the CQDs and CS-based composites was assessed through X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements performed with a Philips PW1730 diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.15 Å) over a scanning range of 2θ = 10°–80°. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was carried out using a Bruker Analytik GmbH instrument, where ¹H NMR spectra were collected at 75 MHz over a chemical shift range of 0–12 ppm and ¹³C NMR spectra over 0–200 ppm to analyze the molecular structure of the CQDs. Photoluminescence (PL) behavior was investigated by exciting the samples with a helium–cadmium laser, and emission spectra were recorded from 300 to 500 nm using a Jobin Yvon monochromator (France), enabling evaluation of excitation-dependent emission and the determination of quantum yield. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi using a monochromated Al Kα source (1486.6 eV), providing high-resolution spectra, chemical imaging, charge compensation, and Ar⁺ ion sputtering for accurate surface and depth characterization. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was conducted using an FEI Tecnai G2 F30 microscope operating at 300 kV, allowing lattice-resolved imaging of nanostructures; the CQD samples were dispersed onto carbon-coated copper grids to examine particle size, morphology, and crystallinity.

Results and discussion

Structural analysis

HR-TEM study

The morphology and size distribution of the as-prepared carbon quantum dots (CQDs) synthesized from blue flag extract were examined by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM). As shown in Fig. 3a, the CQDs appear as nearly spherical nanoparticles that are uniformly dispersed, with no evidence of large agglomerates, so this observation suggests that the hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 8 h facilitated homogeneous nucleation and restricted excessive growth, thereby yielding nanosized carbon dots with consistent morphology. Such spherical geometry is in good agreement with previous reports on plant-derived CQDs prepared under similar conditions, where the hydrothermal process promotes isotropic growth of carbonaceous nuclei and stabilizes their morphology26,27. The particle size distribution histogram presented in Fig. 3b confirms that the majority of the CQDs fall within the range of 2–4 nm, with an average size of 3.03 ± 0.82 nm. The average particle size was determined from measurements of approximately 120 individual nanoparticles obtained from two independent HRTEM images, ensuring statistical reliability and reproducibility of the reported distribution. The narrow distribution indicates good control of particle growth during the synthesis process. The relatively small particle size can be attributed to the thermally stable phytochemicals in the blue flag extract, which undergo partial carbonization and surface passivation during the hydrothermal process, thereby acting as both reducing and stabilizing agents for the CQDs28. These biomolecules, rich in hydroxyl, amine, and carboxyl functional groups, effectively cap the surface of the forming nanocrystals. This capping process prevents excessive growth and minimizes aggregation, resulting in smaller and well-dispersed CQDs. Similar behavior has been observed in other green-synthesis studies, where naturally occurring metabolites regulate nucleation and enhance surface passivation of CQDs, yielding smaller and more uniform nanostructures than those produced by conventional chemical routes29,30. The structural characteristics of the CQDs were further analyzed by selected area electron diffraction (SAED), as depicted in Fig. 3c. The broad and diffuse concentric rings observed in the pattern confirm the amorphous or poorly crystalline nature of the synthesized CQDs. The absence of sharp diffraction spots rules out the presence of large crystalline domains and instead points to short-range ordering within sp²-hybridized carbon clusters embedded in an amorphous carbon matrix, so this finding is consistent with typical hydrothermally synthesized CQDs, where the relatively mild reaction temperature compared to pyrolytic carbonization leads to incomplete graphitization and the formation of amorphous nanostructures31,32. The combination of small particle size, amorphous carbon structure, and surface passivation by biomolecules derived from the blue flag extract imparts favorable luminescence and solubility properties to the CQDs, highlighting their potential for future applications in sensing, bioimaging, and environmental remediation. Overall, the HRTEM, size distribution, and SAED analyses establish the successful synthesis of spherical and uniformly distributed CQDs with a mean size of approximately 3 nm. Compared with plant-derived CQDs reported previously, such as those obtained from Calotropis gigantea with an average size of ~ 5.7 nm33, the CQDs synthesized from blue flag extract demonstrate enhanced size control, reflecting the stabilizing influence of the phytochemicals present, so these results establish that the green hydrothermal route employed in this study is effective in producing high-quality, ultra-small CQDs with structural features characteristic of amorphous carbon nanodots.

Structural analysis and crystallite size–microstrain evaluation

The structural characteristics of the CQDs synthesized from blue flag extract were evaluated using powder XRD with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The XRD diffractogram exhibits a broad halo centered at 2θ ≈ 20–25°, which is characteristic of amorphous carbon domains formed by disordered sp² clusters and short-range graphitic ordering34,35. This diffuse feature indicates that the CQDs are not fully crystalline but instead possess a hybrid amorphous–polycrystalline structure, typical of carbon dots synthesized under mild hydrothermal conditions. In addition to the broad halo, several distinct reflections are observed at 2θ = 28.29°, 32.75°, 40.36°, 50.01°, 58.33°, 66.01°, and 73.38°, suggesting the coexistence of graphitic and defective carbon phases within the CQDs. Among these, the major reflections at 28.29°, 40.36°, 50.01°, and 58.33° can be indexed to the (002), (100), (102), and (103) planes, respectively as shows in Fig. 4, consistent with mixed sp²/sp³ carbon phases reported in previous studies of biomass-derived CQDs36,37. The (002) reflection is associated with the interlayer spacing of stacked graphitic domains, while the (100) reflection arises from in-plane ordering of sp² carbon. The higher-angle reflections (102 and 103) are attributed to diamond-like sp³ contributions, reflecting the coexistence of both graphitic and defective carbon phases. Additional weak peaks at 32.75°, 66.01°, and 73.38° remain unassigned, and are likely due to either residual inorganic constituents from the plant precursor or defective carbon domains rather than ordered graphite. Crystallite sizes (D) were calculated from the peak broadening using the Debye–Scherrer equation , where K = 0.9. The estimated values fall in the range of 7–53 nm depending on the reflection, as shown in Table 1, with the most representative graphitic planes (002, 100, 102, 103) yielding sizes between 7.7 and 26.2 nm, so these nanoscale dimensions are consistent with the small sp² clusters observed in HRTEM analysis and highlight the limited crystalline ordering within the CQDs. In parallel, lattice microstrain (ε) values were obtained from a single-line Williamson–Hall analysis, using the relation. The calculated microstrains fall in the range of 1.55–10.59 × 10⁻³, confirming the presence of significant lattice distortions . Such microstrain originates from surface defects, heteroatom incorporation, and the confinement of small carbon domains within an amorphous matrix, which disrupt long-range periodicity and broaden diffraction peaks37. The relatively high microstrain (1.55–10.59 × 10⁻³) in these biomass-derived CQDs increases defect-related localized states, which accounts for the observed Urbach tail growth, band-edge narrowing, higher refractive index/dielectric loss, and reduced impedance, while surface passivation by O/N functionalities sustains colloidal and photostability. The d-spacing values derived from Bragg’s law further support this interpretation. The interlayer spacing for the (002) reflection was found to be ~ 0.315 nm, slightly larger than that of bulk graphite (0.335 nm), suggesting incomplete graphitization and the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups at the CQD surface. Similarly, the (100), (102), and (103) planes correspond to spacings of 0.223, 0.182, and 0.158 nm, respectively, in agreement with previously reported graphitic carbon lattices38,39, so these values reinforce the presence of nanocrystalline sp² clusters embedded in a disordered carbon framework. In summary, the XRD analysis confirms that the CQDs synthesized from blue flag extract exhibit a polycrystalline–amorphous hybrid structure, characterized by graphitic sp² domains alongside diamond-like sp³ contributions, confined within an amorphous carbon matrix. The small crystallite sizes, broadening of reflections, and relatively high microstrain values reflect the influence of the hydrothermal synthesis route and the phytochemical-rich precursor, which favor partial graphitization, defect incorporation, and stabilization of ultra-small domains. Such structural heterogeneity is advantageous for optical and electronic applications, since the combination of sp² clusters and functionalized amorphous carbon domains contributes to tunable photoluminescence, high surface reactivity, and improved solubility.

XPS analysis

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to characterize the surface chemistry and bonding configurations of the CQDs. The survey spectrum (Fig. 5a) displays the three characteristic photoemission lines of C 1 s (~ 285 eV), N 1 s (~ 400 eV), and O 1 s (~ 532 eV), confirming that the dots are primarily composed of carbon with oxygen- and nitrogen-bearing functionalities40–42. The inset quantification indicates carbon as the dominant element with minor but non-negligible nitrogen and oxygen, the corresponding atomic ratios give N/C ≈ 0.08 and O/C ≈ 0.21 (inset, Fig. 5a), consistent with N incorporation and oxygenated groups originating from the phytochemicals present in the blue-flag extract and from partial oxidation during hydrothermal carbonization43,44. The CQDs exhibit N/C = 0.08 and O/C = 0.21, while their photoluminescence shows λem = 415 nm at λex = 330 nm with QY = 7.41%. Within this composition, nitrogen (pyridinic/pyrrolic) likely introduces emissive defect states, whereas oxygenated groups (C = O/C–O) provide surface passivation and additional band-tail states, jointly supporting blue emission with moderate QY. Although a full quantitative trend (e.g., QY or spectral shift vs. N/C or O/C) demands a series of CQDs with deliberately varied heteroatom content, the present values substantiate that heteroatom enrichment (N, C = O/C–O) coexists with the observed PL efficiency and emission energy in this system. Such heteroatom content is typical for green-synthesized CQDs and is known to modulate electronic structure, colloidal stability, and photoluminescence through surface states and dopant-induced defect levels45–47. High-resolution C 1 s spectra (Fig. 5b) were deconvoluted into three components centered at ~ 284.6 eV (C = C/C–C, sp² carbon), ~ 285.7 eV (C–O/C–N, sp³ carbon bonded to O/N), and ~ 288.5–288.8 eV (C = O/C = N, carbonyl/imine/amide)48,49. The prominence of the sp² component evidences the presence of small graphitic domains, in agreement with XRD/SAED. Quantitatively, the C 1 s sp² (C = C/C–C) component accounts for approximately 51% of the total C 1 s peak area (Table S1), which is consistent with the graphitic signatures observed by XRD—namely the (002)/(100) reflections and nanometer-scale coherent lengths—and with the short-range lattice fringes seen in TEM/SAED for ~ 3 nm CQDs. While the substantial C–O/C–N and C = O/C = N contributions reflect abundant oxygenated and nitrogenous moieties (hydroxyl/ether/epoxy, amine/amide, carbonyl/carboxyl/imine) on the dot surface, so these functionalities are expected when polyphenols, amino acids, and organic acids from plant extracts act as carbon sources and in-situ passivating/capping agents during hydrothermal synthesis, thereby arresting growth and preventing aggregation50,51. The relatively large fraction of high-binding-energy carbonyl/imine species (C = O/C = N) is particularly important because such electron-withdrawing groups create surface states within the bandgap that can serve as radiative recombination centers, often shifting emission toward longer wavelengths (“red-shift”) and enhancing solvatochromic response52. The N 1 s envelope (Fig. 5c) resolves into two chemically distinct species assigned to pyridinic-N (~ 398.6–399.5 eV) and pyrrolic-N (~ 400.0–401.0 eV), in line with N incorporation at edge/defect sites of sp² domains and in five-membered rings, respectively42,53. Pyridinic-N acts as an electron-donating site that tunes the local electronic density and introduces n-type states, whereas pyrrolic-N contributes to localized defect levels and can improve quantum yield by facilitating radiative transitions, together they are widely recognized as key contributors to excitation-dependent photoluminescence in N-doped CQDs54. The coexistence of both nitrogen configurations corroborates the mixed sp²/sp³ microstructure inferred from XRD and TEM and is consistent with mild hydrothermal graphitization pathways where amine-containing phytochemicals are incorporated into developing carbon nuclei55,56. The O 1 s spectrum (Fig. 5 d) is well described by two components at ~ 531.2 eV (C = O in carbonyl/amide/carboxylate) and ~ 532.8–533.4 eV (C–O in hydroxyl/ether/epoxy)57, also the significant population of C = O together with C–O underscores extensive surface oxygenation, which rationalizes the excellent water dispersibility observed experimentally (deprotonated carboxyl/hydroxyl groups confer negative surface charge) and aligns with the amorphous/poorly crystalline carbon matrix detected by XRD/SAED. The detailed peak-fitting parameters, including binding energies, FWHM values, and integrated areas for all C 1 s, N 1 s, and O 1 s components, are summarized in Table S1 (Supporting Information). These quantitative parameters confirm the reliability of the deconvolution and support the proposed chemical-state assignments discussed above. Taken together, the survey composition and the deconvolution of C 1 s, N 1 s, and O 1 s regions demonstrate that the blue-flag–derived CQDs possess sp²-rich carbon cores decorated by abundant C–O/C = O and C–N/C = N functionalities, with both pyridinic- and pyrrolic-type nitrogen incorporated at edge/defect sites, so this heteroatom-rich, passivated surface explains the dots’ small size and good colloidal stability (phytochemical capping), as well as their expected optical behavior (defect-/surface-state emission and potential red-shift with increasing carbonyl/imine content).

Fluorescence spectra

The fluorescence spectra of the CQDs were obtained for excitation wavelengths between 300 and 390 nm. Figure 6 clearly illustrates that the optimal emission peak is observed at 415 nm when excited at 330 nm. The emission intensity increases with excitation wavelength up to 330 nm, reaching a maximum at 415 nm, and then gradually decreases at higher excitation wavelengths. This behavior reflects the excitation-dependent emission typical of carbon dots, where different emissive sites and surface defect states contribute variably to the overall fluorescence. The intensity enhancement at lower excitation energies is attributed to band-edge transitions, while the subsequent decrease results from reduced excitation efficiency and nonradiative relaxation processes. Furthermore, the excitation spectrum at 415 nm further confirms that 330 nm is the most effective excitation wavelength (Fig. 7 A). To further evaluate the fluorescence properties of the CQDs, their QY was determined to be 7.41%, using Fluorescein (QY = 95%) as the reference standard. Taken together, the N/C and O/C ratios and the 415 nm/7.41% PL response are consistent with surface/defect-state emission governed by pyridinic/pyrrolic N and carbonyl/ether groups; future work will modulate these ratios to derive quantitative structure–property relationships (e.g., QY vs. N/C and Stokes shift vs. C = O fraction).

Compared with other biomass- and natural-dye-derived carbon quantum dots (CQDs), the blue flag (Iris germanica) flower–based CQDs synthesized in this study exhibit a moderate quantum yield (7.41%) with strong excitation-dependent emission and excellent colloidal stability. A recent comprehensive analysis by Usman and Cheng58, reported that the optical efficiency of biomass-derived CQDs varies significantly depending on precursor composition, carbonization degree, and synthesis temperature. For example, rose-flower-derived CQDs synthesized hydrothermally at 200 °C for 120 min achieved a QY of 43–48%59, while banana-peel-derived CQDs prepared under similar temperature but longer duration (200 °C, 1440 min) showed a lower QY of 23%60. Agarose-waste-derived CQDs produced at 450 °C for 240 min exhibited one of the highest QYs (62%)61, whereas lignin-based CQDs synthesized at 240 °C for 360 min displayed only 10%62. Other reported natural feedstocks, such as mustard flowers (18.4%)63, sugarcane bagasse (14.5%)64, and Polyalthia longifolia leaves (22%)65, further demonstrate the wide variation in luminescence performance depending on the precursor’s organic composition and synthesis severity. These variations largely arise from differences in the degree of graphitization, heteroatom content, and surface functionalization achieved during synthesis. High-QY systems such as those from agarose waste61 and rose flowers59 typically require either high-temperature treatments or prolonged hydrothermal durations, which enhance carbonization efficiency and extend π–π conjugation. However, such conditions also increase energy consumption and often destroy fragile oxygenated or nitrogen-containing functional groups that are crucial for biocompatibility and aqueous stability. Additionally, several of the high-QY systems reported in the literature rely on polymer-assisted passivation or dopant incorporation—such as polyethyleneimine (PEI) or polyethylene glycol (PEG)—to suppress nonradiative recombination pathways. While these modifications can elevate QY above 30%, they introduce synthetic reagents that undermine the “green” character of the synthesis and may pose environmental or cytotoxic concerns. In contrast, the present blue flag flower–derived CQDs were synthesized through a single-step, mild hydrothermal process (200 °C, 480 min) without any external dopants or surface-passivating polymers. Although the resulting QY (7.41%) is lower than that of agarose- or rose-derived systems, the synthesis route offers significant advantages in sustainability, simplicity, and functional stability. The moderate temperature preserves oxygenated and nitrogenous phytochemical residues on the CQD surface, which act as natural passivating agents and stabilize the nanodots in aqueous media. The presence of abundant hydroxyl, amine, and carbonyl groups—confirmed by FTIR and XPS analyses—facilitates radiative recombination through defect-state emission while maintaining high solubility and dispersion stability. Furthermore, the use of Iris germanica as a carbon precursor ensures a renewable, non-toxic, and biodegradable source material consistent with current trends in sustainable nanomaterial development. Therefore, although the quantum yield of the blue flag flower–derived CQDs is moderate compared to some highly engineered systems, the combination of green synthesis, strong emission stability, and intrinsic surface functionality represents a significant advantage. This work provides an environmentally benign and reproducible approach to CQD fabrication that aligns with circular-economy principles, balancing optical efficiency with ecological responsibility and demonstrating that natural precursors such as blue flag flower dye can serve as efficient and sustainable alternatives to chemically modified CQDs. Lastly, The photoluminescence stability of CQDs in aqueous environments is a crucial factor in evaluating their practical applicability. To examine this, the fluorescence emission intensity of the CQDs was visually observed in solutions with varying pH values under UV illumination. As shown in Fig. 7B, the fluorescence intensity of the CQDs changes with pH. The synthesized CQDs exhibit good stability across a pH range of 3.0 to 11.0, indicating their potential for a wide range of applications.

FTIR study

The FTIR spectra of the neat CQDs, pristine chitosan, and the CQD–chitosan films (Fig. 8a–d) reveal a progressive evolution of the polymer–nanodot interface that accounts for the optical improvements observed in the doped films. The CQDs show a broad ν(O–H)/ν(N–H) envelope in the 3200–3300 cm⁻¹ region together with weak aliphatic ν(C–H) at 2960–2865 cm⁻¹, a carbonyl band near 1710 cm⁻¹, and intense C–O/C–O–C features between 1170 and 1040 cm⁻¹—signatures of an oxygen-/nitrogen-functionalized molecular shell surrounding sp² carbon cores typical of hydrothermal, plant-derived dots66,67. Pristine chitosan exhibits the expected polysaccharide pattern, a very broad, hydrogen-bonded ν(O–H) and ν(N–H) band centered near 3250–3100 cm⁻¹, residual amide I/II vibrations at ~ 1630 and ~ 1535–1550 cm⁻¹, δ(CH₂/CH₃) near ~ 1400 cm⁻¹, a glycosidic ν(C–O–C) band at ~ 1150 cm⁻¹, and a strong C–O/C–O–C envelope at ~ 1080–1020 cm⁻¹ arising from the glucosamine backbone68,69. Introducing CQDs into the chitosan matrix generates three reproducible FTIR changes that diagnose interfacial bonding, firstly, the hydrogen-bonded ν(O–H)/ν(N–H) envelope broadens and shifts slightly to lower wavenumber with increasing CQD content (b → c → d), evidencing stronger H-bond networks formed between CQD carbonyl/phenolic groups (C = O, C–OH) and chitosan –NH₂/–OH sites70,71. Second, the amide region intensifies and subtly shifts (amide I/II), consistent with electrostatic complexation between deprotonated –COO⁻ groups on CQDs and protonated –NH₃⁺ sites of chitosan under the mildly acidic solvent-casting conditions, together with ion–dipole/H-bond interactions around C = O/C = N functionalities at CQD edge defects72,73, thirdly, the saccharide fingerprint (1150 and 1080–1020 cm⁻¹) becomes more structured and slightly displaced, indicating perturbation of the glycosidic environment and partial disruption of the intrinsic chitosan H-bond network as the polymer chains coordinate and wrap around the functionalized CQD surface74, so these spectral signatures strengthen monotonically from the 15-mL to the 30-mL CQD loading, confirming a concentration-dependent increase in interfacial contact area and bonding density. Mechanistically, the interface can be described as a noncovalent, percolating hybrid layer in which (i) hydrogen bonds (CQD C = O/C–OH ↔ CS –NH₂/–OH), (ii) Coulombic pairs (–COO⁻(CQD) ↔ –NH₃⁺(CS)), and (iii) secondary C–N···H/ion–dipole interactions at CQD defect sites collectively anchor the dots and reorganize local polymer packing75. Covalent amidation (–COOH + –NH₂) is not required under the mild processing used, if it occurs at isolated defects it would contribute weakly to amide-band growth without eliminating the CQD carbonyl shoulder—consistent with the spectra, so the net effect is a core–shell–like interphase, quantum-confined sp² cores supply π-states, while the heteroatom-rich shell provides dense H-bond/ionic contacts to the biopolymer and a manifold of surface electronic states (Fig. 9)76, so these molecular-level interactions explain the optical enhancements of the doped films relative to pristine chitosan. Specific anchoring suppresses CQD aggregation, preserving quantum confinement and maximizing radiative recombination from surface/edge states, which yields strong, stable photoluminescence from the films2,3,12,76. The interfacial H-bond/ionic network reduces chitosan’s crystalline coherence and increases electronic coupling between CQD π-domains and the polysaccharide matrix, thereby (i) red-shifting the absorption edge and narrowing the effective optical gap through additional π–π* and n–π* pathways and defect-state tails (Urbach states), and (ii) raising the refractive index/extinction coefficient by introducing polarizable sp² domains and increasing the density of localized states77,78. As loading increases (c → d), the denser interphase supplies more surface states and stronger charge-transfer/energy-transfer channels, which is consistent with the observed tunability of the films’ UV–Vis and dielectric responses, thus, the FTIR-resolved interfacial chemistry directly underpins the macroscopic optoelectronic improvements of the CQD–chitosan composites. A detailed summary of FTIR peak positions, their corresponding functional group assignments, and related chemical interpretations is provided in Table S2. In summary, the FTIR results clearly confirm the successful incorporation of CQDs into the chitosan matrix through a combination of hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and perturbation of the polysaccharide network. These spectroscopic signatures correlate strongly with the observed modifications in optical band gap, refractive index, and dielectric behavior, demonstrating that the CQD–CS interphase plays a central role in tuning the optoelectronic performance of the films. The FTIR analysis therefore provides direct molecular-level evidence supporting the structure–property relationships established throughout this work. These findings further validate the suitability of CQD doping as an effective strategy for engineering chitosan-based optical materials.

Dielectric constant and localized density of states (N/m*) study

The dielectric constant is a critical optical parameter that governs the ability of a material to store and polarize under the influence of an external electromagnetic field, and thereby influences the fundamental optical transitions in polymeric and hybrid photonic materials. The dielectric response is commonly divided into two contributions: the real part (εr​) and the imaginary part (εi) of the complex dielectric function (ε* = εr − iεi). The real component quantifies the extent of electromagnetic wave attenuation through polarization, whereas the imaginary part represents the energy dissipated due to absorption processes135. Conclusions

In conclusions, CQDs were successfully synthesized via a green hydrothermal approach using flag blue flower extract as a natural precursor. The CQDs showed nano-scale features as confirmed by XRD and HR-TEM with abundant oxygen- and nitrogen-containing surface groups as evidenced by XPS. was performed in distilled water without supplementary pH adjustment. The nearly neutral conditions likely enabled a mild carbonization pathway, preserving surface functional groups containing oxygen and nitrogen, which influence the optical properties and solubility of the resulting CQDs. Although improve desired interaction between CQDs and CS matrix. When incorporated into chitosan through solvent casting, FTIR analysis revealed strong hydrogen bonding, electrostatic pairing, and ion–dipole interactions between CQD surface functionalities (–COOH, –OH, –C = O, –NH2) and the amine and hydroxyl groups of chitosan chains, so these molecular-level interactions not only modified the structural configuration of the films but also disrupted the intrinsic hydrogen-bonding network of pristine chitosan, creating a more polarizable and electronically active hybrid interface. The incorporation of CQDs significantly enhanced the optical properties of the composite films compared to pure chitosan. The doped systems illustrated improved light absorption, reduced band gap energy, and increased refractive index, all of which are linked to the higher density of electronic states and the effective coupling between the π-domains of CQDs and the polysaccharide backbone. Increase of π-domains through the host CS via FBCDs insertion explains the strong absorption of CS doped films. These improvements confirm the ability of naturally derived CQDs to act as functional nanofillers that impart tunable optoelectronic properties to biopolymers. This improvement in polarizability results in a more robust interaction between the incoming electromagnetic radiation and the material, thereby increasing the localized density of states (N/m*), dielectric values at lower wavelengths, plasma angular frequency, plasma energy, Fermi energy, and Penn energy. Overall, this study demonstrates the viability of flag blue flower extract as a sustainable precursor for CQD synthesis and highlights the potential of CQD to synthesis of chitosan composites as an eco-friendly, cost-effective materials for optoelectronic and photonic applications.
