Engineering a self-protecting dermal filler: active hyaluronidase inhibition extends the in vivo persistence of cross-linked hyaluronic acid hydrogels
Authors: Kamil Budak, Umran Aydemir Sezer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a naturally occurring polysaccharide with high biocompatibility and an exceptional capacity for water retention, making it a critical component in drug delivery and soft tissue applications. However, native HA exhibits a very short in vivo half-life due to rapid enzymatic degradation. We developed a cross-linked hyaluronic acid (CLHA) hydrogel incorporating active hyaluronidase inhibitors to delay enzymatic degradation and prolong its in vivo persistence. The formulations underwent physicochemical characterisation and rheological profiling to assess their performance. Additionally, in vitro cytotoxicity, in vivo biodegradation, systemic toxicity, and pharmacokinetic evaluations were conducted. Evaluation of several inhibitors identified sodium aurothiomalate (AHM) at a 0.6% (w/w) concentration (AHM0.6) as the most effective formulation, providing sustained resistance against enzymatic erosion while maintaining a favourable safety profile. By limiting hyaluronidase activity, the hydrogel preserves its viscoelastic properties and improves the functional performance of hyaluronic acid. Overall, these findings suggest that localized enzymatic inhibition is a viable strategy for enhancing the durability and performance of HA-based biomaterials in clinical procedures such as soft tissue augmentation.

Introduction

Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a prominent linear polysaccharide that serves critical structural and biological roles within the human extracellular matrix. In recent years, HA has gained considerable attention for a wide range of biomedical applications.1,2 Owing to these properties, HA has attracted growing interest in drug delivery applications. HA can retain a large amount of water (up to 1000 times its weight). It also exhibits excellent biocompatibility and is generally non-immunogenic.3

HA mimics the natural cellular microenvironment by forming a three-dimensional matrix, providing space for cell proliferation while enabling the delivery of therapeutic molecules through this network.4–7 Due to its viscoelastic properties, HA has become the material of choice for a wide range of clinical applications, ranging from viscosupplementation to dermal fillers.8–12

Despite its clinical versatility, native HA is characterized by a rapid turnover rate, typically undergoing complete degradation within 24 hours due to its high susceptibility to endogenous enzymes.9,13 The degradation of hyaluronic acid is primarily mediated by enzymes in the hyaluronidase (HAase) family, which cleave the polymer chains at their glycosidic linkages.1,6,14 To address this limitation, chemical crosslinking is commonly employed to improve structural stability and reduce enzymatic breakdown, thereby extending the persistence of the filler within soft tissues. This results in hydrogels with enhanced durability and mechanical strength, enabling prolonged residence within soft tissues.2,15 These cross-linked hydrogels provide a widely used basis for dermal fillers with favourable mechanical properties and tissue integration.16–18

Commercially prevalent hyaluronic acid technologies typically involve the formation of covalent cross-linkages between polymer chains via 1,4-butanediol diglycidyl ether (BDDE).2,11,17 This principle has been adopted by market-leading formulations, such as Restylane® Juvederm®, Belotero®, and Teosyal®, all of which are BDDE-crosslinked elastic hydrogels.16,19

Although cross-linking extends the residence time of the gel, excessive cross-linking increases rigidity, complicates injection, and elevates the risk of irritation due to residual cross-linking agents.11,18,20,21 Among the severe side effects reported for dermal filler products are inflammation and swelling at the injection site, delayed-onset reactions, bruising, nodules, and granuloma formation.12,22

Good clinical performance of these materials is directly linked to their rheological properties.11,18,23 The elastic (G′) and loss (G″) moduli of filler materials determine their lifting capacity, mouldability, and resistance to deformation caused by facial movements.24–26 Although highly cross-linked hydrogels may exhibit favorable initial rheological properties, they are ultimately degraded by enzymes. This degradation leads to a progressive loss of mechanical integrity, necessitating repeated treatments to maintain clinical outcomes.11,15,27

A complementary approach is to incorporate bioactive agents into the hydrogel, enabling controlled release while reducing potential side effects. By developing a degradable hydrogel filler that resists enzymatic degradation through hyaluronidase inhibition, the in vivo longevity of fillers, as well as patient safety and treatment success, can be significantly enhanced.

In this study, we developed a drug-loaded hydrogel system to extend material longevity and improve safety. This system incorporates a hyaluronidase inhibitor into the hydrogel matrix, which targets hyaluronidase (HAase), the endogenous enzyme responsible for hyaluronic acid degradation.2,28 The incorporation of such active components enables localized inhibition of hydrogel degradation, thereby delaying enzymatic erosion and providing a controlled release mechanism that may reduce side effects associated with hydrogel filler applications.

A comprehensive characterisation program was conducted for the inhibitor-loaded crosslinked hyaluronic acid hydrogels, including physicochemical analysis, rheological evaluation, in vitro drug release, cytotoxicity testing, and in vivo biodegradation studies. Among the tested HAase inhibitors, the formulation containing 0.6% sodium aurothiomalate (AHM0.6) was the most effective, showing the strongest inhibition of enzyme-mediated hydrogel degradation while maintaining a favourable safety profile. The developed AHM-loaded hydrogel may have potential relevance for long-acting dermal filler applications. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that enzymatically protected HA hydrogels resist enzymatic degradation and enhance mechanical robustness in HA-based medical applications. Consequently, this system may provide an alternative strategy with improved longevity and safety for aesthetic applications.

Inhibitors used in the study

Initially, six active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) representing different therapeutic classes were evaluated for their compatibility with the CLHA hydrogel and their stability under the applied sterilisation conditions. The selected compounds included the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs nimesulide (NMS), indomethacin (IDM), and celecoxib (CXB); the antibacterial agents sulfamethoxazole (SFX) and neomycin sulfate (NMY); and the gold-based anti-inflammatory compound sodium aurothiomalate (AHM).

After the drug-loaded hydrogels were prepared, they were subjected to sterilisation (15 min. at 121 °C). Visual examination following steam sterilisation of the preparations containing NMS, IDM and SFX showed significant physical instability problems. Although the sterilisation temperature was below the melting points of NMS (147 °C) and IDM (158 °C), the elevated temperature may have transiently increased the apparent solubility of these drugs in the hydrogel matrix. Subsequent cooling may then have induced supersaturation and recrystallization.29

The solubility of IDM is known to vary markedly between acidic and neutral conditions. In addition, SFX has been reported to exhibit a high tendency toward aggregation and crystallization near pH 5.7, where its solubility is minimal.30,31 For this reason, changes occurring in the hydrogel's microenvironment during thermal treatment may have shifted local conditions into this low-solubility range and led to instability of the active ingredient within the hydrogel system.

Upon cooling after sterilisation, the reduced drug solubility likely promoted supersaturation within the hydrogel network, leading to the formation of drug aggregates and crystallites. The precipitation observed in the SFX formulation may also be related to the poor solubility of sulfonamides at low pH.

Owing to irreversible physical instabilities, including crystallization and inhomogeneity, the SFX, IDM, and NMS containing formulations were not subjected to further characterisation. In contrast, formulations containing NMY, CXB, and AHM maintained a homogeneous distribution and preserved their physicochemical integrity within the hydrogel matrix after sterilisation. Consequently, subsequent experiments were conducted using these three compounds.

Rheological properties, in vitro degradation and cytotoxicity assessments

The mechanical properties of HA filler materials before and after injection, as well as after implantation in soft tissue, are important determinants of their long-term performance and safety.

The rheological properties of these materials influence both product selection and the choice of injection technique in clinical practice.24Fig. 2 summarises the rheological properties and cytotoxicity results of HAase inhibitor-free CLHA and inhibitor-loaded hydrogel fillers.

The viscoelastic properties of gels containing the active ingredient at certain concentrations differed from those of the control group. While no significant differences were observed for CXB0.2 and CXB0.4, the rheological properties (G′ and G″) of CXB0.6 were found to be significantly higher than those of the CLHA control (p < 0.05). No significant differences in rheological properties were observed between AHM-containing formulations and the CLHA control (p > 0.05). Conversely, the rheological values for all filler materials containing NMY were lower, presenting a significant difference compared to the control group (Table S1) (p < 0.01). The viscoelastic values of all samples prepared with the NMY HAase inhibitor were considerably lower than those of CLHA. Although the NMY inhibitor formed a homogeneous mixture with HA at a suitable pH, it is hypothesized that the thermal processing step may have altered the interaction between the NMY and the HA network within the gel structure. As the rheological results indicated that these hydrogel fillers lacked suitable mechanical properties, hydrogels containing NMY were excluded from further in vitro degradation and cytotoxicity studies.

The in vitro degradation of the hydrogel fillers was evaluated in the presence of HAase an enzyme that degrades hyaluronic acid. Incorporation of HAase inhibitors into the cross-linked hydrogel structure was expected to improve resistance to enzymatic degradation. For these fillers, resistance to enzymatic degradation is essential for prolonged clinical performance. The in vitro degradation study was conducted using the Bitter carbazole method,32 which is widely used for quantifying glucuronic acid released during HA degradation. The method is based on quantifying glucuronic acid (GlcA) released during polysaccharide degradation. The GlcA standard curve obtained using this method is shown in (Fig. S1).

Hyaluronic acid-based hydrogels degraded at varying rates upon exposure to HAase over a 48 h period, depending on the type and concentration of the HAase inhibitor present (Fig. 3). While samples containing 0.2% and 0.4% CXB did not differ significantly from the control group (p > 0.05), formulations containing 0.6% inhibitor exhibited significantly reduced degradation (p < 0.05).

In vitro degradation profiles of hydrogel fillers.

In samples treated with the AHM HAase inhibitor, each concentration differed significantly from both the control group and the other AHM formulations. The modified hydrogels exhibited greater resistance to enzymatic degradation than the unmodified control at a 95% confidence level, although no significant differences were observed at the 48 h time point.

The 32 h degradation value for AHM0.4 was significantly lower than that of the control group (p < 0.05). The AHM0.6 formulation differed significantly from the control and all other treatment groups at all evaluated time points (p < 0.05).

In this study, AHM concentrations were limited to 0.6% following an optimisation-based approach. This decision was primarily driven by safety considerations, as higher gold-based drug loads may carry risks of systemic side effects, such as systemic accumulation and renal adverse effects.33

Furthermore, the potential for excessive drug loading to adversely alter the hydrogel's essential viscoelastic and injectability properties was taken into account.

The order of degradation resistance among the AHM formulations was AHM0.6 > AHM0.4 > AHM0.2, while for the CXB formulations it was CXB0.6 > CXB0.4 = CXB0.2. A significant difference was observed when comparing the highest concentrations of the two inhibitors (CXB0.6 vs. AHM0.6). Although AHM0.6 demonstrated the greatest resistance to enzymatic degradation, CXB0.6 may also represent a candidate formulation for further consideration. Representative images from the degradation studies are provided in (Fig. S2).

The effects of the hydrogels on RAW 264.7 macrophage and L929 fibroblast viability were evaluated over a 7 days period. Cell viability profiles for RAW 264.7 macrophages and L929 fibroblasts are given in (Fig. 2B and C) respectively.

In RAW264.7 macrophages, the viability of cells exposed to the drug-loaded hydrogels gradually decreased over time compared with the control group (Fig. 2B). This reduction was observed for both AHM- and CXB-containing formulations, indicating that drug incorporation reduced macrophage metabolic activity/viability under the experimental conditions evaluated. No conclusions regarding anti-inflammatory activity were drawn, as inflammatory cytokines or macrophage polarization markers were not assessed in the present study. Among the AHM-containing formulations, AHM0.4 showed the lowest RAW 264.7 metabolic activity on day 7, while the other formulations showed a less pronounced effect. These findings indicate reduced metabolic activity of RAW 264.7 macrophages under the tested conditions. As cytokine secretion, macrophage phenotype, and inflammatory signaling pathways were not assessed, no conclusions regarding anti-inflammatory or immunomodulatory activity can be drawn.

In contrast, L929 fibroblasts exhibited a more favourable response to the formulations (Fig. 2C). On day 3, the drug-loaded hydrogels showed slightly lower cell viability than the control, which may be associated with the initial cellular adaptation to the drug-loaded matrix and early release kinetics. By day 5, the CXB-treated hydrogels showed improved fibroblast compatibility, with CXB0.4 and CXB0.6 performing better than CXB0.2 (p < 0.05). In the AHM-treated group, AHM0.2 and AHM0.4 showed no significant difference compared with the control, whereas AHM0.6 resulted in lower cell viability. By day 7, fibroblast viability increased in both AHM- and CXB-loaded hydrogels compared with the control (p < 0.05), indicating that these formulations were able to support cell viability/proliferation over time. This trend is consistent with the enhanced enzymatic stability of HAase inhibitor-loaded hydrogels, which helps preserve structural integrity and provides a more stable microenvironment for sustained cell growth compared to the rapidly degrading control hydrogel.

Given the superior stability observed in the degradation studies (Fig. 3), together with the observed reduction in macrophage metabolic activity and the ability to support fibroblast viability over time, CXB0.6 and AHM0.6 were selected as the lead formulations for subsequent experiments. Accordingly, CXB0.6 and AHM0.6 were carried forward for subsequent experiments.

Chemical characterisation of drug-loaded hydrogels

FTIR and NMR

Cross-linking of HA with BDDE involves the formation of ether bonds between HA chains through nucleophilic attack of hydroxyl groups on the epoxide groups of BDDE under alkaline conditions.9 The chemical structure of the cross-linked hydrogel was characterized using complementary FTIR and 1H-NMR analyses. The C–H stretching vibration of the aliphatic chains formed by BDDE cross-linking gives a band around 2900 cm−1 in the FTIR spectrum. In the NMR spectrum, the appearance of a signal at δ 1.60 ppm, assigned to BDDE-derived methylene protons, was consistent with the incorporation of BDDE-derived moieties into the HA network. The integral of this signal, normalized to the HA N-acetyl methyl signal at δ 1.95 ppm, was used to calculate the degree of modification (%MoD).

Fig. 4A and B show the FT-IR spectra of the hydrogel fillers. The linear hyaluronic acid (LHA) exhibits a broad hydroxyl (−OH) absorption band at 3340 cm−1, a carboxylate (COO−) asymmetric stretching band at 1620 cm−1, and a carboxylate (COO−) symmetric stretching band at 1402 cm−1. Following cross-linking with BDDE, the O–H band near 3340 cm−1 became narrower and less intense, while an absorption band associated with ether (C–O–C) linkages became more apparent near 1320 cm−1. These spectral changes are consistent with the formation of a BDDE-cross-linked HA network. A distinct band near 2920 cm−1 appeared in the CLHA spectrum and was assigned to the C–H stretching vibrations of aliphatic –CH2- groups introduced by BDDE. This band provided a useful spectral marker for the cross-linked HA matrix. The aliphatic nature of the cross-linking agent BDDE allows this region to be used as a convenient indicator region for the cross-linked gel matrix. The integral values used to calculate the degree of modification (% MoD), based on the BDDE-derived methylene signal at δ 1.60 ppm and the HA N-acetyl methyl signal at δ 1.95 ppm, are shown in the supplementary 1H-NMR spectra (Fig. S3–S8). The CLHA spectrum reveals the δ 1.95 ppm N-acetyl signal and the δ 3 ppm to δ 4 ppm saccharide region unchanged; however, a new peak at δ 1.6 ppm, assigned to the methylene protons of the BDDE, is visible. The AHM reference spectrum displayed characteristic aliphatic signals in the δ 2.5–3.0 ppm region (Fig. S7). The % MoD values ranged from 15% to 18% across all formulations, indicating consistent BDDE cross-linking of the HA network (Table S2). The small differences between groups may reflect spectral overlap and the distinct physicochemical properties of the incorporated active agents.

The morphology, surface topography, and internal structure of the drug-loaded hydrogel were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). SEM images (Fig. 5A–D) revealed a porous network structure that may facilitate drug distribution within the hydrogel matrix. The morphology of the LHA hydrogel appears as a non-porous, layered sheet, while CLHA has a hydrogel structure which is porous due to the cross-linked network (Fig. 5A and B). The active ingredients CXB and AHM are seen to be embedded within the hydrogel filler without disrupting its cross-linked structure (Fig. 5C and D). Fig. S9 confirms the uniform porous morphology of the drug-loaded hydrogels without evident phase separation. Fig. 5E shows the water retention capacities of the hydrogel samples. As expected, the equilibrium swelling ratio of LHA is significantly higher than that of the other hydrogel fillers.

The high equilibrium swelling ratio of LHA may be attributed to the high mobility of its non-cross-linked HA chains, which facilitates water uptake. The equilibrium swelling ratio of CLHA is 36.7% lower than that of LHA (p < 0.001). Both CXB0.6 and AHM0.6 formulations exhibited a decrease in equilibrium swelling ratio compared to CLHA (p < 0.05). CXB0.6 has a lower equilibrium swelling ratio than AHM0.6 (p < 0.05). The relatively atypically lower equilibrium swelling ratio observed for CXB0.6 likely reflects the hydrophobic character of celecoxib, which may restrict the entry of water into the network. The comparatively higher equilibrium swelling ratio of AHM0.6 than CXB0.6 may be associated with the hydrophilic and ionic character of sodium aurothiomalate.

Drug release studies

The in vitro release profiles of the HAase inhibitors were evaluated to assess the potential of the hydrogel matrix for controlled delivery. The release behaviour of CXB and AHM from the hydrogel matrix is important for understanding their availability within the local hydrogel environment. Fig. 6 shows the release profiles of hydrogel fillers containing the HAase inhibitors.

Drug release profiles of hydrogel fillers: (A) CXB0.6, (B) AHM0.6, (C) CXB0.6 and AHM0.6 hydrogel fillers. Data represent the mean of three independent results (mean ± SD).

CXB and AHM concentrations were determined by spectro-photometry. Calibration curves showed good linearity (R2 > 0.97) for both drugs within the studied concentration ranges (Fig. S10).

Fig. 6A and 6B show the release kinetic profiles of the CXB0.6 and AHM0.6 hydrogel formulations. The Korsmeyer–Peppas model, which is widely utilized for describing drug release from polymeric systems, was employed to evaluate the release kinetics. For CXB0.6, the R2, Kp, and n values were found to be 0.99, 0.28, and 0.23, respectively, while for AHM0.6, they were calculated as 0.98, 0.62, and 0.17. The calculated n values below 0.45 are consistent with predominantly Fickian diffusion under the applied Korsmeyer–Peppas model.

These findings are consistent with a diffusion-controlled release mechanism, suggesting that the cross-linked network regulates the movement of the incorporated agents into the surrounding medium. Overall, the developed hydrogel systems exhibited controlled and sustained drug release profiles over the studied period. The in vitro cumulative releases of the hydrogel fillers are shown in (Fig. 6C). The AHM0.6 hydrogel showed a higher release rate constant (Kp = 0.62) compared to the CXB0.6 formulation (Kp = 0.28). This kinetic advantage resulted in a cumulative release of nearly 30% for AHM0.6 over the monitored period, whereas the release from the CXB0.6 matrix remained limited to approximately 15%. The restricted release profile of celecoxib (CXB) is likely attributed to its high hydrophobicity and low aqueous solubility, which favour strong hydrophobic interactions with the cross-linked polymeric network, thereby hindering diffusion into the surrounding aqueous medium. In contrast, the more hydrophilic and ionic character of sodium aurothiomalate (AHM) may promote its partitioning from the hydrogel matrix into the surrounding aqueous environment. For the subsequent in vivo evaluation, a formulation with measurable and sustained inhibitor release was considered more suitable. The limited release observed for CXB0.6 may reduce the amount of inhibitor available in the local environment, which could limit its contribution in the in vivo setting. Therefore, based on its higher cumulative release and more favourable release kinetics, AHM0.6 was selected for the in vivo experimental phase.

Biodegradation

Although results from in vitro tests provide strong data on the stability of the developed hydrogel formulations, the complexity of biological systems, particularly factors such as HAase enzyme activity and mechanical stress that cause HA degradation, necessitates in vivo evaluation to better understand the performance of the hydrogel fillers under physiological conditions.

The principle underlying the main hypothesis of this study, specifically whether the incorporated inhibitors could protect the cross-linked HA matrix against enzymatic hydrolysis, was evaluated. To this end, time-dependent changes in the residual mass of explanted hydrogels were monitored following subcutaneous injection of the selected AHM0.6 hydrogel filler and compared with the control.

The residual masses of the control (CLHA) and AHM0.6 hydrogel groups on days 14, 30, 90, and 180 are shown in Fig. 7 while representative images of the explanted hydrogels are presented in Fig. 8. Following subcutaneous injection, the in vivo degradation of the hydrogel formulations was monitored gravimetrically over a 180 days period (Fig. 7). Both groups exhibited a biphasic degradation profile.

CLHA and AHM0.6 hydrogel fillers in vivo degradation profile. Each point represents the mean ± SD (n = 4). *Significant differences (p < 0.05). ** Significant differences (p < 0.01).

Representative images of explanted CLHA and AHM0.6 hydrogels at 14, 30, 90, and 180 days after subcutaneous implantation.

During the initial phase (0–90 days), a relatively rapid mass loss was observed, likely attributed to the enzymatic erosion of the loosely bound outer polymer chains. However, a significant plateau was observed between days 90 and 180, where the degradation rate markedly decelerated. This behaviour reflects the structural stability of the cross-linked HA network; as the outer layers degrade, a highly cross-linked core structure is exposed, demonstrating intrinsic resistance to hydrolytic cleavage. The persistence of even the CLHA group at day 180 confirms the suitability of the selected crosslinking density for long-term tissue filler applications (Fig. 7). Nevertheless, the superior persistence of the AHM0.6 formulation compared to the CLHA control (p < 0.01) cannot be explained by the polymeric structure alone. This suggests an active protection mechanism mediated by the active substance. As AHM diffuses from the hydrogel matrix into the surrounding tissue fluid, it does not merely act as a passive payload; rather, it modulates the local micro-environment by inhibiting endogenous HAase activity. By inhibiting local HAase activity in the immediate vicinity of the implant via this release-dependent mechanism, AHM effectively shields the remaining hydrogel mass from accelerated degradation. Consequently, the twofold higher residual mass of AHM0.6 at 180 days supports the concept of using a release-dependent enzymatic inhibition to enhance the stability of hyaluronic acid fillers.

A critical consideration for any dermal filler is its reversibility in case of clinical complications. Although the AHM0.6 formulation demonstrated enhanced stability, our in vitro data (Fig. 3) indicate that it remains susceptible to enzymatic degradation. Complete degradation of the AHM0.6 hydrogel was observed within 48 h in the presence of hyaluronidase, demonstrating that AHM delays, rather than completely prevents, hydrogel degradation. In clinical practice, the concentrations of exogenous hyaluronidase used for rescue procedures are substantially higher than those encountered under physiological conditions. Therefore, the inhibitory effect of AHM is expected to be overcome by the higher enzymatic activity used during clinical rescue procedures, allowing effective degradation of the filler when required. Accordingly, the term “self-protecting” used in this study refers to protection against premature endogenous hyaluronidase-mediated degradation rather than complete resistance to enzymatic degradation, thereby preserving the clinical reversibility and safety of the filler.

Pharmacokinetic parameters of the active ingredient AHM

As shown in the semi-logarithmic plot (Fig. 10E), AHM displayed a biphasic plasma concentration–time profile, consisting of a delayed absorption phase followed by a terminal elimination phase. The time to reach maximum plasma concentration Tmax was found to be 6.1 ± 2.7 h.

This delayed Tmax, compared to the rapid absorption typically observed with free AHM drug solutions (often < 1 h for subcutaneous injections), suggests that the cross-linked HA matrix acts as a reservoir, regulating the initial diffusion of the drug into the systemic circulation.35 The area under the curve AUC of 8788.1 ± 2171 ng h mL−1 confirms the systemic exposure to AHM. The maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) was 115.1 ± 13.4 ng mL−1. Together with the delayed Tmax, the concentration–time profile, and the prolonged MRT, this PK profile is consistent with sustained, release-limited absorption from the hydrogel matrix. The linear decline observed in the terminal phase of the semi-logarithmic plasma concentration–time graph indicates that AHM elimination follows first-order kinetics.36 This behavior is consistent with predictable systemic elimination.

Pharmacokinetic data support the hydrogel's high stability strategy. The prolonged apparent half-life of approximately 36 h is consistent with a release-limited disposition profile from the hydrogel depot. Additionally, the MRT of approximately 52 h indicates that the residence time of the drug is primarily governed by its release from the hydrogel matrix, a behaviour consistent with flip-flop kinetics.37,38

The developed platform enables sustained local retention of AHM in the implantation site, creating a sustainable inhibitory microenvironment against endogenous HAase. Instead of rapid depletion, the local concentration of the inhibitor is maintained, thereby increasing the stability of the HA hydrogel structure, as confirmed by in vivo biodegradation results (Fig. 7). The prolonged persistence of the hydrogel suggests that a portion of AHM remains retained within the matrix over time. Overall, the hydrogel system demonstrates controlled drug release behavior.

Rats were selected for the pharmacokinetic analysis to enable serial blood sampling at predetermined time points. This ensured that a sufficient plasma volume was collected for reliable measurement of AHM concentrations, while aiming to minimize the physiological burden associated with repeated sampling. The use of rats for the pharmacokinetic characterization of long-acting subcutaneous formulations is consistent with previous studies on injectable hydrogel delivery systems.28 The systemic toxicity assessment in mice and the pharmacokinetic assessment in rats were designed to provide complementary information regarding systemic tolerability and AHM exposure following administration of the AHM-loaded hydrogel.

Conclusion

In this study, we engineered a “self-protecting” CLHA hydrogel system by incorporating sodium aurothiomalate (AHM) as a HAase inhibitor. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrate that this approach effectively addresses the challenge of enzymatic degradation, a major limitation of hyaluronan-based biomaterials. The optimized formulation, AHM0.6, exhibited enhanced resistance to enzymatic erosion while maintaining viscoelastic properties comparable to those of the CLHA control.

In vivo biodegradation studies showed that AHM0.6 retained approximately twofold higher residual mass than the CLHA control at day 180, while the pharmacokinetic findings indicated sustained release of the incorporated inhibitor. The observed flip-flop kinetics and extended apparent half-life (t½ ≈ 36 h) indicate that drug release was governed by the cross-linked matrix, thereby limiting rapid systemic exposure and maintaining a localized inhibitory microenvironment. Long-term systemic toxicity assessments further demonstrated good biocompatibility, with no histopathological alterations detected in major organs, indicating that localized inhibitor retention does not adversely affect systemic safety.

Rather than relying solely on increased crosslinking density, this approach introduces a release-dependent enzymatic protection mechanism that complements the structural stability of HA-based hydrogels.

Beyond these mechanistic insights, the findings highlight the translational potential of the developed system, particularly for applications in aesthetic medicine and soft tissue augmentation. The ability of the AHM-loaded hydrogel to resist biodegradation without excessive chemical crosslinking positions it as a promising candidate for next-generation dermal fillers, offering the potential for extended durability and reduced treatment frequency. The modular nature of this enzyme-protected platform may warrant further investigation for other applications requiring long-acting viscoelastic support, including viscosupplementation and soft tissue reconstruction.

In conclusion, this work presents a promising strategy for enhancing the longevity of HA-based implants. By integrating mechanical stabilization with active hyaluronidase inhibition, the CLHA–AHM system offers a promising strategy for the design of functional, long-lasting hydrogel-based soft tissue fillers.

Materials and methods

Materials

The hyaluronic acid used in this study was supplied by Contipro a.s. (Dolní Dobrouč, Czech Republic) with a molecular weight of 1.68 × 106 Da. The chemicals required for this research included BDDE (1,4-butanediol diglycidyl ether), NaOH (sodium hydroxide), hydrochloric acid (HCl), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). In the study, a cellulose dialysis membrane was used (MWCO = 14 kDa). All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). The reagents were of analytical grade and were used without further purification. All experiments employed ultrapure water.

Preparation of BDDE cross-linked hyaluronic acid hydrogel and drug loading

CLHA was synthesized according to a modified protocol based on the method described by Al-Sibani et al.13 A solution of 11 g of HA (molecular weight 1.68 × 106 Da) in 0.25 M NaOH containing 2% (v/v) of the cross-linking agent 1,4-butanediol diglycidyl ether (BDDE) was stirred until homogeneous. Under strongly alkaline conditions (pH ≈ 13), the epoxide groups of BDDE are activated, thereby promoting cross-linking reactions between the polymer chains of hyaluronic acid.

The pH of the reaction mixture was then adjusted to approximately 7.0 by the dropwise addition of dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl). Following cross-linking, the reaction mixture was transferred to an oven and maintained at 45 °C for 3 h. Following the reaction, the resulting dough-like hydrogel was diluted with PBS to obtain a final hyaluronic acid concentration of 20 mg mL−1. The diluted CLHA hydrogel was dialyzed against PBS for 3 days to remove unreacted BDDE.

Following dialysis, the CLHA hydrogels were mixed with HAase inhibitors at final concentrations of 0.2%, 0.4%, and 0.6% (w/w) relative to the hydrogel mass. To ensure uniform distribution of the active ingredients within the hydrogel matrix, the formulations were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min and subsequently transferred into 50 mL falcon tubes. After homogenization, the drug-loaded CLHA hydrogels were aseptically drawn into 1 mL syringes and subjected to steam sterilisation in accordance with ISO 17665, at 121 °C for 15 min.

Physicochemical characterisation

pH and visual controls

Following sterilisation, the pH of the hydrogel formulations was measured. A physiological pH range (6.8–7.4) was used as a reference. Each syringe was inspected for phase separation, turbidity, or particulate formation.

Rheological properties

Rheological measurements were performed using an MCR 102 rheometer (Anton Paar, Austria). A strain sweep was performed in order to establish that a 0.1% strain level fell within the linear viscoelastic region. Further oscillatory tests were carried out over a frequency range of 10 to 0.01 Hz to determine the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of the filler material. All tests were conducted at 37 °C utilising a PP25 plate with one millimetre gap. Within the physiological frequency range of 0.7 Hz, the storage modulus (G′), the loss modulus (G″), tan delta, and the complex shear modulus were calculated.39 Following the assessment of the properties of the developed scaffold materials, the selection was made of which of these to use in the subsequent degradation experiments in a laboratory setting.

In vitro degradation

The enzymatic stability of HAase inhibitor-loaded hydrogels was evaluated in vitro using bovine testis HAase. In the degradation study the technique employed was a modification of the method of Bitter et al. for determining purity.32 Degradation products were quantified as d-glucuronic acid (GlcA) units using the carbazole colorimetric assay. Hydrogel samples (1 mL) containing HAase inhibitors at concentrations of 0.2%, 0.4%, or 0.6% were placed in Falcon tubes containing 5 mL of PBS supplemented with 20 U mL−1 bovine testis HAase. The falcon tubes were placed in an orbital shaker rotating at 100 rpm and incubated at 37 °C. Samples were collected at 0.5, 3, 6, 9, 24, 32, and 48 h. Additionally, a control group without the HAase inhibitors product was also subjected to incubation and degradation stages. At set intervals the supernatant was taken from the falcon tubes and treated with 1 mL of 1 M hydrochloric acid to inactivate the enzyme. Following filtration through a 0.22 µm PTFE membrane, the samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis. In the Bitter method32 assay, the samples were treated with carbazole. This was followed by measuring the absorbance at 530 nm with a microtitre reader.

In vitro cytotoxicity

The alamar blue assay was used to assess cell viability and proliferation in hydrogel dermal filler materials. The assay quantifies the metabolic reduction of resazurin to resorufin by viable cells, which occurs as a result of cell metabolism, and the resultant change in colour. The required quantity of hydrogel fillers was placed in a 24-well plate in triplicate. Following lyophilization, 2.5 × 104 L929 mouse fibroblast cells per well were seeded directly into the 24-well plate with culture medium. Viability of the cells was assessed on the 3rd, 5th and 7th day by adding 10% alamar blue dye to the medium and then incubating at 37 °C for four hours in a humidified incubator. In this case 100 microliters of medium from each well was placed into a 96 well plate, then a plate reader (Thermo Scientific Varioskan LUX) was used to measure fluorescence intensity at an excitation of 460 nm and an emission of 490 nm. A hydrogel filler, which did not contain an HAase inhibitor, served as a control. The Alamar Blue medium was removed from the cultures and replaced with fresh medium. RAW 264.7 macrophages were evaluated using the same experimental procedure.

Chemical characterisation

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The synthesis of cross-linked hydrogel filler materials and the molecular chemical characterisation of the filler materials containing inhibitors were performed using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) (Thermo Scientific, Nicolet iN10, USA). All spectra were recorded in the range of 400 to 4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1, and the data were obtained using OMNIC software.

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-NMR)

For the 1H-NMR analyses, firstly the samples were subjected to the bovine testicular hyaluronidase (BTH) treatment. A 5 mL sample of the hydrogel was immersed in 10 mL PBS containing 1 mL BTH (4 units per mL) at 37 °C for 24 h.40 Following enzymatic hydrolysis, the resulting products were dried under vacuum to form a powder which was then dissolved in 600 µL of D2O allowing the solution to stand for 24 h before analysis. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) 1H-NMR spectra were recorded at 500 MHz on a Bruker Ascend 500 spectrometer. The NMR data were collected on Bruker 500 MHz instrument and processed with MestReNova 15.0 software (Mestrelab Research, USA). The modification degrees (% MoD) were calculated from the 1H-NMR spectra. The degree of modification (% MoD) was calculated from the ratio of BDDE and HA proton signals. After crosslinking, it was found that the polymer contained epoxy groups from the BDDE. This was shown using appropriate techniques which gave a measure of the quantity of these groups. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

In this study, surface morphology analysis was performed using a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM, FEI, QUANTA FEG 250, USA). The analyses of the lyophilized samples were conducted using the low vacuum mode at a chamber pressure of 60 Pa.

This testing was performed using an analytical balance with a precision of 0.0001 g (Ohaus, USA). In vitro drug release study

Hydrogel samples (1 mL) containing HAase inhibitors were placed in dialysis membrane bags. The falcon tubes, each containing 50 mL PBS solution at pH 7.2, were placed on an orbital shaker operating at 100 rpm and 37 °C. At predetermined time points (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 h, and up to 15 days) a 1 mL sample was taken from the falcon tubes for analysis. After each sampling, the medium was replaced with fresh PBS to maintain sink conditions. The maximum wavelength for the active ingredients was determined to be 254 nm for CXB and 300 nm for AHM. In the study, a calibration curve for CXB was obtained using standards containing CXB at concentrations of 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, and 50 µg mL−1. A calibration curve for the AHM active ingredient was obtained at concentrations of 1, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 30 µg mL−1. The amount of HAase inhibitors released into the PBS solution was determined by using a plate reader (PerkinElmer, USA), with the cumulative release of the drug from the gels being calculated. The kinetics of drug release were studied using the Korsmeyer–Peppas equation.

Biodegradation study

Approximately 0.2 mL of hydrogel was injected subcutaneously into the dorsal region of the female mice (BALB/c strain) and the residual mass of the explanted hydrogels was measured at predetermined time points 14, 30, 90, and 180 days.42 The control group received CLHA hydrogel without an HAase inhibitor. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the protocol approved by the Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of Burdur Mehmet Akif Ersoy University (HADYEK) (28.08.2024/1.368).

Systemic toxicity study

Systemic safety was evaluated through serum biochemistry and histopathological examination in mice receiving inhibitor-loaded hydrogels.43 Drug-loaded hydrogels were compared with inhibitor-free CLHA controls. A total of 0.2 mL of the filler materials were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal regions of female mice. On the 30th, 90th and 180th days post-injection, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture. Tests were conducted for the levels of serum biochemical parameters, including ALT, AST, BUN, and ALB. The groups which were injected with the substance were compared to a control group of animals which were not injected. At the scheduled endpoint, mice were euthanized and the liver, lungs, kidneys, heart, and spleen were collected for histopathological examination. The tissues were first preserved in 10% neutral formalin. The samples were processed in the standard way through tissue and then embedded in paraffin wax. Sections of a thickness of 4–5 µm were cut out from the paraffin wax blocks. Following dehydration with alcohol, the sections underwent processing in xylene. They were then hydrated. The specimens were stained with H & E and viewed under a microscope.

In vivo pharmacokinetic study

Studies of the pharmacokinetics of active agents contained in hydrogel fillers were conducted on female Wistar albino rats whose weights were in the range of 200–250 g. A single subcutaneous injection (0.2 mL) was administered in the dorsal region. Blood samples were collected at predetermined time points (1, 3, and 6 h, and 1, 3, and 7 days post-injection). Blood samples of 250 µL were taken from the rats' tails. Following centrifugation, plasma samples were stored at −20 °C until LC-MS/MS analysis. Plasma AHM concentrations were quantified using a modified UHPLC-MS/MS method based on previously reported literature.44 A solution comprising 0.1% v/v formic acid in 50 : 50 water:methanol, 1 mL of which, was used for the extraction of plasma samples of 50 µL volume. Following a vortexing procedure of five minutes, the mixture was then subjected to centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min. and at 4 °C. Following this centrifugation the mixture was filtered through a membrane with a pore size of 0.22 µm. It was then injected into the UHPLC-MS/MS. The analysis lasted a total of seven minutes. Analyte detection and quantification were carried out using a UPLC system coupled to a TSQ Vantage mass spectrometer from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The Xcalibur software was employed to conduct the chromatographic analysis. The separation was performed on a Hypersil Gold 18 Reversed Phase 1.9 µm UHPLC column (50 × 2.1 mm) provided by Thermo Scientific of USA. The mobile phase A was a water/methanol mixture (95 : 5 v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid and 4 mM ammonium formate. The mobile phase B was a methanol/water (95 : 5 v/v) mixture containing 0.1% formic acid and 4 mM ammonium formate. The flow rate employed was 0.3 mL min−1. The retention time for the main ion was 1.76 minutes; the CID voltage was 25 volts and the m/z of the ion peak was 149.1. Fragment ions were detected at m/z 104 and m/z 45. Calibration curves were plotted using samples of known concentration. Analysis results for plasma samples were compared quantitatively to the results obtained from the same mass scan which was used as the standard. Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated from the plasma concentration–time profiles using non-compartmental analysis with the PKSolver add-in for Microsoft Excel. The parameters calculated include the mean residence time (MRT), half-life (t1/2), time to reach maximum concentration (Tmax), maximum concentration in plasma (Cmax), and the area under the concentration-time curve (AUC).

Statistical analysis of the data

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26 software (IBM Corp., USA). Prior to the selection of the appropriate statistical tests, the normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene's test. For data sets that met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by the Mann–Whitney U test was used for statistical evaluation. All experiments were conducted with at least three independent replicates (n ≥ 3), and results are expressed as means ± standard deviations. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
