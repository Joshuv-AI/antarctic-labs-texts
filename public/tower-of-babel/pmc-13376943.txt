Recyclable dual Z-scheme perovskite/g-C3N4 heterojunction enables photoredox nickel-catalyzed C(sp2)–C(sp3) cross-electrophile coupling
Authors: Rui Wang, Donghao Huo, Shentong Xie, Honghui Ou, Renyi Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nickel-catalyzed cross-electrophile coupling provides an efficient and cost-effective strategy for constructing C(sp2)–C(sp3) bonds, a pivotal transformation for diversifying molecular architectures in pharmaceutical and agrochemical synthesis. However, conventional methodologies usually require stoichiometric metal reductants, which pose safety risks, reaction instability, and environmental concerns. To circumvent these limitations, merging nickel catalysis with photoredox catalysis has emerged as a promising alternative. Central to this approach is to develop highly efficient, easy-to-prepare and recyclable photocatalysts, which can drive the reaction under mild conditions. In this work, we present a recyclable LaFeO3/LaCoO3/g-C3N4 heterojunction. The stepped band structure and dual Z-scheme carrier migration paths significantly enhance photocatalytic performance by synergistically increasing redox potentials and extending the lifetime of photogenerated charge carriers through efficient interfacial charge transfer. Leveraging this heterojunction, we have developed a nickel/photoredox dual-catalyzed cross-electrophile coupling between aryl iodides and alkyl halides, affording the desired products with up to 98% yields.

Introduction

Organic chemistry primarily revolves around the formation of carbon-carbon bonds. One of the most reliable methods for constructing C(sp3)–C(sp2) bonds is the transition-metal-catalyzed cross-coupling reactions between electrophilic organohalides and nucleophilic organometallic reagents1–7. However, due to the inherent limitations associated with preformed carbon nucleophiles, which are typically derived from the corresponding organohalides, cross-electrophile coupling (XEC) of electrophilic organohalides has emerged as a viable alternative for constructing C(sp3)–C(sp2) bonds8–16. The most prevalent strategy for XEC involves the combination of nickel catalysis with metallic reducing agents, such as Mn or Zn powders (Fig. 1a)17–20. The use of metal powders poses several challenges: a) safety hazards stemming from the large excesses of flammable metal powders; b) the production of environmentally unfriendly metal waste (e.g., MnX2, ZnX2) that is difficult to separate and is subject to stringent disposal regulations; c) reaction instability due to heterogeneity/mixing, inconsistent metallic reductant quality.Fig. 1Photoredox cross-electrophile coupling and our protocol.a Cross-electrophile coupling. b Photoredox cross-electrophile coupling. c Perovskite-modified g-C3N4 as the photocatalyst. d Perovskite/g-C3N4 heterojunction enabling photoredox nickel-catalyzed C(sp2)-C(sp3) cross-electrophile coupling.

In response to these concerns, photochemical alternatives for cross-electrophile coupling have been developed21. Significant advancements have been made by integrating nickel catalysis with photocatalysis to create activation modes of substrates and circumvent the need for metal powders (Fig. 1b)22–45. However, most of these nickel/photoredox dual-catalyzed cross-electrophile coupling reactions rely on precious metal photocatalysts, such as Ir, Ru or unrecyclable organic photocatalysts. Furthermore, homogeneous reaction conditions limit photocatalyst recyclability. There is consequently an urgent need to develop highly efficient, easily prepared, and recyclable photocatalysts to enable greener and more sustainable cross-electrophile coupling reactions.

The pioneering work by Weix and colleagues demonstrated the application of semiconductor quantum dots (QDs) coupled with nickel catalysts for C–C bond formation43. However, broader implementation remains challenging due to limited recyclability and stability of the QD-based photocatalytic system. Graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4), an earth-abundant, metal-free polymeric semiconductor, has attracted intense interest for artificial photosynthesis, optoelectronics and heterogeneous catalysis owing to its low cost, facile synthesis from molecular precursors and chemical robustness. Its high density of C/N surface defects and large specific surface area endow pronounced adsorption affinity toward organic substrates46–53. Nevertheless, the practical implementation of bare g-C3N4 in photocatalytic redox processes is severely hampered by (i) massive aggregation that demands excess solvent to dissolve reactants and disperse the catalyst, (ii) ultrafast charge-carrier recombination, (iii) low electron mobility, (iv) poor crystallinity and (v) a narrow visible-light-harvesting window, all of which conspire to suppress quantum efficiency and limit the scope of heterogeneous reaction manifolds. Perovskite materials demonstrate significant potential for addressing these challenges, owing to their high light-harvesting capacity, adjustable band structures, and charge separation/transport characteristics (Fig. 1c)54–65.

Here we introduce a fully recyclable LaFeO3/LaCoO3/g-C3N4 double Z-scheme heterojunction that rectifies these intrinsic shortcomings. The engineered step-like band alignment and dual Z-pathway enforce vectorial interfacial charge transfer, simultaneously elevating the reduction/oxidation potentials and prolonging the lifetime of photogenerated carriers, thereby delivering a dramatic boost in photocatalytic performance. Capitalizing on this architecture, we establish a nickel/photoredox dual-catalytic cross-electrophile coupling (XEC) between aryl iodides and alkyl halides that operates without stoichiometric metallic reductants. The protocol exhibits broad substrate scope (>40 examples) and furnishes the desired C(sp2)–C(sp3) products in isolated yields up to 98 %, underscoring the transformative potential of perovskite-decorated carbon nitride heterostructures for sustainable visible-light-driven organic synthesis (Fig. 1d).

Results and discussion

g-C3N4 materials were obtained through a pyrolysis method, whereas the target heterojunction catalyst was synthesized through a sol–gel-calcination-solvothermal protocol66. The transmission electron spectroscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show a lump-like morphology of perovskite/g-C3N4. 2D sheet-like LCFOCN-2 scaffolds are visualized by TEM, wherein LaFeO3/LaCoO3 (LCFO) nanodomains are periodically embedded (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. S5). Anticipatedly, LaFeO3 and LaCoO3 existed as single granular on graphite-like carbon nitride. The microstructure of the material may enhance electron transfer from the bulk to the surface phase, optimize interfacial properties, and improve catalytic performance. Figure 2b illustrates the crystal microstructure of LCFO species on g-C3N4, with measured facet spacings of 0.272 nm (LaCoO3 (-110)) and 0.383 nm (LaFeO3 (110)). This indicates that the fired LCFO bulk phase contains two crystalline phases. Supplementary Fig. S6a shows that the XRD pattern of LCFO-1 exhibits characteristic peaks corresponding to LaCoO3 (JCPDS card no. 84-0848). Due to the low content of LaFeO3, characteristic reflections of LaFeO3 are lacking. The broader peaks observed in LCFO-1 compared to those in LaCoO3 may be attributed to the incorporation of LaFeO3. Supplementary Fig. S6b presents the XRD patterns of pure LaCoO3, LaFeO3, g-C3N4, LCFO, and LCFOCN-2, revealing characteristic peaks for LCFO and g-C3N4 in LCFOCN-2. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Fig. 2c) reveals a uniform distribution of C and N, with La, Co, Fe, and O predominantly concentrated in LCFO-1. FTIR spectra reveal that the characteristic peaks of LCFOCN-2 primarily originate from g-C3N4. While the presence of LCFO-1 species was confirmed by XRD and TEM, their characteristic peaks were difficult to discern due to weak IR activity. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis of LCFOCN-2 indicated a high specific surface area of 61 m2/g (Supplementary Fig. S10). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirms the interaction between LCFO-1 and g-C3N4. In the C1s XPS spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S11a), peaks at 284.8 eV and 288.2 eV correspond to C-C and N=C-N species, respectively. The N1s XPS spectra (Supplementary Fig. S11b) show binding energies for C-N, C=N, N-(C)3, and N-H. Further analysis of other elements is shown in Supplementary Fig. S11c, d. Comparison of the XPS C1s and N1s spectra of LCFOCN-2 with those of g-C3N4 (Fig. 2d, e), LCFOCN-2 C and N exhibit shifts to lower binding energy positions, suggesting electron transfer from LaCoO3 to C and N, a state of electron excess in g-C3N4 due to the absence of reaction substrates and sacrificial agents during testing. The electron transfer between different crystalline phases indicates the successful loading of LCFO onto g-C3N4.

To gain deeper insight into the photocatalytic mechanism of the LCFOCN-2 system, we performed Mott–Schottky analysis and solid-state UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-Vis DRS) to evaluate its band structure characteristics. As shown in Supplementary Figs. S8a–c, the flat-band potentials determined from Mott–Schottky plots were −1.30 eV for g-C3N4, −1.15 eV for LaCoO3, and −1.40 eV for LaFeO3. Considering that all three materials are n-type semiconductors, their conduction band (CB) potentials can be estimated to lie ~0.1 eV more negative than the flat-band values. Based on this, we deduced the relative positions of the CBs in the composite system. The corresponding bandgap energies in Supplementary Fig. S8d–f, derived from Tauc plots based on UV-Vis DRS measurements in Supplementary Fig. S8g–i, were calculated to be 2.82 eV for g-C3N4, 2.88 eV for LaCoO3, and 2.04 eV for LaFeO3. To investigate the light-induced evolution of surface electronic states, in situ XPS measurements were performed on the LCFOCN-2 sample (see Supplementary Fig. S12, Supporting Information). Spectra were firstly recorded under dark conditions, followed by repeated scans under continuous illumination. The results reveal a distinct shift of the O 1s peak toward higher binding energy, whereas the C 1s, N 1s, and La 3d peaks all shift toward lower binding energies. This indicates the accumulation of photogenerated holes at oxygen sites and the localization of electrons in C/N/La-associated regions. These observations provide direct evidence for efficient and element-specific spatial charge separation within the material under photoexcitation.

These electronic structure parameters collectively support the formation of a stepwise dual Z-scheme heterojunction, as illustrated in Fig. 6a. This configuration facilitates directional charge migration and effective charge separation, thereby enhancing the redox capability of the composite catalyst under visible light irradiation. To further elucidate the photophysical characteristics, photoluminescence (PL) and Time-Resolved Fluorescence Spectroscopy (TRFS) are depicted in Fig. 6b, c. Under 320 nm excitation, PL emission was recorded using a 340 nm long-pass filter to eliminate scattered excitation light. In general, PL intensity correlates inversely with carrier lifetime at a given quantum yield—higher emission intensity often reflects rapid carrier recombination. The significantly quenched PL intensity of LCFOCN-2 thus reveals suppressed carrier recombination and prolonged carrier lifetimes. The LCFOCN-2 sample exhibits a markedly reduced carrier recombination rate, which can be attributed to its double-Z band alignment. This architecture promotes more efficient spatial separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs. In Fig. 6c, LCFOCN-2 exhibits a mono-exponential lifetime of 9.2 ns, 12 % longer than pristine g-C3N4, evidencing fewer surface traps and rationalizing its higher photocatalytic evolution. In agreement with this, in Fig. 6d, LCFOCN-2 exhibits the highest photocurrent density among all tested samples, indicative of its high charge separation efficiency, enhanced interfacial charge transport, and potential in photocatalytic applications.

To gain a deeper understanding of the mechanism underlying this reductive cross - coupling reaction, a series of control experiments were conducted (as shown in Fig. 7). When a radical scavenger, such as 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO), was introduced into our standard reaction, no desired product was observed (Eq. (1)). Instead, the benzyl radical adduct, 1-(benzyloxy)-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine, was obtained in a 46% yield. The addition of 1,1-diphenylethylene to the reaction also led to the formation of prop-1-ene-1,1,3-triyltribenzene 6, which was detected by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (Eq. (2)). These findings indicate that alkyl radicals may be involved in the coupling reactions. Under standard reaction conditions without a nickel catalyst, the reaction of benzyl chloride, (3-bromopropyl)benzene, or (3-iodopropyl)benzene with TEMPO led to the formation of the corresponding radical adducts 5 or 7, respectively (see Eqs. (3)–(5)). This indicates that the direct single-electron transfer (SET) between alkyl halides and the photocatalyst results in the generation of alkyl radicals. The reaction between (iodomethyl)cyclopropane and 1a yielded 12% of 3af with an opened cyclopropyl ring (Eq. (6)). Additionally, the reaction of 6-iodohex-1-ene and 1a gave a 42% yield of 1-(cyclopentylmethyl)-4-methoxybenzene (Eq. (7)). These results further support the notion that alkyl radicals are likely involved in the coupling reactions. Under identical conditions, the Ni0 precursor afforded only 28% yield of 3a, whereas the NiI precursor delivered 95%. Based on the results described above, a plausible mechanism is proposed as shown in Fig. 8. For the LCFOCN-2 photocatalyst, irradiation with blue light promotes electron excitation from the valence band to the conduction band, generating electron-hole pairs. The photogenerated electrons reduce the NiII catalyst precursor to a NiI species (A). Subsequent oxidative addition of aryl halides to the NiI species produces a NiIII species (B). The NiIII intermediate (B) is reduced by the photocatalyst and undergoes halide ion removal to yield the NiII intermediate (C). Simultaneously, single-electron transfer from the excited photocatalyst surface (where low-valent nickel species may also participate) to alkyl halides produces alkyl radicals, which are captured by C to generate a NiIII intermediate (D). In summary, we have successfully developed a three-component dual Z scheme heterojunction that demonstrates photocatalytic performance. This heterojunction is prepared from cost-effective, readily available materials with low perovskite loading. Leveraging this heterojunction, we have developed a nickel/photoredox dual-catalyzed cross-electrophile coupling between alkyl halides and aryl iodides under mild reaction conditions. The methodology demonstrates broad substrate scope, high functional group tolerance, and good chemoselectivity. The perovskite/g-C3N4 heterostructure enables quantitative catalyst recovery and maintains consistent activity over 5 cycles (>90% yield), substantially improving process sustainability. Mechanistic studies reveal that photogenerated electrons from the heterojunction simultaneously mediate both alkyl halide activation and nickel catalyst reduction. Our work establishes a paradigm for designing heterogeneous photocatalysts for cross-electrophile coupling reactions.

Solvents were transferred into the glove box under air-free conditions to avoid exposure to atmospheric air.

Preparation of photocatalysts

In a typical synthetic procedure, pure g-C3N4 was prepared by placing a certain quantity of urea into a ceramic crucible and drying it at 80 °C in a forced-air oven for 10 h. Subsequently, the sample was calcined in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 1.7 h with a heating rate of 5 °C/min. LaCoO3 was synthesized using cobalt nitrate, lanthanum nitrate, and citric acid at a molar ratio of 1:1:2, with the amount of lanthanum nitrate fixed at 5 mmol per batch, and the masses of the starting materials were calculated accordingly. The three reagents were weighed and dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water, forming a purplish-red solution. The mixture was then transferred to a round-bottom flask and heated in an 80 °C water bath under magnetic stirring until a purple sol was formed. The sol was transferred to a clean ceramic crucible and dried at 120 °C in a forced-air oven, yielding a swollen purple xerogel, which was ground and stored for further use. The resulting solid was calcined in a muffle furnace at 400 °C for 4 h, followed by another calcination step at 700 °C for 4 h. After natural cooling to room temperature, black LaCoO3 powder was collected.

For the preparation of LaFeO3, ferric nitrate, lanthanum nitrate, and citric acid were used at a molar ratio of 1:1:2, with the amount of lanthanum nitrate fixed at 5 mmol per batch, and the corresponding masses were calculated precisely. The three reactants were weighed and dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water to produce a light yellow solution. The solution was moved to a round-bottom flask and heated in an 80 °C water bath with magnetic stirring until an orange sol was obtained. This sol was poured into a clean ceramic crucible and dried at 120 °C in a forced-air oven, giving an orange-red dry gel that was ground and reserved. The solid product was calcined in a muffle furnace at 400 °C for 4 h and then at 700 °C for 4 h. Following natural cooling, brownish LaFeO3 powder was obtained.

The synthesis of a series of LCFO photocatalysts was carried out in a similar way to the two substances mentioned above, by adjusting the amounts of cobalt nitrate and iron nitrate to modulate the elemental cobalt and elemental iron in the product. The obtained catalysts LaCoxFe1–xO3 (x = 0.9, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, and 0.2) were denoted as LCFO-1, LCFO-2, LCFO-3, LCFO-4, LCFO-5. A series of LCFOCN were prepared by the solvothermal method. A certain weight of LCFO and of g-C3N4 were dispersed in 10 mL anhydrous ethanol separately and sonicated for half an hour. The suspension of LCFO was slowly added to the suspension of g-C3N4. The mixed solution was sonicated continuously for 30 min at ambient temperature, with the system temperature maintained unchanged throughout the process. After being stirred magnetically for 10 h at room temperature, the resulting suspension was transferred into a clean Teflon-lined autoclave and hydrothermally treated at 120 °C for 8 h. The product was then collected via centrifugation for 15 min and dried in an oven at 80 °C for 10 h. LCFO@g-C3N4 nanoflakes with 2, 5, 10, 15, and 20 wt % LCFO contents were prepared and denoted as LCFOCN-1, LCFOCN-2, LCFOCN-3, LCFOCN-4, LCFOCN-5.

General procedure for photoredox nickel-catalyzed C(sp2)–C(sp3) cross-electrophile coupling

An oven-dried vial (19 ×80 mm) equipped with a stir bar was charged with the LCFOCN-2 (15 mg), p-Methoxyiodobenzene (1.5 equiv.), 1, 10-Phenanthroline (5 mol%). Subsequently, the NiCl2 (5 mol%), DMSO (anhydrous, 3.8 mL), benzyl chloride (0.3 mmol, 1equiv.) and triethanolamine (5 equiv.) were added in the glove box. Close and tighten the lid with the silicone gasket and remove the reaction flask from the glove box. The reaction mixture was sonicated for 5–10 min followed by stirring for 5 min until a fine dispersion of the solids was achieved. The mixtures were subjected to 20 W blue light irradiation in a photoreactor at 25 °C with rapid stirring (500 rpm). The photocatalytic light source is manufactured by Zhonglian Optical Factory of Shenzhen Zhonglian Jianchao Technology Co., Ltd., China, with a power of 20 W and a wavelength of 395 nm. The distance between the lamp and the reaction tube is 5 cm. Upon reaction completion, the product was extracted from the mixture using ethyl acetate (4 × 30 mL). The combined organic phases were then washed with saturated NaCl solution (60 mL). The separated organic layer was dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, filtered, and concentrated using rotary evaporator. The resulting crude product was purified by silica gel column chromatography using petroleum ether/ethyl acetate as the eluent.
