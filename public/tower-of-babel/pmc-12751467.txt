Investigation of a suspected cholera outbreak within a closed community of trainees at a college in Hammanskraal, Gauteng Province, South Africa, June 2023
Authors: Phuti Sekwadi, Rixongile Malomane, Danai Kwenda, Keabetswe Kodi, Andani Marumo, Lerato Kgatswetswe, Refilwe Mokgetle, Tebogo Matjokotja, Anthony M. Smith, Mimmy Ngomane, Linda Erasmus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cholera, as defined by the US Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), is an acute diarrhoeal disease caused by infection of the intestine with the toxin-producing Vibrio cholerae serogroup O1 or O139 bacterium. We investigated a suspected outbreak of cholera at a training college with the aim of determining the magnitude of the outbreak and identifying possible risk factors. We conducted a retrospective cohort study including all persons attending training or living in the college premises between 8 May and 1 June 2023. A case was defined as anyone attending training courses on campus who experienced diarrhoea and/or vomiting between 8 May and 1 June 2023. Data were collected through an online questionnaire. R Studio was used to calculate attack rates and risk ratios to identify possible risk factors associated with illness. Two hundred and thirty-eight participants completed the online questionnaire on 1 June 2023, of which 37% (88/238) reported illness during the study period. The median age of cases was 35 years with a predominance of males (67/88; 76%). Risk factors associated with illness included brushing teeth with tap water (RR = 1.8; CI = 1.2–2.6), sex (RR = 1.6; CI = 1.1–2.5) and unavailability of hand washing soap in the bathrooms (RR = 1.6; CI = 1.2–2.3). Eating food bought outside the college (RR = 0.5; CI = 0.4–0.8) and availability of hand washing soap in the bathrooms were protective against illness (RR = 0.4; CI = 0.3–0.7). This diarrheal disease outbreak was likely caused by multiple pathogens including Vibrio cholerae, a pattern usually seen when there is suspected/possible fecal contamination of drinking water sources. Recommendations included ongoing provision of safe water to the trainees attending courses at the college and anyone living in the college until such time that the municipal drinking water was declared safe for drinking by the responsible stakeholders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11739-z.

Background

Cholera, as defined by the US Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), is an acute diarrhoeal disease that is caused by infection of the intestine with the toxin-producing Vibrio cholerae serogroup O1 or O139 bacterium [1]. Most people infected with cholera will have mild to moderate symptoms, while one in ten will develop severe disease. Common symptoms include profuse watery diarrhoea that has a rice-water appearance especially in people with severe disease, vomiting, thirst, leg cramps and restlessness [1, 2]. Transmission occurs through the fecal-oral route, typically by the ingestion of food or water contaminated by the faeces of an infected person [3, 4]. Cholera prevention is mainly through the provision of access to safe water and improved sanitation facilities coupled with improved personal hygiene (WASH) [5]. The use of oral cholera vaccines (OCV) is another method for cholera prevention and control. Currently there are three OCVs that are licensed by the World Health Organization (WHO); which are recommended for use in emergency settings such as during large outbreaks and in non-emergency settings through immunisation programs in areas that are endemic to cholera [5, 6].

The WHO estimates that between 1.3 and 4 million annual cases of cholera and 21 000 to 143 000 associated deaths occur globally [2, 7]. The highest burden of cholera is in South East Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, accounting for over 80% of the global cholera disease burden [7]. Between January 2022 and February 2024, 18 African countries reported outbreaks of cholera with a cumulative total of over 300 000 cases and over 6000 associated deaths. Democratic Republic of the Congo, Ethiopia, Malawi, Mozambique, and Nigeria accounted for 74% and 67% of the cumulative cases and deaths respectively [8]. Although cholera is not endemic in South Africa, the risk of importation of cases remains high especially with neighboring countries experiencing cholera outbreaks. Cholera outbreaks in South Africa often start with an importation of cases from neighboring countries that are cholera endemic [9, 10]. Cross border migration is an important factor that fuels recurrent and continuous cholera outbreaks in Africa [11]. South Africa’s neighboring countries including Mozambique and Zimbabwe have experienced outbreaks of cholera in the recent past including during our study period [8, 12].

On 18 May 2023, the Center for Enteric Disease (CED) of the National Institute for Communicable Diseases (NICD) identified a case of cholera by isolation of a toxin-producing Vibrio cholerae O1 Ogawa on stool culture and PCR. The stool specimen, which was referred to CED from a private laboratory in Pretoria, was collected from a patient who was admitted at a private hospital. The result triggered a rapid outbreak response by the national and provincial outbreak response teams. A cholera outbreak was subsequently confirmed in the Hammanskraal area, where 164 laboratory-confirmed cases were identified between 6 May and 17 June 2023. The index case was identified as a trainee who was attending a course and living at a college in Hammanskraal, Gauteng Province. The Department of Health outbreak response teams and the CED were notified of further suspected cases of cholera from the college who presented to private healthcare facilities in Hammanskraal. This study focuses on the investigations conducted at the college, which is a closed community of trainees who live and attend courses on the campus. The aim of the study was to determine the magnitude of the outbreak, identify possible risk factors and provide recommendations to stop the transmission and prevent similar outbreaks in the future.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in a closed community of trainees at a college in Hammanskraal, City of Tshwane. The trainees came from different provinces to attend courses on the campus, where they also lived. A total of 466 trainees were on campus during the study period with the majority of them being male. The campus consists of bungalows, classrooms and a central kitchen with a dining hall. Throughout the course, the trainees live, sleep, dine, and attend training and classes on campus, with little interaction with the surrounding communities during weekends.

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study. The cohort included everyone who took training classes on campus between 8 May and 1 June 2023. A case was defined as anyone who attended training courses on campus between 8 May and 1 June 2023 and had diarrhoea and/or vomiting.

Diarrhea was defined as the passage of three or more loose stools per day between 8 May and 1 June 2023. Vomiting was defined as one or more episodes per day between 8 May and 1 June 2023.

Epidemiological investigations

On 1 June 2023, a standardised online questionnaire was prepared using Google Forms and distributed to trainees via the instructors, who disseminated the link to the questionnaire via WhatsApp groups used for trainee-instructor communications. The investigation team instructed the trainees on how to fill out the online form and supplied wi-fi access to those who did not have internet. The questionnaire gathered information on participants’ demographics, sickness details, food and water sources, and hygiene routines while at college. A copy of the case investigation form is given as an additional file (Supplementary file 1).

Human and environmental samples investigations

On 18 May 2023 rectal swabs and stool samples were collected from trainees who experienced symptoms of diarrhoea and/or vomiting since arriving at the college on 8 May 2023. The specimens were delivered to the CED laboratory for stool culture on the same day.

On 17 May environmental assessments were conducted on the college premises, with focus on the kitchen (mess). Stool samples and rectal swabs were collected from food handlers who worked in the mess. Food samples were collected from some of the food supplies that were available in the mess on the day of the investigation. Surface swabs were taken from some of the utensils that were used in the mess for food preparation.

Water samples were collected from the Jojo water storage tanks that are on campus, municipal water taps on campus and in the bungalows. The water samples were sent to the National Health Laboratory Service (NHLS) infection control service laboratory in Johannesburg for water quality indicator testing.

Laboratory methodologies of human specimens

Stool and rectal swab samples were screened for Vibrio cholerae using culture methods. The samples were inoculated on 5% Blood agar, Thiosulfate–citrate–bile salts–sucrose agar (TCBS), and then incubated at 37◦C under aerobic conditions for 18–24 hours. Suspected yellow colonies on TCBS were biochemically identified using the VITEK automated system (BioMérieux, Marcy I’ Étoile, France). Colonies that identified as Vibrio cholerae were further classified by performing a slide agglutination test with polyvalent somatic (O) antigen grouping sera and specific monovalent antisera (MAST Diagnostics, Merseyside, UK) to differentiate between Vibrio cholerae O1 and Vibrio cholerae non-O1 strains.

Genomic DNA was isolated from bacteria using the Invitrogen PureLink Microbiome DNA Purification Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Carlsbad, California, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Real-time PCR was used to detect the presence of cholera toxin (ctxA gene) [13]. Conventional PCR and analysis of PCR products using agarose gel electrophoresis was used to detect the presence of allelic variants of the toxin co-regulated pilus (tcpA gene) which determined the biotype (classical or El Tor) of Vibrio cholerae O114. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed using Illumina NextSeq (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) next generation sequencing technology, with DNA libraries were prepared using a Nextera DNA Flex Library Preparation Kit (Illumina), followed by 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing runs with ~ 80 times coverage. All these methodologies associated with WGS, were performed using the protocols and standard operating procedures as described by the manufacturers of the kits and equipment.

Raw WGS data (FastQ files of paired-end reads) were uploaded to the EnteroBase web-based platform (http://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk/species/index/vibrio), for further analysis using the multiple bioinformatics tools available at the platform. Analysis included core-genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) which was used to investigate the genetic relatedness of isolates. The EnteroBase cgMLST scheme (Vibrio cgMLST + HierCC) incorporates 1128 core genes. Phylogenetic cluster analysis of cgMLST data was depicted using a GrapeTree-generated minimum spanning tree using the EnteroBase ‘MSTree V2’ algorithm (https://bitbucket.org/enterobase/enterobase-web/wiki/GrapeTree)15.

WGS data have been deposited at the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject identification number PRJEB39740. The WGS data is also available at EnteroBase (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk/species/index/vibrio).

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected through an online questionnaire and saved onto an excel spreadsheet that was encrypted with a password. Access to the data was limited to the investigation team members who managed the data. R Studio was used to calculate attack rates and risk ratios to identify possible risk factors associated with illness.

Results

Epidemiological findings

During the study period, there were 466 trainees on campus, and the questionnaire was distributed to each of them. Two hundred and thirty-eight participants completed the online questionnaire on 1 June 2023, with a response rate of 51% (238/466). 37% of the participants (88/238) reported being ill. Participants came from all nine provinces of South Africa with KwaZulu-Natal having most participants (n = 39), followed by Gauteng (n = 37) and Northern Cape provinces (n = 27); while the Free State Province had the least number of participants (n = 14). Males constituted the majority (66%; 157/238) of the total participants (Table 1; Fig. 1). Eighty-eight cases were identified (Fig. 2), of whom 78% (69/88) reported acute onset of watery diarrhea. Other symptoms reported included abdominal cramps (53%; 47), headache (42%; 37), body weakness (39%; 34), vomiting (19%; 17) and leg pains (Fig. 3). The median age of cases was 35 years and males accounted for 76% (67/88) of the cases. Among those who reported illness duration, median illness duration was four days (IQR: 2 and 8 days).

Attack rates were calculated for various factors, including water sources on campus, availability of soap in the bathrooms, water availability in the bathrooms, sources of food and water outside the campus and sex. Five factors were identified that were either associated with risk of illness or protection against illness. Risk factors associated with illness included brushing teeth with tap water (RR = 1.8; CI = 1.2–2.6), sex (RR = 1.6; CI = 1.1–2.5) and unavailability of hand washing soap in the bathrooms (RR = 1.6; CI = 1.2–2.3); while eating food bought outside the college (RR = 0.5; CI = 0.4–0.8) and availability of hand washing soap in the bathrooms were protective against illness (RR = 0.4; CI = 0.3–0.7) (Table 2).

Laboratory findings

Seventy-two cases provided specimens which included 49 stool specimens and 23 rectal swabs. Stool culture was performed on all the specimens. Vibrio cholerae was isolated from two of the 72 specimens. The two isolates were further characterised as Vibrio cholerae O1 Ogawa; multilocus sequence type 69 (ST69). Through the use of a multiplex cholera PCR the presence of the Vibrio cholerae toxin-gene was also confirmed on both isolates. Further analysis using cgMLST showed that these two isolates were highly genetically related (they showed no allele differences on cgMLST). In addition, cgMLST showed that these two isolates were also highly related to cholera isolates associated with a community outbreak of cholera in the surrounding area of Hammanskraal that occurred during the same period (Fig. 4).

Environmental findings

During the environmental assessment, gaps were noted in the general hygiene standards of the kitchen. These included a cold room that was not operational, lack of cold chain monitoring systems and lack of adherence to wearing of personal protective equipment by the food handlers.

Seven non-human samples were collected during the investigation, including three drinking water samples from different sources within the campus (jojo tank, kitchen water tap, water tap outside the kitchen), three food samples from the kitchen (raw chicken thigh, raw steak, raw cut vegetables) and a surface swab from the kitchen chopping table. Two of the seven samples were non-compliant with standard regulations, these included raw chicken thigh and the drinking tap water. Laboratory analysis of the raw chicken thigh sample showed the presence of Escherichia coli (>1 500 cfu/g), Listeria spp., and a high coliform count (>150 000 cfu/g). The water sample from drinking tap water had a fecal coliform count of (>150 cfu/100 ml), above the recommended standard of 0 cfu/100 ml in drinking water [16, 17].

Discussion

In this outbreak, diarrhoeal illness was associated with the brushing of teeth using municipal tap water, unavailability of handwashing soap in the bathrooms and being of male sex. The risk of illness was twofold higher among people who brushed their teeth with tap water compared to those who brushed their teeth with other water sources. The availability of soap in the bathrooms at all times was protective against illness. Being male carried a higher risk of illness compared to females.

The presence of fecal coliforms in the municipal tap water is an important factor in the context of the epidemiological findings; as it indicates fecal contamination of the water. When there is fecal contamination of drinking water sources, a variety of waterborne pathogens can be introduced into the water that may lead to waterborne diseases including cholera [18–20]. This diarrheal outbreak was likely caused my multiple pathogens due to the consumption of contaminated water.

From the analysis, the people who brushed their teeth with municipal tap water were at a higher risk of developing illness. This is supported by the fact that fecal coliforms were detected in the tap water samples. Although the trainees were advised not to drink tap water upon their arrival at the college, most of them continued to brush their teeth using tap water. An association between unintentional consumption of contaminated through brushing of teeth and cholera infection have been reported in literature [21, 22].

The absence of handwashing soap in the bathrooms was associated with illness. This is scientifically plausible and has been reported in other studies [23–25]. Waterborne diseases are generally transmitted through the oral-fecal route and good hand hygiene is one of the proven methods to curb person-to-person transmission of such diseases. The lack of soap in the bathrooms was significantly associated with the risk of illness while the presence of soap was protective as shown in other outbreak studies [23, 24]. This suggests that some of the infections were secondary infections that occurred through person-to-person transmission as a result of poor hand hygiene. Although none of the food handlers reported illness, secondary infections may also occur through sharing of food and drinks.

Being of male sex was a significant risk associated with illness. This could be explained by the fact that the number of male trainees attending the training was almost double that of females. The differences in hygiene practices between males and females may also be another factor driving this association [26]. Buying food from outside the college was associated with a decreased risk of developing illness. This could be attributable to the fact that those who buy food off campus are less likely to drink tap water because there are no communal water taps off campus.

Although there were only two laboratory-confirmed cases of cholera in this study, it is likely that cholera may have been the major cause of illness reported by the other cases (suspected cases). The symptoms reported by the cases are typical of cholera disease, with profuse watery diarrhea being the predominant symptom. More laboratory-confirmed cholera cases were also identified in the communities surrounding the college which receive water from the same supply. When there is fecal contamination of drinking water sources illness is usually caused by multiple pathogens including enteric virus, bacteria and parasites.

Limitations

Due to resources constraints, multiplex PCR for a variety of enteric bacterial, viral and parasitic pathogens as well as PCR test for presence of Vibrio cholerae and toxin gene, were not performed on all the clinical specimens collected from the college. Only the two specimens that tested positive for Vibrio cholerae on stool culture underwent a Vibrio cholerae PCR test. PCR has a higher sensitivity than culture and may have identified additional cholera cases or other pathogens in culture-negative stool specimens. Stool culture was used to screen a limited number of bacterial pathogens, including Vibrio cholerae, Salmonella and Shigella species.

Members of staff and food handlers were not included in the questionnaire distribution, thus data from these groups is unknown.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this was a diarrheal disease outbreak likely caused by multiple pathogens including Vibrio cholerae, a pattern usually seen when there is fecal contamination of drinking water sources. Based on the findings of this study, we recommend that handwashing soap be made available at all times in all the bathrooms on the college premises by the college management. Provision of safe water to the trainees attending courses at the college should be continued until such a time that the municipal drinking water has been declared safe for drinking by the responsible stakeholders. In addition, trainees at the college should be reminded to brush their teeth with safe (boiled or treated) water only and to avoid getting excess water in their mouths during showering.
