Lifespan exposure to hormone therapies and structural brain morphometry in older women
Authors: Robyn A. Honea, Amber Watts, Shannon D. Donofry, Cristina Molina-Hidalgo, Hayley S Ripperger, Sarah L. Aghjayan, Chaeryon Kang, Swathi Gujral, Lauren E. Oberlin, George Grove, Haiqing Huang, Bradley P. Sutton, Jeffrey M. Burns, Eric D. Vidoni, Arthur F. Kramer, Edward McAuley, Charles H. Hillman, Anna L. Marsland, M. Ilyas Kamboh, Kirk I. Erickson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Although many studies support a neuroprotective role for estrogens and other ovarian hormones in women, findings across imaging studies remain mixed. Few studies have explored both early- and midlife-hormone exposures simultaneously or incorporated whole-brain, voxel-wise approaches. This study examined the effects of early- and midlife exposure to ovarian hormones- via hormonal birth control (BC), menopausal hormone therapy (MHT)- and their timing on brain health in older women. We also studied the relationship of age of menopause (i.e., greater endogenous exposure to ovarian hormones) to brain health in older adulthood.Methods:We analyzed baseline data from 459 women (ages 65–80) in the multi-site IGNITE study, a 12-month randomized aerobic exercise study. We examined retrospective self-report of BC and MHT use in relation to structural MRI metrics using voxel-based morphometry (VBM) for gray matter volume and surface-based morphometry (SBM) for cortical thickness.Findings:BC use compared to no BC use was associated with greater gray matter volume in temporal, occipital, and frontal regions in older adulthood. Longer BC duration was linked to larger fusiform gyrus volume. Combined BC and MHT use compared to no use was associated with greater volume in parietal and temporal areas and thicker cortex in the posterior cingulate and temporal gyri. Later menopause onset correlated with greater posterior cortical thickness. No associations were found for MHT timing or BC start age.Interpretations:Both endogenous and exogenous lifetime exposure to ovarian hormones were associated with structural brain measures generally consistent with preserved brain aging. These findings highlight the importance of exposure timing in women’s brain health and AD risk prevention.

Introduction

The menopause transition is a critical period for understanding possible interventions for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) risk and trajectories for healthy aging (Brinton et al., 2015). Menopausal symptoms reflect physiological changes that influence brain morphology, aging, and cognitive function (Kim et al., 2018, 2014). Compared to age-matched men and pre-menopausal women, women in peri- and post-menopause have greater declines in estrogen-dependent memory and cerebral metabolic rate of glucose consumption, greater hippocampal volume loss, and higher rates of amyloid beta deposition (Mosconi et al., 2018). There is ample evidence that endogenous estrogens, specifically 17β-estradiol, is neuroprotective in the female brain, promoting neural plasticity, adult neurogenesis, and signaling with other neuroprotective factors (Arevalo et al., 2015; Cutter et al., 2003; Green and Simpkins, 2000; Norbury et al., 2003; Rahman et al., 2019; Sahab-Negah et al., 2020). Other ovarian hormones, including progestogens, have been largely overlooked despite evidence that they may play important roles in brain connectivity and cognitive function (Beltz and Moser, 2020; Giatti et al., 2016). Key assessments of endogenous exposure to ovarian hormones include age of menarche, pregnancy history, and menopause onset, with variables such as later age of menopause as an indicator of higher endogenous exposure. Exogenous exposure to estrogens and progestogens, including use of hormone-based birth control (BC) in young adulthood and optimal timing of menopausal hormone therapy (MHT), has also been associated with better cognitive function in older adulthood and lower dementia risk (Andy et al., 2024; Matyi et al., 2019; Ryan et al., 2009; Tierney et al., 2013; Watts et al., 2025). For example, Egan and Gleason (Egan and Gleason, 2012) showed that hormonal contraceptive usage in early adulthood is linked to better verbal memory in later life. Our prior work in this same sample of older women from the Investigating Gains in Neurocognition in an Intervention Trial of Exercise (IGNITE) study demonstrated that early life BC use was associated with better cognitive performance in working memory and executive function domains in older adulthood (Watts et al., 2025). That said, evidence for the benefits of BC/MHT use on later life cognitive outcomes is equivocal. A recent meta-analysis reported that overall, MHT had no effects on cognitive scores (Andy et al., 2024). However, when initiated specifically in midlife or close to menopause onset, MHT containing estrogens and other ovarian hormones was associated with improved verbal memory. To help untangle the mixed results, brain imaging markers may be more sensitive to the effects of hormone medications than cognitive outcomes.

A growing body of work demonstrates that estrogen-based therapy before and during menopause is associated with markers of better brain health, including increased fractional anisotropy in white matter, decreased white matter lesions, and higher volume and thickness in regions typically at risk for aging-related atrophy (Lee et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Witt et al., 2024). Importantly, prior research suggests that the timing and duration of exposure to estrogens and other ovarian hormones matters, often called the Critical Window Hypothesis. Consistent with the Critical Window Hypothesis, several studies have found that shorter intervals between menopause and the initiation of hormone treatment are associated with larger hippocampal volume (Erickson et al., 2010). In contrast, starting MHT 5 or more years after menopause is associated with greater white matter hyperintensity volume (i.e., vascular lesions), and no evidence of MHT-based protection against cognitive decline (Lee et al., 2024; Matthews et al., 1999). Timing of the use of hormone therapy is essential for its safety and efficacy for the treatment of menopause symptoms (Tierney et al., 2013). Based on prior evidence, we hypothesized that timing and duration of hormone-based BC and MHT will be associated with brain volumes in older adult women. The majority of studies investigating reproductive history data and neuroimaging measures have focused on specific AD-related regions of interest (Erickson et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2024), and no studies have expanded to look across the whole brain and all regions using sensitive voxel-based methodology, especially important given both alpha and beta estrogen receptors, as well as other estrogen-related receptors, are located in the cortex (Turek and Gasior, 2023). This study sought to fill a key gap in the literature by examining both early- and midlife exposure to hormone therapy and their timing, using voxel- and vertex-based imaging in women, to explore which brain regions may be most sensitive to BC and MHT use over the lifetime, without limitations to AD-specific regions.

Based on prior evidence that: 1) lifetime exposure to ovarian hormones is beneficial to healthy brain aging, and 2) the timing of exposure matters such that there is a critical window (i.e., Critical Window Hypothesis) for any beneficial effects to occur, the present study evaluated associations of hormonal BC and MHT use history and neuroimaging markers across the whole brain using voxel (VBM) and surface-based morphometry (SBM). VBM and SBM capture complementary aspects of brain structure. Gray matter volume derived from VBM reflects the combined contributions of cortical thickness, surface area, and cortical folding, whereas SBM metrics such as cortical thickness provide a direct estimate of the distance between the pial and white matter boundaries, offering a more anatomically specific measure of cortical laminar structure and one that may be particularly sensitive to early neurodegenerative changes (Fischl and Dale, 2000; Hutton et al., 2009; Winkler et al., 2010). We proposed the following hypotheses: 1) hormone-based BC use and duration in younger adulthood would be associated with greater brain volumes and cortical thickness in older adulthood, 2) MHT and its timing near the menopause transition would be associated with greater brain volumes and cortical thickness in older adulthood 3) later age of menopause, an indicator of endogenous exposure to ovarian hormones, will be associated with greater brain volumes and cortical thickness in older adulthood. The present study specifically extends prior knowledge by evaluating contributions of both early and later exposure to exogenous hormone-based medications to brain volume in older adulthood, and by using thickness surface-based morphometry- an imaging metric rarely used in previous studies on hormone use.

Methods

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committees and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The IGNITE study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards at the University of Pittsburgh(IRB1910244; approved on May 10, 2016), Northeastern University (IRB17–05–02; approved on July 18, 2017), and the University of Kansas Medical Center (IRB00140896; approved on June 5, 2017). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. All study procedures complied with relevant institutional guidelines and federal regulations governing research involving human subjects. Data were de-identified prior to analysis.

Study design

The IGNITE study (Investigating Gains in Neurocognition in an Intervention Trial of Exercise: NCT02875301, R01AG053952) was a multi-center (Pittsburgh, Boston, Kansas City) randomized clinical trial that examined the effects of a 12-month aerobic exercise intervention on cognitive performance and neuroimaging markers of brain health in physically inactive, cognitively unimpaired older adults. The present study is a secondary analysis of baseline data from the IGNITE study. Thus, results are not affected by participation in the exercise intervention.

Participants

Recruitment took place through newspapers, health system research registries, direct mailings, senior centers, churches, and online media, and have been previously described (Erickson et al., 2019; Oberlin et al., 2025). Participants were enrolled on a rolling basis between 2017 and 2022 with recruitment of racially and ethnically underrepresented participants proportional to the demographic characteristics of each study site. Female participants made up 71.1 % of the total study sample. The present study included data from only female participants with neuroimaging data and hormone history data (N = 459). Detailed study inclusion criteria are published elsewhere (Erickson et al., 2019). Briefly, participants were required to be 65–80 (mean age 69.57) years old, relatively physically inactive (i.e., exercise <3 days per week, and exercise <20 min per day on days of exercise), and able to safely engage in moderate intensity exercise. Cognitive status was adjudicated by a panel of clinical neuropsychologists using 2011 National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) criteria and only cognitively unimpaired participants were eligible for continued participation (Albert et al., 2011; McKhann et al., 2011).

Measures

Reproductive history and hormone therapy

Participants completed a self-report questionnaire regarding menopause and reproductive history that was designed for the current study, including age of menarche, experience of menopause and the age of onset. Participants reported whether they had ever used birth control (BC) or hormone therapy (MHT), and the approximate age they started and stopped using the medications. Participants reported whether the drugs they reported contained estradiol. It is important to note that BC and MHT may contain a variety of estrogens, progestins, and potentially other reproductive hormones, and participants may not accurately report which of these medications contain. We calculated the duration of therapy of MHT and BC, and the distance between MHT start and menopause onset. All women were included regardless of whether they had surgical menopause or spontaneous menopause onset, and for women with surgical onset age at surgical intervention was used as menopause onset age. We classified current medication use according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) system and the Defined Daily Dose (DDD) as a unit of measure according to the World Health Organization methodology (Hollingworth and Kairuz, 2021). In the present study, we included medications classified as G3, indicating that they contain “sex hormones and modulators of the genital system” such as estradiol and progestins, removing reports of thyroid hormones, insulin, and other hormones not related to estrogens or progestins. Some participants reported hormone-based medication use but did not report the medication type and were still included in analyses.

APOE genotyping

The common APOE polymorphism has three alleles (APOE2, APOE3, and APOE4). Carriers of the APOE ε4 allele have an increased risk of AD, with homozygous (ε4/ ε4 genotype) having the greatest elevation in risk (Yamazaki et al., 2019). Genotyping for the two APOE SNPs resulting in six genotypes was performed on DNA samples using TaqMan assays (Fan et al., 2023). Participants with at least one ε4 allele (ε4/ε2, ε4/ε3, or ε4/ε4) were classified as APOE4 carriers following standard conventions in the field where ε4 confers dominant risk even in ε2/ε4 heterozygotes. The ε2/ε4 genotype was rare overall (n = 17, 3.7 % of sample) and similarly distributed across hormone exposure groups (4.4 % in never-users vs. 2.7 % in ever-users; p > 0.2), minimizing potential bias from this mixed-risk genotype. We included APOE ε4 (APOE4) carrier status as a covariate.

Participant demographics

Age and years of formal education were determined by participant self-report and included as covariates in our analyses. A composite score reflecting SES was generated from measures of income, savings, debt-adjusted savings, and financial stability from the MacArthur Socioeconomic Status Index (Seeman et al., 2004). A description of the creation of this composite score can be found in pre-print form at the following link https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5062727. This variable was used for sample descriptive purposes only.

Structural brain imaging acquisition

Detailed scan acquisition parameters and quality control for the IGNITE study have been reported previously (Erickson et al., 2019). Briefly, participants underwent an MRI scan at baseline and following the 12-month intervention. The University of Pittsburgh and Northeastern University used a Siemens Prisma 3T with a 64-channel head coil, and the University of Kansas Medical Center used a Siemens Skyra 3T scanner with a 32-channel head coil. Image sequences and image quality were standardized across sites, as imaging protocols were designed to be exact matches. Phantom scans were run at each site on a regular basis to ensure the stability of the scans and geometry across scanners, and a human phantom was run through the protocol annually, thus we had a strict QC and identical protocol, and site is modelled as a variables and we did not perform other harmonization approaches. For the present analysis, only the baseline high-resolution three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition with gradient echo (MPRAGE) T1-weighted anatomical scan was used (Field of View 256 mm, 224 slices, sagittal, 0.8 mm isotropic resolution, TE/TI/TR=2.31/1060/2400 ms). Following data collection, all MRI data were transferred to the XNAT server at the coordinating center (University of Pittsburgh) for quality control assessment, and 459 women had baseline brain imaging data that met quality control parameters.

Voxel-Based and surface-based morphometry

For voxel based morphometry (VBM) and surface-based morphometry (SBM) analysis and pre-processing of T1-weighted images, we used the Computational Anatomical Toolbox 12 (CAT12 (Gaser et al., 2024) Version 12.6, C. Gaser, Structural Brain Mapping Group, Jena University Hospital, Jena, Germany; http://dbm.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/) through Statistical Parametric Mapping version 12 (SPM12; Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK; http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/) that operate under Matlab (R2023a; the Mathworks, Natick, MA) on MacOS Sequoia. This was used for gray matter volume (VBM) and surface-based measures (SBM) of cortical thickness (CT). The SBM procedure (http://www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat12/CAT12-Manual.pdf) was conducted using default settings for cross-sectional analysis.

T1 images were corrected for bias-field inhomogeneities, registered using linear (12-parameter affine) and non-linear transformations, spatially normalized with the high-dimensional DARTEL algorithm into Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space (Ashburner, 2007), and segmented into gray matter (GMV), white matter (WMV), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and white matter hyperintensity (WMH) maps. We used the “calculate white matter hyperintensity as its own class” option in the CAT12 processing stream, which is useful in aging populations because it excludes lesion intensities from the traditional white or gray matter classes and improves segmentation accuracy. Total intracranial volume (TIV) was calculated using GMV, WMV, and CSF volumes. Volume changes were scaled to preserve the original local volumes by modulating the segmentations. The modulated gray matter segmentations were smoothed with a 12 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel before group-level voxel-wise analysis. Resampled surface data for cortical thickness (CT) was smoothed with a 15 mm FWHM kernel prior to second-level analyses, as recommended by CAT12. Gray matter volume regions of interest were calculated using the Neuromorphometric atlas and adjusted for total intracranial volume.

Statistics; VBM and SBM statistical analysis

For all analyses, voxels are reported with reference to the MNI standard space within SPM12. To avoid possible edge effects at the border between gray and white matter and to include only relatively homogeneous voxels, we used an absolute threshold masking of 0.10 for each analysis. We used a full-factorial model to investigate voxel- and vertex-wise differences between groups based on combinations of history of use or non-use of BC and MHT, accounting for age, education, study site, APOE4 carrier status, and TIV (included only for VBM analyses per CAT12 recommendations) as covariates.

The group comparisons followed a structured order to evaluate main effects and interactions between BC and MHT exposure (see Table 1). First, we compared BC+ vs. BC− as an overall main effect. Next, we assessed the effect of MHT within BC+ women (MHT+ vs. MHT−) and within BC− women. We then tested whether BC history modified effects among MHT+ (BC+ vs. BC−) and MHT− (BC+ vs. BC−) groups. Finally, we compared women with both BC and MHT use to those with neither exposure (MHT+ BC+ vs. MHT− BC−) to evaluate potential cumulative hormonal exposure effects. We did not evaluate the contrast of MHT− BC+ vs. MHT+BC− as it did not fit our hypothesis.

To evaluate the role of hormone timing and duration (see Table 1), we conducted linear regression models of brain morphometry using continuous predictors, including: age at BC initiation, duration of BC use, age at menopause, age at MHT initiation, MHT use duration, and the interval between menopause and MHT onset. For age at BC initiation and duration of BC use, only individuals that identified that they used BC were included, regardless of their later MHT use. For age at MHT initiation and MHT use duration, only individuals that identified that they used MHT were included, regardless of their earlier BC use. All timing models were adjusted for age, education, study site, APOE4 status, and TIV (in VBM only).

All in-brain statistical analyses were conducted using SPM in voxel-wise or vertex-wise space. Family-wise error (FWE) correction was applied to the entire brain, and we considered a corrected p < 0.05 as significant. We also ran non-parametric statistical analyses using the threshold-free cluster enhancement method (TFCE) (Smith and Nichols, 2009; Spisak et al., 2019), which allows for cluster-based inference without the need to pre-specify arbitrary thresholds. This implementation in the TFCE toolbox for CAT12 performs parametric permutation tests, thus avoiding problems inherent to parametric statistics (Eklund et al., 2016), and has been recommended in similar SBM-based whole-brain analyses (Bachmann et al., 2023). Because the categorical and timing predictors represented distinct biologically motivated questions, they were analyzed in separate whole-brain models rather than collapsed into a single omnibus framework, with VBM and SBM considered complementary but distinctly different modalities. Anatomical labeling of peak coordinates in VBM was conducted using the Wake Forest Pickatlas AAL atlas. For surface-based morphometry (SBM), regional labeling for plotting and interpretation was based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas (Desikan et al., 2006).

Results

Participant demographic characteristics

The present study included N = 459 female participants from the IGNITE study between the ages of 65 and 80 years. More than three-quarters of women in our sample reported a history of BC use (n = 350, 76.8 %). The average age of starting BC was 21.69 (SD = 4.75), with an average duration of 8.41 years (SD = 6.94). Approximately 40 % of women in our sample reported MHT (n = 185), with an average starting age of 47.36 (SD = 7.05), and an average duration of 8.95 years (SD = 10.33). Birth control use was not associated with age at natural menopause (t=−0.234 (df) =372 p = 0.879). Discussion

Our study demonstrates that lifetime exposure to ovarian hormones through hormonal contraceptives (birth control, BC) and MHT is associated with higher gray matter volume in AD-vulnerable regions, particularly the parahippocampal gyrus and middle temporal cortex. These findings align with preclinical evidence for a neuroprotective role of estrogens in synaptic plasticity and neurogenesis (Woolley, 1998). Importantly, our results suggest that exposure to ovarian hormones during distinct hormonal windows- early adulthood and the menopausal transition- may have be related to more favorable morphometric profiles in the brain reflective of brain health. Our data support hypothesis 1, that BC use in young adulthood (average start age 22) was associated with larger regional volumes across the temporal, limbic, and frontal cortices in older adulthood. This suggests that hormone exposures early in life may have long-term structural correlates in the brain. Consistent with hypothesis 2, we found that MHT use, with and without BC, was associated with larger volume and thickness in the parietal and temporal cortices. These results partially support the Critical Window Hypothesis; later menopause onset was associated with greater cortical thickness in temporal and parietal brain regions. BC use in early adulthood also predicted larger parahippocampal volumes decades later. Together, these findings underscore the possibility that hormone timing- both in relation to reproductive milestones and the lifespan- may shape brain aging trajectories. Because hormone use patterns reflect complex reproductive and health histories across the lifespan, the structural associations observed here likely represent the combined influence of exposure to ovarian hormones and broader biological and health trajectories.

A systematic review suggests that neuroimaging markers of hormone exposure vary by modality, hormone type, and life stage (Dubol et al., 2021). Compared to earlier studies in young adults, our data provide unique insight into the long-term associations between hormone exposure and late-life brain structure. Most notably, we observed that combined exposure to BC and MHT was associated with both increased cortical thickness and gray matter volume, particularly in posterior parietal and temporal regions. The association we found between BC use and larger gray matter volume in the left inferior frontal gyrus and right parahippocampal gyrus extends prior work showing BC-related volumetric increases in prefrontal and temporal regions (Pletzer et al., 2019). Of note, in prior studies birth control use has had mixed associations with parahippocampal region volume. For example, Lisofsky et al. (Lisofsky et al., 2016) found decreased parahippocampal volume in BC users, whereas Pletzer et al. found increased volume in BC users, however these studies were conducted in young adults (mean age early to mid 20 s), not older adults (Pletzer et al., 2010). Pletzer et al. suggest that an explanation may be that some progestins in BC are androgenic while others are anti-androgenic (Pletzer et al., 2010). They found larger gray matter volumes in the parahippocampal gyri in users of anti-androgenic progestins, but not in users of androgenic progestins. Our findings of larger parahippocampal volume in older women with a history of BC use are consistent with the possibility that BC plays a critical role in older adult brain health.

Moreover, longer duration of birth control use was associated with greater volume in the right fusiform gyrus, a smaller region within the superior temporal gyrus/parahippocampal gyrus complex. The relationship between BC duration and the fusiform gyrus aligns with prior studies showing fusiform volume increases following sustained exposure to estrogens during mid-cycle peaks, possibly reflecting estrogen-dependent synaptic density changes in the object and face-processing regions (Pletzer et al., 2010; Protopopescu et al., 2008). Fusiform gyrus activity, particularly in response to facial emotion stimuli, has been shown to vary across the menstrual cycle, with increased activation during the follicular phase in women (Derntl et al., 2008; Protopopescu et al., 2005). While speculative, it is possible that this cyclical modulation may reflect an evolutionary adaptation, enhancing facial emotional recognition and social attunement during phases of high fertility, potentially improving mate selection or social bonding (Pearson and Lewis, 2005). Our parahippocampal gyrus findings are particularly notable given its role in spatial cognition and mixed results in younger cohorts. Our observation that longer BC duration correlated with fusiform gyrus volume aligns with evidence that previous contraceptive treatment duration positively correlates with hippocampal and basal ganglia volumes bilaterally, though that study reported that effects on the hippocampus appear to reverse after discontinuation, while basal ganglia effects persisted (Pletzer et al., 2019).

We also found that combined BC and MHT use was associated with larger volumes in the parietal cortex and thicker middle temporal cortex compared to individuals with no BC use and without MHT use. The larger thickness in the middle temporal cortex in combined BC+MHT users supports the broader literature demonstrating that estrogens favor synaptic formation and are necessary for axonal growth and synaptic plasticity, ultimately improving performance on memory and cognition tests (Luine, 2014). A smaller VBM study on 41 women with and without MHT showed that women using MHT had significantly larger volumes across very similar regions of cortex to our results, including the precuneus, inferior temporal cortex, parahippocampal cortex, and frontal cortex (Kim et al., 2023). These regional effects likely reflect estrogen’s capacity to increase dendritic spine density and enhance sensitivity to excitatory synaptic input through both AMPA and NMDA receptors, creating more complex neural circuits with greater numbers of synaptic contacts (Woolley, 1998).

We investigated the relationship of gray matter volume and thickness with the interval between menopause onset and hormone treatment initiation. While a prior study found that a shorter interval between menopause onset and hormone treatment initiation is associated with larger hippocampal volumes (Lord et al., 2008), we did not find a linear relationship in our voxel-based regression model between that interval and gray matter volume or thickness. However, multiple longitudinal and cross-sectional neuroimaging studies have shown that volumetric changes in the hippocampus and adjacent temporal regions are most pronounced during early exposure to estrogens. For example, Protopopescu et al. demonstrated cyclical gray matter volume increases in the anterior hippocampus during the high-estrogen mid-cycle period (Protopopescu et al., 2008). Complimenting this, Lisofsky et al. (2016) and De Bondt et al. (2013) reported associations between circulating estradiol and hippocampal/parahippocampal volume across menstrual phases. These findings collectively suggest that timing, even on a cyclic scale, can have measurable effects on hippocampal structure. Although we found no significant association between MHT timing and brain structure, later menopause onset, an indicator of endogenous exposure to ovarian hormones, was associated with greater cortical thickness in posterior brain areas, in line with studies linking longer reproductive span to preserved brain structure and lower dementia risk. These findings suggest that endogenous hormonal duration may be as relevant—if not more so—than therapeutic intervention timing in supporting brain health.

Given the growing literature, the clinical relevance of these findings extends to precision medicine approaches for menopausal therapy. Early BC use with a mean start age around 22 years and MHT initiation within 5 years post-menopause may optimize brain health by capitalizing on the “healthy cell bias of estrogen action,” which posits that women benefit most from hormone therapy when treated at the time of the menopause transition before neurological compromise occurs. The timing of neurons’ exposure to ovarian hormones is crucial for producing positive effects on healthy cells while avoiding potential harm to functionally compromised cells. Users of hormone therapy containing estrogens during menopause show increased hippocampal volumes (Braden et al., 2017; Lord et al., 2008). We found that women in this study who used MHT at all had larger volumes of the cuneus, precuneus, and temporal cortex, and individuals that used MHT but did not ever use BC had thicker cortex in the lingual gyrus, such that even MHT initiation without BC history has benefits for brain health.

Limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of whole-brain, voxel-wise and vertex-wise morphometry, a relatively large sample size, and consideration of both early-life and midlife hormone exposures. However, limitations include reliance on retrospective self-report of hormone history, absence of formulation-specific data, and the cross-sectional design, which limits causal inference. While our findings are broadly consistent with models suggesting a neuroprotective role of lifetime estrogen exposure, they should also be interpreted within a broader multiple-model framework. Alternative explanations, including heterogeneity in hormone formulations, dosing, and timing, as well as unmeasured health and lifestyle factors, may contribute to the observed associations. It remains possible that underlying brain structure or health status may influence hormone use patterns or reproductive characteristics, rather than the reverse. Other ovarian hormones, including progestogens, have been largely overlooked in brain health research and are a key component of many hormone therapy regimens. Longitudinal data would be needed to track hormone exposure trajectories and corresponding brain changes over time. Retrospective self-report of hormone use introduces potential recall bias, despite the use of validated reproductive history instruments, particularly given the decades-long intervals between exposure and assessment. The heterogeneous nature of MHT formulations presents another limitation, as we did not analyze unopposed estrogens versus progestin-combined therapies separately, despite evidence that progestogens may modulate estrogen-related neural effects. MHT can contain multiple differing types of estrogens (e.g., natural or bioidentical, conjugated), may include or exclude progestins or testosterone, may be taken according to differing timing regimens (e.g., cyclical, or continuous), include a variety of dosages, and be administered via differing routes (e.g., oral, transdermal), all of which may influence its effects on cognitive performance and brain health (Andy et al., 2024; Puri et al., 2025). Additionally, unmeasured confounders, including specific contraceptive formulations, progestin androgenicity, and endogenous hormone levels, were not assessed. Although our focus was on exogenous exposures, endogenous factors such as pregnancy history, age at menarche, and other features of the menopausal transition may also contribute to total lifetime hormone exposure.

In addition, because multiple planned categorical contrasts and timing regressions were conducted across two complementary morphometric modalities, the broader study-wide multiplicity burden should be considered when interpreting the results, even though each individual whole-brain analysis was controlled using stringent family-wise error correction. Hormone use across the lifespan is imbedded within broader health and socioeconomic contexts that may also influence brain structure in aging. Decisions regarding hormonal contraceptive use or menopausal hormone therapy are often associated with factors such as healthcare access, reproductive history, cardiometabolic health, and lifestyle behaviors. Variables including vascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, or body mass index), parity, age at menarche, depressive history, and physical activity may independently relate to brain morphometry and could potentially contribute to observed differences between hormone users and non-users. Although our models adjusted for key demographic and genetic factors including age, education, study site, APOE4 carrier status, and total intracranial volume, we did not model the full range of life-course health variables that may influence both hormone exposure patterns and brain structure. As such, the associations observed in this study should be interpreted as reflecting relationships between reported exposure to ovarian hormones and brain morphology within a broader context of lifetime health factors. In addition, participants were relatively physically inactive at baseline (self-reported structured moderate-to-vigorous exercise <20min/day on <3days/week), consistent with IGNITE inclusion criteria designed to detect intervention effects. While this restricted range in recent activity likely minimized confounding by current exercise levels in our cross-sectional hormone analyses, it may limit generalizability to more active older women. Future studies in more heterogeneous activity cohorts could test whether these hormone-brain associations vary by fitness or activity level. Finally, a small number of ε2/ε4 heterozygotes (n = 17) were included in the ε4 carrier group due to the dominant risk effect of ε4; however, their low prevalence and balanced distribution across hormone exposure groups makes it unlikely that this influenced the findings. Future longitudinal studies incorporating detailed reproductive, hormonal, and cardiometabolic measures will be important clarifying the temporal relationships and disentangling the contributions of hormone exposure from broader life-course influences on brain aging.

Future directions

Several critical research directions emerge from these findings that could advance our understanding of the neuroprotective mechanisms of estrogens and other ovarian hormones. Longitudinal imaging studies are essential to track the temporal dynamics of BC and MHT effects on brain atrophy trajectories, particularly during the menopausal transition when the window of opportunity for neuroprotection may be most critical. Longitudinal study design would also help disentangle discrepancies between findings in volume versus thickness, although for most analyses, there were findings in both modalities, they just did not reach the threshold for statistical significance in one or the other; thus, a larger sample will also aid in teasing out these relationships with brain morphometry. Molecular imaging approaches, such as estrogen receptor PET imaging, could quantify receptor density differences in women with different hormone replacement and menopause histories. Intervention trials testing estrogen formulations optimized for menopausal timing and genetic risk profiles represent the next logical step for translating these observational findings into clinical practice. Future studies should also investigate the mechanistic pathways underlying effects of estrogens on synaptic plasticity, including its role in promoting dendritic spine formation and axonal growth, which are crucial for the survival of new neurons and maintenance of cognitive function (Luine, 2014).

Conclusions

In summary, our findings suggest that both early and midlife exposure to ovarian hormones are associated with brain structure measures generally consistent with preserved morphology in aging. Notably, use of hormonal contraceptives was associated with larger volumes in the temporal and limbic cortices, and longer durations of hormonal contraceptive use were linked to greater gray matter volume in the fusiform, while timing of MHT initiation showed no consistent relationship with cortical structure. These results partially support the Critical Window Hypothesis and highlight the potential role of cumulative ovarian hormone exposure across the lifespan. Future studies are needed to clarify the influence of hormone type, formulation, and timing on long-term brain outcomes and dementia risk.
