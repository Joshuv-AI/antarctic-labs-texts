Aligning Diffusion Language Models via Unpaired Preference Optimization
Vaibhav Jindal 1 * † Hejian Sang 1 * Chun-Mao Lai 3 Yanning Chen 1 Zhipeng Wang 1 1 LinkedIn Corporation, CA, USA 3 University of California San Diego, CA, USA

Abstract
Diffusion language models (dLLMs) are an emerging alternative to autoregressive (AR) generators, but aligning them to human preferences is challenging because sequence log-likelihoods are intractable and pairwise preference data are costly to collect. We introduce ELBO-KTO, which combines an ELBO surrogate for diffusion log-likelihoods with a prospect-theoretic, unpaired preference objective (Kahneman–Tversky Optimization, KTO). We analyze the bias and variance induced by the ELBO substitution and employ variance-reduction practices that stabilize gradients during training. Applied to LLaDA-8B-Instruct, ELBO-KTO yields 65.9% and 62.3% adjusted win rates on kto-mix-14k and UltraFeedback-Binary, respectively, versus the base model under an automatic LLM judge. Across downstream tasks, including GSM8K, MMLU, and additional reasoning/knowledge benchmarks, ELBO-KTO trained on UltraFeedback-Binary performs on par with or better than the base model under identical decoding. This establishes unpaired preference optimization as a viable alternative to pairwise alignment in diffusion LLMs.

INTRODUCTION

Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences is central to building helpful systems. In autoregressive LLMs, methods such as Reinforcement learning
from human feedback (RLHF)
(
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Stiennon et al. 2020
;
Schulman et al. 2017
)
and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
and its variants
(
Meng et al. 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2023
;
Azar et al. 2024
)
are standard and rely on curated
paired
comparisons for each prompt. Diffusion-style large language models (dLLMs) extend text generation beyond left-to-right decoding by iteratively refining a sequence in parallel
(
Ye et al. 2025
;
Ou et al. 2024
;
Lou et al. 2024
;
Nie et al. 2025
;
Khanna et al. 2025
)
which makes sentence-level likelihoods intractable and complicates preference optimization.

Recent approaches to dLLMs have replaced log-likelihoods with ELBO-based surrogates and incorporated variance reduction techniques to stabilize training, but remain constrained by the assumption of paired preference data
(
Zhu et al. 2025
)
. This assumption limits scalability and applicability in practical settings where feedback is abundant but inherently unpaired—as in binary “good/bad” user ratings or safety filter signals.

In this work, we take a fundamentally different direction by proposing ELBO-KTO as a framework to train dLLMs directly from unpaired preference signals. Our approach integrates ELBO estimates into KTO’s value computation via an ELBO margin (policy vs. reference) and a baseline for variance control. This formulation unlocks learning from a broader class of real-world “good/bad” feedback signals while preserving training stability through lightweight variance reduction techniques.

The main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We present ELBO-KTO, a principled preference optimization framework that enables alignment of diffusion language models (dLLMs) from unpaired feedback.

•

We provide a theoretical analysis of the bias and variance tradeoff introduced by ELBO substitution, and prove that our estimator enjoys bounded bias with controlled variance.

•

We empirically validate our proposed method ELBO-KTO on kto-mix-14k
(
TRL contributors 2024
)
and UltraFeedback-Binary
(
Cui et al. 2023
)
, where it achieves
65.9%
and
62.3%
adjusted win rates over the base model, demonstrating consistent improvements. Extensive analysis confirms that our method provides a simple yet effective way to align dLLMs with stronger models. On downstream reasoning/knowledge tasks such as GSM8K and MMLU, ELBO-KTO trained on UltraFeedback-Binary performs on par with or better than the LLaDA-8B-Instruct.

PRELIMINARIES

2.1
Diffusion Large Language Models and LLaDA

LLaDA
(
Nie et al. 2025
)
is an 8B-parameter masked diffusion model (MDM) pretrained on 2.3T tokens (LLaDA-8B-Base) and fine-tuned on 4.5M instruction pairs (LLaDA-8B-Instruct). LLaDA achieves performance competitive with leading autoregressive LLMs, demonstrating strong scalability, in-context learning, and instruction-following capabilities. In this work, we adopt LLaDA-8B-Instruct as our base model for studying alignment in diffusion LLMs using the KTO algorithm.

2.2
VRPO: Variance Reduced Preference Optimization for dLLMs

While Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) operates on sentence-level log-likelihoods, such quantities are intractable in masked diffusion LMs. Variance-Reduced Preference Optimization (VRPO) addresses this by replacing log-likelihoods with ELBO surrogates that decompose over diffusion steps
(
Zhu et al. 2025
)
.

ELBO.

Given a prompt
x
, and a corresponding full response
y
, we draw a noised intermediate
y_{t}
from the forward process
q(y_{t}\mid t,y,x)
at a randomly sampled diffusion timestep
t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
and evaluate a per-step loss
\ell_{\pi}
. Averaging over
t
and
y_{t}
yields the ELBO

\mathcal{B}_{\pi}(y\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]}\ \mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\sim q(\cdot\mid t,y,x)}\!\big[\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y\mid x)\big],

(1)

where
\ell_{\pi}
is the per-step loss of the mask prediction model as defined in Appendix A.
\mathcal{B}_{\pi}
is a lower-bound of
\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)

(
Lou et al. 2024
;
Ou et al. 2024
)
.

Monte Carlo Estimator.

Sampling
n_{t}
time steps,
\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
, and
n_{y_{t}}
draws per timestep,
\{y^{(i)}_{t_{j}}\}_{i=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\sim q(y_{t_{j}}\mid t_{j},y,x)
,

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\frac{1}{n_{y_{t}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\ell_{\pi}\!\big(y^{(i)}_{t_{j}},t_{j},y\mid x\big)

(2)

is the Monte Carlo Estimator of
\mathcal{B}_{\pi}
.

VRPO introduces techniques to reduce the variance of this estimator by (i) increasing the sampling budget, (ii) allocating more samples across diffusion steps by increasing
n_{t}
, and (iii) using common random numbers shared between policy and reference. These techniques help stabilize training and reduce the variance of loss and gradient.

Reference-Adjusted ELBO margin.

Given a desirable completion
y_{w}
and a undesirable completion
y_{l}
, VRPO compares their
policy-vs-reference
ELBO differences:

\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{\theta}(x;y_{w},y_{l})

\displaystyle=\Big[\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w}\mid x)-\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l}\mid x)\Big]

(3)

\displaystyle-\Big[\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{w}\mid x)-\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{l}\mid x)\Big].

VRPO Loss.

Finally, the DPO-style logistic loss over the margin is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[-\log\sigma\bigl(\beta\,\widehat{m}_{\theta}(x;y_{w},y_{l})\bigr)\bigr]

(4)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid and
\beta>0
is a temperature. This loss encourages the policy to assign a relatively higher probability mass to
y_{w}
than to
y_{l}
under ELBO surrogates, thus teaching the preference to the model.

2.3
Kahneman–Tversky Optimization (KTO)

KTO
(
Ethayarajh et al. 2024
)
is a preference optimization algorithm inspired by prospect theory in behavioral economics, which models human perception of gains and losses as asymmetric. Unlike DPO, which relies on paired preference data, KTO can learn directly from unpaired binary signals (“desirable” vs. “undesirable”), making it well-suited for settings with limited or imbalanced feedback.

Let
\pi_{\theta}
be the policy,
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
be a frozen reference, and
\sigma(\cdot)
the sigmoid function. KTO defines the
reference–adjusted reward
as

r_{\theta}(x,y)\;=\;\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}.

(5)

This reward is mapped through a prospect-theoretic
value function
with separate loss-aversion weights for desirable and undesirable examples:

v(x,y)=\begin{cases}\lambda_{D}\sigma(\beta\,(r_{\theta}(x,y)-z_{0})),&y\sim y_{\text{desirable}}|x,\\[3.0pt]
\lambda_{U}\sigma(\beta\,(z_{0}-r_{\theta}(x,y))),&y\sim y_{\text{undesirable}}|x.\end{cases}

(6)

The baseline

z_{0}=\text{KL}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x))

acts as a reference point to control loss saturation. Here,
\beta>0
controls risk aversion, while
\lambda_{D}
and
\lambda_{U}
govern asymmetric loss aversion for desirable and undesirable samples.

Assigning
\lambda_{y}=\lambda_{D}
for desirable and
\lambda_{y}=\lambda_{U}
for undesirable samples, the KTO objective is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KTO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\lambda_{y}-v(x,y)].

(7)

Intuitively, desirable examples increase utility when the policy’s log-likelihood exceeds that of reference, while undesirable ones increase utility when it falls below the reference. The term
\lambda_{y}
ensures non-negativity. In practice, gradients are only taken with respect to
\pi_{\theta}
, while
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and
z_{0}
are treated as stop-gradient terms, and we don’t backpropagte through them.

3
METHOD

3.1
ELBO-KTO for dLLMs

KTO scores a response by the log-likelihood ratio

\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
,
which is intractable for diffusion LMs. To solve for this, we replace each
log-likelihood with a Monte Carlo ELBO lower bound and work with the

ELBO margin

\widehat{r}_{\theta}(x,y)\;=\;\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y\mid x)\;-\;\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y\mid x),

(8)

where
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}
is defined in (
2
) as

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\frac{1}{n_{y_{t}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\ell_{\pi}\!\big(y^{(i)}_{t_{j}},t_{j},y\mid x\big),

and
\ell_{\pi}
represents the per-step loss of the mask prediction model, whose exact formulation is defined in Appendix A.

3.2
Global Per-Batch Baseline for Variance Control

Classical KTO introduces a per-prompt reference point

z_{0}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)}],

i.e. a KL term. This reference is intractable for dLLMs and estimating it with Monte Carlo methods is prohibitively expensive.
Thus, for computational efficiency and stability, we use a
single scalar
global baseline
computed per mini-batch
S

\hat{b}_{0}(S)\;=\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\widehat{r}_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i}),\qquad S=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m},

(9)

and treat
\hat{b}_{0}(S)
as stop-gradient, i.e., we do not backpropagate through it. This is a standard control variate which recenters the scores entering the KTO sigmoid function. It reduces the gradient variance without any
additional ELBO evaluations or KL baseline calculations using mismatched pairs as done in
Ethayarajh et al. 2024
.

3.3
Instantiated Loss for Diffusion Language Models

Let
s_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}
encode desirable/undesirable and

g(u)=\sigma(\beta u)
be the logistic link, where
\beta
controls the KTO risk aversion. We instantiate the general KTO
Loss for a mini-batch
S
with the MC ELBO margin
(
8
) and the global baseline (
9
):

\widehat{L}(S)\;=\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{s_{i}}\;\Big(1-g(s_{i}[\widehat{r}_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i})-\hat{b}_{0}(S)])\Big),

(10)

where only the
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}
term backpropagates; the reference term,
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}
and

\hat{b}_{0}(S)
are treated as constants. This technique centers the scores
around the batch mean to improve stability and efficiency while preserving the ELBO-difference
structure needed for diffusion LLMs.

Subtracting a constant baseline minimizes the variance of the centered scores;
with logistic
g
, this keeps
s_{i}[\widehat{r}_{\theta}-\hat{b}_{0}]
near the
high slope region, avoiding loss saturation and reducing the variance of the gradient.
Using the batch mean (
9
) gives this benefit without additional compute. A detailed bias-variance discussion for the instantiated loss and the gradient bounds
for (
10
) is provided in the Section
4
.

3.4
Variance Reduction

We show theoretically in Section
4
that VRPO-style ELBO variance controls work for ELBO-KTO as well and we use these techniques in our implementation. Particularly, we allocate MC budget across
diffusion steps and share random draws between the policy and
the reference to induce positive covariance between their
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
estimators.

3.5
Efficiency Considerations

Half the Forward-Backward Passes.

Compared to pairwise DPO on dLLMs, ELBO-KTO uses one policy forward/backward and one reference forward per example (vs. two of each), yielding
\sim 2\times
lower activations or
\sim 2\times
effective batch size at the same memory.

Cheaper Data.

Binary, unpaired labels avoid pair construction. KTO handles the data imbalance via asymmetric loss aversion for desired and undesired samples.

Zero Compute Baseline.

The global baseline (
9
) is computed from the same batch used for training; no mismatched pairs or additional MC evaluations are required to estimate the KL baseline.

4
THEORETICAL ANALYSIS

We present a theoretical analysis of ELBO-KTO and give bounds for bias and variance of loss and gradient. Using these bounds, we further motivate the use of VRPO-style variance reduction techniques to stabilize training. We also demonstrate the optimality of the global per-batch baseline
\hat{b}_{0}
as a constant baseline for a mini-batch. For clarity, we only present the main theorems in this section and defer a detailed analysis with proofs to Appendix B.

4.1
Setup and Notation

Dataset and Minibatch.

Let the finite dataset be
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
, where
x_{n}
is prompt and
y_{n}
is response.
A minibatch is
S=\{(x_{i},y_{i},s_{i},\lambda_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
, sampled uniformly. Here
s_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
denotes undesirable/desirable,
and
\lambda_{i}\in\{\lambda_{D},\lambda_{U}\}
are class weights with

\lambda_{\max}=\max\{\lambda_{D},\lambda_{U}\}
.

Policies and ELBO Plug-In.

Let
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
be the current policy and

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)
be the frozen reference.
We use an ELBO surrogate
B_{\pi}(y\mid x)
and an unbiased MC estimator

\widehat{B}_{\pi}(y\mid x)
satisfying

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{B}_{\pi}(y\mid x)]=B_{\pi}(y\mid x)
.

ELBO Margins and Global Baseline.

Define the ELBO margin as

r_{i}\;:=\;B_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})-B_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i}),

(11)

and its MC estimate as

\hat{r}_{i}\;:=\;\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})-\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i}).

(12)

The global mini-batch baseline is

\hat{b}_{0}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{r}_{j}
, and the signed centered margin is

\hat{\delta}_{i}=s_{i}(\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{b}_{0})
.
For gradients, we treat
\hat{b}_{0}
and the reference term as stop-grad.

Objective and Target.

With logistic link
g(u)=\sigma(\beta u)
, the per-item and batch losses are

\displaystyle\hat{\ell}_{i}\;=\;\lambda_{i}\big(1-g(\hat{\delta}_{i})\big),\qquad\hat{L}(S)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\ell}_{i}.

(13)

The corresponding noise-free target
global-baseline
replaces the MC estimates by expectations:

\displaystyle L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}(S;\theta)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\big(1-g\big(s_{i}(r_{i}-b_{0})\big)\big),

(14)

where
b_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{b}_{0}]=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}
.

Lipschitz Constants.

We use
L_{g}
to denote Lipschitz constant of
g
and
L_{g^{\prime}}
to denote the Lipschitz constant of
g^{\prime}
. Since
g=\sigma(\beta u)
, the values of these constants can be derived exactly as shown in Appendix B. These values come out to be
L_{g}=\beta/4
and
L_{g^{\prime}}=\beta^{2}/(6\sqrt{3})
.

MC Design and Correlation Structure.

Conditioning on the batch
S
, we can write
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{r}_{i}]=r_{i}
. Assuming the exchangeability of the indices under the MC design, let
v(S):=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{r}_{i})
and
c(S):=\mathrm{Cov}(\widehat{r}_{i},\widehat{r}_{j})
for
i\neq j
.

Centered-Margin Variance Aggregator.

All our bounds depend on the scalar

\displaystyle\Psi(S)

\displaystyle:=\;\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{\delta}_{i})\big]

(15)

\displaystyle=\;\tfrac{m-1}{m}\,\big(v(S)-c(S)\big),

where the identity follows by expanding
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{b}_{0})
under the exchangeability assumptions as shown in Appendix B.

4.2
Loss Bias

Theorem 1

(Loss bias bound)
.

The minibatch loss bias relative to the global-baseline target satisfies

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{L}(S)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}(S;\theta)]\Big|\;\leq

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}L_{g}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\Psi(S)}\big],

where
\Psi(S)
is the centered-margin variance
aggregator defined in equation
15
.

This result shows that the loss bias arises purely from applying the link function
g
to noisy centered margin
\hat{\delta}_{i}
. Shrinking
\Psi(S)
directly tightens this gap.

4.3
Loss Variance

Theorem 2

(Loss variance bound)
.

For the minibatch loss
\hat{L}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\big(1-g(\hat{\delta}_{i})\big)

with
g(u)=\sigma(\beta u)
,
the MC-induced variance satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{L}(S))\;\leq\;(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\Psi(S)\big].

This bound again depends on the centered-margin variance aggregator
\Psi(S)
and shrinking it will help reduce the loss variance.

4.4
Gradient Bias

We define the stochastic gradient for a minibatch
S
as

\displaystyle\hat{G}(S)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{a}_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i},

(16)

where
\hat{a}_{i}=-\lambda_{i}s_{i}g^{\prime}(\hat{\delta}_{i})
and

\hat{\delta}_{i}=s_{i}(\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{b}_{0})
. Also, let

G=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}r_{i},

(17)

where
a_{i}=-\lambda_{i}s_{i}g^{\prime}(\delta_{i})
and

\delta_{i}=s_{i}(r_{i}-b_{0})
.
We assume unbiased ELBO gradients
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}]=\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}
.

Theorem 3

(Gradient bias bound)
.

Let
\|\cdot\|
be the
L_{2}
-norm.
If
\|\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\|\leq\bar{G}
for all items, then

\displaystyle\bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{G}(S)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[G(S)]\bigr\|\leq

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\Psi(S)}\big]\,\bar{G}+\lambda_{\max}L_{g}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}\big],

where
\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\|^{2}\big]
.

This result shows that gradient bias has two contributions:
weight noise and score-gradient noise. Weight noise arises from applying
g
to noisy centered margin
\hat{\delta}_{i}
, and is controlled by

\Psi(S)
. Score-gradient noise arises from stochastic ELBO gradients
controlled by
\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)
, which can be reduced by increasing the MC budget.

4.5
Gradient Variance

We define

\begin{gathered}\tilde{G}^{2}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}\|^{2}\big],\\
\bar{c}_{\nabla}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\rangle],\end{gathered}

where
\xi_{i}=\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}
, and assume unbiased ELBO gradients
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}]=\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}
.

Theorem 4

(Gradient variance)
.

For mini-batch
S
, let
U(S)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big(\hat{G}(S)\big)\leq\;

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big(U(S)\big)}\;+\;\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\ \sqrt{\Psi(S)}\;\tilde{G}(S)\Big)^{\!2},

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U)\;\leq\;(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}\Big(\frac{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}{m}\;+\;\frac{m-1}{m}\ \bar{c}_{\nabla}(S)\Big).

For the special case of independent per–item MC seeds,
we can assume that the per–item MC randomness is conditionally independent across items in the batch, so that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\rangle]=0
for
i\neq j
.
Then the variance of
U(S)
collapses to the diagonal:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big(U(S)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\xi_{i}\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\;(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}\,\frac{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}{m},

where
\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\xi_{i}\|^{2}\big]
.
Substituting into Theorem
4
gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big(\hat{G}(S)\big)\leq

\displaystyle\Big((\lambda_{\max}L_{g})\,\sqrt{\tfrac{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}{m}}\;+\;\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\ \sqrt{\Psi(S)}\;\tilde{G}(S)\Big)^{\!2}.

Similar to gradient bias, gradient variance can also be bounded by two sources, weight noise and score-gradient noise.

4.6
Estimator Design Strategies

We now make the dependence on the estimator design explicit.
Throughout this section we assume the exchangeable MC setting of
Section
B.1
, so that
v(S)
and
c(S)
are well-defined. Our goal is to strategically decrease
v(S)
and increase
c(S)
, thereby shrinking the common driver
\Psi(S)
.

Decreasing
v(S)
.

Expanding the formula for
v(S)
, we get

\displaystyle v(S)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{r}_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})-\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i}))+\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i}))

\displaystyle\qquad-2\mathrm{Cov}(\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i}),\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i}))

Using Theorem 2 from
Zhu et al. 2025
, we get that the
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{B})
terms decrease on (i) Increasing the overall sampling budget, and (ii) Allocating the full budget to timesteps. Additionally, from Theorem 3 of
Zhu et al. 2025
, we get that using antithetic sampling, i.e., using shared random numbers to compute
\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\theta}}
and
\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}
, leads to a positive covariance term, thus decreasing
v(S)
.

Increasing
c(S)
.

Recall
c(S)=\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{r}_{i},\hat{r}_{j})
under the exchangeability of indices. Intuitively, sharing random numbers across items in the minibatch
S
will lead to a positive co-movement of
\hat{r}_{i}
and
\hat{r}_{j}
, thereby increasing
c(S)
. This can further reduce
\Psi(S)
by raising
c(S)
. For now, we do not use this technique in our experiments due to time constraints and leave this for future work.

4.7
Global Baseline Optimality

Lemma 1

(Global Baseline Optimality)
.

For any baseline
b
that is constant across items in a batch and may depend on MC randomness,

\Psi_{b}(S)-\Psi_{\hat{b}_{0}}(S)\;=\;\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big(b-\hat{b}_{0}\big)\;\geq\;0,

where
\hat{b}_{0}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j}\hat{r}_{j}
. Hence every baseline of the form

b=\hat{b}_{0}+K
with deterministic constant
K
attains the same minimum value

\Psi_{b}(S)=\Psi_{\hat{b}_{0}}(S)
. If, in addition,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[b]=b_{0}
, then
K=0

and the unique minimizer is
b=\hat{b}_{0}
.

This result says that
\hat{b}_{0}
is variance-optimal for all possible values of
b
. It requires no additional compute and is thus the principled default for ELBO-KTO.

Related Work

Image Diffusion Model Alignment

Preference optimization for diffusion models on image data has received significant attention.

Wallace et al. 2024
propose
Diffusion-DPO
, which adapts DPO to diffusion models by defining a diffusion-based likelihood and directly optimizing over paired human preferences.

Hong et al. 2024
introduce a margin-aware preference optimization method that removes the need for a reference model, while
Lee et al. 2025
(CaPO) further improve alignment through reward calibration and Pareto-frontier-based pair selection.
Similarly,
Li et al. 2024
present
Diffusion-KTO
, which aligns text-to-image diffusion models by maximizing expected human utility.
Together, these approaches advance preference alignment in text-to-image diffusion models, whereas our proposed method specifically targets diffusion
language
models.

Alignment for Diffusion Language Models

Diffusion language models are emerging as promising alternatives to autoregressive LLMs, including architectures such as DiffuLLaMA
(
Gong et al. 2024
)
, Dream
(
Ye et al. 2025
)
, LLaDA
(
Nie et al. 2025
)
, and Mercury
(
Khanna et al. 2025
)
.
Recent work has begun exploring how to align dLLMs with preferences or reasoning objectives. The most closely related method is VRPO in LLaDA 1.5
(
Zhu et al. 2025
)
, which still operates under the paired preference regime and uses variance reduction to stabilize learning.
Other diffusion-LM works emphasize reasoning or model structure rather than alignment:

Zhao et al. 2025
scale reasoning in dLLMs via reinforcement learning,

Tang et al. 2025
apply weighted policy optimization for reasoning in dLLMs,

Yang et al. 2025
explore masked diffusion models in code generation or multimodal settings (focusing on model performance rather than feedback alignment), and

Han et al. 2025
propose trajectory-level alignment via stepwise decomposition of discrete diffusion processes.
While these advance reasoning, architecture, or decoding, they generally do not support preference alignment from unpaired feedback. Our proposed ELBO-KTO method fills in this gap.

7
CONCLUSION

We introduced a simple and effective recipe for aligning diffusion LLMs with unpaired feedback by plugging ELBO surrogates into KTO’s value formulation: an ELBO margin for the policy–reference contrast and a mini-batch ELBO baseline, with VRPO-style variance reduction for stability. On LLaDA-8B-Instruct, the approach achieves strong automatic-judge gains on two test sets (65.9% on kto-mix-14k, 62.3% on UltraFeedback-Binary) and shows modest improvements on downstream tasks. Together, these results indicate that unpaired preference optimization is a viable path for diffusion LLM alignment and a practical complement to paired alignment methods.

Limitations include reliance on an ELBO surrogate (with potential bias/variance). Future work includes human evaluation, broader downstream tasks, principled choices for class weights
(\lambda_{D},\lambda_{U})
, stronger variance-reduction schemes, and combining unpaired ELBO-KTO with paired or reward-model objectives in dLLM settings.

Appendix A
ADDITIONAL FORMULATION

Diffusion Language Models (dLLMs) are inherently Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs), which incorporate a discrete random masking forward process and train a mask predictor to approximate the reverse unmasking process
(
Sahoo et al. 2024
;
Ou et al. 2024
;
Austin et al. 2023
;
Lou et al. 2024
;
Nie et al. 2025
)
.

A.1
Forward Process for Diffusion Language Models

During the forward process of MDMs, an original sequence
y
is progressively corrupted by masking tokens independently at a noise level
t\in[0,1]
. Let
x
be the prompt,
y
be the original response to the prompt,
y^{i}
denote the
i-
th token of
y
,
L
denote the total number of tokens in
y
,
K
denote the vocabulary size, and
\mathbf{M}
denote the mask token. The forward process
q
to obtain the masked response
y_{t}
at time
t
can be formulated as

q(y_{t}\mid y,x,t)=\prod_{i=1}^{L}q(y_{t}^{i}\mid y^{i},x,t),

(18)

where

q(y_{t}^{i}\mid y^{i},x,t)=\begin{cases}t,\qquad&y_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{M},\\
1-t,\qquad&y_{t}^{i}=y^{i}\end{cases}.

A.2
Reverse Process for Diffusion Language Models

The reverse process starts at
t=1
from a fully masked sequence and gradually unmasks tokens till
t=0
to recover a fully unmasked language sequence. Let
p_{\theta}
be the mask prediction models. Then, for timesteps
0\leq s<t\leq 1
, the conditional distribution for the reverse process can be defined as

q(y_{s}\mid s,t,y_{t},x)=\prod_{i=1}^{L}q\!\left(y_{s}^{\,i}\mid s,t,y_{t},x\right)

(19)

q\!\left(y_{s}^{\,i}\mid s,t,y_{t},x\right)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{t-s}{t}\;p_{\theta}\!\left(y^{\,i}\mid y_{t},x\right),&y_{t}^{\,i}=\mathbf{M}\ \wedge\ y_{s}^{\,i}\neq\mathbf{M},\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{s}{t},&y_{t}^{\,i}=\mathbf{M}\ \wedge\ y_{s}^{\,i}=\mathbf{M},\\[6.0pt]
1,&y_{t}^{\,i}\neq\mathbf{M}\ \wedge\ y_{s}^{\,i}=y_{t}^{\,i},\\[4.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(20)

A.3
Log-Likelihood ELBO

The exact log-likelihood
\log\pi(y\mid x)
intractable for dLLMs because of the nature of forward and reverse process. To tackle this, the log-likelihood is usually approximated by its ELBO
(
Lou et al. 2024
;
Ou et al. 2024
;
Nie et al. 2025
)
:

\mathcal{B}_{\pi}(y\mid x)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]}\;\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\sim q(y_{t}\mid t,y,x)}\,\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y\mid x),

(21)

where

\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y\mid x)\;\triangleq\;\left[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}\!\left[y_{t}^{\,i}=\mathbf{M}\right]\;\log p_{\theta}\!\left(y^{\,i}\mid y_{t},x\right)\right]

(22)

is the per-step loss of the mask prediction model.

Let
l
be uniformly sampled from
\{1,2,\ldots,L\}
and
y_{l}
denote the sequence obtained by masking
l
tokens without replacement. Using this terminology,

Zhu et al. 2025
;
Ou et al. 2024
define another equivalent formulation for the ELBO approximation:

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\pi}(y\mid x)\;\triangleq\;\mathbb{E}_{l\sim\mathcal{U}(\{1,2,\ldots,L\})}\;\mathbb{E}_{y_{l}\sim q(y_{l}\mid l,y,x)}\,\ell^{\prime}_{\pi}(y_{l},l,y\mid x),

(23)

where

\ell^{\prime}_{\pi}(y_{l},l,y\mid x)\;\triangleq\;\left[\frac{L}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}\!\left[y_{l}^{\,i}=\mathbf{M}\right]\;\log p_{\theta}\!\left(y^{\,i}\mid y_{l},x\right)\right].

(24)

Following
Zhu et al. 2025
, we adopt the
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\pi}(y\mid x)
formulation for our experiments. Although
\mathcal{B}_{\pi}(y\mid x)
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\pi}(y\mid x)
are equivalent in expectation, the latter generally exhibits lower variance during estimation. This improvement arises because Eq. equation
23
deterministically masks exactly
l
out of
L
tokens in each sequence, producing more consistent samples, whereas Eq. equation
21
masks an expected fraction
t
of tokens, introducing greater stochasticity. Consequently, we also employ the
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
formulation in Eq. equation
23
as our log-likelihood estimator.

Appendix B
THEORETICAL ANALYSIS AND PROOFS

B.1
Setup and Notation

Dataset and Minibatch.

Let the finite dataset be
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
, where
x_{n}
is prompt and
y_{n}
is response.
A minibatch is
S=\{(x_{i},y_{i},s_{i},\lambda_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
, sampled uniformly. Here
s_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
denotes undesirable/desirable,
and
\lambda_{i}\in\{\lambda_{D},\lambda_{U}\}
are class weights with

\lambda_{\max}=\max\{\lambda_{D},\lambda_{U}\}
.

Policies and ELBO Plug-In.

Let
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
be the current policy and

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)
be the frozen reference.
We use an ELBO surrogate
B_{\pi}(y\mid x)
and an unbiased MC estimator

\widehat{B}_{\pi}(y\mid x)
satisfying

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{B}_{\pi}(y\mid x)]=B_{\pi}(y\mid x)
.

ELBO Margins and Global Baseline.

Define the ELBO margin as

r_{i}\;:=\;B_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})-B_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i}),

(25)

and its MC estimate as

\hat{r}_{i}\;:=\;\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})-\widehat{B}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i}).

(26)

The global mini-batch baseline is

\hat{b}_{0}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{r}_{j}
, and the signed centered margin is

\hat{\delta}_{i}=s_{i}(\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{b}_{0})
.
For gradients, we treat
\hat{b}_{0}
and the reference term as stop-grad.

Objective and Target.

With logistic link
g(u)=\sigma(\beta u)
, where
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
, the per-item and batch losses are

\displaystyle\hat{\ell}_{i}\;=\;\lambda_{i}\big(1-g(\hat{\delta}_{i})\big),\qquad\hat{L}(S)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\ell}_{i}.

(27)

The corresponding noise-free target
global-baseline
replaces the MC estimates by expectations:

\displaystyle L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}(S;\theta)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\big(1-g\big(s_{i}(r_{i}-b_{0})\big)\big),

(28)

where
b_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{b}_{0}]=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}
.

Lipschitz Constants.

We use
L_{g}
to denote Lipschitz constant of
g
and
L_{g^{\prime}}
to denote the Lipschitz constant of
g^{\prime}
. Since
g=\sigma(\beta u)
, the values of these constants come out to be
L_{g}=\beta/4
and
L_{g^{\prime}}=\beta^{2}/(6\sqrt{3})
.

Lemma 2

(Lipschitz constants of the scaled sigmoid)
.

Let
g(u)=\sigma(\beta u)
with
\sigma(z)=\tfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
and
\beta>0
.
Then
g
is
L_{g}
-Lipschitz with
L_{g}=\beta/4
, and its derivative
g^{\prime}
is

L_{g^{\prime}}
-Lipschitz with
L_{g^{\prime}}=\beta^{2}/(6\sqrt{3})
.

Proof.

Since
g^{\prime}(u)=\beta\,\sigma(\beta u)(1-\sigma(\beta u))

and
\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
for all
z
,
we have
\sup_{u}|g^{\prime}(u)|=\beta/4
,
so
g
is
\beta/4
-Lipschitz.

For
g^{\prime}
, differentiate once more:

g^{\prime\prime}(u)=\beta^{2}\,\sigma(\beta u)(1-\sigma(\beta u))(1-2\sigma(\beta u)).

Let
p=\sigma(\beta u)\in(0,1)
and define
h(p)=p(1-p)(1-2p)
.
The extrema of
|h(p)|
satisfy
h^{\prime}(p)=0
, where

h^{\prime}(p)=1-6p(1-p)=0\!\implies\!p_{\star}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1\pm 1/\sqrt{3})
.
At these points,
|h(p_{\star})|=1/(6\sqrt{3})
,
so
\sup_{u}|g^{\prime\prime}(u)|=\beta^{2}/(6\sqrt{3})
.
By the mean-value theorem,

|g^{\prime}(u)-g^{\prime}(v)|\leq(\sup_{t}|g^{\prime\prime}(t)|)\,|u-v|
,
giving
L_{g^{\prime}}=\beta^{2}/(6\sqrt{3})
.
∎

Lemma 3

(Centered-Margin Variance Aggregator)
.

Fix a batch
S=\{(x_{i},y_{i},s_{i},\lambda_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
and assume the MC design renders

(\widehat{r}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{r}_{m})
exchangeable when conditioning on
S
.
Let
v(S):=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{r}_{i})
and
c(S):=\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{r}_{i},\widehat{r}_{j})
for
i\neq j
.
With
\widehat{b}_{0}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widehat{r}_{j}
and
\widehat{\delta}_{i}=s_{i}\big(\widehat{r}_{i}-\widehat{b}_{0}\big)
, we have

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})=\frac{m-1}{m}\big(v(S)-c(S)\big),

and consequently,

\Psi(S):=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})\big]=\frac{m-1}{m}\big(v(S)-c(S)\big).

Proof.

Since
s_{i}^{2}=1
,
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{r}_{i}-\widehat{b}_{0})
.
Write

\widehat{r}_{i}-\widehat{b}_{0}=\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{m}\Big)\widehat{r}_{i}-\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j\neq i}\widehat{r}_{j}=:a\,\widehat{r}_{i}+b\sum_{j\neq i}\widehat{r}_{j},

with
a=\tfrac{m-1}{m}
and
b=-\tfrac{1}{m}
.
By bilinearity of covariance,

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{r}_{i}-\widehat{b}_{0})=a^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{r}_{i})+b^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}\widehat{r}_{j}\Big)+2ab\,\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\Big(\widehat{r}_{i},\sum_{j\neq i}\widehat{r}_{j}\Big).

Exchangeability of
(\widehat{r}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{r}_{m})
implies

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}\widehat{r}_{j}\Big)=(m-1)v(S)+(m-1)(m-2)c(S),\qquad\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\Big(\widehat{r}_{i},\sum_{j\neq i}\widehat{r}_{j}\Big)=(m-1)c(S).

Substituting and simplifying with
a=\tfrac{m-1}{m}
and
b=-\tfrac{1}{m}
yields

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{r}_{i}-\widehat{b}_{0})=\frac{m-1}{m}\big(v(S)-c(S)\big).

Conditioned on
S
,
\widehat{\delta}_{i}
depends only on MC randomness,
so
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})\big]=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})
.
Since this value is identical for all
i
, averaging gives

\Psi(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})=\frac{m-1}{m}\big(v(S)-c(S)\big).

∎

The result relies only on exchangeability
(common variance
v(S)
and common covariance
c(S)
)
and centering by the batch mean
\widehat{b}_{0}
.
The signs
s_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
do not affect the variance
since
s_{i}^{2}=1
.

B.2
Loss Bias

Theorem
1
(Restated).

The minibatch loss bias relative to the global-baseline target satisfies

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{L}(S)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}(S;\theta)]\Big|\;\leq\;\lambda_{\max}L_{g}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\Psi(S)}\big],

where
\Psi(S)
is the centered-margin variance
aggregator defined in equation
15
. Here
|\cdot|
refers to the absolute value.

Proof.

We have

\widehat{L}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\big(1-g(\widehat{\delta}_{i})\big),\qquad L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}(S;\theta)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\big(1-g(\delta_{i}^{\mathrm{gb}})\big),

with
\widehat{\delta}_{i}=s_{i}(\widehat{r}_{i}-\widehat{b}_{0})
,
\delta_{i}^{\mathrm{gb}}=s_{i}(r_{i}-b_{0})
,

\widehat{b}_{0}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widehat{r}_{j}
, and
b_{0}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{j}
.
Since
L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}(S;\theta)
is deterministic under MC randomness for fixed
S
,

\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{L}(S)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}(S;\theta)]\Big|=\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{L}(S)]-L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}(S;\theta)\big]\Big|\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big[\big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{L}(S)]-L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}(S;\theta)\big|\Big],

where the inequality uses
|\mathbb{E}[Z]|\leq\mathbb{E}[|Z|]
(triangle inequality for
|\cdot|
).
Expanding
\widehat{L}
and
L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}
and applying
|g(u)-g(v)|\leq L_{g}|u-v|
then yields

\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{L}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}]\Big|\leq L_{g}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\,\big|\widehat{\delta}_{i}-\delta_{i}^{\mathrm{gb}}\big|\right]\leq\lambda_{\max}L_{g}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big|\widehat{\delta}_{i}-\delta_{i}^{\mathrm{gb}}\big|\right].

Conditioned on
S
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{r}_{i}]=r_{i}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{b}_{0}]=b_{0}
, so
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{\delta}_{i}]=\delta_{i}^{\mathrm{gb}}
and

\big|\widehat{\delta}_{i}-\delta_{i}^{\mathrm{gb}}\big|=\big|\widehat{\delta}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{\delta}_{i}]\big|
.
By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\big|\widehat{\delta}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{\delta}_{i}]\big|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[(\widehat{\delta}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{\delta}_{i}])^{2}\big]}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})},

since
\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}|X|\big)^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[X^{2}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[1]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[X^{2}]
for
X=\widehat{\delta}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{\delta}_{i}]
.
Therefore,

\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{L}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}]\Big|\leq\lambda_{\max}L_{g}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})}\right].

Using concavity of
\sqrt{\cdot}
for fixed
S
,

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})},

and noting that the variances are deterministic under MC conditioning, we identify

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})\big]=\Psi(S).

Taking
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}
completes the claim:

\Big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\widehat{L}(S)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[L^{\mathrm{sg}}_{\mathrm{GB}}(S;\theta)]\Big|\leq\lambda_{\max}L_{g}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\Psi(S)}\big].

∎

B.3
Loss Variance

Theorem
2
(Restated).

For the minibatch loss
\hat{L}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\big(1-g(\hat{\delta}_{i})\big)

with
g(u)=\sigma(\beta u)
,
the MC-induced variance satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{L}(S))\;\leq\;(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\Psi(S)\big].

Proof.

Fix a batch
S
and write
Z_{i}:=\lambda_{i}\,g(\widehat{\delta}_{i})
so that

\widehat{L}(S)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\big(1-g(\widehat{\delta}_{i})\big)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{L}(S))=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}\Big),

since subtracting the constant
\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
does not affect variance. Using
\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{i})\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{j})}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{i})+2\!\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\!\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{i})+2\!\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\!\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{i})\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{j})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{i})}\Big)^{\!2}.

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz again in the form
(\sum_{i}a_{i})^{2}\leq m\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}
with

a_{i}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{i})}
gives

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}\Big)\;\leq\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{i}).

Next, use Lipschitz contraction for each
i
:
since
u\mapsto\lambda_{i}g(u)
is
(\lambda_{i}L_{g})
-Lipschitz,

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(Z_{i})=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\lambda_{i}g(\widehat{\delta}_{i}))\;\leq\;(\lambda_{i}L_{g})^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i}),

where we used
\mathrm{Var}(f(X))=\min_{a}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[(f(X)-a)^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[(f(X)-f(\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}X))^{2}]\leq L^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[(X-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}X)^{2}]=L^{2}\mathrm{Var}(X)
for an
L
-Lipschitz
f
.
Hence,

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{L}(S))\;\leq\;\frac{(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})\;=\;(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}\,\Psi(S),

using the definition (and MC-determinism) of
\Psi(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{\delta}_{i})
.
Finally, taking
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\cdot]
over batches yields

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\widehat{L}(S))\big]\;\leq\;(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\Psi(S)].

∎

B.4
Gradient Bias

Theorem
3
(Restated).

Let
\|\cdot\|
be the
L_{2}
-norm.
If
\|\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\|\leq\bar{G}
for all items, then

\displaystyle\bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{G}(S)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[G(S)]\bigr\|\leq

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\Psi(S)}\big]\,\bar{G}+\lambda_{\max}L_{g}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}\big],

where
\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\|^{2}\big]
,
\hat{G}(S)
is the stochastic gradient for the minibatch
S
as defined in equation
16
, and
G
is defined in equation
17
.

Proof.

We start by expanding the difference of the expectations and splitting it into two parts:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{G}(S)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[G(S)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\hat{a}_{i}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-a_{i}\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\Big(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i}]}_{\text{(A) weight noise}}\;\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\hat{a}_{i}(\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i})\big]}_{\text{(B) score-gradient noise}}\Big).

(29)

(A) Weight-noise term.

Using
a_{i}=-\lambda_{i}s_{i}g^{\prime}(\delta_{i})
and
\hat{a}_{i}=-\lambda_{i}s_{i}g^{\prime}(\hat{\delta}_{i})
with
g^{\prime}

L_{g^{\prime}}
-Lipschitz,

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i}]\big|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{i}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\,|g^{\prime}(\hat{\delta}_{i})-g^{\prime}(\delta_{i})|\,\big]\leq\lambda_{i}L_{g^{\prime}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\,|\hat{\delta}_{i}-\delta_{i}|\,\big]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{i}L_{g^{\prime}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[(\hat{\delta}_{i}-\delta_{i})^{2}\big]}=\lambda_{i}L_{g^{\prime}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{\delta}_{i})\big]},

where we used Cauchy–Schwarz
\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\big[|\hat{\delta}_{i}-\delta_{i}|.|1|\big]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\big[(\hat{\delta}_{i}-\delta_{i})^{2}\big]\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\big[1^{2}\big]}\big)
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{\delta}_{i}]=\delta_{i}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i}]\;\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\,|\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i}]|\ \|\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\|\,\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\Big[\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{\delta}_{i})}\,\|\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\,\bar{G}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\Big[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{\delta}_{i})}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\,\bar{G}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\Psi(S)}\big],

(30)

using the concavity of
x\mapsto\sqrt{x}
, and where we (re)use

\Psi(S):=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\delta}_{i})
.

(B) Score-gradient noise term.

By Cauchy–Schwarz and the uniform bound
|g^{\prime}(u)|\leq L_{g}
,

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{a}_{i}(\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i})]\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{a}_{i}^{2}]}\;\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\|^{2}\big]}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}L_{g}\;\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\|^{2}\big]}.

Averaging over
i
and applying the same concavity argument gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\Big[\big\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{a}_{i}(\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i})]\big\|\Big]\;\leq\;\lambda_{\max}L_{g}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}\big],

(31)

with
\displaystyle\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}\|^{2}\big]
.

Conclusion.

Combining equation
30
and equation
31
in equation
29
and applying the triangle inequality yields

\Big\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{G}(S)]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[G(S)]\Big\|\leq\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\Psi(S)}\big]\,\bar{G}\;+\;\lambda_{\max}L_{g}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\big[\sqrt{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}\big],

which is the desired bound.
∎

This result shows that gradient bias has two contributions:
weight noise and score-gradient noise. Weight noise arises from applying
g
to noisy centered margin
\hat{\delta}_{i}
, and is controlled by

\Psi(S)
. Score-gradient noise arises from stochastic ELBO gradients
controlled by
\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)
, which can be reduced by increasing the MC budget.

B.5
Gradient Variance

Let

\begin{gathered}\tilde{G}^{2}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}\|^{2}\big],\\
\bar{c}_{\nabla}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\rangle],\end{gathered}

where
\xi_{i}=\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}
, and assume unbiased ELBO gradients
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}]=\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}
.

Theorem
4
(Restated).

For mini-batch
S
, let
U(S)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big(\hat{G}(S)\big)\leq\;

\displaystyle\Big(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big(U(S)\big)}\;+\;\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\ \sqrt{\Psi(S)}\;\tilde{G}(S)\Big)^{\!2},

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U)\;\leq\;(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}\Big(\frac{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}{m}\;+\;\frac{m-1}{m}\ \bar{c}_{\nabla}(S)\Big),

and
\hat{G}(S):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{a}_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}
is the stochastic gradient of the mini-batch
S
.

Proof.

Let

U(S):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i},\qquad V(S):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i})\,\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i},

so that
\hat{G}=U+V
.
We also use
\xi_{i}:=\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}-\nabla_{\theta}r_{i}
, so
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\xi_{i}]=0
.

(A) Relating
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{G})
to
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U)
and
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(V)
.

By definition of vector variance,

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{G})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\left[\left\|\hat{G}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{G}]\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

Since
\hat{G}=U+V
, write

\hat{G}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\hat{G}]=(U-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[U])+(V-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[V])=:A+B.

Using the pointwise triangle inequality
\|A+B\|_{2}\leq\|A\|_{2}+\|B\|_{2}
and then squaring and taking expectation, we get

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\left[\|A+B\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\left[(\|A\|_{2}+\|B\|_{2})^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\left[\|A\|_{2}^{2}\right]+2\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\left[\|A\|_{2}\|B\|_{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\left[\|B\|_{2}^{2}\right].

Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the cross term:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\left[\|A\|_{2}\|B\|_{2}\right]\ \leq\ \sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\|A\|_{2}^{2}]}\ \sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\|B\|_{2}^{2}]}.

Combining,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\left[\|A+B\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\Big(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\|A\|_{2}^{2}]}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\|B\|_{2}^{2}]}\Big)^{2}.

Recognizing
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\|A\|_{2}^{2}]=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U)
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\|B\|_{2}^{2}]=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(V)
, we conclude

\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{G})}\ \leq\ \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U)}+\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(V)}.

(32)

(B) Bounding
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(V)
.

By the Lipschitz property of
g^{\prime}
,

|\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i}|=\lambda_{i}\,|g^{\prime}(\hat{\delta}_{i})-g^{\prime}(\delta_{i})|\leq\lambda_{i}L_{g^{\prime}}\,|\hat{\delta}_{i}-\delta_{i}|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(V)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\Big(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}(\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i})\,\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}\Big)\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\Big\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}(\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i})\,\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[(\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i})(\hat{a}_{j}-a_{j})\,\langle\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i},\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{j}\rangle\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[(\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i})^{2}\,\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\big]}\Big)^{2}\quad\text{(Cauchy--Schwarz across $i$)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[(\hat{a}_{i}-a_{i})^{2}]}\ \sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big)^{2}\quad\text{(Cauchy--Schwarz in expectation)}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}^{2}L_{g^{\prime}}^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[(\hat{\delta}_{i}-\delta_{i})^{2}]}\ \sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}^{2}L_{g^{\prime}}^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[(\hat{\delta}_{i}-\delta_{i})^{2}]\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{r}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\quad\text{(Cauchy--Schwarz on the index $i$)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}^{2}L_{g^{\prime}}^{2}\ \Psi(S)\ \tilde{G}^{2}(S).

Taking square roots yields

\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(V)}\ \leq\ \lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\ \sqrt{\Psi(S)}\ \tilde{G}(S).

(33)

(C) Bounding
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U)
.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\xi_{i}]=0
and
U-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[U]=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i}a_{i}\,\xi_{i}
with deterministic
a_{i}
,

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\Big\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\,\xi_{i}\Big\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}a_{i}a_{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\big[\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\rangle\big].

(34)

Using
|a_{i}|\leq\lambda_{\max}|g^{\prime}(\delta_{i})|\leq\lambda_{\max}L_{g}
and splitting diagonal/off-diagonal terms,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}}{m^{2}}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\|\xi_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\|\xi_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{m-1}{m}\cdot\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}\Big(\frac{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}{m}+\frac{m-1}{m}\ \bar{c}_{\nabla}(S)\Big),

which is the claimed proxy bound.

(D) Combine.

From equation
32
and equation
33
,

\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{G}(S))}\ \leq\ \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U(S))}\;+\;\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\ \sqrt{\Psi(S)}\ \tilde{G}(S).

Squaring both sides gives

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(\hat{G}(S))\ \leq\ \Big(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U(S))}+\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\,\sqrt{\Psi(S)}\,\tilde{G}(S)\Big)^{\!2}.

Together with the bound on
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}(U)
above, this establishes the theorem.
∎

For the special case of independent per–item MC seeds,
we can assume that the per–item MC randomness is conditionally independent across items in the batch, so that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[\langle\xi_{i},\xi_{j}\rangle]=0
for
i\neq j
.
Then the variance of
U(S)
collapses to the diagonal:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big(U(S)\big)=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\xi_{i}\|^{2}\big]\;\leq\;(\lambda_{\max}L_{g})^{2}\,\frac{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}{m},

where
\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\|\xi_{i}\|^{2}\big]
.
Substituting into Theorem
4
gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big(\hat{G}(S)\big)\leq\Big((\lambda_{\max}L_{g})\,\sqrt{\tfrac{\bar{v}_{\nabla}(S)}{m}}\;+\;\lambda_{\max}L_{g^{\prime}}\ \sqrt{\Psi(S)}\;\tilde{G}(S)\Big)^{\!2}.

B.6
Global Baseline Optimality

Lemma
1
(Restated).

For any baseline
b
that is constant across items in a batch and may depend on MC randomness,

\Psi_{b}(S)-\Psi_{\hat{b}_{0}}(S)\;=\;\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big(b-\hat{b}_{0}\big)\;\geq\;0,

where
\hat{b}_{0}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j}\hat{r}_{j}
. Hence every baseline of the form

b=\hat{b}_{0}+K
with deterministic constant
K
attains the same minimum value

\Psi_{b}(S)=\Psi_{\hat{b}_{0}}(S)
. If, in addition,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[b]=b_{0}
, then
K=0

and the unique minimizer is
b=\hat{b}_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta:=b-\hat{b}_{0}
. Using
\mathrm{Var}(X-Y)=\mathrm{Var}X+\mathrm{Var}Y-2\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)
and
\Psi(S)
from Lemma
3
,

\Psi_{b}-\Psi_{\hat{b}_{0}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\mathrm{Var}((\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{b}_{0})-\Delta)\big]-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}\!\big[\mathrm{Var}(\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{b}_{0})\big].

This equals

\mathrm{Var}(\Delta)-\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{r}_{i}-\hat{b}_{0},\Delta)=\mathrm{Var}(\Delta)-2\,\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{b}_{0}-\hat{b}_{0},\Delta)=\mathrm{Var}(\Delta)\geq 0,

since
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{r}_{i},\Delta)=\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{b}_{0},\Delta)
.
Equality holds iff
\mathrm{Var}(\Delta)=0
, i.e.
b-\hat{b}_{0}
is a constant.
If moreover
\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[b]=b_{0}
, then that constant must be
0
, so
b=\hat{b}_{0}
.
∎

This result says that
\hat{b}_{0}
is variance-optimal for all possible values of
b
. It requires no additional compute and is thus chosen as the principled default for ELBO-KTO.

C.5
Win-Rate, Majority-Vote and Cohen’s
\kappa

Win-rate protocol.

We report
Adjusted Win Rate
(AWR) of the tuned model versus the base model using an open-source LLM-as-a-judge (FastChat
lm_judge
).
§
§

§

https://github.com/lm-sys/FastChat

For each prompt
x
, we generate two completions under identical decoding:
y^{(A)}
from the tuned model and
y^{(B)}
from the base model.
The judge receives
(x,y^{(A)},y^{(B)})
with a standardized instruction template and returns a label in
\{\text{A wins},\text{B wins},\text{tie}\}
.
To mitigate position bias, we evaluate
both
orderings,
(x,y^{(A)},y^{(B)})
and
(x,y^{(B)},y^{(A)})
, and declare a win for a model only if
both
orderings favor the same model; otherwise the outcome is counted as a tie.
We then compute

\mathrm{AWR}\;=\;\frac{\#\text{A wins}+0.5\,\#\text{ties}}{\#\text{A wins}+\#\text{B wins}+\#\text{ties}}\,,

and report the mean AWR over the full test set.

Two-judge majority vote and agreement.

We also report AWR under a majority vote of two independent judges.
The same two-ordering rule is applied per judge; the ensemble outcome is a win for a model only if
both
judges, under
both
orderings, favor that model; otherwise it is treated as a tie.
Inter-judge agreement is measured with Cohen’s
\kappa
, treating win, loss, and tie as three distinct classes.

Uncertainty.

We report
90\%
confidence intervals via nonparametric bootstrapping with
5{,}000
resamples of the test set (sampling prompts with replacement and recomputing metrics per resample); intervals are taken from the
5^{\text{th}}
and
95^{\text{th}}
percentiles.
