Tackling Antimicrobial Resistance in the WHO African Region: Progress, Challenges, and Strategic Priorities for the Future
Authors: Yahaya Ali Ahmed, Walter Fuller, Yidnekachew Degefaw Mazengiya, Laetitia Gahimbare, Joseph Chukwudi Okeibunor, Olushayo Oluseun Olu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is the development of resistance in a microorganism to an antimicrobial agent to which it was previously sensitive. 1 The main drivers of AMR are misuse and overuse of antimicrobials. 2 The World Health Organisation (WHO) African Region (AFRO) bears a huge brunt of the burden of AMR with an estimated 1.05 million deaths linked to bacterial AMR in 2019, including 250,000 directly attributed to bacterial AMR. 3 Additionally, it is estimated that 10 million people, including 4.1 million in the Region, will die from AMR-related causes globally by 2050. 4 Additionally, forecasting estimates show that 25.2 million lives could be saved in sub-Saharan Africa between 2025 and 2050 by improving quality of healthcare and access to antibiotics. 5 Furthermore, recent global events have influenced AMR dynamics, including the COVID-19 pandemic, which affected infection patterns and antibiotic use. Evidence from a systematic review reported a slight increase in the incidence of Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacteremia during the pandemic, despite strengthened infection prevention and control measures. 6 In parallel, global data show notable changes in antibiotic consumption, with increased use during the pandemic and projected continued growth through 2030 if current practices persist. 7

Identified as one of the most urgent health challenges of the decade, AMR requires the joint engagement and coordinated efforts of countries and partners. 8 Such joint engagement and coordinated support will ensure that countries enhance national leadership, foster partnerships including technical support from partners, adapt and monitor implementation of norms and standards, build institutional capacity, and ensure generation and dissemination of knowledge as they relate to AMR.9,10 In 2014, information on the extent of AMR was limited to a few countries in the region due to paucity of surveillance data. Moreover, the limited collaboration among surveillance programmes in the Region hindered systematic assessment of AMR trends11,12 and the response to AMR was limited to surveillance, laboratory diagnosis, and pharmaceuticals.

The World Health Assembly (WHA) thus proposed a resolution which called for a Global Action Plan (GAP) to combat AMR in 2014. The resolution, WHA68.7, adopted in 2015, stressed the importance for all WHO levels to actively engage and collaborate to combat AMR. All Member States committed to develop national action plans (NAPs) aligned with the GAP in collaboration with various stakeholders and partners.13,14 Thus, other focus areas such as multisectoral collaboration and coordination, awareness and rational use of antimicrobials were incorporated ensuring a holistic approach to AMR. Consequently, countries were supported to develop and implement AMR NAPs.15,16

To monitor and assess country progress in implementing national AMR NAPs, this paper draws on evidence from the 2024 AMR Country Self-Assessment Survey (TrACSS) data, which is the 8th round of the Tripartite AMR Country Self-Assessment Survey. The review focused on indicators derived from multi-sectoral questions (Indicator 2.1: Multi-sector and One Health collaboration/coordination; Indicator 2.3: Country progress with development of a national action plan on AMR; Indicator 2.9: Raising awareness and understanding of AMR risks and response) and those specific to the Human Health sector (Indicator 3.6: Optimizing antimicrobial use in human health). TrACSS data were complemented by the Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS), which provided information on country participation, AMR data collection, and reporting.

The TrACSS, jointly administered by WHO, Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH), and the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP), is the primary tool for monitoring national implementation of AMR NAPs. It is completed through self-assessment by relevant authorities and technical focal points across multiple government sectors, providing a multi-sectoral perspective on national AMR responses. The survey captures progress across key interventions, including policy implementation, surveillance systems, antimicrobial stewardship, infection prevention and control, and awareness, enabling standardized measurement of progress across AMR interventions. 17 GLASS, established in 2015, monitors AMR in common bacteria and invasive fungi, as well as antimicrobial consumption in humans. It provides a standardized framework for the collection, analysis, and sharing of AMR and antimicrobial consumption data, supporting the documentation and assessment of national AMR surveillance systems. 18

While several studies and reports have documented the achievements in the various aspects of AMR management, none to the best of our knowledge has comprehensively documented the progress made and challenges encountered in AMR governance architecture and monitoring uptake in the WHOAFRO. This paper thus describes the progress made from 2015 to 2024 by WHO/AFRO countries in the implementation of AMR NAPs, based on evidence from TrACSS and GLASS. The paper also reflects on enabling factors, challenges, and proposes recommendations for ensuring more impactful results in AMR management in the Region.

National Action Plan Development, Policy Frameworks, and Monitoring

The number of countries with NAPs on AMR developed under “One Health” approach grew from 2 (4.3%) in 2015 to 47 (100%) in 2024. Thirty-five (74.5%) of these plans were endorsed by national authorities. As of 2024, 20 countries developed national guidelines for the appropriate use of antimicrobials, and thirty-seven (78.7%) countries have laws or regulations on prescription and sale of antimicrobials, for human use. In addition, the number of countries that have IPC policies and plans increased from 7 (14.9%) in 2016 to 32 (68%) in 2024. 20 The number of countries conducting in-country monitoring and evaluation activities to track and report the implementation progress of NAPs increased from 27 (57.4%) in 2022 to 47 (100%) in 2024. The TrACSS, now offers the added advantage of real-time country and regional AMR profiles, showcasing the progress made across key targets of the AMR regional strategy. 19 Developing and implementing NAPs by Member States with effective monitoring systems can improve accountability and empower stakeholders to advocate effectively for combating AMR. 21

Despite this progress, implementation remains uneven across Member States. While 74.5% of plans have received formal endorsement, the remaining plans have not, highlighting an important implementation gap. Formal endorsement is a critical prerequisite for securing government commitment, mobilizing resources, enabling operationalization, strengthening institutional accountability, and facilitating coordinated implementation across sectors. Although the development of NAPs represents a major milestone, their impact ultimately depends on effective implementation through adequate financing, robust monitoring and evaluation, and sustained multisectoral coordination. As shown in table 2, countries continue to differ widely in their capacity to implement AMR interventions, as reflected by the TrACSS five-point scale (A–E), which ranges from no capacity (A) to sustained capacity (E). Each scale level is accompanied by a standardized colour code defined by the TrACSS framework: A (red, no capacity), B (orange, limited capacity), C (yellow, developed capacity), D (green, demonstrated capacity), and E (dark green, sustained capacity). According to the 2024 TrACSS data, 22 countries have attained developed capacity or higher (C, D, or E) in multi-sector and One Health collaboration and coordination. Developed capacity or above has been achieved in 33 countries for raising awareness and understanding of AMR risks and response, and 32 countries have achieved similar capacity levels for training and professional education on AMR. Progress in national monitoring systems for consumption and rational use of antimicrobials remains limited, with only 14 countries demonstrating developed capacity, while 20 remain at limited capacity. Developed capacity or higher has been reported in 26 countries for AMR surveillance, 29 countries for infection prevention and control, and 23 countries for optimizing antimicrobial use. These variations highlight persistent gaps in technical, operational, and financial capacities that hinder uniform implementation across the region. Policymakers must therefore continue to advocate, endorse, support, and fund existing and new AMR initiatives. 22

Enhancing National Multisectoral Coordination

Notable progress has been made in strengthening multisectoral coordination for AMR in the WHO African Region. In 2016, the Joint External Evaluation (JEE), a voluntary component of the International Health Regulations (2005) monitoring and evaluation framework that assesses national health system capacities and multisectoral coordination mechanisms for AMR containment, revealed that only one country demonstrated capacity in AMR multisectoral coordination. 23 This number increased from 13 (27.7%) in 2017 to 41 (87.2%) in 2024. Coordination has been shown to be a key catalyst for ensuring integration of AMR activities into existing programs through joint planning and monitoring across initiatives, promoting a one health and interdisciplinary approach to implementation rather than a siloed approach, thus ensuring effectiveness of interventions and sustainability. 24 Collaborative, multisectoral One Health approaches can leverage expertise and mandates across different organizations and sectors to prevent and control AMR, with the potential to facilitate health and economic benefits for all. 25 Although progress has been made in establishing multisectoral coordination mechanisms, only 22 countries have fully functional coordination systems, while others continue to function at a suboptimal level. 26 Common challenges include lack of clearly defined roles and responsibilities for members, limited structured procedures for prioritizing discussions and decision-making, and insufficient accountability frameworks to ensure planned interventions are achieved. Addressing these challenges is critical for strengthening the functionality of coordination mechanisms and enabling countries to fully harness their potential in advancing AMR response across sectors.

Improving AMR Awareness and Understanding

Inappropriate antimicrobial use, often due to a lack of knowledge, is a major factor driving antimicrobial resistance. 27 Education and awareness are an overarching determinant for effective implementation of key AMR interventions as it aims to promote understanding, advocacy, behaviour change and change of practice across all sectors and systems. 28 The number of countries conducting nationwide AMR awareness campaigns targeting majority of priority stakeholder groups has seen only a marginal increase from 2 (4.3%) in 2017 to 16 (34%) in 2024 when compared to other intervention areas. Awareness of AMR remains limited despite numerous efforts to consolidate its implementation. In the region, the pooled prevalence of knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to AMR among various groups remain low, highlighting the need for enhanced education and training programs across sectors. 29 A recent study underscored the critical need for enhanced, context-specific educational and behaviour change interventions while another one highlighted the need for AMR campaigns to address inappropriate antimicrobial behaviours that include multidisciplinary and multisectoral experts.28,30

While the yearly World AMR Awareness Week (WAAW) events have contributed to narrowing the AMR knowledge gaps and increased advocacy for responsible antimicrobial use, the commitment and support of governments for such awareness campaigns remains limited. To address this, advocacy to countries to increase funding for conducting regular AMR awareness campaigns targeting majority of priority stakeholder groups and educational initiatives using diverse approaches and targeting specific population segments across sectors are needed. Such initiatives would be instrumental to promote behaviour change towards combating AMR. 31 Additionally, intensive community engagement to secure their buy-in, participation and ownership of community AMR initiative is also an imperative.

Enhancing AMR Surveillance Systems to Generate Evidence

Data from 2016 to 2020 showed that the bacterial resistance remains substantial in the region with Escherichia coli and Streptococcus pneumoniae showing high resistance rates.32,33 The number of countries participating in the GLASS, which was launched in 2015 to strengthen national surveillance systems, increased from 7 (14.9%) in 2016 to 41 (87.2%) in 2024. In 2024, twenty-five (53.1%) countries used relevant antimicrobial resistance surveillance data to inform policies and infection prevention and control actions, including updating treatment guidelines and essential medicines lists, detecting emerging resistance threats, and guiding the design and evaluation of intervention strategies. Reporting and use of GLASS data has helped to shape policy and coordinate efforts among various stakeholders to enhance the fight against AMR. 34

Nevertheless, challenges remain in AMR surveillance and reporting. While most countries in the region are enrolled in the GLASS platform to monitor AMR and antimicrobial consumption trends, gaps remain in reporting regularly and collecting representative data for decision-making. Between 2020 and 2022, only 13 countries consistently submitted AMR data to GLASS. Mauritius and South Africa contributed the largest amount of AMR data, sourced from a relatively larger number of health care facilities and in-country locations, making their data more representative of AMR patterns in their respective settings. 35 Therefore, countries, with support from stakeholders, should strengthen surveillance systems and laboratory capacity to improve data quality and timeliness, implement periodic nationally representative prevalence surveys on AMR and use surveillance findings to guide policy development and community initiatives. In addition, challenges remain in the funding of the AMR surveillance activities. In this regard, the countries are encouraged to identify opportunities for the mobilization of sustainable resources through existing and new funding partners while prioritising domestic financing.

Optimising the Use of Antimicrobials to Enhance Patient Outcomes

To optimize the appropriate use of antimicrobial medicines, it is essential to implement antimicrobial stewardship interventions, with a coordinated effort to educate and guide healthcare professionals in adopting evidence-based practices for prescribing and dispensing antimicrobials. 36 Progress in this area has been observed. The number of countries implementing antimicrobial stewardship programmes in healthcare facilities increased from 10 (21.3%) in 2017 to 20 (42.6%) in 2024. In these countries, antimicrobial data are used to review prescribing guidelines, inform formulary updates, and monitor adherence to stewardship interventions.

While progress is evident, inappropriate access to antimicrobials persists as a major challenge. The continued availability of antibiotics without prescriptions through private pharmacies, informal drug vendors, and over-the-counter sales remains a major driver of AMR in the region. 37 To address this challenge, stronger government efforts are needed to enforce laws and regulations that prevent the sale of antimicrobials without prescription and reduce the circulation of falsified and counterfeit medicines. Studies on status of national core elements for Antimicrobial Stewardship Programmes in the WHO African Region emphasizes the need for stronger leadership to promote responsible antimicrobial use.38,39

Effective sanitation, hygiene, infection prevention measures, and increased vaccine coverage to reduce the incidence of infections

Infection prevention and control (IPC) and Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) are crucial for reducing the spread of infections, including drug-resistant ones. Notable progress in the presence of WASH at health facilities was made in 26 countries in 2024. 40 In 2016, 84% of hospitals had hand hygiene facilities at points of care, and by 2021, this increased to 87%. Despite these gains, significant challenges remain. Implementation is often constrained by limited financing, inadequate infrastructure, and insufficient numbers of trained personnel, which hinder consistent adherence to IPC and WASH standards.

Vaccines are vital in reducing the incidence of infections in healthcare facilities and communities, thereby limiting the use of antimicrobials and the emergence of AMR. 41 Stakeholders across Africa, including WHO, the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, and the African Development Bank, are intensifying their efforts to collaborate on the local production of safe and effective vaccines across the continent. 42 According to WHO/UNICEF estimates of national immunization coverage (WUENIC), the coverage for the 3rd dose of the Hib vaccine rose from 71% in 2015 to 74% in 2023. Similarly, the coverage of the final dose of the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine increased from 60% in 2015 to 70% in 2023. 43 While African countries include vaccination as a strategy in their national action plans on AMR, detailed implementation and evaluation frameworks are lacking. 44

Enabling Factors for the Implementation of Antimicrobial Resistance Interventions

The above findings demonstrate notable progress and challenges in the implementation of AMR policies, strategies and plans in the African region over the past 10 years. The successes achieved thus far can be attributed to the following key factors:

First, the endorsement and adaptation of global and regional AMR strategies and initiatives by WHO/AFRO countries, including the AMR GAP, Regional strategy for expediting the implementation and monitoring of AMR NAPs, and WHO strategic and operational priorities to address drug-resistant bacterial infections, have enhanced commitment, ownership, and leadership of AMR interventions in the region. Additionally, the use of multisectoral and health system strengthening approaches, through mainstreaming AMR interventions into primary health care initiatives under the leadership of national authorities, has played a crucial role in fostering mutual trust 45 and effective implementation of the AMR NAPs. This we believe will contribute to the progress towards Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and overall health security in the Region in the medium to long-term. 46

Second, the leveraging of existing coordination and cross-cutting platforms resulted in integrated and sustainable implementation of AMR interventions. For instance, the use of existing One Health coordination platforms to address health security and health system strengthening including AMR created synergies in programme implementation. 47 Additionally, this enhanced collaboration, policy coherence, planning, resource optimisation, and accountability. 48 Furthermore, WHO/AFR countries implemented organizational changes to integrate disease programmes more effectively within a health system strengthening framework while addressing health security. This transformation employed a results-driven and integrated cluster management approach. 49

Third, the collaborations between United Nations (UN) agencies namely the FAO, WOAH, UNEP, WHO/AFRO, civil society and other international organizations have been instrumental in providing the required technical guidance, resources mobilization, advocacy, community awareness and capacity building support for AMR by WHO/AFR countries. Additionally, the collaboration between WHO/AFRO and the African Union Commission (AUC) through a signed framework leveraged their respective strengths on policy advocacy, infectious disease management and AMR prevention and control in the Region. 50 The establishment of a dedicated AMR support unit by WHO/AFRO which works in close collaboration with the quadripartite organizations (FAO, WHO, WOAH, UNEP) and the AUC have been crucial for implementation and monitoring of the GAP on AMR and ensuring a strategic advocacy and communication plan. 51 These partnerships also helped to strengthen coordination and advocate for sustainability of interventions. 52

Fourth, the availability of state-of-the-art platforms have been key to enhancing overall implementation progress. The GLASS platform for the surveillance of AMR and antimicrobial use data has enhanced global data representativeness and quality, while also providing standardized tools, technical support, and global visibility that motivate countries to actively participate and contribute quality data. The WHONET microbiology laboratory database software has enabled countries to manage and analyse their microbiology data, further strengthening the availability and reliability of AMR data. In addition, TrACSS tool allows countries to access up-to-date progress report on the AMR NAPs implementation. 53 By promoting multisectoral coordination, strengthening accountability, and informing policy decisions, TrACSS encourages countries to report regularly and sustain efforts toward AMR containment.

Fifth, the engagement of stakeholders to strengthen Africa’s capacity for local production of vaccines and health products have been essential to guaranteeing core medicine regulatory functions and improving equitable and timely access to quality medicines towards achievement of the universal health coverage.54-56 Local production of vaccines and medicines supports AMR containment by reducing the burden of vaccine-preventable infectious diseases and thereby lowering antimicrobial use, while minimizing reliance on substandard or falsified medicines, which are known drivers of resistance. 42

Looking ahead, the combined efforts of both regional and national AMR stakeholders are essential to foster an environment that accelerates progress toward achieving the 2030 SDG and AMR goals, while addressing the remaining AMR challenges. This will contribute to reducing AMR associated morbidity and mortality in the region and ultimately, in realizing the SDGs and UHC.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of a few limitations. Responses to the TrACSS are based on self-assessment and are not independently verified, which introduces the potential for response bias that may either overstate or understate national progress, strengths, and weaknesses at the national level. Moreover, the study relied primarily on TrACSS and GLASS data, and triangulation with independent country experiences, external evaluations, or peer-reviewed assessments was not undertaken, which may limit the breadth of perspectives captured. The study also uses the presence of policies as proxies for implementation, without assessing actual outcome measures or impact. Additionally, AMR data reported through GLASS are constrained by limited country participation, as well as incomplete and inconsistent reporting, which affects the overall quality and representativeness of the data across the region. Nevertheless, the TrACSS and GLASS remain consistent and valuable sources of information for monitoring multisectoral AMR responses and the implementation of national action plans.

Conclusion

A decade after the adoption of WHA68.7, African countries have made notable strides toward achieving the goals set in the subsequent regional strategy to fast-track the implementation of AMR NAPs. Many Member States now have ‘One Health’ governance systems for AMR and have carried out nationwide awareness campaigns. Moreover, participation in GLASS has grown, with countries increasingly using its data to shape AMR policies.

However, major challenges persist, including the lack of high-quality AMR data, insufficient community engagement and awareness about AMR, inadequate resource mobilization, and limited political commitment. As the 2030 deadline for achieving UHC and the SDGs approaches, collective effort from all AMR stakeholders is essential to consolidate progress and leverage new strategies and technologies to comprehensively tackle AMR in Africa. To accelerate NAP implementation, WHO/AFR countries, with the support of their partners, must strengthen ownership and enhance multisectoral governance by defining roles and responsibilities across sectors, establishing monitoring and accountability frameworks, and promoting transparency through regular progress reporting to stakeholders. In addition, countries should mobilize sustainable resources using costed AMR operational plans and make better use of data for informed decision-making. Additionally, future research should examine more granular outcomes or AMR intervention impact measures, including the impact of governance and monitoring interventions on antimicrobial use patterns, resistance trends, and clinical outcomes. Implementation research to develop and test new strategies and methodologies for AMR prevention and control is also a research imperative.
