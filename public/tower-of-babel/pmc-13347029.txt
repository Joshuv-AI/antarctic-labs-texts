Modeling and simulation of magnesium electrodeposition on TiO2 nanotubes for Mg-ion microbattery applications
Authors: Raigul Jumanova, Khaisa Avchukir, Akmaral Argimbayeva, Gulmira Rakhymbay, Florence Vacandio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanostructured titanium dioxide (TiO2) has gained recognition as a promising material for advanced microbattery electrodes, attributed to its high surface area and advantageous electrochemical properties. Nonetheless, the impact of electrode geometry and current microdistribution on surface degradation and ion transport remains inadequately understood. This study examines magnesium discharge behavior on TiO2 nanotube (TiO2-NTs) substrates through a combined experimental and computational methodology. TiO2-NTs were synthesized and subjected to electrochemical characterization, and a two-dimensional COMSOL model was developed to simulate Mg2+ discharge, incorporating variable nanotube length, mass transport, and reaction kinetics. The simulations demonstrated that nanotube geometry significantly influences current distribution, potentially leading to gap closure and a reduction in active surface area due to non-uniform Mg deposition. Enhanced diffusion coefficients and optimized reaction kinetics facilitated more uniform coverage, whereas deeper nanotubes presented greater challenges for effective ion transport. These findings highlight the critical importance of nano-architectural optimization in the design of high-performance Mg-ion microbatteries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52374-1.

Introduction

The potential demand for microbatteries for energy storage in medical and cosmetic devices, sensors, solar cells and smart cards has stimulated the development of microscale energy devices with improved characteristics such as high power, energy density and long cycle life1–3. To achieve high areal performance within a limited footprint, microbattery concepts increasingly rely on nanoarchitectured and 3D-like electrodes, where increased active surface area and shortened ion-transport pathways can enhance both energy and power. Recently, considerable attention has been focused on nanostructured electrode materials such as nanopillars, nanorods and nanotubes, which are promising for microbatteries due to their high capacity, large surface area and short ion diffusion paths4. These nanostructures can be synthesized from titanium oxide, which has been extensively studied as a material for nanoarchitectural electrodes in energy storage applications5–7. Nanostructured TiO2 exhibits excellent capacity retention under cyclic loading, minimal self-discharge and volume changes of less than 4% upon insertion and extraction of lithium from the lattice2,8–10. However, the performance of nanoarchitectured electrodes is not governed only by bulk insertion properties. In confined geometries, the spatial distribution of ionic transport and local interfacial kinetics can lead to non-uniform current density (“current microdistribution”), which may progressively reduce the accessible electrolyte volume and the electrochemically active surface area. The performance of nanostructured electrodes depends on the parameters that determine their compatibility with electrolytes and regulate the formation of a stable solid electrolyte interfacial layer (SEI)11. The precise formation, structure, and functional mechanisms of SEI have challenged many researchers and remain among the most contentious issues in battery science. Interfacial films may affect charge-transfer kinetics and overpotential. However, in the present work SEI formation is not modelled explicitly as a separate transport/resistive layer. Instead, its influence is captured implicitly through effective interfacial kinetic parameters extracted from experimental characterization.

It is established that the operation of an electrochemical system is influenced not only by kinetic factors directly related to the nature of the processes occurring at the phase boundary, but also by certain macroscopic characteristics such as the relative position of the electrodes and cell constant12. It is noteworthy that when modelling nanostructured electrodes such as TiO2 nanotubes (TiO2-NTs), they should not be considered as planar electrodes13. Although the thickness of the nanostructured layer ranges from several tens of nanometers to a micrometer, the influence of a thin layer on the current distribution should not be disregarded. In previous works, we demonstrated the possibility of some intercalation of magnesium like lithium into TiO2-NTs14. Therefore, two competing processes may occur during discharge on TiO2-NTs based Mg configurations: (i) Mg2+ insertion into the host and (ii) Mg metal deposition on accessible conductive surfaces. In this context, non-uniform deposition is particularly critical because it can locally constrict the electrolyte pathways and decrease the active surface area available for both mass transfer and electrochemical reaction. As anticipated, magnesium intercalation in TiO2-NTs electrodes is characterized by slow kinetics. Analysis of the electrode material surface after discharge/intercalation revealed that some areas of the TiO2-NTs surface are covered with a layer of reaction products. This indicates a possible rapid saturation of the active intercalating layer of TiO2 with magnesium and the occurrence of overpotential deposition of magnesium. Consequently, further cathode growth is dominated by magnesium electrodeposition rather than intercalation. Furthermore, the question arises regarding the efficiency of utilizing nanotube geometry and studying the effect of current micro distribution on changes in electrode homogeneity during electrochemical processes. This provides the basis for focusing the present modeling approach on deposition processes. Yet, for nanochannel/trench-like geometries relevant to TiO2-NTs arrays, capturing deposition-driven surface evolution requires coupling ionic transport (diffusion and migration), interfacial kinetics, and the time-dependent change of electrode geometry.

In this work, we investigate the efficiency of utilizing nanotube geometry under Mg discharge conditions by combining experiments with a two-dimensional (2D) finite element model of a representative TiO2-NT cross-section. The model quantifies how (a) nanotube geometry, (b) diffusion coefficient, and (c) exchange current density control current distribution and deposition non-uniformity and demonstrates how gap bridging/closure can reduce active surface area and mass transfer. The 2D approach enables systematic sensitivity studies at moderate computational cost, nevertheless, we also discuss its limitations and outline how the framework can be extended to 3D unit-cell simulations in future work to account for full 3D surface area and geometric effects.

Experimental

Preparation and characterization of TiO2 nanotubes

The fabrication of TiO2 nanotubes was accomplished using titanium foil (grade 2, ≥ 99.7%, Sigma-Aldrich) and an electrolyte solution consisting of 96.7 wt.% glycerol (≥ 99.7%, VWR Chemicals BDH), 1.3 wt.% NH4F (≥ 98%, Sigma-Aldrich), and 2 wt.% water14. The electrochemical anodization process was carried out at 60 V for a duration of 3 h in a two-electrode cell configuration, with titanium foil functioning as the working electrode and platinum foil as the counter electrode. To induce the formation of the anatase phase, the synthesized nanotubes underwent thermal annealing in air at 450 °C for 3 h, with a temperature ramp rate of 5 °C min-1.

Morphological characterization of the prepared samples (Fig. 1a) was conducted using high-resolution scanning electron microscopy (ZEISS Gemini SEM 500), operating at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Complementary to the SEM analysis, X-ray diffraction studies were undertaken to elucidate the compositional structure of the samples (Fig. 1b). The X-ray diffractograms were acquired using a Bruker D5000 diffractometer, using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm, 38 kV, 30 mA), with a step size of 0.04° and a step duration of 2 s.Fig. 1SEM image (A) and XRD pattern (B) of self-assembled TiO2 nanotubes.

SEM image (A) and XRD pattern (B) of self-assembled TiO2 nanotubes.

Magnesium discharge-ionization

The electrochemical behaviour of TiO2-NTs material was investigated using cyclic voltammetry in an electrolyte comprising 0.5 M MgTFSI2 dissolved in a 1:1 mixture of ethylene carbonate and dimethyl carbonate (EC:DMC). A Swagelok three-electrode cell was employed for the experiment, wherein a metallic magnesium plate served as both counter and reference electrode, and the synthesised material (Fig. 1A) functioned as the working electrode.

In experiments designed to examine the overpotential deposition of magnesium, metallic magnesium was utilised as the working electrode. All electrochemical experiments were conducted in an inert argon atmosphere, within a glovebox (Ossila) at room temperature. Measurements of cyclic voltammograms for magnesium discharge-ionisation were performed using a BioLogic SP-300 potentiostat.

Model description

Model geometry

In the modelling of magnesium discharge, a surface of the cathode was considered as a trench-like surface, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The planar magnesium electrode, serving as the anode, was positioned parallel (coaxially placed). The distance between the cathode and anode, with a thickness of 1 µm, represents the electrolyte containing a solution of 0.5 M MgTFSI2 salt dissolved in ethylene carbonate/dimethyl carbonate (1/1) mixed solvent. Figure 2B illustrates a geometric configuration that incorporates the presence of an adjacent nanotube. The spatial separation between nanotubes is represented by an average distance of 50 nm. To elucidate the influence of depth on mass transfer, the slit depth within the nanotubes was adjusted; the geometry presented above features a depth of 450 nm.

The deformation of geometry resulting from magnesium electrodeposition was modeled utilizing a moving mesh approach, specifically the Arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian method, which facilitates explicit tracking of the evolving electrode/electrolyte interface. The implementation follows established electrodeposition modeling strategies, similar to those presented in the COMSOL Application Library example (Application ID: 2112), where surface growth is governed by electrochemical reaction kinetics. The computational domain was discretized using a physics-controlled mesh with an “Extra fine” setting. Additional mesh refinement parameters included a maximum element size of 5 × 10-8m and minimum element size 3 × 10-10m, a curvature factor of 1, and a resolution of narrow regions set to 10. These settings ensured precise resolution of interfacial deformation and concentration gradients during the time-dependent simulation.

Physics of the model

The model used in this work contributes the studying of magnesium electrodeposition under potentiostatic condition, accounting for variable geometry. The model was developed based on the model proposed in15,16, with modifications to the geometry and incorporation of the kinetic characteristics of magnesium discharge, using the Electrodeposition module of COMSOL Multiphysics. A two-dimensional geometry was implemented in the model, considering its alteration during the electrochemical reduction of magnesium on the cathode. The process is inherently time-dependent, as the cathode boundary undergoes displacement during the Mg deposition process. The transformation of the cathode geometry was achieved through the application of the Arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian moving mesh formulation, a feature of the Electrodeposition module in COMSOL Multiphysics. At the initial stage of deposition, the open geometry allows relatively uniform ion transport, resulting in higher current values. As magnesium deposition proceeds, the narrowing of the electrolyte domain within the nanotube structure leads to increased mass transport limitations and concentration polarization. Consequently, the local current density decreases in regions with restricted ion supply, which results in a gradual decrease of the total current over time.

The progression of gap filling is therefore directly related to the time-integrated current (in accordance with Faraday’s law), rather than time alone. As the gap approaches closure, strong transport limitations reduce the deposition rate, which can significantly influence the final morphology of the deposit.

The Butler-Volmer boundary condition is evaluated on the instantaneous, deformed cathode boundary. The deposition velocity is calculated locally from the normal current density via Faraday’s law (Eq. 1) and applied as a normal mesh velocity. Results and discussion

Investigation of magnesium discharge-ionization

The cyclic voltammogram illustrating magnesium discharge on a metallic magnesium electrode exhibits an exponential curve, characteristic of a reaction with sluggish charge transfer kinetics (Fig. 3A). This observation is consistent with expectations, given the high bulk concentration of magnesium ions (0.5 M), which should not present mass transfer limitations. Notably, the magnesium reduction process is associated with substantial overpotential, which varies depending on the scanning direction in the voltammogram. The observed increase in overvoltage during the reverse scan may be attributed to the formation of an additional barrier, namely the SEI, which further complicates the charge transfer process. CV of the TiO2-NTs electrode exhibits peak C1, indicative of restricted mass transfer during magnesium discharge. Notably, the CV lacks a “nucleation loop”, which may be attributed to magnesium intercalation occurring without the formation of a new phase on the electrode surface. The anodic peak A1, corresponding to the quantity of intercalated magnesium, demonstrates favourable reversibility. The insertion and extraction mechanism can be elucidated by the model proposed in the study20. Scanning electron microscopy imaging of the TiO2-NTs electrode post-magnesium ion discharge reveals partially covered areas (Supplementary Information Figure S1 (A), highlighted in green), potentially resulting from overpotential deposition of Mg (Supplementary Information Figure S1 (B)). This phenomenon is further corroborated by Fig. 5 in the work14.

The results indicate rapid saturation of the thin layer of active TiO2 material with magnesium, and the occurrence of magnesium electrodeposition on the surface, reducing the active surface (nano structuring) and ultimately leading to a flat surface. In this instance, the surface of nanostructured TiO2 can be considered as a cathode, and magnesium electrodeposition on this surface can be studied using simulation. In the subsequent sections, the effects of various factors on the alteration in electrode geometry and the formation of voids during the coating of the electrode surface with magnesium are investigated.

It should be noted that the experimental polarization curves (Fig. 3) were obtained under potentiodynamic conditions, whereas the simulations were performed under potentiostatic control. Therefore, direct quantitative comparison is not possible. However, the experimental data were used to find the kinetic parameter as exchange current density.

Figure 4A clearly illustrates that the geometric features result in a non-uniform distribution of current density across the entire surface of the nanotube cathode, leading to the closure of the gap within the nanotube. The non-uniform growth of the electrodeposited magnesium layer becomes increasingly evident with prolonged electroreduction time, particularly at 600 s. It can be observed that after 1000 s, the gap in the nanotube cathode is completely closed, thereby obstructing mass transfer into the tube’s depth. This formation of voids consequently diminishes the capacitive characteristics of the battery, rendering most of the surface inactive and making this method of nanostructuring (nanotubes) impractical.

However, it is important to note that the kinetic characteristics previously mentioned pertain to magnesium intercalation, which fundamentally differs from the overpotential deposition that describes the growth of the magnesium layer. It is established that the capacity of the thin oxide layer of TiO2 is relatively small14, and the saturation of the TiO2 layer with magnesium should not require significant time. In our view, once the TiO2 layer is saturated with magnesium, further growth in cathode thickness can be attributed to the overpotential deposition of Mg, and the kinetic characteristics of magnesium electrodeposition should be employed in the modeling. It is recognized that kinetic parameters, such as the rate constant, exchange current density, and transfer coefficient, depend not only on the cathode-substrate material but also on the composition and nature of the solution22. Regrettably, we were unable to locate a more comprehensive description of the kinetics of magnesium electrochemical deposition that provides numerical kinetic data. According to the work19, the exchange current density for the overpotential deposition of Mg is 10 A m-2 (1 mA cm-2), and the simulation results using this value are presented in Fig. 4B.

The increase in exchange current density from 1.5 × 10−4 A m−2 to 10 A m−2 significantly alters the growth dynamics of the magnesium layer. Initially, the rapid kinetics of electroreduction dramatically reduce the overlapping time from 1000 s (observed under slower kinetics Fig. 4A) to just a few tens of milliseconds (Fig. 4B). Additionally, a high exchange current density facilitates the formation of dendrites, as thoroughly explained and illustrated in Fig. 6 of the work23.

It is evident that dendrite formation is influenced not only by fast reaction kinetics but also by electrolysis conditions, such as the applied potential. The effect of the applied potential on magnesium growth on the cathode is depicted in Figure S2 in the Supplementary Information. It was found that at lower applied potentials (–0.3 V), dendrite formation does not occur, whereas a 60 mV shift towards the negative region (–0.36 V) encourages dendrite formation (Figure S2 (A)). Moreover, the time required for the complete closure of the nanotube is affected by the applied potential, with 80 ms and 60 ms needed at potentials of −0.3 V and −0.33 V, respectively (Figure S2 (B)). At more negative potentials of −0.36 V and −0.4 V, dendrite formation prevents the complete slow, possibly due to the limited diffusion of magnesium ions. To further elucidate the impact of mass transfer on the growth characteristics of the magnesium layer, investigations were conducted by varying the diffusion coefficient and the concentration of magnesium ions, as discussed in the following section.

The application of an appropriate diffusion coefficient value significantly alters the understanding of the process from a physical perspective. A high diffusion coefficient facilitates rapid mass transfer, enabling the electrodeposition of magnesium into the depths of the nanotube, resulting in uniform growth of the nanotube wall (Fig. 5B) as opposed to the effects of slow diffusion (Fig. 5A).

A critical factor affecting mass transfer is the concentration of magnesium ions. An increase in concentration enhances mass transfer and accelerates the reaction rate, as clearly demonstrated in Figure S3 provided in the Supplementary Information. The concentration distribution profile (Figure S3) indicates that the concentration of magnesium ions in the cathode region (near cathode surface boundary) is slightly lower than in the bulk of the solution (bulk concentration). It is also noteworthy that, with a deposition time of 100 s, magnesium is uniformly deposited on the cathode surface, covering the nanotube wall, and the layer thickness increases significantly with higher concentrations.

The results demonstrated in Fig. 8 show that in areas closer to the cathode surface, the thickness of the deposited magnesium layer is twice as thick (6 × 10−2 µm) as in the deeper walls of the nanotube (2 × 10−2 µm). Based on these observations, it can be concluded that the 3D simulation yields result similar to the 2D simulation (Fig. 6).

Extending the model to three dimensions would offer a more accurate representation of the nanotube geometry. However, such simulations are computationally intensive, especially when combined with moving mesh techniques, due to significant mesh deformation and increased numerical complexity. Consequently, the current two-dimensional approach represents a reasonable compromise between physical accuracy and computational feasibility, facilitating a systematic investigation of the dominant processes influencing deposition behavior.

In conclusion, the findings presented in this study effectively demonstrate the significance of cathode geometry, specifically nanostructuring, and the potential issue of gap closure when the material is a nanotube. This phenomenon is not limited to the electrodeposition of magnesium but also occurs during the electrodeposition or coating of TiO2-NTs cathodes with a solid electrode or polymer. We posit that these results will serve as a prerequisite for future research, which will concentrate on three-dimensional modeling using alternative methodologies, such as phase-field methods, to more accurately capture the evolution of complex morphologies.

Conclusion

This research explores the effect of nanotube geometry on the discharge behavior of magnesium and the surface morphology of TiO2 nanotube electrodes, utilizing both experimental techniques and COMSOL Multiphysics simulations. The study reveals that TiO2-NTs, with their high surface area, are promising for Mg-ion battery applications; however, their performance is highly contingent on geometric and kinetic factors. It is imperative to achieve a precise balance between intercalation kinetics, electrode geometry, and mass transport properties to optimize Mg deposition and avert adverse phenomena such as dendrite formation or gap closure. By employing empirically derived parameters, such as an exchange current density of 0.665 A m-2, realistic modeling offers accurate insights into deposition behavior, potentially guiding the development of stable, high-performance Mg-ion microbatteries.
