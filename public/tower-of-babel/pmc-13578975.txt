Preparation and collaborative study of a recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) genome copy number reference material
Authors: Hua Bi, Wenxiu Hou, Youxue Ding, Haohang Sun, Zhihao Fu, Xinchang Shi, Wei Jiang, Zengmin Du, Dening Pei, Xiang Li, Xi Qin, Chenggang Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) vectors are central to gene therapy for their safety and transduction efficiency [1]. Genome titer assays, vital for evaluating product quality and clinical dose safety/efficacy, are challenged by the lack of standardized reference materials and unified methods, hindering inter-laboratory consistency. Unlike quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), digital polymerase chain reaction (dPCR)—an absolute quantification method with lower coefficient of variation (CV) and higher sensitivity—offers superior accuracy [2]. Developing standardized reference materials paired with dPCR-based genome titer methods could resolve these challenges, ensuring reliable rAAV quantification.

In this study, a single-stranded rAAV (rAAV(ss)), genome copy number reference material (GNR), hereafter referred to as rAAV(ss) GNR, was developed using a three-plasmid transfection system (constructed by Belief BioMed, Shanghai, China). The system included AAV843 capsid, helper, and gene-of-interest (GOI) plasmids, co-transfected into HEK293T cells (obtained from the European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC; Salisbury, UK)) [3]. Post-transfection, viral vectors were purified by ultracentrifugation and anion exchange chromatography, formulated in stock solution buffer, and stored at below −60 °C for stability. A highly sensitive and reproducible genome titer assay protocol based on dPCR was established to support the standardization of the titer assay of rAAV vectors. Comprehensive quality analysis of the rAAV(ss) GNR mother liquor was performed. The prepared GNR, along with the standard assay protocol and reagents, was distributed to 11 gene therapy companies and a testing facility for evaluation.

The quantification assay of rAAV products is a key indicator of both process and product quality. It plays a vital role in supporting process development and preclinical/clinical studies and toxicity studies [4]. The rAAV(ss) GNR mother liquor underwent comprehensive quality characterization (Table S1), including titer assays, purity analysis, empty capsid/full capsid ratio, and functional validation, the mother liquor showed a genome titer of 2.56 × 1013 vector genomes per milliliter (vg/mL). Sanger sequencing results confirmed 100% sequence consistency of the GOI. (Fig. S1), while 2-plex dPCR and capillary electrophoresis-sodium dodecyl sulfate (CE-SDS) analysis showed 94.2% and 91.5% genome integrity, respectively (Figs. S2 and S3). Analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) revealed a 99.4% full capsid ratio, matching genome/capsid titers at 99.6% (Fig. S4). Purity metrics were excellent (0.5% aggregates, 97.5% capsid purity). Functional assays results met criteria (94%). The consistency across test dimensions mutually corroborated the stable quality and good uniformity of the mother liquor, meeting GNR use requirements. Then, the rAAV(ss) GNR was prepared by diluting the mother liquor to a titer of 1 × 109 vg/mL and stored at temperatures below −60 °C.

In rAAV genome titer detection by dPCR, two main pretreatment methods are lysis (high-temp or proteinase K) and direct dilution with in-situ lysis. This study compared three methods: proteinase K lysis, thermal lysis (95 °C), and direct dilution, all with deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) pretreatment. Using the Stilla Naica® System, six parallel tests showed the direct dilution method (9.92 × 108 vg/mL) gave significantly higher titers than proteinase K lysis (7.02 × 108 vg/mL) and thermal lysis (7.87 × 108 vg/mL) (Fig. 1 A and Table S2). The direct dilution method quantifies rAAV genome titers by diluting intact rAAV, which is added to the dPCR system and encapsulated into droplets. Capsid lysis during PCR pre-denaturation releases the genome for detection. It is preferred for in situ lysis avoiding inter-strand annealing, improved accuracy, simple operation, and strong anti-interference.

The results (Table S3) show that the direct dilution method maintained relative standard deviations (RSD) within 10% across different operators when testing GNR samples at various process stages (virus harvesting, deep filtrate, affinity/anionic chromatography eluates and drug substance), confirming its applicability to samples with diverse matrix components.

The method was validated using rAAV(ss) GNR with primers targeting inverted terminal repeat (ITR) and GOI. Specificity was confirmed as blank groups showed no signal and interference groups (human DNA, plasmids) had recovery rates >93%, indicating no interference from formulation buffer, host DNA, or plasmids (Fig. 1B). Precision (Figs. 1C and D) was demonstrated by RSDs for ITR/GOI ≤6% across two labs and four operators, with inter-laboratory relative percent difference (RPD) ≤ 6%, ensuring high reproducibility. Accuracy (Fig. 1E) was verified by mixing rAAV with empty capsids at 1:3 to 3:1 ratio, yielding recovery rates of 99.6%–107.6% for both primer sets. Linearity (Fig. 1F) was established through five-fold dilutions with R2 > 0.997 and linear ranges of 1.0 × 108–1.6 × 109 vg/mL (ITR) and 1.2 × 108–1.7 × 109 vg/mL (GOI). Robustness (Fig. 1G) was confirmed as shortening DNase I digestion to 0.5 h and storing samples at 2–8 °C/–20 °C for 48 h resulted in no titer changes, demonstrating method stability. Overall, the direct dilution dPCR method enables reliable operator/laboratory-independent genome titer quantification for GNR across production stages.

The established direct dilution dPCR method monitored rAAV genome titer stability with staggered testing (monthly for first 12 months, then bi-monthly). Genome titers were quantified via GOI/ITR primers at 16 time points (months 0–12, 14, 16, and 18), with only the month 11 GOI titer (8.8 × 108 vg/mL) approaching the GOI mean−2SD (8.9 × 108 vg/mL) and all other time points staying within ±2 standard deviation (SD) of the mean (Fig. 1H and Table S4), confirming robust stability of rAAV(ss) GNR.

Our testing facility distributed pre-prepared GNRs, protocols, and reagents to 11 Chinese gene therapy companies (Table S5) via cold chain (on dry ice). These stored rAAV(ss) GNR ≤ −60 °C, subpackaging 3 standards (S1–S3) into ≥3 tubes each (>20 μL/tube). For testing, one tube from each was randomly selected. Two operators per company performed calibrations per protocol: each pretreated 3 aliquots, assayed GNR titer with GOI/ITR primers, using triplicate chambers per sample (average as result). Criteria required: single-well confidence interval (95% CI) ≤ 15%, triplicate CV ≤ 10%, no template control (NTC) ≤ 10 copies/μL. Eleven companies and National Institutes for Food and Drug Control (NIFDC) used dPCR instruments from 6 manufactures.

Droplet dPCR: Bio-Rad, Stilla, Sniper, TargetingOne; microchamber dPCR: Qiagen, and Thermo Fisher, generating 14 datasets (18 results each: 2 operators × 3 tests × 3 wells). Platforms showed consistent results (e.g., ITR: 8.1 × 108–9.4 × 108 vg/mL), with similar GOI/ITR outcomes (Tables S6 and S7; Figs. 1I, 1J, and S5). This confirms direct dilution method is applicable across platforms. GNR final titer 95% CI of mean: 8.6 × 108–8.8 × 108 vg/mL (ITR primers, RSD 10.2%) and 8.6 × 108–8.9 × 108 vg/mL (GOI primers, RSD 10.6%), no outliers (Table S8 and Figs. 1K–M).

In this study, we successfully generated a high-integrity, high full/empty capsid ratio single-stranded rAAV sample, which was subsequently utilized as a dPCR genome normalization reference. Its precise calibration was achieved through multi-center joint calibration. Furthermore, we established a comprehensive genome titer detection method applicable across the entire product lifecycle, from process development to final release. Detection results demonstrated excellent consistency between ITR and GOI quantification. This versatile method employs either GOI-specific primers or universal AAV2 ITR primers, enabling serotype-independent quantification of rAAV products containing AAV2 ITR elements [5] (Table S9). Potential applications of this rAAV(ss) GNR include validation of analytical methods, standardization of sample stability monitoring, and calibration of assay results (Table S10). Collectively, this GNR serves as a critical tool for ensuring accurate quantification of rAAV gene therapy products and enhancing overall quality control standards.
