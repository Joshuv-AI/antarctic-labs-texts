Cytokine release syndrome complicated with sepsis induced by tislelizumab in a patient with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma: A case report
Authors: Hongliang Chu, Xuchen Zhang, Jinjin Li, Bingsen Zhou, Junyan Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this study, we report a 69-year-old female patient with stage IIIB hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) who developed sepsis concomitant with cytokine release syndrome (CRS) following treatment with tislelizumab (an anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody) and subsequently demonstrated self-healing. After receiving tislelizumab-based immunotherapy, the patient presented with severe clinical manifestations, including fever, hypotension, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS); however, after multidisciplinary evaluation regarding the potential risks of immunosuppression, immunosuppressive agents such as tocilizumab or corticosteroids were not applied, and the patient still experienced a good recovery through routine anti-infective therapy and aggressive supportive care. This case highlights the diagnostic complexity in distinguishing CRS from sepsis during anti-tumor immunotherapy and demonstrates that immunomodulatory therapy may not be mandatory in all patients. Collectively, the favorable outcomes achieved without tocilizumab or corticosteroids suggest that selected patients can recover through meticulous clinical monitoring and targeted interventions alone, thus highlighting the clinical importance of selecting patients who can be spared from immunosuppressive therapy.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) antibodies, have become essential components in the treatment of advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).1,2 The therapeutic landscape for liver tumors has evolved significantly with the introduction of novel systemic treatments, including combination therapies involving ICIs and targeted agents, which have improved survival outcomes for patients with advanced disease.3,4 However, these therapies are associated with increased risks of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), notably including cytokine release syndrome (CRS), which is a systemic inflammatory response that can lead to life-threatening complications such as hypotension and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).5,6 The overlapping clinical features of CRS and sepsis have posed significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly in patients receiving ICIs who are also at risk of infections due to underlying malignancy or immunosuppressive therapies.7,8 Current guidelines recommend immunosuppressive agents, such as tocilizumab (an anti-IL-6 receptor antibody) or corticosteroids, for the management of CRS.9 However, the clinical benefits of the applications of these agents in patients with concurrent sepsis remain controversial, as immunosuppression may exacerbate infection and worsen prognosis in patients.10,11

In a phase 3 randomized clinical trial of patients with unresectable HCC, tislelizumab (as a first-line treatment) demonstrated a higher objective response rate (ORR), a prolonged median progression-free survival (PFS) and a more favorable safety profile compared with sorafenib.12 Based on these findings, tislelizumab was approved as a first-line treatment option for unresectable or metastatic HCC in China in 2024. Common adverse reactions of this treatment include increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels (23.1%) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels (16.6%), as well as increased blood bilirubin levels (12.4%), pruritus (10.4%) and rash (10.1%).12 However, reports of tislelizumab-induced CRS remain scarce in the literature, with only a few cases having been reported.13,14 Here, we present the case of a 69-year-old female with advanced HCC who developed concurrent sepsis and CRS following treatment with the anti-PD-1 antibody tislelizumab and who demonstrated good recovery following routine anti-infection therapy. This case highlights the diagnostic dilemma of distinguishing CRS from sepsis in ICI-treated patients and demonstrates that immunomodulatory therapy may not be mandatory in all patients (even those with severe clinical manifestations). This patient achieved smooth full recovery through anti-infective therapy and aggressive supportive care alone (without the use of tocilizumab or corticosteroids). This report contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting individualized management strategies for CRS in the era of immunotherapy.

Case report

A 69-year-old female presented with a 3-month history of abdominal distension and nausea, which had significantly worsened over the 5 days prior to her first visit in November 2023. Both contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CE-CT) and contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (CE-MRI) of the abdomen revealed advanced cirrhosis, splenomegaly and a neoplastic lesion in the right hepatic lobe with tumor thrombus formation in the right portal vein branch, in addition to periportal collateral circulation and surrounding exudation. The laboratory results were as follows: alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) concentration >2000.00 ng/mL, ALT concentration of 35 U/L, AST concentration of 233 U/L, direct bilirubin (DBIL) concentration of 9.4 μmol/L, indirect bilirubin (IBIL) concentration of 14.9 μmol/L, hepatitis B virus surface antigen (HBsAg) concentration of 1537.65 IU/mL, hepatitis B virus core antibody (HBcAb) concentration of 8.72 s/co, and hepatitis B virus deoxyribonucleic acid (HBV-DNA) concentration of 3.328E5 IU/ml. Even though laboratory testing results indicated HBsAg positivity and HBcAb activity, she never received related anti-HBV antiviral therapy. In addition, she had a history of hypertension and mild coronary artery disease (CAD) for more than 3 years, and she regularly orally received valsartan capsules; moreover, no specific treatment was needed for her clinically stable CAD. The patient denied a history of diabetes, neurological disorders, kidney disease, tuberculosis, or other infectious diseases.

The patient was subsequently diagnosed with stage IIIB (cT3bN0M0) HCC with a Child-Pugh class A classification and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance score of 1. Following a comprehensive evaluation and multidisciplinary treatment (MDT) discussion, a combined antiviral and antineoplastic treatment strategy was established. To control the replication of HBV, amlivatamab was added to the antiviral therapy. To achieve tumor downstaging and create opportunities for surgical resection, we implemented a conversion therapy regimen combining transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) with lenvatinib (a multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitor) and tislelizumab (an anti-PD-1 ICI). The patient received TACE on November 18, 2023, with subsequent administration of lenvatinib (8 mg orally daily) and tislelizumab (200 mg intravenous infusion every 21 days). During the first 8 treatment cycles, the patient tolerated the therapy well without any severe adverse reactions. On July 13, 2024, the patient received a ninth cycle of tislelizumab immunotherapy. On the following day (July 14, 2024), vital signs (including body temperature, blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation) remained within normal ranges. Posttreatment routine laboratory evaluation revealed elevated liver function parameters, including an AST concentration of 2013 U/L, a DBIL concentration of 32.1 μmol/L, and an IBIL concentration of 43.5 μmol/L (Table 1). As the patient remained completely asymptomatic, we initiated hepatoprotective therapy with close clinical monitoring. On July 15, the patient experienced low-grade fever (37.2–37.8 °C) with worsening abdominal pain and distension, accompanied by elevated levels of the following inflammatory markers: a procalcitonin (PCT) concentration of 1.37 ng/mL, a C-reactive protein (CRP) concentration of 107 mg/L, and a neutrophil count of 8.6 × 109/L. Cefoperazone-sulbactam (Sulperazon) was initiated for antibiotic therapy after blood culture collection. On July 16, the patient developed drowsiness, with an elevated blood ammonia concentration of 78.5 μmol/L. Given the potential association between hyperammonemia and neuropsychiatric symptoms, L-ornithine L-aspartate was administered as an ammonia-lowering therapy. However, by July 17, the patient had progressed to a coma, despite demonstrating normalized ammonia levels (70.1 μmol/L). Concurrently, the oxygenation index decreased, which required supplemental oxygen via a nasal cannula at 3 L/min to maintain peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) above 93%. Infection markers were elevated (PCT at 8.4 ng/mL, CRP at 177.2 mg/L, and neutrophil count at 8.24 × 109/L), and blood cultures were positive for Escherichia coli, thus prompting antibiotic escalation to meropenem. Notably, when considering the high interleukin-6 (IL-6) concentration of >1200 pg/mL, a concern regarding sepsis with concomitant CRS was elicited. After the MDT evaluation, we decided to continue anti-infective therapy and aggressive supportive care without the use of tocilizumab or corticosteroids under close monitoring. This decision was based on several key considerations: first, the persistent elevation of infection markers (PCT, neutrophils) suggested sepsis was the primary driver of clinical deterioration; second, the patient’s underlying cirrhosis increased the risk of infection exacerbation with immunosuppression; and third, we observed that some biochemical markers of CRS (IL-6, CRP, AST, bilirubin) were already improving, suggesting possible self-limitation of the CRS component. Fortunately, the patient’s consciousness status gradually recovered and had fully recovered by July 19. Afterward, on July 24, meropenem therapy was discontinued following confirmation of infection control, as evidenced by the resolution of clinical symptoms and significant reductions in PCT, CRP, and the neutrophil count. By day 14 (August 2), all of the laboratory parameters had become normalized; moreover, after several days, the patient was discharged in stable condition (Figure 1). Discussion

In this study, we report a rare case of tislelizumab-induced CRS with concurrent sepsis. From our clinical perspective, this case presented a significant diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma that required careful balancing of competing risks. The decision to withhold immunosuppressive therapy was not taken lightly and was based on continuous reassessment of the relative contributions of infection versus CRS to the patient’s clinical status. The patient presented with altered mental status, hypotension, and multiorgan dysfunction, along with demonstrating a low-grade fever (37.2–38.1 °C). The initial diagnosis proved to be challenging due to the atypical normothermic CRS presentation, which overlapped with clinical features with sepsis, as well as the limited experience in managing ICI-related CRS, thereby leading to our initial attribution of the manifestations being related to sepsis. Key diagnostic clues emerged on July 17 (including an IL-6 concentration >1200 pg/mL), and a retrospective review of the data from July 14 to 17 was performed (Table 1). Neither condition alone explained all of the findings. On July 17, despite hypotensive episodes being noted, the MAP fluctuated to a level of approximately 65 mmHg (range: 64–67 mmHg) without necessitating vasopressor support. Based on SOFA and ASTCT grading9,15 (Table 2), we prioritized sepsis control with fluid resuscitation and meropenem, thereby deferring CRS-specific therapy. This approach proved to be successful, with rapid clinical improvement after increased antibiotic use being noted. The progression of laboratory markers provided crucial insights for clinical decision-making. As illustrated in Figure 2, the differential trajectories of various biomarkers helped distinguish the predominant pathophysiology. While infection markers (PCT, neutrophils) continued to rise until July 17, parameters more specific to CRS and liver injury (AST, bilirubin) had already begun declining from their peak values. The IL-6 level >1200 pg/mL was markedly elevated beyond typical sepsis ranges, confirming CRS contribution. However, the improving trend in liver enzymes suggested the CRS component might be self-limiting, supporting our decision to focus on infection control. ICI-induced irAEs exhibit a relatively low incidence of CRS, with clinical manifestations typically being milder than those observed in patients with CAR-T-cell therapy-associated CRS.16 Current evidence indicates that mild CRS patients primarily present with fever, whereas severe patients may develop systemic manifestations, including hypotension and multiorgan dysfunction.9 In the present case, the patient developed clinical symptoms (including low-grade fever, hypotension, hypoxemia, and altered mental status) under the combined effects of sepsis and CRS. Notably, the particularity of this case is demonstrated by the patient’s underlying cirrhosis, which is a factor that may significantly alter the clinical manifestations of CRS. More than 90% of liver cancer patients develop this disease in the context of cirrhosis.17 The broader interplay between cancer risk, chronic inflammation, and underlying conditions such as metabolic syndrome further underscores the complex pathophysiological background in which these clinical scenarios unfold.18 Cirrhosis represents an acquired immunocompromised status that is characterized by an increased susceptibility to infections.19 This increased susceptibility to infections stems from multiple pathophysiological mechanisms, including immune dysfunction, impaired detoxification capacity due to hepatic failure, and gut microbiota dysbiosis.19–21 This nonspecific presentation makes the differentiation between CRS and sepsis particularly challenging. Although CRS was considered as a diagnosis in this patient, the absence of a characteristic high fever may be attributed to advanced age, impaired inflammatory mediator metabolism due to hepatic failure, and a blunted thermoregulatory response to pyrogens.22,23 Such clinical presentation variability is not uncommon in end-stage liver disease patients and warrants increased clinical vigilance. This paradoxical phenomenon can be explained by the unique pathophysiological alterations associated with cirrhosis. Notably, upon the diagnosis of concurrent CRS on day 17, the patient’s CRP, AST, and bilirubin levels had already begun to decrease, whereas infection-related markers such as the neutrophil count and PCT level continued to increase. The dynamic changes in these biochemical markers are presented in Figure 2. This dynamic profile suggested that clinical deterioration was predominantly driven by uncontrolled septic infection rather than CRS. The observed 2nd-grade CRS may have undergone spontaneous resolution because of self-limiting characteristics, thereby playing a limited role in subsequent clinical worsening of the condition. Additionally, in sepsis, IL-6 levels markedly increase but typically do not exceed 200 pg/mL,24,25 whereas patients with liver cirrhosis also often exhibit elevated IL-6 levels.26,27 Therefore, an IL-6 concentration >1,200 pg/mL in a patient suggests contributions from both CRS and sepsis, thereby potentially explaining why elevated IL-6 levels did not precipitate severe CRS (manifesting only as grade 2). Given the abovementioned findings, our management strategy prioritized aggressive antimicrobial therapy and organ support, with close monitoring being utilized instead of targeted intervention for CRS. This approach was justified by three key factors, including the persistent increase in infection markers (such as neutrophils and PCT), which indicated that sepsis was the primary contributor to clinical deterioration. Moreover, preexisting liver disease likely modified the typical CRS-associated cytokine response.

These observations compel a deeper analysis of the interplay between immune dysregulation and infection in this unique patient context. The pathophysiological distinctions between CRS and sepsis in cirrhotic patients deserve special emphasis. Cirrhosis creates a unique immunological milieu characterized by simultaneous systemic inflammation and immunodeficiency.28 This “immunological paradox” profoundly affects both CRS and sepsis manifestations. In CRS, cirrhosis may blunt the characteristic cytokine storm presentation due to impaired Kupffer cell function and reduced cytokine production capacity. Conversely, in sepsis, the baseline immune dysfunction of cirrhosis amplifies infection susceptibility while paradoxically attenuating some inflammatory responses. This complex interplay explains why our patient presented with modest fever despite severe systemic involvement. The observed 2nd-grade CRS may have undergone spontaneous resolution because of self-limiting characteristics, thereby playing a limited role in subsequent clinical worsening of the condition. Building upon this pathophysiological context, the relationship between CRS, sepsis, and immune modulation in our specific case warrants deeper analysis. While CRS following ICI therapy remains uncommon, cases have been reported with various agents, including pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and the combination of ipilimumab plus nivolumab.29–31 These cases often present with high fever and hypotension, a pattern distinct from our tislelizumab-treated patient who presented with low-grade fever in the context of underlying cirrhosis and concurrent sepsis. Given the high risk of infection exacerbation in the cirrhotic patient, our decision to withhold tocilizumab and corticosteroids was ultimately driven by the following clinical evidence: confirmed E. coli bacteremia, persistently rising PCT levels indicating ongoing active infection, and a self-limiting trend in CRS-related parameters. This cautious approach finds indirect support in a recent study, which reported that the majority of patients with mild grade 1–2 CRS following ICI therapy did not require tocilizumab and exhibited a short fever duration (median 3 days), suggesting a self-limiting course in selected cases.32 However, that study did not specifically address the management of CRS with concurrent sepsis, a scenario present in our case which inherently carries a greater risk. Therefore, in our patient with confirmed concurrent sepsis, the rationale for avoiding immunosuppression was even more compelling. The rapid clinical improvement following targeted antibiotic escalation alone, without immunosuppressive therapy, demonstrates that in carefully selected patients with concurrent CRS and sepsis, prioritizing and achieving infection control can allow the innate immune system to resolve mild-to-moderate CRS autonomously.

This successful treatment highlights the critical importance of accurate differentiation, as sepsis and CRS exhibit substantial overlap in both clinical manifestations and laboratory parameters (Table 3), thus posing significant diagnostic challenges. However, the differentiation between these two conditions is critical because of their fundamentally distinct therapeutic principles; specifically, sepsis necessitates immune enhancement to combat infection, whereas CRS requires immunosuppression to mitigate the cytokine storm. Although glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants can improve the clinical outcomes of CRS, they may also increase the risk of life-threatening secondary infections caused by immunosuppression.40,41 This risk is particularly concerning in cases of concurrent sepsis and CRS, where indiscriminate immunosuppressive therapy without precise evaluation may paradoxically worsen prognosis. Cancer patients represent a high-risk population for sepsis, with studies indicating approximately tenfold greater susceptibility compared to the general population.42 During CRS, the massive release of cytokines induces an “immunoparalysis” status that further compromises host antimicrobial defenses.43 This dual-pathology markedly increases infection risk in CRS patients, thereby resulting in the commonly observed co-occurrence of CRS and sepsis in immunotherapy settings, including CAR-T-cell and ICI treatments.7,8 Therefore, the co-occurrence of sepsis and CRS is relatively common following anti-tumor immunotherapy; consequently, in current clinical practice, a dual-strategy combining antimicrobial therapy with immunosuppression/glucocorticoids is often adopted. Notably, current sepsis guidelines recommend the cautious use of corticosteroids,10 and tocilizumab is currently contraindicated in patients with uncontrolled severe infections.11 For patients with suspected sepsis-CRS overlap, we recommend a comprehensive diagnostic workup including cytokine profiling and PCT, as well as quantitative assessments of relative contributions from infection versus Based on the outcomes of this study, we determined several conclusions. First, clinicians should fully understand the fundamental distinctions between CRS and sepsis in terms of pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and therapeutic principles. Second, a comprehensive stratified evaluation system should be established for complex cases presenting with both CRS and sepsis to accurately identify the predominant pathological process. Third, treatment strategies should be developed based on precise assessments and tailored to individual patients, with a particular emphasis on preventing severe secondary infections following CRS control. These findings provide important insights into the clinical management of immunotherapy-related complications and highlight the critical role of personalized treatment strategies in clinical practice. Further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to develop more accurate risk prediction models and evidence-based treatment guidelines.
