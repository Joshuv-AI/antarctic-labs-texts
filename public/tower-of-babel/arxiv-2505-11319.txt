Time-dependent Hole States in Multiconfigurational Time-Dependent Hartree-Fock Approaches: Applications in Photoionization of Water Molecule Preprint: APS/123-QED
Zhao-Han Zhang 1, Yang Li 1, Himadri Pathak 3,4, Takeshi Sato 5,6,7, Kenichi L. Ishikawa 5,6,7,8, Feng He 1,2

Abstract
By simulating the real-time multielectron wavefunction with the multi-configurational time-dependent Hartree-Fock (MCTDHF) approach, we conduct an ab initio study of the single-photon ionization process of a body-fixed water molecule ( H 2 ​ O \mathrm{H_{2}O} ) driven by attosecond pulses. To this end, we present a full-dimensional implementation of the MCTDHF method based on one-center expansions, allowing for the simulation of arbitrarily polarized lasers and multi-center polyatomic potentials. With a rigorous definition of the time-dependent hole state (TDHS) using the time-domain generalization of extended Koopmans’ theorem (TD-EKT), we derive the reduced ion density matrix within the MCTDHF framework, which inherently encodes the total and channel-resolved photoionization cross sections of H 2 ​ O \mathrm{H_{2}O} . The cross sections obtained are benchmarked against existing experimental and theoretical results, validating the TDHS formalism. Furthermore, by adjusting the phase delay and intensity ratio of a pair of orthogonally polarized attosecond pulses, we explore the ultrafast control of attosecond coherence between electronic states of H 2 ​ O + \mathrm{H_{2}O^{+}} .



I
Introduction

The wavefunction-based multi-configurational time-dependent Hartree-Fock (MCTDHF) theory
Lode et al. 2020
;
Ishikawa and Sato 2015
has emerged as a general and effective framework for solving the multi-electron time-dependent Schrödinger equation (ME-TDSE). As an
ab initio
theory, it enriches the theoretical toolbox for the study of ultrafast electron dynamics in real-world systems, particularly in laser-driven many-electron atoms and molecules. The original formulation of the MCTDHF method represents the correlated time-dependent multi-electron wavefunction with a multi-orbital formalism, where both single-particle orbitals and configuration interaction coefficients evolve dynamically over time
Zanghellini et al. 2003
;
Kato and Kono 2004
;
Caillat et al. 2005
. The approach achieves a balance between accuracy and computational efficiency by efficiently exploring a necessary subset of the multi-electron Hilbert space, thereby capturing the essential physics of a broad range of laser-driven processes.

Despite a wealth of successful applications in various contexts

Caillat et al. 2005
;
Kato and Kono 2008
;
Jordan and Scrinzi 2008
;
Sukiasyan et al. 2009
;
Haxton et al. 2011
;
Haxton et al. 2012
;
Hochstuhl et al. 2014
;
Sawada et al. 2016
,
the MCTDHF method adopts an all-electron complete active space (CAS) scheme, in which the number of electronic configurations grows exponentially with the number of electrons. For this reason, even though the method formally converges to the
exact
solution of ME-TDSE, achieving full convergence is often computationally prohibitive. To address this challenge, generalized MCTDHF methods have been proposed, incorporating restricted active space (RAS) strategies. These advancements have led to the broader time-dependent multi-configurational self-consistent-field (TD-MCSCF) framework, which incorporates various approaches with a unified structure of equations of motion (EOMs). Notable examples include the time-dependent complete-active-space self-consistent-field (TD-CASSCF) method
Sato and Ishikawa 2013
, the time-dependent restricted-active-space self-consistent-field (TD-RASSCF) method
Miyagi and Madsen 2013
;
Miyagi and Madsen 2014
, and the time-dependent occupation-restricted multiple-active-space (TD-ORMAS) method
Sato and Ishikawa 2015
. By elaborating the configuration spaces, these methods have significantly improved computational efficiency
Sato et al. 2016
;
Omiste and Madsen 2018
, making the calculations practical for large systems with a limited number of active electrons. They have been successfully applied to a variety of problems, including few-photon ionization
Li et al. 2016
;
Omiste et al. 2017
;
Omiste and Madsen 2019
, strong-field ionization, and high harmonic generation
Tikhomirov et al. 2017
;
Orimo et al. 2019a
;
Orimo et al. 2019b
;
Li et al. 2024
.

The rapid advancement of laser technology has enabled the generation of ultrashort extreme ultraviolet (XUV) pulses on the attosecond timescale
Sansone et al. 2006
;
Chini et al. 2014
;
Gaumnitz et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2024
. When a neutral target is ionized by attosecond XUV pulses, the residual ion can occupy multiple symmetry-allowed states, leading to a multichannel photoionization process where quantum entanglement and coherence naturally arise in photofragments
Vrakking 2021
;
Koll et al. 2022
;
Ishikawa et al. 2023
;
Ruberti et al. 2024
;
Laurell et al. 2025
. Usually, the internal state of the photoion is difficult to measure directly as it often decays away before reaching the detector, whereas the emitted photoelectron is able to carry information about the ionization process to infinity, fulfilling the basis of photoelectron spectroscopy. However, for attosecond pulses, when the energy separation between different ionic states is small, it becomes challenging to determine which state the photoelectron is entangled with, making state-resolved analysis crucial for both experimental and theoretical investigations. As an important indicator to study the dynamics of ionization, photoelectron momentum distributions (PMD) are frequently calculated in TD-MCSCF simulations
Haxton et al. 2012
;
Omiste et al. 2017
;
Orimo et al. 2019a
;
Orimo et al. 2019b
;
Omiste and Madsen 2019
, where the total PMD is directly available from the expectation value of the electron density operator in momentum space. However, unlike theoretical frameworks that explicitly expand the wavefunction in terms of predefined ion states
Clarke et al. 2018
, or experimental setups that enable coincidence measurements
Dörner et al. 2000
, there is no general framework within the TD-MCSCF method for extracting ion-state-resolved observables, such as ion-state-resolved PMDs. This limitation significantly restricts the applicability of the MC-TDSCF method to address the challenges of attosecond photoionization dynamics.

In the accompanying paper
Let
, we systematically overcome this difficulty by generalizing the extended Koopmans’ theorem to the time domain, where a definition of the time-dependent hole state (TDHS) naturally arises. As a general formalism, it is suitable for any TD-MCSCF methods, including TD-CASSCF, TD-RASSCF, TD-ORMAS, etc. By combining precise TDHS with projection approaches
Madsen et al. 2007
;
Argenti et al. 2013
or the time-dependent surface flux (t-SURFF) method
Tao and Scrinzi 2012
;
Scrinzi 2012
, we can obtain hole-state-resolved PMDs. In this work, we present a detailed description of the TDHS formalism. As a demonstration, we apply this method to the single ionization process of a body-fixed water molecule to validate this approach.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we briefly review the formal theory of TD-MCSCF and derive the EOM of TDHS. Based on this, we systematically develop approaches to extract channel-resolved observables, with an emphasis on the channel-resolved momentum distribution and the reduced density matrix of photoion.
In Sec.
III
, we present the spin-adapted numerical implementation of the non-relativistic MCTDHF theory, incorporating a gauge-invariant frozen-core extension. Our implementation employs a one-center expansion of the single-particle orbitals using radial B-spline functions and spherical harmonics, enabling the treatment of arbitrarily polarized laser pulses and polyatomic molecular potentials. To enhance computational efficiency, we introduce an angular discrete variable representation for the evaluation of the two-electron Coulomb term.
In Sec.
IV
, we apply our theory and numerical implementation to extract both the total and partial photoionization cross sections of a body-fixed water molecule. Good agreement is achieved with existing theoretical and experimental values, validating the accuracy of our approach. Furthermore, by analyzing the reduced ion density matrix, the control of the quantum coherence of the water molecular ion with a pair of orthogonally polarized attosecond pulses is also investigated.

II
Theory of Time-dependent Hole States

II.1
EOM of TD-MCSCF

The TD-MCSCF approach is devoted to the numerical solution of the ME-TDSE

i|\dot{\Psi}(t)\rangle=\hat{H}(t)|\Psi(t)\rangle,

(1)

where the time-dependent Hamiltonian has a general second-quantized form

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}(t)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}(t)\hat{a}_{\nu}(t)

(2)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\nu\lambda\tau}g_{\mu\nu\lambda\tau}(t)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}(t)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\nu}(t)\hat{a}_{\lambda}(t)\hat{a}_{\tau}(t).

The time-dependent operators
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}(t)
and
\hat{a}_{\mu}(t)
create and annihilate an electron in the time-dependent single-electron state
\phi_{\mu}(t)
, respectively. We use Greek letters
(\mu,\nu,\lambda,\tau,\cdots)
for the indices of single-electron states. The one-body integral
h_{\mu\nu}(t)
incorporates the kinetic terms, the laser-electron interaction, and the nuclei-electron Coulomb interaction. The two-body integral
g_{\mu\nu\lambda\tau}(t)
accounts for the electron-electron interactions.

In the TD-MCSCF method, the ME-TDSE is solved approximately using the multi-configurational wavefunction
ansatz

\displaystyle|\Psi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}C_{I}(t)|I(t)\rangle,

(3a)

\displaystyle|I(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\prod_{\mu}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}(t))^{I_{\mu}}|0\rangle.

(3b)

Here, the integer array
I
specifies the occupation numbers of the single-electron states in the time-dependent electron configuration
|I(t)\rangle
. The size of
I
is equal to the number of electrons
N
and
I_{\mu}\in\{0,1\}
. A practical calculation only incorporates a finite number of
\phi_{\mu}
,
\mu\in\mathcal{O}
. The single-electron Hilbert space is thus separated into two orthogonal parts, the occupied and unoccupied spaces, spanned by
\{\phi_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathcal{O}}
and
\{\phi_{\mu}\}_{\mu\notin\mathcal{O}}
, respectively. Those
\mu\notin\mathcal{O}
will be excluded from the summation in Eq. (
3
).

Under the orthonormal condition
\langle\phi_{\mu}|\phi_{\nu}\rangle=\delta_{\mu\nu}
, the EOMs for the expansion coefficients
C_{I}
and orbitals
\phi_{\mu}
are derived from the time-dependent variational principle (TDVP)
Dirac 1930
;
Frenkel et al. 1934
;
McLachlan 1964
, yielding the following coupled nonlinear differential equations (for simplicity, we omit the explicit time dependence in the notation in the following)

\displaystyle i\dot{C}_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{J}\langle I|\hat{H}-\hat{R}|J\rangle C_{J},

(4a)

\displaystyle i|\dot{\phi}_{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{R}|\phi_{\mu}\rangle+\hat{Q}\hat{F}|\phi_{\mu}\rangle.

(4b)

Here,
\hat{Q}
is the projection operator onto the unoccupied space.
\hat{R}
is an auxiliary operator to remove the redundant degree of freedom in the
ansatz
, whose matrix elements,
R_{\mu\nu}\equiv\langle\phi_{\mu}|\hat{R}|\phi_{\nu}\rangle\equiv i\langle\phi_{\mu}|\dot{\phi}_{\nu}\rangle
with
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{O}
, need to be designated in advance. The redundancy arises from the arbitrariness of distributing time-dependent global phases between orbitals and CI coefficients without altering physics
Meyer et al. 1990
;
Caillat et al. 2005
. In the standard all-electron MCTDHF theory
Zanghellini et al. 2003
;
Kato and Kono 2004
;
Caillat et al. 2005
,
R_{\mu\nu}
can be an arbitrary time-dependent Hermitian matrix, while for the various RAS extensions of MCTDHF, such as TD-CASSCF
Sato and Ishikawa 2013
, TD-RASSCF
Miyagi and Madsen 2013
and TD-ORMAS
Sato and Ishikawa 2015
,
R_{\mu\nu}
should be determined by special procedures. The generalized Fock operator
\hat{F}
is defined as

\hat{F}|\phi_{\mu}\rangle\equiv\bigg(\hat{h}|\phi_{\mu}\rangle+\sum_{\nu\lambda\tau\in\mathcal{O}}\overline{\rho}_{\mu\nu\lambda\tau}\hat{g}_{\nu\lambda}|\phi_{\tau}\rangle\bigg),

(5)

with matrix elements
F_{\mu\nu}=\langle\phi_{\mu}|\hat{F}|\phi_{\nu}\rangle

Sato and Ishikawa 2013
.
Here,
\hat{h}
is the one-electron Hamiltonian, and
\hat{g}_{\nu\lambda}
characterizes the Coulomb potential excited by the electrons. Formally, we have

\displaystyle\hat{h}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\hat{a}_{\mu},

(6a)

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\tau}g_{\mu\nu\lambda\tau}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\hat{a}_{\tau},

(6b)

and

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{\lambda\mu\nu\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{O}}\rho^{-1}_{\lambda\xi}\rho_{\chi\mu\nu\tau},

(7a)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\hat{a}_{\nu}|\Psi\rangle,

(7b)

\displaystyle\rho_{\lambda\mu\nu\tau}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\nu}\hat{a}_{\lambda}\hat{a}_{\tau}|\Psi\rangle.

(7c)

The last two equations correspond to the one-body reduced density matrix (1RDM) and the two-body reduced density matrix (2RDM), respectively.

By solving Eq. (
4
), one obtains a multielectron wavefunction as an approximate solution of the ME-TDSE. The convergence of the method is controlled by the size of the occupied space. At the full configuration interaction (FCI) limit, the
ansatz
is
complete
, and
\Psi
converges to the
exact
solution of ME-TDSE. When the number of orbitals equals the number of electrons, the
ansatz
reduces to the time-dependent Hartree-Fock (TDHF) method.

II.2
EOM of TDHS

For the consistency with TD-MCSCF theory, we construct the
(N-1)-
electron state by a linear combination of the single-vacancy states
|\Psi_{\mu}\rangle\equiv\hat{a}_{\mu}|\Psi\rangle
,
\mu\in\mathcal{O}
:

|\gamma\rangle=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{O}}|\Psi_{\mu}\rangle Z_{\mu\gamma},

(8)

where
Z_{\mu\gamma}
is a matrix of undetermined coefficients. The state
|\gamma\rangle
is a one-hole state, which we call the TDHS. We assume that
|\gamma\rangle
satisfies the projected ME-TDSE

i\hat{\varPi}|\dot{\gamma}\rangle=\hat{\varPi}\hat{H}|\gamma\rangle,

(9)

where
\hat{\varPi}
is the projection operator of the
(N-1)-
electron Hilbert space spanned by all single-vacancy states. This yields the following set of coupled differential equations for each
\mu\in\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{O}}i\big(\langle\Psi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\hat{a}_{\nu}|\dot{\Psi}\rangle+\langle\Psi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\dot{\hat{a}}_{\nu}|\Psi\rangle\big)Z_{\nu\gamma}+i\rho_{\mu\nu}\dot{Z}_{\nu\gamma}

(10a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{O}}\big(\langle\Psi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\hat{a}_{\nu}\hat{H}|\Psi\rangle+\langle\Psi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}[\hat{H},\hat{a}_{\nu}]|\Psi\rangle\big)Z_{\nu\gamma}.

(10b)

Using Eqs. (
82a
) and (
82b
) (see Appendix
A
), Eq. (
10
) is eventually simplified to

-\sum_{\nu\tau\in\mathcal{O}}(F_{\nu\tau}-R_{\nu\tau})\rho_{\mu\tau}Z_{\nu\gamma}=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{O}}i\rho_{\mu\nu}\dot{Z}_{\nu\gamma},

(11)

which is the EOM of TDHS. By introducing the
\rho
-weighted matrix

\tilde{F}_{\nu\mu}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{O}}(F_{\nu\tau}-R_{\nu\tau})\rho_{\mu\tau},

(12)

the EOM of TDHS has a simpler form of
i\rho\dot{Z}=-\tilde{F}^{T}Z
.

It is beneficial to show that the stationary version of Eq. (
11
) reproduces the extended Koopmans’ theorem.
If we impose the stationary condition
i\dot{Z}=Z\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}
,
i|\dot{\gamma}\rangle=|\gamma\rangle E_{\gamma}
, and
i|\dot{\Psi}\rangle=|\Psi\rangle E_{0}
to Eq. (
10
), it follows that
\rho Z(E_{\gamma}-E_{0}-\mathcal{E})=0
. Therefore,
\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}=E_{\gamma}-E_{0}
is the energy difference between
|\Psi\rangle
and
|\gamma\rangle
, known as the ionization energy. By imposing the same stationary condition to Eq. (
11
), it follows the extended Koopmans’ theorem (EKT)
Koopmans 1934
;
Day et al. 1974
;
Smith and Day 1975
;
Morrell et al. 1975
:

-\tilde{F}^{T}Z=\rho Z\mathcal{E}.

(13)

This explains why the ionization energy in TD-MCSCF should be described by EKT, as observed in Ref.
Hochstuhl et al. 2014
.
We note that
\tilde{F}
is Hermitian if and only if
\dot{\rho}=0
, as evident from the EOM of 1RDM:

\displaystyle i\dot{\rho}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi|[\hat{a}_{\mu}^{\dagger}a_{\nu},\hat{H}]|\Psi\rangle+\langle\Psi|i\dot{\hat{a}}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\nu}+i\hat{a}_{\mu}^{\dagger}\dot{\hat{a}}_{\nu}|\Psi\rangle

(14)

\displaystyle=(\tilde{F}^{T}-\tilde{F}^{*})_{\mu\nu}.

We then prove the orthonormality of TDHS.
As
Z(0)
is set as the
\rho
-orthonormal eigenvectors of Eq. (
13
),
Z^{\dagger}\rho Z=I
is automatically satisfied at
t=0
. Due to Eq. (
14
), we have

i\frac{d}{dt}(Z^{\dagger}\rho Z)=Z^{\dagger}(-\tilde{F}^{T}+\tilde{F}^{*}+i\dot{\rho})Z=0,

(15)

indicating that the orthonormality is held at any time
t
.

II.3
Total Photoelectron Momentum Distribution

Following photoionization, the multi-electron system is torn apart and fragments will move to infinity. The PMD carries abundant information about the dynamics near the interaction region.
Various theoretical approaches have been raised for extracting it, among which the most rigorous approach is directly projecting the final state onto the exact multi-electron scattering state immediately after the end of the laser
Madsen et al. 2007
;
Argenti et al. 2013
.
However, acquisition of the exact scattering states is often more complicated than the propagation itself.
As a practical alternative, it is possible to compute PMD by projecting the departing wavepackets onto plane waves or outgoing Coulomb waves after a sufficiently long time (
t_{f}
) until the photoelectron has reached the asymptotic region
(r>r_{0})
.

We will denote the combined spin and momentum coordinate by
\bm{\kappa}=(\bm{k}\sigma)
, where
\bm{k}
is the asymptotic kinetic momentum of the electron and
\sigma
is its spin polarization species. The operator
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{\kappa}}
creates an electron in the asymptotic region with momentum
\bm{k}
and spin polarization
\sigma
. In practice, the state
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{\kappa}}|0\rangle=|\bm{\kappa}\rangle
is often realized by a masked plane wave state
|\bm{\kappa}\rangle=\hat{\Theta}|\chi_{\bm{\kappa}}\rangle
, where

\hat{\Theta}=\sum_{\mu\nu}\Theta_{\mu\nu}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mu}\hat{a}_{\nu},\quad\Theta_{\mu\nu}=\langle\phi_{\mu}|\hat{\Theta}|\phi_{\nu}\rangle

(16)

is the density operator in the exterior region (usually defined by a unit step function for single-electron states) and
\chi_{\bm{\kappa}}
is a plane wave with spin polarization
\sigma
. The total PMD is defined as the differential probability density

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\bm{\kappa})

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{\kappa}}\hat{a}_{\bm{\kappa}}|\Psi\rangle

(17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\nu\in\mathcal{O}}\rho_{\mu\nu}\phi^{*}_{\mu}(\bm{\kappa})\phi_{\nu}(\bm{\kappa}),

where
\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\kappa})=\langle\bm{\kappa}|\phi_{\mu}\rangle
. We mention that while Eq. (
17
) theoretically includes both single and multiple ionization contributions, in practice, and particularly in this work, single ionization dominates, making it a good approximation for the single-ionization PMD.

II.4
Channel-Resolved Projection Approaches

The introduction of TDHS allows us to define channel-resolved PMD, where the state of the photoelectron and the photoion are resolved simultaneously.
We will discuss two choices of the ion state
|\gamma\rangle
for the projection approach, i.e., the TDHS and the field-free HS.
For a given reference ion state
|\gamma\rangle
, the projection
\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})=\langle\gamma|\hat{a}_{\bm{\kappa}}|\Psi\rangle
is always a linear combination of
\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\kappa})
, namely

\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{O}}D_{\gamma\mu}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\kappa}).

(18)

Hence, the channel-resolved PMD associated with state
|\gamma\rangle
is defined as
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})=|\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})|^{2}=|\langle\gamma|\hat{a}_{\bm{\kappa}}|\Psi\rangle|^{2}
. If
\gamma
is chosen as the TDHS defined by Eq. (
8
) and evolved according to Eq. (
11
), the coefficient matrix
D
is actually the inverse of the matrix
Z
:

D_{\gamma\mu}=\langle\gamma|\hat{a}_{\mu}|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{O}}(Z^{\dagger})_{\gamma\nu}\rho_{\nu\mu}=(Z^{-1})_{\gamma\mu}.

(19)

Hence, its EOM can also be derived,

i\dot{D}_{\gamma\mu}=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{O}}(F_{\mu\nu}-R_{\mu\nu})D_{\gamma\nu},

(20)

which is useful for extracting channel-resolved PMD.

Alternatively,
\gamma
can be chosen as the field-free HS given by Eq. (
13
). The corresponding projection is denoted by
\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{F}}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})
, and the coefficient matrix
D
should be replaced by
D^{\mathrm{F}}
, where

D^{\mathrm{F}}_{\gamma\mu}(t)=\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{O}}(Z^{\dagger}(0))_{\gamma\nu}\langle\Psi(0)|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\nu}(0)\hat{a}_{\mu}(t)|\Psi(t)\rangle.\\

(21)

Combining Eqs. (
8
)(
19
)(
21
), the self-correlation functions of TDHS can be expressed by
D^{\mathrm{F}}
and
D
alone,

\mathcal{C}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}(t)=\langle\gamma(0)|\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rangle=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{O}}D^{\mathrm{F}}_{\gamma\mu}(t)D^{-1}_{\gamma^{\prime}\mu}(t),

(22)

suggesting a fundamental relation between the two kinds of channel-resolved PMD

\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{F}}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})=\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}}\mathcal{C}_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\bm{\kappa}).

(23)

We also mention that the
sum condition

\mathcal{P}(\bm{\kappa})=\sum_{\gamma}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})

(24)

requires that
D^{\dagger}D=\rho
. For TDHS, we have
D=Z^{\dagger}\rho
, so that the requirement is automatically satisfied. Other choices, including field-free HS, will generally fail this requirement.

II.5
Channel-Resolved t-SURFF Approaches

One significant drawback of projection approaches is the requirement of a large simulation region to retain the electron wavepackets before the projection is performed. Although the complexity of the present MCTDHF implementation only scales linearly with the simulation radius, an efficient evaluation of PMD remains highly desirable. A good candidate is the t-SURFF approach
Tao and Scrinzi 2012
;
Scrinzi 2012
, where the projection is turned into a time-integrated surface flux evaluation, thus eliminating the necessity to store the entire outgoing wavepacket.

As an advantage of TDHS, the channel-resolved projection onto TDHS can be effectively integrated with t-SURFF, yielding a channel-resolved PMD method that we refer to as channel-resolved t-SURFF. The central idea of this method is to derive an EOM for
\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})
. According to Eqs. (
4b
)(
18
)(
20
), we have

i\dot{\mathcal{Q}}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{O}}D_{\gamma\mu}J_{\mu}(\bm{\kappa}),

(25)

where the right-hand side is the channel-resolved surface flux given by

J_{\mu}(\bm{k})\equiv\langle\chi_{\bm{\kappa}}|\hat{\Theta}\hat{F}-\hat{H}_{V}\hat{\Theta}|\phi_{\mu}\rangle\approx\langle\chi_{\bm{\kappa}}|[\hat{\Theta},\hat{H}_{V}]|\phi_{\mu}\rangle,

(26)

where
\hat{H}_{V}
is the Volkov Hamiltonian, and
\chi_{\bm{\kappa}}
is a Volkov solution in the presence of external laser field which ends up to a plane wave with momentum
\bm{k}
and spin
\sigma
. Implementing the continuum as a screened Volkov solution ensures the gauge invariance of PMD, as
\hat{\Theta}
maintains its form under local gauge transform. When the Coulomb terms play a negligible role, for example, the t-SURFF boundary
(r=r_{0})
is extremely large, the channel-resolved t-SURFF approach produces identical results to the projection approach. For practical use, the Coulomb terms are approximately neglected in t-SURFF, retaining only the commutator
[\hat{\Theta},\hat{H}_{V}]
, which is zero except at the t-SURFF boundary. The validity of this approximation in TD-MCSCF is demonstrated in Ref.
Orimo et al. 2019a
.

Given that the surface flux will vanish at infinity time, the
unitarity
of
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})
is strictly guaranteed. Combining this property with Eq. (
24
), the unitarity of the entire PMD is also proved.

II.6
EOM of time-dependent Dyson orbitals

As an analog to Eq. (
18
), we define the projection in real space

\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}(\bm{\varrho})=\langle\gamma,\bm{\varrho}|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{O}}D_{\gamma\mu}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\varrho}),

(27)

where
\bm{\varrho}
is the collection of spatial coordinates and spin.

\mathcal{D}_{\gamma}(\bm{\varrho})
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{\bm{\kappa}})
are interpreted as the time-dependent Dyson orbitals in coordinate and momentum representations, respectively, which is a conceptual generalization of the static Dyson orbital.
With the EOM of
D
, the EOM of the underlying state,

|\psi_{\gamma}\rangle=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{O}}D_{\gamma\mu}|\phi_{\mu}\rangle,

(28)

can be derived

i|\dot{\psi}_{\gamma}\rangle=\hat{F}|\psi_{\gamma}\rangle.

(29)

Note that
\psi_{\gamma}
does not have unit normalization except in the Hartree-Fock limit.

II.7
Photoion and Photoelectron Reduced Density Matrix

In multi-channel processes, the fragments are typically in mixed states. Therefore, a quantum theory of the photoion or photoelectron naturally involves the density matrix formalism. Recently, there has been an emergent interest in the quantum coherence and quantum entanglement property of the photoelectron and photoion
Vrakking 2021
;
Koll et al. 2022
;
Ishikawa et al. 2023
;
Ruberti et al. 2024
;
Laurell et al. 2025
, which is quantitatively characterized by the photoion or photoelectron’s reduced density matrix (iRDM or eRDM). With a well-defined TDHS, we are now able to extract iRDM and eRDM from the TD-MCSCF theory.

We start from the total density operator for the ionized part,

\hat{\rho}=\mathcal{N}^{-1}\SumInt_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime},\bm{\kappa}\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})|\gamma,\bm{\kappa}\rangle\langle\gamma^{\prime},\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}|\mathcal{Q}^{*}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}),

(30)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization factor to be given later. By taking the partial trace over the electronic or ionic degree of freedom, we get the density operator of the photoelectron and the photoion,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{(\mathrm{e})}

\displaystyle=\SumInt_{\bm{\kappa}\bm{\kappa^{\prime}}}\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\bm{\kappa},\bm{\kappa}^{\prime})|\bm{\kappa}\rangle\langle\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}|,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}

\displaystyle=\SumInt_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\rho^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})|\gamma\rangle\langle\gamma^{\prime}|,

where the matrix elements are

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\bm{\kappa},\bm{\kappa}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}^{-1}\SumInt_{\gamma}\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})\mathcal{Q}^{*}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}^{-1}\SumInt_{\bm{\kappa}}\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{\kappa})\mathcal{Q}^{*}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\bm{\kappa}).

In the above definition, the dynamic phases are attributed to
|\gamma\rangle\langle\gamma^{\prime}|
and
|\bm{\kappa}\rangle\langle\bm{\kappa}^{\prime}|
, hence
\rho^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
and
\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\bm{\kappa},\bm{\kappa}^{\prime})
will approach stationary at infinite time.
By enforcing
\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{(\mathrm{i})}=\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}=1
, the normalization factor is determined as

\mathcal{N}=\SumInt_{\bm{\kappa}}\mathcal{P}(\bm{\kappa})=\sum_{\mu\nu\in\mathcal{O}}\rho_{\mu\nu}\Theta_{\mu\nu},

(33)

which is actually the average photoelectron number in the exterior region. Eqs. (
32
) and (
32
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{e})}(\bm{\kappa},\bm{\kappa}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}^{-1}\sum_{\mu\nu\in\mathcal{O}}\rho_{\mu\nu}\phi^{*}_{\mu}(\bm{\kappa}^{\prime})\phi_{\nu}(\bm{\kappa}),

(34a)

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}^{-1}\sum_{\mu\nu\in\mathcal{O}}D^{*}_{\gamma^{\prime}\mu}D_{\gamma\nu}\Theta_{\mu\nu}.

(34b)

We note that the calculation of eRDM does not need any knowledge of TDHS, while such knowledge becomes essential for iRDM. Due to the orthonormality of spin-orbitals, the matrix elements
\Theta_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}-\Theta^{<}_{\mu\nu}
can be uniquely determined by the information in the interior region. Therefore, iRDM does not require retaining the wavepacket in the exterior region, which significantly reduces the numerical cost.

III
Computational Approaches

The approach described in this section is a gauge-invariant generalization of the standard all-electron MCTDHF, and is closely related to the aforementioned RAS extensions of MCTDHF.

We employ the spin-adapted formalism in practical computations, which is advantageous for closed-shell systems where spatial orbitals are always identical for the two spin species. We introduce the notation
\sigma\in\{\alpha,\beta\}
where
\alpha,\beta
are two possible spin species, and assign the Latin letters
(i,j,k,\cdots)
for the indices of spatial orbitals. The Greek indices will thus be replaced according to
\mu=(i_{\mu},\sigma_{\mu})
, and further simplified to
(i\sigma)
. The common spatial part shared by
\phi_{i\alpha}
and
\phi_{i\beta}
is denoted by
\phi_{i}
.
The computational approach classifies the occupied orbitals as active or frozen, denoted by
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{A}}
and
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{C}}
, respectively. The size of the active (frozen) space is defined as
N_{\rm a}=|\mathcal{A}|

(N_{\rm c}=|\mathcal{C}|)
, and there are totally
\mathcal{N}_{\rm o}=N_{\rm c}+N_{\rm a}
occupied orbitals. The number of
\alpha
- and
\beta
-electrons is denoted by
N_{\alpha}
and
N_{\beta}
, respectively.

III.1
Lasers

We describe the laser field using classical electromagnetic theory, modeling the laser pulses as coherent light. The dipole approximation is adopted throughout this work, as the wavelength of the laser field significantly exceeds the spatial extent of the interaction region by more than two orders of magnitude. Both the velocity gauge (VG) and the length gauge (LG) are implemented.
The vector potential
\bm{A}
in VG is transformed into

\displaystyle A_{\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\mp\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(A_{x}\pm iA_{y}),

(35a)

\displaystyle A_{0}

\displaystyle=A_{z},

(35b)

where the components satisfy
A_{\mu}=(-1)^{\mu}A^{*}_{-\mu}
for
\mu=-1,0,+1
.
The unit vector
\bm{e}_{\mu}
is defined in the same manner so that
\bm{e}_{\mu}^{*}\cdot\bm{e}_{\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}
and
\bm{A}=A_{x}\bm{e}_{x}+A_{y}\bm{e}_{y}+A_{z}\bm{e}_{z}=A^{*}_{-1}\bm{e}_{-1}+A_{0}^{*}\bm{e}_{0}+A^{*}_{+1}\bm{e}_{+1}
.

A_{-1}
and
A_{+1}
correspond to the right-handed and left-handed components, respectively. In LG, the electric field components are denoted as
E_{\mu}
, derived from the vector potential via
\bm{E}=-d\bm{A}/dt
to ensure gauge invariance.

III.2
Potentials

To describe the polyatomic potential, we use the general one-center expansion for the Coulomb interaction

V(\bm{r})=V_{0}(r)+\sum_{\lambda=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{\mu=-\lambda}^{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{2\lambda+1}}Y_{\lambda}^{\mu}(\theta,\varphi)V^{\mu\lambda}(r).

(36)

Here, the central part
V_{0}(r)=-Z_{0}/r
represents the Coulomb potential arising from the nucleus located at
r=0
, while the contributions from all off-center nuclei are included in the multipolar terms
V^{\mu\lambda}(r)
, explicitly given by

V^{\mu\lambda}(r)=\sum_{d=1}^{N_{\rm pole}}-Z_{d}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{2\lambda+1}}Y_{\lambda}^{\mu*}(\Theta_{d},\Phi_{d})\frac{r_{<}^{\lambda}}{r_{>}^{\lambda+1}}.

(37)

Here,
Z_{d}
and
(R_{d},\Theta_{d},\Phi_{d})
are the charge and spherical coordinates of the
d
-th off-center atomic nucleus, respectively.
r_{<}=\min(r,R_{d})
and
r_{>}=\max(r,R_{d})
denote the smaller and larger radii between the electron and the nucleus, respectively.

III.3
Multiconfiguration Wavefunctions

In this work, frozen-core orbitals are always doubly occupied. All remaining orbitals are treated as active, forming a complete active space (CAS) in which any electron occupation is permitted. This classification significantly reduces computational complexity while preserving essential physical insights, enabling efficient simulations of ultrafast electron dynamics in molecular systems.

Our spin-adapted
ansatz
corresponding to Eq. (
3
) is

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\in\text{CAS}}C_{I}|I\rangle,

(38a)

\displaystyle|I\rangle

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{C}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i\alpha}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i\beta}\prod_{j\in\mathcal{A}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j\alpha})^{I^{\alpha}_{j}}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j\beta})^{I^{\beta}_{j}}|0\rangle.

(38b)

Here, the spin-adapted formalism naturally separates the occupations into spin-specific substrings
I^{\sigma}
for the occupation of
\sigma
-electrons. The spin-adapted EOM for the CI coefficients has the same form as Eq. (
4a
).

III.4
Spin-adapted Operators

As the spin is conserved due to
[\hat{S}_{z},\hat{H}]=0
, the spin indices in the Hamiltonian Eq. (
2
) are traced off to give

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}h_{ij}\sum_{\sigma}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{a}_{j\sigma}

(39)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijkl}g_{ijkl}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{a}_{k\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{a}_{l\sigma^{\prime}}.

The spin-free one- and two-electron integrals are

h_{ij}(t)=\int d^{3}\bm{r}\phi^{*}_{i}(\bm{r},t)\mathcal{H}\phi_{j}(\bm{r},t),

(40a)

\displaystyle g_{ijkl}(t)=\int d^{3}\bm{r}d^{3}\bm{r}^{\prime}\frac{\phi^{*}_{i}(\bm{r},t)\phi^{*}_{j}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t)\phi_{k}(\bm{r}^{\prime},t)\phi_{l}(\bm{r},t)}{|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}|},

(40b)

where the one-electron Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\rm LG}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\nabla}^{2}+\bm{E}(t)\cdot\bm{r}+V(\bm{r}),

(41a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\rm VG}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\nabla}^{2}-i\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{\nabla}+V(\bm{r})

(41b)

in LG and VG, respectively. Note that the gauge invariance of MCTDHF theory is ensured by construction.

For tightly bound core electrons, the gauge-invariant frozen-core approximation is adopted
Sato et al. 2016
, so that we do not need to solve their orbitals from an EOM, but directly set their value by:

\displaystyle\phi^{\rm LG}_{i}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\phi_{i}(\bm{r},0)\text{ for }i\in\mathcal{C},

(42a)

\displaystyle\phi^{\rm VG}_{i}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(i\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{r}\bigg)\phi_{i}(\bm{r},0)\text{ for }i\in\mathcal{C}.

(42b)

The initial values of the frozen orbitals
\left\{\phi_{i}(\bm{r},0)\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{C}}
are set to the canonical Hartree-Fock orbitals obtained in field-free Hartree-Fock calculations using the same method.

For active orbitals, the spin-adapted EOM has almost the same form as Eqs. (
4
), except that the reduced density matrices should be replaced by the spin-traced version, i.e.,

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}}\rho^{-1}_{ii^{\prime}}\rho_{i^{\prime}jkl},

(43a)

\displaystyle\rho_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}\langle\Psi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{a}_{j\sigma}|\Psi\rangle,

(43b)

\displaystyle\rho_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\langle\Psi|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{a}_{k\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{a}_{l\sigma}|\Psi\rangle.

(43c)

We have merged the core-active interaction into an effective one-body operator
\hat{f}
, so that the generalized Fock operator is equivalently reduced to

\hat{F}|\phi_{i}\rangle\equiv\bigg(\hat{f}|\phi_{i}\rangle+\sum_{jkl\in\mathcal{A}}\overline{\rho}_{ijkl}\hat{g}_{jk}|\phi_{l}\rangle\bigg).

(44)

The effective one-body operator,

\hat{f}=\sum_{ij}f_{ij}\sum_{\sigma}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{a}_{j\sigma},

(45)

now includes the usual one-body terms, the Coulomb and exchange interactions from the frozen-core, namely,

f_{ij}=h_{ij}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{C}}\bigg(2g_{ikkj}-g_{ikjk}\bigg).

(46)

In this work, we set the matrix elements
R_{ij}
within the active orbital space to zero, whereas those connecting active and frozen orbitals are determined from their instantaneous values as
Sato et al. 2016
:

R_{ij}=i\langle\phi_{i}|\dot{\phi}_{j}\rangle=R_{ji}^{*},\quad i\in\mathcal{A},j\in\mathcal{C}.

(47)

III.5
Discretization

To discretize the EOMs, the time-dependent orbitals are expanded via the one-center expansion

\phi_{i}(\bm{r},t)=\sum_{mln}c_{imln}(t)\frac{B_{n}(r)}{r}Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\varphi).

(48)

Here, the radial basis
B_{n}(r)
is a set of B-spline functions
Bachau et al. 2001
of the same order on a given knot sequence, and
Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\varphi)
is the spherical harmonics. In our notation, we denote by
(n|\mathcal{P}|n^{\prime})
the integral by sandwiching an operator
\mathcal{P}
between
B_{n}(r)
and
B_{n^{\prime}}(r)
. Bold uppercase(lowercase) letters are used for matrices(vectors) in the spherical-B-spline basis. For brevity, we introduce a combined index
\iota
to abbreviate the triplet
(m,l,n)
when referring to their matrix elements.

To address the stiffness in the resulting discretized orbital EOMs, the right-hand side is separated into parts:

i\dot{\bm{c}}_{i}=\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{T}\bm{c}_{i}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{O}}\bm{c}_{j}X_{ji}+\bm{Q}\bm{u}_{i}.

(49)

Here,
\bm{S}_{\iota\iota^{\prime}}=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}(n|n^{\prime})
is the overlap matrix of the basis, and
\bm{T}_{\iota\iota^{\prime}}=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}(n|-\nabla^{2}/2+V_{0}|n^{\prime})
is the stiff part of the one-particle Hamiltonian matrix.
In this work, interaction terms arising from the laser and the off-center atoms are considered to be non-stiff.
The orbital-mixing matrix is defined as
X_{ji}=T_{ji}-R_{ji}
with
T_{ji}=\bm{c}^{\dagger}_{j}\bm{T}\bm{c}_{i}
.
The orbital-projection matrix, expressed formally as
\bm{Q}=\bm{S}^{-1}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{O}}\bm{c}_{j}\bm{c}_{j}^{\dagger}
, does not need to be stored in practical calculations.
The vector
\bm{u}_{i}
collects all remaining terms, including the laser interaction, the off-center atomic potential, the core-active interaction, and the active-active interaction:

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{i}

\displaystyle=\big(\bm{V}+\bm{W}\big)\bm{c}_{i}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}}2\bm{G}_{jj}\bm{c}_{i}-\bm{G}_{ji}\bm{c}_{j}

(50)

\displaystyle+\sum_{jkl\in\mathcal{A}}\overline{\rho}_{ijkl}\bm{G}_{jk}\bm{c}_{l},

where

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{\iota\iota^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{\mu=-\lambda}^{\lambda}C^{\mu\lambda}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}V^{\mu\lambda}_{nn^{\prime}},

(51a)

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{\rm VG}_{\iota\iota^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\mu=-1}^{1}A^{*}_{\mu}\big(C^{\mu 1}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}D_{nn^{\prime}}+\overline{C}^{\mu 1}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}L_{nn^{\prime}}\big),

(51b)

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{\rm LG}_{\iota\iota^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=-1}^{1}E^{*}_{\mu}C^{\mu 1}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}R_{nn^{\prime}},

(51c)

\displaystyle(\bm{G}_{jk})_{\iota\iota^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{\mu=-\lambda}^{\lambda}C^{\mu\lambda}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}G^{\mu\lambda}_{jk,nn^{\prime}}.

(51d)

The radial integrals in Eq. (
51
) are given by

\displaystyle D_{nn^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(n|i\frac{d}{dr}|n^{\prime}),

(52a)

\displaystyle L_{nn^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(n|\frac{1}{r}|n^{\prime}),

(52b)

\displaystyle R_{nn^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(n|r|n^{\prime}),

(52c)

\displaystyle V_{nn^{\prime}}^{\mu\lambda}

\displaystyle=(n|V^{\mu\lambda}(r)|n^{\prime}),

(52d)

\displaystyle G^{\mu\lambda}_{jk,nn^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(n|G^{\mu\lambda}_{jk}(r)|n^{\prime}).

(52e)

Here,
V^{\mu\lambda}(r)
is given in Eq. (
37
).
G^{\mu\lambda}_{jk}(r)
is the radial component (see Appendix
B
) accompanying
Y_{\lambda}^{\mu}(\theta,\varphi)
in the one-center multipole expansion of the inter-electron Coulomb interaction arising from the source term
\phi_{j}^{*}(\bm{r})\phi_{k}(\bm{r})
.
Using 3-j symbols, we write the angular integrals in Eq. (
51
) in a compact form

\displaystyle C^{\mu\lambda}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}=(-1)^{m}\sqrt{(2l+1)(2l^{\prime}+1)}

(53)

\displaystyle\times\bigg(\begin{matrix}l&\lambda&l^{\prime}\\
0&0&0\\
\end{matrix}\bigg)\bigg(\begin{matrix}l&\lambda&l^{\prime}\\
-m&\mu&m^{\prime}\\
\end{matrix}\bigg),

which satisfies
C^{\mu\lambda}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}=(-1)^{\mu}C^{-\mu\lambda}_{m^{\prime}m,l^{\prime}l}
.
The symbol
\overline{C}^{\mu 1}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}=i^{l^{\prime}-l}\max(l,l^{\prime})C^{\mu 1}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}
is also introduced.

As the EOMs of MCTDHF inherently conserve the symmetry properties of the orbitals (a detailed proof can be found in the appendix of Ref.
Omiste and Madsen 2018
), the expansion scheme in Eq. (
48
) should be the same for the orbitals inside the same symmetry group but could be different between symmetry groups in principle. For consistency, the same design in our previous single-electron work
Zhang et al. 2023
is adopted for all orbitals.
We define
N_{\rm b}
as the number of independent radial B-spline functions,
N_{\rm l}
as the number of different angular quantum numbers
l
for each magnetic quantum number
m
, and
N_{\rm m}
as the total number of different
m
for each orbital. As the orbital functions of deeply bound electrons oscillate rapidly near the central atom, we specifically fine-tune the B-spline knot distribution in this region to accurately capture this character.
If the laser polarization aligns with the mirror plane of the molecule, it saves significant numerical efforts to adopt the
\rm C_{s}
point group. Specifically, we may set the mirror plane as the
x
-
y
plane, so that the terms in the expansion Eq. (
48
) vanish depending on whether
l-m
is odd or even, as dictated by symmetry. In more general situations without evident symmetry, we implement the full expansion.

Although the one-center expansion in Eq. (
48
) is notably efficient for atomic systems, its convergence for molecular orbitals tends to be considerably slower. Consequently, large values
N_{\rm l}
and
N_{\rm m}
are often indispensable for accurately describing bound molecular orbitals
Toffoli et al. 2002
.
Nevertheless, if all off-center atoms are hydrogen atoms and relatively close to the expansion center, reliable results can still be achieved with moderate choices of
N_{\rm l}
and
N_{\rm m}
, typically around 20. This enables practical real-time simulation for small molecules such as
\rm NH_{3}
,
\rm H_{2}O
,
\rm CH_{4}
near equilibrium geometry. When the total number of basis (
N_{\rm m}N_{\rm l}N_{\rm b}
) grows up to
10^{4}
, severe bottlenecks could arise in computing the two-electron Coulomb integral at each time step. To address this, we introduce an efficient computational strategy in Appendix.
B
.

In cases with large numbers of active orbitals, typically a few tens, two additional computational bottlenecks emerge, i.e., the evaluation of reduced density matrices and the matrix-vector product in the EOMs of CI coefficients. In this work, we limit the number of active orbitals to no more than 10, and there are no more than a few thousand electronic configurations in the CI expansion. We will discuss the treatment of larger CI space in future work.

III.6
Propagation

The discretized EOMs are propagated by Krogstad’s ETD-RK4 solver
Krogstad 2005
;
Kidd et al. 2017
. Here, the time-independent term
\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{T}
is regarded as the only stiff part. We have verified that merging the monopole component of
\bm{G}_{jj}
into
\bm{T}
improves numerical stability, particularly when the orbital
\phi_{j}
contains a deeply bound state localized around the central atom.
A more rigid treatment of the stiffness problem involving the
\bm{A}\cdot\bm{\nabla}
term can be found in Ref.
Li et al. 2021
.
The
\varphi
-functions in the ETD-RK4 solver are implemented with the Lanczos-Arnoldi algorithm
Saad 2003
. Throughout this work, the dimension of the Krylov subspace is fixed to
30
for all partial waves during propagation.

For the EOMs of
C_{I}
, no stiffness will occur, thus the algorithm degenerates to the conventional RK4 solver.
The right-hand side of Eq. (
38
) is given by

\langle I|\hat{H}-\hat{R}|J\rangle=\langle I|\hat{H}_{a}|J\rangle,

(54)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{A}}f_{ij}\sum_{\sigma}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{a}_{j\sigma}

(55)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijkl\in\mathcal{A}}g_{ijkl}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{a}_{k\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{a}_{l\sigma}.

It is suggested
Sato and Ishikawa 2013
that the diagonal entries of the CI matrix in Eq. (
54
) should be offset by
-\langle\Psi|\hat{H}_{a}|\Psi\rangle
to further suppress the numerical instability during propagation.

The propagation method can operate in either real or imaginary time. Real-time propagation (RTP) addresses problems driven by laser fields, while imaginary time propagation (ITP) is employed to generate the initial states in a self-consistent manner.

III.7
Channel-Resolved Momentum Distribution

In spin-adapted formalism, the invariance under spin inversion guarantees
\mathcal{P}(\bm{k}\alpha)=\mathcal{P}(\bm{k}\beta)
. For simplicity, one often uses the spin-adapted PMD,
\mathcal{P}(\bm{k})\equiv\mathcal{P}(\bm{k}\alpha)+\mathcal{P}(\bm{k}\beta)=2\mathcal{P}(\bm{k}\alpha)
, which is explicitly evaluated by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{A}}\rho_{ij}(t_{f})\phi^{*}_{i}(\bm{k},t_{f})\phi_{j}(\bm{k},t_{f}),

(56a)

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(\bm{k},t_{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\int d^{3}\bm{r}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\Theta(r-r_{0})\phi_{i}(\bm{r},t_{f}).

(56b)

The numerical method for evaluating Eq. (
56b
) is quite similar to single-electron problems, and further details are omitted here.

The single-vacancy Hilbert space decouples into two degenerate subspaces distinguished by spin orientation. We denote the pair of degenerate hole states in these subspaces by

\displaystyle|\gamma\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle\in\text{span}\{\hat{a}_{i\alpha}|\Psi\rangle|i\in\mathcal{A}\},

(57a)

\displaystyle|\gamma\beta\rangle

\displaystyle\in\text{span}\{\hat{a}_{i\beta}|\Psi\rangle|i\in\mathcal{A}\}.

(57b)

Given spin-inversion symmetry, we isolate and redefine the spin-irrelevant part in the projection as

\displaystyle\langle\gamma\alpha|\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\alpha}|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\gamma\beta|\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\beta}|\Psi\rangle\equiv\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{k}),

(58a)

\displaystyle\langle\gamma\beta|\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\alpha}|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\gamma\alpha|\hat{a}_{\bm{k}\beta}|\Psi\rangle=0,

(58b)

such that
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\bm{k})\equiv 2|\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{k})|^{2}=\mathcal{P}_{\gamma\alpha}(\bm{k}\alpha)+\mathcal{P}_{\gamma\beta}(\bm{k}\beta)
. Similarly, we redefine the identical parts in
D
as

\displaystyle D_{\gamma\alpha,i\alpha}

\displaystyle=D_{\gamma\beta,i\beta}\equiv D_{\gamma i},

(59a)

\displaystyle D_{\gamma\alpha,i\beta}

\displaystyle=D_{\gamma\beta,i\alpha}=0.

(59b)

With these spin-adapted notations, the final expression of the spin-adapted channel-resolved PMD is

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\bm{k})=2\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{A}}\phi^{*}_{i}(\bm{k},t_{f})D^{*}_{\gamma i}(t_{f})D_{\gamma j}(t_{f})\phi_{j}(\bm{k},t_{f}).

(60)

For both TDHS and field-free HS, the expression for
D_{\gamma i}
is almost identical to the spin-unadapted theory in Eqs. (
20
) and (
21
), except that the Greek subscripts are replaced by Latin ones. In this work, Eq. (
20
) is integrated by an explicit exponential integrator simultaneously with the integration of MCTDHF EOMs. At each time step, we read
F-R
from MCTDHF and evaluate

D_{\gamma}(t+\tau)\approx\exp\{-i\tau(F(t)-R(t))\}D_{\gamma}(t).

(61)

The initial condition for Eq. (
61
) is given by
D(0)=Z(0)^{-1}
, where
Z(0)
is solved from the spin-adapted EKT at
t=0
,

-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}}(F_{ji}-R_{ji})Z_{j\gamma}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}}\rho_{ij}Z_{j\gamma}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}.

(62)

Similarly to the projection approach, the spin-adapted channel-resolved t-SURFF approach has an almost identical formalism except that the Greek letters are replaced by Latin ones. The numerical implementation of the surface flux term Eq. (
26
) is quite similar to the single-electron problem, so we will not repeat any more. Usually, the t-SURFF approach is combined with absorption boundary conditions. In this work, we will explicitly indicate instances whenever an absorber is utilized to eliminate outgoing wavepackets.

III.8
Channel-Resolved Single Ionization Yield

Generally, the ionization yield for a neutral system refers to the fraction of specific atomic or ionic species present after ionization, e.g.
\rm X
,
\rm X^{+}
,
\rm X^{2+}
, etc. In Ref.
Sato and Ishikawa 2013
, the yield of
\rm X^{n+}
is equivalently defined as the gauge-invariant probability to find exactly
n
electrons outside a predefined sphere
r_{0}
. Methods for computing these yields are thoroughly discussed. We will denote them by
\mathcal{Y}^{(n)}
. Alternatively, one can conveniently measure the fraction of electrons detached from the atom or molecule by calculating the expectation value of
\hat{\Theta}
(defined in Eq. (
16
)), whose explicit form in spin-adapted formalism is

\mathcal{N}=\sum_{ij}\rho_{ij}\Theta_{ij}.

(63)

It is important to realize that
\mathcal{Y}^{(n)}
is different from
\mathcal{N}
in general cases, as the sum of the former never exceeds unity, while the latter can be as large as the number of electrons:

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathcal{Y}^{(n)},

(64a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}n\mathcal{Y}^{(n)}.

(64b)

However, under the conditions dominated by single ionization, we can safely approximate
\mathcal{Y}^{(n)}\approx 0
for
n>1
, so that
\mathcal{N}\approx\mathcal{Y}^{(1)}\approx 1-\mathcal{Y}^{(0)}
.
Analogous to the procedure used for calculating the PMD, we define the channel-resolved photoelectron number collecting the contributions from the
|\gamma\alpha\rangle
and
|\gamma\beta\rangle
channels as

\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}=2\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{A}}D^{*}_{\gamma i}D_{\gamma j}\Theta_{ij},

(65)

which satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}\bm{k}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\bm{k}),

(66a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma}\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}.

(66b)

As verified in subsequent sections, under single-ionization dominance,
\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}
corresponds to the population of the ion in the state
|\gamma\rangle
, which makes it particularly useful for calculating the partial photoionization cross sections.

III.9
Photoion’s Density Matrix

In spin-adapted formalism, the elements of the iRDM are equal for the two spin subspaces,

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma\alpha,\gamma^{\prime}\alpha)

\displaystyle=\rho^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma\beta,\gamma^{\prime}\beta)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}),

(67a)

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma\alpha,\gamma^{\prime}\beta)

\displaystyle=\rho^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma\beta,\gamma^{\prime}\alpha)=0,

(67b)

where we have defined the spin-adapted iRDM
\tilde{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}
normalized to
\mathrm{Tr}\tilde{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}=1
.
The spectral decomposition of the ion density operator is simplified into

\hat{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}=\sum_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\sum_{\sigma}|\gamma\sigma\rangle\langle\gamma^{\prime}\sigma|,

(68)

where the matrix elements corresponding to Eqs. (
32
) and (
34b
) are calculated by

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\mathcal{N}}\int d^{3}\bm{k}\mathcal{Q}^{*}_{\gamma^{\prime}}(\bm{k})\mathcal{Q}_{\gamma}(\bm{k})

(69)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{A}}D^{*}_{\gamma^{\prime}i}D_{\gamma j}\Theta_{ij}.

One may immediately recognize from Eq. (
65
) that the diagonal entry
\tilde{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma,\gamma)=\mathcal{N}_{\gamma}/\mathcal{N}
is the branching ratio, ignoring the subsequent nuclear dynamics.
The off-diagonal entry
\tilde{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
is a measure of quantum coherence between
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
.
Non-zero off-diagonal entries in iRDM may induce oscillations at frequencies of
\omega_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}=|E_{\gamma}-E_{\gamma^{\prime}}|=|\mathcal{E}_{\gamma^{\prime}}-\mathcal{E}_{\gamma}|
in the ion charge density

\displaystyle\varrho^{(\mathrm{i})}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\langle\gamma^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}|\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}\sigma}\hat{a}_{\bm{r}\sigma}|\gamma\sigma^{\prime}\rangle

(70)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{ijkl\in\mathcal{A}}\rho_{ijkl}\Theta_{il}\phi_{j}^{*}(\bm{r})\phi_{k}(\bm{r}),

leading to the migration and localization of charge
Rodríguez-Cuenca et al. 2024
;
Calegari et al. 2014
;
Kraus et al. 2015
;
He et al. 2022
. In the next section, we will present a real-time simulation of this process, considering fully the detached electron and the residual ion. To visualize it, we calculate the hole density by the difference between the charge density of the neutral species and the ion:

\displaystyle\mathcal{\rho}^{(\rm h)}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{\rho}^{(\rm n)}(\bm{r})-\mathcal{\rho}^{(\mathrm{i})}(\bm{r})

(71)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{ij\in\mathcal{A}}\bigg(\rho_{ij}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{kl\in\mathcal{A}}\rho_{ijkl}\Theta_{il}\bigg)\phi_{j}^{*}(\bm{r})\phi_{k}(\bm{r}).

Here, we have replaced the charge density of the neutral species with the one of the full system, which is only valid in the weak-field regime. Nevertheless, the integral of the hole density over the entire space still equals exactly unity.

\displaystyle A_{x}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{\max}f(t,M)\sin(\omega t),

V
Conclusion

In summary, we have advanced the theoretical methods for studying attosecond photoionization dynamics in atoms and molecules by introducing the time-dependent hole state formalism into the multi-configurational time-dependent Hartree-Fock framework. Our full-dimensional, one-center expansion numerical implementation enables the simulation of arbitrarily polarized laser fields and multi-center molecular potentials, providing a versatile tool for
ab initio
investigations of ultrafast electron dynamics. The rigorous definition of the time-dependent hole state, derived from a time-domain generalization of the extended Koopmans’ theorem, allows accurate extraction of the reduced ion density matrix and channel-resolved observables, capabilities previously unrealized within the general time-dependent multi-configurational self-consistent-field framework.

By applying this approach to the single-photon ionization of the water molecule, we successfully calculated both total and partial photoionization cross sections, achieving excellent agreement with existing experimental and theoretical benchmarks. This validation underscores the reliability of the time-dependent hole state formalism in capturing multichannel ionization processes. Furthermore, we demonstrated the ultrafast control of electron coherence in
\mathrm{H_{2}O}
by tailoring the phase delay and polarization of attosecond pulses, highlighting the potential of attosecond spectroscopy to manipulate quantum states in molecular ions.

Our results bridge a critical gap in state-resolved attosecond photoionization studies, offering a generalizable framework for disentangling entangled photofragment dynamics. The compatibility of the methodology with the advanced t-SURFF method paves the way for constructing ion-state-resolved photoelectron momentum distributions in complex systems. While the computational demands of fully converged MCTDHF simulations for large molecules remain non-trivial, the efficiency enhancement strategies in our implementation, such as the gauge-invariant frozen-core approximation and the angular discrete variable representation for accelerating two-electron integrals, extend its applicability to larger molecular targets. Future extensions could incorporate hybrid restricted active
space strategies, such as occupation-restricted multiple active spaces, and non-adiabatic nuclear motion, such as extended MCTDHF, to balance accuracy and scalability, enabling real-time tracking of correlated electron dynamics in ever more complex environments.

Acceleration of Two-electron Term

The major computational bottleneck of Eq. (
50

\bm{G}
. Instead of covering all possible non-zero multipoles, we truncate the spherical expansion in Eq. (
51d
) at
\lambda_{\max}=\max(\lambda)
and
\mu_{\max}=\max(|\mu|)
, as the contributions from higher-order terms are typically negligible. It significantly reduces the numerical cost.

To further improve efficiency without compromising accuracy, we use the methods described in Ref.
McCurdy and Martin 2004
to evaluate
G^{\mu\lambda}_{jk,nn^{\prime}}
, which proves particularly efficient.
This approach extracts the radial component
G^{\mu\lambda}_{jk}(r)
by solving the radial Poisson equation

\bigg(\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}-\frac{\lambda(\lambda+1)}{r^{2}}\bigg)rG(r)=-\frac{2\lambda+1}{r}\varrho(r),

(83)

subject to proper boundary conditions. The complex-valued source term is given by

\displaystyle\varrho^{\mu\lambda}_{jk}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}c^{*}_{jml}(r)C^{\mu\lambda}_{m^{\prime}m,l^{\prime}l}c_{km^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(r),

(84a)

\displaystyle c_{iml}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}c_{imln}B_{n}(r).

(84b)

Only half of these terms need to be calculated due to the symmetry relation
G^{\mu\lambda}_{jk}(r)=[G^{-\mu\lambda}_{kj}(r)]^{*}
. After preparing
\bm{G}_{ij}
, the update vector
\bm{p}_{ijk}=\bm{G}_{ij}\bm{c}_{k}
is calculated as follows

\displaystyle(\bm{p}_{ijk})_{\iota}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\lambda n^{\prime}}G^{\mu\lambda}_{ij,nn^{\prime}}d^{\mu\lambda}_{kmln^{\prime}},

(85a)

\displaystyle d^{\mu\lambda}_{kmln}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}C^{\mu\lambda}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}c_{km^{\prime}l^{\prime}n}.

(85b)

In a direct implementation, the numerical cost of the transforms scales with
O(N_{\rm a}\mu_{\max}\lambda_{\max}N_{\rm m}N_{\rm l}^{2}N_{\rm b})
and
O(N_{a}^{3}\mu_{\max}\lambda_{\max}N_{\rm m}N_{\rm l}N_{\rm b}N_{\rm k})
for Eqs. (
85b
)(
85a
), respectively, representing a severe computational bottleneck.
This difficulty may be alleviated using the Discrete Variable Representation (DVR) for the angular variables
\eta=\cos\theta
and
\varphi
as discussed in Ref.
Sukiasyan and Meyer 2001
;
Haxton 2007
and reviewed in Ref.
Hochstuhl et al. 2014
. Here, we choose Sukiasyan’s method
Sukiasyan and Meyer 2001
.
We start from the Gauss-Legendre quadrature of
C^{\mu\lambda}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}
on the interval
[-1,1]
, where

\displaystyle C^{\mu\lambda}_{mm^{\prime},ll^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{m^{\prime},m-\mu}\sqrt{\frac{2}{2\lambda+1}}\times

(86)

\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{N_{\rm G}}w_{g}N^{m}_{l}(\eta_{g})N^{\mu}_{\lambda}(\eta_{g})N_{l^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}(\eta_{g}),

where
N^{m}_{l}
is the normalized associated Legendre polynomials,
N_{\rm G}
is the quadrature order, and
\eta_{g}
,
w_{g}
are the quadrature points and weights, respectively. As Eq. (
86
) is numerically
exact
when
2N_{\rm G}-1\geq 2l_{\max}+\lambda_{\max}
, one may equivalently treat the two-electron terms in
\eta
’s DVR, where the orbitals and the multipole potentials are transformed by

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{imgn}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}\chi^{m}_{gl}c_{imln},

(87a)

\displaystyle c_{imln}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g}\chi^{m}_{gl}\tilde{c}_{imgn},

(87b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\Lambda^{\mu}_{g\lambda}G^{\mu\lambda}_{ij,nn^{\prime}}

(87c)

with
\chi^{m}_{gl}=\sqrt{w_{g}}N^{m}_{l}(\eta_{g})

\Lambda^{\mu}_{g\lambda}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{2\lambda+1}}N^{\mu}_{\lambda}(\eta_{g})

\eta

where we have introduced the symbol
\tilde{\iota}=(m,g,n)

Similarly, adopting a uniform grid of
\varphi

\delta_{m^{\prime},m-\mu}=\frac{1}{N_{\rm F}}\sum_{f=1}^{N_{\rm F}}e^{im\varphi_{f}}e^{-i\mu\varphi_{f}}e^{-im^{\prime}\varphi_{f}}
