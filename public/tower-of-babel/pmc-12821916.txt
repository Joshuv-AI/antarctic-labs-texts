Adolescent Resilience-Building In Urban Slums—A Multifaceted Implementation Trial of Life Skills Education in India (ANUMATI 2.0) among older adolescents: a protocol for a double blind, three arm, cluster randomised controlled trial

Background

Adolescence is a period of rapid physical, psychological, and social change. Older adolescents (15–19 years) experience many key transitions including educational advancement, developing intimate relationships with peers, and increasing responsibility in decision-making. Successfully navigating these transitions by promoting resilience is likely to reduce short-term stress and mitigate the risk of developing depression into adulthood. Further, it is important to address associated behavioural risks including low physical activity and drug and alcohol use. This is especially important, given that stress and depression in young people are highly correlated with other behavioural risk factors associated with non-communicable diseases (NCD) [1–4]. The burden due to mental disorders1 among adolescents in India is high; the Indian National Mental Health Survey (2015–2016) reported a 7% prevalence of psychiatric disorders2 in 13–17-year-olds with minimal gender differences [5]. It is estimated that about 50% of adult mental disorders manifest during adolescence [6, 7]. Of those with depressive disorders, only 1 in 27 people receive effective treatment in low- and middle-income countries, such as India [8]. Barriers to care include both demand and supply factors such as lack of awareness of mental health needs, public and internalised stigma, and few accessible health staff. These problems are exacerbated in urban slums where rates of poverty, unemployment, and interpersonal violence (among males and females) lead to increased rates of mental disorders, with less access to mental health care [9].

A systematic review of policies related to child and adolescent mental health found that inadequate institutional capacity and resources act as barriers to seeking mental health care in LMICs [10]. A report by UNICEF India found that there is a lacuna in identifying the drivers of mental ill-health and factors promoting wellbeing. The report also pointed out that the few interventions that exist were reactive in nature and were poorly implemented [11].

The Government of India recognises adolescent health as important and is investing in adolescent health and wellbeing. To ensure holistic development of the adolescent population, the government has launched its flagship programme, the Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (RKSK, National Adolescent Health Programme), to reach out to adolescents, with a special focus on marginalised and underserved groups. The RKSK expands the scope of adolescent health programming in India—from being limited to sexual and reproductive health, it now includes in its ambit mental health and substance misuse among other issues [12] and focusses on reaching adolescents in their own environment such as communities, families, and schools. Another government initiative is the National Programme for Youth Adolescent Development (NPYAD), which focusses on the development of leadership qualities and the capacity of adolescents to respond to challenging situations. The NPYAD recognises that adolescents have very special and distinct needs, which need to be addressed, and recognises that adolescents can grow up to be healthy and responsible members of society if they are exposed to a climate of positive stimulation and to a congenial environment. ANUMATI 2.0 aligns with NPYAD’s focus on education and life skills development in adolescents.

Other NCD risk factors among adolescents

Many lifestyle behaviours such as physical inactivity, unhealthy diets, irregular sleep patterns, smoking, and alcohol and substance use are established during adolescence. A survey of 14,227 individuals across four regions, representing the south, west, east, and north of India, showed that 58.7% of men and 71.2% of women in urban India were insufficiently physically active [13]. A study on a sample size of 226 patients from Delhi hospitals showed a high prevalence of overweight/obesity among both children (19.2% in males and 18.1% in females) and their mothers (65%); it also showed limited knowledge about physical activity and diet as key factors associated with obesity [14]. In their international survey of physical activity among adolescents (11–17 years) from 146 countries, Guthold et al. [15] reported that 74% of adolescents in India had inadequate physical activity. The National Family Health Survey 5 (NFHS-5) reported that 24% and 23% of females and males, respectively, aged 15–19 years were overweight or obese [16]. Studies have shown that adolescents who had a higher Body Mass Index Standard Deviation Score also displayed more behavioural difficulties [17, 18] and had a lower health-related quality of life [19].

The NFHS-5 data also revealed that 9% of women and 38% of men aged 15–49 years used tobacco, and alcohol consumption was significantly higher in men (22%) than in women (1%). In a trial among slums in Delhi, tobacco use among adolescents was found to be between 6 and 8% [20]. Several studies have shown that smoking during adolescence results in an increased risk for the subsequent onset of depressed mood [21, 22].

In a study from Goa, a third of the males who reported drinking alcohol had started during adolescence, and adolescent alcohol use was associated with three times higher rates of psychological distress, alcohol dependence, and alcohol-related injuries [23]. It has been established that the use of substances during adolescence reduces physical fitness, increases risks for many NCDs that manifest in later life, and also increases the risk for substance use disorders that develop later [24]. Research has also shown that the use of certain substances also affects cognitive development in adolescents and ultimately reduces peak cognitive abilities [25].

The prevalence of hypertension and diabetes among older adolescents in India is on the rise. A study on approximately 200,000 older adolescents in India surveyed during the NFHS-5 found that the age and sex-adjusted prevalence of diabetes was 3.5%, while that of hypertension was 24.3% [26]. Another study that used Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey data (CNNS, 2016–2018) to estimate the prevalence of blood pressure among adolescents found that in approximately 12,000 adolescents, the prevalence of high blood pressure was 35.1% in adolescents aged 10–12 years and 25.1% in adolescents 13 years and older [27].

Need for community-based mental health services for adolescents

Adolescents in slums are at increased risk of stress and NCD risk factors and have limited access to care. There are large knowledge gaps about child and adolescent wellbeing in slums, with most information coming from small studies [28, 29]. A study conducted in northern India found 12% of adolescents (10–19 years) reported alcohol or tobacco use, and 9% described a stressful atmosphere at home [30]. Another study reported the prevalence of common mental disorders (CMDs), defined as depression, anxiety, and suicide/self-harm risk, to be as high as 14.8% among young people (18–24 years) living in a non-notified slum of Mumbai [31]. A study of children (5–10 years) in a slum in Andhra Pradesh found that the strongest correlates of emotional and behavioural disorders were financial hardship, paternal alcohol dependence, family conflict, male gender, maternal depression, and younger maternal age [29]. Our earlier study conducted among older adolescents in slums showed the association of slum adversity and depression scores; adolescents who experienced adversities had a three-fold higher likelihood to report depression than those who did not experience adversities [32].

These studies suggest early onset of NCD risk factors, peer pressure, stress, lack of role models, and discordant family settings are factors that should be mitigated to promote resilience among adolescents.

Life skills education

Life skills education (LSE) is an evidence-based approach to enhance young people’s resilience to stressors. It focusses on improving communication, assertiveness, self-awareness, decision-making, problem solving, and creative thinking. LSE is informed by social learning and problem behaviour theory and focusses on providing individuals with appropriate knowledge about risk-taking behaviours and skills to improve communication, assertiveness, self-awareness, decision-making, problem solving, and critical and creative thinking to help them overcome challenges faced in life [33]. A systematic review of LSE programmes in high-income countries demonstrated significant reductions in smoking, alcohol, substance use, and improvements in self-efficacy [34]. Another meta-analysis of LSE programmes for adolescents in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) across 19 trials reported that these programmes demonstrated the capacity to reduce adolescents’ anger, depression, and anxiety and improved life skills and functioning [35].

Prior work that informed the development of the intervention

The ANUMATI 2.0 study builds upon the prior implementation research conducted by the research team to improve adolescent health and wellbeing in slums. Entitled MentAl Health Risk Factors among Older AdolesceNts living in Urban SluMs: An InTervention to Improve ResIlience (ANUMATI 1.0), the study included a survey that was administered to 3950 older adolescents in two slums of North India (Faridabad) and South India (Hyderabad) Municipal Corporations and that focussed on understanding non-health sector opportunities to enhance older adolescents’ (15–19 years) resilience in slums [32]. Key findings of ANUMATI 1.0 highlighted the need for community-based resilience-enhancing strategies to support adolescent wellbeing and mitigate the future onset of mental illness and other NCD risk factors. ANUMATI 2.0 also builds on the learnings from a recently completed cluster randomised controlled trial (Adolescents’ Resilience and Treatment nEeds for Mental health in Indian Slums—ARTEMIS) [36] which was implemented in 60 slum clusters across two cities (New Delhi and Vijayawada) to reduce depression among adolescents. A major component of ARTEMIS included the establishment of Adolescent Expert Advisory Groups (AEAGs) [37] to co-design an anti-stigma campaign aimed at raising awareness about mental health in the community and to identify optimal implementation strategies for improving mental health care for adolescents. The AEAG also acted as ambassadors for its implementation. The AEAGs and other stakeholders identified the need for community-based strategies outside of the health service sector to promote positive social interactions and increase capacity to address stressors. These learnings were incorporated into ANUMATI 2.0, whose overall goal is to reduce depression, improve the quality of life, increase resilience, and reduce suicide/self-harm risk and other NCD risk factors such as consumption of tobacco, alcohol, and other substances and lack of physical activity and a balanced diet in older adolescents at high risk of depression and/or suicide/self-harm living in resource-constrained settings like slums.

Methods

Aim

ANUMATI 2.0 aims to evaluate the feasibility, clinical effectiveness, and cost-effectiveness of two community-based implementation strategies for life skills education among older adolescents (15–19 years) with or at high risk of depression who are living in slums in the Indian cities of New Delhi and Hyderabad/Secunderabad compared to usual care. The hypothesis is that life skills administered either in-person or through social media will improve wellbeing, depression symptoms, and lead to a reduction in other non-communicable disease risk factors (smoking, alcohol and substance use, physical inactivity) relative to enhanced usual care. A secondary hypothesis is that the social media-based programme will be more cost-effective than the in-person programme.

Intervention development

The core component of the intervention includes a Life Skills Education Programme (LSEP) for addressing physical inactivity, unhealthy diets, lack of sleep, smoking, alcohol, and substance use, promoting mental health and building resilience among older adolescents. Trial design

A 3-arm hybrid type 2 cluster randomised controlled trial (cRCT) with process and economic evaluations will be conducted in slum communities to assess the impact of the intervention over a 12-month period. Arm 1 will receive the LSEP delivered in-person by trained facilitators. Arm 2 will receive the LSEP delivered via social media platforms moderated by skilled facilitators. The study is a superiority trial, where we hypothesise that either one of the intervention arms is superior to the control arm.

Arm 3 will be provided with enhanced usual care. Usual care for mental health needs is rudimentary; there is no structured system, health care provision is ad hoc, and driven by individual awareness, motivation, and ability to access care. Enhanced usual care will include providing information by the field staff and community volunteers related to physical activity, healthy diet, and substance use through a brochure. Information on mental ill-health and information on when and where to seek care if required will also be included (Fig. 1).

Study schema

Setting

The implementation trial will be conducted in 105 randomly selected slum clusters in the cities of New Delhi in the north Indian state of Delhi and Hyderabad/Secunderabad in the south Indian state of Telangana. Both New Delhi and Hyderabad are metro cities. New Delhi is the capital of India and has 6343 slums [40] in which 1.7 million people reside; the average literacy rate of the slum population is 65.5% [41]. Hyderabad is the capital of the southern state of Telangana in India. It has 1476 slums [42] in which 1.9 million people reside; the average literacy rate of the slum population is 46.1% [41].

Eligible population

Slum inclusion criteria

Slums will be identified using government administrative data in each state and factoring in the distance from the project office. A total of 54 slum clusters in New Delhi and 51 slum clusters in Hyderabad/Secunderabad will be selected (105 slums in total). Non-contiguous slums will be recruited to mitigate contamination risk by selecting slums which are at a distance of at least 3 km and do not share common marketplaces.

Participant recruitment

Assuming 6% of adolescents (15–19 years) will be eligible (based on screen positive criteria from our earlier study with adolescents in urban slums [32, 36], we anticipate that around 43,750 adolescents (25 adolescents per slum cluster) will need to be screened to achieve the required sample size. To ensure that we achieve the required number per slum cluster, we will continue to screen adolescents until we identify 25 adolescents who meet the inclusion criteria.

Participant inclusion criteria

Consenting adolescents aged 15–19 years will be invited to be screened for depression and risk of self-harm/suicide. Informed consent will be sought from participants aged 18–19 years by field staff. For those aged 15–17 years, assent will be obtained from them and consent from their parents/guardians. The participant information sheet and consent form will be translated into Hindi and Telugu, the local languages spoken in New Delhi and Hyderabad/Secunderabad, respectively. Participants who are unable to read or write will be assisted by the field staff who will read out the participant information sheet and the consent form in the presence of an impartial witness to obtain written consent.

To be eligible for inclusion in the study, participants must have the following:

A score of ≥10 on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 items (PHQ-9) [43] and/or a positive response (score ≥ 2) to the suicide risk question on the PHQ-9. Such individuals will be deemed ‘screen positive’.

Field investigators will be trained by the research team to conduct screening assessments for depression and suicide risk using a mobile tablet-based version of the PHQ-9. Older consenting adolescents who meet the inclusion criteria will be considered screen positive and included in the study. The interviewers will continue screening until the required number of consenting screen positive individuals is identified (see sample size estimations in the Statistical Consideration Section). This will form the study cohort. Consenting participants meeting the inclusion criteria will be asked further questions about their socio-demographic, slum adversity, health characteristics, suicide ideation, smoking, alcohol and substance use, physical activity, resilience, quality of life, and social connectedness.

Randomisation

Randomisation of the 105 slums will be conducted in six phases. In each phase, research activities will be conducted chronologically. It will start with adolescent screening for inclusion criteria, followed by baseline data collection, and then randomisation of the cluster. For phases 1 to 5, 9 slum clusters will be randomised in each site, and in the final phase, 9 slum clusters will be randomised in New Delhi, while 6 slum clusters will be randomised in Hyderabad/Secunderabad (Fig. 2).

Phase-wise screening, randomisation, and data collection timelines

Slum clusters will be randomly allocated 1:1:1 to each study arm. In each phase, covariate-constrained randomisation will be conducted using the following cluster-level covariates: (i) mean PHQ-9 score, (ii) % male, (iii) % who reported life in the 2 weeks being ‘good’ or ‘very good’ on the Quality of Life Brief Version (WHOQoL-BREF) survey, (iv) mean suicidal ideation attributes scale (SIDAS) score, and (v) positivity rate. We will generate all possible allocation schemes, balance for each allocation scheme will be assessed using minimum (Kruskal-Wallis p value), and a threshold of 0.3 will be used to identify a list of schemes with acceptable balance [44]. An allocation scheme will then be randomly selected from this list. In phases 2 to 6, allocation of clusters in previous phases will be accounted for while assessing balance. An unblinded statistician will generate the allocation sequence based on baseline data. The random allocation will be performed using a software-based random number generator by a statistician not involved in study conduct or analysis. The PI and CoPI will be blinded to the allocation, in addition to the blinded statistician, until database lock and unblinding.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions for a given trial participant: Refusal by the participant of any arm and out migration for the in-person arm is a criteria for discontinuation. There is no provision for modifying the allocated interventions for a given trial participant. For both conditions (discontinuation or modification), once a participant has been randomised to an arm, they will be considered as allocated to that arm for analysis purposes. However, if a participant wants their data to be removed, it will be done, and the arm in which the participant belonged will have one less participant; the participant will not be replaced by a new recruit.

Intervention components

Arm 1 (in-person LSEP)—participants will be exposed to four modules with ten sessions in all, that will focus on increasing resilience and building skills to overcome stress, reduce risky behaviours, and increase health-promoting lifestyle choices that will be delivered by trained facilitators. It will include modules incorporating short video messages, movie clips, serious games, and activities. Additionally, parents, teachers (especially private tutors which is a common practice in the slums), and key community members will be informed about the project and the intervention objectives. The programme and its materials will be developed in Hindi and Telugu, the languages spoken in Delhi and Hyderabad/Secunderabad. It will be implemented post-randomisation. Refresher activities will be conducted after all the modules have been delivered to reinforce the key learnings from each module between the 9th and 12th months after the start of the intervention.

Arm 2 (social media LSEP)—The social media-based intervention will be implemented over a 1-year period. There will be two components:

A dedicated private page on Facebook and/or Instagram will be created for access only by participants in this arm of the intervention. This will have curated contents based on discussions with AEAG members. It will include videos, memes, short video bytes of young people talking about their experiences, online resources and films on common mental disorders (CMDs) such as depression, anxiety, increased suicide risk, alcohol use, substance use, physical activity, healthy sleeping and eating habits its effect on health, and information about ways to seek care from appropriate service providers.

Facilitator-led WhatsApp groups—The purpose of this group is mainly to facilitate communication. In these groups, peers will be encouraged to engage with the other group members and the research team by providing feedback, responding to polls and quizzes and post content, including videos, photos, short movie clips featuring comments from persons with lived experiences, and resources to seek care.

Arm 3 (enhanced usual care)—The enhanced usual care arm will receive general information about CMDs and the usefulness of physical activity, adequate sleep, a balanced diet and information on mental health services. This will be done using brochures and pamphlets, and community volunteers will discuss these with the adolescents and their parents/guardians.

Anyone with severe depression (PHQ-9 score ≥ 15) or at increased suicide risk (PHQ-9 score on the self-harm/suicide-related question ≥ 2) during screening, irrespective of their intervention status, will be advised to seek immediate mental health care and will be provided with information and contact details about available mental health services within their vicinity. Anyone with a score ≥ 2 on the suicide-related question in PHQ-9 will be asked additional questions about ‘do they have any plans’ and ‘if there has been any prior attempt’. If the response is ‘yes’ to either of the two questions, they will be asked to visit a mental health professional immediately for further assessment. After securing the permission of the individual, family members will also be notified and informed about the urgency to visit a mental health professional. The contact of the nearest mental health professional will be shared. In addition to this, those who have answered ‘yes’ to the self-harm/suicide-related question will be regularly monitored by the field staff to check on their wellbeing. If required, the field staff will counsel the individual and the family about the same.

Concomitant care

This trial does not use drugs or other treatments, and therefore there are no prohibitions during the trial.

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention

To improve attendance in the in-person sessions, project implementation staff (mobilisers) will be recruited in all the in-person slum clusters, whose primary responsibility will be to visit study participants and meet with their parents to motivate them to attend the sessions. Further, facilitators will conduct follow-up sessions with participants who missed attending any session. For the social media arm, field staff will visit study participants who are not actively engaging with the ANUMATI social media platform and nudge them to engage with the content.

Trial management and audit

Project Management Group will meet to review trial conduct on a weekly basis.

The Trial Steering Group will meet four times over the course of the study. The first meeting will be before the start of the trial, after the trial has run for about 3 months, in the middle of the trial, and at the end to share the results.

Independent Data Monitoring Committee (DSMC) will meet at the beginning of the trial and after 6 months data has been collected from 50% of the study sites. In this meeting, both the baseline data and the interim analysis will be presented based on which decisions to stop or continue with the trial will be taken. The DSMC will meet again when the final results of the trial are presented.

The Ethics Committee will not meet to discuss trial updates; however, in cases of protocol amendment, approval will be sought from them.

Data collection

Quantitative data will be collected prior to randomisation at baseline (time 0) and then subsequently at 6 and 12 months of the intervention by data collectors blinded to the intervention. Data will be collected from the study cohort across all arms by trained data collectors using tablets. Questionnaires will include the WHOQoL-BREF, PHQ-9, SIDAS, CD-RISC–10, and ASSIST. We will also measure social connectedness using the Social Network Index (SNI) at baseline and 12 months. Data will be collected electronically using CSPro on handheld devices.

Patient Health Questionnaire-9 items (PHQ-9) [43]—It consists of 9 items. The final score is calculated by assigning scores of 0, 1, 2, and 3 to the response categories of ‘not at all’, ‘several days’, ‘more than half the days’, and ‘nearly every day’, respectively. PHQ-9 total score for the nine items ranges from 0 to 27, and scores 5–9 indicate mild, 10–14 indicate moderate, 15–19 indicate moderately severe, and 20–27 indicate severe depression. This instrument provides a reliable estimate of depression in the communities, and PHQ-9 is the main screening tool that will be utilised. A score of ≥10 on PHQ-9 has been found to be indicative of clinically significant depression needing further assessment and treatment. The tool has been translated into Hindi and Telugu and validated for use in India [45].

Suicidal ideation attributes scale (SIDAS) [46]—SIDAS is designed to screen individuals in the community for the presence of suicidal thoughts and assess the severity of these thoughts. It consists of five items, each targeting an attribute of suicidal thoughts: frequency, controllability, closeness to attempt, level of distress associated with the thoughts, and impact on daily functioning. Responses are measured on a 10-point scale. Total SIDAS scores are calculated as the sum of the five items, with controllability reverse scored (10 = 0, 9 = 1, …, 0 = 10). Total scores range from 0 to 50. Items are coded so that a higher total score reflects more severe suicidal thoughts.

The Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST) [47]—The ASSIST consists of eight questions covering tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, cocaine, amphetamine-type stimulants (including ecstasy), inhalants, sedatives, hallucinogens, opioids, and ‘other drugs’. A risk score is provided for each substance, and scores are grouped into ‘low risk’, ‘moderate risk’, or ‘high risk’.

The Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [48]—It collects information on physical activity participation in three settings (or domains) as well as sedentary behaviour, comprising 16 questions. The domains are activity at work, travel to and from places, and recreational activities. The Hindi translation has been validated [49], and the tool was translated and back translated into Telugu.

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC–10 items) [50]—This is a 10-item scale that measures resilience or how well one is equipped to bounce back after stressful events, tragedy, or trauma. The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale measures several components of resilience including the ability to adapt to change, the ability to deal with what comes along, the ability to cope with stress, the ability to stay focussed and think clearly, the ability to not get discouraged in the face of failure, and the ability to handle unpleasant feelings such as anger, pain, or sadness. The Hindi and Telugu translations of the scale have already been done by the creators.

Quality of Life Brief Version (WHOQoL-BREF) [51]—This tool is used to measure quality of life which is assessed based on a four-domain structure which includes physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and the environment. It has 26 items, each tapping into certain aspects of quality of life. The questions are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, and the domain scores are transformed into 0 to 100 ratings, higher scores reflecting better quality of life. It has been validated in Hindi and Telugu.

The Social Network Index (SNI) [52] measures social connectedness across 12 relationship types. These include relationships with a spouse, parents, parents-in-law, children, other close family members, close neighbours, friends, workmates, schoolmates, fellow volunteers, members of groups without religious affiliation, and religious groups. One point is assigned for each type of relationship (possible score of 12) for which respondents indicate that they speak (in person or on the phone) to persons in that relationship at least once every 2 weeks.

Adverse event reporting

Serious adverse events will be recorded after the start of the intervention phase. A serious adverse event is defined as death due to any cause in the intervention or control arm, hospitalisation due to psychiatric disorders, or a history of self-harm or attempted suicide during the intervention period. These events will be captured using a standardised case report form and reported to an independent Data Safety Monitoring Committee Chairperson on a monthly basis and Ethics Committee Chairperson within 24 hours of the implementing team being aware of the incident. This data will be analysed during the Data Safety Monitoring Committee meetings held in accordance with the terms of reference.

To address safety concerns, one formal interim analysis will be conducted approximately when 50% of participants will have 6-month data available. At this time, we will analyse the 6-month suicide score of 50% of the population. If the interim analysis shows a benefit from the intervention, the study will proceed as planned, as the sustained effect at the end of the study will remain an ongoing question. A recommendation to discontinue the study prematurely will be based upon there being clear evidence that the intervention provides harm. Haybittle-Peto boundaries will be used, meaning that an increase in the proportion of participants with a suicide risk score ≥ 2 at 6 months in the intervention arm of more than 3 standard deviations (or a p value < 0.0027 against the control arm) would be regarded as strong evidence of early harm. The DSMC will reveal the unblinded results to the Trial Steering Committee if, based on statistical issues, clinical issues, and exercising their best clinical and statistical judgement, the unblinded results provide sufficient evidence that the trial treatment is on balance harmful.

Statistical considerations

A total of 105 slum clusters will be recruited into the study (35 per study arm) with 25 adolescents recruited per cluster (2625 adolescents). In New Delhi, 54 slum clusters will be selected (18 in each arm), while in Hyderabad/Secunderabad, 51 slum clusters will be selected (17 in each arm). Assuming a parallel cluster design with a baseline measure [54], this sample size will provide at least 90% power to detect a standardised mean difference ≥ 0.25 between arm 1 and arm 3 or arm 2 and arm 3, assuming an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.13, cluster auto correlation of 0.87, individual auto correlation of 0.26, a coefficient of variation of cluster sizes of 1.01, a two-sided significance level of 0.025 (to account for two main comparisons), and a 20% loss to follow-up over 12 months. Assuming a mean score of 5.1 (SD = 5.3) in the control arm and 3.8 (SD = 4.8) in the intervention arm (estimates are from another study in adolescents [36, 55]) will result in a standardised mean difference of around 0.25. The sample size was calculated using a web-based tool, the Shiny CRT Calculator [56].

Primary and secondary effectiveness outcomes will be analysed using intention to treat (ITT) principle, at the participant level using a mixed model for repeated measures to account for within-cluster correlations. The main analysis will consist of a linear mixed model with the PHQ-9 score collected at 6 and 12 months as the dependent variable and the following fixed effects: baseline PHQ-9 score, visit as a categorical variable (6 or 12 months), intervention arm (arm 1, 2, or 3), and the interaction between visit and intervention. A repeated participant effect will be included to model within-participant correlation due to multiple visits. A random cluster effect will be added to model within-cluster correlations, assuming an exchangeable correlation structure. The effect of each intervention at 12 months (primary timepoint) will be estimated from the model as the mean difference and 97.5% confidence interval. The same model will be used to estimate the effect at 6 months. Sub-group analyses will be defined following blinded baseline analyses and will include site and patient-level characteristics (demographic and clinical factors, e.g. depression severity at baseline). A detailed statistical analysis plan will be developed prior to unblinding and database lock. The statistical analysis plan will also have details of how missing data will be dealt with and will be available in a public domain.

Post-trial phase

The post-trial phase will comprise three components: an economic evaluation, process evaluation, and policy symposia.

Economic evaluation

A trial-based cost utility analysis will be undertaken to address the study objective of determining whether social media is more cost-effective than life skills education. A cost-consequence analysis will also be undertaken to assess the longer-term costs and outcomes beyond the trial intervention period. The within-trial cost utility analysis will be conducted to compare the incremental difference in costs between each trial arm with the incremental difference in the effectiveness, or utility, of each trial arm as measured by change in health-related quality of life. Intervention costs will be collected prospectively during the trial from programme expenditure records and key informant interviews with intervention deployment field staff in each trial arm. Utility will be measured in each participant in each intervention arm using WHOQoL-BREF [51]. Quality-adjusted life years (QALY) will be derived by mapping in-trial WHOQoL-BREF data to the Euroqol 5 Dimensions 5 Levels (EQ5D-5L) using a validated mapping algorithm [57] and an incremental cost per QALY value generated.

We will also conduct a cost-consequence analysis to capture the spectrum of benefits from each implementation strategy beyond those reflected in the cost per QALY estimates, considering a broader range of costs and consequences prospectively accrued to each arm of the trial beyond the trial period. These costs and consequences will be compared across each trial arm to inform a broader understanding of the value for money of investment in each intervention.

Process evaluation

The NASSS framework [58] will provide an overarching framework to help guide and structure the approach to understanding intervention adoption and impact. This will help identify factors that promote and inhibit the incorporation of a complex intervention into routine practice. Several data sources will be used including site-specific analyses of trial data, usage analytics of the social media platform, and in-depth, semi-structured interviews with key stakeholders. Purposive sampling using the principle of maximum variation will be used to select the participants for the qualitative study [59]. This approach will be helpful to understand the diverse factors which impact implementation in various contexts. Survey and interview instruments will be iteratively developed, guided by overarching theories, and tested prior to implementation. A mixed methods analysis of quantitative and qualitative data will be conducted by a multidisciplinary team comprising the lead academics.

Policy symposia

Three policy symposia will be conducted at different points of time in the study (formative phase, trial implementation phase, and post intervention phase) to determine opportunities for incorporation of the implementation strategies into existing and/or expanded programmes. The first policy symposium will be held during the formative phase and will present the findings from the feasibility testing along with the theory of change. The second symposium will be held in the trial implementation phase with a focus on discussing implementation constraints and barriers and understanding policy maker priorities. This information will be used to assist with the process evaluation. The third policy symposium will be conducted in the post intervention phase of the study to disseminate study findings and to engage government, non-government, and industry stakeholders to discuss transitioning the intervention to a non-research environment.

Programme milestones

Prior to the commencement of the actual trial, in the preparatory phase, we will seek ethical approval, site approvals, and modification of life skill modules (both in person and social media) will be completed 30 months after the commencement of the study. Screening of the eligible adolescent population and baseline interviews of study participants will be done in six phases that will be completed by 33 months. Randomisation of slums into the two intervention arms and control arm will be done soon after the baseline is completed in each phase. Implementing the intervention (imparting life skills in person and through social media), 6-month and 12-month data collection will be completed by 39 and 45 months respectively. Publications will be done to highlight various aspects of the project throughout the project period and to share the final results. Findings will also be disseminated to various stakeholders including policy makers at a policy symposium held before the end of the study. The final trial report will be written by the researchers of ANUMATI 2.0, guided by the PI and CoIs. Professional writers will not be hired to write the trial report.

Discussion

ANUMATI 2.0 involves testing whether a life skills education programme can result in improved mental health outcomes and reduced risky behaviour among older adolescents residing in slums and at high risk of depression and/or suicide ideation. It will test the effectiveness of this programme and compare whether there is any difference in effects when delivered through in-person face-to-face sessions or virtually via commonly used social media applications. It builds on learnings from previous research studies conducted by the research team on the mental health of adolescents living in slums. The evaluation incorporates a robust outcome evaluation design and process evaluation to generate knowledge on the future spread and scale-up of the programme, should the results be promising. The findings are likely to advance locally relevant knowledge on how best to overcome entrenched inequities in community-based health promotion strategies for underserved populations.

Strengths and limitations

Study strengths are that the intervention is targeting multiple risk factors for mental health and other NCDs among adolescents in marginalised urban communities in India. LSEPs in high-income countries have demonstrated significant reductions in smoking, alcohol, substance use, and improvements in self-efficacy. However, these have not been successful when applied to the LMIC context [60–62], underscoring the need for locally developed interventions to improve adolescent resilience. By co-creating the life skills modules with the adolescent expert advisory group, ANUMATI 2.0 will be able to provide a contextually relevant model that has a greater likelihood of being feasible and acceptable among adolescents living in slums. Further, if the social media arm proves to be more cost-effective than the in-person arm, then it would be a novel strategy suitable for implementation in resource-constrained settings. Study limitations include (1) implementation of the study in two regions and potential lack of generalisability to other settings; (2) potential for contamination across study arms, although careful selection of participating slums will mitigate this; and (3) follow-up time limited to 12 months, although this is substantially longer than most studies published to date in the literature.

Significance and potential contributions to the field

By placing this research at the intersection of policy, industry, health care providers, and consumers, we aim to generate evidence that can inform decision-making for system planners and is of relevance to supporting the Government of India’s National Programme for Youth and Adolescent Development (NPYAD) and the Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (RKSK) programme. Such approaches have the potential to positively impact wellbeing and reduce NCD risks for millions of adolescents in some of India’s most marginalised communities and may have wider applicability for other LMICs. Furthermore, this study will provide insights into LSEP as a potential method to reduce depression in LMICs and promote mental wellbeing and resilience in community-based settings. It will also provide evidence on the effectiveness of two implementation strategies (in person and social media) for delivering LSEP to enhance older adolescents’ wellbeing and reduce depressive symptoms as well as other NCD risk factors in slums. Finally, the findings will generate evidence on scalable strategies to enhance resilience and reduce NCD risk factors for young people living in resource-limited settings both in India and elsewhere.
