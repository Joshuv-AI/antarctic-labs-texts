Balancing brain exposure, pharmacokinetics and safety of transferrin receptor antibodies for delivery of neuro-therapeutics
Authors: Benjamin A Smith, Caitlin B Meeks, Isabel Murray-Brown, Elizabeth A McCarthy, Mark C Julian, Joe Arndt, Matthew Stewart, Kerri Spilker, Nadia D’Lima, Jana Dolnikova, You Li, Shikha Prasad, Fang Qian, BangJian Gong, Di Jiang, Long Yuan, Taras Tuczkewycz, Joseph Amatucci, Rachel Doyle, Akiko Wagatsuma, Fu-Chia Yang, Blake Pepinsky, Paul H Weinreb, Thomas O Cameron, Adem C Koksal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProgress in developing effective large-molecule therapies for neurological diseases is limited by exposure at the sites of action, beyond the blood–brain barrier (BBB). While transferrin receptor (TfR1)-mediated transport is gaining validation as a mechanism to deliver medicines of multiple modalities to the brain, there is much still to be learned about maximizing the potential of TfR1 targeting. We systematically vary for the first time affinity and valency of two anti‑TfR1 antibodies, which have distinct epitopes and pH sensitivities, to investigate their effects on cellular trafficking, biodistribution, safety, and pharmacokinetics. We establish how transcytosis and receptor degradation trend with affinity and connect these in-vitro functions to in-vivo behaviors in brain uptake and reticulocyte depletion. We identify unique anti-TfR1 antibody profiles for either short-term maximal or long-term sustained brain delivery and demonstrate the utility of these shuttles for different pharmacological applications. Our results show that bivalent anti-TfR1 antibodies can be equally effective in brain uptake as monovalent antibodies if engineered to have similar cell-surface TfR1 binding strength, with prolonged brain exposure and less severe adverse effects, but epitope, as well as affinity and valency, factors into selecting a shuttle with maximal performance. These results challenge the view that monovalent formats are inherently superior and instead establish that affinity, valency, and epitope can be tuned to select TfR1 shuttles optimized for different therapeutic needs.KEYWORDS: blood–brain barrier (BBB), transferrin receptor (TfR1), receptor-mediated transcytosis, Antibody engineering, brain delivery, valency, affinity tuning, antibody–oligonucleotide conjugates, bispecific antibodies, ted platform

Introduction

There is substantial unmet medical need in treating neurodegenerative diseases, due in part to the challenges associated with delivery and sustained exposure of biotherapeutics across the central nervous system (CNS) from systemic administration.1 For immunotherapies, the blood–brain barrier (BBB) presents both a highly restrictive and extensively intercalated surface. As a network of blood vessels with ~20 m2 total surface area and within ~20 µm reach of every neuron in the human brain, the BBB provides access to all regions of the CNS.2,3 It is well established that antibodies or other biological agents that target the transferrin receptor (TfR1) can undergo receptor-mediated transcytosis at the BBB, resulting in enhanced brain exposure relative to non-targeted antibodies.4–12 Furthermore, the coupling of biotherapeutics to anti-TfR1 antibodies can improve bioavailability and pharmacodynamic effects in the CNS, and such strategies have realized clinical success.13–17 However, challenges remain in finding the optimal biomolecular design of the brain delivery agents for each therapeutic application, as TfR1 targeting also increases biodistribution and disposition in peripheral tissues and can induce undesirable effects on iron homeostasis and hematology.18

The effects of antibody affinity5,8–10,18–20 or valency6,21 for TfR1-mediatated brain delivery have been studied previously. Early work with an anti-mouse TfR1 antibody showed a clear benefit of monovalent over bivalent antibody format in efficient trafficking across brain endothelial cells, avoiding lysosomal retention/degradation, in vitro and in vivo.6 However, it is unclear if this bivalent antibody was limited in BBB transport because of valency directly or the corresponding impact of valency on binding strength through avidity. The approach of varying antibody affinity to optimize brain exposure by balancing differing effects on TfR1 engagement at the BBB, TfR1-mediated transcytosis, and systemic clearance in peripheral tissues has been demonstrated with both monovalent5,18,19,22 and bivalent8,9 antibodies. The latter examples indicate that bivalent anti-TfR1 antibodies can indeed be engineered to have substantially improved CNS exposure over non-targeted antibodies. However, a direct comparison of brain exposure of a monovalent antibody with optimized affinity and the corresponding bivalent antibody with optimized affinity has not been demonstrated to date. For biotherapeutic drug development, there is an advantage to understanding the utility of bivalent antibody shuttles, as these symmetric antibodies do not require Fc-heterodimer engineering and are more easily produced with high purity.23 Other antibody properties, including epitope, orientation in complex with TfR1, and pH sensitivity of binding, may also play a role in BBB transcytosis,6,12,24–27 but are more difficult to investigate systematically. As such, mechanistic statements based solely on affinity or valency may not hold true for every antibody.

Beyond the mechanistic challenges in maximizing transport, there are risks in targeting TfR1 with antibodies for delivery of biotherapeutics. The primary role of TfR1 in iron homeostasis, both in delivery of iron into the nervous system and in the development of erythrocytes,28 raises safety concerns even if direct inhibition of transferrin binding to TfR1 is avoided.18 While the most severe adverse effects of TfR1 targeting are observed for antibodies with effector function (e.g., acute lethargy, spasms, drop in body temperature, cytokine release), depletion of reticulocytes (immature erythrocytes) has been observed even for antibodies with markedly reduced effector function.18,29,30 Antibody-induced loss of TfR1 in vitro and in vivo has also been reported,31 which could limit sustained drug transport efficiency or disrupt iron homeostasis. Clinical trials with anti-TfR1 enhanced therapeutics have met with mixed success, and in some cases have noted signs of acute anemia.32–35 Thus, while affinity-dependent, antibody-induced TfR1 degradation and reticulocyte depletion has been demonstrated previously, there is value in investigating these effects further for a large panel of antibodies with a broad range of properties (affinity, valency, epitope, pH-sensitivity), to better understand the balance of functional brain delivery and safety for TfR-shuttled therapeutic design.

Here, we describe the development of our TEDTfR platform comprising a diverse set of anti-TfR1 antibodies and demonstrate enhanced CNS exposure for a variety of pharmacological applications, including biologics and oligonucleotide neurotherapeutics. Systematic affinity modulation of two exemplary antibodies with well-matched cross-reactivity to human and cynomolgus monkey TfR1 are characterized in both monovalent and bivalent formats. Optimal design of antibodies for cellular transport, short- or long-term brain exposure, and minimal TfR1 loss and reticulocyte depletion are described. Clear trends are identified between these functional properties and cell-surface TfR1 affinity, whether antibodies are monovalent or bivalent, but notable exceptions are also found. Overall, this anti-TfR1 platform comprises a brain delivery toolbox for tailored therapeutic applications and mitigates the common risks associated with TfR1-mediated tissue delivery.

Results

Discovery and engineering of a diverse panel of anti-TfR1 antibodies

To interrogate the relationships between structure, binding properties, and function of anti-TfR1 antibodies, two parent clones were selected for further engineering to generate a diversified panel. The first antibody, TEDTfR-v1, was identified from a naïve human IgG yeast display library, and the second antibody, TEDTfR-v2, from B-cell repertoire isolated from a rabbit immunized with recombinant TfR1 ectodomain. Both antibodies were selected for binding human and cynomolgus monkey TfR1 without blocking holo-transferrin binding to TfR1. These antibodies have distinct monovalent affinities for human TfR1 extracellular domain (ECD), as assessed by surface plasmon resonance (SPR): equilibrium dissociation constants (KD) were measured to be 40 nM for TEDTfR-v1 and 2 nM for TEDTfR-v2, at 25°C and neutral pH 7.4, and have good cross-reactivity to cynomolgus monkey TfR1 ECD (Table 1). Despite similar selection criteria to avoid interference with transferrin binding, TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2 also have distinct epitopes, as shown by cryo-electron microscopy of antibody antigen-binding fragments (Fab) in ternary complexes with TfR1 ectodomain and holo-transferrin (Figure 1A). Notably, TEDTfR-v1 contacts both the apical and protease-like domains of TfR1, such that the Fab is oriented perpendicular to the TfR1 dimer interface, whereas TEDTfR-v2 contacts only the apical domain and is oriented away from the plasma membrane interface of TfR1. These antibodies are also notably different in the pH sensitivity of their binding, such that TEDTfR-v1 losses binding to TfR1 under more acidic conditions (pH 5.5), whereas TEDTfR-v2 does not (Figure 1B). Moreover, since it is the level of TEDTfR-v1 binding response rather than the KD value that changes with decreasing pH, it appears that the pH sensitivity is associated with loss of availability of binding epitope on the TfR1 ECD that rather than the specific activity of TEDTfR-v1 itself. While there are no histidine residues as direct contacts in the TEDTfR-v1 epitope, which might explain the pH sensitivity due to histidine side chain protonation at pH ~6, the loss of binding could result from a conformational change in TfR1 that disrupts the TEDTfR-v1 epitope without affecting the TEDTfR-v2 epitope. Therefore, the properties of epitope and pH sensitivity of TEDTfR-v1 are intertwined.

Guided by insight into the structure of the antibody-TfR1 contacts, mutations were introduced in both parent antibodies to modulate binding affinity while maintaining cross-reactivity between human and cynomolgus monkey TfR1 (Fig. S1). In total, 13 variants of TEDTfR-v1 and 14 variants of TEDTfR-v2 were generated (with 1–4 amino acid substitutions per variant), most of which were produced as both monovalent and bivalent antibodies (Table 1). To minimize potential adverse reactions when administered in mice, these antibodies were generated with reduced effector function by the aglycosylation mutation N297Q in the Fc and, for TEDTfR-v2 variants, by changing the isotype of the CH1 and CH2 domains to hIgG4. These variant antibodies were characterized in monovalent format for binding to TfR1 ECD by SPR and demonstrated a range of affinities of KD = 40 to ~6000 nM for TEDTfR-v1 and KD = 2 to ~16000 nM for TEDTfR-v2, many of which maintained less than a threefold difference in affinity for human and cynomolgus monkey TfR1 (Table 1, Figure S2).

Depending on the context, either the monovalent affinity or the avidity of bivalent antibodies may be the more relevant property for understanding target-mediated biodistribution, and therefore both properties were characterized for downstream analysis. To compare monovalent and bivalent antibodies for binding cell surface TfR1, considering both affinity and avidity of binding, Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells were engineered to express human TfR1, and flow cytometry was used to detect antibody bound to hTfR1-CHO following incubation of cells with antibody at a range of concentrations at 4°C (Fig. S3). The strength of this cell surface binding interaction is reported as the antibody effective concentration to reach 50% receptor saturation, or EC50 (Table 1). In some cases, the monovalent affinity of an antibody variant was too weak to measure reliably, but the cell surface binding of that variant as a bivalent antibody was characterized (e.g., TEDTfR-v2.13). In many cases, the EC50 for bivalent antibody binding is ~100-fold lower than for monovalent antibodies bearing the same mutations, reflecting the effect of valency on strengthening binding through avidity. It is important to note that the avidity effect in this binding assay may not accurately reflect avid binding to TfR1-expressing cells in vivo, as this is an over-expression cell line with measurements made at non-physiological temperatures, and receptor expression level and temperature can both impact bivalent binding.36 For monovalent antibodies, some of the measurements of the cell surface EC50 (at 4°C) align closely with the monovalent KD measured by SPR (at 25°C), while others do not (Table 1), underscoring that assay conditions like antigen surface density/immobilization and temperature can affect the absolute affinities measured, even before considering the avidity component of binding for bivalent antibodies. Nevertheless, we provide both sets of measurements for comparison of trends of functional properties of anti-TfR1 antibodies to either EC50 or KD properties, as described herein.

Cellular transcytosis is optimized by affinity modulation

To characterize the impact of anti-TfR1 affinity on trans-cellular trafficking, antibody variants were tested in a cellular barrier transcytosis model. Here, Madin-Darby canine kidney II (MDCKII) epithelial cells, which, when cultured on transwell membranes, establish cell–cell contacts that are sufficiently tight to restrict paracellular antibody flux, were engineered to express human TfR1 at levels that are comparable to human cerebral microvascular endothelial cell D3 model (hCMEC/D3 cells; Figure S4). hCMEC/D3 cells are a common model to study human BBB cellular functions, characterized to have a brain endothelial phenotype and TfR1 expression, but do not form a tight enough barrier in vitro for use in antibody transport assays.37,38 Transcytosis of TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2 variants, in monovalent and bivalent format, measured as an apparent permeability (Papp) of antibodies over a 2-day incubation at 100 nM with hTfR1-MDCKII cells, showed a bell-shaped dependence on cell-surface affinity of these antibody variants (Figure 2A). From this data, the optimal range of cell-surface affinities (EC50) for maximal transcytosis (Papp > ~1 × 10−5 cm/s) was ~1 to ~50 nM. Surprisingly, this relationship between transcytosis and cell-surface affinity was comparable for both TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2 variants, in either monovalent or bivalent format, despite differences in valency, epitope, and pH dependence of binding. However, there were some notable exceptions where a few monovalent antibodies, with EC50 from ~10 to ~50 nM, had superior transcytosis rates (Papp > 2 × 10−5 cm/s) relative to other antibodies in the same cell-surface affinity range. Thus, while a general relationship between cell surface affinity and transcytosis was observed regardless of other antibody characteristics, the highest performing individual antibodies were identified as outliers to this trend.

Degradation of cellular TfR1 is minimized by weakening affinity

Since our data confirm that the binding properties of anti-TfR1 antibodies affect their trans-cellular trafficking,6,31 we questioned whether the trafficking of the receptor itself was also dependent on antibody affinity and valency for TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2 variants. We subjected hCMEC/D3 cells to treatment with high-concentration (1 M) of TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2 variants in monovalent and bivalent format for 24 h and measured the remaining level of TfR1 in cell lysate by capillary Western blot. Treatment with either the parent TEDTfR-v1 or TEDTfR-v2 bivalent antibodies induced a loss of TfR1 expression, and this effect was reversed by inhibiting lysosomal activity (Figure 2B). For the affinity variants, a general trend between TfR1 degradation and the strength of cell-surface binding (EC50) was observed (Figure 2C), but the details of this relationship were not the same for the bivalent as for the monovalent antibodies. For bivalent antibodies with cell surface binding EC50 > 10 nM, TfR1 loss was limited to <50% and almost completely avoided for EC50 > 100 nM. Although the monovalent antibodies also demonstrated decreased TfR1 degradation with weaking affinity, they generally showed more TfR1 degradation than the bivalent antibodies with similar apparent cell surface affinity. To better understand this discrepancy, we compared the monovalent affinities (KD) of the antibodies, which are weaker for bivalent antibodies than those of the monovalent antibodies in the same EC50 range (Table 1) and asked which measure of affinity is a better correlate for antibody-induced TfR1 degradation. While neither EC50 nor KD is a universal determinant of TfR1 degradation for all sets of antibodies, there is a better correspondence of the degradation effects in this assay with monovalent binding properties than the measured cell surface interaction (Fig. S5).

Differentiated pharmacokinetic and brain distribution profiles for anti-TfR1 variants

Given the breadth of binding and functional properties characterizing our anti-TfR1 variants, we sought to explore the impact of these properties on in vivo biodistribution. TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2 variants in either bivalent or monovalent format were administered, at 20 mg/kg molar IgG equivalent (130 nmol/kg) intravenously (IV), to mice with a human TfR1 sequence (hTfR1 knock-in (KI) mice). Antibody concentrations were quantified in serum and brain lysate samples harvested either one or 7 days after dosing.

At day 1, all bivalent antibodies were present in serum at similar concentrations (1000–3000 nM), comparable to a non-targeted control IgG (~2500 nM), with only a slight trend toward higher levels for weaker binding antibodies (Figure 3A). Monovalent antibodies, on the other hand, were at lower levels (200–1000 nM) and had a stronger trend with affinity. This difference in peripheral clearance could be due to either format/size dependent clearance or that the bivalent antibodies engaged TfR in the periphery in a monovalent binding mode (at least at the dose used here) and thus clearance scaled with monovalent affinity (Fig. S6). Therefore, only bivalent antibodies were tested for long-term biodistribution. One-week post-dose, there was a strong effect of affinity on clearance from serum (Figure 3B), with the stronger binding antibodies (EC50 < 1 nM) dropping to <10 nM concentrations in circulation, whereas the weakest bivalent antibodies (EC50 ~ 100 nM) were still circulating at ~600 nM, nearly equivalent to the concentration measured at day 1. This demonstrates the advantage of the weak affinity bivalent format in minimizing peripheral clearance while still maintaining significant TfR1 binding through avidity.

In brain lysate, a complex relationship between exposure and affinity was observed. Bivalent affinity variants of both TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2 had elevated exposure in brain lysate (10–15 nM vs ~2 nM for a control IgG) 1 day after IV administration for a broad range of cell surface affinities, EC50 ~0.5 to 50 nM (Figure 3C). Conversely, monovalent antibody variants generally showed optimal brain uptake in the range EC50 ~20 to 200 nM, with decreasing brain exposure for either higher or lower affinities outside this range. However, the maximal brain exposure reached by monovalent TEDTfR-v1 variants (~24 nM) was greater than that of monovalent TEDTfR-v2 variants (~16 nM) or either of the sets of bivalent antibody variants. Thus, the distinct binding properties of affinity-tuned monovalent TEDTfR-v1 may support optimal TfR1-mediated brain delivery.

At day 7 post IV administration, a different trend of brain exposure versus affinity emerges, relative to the observations at day 1 (Figure 3D). Here, brain levels of anti-TfR1 antibodies increased with weakening affinity for TfR1, with notably higher brain biodistribution for antibodies with cell surface binding characterized by EC50 > 10 nM compared to the stronger binding antibodies and control IgG. This parallels the effect of weakening affinity on long-term serum exposure (Figure 3B), suggesting that increasing brain exposure with weakening affinity is a result of reduced clearance into peripheral tissues. However, TEDTfR-v1 weak-affinity variants are found at higher levels in brain (~10 nM) than TEDTfR-v2 variants (~4 nM) in the same affinity range, despite similar levels in serum, suggesting an advantage of affinity-optimized bivalent TEDTfR-v1 in sustained BBB transport. While antibodies with EC50 > 100 nM were not tested for 7-day biodistribution, we expect there to be a drop in brain exposure due to reduced TfR-engagement and BBB transport, as seen in vitro (Figure 2A). Thus, the optimal affinity for long-term brain exposure is shifted to weaker affinity relative the optimal range for short-term delivery for bivalent anti-TfR1 antibodies, depending on the use case.

To examine the biodistribution of anti-TfR1 antibody variants within the brain of hTfR1 KI mice following IV administration, a brain hemisphere from each mouse was analyzed for hIgG content by immuno-histochemistry (IHC). Increased hIgG staining was observed in the brain sections for mice dosed with anti-TfR1 antibodies compared to mice dosed with control hIgG 1 day after IV administration (Figure 4), consistent with the brain lysate hIgG quantitation results. Importantly, for all antibodies that demonstrated significantly elevated uptake in brain lysate (>10 nM), increased hIgG staining was observed in the brain parenchyma in addition to the microvasculature, with some evidence of further optimization of parenchymal exposure for particular antibodies (e.g., TEDTfR-v1.09 bivalent and TEDTfR-v2.12 bivalent). Additionally, within this panel, more hIgG staining of neuronal cell bodies was observed for the higher affinity variants than for the weaker affinity variants, despite similar parenchymal exposure levels. By 1 week after dosing, hIgG staining in the brain parenchyma had faded for the higher affinity antibodies, but some staining persisted for the weaker variants, again consistent with the quantitation in brain lysate. In some cases, vesicular staining of hIgG was observed within neuronal cell bodies, suggestive of endo/lysosomal accumulation. Thus, the intra-CNS distribution of anti-TfR1 antibodies is not only dependent on TfR binding properties, but is also highly dynamic and associated with vascular, parenchymal, and neuronal biodistribution.

Reticulocyte depletion by TfR1 antibodies depends on multiple antibody properties

Reticulocyte depletion is an expected adverse effect of TfR1 antibodies, commonly associated with immune response mechanism. To evaluate this response for our anti-TfR1 antibodies, we generated exemplary TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2 variants as monovalent antibodies (TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2.04 monovalents, selected based on high brain uptake activity) with three different constant domain frameworks with differing degrees of effector function: wild type hIgG1 > aglycosylated hIgG1 > hIgG1 with the LALAPG mutations for effector silencing. In hTfR KI mice, these antibodies induced different degrees of reticulocyte depletion in whole blood sampled 24 h following IV administration (at 20 mg/kg IgG equivalent dose), with full effector function variants resulting in nearly 100% loss and the LALAPG mutants resulting in minimal variability in reticulocyte levels (0–20% loss) (Figure 5A). However, for aglycosylated antibodies with intermediate effector function, very different levels of reticulocytes remained for the TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2.04 example antibodies, despite similar affinities for TfR1.

Points are individual mice, lines are means, and error bars are standard deviations. To better understand the properties of TfR1 antibodies that affect the reduction of circulating reticulocytes in mice, we tested a broad panel of TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2 variants for reticulocyte depletion 1 day following IV administration (20 mg/kg IgG equivalent dose). While both sets of antibody variants were generated with reduced/partial effector function through mutations that eliminate Fc glycosylation, TEDTfR-v2 variants were expressed with hIgG4 CH1 and CH2 constant domains to suppress complement-mediated Fc function. Despite these differences in residual effector function, the induced loss of reticulocytes mostly followed a single trend with the cell-surface TfR1 binding strength (EC50) for TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v2 bivalent affinity variants as well as TEDTfR-v1 monovalent variants (Figure 5B). However, the exception to this general trend was that the monovalent TEDTfR-v2 variants, even those with weak affinity, reduced reticulocyte counts significantly more than the other antibody variants. This suggests that while the primary determinants of reticulocyte depletion by anti-TfR1 antibodies are Fc effector function and affinity, additional factors such as epitope and valency can accentuate reticulocyte loss.

Anti-BACE1/TfR1 bispecific antibodies have improved pharmacology compared to anti-BACE1 alone

To demonstrate the utility of our anti-TfR1 brain targeting antibodies to deliver therapeutic cargo to the CNS, we designed and generated bispecific antibodies targeting both BACE1 and TfR1. The anti-BACE1/TfR1 bispecific is a useful model therapeutic to assess enhanced CNS delivery, as the BACE1 inhibitory antibody produces a pharmacodynamic response of lowering soluble amyloid beta (A1–40) levels in brain only while antibody concentrations are maintained above ~10 nM (as measured in brain homogenate), which is typically only achieved with high doses (100 mg/kg or more) of the monospecific antibody, or by enhancing delivery through BBB transport.5,12,39 Here we generated anti-BACE1/TfR1 bispecifics using anti-TfR TEDTfR-v1 as both bivalent and monovalent fusions to the C-terminus of a bivalent BACE1 antibody. hTfR KI mice were administered a single 50 mg/kg IgG molar equivalent IV dose of either control non-targeted hIgG, anti-BACE1 alone, or either of these two bispecific antibodies. Plasma and brain pharmacokinetics (PK; hIgG levels) and brain pharmacodynamics (PD; amyloid beta A1–40 levels) were monitored for up to one week.

Both bispecific antibodies demonstrated faster plasma clearance, but enhanced brain exposure compared to either the non-targeted or BACE1 control antibodies (Figure 6). The bispecific with the monovalent anti-TfR1 shuttle cleared faster in plasma than the bispecific with the bivalent shuttle, suggesting that valency is a stronger determinant of peripheral disposition than the strength of binding to cell surface TfR1. In brain, the monovalent shuttle provides a higher maximal exposure, but was more dynamic, dropping to similar levels as for the bivalent shuttle by one week. Nevertheless, both monovalent and bivalent shuttles provided sufficiently enhanced brain exposure to elicit a sustained PD effect, with amyloid beta A1–40 levels in brain dropping to ~50% of control for the entire week.

Anti-TfR1 conjugation provides differential delivery and productive uptake of oligonucleotides in the CNS

As another example of the potential utility of our anti-TfR1 TEDTfR-v1 shuttle technology for delivery of therapeutic cargo to the CNS, we generated antibody-oligonucleotide conjugates and tested these for productive delivery in knockdown of target mRNA in the brain of hTfR1 KI mice following IV administration.40 An antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) targeting the metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (Malat1)41 was conjugated to either monovalent or bivalent anti-TfR1 TEDTfR-v1, in a 1:1 antibody-ASO complex. Following four weekly IV injections of 1.5 mg/kg ASO equivalent in hTfR KI mice, brain hemispheres were collected and analyzed separately for ASO content (PK) and target mRNA expression (PD).

Mice administered with TEDTfR-v1-ASO conjugates had much higher levels of ASO in brain than mice administered with either unconjugated ASO or ASO conjugated to a non-targeted hIgG control antibody, which translated into a significant knockdown of Malat1 mRNA (Figure 7). The monovalent TEDTfR-v1 conjugate also demonstrated significantly higher delivery and activity than the bivalent TEDTfR-v1 conjugate, lowering target mRNA by ~50%. Similar patterns of activity were seen in spinal cord as in brain across the different treatment groups, although target mRNA was lowered further in spinal cord than brain (~62% knock-down for the monovalent TEDTfR-v1-ASO conjugate). In peripheral tissues the effect of antibody conjugation and TfR targeting on ASO activity were different. In muscle (tibialis anterior as an example), there was a trend in knockdown of ~15% for both unconjugated and non-targeted antibody conjugated ASO, but substantial knockdown (65–70%) for both bivalent and monovalent TEDTfR-v1-ASO conjugates. In liver tissue, whereas ASO alone induced ~75% knock-downs, all antibody-ASO conjugates, both the TfR-targeted and non-targeted hIgG, were less active in liver than unconjugated ASO.

Thus, our anti-TfR1 antibodies have demonstrated improved activity in two different therapeutic modality contexts, a biologic and an oligonucleotide, although the choice of the antibody variant/format for optimal PK/PD may differ depending on the type of cargo and desired pharmacology.

Discussion

Development of efficacious biologics and genetic medicines for the treatment of neurological disorders, enabled by technologies that enhance exposure in the CNS, is beginning to achieve clinical success following several decades of research, and has the potential to transform the landscape of therapies for patients suffering from these debilitating diseases.1,13,42,43 Of these delivery approaches, TfR1-targeting antibodies are the most well validated for improving brain uptake through receptor-mediated transport at the BBB. However, the molecular properties that contribute to the optimal balance of BBB transport, peripheral disposition, and safety, tailored for specific pharmacological applications, is still not fully understood.

This study describes the characterization of two broad families of anti-TfR1 antibodies, targeting different epitopes, systematically modulated to investigate whether there is a single optimal profile, defined by affinity and valency, for maximal BBB transport and brain exposure. We demonstrate that while elevated brain exposure can be achieved for a diverse set of molecular profiles (both monovalent and bivalent antibodies across a broad set of affinities), the antibodies with highest performance in short-term maximal brain uptake or long-term sustained brain exposure have a narrow and distinct set of properties, including affinity, valency, and epitope. Within this spectrum of delivery profiles, there are varying degrees of adverse effects such as TfR1 receptor degradation and reticulocyte depletion, which scale differently with molecular properties than does CNS biodistribution. Therefore, careful examination of the full set of structure-function-safety features should be considered in selecting the appropriate anti-TfR1 antibody for each potential therapeutic delivery application.

At a cellular level, TfR1-mediated brain uptake is related to trafficking in brain microvascular endothelial cells. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain how some antibodies demonstrate preferential trafficking through transcytosis pathways, avoiding lysosomal degradation.6,31 Antibodies that bind too strongly to TfR1 in this endo/lysosomal sorting pathway, either through affinity or avidity, tend to remain trapped in these vesicles, and thus weak affinity,31 monovalency,6 and pH-sensitive binding24 have all been proposed to promote efficient transcytosis. Sub-cellular trafficking studies have suggested that transcytosis is regulated by sorting tubules and that bivalent TfR1 antibodies form and remain associated with receptor clusters that are less efficient than monovalent antibody-receptor complexes at entering these tubules.44 However, it is clear that bivalent anti-TfR1 antibodies are not categorically negative for BBB transport, particularly if affinity is weakened,8,9 where increased transcellular trafficking is also linked to a shift in association with early vs late endosomal or lysosomal markers for these weak-affinity bivalent antibodies.45 Indeed, our results demonstrated that bivalent antibodies belonging to two families defined by distinct binding epitopes on TfR1, and with a broad range of affinities, exhibited transcytosis activity in vitro and elevated brain exposure (>5x over control non-targeted IgG) in vivo. For this set of antibodies, cellular transcytosis activity is nearly completely described by a single bell-shaped relationship with affinity for cell surface TfR1, across monovalent and bivalent mAbs, regardless of pH sensitivity of binding. The few outliers are a subset of monovalents with affinity in the range of 20–50 nM that exhibit exceptional transcytosis rates. However, while transcytosis has a bell-shaped dependence on affinity, antibody-induced TfR1 degradation scales in proportion to affinity, and is different for monovalent versus bivalent antibodies, with weak affinity bivalent antibodies (EC50 > 10 nM) inducing less receptor degradation than monovalent antibodies in the same cell surface affinity range. Thus, rather than promoting lysosomal trafficking, our data suggest that weak affinity bivalent antibodies can avoid this pathway more than monovalent antibodies, possibly through more dynamic binding properties or binding modes that promote monovalent engagement with the receptor along trafficking pathways.21 The cellular trafficking of both antibody and receptor must be considered in balancing function and risk of anti-TfR1 based BBB transport.

In mice, the maximal brain exposure for our two sets of bivalent antibodies and one of the two sets of monovalent variants is nearly identical, at ~15 nM. However, the exception to the general behavior is that only the monovalent TEDTfR-v1 variants reach up to ~24 nM brain concentration. It is notable that the antibodies with high performance in brain uptake are generally well predicted by the hTfR1-MDCKII transcytosis model (Fig. S7). The differentiation of TEDTfR-v1 over TEDTfR-v2 may relate to the epitope, pH sensitivity, or geometry of binding TfR1, which cannot be resolved from our data alone. The TEDTfR-v1 variants were also found to be advantageous over the TEDTfR-v2 variants in long-term brain exposure in bivalent format, despite no class difference in circulating antibody levels. Thus, the data suggests that there is an intrinsic property of TEDTfR-v1 that provides benefit over TEDTfR-v2 in sustaining brain exposure in weak-affinity bivalent form, which could relate to reduced degradation of TfR1 at the BBB, reduced clearance from the brain, or reduced antibody degradation within the brain. Another limitation of TEDTfR-v2 is that in monovalent format it more strongly depleted circulating reticulocytes than the other sets of antibodies tested, despite having reduced effector function. Since reticulocyte loss is strongly associated with immune cell engagement through Fc effector function, it seems likely that geometry of binding TfR and Fc presentation on the reticulocyte cell surface may relate to the underlying molecular mechanism for this differential effect of monovalent TEDTfR-v2 variants. Overall, while some common trends are found in cellular functions and in vivo biodistribution through systematic modulation of affinity/avidity for cell surface TfR1, antibodies with exceptional behavior can also be identified by exploring a holistic set of binding properties, including affinity, valency, epitope, geometry, and pH sensitivity.

Taken together, these findings reveal several generalizable trends that can guide the design of TfR1 shuttles, albeit with important exceptions as described above. First, transcytosis efficiency follows a bell-shaped dependence on cell-surface binding strength, with intermediate affinities supporting transport. Second, stronger binding is consistently associated with increased TfR1 degradation and reticulocyte loss, reflecting a safety trade-off distinct from brain biodistribution. Third, monovalent formats achieve higher short-term peak exposure, whereas bivalent formats engineered with appropriately tuned affinity can achieve more sustained circulation and exposure. These design principles provide a framework for selecting shuttle properties that align with the pharmacological requirements of different therapeutic modalities.

In developing anti-TfR1 antibody delivery technology to enhance brain exposure of neurotherapeutics, it is important to recognize that each application may have different requirements. There are several considerations, including 1) the therapeutic pharmacology, for example driven by either short-term maximal concentrations or long-term sustained exposure in the CNS; 2) the therapeutic cargo, which may contribute to the biodistribution, molecular stability, and pharmacokinetics; 3) the differential distribution and biological effect in peripheral tissues, relating to expression of both TfR1 and the therapeutic target; 4) cell-type specific targeting within the CNS; and 5) the safety profile and tolerance for effects on hematology and iron homeostasis. For these reasons, there is potential advantage of a diverse set or toolbox of anti-TfR1 antibodies, as described here, for selecting the optimal shuttle, tailored for each application. This toolbox aspect of our platform is not found in other brain delivery technologies that focus on exclusively monovalent TfR1 targeting,6,10,12,19 a limited set of affinity variants,8,9,46 or just short-term delivery.20 As an initial proof of concept, our data from two model therapeutic systems demonstrate high performance of our anti-TfR1 shuttle technology for neurotherapeutic applications.

In the BACE1 model, the goal is to have sustained suppression of Aβ production in the brain. While either monovalent or bivalent TEDTfR-v1 shuttles on the anti-BACE1 antibody resulted in sustained effects over the course of a one-week study, brain exposure of the bispecific with the bivalent shuttle was more stable than with the monovalent shuttle. These differences in brain PK could have resulted from differences in plasma clearance, BBB transport, TfR degradation, or antibody catabolism in brain. Furthermore, the increased short-term brain exposure provided by the monovalent shuttle did not translate into increased biological effect, due to saturation of BACE1. Therefore, for this application, the benefits of bivalent shuttles, particularly those with weakened affinity, having prolonged pharmacokinetics (likely due to preferential distribution to tissues with high TfR1 expression density47), less impact on TfR1 expression and reticulocyte levels, and simplicity of molecular design for scalable manufacturing,23 may outweigh the higher but transient increase in brain exposure that a monovalent shuttle provides.

For oligonucleotide therapeutics, short-term delivery can provide a lasting effect on target mRNA knock-down, due to retention of ASOs in the CNS.48 Antibody-ASO conjugates also have short circulating half-lives due to rapid clearance and molecular instability.40,49,50 Therefore, the goal for an anti-TfR1 shuttled ASO is to provide rapid CNS delivery and maximize functional uptake in the brain cell types of interest. For this application, a monovalent shuttle with maximal short-term brain uptake, and stronger targeting into cells with low TfR1 expression, may be the right choice. Indeed, we demonstrated improved PKPD for the monovalent TEDTfR-v1-ASO conjugate over the bivalent comparator, but it is yet to be determined how affinity, pH sensitivity, and other shuttle properties, as well as the physiochemical properties of the linker50,51 and ASO,48,52 affect the functional delivery. An additional advantage of the anti-TfR1 conjugate approach for oligonucleotide delivery is that the activity in peripheral tissues is altered, increasing activity in muscle but decreasing activity in the liver, which could have desirable effects depending on the therapeutic strategy.

Overall, through our systematic engineering approach, we have developed a diverse toolbox of anti-TfR1 antibodies with a broad spectrum of functional properties, demonstrated the value of both bivalent and monovalent shuttles, highlighted the complexity of the role of binding modes for cellular TfR1 engagement and trafficking, and showcased the versatility of this technology in application to diverse therapeutic modality contexts. Yet, there is still work to be done to understand which of the shuttles described here is best suited for each therapeutic approach. Future engineering efforts to further optimize CNS-targeted delivery could explore additional epitopes, avidities and antibody formats, selecting shuttles in combination with the therapeutic cargo, improving cargo stability or harnessing cargo-mediated distribution, and engaging novel delivery mechanisms by pairing this anti-TfR1 shuttle technology with targeting of additional receptors on the BBB53 or brain cell-types of interest54,55 to reach new levels and specificity of delivery.

Materials and methods

Reagent and cell line generation

Human and cynomolgous monkey TfR1 extracellular domains with an N-terminal 8X histidine tag were cloned into a mammalian expression vector under the control of cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter. An N-terminal signal sequence preceded the His tag to facilitate secretion of the recombinant protein into the culture media. Protein expression was carried out either by transient transfection of CHO-S cells using Fectopro (Polyplus) at 36°C, 5% CO2, 160 rpm, 60% humidity followed by batch production at 28°C for 9–14 days or by stable CHO cell line generation, followed by batch production for 14–21 days. Conditioned media were collected by centrifugation and filtration through 0.45 µm filters. Proteins were purified by loading supernatants on NiSepharose excel resin, eluting with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 500 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole-HCl, pH 7.4, then further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 column. Material was stored in a PBS buffer with 200 mM NaCl and 10% glycerol for stability.

Full-length sequence of human or cynomolgous monkey TfR1 gene was cloned into a lentiviral vector under human EF1a promoter or retroviral vector under murine stem cell virus (MSCV) promoter and Ires-Puromycin resistance cassette. VSV-G pseudotyped lentiviral or retroviral particles were generated in human embryonic kidney (HEK293T) cells. Lentiviral particles were used to transduce MDCK II (ECACC 00062107) or CHO-S cells from which endogenous hamster TfR1 was knocked out by CRISPR-Cas9 manipulation. Retroviral particles were used to transduce Ba/F3 cells. Transduced cells were selected by successive passaging in media with 2 µg/ml Puromycin (Gibco, A1113803). Human TfR1 expression was verified by staining with 10 µg/ml Alexa647-labeled human transferrin (Thermo Fisher T23366) or appropriate antibodies against TfR1 followed by analysis by flow cytometry.

Antibody discovery

TEDTfR-v1 was selected from the human synthetic Adimab yeast libraries as described previously.56 Multiple sequence diverse libraries were incubated with recombinant human TfR protein and enriched for binders via two rounds of magnetic bead enrichment and one round of flow cytometry. Two subsequent flow cytometry rounds isolated binders with cyno cross-reactivity and a lack of transferrin ligand competition. Following selection, individual clones were isolated and sequenced. Unique hits were produced in yeast and purified using Protein A columns and eluted using acetate buffer and subsequently neutralized to pH7 with HEPES.

TEDTfR-v2 was generated from rabbits immunized with recombinant human and cyno TfR protein as described previously.57 Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from rabbits with positive serum titer against Ba/F3 cells over-expressing TfR. Recombinant cyno TfR was used to pan for reactive B-cells, and supernatants from the captured and sub-cultured hits were used to screen for human and cyno TfR binding via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and for lack of transferrin ligand blocking via ELISA. Positive hits were sequenced via RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and PCR amplification of rabbit VH and VL genes. Sequences were subsequently cloned and expressed recombinantly in CHO-S cells and purified via Protein A columns.

Recombinant antibodies from both yeast libraries and rabbit immunizations were evaluated for human and cyno cross-reactivity via FACS binding against Ba/F3 or CHO-S cells over-expressing TfR. A lack of transferrin ligand blocking was determined via competitive ELISA or BLI-Octet screening. Yeast-derived antibodies passing this screening funnel were sub-cloned into mammalian expression vectors and produced recombinantly in CHO-S. Rabbit-derived antibodies with desired binding profiles were humanized via complementary-determining region (CDR) grafting into consensus human frameworks, and sub-cloned into mammalian expression vectors for recombinant production in CHO-S cells.

Cryo-EM of antibody Fab-TfR1-Tf complexes and structure guided mutagenesis

To identify the molecular basis for antigen recognition of anti-TfR TEDTfR-v1 and TEDTfR-v1, a cryo-EM structure was generated of the ternary complex of the Fabs of these antibodies, huTfR1 ECD, and holo human transferrin (Sigma T0665) to a resolution of 3.6 Å. Atomic models of the complexes were obtained by fitting a homology model of each Fab and available crystal structures of TfR1 and Tf into the cryo-EM density map. This revealed that TEDTfR-v1 binds to a conformational epitope spanning the apical and protease-like domains of TfR1, whereas TEDTfR-v2 binds exclusively within the apical domain.

There are 17 residues from TfR1 and 19 residues from the TEDTfR-v1 Fab that are within 4 Å of the binding partner. The discontinuous epitope is formed by residues Q285, T286, K287, P289, E343, D352, C353, P354, S355, K358, D360, S361, R364 from the apical domain and S492, D560, T561, and R579 from the protease-like domain of hTfR1. The antigen-binding site of TEDTfR-v1 is formed by all six CDRs (H1, H2, H3, L1, L2, and L3) and is composed of S32, S33, S40, S60, S61, S66, Y67, K109, Y110, R111, and A112 of the heavy chain, and S33, S39, Y40, N69, Q109, and S110 of the light chain, plus additional framework contacts from Y57 and S83 of the light chain (Fig. S1A).

Positions of the TEDTfR-v2 paratope to mutate were selected by examining the primary sequence and the three-dimensional structure of TEDTfR-v2 with TfR1 (Fig. S1B). The amino acids alanine, histidine, and aspartate were chosen as residues with diverse structures and properties that are often well tolerated in antibodies. In some cases, mutation to Ser was tested (VL-N30S, VL-N32S, VL-Y111S) to increase humanness and thereby reduce immunogenicity.

Antibody expression and purification

Antibody heavy and light chains were cloned into a standard mammalian expression vector with an N-terminal signal sequence for secretion into culture media. Antibody heavy chains were expressed on various Fc regions, including aglycosylated (N297Q) hIgG1 (TEDTfR-v1 variants) or chimeric hIgG4(CH1,CH2)/hIgG1(CH3) S228P/N297Q (TEDTfR-v2 variants). Monovalent versions were generated by co-expression of an Fc, and hetero-pairing was facilitated via knob-in-hole mutations (T366W, T366S/L368A/Y407V).58 Shuttled anti-BACE1 bispecifics were designed by fusion of anti-TfR TEDTfR-v1 Fab to one (monovalent) or both (bivalent) C-termini of anti-BACE1 bivalent antibody via 2X G4S linkers. hIgG1 with effector function-attenuating mutations (L234A L235A P329G) was used. Knob-in-hole mutations facilitated hetero-Fc pairing where appropriate. Mutations in the anti-BACE1 Fab (CH1: L128R K147R; CL: Q124E V133Q T178E) and the anti-TfR Fab (CH1: L145Q K147E S181E; CL: T129R T178R T180Q) were used to promote proper light-chain pairing.55

Antibody expression was carried out by transient transfection of CHO-S using Fectopro (Polyplus) at 36°C, followed by batch production at 28°C for 9–14 days. Conditioned media were collected by centrifugation and filtration. Antibodies were purified by loading supernatants on Protein A Sepharose FF, eluting with 25 mM sodium phosphate, 100 mM NaCl, pH 2.8, and then neutralizing with 500 mM sodium phosphate pH 8.6. Secondary purification was performed using size-exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200) as appropriate to remove aggregate and/or heterodimer mispairings.

To generate anti-TfR1 antibody/ASO conjugates, a bivalent and a monovalent forms of TEDTfR-v1, as well as a control hIgG, were produced with a cysteine modification for conjugation (S442C). A 3–10-3 cEt gapmer antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) targeting Malat141 with potency of ED50 = 0.3 µmol/kg/week for reducing Malat-1 mRNA in mouse liver following three weekly doses was synthesized (WuXi) and functionalized for conjugation by reaction with maleimide-PEG2-succinimidyl ester (SM(PEG)2, Thermo 22,103). Antibodies were reduced (1 mM TCEP, 60–90 min, room temperature (RT)), loaded on a Mabselect SuRe column, reoxidized (2 mM DHAA, 2 hr RT), washed, eluted (25 mM NaPi pH 2.8, 0.1 M NaCl), neutralized to pH 5.0, and then reacted at ~1:1 molar equivalent with maleimide-ASO. Conjugates with 1:1 Ab/ASO ratio were isolated by anion exchange chromatography (TMAE column, elution with 10 mM NaPi pH7.0, 0.6 M NaCl) and confirmed by SDS-PAGE and UV spectrophotometry. Final pools were dialyzed into PBS, and protein concentrations were determined by the BCA assay (Thermo 23225).

Surface plasmon resonance analysis of monovalent affinity for TfR1 ECD

Monovalent binding kinetics were measured by SPR on a Biacore 8K+ instrument (Cytiva). Histidine-tagged human or cynomolgus monkey TfR1 ECD was immobilized at 20-40RU on a Series S CM5 sensor chip (Cytiva #2914603) using a His Capture Kit (Cytiva #29234602). Serial dilutions of monovalent antibodies were injected at 30 µL/min for 240 s, followed by a 600 s dissociation in running buffer (HEPES, EDTA, Surfactant P20, bovine serum albumin (BSA), pH7.4). For TEDTfR-v1, monovalent antibodies were injected in threefold serial dilutions at appropriate concentrations ranging from 4.1–1000 nM, 12.3–3000 nM, or 37–9000 nM. For TEDTfR-v2, monovalent antibodies were injected in fourfold serial dilutions at appropriate concentrations ranging from 0.98–1000 nM or 7.8–8000 nM. Data was analyzed using Biacore Insights Evaluation Software (Cytiva), applying a 1:1 Langmuir kinetic binding model for calculation of ka, kd, and KD. Data with dissociation rate constants greater than 0.3/s were analyzed using a steady-state model to determine KD values only.

To determine pH sensitivity of binding, SPR analysis was performed as described above with the system equilibrated in a running buffer (HEPES, EDTA, Surfactant P20, BSA) adjusted to pH 5.5, 6.0, 6.5, or 7.4. Additional data analysis included Rmax calculated as a percentage of the expected signal based on surface receptor density.

Flow cytometry analysis of binding to cell surface TfR1

CHO cells expressing full-length human TfR1 without endogenous hamster TfR1 were maintained as suspension cells in chemically defined proprietary media. Cells were resuspended in FACS buffer containing PBS with 1% BSA and incubated with antibodies serially diluted threefold, starting at 75 or 2000 nM for 1 hr on ice. After 1 hr, cells were washed three times with FACS buffer, followed by staining with a fluorescently labeled anti-human Fc reagent (1:200, Jackson #109-116-098) for 1 hr on ice. Cells were washed twice with FACS buffer, fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min at RT, washed once more, and then resuspended in FACS buffer for data collection. Samples were run on a BD FACSCalibur, and data was analyzed in FlowJo software to acquire the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the fluorophore. Data was plotted as MFI versus antibody concentrations, and curves were fit to a sigmoidal log(dose)-response function to generate EC50 affinity measurements, effective concentration for 50% saturation of the cell surface TfR1. For molecules in which the binding curves do not show a clear upper plateau, an approximate EC50 is reported, and additional uncertainty is indicated with a “~.”

Transcytosis assay

MDCKII cells expressing full-length human TfR1 were maintained in EMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1x Glutamax, 1x Pen/Strep, and 2 µg/mL puromycin. Cells were plated (25,000 cells/insert) on 24-well HTS transwell plates (Corning #3379) in media without puromycin added. Cells were incubated for 5 days, with a media change on day 4, and then antibodies were diluted in the media and added to the apical chamber to a final concentration of 100 nM. Cells were incubated with antibodies for 48 hr, and then media from the basolateral chamber were collected. Antibody concentration in the basolateral chamber was quantified using a standard anti-human IgG sandwich-format electrochemiluminescence immunoassay on a Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) platform. Samples were incubated on MSD plates (MSD #L15XB-3) coated with anti-human IgG capture reagent (Jackson #709-006-098), detected with SULFO-TAG labeled anti-human antibody detection antibody (MSD #R32AJ), and quantified by interpolation on a standard curve generated for each test antibody. Antibody apparent permeability coefficient (Papp) is reported according to the following equation: Papp=dQdt×1A×C0, where dQ/dt is the amount of antibody transported per time, A is the surface area of the membrane, and C0 is the initial concentration of antibody.

TfR1 degradation assay

hCMEC/D3 cells (Millipore #SCC066) were maintained in Endothelial Cell Medium (ECM, ScienCell #1001) on collagen-coated surfaces. Cells were seeded in 12-well plates coated with rat-tail collagen 1 at a density of 30,000 cells/well and cultured for 3 days. On day 3, cells were treated with 1000 nM of anti-TfR1 antibodies or a non-binding isotype control and incubated for 24 hr. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with pre-chilled DPBS and then lysed with RIPA buffer containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Sigma: PhosSTOP, cOmplete) on ice for 30 min. Total protein content was analyzed by BCA Protein Assay (Pierce #23227) so that lysates could be normalized to 100 µg/mL. TfR1 levels were quantified using a capillary-based Western blot (ProteinSimple, Jess), following manufacturer’s kit instructions (ProteinSimple #SMW004). Lysates were probed for TfR1 (ThermoFisher #13-6800) and housekeeping genes (GAPDH, Novus Biologics #NB300-221 or beta-actin, R&D Systems MAB8929) and then detected with chemiluminescent secondary antibodies (ProteinSimple #DM002). Normalization across the samples was achieved using the Total Protein Detection Module (ProteinSimple #DM-TP01). Results are reported as % TfR remaining compared to cells treated with non-binding isotype control, set at 100%.

Hu TfR KI mouse generation

To maximize human epitopes while allowing for maintenance of native murine TfR function, a human TfR mouse model was generated by partial replacement of mouse exons encoding the extracellular domain of TfR with corresponding human exons. The humanized region of the construct also included human intronic segments. The humanized segment was knocked into the mouse Tfrc genomic locus using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing (Taconic) to allow for gene expression under the control of the endogenous mouse Tfrc promoter. The construct, along with a plasmid encoding Cas9 and specific guide RNAs, was co-transfected into C57BL/6N ES cells (Taconic). Targeted ES cell clones were validated by Southern blot analysis followed by injection of targeted ES cells into diploid blastocyst-stage embryos for chimera generation.

Animal care

All mouse studies were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Biogen Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All mouse studies used homozygous male and female human TfR KI mice between 8 and 12 weeks of age.

In vivo biodistribution

Antibodies were administered IV through tail-vein injection in human TfR KI mice (hTfR KI) at 20 mg/kg molar IgG equivalent. After 1 or 7 days, mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (100/10 mg/kg, intraperitoneal (IP) injection), blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture, and mice were perfused, through the left ventricle, with ice-cold PBS/Heparin (1 µg/mL) at 2 mL/min for 10 min to clear the vasculature of blood. Blood samples were analyzed for complete blood cell count and reticulocytes (IDEXX). Additionally, serum was generated by allowing blood to clot for 15–30 min at RT and clarified by centrifugation at 2000 g for 10 min. Brains were removed and hemisected with one hemisphere flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and the other hemisphere fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 hr. Frozen brain hemispheres were homogenized in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.25% Na deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP40, cOmplete Protease inhibitors) with zirconium oxide beads (ZROB05 and ZROB10) in a tissue homogenizer (NextAdvance Bullet Blender) for 10 min and then incubated at 4°C, rotating for 1 hr. Lysates were then cleared of debris by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 20 min.

Human IgG levels were analyzed in serum and brain lysate by MSD immunoassay. Samples were incubated on MSD plates (MSD, Cat# L15XB-3/L11XB-3) coated with anti-hIgG Fc capture reagent (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 709-006-098), detected with sulfo-tagged anti-hIgG (MSD, Cat# R32AJ-1), and quantified by interpolation on a standard curve generated for each test antibody. Formalin-fixed brain hemispheres were transferred into PBS, paraffin embedded, separated into six 5 mm coronal segments, sectioned 3–5 mm thick, proteinase-K treated for antigen retrieval, and stained for human IgG (Southern Biotech 6145–01, 2ug/ml, then Leica Refine HRP polymer and DAB chromogen).

BACE1 PKPD study

BACE1/hTfR bispecific, BACE1 monospecific, or control hIgG was administered in hTfR KI mice at 50 mg/kg molar IgG equivalent IV. After either 1, 3, or 7 days, animals were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine IP, blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture, vasculature was perfused with ice-cold PBS/heparin, and brains were harvested, as described above. Frozen brain hemispheres were lysed and separately analyzed for total hIgG (as described above) and amyloid beta 1–40 (Aβ1–40) by an MSD immunoassay (V-PLEX Aβ Peptide Panel 1 4G8 Kit).

Malat1 PKPD study

Antibody-ASO, ASO, or DPBS vehicle control was administered at 1.5 mg/kg ASO molar equivalents four times weekly IV in hTfR KI mice. To prevent an anti-drug antibody response in the mice, anti-mouse CD4 GK1.5 (BioXcell BP003-1) was administered at 20 mg/kg IP 4 days before the first and third doses of the test articles. Mice were sacrificed 3 days after the last dose, and brain hemispheres, spinal cord, tibialis anterior, and liver were harvested and flash frozen. A brain hemisphere from each animal was analyzed for free ASO accumulation using LC-MS/MS (see below). The tissues were homogenized in TRIzol reagent (Qiagen). Total RNA was then isolated using PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen) combined with in-column DNA digestion with DNase I (Qiagen). Reverse transcription was performed using SuperScript VILO Master Mix (Invitrogen) to generate cDNA. The Q×200AutoDG Droplet Digital PCR System (Bio-Rad) was used to detect and quantify transcripts of Malat1 and house-keeping gene according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Q×200Automated Droplet Generator (Bio-Rad) provided microdroplet generation, and PCR was performed on the T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). Plates were read on the Q×200Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad) and analyzed using QuantaSoft Analysis Software (Bio-Rad) to quantify copies of transcripts per well. The expression level of Malat1 was normalized to that of Actb, and this was further normalized to the level in vehicle-treated animals.

Concentrations of free Malat1 ASO in brain lysate were determined using a hybridization LC-MS/MS method adapted from a previous publication with modification.59 Briefly, brain samples were homogenized with 9x (v/w) of the lysis buffer. The yielded tissue homogenates were incubated with streptavidin magnetic beads coated with biotinylated DNA probe complementary to Malat1 ASO. The ASO analyte in tissue homogenates was captured on the beads, washed, and eluted to a new plate using heat denaturation, and the extracted ASO was then quantified by LC-MS/MS. The LC separation was achieved using a Clarity Oligo-TX column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 µm, Torrance, CA, USA) on a Sciex ExionLC AD ternary UHPLC system (Framingham, MA, USA) with mobile phase A (water), mobile phase B (acetonitrile), and mobile phase C (250 mM HFIP and 150 mM DMCHA in acetonitrile). The total flow rate was 0.50 mL/min, with 0.45 mL/min mobile phase A/B and 0.05 mL/min mobile phase C. The gradient for the LC separation was 0% B to 20% B from 0.2 to 2.5 min, followed by washing and equilibration. The MS/MS detection was performed on a Sciex QTRAP 6500+ mass spectrometer (Framingham, MA, USA) operated in electrospray negative ion mode. The multiple reaction monitoring transitions used were m/z 772.1 → 95 for Malat1 ASO and m/z 879.5 → 95 for its internal standard, an analog ASO.
