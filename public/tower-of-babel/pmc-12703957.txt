Common and rare variant analyses reveal genetic factors underlying idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and its shared aetiology with severe COVID-19
Authors: Athanasios Kousathanas, Christopher A. Odhams, James Cook, Yao Hu, Stephan Klee, A. Mesut Erzurumluoglu, Fidel Ramirez, Christoph Mayr, Dennis Schäfer, Lara Beck, Ingrid Christ, Taekyu Lee, James Christopher Tarr, Steven W. ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and debilitating respiratory disease with limited therapeutic options. Genetic association studies for IPF have identified several associations and probable effector genes that could not only help understanding IPF pathogenesis but also develop effective treatments. Assessing genetic overlap between IPF and severe COVID-19, an acute respiratory disease that can trigger pulmonary fibrosis, may reveal shared aetiology and mechanisms, thereby supporting the development of common treatments.MethodsWe carried out genome-wide association studies (GWAS), post-GWAS, and rare variant analyses using whole genome sequencing data from the 100,000 Genomes Project IPF cohort (n = 586). We performed a meta-analysis combining 100 kGP with published IPF GWASs (total 11,746 cases and 1,416,493 controls). We tested inhibition in vitro for a probable effector gene of an identified association. We also investigated genetic colocalisation between IPF and severe COVID-19 and leveraged their genetic correlation through multi-trait meta-analysis for discovery.FindingsIPF meta-analysis identified an additional association at 1q21.2 (rs16837903, OR [95% CI] = 0.88 [0.85, 0.92], P = 9.5 × 10−9), which was replicated in independent data. MCL1, one of the probable effector genes of the 1q21.2 signal has a known antiapoptotic role, but MCL1 inhibition in vitro did not selectively deplete senescent alveolar epithelial cells. Rare variant burden analysis identified ANGPTL7, a secreted glycoprotein involved in the regulation of angiogenesis, as an IPF candidate gene (OR [95% CI] = 28.8 [8.51, 97.4], P = 6.7 × 10−8). We discovered additional shared genetic loci between IPF and severe COVID-19 at 1q21.2, 6p24.3, and 16p13.3, with probable effector genes MCL1, DSP, and RHBDF1, implicating regulation of apoptosis, cell adhesion, and epidermal growth factor signalling, respectively. The genetic correlation between IPF and severe COVID-19 was rg [95% CI] = 0.39 [0.25, 0.53]. Using multi-trait meta-analysis, we identified and replicated an additional candidate IPF signal at 2p16.1 with probable effector gene BCL11A, a regulator of haematopoiesis and lymphocyte development.InterpretationThese findings prioritise probable effector genes mediating IPF risk and identify potential therapeutic targets that require validation, with genes colocalising with severe COVID-19 suggesting potential for developing common treatments.FundingNone.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Previous studies had used a variety of approaches to discover genetic risk factors for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), a disease with unmet therapeutic need. Common variant genome-wide association studies (GWAS) for IPF had identified several associations, implicating genes related to host defence, telomere maintenance, mTOR signalling, and cell–cell adhesion. Rare variant analyses had also uncovered stronger effect risk factors for telomere-related genes (TERT, RTEL1) mRNA stability and degradation (PARN) and spindle assembly during cell division (SPDL1 and KIF15). Given the limited data in IPF, colocalisation analyses with other related diseases, such as severe COVID-19 allowed to discover shared genetic risk factors such as MUC5B, potentially enabling development of common treatments.

Added value of this study

In this study we set out to analyse common and rare variants from whole genome sequencing data of a unique IPF cohort from the 100,000 genomes project combining results with existing datasets to amplify discovery power and expand known IPF risk factors. Through meta-analysis of GWAS results with the largest previous IPF study, we identified and robustly replicated an additional association at 1q21.2 locus. We followed MCL1, one of the probable effector genes of the associated locus with in vitro experiments to explore a hypothesis for therapeutic potential of inhibition to induce apoptosis of senescent epithelial cells. Colocalisation and multi-trait analyses with severe COVID-19 not only expanded the known shared signals between these diseases, but identified an additional IPF association with probable effector gene BCL11A, a regulator of haematopoiesis and lymphocyte development.

Implications of all the available evidence

The risk factors for IPF identified in this study can help to better understand mechanisms contributing to IPF and to develop targeting strategies that could lead to effective treatments. We set out to demonstrate this potential by performing experiments to test a specific therapeutic hypothesis of inhibition of MCL1 to selectively deplete senescent epithelial cells. Despite the unsuccessful outcome in this instance, our findings could support further experimental work on this target (or other probable effector genes) in other cell types. The identified shared risk factors with severe COVID-19 could help in developing common therapeutic strategies and drug repurposing.

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a debilitating lung disease characterised by irreversible loss of lung function. In the UK alone, around 6000 new IPF cases are diagnosed per year with a prevalence rate of about 50/100,000.1 IPF has a poor prognosis and limited treatment options that only slow disease progression rather than being curative,2,3 presenting a major unmet therapeutic need.

Identifying genetic contributors to IPF using hypothesis-free genetic studies can help to uncover potential genes and pathways contributing to IPF pathology, supporting the discovery of new treatments. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have been successful in identifying several genetic variants associated with IPF, implicating pathways involved in host defence, telomere maintenance, mTOR signalling, and cell–cell adhesion.4 As IPF is a rare disease, GWAS discovery for IPF has been powered by consortia that aggregate Biobank data around the world through meta-analysis.4, 5, 6 The latest and best-powered effort was led by the Global Biobank Meta-analysis initiative (GBMI) that assembled multi-ancestry data from 13 Biobanks and from the International IPF Genetics Consortium, to identify 25 independent genetic associations with IPF including three previously unreported loci.6 These IPF GWAS meta-analyses have mostly aggregated summary data from studies using SNP microarrays for genotyping which has limited the investigation of rare pathogenic variants contributing to IPF susceptibility. More recently, whole exome and whole genome sequencing (WES/WGS) rare variant association studies have added evidence for rare variant effects on telomere-related genes (TERT, RTEL1),7,8 mRNA stability and degradation (PARN) and spindle assembly during cell division (SPDL1 and KIF15).9,10

Another strategy to address the limited genetic data for IPF is by identifying overlaps with related diseases. One such disease is severe COVID-19, defined by SARS-CoV-2 positive individuals that were hospitalised and/or required intensive care.11 Severe COVID-19 is a respiratory disease that causes alveolar injury and has been shown to share genetic susceptibility6,12,13 and transcriptomic signatures14 with IPF. Severe COVID-19 is an acute disease but can trigger profibrotic pathways similar to those in IPF, with a fraction of survivors, especially genetically susceptible individuals, developing pulmonary fibrosis.15 In some cases, patients with COVID-19 can develop acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) leading to fulminant fibrosis that may necessitate lung transplantation.16 Therefore, studying these genetically and clinically overlapping diseases together could not only boost statistical power for target discovery but also support development of potential common treatments.

The 100,000 Genomes Project (100 kGP) is one of the world's largest rare disease projects, in which undiagnosed patients with suspected monogenic diseases were recruited and screened by WGS to aid in their diagnosis.17 100 kGP participants include people affected with familial forms of IPF, as well both prevalent and incident sporadic cases, the latter identified by data linkage with electronic health records (EHRs).

In this study, we set out to perform a meta-analysis of IPF by leveraging the 100 kGP resource and GBMI GWAS summary statistics to further enhance power of common variant analysis1 as well as to search for associations in the rare variant space enabled by the 100 kGP WGS data. We also revisited the genetic overlap of IPF and severe COVID-19 and leveraged the correlation of IPF and severe COVID-19 to amplify statistical power for GWAS discovery.

Methods

Ethics

100,000 Genomes Project: the 100,000 Genomes Project was approved by the East of England–Cambridge Central Research Ethics Committee (REF 20/EE/0035). Only participants of the 100,000 Genomes Project for whom WGS data were available and who consented for their data to be used for research purposes were included in the analyses.

Human samples for in vitro experiments: All patients gave written informed consent. The study was approved by and conducted according to the requirements of the local ethics committee at Hannover Medical School (8867_BO_K_2020).

IPF cohort

The IPF cohort was constructed from participants of the 100 kGP with available WGS data in aggregate form (the Genomics England “aggV2”). The IPF cohort comprised of the cases that were specifically recruited into the 100 kGP for familial pulmonary fibrosis (FPF) as well as ‘incidental’ cases of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) that were identified based on diagnostic ICD-10 codes in linked Electronic Health Records (EHRs). EHRs derived ICD-10 diagnoses from admitted patient care, outpatient care, and accident and emergency Hospital Episode Statistics and ICD-10 causes of death from the Office of National Statistics Mortality. To improve specificity, these definitions were supplemented with the more granular clinical terms from the 100 kGP data model (Labkey query algorithm provided in Supplementary Material). The incidental cases are expected to represent predominantly sporadic forms of pulmonary fibrosis but may also include a small proportion of additional familial cases. The primary IPF cohort was built using Genomics England data release 14, with some analytical resources derived from other releases (noted where applicable).

Statistics

When assessing sample size for this study we aimed for case counts comparable to previous individual IPF studies and contributions to IPF meta-analyses (typically n ≥ 100, Table 2) and also evaluated discovery power for a range of allele frequencies and effect sizes for calibrating our expectations for additional discoveries (GWAS analysis and power; Supplementary Material). As part of site quality control, we performed Fisher's exact tests to identify variants violating Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium and for detecting differential missingness with plink v.1.9.18 We used a mixed-effects logistic regression model for association analyses of common and rare variants with binary outcomes as implemented in SAIGE (version 0.44.6.5)19 followed by meta-analysis and post-GWAS analyses. Odds ratios were computed by exponentiation of the effect size (beta) estimates obtained from the logistic regression model fitted with SAIGE, with 95% confidence intervals computed as exp (beta ±1.96 × standard error). Meta-analysis across studies used inverse variance weighting and heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q test. LDpair20 pairwise linkage disequilibrium estimates were obtained via the web server https://ldlink.nih.gov/ using 1000G GRCh38 high coverage data. Assessment of patterns of expression from single-cell data in IPF Atlas was done by visual inspection of two dimensional UMAP explorer plots and gene Explorer boxplots through interactive visualisation, as the tool does not allow statistical analysis. Analyses for Fig. 2 were run in GraphPad Prism 10.4.1. Control and bleomycin-treated cells were compared with one-way ANOVA and paired t-tests, P-values were Tukey-adjusted for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Table S18). Due to the very small sample size per contrast, normality assumption was not tested and we relied on visual inspection of individual donor measurements for assessment of outlier effects (Supplementary Figure S14). All other statistical analyses were performed in R v3.6.

Sample quality control

We filtered for European ancestry individuals (classification probability ≥0.8; following the approach from Kousathanas et al. (2022))21 because for the other ancestries the case numbers were insufficient (n < 100). Participants were further required to have concordance between reported phenotypic sex and WGS inferred sex, assigned either XX or XY from WGS sex chromosome ploidy checks, passed internal WGS sample QC (from BAM and VCF-level metrics) based on Blood sample, EDTA extraction, Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free library preparation. Related individuals were included and for individuals with a monozygotic twin-pair (identified by KING kinship coefficient ≥0.354), one of the pair was excluded (cases prioritised).14

GWAS analysis

100 kGP GWAS site quality control

Site-QC protocol followed best practices established with international consortia for common variant GWAS analyses11 with adjustments appropriate for the analysed data as described.

Small variants (SNPs and indels) were selected with the following site-level quality control filters: we used bcftools to calculate and filter on median read depth (DP) ≥ 10, median genotype quality (GQ) ≥ 15, ABratio for het calls ≥ 25%. We also used plink v1.9 functions to filter for minor allele frequency (maf) > 0.5%, minor allele counts (MAC) > 20, site-wise missingness <2% and for differential missingness between cases and controls (Fisher's Exact Test P-value <10−5, plink option “--test-missing” with mid-p adjustment) and Hardy Weinberg Equilibrium test violations using unrelated controls (Fisher's Exact Test P-value <10−6, plink option “--hwe” with mid-p adjustment). We only kept variants where the SAIGE SPA correction converged.

Principal component analysis

We used a set of 63,523 common (maf >5%) (the “HQ common SNPs” from Kousathanas et al. (2022)21) to derive genetic principal components (PCs) for European ancestry individuals with GCTA 1.9.3.22 PCA was performed for n = 45,543 unrelated Europeans of 100 kGP (aggV2) and SNP loadings were used to project the rest of the 100 kGP cohort and to obtain the PCs to be used as covariates for the association analyses of this study (scree plot in Supplementary Figure S4).

100 kGP IPF GWAS

To perform GWAS analysis in the 100 kGP cohort, we used SAIGE (version 0.44.6.5).19 SAIGE performs mixed-effects logistic regression using a two-step approach where random effects from a multivariate normal distribution with a mean of zero and a covariance matrix defined by the genetic relationship matrix (GRM) are estimated in step 1 (null model fit) and step 2 calculates association statistics for each variant while controlling for effects estimated in step 1. This approach aims to account for relatedness among individuals as well as population stratification. We used the “HQ common SNPs”21 to construct the GRM and fit the null model for SAIGE part 1. We included sex, age, age,2 age∗sex, and the first 20 PCs calculated in Europeans as covariates in the null model fit, following recent GWAS consortia recommendations11 and to align with previous IPF and COVID-19 GWAS studies that are used for meta-analysis and colocalisation.

Meta-analysis and quality control

We contacted the Global Biobank Meta-analysis Initiative (GBMI) consortium and gained access to the summary GWAS results from their latest meta-analysis.6 This meta-analysis aggregated results from 13 Biobanks as well as another large IPF dataset described by Allen et al., 2020.4 GBMI in-house site quality control had removed variants from each biobank with imputation quality score <0.3 or allele frequencies differing from gnomAD. A total of 32,046,319 variants were present in the provided GBMI IPF summary statistics file, of which 10,781,762 were also present in the Allen et al. (2020)4 results (header column “with_Allen”). To meta-analyse with the 100 kGP IPF GWAS summaries, we included variants present in a minimum of 2 GBMI biobanks (max 13) and used inverse variance weighted fixed-effect meta-analysis as implemented in METAL v.2011-03-25.23 Cochran's Q test from METAL was used to assess heterogeneity. The meta-analysis resulted in 10,644,973 variants (set 1) that were present in both GBMI meta-analysis and the 100 kGP IPF GWAS. For downstream analyses, we further removed the following variants: (i) variants with strand flips in the GBMI dataset (i.e, strand_flip = “yes”); (ii) variants with calculated allele frequency >1 in either the GBMI or 100 kGP IPF GWAS (which can arise from erroneous calculations of the number of alleles for chrX); (iii) variants with differences in absolute allele frequency between GBMI and 100 kGP IPF GWAS >0.1. These filtering steps resulted in a set of 10,287,302 variants (set 2).

Creation of 100 kGP LD reference panel

We used an LD reference panel based on the 100 kGP cohort by creating an EUR-only cohort of 44,590 unrelated individuals (LD-100 kGP-EUR) and for a bi-allelic variant site subset of 9,044,108 variants (set 3). We used only Europeans for creating the LD reference panel as this ancestry represented 85% of the cases and 80% of the controls contributing to the IPF meta-analysis dataset.

Variant LD-clumping, conditional analysis and fine-mapping

We performed LD-clumping, conditional analysis and fine-mapping procedures using the IPF meta-analysis GWAS summary statistics for variant set 3 and the LD-100 kGP-EUR as LD-reference panel.

LD-clumping was used to collect variants in LD with GWAS signals with plink1.9 with P1 set to 5 × 10−8, clump distance 1500 kb, P2 = 0.01, r2 = 0.1.

To discover conditionally independent GWAS signals we ran a step-wise conditional analysis using GCTA 1.9.322 and --cojo-slc function.24 We used a P-value threshold of 5 × 10−8, a distance of 10,000 kb and a collinear threshold of 0.1.

To narrow down the list of likely causal variants surrounding independent signals, we performed fine-mapping using R-package SusieR v0.11.42.25 We split the genome into 3 Mbp non-overlapping windows and for each window containing at least one GW-significant variant (at P-value<5 × 10−8), we assigned the variant with the lowest P-value as the focal variant of the window and analysed the GWAS summaries and LD of variants within 1.5 Mbp on either side of each focal variant. This approach minimised the number of search windows for clustered signals while simultaneously centring on each signal.

Replication

We contacted the PFgenetics consortium (https://github.com/genomicsITER/PFgenetics) to gain access to the IPF 5-way meta-analysis summaries and 3-way meta-analysis summaries. The 3-way meta-analysis summaries had been previously included in the GBMI meta-analysis. We obtained independent IPF summary data through mathematical subtraction of the 3-way meta-analysis summaries from the 5-way meta-analysis summaries, using MetaSubtract R-package. Replication of the putatively novel signals of the present study was determined by requiring a Bonferroni-corrected P-value <0.05 in the replication 2-way summaries and that the effect size direction matched.

Variant annotation

We used Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) v.105 for mapping variant effects.26 For each variant, we selected the worst predicted consequence across transcripts as the probable effector gene unless the variant was mapped to a regulatory or an intergenic region in which cases we assigned the nearest gene.

Aggregate variant burden testing (AVT)

AVT site quality control

Site-QC protocol followed best practices established with international consortia for rare variant aggregate analyses27 with adjustments appropriate for the analysed data. We used 100 kGP aggregated data of bi-allelic variants generated with the Genomics England Data Release Version 12 and a few participants were removed due to differences in included individuals compared to the base cohorts generated with Release Version 14. This dataset had been processed with a masking procedure to effectively mask low-quality genotypes by setting genotypes with depth of coverage (DP) < 10, genotype quality (GQ) < 20 and heterozygote genotypes failing an ABratio binomial test with P-value < 10−3 to missing with bcftools setGT module, following the approach by Kousathanas et al. (2022).21 Masking was followed by a site quality control procedure that removed variants with >2% missingness and used plink v1.9 to perform a Fisher's exact test of missingness between cases and controls and filtered-out variants with P-value <10−5 before inclusion in AVT tests.

AVT masks

Small variants (SNPs and indels) were annotated using Ensembl VEP26 and a suite of additional functional and allele frequency datasets (including CADD, LOFTEE, and gnomAD). Variants annotated against all protein-coding transcripts from Ensembl VEP 105 (including canonical and non-canonical transcripts) were aggregated by gene and grouped into four variant masks shown in Supplementary Table S12. These masks were selected to align with existing AVT studies in IPF.7,9

AVT model

Aggregate variant burden testing (AVT) was performed using mixed-effects logistic regression as implemented in software SAIGE-GENE+ (version 0.44.6.5)28 that accounts for relatedness and population stratification. We used the “HQ common SNPs” for constructing the GRM and we included high quality rare SNPs from multiple allele frequency classes for computing variance ratios and fitting the null model (following the AVT approach from Kousathanas et al. (2022)21). Sex, age, age,2 age∗sex, and the first 20 principal components calculated in Europeans were included as covariates in the SAIGE-GENE + null model fit. Sensitivity analysis was performed for an alternative covariate adjustment for age using multivariable fractional polynomials29 (Supplementary Table S14).

Post-GWAS analyses

Transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS)

We conducted a transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) for lung tissue using the MetaXcan framework30 along with the GTEx v.8 (eQTL) MASHR-M model (downloaded from http://predictdb.org/) using the S-PrediXcan function. We identified significant genes using the Bonferroni correction.

Colocalisation for TWAS

We tested for colocalisation between IPF and cis-eQTL summary statistics for genes with significant TWAS results. For each significant TWAS gene, we selected eQTLs located 1.5 Mbp on either side of the transcription start site (TSS) of the gene (as defined in Ensembl v104). We used Bayesian colocalisation analysis utilising the coloc_abf function implemented in R-package coloc v5.1.0.31 We declared that a locus had significant evidence for colocalisation when both of two criteria were met: (1) At p12 = 5 × 10−5, the probability of colocalisation with a shared causal variant (PPH4) was >0.5, and (2) at p12 = 10−5, the probability of distinct causal signals (PPH3) was not the main hypothesis (PPH3 < 0.5). For each locus we determined the lead colocalisation variant as the one with the highest PPH4.

Severe COVID-19 definition and summary statistics

In this study we refer to the severe COVID-19 phenotype as defined by the COVID-19 Host Genetics Initiative (HGI) for patients who were hospitalised owing to symptoms associated with laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection. We downloaded summary statistics from website (https://www.covid19hg.org/) release version v7 for B2_ALL_leave_23andme (Hospitalised COVID-19 vs population), as this has produced the best balance between a carefully curated phenotype for severity and statistical power11,32 (Supplementary Table S15).

Colocalisation for IPF and severe-COVID-19

We tested for colocalisation of each genome-wide significant IPF signal with severe COVID-19 by selecting variants located 1.5 Mbp on either side of the lead variant and performing colocalisation analysis using coloc v5.1.0 as for TWAS described above.

Genetic correlation analysis

To compute an estimate of the genetic correlation (rg) between IPF and COVID-19 we used ldsc (https://github.com/bulik/ldsc).33 For this analysis, we used pre-computed European ancestry LD-score weights for GRCh38 coordinates. We used the files with prefix “weights.hm3_noMHC” that are split by chromosome. Running ldsc was performed in two steps: (1) Cleaning the summary statistics of each analysed trait for flip strands and taking an intersection with the LD-scores variants with script munge_sumstats.py, (2) Running genetic correlation analysis on pairs of traits with script ldsc.py. 95% confidence intervals for rg were computed as rg + −1.96 × standard error from the ldsc output.

Multi-trait meta-analysis (MTAG)

To perform multi-trait meta-analysis between IPF and COVID-19 we used MTAG (https://github.com/JonJala/mtag).34 For this analysis, we used European ancestry pre-computed LD-score weights for GRCh38 coordinates. We used the files with prefix “weights.hm3_noMHC” that are split by chromosome. We performed variant clumping to collect variants in LD with GWAS signals and to help identify putatively novel signals compared to the single trait meta-analysis, a process that resulted in 71 MTAG-derived significant LD-clumps for IPF. Due to the genetic correlation between IPF and severe COVID-19 being only moderate and the MTAG assumption for a constant genetic correlation across the genome, we expected that many signals in either trait will be entirely driven by strong signals of the paired rather than the focal trait. Therefore, to reduce the number of false positives when declaring significance, we required that the P-value of the focal SNP in each MTAG IPF LD-clump was also significant in the original single trait meta-analysis at Bonferroni corrected P < 0.05 for 71 tests (number of significant clumps in the MTAG results), and that the effect sizes were consistent between the original and the MTAG meta-analyses. 13 LD-clumps passed these criteria and we identified putatively novel signals as those occurring in non-overlapping LD-clumps as well as different loci compared to the base IPF meta-analysis.

Experimental follow-up of MCL1

RNA expression analysis

After removing the media and washing with PBS, cells of each well were lysed with 370 μL RLT buffer. After lysate collection, RNA was isolated using the Mag-MAX™- 96 Total RNA Isolation Kit (Thermofisher AM1830). The kit is based on magnetic bead purification. Isolation was performed according to manufacturer's protocol. After RNA-extraction, the content of RNA was determined using the NanoDrop.

cDNA was synthesised using the high-capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Life Technologies) according to manufacturer's protocol. The cDNA was adjusted to 10 ng/mL for each run.

Quantitative real time -polymerase-chain-reactions (qRT-PCR) were ran by using the TaqMan® Fast Advances Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) and human specific gene expression assays. Gene expression analysis was performed with the ViiA7 and the Quant6 Real-Time PCR Systems and software (Applied Biosystems). The gene expression was normalised to the expression level of GAPDH which was used as housekeeping gene.

Protein purification

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed in MSD-Lysis Buffer including 1× protease-phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo ScientificTM 1861281).

Before further use, the protein concentration was to quantify with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo ScientificTM A55864) according to the instruction manual.

Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) assay

For the quantification of MCL-1, MCL-1-BAK and MCL-1- BIM complexes in protein lysates, three different Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) assays were used (K1516XR, K1518BR, K1518AR MSD). All of these are human R-Plex ELISAs and were per-formed according to the instruction manual.

Western blot

The protein lysates were diluted to the same total amount of protein, which was 300 μg or 500 μg, depending on the lowest protein level.

The Western blots (Supplementary Figures S12–S13) were performed according to the user manual to “RePlex with Jess” according to the Protein Simple protocol (device–Jess, ProteinSimple).

We used the RePlex protocol with one primary antibody (Supplementary Table S17) in combination with the Total Protein Assay.

The compass software version 5.1 was used for quantification. Calculation was done by dividing the area of antibody and area of total protein.

ApoToxGlo assay (determination of apoptosis, cytotoxicity and viability of cells)

Human primary cells were seeded 72 h before Bleomycin treatment on collagen-precoated 96-well glass bottom plates (Revvity 6005182). After 72 h, half of the plate was stimulated for 24 h with 25 μg/mL Bleomycin. After the recovery time of 48 h cells were treated for 24 h with our MCL-1 antagonists in several concentrations. Then, the ApoToxGlo- Assay (G6321, Promega) was performed according to the instruction manual to determine viability, cytotoxicity, and apoptosis of the cells.

The funders of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing.

IPF GWAS analysis and meta-analysis

100 kGP GWAS

We performed GWAS analysis for the 100 kGP IPF cohort with Ncases = 586 and Ncontrols = 52,083 on 11,646,047 common variants (MAF >0.5%) that yielded one genome-wide significant (P-value <5 × 10−8) signal at locus 11p15.5 with top variant chr11:1219991_G_T (rs35705950), P-value = 2.1 × 10−25, OR [95% CI] = 2.94 [2.40, 3.60] (Supplementary Figure S5). This is the well-known IPF-associated common variant in the promoter region of MUC5B.3

Two association signals identified in this study were not previously reported in the Partanen et al. Replication of IPF signals

To replicate the two putatively novel signals identified in the meta-analysis of this study we generated an independent IPF dataset through mathematical subtraction from another, partially overlapping, IPF GWAS dataset5 (Methods). Both variant signals had consistent direction of effects in the replication dataset, but only rs16837903 replicated with Bonferroni-corrected P-value <0.05 after adjusting for two tests (Table 3).

We assessed the novelty of these findings by cross-referencing with three additional recent studies: Allen et al., 2020,4 Allen et al. (2022),5 which analysed data partially overlapping with the Partanen et al. (2022), and Peljto et al. (2023),8 which examined an entirely independent cohort. Allen et al. 20204 identified variants previously associated with lung function and reported variant rs878471 near MCL1 (Allen: P-value = 2.94 × 10−5, OR [95% CI] = 1.15 [1.08, 1.23]; this study: P-value = 3.9 × 10−7, OR [95% CI] = 1.07 [1.05, 1.11]). Weak linkage disequilibrium (LDpair R2 = 0.23 in Europeans; Methods) between rs878471 and rs16837903 and opposite effect directions are suggestive of potentially two separate signals (see fine-mapping in Supplementary Material). Peljto et al. (2023)8 identified a non-replicated indel signal association with IPF within the third intron of MCL1 (P-value = 6.4 × 10−9, OR [95% CI: 0.77 [0.71, 0.84]), however, this variant was absent in our dataset, precluding calculating LD to evaluate whether the variants in the two studies underlie the same signal.

1q21.2/MCL1 IPF association and functional follow-up

We then performed transcriptome-wide association analysis (TWAS) using eQTL data models from GTEX v8 bulk RNAseq data to assess whether genetically determined variation in gene expression in lung might impact IPF disease susceptibility (Supplementary Figure S8; Supplementary Table S8). Top TWAS genes within locus 1q21.2 included MCL1, ARNT and ECM1, but were not supported by colocalisation evidence, presumably due to lack of power (Supplementary Table S9). MCL1 was also among the top five candidate effector genes with highest Open Target Genetics prioritorisation V2G scores (Supplementary Table S10), and stood out compared to other candidates due to its compelling anti-apoptotic role.35 We evaluated its expression in single-cell expression data generated in lung tissue for different cell types and for idiopathic lung disease (ILD) patients vs controls (GSE13589336, Supplementary Figure S11). We observed that MCL1 and CDKN1A, a gene coding for p21 and a marker of senescence, were both expressed in ATI cells as well as KRT5-KRT17+ cells (aberrant basaloid cells, intermediate of ATII cells differentiating towards ATI cells36; Supplementary Figure S11A and B).37 We also observed that MCL1 expression was increased in patients with ILD vs controls in several tissues, including alveolar epithelial type I (ATI) cells, differentiating ciliated cells, KRT5-KRT17+ cells and PLIN2+ Fibroblasts (Supplementary Figure S11E). The occurrence of increased levels of senescence is well described for IPF.38 Moreover, it has previously been shown that alveolar epithelial cells exhibit an increased level of senescence and contribute to disease progression.39,40 Considering MCL1 is an anti-apoptotic member of the BCL2 protein family and the survival of senescent cell relies on anti-apoptotic proteins35 we hypothesised that inhibiting MCL1 in senescent alveolar epithelial cells could selectively deplete these cells through apoptosis, thus removing these cells from the lung and potentially allowing for lung regeneration.39

As we wanted to test the potential senolytic effect of an anti-MCL1 antagonist but the KRT5−/KRT17+ cells cannot be isolated and cultured, we used senescent ATII cells as a surrogate for our studies. We therefore induced cellular senescence in primary human lung epithelial cells enriched for HT2-280-positive cells (marker of ATII cells) using bleomycin (Fig. 2, Supplementary Figure S14, Supplementary Table S18). CDKN1A mRNA increased over time (control vs bleomycin at 48 h, paired t test P = 0.0082; Fig. 2A), and p21 protein trended higher (48 h no bleomycin vs 48 h bleomycin, paired t test P = 0.063; Fig. 2B), consistent with senescence induction. We then analysed the expression of MCL1 as well as the complexes of MCL1 with BIM and BAK, which are important pro-apoptotic proteins of the BCL2 protein family. Binding of MCL1 to BIM and BAK can suppress pro-apoptotic functions, and conversely, releasing them from the complex can induce these pro-apoptotic functions. However, we did not observe increased expression of MCL1, and only small increases in the MCL1:BIM and MCL1:BAK complexes (Fig. 2C–E). IPF aggregate rare variant burden testing (AVT) analysis

We then expanded the analysis to rare variants that could potentially underlie additional associations with IPF by performing aggregate rare variant burden testing (AVT) analysis for the 100 kGP IPF cohort with Ncases = 569 and Ncontrols = 50,847 for bi-allelic variants with MAF <0.5% (Supplementary Table S11). We ran AVT analyses for four variant masks: loss of function (LOF), ultra-rare damaging (URD), rare damaging (RD) and flexible damaging (FD) (Supplementary Table S12). These analyses identified two genes, ANGPTL7 and TERT, that achieved study-wide significance in at least one mask for the composite SKAT-O test (Bonferroni corrected P-value <0.05 after adjusting for 73,382 tests; Table 4, Supplementary Figure S9). Rare deleterious genetic variants in TERT have been previously robustly associated with IPF,7 whereas ANGPTL7 represents a putatively novel association signal. As SKAT-O analysis does not provide odds ratios, we also examined results from the constitutive burden test. The gene-wise burden odds ratios suggested weak effects for both genes (Table 4), but examination of the individual variant statistics contributing to ANGPTL7 and TERT signals revealed that subsets of the variants had stronger effects (Supplementary Table S13). As burden testing is sensitive to variance in directionality of aggregated variants, gene-wise signals can get attenuated. Specifically, we identified a single loss of function variant rs143435072 (chr1:11193631_C_T, HGVSp p.Arg177Ter, VEP predicted effect: stop gained) as the main driver of the ANGPTL7 signal with P-value = 6.7 × 10−8 and OR [95% CI] = 28.8 [8.51–97.4] (Supplementary Table S13). Allele frequency for rs143435072 was 7.91 × 10−3 in cases and 3.05 × 10−4 in controls, the latter being similar to the allele frequency for non-Finnish Europeans (3.24 × 10−4) in GnomAD v3.1.2, indicating that control composition is unlikely to underlie this signal. IPF and COVID-19 colocalisation analysis

The availability of updated COVID-19 GWAS summary statistics compared to previous studies6 enabled us to revisit the genetic overlap of IPF and severe COVID-19 by conducting colocalisation analysis. We also re-estimated and leveraged the genetic correlation between the traits to amplify statistical power to discover additional IPF associations by performing multi-trait meta-analysis (MTAG).34 For these analyses we used the IPF meta-analysis statistics produced in this study and the latest meta-analysis summaries (Freeze 7) from the COVID-19 Host Genetics Initiative (HGI), for the Hospitalised covid vs. population phenotype (B2_ALL_leave_23andme), as this has produced the best balance between a carefully curated phenotype for severity and statistical power11,32 (Supplementary Table S15). We assessed colocalisation between IPF and severe COVID-19 for the 23 loci significantly associated with IPF identified in this study, finding significant evidence of colocalisation at six loci (Table 5, Supplementary Figure S10). Three signals at loci 11p15.5, 13q34 and 19p13.3 with lead variants with probable effector genes MUC5B, ATP11A and DDP9 reproduce signals previously reported,13 while an additional three signals at 1q21.2, 6p24.3 and 16p13.3 with probable effector genes MCL1, DSP and RHBDF1, represent putatively novel colocalisation signals (Table 5). The odds ratios for MCL1, DSP, RHBDF1 and DDP9 signals were in the same direction (all risk-increasing), supporting a similar mechanism for the two diseases, while MUC5B and ATP11A displayed opposite directions (Table 5), suggesting that although they share causal variants, the specific biological pathways mediating risk might be different. Discussion

In this study we performed the largest meta-analysis of IPF to date by integrating GWAS results from the 100,000 Genomes Project (100 kGP) IPF cohort with previous meta-analysis summary statistics to increase GWAS discovery power and we also leveraged WGS data for rare variant analysis of this cohort. The increased statistical power of common variant meta-analysis allowed us to identify and replicate an association signal at 1q21.2/MCL1 locus and rare variant burden analysis revealed a candidate gene association for ANGPTL7. We also updated the genetic correlation estimate and colocalised signals with severe COVID-19, and we leveraged that correlation through multi-trait meta-analysis to discover an additional robustly replicated association signal (at 2p16.1/BCL11A). Following up on one of the probable effector genes of the 1q21.2 GWAS signal from this study, we investigated potential mechanisms linking expression of MCL1 to IPF via in silico and in vitro analyses with a focus on the gene's known anti-apoptotic properties and exploration of this gene as a potential therapeutic target.

1q21.2/MCL1 IPF association and functional follow-up

The lead variant from the meta-analysis GWAS, rs16837903, is in the 5′UTR of myeloid leukaemia 1 (MCL1) gene and near many other genes including GOLPH3L, HORMAD1, CTSS and CTSK, all with similar variant-to-gene (V2G) prioritorisation scores (Supplementary Table S10). In the functional follow-up, we focused on MCL1 as it’s the nearest gene to the lead variant, has VEP-predicted functional consequences and a compelling biological function and targeting potential.

MCL1 is an anti-apoptotic member of the BCL-2 family, which are regulators of apoptosis41 and are involved in various (patho-)physiological events, including development, wound repair, and cancer. In a healthy environment, senescent cells initially contribute to tissue remodelling and repair and are subsequently cleared by NK cells or macrophages.42 Members of the BCL-2 protein family, in particular the anti-apoptotic members BCL2 and BCL-XL block apoptosis in these cells and thereby can contribute to IPF pathogenesis.38 Targeting BCL2 and BCL-XL in IPF might not be feasible, as current pharmacological interventions are associated with a broad range of side effects.43 Thus, the identification of MCL1 as a probable effector gene of the IPF association signal at 1q21.2 offered a potential new avenue of targeting senescent cells in IPF.

In the absence of compelling evidence for directionality of expression effects from TWAS analysis using bulk lung tissue (GTEX v8), we assessed expression of MCL1 in patients with idiopathic lung disease (ILD) vs controls using single cell sequencing data from IPF Cell Atlas.44 We observed expression of MCL1 in alveolar epithelial cells and increased levels in ILD vs controls for several cell types, including epithelial cells. However, in our follow-up experimental work we did not detect an increase in MCL1 expression in induced senescent primary lung epithelial cells from different donors. Even though MCL1 was not significantly upregulated in senescent cells, we found that pro-apoptotic members (BIM and BAK and their complexes with MCL1) were increased in the senescent cells, indicating MCL1 inhibition may have put an additional apoptotic pressure on senescent primary lung epithelial cells. We tested the effects of an MCL1 inhibitor on its ability to selectively deplete senescent primary lung epithelial cells through inducing apoptosis. However, we did not see an earlier induction of apoptosis in these cells, which was comparable to healthy primary lung epithelial cells along with effects on viability and cytotoxicity. Alongside the HT2-280-enriched cells, we have also tested small airway epithelial cells (data not shown) and observed similar results for both. In IPF, not only epithelial cells are shown to have an increased senescence phenotype, but also immune and mesenchymal cells.45,46 If MCL1 is the effector gene for the 1q21.2 association (see limitations below), our results suggest that epithelial cells might not be the prime target for an anti-MCL1 strategy to induce senolytic effects and improve the outcome for patients with IPF. Targeting other cell types using an anti-MCL1 strategy might have beneficial effects and further studies will be required to address these questions.

Clinical significance

Variant rs16837903 is common (allele frequency is 15% in Europeans) and lowers IPF risk by approximately 12% (OR [95% CI] = 0.88 [0.85–0.92]). The variant's rs16837903 effect size is too small for clinical use, but could contribute to polygenic risk stratification approaches for IPF due to its moderately high frequency.

Rare variants and ANGPTL7

Rare variant analysis identified rs143435072 as a strong risk-increasing factor for IPF that has previously been associated with lower intraocular pressure and protection against glaucoma.47 rs143435072 predicted effect is gain of a stop codon for ANGPTL7 which is within an intronic region of mTOR. mTOR is a critical regulator of fibrotic pathways in IPF pathogenesis,48,49 but rs143435072 - being in an intron of mTOR-is unclear how it could be impacting its function. Conversely, ANGPTL7 belongs to the functionally heterogeneous family of angiopoietin-like proteins (ANGPTLs)50, 51, 52 and apart from glaucoma47,53 it has also been implicated in corneal angiogenesis, and tumour growth.54

We performed a systematic review of the expression of ANGPTL7 in the lung using IPF Cell Atlas44 and four single-cell expression datasets to develop hypotheses for a potential role of ANGPTL7 on IPF (Supplementary Material, Supplementary Figures S15–S18). We observed that ANGPTL7 is specifically expressed in lung type-I alveolar epithelial cells (ATI) and its expression is either absent or reduced for patients with IPF compared to controls for three out of four the IPF Atlas datasets examined (Supplementary Figure S18). We hypothesise that loss of ANGPTL7 may contribute to IPF by disrupting interstitial ECM formation or by promoting excessive alveolar angiogenesis. Alternatively, defective secretion of the aberrant, truncated, protein might trigger ATI cell death as is believed to occur in ATII cells for surfactant mutations associated with IPF.55 A critical next step is to replicate the ANGPTL7 rare variant signal identified here in other independent cohorts. Further investigations using functional assays and mouse models could then help to elucidate potential involvement of ANGPTL7 loss in lung function and IPF pathogenesis.

Clinical significance

The stop-gain variant rs143435072 confers a 29-fold increase in IPF risk (OR [95% CI] = 28.8 [8.51–97.4]), but is very rare (allele frequency is 0.032% in Europeans). The rs143435072 variant therefore has limited clinical utility individually and additional ANGPTL7 IPF risk variants would need to be identified in larger population samples for inclusion of ANGPTL7 in gene-based screening.

IPF and COVID-19: colocalisation

Several previous studies have set out to identify shared genetic signals between IPF and severe COVID-19 to investigate potential shared aetiology and mechanisms between the two diseases.6,12,13 Following similar colocalisation analysis and using updated IPF and severe COVID-19 summaries we identified putatively novel shared risk-increasing signals at 1q21.2, 6p24.3 and 16p13.3 with MCL1, DSP and RHBDF1 as probable effector genes. The identification of colocalisation signals with an additional lung-related disease such as severe COVID-19 with consistent directions of effects further strengthens support of these associations.

We discuss hypotheses for potential common mechanisms of probable effector genes. As discussed above, dysregulation of MCL1 could contribute to IPF by enabling survival of epithelial cells exacerbating fibrosis. MCL1 could follow an analogous mechanism in contributing to severe COVID-19 through dysregulation of apoptosis for SARS-CoV-2 infected cells. This hypothesis is supported by a recent study showing that SARS-CoV-2 suppresses apoptosis in cultured cells, human organoids and mice by interacting with MCL1, a mechanism also known as virus-induced senescence.56 DSP is a key component of desmosomes, structures involved in cell–cell adhesion in epithelial tissue and cardiac muscle and is already established as a risk factor for IPF.57 A recent study implicated increased levels of desmoglein-2, another desmosome protein as exacerbating cardiac related complications for patients with COVID-19.58 This observation would suggest that risk for the DSP association might be mediated through effects on epithelial cell adhesion or on cardiac function. Colocalisation analyses with additional diseases and molecular measurements could help in narrowing down the underlying mechanism. RHBDF1 encodes an intramembrane serine protease involved in the regulation of EGFR (Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor) signalling, which is critical for cell proliferation, survival, and wound healing,59 making it a plausible gene mediating risk effects on IPF and severe COVID-19. Moreover, activation of EGFR through binding of either EGF or EGF-like ligands, such as TGF-α, triggers downstream signalling pathways (e.g., JAK/STAT) that promote the progression of cancer and virus infection and targeting the EGFR signalling pathway has been proposed as a potential therapeutic option for severe COVID-19.60

IPF and COVID-19: multi-trait analysis

In this study we also leveraged the genetic correlation between IPF and severe COVID-19 for GWAS discovery through multi-trait meta-analysis. The moderate genetic correlation between these traits (rg = 0.39) suggests that multi-trait meta-analysis is expected a priori to have limited benefits in terms of amplifying statistical signal.34 Our analysis identified five putatively novel IPF locus associations of which one was robustly replicated at 2p16.1. Association signal at 2p16.1 displayed a protective effect and had lead variant rs1123573, which is in an intron of gene BCL11A, a zinc finger protein transcription factor involved in the regulation of the developmental switch from foetal to adult haemoglobin,61 is essential for lymphocyte development62 and have previously been linked with a wide range of diseases.63 The pleiotropic association with severe COVID-19 and IPF suggests that this gene could potentially contribute to both diseases through effects on immune responses mediated by lymphocytes.

Study limitations

Our study has several limitations:

Firstly, the GWAS summaries sourced from the GBMI meta-analysis exhibit large variations in sample size across the genome, significant heterogeneity in effect sizes due to inclusion of data from both clinical and Biobank sources,6 and contain multi-ancestry cohorts, particularly of East Asian ancestry (Table 2). This heterogeneity can generate local inconsistencies between summary statistics, and linkage disequilibrium computed using a European LD-reference panel may have limited our ability to identify independent signals and perform fine-mapping. For these reasons, we strictly required non-overlap of LD-clumps as well as cytogenetic loci (which encompass large genomic regions) between the current and previous studies to declare putatively novel signals.

Secondly, to map probable effector genes for variant associations we mapped consequences for lead variants of each association with VEP and selected the gene affected or nearest gene if consequence was not genic. Our selected effector gene might not correspond to the true effector gene. Analysis on worst consequence gene in credible set and TWAS analysis integrating QTL data has highlighted additional genes that could be considered as potential effector genes to follow-up.

Thirdly, we selected MCL1 as effector gene from the 1q21.2 association to follow up with in vitro experiments due to its intriguing connection to apoptosis and our specific hypothesis in respect to the IPF mechanism involving senescent cells. Other nearby genes in the genomic region could be potential valid targets underlying the 1q21.2 association.

Fourthly, our rare variant analysis was limited by the small sample size of the 100 kGP IPF cases, potential unmeasured confounding and/or misspecification of the regression model. In particular, the estimated odds ratio and confidence interval for rs143435072, the main driver of the ANGPTL7 signal, was particularly large (OR [95% CI] = 28.8 [8.51–97.4]) and could have been inflated by sparse data bias.64 Additionally, our electronic health record lacked data on smoking behaviour, an important lifestyle covariate, that could possibly confound association analyses with lung disease.65
