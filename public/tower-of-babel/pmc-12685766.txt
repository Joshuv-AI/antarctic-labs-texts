Patterns and Predictors of Steroid Use in a Real‐World Inflammatory Bowel Disease Cohort
Authors: Rodger Wu, Consuelo Rivas, Wai Kin Su, Renée Deschenes, William Wilson, Joseph L. Pipicella, Susan J. Connor, Jane M. Andrews, Yoon‐Kyo An, Yoon‐Kyo An, Jakob Begun, Ray Boyapati, Shoma Dutt, Richard B Gearry, Simon Ghaly, Craig Haifer, Ian C Lawrance, Kate Lynch, Gregory Moore, Graham Radford‐Smith, Asif Shahzad, Michael Schultz, Heidi Su, Watson Ng, Christine Verdon, Gareth Walker, Gabrielle Wark
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatterns of steroid use in inflammatory bowel disease remain poorly characterized in real‐world settings. Steroid exposure is associated with adverse effects and often indicates suboptimally controlled disease. Therefore, patterns and predictors of steroid use in a large inflammatory bowel disease cohort were examined.MethodsSteroid exposure over a 3‐year window was explored. Use was classified by duration—short (1–28 days), moderate (29–56 days), prolonged (> 56 days), and recency (within last year, prior years or no exposure). Associations with demographic and disease‐related factors were assessed using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsAmong 5436 people (median age 42 years, IQR 32–56), 18.3% (n = 994) were steroid exposed. 57.6% had Crohn's disease and 50.2% were female. Crohn's disease was associated with lower odds of both prolonged and recent exposure compared to ulcerative colitis (AOR 0.72, p = 0.001 and AOR 0.78, p = 0.037, respectively). Females had a greater likelihood of both prolonged and recent exposure (AOR 1.22, p = 0.048 and AOR 1.23, p = 0.041, respectively). Young adults (20–29 years) had higher odds of prolonged and recent use than those > 70 years (AOR 6.59 and 9.12, respectively, p < 0.001). Combination immunomodulator and advanced therapy use was associated with a higher likelihood of both prolonged and recent use compared to 5‐aminosalicylic acid therapy alone (AOR 4.01, p = 0.002 and AOR 4.54, p < 0.001). Age at diagnosis had a modest effect size (AOR 1.03, p < 0.001).ConclusionSteroid use was modest, with over 80% unexposed over 3 years. Proactive optimization of therapy, particularly in younger individuals and those with ulcerative colitis, may further reduce steroid exposure.

Introduction

Steroids are considered a legitimate and viable treatment option in the management of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), due to the rapid induction of symptomatic improvement in patients experiencing disease exacerbation (flares) [1].

Common factors driving the use of steroids likely include historical practices; easy and cheap access compared to other therapies, representing a time advantage; familiarity, for both consumers and clinicians, and a quick response profile.

While steroids are highly effective in rapid symptom reduction, their adverse effects are well‐documented, including nausea, weight gain, osteopenia and osteoporosis, mood disturbances, deranged liver function tests, hyperglycemia, and increased risk of opportunistic infections. Furthermore, the literature emphasizes that steroids give no benefit as maintenance therapy and that conventional corticosteroids are a risk factor for serious infection and death [2, 3, 4].

Prolonged steroid exposure is increasingly recognized as a marker of suboptimal disease control and has been proposed as a key performance indicator in IBD management [1]. Given the associated risks with prolonged use, major guidelines have emphasized the importance of minimizing steroid use through early initiation of effective maintenance therapies such as immunomodulators (IMS) and advanced therapies (ADVs). Despite this, “excessive” steroid use remains a concern in routine clinical practice. There is much coverage during IBD educational meetings and the literature about “excess” steroid use, yet there are sparse data from large cohorts under routine care to help us judge whether this is a genuine clinical issue, or a marketing strategy to drive uptake of other therapies. Moreover, we know little about the demographic and/or disease characteristics that influence steroid use in real‐world, routine care.

We therefore explored steroid use in a large real‐world cohort of people with IBD in routine ambulatory care. Additionally, demographic and disease‐related variables possibly implicated in steroid exposure and clinical outcomes were examined.

Design

Observational, retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to describe real‐world patterns of corticosteroid use among people with IBD receiving routine clinical care. Secondary aims were to examine whether specific demographic, disease‐related, or treatment‐related characteristics were associated with different patterns of corticosteroid exposure.

Methods

Crohn's Colitis Care (CCCare) is a cloud‐based, IBD‐specific electronic medical record (EMR) used in several public and private centers in Australia and New Zealand (https://www.c‐c‐cure.org/cccare). These are predominantly tertiary referral centers with structured care models and represent a cohort typically seen in higher‐resourced, specialist IBD services. Deidentified data prospectively entered by clinicians and consumers during routine care flow into the paired Clinical Quality Registry (CQR). Data from the CQR were retrospectively analyzed. People with IBD under active care, defined as having had an assessment during the last 14 months were included. For these individuals, data for the preceding 3 years were included in the analysis. Data were extracted on August 15, 2024.

Prospective consent for future data use for audit and research is obtained from people when initially entering their details into CCCare. In this observational study, where only de‐identified data were used, specific ethical review was not required. Moreover, CCCare site contracts specifically endorse secondary use of deidentified data for CCCure's stated purposes of “Optimizing care now and Being instrumental in cure” for people with IBD (https://c‐c‐cure.org/).

Steroid use was extracted from structured medication fields within CCCare, specifically from the “IBD Medications & Therapies” section of the management plan. This section is reserved for medications prescribed specifically for IBD and does not include therapies for other indications which are documented separately. Steroids captured included oral prednisone, prednisolone, and budesonide, as well as intravenous methylprednisolone and hydrocortisone. Other corticosteroids not used in IBD (e.g., dexamethasone) are not captured in the IBD medication section. Steroid exposure was categorized based on both duration and recency. Duration was defined as short (1–28 days), moderate (29–56 days), or prolonged (> 56 days). These thresholds were chosen to reflect real‐world prescribing patterns, including scenarios where short tapering courses are used. Recency was defined as recent (within 365 days), previous (365–1085 days), or no exposure. Demographic and disease‐related variables examined included age, gender, height, weight, age at diagnosis, highest education achieved, and English as a second language. Health variables comprised duration of disease, admission to hospital, days out of role, opiate use, patient wellbeing, bone mineral density (BMD), incontinence, and comorbidities. Steroid use patterns were assessed by the number of steroid courses during the 3‐year period (oral and intravenous), duration of exposure, and recency of exposure. A 3‐year window was selected to maximize generalizability, as site participation in CCCare expanded substantially from 2021 onwards, and to ensure that results reflected contemporary practice given recent advances in therapeutic options.

Stepwise logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate potential relationships between steroid use (duration and/or recency) as dependent variables, and demographic and/or disease characteristics (IBD diagnosis, body mass index (BMI), age, extraintestinal manifestations (EIMS), gender, previous IBD surgery, stoma surgery, IBD therapies, and age at diagnosis) as independent variables. Independent variables that achieved statistical significance (p < 0.05) in univariate analyses were retained in the full regression models. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) were calculated from the estimates of the logistic regression models. Missing data were addressed using a complete case approach, whereby individuals with missing values for a given variable were excluded from analyses involving that variable, and no interpolation or imputation was applied.

Phenotype variables were included in separate models for Crohn's disease (CD) and UC, using a dynamic Montreal classification criterion (as documented at each individual's most recent assessment). Individuals were classified as having no visible disease if they had evidence of both endoscopic and radiologic remission based on predefined data extraction criteria. Endoscopic remission for UC was defined as a Mayo endoscopic subscore of 0, a UCEIS ≤ 1, or documentation of no segmental inflammation (“mild,” “moderate,” or “severe” not recorded in any segment). For CD, it was defined as a SES‐CD < 3 or absence of ulcerations, or similarly, no segmental inflammation reported. Radiologic remission, applied to both groups, was defined as no abnormal findings recorded on magnetic resonance enterography, computed tomography enterography, intestinal ultrasound, or pelvic magnetic resonance imaging.

Results

The eligible cohort comprised 5436 people 3136 (57.6%) with CD, 2165 (39.7%) UC and 135 (2.5%) IBD‐unspecified (IBD‐U). The cohort was nearly evenly distributed in terms of sex—50.2% female, 48.8% male—with a median age of 42 years (IQR 32–56). During the study period, 71.6% of those with CD and 50.5% of those with UC received ADV (see Table 1 for demographic details).

Sex

Females had a higher likelihood of prolonged exposure to steroids than males (AOR 1.22, 95% CI 1.0–1.4, p = 0.048).

Age Brackets

Younger people, specifically aged 20–29 years, had a higher likelihood of prolonged exposure to steroids than people > 70 years of age (AOR 6.59, 95% CI 2.96–14.64, p < 0.001).

Prior IBD Surgery and Stoma

People with prior IBD surgery had a lower likelihood of prolonged exposure to steroids compared to those without prior IBD surgery (AOR 0.60, 95% CI 0.48–0.76, p < 0.001), except for stoma surgery, where a higher likelihood of prolonged exposure was noted (AOR 2.59, 95% CI 1.34–5.02, p = 0.004).

Therapy

Among individuals on IBD therapies, those receiving a combination of IMS plus ADV had the highest odds of prolonged steroid exposure compared to those on 5‐ASA alone (AOR 4.01, 95% CI 2.96–5.43, p = 0.002). While all treatment groups had higher odds of prolonged steroid exposure than those on 5‐ASA alone, those on ADV alone had the lowest AOR among them (AOR 1.72, 95% CI 1.20–2.45, p < 0.001).

Age at Diagnosis

Younger age at diagnosis reached statistical significance in our regression model. However, the effect size on increasing odds of prolonged steroid exposure is modest (AOR 1.04, 95% CI 1.03–1.05, p < 0.001).

Recency of Exposure Model

IBD Diagnosis

The model using recency of exposure as the dependent covariate, indicated that people with CD had a lower likelihood of recent steroids compared to people with UC (AOR 0.78, 95% CI 0.62–0.99, p = 0.037).

Sex

Females had a higher likelihood of recent exposure than males (AOR 1.23, 95% CI 1.01–1.50, p = 0.041).

Age Brackets

Younger people (ages 20–29 years) had a higher likelihood of recent exposure than those > 70 years of age (AOR 9.12, 95% CI 3.0–14.7, p < 0.001). The odds of recent steroid exposure generally decreased with age (see Table 3).

Prior IBD Surgery and Stoma

People with prior IBD surgery had a lower likelihood of recent exposure compared to those without (AOR 0.61, 95% CI 0.49–0.77, p < 0.001), except for people with stoma surgery, who had a higher likelihood of recent steroid exposure than those without stoma surgery (AOR 2.57, 95% CI 1.33–4.97, p = 0.005).

Therapy

Individuals receiving a combination of IMS and ADV had the highest likelihood of recent steroid exposure compared to those on 5‐ASA alone (AOR 4.54, 95% CI 2.80–5.13, p < 0.001). While all treatment groups had higher odds of recent steroid exposure than those on 5‐ASA alone, individuals on ADV alone had the lowest AOR among them (AOR 2.02, 95% CI 1.16–2.36, p = 0.005).

Age at Diagnosis

Similar to steroid exposure duration, age at diagnosis also influenced the recency of steroid use, with those at a younger age of disease onset associated with a higher likelihood of recent steroid exposure, though the effect was modest (AOR 1.04, 95% CI 1.03–1.05, p < 0.001).

Phenotype Analyses Per IBD Diagnosis

Phenotype encompasses age at IBD onset (for both CD and UC), extent (UC only), behavior and location (CD only). Therefore, two separate statistical models were created. In the CD model including behavior, people with inflammatory and stricturing phenotypes had a lower likelihood of prolonged exposure to steroids compared to those without visible disease (AOR 0.389, 95% CI 0.1–0.9, p = 0.039 and AOR 0.382, 95% CI 0.1–0.9, p = 0.031, respectively). However, they had a higher likelihood of recent steroid exposure compared to those without visible disease (2.656, 95% CI 1.0–6.5, p = 0.035 and AOR 2.833, 95% CI 1.1–7.1, p = 0.028, respectively).

Phenotype in UC (extent) did not reach statistical significance in the models.

In the Australian cohort, younger age was associated with both prolonged and recent steroid exposure, with the highest odds observed among those aged 20–29 years compared with those > 70 years (AOR 8.66, 95% CI 2.74–27.34, p < 0.001 for duration; AOR 7.42, 95% CI 2.37–23.26, p < 0.001 for recency). Gender and IBD subtype were not significant predictors in either model. People with prior IBD surgery had a lower likelihood of prolonged and recent steroid exposure (both p = 0.003) while those with stoma surgery had a higher likelihood in both models (p = 0.02 and 0.03, respectively). Among those receiving IBD therapy, those receiving a combination of IMS and ADV had the highest odds of both prolonged (AOR 4.92, 95% CI 3.17–7.64, p < 0.001) and recent (AOR 4.50, 95% CI 2.91–6.95, p < 0.001) steroid exposure compared to those on 5‐ASA alone. Younger age at diagnosis was associated with greater steroid exposure in both models (p < 0.001).

In the New Zealand cohort, CD was associated with lower odds of both prolonged (AOR 0.56, 95% CI 0.40–0.78, p < 0.001) and recent (AOR 0.60, 95% CI 0.43–0.84, p < 0.001) steroid exposure compared with UC. Again, younger age groups had higher odds of both prolonged and recent steroid exposure with the highest odds in those aged 20–29 years compared to those aged > 70 years (p = 0.02 and 0.01, respectively). Confidence intervals were wide for younger age groups in the recency model. Gender, prior IBD surgery, and stoma surgery were not significant predictors. Regarding therapies, those receiving a combination of IMS and ADV again had the highest odds of prolonged and recent steroid exposure compared with those on 5‐ASA alone (both p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study presents a large, comprehensive real‐world analysis of steroid use in IBD within a cohort of 5441 individuals across multiple care sites in Australia and New Zealand. Notably, only 1 in 5 individuals had steroid exposure over the past 3 years. This is encouraging as these findings suggest that steroid‐related risks are well recognized by people with IBD and their clinicians and generally avoided in routine care. To our knowledge, this is one of the largest, contemporaneous real‐world cohorts to examine steroid use in people with IBD, providing valuable insights into steroid use patterns.

The associations between steroid exposure, whether quantified as duration or recency are similar in the two models used, showing that those exposed longer were also more likely to be exposed recently. This insight is helpful as it enables us to clearly recognize the clinical scenarios where clinicians should concentrate efforts to review and perhaps reduce steroid use. Predictors of steroid exposure included female sex, younger age, previous stoma surgery and type of IBD therapy, namely a combination of IMS and ADV, perhaps suggesting more refractory disease.

Steroid Use Trends in IBD

Steroids remain essential in inducing remission in acute IBD flares, but their long‐term use is increasingly discouraged due to their well‐recognized adverse effects. While reported steroid exposure varies across different cohorts, there is a clear global trend in reducing corticosteroid use, reflecting growing awareness of its risks and the increasing adoption of steroid‐sparing therapies.

Earlier studies reported persistently high corticosteroid use, likely due to limited availability of ADVs at the time. A Manitoba population‐based study of 5300 individuals with IBD found no significant reduction in corticosteroid use observed between 1995 and 2004 despite greater IMS use [5]. This may reflect a population with suboptimal disease control during a period where ADVs were more limited. Similarly, a large US study analyzing Veterans' Health Administration data (2002–2010) reported 32% corticosteroid exposure, a higher rate than our cohort [6]. Notably, only a quarter of their cohort who required repeated or prolonged steroid prescriptions were escalated to steroid‐sparing agents, suggesting suboptimal treatment escalation contributing to steroid overuse.

More recent studies suggest a positive shift toward reducing corticosteroid dependency. Selinger's multicenter UK audit (2017) found that 30% of individuals with IBD had steroid exposure over a 12‐month period, similar to previous studies [7]. However, it was noted during the study period that antitumor necrosis factor biological agents were not generally reimbursed for those with UC, potentially resulting in increased steroid use for management of ongoing active disease in this subgroup. Our findings closely align with Kim et al.'s nationwide South Korean study, which reported a decline in corticosteroid use from 32.1% to 25.2% from 2010 to 2018 in CD, and from 23.8% to 18.2% in UC over the same period [8]. Similarly, the DICE study [9] which evaluated four French referral centers, found 24% corticosteroid exposure over 12 months (20.1% in CD, 30.2% in UC). As most of these studies reported 12‐month exposure rates, cumulative steroid use over a comparable 3‐year window would likely be higher. The variability in corticosteroid exposure across different cohorts likely reflects regional differences in prescribing habits, ADV accessibility and evolving treatment guidelines. The overall decreased rates of steroid use suggest an increased availability of steroid‐sparing agents and adherence to early treatment escalation, a strategy increasingly emphasized in our practice.

Steroid Use by IBD Subtype

Our study found that individuals with UC were significantly more likely to receive corticosteroids than those with CD, a pattern consistently observed in previous research. Historically, this disparity has been attributed to differences in treatment paradigms and access to ADVs. In a UK‐based study, while corticosteroid dependency in CD declined between 1990 and 2010, individuals with UC experienced an increase in repeated steroid exposure despite a rise in thiopurine and 5‐ASA use [10]. Similarly, a multicenter audit reported that individuals with moderate‐to‐severe UC had significantly higher rates of steroid exposure and dependency than those with CD, an effect attributed to the limited availability of anti‐TNF maintenance therapy for UC at the time [7]. In contrast, CD had already benefited from earlier access to biologics, leading to reduced reliance on corticosteroids in this population.

While the availability of biologic therapies is no longer a limiting factor in our study period, the continued higher corticosteroid use in UC may reflect both a historical preference for steroids in UC induction therapy [4, 11] and a perception of UC as a less severe disease compared to CD [12]. This perception may have contributed to more conservative treatment paradigms in UC, with delayed initiation of ADVs. Supporting this, our cohort demonstrated lower rates of ADV use in UC compared to CD (50.5% vs. 71.6%), suggesting possible delays in treatment escalation that may contribute to increased steroid exposure. Unlike CD, where early biologic escalation is well‐established, the role of early ADV initiation in UC has been less emphasized, potentially leading to delayed treatment escalation and prolonged steroid use. Another possible explanation is that our cohort may include individuals with more severe or refractory UC, though disease severity was not explicitly analyzed.

The landscape of UC treatment is evolving with the recent approval of additional ADVs, including Janus kinase inhibitors [13] and sphingosine‐1‐phosphate receptor modulators [14, 15]. These agents offer alternative induction and maintenance options, which may further reduce corticosteroid reliance in UC in the coming years. Future studies should assess whether these newer therapies contribute to a shift in prescribing patterns and decrease long‐term steroid exposure in UC.

Differences Between Sexes

Our findings indicate that female sex was independently associated with more recent and prolonged corticosteroid exposure. While hormone‐related gastrointestinal symptom variation, particularly during the menstrual cycle has been proposed as a contributing factor, evidence linking these symptoms to objective inflammation remains inconsistent [16, 17, 18, 19]. Importantly, this disparity may also reflect a systemic or unconscious gender bias. A multicenter observational study reported that women with IBD experienced significantly longer diagnostic delays than men, despite no substantial differences in clinical presentation [20]. These delays were largely attributed to misdiagnoses, often involving functional gastrointestinal disorders, reflecting a broader pattern of symptom minimization and misattribution in female patients throughout the healthcare system. Similar disparities have been observed in other chronic inflammatory conditions, including spondylarthritis [21, 22].

Although diagnostic delay alone may account for increased steroid exposure, it likely reflects broader patterns of clinical bias that persist beyond diagnosis. These include the potential underestimation of disease activity in women leading clinicians to favor steroids as an interim measure rather than timely escalation to ADVs. These findings underscore the need for sex‐informed care models that address not only biological variation but also structural and perceptual biases in both diagnostic and therapeutic pathways.

Prior IBD Surgery and Stoma Surgery

Individuals with prior IBD‐related surgery had lower odds of recent and prolonged steroid exposure, suggesting improved long‐term disease control following surgical intervention. Targownik et al. reported that people with IBD and steroid dependency or excess use were three times more likely to undergo intestinal resection [5], indicating that steroids are often used in severe or refractory disease before surgery. Following resection, disease control tends to improve, reducing the need for ongoing steroid use.

A meta‐analysis demonstrated that IBD surgery rates have significantly declined over the recent decades [23], attributable to earlier disease detection, evidence‐based guidelines, and advances in medical therapy. Notably, rescue therapies have lowered colectomy rates in fulminant UC refractory to intravenous corticosteroids. Today, surgery is more often reserved for steroid‐dependent or medically refractory disease several years after diagnosis, often reflecting suboptimal maintenance therapy. Together, these findings suggest that individuals requiring surgery may represent a subgroup with poorly controlled disease who stabilize postresection, reducing steroid reliance.

Conversely, individuals who underwent stoma surgery exhibit higher steroid exposure, likely reflecting a subgroup with more aggressive, refractory disease. Aleyadeh et al. found that over half of people undergoing permanent ostomy had refractory disease, most had active inflammation on pathology, and half required perioperative steroids [24]. Just over half had previous abdominal surgeries, with an average of 2.9 surgeries. These findings support stoma formation as a marker of severe, refractory disease where steroids remain necessary despite prior surgery.

Age and Age at Diagnosis

Younger individuals, particularly those aged 20–29 years, had the highest likelihood of recent and prolonged corticosteroid exposure. This may reflect a more progressive disease course often seen in early‐onset IBD. Ministro et al. found that individuals diagnosed at an older age had a significantly lower risk of progressive disease, suggesting that younger‐onset IBD requires more intensive medical management [25].

However, biological factors alone may not fully explain this trend. Clinician hesitancy and patient concerns may contribute to prolonged steroid use in younger individuals by delaying the escalation to ADVs. This is particularly relevant early in the disease course, when the trajectory of disease activity may be unclear and perceived risks associated with escalation contribute to more conservative treatment decisions.

In contrast to our findings, Parian et al. reported that 40% of elderly individuals with IBD remained on chronic corticosteroids, likely reflecting underutilization of steroid‐sparing therapies due to safety concerns [26]. We did not observe this pattern within our cohort, which may be partly explained by the growing availability and uptake of gut‐selective agents such as anti‐integrin inhibitors. These therapies offer a more favorable safety profile, particularly with respect to infection risk, and may enable more confident escalation in older adults. In our study, steroid exposure was instead concentrated in younger individuals, possibly reflecting therapeutic inertia during treatment optimization or delays in transitioning to maintenance therapy.

While corticosteroids remain essential for managing acute flares, these findings highlight the importance of timely and proactive escalation to ADVs, particularly in younger people with IBD to reduce steroid dependence and optimize long‐term outcomes.

IBD Therapy

As expected, individuals not receiving any maintenance therapy had the highest likelihood of steroid exposure. However, among those receiving treatment, we found that individuals receiving a combination of IMS and ADV had the greatest steroid exposure. This contrasts with findings from the SONIC, TOP‐DOWN, and more recently the PROFILE trials which demonstrated that the early combination of infliximab and azathioprine in CD resulted in higher rates of steroid‐free remission compared to the conventional step‐up approach [27, 28, 29]. One possible explanation is that the top‐down approach may not yet be widely adopted in routine clinical practice, meaning that combination therapy among this subgroup may reflect a more reactive escalation strategy rather than proactive treatment. Alternatively, these individuals may reflect a subgroup with more severe or refractory disease, requiring multiple therapeutic agents for disease control. Given that disease severity was not specifically assessed in our study, further research is needed to characterize this cohort. Another plausible explanation relates to treatment sequencing. Individuals on combination therapy may have recently required or are currently requiring steroids to manage a disease flare before transitioning to monotherapy. This pattern may also reflect the use of steroids during a transitional phase of treatment, driven by delays in accessing ADV due to Pharmaceutical Benefit Scheme prescribing restrictions.

To better understand whether the combination of IMS and ADV is associated with increased steroid use, further research should aim to characterize the treatment history of these individuals, particularly regarding prior therapy failures, biologic switching patterns, and severe or refractory disease phenotypes.

Regional Subanalyses

When examined by country of residence, the Australian and New Zealand cohorts demonstrated broadly consistent patterns of steroid exposure. In the Australian cohort, IBD diagnosis and gender were not significant predictors, whereas in the New Zealand cohort, gender, prior IBD surgery, and stoma surgery were not significant. CD remained associated with lower odds of steroid exposure in New Zealand but not in Australia.

These differences should be interpreted with caution. The direction of effects was consistent across both countries, suggesting that the loss of significance in some predictors likely reflects smaller sample sizes and reduced power in stratified models rather than true regional differences. This was most evident in the New Zealand recency model, where some estimates had wide confidence intervals, likely due to smaller subgroup sizes.

Clinical Implications

These findings identify key groups at higher risk of corticosteroid exposure, including younger individuals, females and those with stoma surgery or receiving combination IMS and ADV. In clinical practice, recognition of these subgroups should prompt early review of treatment adequacy and timely escalation to steroid‐sparing agents. Objective monitoring using biomarkers and patient‐reported outcomes can help ensure that treatment decisions are guided by disease activity rather than symptom perception or clinician bias.

For individuals with refractory or difficult‐to‐control disease, early consideration of newer targeted therapies or, in selected cases, combination biologics may help minimize steroid dependence. Embedding these approaches within structured steroid stewardship programs, supported by multidisciplinary review and routine registry‐based audit, may further improve the safety, equity and quality of IBD care.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is its large, real‐world multicenter cohort, capturing structured prescribing data across a broad network of IBD care sites in Australia and New Zealand. While participating centers are predominantly tertiary referral settings, this dataset offers valuable insight into steroid use patterns in routine clinical practice, particularly in contexts where structured, multidisciplinary IBD care is well established. The use of clinician‐entered, structured medication fields within an IBD‐specific EMR recorded during routine clinical care, reduces recall bias and improves the accuracy of steroid exposure classification by providing objective prescribing data. Only steroids prescribed for IBD were captured through the designated medication section, reducing misclassification from non‐IBD indications. Stepwise logistic regression identified independent predictors, strengthening the analytical validity of the findings.

Limitations include the absence of concurrent disease activity measures and detailed treatment history, limiting the interpretation of whether steroid use reflected refractory disease or delays in treatment escalation. Formal metrics on data completeness or external verification were not available at the time of analysis. However, the dataset was drawn from structured, clinician‐entered fields completed during routine care, which likely supports consistency and reduces reliance on free‐text or retrospective entry. Treatment adherence and dose adjustments between visits were not captured, nor was corticosteroid use outside the study window. These data reflect real‐world prescribing as recorded in the EMR, and while this approach cannot account for all treatment nuances, it provides valuable insight into steroid use patterns across a large, contemporary IBD cohort. Additionally, while the registry captures both current and historical ADV use, documentation of corticosteroid exposure around the time of treatment initiation, particularly for individuals not followed from diagnosis, may be incomplete or inconsistently recorded. Consequently, distinguishing between early and late ADV use could not be reliably assessed in this analysis. As the CCCare inception cohort matures, this will become an important focus for future research to better characterize the timing and impact of early ADV use. The cohort was predominantly from tertiary referral centers and may not reflect care patterns in regional or community settings, potentially underestimating overall steroid exposure. Future research should incorporate clinical and longer‐term longitudinal data to better understand steroid use patterns.

Conclusion

This large, real‐world cohort study highlights that steroid use remains prevalent in IBD care, with approximately one in five individuals exposed over a 3‐year period. Steroid exposure was more likely among younger individuals, females, and those with UC, as well as individuals receiving combination IMS and ADV. These findings suggest that prolonged steroid use may be influenced not only by disease severity, but also by treatment delays and therapeutic inertia.

While corticosteroids remain essential for managing acute flares, their long‐term use is largely avoidable with timely escalation to steroid‐sparing therapies. Structured IBD clinics, multidisciplinary care, and improved coordination with primary care offer opportunities to reduce unnecessary steroid exposure. Future research should focus on identifying barriers to escalation and optimizing treatment pathways to support safer, guideline‐based care across the IBD population.
