On quivers satisfying Quantum Yang-Baxter equation, and Hecke condition
Cody Gilbert, Ashish K. Srivastava

Abstract
. In this paper we initiate the study of quivers satisfying quantum Yang-Baxter equation. By considering quivers with quantum group like relations such as quantum Yang-Baxter equation, Hecke condition, we follow RTT construction to produce examples of Leavitt path algebras that contain quantum matrix algebra as subalgebra. Furthermore, We show that the quantum Yang-Baxter equation, and Hecke condition for our RTT construction can be generated intrinsically from the adjacency matrix of certain quivers.



1.
Introduction

The Quantum Yang-Baxter Equation (QYBE) is a fundamental consistency condition that arises in mathematical physics, quantum groups, and noncommutative algebra. Its origin traces back to influential papers of Yang
[
15
]
on quantum mechanical many-body problem and Baxter
[
3
]
on solution of the eight vertex model. A solution to the quantum Yang-Baxter equation is a linear map
R:V\otimes V\rightarrow V\otimes V
where
V
is a vector space over a field such that the equality
(R\otimes I_{V})(I_{V}\otimes R)(R\otimes I_{V})=(I_{V}\otimes R)(R\otimes I_{V})(I_{V}\otimes R)
of operators holds in the space
\End(V\otimes V\otimes V)
. The matrix of such a linear map is called an
R
-matrix.

The term Quantum Yang–Baxter Equation (QYBE) was coined by Faddeev in the late 1970s. Intuitively, the QYBE ensures that when three interacting particles or algebraic objects are rearranged, the final outcome does not depend on the order in which the pairwise interactions occur. It has applications in the study of exactly solvable models in statistical mechanics and quantum field theory, and has connections to knot theory, braid theory and Hopf algebras. In
[
7
]
Drinfeld suggested to study the set-theoretical solutions of QYBE. A set-theoretical solution is a solution for which
V
is a vector space spanned by a set
X
, and
R
is the linear operator induced by a mapping
X\times X\rightarrow X\times X
. For geometric and algebraic interpretations of such solutions, the reader is referred to Etingof et al.
[
9
]
.

In Section 2 of this paper we introduce the notion of quiver satisfying the quantum Yang-Baxter equation. Our approach is quite different from the one in
[
2
]
. We say that a quiver
Q
satisfies the QYBE if the adjacency matrix of the Kronecker square quiver satisfies the QYBE. We show that if a quiver
Q
satisfies the QYBE then its adjacency matrix
A
satisfies the “Temperley-Lieb idempotent” property, that is,
A^{2}=\mu A
for some scalar
\mu
. In particular, we show that a finite symmetric quiver
Q
satisfies the QYBE if and only if each nontrivial connected component of
Q
is a complete weighted quiver (that is, there is a nonzero weighted arrow between any two vertices in the component).

Closely linked to the QYBE is the Hecke condition, which places an additional polynomial constraint on the
R
-matrix. In its common form, the Hecke condition states that
(R-qI)(R+q^{-1}I)=0
for some nonzero parameter
q
. Together, the QYBE and the Hecke condition provide a powerful algebraic framework used to construct quantum symmetries and invariants. Solutions that satisfy both conditions lead to braided monoidal categories, quantum knot invariants like the Jones polynomial, and integrable lattice models in statistical mechanics. In noncommutative algebra, these equations guide the formation of deformed algebras where commutation rules are governed by the
R
-matrix. In Section 3 of this paper, we give a recipe to construct Hecke
R
-matrices from the weighted adjacency matrices of certain quivers.

Recently, there has been a lot of interest in quantum graphs among researchers working in quantum communication theory. Quantum graphs were first introduced in 1930s by Pauling to describe organic molecules and since then they have been studied from different perspectives. Physicists define it as a metric graph with the Schrödinger operator defined at the edges (according to the physical motivation) and some coupling conditions at the graph vertices that specify the transition probabilities across the vertices (see for example
[
8
]
). Weaver
[
13
]
proposed quantum graphs as reflexive symmetric quantum relations on a von Neumann algebra. In
[
4
]
, quantum graphs are defined in terms of a self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(H)
, for a finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra
H
, which is idempotent under pointwise multiplication generalizing the usual adjacency matrix of a graph. Brannan at al adapt this notion in
[
5
]
to directed graphs and develop the notion of quantum graph
C^{*}
-algebras. Leavitt path algebras are algebraic analogues of graph C*-algebras defined as certain quotients of path algebras where the relations are inspired from Cuntz-Krieger relations for graph
C^{*}
-algebras
[
1
]
. Leavitt path algebras have deep connections with symbolic dynamics and since they capture the homological properties of both path algebras and their Koszul dual, they form an important class of noncommutative algebras. It is known that the Leavitt path algebra over Dynkin quiver
A_{n}
is the matrix algebra
M_{n}(\mathbb{K})
and so it is natural to ask if quantum matrix algebra can be realized as Leavitt path algebra over some quiver.

In Section 4, we consider quivers with RTT relations determined by Hecke R-matrices and quantum Yang-Baxter equation and obtain quantum matrix algebra as subalgebra of the Leavitt path algebras over quivers with such relations. There is a natural operator-algebraic interpretation of the RTT-enhanced Leavitt path algebras constructed in this section. The imposition of local RTT relations at vertices with loop spaces replaces the free
C^{*}
-correspondence of
[
6
]
by a braided tensor correspondence determined by the Hecke

R
-matrix
R_{u}
. In the classical setting (
R_{u}=\mathrm{Flip}
), the correspondence is
symmetric and yields the usual graph
C^{*}
-algebra. When
R_{u}
is nontrivial,
the tensor product over loop space
L(u)
becomes a
braided
tensor product, placing

L_{\mathbb{K}}(Q,R)
into the framework of Hayashi’s face-type quantum group
correspondences
[
10
]
and more general quantum graph
C^{*}
-algebras. In particular, for a vertex
u
with
\dim_{\mathbb{K}}L(u)=n^{2}
and Hecke
R
-matrix
arising from
GL_{q}(n)
, the subalgebra generated by the loop edges yields the
quantum matrix algebra
\mathcal{O}_{q}(M_{n})
,
and the resulting
L_{\mathbb{K}}(Q,R)
may be viewed as a
quantum graph algebra
whose
quantum symmetries are encoded combinatorially by the quiver and the RTT relations.

Thus, the construction developed here produces natural algebraic models for
quantum Cuntz–Krieger algebras and braided graph correspondences, enriching the
interface between Leavitt path algebras, quantum groups, and noncommutative
topology.

2.
Quivers Satisfying the QYBE

Definition 2.1
.

Let
Q
be a quiver (that is, a directed graph) with the adjacency matrix
A=[a_{ij}]
. The Kronecker square
\widehat{Q}
is a quiver whose adjacency matrix is
A\otimes A
where
A\otimes A
is the Kronecker product matrix given as
A\otimes A=[b_{ij}]
with
b_{ij}=a_{ij}A
.

Let
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,r)
be a quiver where
Q_{0}
is the set of vertices,
Q_{1}
is the set of arrows and both
s
, and
r
are functions from
Q_{1}
to
Q_{0}
such that for any arrow
e
from
u
to
v
,
s(e)=u
and
r(e)=v
.
Then the Kronecker square
\widehat{Q}=(\widehat{Q_{0}},\widehat{Q_{1}},\widehat{s},\widehat{r})
is described as

\displaystyle\widehat{Q_{0}}=\{[v_{i},v_{j}]\,:\,v_{i},v_{j}\in Q_{0}\}

\displaystyle\widehat{Q_{1}}=\{[e_{i},e_{j}]\,:\,e_{i},e_{j}\in Q_{1}\}

\displaystyle\qquad\widehat{s}([e,f])=[s(e),s(f)]

\displaystyle\qquad\widehat{r}([e,f])=[r(e),r(f)]

Recall that an
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrix
X
is said to satisfy the quantum Yang-Baxter Equation (QYBE) if

(X\otimes I_{n})(I_{n}\otimes X)(X\otimes I_{n})=(I_{n}\otimes X)(X\otimes I_{n})(I_{n}\otimes X)

where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
Q
be a quiver with adjacency matrix
A
. We will say that
Q
satisfies the quantum Yang-Baxter Equation if the adjacency matrix
X=A\otimes A
of
\widehat{Q}
satisfies the quantum Yang-Baxter Equation.

We first investigate when an adjacency matrix
X=A\otimes A
satisfies the QYBE. It will quickly be apparent that such
X
have a highly degenerate structure. This means
X
will generally not be an
R
-matrix as it will be singular (barring the identity matrix); however, in the next section, we will use such
X
to produce Hecke
R
-matrices,
\check{R}(q)
which arise in a straightforward, combinatorial way via weighted adjacency matrices of complete quivers.

A quick computation introduces the equation of interest for this section. Suppose we have a matrix
Y=A\otimes B\in\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}\times n^{2}}
where
A,B\in C^{n\times n}
. We want to determine when
Y
satisfies the QYBE. We must have

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,(Y\otimes I_{n})(I_{n}\otimes Y)(Y\otimes I_{n})=(I_{n}\otimes Y)(Y\otimes I_{n})(I_{n}\otimes Y)

\displaystyle\iff(A\otimes B\otimes I_{n})(I_{n}\otimes A\otimes B)(A\otimes B\otimes I_{n})=(I_{n}\otimes A\otimes B)(A\otimes B\otimes I_{n})(I_{n}\otimes A\otimes B)

\displaystyle\iff\qquad\qquad\quad\,(A\otimes BA\otimes B)(A\otimes B\otimes I_{n})=(A\otimes AB\otimes B)(I_{n}\otimes A\otimes B)

\displaystyle\iff\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,A^{2}\otimes BAB\otimes B=A\otimes ABA\otimes B^{2}

In the case where
X=A\otimes A
, the above equation becomes

(1)

A^{2}\otimes A^{3}\otimes A=A\otimes A^{3}\otimes A^{2}.

The main theorem of this section, Theorem
2.3
, characterizes the matrices
A
that satisfy equation (
1
) above and finds that such matrices have the “Temperley-Lieb idempotent” property, that is,
A^{2}=\mu A
for some scalar
\mu
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
A
be a
n\times n
matrix, such that
A^{3}\neq 0
, then

A\otimes A^{3}\otimes A^{2}=A^{2}\otimes A^{3}\otimes A

if and only if
A^{2}\otimes A=A\otimes A^{2}
if and only if
A^{2}=\mu A
.

Theorem
2.3
is an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.4
, Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
2.6
below.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
X,Y,U,V
are nonzero square matrices of the same size, then if
X\otimes Y=U\otimes V
, then there exists a nonzero scalar
\lambda
such that
X=\lambda U
and
Y=\frac{1}{\lambda}V
.

Proof.

Let
X,Y,U,V
be
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{R}
(a similar argument works for
\mathbb{C}
). Define

x=\text{vec}(X),\quad y=\text{vec}(Y),\quad u=\text{vec}(U),\quad v=\text{vec}(V).

The equality
X\otimes Y=U\otimes V
thus corresponds to the equality
x\otimes y=u\otimes v
. Note, this is not to say
\text{vec}(X\otimes Y)=x\otimes y
, but rather, if
x\otimes y=u\otimes v
, then the entries of
X\otimes Y
must be the same as the entries of
U\otimes V
, so it suffices to show
x\otimes y=u\otimes v
. Furthermore, the equality
x\otimes y=u\otimes v
is equivalent to the equality
xy^{T}=uv^{T}
of rank one matrices.

As a rank one matrix, the singular value decomposition of
xy^{T}
can be written as
xy^{T}=\sigma pq^{T}
, where

\sigma=||x||\cdot||y||,\quad p=\frac{x}{||x||},\quad q=\frac{y}{||y||}.

Similarly, one can write
uv^{T}=\tau rs^{T}
where

\tau=||u||\cdot||v||,\quad r=\frac{u}{||u||},\quad s=\frac{v}{||v||}.

As
xy^{T}=uv^{T}
, we must have
\sigma pq^{T}=\tau rs^{T}
.

For a nonzero rank one matrix, the left and right singular values are unique up to sign, thus there is a scalar
\alpha
with

p=\alpha r,\quad q=\frac{1}{\alpha}s,\quad\sigma=\tau.

Substituting into the equations
p=\frac{x}{||x||}
and
q=\frac{y}{||y||}
we find

x=\left(\frac{\alpha||x||u}{||u||}\right),\qquad y=\left(\frac{\alpha^{-1}||y||v}{||v||}\right).

Letting
\lambda:=\frac{\alpha||x||}{||u||}
, it follows
x=\lambda u
and, as
\tau=||u||\cdot||v||=||x||\cdot||y||=\sigma
,

\lambda^{-1}v=\frac{\alpha^{-1}||u||}{||x||}v=\frac{\alpha^{-1}||y||}{||v||}v=y.

As
x=\lambda u
and
y=\lambda^{-1}v
, we must have
X=\lambda U
and
Y=\frac{1}{\lambda}V
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix and suppose
A^{3}\neq 0
, then
A^{2}\otimes A=A\otimes A^{2}
if and only if
A^{2}\otimes A^{3}\otimes A=A\otimes A^{3}\otimes A^{2}
.

Proof.

Suppose
A^{2}\otimes A=A\otimes A^{2}
. As
A^{2}\otimes A=A\otimes A^{2}
, we must have

\displaystyle(A\otimes A)(A^{2}\otimes A)

\displaystyle=(A\otimes A)(A\otimes A^{2})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow A^{3}\otimes A^{2}

\displaystyle=A^{2}\otimes A^{3}

Furthermore, if
I
is the
n\times n
identity matrix, then we find

\displaystyle(A\otimes I)(A^{2}\otimes A)

\displaystyle=(A\otimes I)(A\otimes A^{2})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow A^{3}\otimes A

\displaystyle=A^{2}\otimes A^{2}

and similarly,
A^{2}\otimes A^{2}=A\otimes A^{3}
. All together, we have the equality

A\otimes A^{3}=A^{2}\otimes A^{2}=A^{3}\otimes A.

Using the above equalities, we get the equation

A^{2}\otimes A^{3}\otimes A=A^{3}\otimes A^{2}\otimes A=A^{3}\otimes A\otimes A^{2}=A\otimes A^{3}\otimes A^{2}.

For the converse, we write

x=\text{vec}(X),\qquad y=\text{vec}(A^{3})\qquad z=\text{vec}(A^{2}).

The equality
A\otimes A^{3}\otimes A^{2}=A^{2}\otimes A^{3}\otimes A
is equivalent to the equality
x\otimes y\otimes z=z\otimes y\otimes x
. As
A^{3}\neq 0
, we have
x,y,z\neq 0
. Further, the uniqueness of a pure tensor decomposition for three non-zero factors implies there exists scalars
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
with

x=\alpha z,\quad y=\beta y,\quad z=\gamma x,\quad\alpha\beta\gamma=1.

As
\beta=1
, we get
\alpha\gamma=1
. As such, after translating the vectorized equalities back into square matrices, we find
A=\alpha A^{2}
and
A^{2}=\gamma A
. It follows

A^{2}\otimes A=(\gamma A)\otimes A=A\otimes(\gamma A)=A\otimes A^{2}.

∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
A
be a nonzero
n\times n
matrix, then
A^{2}\otimes A=A\otimes A^{2}
if and only if
A^{2}=\mu A
for
\mu
a scalar.

Proof.

The converse was shown in the above proof, so we prove sufficiency. If
A^{2}\otimes A=A\otimes A^{2}
, then by Lemma
2.4
, assuming
A^{2}\neq 0
, there exists
\lambda\neq 0
with
A=\lambda A^{2}
and
A^{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda}A
. Letting
\mu=\frac{1}{\lambda}
, the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.
By combining Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
2.6
, we have

A^{2}\otimes A^{3}\otimes A=A\otimes A^{3}\otimes A^{2}\quad\iff\qquad A^{2}\otimes A=A\otimes A^{2}\quad\iff\qquad A^{2}=\mu A

for
\mu
a scalar.

∎

We now restrict our focus to adjacency matrices of a graph
\Gamma
. Equivalently, we can interpret these graph adjacency matrices as adjacency matrices of a quiver
Q
with bidirectional arrows.

In this section, we assume
A^{3}\neq 0
. From Theorem
2.3
, it suffices to find graphs whose adjacency matrix satisfies
A^{2}=\mu A
for some scalar
\mu
. We consider three different cases: The case where
\Gamma
is without loops and multiple edges (Proposition
2.7
), the case where
\Gamma
has loops but no multiple edges (Proposition
2.8
) and lastly, when
\Gamma
has loops and multiple edges (Theorem
2.9
).

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose
A
is an adjacency
n\times n
matrix corresponding to a graph
\Gamma
without any loops or multiple edges, then
A^{2}=\mu A
if and only if
A
is the zero matrix, i.e.
\Gamma
consists entirely of isolated vertices.

Proof.

As
\Gamma
does not have any loops, we have
A_{ii}=0
for all
i
, further each
A_{ij}
is either
0
or
1
. By Theorem
2.3
, it follows

0=\mu A_{ii}=(A^{2})_{ii}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}A_{ji}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}=\text{deg}(i).

We have thus shown
\deg(i)=0
for all
i
, as such the graph
\Gamma
has no edges and
A
is the zero matrix.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Suppose
A
is an adjacency
n\times n
matrix corresponding to a graph
\Gamma
without multiple edges (but it possibly has loops), then
A^{2}=\mu A
if and only if
\Gamma
is a disjoint union of complete graphs with
\mu
vertices, where
\mu
is the scalar satisfying
A^{2}=\mu A
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists a scalar
\mu
such that
A^{2}=\mu A
. As
\Gamma
does not have multiple edges,
A
is a symmetric matrix with entries being either
0
or
1
. Further, as we are allowing loops, we can have
A_{ii}\neq 0
. Similar to the proof of the previous proposition, we have

\mu A_{ii}=(A^{2})_{ii}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}A_{ji}=\sum^{n}_{j=1}A_{ij}=\deg(i)

where
\deg(i)
is equal to the number of neighbors of
i
(including loops). As
A^{2}=\mu A
, it follows
\deg(i)=0
, if
A_{ii}=0
, or
\deg(i)=\mu
, if
A_{ii}=1
. As such, we can partition our vertices of
A
into a set of isolated vertices and the set
S=\{i:A_{ii}=1\}
, where every element
i
of
S
has
\deg(i)=\mu
.

Restricting our attention to off-diagonal entries, we have
(A^{2})_{ij}=\mu A_{ij}
, so if
A_{ij}=0
, then
A_{ij}^{2}=0
and there are no
2
-paths from
i
to
j
. If
A_{ij}=1
, then
(A^{2})_{ij}=\mu
, so there are
\mu
common neighbors of
i
and
j
. As a corollary, the induced subgraph on
S
is a disjoint union of complete graphs (with loops), each with
\mu
vertices.
∎

Theorem 2.9
.

Suppose
A
is an
n\times n
adjacency matrix with
A^{2}=\mu A
.

(1)

If
\mu=0
, then
A^{2}=0
and all vertices are isolated.

(2)

If
\mu\neq 0
, then
A
is a block-diagonal matrix with blocks
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots A_{m}
(and possibly zero rows/columns for isolated vertices). Each block
A_{i}
can be described using an equation

A_{i}=a_{i}\cdot a_{i}^{T},\qquad a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n_{i}}.

With this in mind, each
A_{i}
is a symmetric, nonzero matrix of rank one.

Additionally, for each block
i
,

\mu=a_{i}^{T}\cdot a_{i}=||a_{i}||^{2}=\sum_{l=1}^{n_{i}}a_{i,l}^{2}

Conversely, any matrix constructed in such a way satisfies
A^{2}=\mu A
.

Proof.

Suppose
A^{2}=\mu A
. If
\mu\neq 0
then we can define
P:=\frac{1}{\mu}A
, which is a symmetric matrix with the same rank as
A
. Additionally,

P^{2}=(\frac{1}{\mu}A)^{2}=\frac{1}{\mu^{2}}A^{2}=\frac{\mu}{\mu^{2}}A=\frac{1}{\mu}A=P,

so
P
is a projection matrix and thus can only have eigenvalues of
0
or
1
.

Consider the set
S=\{(i,j):P_{ij}>0\}
. As
P^{2}=P
, we have

P_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}P_{ik}P_{kj}

so if
P_{ij}>0
, there exists some
k
with
P_{ik}>0
and
P_{kj}>0
, hence connectivity on the induced subgraph from
S
induces an equivalence relation. We thus partition
S
into distinct components
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots C_{m}
.

Restricting
P
to the component
C_{l}
, the submatrix
P_{l}
is symmetric, entry-wise positive and idempotent. By the Perron-Frobenius theorem, the spectral radius is
1
, so
P_{l}
has exactly one eigenvalue that is
1
, while the other eigenvalues are zero. It follows that the rank of
P_{l}
is one, as
P_{l}
is symmetric, and thus diagonlizable. As
P_{l}
is rank one and symmetric, we may write
P_{l}=w\cdot w^{T}
for some vector
w
. Additionally, we can normalize
w
so that
||w||=1
.

Letting
a:=\sqrt{\mu}w
, it follows

A_{l}=\mu P_{l}=\mu w\cdot w^{T}=a\cdot a^{T}.

Furthermore, one finds
a^{T}\cdot a=\mu w^{T}\cdot w=\mu||w||^{2}=\mu
. Hence each nonzero block
A_{l}
is an integer outer product
a\cdot a^{T}
with
||a||^{2}=\mu
and the description of
A
in the Theorem statement holds true.

Conversely, any block
a\cdot a^{T}
satisfies

(a\cdot a^{T})^{2}=(a^{T}\cdot a)(a\cdot a^{T})=\mu(a\cdot a^{T})

thus
A^{2}=\mu A
.
∎

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
Q
be a finite quiver with symmetric adjacency matrix
A
over a field
K
, and assume
A^{3}\neq 0
.
Then
Q
satisfies the quantum Yang–Baxter equation if and only if, after a suitable reordering of vertices,
A
is block diagonal

A\;=\;\operatorname{diag}(A_{1},\dots,A_{m},0,\dots,0),

where each nonzero block
A_{i}
corresponds to a connected component of
Q
and can be written as

A_{i}\;=\;a_{i}a_{i}^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad a_{i}\in K^{n_{i}},

with all coordinates of
a_{i}
nonzero.

In particular, each nontrivial connected component of
Q
is a complete weighted quiver
(that is, there is a nonzero weighted arrow between any two vertices in the component).

Proof.

By the computation in Section 2, a quiver
Q
with adjacency matrix
A
satisfies the
quantum Yang–Baxter equation if and only if
the matrix
X=A\otimes A
satisfies

(X\otimes I_{n})(I_{n}\otimes X)(X\otimes I_{n})\;=\;(I_{n}\otimes X)(X\otimes I_{n})(I_{n}\otimes X)

in
\End(K^{n}\otimes K^{n}\otimes K^{n})
, and this is equivalent to the single matrix
identity

(2)

A^{2}\otimes A^{3}\otimes A\;=\;A\otimes A^{3}\otimes A^{2}.

By Theorem 2.3, and using the assumption
A^{3}\neq 0
, the equality
(
2
) holds if and only if there exists a scalar
\mu\in K
such that

A^{2}\;=\;\mu A.

Thus
Q
satisfies QYBE if and only if its adjacency matrix
A
satisfies the
“Temperley–Lieb idempotent” relation
A^{2}=\mu A
. The structure of such
matrices is described in Theorem 2.9: after reordering vertices,
A
is block
diagonal,

A\;=\;\operatorname{diag}(A_{1},\dots,A_{m})

(possibly with additional zero rows/columns for isolated vertices), and each
nonzero block
A_{i}
can be written as

A_{i}\;=\;a_{i}a_{i}^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n_{i}},

so each
A_{i}
is symmetric of rank one and satisfies
A_{i}^{2}=\mu_{i}A_{i}
with

\mu_{i}=a_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}a_{i}
.

Now fix a nonzero block
A_{i}
corresponding to a connected component
C_{i}
of
Q
.
We show that all coordinates of
a_{i}
are nonzero. Write
a_{i}=(a_{i,1},\dots,a_{i,n_{i}})^{\mathsf{T}}
,
and suppose, for a contradiction, that
a_{i,j}=0
for some
j
. Partition the vertex set of
C_{i}
as

S:=\{k:a_{i,k}\neq 0\},\qquad T:=\{k:a_{i,k}=0\}.

If

and similarly

. Thus there are no edges between

and
T
.
Moreover, if

the component

, and there are no edges between

and
T

the assumption that

. Therefore

empty, and all coordinates of

are nonzero.

It follows that for each nonzero block

corresponding to a
connected component, we have

.
Thus each nontrivial connected component of

Isolated vertices correspond to zero blocks and do not affect the QYBE condition.

This proves the “only if” direction.

Conversely, suppose that after reordering vertices,

where each

trivially. Hence

holds blockwise (with appropriate scalars
on each component), and in particular

(
2

satisfying
the quantum Yang–Baxter equation. Therefore

This establishes the equivalence and completes the proof.
∎

3.
Construction of Hecke
R
-matrices

It is known that one can produce constant solutions to the quantum Yang-Baxter equation through the Temperley-Lieb algebra
TL_{N}

[
3
,
12
]
. The Temperley-Lieb algebra
(TL_{N})
has
N-1
generators
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}\}
with relations

\displaystyle X_{k}^{2}=dX_{k}

\displaystyle X_{k}X_{j}X_{k}=X_{k}\qquad\qquad|j-k|=1

\displaystyle X_{j}X_{k}=X_{k}X_{j}\qquad\qquad|j-k|>1.

Temperley-Lieb algebra played a central role in Vaughan Jones’ discovery of his new polynomial invariant of knots which triggered a plethora of mathematical developments relating knot theory, topological quantum field theory, and statistical physics.

Here we follow Kulish’s rank-one tensor construction
[
11
]
to create a generator
X
of
TL_{N}
that we can then use to form the Hecke
R
-matrix
\check{R}=qI+X
. The matrix
X
can be viewed as a weighted adjacency matrix of the complete quiver with
n^{2}\times n^{2}
vertices, which will have special combinatorial properties, as we will see later.

Before describing
X
in terms of quivers, we first remind the reader of the aforementioned construction of
X
introduced by Kulish
[
11
]
. The recipe is as follows:

Fix a local realization of the
TL_{N}
algebra
\{1,X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{N-1}\}
in the space

\mathcal{H}_{N}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{C}^{n}_{k}.

(1)

To define the above realization, take an
n\times n
invertible matrix
b
, with inverse
\overline{b}=b^{-1}
.

(2)

Through vectorization, view
b,\overline{b}
as vectors of the
n^{2}
dimensional space
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
with components
b_{ij},i,j=1,2,\ldots,n
.

(3)

Define the rank-one, Temperley-Lieb idempotent matrix
X\in\text{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})
as

\text{vec}(b)\otimes\text{vec}(\overline{b}),\qquad X_{ab;cd}=b_{ab}\overline{b}_{cd}.

X
is a generator of
TL_{N}
with
X_{k}
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{n}_{k}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}_{k+1}
of
\mathcal{H}_{N}
.

(4)

The matrix
\check{R}=qI+X
is a Hecke
R
-matrix satisfying the quantum Yang-Baxter equation

\check{R}_{12}\check{R}_{23}\check{R}_{12}=\check{R}_{23}\check{R}_{12}\check{R}_{23}

where
\check{R}_{12}=\check{R}\otimes I
and
\check{R}_{23}=I\otimes\check{R}
.

Quiver Realizations of
X

From the above construction, matrices of the form
X=\text{vec}(b)\otimes\text{vec}(\overline{b})
lead to nontrivial
R
-Hecke matrices. Following in the vein of the adjacency matrices of the previous section, we ask which quivers have adjacency matrices that can be expressed as
\text{vec}(b)\otimes\text{vec}(\overline{b})
.

To begin,
b
is an arbitrary
n\times n
invertible matrix, as such,
\text{vec}(b)\,\otimes\,\text{vec}(\overline{b})
can have negative entries. With this in mind, it is more natural to consider weighted quivers, otherwise, the restrictions we impose on
b
will be too rigid to lead to interesting Hecke
R
-matrices. Regardless, the weighted quivers we obtain have an underlying quiver structure that is similar to the quivers we have looked at in previous section; the connected components of the quiver are complete.

The following theorem is a simple yet quite useful observation, but before stating the theorem, we introduce some terminology. We will call
b_{ij}
the source entries and
\overline{b}_{kl}
the target entries for reasons that will become apparent in the proof of the following theorem. Furthermore, as
b
can be viewed as a weighted matrix, we may view any
b_{ij}
as a weight
\alpha_{ij}
. We call such an
\alpha_{ij}
a source weight. Similarly, we may view
\overline{b}_{kl}
as a weight
\beta_{kl}
, which we call a target weight.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
be a
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrix. Then
X=\text{vec}(b)\,\otimes\,\text{vec}(\overline{b})
for
b
an invertible
n\times n
matrix if and only if
X
is a weighted adjacency matrix of a quiver
Q_{X}
with the following structure:

•

Q_{X}
has
n^{2}
vertices indexed as pairs
\{(i,j):1\leq i,j\leq n\}
.

•

Edges exist between two vertices
(i,j)
and
(k,l)
exactly when the entries
b_{ij}
and
\overline{b}_{kl}
are both nonzero. In particular,
Q_{X}
is a complete graph among the nonzero source entry vertices and the nonzero target entry vertices.

•

The weights of
Q_{X}
factor multiplicatively as
w(\text{source})\cdot w(\text{target})
. Additionally, the source weights and the target weights form inverse matrices.

Lastly, we observe the following four-cycle equation holds:

X_{(i,j),(k,l)}\cdot X_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),(k^{\prime},l^{\prime})}=X_{(i,j),(k^{\prime},l^{\prime})}\cdot X_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),(k,l)}

Proof.

First, we suppose
X=vec(b)\,\otimes vec(\overline{b})
for
b
an invertible
n\times n
matrix. We write
u=vec(b)
and
v=vec(\overline{b})
, thus
X=uv^{T}
, where
u,v
are column vectors with
n^{2}
entries and
X
is an
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrix.

Regarding the first bullet point, suppose we have a basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then
\{e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
is a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
, meaning the basis vectors are indexed by ordered pairs
(i,j)
. In particular, any operator
X:\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
is represented by an
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by pairs
(i,j)
. In the
\{e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
basis, we may write
vec(b)=\displaystyle{\sum_{i,j}^{n}b_{i,j}e_{i}\otimes e_{j}}
and
X_{(i,j),(k,l)}=b_{ij}\cdot\overline{b}_{kl}
. As the basis vectors of
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
are
e_{i}\otimes e_{j}
, the vertices of
Q_{X}
can be expressed as

\text{vertices}(Q_{X})=\{(i,j)\,\mid\,1\leq i,j\leq n\}.

For the second bullet point, the entries of
X_{(i,j),(k,l)}
are defined as

X_{(i,j),(k,l)}=b_{ij}\overline{b}_{kl};

therefore, if
b_{ij}=0
, then
X_{(i,j),(k,l)}=0
for all
(k,l)
, meaning vertex
(i,j)
has not outgoing arrows. Similarly, if
\overline{b}_{kl}=0
, then
(k,l)
has no incoming arrows. This is the intuition behind calling
b_{ij}
source entry vertices and
\overline{b}_{kl}
target entry vertices. Thus in order to have an arrow
(i,j)\rightarrow(k,l)
, one must have both
b_{ij}\neq 0
and
\overline{b}_{kl}\neq 0
.

The observation that
Q_{X}
is complete among nonzero source entries
b_{ij}
and nonzero target entries
\overline{b}_{kl}
follows immediately from the above realization.

For the final bullet point, suppose we have an arrow
(i,j)\rightarrow(k,l)
, then if we label
(i,j)
with the scalar
\alpha_{ij}=u(i,j)=b_{ij}
and the vertex
(k,l)
with the scalar
\beta_{k,l}=v(k,l)=\overline{b}_{kl}
, then the weight of the arrow
(i,j)\rightarrow(k,l)
is

w((i,j),(k,l))=X_{(i,j),(k,l)}=b_{ij}\cdot\overline{b}_{kl}=\alpha_{ij}\cdot\beta_{k,l}=w((i,j))\cdot w((k,l)).

By definition, we have
b=(\alpha_{ij})
and
\overline{b}=(\beta_{kl})
, meaning the source weights and the target weights form inverse matrices.

The converse of the theorem follows by defining
b=(\alpha_{ij})
and
\overline{b}=(\beta_{kl})
; the structure is entirely determined by the weights.

The four-cycle identity is immediate from definition:

\displaystyle X_{(i,j),(k,l)}\cdot X_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),(k^{\prime},l^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=b_{ij}\cdot\overline{b}_{kl}\cdot b_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\cdot\overline{b}_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=b_{ij}\cdot\overline{b}_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\cdot b_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\cdot\overline{b}_{kl}

\displaystyle=X_{(i,j),(k^{\prime},l^{\prime})}\cdot X_{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}),(k,l)}.

The four-cycle identity is really just equivalent to
X
being a rank-one matrix.
∎

4.
Application: Leavitt path algebra over quiver with relations

Let
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,r)
be a quiver where
Q_{0}
is the set of vertices,
Q_{1}
is the set of arrows and both
s
, and
r
are functions from
Q_{1}
to
Q_{0}
such that for any arrow
e
from
u
to
v
,
s(e)=u
and
r(e)=v
and let
\mathbb{K}
be a field. A quiver with relations is a pair
(Q,R)
where
Q
is a quiver and
R
is a two-sided ideal of
\mathbb{K}Q
generated by a set of relations. The path algebra over a quiver with relations
(Q,R)
is

P_{\mathbb{K}}(Q,R)=\mathbb{K}Q/R.

Given a quiver
Q
, we extend it to a new quiver
\overline{Q}
, where for each arrow
e\in Q_{1}
, we have, in addition, an arrow
e^{\ast}
in the opposite direction, called the ghost arrow. So we have
r(e^{\ast})=s(e)
, and
s(e^{\ast})=r(e)
. We call the new quiver
\overline{Q}
the double quiver. The Leavitt path algebra
L_{\mathbb{K}}(Q)
over quiver
Q
is defined as the quotient algebra of
\mathbb{K}\overline{Q}
by the so-called Cuntz-Krieger relations.

(CK1)
e^{\ast}f=\delta_{e,f}r(e)
for each
e,f\in Q_{1}
.

(CK2)
v=\sum_{e\in Q_{1},s(e)=v}ee^{\ast}
for every regular vertex (that is, vertex which emits at least one and only finitely many arrows)
v\in Q_{0}
.

Now, suppose we have a quiver with relations
(Q,R)
, then the Leavitt path algebra over
(Q,R)
is defined as

L_{\mathbb{K}}(Q,R)=L_{\mathbb{K}}(Q)/R.

This motivates us to study quivers with RTT relations determined by Hecke R-matrices and we study Leavitt path algebras over quivers with such relations.

Let
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,r)
be a row-finite quiver. For each
u\in Q_{0}
, let
L(u):=\Span_{\mathbb{K}}\{l\in Q_{1}:s(l)=r(l)=u\}
be the
\mathbb{K}
-vector space of loops at
u
. Consider invertible linear maps
R_{u}\in GL(L(u)\otimes L(u))
for each
u\in E_{0}
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

(Quantum Yang-Baxter Equation)

(R_{u}\otimes I)(I\otimes R_{u})(R_{u}\otimes I)=(I\otimes R_{u})(R_{u}\otimes I)(I\otimes R_{u})

(2)

(Hecke Condition)

R_{u}^{2}=1+(q-q^{-1})R_{u}

Let
L(u)
be
k
-dimensional. Suppose
L(u)\cong U\otimes W
. We define the matrix of loop generators
X^{(u)}
at
u
as
X^{(u)}=\sum_{i,j}E_{ij}\otimes x_{ij}\in End(U)\otimes P_{\mathbb{K}}(Q,R)
. We impose the following RTT condition:

R_{u}X_{1}^{(u)}X_{2}^{(u)}=X_{2}^{(u)}X_{1}^{(u)}R_{u}

where
X_{1}^{(u)}=X^{(u)}\otimes I
and
X_{2}^{(u)}=I\otimes X^{(u)}
.

Let
I_{loop}
be the two-sided ideal of
\mathbb{K}Q
generated by the union of all these local RTT relations over
u\in Q_{0}
.

The path algebra of the quiver with these RTT relations determined by Hecke condition is defined as
P_{\mathbb{K}}(Q,R)=\mathbb{K}Q/I_{loop}
and the Leavitt path algebra over the quiver with these RTT relations determined by Hecke condition is defined as the quotient algebra of
P_{\mathbb{K}}(\overline{Q},R)
modulo Cuntz-Krieger conditions.

We give below some examples to illustrate the above construction.

Fix an integer

We identify

by sending

e_{i}\otimes f_{j}
,

\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}

Let
\{E_{ij}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
denote the standard matrix units in
M_{n}(K)
.
The standard
R
-matrix
R\in\End(K^{n}\otimes K^{n})
for
GL_{q}(n)
is defined by

(3)

R\;=\;q\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{ii}\otimes E_{ii}\;+\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}E_{ii}\otimes E_{jj}\;+\;(q-q^{-1})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\

Equivalently, in terms of its action on the standard basis

\{e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
of

one has

R(e_{i}\otimes e_{j})=\begin{cases}q\,e_{i}\otimes e_{i},&i=j,\\[4.0pt]
e_{i}\otimes e_{j},&i<j,\\[4.0pt]

It is well known that

satisfies the quantum Yang–Baxter equation and the Hecke condition

We now define

X_{1}:=T\otimes I_{n}\in\End(K^{n}\otimes K^{n})\otimes P_{\mathbb{K}}(Q,R),\qquad X_{2}:=I_{n}\otimes T\in\End(K^{n}\otimes K^{n})\otimes P_{\mathbb{K}}(Q,R),

(4)

is the path algebra of

4
).

R_{ij}^{ab}=\begin{cases}q,&a=i=j,\,b=i,\\[4.0pt]

\displaystyle t_{ij}t_{i\ell}

\displaystyle=q\,t_{i\ell}t_{ij},

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
Q
be a finite quiver with symmetric adjacency matrix
A
satisfying
A^{2}=\mu A
and
A^{3}\neq 0
. Then
P=\mu^{-1}A
is an idempotent commuting with the flip
F
on any tensor realization of
A
(such as
A\otimes I
,
I\otimes A
, and linear combinations thereof), and
R(q)=qP-q^{-1}(I-P)
solves the quantum Yang-Baxter equation and the Hecke condition. For each vertex
u
, the restriction of
R(q)
to
L(u)\otimes L(u)
yields Hecke matrices
R_{u}
that produce the RTT relations of this section.

Proof.

Let
Q
be a finite quiver with symmetric adjacency matrix
A
satisfying

A^{2}=\mu A
and
A^{3}\neq 0
for some scalar
\mu\in K^{\times}
.

Define

P:=\mu^{-1}A.

Then

P^{2}\;=\;\mu^{-2}A^{2}\;=\;\mu^{-2}\mu A\;=\;\mu^{-1}A\;=\;P,

so
P
is an idempotent. Since
A
is symmetric,
A^{T}=A
, hence

P^{T}=P
, so
P
is also symmetric.

Let
V
be the vertex space with basis indexed by
Q_{0}
, and consider
the tensor product
V\otimes V
. Let
F:V\otimes V\to V\otimes V

be the flip map,
F(v\otimes w)=w\otimes v
. Any tensor realization
of
A
(such as
A\otimes I
,
I\otimes A
, and linear combinations thereof)
is represented by a symmetric matrix; thus conjugating by
F
simply transposes
the corresponding matrix. As
P
is symmetric, we have

FPF=P^{T}=P.

Equivalently,
P
commutes with
F
:

FP=PF

on any tensor realization of
A
.

Define

R(q):=qP\;-\;q^{-1}(I-P)\;=\;(q+q^{-1})P\;-\;q^{-1}I

as an operator on the relevant tensor space (
V\otimes V
or
L(u)\otimes L(u)
).
We claim that
R(q)
satisfies the Hecke relation

(R(q)-qI)(R(q)+q^{-1}I)=0.

A direct computation using only
P^{2}=P
shows this. Indeed,

R(q)-qI=qP-q^{-1}(I-P)-qI=qP-q^{-1}I+q^{-1}P-qI=(q+q^{-1})P-(q+q^{-1})I=(q+q^{-1})(P-I),

and

R(q)+q^{-1}I=qP-q^{-1}(I-P)+q^{-1}I=qP-q^{-1}I+q^{-1}P+q^{-1}I=(q+q^{-1})P.

Thus

(R(q)-qI)(R(q)+q^{-1}I)=(q+q^{-1})^{2}(P-I)P=(q+q^{-1})^{2}(P^{2}-P)=(q+q^{-1})^{2}(P-P)=0,

since
P^{2}=P
. This proves the Hecke condition

(R(q)-qI)(R(q)+q^{-1}I)=0.

We now show that
R(q)
satisfies the quantum Yang–Baxter equation

R_{12}(q)R_{23}(q)R_{12}(q)=R_{23}(q)R_{12}(q)R_{23}(q),

where
R_{12}(q)=R(q)\otimes I
and
R_{23}(q)=I\otimes R(q)
act on

V^{\otimes 3}
(or, locally, on
L(u)^{\otimes 3}
).

First, note that
A^{2}=\mu A
and
A
symmetric imply, by Theorem 2.9,
that after reordering vertices,
A
is block diagonal with symmetric rank-one
blocks:

A=\operatorname{diag}(A_{1},\dots,A_{m}),\qquad A_{i}=a_{i}a_{i}^{\mathsf{T}},\quad a_{i}\in K^{n_{i}}.

Consequently,

P=\mu^{-1}A=\operatorname{diag}(P_{1},\dots,P_{m}),\qquad P_{i}=\mu_{i}^{-1}A_{i}

is a direct sum of rank-one idempotents. Each
P_{i}
is a projection onto the
one-dimensional subspace
Ka_{i}
, and the corresponding tensor operators

P_{i,12}:=P_{i}\otimes I,\qquad P_{i,23}:=I\otimes P_{i}

on
K^{n_{i}}\otimes K^{n_{i}}\otimes K^{n_{i}}
generate a representation
of the Temperley–Lieb algebra:

P_{i,12}^{2}=P_{i,12},\quad P_{i,23}^{2}=P_{i,23},\quad P_{i,12}P_{i,23}P_{i,12}=\frac{1}{\mu}P_{i,12},\quad P_{i,23}P_{i,12}P_{i,23}=\frac{1}{\mu}P_{i,23}.

The last two relations follow from the rank-one nature of
P_{i}
:

P_{i,12}
and
P_{i,23}
are projections whose composition factors through
the one-dimensional subspace
Ka_{i}\otimes a_{i}\otimes a_{i}
, and a
straightforward computation shows that

P_{i,12}P_{i,23}P_{i,12}
and
P_{i,23}P_{i,12}P_{i,23}

reduce to
P_{i,12}
and
P_{i,23}
, respectively.

By summing over the blocks, we obtain operators

P_{12}:=P\otimes I,\qquad P_{23}:=I\otimes P

on
V^{\otimes 3}
satisfying the Temperley–Lieb relations

P_{12}^{2}=P_{12},\quad P_{23}^{2}=P_{23},\quad P_{12}P_{23}P_{12}=\frac{1}{\mu}P_{12},\quad P_{23}P_{12}P_{23}=\frac{1}{\mu}P_{23}.

Now set

R_{12}(q):=qP_{12}-q^{-1}(I-P_{12}),\qquad R_{23}(q):=qP_{23}-q^{-1}(I-P_{23}).

Using only the idempotent relations
P_{12}^{2}=P_{12}
,
P_{23}^{2}=P_{23}

and the Temperley–Lieb relations

P_{12}P_{23}P_{12}=\frac{1}{\mu}P_{12}
,
P_{23}P_{12}P_{23}=\frac{1}{\mu}P_{23}
,
a routine algebraic expansion (see, for example, Kulish
[
11
]
)
shows that

R_{12}(q)R_{23}(q)R_{12}(q)=R_{23}(q)R_{12}(q)R_{23}(q)

as operators on
V^{\otimes 3}
. In other words,
R(q)
solves the
quantum Yang–Baxter equation.

For each vertex
u\in Q_{0}
, let
L(u)
denote the
K
-vector space spanned by
loops at
u
. The adjacency matrix
A
decomposes compatibly with the
direct sum
\bigoplus_{u\in Q_{0}}L(u)
, and the above construction restricts
to each
L(u)\otimes L(u)
. Thus we obtain local operators

R_{u}(q):=R(q)\big|_{L(u)\otimes L(u)}

satisfying the same Hecke and the quantum Yang–Baxter relations. These
R_{u}(q)
are
the Hecke matrices that produce the RTT relations used
to define the corresponding path and Leavitt path algebras over quivers
with RTT relations. This completes the proof.
∎
