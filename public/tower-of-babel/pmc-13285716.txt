An advanced automated pipeline for brain tumour segmentation on magnetic resonance imaging for gamma knife radiosurgery
Authors: Prabhakar Ramachandran, Akshay C Dhargave, Zachery M Colbert, Akash Mehta, Catherine Jones, Ryan Lusk, Michael Jenkins, Heath Foley, Mihir Shanker, Michael Huo, Matthew Foote, Mark B Pinkham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) enables precise delivery of ionising radiation to intracranial targets with steep dose gradients, demanding strict geometric fidelity of target contours [1]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides superior soft-tissue contrast for intracranial SRS planning, though geometric distortion and signal non-uniformity remain challenging [2], [3]. Manual delineation is time-consuming and subject to inter-observer variability (IOV), particularly for small or complex lesions [4], [5], [6], [7]. With increasing same-day SRS utilisation [8] (MRI-to-treatment time < four hours), automated tools that generate clinically acceptable contours are becoming increasingly necessary [9], [10].

Deep learning (DL) has revolutionised biomedical image segmentation, with U-Net and self-configuring frameworks, such as nnU-Net ('no-new-Net'), demonstrating robust performance across diverse tasks [11], [12]. Automated organs-at-risk (OAR) delineation has shown substantial time savings and improved consistency [13], [14]. In intracranial oncology, encouraging results have been reported for brain metastases [15], [16], vestibular schwannomas [17], [18], pituitary adenomas [19], and meningiomas [20], [21]. Alternative approaches, including Segment Anything Model (SAM)-based methods, have also shown potential [22]. However, clinical translation remains limited; most methods operate as standalone pipelines or address single tumour-type constraints, which limit their impact within Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM)-based workflows [10].

Common intracranial SRS targets differ substantially in imaging characteristics and segmentation challenges. Brain metastases are often multiple and variably sized, requiring sensitive detection across the entire intracranial volume. Accurate identification of the number and localisation of all lesions, irrespective of size, is critical [8], [15], [23]. At the same time, minimising false-positive (FP) detections is vital to preserving the practical utility of auto-generated contours during radiation oncologist (RO) reviews. Typically, vestibular schwannomas are solitary tumours with well-defined margins, facilitating robust auto-segmentation [17], [18], [24]. In contrast, pituitary adenomas often have ambiguous boundaries due to their proximity to critical structures [19], [25], [26]. Meningiomas, the most common primary intracranial tumours [21], exhibit variable morphology, due to their proximity to dural extensions and skull, which complicates delineation [27], [28], [29]. These variations require segmentation approaches that are robust across multiple tumour-types with differing morphological and imaging characteristics.

To our knowledge, no commercial solutions currently provide a fully integrated, multi-tumour auto-segmentation pipeline that automatically routes incoming DICOM MRI studies, infers tumour-type, and exports Radiotherapy Structure-Set (RTSTRUCT) objects directly to the SRS treatment planning system (TPS). This gap motivated an in-house development focused on clinical deployment through a pipeline that automatically processes incoming DICOM images, infers tumour-type, executes a suitable three-dimensional (3D) full-resolution nnU-Net model, and exports both DICOM images and RTSTRUCT objects to the SRS-TPS for immediate RO review. In this study, we report on the development and clinical integration of a multi-tumour auto-segmentation pipeline into the SRS planning workflow, enabling automated processing of brain metastases, pituitary adenomas, vestibular schwannomas, and meningiomas.

Materials and methods

Study design and dataset

We curated 400 post-contrast T1-weighted Magnetisation-Prepared RApid Acquisition Gradient Echo (MPRAGE) MRI cases acquired for Leksell Gamma Knife® (LGK) (Elekta Oncology Systems, Sweden) radiosurgery between 2017 and 2025. These included brain metastases, pituitary adenomas, vestibular schwannomas, and meningiomas for model development (100 per tumour-type). The study cohort comprised patients aged 28 to 85 years (median age 59 years), with a gender distribution of 268 women (54%) and 232 men (46%). All MRI examinations were performed on three different 3-Tesla MAGNETOM® Vida MRI scanners (Siemens Healthineers, Germany). Acquisition parameters: repetition time 1810–2300 ms (median 2200 ms), echo time 2.47–3.39 ms (median 2.88 ms), inversion time 900–936 ms (median 900 ms), 3D mode with acquisition matrices ranging from 256 × 256 to 352 × 352 (60% used 320 × 320, 30% used 352 × 352, and 10% used 256 × 256). Slice thickness was constant at 1 mm across the entire cohort. Reference segmentations consisted of original clinical contours created by experienced ROs during routine treatment planning. These archived contours, stored as RTSTRUCT objects, were used without re-contouring or modification. All patients provided written informed consent prior to treatment for the use of their clinical and DICOM imaging data for research purposes. The use of DICOM data in this study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (HREC/2024/QMS/110551).

A separate clinical workflow test set of 100 cases (25 per tumour-type) was utilised for prospective evaluation of the pipeline. Specifically, all vestibular schwannoma cases were single lesions (25/25), while pituitary adenomas and meningiomas were solitary in 24 out of 25 cases each. Post-contrast T1-weighted MPRAGE sequences were used, and reference segmentations were independently created by experienced ROs, with consensus, following the same clinical contouring approach used for the training dataset, prior to auto-segmentation.

Model development: data pre-processing and nnU-Net training

The DICOM series and RTSTRUCT contours were converted to Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative (NIfTI) using SimpleITK v2.3.1 and pydicom v2.4.4. Four independent tumour-specific 3D full-resolution nnU-Net models [12] (v2.2.1) were trained in Python v3.11.9 with PyTorch v2.7.1 and CUDA v11.8, one model per tumour-type. Each model was trained on 100 cases, with an 80/20 training/validation split. For each case, NIfTI files underwent standard nnU-Net pre-processing: intensity normalisation, resampling to the 3D full-resolution target voxel spacing, foreground cropping, z-score normalisation, and default data augmentation. No tumour-specific pre-processing modifications were applied.

The patch size, network depth, and learning schedule were automatically self-configured by nnU-Net. Models were trained to convergence using the default nnU-Net optimisation (Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) + cross-entropy loss) and default data augmentation, with the final weights corresponding to the best validation checkpoint. Training was conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA® RTX™ 3090 Ti (24 GB VRAM) graphical processing unit (GPU), Intel® i7-12700 K (12 cores, 3.6 GHz), 96 GB RAM, and 1 TB NVMe SSD.

Clinical integration

The automated clinical workflow established for MRI-driven SRS planning is summarised in Fig. 1. A Python script, utilising watchdog v3.0.0, was developed to serve as a DICOM listener. This script continuously monitored incoming MRI examinations from the MAGNETOM® Vida scanner for those requiring SRS planning. Relevant study metadata (e.g., study description) was extracted to identify the tumour-type and routed to the corresponding nnU-Net model for inference. Models generated voxel-wise softmax probability maps, which were converted to binary segmentations using tumour-specific thresholds self-determined by the default nnU-Net pipeline to maximise validation DSC. Binary masks were converted to RTSTRUCT objects and automatically exported to Leksell GammaPlan® (LGP) v11.4.1 for RO review prior to dose calculation and treatment planning. Lesion-level quantitative and qualitative analysis

Auto-segmentations were evaluated using Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) for volumetric overlap, 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95) for robust surface agreement, and Average Symmetric Surface Distance (ASSD) for mean bidirectional surface separation [30], [31]. Metrics were calculated in physical units (mm, cm) using scikit-image v0.21.0 and SciPy v1.11.2. Lesion-level false discovery rate (FDR) was calculated as the proportion of FP relative to true positives (TP) lesion identifications produced by the nnU-Net models.

Evaluation was performed at the lesion-level (each tumour evaluated separately) rather than the patient-level (all tumours within a patient averaged together). For brain metastases, multiple lesions per patient were treated as independent instances. Connected components were extracted from both reference and predicted masks to form individual lesion objects for scoring.

A qualitative assessment was performed by experienced ROs with SRS experience through a systematic visual inspection of automated and manual contour overlays to corroborate the quantitative findings and characterise the failure modes. A four-point Likert scale was employed to assess the clinical usability of the generated contours (4 = no changes required; 3 = minor edits required; 2 = major edits required; 1 = unacceptable). A severely degraded MRI case was processed to probe for out-of-distribution behaviour; performance was analysed separately from main-cohort statistics.

Discussion

We developed and deployed an end-to-end, DICOM-driven auto-segmentation pipeline for intracranial SRS planning that routes post-contrast T1-weighted MPRAGE MRI studies to tumour-specific 3D nnU-Net models and exports auto-generated RTSTRUCT objects directly into LGP for RO review. The workflow produced review-ready contours within two to four minutes and achieved tumour-type-dependent geometric agreement with clinical contours. Performance was highest for vestibular schwannomas and brain metastases, moderate for pituitary adenomas, and most variable for meningioma, consistent with differences in lesion morphology and anatomical complexity. Several lesions demonstrated moderately elevated HD95 despite reasonable volumetric overlap, suggesting localised boundary discrepancies in otherwise well-segmented lesions.

Our brain metastases model achieved a DSC = 0.83 ± 0.08, with an HD95 = 1.33 ± 0.55 mm and an ASSD = 0.47 ± 0.19 mm across 73 lesions. This performance is competitive with contemporary multi-institutional data reporting DSC values for brain metastases segmentation, ranging 0.76–0.95, with HD95 typically around 1–1.5 mm [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]. Our FDR = 0.15 aligns with the literature, which documents persistent challenges in distinguishing small enhancing foci, vessels, and noise [33], [35] (see Supplementary Material A).

Our vestibular schwannoma model yielded high overlap and sub-millimetre surface distances, consistent with the relatively uniform morphology and conspicuous enhancement typically observed in these lesions [17], [36]. The systematic volumetric underestimation observed in our workflow (mean difference: 0.23 cm3) aligns with the findings of Lee et al. [37] (see Supplementary Material B). Cornelissen et al. [18] reported strong agreement in the context of clinical implementation and reliability assessment (with DSC in the ∼0.9 range), reinforcing that vestibular schwannoma is a tumour entity for which high-accuracy automation is achievable under controlled imaging and contouring conventions.

Our pituitary adenoma results showed clinically acceptable overlap but larger surface deviations than the vestibular schwannoma and brain metastases models. This reflects the complex sellar/parasellar anatomy, as well as the frequent ambiguity at tumour interfaces with the cavernous sinus and adjacent enhancing structures. The broader DSC range (0.58–0.92), with HD95 extending to 4.70 mm, and the elevated ASSD values observed in our cohort are consistent with boundary ambiguity near the cavernous sinus and pituitary stalk, where manual contouring exhibits inherent IOV [19], [38], [39], [40] (see Supplementary Material C). Importantly, pituitary adenoma model demonstrated zero FP lesions (FDR = 0.00), indicating high specificity and suggesting that the model appropriately restricted predictions to the expected anatomical region, which is advantageous for clinical usability even when boundary edits are required.

Meningiomas generated by nnU-Net showed the greatest dispersion in our cohort. Multiple lesions demonstrated concurrent reductions in DSC and elevations in HD95/ASSD, indicating substantial boundary disagreements [41], [42], [43]. These discrepancies were most pronounced along irregular margins adjacent to bone and dural interfaces, where contrast enhancement patterns can be ambiguous. This is consistent with known challenges, including heterogeneous locations, variable contrast, dural tail extensions, and bone/air interfaces that degrade boundary conspicuity [44] (see Supplementary Material D).

Consistent volumetric underestimation across all tumour-types (ASV-MCV differences: −0.23 to −0.95 cm3) likely reflects training paradigms in which models learn from RO-generated contours that incorporate implicit safety margins or boundary uncertainty. Krebs et al. [45] reported a similar bias due to low-contrast conservatism. In SRS, modest underestimation may be acceptable if ROs adjust contours. Export of DICOM RTSTRUCT within two to four minutes supports LGK workflows, compared to 15–30 min of manual processing, with studies showing 30–60% time savings and reduced variability [4], [46], [47]. Our system serves as decision support; all contours require RO approval [48]. Rapid turnaround enables same-day integration, and automated DICOM export removes manual handling. Representative cases showed close concordance, with minor discrepancies in complex regions.

Clinical evaluation using a four-point Likert scale demonstrated generally high usability of the automatically generated contours. The vestibular schwannoma model showed the highest acceptability, with no contours rated unacceptable and most requiring only minor or no edits. Brain metastases also performed well, with over half requiring no modification and a low rate of unacceptable results. In contrast, pituitary adenomas required more major edits, mainly due to the anatomical complexity of the sellar region and its proximity to critical structures. Meningiomas showed intermediate performance, with most contours needing minor refinement but a small proportion deemed unacceptable. In general, Likert scoring complements geometric metrics by capturing clinical edit burden and supports the model's use as decision support with mandatory RO review (see Supplementary Material E).

One outlier case with severe image artefact resulted in catastrophic segmentation failure (DSC = 0.02, HD95 = 21.23 mm), highlighting the need for prospective quality assurance mechanisms; pipeline guardrails and post-processing strategies to mitigate such failures are discussed in Supplementary Material F.

This study is limited by its single-institution design, which may affect generalisability (see Supplementary Material G). Tumour-type identification relied on DICOM metadata heuristics that could be enhanced with natural language processing-based classification or image-driven models. Multi-centre validation will be needed to assess the generalisability of model performance across different institutions and to include additional tumour-types, such as cavernoma and craniopharyngioma. Integration with automated quality assurance and decision-support systems, along with RO-led evaluations of clinical acceptance and efficiency gain, could further enhance clinical reliability of the proposed auto-segmentation workflow.

In conclusion, this study developed a clinically integrated, multi-tumour auto-segmentation pipeline for four intracranial tumour-types in SRS, based on tumour-specific 3D full-resolution nnU-Net models trained on 100 cases per tumour. On independent clinical test sets (25 cases per tumour), the system achieved Dice scores of 0.80–0.90 with sub-millimetre to millimetre-scale surface errors for most tumour-types and produced contours within two to four minutes per case. The pipeline demonstrates the feasibility of incorporating DL-based auto-segmentation into routine LGK planning, providing a foundation for expansion to additional lesions and multi-centre evaluation.
