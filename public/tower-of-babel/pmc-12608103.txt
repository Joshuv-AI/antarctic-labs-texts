Genetic and metabolomic pathways linking frailty, chronic kidney disease, and kidney failure: a Mendelian randomization and multi-omics analysis
Authors: Yiding Chen, Kun Wang, Xiangyu Zhang, Feixiang Yang, Tianrui Liu, Andong Cheng, Yu Guan, Jinbiao Zhong, Jialin Meng, Xiansheng Zhang, Guiyi Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal population aging highlights frailty as a critical health concern, yet its genetic basis remains poorly understood. We employed Mendelian randomization (MR) and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine causal relationships between frailty index (FI) and 36 urological diseases, investigating shared genetic mechanisms. The FI was calculated using genome-wide association data, and bidirectional MR analysis with multiple statistical methods was applied to investigate causal links between FI and 36 urological diseases. Complementary observational analyses using NHANES and generalized summary MR validation were conducted, followed by genetic correlation assessments, genome annotation, and metabolomic explorations to identify mediating pathways. MR analysis identified a significant causal association between FI and renal tubule-interstitial disease, chronic kidney diseases (CKDs), diabetes with renal complications, renal failure, and acute kidney injury. Reverse MR analysis indicated a bidirectional causal relationship between FI and acute renal failure, as well as CKD and type 1 diabetic kidney disease. NHANES data confirmed FI as an independent risk factor for CKD and renal failure. Genetic linkage analyses identified strong regional correlations between FI and CKD/renal failure within the chromosome 6 locus (31,571,218–32,682,664). Genome-wide analyses uncovered 103 novel single-nucleotide polymorphism with pleiotropic effects on the frailty-urinary disease relationship. Mediation analyses implicated the complement pathway (C2), SLITRK1, and 4-methylhexanoylglutamine as putative mediators of FI effects on CKD and kidney failure. This study elucidates the bidirectional causal relationship between frailty and CKD, while identifying novel genetic variants and metabolic pathways. These findings provide a molecular basis for developing personalized therapeutic strategies targeting both frailty and urological disorders.Keywords: Frailty index, chronic kidney disease (CKD), renal failure, Mendelian randomization, multi-omics

Introduction

With the acceleration of global population aging, the health of the elderly population has gradually become an important challenge in the field of public health. Frailty is a multisystem syndrome marked by diminished physiological reserves and increased susceptibility to stress, primarily affecting metabolic, neuromuscular, and immune systems [1]. Frailty appears to disproportionately affect vulnerable populations, with pooled prevalence estimates of 47.3% in hospital-based settings and 18.8% in community-based populations [2].

Recent studies have highlighted a close association between frailty and urological diseases. Frail individuals often exhibit reduced muscle mass and weakened immune function, predisposing them to conditions such as urinary incontinence [3], overactive bladder [4], and recurrent urinary tract infections [5]. Conversely, prostate disorders [6] and other urological conditions can contribute to the development and progression of frailty through mechanisms including chronic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and malnutrition. Clinical evidence indicates that frailty significantly affects the prognosis of urological surgeries, with frail patients experiencing higher rates of postoperative complications, prolonged recovery, and increased mortality [7]. Furthermore, frailty has been shown to negatively impact treatment tolerance and outcomes in patients with urological malignancies, such as bladder [8] or renal cancer [9]. Research has demonstrated a bidirectional relationship between frailty and kidney health. Acute kidney injury (AKI), common in elderly patients, involves a rapid decline in kidney function, elevated serum creatinine, and decreased urine output. AKI significantly increases mortality in hospitalized patients and accelerates the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Frailty increases the risk of kidney disease, especially in older patients who are more prone to AKI and face worse prognoses [10]. Hemodynamic vulnerability leads to renal hypoperfusion, causing a rapid decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and higher rates of prerenal AKI [11]. Frail patients with comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and hypertension, experience further kidney damage. Inappropriate drug use, including diuretics, NSAIDs, and renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, exacerbates renal function deterioration in frail patients.

Conversely, the onset and progression of kidney disease accelerate frailty development. CKD patients often face inflammatory activation, oxidative stress, and toxin accumulation, leading to systemic decline, muscle atrophy, metabolic imbalances, and reduced quality of life, further aggravating frailty [12]. Renal insufficiency limits daily activities, accelerates physical decline, and exacerbates functional impairment, creating a vicious cycle of ‘kidney damage-frailty-re-injury’.

Although a preliminary link between frailty and urinary diseases has been established, the genetic mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear. Therefore, based on previous findings, we hypothesized a potential association between frailty and urological diseases mediated by protein–protein interactions.

Materials and methods

Our analytical framework comprised four sequential phases: (1) Bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (TSMR) analysis to investigate the frailty index (FI)-urological disease association based on genome-wide association study (GWAS); (2) Validation phase employing logistic regression with National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data; (3) Multi-omics mediation analysis integrating immunome, metabolome, and proteome data; (4) Genetic comorbidity between FI and urological diseases. It is important to note that our study adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiologically MR (STROBE-MR). The specific process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Causal associations between FI and urological diseases in MR analysis

The GWAS data for FI were extracted from a genome-wide meta-analysis conducted by Atkins et al [13]. The GWAS from two aging cohorts: UK Biobank (n = 164,610; age 60–70 years) and Swedish TwinGene (n = 10,616; age 41–87 years). Specifically, the FI quantification incorporated 49 and 44 self-reported health indicators encompassing symptoms, functional limitations, and medical diagnoses in the UK Biobank and TwinGene cohorts, respectively. The sample sizes are 175,226 (Table 1). A total of 36 common diseases of the urinary system, such as kidney diseases, urological inflammation, stones, and cancers were included in this study. The GWAS data were derived from the R9 releases of the FinnGen, with participant sample sizes ranging from 119,826 to 377,277 (https://www.finngen.fi/en/access_results) (Table 1) [14]. Furthermore, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the GWAS data sources to minimize potential sample overlap and reduce the likelihood of false-positive results.

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with FI were identified using a genome-wide significance threshold of p value <5 × 10−8 and a linkage disequilibrium threshold of a variance explained (R2) cutoff of 0.001 within a 10 Mb window for instrument variants (IVs) selection. The strength of association of the genetic variants for FI and urological diseases was quantified by the F-statistic. SNPs with F-statistic <10 were considered to have potential weak instrument bias and were excluded. To reduce the potential impact of allele effects on causal inference results, palindromic SNPs were excluded from the analysis. We further supplemented key statistical assessments to validate the reliability of instrumental variables and causal inference results. Specifically, we calculated three key metrics: (1) the average F-statistic of the instrumental variable model, (2) the cumulative R2 explained by the selected SNPs, and (3) the statistical power of the model. The integration of these three metrics allowed us to comprehensively verify the validity of our analytical framework and further ensure the robustness of the final causal inference results.

The bidirectional MR analysis was utilized to examine the associations between FI and urological diseases. We employed inverse variance weighted (IVW) to evaluate causal effects. To increase the reliability of our findings, we supplemented the study with three additional analyses methods: MR Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode. A two-sample MR analysis was conducted to estimate the causal effect of frailty on urological diseases. We also applied the Robust Adjusted Profile Score (MR-RAPS) method to further investigate the relationship between FI and urological diseases. This method allows the inclusion of some weak instrumental variables, making the results more accurate. Meanwhile, reverse MR analysis was performed to explore the causal effects of urological diseases on FI with a larger p value of 5e-6 for obtaining sufficient IVs. To evaluate the existence of heterogeneity among the instruments, we conducted Cochran’s Q test [15]. Furthermore, MR-Egger regression was conducted to determine whether the included SNPs exhibited horizontal pleiotropic effects [16]. In addition, we also used leave-one-out analysis to observe whether the causal effect estimates were stable and whether a single SNP had an excessive influence on the results. Furthermore, we used the Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) and Radial MR to identify pleiotropic effects and outliers. Meanwhile, the MR-PRESSO and Radial MR also as the methods to further verify the association between FI and urological diseases. To avoid the occurrence of false positives, we also conducted FDR correction to ensure the robustness of the results.

Exploring the relationships between FI and urological diseases in NHANES analysis

The present observational study leveraged data across three NHANES cycles spanning from 2013 to 2014 through 2017–2018. Frailty status, defined by the FI, was identified as the primary outcome measure [17–19]. FI was determined using 53 accessible items, based on the deficit accumulation approach proposed by Rockwood et al. FI was computed by dividing the sum of deficits by the total number of items, yielding a score between 0 and 1 (Table S1). Participants possessing an FI greater than 0.21 were classified as frail [17,20].

The eGFR scores were calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration algorithm. ACR ≥30 mg/g and/or eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 were used to diagnose CKD [21]. Diabetes was defined as (1) a previously reported diagnosis by medical professionals, or (2) fasting blood glucose >126mg/dL, or (3) glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥6.5 mmol/L, or (4) taking diabetes drug [22]. The urine albumin/creatinine ratio was used to compute the ACR. Collaboration algorithm. ACR ≥30 mg/g was used to diagnose DKD in T2DM patients [23]. Data on renal failure, prostate cancer, kidney cancer, testicular cancer, and bladder cancer were obtained from the results of the questionnaire.

To analyze the association between FI and urological diseases, the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using multivariate logistic regression analysis. We adjusted for age, sex, race, education level, household income, BMI, alcohol consumption, and smoking in the model.

Additional sensitivity analyses

Generalized summary data-based MR (GSMR), an extension of SMR, weights of a variance-covariance structure constructed using correlations between instrumental variables were used to calculate the total causal effect size using generalized linear models [24,25]. GSMR can more accurately assess the influence of genetic factors on complex traits. We used GSMR to further validate the association between FI and urological diseases. We performed the Steiger test to check directionality. In addition, CASUE analysis was used to explore whether the correlation between FI and urological diseases was caused by a causal relationship or a shared relationship [26].

We used the ‘coloc’ package to quantify the probability of shared causal variants between exposures and outcomes that showed significant causal effects. This package employs an approximate Bayes factor (ABF) framework to compute posterior probabilities (PP) for five mutually exclusive hypotheses: (1) neither trait shows a genetic association (PPH0); (2) only trait 1 is associated (PPH1); (3) only trait 2 is associated (PPH2); (4) both traits are associated but with distinct causal variants (PPH3); and (5) both traits are associated and share a single causal variant (PPH4). According to established conventions, colocalization was considered significant when PPH3 + PPH4 exceeded 0.8 [27].

Mediating pathways with immunomics, metabolomics, and proteomics

We investigated the potential involvement of 731 immune cells, 1400 blood metabolites, and 4907 proteins as mediators in the biological pathway linking the FI to urinary diseases. In a large-scale GWAS by Orrù et al. involving 3757 individuals, 731 immune cell parameters were explored. The classification of immune cell subtypes was based on flow cytometric measurements [28]. The data for metabolomics were resourced from Chen et al.’s study with sample size of 7659 individuals [29]. We obtained GWAS data for proteomics from a comprehensive protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) study involving 35,559 individuals of Icelandic descent [30]. The primary statistical model employed for the analyses was the IVW method. Indirect effects were calculated by multiplying the causal effect size of the FI on the mediation by the causal effect size of the mediation–urinary diseases association. The mediation ratio was subsequently computed by dividing the indirect effect by the total effect. Mediation analyses were restricted to results where the direction of the total effect coincided with the direction of mediation effect.

Genetic comorbidity between FI and urological diseases

We employed Linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) to estimate the heritability for a single trait and the genome-wide genetic correlations between frailty and chronic kidney disease, as well as renal failure. We then applied ρ-HESS to evaluate the local genetic correlation between frailty and chronic kidney disease, as well as renal failure. The local single-trait SNP heritability and local cross-rait genetic covariance estimates were used to calculate the local genetic correlations. Bonferroni correction was then applied to adjust for multiple testing (0.05/1703).

We conducted tissue-specific enrichment and gene set enrichment analyses via MAGMA [31]. We employed GWAS summary data for gene-level association analyses and the FUMA gene set for gene-set enrichment analysis. Then we used MAGMA gene property analysis to investigate the tissue specificity of the phenotype. This approach tests the correlation between disease–gene associations and tissue-specific gene expression profiles.

We conducted a multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) to identify shared risk SNPs in frailty and chronic kidney disease, and in frailty and renal failure [32]. We performed a CPASSOC analysis to check if equal SNP heritability assumptions and perfect genetic covariance between traits could bias our MTAG results [33]. Subsequently, LD clumping was performed in PLINK (1.9) to obtain independent SNPs most significantly associated with each phenotype [34]. Novel loci were defined as these independent SNPs, of which not in the linkage disequilibrium (LD r2 > 0.2 within a 1000 kb window). Finally, we further filtered the novel SNPs obtained by selecting regions of local genetic positivity identified in the HESS analysis.

Causal effects of FI on urological diseases in MR analysis

Following the removal of confounding and pleiotropic SNPs, 15 SNPs were retained as valid instrumental variables for the FI. All the instrumental variables were of sufficient strength to achieve F-statistics greater than 10. Comprehensive exclusion criteria and variant characteristics are summarized in Tables S2 and S3. At the same time, all the GWAS data were derived from different cohorts, which maximally avoided potential sample overlap (Table S4).

The two-sample MR analysis indicated that increased FI was genetically associated with higher risk of renal tubulo-interstitial diseases (p = 1.1 × 10−3, OR = 1.39, and 95% CI: 1.14–1.69), CKD (p = 1.5 × 10−5, OR = 2.34, and 95% CI: 1.59–3.45), type 1 diabetes with renal complications (p = 0.04, OR = 56.93, and 95% CI: 1.30–2489.94), type 2 diabetes with renal complications (p = 8.6 × 10−4, OR = 3.95, and 95% CI: 1.76–8.85), renal failure (p = 5.5 × 10−6, OR = 2.07, and 95% CI: 1.51–2.83), and acute renal failure (p = 1.6 × 10−3, OR = 2.15, and 95% CI: 1.34–3.45) (Figure 2(A) and Table S5). After correction by FDR, we found that FI only increase the risk of acute renal failure, CKD, renal failure, renal tubulo-interstitial diseases, and Type 2 diabetes with renal complications (Table S5). Taking into account the presence of weak IVs, we also verified the FI through MR-RAPS and found it to be related to these diseases (correction by FDR) (Table S6). The MR-PRESSO results also indicate that FI has an impact on these diseases and the effects are consistent in direction (correction by FDR). MR-PRESSO discovered that there were outliers in the analysis of ‘Neuromuscular dysfunction of the bladder’ and ‘Type 1 diabetes with renal complications’ (Table S7). Even after removing the outliers, no correlation was found. Radial MR also found that an increase in FI increases the risk of these diseases (correction by FDR). However, we found some outliers in the analysis of Type 2 diabetes with renal complications (Table S8). After removing the outliers, our MR analysis revealed that Type 2 diabetes with renal complications still maintained a correlation (Table S9). Through the analysis of various methods and FDR correction, the results indicated that FI increases the risks of acute renal failure, CKD, renal failure, renal tubulo-interstitial diseases, and type 2 diabetes with renal complications (Figure 3). However, it should be noted that only CKD and Renal failure have sufficient power (>0.8) (Table 1). Next, we will focus on the results of CKD and renal failure. A bidirectional causal relationships were identified between frailty and acute renal failure (p = 0.03, OR = 0.97, and 95% CI: 0.94–1.00), CKD (p = 0.02, OR = 1.03, and 95% CI: 1.00–1.06) and type 1 diabetes with renal complications (p = 2.2 × 10−5, OR = 1.02, and 95% CI: 1.01–1.02), while the acute renal failure decreased the risk of frailty, and CKD and type 1 DKD increased the risk of frailty (Figure 2(B)). No pleiotropy and heterogeneity were observed in MR-Egger regression and Cochran’s Q test (Table S10). No influential SNPs were identified in the leave-one-out analysis (Figure S1).

Exploring the relationships between FI and urological diseases in NHANES analysis

In the NHANES, we extracted only outcomes for kidney, prostate, bladder, testicular cancer, CKD, renal failure, kidney stones, and DKD. Additional sensitivity analyses

The findings of the GSMR support the results of conventional MR analyses and cross-sectional studies (Figure 4(B) and Table S11). The results of Steiger test support that the association of FI with CKD and renal failure is caused by the effect of exposure on outcome (Table S11). The association between FI and renal failure is mediated by causality, and there may be a shared association between FI and CKD (Figure 4(C)).

Potential downstream mediations affecting chronic kidney disease and renal failure

Immunome-, metabolome-, and proteome-wide MR analyses were conducted to explore the potential mediating pathways linking frailty to CKD and renal failure. The detailed information of the instrument variables used by the mediations can be found in Table S12. We identified 62 immune features, 93 metabolites, and 76 proteins associated with renal failure (Figure 5(A) and Table S13), and 40 immune features, 62 metabolites, and 92 proteins linked to CKD (Figure 5(B) and Table S13). We then investigated the relationship between FI and the immunome, metabolome, and proteome related to CKD or renal failure. Among them, 5 immune signatures, 3 metabolites, and 3 proteins associated with renal failure were associated with FI (Figure 5(C) and Table S14). For CKD, 7 immune signatures, 5 metabolites, and 5 proteins were associated with FI (Figure 5(D) and Table S14). We identified FI associated with 5 immune features, 3 metabolites, and 3 proteins, through further two-step mediation analysis, observed that three metabolites and one circulating protein played important roles as mediators in the mediation network of frailty and CKD, as well as one metabolite and three circulating proteins in the mediation network of frailty and renal failure, while no immune features were found to mediate these relationships (Figure 5(E) and Table S15). 4-methylhexanoylglutamine levels and C2 play a mediating role in both CKD and renal failure. SLITRK1 is a key factor that mediates 18.9% of the frailty effect on CKD. Subsequently, it should be noted that the results of the colocalization analysis indicate that only C2 is not only associated with FI but also with CKD or renal failure (Table S16).

Genetic comorbidity between FI and urological diseases

Genome-wide genetic correlations

To corroborate the results of the MR analysis, we further employed bivariate LDSC to assess genetic correlations. The analysis yielded two positive results, highlighting strong genetic correlations between frailty and chronic kidney disease (rg = 0.3479, p = 5.01 × 10-6) and between frailty and renal failure (rg = 0.3306, p = 2.27 × 10-8) (Tables S17).

Local genome-wide genetic correlations

We employed the ρ-HESS method to evaluate local genetic correlations across the genome between frailty and chronic kidney disease, as well as renal failure. The local genetic correlation was assessed across 1703 genomic partitions, with Bonferroni corrections applied for significant regions (p = 0.05/1703). During the polygenicity comparison, CKD and renal failure showed high genomic coverage, indicating strong genetic consistency, while frailty revealed lower coverage and regional imbalance, suggesting that their genetic correlation may be concentrated in specific genomic regions (Figure S2). Following multiple testing corrections, strong local correlations were identified in one distinct region in frailty and chronic kidney disease (chromosome 6, 31571218 to 32682664, p = 5.71 × 10-4), as well as frailty and renal failure (chromosome 6, 31571218 to 32682664, p = 7.34 × 10-4) (Table S18). In addition, we conducted a colocalization analysis of FI and CKD, as well as renal failure in the region. The colocalization analysis indicated a significant correlation between FI and these diseases (PPH3 + PPH4 > 0.8), as shown in Table S19. The colocalization results are presented in Figure S2. However, the region is in the major histocompatibility complex (MHC). The correlation might be caused by linkage disequilibrium.

Tissue-level SNP heritability enrichment

We used MAGMA to evaluate tissue-level enrichment of SNP heritability via GTEx data for 54 tissues (Table S20). Our approach included gene-level association analysis and enrichment analysis, which highlighted several significant genes. By conducting a gene-level analysis of SNPs near tissue-specific genes and their association with diseases, we identified significant associations between specific genes and corresponding diseases (Figure 6(A) and Table S21). We identified significant SNP heritability enrichment in several tissues in frailty, chronic kidney disease and renal failure by applying an adjusted significance threshold of p = 0.05 (Figure 6(B)).

Identification of shared risk SNPs

We conducted two complementary cross-trait meta-analyses MTAG and CPASSOC using evidence of substantial genetic correlations (Tables S22 and S23). Subsequent SNP refinement was achieved via PLINK 1.9 for clumping, setting stringent criteria to ensure SNP independence. Novel loci were designated for independent SNPs not in linkage disequilibrium (LD r2 > 0.2 within a 1000 kb radius), with SNPs flagged in our cross-trait GWAS meta-analysis. Through targeted screening of regions highlighted by Hess analysis, we pinpointed 13 novel, genome-wide significant independent risk SNPs shared between frailty and CKD, and 14 shared SNPs for renal failure (Figure 6(C), Tables S24 and S25).

Discussion

This study systematically examined the association between the FI and CKD and renal failure using MR analysis. It also explored potential mechanisms underlying these relationships. The results suggest that FI is a causal risk factor for CKD and renal failure, rather than just a comorbidity. This conclusion was supported by two-sample MR analysis, logistic regression, and the Steiger test, which excluded the possibility of reverse causality. In light of the substantial global CKD burden reported by the Global Burden of Disease collaboration, the observed effects underscore prevention and early intervention [35]. Moreover, the role of frailty as a prognostic – and potentially modifiable – construct in nephrology aligns with recent comprehensive reviews [36,37]. Chen et al.’s demonstrated that FI remained a risk factor for CKD after adjusting for potential confounders, including body mass index, inflammatory bowel disease, waist-to-hip ratio, and C-reactive protein [38]. Meanwhile, studies have shown that physical frailty is linked to a higher mortality risk in CKD patients, and its prevalence is lower in kidney transplant recipients than in dialysis patients. This suggests that CKD severity and dialysis may influence frailty incidence, with frailty serving as a predictor of poor prognosis in CKD [39]. Therefore, FI holds significant public health importance. It may serve as an early predictor of CKD, and its assessment in high-risk populations may help reduce CKD incidence.

Chronic inflammation is recognized as a key mechanism through which FI influences CKD progression. This study identified C2 as a key complement system component that may contribute to FI-related CKD development, reinforcing the role of inflammation in CKD progression. Although direct evidence linking C2 to CKD is limited, C2 deficiency is typically associated with impaired complement system function. C2-deficient patients frequently experience bacterial infections [40] or autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [41]. C2 serves a crucial regulatory function in the early activation phase of the classical complement pathway and contributes to the formation of the classical pathway C3 convertase, C4b2a [42]. C4b2a triggers the downstream complement cascade, leading to the production of pro-inflammatory mediators, including C3a and C5a, and the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) [43]. And C5a promotes the pericyte-to-myofibroblast transition by activating the pERK signaling pathway, directly contributing to renal fibrosis [44]. Studies indicate that recombinant human C2 (rhC2) can restore classical complement pathway activity in vitro. It enhances bacterial defense by activating the complement cascade and promoting C3 fragment deposition in serum, suggesting its potential therapeutic value [45]. Complement system inhibitors, such as C5a receptor antagonists, have demonstrated anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects [46]. Additionally, regulating C2 may offer a novel approach to modulating excessive complement activation.

Metabolic dysfunction promotes the development of CKD and frailty, while the occurrence of CKD and frailty further exacerbates metabolic dysfunction [47]. And this study is the first to identify 4-methylhexanoylglutamine as a potential metabolic mediator that may affect the development of CKD and renal failure. 4-methylhexanoylglutamine may be a glutamine derivative. The present study revealed that 4-methylhexanoylglutamine levels are significantly elevated in non-endometrioid endometrial cancer and correlate with an increased disease risk [48]. Additionally, another study suggested that 4-methylhexanoylglutamine may play a protective role against the risk of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [49]. To date, no direct studies have examined the relationship between 4-methylhexanoylglutamine and frailty or CKD, and its potential as a biomarker requires further validation.

SLITRK1 is a key factor that mediate 18.9% of the frailty effect on CKD. SLITRK1 is primarily and differentially expressed in both immature and mature neural tissues in humans and mice [50,51]. SLITRK1 is involved in regulating the noradrenergic system. Studies have demonstrated that Slitrk1 knockout mice exhibit excessive noradrenergic fiber growth during the neonatal period, elevated norepinephrine (NE) levels in the prefrontal cortex, and anxiety-like behavior [52]. The influence of the sympathetic nervous system on the progression of CKD is well known. CKD is commonly associated with renal sympathetic hyperactivity [53]. Increased renal sympathetic nerve activity stimulates renin secretion, facilitates tubular sodium reabsorption, and decreases renal blood flow [54]. Increased NE levels are observed in CKD patients and are strongly linked to renal injury progression [55]. Therefore, SLITRK1 protein may play an important role in kidney damage progression.

Within chromosome 6 (positions 31,571,218–32,682,664), we identified 10 SNPs linked to CKD and 13 SNPs linked to renal failure. All 10 SNPs linked to CKD are also associated with renal failure.

Among these 10 SNPs, three SNPs (rs805262, rs3020644, and rs17425622) were associated with immune diseases. The rs805262 has been implicated in the association between multiple autoimmune diseases and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD). The rs805262 variant within the BAG6 gene has been identified as a key genetic factor contributing to the progression of autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD), Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) and SLE toward NMOSD. This suggests that rs805262 may influence the development of these autoimmune diseases via BAG6, thereby increasing the risk of NMOSD [56]. The rs3020644 variant is located within the C2 gene region. This finding partially supports the abnormality of the C2 system we have concluded above. However, whether C2-rs3020644 directly influences C2 protein function or is linked to other pathogenic loci through linkage disequilibrium requires further validation via molecular experiments [57]. The rs17425622, located in the HLA-DRB5 gene, contributes to the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis and Parkinson’s disease through immune regulation [58]. As a key component of the antigen presentation pathway, HLA-DRB5 facilitates immune recognition by presenting extracellular peptide antigens to T cells. Its expression was significantly altered between healthy and diseased states in diverse cell populations, including both immune cells (B cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, mast cells, T cells) and structural cells (endothelial and epithelial cells), suggesting a broad involvement in disease-associated immune responses [59]. Variations in rs17425622 may disrupt HLA-DRB5 function, potentially leading to immune system dysregulation. None of these three SNPs have been directly linked to CKD or renal failure; however, they may contribute to immune system diseases by disrupting autoimmune regulation. These autoimmune diseases, such as SLE and SS, are risk factors for CKD and renal failure [60,61].

Four SNPs (rs3134943, rs1980496, rs11751024, and rs3135002) have been identified as associated with metabolic diseases. Metabolic disorders are closely associated with frailty, CKD, and renal failure [62]. Rs3134943, located in the AGER gene, has been associated with diabetes and atherosclerosis in recent studies [63–65]. Rs1980496 was reported associates with T2D at a genome-wide significance level. Rs11751024 is associated with multiple clinical traits, including psoriasis, type 1 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases [66]. Current research on rs3135002 primarily focuses on its association with type 1 diabetes, particularly its impact on fasting and random C-peptide levels [67]. No studies have demonstrated a direct link between these SNPs and frailty, CKD, or renal failure. Therefore, we hypothesize that these SNP increases the risk of CKD or frailty by influencing diabetes or metabolic syndrome, which may contribute to the development of the other, further exacerbating metabolic syndrome.

Although this study provides important new insights, the following limitations still exist. The GWAS data of this study mainly come from individuals of European descent, and future verification in multi-ethnic populations is needed to improve the universality of the conclusions. MR analysis relies on SNP as an instrumental variable, and there may be horizontal pleiotropy effects. Measurement error of FI as a phenotype: There is heterogeneity in the definition of FI in different studies. Future studies should combine a standardized FI scoring system and longitudinal cohort data to further verify the clinical value of FI in affecting CKD. At the same time, we acknowledge that although the GWAS datasets used in the MR analysis were derived from independent cohorts, all cohorts included participants of European ancestry, making some degree of sample overlap unavoidable. Finally, our mediation analysis did not apply multiple hypothesis testing correction, which may reduce the robustness of the results.

Previous studies only observed the correlation between FI and CKD outcomes, but could not distinguish the causal relationship. This study used MR analysis for the first time to provide causal evidence that FI affects the development of CKD, excluding the interference of confounding factors and reverse causality. The key molecular mechanism of FI affecting CKD was revealed for the first time. And this study first reported the role of C2 (complement factor), 4-methylaminoglutamine (metabolite) and SITRK1 (gene) in the effect of FI on CKD progression, which suggesting that FI may promote the development of CKD through immune, metabolic and neuroinflammatory pathways. Through LDSC genetic correlation analysis, SNP heritability enrichment regions between FI, CKD and renal failure were found, and new genome-wide significant independent risk SNPs were identified, providing new targets for future genetic research and personalized treatment.
