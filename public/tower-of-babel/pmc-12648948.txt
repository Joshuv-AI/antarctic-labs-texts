Uncovering the hidden costs of free tuberculosis care: economic narratives of informal treatment supporters in rural Ghana
Authors: Robert Bagngmen Bio, John Azaare, Richard Ali Laar, Abraham Titiati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) control strategies in Ghana emphasize free diagnosis and treatment, often overlooking the economic burden borne by informal treatment supporters typically family members, friends or community members who provide day-to-day care remains largely invisible in health policy discourse. These supporters play a crucial, yet underrecognized role in TB management, especially in rural areas where health system limitations intensify reliance on unpaid informal treatment supporters.ObjectivesThis study explores the direct and indirect costs incurred by informal TB treatment supporters in rural Ghana, aiming to uncover the economic impact of caregiving in a context where TB care is officially free.MethodsA qualitative narrative inquiry design was employed involving 60 purposively selected treatment supporters across three districts in the Bono Region of Ghana. Participants were selected based on their support role to TB patients currently or recently on treatment. Data were collected through in-depth interviews, transcribed verbatim, and analysed thematically using NVivo 12 software. Five (5) major themes emerged from the analysis these includes; Direct costs of treatment support (these include out-of-pocket on transport to the health facility, food and communication not covered by the TB program), Income loss due to travel and waiting time at health facility, Income loss due to time away from work (absenteeism from farming, trading and other wage labour), Economic sacrifices and structural neglect of informal treatment supporters.ResultsAlthough TB treatment is officially free in Ghana, informal treatment supporters incur substantial hidden costs. These include direct out-of-pocket expenses for transport, food, and communication, and indirect costs such as lost income and disrupted livelihoods. Supporters adopt coping strategies like borrowing, asset sales, and meal reduction, deepening household vulnerability. Many experiences emotional distress, social stigma, and receive no institutional recognition or support, despite being central to patient adherence and care continuity.ConclusionDespite free TB care, informal supporters in rural Ghana face significant hidden economic and emotional burdens. These unrecognized costs strain households and threaten the sustainability of community-based care. Policy reforms should acknowledge their role and extend social protection to reduce their financial vulnerability.Keywords: Costs, Economic burden, Ghana, Bono region, Informal treatment supporters, Psychosocial burden, Tuberculosis

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global public health burden, with Sub-Saharan Africa disproportionately affected due to intersecting social, economic, and health system challenges. In Ghana, the World Health Organization (WHO) classifies the country as a high Tuberculosis/Human Immunodeficiency Virus (TB/HIV) burden setting. In 2022, an estimated 45,000 people were living with TB in Ghana, with pulmonary TB accounting for more than 80% of all notified cases and approximately one-quarter of patients co-infected with HIV. The disease burden is highest among adults aged 15–49 years the most economically active segment of the population placing additional strain on households when patients fall ill [1].

Ghana’s national TB policy, recommends particularly the inclusion of treatment supporters within the Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course (DOTS) strategy.

Treatment supporters are typically nominated by TB patients and trained by facility-based TB officers to supervise treatment adherence, provide psychosocial support, and sometimes accompany patients to the clinic. In this case, their work intersects with the health system, creating routine interactions such as accompanying patients to collect medication. Because they are not officially remunerated by the health system, they remain outside the formal cadre of community health workers. This contextualization highlights the complementary yet distinct role of informal treatment supporters within Ghana’s TB program.

Standard TB treatment requires a minimum of six months of daily medication, with longer regimens (9–12 months or more) prescribed for drug-resistant TB [2]. Throughout this period, patients often depend heavily on treatment supporters for accompaniment to health facilities, reminders for medication adherence, and provision of food and emotional care. Despite their vital contributions, these informal treatment supporters family members, friends, or community members providing unpaid labor, time, and resources remain largely unrecognized in TB programs and policy. These roles, however, come at a hidden cost, including forgone income, transportation expenses, food provision, and the psychological toll of prolonged caregiving [3].

Despite improvements in health infrastructure over the past decade, access to TB services remains unevenly distributed, with logistical barriers such as distance to health facilities, transportation costs, and treatment associated expenditures disproportionately affecting poor and rural households [4].

Recent studies have emphasized the need to understand TB-related costs beyond direct medical expenses [5, 6]. Yet, the majority of research has focused on the patient perspective, often neglecting the household or broader social unit that absorbs the burden of care. In Ghana, national TB policy acknowledges the importance of treatment supporters. However, there are no dedicated financial incentives, stipends, or structured training programs provided to support these individuals. Informal treatment supporters operate on a voluntary basis, without reimbursement for transport, food, or time lost from income-generating activities, despite their frequent involvement in accompanying patients to clinics and ensuring adherence to directly observed therapy [2, 3, 7]. The economic contribution and sacrifices of these caregivers are not quantified in national health accounts or TB control budgets, rendering their role largely invisible in policy planning and resource allocation.

This study seeks to illuminate the economic burden borne by treatment supporters in Ghana, focusing on the Bono Region as a case study.

In the Bono Region specifically, preliminary data suggest that treatment supporters experience economic strain that could compromise their willingness or ability to provide sustained support [8]. This is particularly concerning given that poverty and food insecurity are already widespread in the region, and any added burden may tip TB affected households into deeper economic distress. Furthermore, women who traditionally assume treatment support roles are disproportionately affected, leading to gendered economic inequities and reinforcing cycles of intergenerational poverty [9, 10].

Understanding the economic narratives of TB treatment supporters in the Bono Region is therefore not only a matter of academic interest but of public health urgency. It is essential for informing equity-oriented health financing strategies, community-based TB interventions, and policies that recognize and compensate the labor of informal health actors. This study adopts a qualitative, narrative approach to document the lived experiences and hidden costs borne by treatment supporters, contributing to the global discourse on people-centered TB care and social protection.

By focusing on the Bono Region, the study provides context-specific insights that can be extrapolated to other rural regions in Ghana and across West Africa. It also aligns with Ghana’s commitment to achieving Universal Health Coverage and the WHO End TB Strategy, which calls for eliminating catastrophic costs for all affected people, including informal treatment supporters. Without a clear understanding of the economic toll on treatment supporters, TB control efforts risk being undermined by unacknowledged barriers to treatment adherence.

Methods and materials

Study design

This study employed a qualitative narrative inquiry design, appropriate for exploring the lived economic experiences of informal supporters of TB patients in a rural Ghanaian context. The choice of method was guided by the exploratory nature of the research question, which sought to understand the hidden financial and emotional costs associated with supposedly “free” TB care.

Study setting

The Bono Region of Ghana, is primarily rural and agrarian, with over 60% of the population engaged in subsistence farming and petty trading [11]. The region has a projected population of 1,116,015 as at 2021 with an annual population growth rate of 2.3% [11]. Health care delivery in the region also follows the decentralized structure: regional, district, sub-district and community level [2]. The region has a total of 212 health facilities comprising one Regional Hospital (located at the regional capital-Sunyani), 9 District Hospitals, 82 Health Centres, 56 Clinics, 35 Private Maternity Homes and 112 Functional Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) and several other demarcated CHPS doted across the region. Two [2] out of the 11 districts do not have district hospitals - Banda and Sunyani West Districts [12]. These health facilities render both preventive and curative health care services to the population within their catchment areas [12]. In Ghana, TB epidemic is generalize, affecting every region, district and communities (National Tuberculosis Control Programme (NTCP), 2018). In Ghana, the estimated incidence of tuberculosis was 152 cases per 100,000 people and 500 deaths per 100,000 as well as 10% defaulters with the then Brong Ahafo Region alone contributing over 8% of the defaulters recorded as at 2017 [13]. TB Defaulters produce drug resistance tuberculosis, which is very difficult and more expensive to treat [14].

The study was conducted specifically in three rural districts Dormaa West, Jaman North, and Banda District characterized by high TB burden, limited health infrastructure, low socioeconomic indices and widespread reliance on informal treatment supporters due to health system limitations. These areas are predominantly agrarian, with poor road networks and limited access to health services. The Ghana Health Service delivers TB services through designated health centers and district hospitals. Despite the availability of free TB diagnosis and treatment, significant gaps in transportation, food access, and supportive care infrastructure persist, placing unintended economic burdens on treatment supporters [2].

Sampling and participants recruitment

Health facilities were purposively selected from the study districts based on their status as Designated TB Diagnostic and Treatment Centers and their recorded TB case load over the past two years. This approach ensured that recruitment reflected the actual number of patients formally diagnosed and receiving care within the health system.

In total, six facilities (2 per district) were purposively selected, comprising both district hospitals and primary health centres. These facilities were strategically located to serve large catchment populations, with distances ranging from centrally located hospitals within district capitals to peripheral health centres situated up to 20–40 km from surrounding rural communities. Such distances often required patients and their supporters to make repeated trips via transport, directly contributing to the travel-related costs highlighted in our findings.

Informal TB treatment supporters defined as non-remunerated individuals who provided care, accompaniment, or psychosocial support to TB patients during treatment were purposively recruited through these facilities. Eligible participants included family members, friends, or community members identified by TB patients as their primary sources of daily support during the course of TB treatment (intensive or continuation phase).

In our study we first identified TB patients currently receiving treatment through health workers at the selected health facilities. Tuberculosis patients then referred their supporters directly to the research team and those who provided informed consent were interviewed.

Out of 72 treatment supporters approached, 60 agreed to participate. Twelve [12] individuals declined, citing personal reasons unrelated to the research.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using a semi structure interview guide. The tools were developed based on review of existing literature on the economic impact of TB treatment support and were tailored to the Ghanaian sociocultural context through pretesting. The interview guide captured the economic dimensions of treatment support, including direct costs (e.g., transportation, food, and communication), indirect costs (e.g., loss of income, time away from work), coping mechanisms and the emotional or social implications. Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics such age of respondent, sex, marital status, educational level, monthly income and others were included in the interview guide.

In our study, one time in-depth interviews were conducted in participants’ preferred languages (Twi or English) by trained qualitative researchers fluent in both languages. Interviews were held at designated office at the selected health facilities, after treatment supporters were done with their routine schedules, to ensure comfort and confidentiality. Each interview lasted between 30 and 45 min and was audio-recorded with the participant’s informed consent. Recruitment and data collection took place from August 2019 to October 2019.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim and, where necessary, translated into English. Transcripts were imported into NVivo 12 software for coding and thematic analysis. A narrative approach was used to analyse data, focusing on how participants constructed and conveyed their support experiences. Thematic analysis was guided by Braun and Clarke’s six-step approach: familiarization, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining/naming themes, and producing the report [15].

To enhance rigor and trustworthiness, multiple strategies were applied: independent coding by two researchers, peer debriefing, member checking with selected participants, and maintenance of an audit trail. Five core themes emerged, each representing critical facets of the economic costs borne by informal TB treatment supporters in rural Ghana. The results were then presented in narratives and supported by illustrative quotes from respondents.

Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents (N = 60)

The age of respondents ranged from 18 to 68 years, with a mean age of 41.3 years (SD = 12.4). The majority were female (63.3%), while 36.7% were male. In terms of educational attainment, 40% had no formal education, 35% had completed primary or junior high school, while 25% had attained at least senior high school education.

Most respondents were married (58.3%), while 21.7% were widowed, and 20% were single. In terms of occupation, a significant proportion were engaged in subsistence farming (41.7%) and petty trading (30%), with others working as civil servants (13.3%) or artisans/masonry workers (15%).

Ethnically, Akan (58.3%) constituted the largest group, followed by Northerners (25%) and Ewe (16.7%). The dominant religion was Christianity (68.3%), followed by Islam (25%) and Traditional African Religion (6.7%).

The average household size was 6.1 persons, with most households (53.3%) comprising 5–8 members. Smaller households (4 or fewer members) accounted for 30.0%, while larger households with more than 8 members represented 16.7% of the sample. In terms of relationship to the TB patient, 46.7% were immediate family members (spouse, parent, or sibling), while 35% were extended relatives (uncle, aunt, cousin), and 18.3% were neighbours or close friends.

Respondents had supported TB patients for a mean duration of care of 4.8 months (range: 1–12 months). Regarding monthly income, 43.3% reported earning less than GHS 500, 35% earned between GHS 500–1000, and only 21.7% earned above GHS 1000. The district representation was relatively even, with Jaman North (36.7%), Dormaa West (33.3%), and Banda (30%).

Emerging themes on economic costs of informal TB support

Thematic analysis yielded five major themes representing the economic costs experienced by informal treatment supporters, categorized under direct, indirect costs, economic sacrifices, psychosocial costs and structural neglect. Each theme is supported by illustrative quotes.

Direct costs

The informal treatment support role exposes supporters to direct, out-of-pocket expenditures. These unacknowledged costs including transport, food, and communication remain invisible, yet significantly affect the ability of supporters to sustain their role.

Transport costs

Transport to and from health facilities emerged as the most recurrent financial challenge. Supporters bore the full cost of repeated trips without reimbursement:

“I pay for transport fare every week to and back from the treatment centre without any reimbursement. These costs build up over time and now getting money for transport is a lot of burden on me.” (Female supporter).

“Sometimes I am left with no choice than to get money by all means just to transport him to the health facility. If I do not go with him, I am afraid he might miss his medications. The transport money is never planned for, and it is affecting my small business.” (Female supporter).

“Even though treatment is free, the constant back and forth to the health facility costs me more than I can afford. I have had to stop some farm work to make time for these trips.” (Male supporter).

Food costs

Many supporters described the necessity of purchasing food and water during lengthy clinic visits, which often coincided with the loss of daily income:

“Sometimes we stay long at the clinic, so I have to buy food and water for both of us. That small money could have been used to buy soap or food at home.” (Female supporter).

“On clinic days, I cannot go to the farm. I lose that day’s income and still have to spend on food while we wait. It is a double loss.” (Female supporter).

Communication costs

Supporters also incurred costs by using personal mobile phones to maintain contact with healthcare providers or update treatment plans. Though individually small, these expenses accumulated significantly:

“I buy credit almost every week just to keep in touch, not for myself but because of the patient.” (Female supporter).

“Sometimes the patient is too sick or does not have a phone, so I call the health centre to ask questions or update them. It is small money, but over time it becomes plenty, especially when I do not have steady income.” (Male supporter).

Indirect costs

The treatment support responsibility often required supporters to forgo income-generating activities. Whether it meant abandoning market stalls, missing critical agricultural seasons, or halting small businesses, these time-bound obligations directly translated into lost productivity and income, exacerbating financial vulnerability in already struggling TB affected households.

Several caregivers shared how the time demands of treatment support activities disrupted their livelihoods;

“I lost my market stall space because I had to follow him to the health facility every week. Someone else took over my spot.” (Female treatment supporter).

“Farming is seasonal. I missed the planting season while taking care of my brother. That means no harvest this year.” (Male treatment supporter).

“My petty trading stopped completely. Who will watch the baby and him if I go?” (Female treatment supporter).

Others described the longer-term ripple effects of lost time and opportunity;

“I was saving to learn hairdressing, but now all the time and money go into caring for my cousin. My plans are on hold indefinitely.” (Female treatment supporter).

“Even when I am not at the health facility, I cannot go to work. I am always alert, helping him with everything. It is like being on duty 24/7.” (Male treatment supporter).

Economic sacrifices

Informal treatment supporters, typically family members, reported significant financial strain arising from their caregiving responsibilities. Sustaining a loved one through a prolonged TB treatment regimen compelled many to make difficult trade-offs that threatened household well-being. Coping mechanisms included borrowing money, selling productive assets, and cutting down on essential household needs such as food, education, and future livelihood investments. These sacrifices, largely unrecognized within formal health cost assessments, risk entrenching households deeper into poverty.

One female supporter explained how she sacrificed her livestock to meet food needs:

I sold my goat to buy food so he could eat. The medicine is strong and he could not take it on an empty stomach.

Another described the burden of accumulating debt:

“We borrowed from the susu group. Now we are paying back every week with interest, and I do not know how long we can keep up.” (Female treatment supporter).

For others, the economic impact meant diverting resources away from children’s needs:

“We stopped buying meat and schoolbooks. Everything now is for his treatment journey.” (Male treatment supporter).

The obligation also disrupted future livelihood security, as one male supporter shared experience;

I used money meant for farm seeds to take him to the health facility. Now the land is bare and we do not know how we will eat next season.

In extreme situations, families rationed their own meals;

“We now eat once a day so that he can have enough. It is hard, but what else can we do?” (Female treatment supporter).

Psychosocial costs

The treatment support role, largely undertaken without formal psychosocial support, exposed treatment supporters to stigma, emotional neglect, and internal distress. Beyond the patient’s illness, supporters themselves carried a significant, yet often invisible, psychological burden.

Stigma and social exclusion

Caregivers frequently reported being stigmatized within their communities, leading to social isolation and economic repercussions;

“People stopped coming to my shop when they found out I was supporting a TB patient.” (Female treatment supporter).

Such stigma eroded longstanding community relationships and diminished livelihoods, compounding the burden of caregiving.

Emotional neglect and invisible suffering

Supporters often felt overlooked by health and social systems, which focused narrowly on patients while neglecting their psychological needs;

“No one asked how I was doing; they only looked at the patient.” (Female treatment supporter).

This neglect left many emotionally depleted, struggling in silence to process their own distress.

Dual burden of distress and financial anxiety

Psychological strain was frequently intensified by economic hardship, particularly in relation to transport and loss of household income;

“The stress of seeing him cough at night and worrying about transport money was too much.” (Male treatment supporter).

This dual burden manifested as anxiety, depression, and symptoms of emotional burnout.

Existential anxiety

For some, the experience mirrored the patient’s illness, described as a slow internal decline:

“Even though I was not sick, I felt like I was dying slowly.” (Female treatment supporter).

These accounts reflect the existential weight of treatment support under prolonged conditions of fear and uncertainty.

Structural neglect

The theme structural neglect emphasizes that informal treatment supporters play an indispensable yet unacknowledged role in the control and prevention of TB. Although they provide vital contributions including transporting patients, ensuring medication adherence, managing side effects, and delivering daily care, they remain systematically excluded from TB psychosocial support services, and social protection schemes. This exclusion generates financial strain, psychosocial distress, and a pervasive sense of invisibility, with implications for both supporter well-being and patient outcomes.

On invisibility within the TB program;

“They only call me when they want to talk to the patient. Sometimes I feel invisible as a supporter.” (Male treatment supporter).

On lack of recognition as part of the care team;

“We are part of the treatment team, but no one checks on how we are coping financially or psychologically.” (Female treatment supporter).

On economic sacrifices and lack of support;

“I had to stop work to care for him. Apart from free medicines, no government support came to our aid.” (Female treatment supporter).

On financial strain and psychosocial burden;

“At times I feel more stressed and financially handicapped than the patient, but there is no formal system for us to seek help.” (Female treatment supporter).

On sustainability and burnout;

“We support TB patients from our hearts, but without recognition, how can we keep going?” (Male treatment supporter).

Discussion

This study examined the economic dimensions of caregiving among informal tuberculosis (TB) treatment supporters in rural Ghana, revealing a deeply entrenched, yet overlooked, layer of cost and vulnerability. While TB treatment is officially provided at no cost to patients, our findings affirm that the broader burden of illness, including direct, indirect, psychosocial, and systemic costs, is often offloaded onto informal supporters, predominantly family members. Through five interlinked themes, this study underscores the critical need to reconceptualize TB care as a household and community affair, not merely a patient health facility transaction.

The results illuminate the complex interplay between poverty, informal caregiving, and health system inadequacies. Although TB treatment is advertised as free in Ghana, informal supporters bear considerable direct and indirect costs. These costs are amplified by structural weaknesses in service delivery and the socioeconomic fragility of informal treatment supporters. The findings highlight how informal care labour, often performed by women, remains invisible and unaccounted for in health financing frameworks.

The first theme, direct costs, reveals a paradox: although TB services are offered “free” within Ghana’s public health system, informal supporters absorb recurrent out-of-pocket expenditures for transport, food, and communication. These costs, though unrecorded in official programmatic budgets, represent a substantial financial burden. Prior studies from sub-Saharan Africa have similarly found that transport fares and food purchases during clinic visits significantly inflate the overall cost of TB care, particularly for rural and low-income households [5, 6]. Comparable evidence from HIV caregiving research has also demonstrated that caregivers consistently incur similar hidden on costs transport, meals, and supplementary medical expenses that increase household vulnerability and undermine treatment continuity [16].

Our respondents echoed these realities with lived experiences: a female caregiver who pays weekly fares without reimbursement, another who sacrifices farm time for hospital trips, and several who regularly buy mobile credit to maintain contact with health professionals. These hidden costs erode the economic resilience of households, particularly those already living at or below subsistence levels. Evidence from HIV caregiving settings illustrates that such economic sacrifices are often borne disproportionately by women, who juggle caregiving with productive labor, thereby amplifying the intergenerational cycle of poverty [17]. Although these expenses may seem individually modest, their cumulative effect over the 6-month or longer treatment period for TB creates significant financial stress, especially in the absence of reimbursement schemes or transport subsidies [18].

This burden is further compounded by the structural geography of care long distances to health facilities and infrequent public transport which reinforces existing health access inequities [19]. These findings are consistent with evidence from HIV care studies showing that distance and transport barriers are among the most persistent obstacles to adherence and successful treatment completion [16]. Moreover, when caregivers absorb these costs without support, they face opportunity losses such as reduced time for income-generating activities, a dynamic widely observed in both HIV and TB caregiving contexts [16, 17].

Our results reaffirm international calls particularly from the World Health Organization [20, 21] and the Stop TB Partnership to eliminate catastrophic costs not only for patients but also for all individuals involved in TB care. As echoed in the broader caregiving literature, reducing the economic vulnerabilities of informal supporters is not only a matter of equity but also a necessary strategy for sustaining long-term treatment outcomes.

Beyond direct spending, the study’s second theme reflects a more insidious cost: time. Time lost to caregiving translates into income foregone, whether through missed farming seasons, abandoned market stalls, or halted apprenticeships. The opportunity costs of caregiving are rarely quantified in health economics assessments but are arguably just as devastating. As documented in several resource-poor settings, including Ethiopia and India, treatment supporters of TB patients frequently abandon income-generating work, leading to long-term economic instability [22, 23]. Similar patterns have been widely observed among HIV caregivers in sub-Saharan Africa, where family members particularly women often give up productive labor to provide care, resulting in reduced household earnings and heightened poverty vulnerability [16, 24].

In Ghana, where the informal economy accounts for over 80% of employment [11], such losses can be existential. A treatment supporter who misses the planting season may lose a full year of harvest. A petty trader who forfeits their stall may lose not just a day’s income but also future business. These ripple effects extend well beyond the treatment window, threatening food security and intergenerational opportunities. Comparable to findings in HIV caregiving research, caregivers frequently report that time devoted to care work compromises both immediate income and longer-term economic resilience [16, 24]. This dual burden confirms the arguments of [25, 26], who contend that indirect costs account for the largest proportion of TB-related household expenses, yet remain invisible in most national TB program assessments.

Moreover, qualitative studies on HIV caregiving in South Africa and Uganda demonstrate that the time burden extends beyond direct care tasks, encompassing frequent hospital visits, waiting times at clinics, and emotional labor, which further limit participation in income-generating activities [27]. These parallels suggest that the economic vulnerabilities faced by TB treatment supporters are not isolated but part of a broader pattern of hidden caregiving costs in chronic infectious disease contexts.

Incorporating time-use surveys and valuing caregivers’ lost productivity could provide more accurate cost estimates. These findings support the implementation of social protection measures, such as conditional cash transfers or caregiver stipends, within TB care frameworks to help cushion against these indirect economic shocks. Evidence from HIV caregiver support programs indicates that such interventions can reduce household poverty, improve treatment adherence, and mitigate the long-term consequences of unpaid care work [24].

Faced with these financial pressures, informal treatment supporters often resort to coping mechanisms with long-term consequences. This theme illustrates how families make trade-offs, selling livestock, withdrawing children from school, reducing meal portions, or borrowing at high interest, to support TB treatment. These strategies, while effective in the short term, come at significant opportunity and human capital costs.

Research across TB-endemic settings supports this finding: informal treatment supporters frequently make decisions that may not be economically optimal but are motivated by strong social and familial obligations to care for ill relatives [18, 28]. Similar dynamics have been documented among caregivers of people living with HIV/AIDS, who often engage in financially draining practices such as liquidating productive assets, reducing food consumption, or exiting the labor force to sustain caregiving roles [24, 29]. These decisions reflect what has been described as a moral economy of care, driven by obligation, reciprocity, and affection rather than institutional support [29]. However, such practices also risk deepening poverty traps. For instance, selling productive assets like goats or farm inputs not only compromises future income but also undermines resilience to future shocks [3, 24].

Some households borrow from informal rotating savings schemes or susu groups, entering debt cycles that outlast the treatment period. Comparable patterns have been observed in HIV caregiving contexts, where women caregivers in particular rely on informal borrowing or community-based credit schemes, leading to chronic indebtedness and psychosocial strain [3, 27]. In the absence of TB-specific financial risk protection schemes, these sacrifices remain hidden from policymakers yet profoundly influence health-seeking behaviour, adherence, and long-term household well-being. This aligns with the WHO End TB Strategy’s emphasis on eliminating catastrophic costs associated with TB care, which should extend beyond patient expenses to include the economic burden borne by informal treatment supporters.

Beyond the financial realm, treatment support activities exact a profound psychological toll. The fourth theme, psychosocial costs, highlights a silent crisis: informal treatment supporters, often without formal training or structured support, are exposed to persistent emotional distress, anxiety, and social stigma. The caregiving journey, marked by uncertainty, fear of infection, and exclusion, can culminate in burnout, depression, and secondary trauma. These findings resonate with research on HIV caregivers in sub-Saharan Africa, where similar burdens of emotional exhaustion, stigma, and isolation have been widely reported [24, 30].

Informal treatment supporters in this study described experiences of isolation, sleeplessness, and community stigma. One treatment supporter recounted being avoided by neighbours, while another lamented that no one ever inquired about her own well-being. Such accounts mirror the lived realities of HIV caregivers in South Africa, Uganda, and India, where caregivers are stigmatized, socially excluded, and sometimes blamed for the illness of their relatives [31, 32]. The emotional labor of caregiving though rarely quantified remains central to the sustainability of informal health support systems. Comparable findings from TB and HIV contexts alike suggest that stigma extends beyond patients to encompass their informal caregivers, compounding psychological strain [27, 33].

Despite their central role in ensuring adherence and offering emotional support, informal treatment supporters are routinely excluded from psychosocial interventions, health education, or even programmatic acknowledgment. This echoes the neglect of HIV caregivers in many community and clinical settings, where the absence of psychosocial support has been linked to caregiver burnout, stress-related illness, and reduced quality of care [29, 34]. Such omissions not only undermine the well-being of supporters but also risk compromising patient outcomes due to stress-related dropouts or incomplete care. Integrating mental health services and caregiver-centered interventions into TB programs drawing lessons from HIV caregiver support initiatives could substantially reduce these burdens, strengthen adherence, and acknowledge the hidden emotional economy that sustains informal treatment support.

The final theme centres on the systemic invisibility of informal treatment supporters. Despite their indispensable roles transporting patients, ensuring medication adherence, and coordinating follow-up they remain neither formally recognized nor supported by the TB health system. This institutional blind spot undermines the very foundations of community-based care.

Treatment supporters in this study consistently reported feeling excluded from care processes, social protection schemes, and even from basic transport subsidies. Such exclusion not only generates psychosocial stress but also represents a lost opportunity to strengthen patient-centered care. Similar dynamics have been documented in HIV programs across sub-Saharan Africa, where family caregivers often women shoulder disproportionate burdens without recognition or financial support [16, 24]. Evidence from Kenyan and South Africa shows that the invisibility of HIV caregivers leads to caregiver fatigue, financial strain, and in some cases, reduced quality of support for patients [16, 24]. These insights resonate with the TB context, where informal supporters function as a “hidden health workforce,” yet remain outside of formal health system frameworks.

Recent literature has increasingly called attention to the role of “informal health workers” or lay caregivers in sustaining health systems, particularly in resource-constrained settings [35, 36]. For instance, studies of HIV community caregiving highlight that integrating and supporting caregivers through stipends, training, and psychosocial support improves adherence and retention in care [27, 37]. By excluding informal supporters from TB programming, Ghana’s health system risks undermining adherence and treatment success the very outcomes that DOT is designed to ensure.

Policymakers must therefore consider formalizing support for these informal actors through recognition, training, stipends, and their inclusion in monitoring and evaluation frameworks. This is not merely a welfare issue but a pragmatic, evidence-based strategy, as demonstrated in HIV caregiving programs, where community caregivers have been effectively leveraged to extend health system reach [33, 38, 39]. Incorporating informal supporters into TB policy would help ensure effective TB control, align with sustainable development goals, and ultimately improve health outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

Our study employed a qualitative approach, which allowed us to capture rich, nuanced insights into the lived economic experiences of informal treatment supporters. However, we recognize that the qualitative scope limits the extent to which findings can be generalized across different settings or populations.

While the narratives presented provide valuable depth and contextual understanding, they do not provide population-level estimates of the financial burden incurred.

In addition, the study design did not quantify the indirect or opportunity costs of care in a systematic way, nor did it allow for a comparative assessment across diverse socioeconomic groups. We also acknowledge that our reliance on participants’ self-reported experiences may introduce recall bias or underreporting of sensitive financial hardships.

To build on these findings, future research could adopt a mixed-methods approach that combines qualitative depth with quantitative breadth. This would allow for the measurement of the economic costs borne by informal supporters at scale and for statistical testing of patterns observed in our study. Moreover, such research could evaluate targeted interventions such as transport subsidies, cash transfers, or formal integration of supporters into community health systems to assess their effectiveness in reducing the economic burden on supporters while strengthening TB care outcomes.

Conclusion

This study contributes important insight into the often-overlooked burdens carried by informal treatment supporters in TB care. While not formally integrated into TB programmes, our findings suggest that treatment supporters play meaningful roles in patient adherence, psychosocial support, and continuity of care. However, their economic, emotional, and social contributions are largely absent from formal TB policy discussions. Future interventions and policies could consider ways to acknowledge and support these contributions, thereby strengthening both patient outcomes and community engagement in the TB response.
