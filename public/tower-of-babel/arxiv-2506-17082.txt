Abstract
. A subset S S of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} has the Borsuk property if it can be decomposed into at most d + 1 d+1 parts of diameter smaller than S S . This is an important geometric property, inspired by a conjecture of Borsuk from the 1930s, which has attracted considerable attention over the years. In this paper, we define and investigate the Borsuk property for matroids, providing a purely combinatorial approach to the Borsuk property for matroid polytopes, a well-studied family of ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) -polytopes associated with matroids. We show that a sufficient condition for a matroid—and thus its matroid polytope—to have the Borsuk property is that the matroid or its dual has two disjoint bases. However, we show that this condition is not necessary by exhibiting infinite families of matroids having the Borsuk property and yet being such that every two bases intersect and every two cobases intersect. Kneser graphs, which form an important object from topological combinatorics, play a crucial role in most proofs.



1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to introduce and study the “Borsuk property” of matroids, taking a purely combinatorial approach to address the celebrated Borsuk conjecture. Formulated by Borsuk in 1933
[
4
]
, this latter states that every bounded subset
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be partitioned into at most
d+1
parts, each of diameter smaller than that of
S
. A set
S
admitting such a partition has the
Borsuk property
. This conjecture has attracted considerable attention, even after a counter-example was discovered by Kahn and Kalai in 1993
[
9
]
. Combinatorics has played a prominent role in its study and we aim at exploring further the relationship between combinatorics and this conjecture through the lens of matroids.

Consider a matroid
M
. As usual, the collection of its bases is denoted by
\mathcal{B}(M)
. The map
(B,B^{\prime})\mapsto|B\triangle B^{\prime}|
, where
\triangle
denotes the symmetric difference, forms a distance on
\mathcal{B}(M)
(actually, nothing else than the Hamming distance on the incidence vectors). The
Borsuk number
of a matroid
M
, denoted by
f(M)
, is the minimal number of parts in a partition of
\mathcal{B}(M)
where each part has diameter smaller than that of
\mathcal{B}(M)
. (“Diameter” has to be understood according to the distance just defined.) When such a partition does not exist at all, i.e., when
M
has exactly one basis, we set
f(M)\coloneqq+\infty
.

Denoting by
n
the number of elements of
M
and by
c
the number of its connected components, we say that
M
has the
Borsuk property
if
f(M)
is at most
n-c+1
. (Notice that if
M
has only one basis, then it has not the Borsuk property.) It is not too difficult to see that a matroid has the Borsuk property if and only if its matroid polytope has the Borsuk property in its affine hull(we will see that such a polytope is defined in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
but its dimension is
n-c
). Our definition of the Borsuk property for matroids is thus consistent with the original geometric definition given above. The relations between these definitions, as well as possible consequences of our work in the original context, are discussed below in Section
2
.

Our first contribution is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

If a matroid or its dual has two disjoint bases, then it has the Borsuk property.

As we will see, after introducing a suitable setting, the proof of Theorem
1.1
, given in Section
5
, essentially relies on upper bounds on the chromatic number of “Kneser graphs,” which form an important family of graphs. This makes yet another connection with combinatorics. Studying generalized Kneser graphs allows us actually to get the exact value of the Borsuk number for specific families of matroid.

Combining Theorem
1.1
with other combinatorial considerations will lead to the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Consider a matroid with at least two bases. If its rank is at most two, then it has the Borsuk property.

The proof essentially boils down to check finitely many situations. We establish the following theorem, which shows that it is possible to check in finite time whether there are matroids of a given rank failing to have the Borsuk property. But already for
r=3
, this is purely theoretical since the number of matroids to be considered would be too big.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider a matroid of rank
r
with at least two bases. If every two bases intersect, then its Borsuk number is upper bounded by
2^{r-2}(r+2)^{2}
.

Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
, and
1.3
are established in Section
5
.

The following definition naturally arises from Theorem
1.1
. A matroid has the
basis intersection property
if every two bases intersect. If a matroid and its dual has the basis intersection property, then we say that the matroid has the
strong basis intersection property
. Theorem
1.1
can be alternatively stated: If a matroid does not have the strong basis intersection property, then it has the Borsuk property.

The results above show that it is thus still very possible that every matroid has the Borsuk property. We actually formulate this as a conjecture (Conjecture
7.1
). Theorem
1.1
shows that to disprove this conjecture, it is sufficient to focus on matroids that have the strong basis intersection property. Elementary arguments show that we can actually require connectivity (Corollary
4.3
). The behavior of connected matroids having the strong basis intersection property seems mysterious and, from our experience, they are not immediate to determine. In Section
6
, we provide however many examples that rely on constructions keeping desirable properties, such as the classical series and parallel operations. The class of
\Theta_{n}
matroids will also be considered.

2.
Geometric connection:

Relation with the original Borsuk conjecture, and background

2.1.
Relation with the Borsuk conjecture

The
Borsuk number
of a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the smallest number
f(S)
of subsets in which it can be decomposed with each subset having diameter smaller than that of
S
. As shown by the following proposition, determining
f(P)
for a polytope
P
is essentially a (decidable) discrete problem. Even though this is a known fact for the community working on the Borsuk problem—at least from Larman’s work (see the comments by Kahn and Kalai
[
9
]
)—, we have not been able to find a proper reference from the literature. We provide a full proof for sake of completeness.

Proposition 2.1

(Folklore)
.

For every polytope
P
, the equality
f(P)=f(V(P))
holds (where
V(P)
denotes the vertex set of
P
).

Proof.

Consider a decomposition of
P
into parts with diameter smaller than
\operatorname{diam}(P)
. The latter induces a decomposition of
V(P)
with a non-larger number of parts and so that each part has diameter smaller than
\operatorname{diam}(P)=\operatorname{diam}(V(P))
(the diameter of
P
is attained by a pair of its vertices). Conversely, consider a decomposition of
V(P)
into
k
parts with diameter smaller than
\operatorname{diam}(V(P))
. Complete each part so as to get a decomposition of
P
into
k
parts, and such that if some part contains a vertex, it actually contains a whole neighborhood of it within
P
. Each part in this decomposition has diameter smaller than
\operatorname{diam}(P)
(the diameter of
P
is only attained by pairs of its vertices).
∎

We explain now that our definition of the Borsuk property for matroids is consistent with the original geometric definition. In that regard, we remind the definition of matroid polytopes. Let
M
be a matroid on a ground set
E
. Denote by
\chi^{S}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
the indicator vector of a subset
S
of
E
, defined by

\chi^{S}_{e}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\text{if $e\in S$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The
matroid polytope
of a matroid
M
, denoted by
P_{M}
, is the convex hull of the
\chi^{B}
when
B
ranges over
\mathcal{B}(M)
. (Contrary to what is often assumed, we also consider this definition to be valid when
M
has rank zero. In this case,
P_{M}
is just a single point, namely the point
0
.)

Proposition 2.2
.

The Borsuk number of a matroid equals the Borsuk number of its matroid polytope.

Proof.

Let
M
be a matroid. According to Proposition
2.1
, the equality
f(P_{M})=f(V(P_{M}))
holds. Since
\|\chi^{B}-\chi^{B^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}=|B\triangle B^{\prime}|
, the pair of vertices realizing the diameter of
V(P_{M})
correspond to the pair of bases realizing the diameter of
\mathcal{B}(M)
. We have thus
f(V(P_{M}))=f(M)
.
∎

The original Borsuk property depends also on the dimension of the ambient space, for which it is clearly more interesting to choose the minimal possible value. For matroid polytopes, this dimension is known:
\dim(P_{M})=n-c
, where
n
is the number of elements of
M
and
c
the number of its connected components
[
12
]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

A matroid
M
has the Borsuk property if and only if its matroid polytope
P_{M}
has the Borsuk property in
\mathbb{R}^{\dim(P_{M})}
.

Proof.

Immediate from the definitions and Proposition
2.2
.
∎

A consequence of Proposition
2.3
is that any matroid with at least two bases and without the Borsuk property would provide a counter-example to the Borsuk conjecture. We emphasize that such a counter-example is not known yet.

2.2.
Background on the Borsuk conjecture

Denote by
f(d)
the smallest value such that every subset
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be partitioned into at most
f(d)
parts with diameter smaller than that of
S
. Borsuk’s conjecture, which goes back to 1933, states that
f(d)=d+1
. The vertices of a regular
d
-simplex shows that
f(d)\geqslant d+1
and it is known that
f(d)\leqslant d+1
holds in several cases; see
[
7
,
15
]
and
[
2
, Chapter 31]
for nice surveys. After sixty years, this outstanding conjecture was disproved by Kahn and Kalai
[
9
]
for
d=1325
and for every
d\geqslant 2014
. The latter quantities have been improved and it is currently known to be false when
d\geqslant 64
and true when
d\leqslant 3
.

It is worth pointing out that all counter-examples on Borsuk’s conjecture rely on purely combinatorial arguments. Erdős
[
6
]
and Larman
[
10
]
were among the first to suggest the construction of combinatorial counter-examples. All known non-trivial lower bounds for
f(d)
have been obtained by considering two particular classes of integral polytopes:
(0,1)
-polytopes and
(0,1,-1)
-polytopes (or
cross-polytopes
). Estimations on the asymptotic behavior of
f(d)
for both
(0,1)
-polytopes and cross-polytopes have been treated by Raĭgorodskiĭ
[
16
,
18
,
17
]
. By considering graph coloring problems and optimal binary codes, Ziegler
[
22
, Theorem 1]
showed that every
(0,1)
-polytope of dimension
d\leqslant 9
has the Borsuk property, i.e., can be decomposed into at most
d+1
parts with diameter smaller than its diameter. Closely related results have also been obtained by Payan
[
13
]
, Petersen
[
14
]
, and Schiller
[
19
]
.

3.
Notation and basic facts about matroids

We assume basic knowledge about matroids. In this section, we provide notation and properties of matroids that are maybe less known but that are important for our purpose. For proofs of these properties and complementary information about matroids, we refer the reader to the book by Oxley
[
12
]
.

3.1.
Some notation

The diameter of a set
X
, with respect to a suitable distance, is denoted by
\operatorname{diam}(X)
. The set of bases of a matroid
M
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}(M)
. The
uniform matroid
whose bases are formed by all subsets of size
r
of the ground set
[n]
is denoted by
U_{r,n}
. The graphic matroid associated with a graph
G
is denoted by
M(G)
.

3.2.
Connectivity and direct sum

The
direct sum
of two matroids
M
and
M^{\prime}
, denoted by
M\oplus M^{\prime}
, is the matroid whose ground set is the disjoint union of their ground sets and whose bases are the disjoint unions of their bases. The direct sum commutes with duality:
(M\oplus M^{\prime})^{\star}=M^{\star}\oplus(M^{\prime})^{\star}
holds.

A matroid is
connected
if it cannot be written as the direct sum of two matroids. There is a unique way (up to isomorphism) to write a matroid
M
as a direct sum of connected matroids. Each connected matroid in this sum is a
connected component
of
M
. Notice that by the commutation of direct sum and duality the number of connected components of a matroid is equal to the number of connected components of its dual.

3.3.
Series and parallel connections

Consider two matroids
M
and
M^{\prime}
that share exactly one element
p
. Denoting by
E
and
E^{\prime}
the ground sets of respectively
M
and
M^{\prime}
, we have thus
E\cap E^{\prime}=\{p\}
. Under extra condition on
p
, we can define two operations—the series connection and the parallel connection—that form a new matroid from
M
and
M^{\prime}
.

Assume that
p
is a coloop of neither
M
nor
M^{\prime}
. Then the
series connection
of
M
and
M^{\prime}
, denoted by
\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime})
, is the matroid with
E\cup E^{\prime}
as ground set and with

\bigl\{B\cup B^{\prime}\colon B\in\mathcal{B}(M),\,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(M),\,B\cap B^{\prime}=\varnothing\bigl\}

as set of bases.

Assume that
p
is a loop of neither
M
nor
M^{\prime}
. Then the
parallel connection
of
M
and
M^{\prime}
, denoted by
\operatorname{Par}(M,M^{\prime})
, is the matroid with
E\cup E^{\prime}
as ground set and with

\begin{array}[]{l}\bigl\{B\cup B^{\prime}\colon B\in\mathcal{B}(M),\,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}),\,B\cap B^{\prime}=\{p\}\bigl\}\\[6.45831pt]
\cup\,\,\bigl\{(B\cup B^{\prime})\setminus\{p\}\colon B\in\mathcal{B}(M),\,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}),\,p\in(B\setminus B^{\prime})\cup(B^{\prime}\setminus B)\bigl\}\end{array}

as set of bases.

It is easy to check that these two operations are dual of each other: the series connection of two matroids is the parallel connection of their duals, and vice versa. Formally, this writes

(1)

\bigl(\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime})\bigl)^{\star}=\operatorname{Par}(M^{\star},(M^{\prime})^{\star})\,.

Moreover, if
M
and
M^{\prime}
are connected, then their series connection and their parallel connection are both connected as well
[
12
, Proposition 7.1.17]
.

4.
Preliminaries

In this section, we establish a few results about matroids, their Borsuk numbers, Kneser graphs, and related facts that will be useful for proving our main results. We also believe that some of them are of independent interest.

4.1.
Preliminaries on the Borsuk number

4.1.1.
Duality

The main operations involved in the definition of the Borsuk number and the Borsuk property (number of elements, distance, number of connected components, etc.) are invariant when we move to the dual. Hence, when it comes to the Borsuk number and the Borsuk property, a matroid behaves the same as its dual. The latter is formalized in the following proposition, whose proof is immediate—by remarking that the distance between two bases is equal to the distance between the dual bases—and thus omitted.

Proposition 4.1
.

For every matroid
M
, the equality
f(M)=f(M^{\star})
holds. Moreover,
M
has the Borsuk property if and only if
M^{\star}
has the Borsuk property.

4.1.2.
Direct sum and connectivity

The next proposition shows that to investigate upper bounds on the Borsuk number, it is relevant to start with connected matroids.

Proposition 4.2
.

For every two matroids
M
and
M^{\prime}
, we have

f(M\oplus M^{\prime})\leqslant\min\bigl(f(M),f(M^{\prime})\bigl)\,.

Proof.

Since the matroids
M
and
M^{\prime}
are arbitrary, it will be sufficient to show that
f(M\oplus M^{\prime})\leqslant f(M)
. Consider therefore a partition
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{k}
of
\mathcal{B}(M)
into
k
parts of diameter smaller than
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M))
. The partition
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{k}
induces a partition
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{1},\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{2},\ldots,\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
of
M\oplus M^{\prime}
by setting
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
to be the bases of
M\oplus M^{\prime}
of the form
B\cup B^{\prime}
with
B\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
and
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime})
.

We finish the proof by showing that no
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
contain two bases of
M\oplus M^{\prime}
at distance
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M\oplus M^{\prime}))
. Take two bases
B_{1}\cup B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
of
M\oplus M^{\prime}
at distance
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M\oplus M^{\prime}))
. We have

\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M\oplus M^{\prime}))=|B_{1}\triangle B_{2}|+|B_{1}^{\prime}\triangle B_{2}^{\prime}|\leqslant|B_{1}\triangle B_{2}|+\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}))\,.

Since
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M\oplus M^{\prime}))=\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M))+\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}))
, the two bases
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
of
M
are at distance
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M))
, which means that they cannot belong to the same
\mathcal{B}_{i}
, and therefore
B_{1}\cup B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
cannot belong to the same
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
.
∎

We believe that the inequality in Proposition
4.2
is actually an equality. This is related to the celebrated Hedetniemi conjecture (now refuted); see Section
7
.

The following corollary shows that to find matroids failing to have the Borsuk property, it is enough to focus on connected matroids.

Corollary 4.3
.

Given two matroids, if one of them at least has the Borsuk property, then their direct sum has the Borsuk property as well.

Proof.

Consider two matroids
M
and
M^{\prime}
, and suppose that
M
has the Borsuk property. Denote by
n
(resp.
n^{\prime}
) the number of elements of
M
(resp.
M^{\prime}
) and by
c
(resp.
c^{\prime}
) its number of connected compontents. Proposition
4.2
implies that
f(M\oplus M^{\prime})\leqslant n-c+1
. Since the number of connected components in a matroid is at most the number of elements, we have
c^{\prime}\leqslant n^{\prime}
and hence
f(M\oplus M^{\prime})\leqslant n+n^{\prime}-c-c^{\prime}+1
, as desired.
∎

4.1.3.
Series connection

We end this section with two propositions about the Borsuk number and series connection.

Proposition 4.4
.

For every two matroids
M
and
M^{\prime}
, we have

f\bigl(\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime})\bigr)\leqslant f(M)+f(M^{\prime})\,.

Moreover, if
M
has two disjoint bases that do not contain
p
(the element used for the series connection), then

f\bigl(\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime})\bigr)\leqslant\min\bigl(f(M),f(M^{\prime})\bigr)\,.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{k}
be a partition of
\mathcal{B}(M)
into
k=f(M)
parts of diameter smaller than
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M))
, and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{1},\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}
be a partition of
\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime})
into
k^{\prime}=f(M^{\prime})
parts of diameter smaller than
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}))
.

[First inequality] We build a partition
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{1},\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{2},\ldots,\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{k+k^{\prime}}
of
\mathcal{B}(\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime}))
as follows. Consider a basis
B\cup B^{\prime}
of
\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime})
. If
B^{\prime}
does not contain
p
, then put
B\cup B^{\prime}
in
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
with
i
such that
B\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
. If
B^{\prime}
contains
p
, then put
B\cup B^{\prime}
in
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{k+i}
with
i
such that
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{i}
. We check that no part
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
contains two bases of
\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime})
at distance
\operatorname{diam}\bigl(\mathcal{B}(\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime}))\bigr)
apart. Consider two bases
B_{1}\cup B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
of
\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime})
that are at distance
\operatorname{diam}\bigl(\mathcal{B}(\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime}))\bigr)
apart. We distinguish three cases.

Case 1)
p\notin B_{1}^{\prime}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
. Then
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are at distance
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M))
apart (since otherwise we could increase the distance between the two bases
B_{1}\cup B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
). Thus
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
do not belong to the same
\mathcal{B}_{i}
, and
B_{1}\cup B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
do not belong to the same
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
.

Case 2)
p\in B_{1}^{\prime}\triangle B_{2}^{\prime}
. Without loss of generality, we have
p\in B_{1}^{\prime}\setminus B_{2}^{\prime}
. Then
B_{1}\cup B_{1}^{\prime}
belongs to some
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
with
i>k
, and
B_{2}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
belongs to some
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
with
i\leqslant k
.

Case 3)
p\in B_{1}^{\prime}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}
. Then
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
are at distance
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}))
apart (since otherwise we could increase the distance between the two bases
B_{1}\cup B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
). Thus
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
do not belong to the same
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{i}
, and
B_{1}\cup B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
do not belong to the same
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
.

[Second inequality] We build a partition
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{1},\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{2},\ldots,\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{\min(k,k^{\prime})}
of
\mathcal{B}(\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime}))
as follows.

Consider a basis
B\cup B^{\prime}
of
\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime})
. If
k\leqslant k^{\prime}
, put
B\cup B^{\prime}
in
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
with
i
such that
B\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
. Otherwise, put
B\cup B^{\prime}
in
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
with
i
such that
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{i}
. We check that no part
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
contains two bases of
\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime})
at distance
\operatorname{diam}\bigl(\mathcal{B}(\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime}))\bigr)
apart. Consider two bases
B_{1}\cup B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
of
\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime})
that are at distance
\operatorname{diam}\bigl(\mathcal{B}(\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime}))\bigr)
apart. Notice that if
M
contains two disjoint bases that do not contain
p
, then
\operatorname{diam}\bigl(\mathcal{B}(\operatorname{Ser}(M,M^{\prime}))\bigr)=\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M))+\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}))
. This implies that
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are necessarily at distance
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M))
apart and that
B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}^{\prime}
are necessarily at distance
\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal{B}(M^{\prime}))
apart. Therefore, the bases
B_{1}\cup B_{1}^{\prime}
and
B_{2}\cup B_{2}^{\prime}
cannot belong to the same
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{i}
.
∎

4.2.
Preliminaries on Kneser graphs and matroids

We introduce the
matroid Kneser graph
associated with a matroid
M
, denoted by
\operatorname{KG}(M)
, as the graph with
vertices being the bases of
M
and in which two vertices are joined by an edge if the intersection of the
corresponding bases is empty. (These graphs are actually special cases of the “generalized Kneser graphs” introduced by Dol’nikov
[
5
]
.) The classical
Kneser graph

\operatorname{KG}(n,r)
is the particular case when

M
is
U_{r,n}
. These matroid Kneser graphs appear naturally in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The key connection between matroid Kneser graphs and the Borsuk property when the matroid does not have the strong basis intersection property is given by the following.

Lemma 4.5
.

Consider a matroid
M
with two disjoint bases. Then
\chi(\operatorname{KG}(M))=f(M)
.

Proof.

Since
M
has two disjoint bases, the diameter of
\mathcal{B}(M)
is equal to
2\operatorname{rk}(M)

5.2.
Matroids of rank at most two

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
, which states that every matroid of rank at most two and with at least two bases has the Borsuk property.

We start with a preliminary result.

Proposition 5.1
.

Consider a rank-two matroid with at least two bases. If it has the strong basis intersection property, then it has a loop or a coloop.

Proof.

Let

be the ground set of the matroid for some integer

Without loss of generality, let

and

be two bases of the matroid. Since every two cobases intersect,

as this basis must intersect the two other bases. The element

,
\{1,3\}
, and

∎

The proof considers implicitly the matroid with

as ground set, and with three bases:

,

, and

. This matroid has rank two and has the strong basis intersection property. With the help of Proposition

, which shows in particular that even though this matroid has the strong basis intersection property, it has the Borsuk property. This matroid is actually the smallest example with at least two bases showing that not to have the strong basis intersection property is not a necessary condition to have the Borsuk property. (Formally, a matroid formed only by a single loop is already such an example, but the reader will certainly agree that it is not really an interesting one.) Infinite families of other examples will be given in Section

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Consider a matroid

with at least two bases. It cannot have rank zero. Suppose that

6.
Matroids with the strong basis intersection property

The strong basis intersection property, introduced in Section
1
, finds its motivation in Theorem
1.1
: Not to have the strong basis intersection property implies to have the Borsuk property. Even if this condition is not necessary—see the discussion in Section
5.2
—Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
4.3
show that if we want to find a matroid that does not have the Borsuk property—something which is still possible even if we do not know any such matroid—, it is sufficient to focus on connected matroids with the strong basis intersection property. Since we think that this question is also interesting on its own, we focus in this section on considering some connected matroids that have the strong basis intersection property. Even though there are many such matroids, building them is not a straightforward task.

Notice that if we remove the connectivity condition, it is easy to build matroids that have the strong basis intersection property from matroids that just have the basis intersection property. (We remind the reader that a matroid has the basis intersection property if every two bases intersect, but not necessarily every two cobases.) It is immediate that if a matroid
M
has the basis intersection property, then
M\oplus M^{\star}
has the strong basis intersection property. This shows for instance that
U_{2,3}\oplus U_{1,3}
has the strong basis intersection property.

Among the classical families of matroids,
\Theta_{n}
matroids are connected, have the strong basis intersection property, and satisfy the Borsuk property for
n\geqslant 2
(Proposition
6.1
below). In this section, in addition of proving Proposition
6.1
, we discuss how the classical series and parallel operations for matroids can be used to build arbitrarily large connected matroids with the strong basis intersection property (Section
6.2
). Finally, we also describe a construction allowing to obtain arbitrarily large connected graphic matroids with the strong basis intersection property (Section
6.3
).

6.1.
\Theta_{n}
matroids

For
n\geqslant 1
, the
\Theta_{n}
matroid has its ground set formed by two disjoint sets of the form
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and

and its bases are the subsets

Proposition 6.1
.

For

Proof.

It is immediate to check that it has the basis intersection property. This implies that it has actually the strong basis intersection property since the

[

. Two bases

realize the diameter of

(2)

B\cap B^{\prime}\cap X=\varnothing\quad\mbox{and}\quad|B\cap X|=B^{\prime}\cap X|=2\,.

For

as the set of bases

It is not difficult to establish the following equalities:

f(\Theta_{1})=+\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad f(\Theta_{2})=f(\Theta_{3})=f(\Theta_{4})=2\,.

of Figure
4
, is at most
3
, and an easy case-checking shows that it cannot be
2
or less.

7.
Open questions

We think that the main open question is whether every matroid with at least two bases has the Borsuk property. According to Section
2
, this is equivalent to the question whether there exist matroid polytopes that are counter-examples to the Borsuk conjecture. The answer to this question is unknown.

Conjecture 7.1
.

Every matroid with at least two bases has the Borsuk property.

Another question that arises naturally is whether the inequality in Proposition
4.2
can be strict.

Question 7.2
.

Do there exist matroids
M
and
M^{\prime}
such that
f(M\oplus M^{\prime})<\min(f(M),f(M^{\prime}))
?

This question is actually related the celebrated Hedetniemi conjecture
[
8
]
, which has been refuted by Shitov in 2019
[
21
]
. It deals with the chromatic number of the
categorical product
of two graphs
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
, denoted by
G\times G^{\prime}
and defined as follows. The vertex set of
G\times G^{\prime}
is the set of all ordered pairs
(v,v^{\prime})\in V\times V^{\prime}
. Edges are formed by the unordered pairs
(u,u^{\prime})(v,v^{\prime})
such that
uv\in E
and
u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}
.

Hedetniemi’s conjecture states that
\chi(G\times H)=\min(\chi(G),\chi(H))
. Noticing that

\operatorname{KG}(M\oplus M^{\prime})=\operatorname{KG}(M)\times\operatorname{KG}(M^{\prime})\,,

Lemma
4.5
shows that a positive answer to Question
7.2
for matroids with disjoint bases would build a counter-example to Hedetniemi conjecture with matroid Kneser graphs. No such example is known so far. Note that the symbol ‘
\times
’ is used in this paragraph with two different meanings: it represents the categorical product of graphs, but also the usual Cartesian product of sets (when we consider the product of the vertex sets). In the next paragraph, it is again used with this latter meaning.

By the equality
P_{M\oplus M^{\prime}}=P_{M}\times P_{M^{\prime}}
, Question
7.2
is also related to whether the Borsuk property is kept when we perform the Cartesian product of two matroid polytopes having the Borsuk property. As far as we are aware, this question, even for general polytopes, has not been studied up to now.
