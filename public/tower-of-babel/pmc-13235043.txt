The relationship between shoulder pain and joint position sense, postural control, tactile sensation, and muscle strength in chronic hemiplegia
Authors: Beril Kütükcü, Metehan Yana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study is to investigate the relationship between shoulder pain and joint position sense, postural control, tactile sensation, and muscle strength in chronic hemiplegic stroke patients.MethodsThe study was designed as a cross-sectional descriptive study, with 78 individuals with chronic stroke (48 males, 30 females). The participants were divided into two groups: those with shoulder pain and those without. Assessments were performed on the affected upper limb and included shoulder proprioception (inclinometer), postural control (Postural Assessment Scale for Stroke Patients (PASS-T)), tactile sensation (Semmes-Weinstein Monofilament test), gross grip strength (dynamometer), and pinch grip strength (pinch meter).ResultsThe findings of the study revealed significant differences between individuals with and without shoulder pain across joint position sense, postural control, tactile sensation, and grip strength (all p < 0.05). Effect size analyses indicated that these differences ranged from small-to-moderate (d = 0.49–0.75) to large-to-very large (d ≥ 0.98), supporting the clinical relevance of the observed group differences.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that shoulder pain is significantly associated with motor and sensory functions in chronic hemiplegic individuals. It also underscores the necessity for rehabilitation programs to be tailored according to individuals’ pain conditions, emphasizing the critical role of this factor in patient recovery processes.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06793852. Registered on January 21, 2025.

Background

Stroke is characterized by focal or global loss of brain functions in clinical situations that persist for more than 24 h due to vascular causes or may result in sudden death [1]. Approximately 80% of individuals who experience a stroke exhibit severe motor impairments, which have been reported to cause significant limitations in the quality of life, activities of daily living, and social interactions [2]. Currently, it is observed that mortality, disability, and morbidity cases resulting from stroke are increasing globally [3]. One of the most severe consequences of cerebrovascular events is hemiplegia, which affects half of the body. Neuromotor disorders, such as asymmetric posture, loss of muscle strength, and loss of sensation, can be observed in hemiplegic patients [4, 5]. Hemiplegic shoulder pain (HSP) has been reported to occur in 5% to 84% of cases, while its incidence has been reported to range from 39% to 84% [6, 7]. The condition has a multifactorial etiology and may result from various pathologies, including rotator cuff lesions, biceps tendinopathy, and soft tissue disorders such as myofascial pain, glenohumeral subluxation, spasticity, and alterations in peripheral and central nervous system activity [8, 9]. Loss of postural control following a stroke may impede the recovery of motor function. Shoulder pain may adversely affect both motor control and functional recovery [10]. Impaired functioning of neuromotor mechanisms may complicate the recovery process and affect body position and balance; furthermore, pain may hinder the balanced distribution of body weight, impeding postural control [11]. Proprioception is a crucial sense that contributes to joint stability, postural control, and motor control of the body [12]. Diminished proprioception in individuals who have experienced a stroke may negatively affect their motor function. The tactile sense encompasses sensory stimuli that are perceived through the skin and tissues. In patients with stroke, tactile sensory impairments can be observed due to brain damage [13]. In cases of HSP, pain occurring in the shoulder region can exert various effects on the tactile sensation in the dermatome area. A significant association has been established between shoulder pain and upper extremity function in hemiplegic patients, as well as between muscle strength and HSP [14]. Post-stroke muscle weakness is a chronic condition that can negatively affect shoulder joint stabilization and posture [15]. Additionally, grip strength can be affected by shoulder pain, as shoulder pain can restrict hand and arm movements, potentially weakening the grip strength. Previous research, such as the study by Duray and Baskan, has established that HSP significantly impairs upper extremity motor function and proprioception, suggesting that these deficits are primary targets for clinical management [16]. Understanding these impairments is vital because successful rehabilitation interventions, such as Constraint-Induced Movement Therapy or neurofacilitation techniques, depend on the integrity of sensory-motor integration [2]. While the impact of HSP on general function is recognized, there is a lack of evidence regarding how it specifically relates to different peripheral nerve distributions—such as C6, C7, and the radial, median, and ulnar nerves—and the specific subcomponents of postural control, including posture maintenance and posture change. Identifying these specific deficits is critical for clinical practice, as it informs the development of targeted therapeutic implications such as sensory re-education and proprioceptive neurofacilitation, which are essential for long-term functional recovery. Unlike previous studies that often focus on a single parameter, this study provides an incremental contribution to the literature by offering a multidimensional analysis of sensory-motor associations, simultaneously investigating the relationship between shoulder pain and joint position sense, postural control, tactile sense, and muscle strength in patients with chronic hemiplegia.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a cross-sectional, single-center study and was conducted between December 2023 and April 2024 at the Safranbolu Special Education and Rehabilitation Center in Karabük Province (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06793852). Ethical approval was obtained from the Karabük University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No 2023/1457). All the participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

A total of 78 stroke patients receiving treatment at the Safranbolu Special Education and Rehabilitation Center were included in the study. The participant recruitment and selection process is summarized in the flow diagram (Fig. 1), which aligns with CONSORT guidelines. Of the 86 patients initially assessed for eligibility, four were excluded due to cognitive impairment that could compromise the ability to provide informed consent or to follow assessment instructions, as determined by a brief orientation and memory screening conducted at baseline, one due to a history of shoulder surgery, and three due to major neurological or rheumatological conditions. While a standardized instrument like the Mini-Mental State Examination was not utilized, this clinical screening ensured that all included participants could follow multi-step instructions and maintain attention throughout the assessments; however, the lack of a validated tool is recognized as a study limitation. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 based on an independent-samples t-test, assuming a medium effect size of d = 0.60 derived from Duray & Baskan (2020), α = 0.05, and 80% power. The required sample size was 72 participants. Our final sample of 78 exceeded this threshold, indicating adequate statistical power for the primary comparisons [16]. All sensory and motor assessments in the study were performed unilaterally on the affected upper extremity of the participants. Inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥ 18 years; diagnosis of post-stroke hemiplegia; at least 3 months post-stroke; and sufficient cognitive function and cooperation to comply with study procedures, as confirmed during baseline assessment. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Exclusion criteria included cognitive impairment interfering with informed consent or adherence to study assessments, aphasia, the presence of major neurological or rheumatological diseases affecting the musculoskeletal system other than stroke, and a history of surgery on the affected shoulder prior to stroke.

Outcome measures

The sociodemographic data of the participants, including age, sex, height, weight, marital status, profession, dominant hand, education level, smoking status, affected side, time since stroke, comorbid neurological or rheumatological diseases, history of shoulder surgery, and presence of shoulder pain, were recorded on the evaluation form.

Visual Analog Scale (VAS): is used to assess shoulder pain levels in individuals. It consists of a 100 mm horizontal line, where participants mark a point that subjectively represents their pain intensity. The scores range from 0 to 10 [17].

Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI): The Turkish version of the SPADI includes 13 items: 5 for pain and 8 for disability. Participants marked a 0–10 scale on a horizontal line. The total scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater pain and disability [18].

Joint Position Sense (JPS): was assessed at 45° and 60° of shoulder flexion using a replication protocol [16]. For each target angle, the practitioner positioned the participant’s arm with eyes open, followed by the participant’s active replication of the position with eyes closed. The average of three repetitions was recorded for analysis. These specific mid-range angles were selected to prioritize patient safety and minimize the risk of pain provocation or subluxation-related impingement, which are common in stroke populations beyond 90° [19]. Furthermore, evaluating JPS in the mid-range reduces the influence of mechanical feedback from capsuloligamentous structures that tighten at end-ranges, allowing for a more focused assessment of musculotendinous proprioceptive input [20].

Postural Assessment Scale for Stroke Patients (PASS-T): The Turkish version of the PASS-T evaluates postural control and balance in patients with stroke. It consists of 12 items under two subcategories: posture maintenance and posture change [21].

Semmes-Weinstein Monofilament Test (SWM): SWM assesses light touch and pressure sensation using monofilaments of varying thickness (1.65–6.65). Sensory evaluation was performed in five regions: the thenar eminence for C6, middle finger digital pulp for C7, dorsal first web area for the radial nerve, index finger digital pulp for the median nerve, and hypothenar pulp for the ulnar nerve. The results were initially classified using color-coded thresholds and then converted into their corresponding log-force numeric values for statistical analysis. Higher numeric scores indicate a higher pressure threshold, representing decreased tactile sensitivity [22, 23].

Muscle Strength Assessment Grip strength was measured using a Jamar hand dynamometer, and finger strength was measured with a pinchmeter. Each measurement was repeated thrice, and the average was recorded in kilograms. Standardized positioning was performed according to American Hand Therapists Association guidelines [24, 25].

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequency and percentage for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. Normality was first assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (n > 30). In addition, skewness and kurtosis coefficients were examined, and values within the ± 2 range were accepted as indicating approximate normality. Histogram plots were visually inspected by the study statistician to further verify distributional properties. Although several variables yielded p < 0.05 on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, skewness and kurtosis values remained within acceptable limits, and parametric tests were therefore applied, given the robustness of the independent-samples t-test to mild deviations from normality in samples greater than 30 participants. Homogeneity of variances was verified using Levene’s test. Between-group differences (shoulder pain vs. no shoulder pain) were examined using the independent-samples t-test. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated and interpreted as small (0.20–0.49), moderate (0.50–0.79), and large (≥ 0.80). To verify the robustness of the results, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. These analyses yielded the same pattern of statistical significance as the parametric models.

Table 3 compares measurements between patients with and without shoulder pain. The findings of the study revealed significant differences between individuals with and without shoulder pain across joint position sense, postural control, tactile sensation, and grip strength (all p < 0.05). Sensitivity analyses using the Mann–Whitney U test confirmed the same direction and significance of results (all p < 0.05), indicating that the findings were robust to distributional assumptions. Effect size analyses indicated that these differences ranged from moderate to very large magnitude (Cohen’s d = 0.48–5.90), supporting the clinical relevance of the observed group differences. The significant results are detailed below.

VAS

The mean VAS score of patients with shoulder pain (5.85 ± 1.268) was significantly higher than that of patients without shoulder pain (p < 0.05).

SPADI

The means of SPADI-pain (23.59 ± 8.881), SPADI-disability (58.62 ± 14.166), and SPADI-total (82.21 ± 18.991) scores were significantly higher in patients with shoulder pain compared to those without shoulder pain (p < 0.05).

JPS

The means of 45° JPS (10.56 ± 8.698) and 60° JPS (6.36 ± 9.361) were significantly higher in patients with shoulder pain than in those without (p < 0.05).

PASS-T

The means of PASS-T posture maintenance (9.10 ± 1.314), PASS-T posture change (18.44 ± 2.337), and total PASS-T (27.54 ± 2.694) were significantly lower in patients with shoulder pain than in those without (p < 0.05).

Tactile Sensation (SWM)

The means of hand tactile sensation at C6 (3.91 ± 0.443), C7 (4.02 ± 0.567), radial nerve (3.89 ± 0.404), median nerve (4.00 ± 0.564), and ulnar nerve (3.92 ± 0.615) were significantly higher in patients with shoulder pain compared to those without (p < 0.05).

Grip strength

The means of gross grip strength (9.09 ± 3.484) and finger grip strength (2.57 ± 1.120) were significantly lower in patients with shoulder pain compared to those without (p < 0.05).

Discussion

Shoulder pain in chronic hemiplegic patients adversely affects daily activities and the rehabilitation process. Although the associations between shoulder pain and motor and sensory functions are often discussed, comprehensive analyses of these relationships remain limited. This study examined the relationship between shoulder pain and joint position sense, postural control, tactile sense, and muscle strength. Our results showed that hemiplegic shoulder pain worsens joint position sense, particularly in low-angle flexion; deterioration increases with pain severity. Patients with shoulder pain had poorer posture maintenance and change abilities, lower tactile sensitivity, and weaker grip and finger strength compared to those without pain.

The relationship between HSP and proprioception and upper extremity motor functions are a critical area of research in rehabilitation processes. In a study examining the effects of HSP on upper extremity motor function and proprioception, proprioception was evaluated at 45°, 60°, and 90° shoulder flexion, and it was found that the motor functions of patients with HSP were significantly lower and shoulder proprioception was impaired compared to the painless shoulder. It was determined that these impairments were more pronounced in low-angle flexion positions and that motor function and proprioception impairments increased as the severity of HSP increased [16]. In another study examining the effects of hemiplegic shoulder pain on the motor function and proprioception of the affected upper extremity, it was demonstrated that the sense of proprioception was significantly reduced in patients with HSP [26]. These findings are largely consistent with our study. Proprioception impairment can be attributed to the suppression of proprioceptive feedback by nociceptive signals transmitted from the painful shoulder [27]. Beyond this physiological suppression, pain severity may act as a mediator between muscle strength and proprioceptive acuity [16, 27]. High-intensity nociceptive signals transmitted from a painful shoulder can dominate the central nervous system’s processing capacity, effectively ‘masking’ or suppressing the subtler proprioceptive feedback required for joint stabilization [27]. This sensory interference likely creates a cycle where decreased joint awareness leads to poorer muscle activation, further diminishing the functional strength of the affected limb [28]. Additionally, it is hypothesized that shoulder pain is not only a localized problem but may have systemic effects on motor control and sensory integration, impacting overall postural strategies [29].

Postural control is a fundamental motor mechanism facilitated by the integration of sensory information and appropriate muscle activation [30]. Disruptions in this mechanism following stroke result in impaired balance [29]. Visual and proprioceptive inputs, along with biomechanical factors, play a crucial role in maintaining postural control [31]. Research indicates that shoulder pain impairs postural control by affecting muscle tone and motor control mechanisms through nociceptive signals [32]. However, there is limited research on the association between poststroke shoulder pain and postural control [33, 34]. In the literature, factors such as kinesiophobia, cognitive function, and balance have been predominantly discussed [35–37]. Studies have reported that pain negatively affects postural stability and causes changes in body symmetry in individuals with shoulder pathology [38]. Research has demonstrated that overhead athletes with shoulder pain exhibit lower dynamic balance compared to those without pain [39]. It is reported that pain affects postural stability by reducing proprioceptive sense and muscle strength, leading patients to unconsciously alter their postures and increasing the risk of imbalance [28]. In our study, the PASS-T scores of the patients with shoulder pain were significantly lower. Our findings support the hypothesis that shoulder pain is negatively associated with postural stability by limiting postural strategies and that this may have a systemic effect [29]. Beyond statistical significance, the observed difference in PASS-T scores exceeds the reported minimal clinically important difference for stroke populations, suggesting a clinically relevant impairment in daily postural stability [21, 29]. This association may be driven by biomechanical factors, such as protective guarding and compensatory trunk movements that shift the center of pressure [38, 39], and neurophysiological pathways, where nociceptive input interferes with sensory integration and the internal body schema [30, 33]. Although these mechanisms remain speculative due to the study’s cross-sectional design, they underscore that shoulder pain imposes a tangible functional limitation rather than just a lower test score.

Tactile sensation depends on innervation density, intact neural pathways, and somatosensory responses, but pain also influences sensitivity [40]. Stroke often causes tactile impairment [41]. Sensitivity to cold and light touch is reported to be reduced in patients with shoulder pain [42]. Similarly, hemiplegic patients with shoulder pain show an increased threshold for tactile sensation and electrical stimulation [43]. Our study similarly demonstrates that hemiplegic shoulder pain was negatively associated with tactile sensation. However, while studies in the literature generally focus on a single nerve distribution, our study is more comprehensive in terms of evaluating sensory impairments in different peripheral nerves (C6, C7, radial, median, and ulnar nerves). In a study investigating motor imagery performance and tactile acuity in patients with pain syndromes, it was reported that pain induces cortical reorganization, which may in turn affect tactile sensitivity [44]. In this context, our study not only focuses on the shoulder joint but also offers a broader clinical perspective by suggesting a more detailed evaluation of sensory impairments related to each of the peripheral nerves. However, it is important to acknowledge that the observed tactile deficits may also reflect central mechanisms. Chronic pain and stroke-induced brain damage can lead to maladaptive plasticity and a shrinkage of the cortical representation of the affected limb [44]. Consequently, the sensory loss documented may be a combination of peripheral nerve dysfunction and complex cortical reorganization, where pain-driven ‘learned non-use’ further impairs tactile acuity [45].

Upper extremity strength reflects biomechanical force capacity, while functionality denotes daily use. Muscle weakness is common after stroke [46, 47]. Although pain–function relations are well studied, effects on strength are less clear [48]. It has been established that muscle strength loss following stroke induces pain [49]. However, there is a lack of studies examining the relationship between pain and muscle strength in the context of stroke. Studies conducted on patients with knee osteoarthritis have indicated that pain aggravates muscle weakness [50]. In a study involving individuals with chronic neck pain, it was suggested that cervical muscle strength diminishes as pain intensity increases [51]. The results of our study demonstrated that stroke patients with shoulder pain exhibited significantly lower grip and finger grip strengths compared to patients without shoulder pain. Patients with chronic pain may avoid using the affected extremity due to fear of pain or re-injury. This avoidance behavior may lead to disuse of the extremity and may induce physiological changes such as muscle atrophy and decreased coordination, which can be attributed to further diminished muscle strength [45].

In this study, hemiplegic shoulder pain was operationalized as a single entity based on VAS and SPADI scores. However, it is essential to consider that HSP is a multifactorial condition with heterogeneous etiologies, such as glenohumeral subluxation, rotator cuff lesions, and spasticity [7, 8]. These different pathologies may uniquely influence neuromotor impairments. For example, pain originating from subluxation might primarily disrupt mechanical joint stability and proprioceptive feedback, whereas spasticity-related pain might more significantly impact muscle strength and movement coordination [8, 9]. Since specific diagnostic imaging or clinical stratification was not performed, the precise mechanisms linking specific pathologies to the observed sensory-motor deficits remain speculative. Future research incorporating diagnostic subgroups would provide more nuanced insights into these associations.

This study has limitations. As the participants were selected exclusively from patients undergoing a specific rehabilitation process, the generalizability of the findings to patients who did not receive rehabilitation may be limited. Furthermore, the absence of restrictions on a specific age range may impede the generalization of the study results to individuals in different age groups. Evaluations conducted without considering the type of stroke may result in the study findings not accurately reflecting the association with stroke type. Variations in the duration since stroke onset among patients may cause the study results to inadequately reflect the recovery processes that evolve over time. Another significant limitation is that the potential moderating effects of pre-stroke handedness or affected side dominance were not analyzed, which may influence the severity of neuromotor impairments. The lack of a standardized cognitive assessment tool like the MMSE is a limitation that may compromise the study’s internal validity. Given that cognitive status directly influences the reliability of sensory and proprioceptive testing, this omission might have introduced measurement bias in parameters requiring complex cooperation. Lastly, the analyses did not adjust for potential confounders such as stroke duration or affected side, which may influence neuromotor outcomes. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution. Future research should utilize validated instruments to ensure a more objective evaluation of cognitive function.

Conclusion

Hemiplegic shoulder pain is critically associated with clinically relevant impairments in joint position sense, postural control, tactile sensitivity, and grip strength. Specifically, the observed reductions in PASS-T scores, which exceed the minimal clinically important difference, indicate that shoulder-focused pain management should be prioritized to maintain postural stability. Rehabilitation should transition from general mobility to targeted interventions: shoulder-specific proprioceptive training to stabilize the center of pressure, sensory re-education focusing on affected dermatomes, and distal grip strengthening to counteract functional lateralization. Implementing these targeted sensorimotor interventions in the early stages is essential to prevent the progression of postural instability and enhance safety during daily activities. Future research should utilize longitudinal designs to track neuromotor recovery trajectories while stratifying for stroke subtype and age to better understand the evolution of pain-related functional decline.
