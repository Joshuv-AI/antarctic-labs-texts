A Prospective Single-Arm Cohort Study of Immune-mediated Seizures/Epilepsy Without Encephalopathy (ISEWE) in Children from a Tertiary Care Hospital in South India
Authors: Basavanagowda Thanuja, Mahesh Kamate
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Acute-onset seizures in children pose a diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma. Some epilepsy cases presenting with seizures but without encephalopathy, though treatable with immunotherapy, are often missed due to lack of suspicion of immune mechanism in the context of absent encephalitis. A prospective study was conducted on premorbidly normal children with new-onset seizures occurring in clusters, with normal neuroimaging. Investigations included electroencephalogram (EEG), cerebrospinal fluid examination, and testing for autoantibodies. All cases received methylprednisolone pulse therapy along with antiseizure medications (ASMs), followed by monthly dexamethasone oral pulse therapy. Fifteen cases were enrolled (11 males, four females). Mean age at seizure onset was 4.6 years. Focal seizures were present in 13/15 (86.7%) cases. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain was normal in all. EEG showed interictal epileptiform discharges in 12 cases (focal in nine) and ictal record in two cases. On monthly oral steroids, number of ASMs could be reduced. Eight cases never had a relapse, while seven did have it. Relapses were more common if more than three ASMs were tried and less if steroids were started within 30 days. Immune-mediated seizures/epilepsy without encephalopathy is a new, important, and treatable entity. Early diagnosis and institution of immunotherapy results in significant improvement in seizure control and also reduces the need for long-term polytherapy. Awareness of this entity is crucial, especially when premorbidly normal children present with new-onset clusters of refractory seizures.

Introduction

Acute-onset seizures in children pose a diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma. They can be due to various causes like infections, trauma, structural, immune, or metabolic etiology. When the workup for these disorders is negative, clinicians face a major challenge. Along with antiseizure medications (ASMs), treatment of the underlying cause is also very important to achieve the final outcome. In the last two decades, we are increasingly diagnosing autoimmune encephalitis, which usually presents in a polysymptomatic way with seizures associated with encephalopathy and other neurological features such as speech or behavioral disturbances, a movement disorder, or sleep disturbances. We have observed a few premorbidly normal children presenting with a flurry of seizures occurring over a few days to a few weeks that are resistant to multiple ASMs, in the absence of encephalopathy or other features usually associated with autoimmune encephalitis, and have negative investigative workup for common causes. They fulfill the diagnosis of drug-resistant epilepsy.[1] These children respond to immunotherapy, and early treatment (within 1–2 weeks) is associated with good long-term outcomes, thereby suggesting an immune pathogenesis.[2] We propose the use of the term “immune-mediated seizures/epilepsy without encephalopathy (ISEWE)” to describe this condition. Through this study, we intend to describe the clinico-investigative profile of children with ISEWE and to assess the number of seizures, the disease duration and course, and the response to immunotherapy. We present here the clinical profile of 15 children with ISEWE seen by us in the last 4 years.

Methods

A prospective single-arm cohort study was conducted in the pediatric neurology out-patient and in-patient department. Institutional ethical clearance was obtained. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of all children enrolled in the study. Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) checklist was applied. Cases were enrolled from January 2020 to January 2023, and were followed up for a minimum duration of 1 year after the last relapse [Figure 1]. Premorbidly normal children (with normal development) who presented with new-onset seizures in clusters without fever at the onset of seizures, with normal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were included in the study. Children with seizures of known etiology (metabolic, systemic cause, etc.) or having an MRI abnormality or moderate (26–100 cells/cu.mm) to severe (>100 cells/mm3) cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pleocytosis or increased CSF lactate were excluded. All cases fulfilling the inclusion criteria who were attending the pediatric neurology out-patient department or were admitted were enrolled in the study. MRI (3 T MRI with HARNESS protocol), electroencephalogram (EEG), and routine CSF examination with lactate were performed for all the cases. CSF neuroinflammation panel (including oligoclonal band, immunoglobulin G [IgG] index, IgG synthesis rate, intrathecal IgG synthesis, and albumin index) and autoantibodies (N-methyl-d-aspartate [NMDA], α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methylisoxazole-4-propionic acid [AMPA], voltage-gated potassium channel [VGKC], γ-aminobutyric acid [GABA], glutamic acid decarboxylase [GAD]) were done when feasible. All cases were treated with methylprednisolone pulse therapy (25 mg/kg/day for 5 days) along with symptomatic treatment of seizures with ASMs and followed up with monthly dexamethasone oral pulse therapy (25 mg/m2/day for three consecutive days in a month) for at least 6 months.[34] Cases were followed up for assessment of seizure control (seizure diary) once a month initially and then once in 3 months. Duration of follow-up ranged from 1 to 5 years. Treatment response was defined as a seizure-free period of at least 4 weeks. Relapse was defined as recurrence of seizures after a seizure-free period of at least 4 weeks, with good drug compliance. As this is a hospital-based cross-sectional study, selection bias cannot be ruled out. However, this was minimized as all cases fulfilling the inclusion criteria were enrolled irrespective of seizure outcome at follow-up, and uniform assessment was done at follow-up for all cases, including maintenance of seizure diary. Being a study with follow-up, attrition bias is a possibility. This was managed by phone call reminders to remind parents regarding the follow-up visit, and one case which was lost to follow-up was considered as “not responded to treatment.” Data was analyzed with Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 24 using Chi-square, Fisher exact, and Mann–Whitney’s U test.

Results

Fifteen cases (11 males and four females) were enrolled over the last 4 years. The mean age was 5.5 years (median 2.5 years, range 2.3–13 years), and the mean age at seizure onset was 4.6 years. Thirteen children (86.7%) had focal seizures, of which seven had focal non-motor affective (four- fear, three- gelastic seizures), six had focal to bilateral tonic–clonic, and one each had perioral and eyelid myoclonia, myoclonic, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures, respectively [Table 1]. None of them presented with status epilepticus. Six (40%) cases had one to four seizures per day, four (26.7%) had five to nine seizures per day, and five (33.3%) had more than 10 seizures per day. MRI of the brain was normal in all cases. EEG showed interictal epileptiform discharges in 12 cases (focal in nine), and an ictal record was seen in two cases. CSF oligoclonal band and elevated IgG index were seen in one case each. The autoimmune panel was negative in all five cases in which it was done.

The number of ASMs tried ranged from two to six (median 3) before presentation to us or while they were subjected to tests like MRI, EEG, and CSF examination, but seizures persisted despite drug therapy. All patients were given pulse intravenous methylprednisolone after they fulfilled the study criteria. Almost all of them showed more than 50% reduction on day 4 to day 5 of pulse therapy. Seizures were controlled within 1.2 months after methylprednisolone (25 mg/kg/day) pulse therapy for 5 days as infusion followed by monthly oral dexamethasone pulses (20–30 mg/m2) for 6 months. After steroid therapy, the seizures had reduced in all but one case, which was lost to follow-up. None of them experienced any serious side effects with steroid therapy. Eight cases never had a relapse, and all of them were initiated on immune therapy within 1 month of onset. Seven (46.7%) cases had a relapse at a mean of 5.6 months after the first episode (range: 1–18 months), and the total disease duration ranged from 1 to 48 months. The number of relapses ranged from 0 to 5 (four cases with <3 relapses and three cases with >3 relapses). If steroids were started before 30 days of the onset of seizures (median 30, interquartile range [IQR] 16.25–30), compared to beyond 45 days (median 300, IQR 45–1095), there were fewer relapses (P = 0.022). Eight cases received steroids within 30 days of seizure onset, and only one of them had a single relapse, which responded with a repetition of the initial steroid trial. If more than three ASMs were tried, 80% of the cases had relapses compared to 22.2% if <3 ASMs were tried (P = 0.036). At the final follow-up, two children were off ASM, and six were on single ASM.

Discussion

The importance of immune etiology as a cause of epilepsy has been highlighted by the fact that it was included as one of the six etiologies in the definition of epilepsy by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) in 2017.[2] Identifying the etiology of epilepsy is crucial for the timely initiation of appropriate management strategies, and should begin at the first point when a child presents with seizures that could potentially be epilepsy. When there is inflammation of the central nervous system (CNS), an immune etiology is considered to be the cause of epilepsy. Immune activation leads to neuronal hyperexcitability and epilepsy, and hence, there may be a history of viral prodrome preceding the new-onset seizures in a previously healthy child. Seizures themselves can perpetuate inflammation by the activation of cytokine networks, increasing the permeability of the blood–brain barrier and leading to extravasation of the peripheral immune cells. This may increase the probability of a higher frequency of seizures or clustering of new-onset seizures. Depending on the site of this inflammation, the manifestations can vary like seizures, speech problems, sleep problems, movement disorder, and dysautonomia. ISEWE can be a more localized presentation of autoantibody-associated neurological manifestations affecting neurons mainly in the motor cortex or a non-antibody mediated immune mechanism involving innate/T-cell mediated immunity. An alteration in the adaptive immunity explains the pathomechanism behind autoimmune seizures/epilepsy.[5]

Autoimmune encephalitis has been a well-established entity in pediatric neurology in the past decade. The awareness of its symptomatology in the form of encephalopathy, seizures, presence of speech abnormality, a movement disorder, changes in behavior, and pattern of sleep has increased, resulting in early detection and institution of immunotherapy. The ILAE autoimmunity and inflammation task force has proposed two terminologies concerning epilepsy, namely, acute symptomatic seizures secondary to autoimmune encephalitis (commonly referred to as autoimmune seizures/epilepsy in the literature) and autoimmune-associated epilepsy (seen as a sequelae after the initial attack).[6] Autoimmune epilepsy/seizures that occur in the setting of autoimmune encephalitis mostly present with other neurological manifestations like sleep, behavioral changes, movement disorders, and psychiatric disturbances. But there exists another group of immune-mediated seizures/epilepsy that does not fit into these two terms, which we propose as “ISEWE.” ISEWE does not fit into the former group as there is a lack of “encephalopathy,” but it is responsive to immunotherapy, and many cases present with relapse of seizures. It does not fit into the latter group either, as neuroimaging is normal here without atrophy or changes in the hippocampi that are commonly seen in autoimmune-associated epilepsy as a consequence of an autoimmune encephalitis sequelae. While the latter group does not show a good response to immunotherapy, we can see that there is a significant reduction in seizure frequency, relapses, and the requirement of ASMs in ISEWE. ISEWE can be a more localized presentation of autoantibody-associated or localised T-cell/innate immunity mediated injury affecting neurons mainly in the motor cortex.

Infection is a more common trigger that precedes autoimmune seizures/epilepsy in the pediatric age group.[7] The proposed pathomechanisms by which an infection can trigger the seizures include innate immunity mediated neuronal injury, molecular mimicry, bystander activation, B-cell receptor-mediated co-capture of antigens, polyclonal activation of B cells, epitope spreading and T-cell mediated injury.[8]

The clinical presentation is usually in the form of new-onset refractory seizures occurring in a previously normal child, occasionally with a viral prodrome. The seizures characteristically occur in clusters, are brief, can be of unusual semiology (affective, dacrystic, gelastic seizures), and multiple seizure types can be seen in a single case.

Testing for the causative autoantibody is important to identify the etiology and to help us understand the etiopathogenesis and mechanistic basis of autoimmune seizures/epilepsy. Autoantibodies have been identified and are a part of the investigative workup of autoimmune encephalitis. Many autoantibodies that are responsible for autoimmune encephalitis have been recognized, such as NMDA, AMPA, contactin-associated protein-like 2, VGKC, GABA A and B, GAD 65, leucine-rich, glioma-inactivated 1, etc. However, of the five cases in whom we looked for the autoantibodies, a positive result was not found in any. Though this is a small number, it could suggest the possibility of a new, as yet unrecognized autoantibody, which may be responsible for the non-encephalitic pattern of presentation. It could also be possible that the seropositivity in these cases is low, which is comparable to the mere 10% positivity rate in epilepsy of autoimmune etiology.[57] Further research is required to find out the common autoantibodies, T-cell other innate immunity mediated neuronal injuries that are causative in ISEWE, and long-term studies are necessary to understand the possible outcome and changes in management based on the autoantibody identified in an individual case.

Examination of CSF is useful to diagnose infectious encephalitis and to detect signs of CNS inflammation. Workup for neuroinflammation is seldom positive. Two out of five cases in our study showed a positive result in the neuroinflammation panel in the form of oligoclonal bands and elevated IgG index in one case each.

It is now well established that early immunotherapy is associated with better outcomes in autoimmune encephalitis, in which 60%–80% of cases respond.[5] As the pathomechanism is similar in ISEWE, it should hold true for this condition as well. In our study, the cases initiated on immunotherapy within 1 month of the onset of seizures had fewer relapses and better seizure control.

Since ISEWE presents without encephalopathy in previously normal children, with normal neuroimaging, and often with unusual seizure semiology such as gelastic or focal non-motor affective (fear), it is possible to undermine it as malingering, dissociative disorder or other psychiatric illnesses. This makes early clinical diagnosis challenging, and hence, awareness about the entity is crucial for timely management with immunotherapy. Seizures in ISEWE remain refractory to ASM, but can be controlled with immune therapy. ISEWE is an entity that is seldom described or understood among adult and pediatric neurologists alike. ISEWE is a potentially treatable and preventable cause of drug-resistant epilepsy, which needs to be diagnosed and treated early for better long-term outcomes.

The limitation of our study is that autoantibodies and neuroinflammation panels could not be tested in all patients due to financial constraints. As shown in Table 1, in two cases, we did find evidence of inflammation in CSF. Though we propose an immune mechanism for seizures, we should be aware of the possibility of a transient response to steroids in acute seizures due to various other mechanisms like stabilizing the neuronal cell membrane and anti-inflammatory rather than immune-suppression/-modulation action of steroids. However, we continue to follow-up these cases to understand the same better. Further studies and many reports from other centers are needed to confirm the entity ISEWE, identify the antibodies using live cell-based assay, and standardize the treatment for this condition. The steroid responsiveness and good outcome in cases where steroids were started early prompted us to report about this entity of ISEWE and our experience with this, so that many children with this condition will be benefited. The treatment did not lead to any long-term untoward effects in any of our children.

Conclusion

ISEWE is a new and upcoming entity for which we need to increase awareness to initiate early immunotherapy. It has to be thought of in all premorbidly normal children presenting with cluster of seizures. There is a need for further research in this area among children to identify the mechanisms/antibodies involved and standardize the treatment measures. Knowledge about this entity is necessary to manage, counsel, and prognosticate the disease. Early initiation of immune therapy has a good outcome and fewer relapses.
