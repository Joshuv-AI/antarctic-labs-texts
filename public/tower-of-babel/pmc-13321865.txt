Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a common metabolic disorder that is characterized by high blood glucose concentrations or increased blood hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels. According to the 2025 American Diabetes Association (ADA) guideline, diabetes in non-pregnant adults can be diagnosed by fasting plasma glucose (FPG) of ≥ 126 mg/dL or HbA1c level of ≥ 6.5% or 2-h plasma glucose (2-h PG) during 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) of ≥ 200 mg/dL or presence of the classic symptoms of hyperglycemia, hyperglycemia crisis, a random plasma glucose of ≥ 200 mg/dL [1]. Classic symptoms of hyperglycemia include polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, and blurred vision [2]. According to the 2025 ADA guideline, prediabetes is known as an intermediate state between diabetes and normoglycemia. It has been defined as HbA1c level of 5.7–6.4% or FPG of 100–125 mg/dL, known as impaired fasting glucose (IFG), or 2-h PG during 75-g OGTT of 140–199 mg/dL. Since prediabetes is a major risk factor for progression to diabetes and also a significant risk factor for cardiovascular disease, the early diagnosis and management of prediabetes and asymptomatic type 2 diabetes are essential [1].

Metformin, an oral biguanide antidiabetic agent, is considered as a first-line treatment for type 2 diabetes [3]. It acts through reducing hepatic glucose production, decreasing intestinal glucose absorption, and also improving insulin sensitivity by inducing peripheral glucose uptake [4]. Metformin is primarily eliminated through renal filtration; therefore, it can be administered to patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of ≥ 30 mL/min/1.73 m2. The most common adverse drug reactions (ADRs) associated with metformin use are gastrointestinal intolerances including gastrointestinal discomfort, diarrhea, and bloating. Moreover, metformin can cause lactic acidosis, especially in patients with severe renal impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) [3].

This case report presents a previously unreported ADR, namely early-onset xerostomia and chapped lips associated with metformin therapy, in a patient recently diagnosed with early-stage diabetes.

Case presentation

A 56-year-old Iranian male, known case of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and hyperuricemia presented with medication history list of valsartan 80 mg once daily, hydrochlorothiazide 50 mg once daily, metoprolol tartrate 50 mg twice daily, atorvastatin 20 mg once daily, and allopurinol 100 mg twice daily. Based on recent clinical laboratory results, he was diagnosed with early-stage diabetes due to an FPG of 116 mg/dL and an HbA1c plasma concentration of 6.6%, which was above 6.5% and known as a diagnostic criterion for diabetes according to the 2025 ADA guideline. So, he started taking metformin at a dosage of 500 mg once daily. Immediately after the first dose, the patient developed metformin-induced xerostomia (dry mouth) and chapped lips, which persisted throughout the three-month follow-up period after treatment initiation. Nevertheless, the severity of the xerostomia decreased by time. The metformin-induced chapped lips was somewhat controlled by applying emollient and moisturizing cream. Since metformin is a commonly used medication for patients with diabetes or prediabetes, it is crucial to closely monitor patients and assess ADRs to maintain blood glucose levels in the target range and improve quality of life.

Discussion

To date, a direct association between metformin and xerostomia has not been reported. This association may be due to the effect of metformin on salivary production. Metformin enhances glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity by activating adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which lowers the plasma glucose levels in patients with diabetes. Although AMPK activation has been reported to be essential for salivary restoration [5], as shown in Fig. 1, overactivation of AMPK, which can occur with metformin administration, may result in reduced saliva production and secretion. This occurs through the inhibition of anabolic processes, including the inhibition of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway [6], which can lead to the inhibition of the synthesis of structural proteins in the salivary glands [7]. Furthermore, biguanides including metformin can inhibit mTOR, completely independent of AMPK (Fig. 1) and through the inhibition of Rag GTPase [8]. Since mTOR signaling pathway including mTOR complexes 1 and 2 (mTORC1 and mTORC2) has an essential role in submandibular saliva gland development, thus mTOR inhibition can lead to xerostomia and dry mouth [7].. In addition, metformin activates AMPK, which may alter fatty acid metabolism and lipid synthesis through the phosphorylation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase [9, 10]. The lack of these lipids in saliva could reduce its protective and lubricating properties. Furthermore, salivary secretion depends on Ca2+ channels [11]. Although metformin has been reported to induce Ca2+ signaling in animal models, which can result in saliva secretion and prevent cell infiltration of salivary glands [12], however, it has been reported that unregulated and out of control Ca2+ signaling can inversely result in cell damage in salivary glands and reduced saliva secretion (Fig. 1), leading to dry mouth and xerostomia [13]. As it is obvious in Fig. 1, another possible mechanism of metformin-induced xerostomia may be attributed to the effect of metformin on insulin sensitivity. Given the critical role of insulin in salivary secretion, altered insulin sensitivity due to metformin administration may potentially result in reduced saliva production and xerostomia occurrence [14].

Schematic view of possible mechanisms involved in metformin-induced xerostomia and chapped lips. AMPK, adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase; mTOR, mammalian target of rapamycin

Although there are some reports on the efficacy of metformin in treating chemotherapy-induced xerostomia, especially in animal models [15, 16], the results of a recent phase I/II clinical trial on the administration of metformin to head and neck cancer patient population revealed that the efficacy of metformin as a chemo-radiosensitizer remains unclear. Therefore, more randomized case-controlled clinical trials are needed to confirm these findings [17, 18].

Dry mouth is the side effect of about 1% of metoprolol receiving patients [19]. β-blockers may affect saliva production and induce dry mouth through the interference with autonomic nervous system (ANS) signaling pathways [20]. It has been reported that β-blockers-associated xerostomia can occur as a result of changes in saliva composition (reduced total protein composition and amylase activity in saliva), while salivary flow rate would remain in normal ranges [20]. The results of a previous cross-sectional analytical study revealed that β-blockers and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) resulted in the higher frequencies of xerostomia occurrence (8.6% and 12.1%, respectively) among oral antihypertensive medications, and simultaneous administration of β-blockers (especially metoprolol) and ARBs (especially losartan) induced the highest rates of xerostomia [21]. However, in the reported case in this study, he had been taking metoprolol and valsartan for over 10 years with no symptoms of dry mouth or xerostomia. Xerostomia was reported just after initiation of the first dose of metformin and continued with daily consumption of this medication.

Since sufficient saliva is essential for dental health and oral digestion, xerostomia can lead to some complications including dental decay, digestive problems, and weight loss [22]. Untreated xerostomia can be considered as a serious oral hygiene problem which can induce oral candidiasis, mucosal ulcerations, and accelerated dental decay. Therefore, it is essential for patients with xerostomia to have regular dentist visits to prevent oral health problems that are associated with dry mouth. Artificial saliva or pilocarpine may also be considered to manage xerostomia [23]. Hyposalivation may result in difficulties in food swallowing, dysphagia, and weight changes in some cases. Therefore, prevention and/or early diagnosis and management of xerostomia would be essential to avoid its associated complications [24].

Conclusion

In conclusion, based on the above hypotheses, xerostomia and chapped lips appear to be potential ADRs following metformin administration that have not been reported to date to the best of our knowledge. However, further in vitro and in vivo studies are required to approve these findings. Since metformin is a first-line oral antidiabetic agent commonly administered to a large number of patients with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes, clinicians should aware about xerostomia in patients starting metformin and warn them regarding these previously unreported ADRs for early diagnosis and management. Considering that xerostomia is a major risk factor for dental decay and poor oral cavity hygiene, early diagnosis and predictive actions can improve patients’ quality of life.
