Double humanised lupus mouse model with human immune system and faecal microbiota from patients with SLE
Authors: Tian Xu, Ran Lu, David N Oakland, Rana Estaleen, Amelia Rawlings, Hilary Montano, Santiago Diab, Jacquelyn S Michaelis, Mihai Pop, Adegbenga Bankole, Christopher M Reilly, Xin M Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe aimed to create a double humanised lupus mouse model with a human immune system and faecal microbiota from patients with SLE.MethodsWe established the Double humanised SLE (DhuSLE) mouse by engrafting NSG immunodeficient mice with human CD34+ haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (NSG-hu mice) and performing faecal microbiota transplantation from patients with SLE (SLE-FMT).ResultsWhile FMT in general transiently suppressed the development of human T cells in NSG-hu mice, SLE-FMT but not FMT from non-SLE donors promoted superficial skin lesions. Importantly, the combination of SLE-FMT and pristane in NSG-hu, now called the DhuSLE-P mouse, induced proteinuria although this clinical sign observed in mice did not reflect that of the microbiota donors. DhuSLE-P mice exhibited a higher level of human IgM in the circulation than NSG-hu mice, which was positively correlated with the frequency of plasma cells in the spleen. In the splenic sections of DhuSLE-P mice, nuclear BCL6 was minimally detected but CD138 expression was evident, suggesting that most plasma cells generated were not class switched and produced IgM. Some human IgG was detected in the kidney of DhuSLE-P mice with a trend towards increased total IgG in the serum. Analysis of the faecal microbiota revealed that the gut microbiota compositions were different between DhuSLE-P mice and NSG-hu mice due to SLE-FMT but not the injection of pristane.ConclusionTogether, these results introduced the first humanised lupus mouse model combining the human immune system and gut microbiota from patients with SLE. However, limitations exist and the model may benefit from methods that promote antibody class switching. On further development, the DhuSLE model can be useful for elucidating mechanisms and/or evaluating SLE treatments.

Introduction

SLE is an autoimmune disorder characterised by expansion of autoreactive lymphocytes, production of proinflammatory cytokines and tissue deposition of autoantigen–autoantibody immune complexes,1 2 all of which contribute to inflammation and eventual damage to vital organs such as the kidney.1 SLE-associated inflammation of the kidney, known as lupus nephritis (LN), affects more than half of patients with SLE and is one of the disease’s leading causes of morbidity and mortality.1 3 The gut microbiota is a collection of commensal microbes living in the mammalian intestinal tract that have a mutually beneficial relationship with the host. Disturbances of the gut microbiota have been reported for many chronic diseases48 but their importance in disease pathogenesis remains unclear. Research from our team started a decade ago first described changes of gut microbiota in lupus-prone mice compared with control mice. Specifically, we showed that lupus-prone MRL/lpr mice had decreased Lactobacillaceae,9 and that oral administration of Lactobacillus spp into these mice attenuated the disease via the reversal of a leaky gut naturally present in the MRL/lpr mouse.10 Our studies, along with studies from other research groups using additional models of murine lupus,11 12 have suggested that the gut microbiota may act as a trigger or modulator of lupus in the context of genetic susceptibility and other risk factors.13

Numerous mouse models have been employed to understand the pathogenesis of SLE and to test potential therapies against the disease.14 Classical murine models of spontaneous SLE include NZB/W F1, MRL, MRL/lpr and BXSB/Yaa, as well as their derivatives. Induced models of murine SLE, such as the pristane-induced model, also exist. Each of these models represents a subset of symptoms seen in human patients, including antibody production, lymphoid proliferation or activation and LN. Although each model has unique advantages, effective treatments in mice often do not translate to human disease.15 Therefore, there is an urgent need for a better in vivo model of human SLE. Notably, the roles of gut microbiota in different mouse models of SLE are different. For example, not all models exhibit the leaky gut, which is present in human SLE at least for some patients,16 17 and the effects of Lactobacillus spp versus Clostridia (producers of short-chain fatty acids) differ between the models.91118 This suggests the need to assess the interaction between gut microbiota and immunity in patients with SLE directly. However, this is extremely challenging due to patient accessibility issues and the complexity of disease manifestations (eg, no two patients have the same symptoms). Therefore, a preclinical model closely mimicking human SLE in terms of both host immunity and gut microbiota would be ideal. In this study, we took on the enterprise to create a double humanised lupus mouse model with a human immune system and faecal microbiota from patients with SLE which, with the addition of pristane, generated some lupus-like pathologies similar to those in human SLE.

Materials and methods

Human subjects

A total of 36 patients were enrolled, and 18 patients who met the SLICC criteria for SLE each provided a stool sample. The patient demographics are displayed in online supplemental table S1. The patients were enrolled at the lupus clinic in a single centre in Southwest Roanoke, Virginia, USA. All patients met the 2009 SLICC revised American College of Rheumatology (ACR) diagnostic criteria for SLE. At the time of enrolment, a systemic lupus or associated disease activity index (SLEDAI) score was obtained. The inclusion criteria were age 18–75, fulfilling SLICC revised ACR criteria for SLE (2009) and on or eligible for immunosuppressive therapy. The exclusion criteria were age <18 or >75, not meeting SLICC revised ACR criteria for SLE, disease not needing immunosuppressive therapy, pregnancy, presence of other immune diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease, on or recent use of antibiotics, and vulnerable patients including prisoners, non-English speakers and critically ill patients.

Patient stool sampling and 16S rRNA analysis

Human stool samples were collected from patients at Carilion Medical Center in Roanoke, Virginia, USA. Patients were provided with a collection ‘pot’ and requested to bring in a specimen collected the day before a routine visit. All procedures were approved by the IRB of Virginia Tech Carilion School of Medicine. All microbiota samples were stored at −80°C until being processed at the same time. Sample homogenisation, cell lysis, DNA extraction and PCR of the 16S-V4 region were performed with the same methods described in a previous report. The V4 region of 16S rRNA gene was amplified using PCR. Puriﬁed amplicons were sequenced bi-directionally (paired-end 150 bp) on an Illumina MiSeq at Argonne National Laboratory. Samples were analysed using the R package phyloseq.19 Reads were processed and ASVs were generated using DADA2 in R. Taxonomy was assigned using the SILVA 138 rRNA database training set using the DADA2 functions, assignTaxonomy and addSpecies. Two samples with fewer than 1000 reads were removed from the dataset, resulting in 18 samples and 1311 ASVs used for downstream analyses. Data has been deposited to NCBI SRA number PRJNA1023924.

Generation of double humanised SLE mice

NOD.Scid.γC (NSG) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory and bred under maximum barrier conditions in an animal facility accredited by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University under protocol number 23–057. Neonatal NSG pups (within 2 days of birth) were transplanted intrahepatically with 1×105 human cord blood CD34+ HSPCs (AllCells). Humanised (NSG-hu) pups were weaned at 3 weeks of age, followed by a 3-day course of an antibiotic cocktail (1 g/L ampicillin, 1 g/L neomycin, 0.5 g/L metronidazole and 1 g/L vancomycin) in drinking water, which was then replaced with sterile water. NSG-hu mice were orally gavaged with either pooled faecal microbiota from patients with SLE (DhuSLE mice) at weeks 4, 5, 7 and 9. The faecal microbiota from non-SLE individuals16 were used in addition to the PBS control. Successful colonisation of human microbiota was confirmed at week 8. Human immune cell reconstitution was assessed at 8 and 12 weeks post-transplantation by flow cytometry of peripheral blood and quantified as the percentage of human CD45+ cells among total live cells. Mice that failed to achieve human immune cell reconstitution or microbiota colonisation were excluded from subsequent experiments. DhuSLE-P mice were generated from DhuSLE mice with one intraperitoneal injection of 0.5 mL pristane (Sigma-Aldrich) at 12 weeks of age.

Preparation and administration of human faecal microbiota

On thawing, approximately 5 g of human faeces was suspended in 20 mL of sterile PBS supplemented with 10% sterile pharmaceutical-grade glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich). The suspension was homogenised by vigorous vortexing, filtered through a 70 µm sterile strainer, aliquoted into sterile cryovials and stored at –80°C until use.20 Prior to administration, frozen aliquots were thawed in a 37°C water bath, vortexed to resuspend the bacteria and delivered to mice via oral gavage (200 µL/ mouse) using a sterile, flexible gavage needle.

Confirmation of human microbiota colonisation by PCR

Faecal pellets were collected at week 8 post-transplantation. Total DNA was extracted using QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit with a bead-beating step to improve lysis of Gram-positive organisms. DNA concentration and purity were assessed by Nanodrop spectrophotometry. Colonisation by human-derived microbiota was assessed by conventional PCR targeting the 16S rRNA gene of the human-specific bacterium Neglectibacter timonensis strain SN17.21 The following primers were used: Forward—TAG CCG GAC TGA GAG GTT GA; Reverse—TTC CTC CTC TGC TAC CGT CA. PCR reactions were performed using a standard thermocycling protocol (95°C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 60°C for 60 s, 72°C for 30 s; final extension at 72°C for 5 min). Amplicons were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis (2%) and visualised with ethidium bromide using Odyssey XF imaging system (LICORbio). Presence of a specific amplicon of expected size indicated successful colonisation. Faecal pellets from mice gavaged with PBS served as negative controls.

Cell isolation and flow cytometry

Blood was collected through cardiac puncture at the endpoint or the tail vein at interim time points into EDTA-coated blood collection tubes and centrifuged at 400×g for 10 min to separate plasma and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). PBMCs were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in complete C10 medium (RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1% non-essential amino acids, 10 mM HEPES, 55 µM 2-mercaptoethanol, 2 mM L-glutamine and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin; all from Life Technologies, Grand Island, New York, USA). Spleens were harvested at the humane endpoint, and splenocytes were isolated as previously reported.22 Splenocyte pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of 1×RBC lysis buffer (eBioscience, San Diego, California, USA) and incubated at room temperature for 5 min, followed by neutralisation with 7 mL of C10 medium. The suspension was centrifuged at 400×g for 5 min, and cell pellets were resuspended in 5 mL of fresh C10 medium for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) staining. Both PBMCs and splenocytes were analysed by flow cytometry. For surface staining, cells were blocked by Human Fc Receptor Blocking Solution (BioLegend) then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies. Anti-human antibodies used in this study include CD3-PE/Cy7, CD4-FITC, CD8-APC, HLA-DR-PE, CD197-APC/Cy7, CD45RA-PerCP, CD45-APC, CD19-PE, CD19-PE/Cy7, CD20-FITC, CD27-Pacific Blue, CD38-PerCP/Cy5.5, IgD-Brilliant Violet 421, CD86-PE/Cy7, IgM-APC/Cy7 and IgD-Brilliant Violet 421 (BioLegend). Stained cells were analysed with a BD FACS Aria II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA). Flow cytometry data were analysed with FlowJo.

Proteinuria measurement and normalisation

Urine was collected biweekly after pristane injection till the endpoint, and all samples were stored at −20°C and analysed with the Pierce Bradford Plus Protein Assay Reagent (Thermo Scientific). Because the human cell engraftment rate was highly variable, and that immune cell expansion was expected to cause higher proteinuria and vice versa, data normalisation was performed by dividing the proteinuria level (mg/dL) by the same week’s engraftment rate (ie, frequency of human CD45+ cells). The normalised proteinuria levels were then plotted.

Renal pathology

Kidneys were fixed in formalin immediately after isolation, and fixed renal tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned and stained for periodic acid-Schiff at the Histopathology Laboratory at Virginia-Maryland College of Veterinary Medicine. Glomerular lesions were graded on a scale of 0–3 for increased cellularity, increased mesangial matrix, necrosis, percentage of sclerotic glomeruli and presence of crescents. Similarly, tubulointerstitial lesions were graded on a scale of 0–3 for interstitial mononuclear infiltration, tubular damage, interstitial fibrosis and vasculitis. Slides were scored by a board-certified veterinary pathologist (SD) in a blinded fashion.

ELISAs

Concentrations of TGFβ and autoantibodies in the mouse plasma were measured using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The following kits were used: Human/Mouse TGF-β1 Uncoated ELISA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 88–8350-22), Human IgG (Total) Uncoated ELISA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 88–50550-22), Human IgM Uncoated ELISA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 88–50620-22) and Human anti-dsDNA IgG ELISA Kit (CUSABIO, Cat# CSB-E04911h).

Immunohistochemistry

Kidney and spleen tissues were embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT Compound (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, California, USA) and rapidly frozen in a slurry of dry ice and 2-methylbutane. Frozen OCT blocks were cryosectioned at 6–8 µm thickness, and unstained slides were stored at −80°C until use. Prior to staining, slides were brought to room temperature, air-dried for 30 min and fixed in prechilled (−20°C) acetone for 10 min. After washing in PBS, non-specific binding was blocked with PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and human FcR block (BioLegend) for 40 min at room temperature. Slides were then incubated overnight at 4°C in a dark, humidified chamber with a fluorochrome-conjugated antibody cocktail prepared in PBS+1% BSA. For kidney sections, the following antibodies were used: anti-human IgM-PE (BioLegend), anti-human IgG-FITC (BD Biosciences), anti-human complement C3-APC (BioLegend) and anti-human complement C1q-FITC (MP Biomedicals). Spleen sections were stained with anti-human CD20-AF647 (BioLegend), anti-human BCL6-FITC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and anti-human CD138-AF594 (BioLegend). After overnight incubation, slides were washed three times with PBS and mounted using ProLong Gold Antifade containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Life Technologies). Slides were imaged using a Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH) and a 40× objective. Images were acquired under identical magnification and exposure settings to ensure comparability. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence with machine learning was performed to objectively assess marker expression intensity across experimental groups. Raw image data were processed using Python (V.3.10) with the scikit-image library (V.0.22)2 for systematic background correction and signal quantification. For example, for IgG-FITC staining, a non-local means denoising algorithm was applied using scikit-image’s denoise_nl_means function with optimised patch size and search window parameters. This step effectively reduced random noise and autofluorescence without blurring true immunofluorescent signals. Following denoising, a background floor was calculated as the median intensity of the darkest 50%–80% of pixels, depending on the autofluorescence level of each sample. For CD138-AF594 staining, the red fluorescence channel was extracted and converted to floating-point intensity values. To minimise background autofluorescence, a percentile-based background subtraction approach was applied. Specifically, the lower 30th percentile of pixel intensities was used as the baseline, and all pixels below this threshold were clipped to zero. This fast, non-parametric method efficiently removed low-intensity background across heterogeneous tissue regions. The background-corrected signal was quantified as MFI.

16S rRNA analysis of mouse faeces

Faecal bacterial DNA was extracted and subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing targeting the V4 region to characterise gut microbial communities. Raw paired-end reads were quality filtered, trimmed, merged and denoised using the DADA2 package (V.1.30.0) in R to infer high-resolution ASVs. Chimeric sequences were removed, and taxonomic classification was assigned against the SILVA reference database. Downstream analyses were performed in R (V.4.3.2) using the phyloseq package (V.1.46.0) for data integration, management and visualisation. Alpha diversity was calculated using the Shannon diversity index to assess within-sample diversity, while beta diversity was evaluated using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity followed by principal coordinate analysis implemented via the vegan package (V.2.6.10). Relative abundances at the family and genus levels were calculated by transforming ASV counts to proportions within each sample. Mean relative abundances across samples within each experimental group were then calculated for visualisation. Only taxa with a mean relative abundance greater than 0.5% across samples were retained for visualisation and comparison. Visualisation of alpha diversity, beta diversity ordinations and relative abundance profiles was performed using ggplot2 (V.3.5.2) and plotly (V.4.10.4). Longitudinal and group-specific analyses were conducted by subsetting samples by time point (3, 5, 7, 9, 11 and 13 weeks post-FMT), using the combined pre-FMT samples as a unified baseline control. Data wrangling, normalisation and export of processed tables were handled using tidyverse (V.2.0.0), reshape2 (V.1.4.4) and writexl (V.1.5.4). Statistical comparisons of alpha diversity were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), while differences in beta diversity were assessed via permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) on Bray-Curtis distances. Data has been deposited to NCBI SRA number PRJNA1344621.

Skin scoring and histopathology

To quantitatively evaluate cutaneous disease severity in humanised mice, we established a standardised lesion scoring system based on both lesion area and clinical severity, assessed across six anatomical regions: head/neck, thorax, abdomen and forelimbs/hindlimbs. For each region, the lesion surface area was measured in square centimetres using digital callipers or image analysis software. If lesions were irregularly shaped, their area was estimated using a transparent grid overlay. Lesion areas were then categorised into scores as follows: A region with no visible lesion received a score of 0. A lesion covering less than 1 cm2 received a score of 1. A lesion between 1 and 2 cm2 received a score of 2. A lesion greater than 2 but no more than 5 cm2 received a score of 3. A lesion greater than 5 but no more than 7 cm2 received a score of 4. A lesion larger than 7 cm2 received the maximum area score of 5. In parallel, each region was scored for clinical severity based on visual features such as erythema, scaling, alopecia and ulceration: If no lesion was visible, the severity score was 0. If the lesion exhibited only mild erythema without any hair loss or scaling, the score was 1. Moderate erythema accompanied by mild scaling or patchy hair loss was scored as 2. More severe erythema with pronounced scaling or widespread alopecia was given a score of 3. The most severe lesions, marked by ulceration or extensive tissue damage, were scored as 4. For each anatomical region, a composite lesion score was calculated by multiplying the area score by the severity score. These composite scores from all four body regions were then summed to generate a total lesion score per mouse. H&E stain was performed on formalin-fixed skin tissue sections and histopathology scoring was performed in a blinded fashion by a board-certified veterinary pathologist (SD) based on published literature.23

Statistical analyses of non-sequencing data

For the comparison of two groups, unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For the comparison of three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was used followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Two-way ANOVA was used to reveal time-dependent and group-dependent effects. Pearson correlation test was used as appropriate. Results were considered statistically significant when p<0.05. All analyses were performed with the Prism GraphPad. Statistical significance was labelled as asterisks (*p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, ****p<0.0001).

Results

Characterisation of patient faecal microbiota and identification of a human-specific bacterium

While the gut microbiota has been shown to be associated with the presence of SLE24 and particularly LN,25 there is limited information on the potential relationship between disease serology/urinology and the gut microbiota. We took a cross-sectional approach and enrolled a total of 36 patients, from which 18 patients who met the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) criteria for SLE each provided a stool sample (online supplemental table S1). Bacterial 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequencing was performed to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs); and correlations between bacterial taxa and disease parameters were established using linear or logistic regression models. The results showed that there was no statistical correlation between the gut microbiota and lymphopenia, complement C3/C4 or the presence of LN. There was also no relationship between the gut microbiota and SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) or patient global health assessment. However, we found negative correlations between the level of anti-double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) antibodies in the blood and the phylum Firmicutes, class Clostridia and family Ruminococcaceae, respectively. In patients seronegative for anti-dsDNA antibodies, the relative abundances of these bacterial taxa were significantly higher (online supplemental figure S1). The absence of anti-histone antibodies was also inversely correlated with a significantly higher abundance of the phylum Firmicutes, although the difference in Ruminococcaceae was not statistically significant. Furthermore, we found potentially positive correlations between the level of proteinuria and the genus Eubacterium (family Eubacteriaceae) and the species Parabacteroides distasonis and a Neglecta sp (online supplemental figure S2), though we had a very small sample size of two in one of the arms due to the low levels of proteinuria in all patients. Known SLE pathobionts, including Enterococcus gallinarum17 26 and Mediterraneibacter (Ruminococcus) gnavus,25 27 28 were either absent or present at very low levels in our dataset (online supplemental table S2).

To validate the colonisation of human-derived microbiota after faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) into mice, we generated 16S rRNA-specific primers and evaluated the detection of two bacterial species, P. distasonis and Neglecta timonensis, in the faeces of FMT-treated NSG mice. PCR analysis with appropriate positive and negative controls revealed N. timonensis as the human-specific bacterium that we could use to validate successful colonisation of human-derived microbiota in mice (online supplemental figure S3). In this pilot experiment, we also compared two FMT methods, one via neonatal gavage29 and the other via adult oral gavage following antibiotic treatment of newly weaned NSG mice. The result showed that both methods worked equivalently well, so we chose to use the second method in subsequent experiments.

Transient suppression of T cell development in NSG-hu mice by human FMT

Humanised NSG (NSG-hu) mice were generated by intrahepatic injection of CD34+ haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) from human cord blood to 1-day-old mouse pups of NOD/Scid/γC background at 150 000 cells/pup.30 Once pups were weaned, they were treated with a commonly used antibiotic cocktail (ampicillin, neomycin, vancomycin, metronidazole) for 3 days to transiently remove endogenous microbiota, followed by 3 days of washout period without the antibiotics in the drinking water. The antibiotic treatment was used to facilitate the colonisation of human stool microbiota, which was given via oral gavage at 4, 5, 7 and 9 weeks of age. Human microbiota colonisation was validated at 8 weeks of age, and human cell generation was determined at 8 and 12 weeks of age (figure 1a).

A pooled stool sample from non-SLE individuals16 and a pooled stool sample from patients with SLE (online supplemental tableS1) were used for FMT. Comparing to phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as the FMT/gavage control, regardless of the source of human stool microbiota (SLE or not), FMT significantly suppressed the generation of human CD45+ immune cells in the blood of NSG-hu mice at 12 weeks of age (figure 1b). Consistent with a recent report,31 mouse CD45+ cells were present in NSG-hu mice that were not affected by FMT, leading to significantly reduced human-to-mouse ratios of CD45+ cells (figure 1c). These results suggest that the development of human immune cells in NSG-hu mice is suppressed by the colonisation of human faecal microbiota, which was confirmed by the detection of the human-specific bacterium N. timonensis in the mouse faeces following FMT treatment (figure 1d).

The NSG-hu mouse is known to derive human B cells earlier and in larger numbers than human T cells.30 At 8 weeks of age, we observed a B-to-T ratio of about 7 in NSG-hu mice without FMT treatment. Interestingly, the B-to-T ratios were much higher (about 20) in 8-week-old NSG-hu mice following either non-SLE or SLE-FMT treatment (figure 1e–f), suggesting decreased T cell proportions relative to B cells. By 12 weeks of age, the B-to-T ratios in all three groups were reduced to similar levels, suggesting that human FMT-mediated suppression of human T cell development may be transient. Several cytokines involved in T cell development, including Flt3L, interleukin (IL)-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-7, IL-15, interferon-gamma and TGFβ, were measured by using either ELISA (for TGFβ) or Luminex (for all other cytokines) in the mouse plasma. A significant increase of blood TGFβ was observed in FMT-treated NSG-hu mice at 12 weeks of age (figure 1g and online supplemental figure S4A), but no differences were found for all other cytokines (online supplemental figure S4B). While TGFβ is known to promote negative selection of thymocytes to prevent autoimmunity,3234 the timing of this cytokine’s induction (12 weeks of age) was delayed compared with that of the observed T cell suppression (8 weeks of age). Nevertheless, these results indicate that human FMT in NSG-hu mice, regardless of the donor’s disease state, induces TGFβ production and transiently suppresses the generation or development of human T cells.

Development of superficial skin lesions in NSG-hu mice treated with SLE-FMT

By 24 weeks of age, NSG-hu mice treated with human FMT still had lower percentages of human CD45+ cells in the blood, but the difference between with and without human FMT was no longer statistically significant (figure 2a), suggesting a delay in human cell generation rather than complete abrogation by the colonisation of human faecal microbiota. Comparing to control NSG-hu mice treated with PBS, those treated with either non-SLE or SLE-FMT exhibited similar body weights (figure 2b). As the enlargement of kidney and/or lymphoid tissues is common in lupus-prone mice, we also determined the size of spleen, lymph nodes and kidneys. None of these tissues were different in weight among the three groups (figure 2c). Glomerulonephritis is another hallmark of murine and human SLE, so we measured the urinary protein levels and normalised the proteinuria data to the percentages of human CD45+ cells. This normalisation step was necessary due to the fact that a higher number of human immune cells is more likely to lead to graft-versus-host disease in NSG-hu mice, which also exhibits proteinuria (unpublished observation). Unfortunately, the normalised proteinuria levels were also not different between the control and FMT-treated mice (figure 2d). Analysis of the plasma revealed negligible levels of anti-dsDNA human IgG (hIgG) across all three groups (figure 2e); however, non-SLE FMT treatment appeared to promote activated, major histocompatibility complex-II expressing human T cells in the blood, whose percentage was positively correlated with the level of proteinuria (figure 2f and online supplemental figure S4C). The percentages of activated, CD86 expressing human B cells as well as circulating plasma cells were not different among the three groups (online supplemental figure S4D), and all mice produced a good amount of hIgM but not hIgG (online supplemental figure S4E), consistent with the lack of anti-dsDNA hIgG described earlier. In addition, consistent with the lack of difference in proteinuria, we did not observe any differences among the three groups in renal deposition of hIgM or hIgG (online supplemental figure S4f-g), and C3 and C1q were barely detectable in these kidney sections (online supplemental figure S4H-I). These results suggest that SLE-FMT by itself was not able to induce autoantibodies and common SLE clinical signs including splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy and glomerulonephritis.

Remarkably, however, we observed significantly larger and more obvious skin lesions in NSG-hu mice treated with SLE-FMT compared with those treated with PBS or non-SLE FMT (figure 2g). Skin histopathology revealed mild acanthosis of the epidermis and very mild, focal inflammation of the dermis between folliculo-adnexal structures across all samples (figure 2h), with no difference in histopathological scores comparing SLE-FMT with PBS or non-SLE FMT (figure 2i). These results suggest SLE-FMT may induce superficial skin lesions without affecting skin histopathology.

Glomerular immune complex deposition in NSG-hu mice treated with SLE-FMT and pristane

Pristane has been reported to trigger lupus-like disease in NSG-hu mice,35 so we administered the same dose of pristane as published (0.5 mL/mouse, intraperitoneal injection) to 12-week-old NSG-hu mice and monitored them till 24 weeks of age (online supplemental figure S5A). We did not observe any differences in the NSG-hu mice injected with PBS versus pristane for body weight, weights of the spleen and kidneys, level of normalised proteinuria or plasma levels of hIgM and total or dsDNA-specific hIgG (online supplemental figure S5b-f). Therefore, we decided to combine pristane injection with FMT treatment. In this experiment, untreated NSG-hu mice were compared with NSG-hu mice gavaged with SLE-FMT, called the Double humanised SLE (DhuSLE) mouse, then injected with pristane (figure 3a, where we abbreviated pristane-treated DhuSLE mice as DhuSLE-P). While the body weight, tissue weights and circulating anti-dsDNA hIgG levels did not differ between NSG-hu and DhuSLE-P mice (figure 3b–d), DhuSLE-P mice had significantly higher normalised proteinuria (figure 3e), suggesting the development of lupus-like renal pathology in these mice. Unfortunately, renal histopathology did not show any abnormalities in the kidney of DhuSLE-P mice (figure 3f). All pathological scoring, including mesangial expansion, endocapillary proliferation, crescents, sclerosis and interstitial inflammation, done by a blinded veterinary pathologist, came back as zeros. However, a small amount of hIgG was detectable as deposits in the kidney sections of some DhuSLE-P mice but not NSG-hu mice (figure 3g) that might have contributed to the differences in normalised proteinuria. Renal deposition of complement C3 was not detected in either NSG-hu or DhuSLE-P mice. These results suggest that the combination of SLE-FMT and pristane induced glomerular immune complex deposition in DhuSLE-P mice.

Generation of hIgM-producing plasma cells in the spleen of DhuSLE-P mice

To assess the systemic immune response following treatment with SLE-FMT and pristane, we measured the levels of hIgM and hIgG in the circulation. Human IgM was detectable at μg/mL level in the DhuSLE-P mice, which was significantly more than the hIgM level in NSG-hu mice (figure 4a). In contrast, the majority of DhuSLE-P mice had less than 1 ng/mL of hIgG in the blood, with several in the 5∼20 ng/mL range. This data suggests minimal antibody class switching in our DhuSLE-P model. However, the frequency of splenic plasma cells was trending increase in these mice compared with NSG-hu mice (figure 4b), and the circulatory level of hIgM, but not that of hIgG, positively correlated with the frequency of plasma cells in the spleen (figure 4c), suggesting the generation of unswitched, hIgM-producing plasma cells in DhuSLE-P mice.

When splenic sections were subjected to immunohistochemical analysis, we observed CD138+ cells that were primarily CD20− in DhuSLE-P mice (figure 4d), suggesting the presence of plasma cells in the spleen. BCL6 staining was positive in these mice, but mostly cytoplasmic indicating minimal germinal centre reactions. The splenic sections of NSG-hu mice, on the other hand, showed mainly CD20+CD138− cells with some nuclear staining of BCL6. Quantification of the CD138 signals showed a significantly higher mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in DhuSLE-P than NSG-hu mice, but the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios of BCL6 were comparable (figure 4e), suggesting that plasma cells, but not germinal centre B cells, were upregulated in the DhuSLE-P mouse. Together, these results suggest that hIgM-producing plasma cells could be generated when NSG-hu mice are treated with the combination of SLE-FMT and pristane, but with little evidence indicating germinal centre reactions or antibody class switching.

Similar disease phenotypes in DhuSLE-P mice regardless of the donor lupus nephritis status

We hypothesised that the disease phenotype in DhuSLE-P mice would reflect the disease status of the microbiota donor. In other words, if the microbiota donor had LN, we postulated that the DhuSLE-P mice receiving FMT from this donor would also exhibit increased renal pathology. Surprisingly, however, when grouping the mice by donor LN status, we found that proteinuria levels in DhuSLE-P mice were not different between LN versus No LN donors (figure 5a). The levels of total hIgM and hIgG (figure 5b), as well as the level of anti-dsDNA hIgG (figure 5c), were not different either. In addition, immunohistochemical analyses showed no differences between LN versus No LN donors in renal deposition of hIgG (figure 5d) or splenic CD138 (figure 5e). These results refute our initial hypothesis, instead suggesting that the disease phenotypes in DhuSLE-P mice may not reflect specific clinical signs, such as LN, in their microbiota donors.

Distinct gut microbiota in DhuSLE-P mice due to SLE-FMT but not pristane injection

We next analysed the gut microbiota compositions of NSG-hu versus DhuSLE-P mice by using 16S rRNA sequencing, where we sampled faecal samples from DhuSLE mice at the time of pristane injection and 2 and 4 weeks post injection. The result showed significant β-diversity differences between NSG-hu and DhuSLE-P mice regardless of the sampling time (figure 6a), suggesting that the DhuSLE-P mouse harbours distinct gut microbiota from the NSG-hu mice and that SLE-FMT, but not pristane injection, contributes to this phenomenon. Family level analysis revealed that Bacteroidaceae expanded in DhuSLE-P mice compared with NSG-hu and to compensate, the DhuSLE-P mouse had a lower relative abundance of Lactobacillaceae (figure 6b). Within Bacteroidaceae, the genus Bacteroides was the most abundant in DhuSLE-P mice (figure 6c).

As bacterial colonisation is host species-specific,36 we asked the question of whether DhuSLE-P mice with SLE-FMT harbour similar gut microbiota as the donor material. β-diversity analysis revealed significant differences between the faecal microbiota from patients with SLE and the gut microbiota from DhuSLE-P mice following FMT from the same patients (online supplemental figure S6A). This observation provides additional evidence indicating that some human-derived bacteria favour colonisation in mice, while others do not. Taxonomical analysis showed increased relative abundances of family Lachnospiraceae and genus Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group in DhuSLE-P mice (online supplemental figure S6B), suggesting better fitness of these bacteria in the gut microenvironment of NSG-hu mice. The colonisation of Enterococcus and Ruminococcus, on the other hand, was restricted in DhuSLE-P mice (online supplemental table S3). Regardless of the donors’ LN status, the microbiota of individual DhuSLE-P mouse was different from that of the pooled patient faecal sample (online supplemental figure S6C).

Together, these results suggest that even though some bacteria in the faecal microbiota of patient donors cannot colonise in NSG-hu mice, SLE-FMT is able to establish a stable gut microbiota composition in DhuSLE-P mice that was not influenced by the injection of pristane.

Discussion

The gut microbiota plays an important role in the pathogenesis of SLE, but the exact role remains elusive because studies using different mouse models have yielded different mechanisms. Therefore, we decided to try establishing a humanised mouse model of SLE that might better mimic the host-microbe interactions in the human disease. We used a humanised mouse model that provides reconstitution of multiple human immune cell lineages, NSG-hu and coupled it with transplantation of faecal microbiota from patients with SLE (SLE-FMT). Regardless of the source of the faecal microbiota, FMT unexpectedly inhibited human T cell development in the NSG-hu mice, but fortunately the inhibition was transient. Interestingly, SLE-FMT promoted superficial skin lesions that were not seen in NSG-hu mice treated with PBS control or transplanted with faecal microbiota from non-SLE individuals. To induce lupus-like renal pathology, we injected SLE-FMT treated NSG-hu mice with pristane, which by itself did not induce lupus-like disease in NSG-hu mice in our hands. It is worth noting that fetal liver HSPCs were used when pristane was observed to trigger lupus-like disease in NSG-hu mice.35 Due to the lack of access to aborted human fetuses, we used cord blood CD34+ HSPCs purchased from AllCells. The difference in HSPC source may have contributed to the phenotypic difference by pristane alone in the two studies.

The combination of SLE-FMT and pristane in DhuSLE-P mice induced some glomerular pathology phenotypes similar to human SLE, but not splenomegaly or lymphadenopathy. Lymphoid tissue enlargement is common in murine lupus such as in MRL/lpr mice but uncommon in humans with SLE, so its absence was reasonable in DhuSLE-P mice. However, we also did not detect anti-dsDNA hIgG, a hallmark of the human disease. It turned out that antibody class switching did not occur to a significant extent, and plasma cells generated from the DhuSLE-P mice were primarily producing hIgM. Further, we found that DhuSLE-P mice were stably colonised with some human-derived commensal bacteria and that their gut microbiota was not influenced by the injection of pristane. These results suggest that we have made significant progress in the establishment of a humanised mouse model of SLE with a human immune system and human-derived gut microbiota from patients with SLE.

When human FMT was given to NSG-hu mice, we observed minimal phenotypic differences between non-SLE and SLE donor faecal microbiota. This observation was different from when human FMT was given to germ-free mice without a human immune system.37 In their study, autoantibody production, cytokine dysregulation and immune cell expansion were found to be associated with the SLE faecal microbiota. Indeed, human FMT may induce an influence on human immune cells with a biology that is different from that on mouse immune cells. The explanation may in part reside in the genetics of Fcγ receptors (FcγRs), a feature whereby human lupus and murine lupus are completely distinct from each other. FcγRs mediate clearance of immune complexes; therefore, different patterns of FcγR expression in human versus mouse can lead to differences in immune complex-mediated pathology. For example, mouse FcγRIIb and human FcγRIIB are different.38 Different expression of these inhibitory FcγRs, which makes a complex contribution to immune complex handling and autoimmunity, may explain the distinct impact of human FMT on mice with versus without a human immune system. In mice engrafted with a human immune system, the human FcγRIIB may be more effective at inhibiting the human FMT-mediated activation of FcγR signalling than the mouse FcγRIIb would in the absence of human immune cells, leading to the lack of significant pathology in humanised mice. In addition, the low-affinity receptor FcγRIIIB, not expressed in mice, is constitutively expressed on human neutrophils.38 Human FCGR3B polymorphism and copy number variations influence immune complex uptake by human neutrophils, suggesting that differences in this locus may impact the disease phenotype observed in humanised mice. In future investigations, we will characterise the FCGR3B locus of donor HSPCs used for reconstituting our DhuSLE mice. It is anticipated that donor HSPCs with certain allotypes and low copy numbers of FCGR3B would induce more pronounced lupus-like disease on human FMT in the DhuSLE mouse.

Our current model has several limitations. First, we have used healthy human cord blood CD34+ cells to reconstitute the immune system of NSG-hu mice, which may not reflect the altered immune system in patients with SLE. If we could use cord blood CD34+ cells from a patient with SLE, it might not have been necessary for us to use pristane. Second, due to technical difficulties, we were not able to preserve anaerobic bacteria during the FMT process. This may have led to the significant differences in the microbiota compositions between DhuSLE-P mice and the donor faecal material from patients. Thirdly, our new model has minimal indication of antibody class switching. Class-switch recombination is important for the pathogenesis of SLE because IgG, not IgM, is pathogenic. This may be explained by the intrinsic properties of the NSG mouse as the recipient of human immune cells. NSG-hu mice do not develop a complete human immune system with limited thymic function, absent organised lymphoid structures and impaired germinal centre formation, all of which contribute to the lack of class-switched antibodies. In addition, the observed T cell suppression/altered reconstitution in response to SLE-FMT may reflect incomplete T cell maturation and/or altered thymic selection in our DhuSLE mouse, which may also be missing immune regulatory pathways present in humans. Therefore, while we aimed to develop a double humanised mouse model resembling the host–microbe interactions in humans, our current model remains different from patients with SLE for the following two reasons: (1) there were significant differences in the gut microbiota of DhuSLE-P mice versus that of their patient donors, possibly because anaerobic bacteria were not transferred effectively; and (2) there was incomplete immune system development including suppression of T cells and failure in antibody class switching. These limitations clearly affect the translational relevance of the model as it currently stands.

During the course of our experiments, a paper was published31 where the use of oestrogen conditioning and a different immunodeficient mouse strain led to successful generation of class-switched antibodies. They showed the development of lupus autoimmunity in their humanised mice following pristane injection. It is worth noting that they also used healthy human cord blood CD34+ cells, and they did not introduce human-derived microbiota. While it will be difficult to obtain cord blood CD34+ cells from patients with SLE, in future investigations, we will improve our model by replacing NSG with the NBSGW immunodeficient mouse and adding oestrogen conditioning. Nevertheless, this study marks a significant step towards the generation of a double humanised mouse model of SLE that could eventually mimic the host-microbe interactions in patients. Our DhuSLE model, on further development, will be useful for elucidating human lupus disease mechanisms and/or evaluating novel treatments against SLE.
