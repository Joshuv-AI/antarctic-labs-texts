Achieving clinical and histologic remission with tezepelumab in an adolescent with refractory eosinophilic gastrointestinal disease: A case report
Authors: Destiny Newsome, Colby Sharlin, Cindy Salm Bauer, Shauna Schroeder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTezepelumab is a human monoclonal antibody that binds to human thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), an epithelial cytokine that plays a role in Type 2 signaling. We describe a 17‐year‐old with a history of eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) and eosinophilic gastroenteritis who demonstrated response to tezepelumab with both clinical and histologic remission. The patient had previously failed multiple standard therapies, including extensive dietary elimination, topical swallowed steroids, and dupilumab, with persistent symptoms and esophageal and duodenal eosinophilia. This case underscores the importance of continued research into eosinophilic gastrointestinal diseases (EGIDs) and suggests that TSLP blockade may offer a promising therapeutic option for patients with refractory EoE and non‐EoE EGIDs.

INTRODUCTION

Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a chronic antigen‐driven, Type 2 immune‐mediated disease characterized by esophageal inflammation and dysfunction. Antigen exposure releases alarmins such as human thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), an epithelial‐derived cytokine that activates and amplifies the Type 2 immune response, driving differentiation of T helper 2 (Th2) cells, which release cytokines (interleukin [IL]‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13) that trigger eosinophilic inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and tissue remodeling resulting in the characteristic pathophysiologic features of EoE. 1 Although less is known about the pathophysiology of other eosinophilic gastrointestinal diseases (EGIDs) such as eosinophilic gastritis (EoG) and eosinophilic enteritis (EoN), evidence suggests an overlapping Th2‐mediated immunoetiology with differing molecular features by segment involved. 2 , 3

Conventional EoE treatment includes elimination diets, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), swallowed topical steroids, and more recently, biologics targeting specific immune pathways involved in the pathogenesis of EoE (Figure 1). 4 Tezepelumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits TSLP binding to its receptor, was U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved in 2021 as add‐on maintenance treatment for severe asthma in patients ≥12 years. It is currently under investigation for its use in patients aged 12–80 years with EoE in a randomized, placebo‐controlled Phase 3 trial with enrollment complete (CROSSING). 4 , 5 One case report has described successful use of tezepelumab in an adolescent with EoE, but to our knowledge, no current literature describes its successful use in patients with a more extensive EGID profile. 6 Our patient was started on tezepelumab for treatment of severe asthma and achieved remission of EoE with sustained remission of EoG/EoN. In addition to histologic findings, Endoscopic Reference Score (EREFS), the Index of Severity for Eosinophilic Esophagitis (I‐SEE) score, and Esophageal String Test (EST) were utilized throughout the patient's clinical course to monitor disease activity (Table 1). CASE REPORT

We present a 17‐year‐old diagnosed with EoE at 9 months of age after presenting with vomiting, severe reflux, feeding intolerance, and comorbid atopic conditions (Immunoglobulin E [IgE]‐mediated food allergies and asthma). Family history was notable for asthma in both parents and a sibling who was diagnosed with EoE during our patient's course. Initial management of EoE included elemental formula and PPI with subsequent transition to swallowed budesonide slurry. Over the years, the patient underwent multiple dietary eliminations (cow's milk, egg, soy, wheat, rice, potato, chicken, beans, peas, and turkey) with growth parameters remaining within normal limits despite prolonged dietary restriction. Despite periods of symptomatic improvement with various combinations of dietary and pharmacologic therapies, the patient never achieved histologic remission of EoE from initial diagnosis through adolescence. Further, multiple endoscopies throughout the course demonstrated eosinophilic disease extending to the stomach and duodenum (Table 1). Aside from a single assessment with ~80% adherence (reflecting brief hesitancy regarding long‐term steroid use), the patient was fully adherent at every endoscopy, yet EoE remained persistently active.

By age 12, the patient developed persistent abdominal pain, chest pain, and intermittent vomiting. Endoscopy revealed severe EoE, defined by histologic findings of >100 eosinophils/hpf with eosinophilic micro‐abscesses, lamina propria sclerosis, edema, and furrows (EREFS 2), along with a duodenal bulb ulcer with dense eosinophilic infiltrate despite multiple concurrent therapies including dietary elimination, daily PPI, and swallowed topical steroids. PPI was increased to twice‐daily dosing, topical steroids were discontinued, and dupilumab 300 mg every other week for asthma was initiated. Once dupilumab received FDA approval for EoE, dosing was increased to 300 mg weekly. After 5 months of weekly dosing, endoscopy demonstrated partial improvement in esophageal eosinophilia but persistence of gross findings (84 eosinophils/hpf distally; edema and furrows, EREFS 2; I‐SEE 3) with sustained EoG/EoN remission (Table 1). After 9 months, an EST score of 1.000 (reference ≥ 0.530 suggests a high probability of active disease) confirmed persistent esophageal inflammation. Concurrent EoE therapies over the dupilumab period included dietary elimination and PPI. Asthma scores clinically improved with use of dupilumab, but the patient still required daily controller inhaled corticosteroids (budesonide/formoterol and fluticasone propionate) and albuterol as needed.

Despite persistent active EoE, dupilumab was continued for asthma benefit. However, after 3 years on dupilumab, the patient developed chronic arthralgia and arthritis. These symptoms resolved upon cessation of dupilumab and were attributed to a recognized adverse effect thought to result from IL‐4/IL‐13 blockade skewing the immune response toward Th17‐driven immunopathologies such as seronegative arthritis. 10 After a washout period of 13 weeks, tezepelumab 210 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks was started for asthma. Concurrent EoE therapies at the time of initiation and throughout the course included dietary elimination and PPI. Additional asthma therapies (fluticasone propionate, beclomethasone dipropionate, albuterol) were required only sporadically during tezepelumab treatment, and no systemic steroids were used during either biologic course.

Following the fourth dose of tezepelumab, the patient reported full resolution of symptoms of esophageal dysfunction. Endoscopy at 6 months demonstrated histologic remission with <15 eosinophils/hpf, EREFS 0, and I‐SEE 0; gastric and duodenal biopsies demonstrated no significant eosinophilia, consistent with sustained EoG/EoN remission. The patient subsequently liberalized their diet, reintroducing all foods except wheat, which was only tolerated in trace amounts, and has remained asymptomatic with no adverse effects. Asthma has remained controlled with a rare need for rescue inhalers. A future endoscopy is planned to assess durability of response following diet liberalization.

DISCUSSION

EoE, EoG, and EoN represent a spectrum of chronic, antigen‐driven, Type 2 inflammatory conditions with overlapping but incompletely understood mechanisms. TSLP is an epithelial‐derived cytokine that drives Th2 differentiation and production of IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13. 1 By blocking TSLP at its origin, tezepelumab interrupts the entire downstream signaling cascade, representing an important mechanistic distinction from dupilumab, which only blocks IL‐4 and IL‐13 signaling pathways. 4 This more upstream mechanism may explain why TSLP blockade achieved EoE remission where IL‐4/IL‐13 blockade alone did not.

EoG and EoN transcriptomes demonstrate increased expression of IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐13, and IL‐17, suggesting that Type 2 cytokine signaling pathways also play a key role in disease pathogenesis. 3 This supports the biological plausibility of the sustained EoG/EoN remission observed in our patient, a finding not reported in the prior tezepelumab case. Notably, EoG/EoN also remained in remission during dupilumab therapy, a finding relevant to ongoing trials evaluating dupilumab for EoG. Sharlin et al. demonstrated that tezepelumab induced sustained histologic remission and reversed expression of notable genes downstream of TSLP and related to Type 2 inflammation (Table S1). 6 Although transcriptomic analysis was not performed in our patient, the complete clinical, endoscopic, and histologic remission observed suggests reversal of the same pathogenic cascade.

Our case has several limitations. Causality cannot be established from a single case, and the patient remained on dietary elimination and PPI throughout tezepelumab treatment, though these therapies had been maintained for years without achieving remission. Additionally, the absence of EoE‐Histology Scoring System grades limits histologic characterization, and no formal testing was undertaken to assess for alternative causes of duodenal eosinophilia at the time of the initial EoN diagnosis.

CONCLUSION

This case contributes to a growing body of evidence suggesting that tezepelumab may benefit adolescents with highly refractory EoE, including those with extra‐esophageal involvement and significant atopic comorbidities, in whom complete clinical, endoscopic, and histologic remission was achieved despite inadequate response to multiple conventional therapies. Although larger studies are needed to define its role, upstream TSLP blockade may represent a promising emerging strategy for treatment‐resistant EGIDs.
