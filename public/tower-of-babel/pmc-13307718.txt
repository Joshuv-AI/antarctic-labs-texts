Nanotechnology-enhanced CAR-T therapy strategies in cancer, aging, and autoimmune diseases
Authors: Hongjia Li, Jinxin Li, Xiting Liu, Xueju Wei, Xin Zeng, Qiwei Wang, Yingli Han, He Huang, Pengxu Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has emerged as a transformative immunotherapy, which achieves remarkable success in hematological malignancies. However, broader application of CAR-T therapy is hindered by multiple challenges, including limited efficacy in solid tumors, antigen escape, severe toxicities, and complex, costly manufacturing. Recent studies have emerged that nanotechnology offers innovative solutions to overcome barriers. In this review, we summarized how nanotechnology enhances CAR-T therapy across six dimensions: CAR design, transfection, expansion, modification, monitoring, and combination with other therapies. We discussed the applications of various nanomaterials to improve CAR-T cell function, including lipid nanoparticles, nanogels, and nanobodies. Specifically, we recapitulate that nanotechnology facilitates the generation of CAR-T cells in vivo, enables spatiotemporal control of T cells, remodels the immunosuppressive microenvironment in solid tumors, and enables dual-targeting strategies to mitigate antigen escape. Finally, we outlined the clinical application of nanotechnology in hematological malignancies and solid tumors, as well as non-malignant senescence and autoimmune diseases. Collectively, we believe the integration of nanotechnology enhances the safety and efficacy and broadens therapeutic scope, which will open a new era of precision medicine.

Background

Basic of CAR-T cell therapy

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy is a revolutionary breakthrough for cancer therapy. CAR-T cells are genetically modified T cells that express chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) on the cell surface. Unlike conventional T-cell receptors (TCRs), CARs have antibody-derived antigen-binding domains that allow direct recognition of surface antigens without major histocompatibility complex (MHC), so they can target a broader range of tumor antigens [1]. This property also enables CAR-T to overcome tumor immune escape associated with MHC downregulation, which is commonly observed in many malignancies.

CAR-T cells can be either derived from a patient’s own T cells (autologous) or from donor cells (allogeneic) [2]. Following isolation and purification, T cells are genetically engineered to express CARs and infused into patients to eradicate tumors. Once CAR-T cells bind to the antigen, they are activated and act as “living drugs” to attack tumors [3]. Antigen binding induces CAR clustering on the T cell surface and triggers intracellular signaling via the CD3ζ immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs). This process leads to T-cell activation, cytokine production, T-cell proliferation and cytotoxicity. Subsequently, activated CAR-T cells eliminate tumor cells. In general, CAR-T cells are produced via several steps, involving T-cell isolation, genetic engineering, T-cell expansion and reinfusion into the patients (Fig. 1a).

A chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) is composed of four regions: the antigen recognition domain, the extracellular hinge region, the transmembrane domain and the intracellular signaling domain [4]. The antigen recognition domain typically consists of the variable heavy and light regions of a monoclonal antibody, linked together as a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) by a flexible peptide. The hinge region, also known as the spacer, connects the scFv to the outer cell membrane and provides structural flexibility, allowing the CAR to overcome steric hindrance and bind to the target antigen efficiently. The transmembrane domain anchors the CAR to the T cell membrane, and it is important for stability as a whole [4, 5]. Finally, the intracellular signaling domain is generally composed of the activation region CD3ζ and one or more co-stimulatory regions. The specific combination of these signaling regions defines the generation of a CAR-T cell [6].

Brief history of CAR-T therapy

In 1989, Eshhar and his colleagues engineered T cells to recognize antigens in an MHC-independent manner [7]. By fusing an antibody domain (now known as scFv) with the intracellular signaling domain CD3ζ, they further refined the design and created the first-generation CAR-T cells in 1993 [8]. However, these early CAR-T cells, only mediated by CD3ζ, were insufficient to sustain therapeutic response due to limited proliferation, poor cytokine secretion and apoptosis [8, 9].

To overcome these limitations, the second generation of CAR-T cells incorporated a co-stimulatory domain. By combining CD3ζ with other molecules such as CD28 or 4-1BB (CD137), second-generation CARs provided additional activation signals that can enhance T-cell proliferation, persistence and survival. These signals also promoted cytokine secretion, thereby enhancing CAR-T expansion and antitumor activity [10, 11]. The third-generation CARs further combined the advantages of multiple co-stimulatory domains into a single one, exhibiting better effector functions and persistence compared to the second-generation CARs [6].

Fourth-generation CAR-T, known as TRUCKs (T cells redirected for antigen-unrestricted cytokine-initiated killing), combined the expression of the second-generation CARs with inducible transgenes upon CAR-T cell activation, enhancing CAR-T cell activity, cytotoxicity and efficacy [8]. These transgenes commonly express cytokines that can modulate the tumor microenvironment (TME) and enhance antitumor activity. For example, CAR-T cells engineered to secrete interleukin-15 (IL-15) can promote T-cell memory and improve T-cell persistence, which potently rejected CD19 leukemia [12]. IL-18-secreting CAR-T stimulated endogenous immune response, enhanced in vivo expansion and persistence in syngeneic mouse models of both hematological malignancies (HMs) and solid tumors [13]. Moreover, CAR-T cells express IL-7 and C-C motif chemokine ligand 19 (CCL19) can recruit dendritic cells and T cells into tumor tissues, improving T cell infiltration and CAR-T survival within tumors [14, 15].

Besides cytokines, membrane receptors, enzymes, antibodies, and transcriptional factors can also improve the efficacy of CAR-T therapy [16]. For instance, co-expression of dominant-negative transforming growth factor (TGF)-β receptor 2 blocked TGF-β signaling, thereby promoting CAR-T survival and antitumor activity in a TGF-β-rich TME [17]. Caruana et al. engineered CAR-T to produce heparanase, an enzyme that degrades heparan sulfate in the extracellular matrix (ECM) to enhance T-cell infiltration and efficacy in tumors [18]. CAR-T cells have also been engineered to secrete PD-1 antibodies, inhibiting local immune checkpoints and enhancing T-cell tumoricidal activity [19]. Additionally, overexpression of transcription factors like C-JUN has also been explained to increase cytokines and costimulatory molecules, restoring anti-tumor activity of CAR-T cells in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [20].

Currently, the fifth-generation CAR-T cells (known as the next generation CAR-T) aim to address bottlenecks of CAR-T therapy, including antigen escape, T-cell exhaustion, and immunosuppressive TME [21]. These challenges make specificity, safety and persistence very important for CAR-T cells, because attacking normal tissue causes toxicity, overactivated immune responses lead to cytokine release syndrome, while exhausted CAR-T reduces long-term therapeutic efficacy. To overcome these barriers, some engineering strategies have been developed. One promising approach is IL-2Rβ modification in cytoplasmic domains [8]. This strategy has a higher stimulation capacity for T cells by activating the JAK-STAT pathway, which enhances CAR-T proliferation and persistence (Fig. 1b). Other hopeful designs are logic-gated CARs that improve killing specificity, and switchable CAR-T cells that enable controlled activation or inactivation of T cells to reduce toxicity [8, 21]. These strategies enhance CAR-T specificity, safety and persistence to address these challenges.

Up to now, the first generation of CARs has not been under investigation. All the CAR-T cells approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and currently on the market are second-generation, and they have already achieved clinical progress in various diseases such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), neuroblastoma and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [22–24]. The third, fourth and fifth generations of CAR-T cell therapies are still under clinical studies [8].

Real-world advancement of CAR-T therapy

Hematological malignancies (HMs) occupy more than half of CAR-T clinical trials, and CD19 is the most popular target, followed by B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) [25]. Between 2017 and 2025, seven types of anti-CD19 and anti-BCMA CAR-T products have been approved by the FDA [22, 26–31]. These drugs have shown a great objective response rate in patients with CD19-positive B-cell malignancies and multiple myeloma [32–38] (Table 1).

For HMs, a phase 2 global study of anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy in children and young adults showed that the overall remission rate of CD19 + relapsed or refractory B-cell ALL reached 81% within 3 months [22]. Anti-CD19 CARs are also present in other B-cell-derived diseases such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [39]. Apart from antigen CD19, CAR-T cells targeting BCMA also achieved clinical progress in treating multiple myeloma [40, 41]. Beyond CD19, CD33-targeted CAR-T cells have exhibited great potency for the treatment of AML in preclinical models [42].

Although CAR-T cell therapy is more difficult to treat solid tumors than hematological cancers, it has made some positive progress in treating solid tumors [43]. For relapsed or refractory high-risk neuroblastoma in pediatric patients, a phase 1–2 clinical trial using GD2-targeting CAR-T cells reported a 63% overall response rate, and it is safe and feasible [23]. For gastrointestinal cancers in adults, CARs targeting Claudin 18.2 achieved a 38.8% overall response rate. In advanced sarcoma, pediatric patients showed positive outcomes with anti-HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor) CARs. Moreover, in pediatric patients with diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma (DIPG), GD2 CAR-T cells also showed efficacy [43]. Additionally, a single infusion of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) variant 3 CAR-T cells was enough to present dramatic and rapid regression within days for treating recurrent glioblastoma [44].

With positive progress in HMs and solid tumors, CAR-T therapy has also achieved clinical success for other types of disease. In 2021, a clinical case of a 20-year-old woman with severe and refractory systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) showed that CD19 CAR-T cells may lead to rapid remission of refractory SLE [24]. Aside from autoimmune disease, in 2023, researchers also reported progress about a rare type of immune-mediated disease - idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. A male patient of 41 was successfully treated with CD19 CAR-T cell therapy, and this case showed that CAR-T cells were feasible, tolerable and effective to treat idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, though long-term follow-up tests are still needed [45].

Key bottlenecks and underlying mechanisms of CAR-T therapy

Limited efficacy against solid tumors

Currently, CAR-T cells are mostly delivered by intravenous infusion, which is effective for HMs because T cells can readily find and kill cancer cells. However, it exhibits limited efficacy in other cancers, especially in solid tumors, which account for more than 90% of all cancers [43]. To date, no CAR-T product has been approved for solid tumors by the FDA.

Solid tumors pose many challenges to CAR-T therapy. First, a fundamental challenge is the inefficient trafficking and infiltration of CAR-T cells into tumors. Unlike hematological malignancies spreading in circulation, solid tumors are surrounded by physical tumor barriers such as extracellular matrix (ECM), tumor stroma, and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which hinder the penetration and mobility of CAR-T cells [46, 47]. Secondly, the tumor microenvironment (TME) in solid tumors is highly immunosuppressive, hypoxic and fibrotic, containing myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and immune checkpoint molecules. These elements suppress T-cell activation and limit immune response, leading to the dysfunctional and exhausted state of CAR-T cells [46, 48]. Hypoxia and metabolic competition for nutrients like glucose and amino acids in the TME further reduce CAR-T persistence and promote T-cell exhaustion.

Another challenge is tumor antigen heterogeneity. A solid tumor tissue is heterogeneous in composition and usually deploys immune escape mechanisms such as antigen loss [49]. For example, even in a signal breast tumor, HER2 expression can vary significantly across regions and even individual cells [50]. EDFR variant Ⅲ, is expressed in around 30% of glioblastoma cases, but tends to be antigenic loss because tumor cells may downregulate antigen to escape immune surveillance [51, 52]. Thus, CAR-T cells may not recognize and omit some target antigens expressed by the tumors. As a result, these tumor cells would not be eliminated by CAR-T and would drive a recurrence after the CAR-T therapy.

Antigen escape

Although single antigen targeting CAR-T cells can generate a high immune response rate initially, the tumor cells display either partial or complete loss of antigen expression. This phenomenon is also known as antigen escape. The most common underlying mechanism of antigen escape is antigen mutation that disrupts the antigen epitope recognized by CAR-T [53, 54]. This results in antigen downregulation, preventing CAR-T from recognizing. Another underlying mechanism is the selective expansion of pre-existing antigen-negative clones. Under the therapeutic pressure of CAR-T therapy, tumor cells lacking the targeted antigen may gradually become dominant, thereby escaping CAR-T recognition and finally causing disease relapse [21]. In addition, rare but notable underlying mechanisms have also been reported. In 2018, a study reported a rare “epitope masking” case of relapse and resistance in a B-ALL patient treated with CD19-targeting CAR-T, caused by transferring anti-CD19 CAR into leukemic B cells incidentally when manufacturing. Therefore, CAR bound to the CD19 epitope on leukemic B cells and shielded leukemic blasts from recognition and attack [55]. In solid tumors, antigen escape is aggravated by the complex TME, which hinders CAR-T cell infiltration and regulates antigen plasticity, further inducing immune evasion.

Manufacture and costs

The clinical manufacturing process of CAR-T cells is complex, including several steps across different locations geographically, with many technologies and logistics. Every step is demanding in timing, transportation, cold chain and storage to ensure CAR-T therapy efficiency [56]. Besides, the time from T cell harvest and infusion, referred to as “vein-to-vein time”, ranges between 3 and 4 weeks in developed countries. With rapid disease progression, the delay of vein-to-vein time can be daunting for awaiting patients and even causes death before receiving CAR-T treatment [56, 57]. Due to complex manufacturing, demanding management and adequate technology, the cost of CAR-T cell therapy is expensive. For drug products alone, the price reached $475,000 (Kymriah) and $373,000 (Yescarta) [58].

Adverse effects

On-target off-tumor toxicity

On-target off-tumor toxicity occurs when the targeted antigen is not exclusively expressed on tumor cells but is also found in normal tissues. Since CAR-T recognizes antigens in an MHC-independent manner and cannot distinguish between the tumor cells and healthy cells expressing the same antigen, normal tissues may be wrongly targeted by CAR-T. The on-target off-tumor phenomenon is particularly challenging in solid tumors. Compared to hematological malignancies that express specific antigens like CD19 widely and consistently, the antigens of solid tumors are not strictly tumor-specific but are expressed at different levels on normal cells. It adds difficulty in selecting an antigen and preventing ‘on-target off-tumor’ toxicity [47]. For example, high-affinity anti-GD2 CAR-T cells induce fatal encephalitis in a patient with neuroblastoma because GD2 is also expressed on normal tissues [59].

Cytokine release syndrome

Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is a prevalent inflammatory syndrome caused by an extensively activated immune system, with secretion of cytokines and other immunologic proteins after CAR-T cell treatment [60]. Underlying mechanisms of CRS are that after recognizing tumor antigens, CAR-T cells proliferate and secrete cytokines such as IFN-γ, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF). These cytokines further activate macrophages and other immune cells, which secrete other inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-6. IL-6 in CRS binds to its receptors on endothelial cells, resulting in an increase in vessel permeability and promoting cytokine production to exacerbate the CRS [61]. Such cascades generate a systemic inflammatory response and toxicity.

CRS can present with a range of clinical manifestations, from mild flu-like symptoms to organ failure. Patients with mild CRS exhibit fever accompanied by fatigue, diarrhea, headache, rashes, myalgia and arthralgia. Severely, it’s supposed to present hypotension, cardiac dysfunction, respiratory failure, multiorgan system failure and even death [47]. In a meta-analysis of 163 clinical trials involving 6342 patients, the incidence of all-grade CRS was 81.50% in hematologic malignancies and 54.27% in solid tumors [62].

Neurotoxicity

Neurotoxicity can occur in up to 50% of patients treated with CAR-T cell therapy, leading to encephalopathy and in some cases, mortality [63]. Immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity (ICANS) is a well-recognized form associated with CAR-T therapy. Although precise mechanisms are still unclear, the underlying mechanisms are related to an overactivated peripheral immune response. Increased cytokine levels like IL-6 activate endothelial cells of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), leading to BBB dysfunction and subsequent central nervous system inflammation [64]. Clinically, ICANS causes delirium, expressive aphasia, obtundation and seizure [65]. Other forms of neurotoxicity have also been reported. Movement and neurocognitive treatment-emergent toxicity (MNT) has been reported in patients treated with BCMA CAR-T therapy, presenting Parkinsonian features such as bradykinesia, rigidity, hypomimia [63, 66]. Tumor inflammation-associated neurotoxicity (TIAN) is another toxicity arising from localized inflammation at the tumor site, inducing increased intracranial pressure and neuronal dysfunction [63].

Nanotechnology and Nanomaterials

Nanotechnology refers to the use of technology to develop materials, devices, or systems at the nanoscale level (1–100 nm) [67]. From a materials science perspective, such manipulation enables the exploitation of unique properties of materials. When the size of materials decreases to a nanoscale level, their physicochemical properties will change dramatically. High specific surface area endows nanoparticles with a large potential for surface modification and efficient drug loading [68]. Small size and adjustable surface physicochemical properties enable them to penetrate barriers and precisely achieve the target. And different nanomaterials have different physicochemical properties, cargo loading capacity and immunological functions [69, 70]. For example, liposomal co-delivery of oxaliplatin prodrugs with metformin [71], co-loaded liposomes carrying β-elemene and cisplatin [72], and nanoparticle aggregation strategies to prolong drug retention [73], have all demonstrated antitumor efficacy. These help to solve the bottlenecks in CAR-T therapy, such as low delivery efficiency, CRS and high toxicity.

Lipid-based nanomaterials

Liposome

Liposomes are the classic example of lipid-based nanoparticles and the most commonly used drug delivery systems. It consists of a phospholipid and cholesterol bilayer, which entraps an aqueous core [74]. CPX-351 (Vyxeos) is the well-known liposome drug against AML. It is a dual-drug liposomal encapsulation of cytarabine and daunorubicin synergistically in a 5: 1 molar ratio [75]. Compared to conventional free drugs, such a combination drug nanotechnology has better pharmacokinetic properties and provides more significant clinical benefits for AML patients [76, 77]. In addition to CPX-351, there are other marketed liposomal products, including AmBisome, Arikayce, DepoCyte, DepoDur, and Expel [78].

Lipid nanoparticle

All the current lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) approved by the FDA are composed of four lipids, including an ionizable cationic lipid, helper lipids containing 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC), cholesterol, and a polyethylene glycol (PEG)-lipid conjugate [79]. This structure facilitates nanoparticle formation, enhances its stability, enables the encapsulation of nucleic acid or therapeutic agents, helps cellular uptake, and promotes endosomal escape of cargo.

LNP has the potential to enhance mRNA-based CAR-T cell engineering strategies. Billingsley et al. synthesized an ionizable LNP, which delivered CAR mRNA to human T cells ex vivo [80]. This nanoplatform successfully induced CAR expression and exhibited potent activity in killing Nalm-6 ALL cells. Beyond mRNA, LNP has also been reported to deliver plasmid DNA encoding anti-CD19 CAR to engineer T cells in vitro [81]. Notably, the LNP system can deliver CAR-encoding DNA or mRNA in vivo to engineer T cells within the human body [82, 83]. Apart from nucleic acid, LNP also envelops immunoregulatory molecules, including immune agonists, checkpoint inhibitors, and antibodies [84].

Polymeric nanomaterials

Polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs) are widely used as drug or gene carriers for controlled and sustained release, due to their outstanding biocompatibility, structural flexibility and tunable physicochemical properties [85]. Polymers used in CAR-T therapy mainly involve poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), poly (β-amino ester) (PβAE), polyethylene glycol (PEG), and polyethylenimine (PEI).

Cationic polymers can bind to negatively charged nucleotides such as DNA and mRNA, thereby forming particles to protect the genes from enzymatic degradation [86]. Stephan et al. developed T-cell targeting nanoparticles based on PβAE polymers for CAR DNA delivery in leukemia treatment [87]. The nanoparticle was further integrated with polyglutamate (PGA) carrying an anti-CD3 antibody for producing CAR in vivo. Similarly, Stephan et al. also used PβAE to design an mRNA nanocarrier for in situ CAR-T cell engineering, and the nanoparticle surface was modified by PGA-anti-CD8 [88].

Beyond gene delivery, Dong et al. coupled IL-13-targeting polylactide copolymers loading doxorubicin hydrochloride to the CAR-T cell surface for treating glioblastoma [89]. Appel et al. developed a CAR-T cell-loaded PNP hydrogel containing PEG-PLA nanoparticles and IL-15, resulting in tumor elimination after 12 days of injection into mice [90]. Wang et al. reported that PLGA nanoparticles loaded with Iguratimod could trigger MM cell death [91], which provides new ideas for CAR-T therapy for MM. In addition, Gu’s team designed a PLGA porous microneedle patch carrying CAR-T cells and implanted it into the tumor bed or postoperative excision cavity for in vivo penetration-mediated immune cells seeding to suppress solid tumor growth and recurrence [92].

Metal nanomaterials

Metal nanomaterials include gold, silver, iron oxide and other metal-based materials. They exhibit excellent photothermal therapeutic effects, which damage dense ECM and physical barriers in solid tumors, thereby enhancing CAR-T cells infiltration. Ye et al. designed a biocompatible and tumor-targeting nanoplatform (FA-Gd-GERTs@Ibrutinib) that integrates CD19 CAR-T immunotherapy and chemo-photothermal therapy for Non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma treatment [93]. Kwong et al. injected gold nanorods into mice before CAR-T cells were infused [94]. With the photothermal effects induced by gold nanorods, subsequent CAR-T cells can more easily infiltrate into tumor tissues. Zhao et al. synthesized a HA@Cu2-xS-PEG nanoenzyme that induced ECM destruction, and combined it with CAR-T cells to treat non-small cell lung cancer [95].

Metal nanoparticles with magnetic properties can be remotely controlled by external magnetic fields [96]. Because of such special properties, they are easy to separate under the action of a magnetic field, facilitating the separation of target biological products. Also, since it can aggregate rapidly in a magnetic field, it provides the possibility for targeted drug delivery [97]. Zhang et al. synthesized iron oxide nanoparticles for noninvasive monitoring of CAR-T infiltration and persistence in glioblastoma via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [98].

Strategies for nanotechnology-enhanced CAR-T therapy in hematological malignancies

Over the past few decades, nanotechnology has developed rapidly and brought significant benefits for CAR-T cell therapy [99, 100]. With its capacity to manipulate cells and molecules at the nanometer scale, nanotechnology has the potential to tackle conventional limitations and enhance CAR-T cell therapy [101]. Nanotechnology can enhance the design, transfection, expansion, modification, and monitoring of CAR-T cells, and improve the feasibility and practicality of integrating CAR-T therapy with other therapeutic approaches (such as immune checkpoint blockade and photothermal therapy). Through these six ways, nanotechnology enhances CAR-T cells’ efficacy and addresses challenges in treating tumors and diseases (Fig. 2).

Nanoparticle-based cytokine-secreting-enhanced CAR-T Cell

Cytokines are secreted when T cells recognize and kill tumor cells, which play a vital role in sustaining T cell activation, proliferation and persistence. However, in clinical settings, there are several challenges for CAR-T therapy in HMs, including insufficient cytokine production, T-cell exhaustion and cytokine-related adverse effects [101, 102]. To address these challenges, nanotechnology can enhance cytokine secretion in a controllable and continuous manner without intensifying systemic toxicity.

Nanomaterials, with one or more external dimensions ranging from 1 to 100 nm, have been approved for cancer treatment and shown improved outcomes [100]. It can provide biochemical signals or physical cues to guide cellular responses. Moreover, nanomaterials can be engineered into nanoparticles (NPs) [103]. They can boost drug stability, improve drug accumulation at tumor sites and enhance permeability and retention.

To avoid CRS and systemic toxicity caused by excessive production of cytokines, nanoparticles backpack cytokines onto CAR-T cells, which accumulate cytokines around tumor cells. IL-15 superagonist complex (IL-15Sa) nanogel, a redox-responsive backpacking that has entered clinical trials for lymphomas and solid tumors [104]. When the CAR-T surface reduction potential increased upon recognizing the antigen, IL-15Sa was released from the backpack due to the cleavage of crosslinking disulfide bonds. As a result, IL-15Sa induced a 16-fold CAR-T cell expansion and was administered at least 8-fold higher doses of cytokine without toxicity.

In addition to nanogel, Luo et al. developed nanoparticles loaded with IL-12 [105]. IL-12 was released in response to an increase in mercaptan groups on T cells upon tumor antigen stimulation. The release of IL-12 significantly promoted chemokine secretion, recruiting and amplifying CAR-T cells. Compared with systemic injection, nanoparticle-modified CAR-T cells showed a higher safe dose with limited systemic toxicity [100].

Although nanoparticles enhance cytokine release in a controlled way to reduce systemic toxicity, long-term safety requires further evaluation. NPs may exhibit toxicity as aggregates in non-targeted tissues, triggering chronic inflammation or autoimmune response [106]. Moreover, the ‘backpacking’ strategy depends on the changes in T cell surface reduction potential. However, this response may be unstable because the surface redox status of T cells relies on the local microenvironment [107]. Hence, this response may be unstable in the heterogeneous tumor microenvironment, leading to insufficient cytokine release.

Nanobodies-mediated antigen recognition

Nanobodies are the antigen-specific binding domain derived from the variable domains of camelid heavy chain antibodies, also known as VHH [108]. Nanobodies have diverse outstanding features for CAR-T therapy. Compared to conventional monoclonal antibodies (150 kDa) and scFv (28 kDa), nanobodies are significantly smaller (15 kDa). Such compact and small size induces deeper tissue penetration and enhances accessibility to tumor-associated antigens in TME, thereby enhancing CAR-T cell activation and effector functions. Their small size also enables flexible engineering of multi-specific nanobody-based CARs, which could overcome antigen heterogeneity and tumor escape to enhance antigen recognition by CAR-T. Since the high degree of homology between camelid VHH and human VH domains, nanobodies are relatively low in immunogenicity, reducing the risk of immune rejection when incorporated into CAR constructs. Moreover, since nanobodies can be easily expressed in microorganisms like bacteria or yeasts and selected from libraries, they can be produced on a large scale and at lower manufacturing costs [109].

Recent studies have shown the advantages of nanobody-mediated antigen recognition. For example, nanobody-based CD70 CAR-T cells exhibited strong cytotoxicity and cytokine production against AML cells, indicating that the high stability and compact structure of VHH support efficient CAR expression and T-cell activation [110]. In 2025, nanoCAR targeting CD7 has been reported to show a promising clinical response in patients with AML, achieving 70% complete remission rate in clinical trials [111]. This suggests that high binding affinity and stability of VHH improve CAR-T proliferation and therapeutic effects.

Nanotechnology-based synthetic biology for CAR-T cell reprogramming

Synthetic biology is a developing discipline that generates biological systems with novel functions. It focuses on the design, construction and assembly of circuitries that comprise synthetic biological components and/or naturally occurring biological parts [112]. When combined with nanotechnology, these systems enable external control and functional reprogramming, constructing a safer, intelligent and efficient CAR-T cell.

Nanotechnology-enabled spatiotemporal control of CAR-T cells

To minimize the toxicities of CAR-T cells and achieve more precise immune regulation, a favorable method is to restrict CAR-T cells’ activation spatiotemporally by synthetic switches. The switches are designed to respond to remote regulation at a specific time with exogenous stimulations, such as light, sound and small molecules [113]. Not acting as carriers of gene editing, nanomaterials can also be one of the components of a synthetic switch. Based on previous light-switchable CAR-T (LiCAR-T), Nguyen and his coworkers optimized the optogenetic design to address several issues, including poor cell membrane targeting, undesired nuclear accumulation, and activation in the dark [114]. They combined LiCAR-T with surgically removable upconversion nanoplates (UCNPs), which enhanced under near infrared (NIR)-to-blue luminescence. This nano-optogenetic switch enabled inducible activation of LiCAR-T cells upon stimulation with NIR light and spatiotemporal control of CAR-T cell-mediated toxicity in both hematological malignancies and solid tumors [115].

Nanobody-based dual-targeting CAR-T cells

Antigen escape is a major source of relapse following CAR-T cell therapy. It occurs when tumor cells escape immune detection by altering or losing target antigens. In hematological malignancies, approximately half of the patients with ALL relapse within the first year, with 20%-60% of these cases being antigen-negative [116]. To overcome antigen escape, dual-targeting CAR-T cells, which recognize two different target antigens, broaden the specificity of CAR. They are usually incorporated by two antigen recognition domains in tandem. Dual-targeting CARs can reduce the risk of antigen-negative relapses, such as the bispecific CAR-T cells targeting BCMA/CD19, BCMA/CD24 in relapsed and refractory multiple myeloma [117, 118], dual CD19/CD22 or CD19/CD20 CARs for B cell lymphoma and leukemia [119–123].

To facilitate dual-targeting CAR-T cells, the scFv of bispecific CAR is replaced by two nanobodies to form a nanobody-based CAR. Nanobodies have optimal biophysical and biochemical properties, including stability, solubility, low immunogenicity, thermal tolerance, affinity, and proteolytic resistance [109]. According to these outstanding properties, De Munter et al. developed nanobody-based dual-specific CAR-T cells, targeting CD20 and HER2 [124]. The results demonstrated that in Jurkat cell lines, the bispecific CD20/HER2 nanobody-based CAR elicited robust T cell activation, cytokine secretion and cytotoxicity. In 2023, researchers further constructed nanobody-derived dual CAR-T cells targeting CD30 and CD5 for T cell lymphoma treatment [125]. Consequently, nanobody-based bispecific CAR-T cells significantly enhanced in vivo anti-tumor efficacy in lymphoma compared with bispecific scFv-derived CAR-T cells.

Nanotechnology-based combination strategies for CAR-T therapy

Nanotechnology provides a wide platform to integrate CAR-T cells with other existing therapies, such as immune checkpoint blockade, vaccines, small molecules, antibodies, virus, chemotherapy and radiotherapy, thereby broadening therapeutic scope and boosting CAR-T efficacy [126] (Table 2). Through targeted delivery, improved pharmacokinetics and controlled agents release, nanomaterials increase the local concentration of agents within the TME, enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of combination strategies for CAR-T.

Integration of Nanotechnology with Other Therapies to Enhance CAR-T Cell Therapy

Nanoparticle-mediated immune checkpoint blockade

Immune checkpoint signaling serves as a regulatory strategy of balancing immune responses by downregulating immune activities. However, checkpoint ligands derived from cancer cells mediate immune evasion via exhausting effector cells [127]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors, block immunosuppressive signals and restore the anti-tumor activity of T cells. Combined with ICIs, CAR-T cells can therefore enhance durability and efficacy within TME [126]. Nanomaterials can facilitate the delivery of ICIs in CAR-T cell therapy. Liposomes are widely studied nanomaterials in tumor immunotherapy. Encapsulating ICIs like PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 in liposomes increases their local concentration within the TME. Liposome delivery can prolong systemic circulation, protect therapeutic agents from degradation and enable sustained drug release. These underlying mechanisms enable the localized ICI enrichment which further enhances the efficacy of integrated CAR-T therapy and reduces systemic toxicity [128].

Apart from liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs) can also deliver ICIs. Compared with lipid nanocarriers, PNPs have unmatched adjustable physicochemical properties like size, shape, structure, charge and surface functionality, therefore, they can more flexibly control drug loading and release. Furthermore, PNPs can navigate various biological barriers in the body to precisely target specific sites [129]. These properties allow PNPs to enhance drug retention at tumor sites, thereby facilitating local enrichment. For example, an anti-PD-L1 antibody-conjugated PNP based on polyethylene glycol-poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid), known as PEG-PLGA, was developed to improve immune checkpoint blockade therapy [130]. In this study, the F(ab) fragment of an anti-PD-L1 antibody was attached to a PEG-PLGA polymer to form a nanoparticle. Results showed that the delivery via nanoparticles reduced renal excretion and mononuclear phagocyte uptake, which protected the antibody from immune clearance and allowed it to be retained in the circulation longer. The extended circulation time promoted anti-PD-L1 antibody accumulation, enhancing immune activation. Such PNP-mediated enrichment of ICIs may also improve CAR-T efficacy, because blocking PD-1/PD-L1 axis has the potential to restore CAR-T cytotoxicity and improve their persistence.

However, inappropriate immune checkpoint blockade can lead to immune-related adverse events (irAEs). The immune system depends on a complex system of checks and balances. It effectively responds to pathogens while preserving tolerance to non-tumor self and some commensal organisms [131]. Disturbance of the homeostatic balance by ICIs can contribute to a loss of self-tolerance and incorrect non-tumor self-directed irAEs.

Nanomaterial-based vaccines

Vaccines optimize immune response by disposing of tumor-associated epitopes and inducing adaptive immune systems [127]. The combination of vaccines and CAR-T cells can overcome some limitations, like T cell exhaustion in CAR-T therapy. In this setting, vaccines boost CAR-T cells’ activation, proliferation and persistence [126]. Moreover, employing nanomaterials as a nano-vaccine brings enhanced efficacy in this combination therapy. In 2020, Shah et al. reported a hydrogel-based vaccine for AML with the capability to produce a long-lasting immune response [132]. This biomaterial-based vaccine used MA-alginate hydrogel as the scaffold and loaded with AML-associated antigen peptide WT1126-134 and immune adjuvants in the hydrogel. Nano-vaccines also promote the recognition of antigens. Researchers have developed a vaccine consisting of porous polylactic acid microspheres carrying PD-1 antibodies and leukemia-associated antigen polypeptide pE [133]. This vaccine stimulated the antigen-presenting cells (APCs) response and recruited more APCs, enhancing T cell proliferation in lymph nodes and increasing the cytotoxicity to leukemia cells.

Nanotechnology-based chemotherapy

Chemotherapy amplifies the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy, and clinical research has reported that the combination is more efficient than monotherapy. Chemotherapy can decrease the activity of immunosuppressive cells such as regulatory T cells (Tregs) and MDSCs, as well as immunosuppressive factors like TGF-β [127]. In this case, the use of CAR-T cells in conjunction with chemotherapy inhibits autoimmunity and immunosuppressive TME to enhance CAR-T cells’ persistence, and nanotechnology can further facilitate antigen targeting for this combination. Combining chemo-photothermal therapy with CAR-T therapy, Shi et al. created a nanoplatform based on gadolinium-loaded gap-enhanced Raman tags (Gd-GERTs) for multimodal imaging [93]. The photothermal effect of the nanoprobe promoted the angiogenesis of lymphoma tissue, loosened compact tissue, destroyed the extracellular matrix, stimulated chemokine secretion, and enhanced infiltration of non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma. Moreover, it boosted therapeutic efficacy without impairing CD19 CAR-T cell activity. This study highlighted that the multifunctional nanomaterials with targeted multimodal imaging abilities provided an efficient approach to cancer treatments.

Strategies for Nanotechnology-enhanced CAR-T therapy in Solid Tumors

Nanomaterial-mediated destruction of the physical ECM barrier

The migration of CAR-T cells and access to tumors are critical for antitumor activity. However, fiber-rich extracellular matrix (ECM) is the key barrier for T cell infiltration [134], severely preventing migration and killing. In solid tumors, CAFs mediate the ECM remodeling, resulting in stiff ECM deposition that inhibits T-cell penetration [135]. Malignant cells induce sustained angiogenesis via the overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factors and fibroblast growth factors, leading to dysfunctional vascular networks. The physical barrier and abnormal vascular structure build an antigen-negative shelter for escaping immune detection [136]. Moreover, ECM crosslinking increases interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), restricts oxygen diffusion, and forms hypoxic TME [135]. To enhance T cell penetration in solid tumors, nanomaterials are designed to disrupt this barrier, either by physically loosening the ECM structure or by degrading components via enzymes.

Photothermal nanoparticle-mediated ECM disruption

One study used PLGA nanoparticles loaded with the photothermal agent indocyanine green (ICG) [137]. In a mouse model of melanoma, the nanoparticles were injected intratumorally, and the near-infrared (NIR) light effectively generated heat to ‘burn’ tumor cells. It allowed direct tumor cell killing, decreased interstitial fluid pressure, and partial ECM disruption before injecting CAR-T cells [138]. Furthermore, this treatment promoted CAR-T cells’ penetration into the solid tumor tissue and enhanced antitumor activity compared with CAR-T cells alone. Another example utilized ICG nanoparticles biorthogonally conjugated with CAR-T cells to trigger photothermal effects [139]. Consequently, the mild photothermal intervention reduced IFP, destroyed the ECM, loosened compact tissues, and expanded blood vessels, thereby facilitating CAR-T infiltration. This method induced an immune-favorable TME for recruitment and infiltration of nanoengineered CAR-T cells biohybrids. In a recent study, Liu et al. Developed chromium-based nanocomposites to facilitate infiltration [140]. Under NIR light, nanocomposites generated mild hyperthermia, destroying the TME. Subsequently, the administration of B7-H3 CAR-T cells to mice with hepatoma or breast tumors inhibited tumor growth significantly.

Although photothermal therapy can convert light energy into heat to degrade physical barriers and eliminate tumor cells [141–143], inappropriate temperature control may lead to adverse effects. Due to the difficulty in blocking heat diffusion, the thermal effect may damage normal tissues and induce an inflammatory response [144]. Furthermore, the initial high temperature can also induce a mass of heat shock protein (HSP) expression [145]. HSP can boost cancer cells’ proliferation and increase the heat resistance of tumors, decreasing photothermal therapy efficacy. Apart from that, the long-term retention and clearance efficiency of photothermal agents are of great concern. They may agglomerate during blood circulation, leading to embolism and endangering life [146]. Therefore, the design of nanoparticles must consider the safety during blood transport and the metabolic process in the kidneys and liver to ensure effective accumulation at tumor sites.

Nanogel-mediated destruction of ECM

Instead of nanomaterial-based photothermal therapy, some methods apply the nanomaterials for antifibrotic drug delivery to remove the fibrotic ECM. A recent study has reported a collagenase nanogel backpack for pancreatic cancer treatment [147]. Among solid tumors, pancreatic cancer presents remarkable desmoplastic and highly fibrotic ECM, which accounts for the majority of the tumor mass [148, 149]. In this study, nanogels are constructed by cross-linking collagenase with CXCR4 antagonist modification. Via receptor-ligand interaction, the nanogels bound CAR-T cells to form a delivery system. This delivery system could overcome the collagen-rich ECM and alleviate the physical isolation of CAR-T in the dense stroma network; therefore, the infiltration rate was approximately 5-fold higher compared with the injection of free CAR-T cells [147]. This treatment resulted in a more widespread CAR-T cell penetration throughout the tumor tissue, facilitated their migration through the ECM and enhanced the outcome of CAR-T therapy in pancreatic cancer.

Nanotechnology-mediated immunosuppressive TME remodeling

Solid tumors typically have an immunosuppressive TME, which leads to the exhaustion or even death of CAR-T cells [150]. The therapeutic benefit or failure of CAR-T cells greatly relies on the TME. TME not only consists of heterogeneous tumor cells but also contains various cell types (including immune cells, fibroblasts, inflammatory cells, endothelial cells and lymphocytes) and noncellular components (ECM, vasculature and chemokines) [135, 151]. Beyond acting as a physical barrier, ECM also regulates immune responses and metabolism in the TME. Nanotechnology can remodel and reserve immunosuppressive TME to enhance CAR-T cell therapy. It delivers active ingredients directly to the TME, or blocks immunosuppressive cues and facilitates immunostimulation by loading onto T cell surfaces [103]. In addition, nanomedicine can enhance antitumor immunity by boosting immunogenic cell death (ICD), which promotes antigen release and dendritic cell activation, thereby enhancing downstream T-cell responses [152].

Nanoparticle-mediated immunomodulation

Nanoparticles could shift TME from an immunosuppressive state to a stimulatory phenotype by directly delivering active ingredients. For example, one study used lipid nanoparticles containing both PI3K inhibitor (PI-3065) and T-cell stimulator (7DW8-5), which reduced the concentration of suppressor cells (TAMs, MDSCs and Tregs) and boosted CAR-T cells activity in the TME [153]. In the mouse model of breast cancer, this treatment created a two-week window for CAR-T cells to treat solid tumors effectively. Zhu et al. designed an anti-PD-L1 nanovesicle loaded with the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonist cGAMP to remodel the TME for lung solid tumors [154]. Following the delivery, the nanovesicles rapidly accumulated in the lung tumors and delivered STING agonist to PD-L1-overexpressing cells. It triggered the STING signaling pathway and induced interferon responses, which diminished immunosuppressive cell populations in the TME and promoted CAR-T cell penetration. Moreover, the anti-PD-L1 scFv on the nanovesicle blocked PD-L1 upregulation, preventing CAR-T cell exhaustion. This treatment inhibited tumor growth and recurrence in both the orthotopic lung cancer and lung metastasis models.

Additionally, CAR-T cells depend on chemokine receptors to recognize tumor-derived chemokines and migrate toward tumor tissues. But tumor cells commonly secrete inadequate chemokines, which reduces CAR-T cell targeting efficiency [155]. However, in this study [140], Cr3+ ions, the major product of nanocomposites degradation, boosted chemokine expression of CXCL13 and CCL3. This chemokine upregulation promoted the tertiary lymphoid structures formation in solid tumor tissues, thereby enhancing CAR-T cell penetration into tumors.

Nanozyme-mediated metabolic and hypoxia remodeling

Nanozyme is a type of nanomaterial with intrinsic enzyme-like activities at the nanoscale level (1–100 nm) [156]. Nanozymes display not only catalytic activity but also the physical and chemical properties of nanomaterials. Compared with natural enzymes (e.g., glucose oxidase and sialidase), nanozymes are advantageous in high stability, small size, easy modification, low cost and better tumor penetration [156].

Hence, utilizing tumor-targeting nanozymes to remodel TME can enhance the immune activation of CAR-T cells. For instance, a tumor-specific hyaluronic acid (HA)@Cu2-xS-PEG (PHCN) nanozyme system was designed for non-small cell lung cancer [95]. Researchers observed that PHCN triggered reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in the TME and generated photothermal-nanocatalytic synergistic effects. As a result, it destroyed tumor ECM, killed tumor cells, which induced the release of antigens from tumor cells. These changes altered immune-hostile TME, enhancing the infiltration and effector function of B7-H3 CAR-T cells. To address hypoxia of TME [157], Xu and coworkers recently developed a nanozyme that functions as a catalase to convert H2O2 to O2 [158]. It ameliorated hypoxia in the TME and normalized tumor vessels, promoting the antitumor activity of GPC3-CAR-T cells in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).

Nanoparticles-CAR-T conjugates for local immunomodulation

Nanotechnology modifies CAR-T cells with enhanced capability of blocking immunosuppressive molecules and activation. In a mouse model of ovarian cancer, CD19 CAR-T cells were modified with multilamellar liposomal vesicles on their surfaces [159]. In the TME, the A2a adenosine receptor (A2aR) pathway is triggered by high concentrations of the immunosuppressive molecule adenosine, which has been reported to inhibit T cell proliferation and IFNγ secretion [160]. Loaded with an A2aR antagonist (SCH-58261), the multilamellar liposomal vesicles blocked the binding between adenosine and the A2a receptor on the T cell surface. This therapeutic strategy increased the accumulation of T cells in tumor sites and successfully reversed the immunosuppressive TME, promoting the persistence and functionality of CAR-T cells [159].

To address immunosuppression in a hypoxic environment, researchers designed an injectable immune-microchip (i-G/MC) porous scaffold with oxygen reservoirs to modify CAR-T cells [161]. Once contacting tumors, the outer layer degraded rapidly, and oxygen carriers (Hemo) were released to disrupt the TME. Next, CAR-T cells migrated from IL-15-loaded i-G/MCs into the tumor stroma. HEMOXCell (Hemo), a marine extracellular hemoglobin, has a strong oxygen storage capacity and binds up to 156 oxygen molecules (per Hemo molecule) [162], and cytokine IL-15 boosts T-cell proliferation and persistence [163]. Synergistic effects of the i-G/MC system overcame hypoxia and promoted CAR-T cell cytotoxicity in ovarian cancer models.

Nanotechnology for overcoming tumor antigen loss

Tumor cells may escape T-cell killing by decreasing or eliminating the expression of MHC Ⅰ/antigens, co-stimulatory molecules, and other checkpoint markers on their surface [100]. To overcome low therapeutic efficiency or even resistance caused by antigen escape, nanotechnology promotes CAR-T cell therapy through bispecific targeting and antigen synthesis.

Nanobody-based bispecific targeting

Tumor cells hide a single target to escape recognition [164], and are usually difficult to downregulate the expression of two antigens simultaneously. In this case, dual-targeting CAR-T cells provide a promising opportunity to overcome antigen escape and enhance the precision of recognition. With small sizes and outstanding biochemical properties, nanobodies offer bispecific CAR-T cells a higher efficacy against solid tumors.

Recently, Li et al. have developed an MSLN and MUC1 dual-targeting CAR-T armed with secreting anti-PD1 nanobodies [165]. In this study, the anti-MSLN nanobody was engineered with a pH-dependent property, endowing CAR-T cells with cytotoxicity only at acidic pH in the TME. Targeting MUC1 nanobody was designed based on antigen-density dependence and the anti-PD1 nanobodies were secreted to alleviate immunosuppressive TME. This treatment showed remarkable clinical efficacy: two ovarian cancer patients both achieved partial remission in the first month without any adverse effects, and one eventually achieved complete remission [165].

Nanoparticle delivery of synthetic antigen

In solid tumors, antigens are highly heterogeneous, with low expression, and can be expressed in normal healthy tissues, which contributes to ‘off-tumor’ cytotoxicity and poor efficacy [46, 47]. To overcome these restrictions, Kwong and colleagues have synthesized antigens (syntAgs) to sensitize solid tumors for CAR-T cells’ recognition and elimination by lipid nanoparticle delivery [166]. The synthetic antigens are only expressed on tumor cells and not on any healthy tissues. They are orthogonal to endogenous proteins for specific recognition by CAR-T cells, thereby avoiding off-tumor targeting. In a HER2+/- heterogeneous mouse model of breast cancer, the results demonstrate that this method has the potential to alleviate antigen escape in solid tumors with heterogeneous expression of antigens. However, the immunogenicity of systAgs needs to be considered. After all, they are foreign antigens and may trigger immune responses against non-native epitopes.

Nanotechnology-enhanced CAR-T-cell killing and expansion

With high surface area and customizable functionalization, nanoparticles have great advantages in delivery. They can promote the pharmacokinetics of cargos, overcome barriers in solid tumors and enable the co-delivery of multiple active ingredients in a single formulation [103]. CAR-T cells can be co-delivered to tumors with a nanoparticle backpack or nanoparticle encapsulation.

Nanoparticle backpack

Different from the separate delivery of nanoparticles and T cells, nanoparticles can be conjugated to the CAR-T cell surface, known as ‘backpacking’. When T cells are activated, their redox state changes, leading to the high-level expression of free thiol groups on the cell surface [167]. Based on this phenomenon, nanoparticles are equipped with thiol-reactive groups and are engineered as backpacks attaching to T cells. Upon antigen recognition, backpacking nanoparticles release drugs by responding to an increased reduction potential of the T cell surface [104]. Furthermore, the secretion of perforin during T cell-mediated cell killing triggers the rupture of nanoparticles’ lipid layers, facilitating drug release [168].

Backpacking nanomaterials can encapsulate cytokines to enhance CAR-T cells in vivo. Tang et al. developed a nanogel loaded with IL-15Sa via disulfide bond cross-linking [104]. In a mouse model of melanoma, this CAR-T cell-nanogel co-delivery system increased the efficiency of T-cell expansion by 16-fold, compared with the direct administration of free IL-15Sa. Barberio et al. combined cytokine IL-12 with nanoliposomes in a layer-by-layer form, modifying a layer of poly-L-arginine, a layer of poly-L-glutamate and a layer of hyaluronic acid [169]. Conjugated to T cells, these nanoparticles release IL-12 steadily and show efficacy in both ovarian and colorectal tumors. In a recent study, researchers have developed acid-sensitive responsive CaMnCO3 (CMC) nanoparticles loaded with IL-21 [170]. CMC was designed to simultaneously mitigate acidosis and hypoxia in the TME. Conjugated on CAR-T cell surfaces, CMC nanoparticles remodeled the immunosuppressive TME while enabling sustained release of IL-21. This treatment significantly enhances the infiltration and anti-tumor activity of CAR-T cells.

In addition to cytokines, nanoparticles also envelop small therapeutic molecules to enhance CAR-T cell therapy. For example, Siriwon et al. designed nanoparticles loaded with A2aR antagonist (SCH-58261) to be backpacked by CD19 CAR-T cells [159]. It blocked the binding between adenosine and the A2a receptor on the T cell surface and enhanced CAR-T cells’ proliferation and persistence. Hao et al. backpacked avasimibe-loaded nanoparticles on CAR-T cell surface via anchor-click techniques [171]. Avasimibe, an inhibitor of ACAT1 (a key cholesterol esterification enzyme), increases cholesterol concentrations on cell membranes, which clusters T-cell receptor and enhances effector T-cells activation [172]. In mouse models of melanoma and glioblastoma, nanoparticles locally released avasimibe and exhibited superior antitumor efficacy.

To block immune checkpoints, Chen et al. developed a method to safely anchor anti-PD1-formed nanogels on the CAR-T cell surface [173]. Researchers also designed an innovative microneedle on T cells for the sustained delivery of anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA4 antibodies [174]. The microneedle consisted of hyaluronic acid and pH-sensitive nanoparticles. Antibodies were encapsulated into the nanoparticles and released via the self-dissociation of the nanoparticles in acidic environments.

Nanogel-mediated co-delivery

Instead of backpacking CAR-T cells, nanoparticles can encapsulate CAR-T cells to form a co-delivery system. Hu and coworkers encapsulated CAR-T cells and anti-PD-L1 blocking antibodies in hydrogels. Furthermore, cytokine IL-15 was also encapsulated in nanoparticles loaded in the hydrogels [175]. This co-delivery system blocked the PD-1/PD-L1 immunosuppressive pathway in the melanoma TME and maintained the activity and proliferation of CAR-T cells.

Some technologies employed in co-delivery provide a great opportunity to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Grosskopf et al. engineered injectable PNP hydrogels loaded with CAR-T cells and stimulatory cytokines to improve treatment of solid tumors [90]. The unique architecture of this co-delivery system restricted passive diffusion of entrapped cytokines, avoiding adverse effects such as CRS. It also endowed active motility of entrapped cells to enable CAR-T cells with long-term retention, viability, and activation. Besides anchor-click technology, researchers designed an injectable photocurable Gelatin Methacryloyl hydrogel loaded with CAR-T cells [176]. This method enhanced CAR-T cells’ antitumor effect and extended survival in a mouse model of melanoma, compared with intravenous or local injection of CAR-T cells solution (Fig. 3) (Table 3).

As gene delivery carriers, nanoparticles have outstanding properties, including low toxicity, ease of chemical modification, precise T-cell targeting and high gene delivery efficiency. These properties make them suitable for delivering nucleic acids such as mRNA or plasmid DNA encoding CARs. After cellular uptake, the nanoparticles release nucleic acid cargo into the cytoplasm, where mRNA can be directly translated into CAR proteins and plasmid DNA can be transported into the nucleus for transcription. In this case, T cells express CARs and become CAR-T cells, thereby recognizing and killing tumors. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) and polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs) are the commonly used nanomaterials for in vivo CAR-T cell generation currently [178]. This delivery system has no integration in the host genome and greatly simplifies the production of CAR-T cells ex vivo, lowering the cost [179].

In vivo CAR-T in cancer

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) and polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs) are the two most extensively studied nanoplatforms for in vivo CAR-T generation. Although they can both deliver nucleic acids to T cells, they are different in structure and delivery mechanisms [129, 180]. LNPs are lipid-based nanoparticles, which mimic the components of cell membranes and are suitable to encapsulate nucleic acids [79]. In contrast, PNPs are composed of polymers such as PLGA and PβAE. Cationic polymers can aggregate negatively charged nucleic acids to form complexes, which are more flexible in structure [181]. Besides, the delivery mechanisms and release behavior are also different between these two nanoparticles. The ionizable lipids become protonated in the acidic endosomal environment, which disrupts the endosomal membrane and promotes nucleic acids release into the cytoplasm [182, 183]. Therefore, the LNP system is more suitable for mRNA delivery. However, the core advantage of PNPs is that they can achieve a controlled and sustained cargo release, extending the therapeutic window. Furthermore, PNP has greater potential for surface modification than LNP. Surface of PNP can be easily conjugated with targeting ligands, such as anti-CD3 or anti-CD8 antibodies, to reach specific targeting of in vivo CAR-T cells [87, 88].

LNPs are nanoscale particles, characterized by a lipid core and an outer shell composed of phospholipids or other lipid molecules [180]. The structure provides LNPs with excellent drug loading ability and physical stability. For example, in 2022, Zhou et al. constructed an LNP system modified by a CD3 antibody, loading with the plasmid containing a combination gene of IL-6 short hairpin RNA and CD19 CAR [82]. After injection of the LNPs, CAR-T cells were directly produced in vivo and killed leukemia tumor cells within 90 days, which significantly prolonged the survival time of model mice. Moreover, researchers co-delivered multiplex RNAs (CD19 CAR mRNA, Cas9 mRNA and sgRNA targeting PD-1) into T cells via LNPs and the engineered CAR-T cells with PD-1 knockout exhibited enhanced cell viability and anti-tumor activity [83]. Additionally, Huo et al. have extended the applications of in vivo CAR-T beyond cancer treatment [184]. In this study, an LNP-mRNA delivery system induced the generation of antifibrotic CAR-T cells that selectively eliminated activated fibroblasts, restored ECM homeostasis, and promoted tissue regeneration in pulmonary fibrosis models.

PNPs are nanoscale particles made from natural or synthetic polymers [180]. PNPs with a positive charge can form complexes with negative nucleic acids. Cationic surface also enhances cellular uptake by binding to the negatively charged cell membrane [178]. In 2017, Stephan et al. developed injectable in vivo CAR-T cell-generated cationic polymers for hematological tumors. For example, they designed targeted poly (β-amino ester) (PβAE) nanocarriers with anti-CD3 f(ab’)2 on their surface to promote endocytosis of T cells [87]. PβAE, a type of PNPs, has low cytotoxicity, low immunogenicity, ease of modification, biodegradability and can be manufactured on a large scale under good manufacturing practice (GMP) guidelines [185]. Additionally, for the translocation of DNA cargo into the nucleus, microtubule-associated sequences (MTAS) and nuclear localization signal (NLS) were enveloped in the PNPs [87]. The results confirmed the capability of the nanocarriers to reconstruct cells and reduce leukemia progression in vivo.

Although most current research on in vivo CAR-T cell generation for cancer treatment focuses on hematological malignancies, it also has great potential for treating solid tumors. For instance, Parayath et al. developed an injectable CD8-targeted PNP based on PβAE polymer that delivers CAR mRNA [186]. In vivo, these nanocarriers transiently express the CAR mRNA in circulating T cells, enabling the T cells to recognize antigens in mice with prostate cancer or hepatitis B-induced hepatocellular carcinoma. Results indicated that repeated infusions of these PNPs induced sufficient T cells to express specific CARs, leading to disease regression. Recently, Li et al. constructed a CD3-targeted LNP system containing mRNA encoding both a CAR and IL-7, which successfully engineered T cells to co-express CAR and IL-7 [187]. These in vivo-generated CAR-T cells eliminated tyrosinase-related protein 1 (TRP1)-expressing melanoma cells in mouse models, suggesting that cytokine co-expression can also be applied to in vivo CAR-T therapy. Even though these approaches are still at the preclinical stage, they highlight the potential of nanomaterial-mediated in vivo CAR-T for solid tumor treatment.

In vivo CAR-T in aging

Aging and Senescence

With aging, the combination of increasing tissue damage and declining immune function leads to senescent cells in the human body. These senescent cells are in a stable cell cycle arrest, which develops in response to stress or damage [188]. They are characterized by a lack of proliferation and upregulation of anti-apoptotic pathways. Moreover, senescent cells secrete a plethora of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases, creating a harmful inflammatory environment [189].

Nanotechnology offers an opportunity to eliminate senescent cells by in vivo CAR-T generation. Through delivering nucleic acid cargoes encoding CARs to T cells via nanoparticles, T cells can be reprogrammed in vivo to express CARs that recognize senescence-associated antigens. These in vivo-produced CAR-T cells subsequently eliminate senescent cells and slow the aging-related pathological processes.

In vivo CAR-T against aging

In 2020, Amor et al. reported the upregulation of senescence-associated protein urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) on senescent cells [190]. In 2024, the same team further utilized uPAR targeting CAR-T cells in aged tissues [191]. This study demonstrated that uPAR-positive senescent cells accumulated during the aging process and could be eliminated by CAR-T cells without normal tissue damage or toxic effects. Moreover, it improved the exercise capacity and metabolic dysfunction (such as impaired glucose tolerance) of aged mice.

Based on previous studies [190, 191], in 2025, Zhang et al. developed a nanotechnology-based in vivo CAR-T therapy for inflamm-aging diseases via cardiolipin-mimic LNP delivery [192]. Inspired by cardiolipin, the team identified cardiolipin-like di-phosphoramide lipids (CAMP), which improve T cell transfection without antibody modification. Made into LNPs, these CAMP nanomaterials could deliver mRNA to T cells without any targeting ligands. Furthermore, the mRNA encoding a CAR targeting the senolytic and inflammatory antigen uPAR was circular RNA (circRNA). Compared with conventional linear mRNA, circRNA was more stable against enzymatic degradation and prolonged CAR expression in T cells [193]. Results showed that the in vivo generated CAR-T cells alleviated inflammatory-aging diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and liver fibrosis.

Apart from uPAR, Yang et al. identified that natural killer group 2 member D ligands (NKG2DLs) are upregulated in senescent cells [194]. In this context, the research team developed CAR-T cells targeting NKG2DLs, alleviating multiple aging-related pathologies in mouse models. It also eliminated naturally occurring senescent cells in aged nonhuman primates without observed adverse effects. These findings suggest that nanoparticle-mediated in vivo CAR-T generation could also be applied to produce NKG2DL-targeting CAR-T cells within the body. It provides a promising nanotechnology-based strategy for treating aging and diseases driven by senescence.

In vivo CAR-T in autoimmune diseases

Autoimmune diseases generally occur in genetically susceptible individuals and are affected by environmental factors, resulting in dysregulation of the innate and adaptive immune system [195]. The pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases is complex, but most include the loss of tolerance to self-antigen with the production of autoantibodies by B cells and plasma cells [196]. CAR-T cells have already exhibited great power to treat relapsed or refractory B-cell-derived hematological malignancies [197, 198]. In this context, CAR-T cells can treat patients with B-cell-mediated autoimmune diseases. Directly targeting B cells or plasma cells, CAR-T cells have been applied in patients with autoimmune diseases such as SLE, systemic sclerosis, myasthenia gravis and idiopathic inflammatory myopathy [199].

In Vivo CAR-T in SLE

To eliminate the requirements for complex in vitro manufacturing, nanoparticles can deliver mRNA encoding B-cell targeting CAR to T cells and produce CAR-T cells in vivo. Focusing on SLE, Chen et al. developed HN2301, a CD8-targeted LNP encapsulating CD19 CAR mRNA [200]. This gene-delivery system utilizes targeted LNP (tLNP) to deliver mRNA to specific T cells. In 5 enrolled patients, HN2301 successfully generated CD19-targeting CAR-T cells in vivo. This treatment achieved dose-dependent B-cell depletion and reduced SLE Disease Activity Index without severe toxicity. This treatment confirmed the feasibility of treating SLE with high-level clinical evidence, providing new hope for many patients trapped in recurrence and refractory SLE.

In Vivo CAR-T in myocardial fibrosis

CAR-T cells can also be produced in vivo to treat myocardial fibrosis [201]. It represents a pathological process, not a disease entity. Myocardial fibrosis can occur secondary to autoimmune disease since the heart is one of the common target organs of autoimmune diseases. It affects the whole structure of the heart, resulting in arrhythmias, pericardial damage, myocardial fibrosis and impaired valvular function [202]. In this context, Rurik et al. injected CD5 tLNP to deliver CAR mRNA targeting fibroblast activation protein (FAP) to T cells in vivo [201]. This treatment with modified mRNA-LNPs reduced fibrosis and restored cardiac function in a mouse model of heart failure.

In vivo CAR-T in other autoimmune diseases

Resetting aberrant autoimmunity via profound depletion of B cells can sustain drug-free remission and provide a durable benefit [203]. Hunter et al. provided multi-level evidence [204]. Firstly, they utilized cells derived from patients with different types of autoimmune diseases (such as antisynthetase syndrome, dermatomyositis and rheumatoid arthritis). It confirmed that the CD8 tLNP-loaded anti-CD19 CAR mRNA could effectively generate CAR-T cells in vitro against autoimmune diseases. Furthermore, in cynomolgus monkeys, tLNPs generated CD20 CAR-T cells in vivo and induced a deep depletion of B cells, followed by naïve B cells repopulation, suggesting an immune system reset. This study supports clinical development of the products in autoimmune diseases and other in vivo CAR-T products and innovative treatment concepts for broader applications, including genetic disorders [205] (Fig. 5).

Notably, a recent study developed CAR-engineered follicular helper T (Tfh) cells for autoimmune hepatitis treatment via amino acid-derived LNPs, which loaded self-amplifying RNA encoding both cytochrome P4502D6 (CYP2D6)-targeted CARs and the transcription factor Foxp3 [206]. Unlike common CAR-T cells that primarily eliminate the targeting cells, the reprogrammed CAR-Tfh cells, beyond recognizing CYP2D6-expressing hepatocytes, can simultaneously suppress multiple immune compartments, including pathogenic T cells, B cells, and dendritic cells. In an autoimmune hepatitis mouse model, in vivo-generated CAR-Tfh cells restored hepatic immune tolerance and alleviated tissue inflammation. This strategy highlights a shift from the cytotoxic CAR-T therapy toward immune reprogramming approaches, offering a potential framework for treating multicellular dysregulated autoimmune diseases.

In vivo CAR generation strategies show a promising alternative to both autologous and allogeneic in vitro-generated CAR-T cells, which streamline supply chains and reduce costs and timelines [207]. However, the LNP-mRNA method has only recently entered clinical trials [204], and has many unanswered questions, including short-, medium-, and long-term efficacy and safety. To successfully build an LNP-mRNA delivery system, companies require sufficient expertise and automated processes to do high-throughput screening and testing [208]. Moreover, a complex intellectual property landscape and trade secrets also pose great challenges in manufacturing.

Ongoing clinical trials

With the development of nanotechnology-based CAR-T cell therapy, multiple clinical trials are being carried out worldwide (Table 5). The aim is to evaluate its safety and efficacy in hematological malignancies, solid tumors and even in autoimmune diseases. The trend of these clinical trials demonstrates that the application of nanotechnology in CAR-T cell therapy is expanding from conventional ex vivo modification to in vivo programming [205, 209]. For example, Mei et al. Reported the first in-human clinical data of in vivo CAR-T therapy in patients with relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma [210]. This study utilized ESO-T01, a nanobody-targeted lentiviral vector, to engineer T cells in vivo, which effectively treated 4 patients. Recently, Chen et al. have completed the first human clinical study of in vivo CAR-T therapy based on the mRNA-LNP system [200]. Inrolled 5 female patients with SLE showed efficient elimination of B cells and decreased auto-antibodies. Additionally, various clinical trials of in vivo CAR-T are being carried out, ranging from HMs to solid tumors [211–213].

Ongoing Clinical Trials of Nanotechnology-based CAR-T Cell Therapy

B-cell ALL

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma

Apart from in vivo CAR-T programming, clinical trials also aim to overcome immunosuppressive TME via the autocrine of anti-PD1 nanobodies [214]. Other ongoing trials focus on enhancing CAR-T cells’ function and persistence [215–218]. For CAR-T cells monitoring, metallic NPs are evaluated in clinical trials. For example, 64Cu-labelled superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) have been tested for CAR-T cells imaging [219].

Although most of these initial trials are at Phase Ⅰ, they have exhibited potential to enhance the safety, specificity and persistence of CAR-T cells. For instance, in vivo CAR-T engineering based on the mRNA-LNP system improves safety by lowering the risks associated with complex ex vivo manipulation, such as insertional mutagenesis and T-cell phenotype alterations during prolonged in vitro expansion [200]. Nanobody-based CAR can enhance the targeting specificity of CAR-T cells due to their small size and high affinity to specific epitopes. Furthermore, through overcoming immunosuppressive TME, strategies like the autocrine of anti-PD1 nanobodies and nanomaterial-mediated TME modulation can alleviate CAR-T exhaustion and promote CAR-T persistence [210, 214]. In the future, with the development of therapeutic concepts and the accumulation of more clinical data, nanotechnology is supposed to further promote CAR-T cell therapy.

Summary and future prospects

Summary

In all, nanotechnology is being widely explored to improve CAR-T cell therapy and is supposed to substantially impact this field. It addressed a variety of challenges in CAR-T cell therapy, including sustained in vivo activation, overcoming immunosuppressive barriers, reducing systemic toxicity and generating CAR-T cells in vivo. Nanotechnology has demonstrated great potential for CAR-T cell manufacturing, delivery, activation, expansion and efficacy. Although both CAR-T cell therapy and nanomedicine are in their early stages, the advances of research will lead to a deeper understanding of disease mechanisms and promote precision medicine. The integration of CAR-T cell therapy and nanotechnology is expected to form a paradigm-shifting approach that is widely applied in the clinic.

Future prospects

Delivery of gene editing tools

Nanotechnology in the delivery of gene editing tools is turning from external editing to internal reprogramming. This is the most exciting future direction for nanotechnology-enhanced CAR-T therapy. Nanoparticles (NPs) emerge as vital vectors to deliver CRISPR-Cas9 components into T cells [220]. Future studies need to focus on how to enable the NPs to precisely target the editing sites and how to enhance their loading efficiency.

In the future, researchers first need to figure out the specific target binding sites on the NPs to ensure they can target the tumor epitopes precisely, which prevents off-tumor-derived toxicity. Future research is supposed to design an NP with efficient loading capacity that can combine gene editing with simultaneous delivery of immunostimulatory agents, further enhancing the therapeutic effects of CAR-T [221]. Notably, the CRISPR-Cas9 system enables multiple genome editing, allowing simultaneous targeting of multiple genomic loci. Thus, apart from CAR-associated reprogramming, future NP is expected to deliver multiple sets of gene editing tools that target different genomic loci [220]. In this case, multiple immune checkpoint regulators such as PD-1 and CTLA-4 are knocked out in CAR-T cells, thereby enhancing CAR-T anti-tumor activity.

Development of other nanomaterials for in vivo CAR-T

Polymer-based nanoparticles

Apart from the currently most mature LNP-delivery system, other nanomaterials exhibit large potential for in vivo CAR-T generation. One of the promising nanomaterials is PNP. Polymers-based nanosystems for T cell in vivo engineering contain polyethylenimine (PEI), polyethylene glycol (PEG), poly (2-dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate) (PDMAEMA), poly (β-amino esters) (PβAE), polyurethane (PU) and poly-L-histidine (PHSTD) [222]. Compared to LNP, the molecular weight and side chains of polymers can be precisely customized. For example, by using self-assembling nanoparticles (SNPs) composed of PEG, PEI and polyamidoamine (PAMAM), Yu et al. reported efficient CAR-encoding plasmid DNA delivery into T cells with 10-fold transfection efficacy compared to Lipofectamine 200 transfection reagent [223]. Furthermore, polymers are more stable in the blood than lipids that are prone to aggregate, therefore, PNP can be modified with more complex surface antibodies, such as conjugated anti-CD3e f(ab′)2 fragments on the surfaces of PβAE-based nanoparticles [87].

Inorganic nanoparticles

Inorganic nanomaterials deserve to be further explored in future studies of in vivo CAR-T production. Besides generating photothermal effects, gold nanomaterials also have the potential for gene delivery. Gold nanoparticle (AuNP) has highly adjustable surface chemical properties, enabling various molecules to be conjugated on its surface [224]. Attached with PEG polymers, AuNPs nanocarriers loaded with DNA exhibited high resistance against DNase Ⅰ-mediated degradation and nonspecific adsorption [225]. Other surface modifications, like peptides or ligands, can facilitate cellular uptake of the AuNP loaded with nucleic acids. Thus, it provides a new direction for in vivo CAR-T, that is to construct an AuNP-based multifunctional nanoplatform to deliver CAR genes into T cells. Moreover, Wijaya et al. have developed a nanoplatform by loading DNA oligonucleotides onto gold nanorods. Efficient cargo release was triggered by melting the gold nanorods under ultrafast laser irradiation [226]. Therefore, researchers can also explore the conditions that trigger the CAR-encoding DNA or RNA release from AuNPs, making the nanodelivery system controllable.

In addition to AuNP, future studies can also focus on another inorganic nanomaterial development. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) have a porous structure, which accommodates a broad spectrum of molecular sizes ranging from small drugs to large biomolecules [227]. In the future, researchers can further study the physicochemical properties of MSNs to elevate cargo loading capacity. Delivering gene editing tools and immunoregulatory agents together would further improve the therapeutic outcomes of in vivo CAR-T therapy.

Safety of Nanomaterials

To begin with, further investigations are required to establish strategies for the rational design of nanomaterials in immunotherapy. Many fundamental issues about the effects of nanomaterial composition, shape, size and surface charge on CAR-T cell function must be considered. Toxicity is one of the main concerns of nanomaterials. Due to the extremely small size (1–100 nm) and high surface area to volume ratio, nanoparticles can penetrate through cell membranes and many physiological barriers, posing a potential risk of adverse effects [228, 229]. Evidence shows that 30–99% of administered nanoparticles accumulate and sequester in the liver [230]. It reduces delivery to the targeted cells and potentially increases toxicity at the hepatic cellular level. As referred to above, future work must reduce the non-specific uptake of nanomaterials and consider the toxic effects induced by nanomaterial degradation.

Clinical translation of nanotechnology

Another consideration for future development is the operability of nanotechnology on a clinical scale. As of 2022, the FDA has approved more than 60 nanomedicines [231]. Among utilizing nanomaterials, liposomes/LNPs were the most prevalent carriers (33%), and one reason for their widespread use is the simplicity of manufacturing their structures [232]. With the increasing complexity of nanomaterial design, manufacturing costs and market monitoring procedures also increase. Therefore, researchers should focus on the goal of nanotechnology – widely operable clinical applications.

AI-enabled nanotechnology

With artificial intelligence (AI) rapidly developing, numerous datasets with powerful computing analysis and innovative deep learning architectures are reshaping cancer research. AI-nanotechnology can construct ‘Smart CAR-T Nanosymbionts’ to enhance immunotherapy [233]. Smart CAR-T nanosymbiont, an advanced model that optimizes the manufacturing of nanomaterials, enhances the prediction of in vivo efficacy and provides real-time monitoring abilities.

Integrating AI into nanotechnology creates an autonomous platform for rapidly synthesizing nanoparticles. Mottafegh et al. developed AI-assisted autonomous manufacturing of drug-loaded NPs, reducing production time from days to just 20 min [234]. Apart from that, AI also assists modifications to enhance CAR-T cell function. For example, CAR-T cell microrobots are modified with immunomagnetic beads on the cell surface [235]. Through magnetic-acoustic actuation, it facilitates CAR-T cell infiltration and targeting. Pfister et al. also applied magnetic NPs to guide CAR-T cells to tumor sites [236], which enhances localized cytotoxicity and reduces systemic toxicity.

Developing predictive algorithms provides advanced tools for personalized CAR-T cell therapy. Machine learning can be applied to predict CRS risks and design the optimal phenotype of CAR for each patient [237, 238]. Moreover, AI-based strategies can discover potential antigens and predict drug efficacy based on molecular features [239]. Besides antigens, AI can also discover high-affinity nanobodies. For example, Feng et al. developed a machine learning-driven strategy that predicts nanobody-target binding affinity by analyzing molecular interactions, including hydrogen bonding and aromatic-associated interactions [240]. This data-driven method can predict therapeutic processes and effects based on each patient’s unique biological profile, promoting personalized cancer treatment.
