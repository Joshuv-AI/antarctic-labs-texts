Effect of low dose azithromycin on the fecal microbiome and fecal bile acids in healthy dogs
Authors: Colin Smith, Roman Husnik, Frederic Gaschen, Claudia Kirk, Xiaojuan Zhu, Jan Suchodolski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAzithromycin has prokinetic effects at low doses in people, cats, and anecdotally, in dogs; however, antibiotics alter the fecal microbiome and metabolome.Hypothesis/ObjectivesTo prospectively evaluate the effects of low-dose azithromycin, hypothesizing that it would alter the fecal microbiome and fecal bile acids in healthy dogs.AnimalsEleven healthy research dogs.MethodsProspective study. Dogs received azithromycin (2 mg/kg PO q8h) for 8 days. Fecal samples were collected before (day 1), during (day 6), and after azithromycin administration (days 11, 26, 36, 45). Introduction

Gastrointestinal motility disorders are an underestimated cause of morbidity in dogs and can be primary or, more commonly, secondary to systemic or primary gastrointestinal disease.1,2 Treatment involves addressing the underlying cause and often concurrent use of prokinetics.1,2

Common prokinetics include serotonergic drugs and motilin agonists. Motilin agonists mimic endogenous motilin, which initiates phase III of interdigestive motility.2,3 Macrolides such as azithromycin and erythromycin bind motilin receptors and exert prokinetic effects at low doses. Erythromycin is well-established as a prokinetic in people and has efficacy in dogs and cats.4–8 However, the use of erythromycin in people is limited by cytochrome P450 inhibition and arrhythmogenic potential.9,10 Thus, azithromycin has been explored as an alternative, with limited studies demonstrating comparable prokinetic effects and a better safety profile.11–13 Low-dose azithromycin has established evidence in cats and clinical support in dogs, with prokinetic doses approximately 1/2-1/5 those used for antimicrobial purposes.2,14,15

Despite lower prokinetic dosing, alterations to the gut microbiome with azithromycin remain possible, as seen with metronidazole, tylosin, amoxicillin, and the combination of enrofloxacin and metronidazole, administered at antimicrobial doses in dogs.16–26 Reported effects include reduced species richness and diversity, increased Dysbiosis Index (DI), and decreased Peptacetobacter hiranonis, resulting in reduced conversion of unconjugated primary to secondary bile acids (BAs), a major functional indicator of microbial activity and homeostasis. Furthermore, in some dogs these changes can persist beyond administration and, with select antibiotics, lead to incomplete recovery.16,17,21–23,26

In people, low-dose azithromycin is used in the treatment of gastroparesis and chronic pulmonary disease, with doses approximately half that used for acute infection.27–30 Even at these lower doses, it reduces bacterial richness and diversity and has functional consequences including decreased short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, suppression of beneficial metabolic pathways, and reduced anti-inflammatory cytokines.31

Despite clinical use, no studies have assessed the effects of low-dose azithromycin on the fecal microbiome or metabolome in dogs. Given its use as a prokinetic and importance as an antimicrobial, understanding these effects is clinically relevant. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the effects of low-dose azithromycin on the fecal microbiome and fecal BAs in healthy dogs. We hypothesized that its administration would induce alterations in fecal microbial composition and BA metabolism.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Tennessee. Twelve healthy research Beagles with no gastrointestinal signs or antimicrobial exposure within 6 months were enrolled. Dogs were individually housed, fed a standardized diet, and received prophylactic deworming before study initiation.

A 45-day prospective study was performed with dogs receiving low-dose azithromycin (2 mg/kg PO q8h) for 8 days (day 3-10). Fecal samples were collected at baseline (day 1), during (day 6), and post-azithromycin administration (day 11, 26, 36, and 45), and fecal scores performed daily. Fecal samples were stored at -80 °C and submitted to the Texas A&M Gastrointestinal Laboratory, where all analyses were performed. Schematic representation of the study timeline is noted in Figure 1. Body weight and body condition score (BCS) were collected at baseline and at the end of the study.

Schematic representation of the study timeline. Azithromycin (2 mg/kg PO q8h) was administered from days 3-10. Fecal samples were collected on days 1, 6, 11, 26, 36, and 45. Quantitative PCR analysis of 16 core bacterial taxa and calculation of the DI were performed on all sample days. Fecal primary and secondary UBA concentrations were assessed on days 1, 11, and 45. Abbreviations: UBA = unconjugated bile acid; DI = dysbiosis index.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was used to measure the abundance of 16 core bacterial taxa using methods previously described.32 The DI was then derived using select taxa, following established methods.33 Quantification was performed on select days (day 1, 6, 11, 26, 36, and 45).

A total of 29 conjugated, unconjugated, and oxo-isoforms of primary and secondary BAs were measured using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry method.34 Quantification was performed on select days (day 1, 11, and 45).

Data were assessed for normality and reported as mean ± standard deviation for normal data or median and range for non-normal data. Changes over time were analyzed with Friedman tests and adjusted using Benjamini-Hochberg’s False Discovery Rate. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction method. Related-Samples Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test evaluated pre and post-values in weight and BCS. Analyses were performed in IBM SPSS v28, and figures generated in GraphPad Prism v7. Correlation analysis used repeated measures correlation by R version 4.5.1 rmcorr package version 0.7.0.35 Significance was set at P < .05.

Results

Study sample

A total of 12 dogs were enrolled; however, 1 dog was excluded due to the development of acute thoracolumbar intervertebral disc disease unrelated to the study.

Five dogs were spayed females and 6 dogs neutered males. The mean age was 3.3 ± 0.8 years and initial mean body weight 12.1 ± 1.6 kg. Median initial BCS was 5/9 (range 4-6). Pre-study physical examination and lab work (complete blood count, biochemistry, and urinalysis) were performed in all dogs and yielded no clinically relevant abnormalities. All dogs had a negative fecal flotation and normal fecal scores (Purina Fecal Score [PFS]: 2/7; range 2-3) before the study.

Study period

No gastrointestinal adverse effects were noted throughout the study period, and post-study physical examinations were unchanged from baseline. The mean body weight at the end of the study was 12.0 ± 1.7 kg, and median BCS 4/9 (range 4-6). There were no significant differences in body weight or BCS at the end of the study compared to baseline.

A significant increase in median fecal score occurred on day 6 (PFS: 3/7; range 2-3; P = .009) but returned to baseline on day 11 (PFS: 2/7; range 2-3). Subsequent median fecal scores remained unchanged for the remainder of the study.

Fecal BA concentrations

Low-dose azithromycin significantly affected the proportions of fecal BAs. The percentage of primary and secondary unconjugated bile acids (UBAs) was significantly altered on day 11 compared to baseline, as demonstrated in Figure 5. Specifically, the percentage of primary UBAs increased and secondary UBAs decreased (P = .017). Both returned to their respective baseline by day 45. This shift in primary UBAs showed a strong negative correlation with P. hiranonis (r = -0.936 [95% CI, -0.973, -0.852], P < .001), as demonstrated in Figure 6.

The primary BA, cholic acid, significantly increased on day 11 (P = .03), whereas chenodeoxycholic acid remained non-significantly altered at this time point. Both secondary UBAs, lithocholic acid (P = .003) and deoxycholic acid (P = .001), were significantly decreased on day 11. All returned to baseline at the conclusion of the study.

Two dogs on day 11 maintained low primary UBA and high secondary UBA concentrations. P. hiranonis was minimally affected compared to baseline in these dogs.

Discussion

The study aim was to describe the effects of low-dose azithromycin on the fecal microbiome and fecal BA concentrations in healthy dogs, with the hypothesis that its administration would alter the fecal microbiome and fecal BA metabolism. Our findings support this hypothesis. Although administered at a lower dose than used for antimicrobial purposes, azithromycin altered the fecal microbiome and BA metabolism. Dysbiosis was evident and supported by significant decreases in several core bacterial taxa, including P. hiranonis, Faecalibacterium, and Fusobacterium, which fell below reference intervals in most dogs, and reduced conversion of primary to secondary UBAs. These effects were mostly reversible upon discontinuation, with normalization of fecal BAs and recovery of all but 1 taxon by the end of the study.

Evidence of dysbiosis in this study was supported by significant decreases in several core and functionally important bacterial taxa, including P. hiranonis, Faecalibacterium, and Fusobacterium. Faecalibacterium produces SCFAs, which provide energy for enterocytes and modulate mucosal immunity.36 Additionally, SCFAs maintain an acidic luminal environment that suppresses pH-sensitive pathobionts and regulate microbial virulence factors.36,37 Decreased Faecalibacterium occurs in dogs with gastrointestinal disease and after antibiotic exposure.19,21,22,26,37 Fusobacterium also contributes to SCFA production, most notably butyrate, and primarily ferments amino acids (eg, glutamate, lysine) rather than carbohydrates.38 Additionally, Fusobacterium abundance appears to be associated with dietary protein, as it is present in relatively higher amounts in carnivorous species and dogs fed a raw diet.39,40 This taxon is typically reduced in dysbiosis and similarly decreases with antibiotic use or in chronic enteropathies.17,19,21–23,26,41 P. hiranonis produces 7-α-dehydroxylation enzymes encoded by the bai operon and is the primary converter of primary to secondary UBAs in dogs.34,36 Production of secondary UBAs is essential for intestinal health, as their depletion can allow for overgrowth of enteropathogens and loss of beneficial commensals. High concentrations of primary BAs that can occur due to reduced conversion can also potentially lead to secretory diarrhea in a small subset of animals.36,42 Decreases in P. hiranonis occur after broad-spectrum antibiotic use and in a subset of dogs with chronic inflammatory enteropathies, while a decrease is rarely observed in dogs with acute diarrhea.42–45

Azithromycin use was associated with a significant increase in primary UBAs, alongside decreases in both secondary UBAs and P. hiranonis abundance, consistent with reduced conversion. Primary UBAs were negatively correlated with P. hiranonis, in agreement with previous data.21 Though similar shifts in BAs are reported with metronidazole and tylosin at antimicrobial doses, recovery patterns differ compared to azithromycin. In this study, BAs normalized within 5 weeks, whereas tylosin and metronidazole show sustained elevations in primary UBAs up to 8 weeks in individual dogs.21,22 Furthermore, it is speculated that recovery occurred earlier in this study given normalization of P. hiranonis at day 26; however, BAs were only measured on day 11 and day 45. Thus, recovery of fecal BAs possibly occurs more rapidly after low-dose azithromycin; although, the tylosin and metronidazole studies were conducted in pet dogs, and housing environment (eg, pet vs research colony) has been shown to possibly influence the fecal microbiome of dogs.46

Together, the significant decreases in several core taxa, including P. hiranonis, Faecalibacterium, and Fusobacterium, and the significant decrease in secondary UBAs demonstrate that low-dose azithromycin caused dysbiosis. These changes highlight not only shifts in composition but also functional consequences that could have negative effects on host physiology. Additionally, though not measured in this study, it is probable that SCFAs were reduced in these dogs based upon decreased abundance of key SCFA producers, an outcome observed with metronidazole use in cats.47 SCFAs decrease in dogs with chronic enteropathy (CE), where dysbiosis is also commonly present.37 Furthermore, in these dogs, Faecalibacterium positively correlates with propionate production. It is also conceivable that the decrease in secondary UBAs, in part, influenced the transient increase in fecal scores during azithromycin administration, as increased primary UBAs can potentially contribute to secretory diarrhea due to increased colonic fluid secretion and altered mucosal permeability.36,48 However, cautious interpretation is warranted, as fecal scores remained within the normal range (PFS: 2-3) and their assessment is based on a subjective grading scheme. Thus, the mild increase in fecal scores might not have been clinically relevant or reflected true change. Nevertheless, it is clear that low-dose azithromycin caused dysbiosis on the basis of both compositional and functional changes; however, the net result of these changes remains to be determined given the complexity of the fecal microbiome and possible influence on distant organ systems. Fortunately, the documented changes appear to be reversible given secondary UBAs and all but 1 taxon recovered by the end of the study.

E. coli is a facultative anaerobe and normal commensal of the intestinal tract in dogs. Increased abundance occurs in dogs with CE and after administration of metronidazole, tylosin, or amoxicillin.17,19,20,23,24,26,41 The absence of E. coli overgrowth in this study compared to other antibiotics could reflect differences in antimicrobial activity. E. coli commonly resists aminopenicillins, metronidazole, and inhibits tylosin uptake.18,49 In contrast, azithromycin’s unique macrolide structure confers higher basicity and allows greater intracellular uptake, enhancing activity against Enterobacteriaceae.50 In people, azithromycin demonstrates excellent activity against common enteropathogens, including multiple strains of E. coli.51–53 Thus, the lack of overgrowth in this study, and numerical decrease, could reflect a direct antimicrobial effect of azithromycin.

Streptococcus are gram-positive commensals that often increase during dysbiosis due to their growth advantage as lactic-acid-producing bacteria when luminal pH decreases with excess undigested substrate.36,54,55 Despite evidence of dysbiosis, low-dose azithromycin led to a sustained and significant decrease in Streptococcus, the only taxon to not recover by the end of the study. This contrasts with findings for other antibiotics. Both tylosin and metronidazole increase Streptococcus abundance, whereas aminopenicillins have yielded mixed results.17–21,23,24

Direct suppression of growth is therefore a plausible explanation for the lack of overgrowth. More notable, however, was the sustained decrease throughout the 5-week recovery period. The sustained decrease is unlikely to be explained by azithromycin’s half-life or post-antibiotic effects. Reported half-lives (51-90 hours) in dogs indicate drug elimination within ~19 days, and post-antibiotic effects limited to hours.56,57 The most likely explanation is ecological disruption of the fecal microbiome.

The DI is the only current analytically validated assay for measuring fecal dysbiosis in dogs and cats.33 It incorporates 7 important core bacterial taxa to generate a numerical value to reflect the degree of dysbiosis, and is interpreted together with changes in all included taxa. An increase in the DI correlates with total shifts observed by 16S rRNA and DNA shotgun sequencing, with a higher DI correlating with larger shifts on sequencing in these studies.21,45,58,59 The DI is used to document dysbiosis after antibiotic exposure and shows significant increases after administration of metronidazole, tylosin, and a combination of enrofloxacin and metronidazole.19,21–23,26 In this study, while the DI increased numerically at day 6 and was significantly higher compared to the following days, the total increase was smaller than would be expected based on previous antibiotics studies. This is likely due to the fact that E. coli and Streptococcus, which typically show a strong increase in dysbiosis after antibiotic use, instead decreased with low-dose azithromycin. This emphasizes that the DI should be interpreted together with the abundances of all core bacteria, as the decrease in functionally important core taxa (ie, P. hiranonis, Faecalibacterium, and Fusobacterium) indicated clear change after low-dose azithromycin administration.

Aside from a possible transient, mild decrease in fecal consistency, no other clinically relevant abnormalities were observed in this study. These findings suggest that short-term use of low-dose azithromycin is well tolerated in healthy dogs and unlikely to cause clinically relevant adverse effects. However, healthy dogs do not represent the clinical population in which low-dose azithromycin would be used. Clinical dogs could have pre-existing dysbiosis, altered BA metabolism, and other gastrointestinal disturbances that could increase their susceptibility to microbiome and metabolomic alterations and associated clinical consequences. Alternatively, effects could be similar to those seen in this study. Therefore, results should be interpreted cautiously when considering direct applicability and highlight the need for future research into clinical populations. Furthermore, although antimicrobial resistance was not evaluated in this study, the potential for resistance selection represents an additional consideration when deciding on its use as a prokinetic.

This study had several limitations. First, no control group was included; however, this was considered appropriate given evidence supporting stability of the fecal microbiome in dogs and long-term stability noted in people.21,22,41,60 Second, full sequencing of the fecal microbiome was not performed and metabolomic assessment was restricted to BAs. However, the DI was selected for its established correlation with metagenomic sequencing to mitigate this limitation, and BAs were considered a relevant metabolite given their essential functions and comparative value to established literature. Furthermore, though full microbiome sequencing provides additional data, interpretation and comparison of this information is limited given interlaboratory variability and compositionality of the data, as taxonomy is expressed as relative proportions (ie, data is expressed as a percentage of total) in contrast to measuring total abundance by qPCR.61 Third, as discussed, this study used healthy dogs, which does not represent clinical populations that would be receiving low-dose azithromycin. Therefore, results should be interpreted cautiously when considering direct applicability to such a population. Fourth, though the sample size was sufficient per the power analysis, the number of dogs remained limited. Finally, only 1 dosing regimen was used, meaning results might not apply to alternative protocols. This is of particular importance as prokinetic efficacy of azithromycin in dogs remains to be proven, which could ultimately yield different dosing strategies than used in this study.

This study demonstrated that low-dose azithromycin altered the fecal microbiome and fecal BAs in healthy dogs. The absence of Streptococcus and E. coli overgrowth also indicates a potentially unique response compared to other antibiotics. Therefore, when used short-term at a lower dose for prokinetic purposes, azithromycin appears to induce compositional and functional changes that are mostly reversible.

Appendix

Animals

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Tennessee. Twelve healthy research colony Beagles were included in this study. Dogs were included if they had no history of gastrointestinal signs or antimicrobial exposure within the preceding 6 months. They were deemed healthy based on the absence of clinical disease, physical examination, complete blood count, biochemistry, urinalysis, and negative fecal screening (zinc and sugar flotation). Dogs were housed individually, had a stable daily routine, and were cared for by trained, experienced laboratory staff. The only variability to their routine was multiple supervised group interactions per week at the discretion of the laboratory staff. Long-term diet in these dogs was Purina ONE® Lamb & Rice Formula Dry Dog Food fed once per day in the morning and daily treats of Purina ONE® Plus Vibrant Maturity Adult 7+ Classic Ground Turkey & Barley Entrée Wet Dog Food. The only modification to their dietary schedule as part of this study was restricting daily treat intake to once per day. This was implemented 4 weeks before the study’s onset. Lastly, dogs received prophylactic deworming (fenbendazole 50 mg/kg PO q24h for 3 days) and a bath within 1 week of starting the study.

Study design

A prospective study with twelve healthy research colony Beagles was performed over a 45-day period. Dogs were administered azithromycin (2 mg/kg PO q8h) over 8 days, corresponding to days 3-10. Dogs were then monitored for 35 days for adverse effects, changes to fecal consistency, and select fecal samples collected (day 1, 6, 11, 26, 36, and 45). Body weight and BCS were recorded at baseline (day 1) and at the end of the study (day 45) by a single investigator using the same scale and performed in the evening. No control group was included given the proven stability of the canine gut microbiome.21,22,41,60 Instead, subjects acted as their own control with comparison to their baseline sample (day 1).

Dogs remained individually housed and fed the diet combination as described, which was unchanged. Dogs were monitored daily by laboratory staff for evidence of gastroenteritis (vomiting, diarrhea, anorexia, or abdominal discomfort), drug-related nausea (changes in appetite, hypersalivation), or other signs of illness. Daily fecal scoring was performed by a combination of trained, experienced laboratory staff and 2 investigators using the PFS.62

Fresh fecal samples after spontaneous defecation were acquired within 12 hours, a time at which the gut microbiome has proven to remain stable in dogs.63 Days of collection included baseline (day 1), during (day 6), and post-azithromycin (day 11, 26, 36, and 45). Each fecal sample was weighed, divided into 3 aliquots (minimum 5 grams), and immediately stored at -80°C. All samples were submitted as a single batch at the end of the study to the Gastrointestinal Laboratory at Texas A&M University, where all analyses were performed.

Quantitative PCR analysis and calculation of the DI

Quantitative PCR was used to measure the abundance of multiple bacterial taxa (Blautia, P. hiranonis, E. coli, Faecalibacterium, Fusobacterium, Streptococcus, Turicibacter, Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, Akkermansia, C. perfringens, Enterococcus, Lactobacillus, P. copri, Ruminococcaceae, and R. gnavus) on fecal samples from days 1, 6, 11, 26, 36, and 45 as previously described.32 Results were expressed as the log amount of DNA for each bacterial taxon per gram of feces. The DI was then derived using select taxa following established methods.33 DI values <0: normal DI indicating no shifts in the overall diversity of the intestinal microbiota; 0-2: mild to moderate shift in the overall diversity of the intestinal microbiota; and >2: significant dysbiosis.

Fecal BA concentrations

A total of 29 conjugated, unconjugated, and oxo-isoforms of primary and secondary BAs were measured using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry method.34 Cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid were defined as primary UBAs, and deoxycholic acid and lithocholic acid as secondary UBAs formed via P. hiranonis-mediated 7-α-dehydroxylation.34,64,65 Quantification was performed on select days (day 1, 11, and 45). Results were reported as nanograms per milligram of dry fecal matter and percentage of total UBAs.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined based on data from a previously performed pilot study to ensure adequate power and detection of significance. This pilot study was composed of 5 healthy research dogs who were administered an identical dosing schedule of azithromycin (2 mg/kg PO q8h for 8 days); however, the study duration was shorter. Sample size was estimated using a single factor repeated-measures design and a sample of 12 subjects, measured at 5 time points, achieving 84% power to detect differences in the DI among the means over 5 time points (-4.41, -3.81, -1.21, -4.13, and -3.94) at a P < .05 significance level. The standard deviation across subjects at each time point was assumed to be 2.3.

All data sets were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk W normality test and QQ plots. Descriptive statistics were calculated for each variable. Data are reported as mean and standard deviation for normally distributed data; otherwise, median and range (minimum-maximum) were reported.

For assessment of fecal score (PFS), the DI, qPCR of individual bacterial taxa, and fecal BAs (grouped and ungrouped), Friedman tests were performed for data over time (IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 28) and adjusted for multiple tests using Benjamini-Hochberg’s False Discovery Rate (SAS version 9.4, proc multtest, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, United States). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction method. Related-Samples Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was used to test the difference between pre- and post-values in weight and BCS. IBM SPSS version 28 was used for the statistical analysis (IBM Corp. Released 2021. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) and GraphPad Prism to generate relevant figures (GraphPad Prism 7.0, 180 GraphPad Software Inc). The correlation analysis was conducted using repeated-measures correlation by R version 4.5.1 rmcorr package version 0.7.0.35 Significance was set at P < .05.
