Changes in the expression of microRNAs regulate podophyllotoxin accumulation in Linum album cells to phenylalanine
Authors: Mostafa Sagharyan, Mohsen Sharifi, Farah Karimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Podophyllotoxin (PTOX) is a significant lignan in Linum album that is widely utilized as a precursor to produce excellent anticancer derivatives. Our previous study illustrated that exogenous phenylalanine (Phe) had a beneficial impact on the production of PTOX in L. album cell culture. The current study was focused on investigating the molecular mechanisms through which Phe (1000 µM) regulates the biosynthesis of PTOX in L. album cells. In the current work, the results indicated that Phe increases hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), salicylic acid (SA), and nitric oxide (NO) formation in L. album cells during the treatment period. Also, RT-qPCR analysis implied that Phe upregulates four PTOX biosynthetic genes including PAL, CAD, CCR, and PLR. As expected, Phe accelerates the metabolic fluxes towards PTOX accumulation through a change in the rate of PAL and PLR action in the entry and terminal points of biosynthesis of PTOX in L. album cells. Moreover, a Poly(A)-tailed RT-qPCR analysis for microRNAs revealed that Phe feeding differentially modulates the expression levels of seven conserved miRNAs, namely miR156, miR159, miR171, miR172, miR393, miR396, and miR408. DSPC analysis revealed a strong relationship between the transcript levels of PTOX biosynthetic genes and the expression levels of these miRNAs. Overall, this work demonstrated that Phe feeding regulates the PTOX content, primarily by changing SA, H2O2, and NO generation and, secondly by regulating the expression levels of biosynthetic genes and miRNAs involved in PTOX in L. album cells.

Introduction

Linum album Kotschy ex Boiss. is a perennial herb of the Linaceae family renowned for the presence of lignans, particularly podophyllotoxin (PTOX) [1]. Clinical explorations have illustrated that PTOX and its glycosidic constituents, such as etoposide, teniposide, and etopophos, exhibit antineoplastic and anticancer features [2, 3]. PTOX, as a dilignol originating from the phenylpropanoid pathway, can improve the plant’s defense system as a chemical barrier [4, 5]. Earlier reports have been suggested that PTOX biosynthesis is mainly regulated by specific genes such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamyl-alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD), cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR), and pinoresinol-lariciresinol reductase (PLR) in L. album exposed to abiotic and biotic stimuli [6, 7]. Published studies show that phenylalanine (Phe) is a crucial substrate to the biosynthesis of polyphenolic compounds that serve in plant defense reactions under herbivores and other environmental stresses [8, 9].

This aromatic amino acid is deaminated into a basic phenolic unit, particularly cinnamic acid, by accomplishing PAL enzyme activity [10, 11]. Research by Tashackori et al. [12] demonstrated that PLR downregulated transgenic ihp-PLR in hairy roots of L. album represented a significant reduction in PTOX content, albeit Phe level in this transgenic hairy root was almost similar to that of the control sample. In a recent study, our observations emphasized that different Phe concentrations led to increased PTOX by changing the redox balance and reprogramming carbohydrates and amino acids content in L. album cells [13]. However, the molecular mechanisms by which Phe can regulate the biosynthesis of PTOX are unclear. Also, it has been suggested that an imbalance in the homeostasis of Phe induces a sharp defense reaction, demonstrating that a dynamic signaling network is activated by biotic stimuli [14]. Among the signaling pathways, salicylic acid (SA) can act as a stress hormone, which directly or indirectly mediates cellular metabolic and genetic processes by transmitting multiple signals [15, 16]. Accumulating evidence suggests that SA may be sensed via two classes of the non-expression of pathogenesis-related gene 1 (NPR1) and NPR3/NPR4 receptors, indicating a certain linkage amongst SA, reactive nitrogen species (RNS), and reactive oxygen species (ROS) during infection with pathogens [17]. Among ROS molecules, H2O2 is a highly stable molecule that can amplify signal transduction within plant cells, thereby impairing multiple subcellular constituents such as DNA/RNA, cell membranes, proteins, and carbohydrates [18, 19]. Also, nitric oxide (NO) as a neurotransmitter component can behave in parallel and/or in combination with H2O2 in the biochemical and molecular reactions of the plant cellular responses [20]. An imbalance in intracellular oxidative status led to boosted expression levels of lignan biosynthesis genes and subsequent enhanced PTOX content [7, 21].

In addition to signaling molecules, the footprints of gene expression can be regulated by non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) at the transcriptome levels versus biotic and abiotic stimuli [22]. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are considered as short non-coding RNAs that can modulate the molecular mechanisms concerning the biosynthesis of flavonoids, lignin, and lignans [23, 24]. In L. usitatissimum, in silico prediction of miRNAs represented the involvement of miR156, miR157, miR159, miR160, miR164, miR166, miR167, miR171, miR172, miR319, miR395, miR396, miR399, miR5219, and miR5523 in lignans biosynthesis [25]. Also, it has been displayed five specific miRNAs, including miR172i, miR035, miR1438, miR2275, miR8291, are expressed in PTOX biosynthesis in Podophyllum hexanderum in response to methyl jasmonate (MeJA) [2]. Also, our previous study indicated that miR156, miR159, miR172, and miR396, are negatively involved in the regulation of PTOX- specific biosynthetic genes in L. album cells treated with exogenous MeJA [21]. In the present study, we tracked the action of seven conserved miRNAs (e.g., miR156, miR159, miR172, miR171, miR393, miR396, and miR408) and four PTOX-biosynthetic genes by altering H2O2, NO, and SA in L. album cells in response to exogenous Phe.

Materials and methods

Plant growth condition as well as treatment

In the present study, seeds of L. album were collected based on the relevant legislation and international guidelines. The requirement permissions for plant collection were obtained by the Research Ethics Committee of Tarbiat Modares University. Each plant was taxonomically identified by Dr. Shahrokh Kazempour-Osaloo. A voucher specimen (No. 2450) of L. album was kept in the Herbarium of the Department of Plant Biology of Tarbiat Modares University, Iran. Calli were obtained from the disinfected seedlings of L. album in MS [26] media in solid with NAA (2 mg L− 1), Kinetin (0.4 mg L− 1), and sucrose (3%). Cell suspension culture was set up using Sagharyan and Sharifi [13] protocol. Concisely, 2 g cells of fragile callus were inoculated in Erlenmeyer flasks (100 mL) containing 30 mL of MS media (liquid) supplemented with NAA (2 mg L− 1) and Kinetin (0.4 mg L− 1) (27 °C at 110 rpm in the dark condition). These cells in the subculture medium were cultured for 14 days. After seven days of subculture, the cell suspensions were exposed to a final dosage of Phe (0 and 1000 µM) as described in our previous study [13], a concentration shown to effectively enhance PTOX production in L. album cells, the key metabolite of interest in this research. Subsequently, sampling was conducted at five time points (6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h) following Phe feeding. Each experiment was collected and immediately frozen at -80 °C until further measurements.

Measurement of endogenous phenylalanine and tyrosine by HPLC analysis

For endogenous Phe and Tyr contents, the HPLC instrument (Agilent Technologies 1260 infinity, USA) was employed for analysis using the protocol previously stated by Biermann et al. [27]. In brief, Phe and Tyr isolation was achieved by ethanol (2 mL: 80% (v/v)). After evaporating the solvent, the obtained residue was liquified in water (1 mL), and then, the sample (50 µL) was mixed with OPA (Ortho-phthalaldehyde) (40 µL). Phe and Tyr detection was operated using a column (4.6 × 150 mm, 3.5 μm particle size, Agilent Technologies 1260 infinity, USA) via an FLD detector. The operation of the HPLC instrument was run using the mobile phases were applied as follows (A) Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4 (25 mM, pH 7.2)/tetrahydrofuran (95:5, v/v) and (B) Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4 (25 mM, pH 7.2)/methanol/acetonitrile (50:35:15, v/v/v). Phe and Tyr content was calculated following individual standard curves (nmol g− 1 FW).

H2O2 and NO determination

Velikova et al. [28] method was pursued to measure the intracellular H2O2 level. Fresh cells were obtained from each replication, and the samples (0.2 g) were scaled and homogenized in precooled TCA (trichloroacetic acid) (0.1%, 2 mL). The reaction mixture consisted of 500 µL of supernatant, KI (1 M, 1 mL), and PO43− (0.5 mL, 100 mM, pH ~ 7.0) under lightless condition. The samples’ absorbance was evaluated at 390 nm by a spectrophotometer. The H2O2 alterations was calculated as µmol H2O2 g− 1 FW via the formula given by H2O2 standard curve.

The procedure of Kuar et al. [29] was operated to measure NO content. Frozen cells were ground and mixed with PO43− (1.5 mL, 100 mM, pH ~ 7.0), and centrifuged (15,000 rpm, 20 min). 0.5 mL of supernatant was added to Griess reagent (500 µL) and PO43− (0.5 mL, 100 mM, pH ~ 7.0). After 25 min, the absorbance of the reaction was calibrated at 540 nm followed by sodium nitrite standard curve (µmol g− 1 FW).

Determination of SA content

The protocol of Owen et al. [30] was utilized to analyze the SA content of the cells. Briefly, lyophilized cells (0.2 g) were extracted in pure methanol. The solution then centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 15 min). After evaporating, the dried residue was resuspended in 2 mL of acetonitrile, which was then eluted with 3 mL of n-hexane. The concentrated residue was dispersed with methanol (500 µL). For the detection of SA content, an HPLC device (Agilent Technologies 1260 infinity, USA) was used with a C-18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The wavelength was arranged at 278 and 300 nm in a UV detector. The detection was carried out following Solvent A (2% acetic acid in water) and Solvent B (methanol) [31]. To determine SA content, the peak of the chromatogram was identified by examining retention time and HPLC spectra of the SA standard.

Assaying PAL and PLR enzymes activities

For measuring PAL and PLR activities, the protein extract was first isolated from the treated and untreated L. album cells following the procedure explained by Sagharyan et al. [11]. Bradford [32] method was employed for determining the amount of protein in the extract based on bovine serum standard. To analyze PAL activity, the obtained enzyme extract (100 µL) was poured into L-phenylalanine (200 µL, 100 mM) and PO43− (700 µL, 100 mM, pH ~ 8.0) (37 °C, 60 min) [33]. PAL activity was measured at 290 nm and reported as µg of cinnamic acid mg− 1 protein h− 1.

PLR activity was determined based on Fujita et al. [34] according to the method described by Samari et al. [20]. The reaction solution contained enzyme extract (100 µL), racemic pinoresinol (10 µL, 8 mM), and PO43− (340 µL, 0.1 M, pH 7.1). To activate the enzyme reaction, NADPH (50 µL, 50 mM) was added (30 °C, 60 min). For extracting the samples, ethyl acetate (500 µL) was added and washed twice times each. The ethyl acetate phase was concentrated, and the remain was mixed in pure methanol (200 µL). The analysis of PLR activity was performed on an HPLC device (Agilent Technologies 1260 infinity, USA) equipped with a C-18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). Water and acetonitrile were used as the mobile phases. The isolation of products was examined based on a standard gradient previously described by Samari et al. [20]. The lignans were analyzed at 280 nm based on the standard curve of (+)-lariciresinol and (-)-secolariciresinol from Arbonova (Turku, Finland).

Total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from related cells for PTOX-specific biosynthetic genes using the One-Step RNA reagent (BioBasic, Canada). Its concentration and purity were checked using a Nanodrop ND-1000 (ThermoFisher Scientific) and agarose gel electrophoresis. After that, total RNA (1000 ng) was subjected to reverse-transcribe into the first strand complementary DNA (cDNA) using a synthetic kit (RevertAid, Thermo Fisher, USA). The analysis of PAL, PLR, CAD, and CCR gene expression was administered by RT-qPCR (ABI StepOnePlus thermal cycler, Thermo Fisher, USA) according to the method of Sagharyan et al. [21]. RT-qPCR reactions were accomplished in a final volume of 20 µL (1 µL of cDNA, 0.5 µL of individual gene-specific forward and reverse primers, SYBR PCR Master mix (5x, 4 µL) with ROX stain (Amplicon), and 14 µL of Nucleases-free water). Reactions with Nucleases-free water (1 µL) instead of cDNA were considered in parallel as negative control. Based on our previous literate reports, candidate gene primers were shortlisted in Table 1 [21]. To normalize the cDNA concentration among individual experiments, β-Actin was applied as a housekeeping and reference gene. The RT-qPCR program was set based on the method of Sagharyan et al. [21]. To check the specificity of the amplified RT-qPCR product, we determined the melting curves at the end of the RT-qPCR analysis. The StepOne software (Ver. 2.3) was applied to visualize the relative expression of PTOX biosynthetic genes, and data were appraised by 2−ΔΔCT approach [35].

Polyadenylation and reverse transcription for miRNAs

The expression levels of selected miRNAs were analyzed using the Poly(A) tailing method followed by RT-qPCR with a universal reverse primer, as previously described [21]. For this reason, polyadenylation of total RNA from treated cells was done using poly (A) Polymerase Tailing Kit (New England Biolabs) according its instruction [36]. 20 µL of reaction mixtures containing1500 ng total RNA, 1.3 µL of reaction buffer (10x), 1 unit of poly (A) polymerase, and 1 µL of ATP (10 mM) was incubated at 38 °C for 45 min, and then, 65 °C for 5 min. After this step, the polyadenylation reaction product was then converted to cDNA by a poly (T) adapter universal reverse transcription (RT)-primer primer (5/ GCGTCGACTAGTACAACTCAAGGTTCTTCCAGTCACGACGTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTT-3/) as described previously [21]. All the test sample were placed at 42 °C for 60 min with inactivation of the enzyme at 85 °C for 5 min. No-template control (NTC) was applied in all RT reactions.

RT-qPCR of miRNAs

The RT-qPCR primers of miRNAs were represented by Oligo 7 software (version 7.0) according to Samavatian et al. [36]. In this test, U6 was used as a reference gene for miRNA quantification (Table 2). The reaction mixture (20 µL) contained SYBR PCR Master mix (4 µL, 5x) with ROX dye, forward and Universal reverse primers (0.5 µL), cDNA (1 µL), and DEPC water (14 µL). The RT-qPCR analysis for miRNA was performed in 48-well plates (ABI StepOnePlus thermal cycler) as per the following program: 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. The melting curve was drawn following the conditions: 95 °C for 15 s, and 65 °C stepped up to 95 °C with a step increase in temperature of 0.5 °C per 1 s. Fold change in miRNAs relative expression was evaluated by Livak and Schmittgen [35] method.

Quantification of PTOX content

The PTOX content in all samples was quantified according to the description of Yousefzadi et al. [37]. In brief, the dried cells (200 mg) were extracted using methanol (80%, v/v). After sonication, the collected supernatants were concentrated to dryness in a new test tube. Then, the residual was liquefied in methanol (250 µL). Quantification analysis of PTOX from all samples was performed by the HPLC instrument using a C18-ODS3 column (5 µM; 250 × 4.6 mm). The UV detector was set at 290 nm. The mobile phases contained acetonitrile and deionized water, as followed by a certain gradient timetable [38].

Statistical analysis

All measurements were calculated as the average of three independent replications. Data analysis was performed using One-way ANOVA assumption in SPSS software (Version 27). The average values were dissected using Duncan’s post hoc test (P ≤ 0.05). The chart was designed using Microsoft Office Excel 2023. Gene and miRNA expression analysis was performed using GenEx (Version 6.1.). Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Hierarchical Cluster Analysis (HCA), and Debiased Sparse Partial Correlation (DSPC) analysis were carried out using an online MetaboAnalyst program (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca).

Results

The changes in free Phe and Tyr contents under Phe feeding

To dissect free Phe and Tyr variation attributable to Phe feeding (0 and 1000 µM), we tracked the content of free Phe and Tyr in the treated cells (Fig. 1). The content of Phe ordinary induced in the first 24 h of feeding by Phe, followed by a rapid increment, showing its peak at 48 h, significantly higher than in the untreated groups, with an increase of 3.95-fold compared with the controls (Fig. 1a). Also, Tyr accumulation enhanced sharply with the first 12 h after Phe feeding, peaking at 48 h and significantly higher than in the controls, with an increase of 4.57-fold in comparison to its control (Fig. 1b).

The changes in H2O2, NO, and SA levels under Phe feeding

To shed light on the mechanisms underlying Phe-induced signal transductions, changes in the concentration of H2O2 and NO were followed in L. album cells in a time-specific mode. As shown in Fig. 2a, the content of H2O2 enhanced at 12 h in response to Phe feeding that reached 1.15 times the control. Also, its content decreased 24 h after Phe treatment, reaching an equivalence portion at other times following Phe exposure.

Analysis of the contents of (a) H2O2, (b) NO, and (c) SA after 6 h to 72 h of exposure to exogenous Phe in L. album cells. Values are the mean of three biological replicates ± standard error (SE). Different letters imply significant differences obtained using Duncan test at α = 5%

In comparison with the control sample, the most substantial difference in NO generation was found during Phe feeding, reaching the highest amount after 24 h of treatment (approximately 2.81-fold) (Fig. 2b). The data represented a reduction in NO generation after 48 h Phe feeding (13.73% less than in the control cells), albeit its level increased again after 72 h.

To examine the interaction among plant responses to Phe feeding and SA levels, endogenous SA content was measured (Fig. 2c). In a time-course experiment, SA level significantly raised in response to Phe feeding at the first 12 h of Phe feeding (1.67-fold increase compared with the control). Then, a significant decrease was observed at 24 and 48 h (approximately 1.4 and 1.29-fold decrease compared with their controls, respectively), albeit its level increased at 72 h (1.12-fold increase compared with its control).

The changes in PAL, and PLR actions under Phe feeding

To provide additional evidence toward the actions of Phe in L. album cells, the actions of two committed enzymes in the phenylpropanoid route that resulted in PTOX production namely PAL and PLR were assessed (Fig. 3). Compared with non-treated cells, Phe feeding significantly induced the PAL activity in L. album cells, which increased by 1.13, 1.18, 1.13, and 1.17-fold at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h, respectively (Fig. 3a). On the other hand, our results indicated that PLR activity showed a time-dependent oscillation, which significantly increased after 48 h (a 18.87% raise over the none of the treatment, respectively), although its activity readjusted at different times after Phe feeding (Fig. 3b).

The changes in the expression of PTOX production pathway’ specific genes under Phe feeding

Under Phe action, we specified the transcript levels of PAL, CCR, CAD, and PLR, which are responsible for the production of PTOX in L. album (Fig. 4). Regarding PAL gene, our results indicated that its expression level increased at 24 h, which was up to 1.33 times compared with the control (Fig. 4a). In the case of CCR, the gene expression level was strongly upregulated in the first 24 h in L. album cells exposed to Phe feeding, reaching a peak at 24 h of exposure (approximately 1.59-fold). Likewise, a considerable diminish in the transcription of CCR was found at 48 and 72 h (Fig. 4b). The CAD transcript level was transiently regulated during Phe feeding, reaching a peak value of transcripts (a 25% more than the control) after 24 h (Fig. 4c). PLR expression, a gene which directly regulates PTOX accumulation, increased significantly after 48 h of Phe feeding, achieved up to 1.51-fold increase (Fig. 4d).

The changes in the expression levels of miRNAs under Phe feeding

To study the dynamic expression of miRNAs in L. album exposed to Phe feeding, we selected seven miRNAs based on a previously published in silico prediction of miRNAs involved in lignans production in L. usitatissimum (Harenčár and Ražná 2024). The expression levels of seven conserved miRNAs were differentially altered under Phe feeding. The observations showed that miR156 expression increased strikingly at 12 h post-feeding, which was 2.47-fold more than the control samples (Fig. 5a). While the expression of miR159 significantly amplified at 48 h after Phe feeding, showing a 2.25-fold higher level relative to the control samples (Fig. 5b). For miR171, its expression levels were induced in the treated cells at 12 h, representing a 1.83-fold increase compared with the control, followed by a subsequent downregulation at 24 h post-feeding period, resulting in a 1.59-fold decrease relative to the control (Fig. 5c). miR172 showed an initial increase in expression in the treated cells, which was 1.18-fold more than the control samples. The lowest level of miR172 expression was observed at 24 h post-feeding period, which was 3.77-fold decrease compared to the control (Fig. 5d). miR393, and miR396 relative expression was significantly upregulated at 12 h, which were 1.55- and 1.24-fold higher than the control samples, respectively (Fig. 5e and f). Also, an increase of miR408 expression was detected at the 48 h of post-feeding period, which was 1.63-fold over the control groups (Fig. 5g).

The changes in PTOX accumulation under Phe feeding

To study the functional role of Phe in the biosynthesis of lignans, we addressed the content of PTOX in L. album cells exposed to Phe feeding. 1000 µM Phe led to a rising in PTOX content, reaching its peak at 72 h, remarkably higher than in the untreated cells, with an increase of 3.45-fold compared with its controls (Fig. 6).

Overview of metabolic and molecular changes in L. album cells under Phe feeding

PCA analysis was accomplished for shedding light on the interaction effect of Phe feeding (0 and 1000 µM) and sampling times on the metabolic and molecular characteristics of the L. album cells. It was implied PCA components 1 and 2 (PCA1 and PCA2) regarded as 93.8 and 4.8% of the total variance in the measurements, respectively. PCA score outline also illustrated that the separation of sample replications from each other was accomplished. Among the respective components, high diversity was detected in the Phe-exposed cells after 12 h, suggesting that this time notably affected the total variance in the average values (Fig. 7a).

Discussion

Based on the previous studies, we hypothesize that Phe feeding could change the dynamics of primary metabolism (amino acids and carbohydrates production) toward PTOX biosynthesis by SA-dependent signal transductions [13]. However, little information about the impact of Phe feeding on the regulation of genes and miRNAs that mediate POTX accumulation in L. album cells. The herein investigate studied the signaling and molecular mechanisms of PTOX biosynthesis in L. album cells exposed to Phe (0 and 1000 µM) at different courses of time. The current study indicated a sharp increase in Phe and Tyr levels at 48 and 72 h of exposure to Phe feeding, respectively. It has been suggested that a pathway for Phe catabolism in plants remains largely unknown because Phe hydroxylase is not present in Arabidopsis genome [39]. However, it can be argued that a folate-dependent Phe hydroxylase is discovered in chloroplasts of non-flowering plants [40]. According to the animals’ model, it has been revealed that Phe can be hydroxylated to Tyr preceding degradation [39]. Also, evidence from feeding studies in different plant species indicates that Phe can be used as a main precursor to produce phenylpropanoid compounds [8].

Among phenylpropanoid compounds, SA is a plant growth regulator, which has been discussed as playing a critical function in plants’ reactions to abiotic and biotic stressors [41]. SA defense action requires NPR1 as a redox-sensitive interplay to regulate different signaling agents of SA pathway that can affect other pathways managing plant resistance [42]. Consistent with SA functions in plant cell signaling, our results indicated that SA was sharply enhanced in the treated cells at 12 h of exposure. It offers that this molecule has a dual role by reacting as a pro-oxidant (ROS formation) and an antioxidant (ROS scavenging) under adverse conditions [43]. Likewise, at optimal levels, SA can induce ROS and RNS production at early stages of plant cell signaling and activate adaptive responses versus imbalance oxidative states; however, the excessive SA levels perturb oxidative status by ROS and RNS overproduction [20, 41].

In this research, an increase in ROS formations, measured by H2O2 content, was observed at 12 h of feeding with Phe (1000 µM) that either may control signal transduction pathways and defense-related gene expression or disturb cell’s redox homeostasis, leading to regulate the production of secondary metabolites [44, 45]. Furthermore, NO content as a cellular marker of stress was substantially increased during the Phe-time course feeding, providing evidence for the roles of Phe in generating NO in L. album cells. According to previous reports, NO is well-known as a small free radical, which easily diffuses through cell membranes and acts as a chemical messenger in plants against adverse conditions [46]. Therefore, NO may contribute to protecting plants from adverse conditions such as wounding, ultraviolet, drought, hypoxia, UV radiation, temperature extremes, heavy metals, and salt stresses [47, 48]. Our results are consistent with Sagharyan and Sharifi [13] findings, who suggested the role of Phe feeding in inducing NO formation as a dose-dependent behavior.

Based on our observations, the transient rising in the activity of PAL and PLR enzymes can indicate the incidence of a defense program, resulting in the formation of PTOX and/or its precursor molecules. In the phenylpropanoid pathway, PAL and PLR enzymes are involved in sequential enzymatic reactions, thereby promoting the efficient production of PTOX from Phe amino acid [49, 50]. These observations are consistent with the Tashackori et al. [12] and Corbin et al. [51] results, who previously discussed the central roles of these enzymes in regulating PTOX accumulation. It has been proposed that the synthesis and accumulation of lignans, especially PTOX, displayed a highly intricate reaction affected by several signaling molecules that mediate the expression of decisive genes encoding functional enzymes in metabolic routes [20, 21]. Likewise, we tracked the expression of four critical genes (PAL, CCR, CAD, and PLR) involved in PTOX production. It was found that Phe feeding could upregulate the transcription of these genes in a time-dependent manner. Therefore, there was a lag time between the PAL gene expression and its activity induction. According to our findings, it can be hypothesized that Phe feeding is more associated with changes in PAL activity than with PAL gene expression. Also, the PLR expression was upregulated after 48 h Phe feeding, demonstrating that PTOX biosynthesis was significantly linked to PLR transcript level under action of abiotic and biotic elicitors [6, 52].

Beyond the enzymes directly act to produce PTOX, miRNAs are negative regulators that can modulate PTOX biosynthesis [21, 23]. Several miRNAs, including miR156, miR159, miR171, miR172, miR393, and miR396, have been reported to alter the production of phenolic compounds such as PTOX and its derivatives [2, 25]. In this study, the RT-qPCR data illustrated differential expression of seven miRNAs, including miR156, miR159, miR171, miR172, miR393, miR396, and miR408 in L. album cells exposed to 1000 µM Phe. According to HCA results, there is a positive correlation between miR408 and PAL, PLR, and CCR genes expression (Fig. 7b). Changes in the expression levels of seven conserved miRNAs were significantly regulated PTOX-biosynthetic gene expression, especially PAL and PLR, in L. album cells exposed to Phe feeding (Fig. 7b). Through the data assessment using the DSPC network, the results highlights the crucial roles of specific miRNAs in regulating PTOX biosynthesis (Fig. 7c). could positively affect PTOX production by upregulating the expression of PTOX-biosynthetic genes, such as PAL, CCR, and PLR, whereas miR393 and miR396 negatively altered PTOX accumulation through negative regulation of miR159 expression. On the other hand, miR172 may negatively modulate PTOX accumulation by positively regulating miR393 and miR396, which act as suppressors of key PTOX-biosynthetic genes. Moreover, miR156 may act as negative regulators through indirect pathways, including modulation of H₂O₂ levels. The coordinated expression of PAL and PLR, potentially mediated by miR408 and miR171, suggests a synchronized regulatory mechanism that fine-tunes PTOX accumulation. These findings emphasize the importance of miRNA-mediated post-transcriptional control in balancing metabolite biosynthesis and provide new insights into the network-level regulation of PTOX production. These observations are consistent with the previous results, where we stated that MeJA in L. album could upregulate four selected miRNAs such as miR172, miR156, miR159, and miR396, which contribute to the regulation of PTOX-biosynthetic gene expression [21]. In P. hexanderum, Hazra et al. [2] discussed that five selected miRNAs, including miR172i, miR035, miR1438, miR2275, and miR8291 were downregulated during MeJA treatment, suggesting that these miRNAs can target PTOX-biosynthetic genes, especially CAD. Likewise, it has been suggested that some miRNAs such as miR396b, miR395, miR397, miR408, miR2936, miR502, miR1061-3p, miR1318, and miR3627 are involved in the production of monolignols in the fruit of pear [53]. However, we identified seven conserved miRNAs in L. album based on expression patterns, the direct interaction between the candidate miRNAs and PTOX biosynthetic genes has not yet been experimentally validated. In plant miRNA research, experimental validation, such as degradome sequencing or RNA ligase-mediated 5′ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (5′ RLM-RACE) has been widely employed to provide direct evidence of miRNA-mediated cleavage events beyond expression patterns [54, 55]. For instance, degradome analysis and 5′ RLM RACE has been used to validate miRNA targets in Salvia miltiorrhiza [56], and Camellia sinensis [57], confirming cleavage sites for several miRNAs involved in plant-derived phenolic compounds production. In the current work, the main objective was the identification of miRNAs and the analysis of their expression profiling. However, these finding provide insight into potential regulatory roles of the identified miRNAs, experimental validation of miRNA-target interactions is still required. Finally, we can conclude that Phe as a precursor feeding can regulate PTOX-biosynthetic gene expression and its content by interacting with different signaling pathways and differential expression of miRNAs.

Conclusion

PTOX production in L. album cells is a complex metabolic process. Based on the results, the biosynthesis of PTOX can be regulated by signaling pathways mediated by SA, NO, and H2O2 in L. album cells under Phe exposure. On the other hand, it has been suggested that the transcription of the PTOX biosynthesis pathway’s genes is likely to be regulated by miRNAs (miR156, miR159, miR171, miR172, miR393, miR396, and miR408), which leads to increased PTOX production. Overall, it can be deduced that Phe can upregulate the genes of the PTOX biosynthetic pathway through two possible mechanisms: (1) induction of oxidative stress, and (2) modification of some miRNAs expression, which target PTOX-biosynthetic genes. In the current work, we focused on the identification of seven conserved miRNAs and characterization of their expression patterns, providing an initial framework to better understand the mechanisms underlying the effect of Phe on PTOX biosynthesis in L. album. Building upon these findings, future studies aimed at experimentally validating the interactions between the identified miRNAs and key PTOX biosynthetic genes, using approaches such as degradome sequencing or 5′ RLM RACE, will be essential for establishing their direct regulatory roles and refining the molecular network underlying PTOX biosynthesis.
