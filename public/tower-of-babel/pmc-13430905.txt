Spatially resolved transcriptomic profiling of myelofibrosis spleen reveals compartment specific niche programs
Authors: Edoardo Peroni, Marco Pizzi, Marco Basso, Alessandro Atanasio, Elisabetta Calistri, Michele Gottardi, Antonio Rosato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyelofibrosis (MF) splenomegaly reflects not only extramedullary hematopoiesis (EMH) but a compartmental organization of the splenic microenvironment into spatially distinct niches. Using Spatial whole transcriptome profiling on FFPE spleen tissue from three MF patients, we interrogated three anatomically defined compartments: Intravascular (IV), Perivascular (PV) and Red Pulp (RP). Differential expression was estimated through pairwise compartment contrasts with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction (FDR < 0.05, |log2FC|≥1), and compartment “core signatures” were defined by directional concordance across the two contrasts relevant to each compartment. IV regions of interest (ROIs) showed an endothelial/adhesion and vascular stress program (e.g., PECAM1, VCAM1; antioxidant enzymes). PV ROIs were characterized by fibro-remodeling and immune-structured signals (COL1A1/COL3A1, LOXL1, MMP2/TIMP1; HLA‑DRA/CD74) including CXCL12, consistent with a PV niche coupling extracellular matrix remodeling to hematopoietic retention cues. RP ROIs captured an EMH-associated erythroid/heme program (ALAS2, FECH, BLVRB) with stress and inflammatory alarmins (S100A8/S100A9). Together, these findings support a compartmental “division of labor” in MF spleen, vascular interface activation, PV remodeling/chemokine niches, and RP EMH/redox stress, providing a spatial framework to interpret splenomegaly as structured niche dependencies and to prioritize candidate microenvironmental dependencies for follow‑up validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-026-00811-2.

To the editor,

Myelofibrosis (MF) is a BCR::ABL1-negative myeloproliferative neoplasm driven by clonal hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells and a pro-inflammatory, pro-fibrogenic milieu [1].

Persistent cytokine and growth factor signaling reshapes hematopoietic niches, promotes aberrant extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and angiogenic remodeling, culminating in bone marrow (BM) failure and systemic inflammation [2–4].

Splenomegaly, a hallmark of advanced MF, reflects extramedullary hematopoiesis (EMH) and likely involves a spatial compartmentalization of the splenic microenvironment into pathological niches that sustain abnormal hematopoiesis and immune activation [5–7]. However, human data resolving these processes within splenic microanatomy remain limited.

To interrogate compartmental biology in human tissue, we profiled FFPE spleen sections from three patients with overt MF using GeoMx DSP WTA. ROIs were annotated as intravascular (IV), perivascular (PV) and red pulp (RP). Clinical features, ROI selection, QC metrics and extended analytical details are provided in Supplementary Tables 1–2, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Methods. Pairwise differential expression was estimated using the GeoMx DSP Data Analysis workflow with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction [8]; compartment core signatures were derived by requiring directional concordance and significance across the two contrasts relevant to each compartment.

Despite the limited cohort, compartment programs were coherent and pathophysiologically interpretable (Fig. 1). The IV core signature highlighted an activated vascular interface with endothelial identity and adhesion features (PECAM1, VCAM1), basement membrane/extracellular matrix (ECM) components (COL4A2, COL5A1, FN1, TNC), oxidative-stress defenses (CAT, SOD1, PRDX2) and inflammatory/iron-handling mediators such as CCL2 and FTH1.

Compartment core signatures across splenic microanatomical niches in MF. Dot plots summarize the core transcriptional signatures identified in spatial transcriptomics profiling of MF spleen ROIs annotated as IV, PV and RP. Numbers in parentheses indicate the number of genes included in each compartment core signature (IV, n = 21; PV, n = 17; RP, n = 15); all genes fulfilling the core-signature criteria are shown. For each compartment, genes were retained in the core signature if they were differentially expressed with consistent directionality in the two pairwise contrasts involving that compartment (FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1 in both contrasts). Each dot represents a gene; color encodes log2 fold change for the indicated contrasts (IV-PV/IV-RP; PV-IV/PV-RP; RP-IV/RP-PV). This conservative intersection-based strategy highlights compartment-specific markers rather than contrast-dependent hits

Together, these transcripts support a vascular wall adjacent program in which endothelial activation and matrix remodeling may promote aberrant trafficking, adhesion and retention of hematopoietic and immune cells under inflammatory stress [9].

PV ROIs were dominated by a fibro-remodeling and immune-structured stromal program, including collagens (COL1A1, COL3A1), matrix-remodeling machinery (LOXL1, MMP2, TIMP1), adhesion/inflammation (ICAM1), antigen presentation (HLA-DRA, CD74) and chemokines such as CXCL12, CXCL9, CCL2 and CXCL2.

This pattern is consistent with a PV niche coupling ECM remodeling and altered tissue mechanics to immune organization and chemokine-mediated retention cues: conceptually a “retention and remodeling” layer that may stabilize EMH‑supportive microenvironments in MF [10]. The PV fibro-inflammatory program observed here also resonates with recent single-cell evidence in CHIP/MDS BM showing inflammatory mesenchymal-stromal cell states and stromal-immune remodeling [11], although GeoMx refers to MF spleen and it does not assign transcripts to single-cell stromal subsets but multicellular ROIs. Given the central role of PV stromal niches in hematopoietic regulation, PV remodeling could represent a microenvironmental amplifier of splenic EMH and inflammatory crosstalk.

RP ROIs captured an EMH-associated erythroid/heme and stress-response program, including ALAS2, FECH, BLVRB, GPX1, SELENOT, FTH1, CA2, S100A8 and S100A9, consistent with an EMH “output” compartment marked by erythroid differentiation, redox imbalance and myeloid activation. The absence of globin genes from the RP core signature should not be interpreted as absence of erythroid cells, but rather as a consequence of the stringent intersection-based definition of core signatures and the limited number of patients/ROIs. Thus, the RP program is best viewed as a compartment-level heme-biosynthetic and stress-response signature requiring validation in larger spatial cohorts [12]. This compartmentalization highlights a potential link between erythroid/heme metabolism and inflammatory signaling in the MF spleen, connecting microenvironmental structure to metabolic/redox stress and innate immune activation. Pairwise contrasts supported the same compartmental pattern.

Functional enrichment analysis of compartment core signatures further supported an IV endothelial/adhesion program, a PV ECM-remodeling and chemokine-rich stromal niche, and an RP erythroid/heme and oxidative-stress program (Supplementary Table 3).

Collectively, our findings support a compartmental “division of labor” in MF spleen: (i) an IV activated interface, (ii) a PV fibro‑inflammatory niche characterized by ECM remodeling, and (iii) a RP compartment reflecting EMH with erythroid/redox stress and alarmin signaling. This spatial framework provides a mechanistic lens for splenomegaly, suggesting that vascular activation and PV remodeling may create permissive niches that sustain EMH and inflammatory feedback loops, while RP reflects the downstream hematopoietic output and stress biology.

This study has limitations, including the limited number of patients, absence of non-MF spleen controls and lack of orthogonal protein-level validation. Accordingly, these signatures should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, compartment-resolved programs within MF spleen rather than definitive MF-specific alterations. Larger controlled studies using higher-resolution spatial platforms will be required to determine disease specificity and assign these programs to defined cellular subsets.
