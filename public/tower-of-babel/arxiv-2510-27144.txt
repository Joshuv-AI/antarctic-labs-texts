Calibrating Bayesian Inference


Abstract
While Bayesian statistics is popular in psychological research for its intuitive uncertainty quantification and flexible decision-making, its performance in finite samples can be unreliable. In this paper, we demonstrate a key vulnerability: When analysts’ chosen prior distribution mismatches the true parameter-generating process, Bayesian inference can be misleading in the long run. Given that this true process is rarely known in practice, we propose a safer alternative: calibrating Bayesian credible regions to achieve frequentist validity. This latter criterion is stronger and guarantees validity of Bayesian inference regardless of the underlying parameter-generating mechanism. To solve the calibration problem in practice, we propose a novel stochastic approximation algorithm. A Monte Carlo experiment is conducted and reported, in which we observe that uncalibrated Bayesian inference can be liberal under certain parameter-generating scenarios, whereas our calibrated solution is always able to maintain validity.

Introduction

Recent decades have seen a rise in psychological publications using Bayesian methods
(
Kruschke2021
;
VanDeSchootEtAl2017
;
VanDeSchootEtAl2021
;
volpe2025
, e.g.,)
. Bayesian inference offers intuitive uncertainty quantification using posterior probability measures. Drawing on (approximate) random samples from the posterior distribution, inference on model parameters, prediction of future data, and assessment of model fit can be conveniently performed in an analytics-free fashion
(
GelmanEtAl2013
)
. Off-the-shelf software for Bayesian analysis includes not only generic Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) samplers like JAGS
(
JAGS
)
and Stan
(
Stan
)
, but also programs designed for special modeling frameworks such as M
plus

(
Mplus
)
and blavaan
(
blavaan
)
.

Philosophical and statistical justification of Bayesian inference is rooted in making coherent decisions under uncertainty
(
DeGroot1970
;
Savage1954
)
: encoding
a priori
knowledge by a proper probability measure and updating knowledge by Bayes’ formula after observing new data. However, applications of Bayesian procedures in psychology have been largely instrumental rather than philosophical. Explicit justifications linking priors to researchers’ beliefs remain scarce, whereas the reliance on default or conjugate priors is widespread. Three rationales are typically offered to defend this practice. First, certain default priors, especially “weakly informative” and “objective” priors
(
BergerEtAl2015
;
BergerBernardoSun2024
;
DattaMukerjee2004
;
GelmanEtAl2008
, e.g.,)
, have been shown to exhibit desirable theoretical properties or strong empirical performance in the extant literature. Second, under suitable regularity conditions, the impact of priors diminishes as the sample size increases and the resulting posterior-based inference often resembles its frequentist counterpart in large samples
(e.g., the Bernstein-von Mises theorem;
vanderVaart1998
, Chapter 10)
. Third, sensitivity analysis is typically recommended to ensure the robustness of statistical conclusions to different choices of priors
(
DepaoliEtAl2020
;
Depaoli2022
;
vanErpEtAl2018
, e.g.,)
.

However, the appropriateness of the above justifications is often questionable when applied to real-world psychological studies. First, the principles of “objectivity” and “non-informativeness” in defining default priors are not grounded in a single, unified framework
(
KassWasserman1996
, e.g.,)
. Additionally, which default prior performs best is often contingent upon the model
(
YangBerger1998
)
. Therefore, finding one prior that exhibits universally strong performance is likely an elusive goal. Second, substantive researchers may have to work with small samples due to research focus or practical considerations. For instance, intersectional subpopulations defined by multiple social identities are often too narrow to amass data
(
Cole2009
, e.g.,)
. Statistical procedures based on large-sample theory can be numerically unstable or produce misleading inference in small-sample applications
(
VM2020
, e.g.,)
. Third, prior sensitivity analysis can be inconclusive. It is almost always possible to find a pathological prior distribution, such as one concentrated sufficiently far from the original Bayesian solution, in order to overturn the original conclusion. Bayesian computation can also be too computationally expensive to be repeated a large number of times. As such, prior sensitivity analysis is typically confined to a limited, arbitrarily chosen collection of priors, offering little diagnostic value for prior specification.

To accommodate the prevalence of “pragmatic Bayes” in reality, methodological studies of Bayesian methods focus on performance over repeated sampling of data and/or parameters. Indeed, inferential procedures that systematically produce erroneous conclusions in the long run should be rejected. To this end, a large number of Monte Carlo (MC) experiments have been conducted over the past decades, in which Bayesian inferential procedures (i.e., hypothesis tests and interval estimators) were evaluated under various data and parameter generating mechanisms and design factors
(
FinchFrench2019
;
McNeish2016
;
McNeish2017
;
McNeish2017a
;
PreacherMacCallum2002
;
SmidEtAl2020
, e.g., sample sizes, number of covariates, etc.)
. A major limitation of MC studies is that their conclusions are model- and design-specific, not readily generalizable to scenarios beyond those explicitly tested. Consequently, the credibility of findings from psychological studies using Bayesian analysis has yet to be fully established.

The present paper has two primary objectives: One is pedagogical and the other is methodological. Borrowing results from statistical decision theory
(
Berger1985
)
and inferential models
(
LiuMartin2024
;
MartinLiu2015
, IMs;)
, we call attention to the key notion of Bayesian validity: the principle that implausible statements about parameters are unlikely to exhibit large posterior probability too often, which justifies the long-run performance of Bayesian procedures
(e.g.,
Martin2022a
;
Martin2022b
;
Martin2022c
, a precise definition is provided in the Section “Bayesian Inference and Statistical Validity”)
. In the absence of prior knowledge, where the use of Bayesian methods is purely instrumental, we demonstrate that the stronger notion of frequentist validity should be pursued because it necessarily implies Bayesian validity with any prior specification. Methodologically, we propose a computational procedure to calibrate posterior-based inference to ensure frequentist validity. Our method leverages gradient-free stochastic approximation (SA) and manifold optimization
(
AbsilEtAl2008
;
Spall1992
, e.g.,)
. We apply our method to a linear-normal factor analysis
(
Joreskog1969
, e.g.,)
in an MC experiment, which demonstrates that Bayesian inference can be invalid without proper calibration.

The rest of the article is structured as follows. We first review the theoretical foundations of statistical decision theory, IMs, and statistical validity. Specifically, we elaborate on a crucial fact: Frequentist validity ensures Bayesian validity for any choice of prior. Next, we introduce a practical computational algorithm that calibrates Bayesian inference to achieve frequentist validity. A proof-of-concept MC experiment is conducted to contrast the calibrated inference based on SA against the original inference based on MCMC sampling. The paper is concluded with discussions on implications, limitations, and future avenues of research.

Statistical Validity

For any statistical procedure to be reliable in practice, it is desirable to establish that the procedure produces reliable results across varieties of scenarios. Specifically when making inference about model parameters, it is important not to repeatedly assign low possibility values to frequently occurring events in the long run. More and more Bayesian and frequentist statisticians, albeit holding different view on what probability represents, endorse the fundamental importance to evaluate the performance of inferential procedures over repeated samples
(
Grunwald2018
;
Martin2022a
;
Martin2022b
;
Martin2022c
, e.g.,)
. Next, we review the notions of Bayesian and frequentist validity. We highlight that Bayesian inference based on a posterior possibility measure satisfies Bayesian validity, under the assumption that models for both parameters and data are correctly specified. Meanwhile, procedures with frequentist validity are automatically valid in the Bayesian sense for all priors, provided the data model is correctly specified.

Bayesian Validity

Let

\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{\Theta}}

(4)

In (

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{\Theta}}\{\mathbf{\Theta}\in H(\mathbf{Y}),\ \overline{\Pi}_{\mathbf{Y}}\{H(\mathbf{Y})\}\leq\alpha\}

Frequentist Validity

In complete ignorance of the parameter generating mechanism, a safer option is to rely on procedures that are valid in the frequentist sense. Let
\pi_{\mathbf{y}}:\mathcal{Q}\to[0,1]
be a general possibility contour function (i.e.,
\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{Q}}\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=1
), which is not necessarily derived from any posterior. The
frequentist validity
requires that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{\pi_{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\alpha\}\leq\alpha

(8)

for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Stated in words, Equation (
8
) guarantees that, in the long run, any true data generating parameters will rarely (i.e., with probability no more than
\alpha
) be assigned a low possibility (i.e., less than or equal to
\alpha
). An important family of possibility contour functions that satisfy (
8
) consists of the survival function of a test statistic
T(\mathbf{Y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
under
\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}
evaluated at its observed value
T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
:

\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{T(\mathbf{Y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\}.

(9)

Resulting from the probability integral transform,
\pi_{\mathbf{y}}
defined by (
9
) satisfies (
8
)
(e.g.,
CasellaBerger2002
, Theorem 2.1.10 and Exercise 2.10)
. Note that constructions of the form (
9

-value functions

(
MartinLiu2014
)
.

The frequentist validity (
8
) implies the strong Bayesian validity (
5
) for all parameter-data generating mechanism
\tilde{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{\Theta}}
that composes
\tilde{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathbf{\Theta}}
and
\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}
:

\tilde{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{\Theta}}\{\pi_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{\Theta})\leq\alpha\}=\int\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{\pi_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{\Theta})\leq\alpha\}\tilde{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathbf{\Theta}}(d\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{\pi_{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\alpha\}\leq\alpha.

(10)

Under the assumption that the data model
\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is correctly specified, a major implication of (
10
) is that any valid inferential procedure in the frequentist sense is automatically valid in the Bayesian sense regardless of the parameter generating prior
\tilde{\mathsf{P}}_{\mathbf{\Theta}}
. In contrast, inferential procedures derived directly from a Bayesian posterior are not necessarily valid if the specified prior disagrees with the parameter generating prior. While it is feasible to evaluate model misspecification via goodness-of-fit diagnostics (i.e., correctness of
\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}
), there often lack reliable ways to assess prior misspecification except in MC experiments or in contexts of research syntheses. We therefore recommend that Bayesian methods, whenever used in a “pragmatic” mode, should be calibrated to achieve frequentist validity.

Calibrating Credible Regions

In this section, we present a generic computational strategy to calibrate posterior possibility contours and ensure frequentist validity (
8
). As input, we define a family of nested credible regions by thresholding an observed test statistic. We then use a gradient-free SA algorithm to calibrate the credible regions based on the test statistic’s survival function. Notably, the optimization program we solve is essentially equivalent to finding a “variational-like approximation” to the IM possibility contour (
9
)
(
CellaMartin2024
;
Martin2025a
)
. A unique contribution of our proposal is the novel combination of simultaneous perturbation SA
(
Spall1992
;
Spall2000
;
Spall2009
)
and manifold optimization
(
AbsilMalick2012
;
AbsilEtAl2008
)
, which enhances the scalability of the calibration algorithm and facilitates its applications in models of realistic sizes.

Test Statistics and Nested Credible Regions

In the sequel, we assume that joint models of parameters and data that are fully characterized by their density functions. Formally,
\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}(d\mathbf{y})=f(\mathbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\mu_{\mathcal{Y}}(dy)

is the

is a suitable dominating measure on the data space

\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{\Theta}}(d\boldsymbol{\theta})=g(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mu_{\mathcal{Q}}(d\boldsymbol{\theta})

is the

that governs

Specifically in the current work, we construct nested credible regions from two types of test statistics: the Wald statistic and the posterior density ratio (PDR) statistic, which generate elliptical and HPD credible regions, respectively.

. Geometrically, (
11
) is guaranteed to be an elliptical region because
T_{\rm W}
is a non-negative quadratic form in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Posterior Density Ratio Statistic and Highest Posterior Density Regions

Alternatively, we can define credible regions based on the PDR statistic:

T_{\rm PDR}(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})=-2\left[\log p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})-\log p\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})\,|\,\mathbf{y}\right)\right].

(13)

(
13
) is a generalization of the likelihood-ratio statistic in standard large-sample theory of maximum likelihood estimation. Also note that the PDR statistic (
13
) is a logarithmic transform of the “relative plausibility ordering” defined by
Martin2022b
in a more general context concerning partial priors. Similar to (
12
), credible regions can be constructed by collecting parameter values with sufficiently low PDR statistic values:

D^{\rm PDR}_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{R}^{q}:T_{\rm PDR}(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\xi\},

(14)

where
\xi\geq 0
. Due to the one-to-one correspondence between the PDR statistic (
13
) and the posterior density
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
, the credible region defined by (
14
) amounts to the HPD region.

Under further regularity conditions of the Bernstein von-Mises theorem
(e.g.,
BickelDoksum2015
, Section 5.5)
, the Wald statistic (
11
) and the PDR statistic (
13
) are both asymptotically chi-square. The chi-square approximations, however, can be inaccurate in finite samples, potentially leading to invalid inference. We next discuss a practical SA algorithm that can be used to calibrate the credible regions (
12
) and (
14
) to achieve frequentist validity.

Simultaneous-Perturbation Stochastic Approximation on Riemannian Manifolds

Calibration Problem

To achieve frequentist validity, we can calibrate credible regions generated by the test statistic
T
using its own
p
-value function. Let
\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
be the
p
-value function (
9
) of the test statistic
T
, which defines the credible region
D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{R}^{q}:T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\xi\}
. For each threshold
\xi\geq 0
, calibration amounts to finding

\alpha_{\mathbf{y}}^{*}(\xi)=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})}\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(15)

in which
\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{Q}:T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\xi\}
is the boundary of
D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})
. We term (
15
) the
calibrated
\alpha
level
at the threshold value
\xi
.
For any parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{Q}
, the corresponding
calibrated posterior possibility contour
is defined as

\tilde{\varpi}_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\alpha_{\mathbf{y}}^{*}(T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})).

(16)

Because
\boldsymbol{\theta}
itself is a member of
\partial D_{T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})}(\mathbf{y})
, we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{\tilde{\varpi}_{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\alpha\}\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{\pi_{\mathbf{Y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\alpha\}\leq\alpha

(17)

for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
, establishing frequentist validity (
8
).

We make further assumptions to ensure that the constrained program on the right-hand side of (
15
) is sufficiently regular. First, the feasible region is a
\xi
-level set of the test statistic (viewed as a function of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
), which is difficult to characterize without further restrictions. To enable a neat geometric characterization of the level set, we assume that the test statistic is differentiable in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and that
\xi
is a regular value of the statistic. Under these additional assumptions,
\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})
is a differential submanifold of the
q
-dimensional Euclidean space.
8
8

8

If the Jacobian
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{R}^{q}
does not vanish for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})
, then
\xi
is regular.
Second, we assume that the
p
-value function
\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is differentiable for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{R}^{q}
.

Optimization problems whose feasible regions form differentiable submanifolds of Euclidean spaces can be efficiently solved by Riemannian gradient algorithms (
AbsilEtAl2008
; see also
Liu2020
;
Liu2021
for accessible introductions Riemannian gradient algorithms and applications in psychometric problems). In particular, the Riemannian gradient ascent algorithm generalizes the usual gradient ascent algorithm in Euclidean spaces with two notable differences.
9
9

9

We consider gradient ascent, not descent, because we aim to maximize the objective function (
15
).
First, the steepest ascent direction can only be defined locally at the current iterate by the
Riemannian gradient
, which is obtained by projecting the ambient gradient of the objective function to the tangent space of the submanifold. For our problem (
15
), the submanifold
\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})
is implicitly defined as the level set of the test statistic
T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
; hence, the tangent space at
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, denoted
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})
, is the
(q-1)
-dimensional null space of
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{R}^{q}
. The Riemannian gradient is then defined by

\hbox{grad}\,\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\hbox{proj}_{\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left[\mathbf{I}_{q\times q}-\frac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}}{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\top}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right]\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(18)

in which
\mathbf{I}_{q\times q}
is a
q
-dimensional identity matrix. Second, a step taken along the Riemannian gradient direction leaves the manifold and must therefore be retracted back onto it. Using the notations of our problem (
15
), let
\hbox{retr}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})\to\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})
be a
retraction
at
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})
that satisfies the centering condition,
\hbox{retr}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{0}_{q})=\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and the local rigidity condition,
\nabla_{t}\hbox{retr}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t\mathbf{h})|_{t=0}=\mathbf{h}

(
AbsilEtAl2008
, Definition 4.1.1)
. By definition, a retraction maps a tangent vector
\mathbf{h}\in\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})
onto the manifold and preserves the direction of
\mathbf{h}
in the vicinity of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. A convenient method to define a retraction, known as a
projection-like
retraction, is to find the intersection of the manifold with a one-dimensional linear subspace
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{h})\subset\mathcal{R}^{q}
that passes through the point
\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{h}
and is transverse to the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})

(
AbsilMalick2012
)
. In our problem, we consider the linear space
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{h})=\{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}\in\mathcal{R}^{q}:\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})+x[\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{h}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})],\ x\in\mathcal{R}\}
, which is uniquely determined by the MAP estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})
and the current location
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. The corresponding retraction is then obtained by solving
x
from

\hbox{retr}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{h})=\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})+\chi(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})[\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{h}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})],

(19)

in which
\chi(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{R}
is the solution of
x
to
T(\mathbf{y},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})+x[\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{h}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})])=\xi
.

A further challenge in applying the standard Riemannian gradient algorithm to solve (
15
) arises from evaluating the exact gradient of the
p
-value function,
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. This is because the
p
-value function is an integral whose domain depends on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
through the test statistic. Although analytical derivatives are sometimes available via the generalized Leibniz rule
(
Flanders1973
;
ReddigerPoirier2023
, also known as the Reynolds Transport Theorem; see, e.g.,)
, evaluating them is difficult in general because the computation of the test statistic itself may involve optimization. An naïve attempt is to approximate the
r
th element of the gradient,
r=1,\dots,q
, denoted
\nabla_{\theta_{r}}\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, by an FD estimate:

(2c)^{-1}\mathsf{E}_{\mathbf{Y}|\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big[1\{T(\mathbf{Y},\boldsymbol{\theta}+c\mathbf{e}_{r})\geq T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}+c\mathbf{e}_{r})\}-1\{T(\mathbf{Y},\boldsymbol{\theta}-c\mathbf{e}_{r})\geq T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}-c\mathbf{e}_{r})\}\big]

(20)

for some small perturbation
c>0
, in which
\mathbf{e}_{r}
is an elementary vector with 1 on the
r
th element and 0 elsewhere. Because the bracketed term in (
20
) is an unbiased estimator of its expectation, we may hope that an approximate solution to (
15
) can be found by Riemannian SA algorithms
(
Bonnabel2013
;
TripuraneniEtAl2018
, e.g.,)
. However, this approximation has two problems: (a) the bias of the finite-difference approximation does not vanish if
c
is fixed, and (b) more evaluations of test statistics are required as the number of parameters increases, leading to a “curse of dimensionality.”

To address these issues, we propose an SPRSA algorithm, which combines a simultaneous-perturbation FD for all
q
parameters, which resolves (a), and a decaying sequence of FD rate
\{c_{k}\}
along the Riemannian gradient iterations
k=0,1,\dots
, which resolves (b). Let
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{U},\boldsymbol{\theta})
be a data generating algorithm, in which the random components
\mathbf{U}\sim\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}
and the distribution
\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}
is completely known. At iteration
k
, the simultaneous-perturbation FD estimator for
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})
is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\pi_{\mathbf{y}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})=

\displaystyle(2c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k})^{-1}\big[1\{T(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{U}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}+c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k}),\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}+c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k})\geq T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}+c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k})\}

(21)

\displaystyle-1\{T(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{U}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}-c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k}),\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}-c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k})\geq T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}-c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k})\}\big],

in which
\mathbf{U}^{(k)}\sim\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{U}}
, and
\mathbf{\Delta}_{k}\in\mathcal{R}^{q}
is a vector of
q
independent Rademacher random variables (i.e., taking values
-1
or 1 with 50/50 chance). It is known that, when
c_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, the bias of (
21
) in estimating
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\pi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})
is of order
o(c_{k}^{2})
. When the learning rate sequence
\{a_{k}\}
and the FD sequence
\{c_{k}\}
satisfy

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}=\infty,\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{c_{k}^{2}}<\infty,

(22)

the SPRSA algorithm converges to a local solution of (
15
) on the manifold
\partial D_{\xi}(\mathbf{y})
. To estimate (
15
), the calibrated
\alpha
level
\alpha^{*}(\xi)
at
\xi
, we run the SPRSA algorithm for a large number of (i.e.,
K
) iterations and compute the Polyak-Ruppert averaging estimate

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}^{*}(\xi)=

\displaystyle K^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big[1\{T(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{U}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}+c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k}),\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}+c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k})\geq T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}+c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k})\}

(23)

\displaystyle+1\{T(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{U}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}-c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k}),\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}-c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k})\geq T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}-c_{k}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k})\}\big]/2.

Note that the summand of (
23
) can be replaced by a single indicator function
1\{T(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{U}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}),\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})\geq T(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)})\}
; however, this seemingly more straightforward choice involves an additional evaluation of the test statistic in each iteration, which increases the computational load by roughly 50%. In contrast, the two indicator functions in the summand of (
23
) have already been computed for gradient approximation.

Achieving practical efficiency with the SPRSA algorithm requires the careful, case-by-case tuning of several aspects: the learning rate sequence
\{a_{k}\}
, the FD rate sequence
\{c_{k}\}
, and the total number of iterations
K
. Tuning details of the SPRSA algorithm in our numerical study are provided in the “Sampling and Tuning Details” part of the “Simulation Study” section.

Monte Carlo Experiment

Data Generation

Covariance structure models have been widely used for interpreting correlational patterns among observed response variables
(
Bollen1989
;
Joreskog1970
)
. Let
\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{Z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Z}_{n})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{n\times m}
be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample data, in which each
m
-dimensional response vector
\mathbf{Z}_{i}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)
with a mean vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathcal{R}^{m}
and a covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathcal{R}^{m\times m}_{+}
parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{R}^{q}
. In our numerical illustration, we consider a one-dimensional common factor model
(
Joreskog1969
)
:

\mathbf{Z}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\eta_{i}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i},\ \eta_{i}\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\psi),\ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}\sim\mathsf{N}\left({\bf 0},\hbox{Diag}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon})\right).

(24)

In (
24
),
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{m}
is the factor loading vector,
\psi\geq 0
denotes the common-factor variance, and
\boldsymbol{\upsilon}=(\upsilon_{1},\dots,\upsilon_{m})^{\top}\in\mathcal{R}^{m}_{+}
collects unique factor variance parameters. To identify the model, the common factor
\eta_{i}\in\mathcal{R}
is required to be independent of the unique factors
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}\in\mathcal{R}^{m}
, and the first factor loading
\lambda_{1}
is fixed at one. The following covariance matrix for the response variables are implied by the common factor model:

\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\psi\boldsymbol{\lambda}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}+\hbox{Diag}(\boldsymbol{\upsilon}).

(25)

To avoid bounding the parameter space, we log-transformed all the variance parameters: Let
\zeta=\log\psi/2
be the log standard deviation (SD) of the common factor variance, and
\omega_{j}=\log\upsilon_{j}/2
,
j=1,\dots,m
, be the log SD of the
j
th unique factor variance. We collect these unbounded parameters in the parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\zeta,\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{m},\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m})^{\top}
, whose dimension is
q=2m
. I.i.d. normality of
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, implies that the sample cross-product matrix,
\mathbf{Y}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{Z}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{Z}})(\mathbf{Z}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{Z}})^{\top}
, where
\bar{\mathbf{Z}}
denotes the sample mean vector, follows
\mathsf{Wish}(\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),n-1)
, a Wishart distribution with a scale matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and degrees of freedom
n-1
. Because the cross-product matrix
\mathbf{Y}
is a sufficient statistic for the covariance structure
\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we treat
\mathbf{Y}
as data throughout the simulation study. A direct data generating algorithm for
\mathbf{Y}
is
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{U},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{1/2}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{1/2}
, where
\mathbf{U}\sim\mathsf{Wish}(\mathbf{I}_{m\times m},n-1)
.

Simulation conditions were determined by two crossed factors: number of response variables (
m=5
and 15), and three parameter generating scenarios (Scene 1–3). The sample size is fixed at
n=100
. In Scene 1, the communalities of response variables were randomly sampled from
\mathsf{U}[.2,.8]
across replications, spanning low to high communalities
(
MacCallumEtAl1999
)
. The common factor variance was set to the communality of the first response variable. The unique variances were determined such that all response variables have unit variances. The induced distribution for
\mathbf{\Theta}
serves as
\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{\Theta}}^{*}
. In Scene 2, all response variables have fixed low communalities (.3). In Scene 3, all response variables have fixed high communalities (.7). Common and unique factor variances are determined similar to Scene 1. The parameter generating distribution
\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{\Theta}}^{*}
in Scenes 2 and 3 are Dirac measures (i.e., point mass concentrated at the true parameters). We used MATLAB version 23.2
(
MATLAB
)
to generate data; 512 replications were run under each condition.

Sampling and Tuning Details

We focused on a prior specification recommended by
(
AsparouhovMuthen2010
)
, using improper uniform priors for factor loadings and the inverse Gamma prior
\mathsf{IG}(1,2)
for common and unique factor variances. MCMC sampling was performed using JAGS
(
JAGS
)
. Because JAGS cannot handle improper priors, we specified a diffuse normal prior
\mathsf{N}(0,10^{10})
for factor loadings instead, which is nearly indistinguishable from an improper uniform prior. In each replication, we ran 5 chains in parallel. The number of adaptation, burn-in, and retained iterations for each chain are 1000, 10000, and 10000, respectively. MC samples of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
were stored at a thinning interval of 10 using the retained iterations from the 5 chains, totaled up to 5000 draws. The potential scale reduction factor (PSRF) and the effective sample size (ESS) for each coordinate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
were recorded in each replication. We deemed the replication convergent if PSRF
\leq
1.1 and ESS
\geq
100 for all parameters.

To evaluate posterior possibilities and perform calibration, we computed the Wald test statistics (
11
) and the PDR statistic (
13
). The MATLAB built-in implementation of the trust-region algorithm (through
fminunc
) was used to maximize the log-posterior with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
; we supplied the analytical gradient and the expected Hessian of the posterior to speed up the optimization. The same expected Hessian was involved in the definition of the Wald test statistic to approximate the covariance matrix of the MAP estimator.

Pilot simulations were conducted to tune the SPRSA algorithm. Specifically, the learning rate sequence was determined by
a_{k}=\alpha k^{-\beta}
, in which
\alpha=.1
and
\beta=.65
, and the FD rate sequence was set to
c_{k}=\gamma k^{-\delta}
, in which
\gamma=.05
and
\delta=.149
. It can be straightforwardly verified that these two rate sequences satisfy the condition (
22
). The number of iterations of the algorithm was set to
K=50000
. The FD perturbation at the last iteration is then
c_{50000}=.05\times 50000^{-.149}\approx.01
. Averaging estimates of the calibrated
\alpha
-levels were computed using (
23
). We implemented he SPRSA algorithm in MATLAB; were the paper accepted for publication, we will provide the source code in Supplementary Materials.

Evaluation Criteria

We evaluated the Bayesian validity of posterior-based inference with or without calibration. In each replication, we used the observed data
\mathbf{y}
and the data generating parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{\Theta}}^{*}
to compute the original posterior contour
\varpi_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and the calibrated posterior contour
\tilde{\varpi}_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. We then constructed respective empirical distribution functions (EDFs) for original and calibrated posterior contour values across replications in each simulated condition. An EDF curve below the diagonal line indicates conservative and thus valid inference, whereas a curve above the diagonal suggests the validity requirement has not been met. To account for MC error, comparisons with the diagonal line are benchmarked against its 95% normal-approximation MC confidence band (i.e,.
\alpha\pm 1.96\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)/512}
).

Discussion

Bayesian statistics is popular among psychologists for its intuitive uncertainty quantification, broad applicability to diverse modeling settings, and sometimes strong performance with small samples
(
Depaoli2021
;
MuthenAsparouhov2012
;
VanDeSchootEtAl2021
, e.g.,)
. However, drawing on the crucial notion of Bayesian validity from IM, we demonstrate that Bayesian methods can be unreliable over repeated samples when the specified prior for inference mismatches the true parameter generating prior. Since data analysts rarely have information about this true mechanism in practice, we offer a safer alternative: calibrating posterior-based inference to achieve frequentist validity, a stronger requirement that guarantees Bayesian validity with any parameter generating prior.

To solve the calibration problem, we develop an SPRSA algorithm that integrates manifold optimization with gradient-free SA. We then report an MC experiment concerning a simple one-factor model. We show that standard Bayesian inference with a popular prior specification can be invalid depending on the credible region type, the number of response variables, and the true parameter generating mechanism. In contrast, the calibrated Bayesian inference achieves validity in all simulated conditions. Additionally, we demonstrate that the SPRSA algorithm is scalable to realistic problem sizes common in psychological applications. Suggested graphical displays of calibration results were also provided.

The present study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, our simulations were confined to a simple one-factor model. Given the broad application of Bayesian methods, comprehensive MC experiments are needed to gauge how often popular priors lead to invalid inference and to highlight the general necessity for calibration. Second, the use of Wald and PDR statistics entails finding MAP solutions twice within each iteration of the SPRSA algorithm, which can be computationally expensive for complex models. Future work may explore alternative FD gradient estimates or test statistics to further alleviate computational burden. Finally, our method assumes a differentiable
p
-value function, precluding its direct application to discrete data problems. One promising resolution is to add a random perturbation to the test statistic, thereby forcing its distribution to be continuous.
