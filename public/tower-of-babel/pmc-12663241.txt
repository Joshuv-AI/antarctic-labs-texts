Low strength and a shared size distribution connect the hot and cold Kuiper belt populations
Authors: P. G. Benavidez, A. Campo Bagatin, V. Serra Cardoso, T. Santana-Ros
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding how the observed size distribution of Kuiper Belt objects emerged from the primordial planetesimal population remains a major challenge in planetary science. In particular, the similarity between the size distributions of dynamically hot and cold populations in the 100 − 400 km range calls for a unified explanation. Motivated by this observational constraint and by theoretical models of pebble accretion, we model the coupled dynamical and collisional evolution of the trans-planetary disc under the early instability scenario. Here we show that a common planetesimal formation mechanism operating up to about 400 km and modulated by an exponential taper shape, combined with a transition to a shallow power law distribution in the inner disc, can reproduce the current size-frequency distributions of cold and hot populations. This outcome requires assuming that planetesimals were weaker to impacts than previously thought. Our results connect formation models with current observations, thereby constraining the early conditions of the Solar System and the properties of planetesimals.

Introduction

The inventory of dynamically hot and cold Kuiper belt objects (KBOs) has been proposed to be nearly complete1 for objects with absolute magnitude Hr < 5.3, and this has been confirmed by dedicated surveys2,3. The achievement of near completeness in that magnitude range opens new possibilities to better constrain the early Solar System after the gas dispersal phase.

The Outer Solar System Origins Survey (OSSOS)4, along with following extensions referred to as OSSOS++2,3,5–8, provides a comprehensive debiased sample of dynamically hot and cold Kuiper belt (KB) populations (hereinafter HKB and CKB, respectively), together with their absolute magnitude Hr distribution. Supported by such surveys, the Hr distribution of the cold classical population was found to be accurately approximated by an exponentially tapered function2 in Hr ≃ 5 to 12 range. Later findings indicate that the absolute magnitude distributions of dynamically hot and cold populations in the 5.5 < Hr < 8.3 range (corresponding roughly to 100–400 km diameter range) are essentially indistinguishable3. Such observational evidence provided by OSSOS++ contradicts the previously prevailing view, when it was widely accepted that the cumulative size-frequency distributions (SFD) of the hot and cold components of the KB were well described by broken power laws of the form N(>D) ∝ Dq, where N(>D) is the number of objects with diameter larger than D, and q is the cumulative slope of the distribution. Specifically, at the large-size end (D > 100 km), the cold component was thought to have a steeper slope (qcold = −7.5) than the hot component6,9 (qhot = −4.5). The evidence provided by3 has posed a challenge to the previous notion that the primordial Kuiper belt displayed different SFDs slopes in its inner and outer regions, implying different formation processes.

The general understanding of the outer solar system dynamical evolution is encompassed under the umbrella of the new Nice model10–14. According to this model, the planetesimals surrounding the giant planets, remaining after their formation, were located in a disc extending about 22 to 30 au, with an estimated mass about 30 M⊕. As dynamical instability ensued, the influence of Neptune played a significant role in depleting that disc14. Most of the mass was dispersed at very large heliocentric distance, while some bodies were implanted in the current region of Plutinos, and others eventually found their way to the HKB.

There is a consensus on the hypothesis that the cold population, with a current mass of about 10−3 M⊕, was born in situ and remained primordial11,15, based on the large fraction of binaries still in the CKB and their distinctive red colours16. This argument was later reinforced by the New Horizons images of Arrokoth, showing scarcity of craters on its surface17, suggesting that collisional evolution did not significantly alter the primordial SFD of that population. Based on measurements by OSSOS++3, it has been suggested that the SFD of the cold and hot components may have maintained their primordial shape for objects in the size range of about 100–400 km. Accordingly, the same mechanism responsible for the accretion of bodies in their primordial formation region might be common to both populations3.

As far as planetesimal growth is concerned, the streaming instability (SI) model gives valuable insights into the initial phase of planetesimal formation by providing an efficient and fast mechanism for growing planetesimals from dust particles or pebble clumps within protoplanetary discs18–21. The SI is an aerodynamic process driven by the velocity difference between solid particles and gas and the reaction of solids onto the gas14. This mechanism led to the formation of high-density regions, providing a favourable environment for gravitational collapse and subsequent aggregation into planetesimals22.

Lyra et al.23 found that considering a pebble size distribution in the accretion process (also refereed as polydisperse pebble accretion) following SI was critical in planetesimal formation. According to this study, such a process was responsible for accretion of up to a few hundred km (about 300–400 km) at heliocentric distances as far as the current location of the CKB. This finding is essential in defining a common formation mechanism for the primordial trans–planetary populations (between 22 and 30 au) and the cold population of bodies up to such size, confirming previous conjectures3. According to accretion models23, further growth strongly depends on mass conditions at different heliocentric distances. This is the reason why accretion to Pluto (and possibly larger) size bodies was efficient in the inner region between 22 and 30 au. Instead, beyond 40 au, planetesimals should have remained as such, as further accretion was prevented by low mass density conditions in the nebula.

The inventory of large bodies in the Kuiper belt seems nowadays relatively complete for Hr < 5.3, while the SFD of fainter (smaller) bodies remains largely incomplete. Valuable insights into impactor SFD and collisional processes are achieved by exploring planetary surface impact craters. Thus, the surfaces of Pluto and Charon offer a unique opportunity to examine the crater population in the outer solar system. Robins et al.24 provide results that reconcile earlier discrepancies in the estimated crater size distribution slopes obtained from the New Horizons fly-by of both worlds. They estimate that the size distribution of small craters has a shallower slope than for large ones, consistent with previously reported results by Singer et al.25 (hereinafter S19). Nevertheless, according to the authors, the crater size-frequency distribution slopes are uncertain, in particular at small sizes. In fact, slope values for craters below 10–15 km is −0.7 ± 0.2, and −2.8 ± 0.6 for larger craters; implying a deficit of small KBOs below 1–2 km. Such estimated slopes are very sensitive to the choice of parameters and the craters used in the statistical analysis, and they must be taken as minima in the uncertainty determination24. On the other hand, Morbidelli et al.26 (hereinafter M21) established a connection between their re-examined Pluto and Charon cratering record and Arrokoth’s cratering record, including the dust measurement (down to 2μm approximately) obtained from the New Horizons space mission by NASA. Their findings revealed that the SFD shows a moderate decline below 2 km, which contrasts with the claim made by S19 of an exceedingly shallow slope. In addition, M21 argue that the shallow portion of the SFD extends to around 30 m before transitioning to a steeper slope to match the dust abundance constraint.

Collisional models are commonly used to study small body populations evolution in the solar system. However, since the adoption of the new Nice model framework, only two studies27,28 have examined the collisional evolution of the trans-planetary disc in detail. First, Asteroid-LIke Collisional And Dynamical Evolution Package (ALICANDEP-22)27 was applied to conduct a systematic study exploring different initial distributions for the trans–planetary disc and different scaling laws that govern the impact response. They got a good match with available observational evidence when it was still thought that the CKB and HKB had different SFD slopes at the bright end. Later, the Boulder code was applied to study the collisional evolution of the primordial KB and other related populations as the destabilised population and the Jupiter Trojans28. The main constraints used in the latter include the size distributions of the Trojan asteroids and craters on the giant planet satellites.

The presence of Pluto-sized and larger bodies in the hot population implies that the initial SFD of the inner trans-planetary disc must have extended beyond 400 km. Moreover, theoretical studies of planetesimal accretion29 suggest that, in the inner disc, a power law tail likely developed with a relatively shallow slope, enabling the growth of several thousand dwarf planets. When combined with an exponentially tapered distribution, characteristic of the cold population2, this configuration provides a natural explanation for the properties of the hot and cold Kuiper Belt populations within a unified formation framework.

Here we show that the observed SFDs of both dynamically hot and cold Kuiper Belt populations can be reproduced by modelling their collisional and dynamical evolution from a common primordial distribution. We adopt an initial SFD shaped by a shared formation mechanism up to 400 km, transitioning to a shallow power law tail in the inner disc, and evolve it under the early instability scenario. Our simulations indicate that matching current observations requires assuming low material strength for planetesimals.

Results

Outline of the model

We modelled the KB collisional and dynamical evolution using the ALICANDEP-22 package27,30. This model simulates collisional evolution within the framework of the current interpretation of the Nice model for the dynamical evolution of the outer solar system11,12,14,31. Dynamical evolution is carried out through statistical elimination of bodies from an inner disc and implantation into the current KB. The modelled scenario begins with the initial mass concentrated in an inner disc (outside Neptune’s orbit), ranging from 22 to 30 au. We also considered a small concentration of mass between 42 and 47 au, standing for the in-situ formation of the cold classical population. The initial population born in the inner disc undergoes three evolution phases, including collisional and dynamical evolution processes such as excitation, migration, and mass depletion14. Each phase consists of the following features. Phase 1: We call this the pre-instability phase, lasting from time t0 to ti. It denotes the onset of Neptune’s outward migration as it enters the inner disc. This phase results in an increase in eccentricity and inclination of planetesimals. Such dynamical excitation increased relative velocities (see “Methods”) and, therefore, the overall collisional evolution. Additionally, some smooth mass-loss during this period—according to dynamical models—is also included. Phase 2: This period is known as the instability phase. In our model, it lasts from ti to ti + Δt. In this phase, Neptune passed through the inner disc, which led to minor collisional evolution and significant dynamical depletion. During this time, most planetesimals were ejected from the solar system; some were moved inwards, while others were implanted into the excited Kuiper belt populations. Phase 3: This is the post-instability phase, lasting from ti + Δt to the end of the simulations (4500 Myr). At the beginning of this phase, the inner disc was already depleted, and the outer regions corresponding to Plutinos and hot classical belt populations were populated. Negligible collisional evolution took place in this phase.

In ALICANDEP-22, the outer region of the solar system is divided into three zones, representing different Kuiper belt populations: Plutinos, cold classicals, and hot classicals. The collisional evolution of each zone is performed taking into account mutual interaction between different zones32. The initial SFD is an essential boundary condition in the progress of populations of systems under moderate collisional evolution. In such systems, as in the case of the Kuiper belt, the SFD slopes for large bodies are not modified along the evolution; thus, their features must be primordial30,34. Accordingly, we must reproduce the gradual transition towards larger bodies currently observed in the SFD of the implanted hot component. Therefore, we propose that the initial population in the inner trans–planetary region combines a tapered distribution up to Dtr about 100–400 km, with a decreasing power law distribution for D > Dtr. Such population contained around of 4000 Pluto–size (and larger) bodies. Two values were tested for Dtr: 100 and 400 km. In the case of the primordial cold disc, an SFD identical in shape to the inner disc but with much less mass—and no population of bodies larger than 400 km—was chosen as the initial distribution, following the planetesimal formation mechanisms discussed above. At the small-size end, the cumulative Hr magnitude distribution is governed by an exponent αs (see “Methods,” subsection Initial size distribution). In the following section, we show results for primordial distributions with αs values of 0.4 and 0.3, which are our steep and shallow distributions (see “Methods,” subsection Model flexibility to match observational uncertainties, for a detailed exploration of the initial SFD parameters). The total mass of the primordial population is approximately 30 M⊕, assuming an average bulk density of 1000 kg/m3.

Observational constraints

The main constraint in this study comes from the debiased magnitude distribution of the hot and cold populations provided by updated surveys2,3. The OSSOS++ team graciously provided us with the magnitude distribution of each component, making this task easier. Due to the fact that the OSSOS++ survey may overestimate the size distribution of bright bodies2, we preferred to use the Minor Planet Center (MPC) database for the distribution of bodies with Hr ≲ 5.5. Several pencil-beam surveys have been conducted to identify faint Kuiper belt objects35–39. Bernstein et al.36 used the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) to measure the faintest Kuiper belt objects for which a direct SFD can be obtained. They detected three CKB bodies and none in the HKB population. Kavelaars et al.2 debiased the three detections in the cold population using the same procedure as for OSSOS++. This estimation is also used in this work as an observational constraint. In the Methods section, we utilize observational uncertainties to investigate the flexibility of the model while ensuring it remains consistent with the range of possible SFD permitted by the OSSOS++ data, including the debiased estimation by Bernstein et al.36.

We assumed the average albedo of each population (pr = 0.15 and 0.08 for cold and hot KBOs, respectively9,40) for size conversion. Both tested transition sizes Dtr = 100 and 400 km provide satisfactory results; however, we centred our analysis on the case corresponding to Dtr = 100 km, because it best matched the constraint of a smooth slope transition in the 100–400 km size range to larger bodies. In the subsection Considerations on the Kuiper Belt Populations, two additional constraints are discussed: the change in the SFD slope at 1–2 km and the Jupiter trojans SFD slope for 5–100 km size bodies.

Hot classical population

The match between the modelled hot classical population and the debiased distribution is excellent, particularly in the size range from about 70 to 400 km. For larger bodies, the modelled SFD follows the MPC database, essentially complete at this size range. Nevertheless, for completeness, we also explore the uncertainty range of the OSSOS++ survey (see Methods, subsection Model flexibility to match observational uncertainties).

The hot classical population was born in a massive disc, though with low relative velocities due to small values of eccentricity and inclination. That circumstance only allowed fair collisional grinding down to about 20 km. However, at sizes below 100 km, a moderate wavy behaviour in the SFD can be noticed, in the case where the weakest scaling law is assumed (solid blue line in Fig. 1a). Collisional grinding appears then subtle, and the very characteristics of each fragmentation scaling law leave minor fingerprints in the evolved SFD, even when delaying the onset of instability from 10 to 100 Myr (see Methods, subsection Time dependence). The shift of the minima in scaling laws down to some 20 m is crucial in the propagation of the wavy pattern, even at large sizes (about 10 km).

Cold classical population

Agreement of our results with the debiased distribution is very accurate for the cold component across the comparable size range (70 < D < 400 km). That is particularly evident when the assumed initial distribution has a steep slope at the small–size end (αs = 0.4), as is observed today. Further simulations constrain the possible values of αs in the range of 0.27 to 0.42 to be consistent with the error bar of the debiased estimation of the faintest detected objects in the CKB (black error bar in Fig. 1b). The uncertainties of the OSSOS++ also allow a modest variation in the slope of the taper function at large sizes. The threshold values defining the initial SFD are explored in the Methods section.

Negligible collisional evolution took place in this region, and the final distribution is a slightly depleted version of the primordial one. ALICANDEP-22 can keep track of the amount of objects that are disrupted, or just shattered and gravitationally re-aggregated. In this way, we find that—in the cold classical population—bodies larger than about 1–10 km are expected to be primordial and to have experienced only cratering events, even in the case the weakest shattering scaling law is considered in our model. Consequently, their dynamically relaxed initial conditions strongly support that this population was preserved in its primordial state due to negligible collisional evolution. This confirms findings indicating that Arrokoth is most likely a primordial body27.

Considerations on the Kuiper Belt populations

It has been pointed out in the literature that the KBO SFD exhibits distinctive features. First, a break at 100 km referred to as the “knee” was found, where the cumulative slope transitions from steeper to shallower. Also, a secondary break at about D = 1–2 km, known as the “elbow,” was suggested, where the slope shifts from a value in the range of −2.825 to − 326 to an even shallower slope with a corresponding q in the range of −0.725 to −1.226, in terms of cumulative distribution (see Introduction). The transition region between the knee and the elbow (2 < D < 100 km) must follow a cumulative SFD slope similar to the one observed in the captured population of Jupiter Trojans26,28 (q ~ −2), described by the red dotted line in Fig. 2. We faithfully reproduce such behaviour in all the simulations adopting a steep slope (αs = 0.4) for the current H magnitude distribution. In contrast, beginning the evolution with a shallower slope (αs = 0.3), implies that the final SFD will also be shallower than expected in that transition size range. This is due to the fact that collisional evolution is unable to increase the slope to the one expected for Jupiter Trojans. We show this in Fig. 2, highlighting the need for the chosen slope (αs = 0.4) to meet observational constraints.

The behaviour of the SFD in the vicinity of the elbow deserves closer examination. Following previous studies on crater counts on Pluto and Charon24–26, we consider the impact record estimated for Vulcan Planitia on Charon in S19, as it may best represent the primary population of impactors25. Therefore, we obtained the nominal impactor SFD and plotted it along with the outcome of our model (Fig. 2) by suitably scaling it to the Kuiper belt distribution.

The model that best agrees with the predicted impactor population of cratering record estimations features a steep initial slope (αs = 0.4) and the weakest scaling law. This is represented by the solid blue line in Fig. 2. The match is remarkable in the 0.7–10 km size range, approximately. The S19 impactor distribution at smaller sizes instead bends over along a shallower slope. There is limited data availability in the sub-kilometer size range, so uncertainties related to crater count are relevant. Figure 2b shows the elbow size range in detail, including uncertainty estimation24 within 2σ (for small bodies), using as a reference the number of impactors corresponding to about D = 1.5 km, where the distribution gradually changes its slope. Our best matching case reproduces fairly well the gradual slope change (between 1 and 2 km) confirmed by crater count , while for smaller bodies it is close to the upper 2σ slope uncertainty, for the estimated distribution of impactors. For completeness, the cumulative distribution estimated by M21 is also plotted in Fig. 2b. The slope of the SFD corresponding to M21 model is similar to that found in our study in the 0.1–1 km size range, except for their underestimated population (by a factor about 3) below 1 km. Given the uncertainties in slope estimation of the crater size distribution, mainly due to the smooth transition from large to small craters, S19 and M21 estimations are not necessarily at odds with each other, and our model seems to be compatible with both interpretations within uncertainty limits.

In any case, we find that the only way to reproduce the elbow is to assume a weak strength for bodies in the primordial trans–planetary disc. This finding is in agreement with the 2023 study by Bottke et al.28 of collisional evolution based on current Trojan asteroids SFD and giant planet satellites cratering data, requiring a similar scaling law to be matched. Thus, despite the relatively small influence of collisional evolution, restricted to an early phase and constrained by the onset of the early solar system dynamical depletion, it is still significant in producing a bump at 1–2 km and propagating the wavy shape up to 10 km in the evolved SFD. Observational efforts are imperative for improving our comprehension of sub-kilometre outer solar system bodies and refining our understanding of the distinctive features characterising such population.

While the SFD estimated by M21 broadly corresponds to our overall final SFD in the approximate size range of 2–100 km, differences show up at smaller sizes, particularly around 30 m, where the slope turns steep in M21. In our model, the transition to a steep slope occurs at about 100 m, rather than at 30 m. Therefore, M21 uderestimate the total number of bodies in the 10−2 < D < 1 km range, compared to our results. At the smallest size end (D < 10−3 km), both models tend to converge to the steady state slope. An explanation for such discrepancy may be that their estimation of the total SFD is derived from the impact cratering record of bodies associated with different dynamical populations, such as Pluto and Charon (in the plutinos 3:2 mean motion resonance region), and Arrokoth (CKB). The key difference in our model, compared to M21’s, is the ability to follow the collisional evolution of individual populations, including their mutual interactions, resulting in accurate outcome of the end–state overall populations.

Discussion

In summary, we show that the evolution of a primordial population following an exponential taper function, informed from the cold population, suitably scaled and combined with a power law for larger bodies in the inner trans–planetary disc, matches the observed populations of the Kuiper belt for large sizes (D ≳ 70 km) as reported by OSSOS++2,3. This finding highlights the intricate connection between the initial conditions of the collisional processes and the early dynamical instability phase.

Our study systematically explores the parameters defining the exponential taper shape in the cold population. We find that, in terms of absolute magnitude cumulative distribution, the slope should be in the range 0.27 < αs < 0.42 for small bodies, combined with 0.23 < β < 0.3 for larger bodies, to account for uncertainties in the observational data. When such an exponential taper function is suitably scaled and combined with a power law component with a slope between 0.18 < αl < 0.45 for larger bodies in the inner primordial disc, it produces an SFD that evolves to a final state compatible with observational uncertainties.

The analytical theory of Lyra et al.23 introduced a compelling concept of a two-mode formation scenario for Kuiper belt objects. According to this theory, planetesimal seeds preferentially accrete pebbles of rocky composition until they grow enough (hundreds of km) to start accreting ices efficiently. Our findings match this theory, describing the post-gas dispersal initial SFD as a combination of two characteristic functions associated with different formation processes. For the inner disc, these functions overlap approximately in the 100–800 km size range. Otherwise, only the exponential taper function represents the initial distribution in the less dense region, such as the cold disc around 45 au, where there was insufficient mass density to accrete ice and build dwarf planet-size bodies. This study reconciles observational evidence for the Kuiper populations with newly available knowledge about planetesimal growth processes through polydisperse pebble accretion23. In this way, we model the dynamical and collisional processes that link the formation of the planetesimal populations in the primordial trans–planetary disc to the current hot and cold populations SFDs, coming full circle.

Methods

The collisional model used in this work consists of two main components. One takes care of determining the impact outcome of any collision, the other one handles the collisional evolution, using the output of the first component as input to iterate the evolution of a given initial population over 4500 Myr. The entire code package is named ALICANDEP-22 and was fully described and tested in27,30. We provide here some details that may help understanding the model outcomes described in this work.

Initial size-frequency distribution

Our study aims to build a common primordial SFD for the cold and inner discs for bodies up to a given size. The following methodology was used to build up such initial population. Assuming that the cold classical population retains its primordial SFD, as supported by the estimation of the magnitude distribution (N(<Hr)), by Kavelaars et al.2, we build up the cold population starting from the following relationship:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N( < {H}_{r})={N}_{c1}1{0}^{{\alpha }_{s}({H}_{r}-{H}_{o})}\,\exp \left[-10\,\beta ({H}_{r}-{H}_{B})\right];$$\end{document}N(<Hr)=Nc110αs(Hr−Ho)exp−10β(Hr−HB);where N(<Hr) is the population of bodies with absolute magnitude less than Hr. Parameter values are: H0 = −2.6, αs = 0.4, β = 0.25 and HB = 8.1, from Petit et al.3, and Nc1 is a constant value. To estimate the mass, we assumed an average albedo, pr = 0.15, typical of the cold population, and bulk density ρ = 1000 kg m−3, resulting in a total initial mass M0cold = 1.3 × 10−3 M⊕. In this way, we also obtain the primordial SFD for that region with one largest body of about 375 km in size.

To build up the distribution of the inner population, we use the same functional form as for the cold SFD, but scaled up to obtain a suitable larger mass by several orders of magnitude. Such function extends the distribution up to about D = 800 km size bodies, due to the first term in Eq. (4), supported by streaming instability simulations45. Such distribution needs to be combined with a population of even larger bodies so that the SFD must be extended to larger sizes (dwarf planets and beyond) in the inner disc. The estimation of the population of large bodies is the topic of ongoing research in planet formation. In fact, the evolution of the streaming instability power law initial mass function (IMF) is yet under study. However, some research on pebble and planetesimal accretion suggests that the IMF becomes shallower for larger bodies due to accretion29. A slope of αl = 0.14 was estimated46 for such distribution, implying an upper limit of about 1000 bodies with Hr < 2 (dwarf planet size bodies). Nevertheless, such estimation turns out to be clearly smaller than the requirement of up to 4000 large bodies necessary for the grainy Neptune migration47 mechanism. Here, we adopt a slightly steeper slope (αl = 0.18) at large size range so that grainy migration is preserved and dynamical depletion allows for the right number of dwarf planets currently observed in the Kuiper belt. Therefore, the cumulative SFD for the inner disc is obtained as the combination of an exponential taper function and a power law for bodies larger than Dtr. Impact velocity and intrinsic probability

Abedin et al.48 estimated that impacts between KBOs can currently happen over a wide range of relative velocities, mostly well below 5 km/s, in agreement with previous estimates49. Therefore, we assume a Maxwellian relative velocity distribution around the most probable value (vp) for each modelled population. vp is adjusted to the dynamical conditions governed by eccentricity and inclination at each time step for each population. Collisional probabilities are calculated accordingly, taking into account the corresponding intrinsic collisional probability Pint. The intrinsic collisional probability of bodies occupying a given volume depends on the average relative velocity and the volume itself. The average relative velocity can be calculated as a function of the average orbital eccentricity and inclination of the system (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}_{rel}\propto \sqrt{{e}^{2}+{sin^{2}i}}$$\end{document}vrel∝e2+sin2i), while the volume depends on the range of orbital inclinations and the boundary orbital semi-major axes (see Table 1).

Time dependence

Dynamical models11,12,14,31 give a rough estimate of when the dynamical instability started (ti). In previous studies27,30 the dependence of the evolution on the onset of dynamical instability was searched for, reaching up to 700 Myr. In this new approach, based on an early onset, we test ti = 10, 20, 30, 50, and 100 Myr. The nominal duration of the instability time interval, Δt, is set at 10 Myr as a nominal value, but we also explore longer instability time spans up to Δt = 30 Myr, without significant changes in the final distribution. For all runs in this section, we consider the weakest scaling law.

Collisional evolution is limited for two main reasons in this system. On the one hand, as described by dynamical models, the mass of the inner belt has to be reduced to 20 M⊕ by the onset of dynamical instability. On the other hand, the characteristic initial SFD concentrates most of the mass in large bodies (D > 100 km), lowering collisional probabilities.

We explore the effect on the SFD of assuming different values for such onset time, and we find that delaying the onset of instability from 10 to 100 Myr has the only effect of slightly increasing collisional evolution, causing a dip around 10 km (from 3 to 30 km, approximately). In this case, the wavy pattern triggered at small sizes has enough time to extend to larger sizes (Fig. 5). However, no reliable observational evidence for the existence of such dip is currently available, but cannot be completely ruled out, as it lies below observational completeness (about 100 km).Fig. 5Effect of the onset time and duration of dynamical instability on the size-frequency distribution (SFD).Cumulative SFDs for the total Kuiper Belt population at different evolutionary stages, for varying onset times (ti) and durations (Δti) of the dynamical instability. Model flexibility to match observational uncertainties

In this section, we explore the threshold values of the parameters that define the initial distribution, producing an evolved population consistent with observational uncertainties. It is worth mentioning that, for completeness, we also explore the uncertainty range in the SFD at large sizes provided by the OSSOS++ project. Equation (3) defines the initial cold population, characterised by the parameters Nc1, α1, and β. Following eq. (4), the parameters for the inner population are Ni1, Ni2, αs, β, and αl. Our methodology for constructing the initial distribution begins with the cold population. The αs parameter was constrained using the error band for the debiased estimation for the three detections by Bernstein et al.36 in the cold population (approximately at 15 km). The β parameter was constrained using OSSOS++ uncertainties at large sizes, where these are more significant. Eventually, the normalization parameter Nc1 needed adjustment. Then, we build the initial distribution for the inner zone population. Here, the premise is to keep the slopes of the exponential taper component (αs, β) constant, established in the previous step, leaving Ni1 and the parameters defining the power law component (αl and Ni2) as free parameters. Ni1 and Ni2 are fixed to ensure the primordial distribution starts with about 30 M⊕, keeping the number of dwarf planets consistent with the grainy migration scenario47. For all the runs listed in Table 3, primordial mass in Pluto-class objects (D > 1800 km) is between 18 and 40% of the initial mass contained between about 1900 and 6000 bodies, in agreement with the grainy migration requirement. Furthermore, mass in bodies larger than 100 km is in the range of about 47–88%. According to our best fit, after 4500 Myr of dynamical and collisional evolution, the Poisson probability of having at least one Pluto-class object in the plutinos population is 0.73. The probability of having at least two dwarf planets (D > 1400 km) in the HKB is 0.80.

A deeper statistical analysis, such as a Bayesian MCMC approach, could be useful in the context of a more detailed investigation, which lies beyond the scope of this work. This method would allow for a probabilistic exploration of the parameter space, making it particularly valuable for population matching within specific size ranges, where more precise estimations of the number of bodies could be directly compared with modelling results.
