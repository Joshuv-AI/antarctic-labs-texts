Non-α-biased IL-2 enhances both intratumoral and subcutaneous CpG/α-OX40 therapy, unleashing systemic antitumor immunity in mice
Authors: Kejie Huang, Xiaobo Ding, Xuehuan Wen, Chaoming Huang, Yan Chen, Xujie Zhou, Leying Zhou, Yi He, Zhixiang Bai, Chunhong Zhang, Yi Jin, Jianmin Li, Zhenghua Fei, Wenfeng Li, Chen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe CpG/α-OX40 in situ vaccine strategy has shown efficacy in immune-hot tumors but remains ineffective in immune-cold tumors possibly due to immunosuppressive microenvironments characterized by myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) infiltration and T cell exclusion. This study aims to investigate the antitumor effect of non-α-biased IL-2 combined with CpG/α-OX40 in mice.MethodsUsing 4T1 breast cancer and CT26 colon cancer models, we evaluated the antitumor effects of combining CpG/α-OX40 with a non-α-biased IL-2 variant via both intratumoral and subcutaneous routes. Tumor growth, lung metastasis, immune profile, and in vitro function analysis were assessed through flow cytometry, transcriptomic analysis, and T cell suppression assays.ResultsThe combination therapy significantly inhibited primary and distant tumor growth and reduced lung metastasis in 4T1 models. Subcutaneous administration induced complete tumor regression in 55% (5/9) of CT26-tumor-bearing mice and conferred durable tumor-specific memory. Mechanistically, the treatment enhanced CD8+ T cell activation, metabolic reprogramming, and IFN-γ production, while suppressing MDSC expansion and immunosuppressive function.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that non-α-biased IL-2 synergizes with CpG/α-OX40 to overcome microenvironmental immunosuppression and achieve systemic antitumor immunity via a subcutaneous route, offering a translatable combinatorial strategy for immune-cold tumors.Keywords: Combination therapy, Immunotherapy, Myeloid-derived suppressor cell - MDSC, T regulatory cell - Treg

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment of malignancies by harnessing the host immune system to eradicate tumors, yet a significant proportion of cancers—termed “immune-cold” tumors—remain refractory to these interventions due to their profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironments.1 These tumors are characterized by sparse infiltration of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, dense stromal barriers, and dominance of immunosuppressive cell populations, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and tumor-associated macrophages.2,5 MDSCs play a central role in fostering immune evasion through multiple mechanisms: they suppress T cell activation via arginase-1 and inducible nitric oxide synthase, promote Tregs expansion through cytokine secretion (eg, TGF-β, IL-10), and establish hypoxic niches via vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-driven angiogenesis.6,8 Such microenvironmental resistance poses a formidable challenge, necessitating innovative strategies to reprogram these immune deserts into immunologically active landscapes.

In situ vaccination has emerged as a promising approach to overcome local immunosuppression. By delivering immunostimulatory agents directly into tumors, this strategy aims to convert malignant sites into hubs of systemic antitumor immunity.9 Among these agents, the combination of CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (TLR9 agonists) and α-OX40 antibodies, pioneered by Dr. Levy and colleagues, has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in immune-hot tumors such as A20 lymphoma, CT26 colorectal carcinoma, and Hepa1-6 hepatocellular carcinoma. CpG activates dendritic cells (DCs) via TLR9 signaling, enhancing antigen cross-presentation and Th1 polarization, and α-OX40 antibodies provide co-stimulatory signals to CD4+and CD8+ T cells.10,13 This dual action induces potent local and abscopal antitumor responses, termed the “in situ vaccine” effect. However, in immune-cold tumors like 4T1 breast cancer and B78 melanoma, CpG/α-OX40 combination cannot significantly inhibit tumor growth possibly due to persistent MDSCs-mediated suppression and exclusion of effector T cells.14 15 These limitations underscore the need for combinatorial strategies that simultaneously target immunosuppressive networks and amplify antitumor immunity.

Interleukin-2 (IL-2), a cytokine critical for T cell proliferation and survival, has long been explored as an immunotherapeutic agent. However, its clinical utility is hampered by a narrow therapeutic window: high-dose IL-2 activates immunosuppressive Tregs via the high-affinity CD25 receptor (IL2Rα), exacerbating immune tolerance, while also causing severe toxicities such as capillary leak syndrome.16,19 Recent advances in cytokine engineering have yielded non-α-biased IL-2 variants designed to preferentially engage the intermediate-affinity IL2Rβ/γ receptors on CD8+ effector T cells, minimizing Tregs activation and toxicity.16 Preclinical studies demonstrate that these engineered IL-2 variants enhance the efficacy of checkpoint inhibitors in lymphoma, colorectal carcinoma, and melanoma by expanding tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and sustaining their cytotoxic function.20 21 Despite these advances, the potential of non-α-biased IL-2 to synergize with in situ vaccination strategies remains unexplored—a gap this study seeks to address.

The anatomical constraints of in situ vaccination further limit its clinical applicability. Most protocols require direct intra-tumoral injection, which is feasible only for superficial or accessible lesions. Systemic administration of CpG/α-OX40 at equivalent doses as in situ vaccine fails to induce antitumor immunity, likely due to rapid clearance and insufficient lymphoid priming (data not shown). Overcoming these barriers requires innovative approaches to enhance the bioavailability and immune-priming capacity of subcutaneously administered agents.

Here, we hypothesized that combining CpG/α-OX40 with non-α-biased IL-2 could reprogram immune-cold tumors through complementary mechanisms: CpG/α-OX40 would activate DCs and suppress MDSCs function, while non-α-biased IL-2 would selectively expand tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells without exacerbating immunosuppression such as Tregs activation. Using syngeneic 4T1 breast cancer and CT26 colon cancer models, we demonstrate that this combination not only enhances intra-tumoral efficacy but also enables systemic immunity via subcutaneous administration—a breakthrough with profound implications for treating disseminated or surgically inaccessible tumors. Our findings reveal that subcutaneous delivery of low-dose CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 induces durable tumor-specific memory, suppresses metastasis, and reshapes the immunosuppressive microenvironment through CD8+ T cell activation and MDSCs suppression. These results redefine the potential of in situ vaccination, offering a clinically translatable strategy to expand immunotherapy accessibility to patients with immune-cold malignancies.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and reagents

4T1 breast cancer and CT26 colon cancer cell lines were purchased from Cell Bank/Stem Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, CN) and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination and cultured in complete RPMI media (RPMI media containing 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 50 uM β-Mercaptoethanol), 37℃ 5% CO2 incubator, CpG (ODN 2395) were purchased from SYNBIO Technologies (Suzhou, CN), Anti-OX40 antibody (rat IgG1, clone OX86) is obtained from Bioxcell (West Lebanon, New Hampshire, USA), non-α-biased IL- was provided by Junshi Bioscience (Shanghai, China), briefly, by introducing at least one cysteine residue based on wild-type IL-2, the binding plane of the IL-2 derivative and α receptor subunit is partially or completely blocked, while the affinity of the IL-2 derivative to the complex of β and γ receptor subunits is basically retained.22

Mouse antibodies including anti-CD45-FITC(30-F11), anti-CD4-SuperBright 645(GK1.5), anti-CD8a-eFluor450 (53–6.7), anti-CD8a-APC(53–6.7), anti-FOXP3-PE(FJK-16s), anti-PD1-SuperBright600(J43), anti-IFN-γ-PE-Cyanine7(XMG1.2), anti-Gr1-PE or PB (RB6-8C5) were purchased from Thermo Fisher (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), anti-Tim-3-PE/Dazzle594(RMT3-23), anti-Gr1-PE(RB6-8C5) was purchased from Biolegend (San Diego, California, USA). eBioscience Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780 and T cell proliferation dye Cell Proliferation Dye eFluor 450 was purchased from Thermo Fisher (Waltham).

Animal and tumor model

Bilateral tumor model

Female BALB/C mice (aged 8–10 weeks, weighing 18–22 g) were used to establish a dual-tumor model. A total of 10⁵ 4T1 mouse breast cancer cells were inoculated into the left fourth mammary gland. 2 days later, another 10⁵ 4T1 cells were inoculated into the right fourth mammary gland. 7 days after the first inoculation, all animals were randomly assigned to four treatment groups: (IL-2 group: IL-2 7.5 μg, CpG/α-OX40 group: CpG 50 μg, α-OX40 15 μg, CpG/α-OX-40/IL-2 group: IL-2 7.5 μg, CpG 50 μg, α-OX40 15 μg, control group: PBS). Treatments were administered intratumorally into the first-established tumor every 3 days for a total of five injections. Tumor size was measured every 2–3 days.

Single tumor model

Female BALB/C mice (aged 8–10 weeks, weighing 18–22 g) were used to establish mammary gland implantation model for 4T1 tumor model and ectopic tumor model for CT26 tumor model. For the 4T1 breast cancer model, 10⁵ 4T1 cells were inoculated into the fourth mammary gland; for the CT26 colon cancer model, 5×10⁵ CT26 mouse colon cancer cells were injected subcutaneously. 7 days postinoculation, the animals were randomly divided into the same four treatment groups as described above. Treatments were administered subcutaneously every 3 days for a total of five injections. Tumor size was measured every 2–3 days.

Tumor resection model

To model lung metastasis following tumor resection, 10⁵ 4T1 breast cancer cells were inoculated into the right fourth mammary gland. 7 days later, when tumors reached approximately 5 mm in diameter, the entire tumor along with adjacent normal tissue was surgically removed. 5 days after resection, the different treatment groups received subcutaneous injections on the left flank every 3 days (five injections in total). Lung metastases were assessed 45 days after tumor inoculation.

Tumor rechallenge model

In the CT26 colon cancer model, some mice achieved tumor-free status following subcutaneous treatment with CPG/α-OX40/IL-2. 90 days after complete tumor regression, these mice were rechallenged with either 10⁵ 4T1 cells inoculated into the right fourth mammary gland or 5×10⁵ CT26 cells injected subcutaneously into the left abdomen. Tumor growth was monitored every 2–3 days.

Lung metastasis quantification

Superficial lung metastatic nodules were quantified through India ink perfusion. After euthanasia, the trachea was exposed and instilled with 0.5–1.0 mL of diluted India ink. Gentle chest massage was applied to facilitate even ink distribution. The lungs were then excised, rinsed, and fixed in Fekete’s solution. Following 24–48 hours of fixation, unstained metastatic nodules were counted under a dissecting microscope. Nodules were classified by size as large (>1.5 mm), medium (1–1.5 mm), or small (<1 mm). A metastasis index was calculated as previously described using the formula: (large×3)+(medium×2)+(small×1).23

Tissue and blood processing

Single-cell suspensions from the spleen were prepared by mechanical dissociation through a 70 µm strainer (Falcon, Bedford, Massachusetts, USA) and treated with ACK lysing buffer to remove red blood cells. For peripheral blood immune cell analysis, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were treated according to their respective groups. Three days after the final drug administration, blood was collected via intraorbital bleeding and subsequently treated with ACK buffer to lyse red blood cells.

To generate a single-cell suspension from murine tumor tissue, the Miltenyi Mouse Tumor Dissociation Kit is used according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, the excised tumor is weighed, placed in a C-tube containing the enzyme mix, and mechanically dissociated using the gentleMACS Dissociator on the predefined program. The sample is then incubated at 37°C under continuous rotation to facilitate enzymatic digestion, followed by a second run on the dissociator. The resulting suspension is passed through a 70 µm MACS SmartStrainer placed on a collection tube. Cells are washed with buffer, centrifuged, and resuspended for counting and subsequent applications like flow cytometry. This method ensures efficient tissue dissociation with high cell viability.

Flow cytometry

For immune cell phenotyping, cells were first incubated with TruStain FcX solution to block Fc receptors, followed by staining with antibody cocktails targeting surface markers (1 µg per 10⁶ cells) and a fixable viability dye. Cells were then fixed and permeabilized using the eBioscience Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer Set or the Foxp3/TF Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher) and subsequently stained for intracellular or nuclear markers. Samples were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, California, USA), and data were analyzed using CytoExpert software (V.2.3).

ROS and NO staining

Peripheral blood and tumor-infiltrated immune cells were prepared as described above. Cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were detected using dihydroethidium, 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate, and nitric oxide (NO) was detected using 4-amino-5-methylamino-2′,7′-difluorofluorescein diacetate, following established protocols.6 Cells were then stained for the MDSC surface marker Gr1 and a viability dye. Fluorescence was measured using the CytoFLEX platform.

RNA Isolation and library preparation

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Premixed Version) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Transcriptome sequencing was performed by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China).

mRNA sequencing analysis process

Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw fastq reads were preprocessed with fastp to remove low-quality sequences and obtain clean reads. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM and read counts per gene were generated with HTSeq-count. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted in R (v3.2.0) to assess sample reproducibility. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with a Q value <0.05 and |log₂(fold change)| > 1. Hierarchical clustering of DEGs was visualized in R. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was carried out with GSEA software using predefined gene sets to identify enriched pathways based on ranked differential expression.

Cell type deconvolution from bulk RNA sequencing

Cellular composition of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) was inferred using the BayesPrism deconvolution algorithm,24 a Bayesian method that estimates cell type proportions and cell-type-specific expression from bulk RNA-seq data. A publicly available 5 k Mouse PBMC single-cell RNA-seq dataset from 10x Genomics was used as the reference (https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets/5k_Mouse_PBMCs_5p_gem-x). The algorithm produced a cell type proportion matrix for each sample, enabling group comparisons. Posterior mean estimates of cell-type-specific expression (Z matrix) were extracted, with a focus on polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) for subsequent differential expression and functional profiling via heatmap visualization.

In vitro T cell suppression assay

Peripheral blood is first collected via intra-orbital puncture into anticoagulant-treated tubes, followed by red blood cell lysis using ACK buffer to obtain a leukocyte suspension. The cells are then Fc receptor-blocked with an anti-CD16/32 antibody to prevent nonspecific antibody binding and subsequently stained with Gr-1-PE antibodies, along with a viability dye to exclude dead cells. After washing, the stained cells are resuspended in sorting buffer and passed through a cell strainer to ensure a single-cell suspension for sorting. The target MDSCs population is identified and gated as live, and Gr-1-PE-positive cells, which are then sorted under sterile conditions into collection tubes for functional assays or culture, with the sorted population’s purity confirmed by post-sort flow cytometric analysis.

T cell suppression assay was described previously.25 Briefly, splenic T cells isolated with MagCellect Mouse CD3+ T Cell Isolation Kit (R&D) were labeled with eFluor 450 proliferation dye and cultured with MDSCs at 1:10 and 1:20 E:T ratios in 96-well flat-bottom plates coated with 5 µg/mL anti-mouse CD3e antibody (clone 145-2 C11, BD) and free anti-mouse CD28 antibody (2 µg/mL, clone 37.51, BD) for 4 days, then collected the cells and stained with anti-CD4-SuperBright 645(GK1.5) and anti-CD8a-APC(53–6.7), followed with cell viability dye staining. Fixed the cells and analyzed the samples on CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter, Brea, California, USA).

Statistics

The results are presented as the mean with each individual data point or in bar graph Mean±SD. GraphPad Prism (Prism V9.5.0; GraphPad Software) was used to perform statistical analysis. Data were analyzed using Welch t-test, Wilcoxon signed rank test, or one-way ANOVA. A p value less than 0.05 was considered significant (****p<0.0001, ***p<0.001, **p<0.01, and *p<0.05).

Results

Non-α-biased IL-2 enhances intratumoral CpG/α-OX40 efficacy in immune-cold tumors

The intratumoral administration of CpG/α-OX40, an in situ vaccination strategy pioneered by Dr. Levy, has demonstrated robust local and systemic antitumor immunity in immune-hot models such as A20 lymphoma and Hepa1-6 liver cancer.10,13 This approach leverages TLR9 activation by CpG to recruit and activate DCs, while α-OX40 agonism depletes intra-tumoral regulatory T cells (Tregs) and co-stimulates effector T cells. However, in immune-cold tumors like 4T1 breast cancer and B78 melanoma, CpG/α-OX40 combination fails to induce significant tumor control possibly due to dominant immunosuppressive mechanisms, including MDSCs infiltration and T cell exclusion.15 To address this limitation, we evaluated whether non-α-biased IL-2, a cytokine engineered to selectively activate CD8+ T cells without stimulating Tregs, could enhance the therapeutic efficacy of CpG/α-OX40.

In a bilateral 4T1 breast cancer model, mice were randomized into four groups: PBS control, IL-2 monotherapy (7.5 µg), CpG/α-OX40 (50 µg CpG+15 µg α-OX40), and CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 combination (figure 1A). CpG/α-OX40 intratumoral injection marginally delayed primary tumor growth (p<0.05 vs PBS; figure 1B,D) but had no significant effect on distant tumors (figure 1C,E). IL-2 alone showed negligible antitumor activity in both primary and distant lesions. In contrast, the combination of CpG/α-OX40 with IL-2 achieved robust inhibition of primary tumor growth, with the lowest tumor weight observed at the study endpoint (p<0.0001; figure 1F). Importantly, distant tumor progression was significantly delayed in the combination group (p<0.01; figure 1G), indicating that IL-2 synergizes with CpG/α-OX40 to convert a localized immune response into systemic antitumor immunity. These results suggest that the immune response induced by CpG/α-OX40 intratumoral injection is insufficient to control distant tumors, but the addition of non-α-biased IL2 enhances both local and systemic antitumor effects.

Subcutaneous administration of CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 inhibits primary tumor growth and metastasis

A critical limitation of in situ vaccination is its reliance on direct tumor accessibility, which restricts clinical applicability to superficial or resectable lesions. Intratumoral administration of CpG/α-OX40 at the same dose (50 µg CpG+15 µg α-OX40) has previously failed to inhibit 4T1 tumor growth due to insufficient immune activation.15 Here, we tested whether combining CpG/α-OX40 with non-α-biased IL-2 (7.5 µg) could overcome this limitation and enable systemic efficacy via subcutaneous delivery.

In a unilateral 4T1 subcutaneous tumor model, mice were treated with PBS, IL-2 alone, CpG/α-OX40, or CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 combination subcutaneously away from the tumor site (figure 2A). The CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 combination therapy significantly suppressed primary tumor growth, with tumor volume reduced by over 70% compared with PBS (p<0.0001; figure 2B-D, online supplemental figure S1A). Lung metastasis analysis revealed a significant reduction in metastatic foci (p<0.05; figure 2E) and a lower lung metastasis index in the combination group compared with control and IL-2 group (p<0.05; figure 2F). Notably, IL-2 monotherapy increased the number of small metastatic lesions (p<0.01; figure 2I), underscoring the necessity of combinatorial treatment. Safety assessments confirmed no significant body weight loss or signs of capillary leak syndrome (online supplemental figure S1B), suggesting a favorable safety profile for the combination therapy.

CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 suppresses postsurgical metastasis and induces tumor-specific immunity

To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of subcutaneous CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 in a clinically relevant setting, we employed a postsurgical 4T1 metastasis model. After surgical resection of the primary tumor, mice were treated with PBS, IL-2 alone, CpG/α-OX40, or CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 combination (figure 3A). Both CpG/α-OX40 and CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 combination therapy reduced lung metastasis foci over 80% (p<0.05; figure 3B), whereas IL-2 alone showed no benefit. These results demonstrate that CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 combination can prevent postsurgical metastasis, a critical challenge in cancer therapy.

To assess antigen-specific memory, CT26 colon cancer-bearing mice were treated subcutaneously with CpG/α-OX40/IL-2. The combination induced complete tumor regression in 55% of mice (5/9; figure 3C-E) and prolonged median survival from 28 days (PBS group) to >150 days (p<0.001; figure 3F). Tumor rechallenge experiments confirmed durable CT26-specific immunity, with no recurrence observed 30 days postrechallenge (figure 3G). However, these mice remained susceptible to 4T1 tumor growth (figure 3H), highlighting the antigen-dependent nature of immune memory. These findings suggest that CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 subcutaneous injection induces robust tumor-specific immunity.

CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 drives CD8+ T cell activation and metabolic reprogramming

To elucidate the immunological mechanisms underlying the enhanced antitumor efficacy of CpG/α-OX40/IL-2, we performed transcriptome sequencing of peripheral blood immune cells isolated from 4T1 tumor-bearing mice after CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 subcutaneous administration. PCA revealed distinct clustering of the CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 group, indicating significant changes in immune cell composition and gene expression (figure 4A). Deconvolution analysis demonstrated an increase in CD8+ T cell frequency (p<0.001; figure 4B,C), reduction of Tregs, and elevation in the CD8+ T cell/Tregs ratio (p<0.01; figures 4D, 5G-I). These findings suggest that the combination therapy enhances CD8+ T cell activation while minimizing Tregs-mediated immunosuppression.

The proportion of CD8+ T cells was significantly higher in the CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 group (p<0.001; figure 5A–C, (online supplemental figure S3A, gating strategy shown in online supplemental figure S2), and the frequencies of IFNγ-producing CD8+ and CD4+ T cells were also elevated (p<0.01; figure 5D–F, online supplemental figure S3B). Additionally, the combination therapy reduced the proportion of exhausted PD1+CD8+ and Tim3+CD8+ T cells (p<0.05; figure 5J,K), indicating that IL-2 enhances T cell functionality and mitigates exhaustion.

GSEA of the transcriptomic data revealed enrichment of metabolic pathways, including linoleic acid metabolism, retinol metabolism, and metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, in the CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 group compared with CpG/α-OX40 alone (online supplemental figure S4A and B). These findings suggest that IL-2 enhances T cell metabolic fitness, supporting sustained effector function and proliferation.

CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 suppresses MDSCs expansion and immunosuppressive function

MDSCs play a pivotal role in fostering tumor progression by suppressing T cell activation and promoting Tregs induction. To investigate the impact of CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 on MDSCs, we analyzed the transcriptomic data and performed functional assays.

Transcriptome analysis and flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant reduction in the proportion of monocytic MDSCs (Ly6C+Ly6G-CD11b+) in the CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 group compared with PBS (p<0.05; figure 4C, online supplemental figure S3C), with a trend toward decreased granulocytic MDSCs (Ly6C-Ly6G+CD11b+). The expression of MDSC-related immunosuppressive genes, including TGF-β1, HIF-1α, and VEGFα, was also significantly lower in the combination group (figure 6A,B). These findings suggest that CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 disrupts the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by targeting MDSCs expansion and function.

To further validate these results, we quantified the production of ROS and NO by MDSCs isolated from peripheral blood or tumor of tumor-bearing mice. MDSCs from CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 treated mice produced less ROS and NO compared with those from PBS treated mice (p<0.001; figure 6C, online supplemental figure S3D). Additionally, functional suppression assays demonstrated that MDSCs from the CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 combination group exhibited a significantly reduced capacity to inhibit in vitro CD4+ and CD8+ T cell proliferation (p<0.001; figure 6D,E). These results collectively indicate that CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 therapy not only reduces MDSCs numbers but also impairs their immunosuppressive activity, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity.

Discussion

The combination of CpG/α-OX40 and non-α-biased IL-2 represents a transformative approach to overcoming resistance in immune-cold tumors, a challenge that has long hindered the efficacy of in situ vaccination strategies. Originally pioneered by Dr. Levy, the CpG/α-OX40 regimen functions as an in situ vaccine by activating intra-tumoral DCs via TLR9 agonism and depleting OX40-expressing regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby promoting effector T cell responses in immune-hot tumors such as A20 lymphoma and Hepa1-6 liver cancer.10 11 However, its limited efficacy in immune-cold tumors like 4T1 breast cancer and B78 melanoma underscores the dominance of immunosuppressive mechanisms in these models, including MDSC infiltration and stromal exclusion of cytotoxic T cells.14 15 A lot of efforts are made to improve the efficacy of CpG/α-OX40 intra-tumor treatment by improving the immune cell infiltration, including radiotherapy,15 immune checkpoint inhibitor α-PD1,26 α-CTLA-414 and B cell activator IL12-Fc,27 chemotherapeutic drug Adriamycin (ADM),13 targeted therapy drug Anlotinib,28 etc. Our findings demonstrate that the addition of non-α-biased IL-2 to CpG/α-OX40 not only enhances intra-tumoral efficacy but also enables systemic antitumor immunity through subcutaneous administration—a breakthrough with profound clinical implications.

The synergy between CpG/α-OX40 and non-α-biased IL-2 hinges on their complementary mechanisms. Anti-OX40 therapy (OX86 clone) is less effective at depleting Tregs but can activate effector T cells. Also, our results showed that CpG/α-OX40 treatment didn’t suppress Tregs effectively. The non-α-biased IL-2 we used here alone can activate both Tregs and effector T cells, in consequence of increased lung metastasis, but the effect of Tregs cell induction was minimized when used together with CpG/OX40. This is evidenced by the significant elevation of CD8+ T cell/Tregs ratios in peripheral blood and splenic compartments following combination therapy (figures 4C and 5G–I). The ability of non-α-biased IL-2 to engage CD122/CD132 receptors on effector T cells, rather than the high-affinity IL2Rα receptor on Tregs, is critical to this selectivity.29 30 Furthermore, IL-2-driven metabolic reprogramming of CD8+ T cells, as indicated by enrichment in linoleic acid and retinol metabolism pathways (online supplemental figure S4A and B), likely sustains their cytotoxic function and mitigates exhaustion markers such as PD-1 and Tim-3 (figure 5J,K). These findings align with recent studies highlighting the role of IL-2 variants in enhancing T cell metabolic fitness and persistence.31 32

Equally critical is the suppression of MDSCs, which are central to maintaining immunosuppressive niches in immune-cold tumors.33 CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 therapy reduced both monocytic (Ly6C+) and granulocytic (Ly6G+) MDSCs subsets (figure 6A), while downregulating immunosuppressive mediators such as TGF-β1, HIF-1α, and VEGFα (figure 6B). The diminished capacity of MDSCs from combination-treated mice to produce ROS and NO—key mediators of T cell suppression—further underscores the therapeutic impact (figure 6C–E). These effects may stem from CpG’s ability to repolarize myeloid cells toward immunostimulatory phenotypes via TLR9 signaling,34,37 coupled with IL-2-driven IFNγ production by CD8+ T cells. The disruption of hypoxia-MDSCs feedforward loops, as suggested by reduced HIF-1α levels, represents another plausible mechanism.38 39

The clinical significance of this study lies in its demonstration of subcutaneous efficacy at low drug doses. Traditional in situ vaccines require direct tumor access, limiting their utility to superficial or resectable lesions. By contrast, subcutaneous delivery of CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 induced systemic immunity in 4T1 and CT26 models, suppressing primary tumors, metastases, and postsurgical recurrence without dose escalation (figures2 3). This is particularly notable given that prior attempts to achieve systemic effects with intra-tumoral CpG/α-OX40 alone failed due to insufficient lymphoid priming.15 The addition of IL-2 likely enhances vaccine bioavailability by promoting T cell trafficking to lymphoid tissues, as observed in melanoma models using IL-2-engineered therapies.40 41 The absence of capillary leak syndrome or significant toxicity (online supplemental figure S1B) suggests a favorable safety profile, addressing a longstanding barrier to IL-2’s clinical use.

The durable tumor-specific immunity induced by CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 in CT26 colon cancer models further underscores the potential of this combination. Tumor rechallenge experiments revealed that mice achieving complete regression after subcutaneous treatment were resistant to CT26 rechallenge but not to 4T1 rechallenge. This result clearly demonstrates that the immune response elicited by CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 is tumor-specific rather than non-specific, highlighting the importance of tumor antigenicity in shaping therapeutic outcomes. Importantly, this finding also suggests that the combination therapy does not induce excessive tumor non-specific immune system activation, which could lead to off-target toxicity.

The clinical implications of this study are far-reaching. By enabling subcutaneous administration, CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 expands the applicability of in situ vaccines to a broader range of patients, including those with deep-seated or metastatic tumors. Moreover, the low-dose regimen used in this study minimizes the risk of systemic toxicity, a common limitation of cytokine-based therapies. Future studies should explore the combination of CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 with other immunomodulatory agents, such as PD-1 inhibitors, to further enhance efficacy and address residual T cell exhaustion. Future studies should explore biomarkers such as CD8+ T cell/Tregs ratios, MDSCs levels, and tumor mutational burden to stratify patients most likely to benefit from this regimen.

In conclusion, our work redefines the potential of in situ vaccines by demonstrating that CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 combination therapy can overcome microenvironmental resistance in immune-cold tumors and achieve systemic efficacy through subcutaneous delivery. By simultaneously enhancing CD8+ T cell activation and suppressing MDSCs-mediated immunosuppression, this strategy addresses two major barriers to immunotherapy success (detailed in figure 7). These findings pave the way for clinical trials evaluating subcutaneous CpG/α-OX40/IL-2 in patients with advanced or inaccessible tumors, potentially expanding the reach of cancer immunotherapy to previously untreatable populations.
