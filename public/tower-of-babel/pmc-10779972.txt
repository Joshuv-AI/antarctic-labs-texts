Quality of papers assessed by policy documents, faculty opinion and Mendeley counts in Altmetric sources
Authors: Georgia Micha, Eleftheria Soulioti, Argyro Fassoulaki
Published: 2023

INTRODUCTION

The traditional impact factor measures an article's impact on the academic society. In contrast, https://www.altmetric.com/metrics.com (©Altmetric, London, UK) tracks scholarly outputs' attention in social and mainstream media. The Altmetric Attention Score (AAS) is an integral number derived from an automated algorithm and represents a weighted count of mentions of a scholar's output by the Altmetric sources.[1] Whether it is a measure of the quality of the researcher, the journal or the scholar output itself is debatable.[2,3,4] Nonetheless, policy documents and faculty opinions included in the AAS automated algorithm may represent a measure of a particular scholar's output quality.[2,5] Policy documents are formal documents with government guidelines, advisory committees, reports or white papers, and independent policy institute publications from research institutes and international development organisations.[6] The faculty opinion was created to improve traditional metrics by re-reviewing selected articles and ranking them according to their quality.[7] The online reference manager Mendeley shows the readership counts, and they are not included in the automatic algorithm of AAS.

The study's primary objective was to assess the percentage of policy documents and faculty opinions in articles published in anaesthesia and pain medicine journals. The secondary endpoint was Mendeley counts of articles published in the same journals.

METHODS

This retrospective study involved data collection of all AASs, policy documents and faculty opinions sourced from https://www.altmetric.com/metrics.com (©Altmetric, London, UK) from 17 medical journals in anaesthesia and pain medicine with an impact factor higher than 2 for 2020. The number of articles published in the 17 journals within the same year was recorded, and the Altmetric Impact Factor (AIF) was calculated. Since the British Journal of Anaesthesia and Journal of Clinical Anaesthesia employ the PlumX metrics, data were retrieved from https://www.dimensions.ai/(©2023 Digital Science and Research Solutions Inc). Supplementary data from Mendeley saves were also collected.

AIF was calculated as described previously using the same denominator as the impact factor and the AAS as the numerator.[8] The denominator included the number of original articles, reviews of all types and guidelines. Non-citable articles such as editorials and correspondence were excluded. Results for each journal are presented as a percentage of policy documents, faculty opinion articles and Mendeley readership counts divided by the number of articles, respectively.

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 for Mac (International Business Machines, New York, USA). A Pearson product-moment correlation was run to determine the relationships between AIF and each variable (number of policy documents per number of articles, number of faculty opinions per number of articles and Mendeley counts per number of articles). The frequencies of policy document references measure the impact, faculty opinion selections and Mendeley count readership.

RESULTS

We included 4881 articles published in anaesthesia and pain medicine journals during the year 2020, being scored by Altmetrics.com. British Journal of Anaesthesia received the highest number of policy document citations, and Neurosurgical Anesthesiology Journal the highest number of faculty opinion mentions. The highest Mendeley readership was 63% for the Anaesthesia journal [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Policy documents and faculty opinions might represent a measure of quality. Yet, the results of the present study implementing the Altmetric.com dataset indicate that only a few articles published in high- or medium-impact anaesthesia journals are cited by policy documents or recommended by the faculty opinion.

Tattersall and Caroll investigated citations by policy documents in 96,550 research outputs across all disciplines. Of these articles, 1.41% cited at least one policy document.[9] In the Haunschild and Bornmann[10] study, 11,254,636 research outputs were used, of which only 0.32% were cited at least once in policy documents. Our results show a higher percentage of anaesthesia journals to be cited by policy documents compared to the previous studies, but the overall 2.6% of citations we found is still low.[9,10] Thus, the policy documents citations, independently of their importance, cannot be considered a quality indicator in the AAS. The percentage of articles cited by policy documents may be field-dependent as some disciplines may have a higher social impact. Policy-related sites tracked by Altmetrics may also play a role.

The relationship between altmetrics and the quality of faculty opinion papers is not as strong as the relationship between faculty opinion and citations. On the contrary, readership counts retrieved from the Mendeley reference manager correlate strongly with citations, thus indicating a relation to quality. Also, a meta-analysis including 28 articles showed a strong correlation between traditional citations and readership counts.[11] Despite the potential use of Mendeley readership counts, Mendeley readership is not included in the AAS.

Our dataset was restricted to articles from high- or medium-impact anaesthesia and pain medicine journals. We did not differentiate between articles cited by one or more policy documents but considered each journal's total number of policy documents. Also, we ignored the ranking of articles included in the faculty opinions dataset as the number of articles mentioned by the faculty opinion and the number of journals that published such articles was too small. The present study contributes to the very poor information available in the literature regarding two sources of AAS with the potential to assess the quality of research articles. The low incidence of citing or recommending the 4881 articles does not fulfil our hypothesis.

CONCLUSION

Our results show that policy documents and faculty opinion, sources involved in the AAS calculation, rarely give citations or recommendations to the articles in the present study and do not represent indicators of the academic quality of scholar outputs. Also, these sources do not correlate to the AIF. On the contrary, there is a strong correlation between AIF and readership counts retrieved from the Mendeley manager.
