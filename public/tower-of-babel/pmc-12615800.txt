Mid-Cretaceous sand fly (Psychodidae, Phlebotominae) illuminates early diversification of Old World fauna
Authors: Szymon Kaczmarek, Mathieu Loyer, Jérôme Depaquit, Wiesław Krzemiński, Agnieszka Soszyńska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sand flies (Diptera, Psychodidae, Phlebotominae) are medically important insects with an interesting, but poorly understood, evolutionary history. The abundance and diversity of inclusions of these flies in Myanmar (Burmese) amber provide valuable data on their geological history. In this study, a new genus and species of sand fly, Longiphlebes excelsior gen. et sp. nov., is described based on an inclusion from Cretaceous Kachin amber (northern Myanmar). The systematic position of the new genus is discussed, and its morphological characters are compared with those of selected extant and fossil Phlebotominae taxa. The morphology of the genitalia, head, and wing suggests a closer relationship to recent Old World Phlebotominae fauna than to the more derived fossil genera such as Phlebotomites or the phlebotomid-like Eophlebotomus. This discovery broadens our understanding of Cretaceous sand flies, especially their evolution and diversity.

Introduction

Phlebotomine sand flies (Diptera, Psychodidae, Phlebotominae) are small, blood-sucking insects best known for transmitting various pathogens including Leishmania, arbovirus, and Bartonella, which affect many vertebrates, including humans, livestock, and domestic pets1. They are currently found in warm, humid environments between latitudes of 50°N and 40°S1. As with other blood-sucking dipteran groups, such as true mosquitoes (Culicidae) and black flies (Simuliidae), most females of Phlebotominae exhibit anautogeny, meaning they must consume vertebrate blood to lay eggs. Males typically feed on nectar and other sugary plant secretions2. Due to their high significance for public health, this subfamily is one of the most extensively studied within Psychodidae. Despite numerous studies focusing on the recent fauna, only a few have aimed to explore the diversity of fossil taxa and/or the evolutionary history of this important group.

The recent fauna comprises of over 1000 species/subspecies3, whereas only 30 unquestionable fossil species distributed across 10 genera have been described to date (Table 1). These species are all exclusively preserved as inclusions in various fossil resins. However, this state is not due to a lack of material. Besides Baltic amber, Phlebotominae are common psychodids in most major amber deposits worldwide. The earliest sand flies are known from inclusions found in Lower Cretaceous amber from Lebanon, dated to around 125 Mya (Barremian-Aptian age)4, which is approximately 25 million years older than mid-Cretaceous Myanmar amber. During the mid-Cretaceous period, the area now known as northern Myanmar was most likely part of a long, cigar-shaped tropical island near the southern coast of Asia. The warm, humid climate was and still is highly suitable for sand flies, resulting in their remarkable abundance and diversity in Myanmar amber. Consequently, these Mesozoic inclusions hold immense potential for providing new insights into the diversity, evolution, and relationships of Old World Phlebotominae. Cretaceous phlebotomids are of particular interest from a morphological standpoint.

Fossil taxa exhibit many features considered plesiomorphic, which are rarely seen or absent in extant representatives. For instance, most fossil species have rounded wings at the tip, a feature shared only by the living genera Chinius Leng, 19875 (Old World), Hertigia Fairchild, 19496, and Warileya Hertig, 19487 (New World), which all belong to the Hertigiini tribe. The legs of the genus Libanophlebotomites Azar, Maalouf & Maksoud, 20228, are shorter than its body, and the wing pattern of the Mesophlebotomites Azar et al., 19999 and Phlebotomites Hennig, 197210 show the subcostal vein ending in a bifurcation into the sc-r and sc-c veins. Thus, studying the Cretaceous sand fly fauna is crucial to better understanding the evolutionary pathways within the Phlebotominae subfamily and the origins of the recent fauna.

The present study describes a new phlebotomid fly, Longiphlebes excelsior gen. et sp. nov., which further enhances our knowledge of the diversity and evolution of sand flies during the Cretaceous period. In addition, revisionary remarks on several fossil Phlebotominae taxa are presented.

Description:

Male. Body (Fig. 1A). Brown to black, 1.52 mm (measured from apex of thorax to distal part of genitalia).

Head (Fig. 1C). Antennae with 14 flagellomeres, each bearing short, bristle-like ascoids at least on f1-f8; several papillae present on each flagellomere; flagellomeres tubular, elongate, filiform with the following lengths: 1: 0.36; 2: 0.17; 3: 0.17; 4: 0.15; 5: 0.13; 6: 0.10; 7: 0.11; 8: 0.11; 9: 0.08; 10: 0.09; 11: 0.07; 12: 0.09; 13: 0.07; 14: 0.09 mm; flagellomeres densely covered with numerous setae, including two long setae present at the base of each flagellomere; scapus 0.03 mm; pedicel 0.05 mm, tear-shaped and more robust than scapus. Occiput sclerite with a patch of setae visible on the left side of occiput, behind occiput sclerite, situated above the eyes, dark-coloured, forming a bulge-like ”cap”; eyes dome-shaped, relatively small, positioned essentially lateral to mouthparts; the palpus consists of five segments, with the third being the longest (1: 0.05; 2: 0.07; 3: 0.10; 4: 0.05; 5: 0.08). Mouthparts strongly elongate, longer than head width including eyes, straight. Clypeus 0.07 mm long, thin and elongate, not wider than labium. Labium + labellum approximately 0.25–0.30 mm long; Labellum spatula-shaped, with delicate setation around the distal part. Labrum-epipharynx almost as long as labrum + labellum, thin, blade-like, with smooth edge.

Thorax. Prescutum well differentiated from scutum, prominently extending over the head; prescutum and scutum are covered with alveoli.

Legs. Coxa 0.40 mm long, bearing numerous thin, elongated, and erect setae; femur 0.69 mm, tibia 0.95 mm, tarsi 1.23 mm, all covered with delicate, short setae.

Wing (Figs. 1B and 2A). Length: 1.71 mm; width: 0.57 mm; rounded at the distal end; length/width ratio: 3.00; short and abundant setae present on wing margin and all veins; Sc vein unbranched, originating from costa, running parallel to vein R1, and terminating in it, behind the fork of r-m crossvein and origin of R5; R1 long, ending in Costa, not far from midpoint of vein R2; R2 + 3 about half the length of R2; R3 parallel to R2, longer than R2 but shorter than R4; Radial sector is approximately of same length as R2; R5 is the longest vein in wing, approximately twice the length of R3; M1 + 2 straight, slightly shorter than R2; M1 longer than R3, M2 about the same length as R3; M3 starts from medial-basal vein and almost same length as M1 + 2 + M2; M4 slightly bent, ending opposite R1 vein; Cu1 short, resembling crossvein, terminates distal to origin of Rs.

Abdomen. Consists of seven well-developed, non-genital segments. Seventh segment is the shortest, nearly trapezoidal, remaining tergites are elongated, with rectangular shapes and narrower, more-less pyramid-like sclerites. Tergites of segments 5–7 with patches of erect, long setae, usually longer than width of abdominal segment. Tergites of remaining segments also covered with setae, as alveoli are present on every preserved tergite.

Genitalia (Figs. 1D-E and 2B). Rotation angle: 180°; base of gonostyle small and cylindrical, with four true spines, three of which are located at apex of its base, apical spine positioned most distally on very long prolongation; two strong bristles/setae present on gonostyle base; gonocoxite more massive than gonostyle, spindle-shaped, with tuft of long setae on dorsal and ventral side; cerci thin, digitiform, whose end slightly exceeds that of the aedeagal sheath. epandrial lobes thick, elongated, of length comparable to that of gonocoxite; aedeagal sheath straight and tubular, rounded at their end; genital filaments long, emerging from the aedeagal sheath, isodiametric, with no particular structure visible at their end.; parameres simple, thin and slightly longer than the gonocoxites.

Remarks:

The arrangement of setae and the true morphology of thoracic structures are difficult to describe accurately. The thoracic sclerites are largely deformed and distorted, and much of their setation may have been lost when the insect became trapped in amber.

Female, pre-imaginal stages: unknown.

Discussion

Morphological comparison between Longiphlebes excelsior gen. nov and sp. nov. and other fossil Phlebotominae taxa

The newly described genus Longiphlebes gen. nov. differs from both recent and other known fossil Phlebotominae taxa in many aspects. Notably, it exhibits a significantly lower number of plesiomorphic character states than Cretaceous taxa such as Phlebotomites, Libanophlebotomites, and the unclassified Phlebotominae-like psychodid genus Eophlebotomus Cockerell 192015, bearing a closer resemblance to recent sand flies. The wings of Longiphlebes gen. nov. (Figures 1B and 2A) feature a transformation of the Sc vein into the sc-r, which ends in R1. In some Cretaceous Phlebotominae genera, the Sc vein forks into sc-r and sc-c, with one branch ending in costa and the other in R1. This type of subcostal vein can also be observed in the subfamily Bruchomyiinae, which is considered to be most closely related to Phlebotominae, as well as in phlebotomids such as genus Phlebotomites or Chinius. This makes it more plesiomorphic than in Longiphlebes gen. nov. and most of recent Phlebotominae fauna. The genitalia of the studied fly (Figs. 1D-E and 2B) differ from those of known taxa in that they have a small gonostyle compared to the gonocoxite, which has a long apical spine extension. Four true spines are present, including three located at the base of the gonostyle apex, from which the apical spine arises on an elongated extension. The parameres are simple, and the aedeagal sheaths are rod-like, each terminating in a filament, with one filament being longer than the other. Characters occurring in the new genus, such as the broad, large gonocoxite and small gonostyle with prolongations for spines, are not considered plesiomorphies. The same applies to long genital filaments16. However, the four spines on the gonostyle and the inversion of the genitalia are indeed plesiomorphic features16, as they can also be observed in early fossil phlebotomid forms of the genera Phlebotomites and Eophlebotomus. The mouthparts of Longiphlebes excelsior gen. et sp. nov. (Fig. 1C) are unusual in that they are highly elongated, measuring approximately twice the height of the head capsule. The mouthparts of known fossil male sand flies are significantly shorter, with a broader and more fleshy labellum. In contrast, the labellum of the new genus is only slightly wider and longer than the labrum.

When compared with the extant fauna, Longiphlebes gen. nov. appears to be most closely related to the genera Idiophlebotomus Theodor, 194817 and Spelaeophlebotomus Quate & Fairchild, 196118 belonging to a clade16 (Table 2) including Hertigiini, being the sister group to the rest of the extant Old World Phlebotominae. This is supported by the fact that, unlike other recent Old World genera, the third palpomere is the longest in these genera19. The tribal placement of Longiphlebes excelsior gen. et sp. nov. is currently indeterminate. Due to its similarities with Idiophlebotomus and Spelaeophlebotomus, both of which belong to the Hertigiini tribe, it is likely that the new fossil taxon will also be placed in this tribe. However, the key setation pattern of the holotype, which could confirm its affiliation, is difficult to interpret due to the poor state of preservation of the fossil. In Hertigiini, the setation pattern should be absent from the lower anepisternum and present across the entire occiput20. On the occiput, a patch of setae is preserved on one side, behind the occipital sclerite. However, it is unclear whether setae were present across the entire occiput. Some setation may have detached due to resin flow when the insect became trapped. The anepisternal region is not clearly visible, but no bristles were observed on the thorax below the scutum.

Remarks on the taxonomic position of certain fossil Phlebotominae, and Phlebotominae-like psychodid taxa

The placement of Eophlebotomus, is unclear, as this genus was not assigned to a specific subfamily by Duckhouse (2000)21 or Azar et al. (2003)22. It displays characteristics from different subfamilies, such as 13 flagellomeres on the antennae, surstyli, and fused gonocoxites with the epandrium and epandrial lobes. This raises questions about its designation within Phlebotominae. However, it also shares many characteristics that support its link to this subfamily, including the presence of ascoids and several major spines on the gonostyli. Nevertheless, Duckhouse (2000)21 and Azar et al. (2003)22 propose, however, a relationship with the stem group of the Sycoracinae and Trichomyiinae based on the presence of potential non-homoplastic synapomorphies shared with this group21,22. In our view, considering Eophlebotomus as a Phlebotominae-like psychodid, alongside with the morphologically similar genus Libanophlebotomus Azar et al., 19999 and Xenophlebotomites23, is the most reasonable option until extensive phylogenetic studies are conducted.

To date, the only extant genus of Phlebotominae sand flies reported from fossil resins is the genus Phlebotomus Rondani & Berté in Rondani, 184013. However, taxonomic designations of all described fossil representatives of this genus are doubtful. Phlebotomus is represented in the fossil fauna by Ph. vetus Stebner et al., 201524 (Burmese amber; mid-Cretaceous), Ph. khludae Kaddumi, 200525 (Jordanian amber; Lower Cretaceous), which was described in privately published work without ISBN, Ph. succini Stuckenberg, 197526 (East African copal; Holocene), and Ph. pungens (Loew 1845)27 (Tanzanian copal; Holocene). The generic affiliation of Ph. vetus is questionable and it probably does not represent Phlebotomus sensu stricto. It has inverted genitalia (rather than rotated by 90°), a broad and rounded wing, and a gonostyle with four spines instead of five, the third palpus segment is the same length as the fifth one (rather than the fifth one being clearly longer). At first glance, the male genitalia resemble those of some Phlebotomus species in that they have elongated gonopods. However, the remaining genital structures are poorly visible24, thus a revision of this taxon is required. Phlebotomus khludae definitely does not belong to the genus Phlebotomus, as the visible morphological characteristics mentioned in the description correspond with those of Eophlebotomus. This was also corroborated by Azar25. Phlebotomus succini is a taxon with a problematic taxonomic position. Stuckenberg (1975)26 described it as a Phlebotomus sensu Abonnenc (1972)28 and stated that it was an inclusion found in Baltic amber. Later, Lewis et al., 197729 changed the generic affiliation to Sergentomyia and its designation from Baltic amber to copal, what was evidenced by spectrophotometric study of the holotype piece29. Phlebotomus pungens was originally described by (Loew 1845)27 as Philaematus pungens based on a female sand fly from Tanzanian copal and was later transferred to the genus Phlebotomus by Meunier (1905b)30. Nevertheless, a revision of the holotype is needed, if it has not been lost.

Materials and methods

Studied material, methodology, figures and obtained data

The specimen examined herein is an inclusion embedded within Kachin amber of Cenomanian age (98.79 ± 0.62 Mya), collected in the Hukawng Valley in northern Myanmar. The age of the amber was determined by Shi et al., (2012)31 using the U-Pb method on zircons found in amber-bearing sedimentary rock, and this finding is supported by numerous biological inclusions of plants and animals described that have been described in this fossil resin over the years. The studied amber piece was treated with abrasive and polishing materials to prepare it for detailed photography and morphological analysis. After following preparation, its dimensions were found to be 1.8 × 0.9 × 0.2 cm, and it exhibits predominantly pale yellow colouration. The piece of amber was acquired in 2016, thus in full ethical compliance with the standards for the study of Burmese amber32. The holotype is deposited at the Natural History Museum of the Institute of Systematics and Evolution of Animals, Polish Academy of Sciences in Kraków, Poland, (MP ISEA PAS), under catalogue number: MP/5258. The microphotographs on display were acquired using a Leica MC205 stereomicroscope mounted with a dedicated Leica DMC5400 CCD camera. Image stacking was achieved using Leica Application Suite X RRID: SCR_013673 (LAS X) and Helicon Focus (RRID: SCR_014462) available at Heliconsoft.com, followed by the Microsoft® Windows 10 Images app and Microsoft® PowerPoint for light intensity manipulation. Line drawings were prepared by manually tracing the outlines of visible insect body parts in the microphotographs, which were then refined using CorelDraw X7 or Paint3D. All measurements provided are in millimetres and were rounded to the nearest 0.005 mm. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) analysis of the studied amber (Fig. 4) was performed using a Nicolet iS5 FTIR spectrometer equipped with a diamond crystal attenuated total reflectance (ATR) attachment. As there is no clear consensus on the taxonomic placement of some Phlebotominae species within the Old World fauna at genus or subgenus level, this paper follows the classification established by Rispail & Léger (1998)19. General terminology follows Galati et al. (2017)34, except for the terms “aedeagal sheath” instead of “parameral sheath” and wing venation, which is consistent with Krzemiński & Krzemińska (2003)35.

The choice of taxa to compare

(Taxa selected for comparative analysis) the taxa compared in Table 2 with the genus and species described herein were selected based on following factors: among recent fauna, the genera Spelaeophlebotomus, Chinius, Warileya, and Idiophlebotomus are considered primitive and appear to share the greatest number of evolutionarily stable characteristics with the specimen under study, as discussed above; among Cretaceous fauna, the genera Phlebotomites and Eophlebotomus were described based on well-preserved male specimens, making their genital morphology valuable for taxonomy. Furthermore, these genera were the most thoroughly described and illustrated, providing the greatest quantity of morphological data.

Conclusions

Longiphlebes excelsior gen. et sp. nov. exhibits a low number of plesiomorphic traits compared to other described Cretaceous sand flies. Despite being of a similar age, its morphology indicates a closer relationship with extant species than with primitive fossil phlebotomids found in Lebanese and Burmese ambers. The discovery of the sand fly in Kachin amber, which shares more morphological similarities with modern representatives of this subfamily than with other described Cretaceous phlebotomids, suggests that this evolutionary lineage already existed in the mid-Cretaceous.
