SPARKE: Scalable Prompt-Aware Diversity Guidance in Diffusion Models via RKE Score
Mohammad Jalali, Haoyu Lei, Amin Gohari, Farzan Farnia

Abstract
Diffusion models have demonstrated remarkable success in high-fidelity image synthesis and prompt-guided generative modeling. However, ensuring adequate diversity in generated samples of prompt-guided diffusion models remains a challenge, particularly when the prompts span a broad semantic spectrum and the diversity of generated data needs to be evaluated in a prompt-aware fashion across semantically similar prompts. Recent methods have introduced guidance via diversity measures to encourage more varied generations. In this work, we extend the diversity measure-based approaches by proposing the S calable P rompt- A ware R ény K ernel E ntropy Diversity Guidance ( SPARKE ) method for prompt-aware diversity guidance. SPARKE utilizes conditional entropy for diversity guidance, which dynamically conditions diversity measurement on similar prompts and enables prompt-aware diversity control. While the entropy-based guidance approach enhances prompt-aware diversity, its reliance on the matrix-based entropy scores poses computational challenges in large-scale generation settings. To address this, we focus on the special case of Conditional latent RKE Score Guidance , reducing entropy computation and gradient-based optimization complexity from the 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 3 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{3}) of general entropy measures to 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) . The reduced computational complexity allows for diversity-guided sampling over potentially thousands of generation rounds on different prompts. We numerically test the SPARKE method on several text-to-image diffusion models, demonstrating that the proposed method improves the prompt-aware diversity of the generated data without incurring significant computational costs. We release our code on the project page: https://mjalali.github.io/SPARKE .

According to the conditional entropy guidance in SPARKE, we update the kernel matrix of generated data by taking the Hadamard product with the kernel matrix of input prompts. While the conditional entropy guidance offers flexibility by relaxing the requirement for explicit hard clustering of the prompts, it relies on computing the gradient of the matrix-based entropy of the kernel matrix, which will be computationally expensive in large-scale generation tasks. To improve scalability while preserving diversity-aware generation, we propose applying the order-2 Renyi matrix-based entropy, defined as the RKE Score
[
16

\mathcal{O}(n)

To evaluate the effectiveness of our approach, we perform several numerical experiments on standard text-to-image generation models. Our results indicate that Conditional RKE Score Guidance not only scales efficiently to a large number of prompts but also maintains high sample diversity while preserving fidelity. Compared to prior methods, our approach achieves a more balanced trade-off between diversity and computational efficiency, making it suitable for real-world generative modeling applications that require scalability. 3.1
Kernel Matrices and RKE Diversity Scores

Consider a sample
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
in the support set
\mathcal{X}
. A function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called a kernel function, if for every integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
and sample set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{X}
, the following kernel similarity matrix
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is positive semi-definite (PSD):

{K}_{X}=\begin{bmatrix}k(x_{1},x_{1})&\cdots&k(x_{1},x_{n})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
k(x_{n},x_{1})&\cdots&k(x_{n},x_{n})\end{bmatrix}

(1)

The Rényi Kernel Entropy (RKE) diversity score is defined and analyzed by
[
16
]
as the inverse Frobenius norm-squared of the trace-normalized kernel matrix:

\mathrm{RKE}\bigl(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\bigr):=\Bigl\|\frac{K_{X}}{\mathrm{Tr}(K_{X})}\Bigr\|^{-2}_{F},

(2)

where
\mathrm{Tr}(\cdot)
is the trace operator and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. As theoretically shown in
[
16
]
, the above RKE score can be regarded as a mode count of a mixture distribution with multiple modes.

To extend this entropy-based diversity scores to conditional (i.e., prompt-aware) diversity measurement for prompt-guided generative models,
[
15
]
propose the application of conditional kernel matrix entropy measures, resulting in the following definition of order-
2
Conditional-RKE given the Hadamard product
\frac{1}{\mathrm{Tr}(K_{X}\odot K_{T})}K_{X}\odot K_{T}
of the output data
K_{X}
kernel matrix and input prompt kernel
K_{T}
matrix:

\mathrm{Conditional}\text{-}\mathrm{RKE}\bigl(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\bigr)\,:=\,\Bigl\|\frac{{K}_{T}}{\mathrm{Tr}({K}_{T})}\Bigr\|^{2}_{F}\cdot\,\Bigl\|\frac{{K}_{T}\odot{K}_{X}}{\mathrm{Tr}({K}_{T}\odot{K}_{X})}\Bigr\|^{-2}_{F}

(3)

Note that here the Hadamard product of the prompt
(t_{1},\ldots t_{n})
kernel matrix
K_{T}
and output
(x_{1},\ldots x_{n})
kernel matrix
K_{X}
is defined as the elementwise product of the matrices, which is guaranteed to be a PSD matrix by the Schur product theorem,

{K}_{T}\odot K_{X}\,:=\,\begin{bmatrix}k(t_{1},t_{1})\cdot k(x_{1},x_{1})&\cdots&k(t_{1},t_{n})\cdot k(x_{1},x_{n})\vskip 5.69054pt\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\vskip 5.69054pt\\
k(t_{n},t_{1})\cdot k(x_{n},x_{1})&\cdots&k(t_{n},t_{n})\cdot k(x_{n},x_{n})\end{bmatrix}

(4)

Conditional Latent Diffusion Models

Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs)

\bm{z}_{t}=\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\bm{z}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\bm{\epsilon}_{t}

\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathit{\bm{I}})
and
\alpha_{t}\in
are predefined noise schedule parameters. Then the conditional LDMs with conditions
\bm{y}
are trained to predict the noise
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{y})
at each time step
t

p_{t}(\bm{z_{t}}|\bm{y})

[
17

3

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}_{t},t,\bm{y}}\left[\left\|\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{y})-\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}_{t},t,\bm{y}}\left[\left\|\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{y})+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\nabla_{\bm{z}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\bm{z}_{t}|\bm{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

Scalable Prompt-Aware Diversity Guidance in Diffusion Models

Utilizing the kernel-based entropy diversity scores and latent diffusion models (LDMs) described in Section
3.2
, we introduce a scalable conditional entropy-based framework extending the recent Vendi score-based approach
[
10

\alpha

\alpha

In addition, we demonstrate a computationally efficient special case in this family of diversity score functions by considering the order-2 entropy measures. This choice of entropy function leads to the RKE-guidance and Conditional-RKE guidance approaches for enhancing overall diversity and prompt-aware diversity in sample generation via diffusion models.

4.1
Diversity guidance via general order-
\alpha
Renyi Entropy and RKE scores

The existing diversity score-based guidance approach, including the Vendi guidance in
[
10
]
, optimizes the diversity score of the embedded version of the generated outputs, e.g. the CLIP embedding
[
52
]
of image data generated by the prompt-guided diffusion models. However, such a guidance process by considering an embedding on top of the diffusion model is computationally expensive and may not lead to semantically diverse samples. As discussed in
[
13
,
42
]
, computing the order-1 Vendi score and its gradients requires at least
\Omega(n^{2.367})
computations for
n
samples, and in practice involves
O(n^{3})
computations for the eigen-decompositions of kernel matrices. This computational complexity limits practical window sizes of the existing Vendi guidance approach to a few hundred samples, i.e. the guidance function cannot be computed by a standard GPU processor for a sample size
n>500
.

To reduce computational complexity, we extend the order-1 Vendi score guidance to the general order-
\alpha
Rènyi kernel entropy, and adopt the specific case
\alpha=2
, referred to as the RKE score. This formulation significantly lowers computational cost and is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{RKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})=\frac{1}{\|\widetilde{K}_{Z}\|_{F}^{2}}=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(K_{Z})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2}},\quad\text{where}\,\,\widetilde{K}_{Z}=\frac{K_{Z}}{\mathrm{Tr}(K_{Z})},

(6)

with kernel matrix
(K_{Z})_{ij}=k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})
computed directly on latent representations and
n
denotes the total number of previously generated samples.
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RKE}}
reduces the computational complexity from
O(n^{3})
in order-1 Vendi score to
O(n^{2})
, enabling significantly larger batch sizes during the guided sampling process and leading to greater computational efficiency.

4.2
Diversity-Guided Sampling in Latent Diffusion Models

In contrast to previous work using order-1 Vendi score as the diversity guidance in ambient image space
[
10
]
, we integrate efficient IRKE score diversity guidance directly into latent-space sampling. Standard LDMs employ classifier-free guidance (CFG)
[
46
]
for conditional sampling, iteratively denoising latents
\bm{z}_{t-1}
from the noisy latents
\bm{z}_{t}
at time step
t
via reverse samplers:

\bm{z}_{t-1}\leftarrow\text{Sampler}(\bm{z}_{t},\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}(\bm{z}_{t},t,\bm{y})),

(7)

To efficiently promote diversity within the latent space, we optimize the Inverse-RKE (IRKE) score loss. Proposition
1
defines this loss and its gradient:

Proposition 1
.

Let
Z=\{\bm{z}^{(1)},\bm{z}^{(2)},\ldots,\bm{z}^{(n)}\}
denote a set of
n
generated data. Let kernel function
k:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be symmetric and normalized, i.e.
k(\bm{z},\bm{z})=1
for every
\bm{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
. Then, we observe that the function
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})
in Eq. equation
6
changes montonically with the Inverse-RKE function
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})=1/\mathcal{L}_{\text{RKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})
, whose gradient with respect to
\bm{z}^{(n)}
is:

\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}\propto\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)}).

(8)

The above Proposition
1
implies that an optimization objective maximizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RKE}}
to promote diversity can be equivalently pursued by minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}
, reducing the computational complexity from
O(n^{2})
to
O(n)
. Leveraging this computational efficacy, our approach directly updates the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}
rather than computing the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RKE}}
to the latent
\bm{z}^{(n)}

8

\bm{z}_{t}\leftarrow\bm{z}_{t}-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}\,\,\quad(\textbf{IRKE Diversity Guidance})

4.3
Conditional Prompt-Aware Diversity Guidance

To explicitly capture prompt-conditioned diversity and focusing on computational efficiency of order-2, we introduce the Conditional RKE loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-RKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{z}^{(n)};\bm{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{y}^{(n)}):=\frac{\bigl\|\widetilde{K}_{Y}\bigr\|^{2}_{F}}{\bigl\|\frac{{K}_{Y}\odot{K}_{Z}}{\mathrm{Tr}({K}_{Y}\odot{K}_{Z})}\bigr\|^{2}_{F}},\quad\text{where}\,\,\widetilde{K}_{Y}=\frac{K_{Y}}{\mathrm{Tr}(K_{Y})},

(10)

where
(K_{Y})_{ij}=k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(j)})
denotes the similarity among condition (e.g., prompts) embeddings. Similarly, we reduce the computational complexity from
O(n^{2})
to
O(n)
, by computing the gradient of Conditional Inverse-RKE (Cond-IRKE) defined in Proposition
2
:

Proposition 2
.

Let
Z
denote a set of
n
generated data, and
Y=\{\bm{y}^{(1)},\bm{y}^{(2)},\ldots,\bm{y}^{(n)}\}
is the set of corresponding conditions. Let the kernel function
k
be symmetric and normalized. Then, we observe that the function
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-RKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)};\bm{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{y}^{(n)})
in Eq. equation
10
changes montonically with the Conditional Inverse-RKE function
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)};\bm{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{y}^{(n)})=\|\widetilde{K}_{Z}\odot\widetilde{K}_{Y}\|_{F}^{2}
, whose gradient with respect to
\bm{z}^{(n)}
is:

\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}\propto\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)}).

(11)

Then we propose the following prompt-aware diversity-guided sampling via Eq. equation
11
in each time step
t
:

\bm{z}_{t}\leftarrow\bm{z}_{t}-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}\,\,\quad(\textbf{Cond-IRKE Diversity Guidance})

(12)

6
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed the prompt-aware SPARKE diversity guidance approach for prompt-based diffusion models. The SPARKE method aims to improve the diversity of output data conditioned on the prompt, so that the diversity guidance process takes into account the similarity of the prompts. We also proposed the application of RKE and Conditional-RKE scores in the latent space of LDMs to boost the scalability in the SPARKE method. Our numerical results of applying SPARKE to the stable-diffusion LDMs indicate the method’s qualitative and quantitative improvement of variety in generated samples. A limitation of our numerical evaluation is its primary focus on image generation diffusion models. Future exploration can extend SPARKE’s application to other modalities, such as video and text diffusion models. Furthermore, combining SPARKE’s entropy guidance with other diversity-enhancement techniques for diffusion models presents another interesting future direction.

Appendix A
Preliminaries on Diffusion Models

A.1
Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs)

Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs)
[
1
,
2
]
define a generative process by reversing a fixed Markovian forward diffusion that progressively adds Gaussian noise to data. Given a data sample
\bm{x}_{0}\sim p_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})
, a time step
t\in[T]\triangleq\{1,\dots,T\}
, the forward process gradually adds noise to construct a noisy data point
\bm{x}_{t}=\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\bm{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
, where
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathit{\bm{I}})
is standard Gaussian noise and
\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]
monotonically decreases with time step
t
to control the noise level. The diffusion model
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\times[T]\mapsto\mathcal{X}
is trained to predict the noise
\bm{\epsilon}_{t}
at each time step
t
, also learn the score of
p_{t}(\bm{x_{t}})

[
17
,
3
]
:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}_{t},t}\left[\left\|\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t},t)-\bm{\epsilon}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{t},\bm{\epsilon}_{t},t}\left[\left\|\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t},t)+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(13)

The reverse process obtained by
\bm{x}_{t-1}\sim p_{t-1|t}(\bm{x}_{t-1}|\bm{x}_{t})
is not directly computable in practice, multiple efficient reverse samplers are proposed
[
60
,
65
,
66
]
. In the commonly-used DDIM
[
60
]
sampler, we sample
\bm{x}_{t-1}
by:

\bm{x}_{t-1}=\sqrt{\alpha_{t-1}}\bm{\tilde{x}}_{0|t}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_{t}^{2}}\frac{\bm{x}_{t}-\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\bm{\tilde{x}}_{0|t}}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}}+\sigma_{t}\bm{\epsilon}_{t},

(14)

where
\sigma_{t}
is the DDIM parameter, and the clean sample
\bm{\tilde{x}}_{0|t}
given
\bm{x}_{t}
is estimated according to Tweedie’s Formula
[
67
,
68
]
:

\bm{\tilde{x}}_{0|t}=\frac{\bm{x}_{t}-\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t},t)}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}.

(15)

A.2
Conditional Generation via Guidance.

In conditional generation tasks like text-to-image synthesis, diffusion models learn to approximate the conditional distribution
p(\bm{x}_{0}|\bm{y})
given conditions
\bm{y}
(e.g., text prompts). From the view of score functions
[
17
,
3
]
, we denote the conditional score as:

\underbrace{\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{y})}_{\text{Conditional Score}}=\underbrace{\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})}_{\text{Unconditional Score}}+\underbrace{\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{t})}_{\text{Guidance Score}},

(16)

The strategies of conditional generation via guidance can be roughly divided into two categories: Training-based methods and training-free methods.

Training-based Guidance.
Training-based guidance methods include several strategies. One approach, Classifier-Guidance, initially proposed in
[
3
,
6
]
, requires training an additional time-dependent classifier to estimate the guidance score
f(\bm{x}_{t},t)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}_{0}\sim p_{0|t}(\cdot|\bm{x}_{t})}f(\bm{x}_{0})\approx f(\bm{x}_{0})
. Alternatively, other training-based techniques involve few-shot fine-tuning of base models or the use of adapters
[
4
,
30
]
to achieve conditional control.

A distinct yet training-based approach is Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG), introduced by
[
46
]
. Unlike methods requiring a separate classifier, CFG integrates the condition
\bm{y}
as a direct input to the conditional denoising network
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t},t,\bm{y})
. It is enabled by a joint training procedure where the model also learns to make unconditional predictions by randomly dropping the condition
\bm{y}
with a specific probability during each training iteration. During inference, CFG estimates the conditional noise prediction as follows:

\hat{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t},t,\bm{y})=(1+w)\cdot\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t},t,\bm{y})-w\cdot\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t},t),

(17)

where the guidance scale
w>0
adjusts the strength of conditional guidance. These various training-based guidance methods have demonstrated considerable effectiveness with the availability of training resources.

Training-free Guidance.
An alternative category for conditional guidance is training-free guidance. Instead of requiring additional training, these methods directly introduce a time-independent conditional predictor
f
on the estimated clean data
f(\bm{\tilde{x}}_{0|t},\bm{y})
, which can be a classifier, loss function, or energy function quantifying the alignment of a generated sample with the target condition
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
69
]
. Then the estimation of the guidance score via training-free approaches is:

\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{t}):=-\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}f\left(\bm{\tilde{x}}_{0|t},\mathbf{y}\right),

(18)

where the clean sample for the predictor is estimated by Tweedie’s Formula
[
67
,
68
]
:

\bm{\tilde{x}}_{0|t}=\frac{\bm{x}_{t}-\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t},t)}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}.

(19)

Proof of Proposition 1

Proof.

Let
K_{\mathcal{Z}}
be the kernel matrix with entries
(K_{Z})_{ij}=k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})
.
The RKE loss is given by:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{RKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{z}^{(n)})=\frac{1}{\|\widetilde{K}_{Z}\|_{F}^{2}}=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(K_{Z})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2}},

(20)

The Inverse-RKE function
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}
is defined by:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{RKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}(K_{Z})^{2}},

(21)

Given that the kernel function
k
is normalized, i.e.,
k(\bm{z},\bm{z})=1
for all
\bm{z}\in Z
, the trace of the kernel matrix
K_{Z}
is:

\mathrm{Tr}(K_{Z})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(i)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}1=n,

Substituting this into the expression for
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})
in Eq. equation
21
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2},

(22)

Now, we want to compute the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})
with respect to
\bm{z}^{(n)}
:

\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})=\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2}\right),

(23)

Since
1/n^{2}
is a constant with respect to
\bm{z}^{(n)}
:

\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2}\right),

(24)

Let

. We need to find
\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}S
.
The terms in the sum
S
that involve
\bm{z}^{(n)}
are those where
i=n
or
j=n
(or both). We can split the sum into:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2}\quad(\text{Terms not involving }\bm{z}^{(n)})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}\quad(j=n,i\neq n)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(n)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2}\quad(i=n,j\neq n)

\displaystyle+k(\bm{z}^{(n)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2},\quad(i=n,j=n)

Using the symmetry of the kernel,
k(\bm{z}^{(n)},\bm{z}^{(j)})=k(\bm{z}^{(j)},\bm{z}^{(n)})
, the second and third sums are identical:

S=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}+k(\bm{z}^{(n)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2},

(25)

Now, we compute the gradient of
S
with respect to
\bm{z}^{(n)}
:

\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}S=\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2}\right)+\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}\right)+\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(k(\bm{z}^{(n)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}\right),

(26)

The first term is zero because it does not depend on
\bm{z}^{(n)}
.
For the second term:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}\right)

(27)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}2k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})

\displaystyle=4\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)}),

For the third term, since
k(\bm{z},\bm{z})=1
(normalized kernel), this term is constant:

\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(k(\bm{z}^{(n)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}\right)=\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}(1^{2})=\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}(1)=\bm{0},

(28)

According to Eq. equation
27
and Eq. equation
28
, we have the gradeitn for
S
:

\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}S=4\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)}),

(29)

Substituting this back into the gradient of

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{IRKE}}(\bm{z}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(n)})

:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}S_{C}=

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(j)})^{2}k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(j)})^{2}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle+\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(n)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(n)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\right),

The first term is zero because it does not depend on
\bm{z}^{(n)}
.
For the second term:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\left(2k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})\right)

\displaystyle=4\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)}),

For the third term, since
k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(n)},\bm{z}^{(n)})=1
and
k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(n)},\bm{y}^{(n)})=1
(normalized kernels), this term is constant:

\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\left(k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(n)},\bm{z}^{(n)})^{2}k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(n)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\right)=\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}(1^{2}\cdot 1^{2})=\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}(1)=\bm{0},

(40)

So we have:

\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}S_{C}=4\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)}),

(41)

Substituting this back into the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}(Z;Y)
in Eq. equation
34
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}(Z;Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{4}}\left(4\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})\right)

(42)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{n^{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)}),

Since
4/n^{4}
is a positive constant (for
n\geq 1
), we finally get:

\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}\propto\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)})k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(n)})^{2}\nabla_{\bm{z}^{(n)}}k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(n)}).

(43)

This proves the form of the gradient.

Regarding the monotonic relationship:
From Eq. equation
10
, we have:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-RKE}}(Z;Y)=\frac{\bigl\|\widetilde{K}_{Y}\bigr\|^{2}_{F}}{\bigl\|\frac{{K}_{Y}\odot{K}_{Z}}{\mathrm{Tr}({K}_{Y}\odot{K}_{Z})}\bigr\|^{2}_{F}},

(44)

We have shown that
\mathrm{Tr}(K_{Y}\odot K_{Z})=n
, since
(K_{Y}\odot K_{Z})_{ii}=k_{Y}(\bm{y}^{(i)},\bm{y}^{(i)})k_{Z}(\bm{z}^{(i)},\bm{z}^{(i)})=1\cdot 1=1
.
Also,
\|\widetilde{K}_{Y}\|^{2}_{F}=\left\|K_{Y}/n\right\|^{2}_{F}=\|K_{Y}\|^{2}_{F}/n^{2}
.
Substituting these into the formulation for
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-RKE}}
in Eq. equation
44
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-RKE}}(Z;Y)=\frac{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|K_{Y}\|^{2}_{F}}{\left\|\frac{K_{Y}\odot K_{Z}}{n}\right\|^{2}_{F}}=\frac{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|K_{Y}\|^{2}_{F}}{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\|K_{Y}\odot K_{Z}\|^{2}_{F}}=\frac{\|K_{Y}\|^{2}_{F}}{\|K_{Y}\odot K_{Z}\|^{2}_{F}},

(45)

From the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}(Z;Y)
in Eq. equation
32
, we have:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}(Z;Y)=\|\widetilde{K}_{Z}\odot\widetilde{K}_{Y}\|_{F}^{2}=\left\|\frac{K_{Z}}{n}\odot\frac{K_{Y}}{n}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{4}}\|K_{Z}\odot K_{Y}\|_{F}^{2},

(46)

Let
C_{Y}=\|K_{Y}\|_{F}^{2}
. This term depends only on the conditions
Y
and is constant with respect to
\bm{z}^{(n)}
.
C_{Y}
is always positive since the kernel function
K_{Y}
is positive semi-definite and not all kernel values can be 0. From the formulation for
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}
in Eq. equation
32
, we have
\|K_{Z}\odot K_{Y}\|_{F}^{2}=n^{4}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}(Z;Y)
.
Substituting this into the formulation for
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-RKE}}
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-RKE}}(Z;Y)=\frac{C_{Y}}{n^{4}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}(Z;Y)}.

(47)

Since
C_{Y}/n^{4}
is a positive constant, then
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-RKE}}(Z;Y)
is a strictly monotonically decreasing function of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cond-IRKE}}(Z;Y)
. Thus, they change monotonically with respect to each other.
This completes the proof of Proposition 2.
∎
