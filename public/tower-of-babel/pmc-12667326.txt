Plasma levels of adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine, serotonin, and cortisol in sarcopenia: comparison with non-sarcopenic individuals and the impact of resistance exercise and nutrition interventions
Authors: Ke-Vin Chang, Wei-Ting Wu, Yu-Hsin Chen, Lan-Rong Chen, Yun-Lian Lin, Der-Sheng Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenia may disrupt the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal axis, affecting neurotransmitter and hormone levels. However, the impact of sarcopenia on these markers, particularly in comparison to non-sarcopenic controls, is understudied. Additionally, the effects of resistance exercise and nutritional support on neurotransmitter levels in sarcopenic patients are largely unknown. This study explores these neurochemical changes and their response to therapeutic interventions.MethodsThis post hoc analysis of a randomized controlled trial included 57 sarcopenic and 57 non-sarcopenic participants from the same cohort. Grip strength and body composition were measured. Sarcopenic patients received a 12-week intervention involving resistance exercise and supplementation with branched-chain amino acids, calcium, and vitamin D3. Plasma adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine, serotonin, and cortisol were assessed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).ResultsSarcopenic individuals had significantly lower levels of serotonin (13.28 vs 19.21 ng/mL, p = 0.002), adrenaline (97.6 vs 110.9 pg/mL, p < 0.001), and noradrenaline (169.2 vs 302.5 pg/mL, p < 0.001), with a trend toward reduced dopamine (246.9 vs 270.6 pg/mL, p = 0.053). Cortisol levels were similar between the groups (48.2 vs 49.5 ng/mL, p = 0.436). Generalized estimating equations adjusted for age and gender, showed sarcopenia was linked to reduced serotonin (β = −5.92, p = 0.002), adrenaline (β = −13.32, p < 0.001), and noradrenaline (β = -132.9, p < 0.001), while the intervention raised noradrenaline levels (β = 27.75, p = 0.001).ConclusionSarcopenic patients exhibit lower serotonin, adrenaline, and noradrenaline compared to non-sarcopenic controls, with no significant difference in cortisol levels. Resistance exercise and nutritional interventions increased noradrenaline in sarcopenic individuals. Further studies are needed to evaluate the long-term effects on neurotransmitter and hormone levels.Keywords: Sarcopenia, neurotransmitter, hormone, resistance exercise, nutrition

Introduction

Sarcopenia, a condition characterized by the progressive loss of muscle mass and function, is highly prevalent among older adults, affecting up to 10% of individuals over the age of 60 years and nearly half of those over 80 years [1]. Its impact extends beyond physical decline, as studies increasingly highlight its plausible association with cognitive impairment [2] and mood disorders, including depression [3]. These correlations suggest that sarcopenia may play a key role in disrupting not only the musculoskeletal system but also others, such as the neuroendocrine system. On the other hand, neurologic insults at various levels from the brain to neuromuscular junctions (NMJs) can also cause sarcopenia [4]. Dopaminergic downregulation leading to decline of supraspinal drive, motor unit reorganization, and neurotransmitter inadequacy in NMJs has been suggested to be the etiology of sarcopenia [5].

Meanwhile, the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal axis is central to stress regulation and involves a complex interplay of hormones and neurotransmitters [6]. Adrenaline, primarily produced by the adrenal medulla, and noradrenaline, released both from the sympathetic nerve endings and the adrenal medulla, drive the “fight or flight” response by increasing the heart rate, blood pressure, and energy availability. It increases the force of muscle contraction, allowing for more powerful movements during physical activity [7]. Serotonin, which regulates mood, sleep, and appetite, is predominantly produced by enterochromaffin cells in the gastrointestinal tract, with most of it stored in platelets after being released into the bloodstream [8]. It can promote longitudinal muscle fiber growth, enhance myoblast differentiation and fusion, and potentially modulate muscle stem cell activity [9]. Dopamine is crucial for motivation, attention, and motor control, and in the bloodstream, it originates mainly from the sympathetic nervous system and adrenal glands and can indirectly contribute to muscle growth [10]. Cortisol, synthesized in the adrenal cortex and largely bound to corticosteroid-binding globulin in the bloodstream, is key in managing metabolism, inflammation, and stress responses [11]. It has a mixed impact on muscle growth [12]. In sarcopenia, hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal axis dysregulation may occur, though the mechanisms remain unclear.

Exercise and nutritional interventions have been shown to positively influence both sarcopenia and the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal axis. Aerobic exercise normalizes cortisol levels and enhances neurotransmitter balance [13]. Early resistance exercise and nutritional intervention may be helpful for earlier restoration of lower extremity muscle mass in sarcopenic elders [14]. However, no studies have investigated the association between sarcopenia and the levels of all the aforementioned neurotransmitters, nor have any examined the effects of resistance exercise and nutritional support on neurotransmitter levels in this population. Our study aims to fill this gap by exploring the differences in neurochemical markers between participants with and without sarcopenia and by investigating how therapeutic interventions affect these markers in participants with sarcopenia.

Materials and methods

Participant recruitment

Participants older than 65 years and above attended their annual health examination in a community hospital. To be eligible, they needed to comprehend spoken guidance, walk without assistance, and complete questionnaires independently. Individuals with a history of cancer or uncontrolled medical conditions, such as unstable angina or hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state, were excluded.

During enrollment, participants provided written informed consent and underwent measurements of grip strength and body composition. Those diagnosed with sarcopenia were invited to join a randomized controlled trial (registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT02779088) comparing early versus delayed exercise and nutritional interventions for sarcopenia. The early intervention group began with a comprehensive, supervised intervention followed by a home-based program, whereas the delayed group started with the home-based phase before receiving the comprehensive intervention. Both groups received two 12-week intervention phases separated by a two-week break. Non-sarcopenic participants were paired by age and gender with sarcopenic participants from the same community group for neurotransmitter level comparison.

Definition of sarcopenia

The 2019 criteria established by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) were used to determine sarcopenia [15]. To meet these criteria, participants needed to have low muscle mass along with either reduced muscle function or decreased physical performance. Low muscle mass was characterized by a skeletal muscle mass index less than 7.0 kg/m² for men and less than 5.7 kg/m² for women. Diminished muscle function was indicated by handgrip strength less than 28 kg for men or 18 kg for women. Reduced physical performance was identified by a gait speed of less than 1.0 m/s [16].

Measurements for determining sarcopenia

The Baseline® hydraulic hand dynamometer (Fabrication Enterprises Inc., Irvington, NY, USA) was used to evaluate grip strength in the dominant hand. The participants were asked to compress the device with their maximal effort three times, with a 1-min rest between trials. The highest recorded value served as the measure of grip strength [17]. Before the examinations, all participants were advised to refrain from eating for at least eight hours prior to the study. They were required to change into medical clothing after their regular attire for the measurements was removed. A standard digital scale and height meter were used to measure body weight and height, with a precision of 100 g for weight and 1 mm for height. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight by the square of height (kg/m²). Skeletal muscle mass index was assessed via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA, Stratos dR, DMS Group, Paris, France).

Resistance exercise and nutritional intervention

This study was a post hoc analysis based on data from older adults diagnosed with sarcopenia who were recruited for a randomized controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02779088) [18]. This trial aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of early versus delayed exercise and nutritional interventions on sarcopenia, so skeletal muscle mass, grip strength and gait speed were the primary outcomes. Plasma adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine, serotonin, and cortisol were measured before and after the 12-week intervention, with a post hoc analysis comparing the effects on these biomarkers. Flow diagram of the study process.

The exercise program included a 10-min warm-up, 3 sets of 10 repetitions of strengthening exercises (leg press, leg extension, and leg curl), and a 10-min cool-down on a stationary bike. Training sessions were held twice weekly. The participants received daily nutritional supplementation consisting of two sticks of branched-chain amino acids (BCAA-Amino Vital Pro®, Ajinomoto, Tokyo, Japan) and two tablets of Caltrate, which provided 600 mg of calcium and 800 IU of vitamin D3 (Pfizer, New York, NY, USA). Each BCAA stick (3.6 g) contained 0.54 g of leucine, 0.43 g of isoleucine, 0.36 g of valine, 0.65 g of glutamine, 0.61 g of arginine, and 1.01 g of other amino acids [18].

Determination of plasma neurotransmitter/hormone level

Participants fasted for 8 hours overnight, and in the morning, 10 ml of venous blood was drawn from the antecubital vein. Immediately after collection, the biochemical parameters were analyzed. The plasma was separated immediately by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The samples were stored in 500 μl aliquots at −80 °C until analysis. The plasma concentrations of adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine (3-CAT, LDN #BA E-5600R), serotonin (LDN #BA E-5900R), and cortisol (Cayman #500360) were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits from LDN (Am Eichenhain 1, Nordhorn, Germany) and Cayman (Ann Arbor, MI, USA), respectively. The optical density at 450 nm for 3-CAT and 410 nm for cortisol was measured with a spectrophotometer to determine the plasma neurotransmitter levels.

Statistical analysis

For univariate analysis, continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed data, as determined by the Shapiro‒Wilk test, and the Mann‒Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test, with the latter applied in cases of sparse data. A repeated-measures ANOVA was used to assess the effects of exercise and nutritional intervention on neurotransmitter levels in participants with sarcopenia. Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) was used to examine the associations among neurotransmitter levels, grip strength, and the skeletal muscle index (SMI). Effect sizes were expressed as Cohen's d.

For multivariate analysis, the generalized estimating equation (GEE) method was utilized to evaluate the relationship between neurotransmitter levels and factors such as age, sex, sarcopenia, and intervention (nutrition and exercise), enabling the assessment of longitudinal data, including pre- and post-intervention outcomes in the same individuals [19]. Statistical analyses were conducted using MedCalc version 14.0 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium) and SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0, Armonk, NY, USA). All tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participants recruitment

An RCT initially invited 66 individuals diagnosed with sarcopenia to participate in a study comparing the effects of early versus delayed combined nutrition and exercise interventions. Data from 57 sarcopenic patients who underwent complete plasma analysis of adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine, serotonin, and cortisol before and after the intervention were analyzed. These individuals were compared to a matched group of 57 non-sarcopenic participants, selected based on gender and sex, from the same cohort of 597 older adults undergoing annual health assessments.

Comparison between sarcopenic participants and controls

The sarcopenic group demonstrated significantly lower values for body weight (p < 0.001), body mass index (p < 0.001), grip strength (p = 0.006), and skeletal muscle mass index (p < 0.001) when compared to the control group. Additionally, sarcopenic individuals had reduced levels of adrenaline (p < 0.001), noradrenaline (p < 0.001), and serotonin (p = 0.002), with a trend toward lower dopamine levels (p = 0.053) observed in these patients. However, cortisol levels did not differ significantly between participants with and without sarcopenia (p = 0.436) (Table 1, Figure 2). There was a significant correlation between the skeletal muscle mass index and the baseline levels of adrenaline and noradrenaline (Figure 3). The ROC analysis for discriminating between sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic participants revealed area under the curve values of 0.67 (95% CI, 0.57–0.77) for serotonin, 0.70 (95% CI, 0.60−0.80) for adrenaline, 0.90 (95% CI, 0.85−0.95) for noradrenaline, 0.61 (95% CI, 0.50−0.71) for dopamine, and 0.54 (95% CI, 0.44−0.65) for cortisol (Figure 4).

Impact of exercise and nutritional intervention

After the intervention, the sarcopenic group showed a marked increase in grip strength and skeletal muscle mass index. Furthermore, noradrenaline levels significantly increased (p < 0.001) compared to baseline values (Table 2). Age- and gender-adjusted GEE analysis showed that sarcopenia was significantly associated with reductions in serotonin (p = 0.002), adrenaline (p < 0.001), and noradrenaline (p < 0.001), whereas the intervention specifically led to elevated noradrenaline levels (p = 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study revealed several key findings. First, patients with sarcopenia exhibited lower levels of adrenaline, noradrenaline, and serotonin, with a trend toward reduced dopamine compared to non-sarcopenic controls. Second, exercise and nutritional interventions were linked to increased noradrenaline levels in sarcopenic patients.

We observed lower levels of serotonin, noradrenaline, and adrenaline in patients with sarcopenia compared to non-sarcopenic controls, a finding that has been rarely explored in existing human studies. These neurotransmitters are critical in regulating various physiological processes that influence muscle health, and their decline with aging could contribute to the development and progression of sarcopenia. Both human and animal studies suggest a positive correlation between neurotransmitter levels in the brain and those in the rest of the body [20]. Serotonin, while often associated with mood regulation, also plays a role in muscle function and fatigue. Lower serotonin levels have been linked to depression, increased fatigue, and decreased muscle strength [21], conditions that are commonly observed in individuals with sarcopenia [3]. Furthermore, serotonin influences physical activity levels, and reduced serotonin may lead to lower activity [22], which can exacerbate muscle loss. This neurotransmitter is also involved in modulating inflammation, and a decrease in serotonin may contribute to a pro-inflammatory environment [23], which can accelerate muscle degradation and worsen sarcopenia.

Noradrenaline and adrenaline are key components of the sympathetic nervous system that play vital role in maintaining muscle tone, metabolism, and blood flow. The reference range for noradrenaline and adrenaline are 70 to 1700 pg/mL and 0 to 140 pg/mL, respectively [24]. With aging, sympathetic activity often decreases, resulting in lower catecholamine levels, as observed in animal studies [25]. This reduction can impair muscle regeneration and protein synthesis [26], hindering the repair and maintenance of muscle tissue. Additionally, both noradrenaline and adrenaline play vital roles in energy metabolism by mobilizing glucose and free fatty acids [27], which are crucial for maintaining muscle mass and function. A decline in these neurotransmitters may cause energy deficits in muscles, leading to weakness and reduced endurance, both of which are hallmark features of sarcopenia.

Lower dopamine levels may be linked to sarcopenia, supporting our observation of a trend in this direction. Dopamine is crucial in regulating motor control, energy metabolism, and physical activity—all of which are essential for maintaining muscle function. Reduced dopamine levels are associated with muscle weakness and impaired motor function, as observed in neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson's disease [28], which shares characteristics with sarcopenia. Dopamine influences physical activity and motivation, with lower levels potentially leading to reduced exercise and increased muscle degradation, while exercise may counteract the anticipated decrease in dopamine transporter availability, as observed in a neuromelanin-sensitive magnetic resonance study [29]. While direct studies are limited, the impact of dopamine on these physiological pathways is consistent with the mechanisms underlying sarcopenia.

In this study, we found that resistance exercise and nutritional interventions increased plasma noradrenaline levels in sarcopenic patients. Similarly, Piancone et al. [30]. reported a significant increase in noradrenaline levels (p = 0.016) after a personalized rehabilitation program for 60 sarcopenic patients, which included balance and strength training. Noradrenaline changes were positively correlated with improvements in Tinetti, Barthel (p < 0.0001), and short physical performance battery scores (p = 0.0002). Both our group and Piancone et al. found no significant changes in adrenaline, dopamine, or serotonin levels after the intervention. In sarcopenic patients, elevated noradrenaline may help reduce proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), which are known to decrease following structured exercise and nutritional support [31]. Stolk et al. [32]. found that noradrenaline reduced proinflammatory mediators and enhanced IL-10 production in lipopolysaccharide-challenged volunteers. Exercise is well known to increase serum noradrenaline, as demonstrated by Greiwe et al. [33], who observed a progressive rise in noradrenaline during 10 weeks of cycle ergometry training as the intensity increased from 60% to 85% of VO2 max. In an animal model, increased noradrenergic activity in the hypothalamus was reported during treadmill running as well [34]. This increase likely results from greater sympathetic activation and the recruitment of larger skeletal muscle masses during exercise.

In our study, we observed no elevation in cortisol levels in patients with sarcopenia, which contrasts with the expected increase based on prior theories linking sarcopenia to chronic activation of the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal axis [25]. This finding may be explained by the negative feedback mechanism of the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal axis [35], which tightly regulates cortisol levels. In response to even slight elevations in cortisol, this feedback loop actively suppresses further cortisol release by reducing the secretion of corticotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus and adrenocorticotropic hormone from the pituitary gland. This regulatory process may prevent cortisol levels in patients with sarcopenia from increasing significantly, even if the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal axis is chronically activated. Consequently, the normal cortisol levels observed in these patients could represent a homeostatic effort by the body to maintain balance, despite underlying physiological stress or muscle loss typically associated with sarcopenia. Thus, the lack of elevated cortisol in our study could reflect an adaptive response aimed at avoiding the detrimental effects of sustained high cortisol exposure, such as muscle breakdown, rather than the absence of involvement from the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal axis.

This study has several limitations. First, as a post hoc analysis of a randomized controlled trial involving community-dwelling elders undergoing health checkups, selection bias is unavoidable, which limits the generalizability of our findings and necessitates further validation studies. Second, plasma neurotransmitter levels were measured within one week of the intervention, leaving the long-term effects uncertain. Third, the relatively small sample size calls for larger studies to confirm our results. Fourth, while the control group was matched for age and gender, other potential confounders (e.g. baseline activity levels and comorbidities) were not accounted for, potentially introducing residual confounding.

Conclusion

Compared to non-sarcopenic participants, patients with sarcopenia exhibit lower levels of adrenaline, noradrenaline, and serotonin, with a trend toward reduced dopamine. No significant differences in plasma cortisol levels were observed between the sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic groups. Resistance exercise and nutritional interventions were associated with increased noradrenaline levels in sarcopenic patients. Further large-scale studies are needed to assess the long-term effects of exercise and nutritional interventions on neurotransmitters and related hormones.
