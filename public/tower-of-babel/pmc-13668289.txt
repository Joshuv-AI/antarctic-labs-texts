Research priorities and knowledge gaps in understanding hepatobiliary disease in cystic fibrosis
Authors: Dominique Debray, William R. Hunt, Frank Bodewes, Sarah E. Hempstead, Daniel Peckham, Rebecca S. Petit, Alexander Philbrick, Michael Narkewicz, Zachary M. Sellers, David N. Assis, Meghana Sathe
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Cystic fibrosis (CF) hepatobiliary involvement (CFHBI) occurs in 30%–40% of people with CF (PwCF),1 encompassing a spectrum of manifestations, including but not limited to transaminase elevation, imaging abnormalities, and hepatic fibrosis. A subset of these individuals will progress to advanced CF liver disease (aCFLD), as manifested by F4 fibrosis, multilobular cirrhosis with or without portal hypertension or non-cirrhotic portal hypertension. Recently, the CF Foundation (CFF) updated guidelines for screening, monitoring, and managing CFHBI and aCFLD.2 As part of this process, the Guideline Committee identified critical gaps in knowledge that prevent informed clinical care. This brief report focuses on these key topics needing further research to advance the care of CFHBI in PwCF (Table 1).

SCREENING AND MONITORING FOR CFHBI AND aCFLD

Liver-focused screening in PwCF aims to detect hepatic disease early and stratify the risk of developing aCFLD. Screening tools should be safe, non-invasive, and cost-effective with strong diagnostic and prognostic accuracy. Routinely employed tools in other liver diseases, including liver fibrosis indices (AST-to-platelet ratio index, APRI; fibrosis-4 index, FIB-4; GGT-to-platelet ratio, GPR), liver stiffness measurement (LSM) techniques [transient elastography, ultrasound (US) shear-wave elastography, and magnetic resonance elastography] have recently been studied and validated in CF. Recognizing this, the committee recommended the use of these tools for the screening and monitoring of hepatobiliary disease in CF.2 However, key questions remain on how they should be used in specific populations and circumstances.

Historically, the onset of liver disease in PwCF has been reported to occur in children with a median age of diagnosis of 10 years, and in a few cases after the age of 20 years.3 However, these studies preceded the improvements in CF care that have led to a median survival now expected to be above 60 years of age. Multiple studies in children and adolescents have shown the ability of US to aid in the detection of hepatobiliary complications; therefore, the committee recommended screening with abdominal US every 2 years in PwCF, starting at age 3 years old or at diagnosis through late adolescence.2 However, there is a paucity of such data in adults, and the optimal US screening frequency is unclear; no recommendations were made for this population.

For those diagnosed with CFHBI or aCFLD, an annual LSM assessment is advised to monitor disease progression. Early data from LSM suggest more variability in CF than in other disorders. Research is needed to understand the variability of LSM in CF, true clinical utility, and, if appropriate, to develop thresholds specific to CFHBI for LSM assessment for normal, mild-to-moderate hepatic fibrosis, and aCFLD.4

CFTR MODULATOR EFFECTS

Cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulator therapies have transformed CF care.5 These oral drugs are extensively metabolized by the liver (primarily CYP3A) and eliminated through the feces. Drug-related toxicity is common, with 5%–20% of individuals having elevated transaminases and a much smaller percentage (<1%) demonstrating severe hepatitis, or requiring liver transplantation.5 However, the mechanisms of this hepatic toxicity are unknown. Potential areas of exploration could include characterizing the impact of cytochrome P450 genetic polymorphisms on variation in drug metabolism, the relationship of CFTR modulator drug and metabolite levels and DILI, the impact of drug–drug interactions, and characterizing the immune response in DILI.6

CFF clinical guidelines suggest using CFTR modulators in PwCF with CFHBI, with close monitoring, but refrain from recommending use in decompensated aCFLD due to insufficient evidence and theoretical safety concerns. Small studies support safety in CFHBI, but data are mostly retrospective.7 Early evidence is unclear whether modulators improve LSM and biochemical markers, especially when started early. Longitudinal data are needed to understand the long-term effects of modulator therapy on the risk of development of CFHBI and the impact on those with existing CFHBI. Further efforts to more specifically phenotype individuals with CFHBI may clarify whether CFTR modulators may alter hepatobiliary disease in PwCF, and if so, in what group(s) of people.

Vanzacaftor/tezacaftor/deutivacaftor (VTD) has distinct pharmacokinetic properties and longer half-lives (vanzacaftor 92.8 h, tezacaftor 22.5 h, deutivacaftor 19.2 h) compared with ETI (elexacaftor 27.4 h, tezacaftor 25.1 h, ivacaftor 15 h), allowing for once-daily dosing.8 CFTR modulator pharmacokinetics have been studied for efficacy on sweat chloride levels and lung function, not for CFHBI or aCFLD. Furthermore, portal hypertension and portosystemic shunting may alter drug metabolism by reducing first-pass metabolism, potentially increasing systemic drug levels, which may impact efficacy and toxicity. The role of therapeutic drug monitoring in PwCF and CHBHI treated with CFTR modulators is unclear. It is also unclear whether PwCF who developed liver test elevations with one CFTR modulator may be safely treated with others, given the overlapping use of the same or structurally similar drugs.

NON-CFTR MODULATOR INTERVENTIONS

There are no FDA-approved therapies for the prevention or treatment of aCFLD. There is a lack of clarity of the underlying mechanism(s) that lead to eventual aCFLD. Thus, basic and translational research on the mechanism(s) of aCFLD using clinically relevant models is necessary. Further, there are numerous opportunities for clinical research investigating the potential utility of drugs targeting other liver diseases to benefit PwCF and hepatobiliary disease.

While aCFLD refers to having one (or more) of the following: nodular liver, advanced fibrosis (F4), multilobular cirrhosis with or without portal hypertension, or non-cirrhotic portal hypertension, the frequency of complications seems to be much lower than in portal hypertension from other causes. Research is needed to understand the role of interventions like primary variceal prophylaxis, beta blockade, and other therapies commonly used to manage portal hypertension in the general population for PwCF and portal hypertension. Emerging evidence suggests that many PwCF who have portal hypertension may have portal venopathy and subsequent porto-sinusoidal vascular disease (PSVD) leading to non-cirrhotic portal hypertension. The diagnosis of PSVD requires a high-quality liver biopsy to evaluate for histologic cirrhosis, yet with liver biopsies not part of routine clinical work-up, it is unclear what the prevalence of PSVD is in CF and when to perform liver biopsies.

Obesity is increasingly common in PwCF, particularly following widespread access to ETI. Hepatic steatosis was common in CF prior to ETI, but with an increase in obesity, differentiating metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which is strongly associated with obesity, is important, given the recent approval of therapies for MASLD. Meta-analysis showed that calorie restriction and Mediterranean diet interventions reduce liver enzymes, liver fat, and stiffness.9 Lifestyle interventions may therefore be reasonable. However, only recently has the dogma “any calorie is a good calorie” been challenged in PwCF.

Defective CFTR function in the gut epithelium promotes dysbiosis, barrier dysfunction, and mucosal inflammation, contributing to cholangiopathy, periportal injury, and porto-sinusoidal vascular disease (PSVD). Experimental models show that restoring gut CFTR function, or modulating the microbiota, may prevent CFHBI. CFTR modulators may normalize bile acid metabolism, suggesting a role in restoring gut–liver homeostasis.10 Blood or urine bile acid profiles could serve as biomarkers of response. Future studies should explore microbiome profiles, test targeted microbiome modulation, and evaluate multi-omics in CFBHI, particularly specific sub-phenotypes such as non-cirrhotic portal hypertension.

CONCLUSION

While CFTR modulators benefit pulmonary and nutritional outcomes in CF, their effects on liver disease remain uncertain. Key priorities include optimizing screening strategies, clarifying modulators’ impact on CFHBI and aCFLD, exploring new therapeutic approaches, and understanding the liver–gut axis. With the future ripe for research and comprehensive management, the outlook for PwCF with liver involvement can continue to improve.
