Incidence and prevention of postprocedural bacteraemia and complications following water vapour therapy of the prostate: a retrospective review and quality improvement initiative at a tertiary care centre
Authors: Elias Tannous, Waleed Hassan, Jeffrey Chapman, Maria-Fernanda Bonilla, Rania El Lababidi, Claire Nolan, Nicole Bond, Maria Sabra, You-Mee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia is a common condition affecting ageing men, often associated with lower urinary tract symptoms that impair quality of life. Water vapour therapy has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative to traditional surgical approaches, offering symptom relief with reduced morbidity compared with transurethral resection of the prostate [1]. Clinical trials and observational studies in routine clinical practice settings have demonstrated its safety and efficacy, with most adverse events being expected, self-limiting postprocedural urinary symptoms, such as dysuria and haematuria [1,2].

However, infectious complications, though rare, can occur and may lead to emergency department visits or hospital admission. Published data report urinary tract infection (UTI) rates of approximately 3–6% and bacteraemia as an uncommon but clinically significant event [1,3,[6], [7], [8]]. These benchmarks underscore the importance of preventive strategies, including optimized antimicrobial prophylaxis and patient education, to minimize risk.

This study was initiated as a quality improvement (QI) project following observed cases of postprocedure bacteraemia at our institution. Our aim was to evaluate the impact of a set of targeted interventions, including standardized operating procedures (SOPs), revised antibiotic protocols, and enhanced patient education, on reducing infectious complications and improving overall safety.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective observational review of these procedures performed at Cleveland Clinic Abu Dhabi from January 2021 to December 2025. The pre-intervention cohort included 82 cases (Jan 2021–Sep 2023), and the postintervention cohort included 46 cases (Feb 2024–Dec 2025).

Interventions initiated in Q3 2023 and fully implemented prior to the postintervention analysis period included a set of targeted QI interventions: (1) a SOP for patient selection and microbiological assessment, including routine urine analysis and culture within 30 days prior to the procedure; (2) microbiology-guided antibiotic prophylaxis, with ampicillin 2 g plus gentamicin 5 mg/kg administered within 1 h before the procedure, with timing optimized to ensure adequate tissue levels at the time of intervention, and vancomycin for penicillin allergy; (3) enhanced patient education, including video-based counselling and postprocedural care instructions; and (4) an emergency department alert pathway for suspected postprocedure sepsis.

Urinary retention was classified as a side-effect as it is a recognized and expected transient consequence of postprocedural prostatic oedema, despite occasionally requiring short-term catheterization.

Categorical outcomes were summarized as counts and percentages for the pre-intervention and postintervention cohorts. Comparisons between groups were performed using 2 × 2 contingency tables. Given the relatively small sample size and low event frequencies, including zero-event cells, two-sided Fisher’s exact tests were used rather than χ2 tests as Fisher’s exact test provides more accurate inference in sparse data settings.

Absolute risk differences (postintervention minus pre-intervention risk) and relative risks (RRs) were calculated to quantify the effect of the intervention. For outcomes with zero events in one group, RRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using a Haldane–Anscombe continuity correction (0.5 added to each cell) to allow stable estimation. All analyses were exploratory in nature and undertaken as part of a QI evaluation rather than formal hypothesis testing.

Following implementation of the set of targeted QI interventions, including SOPs, revised antibiotic prophylaxis, and enhanced patient education, no cases of bacteraemia or UTIs were observed in the postintervention cohort (0/46), representing a reduction compared with the pre-intervention cohort (5/82; 6.1% for each outcome). This represents a risk difference of −6.1% for each outcome and an RR of 0.18 (95% CI: 0.01–3.16), reflecting a reduction to zero observed events rather than complete elimination. Although the Fisher’s exact test did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.1589), the clinical impact is substantial given the severity of these complications.

Candiduria, which was absent in the pre-intervention cohort, appeared in one case (2.2%) post intervention. This finding was not statistically significant (P = 0.3594) and is based on a single event, with a wide CI indicating substantial uncertainty and limited precision. While this could theoretically reflect broader antimicrobial exposure, candiduria is commonly associated with urinary catheterization, colonization, and host-related factors rather than true infection. Importantly, there was no concurrent increase in other infectious complications; in contrast, both bacteraemia and UTIs declined following the intervention. The absence of a consistent pattern of fungal complications suggests that this isolated case is most consistent with random variation or patient-specific factors in the context of a small sample size, rather than a systematic unintended consequence of antibiotic use.

Among side-effects, urine retention decreased from 6.1% to 0, mirroring improvements in periprocedural care and catheter management. Haematuria remained relatively stable, increasing slightly from 13.4% to 15.2% (risk difference: +1.8%, P = 0.7954), which is consistent with its classification as an expected, self-limiting effect of thermal therapy rather than a preventable complication. The most notable improvement among side-effects was in dysuria, which dropped from 19.5% to 8.7% (risk difference: −10.8%, RR: 0.45, P = 0.1317). This reduction suggests that enhanced patient counselling and optimized catheter protocols may have contributed to better symptom management and patient experience.

Although none of these changes achieved statistical significance due to sample size limitations, the primary aim was QI rather than hypothesis testing. The observed trends, particularly the reduction to zero observed cases of bacteraemia and UTIs, represent a meaningful clinical benefit, reducing patient risk and potential resource use.

Discussion

Our findings mirror reported evidence from published clinical studies demonstrating that water vapour therapy is a safe and effective minimally invasive treatment with a generally low rate of infectious complications [[6], [7], [8]]. Evidence from recent observational cohorts and real-world studies further support its favourable safety profile and low incidence of postprocedural infections [7,8]. However, although infrequent, infectious complications remain clinically relevant because of their potential to cause morbidity and unplanned healthcare use. International guidelines strongly recommend that antibiotic prophylaxis be administered within 60 min of incision, with broader coverage considered for patients at elevated risk of infection, such as those with indwelling catheters or prior UTIs [4].

In our QI initiative, the intervention focussed on aligning local practice with these evidence-based standards. Specifically, switching to ampicillin plus gentamicin administered within 1 h before the procedure, with timing optimized to ensure adequate tissue levels at the time of intervention, provided enhanced coverage against common uropathogens and optimized tissue penetration. This approach is consistent with the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists and Infectious Diseases Society of America surgical prophylaxis guidelines, which emphasize timing and spectrum of antimicrobial therapy as critical determinants of effectiveness [5].

When comparing our outcomes to published studies, the postintervention cohort showed no observed cases of bacteraemia or UTIs (0/46), compared with reported rates of approximately 3–6% for UTIs in the literature [[6], [7], [8]]. This represents a reduction relative to the pre-intervention cohort (5/82; 6.1% for each outcome), without any increase in haematuria or other adverse events. This suggests that targeted interventions, when implemented systematically, may contribute to lower observed complication rates within the study setting.

These findings reinforce the value of a set of targeted QI interventions, which combine SOPs, patient education, and antimicrobial stewardship. Such interventions not only reduce complications but also enhance patient confidence and streamline care pathways. While statistical significance was not achieved due to sample size limitations, the magnitude and direction of change, particularly the reduction to zero observed cases of serious infections within the study cohort, represent a clinically meaningful improvement that aligns with best practices and international standards [1,2,4,5].

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations. The retrospective design prevents causal attribution of the observed improvements to the intervention bundle alone, while the single-centre setting may limit generalizability to other institutions. In addition, the relatively small sample size and low event frequency reduced statistical power and contributed to wide CIs, increasing the possibility of type II error. Finally, because multiple interventions were implemented simultaneously, the individual contribution of each component could not be determined. Despite these limitations, the observed reduction in infectious complications provides clinically relevant evidence supporting continued use and evaluation of these QI measures.

In conclusion, targeted interventions, protocolized antimicrobial prophylaxis, a formal SOP, and patient education, were associated with a reduction to zero observed infectious complications within the study cohort. Even without statistical significance, the QI focus supports adoption and continued monitoring of these practices as part of routine water vapour therapy care.
