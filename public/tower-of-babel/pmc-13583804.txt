Background

The spread of misinformation on social media is a major public health concern, with prevalence estimates reaching up to 87% of posts depending on the specific topic.1 In response, health professionals are increasingly engaging in online advocacy and knowledge mobilisation activities to counter inaccurate claims and provide reliable information to patients and the public.2 3 Within health professions education (HPE), competency frameworks outline expectations for trainees (ie, health professions students engaged in a clinical training programme resulting in a professional designation or licensure) to develop skills in advocacy, communication and the use of health information technologies.4–7 Social media may provide a platform for trainees to start developing these competencies, but at present, little is known about whether and how they are being equipped with the digital skills and knowledge to use social media appropriately for these purposes.

The social media landscape is rapidly evolving. Web 2.0 transformed the internet from static, read-only pages to collaborative, user-driven content. The emergence of Web 2.0 tools (eg, Wikis, social media, forums, blogs) suddenly allowed citizens around the globe to become creators and users of information, thus changing the ‘rules’ of knowledge creation and ownership in real-time.8–10 Social media, in this era, also created a new ‘third space’ or town square, where users could connect and build community in spaces separate from their homes and workspaces.11 12 Increasingly, Web 2.0 has faced challenges—notably the reliance on centralised platforms with significant control over users’ content and data, which can be monetised or manipulated without user consent.13 It is also facing data privacy breaches, the spread of disinformation and the concentration of power in the hands of big companies.13 These shifts have the potential to alter what it means to participate responsibly in social media-based advocacy and knowledge sharing, potentially creating new requirements for digital literacies in health professions programmes.

Digital literacies refer to the range of skills required to engage in online environments, including the ability to access, analyse, evaluate, create and participate in all forms of media.14 Digital literacy education is particularly important given the difficulty users face in distinguishing credible content from misleading material. Early in 2025, for example, TikTok was temporarily banned and then reinstated in the United States,15 while Meta announced plans to remove fact-checking and simultaneously expand the presence of AI-generated ‘people’ across its platforms.16 17 Indeed, AI ‘slop’—defined as fake, unconvincing content—is rapidly dominating social media platforms, causing people to consume large volumes of content that is not real, meaningful or interesting.18 Such changes heighten the challenges health professionals, trainees and the public encounter when seeking or sharing trustworthy online information and have downstream implications for knowledge mobilisation, education and advocacy activities.

Social media has the potential to be a powerful tool for helping to optimise the adoption, appropriate adaptation and sustainability of effective practices and policies within specific contexts, however realising these benefits is contingent on thoughtful and responsible use.19–22 Within HPE specifically, social media offers both opportunities and challenges for sharing knowledge and engaging in advocacy. On the one hand, platforms—like LinkedIn, X, Instagram, TikTok and YouTube—allow researchers to quickly share their findings with global audiences using hashtags, images, website links, videos and documents.23 They also provide space for health professionals and trainees to find community, learn from others’ experiences and advocate for the health issues that matter to them.24 On the other hand, technology is not neutral and has been designed purposefully to serve specific sociocultural, political and economic interests.25 Algorithms shape what information is made available to users and can often create a filter bubble, where the information available to one person is dramatically different from what is presented to another, based on factors such as browsing history, demographics and postal code, among others. Social media is also rife with misinformation and trolling, exposing trainees to–or forcing them to grapple with–questionable knowledge sharing practices and abuse.26 Indeed, while social media use may provide a sense of community, sponsorship and space for professional development as advocates, educators and networkers,27 engagement in these activities has also led to harassment, trolling and even backlash for minority populations especially.28 Additionally, the role of online power dynamics (ie, among health professionals, trainees, patients and the public) and the influence of algorithms need to be considered in the context of social media-based knowledge sharing in HPE. Social media is being leveraged by users (eg, influencers) to build their own brand or digital identity and followership,29 and while users with large follower bases may facilitate the rapid sharing of information, they may also increase the risk of misinformation as the public may mistake the power that comes with online popularity for credibility.21

Health professions trainees need to develop the knowledge, values and a range of critical thinking, communication and information management skills specific to digital contexts.4 30 31 Teaching digital literacies can help ensure that trainees can engage meaningfully with technology and participate fully in digital cultures, especially those aligned with key competencies and expectations, such as shifts towards digital health.32 33 However, knowing how a digital tool functions is only one component of digital literacy. Digital literacies also include understanding how media and technology shape society.32 34 Consequently, in alignment with existing guidance on digital literacies, health professionals and trainees should understand why a tool is valuable in real-world contexts, when its use is appropriate, and what the potential impacts or repercussions of their activities may be for themselves and for patients and the public.34–36 Furthermore, the ability to instantly access information online must be paired with the capacity to critically assess the quality and trustworthiness of that information,32 especially for health professionals and trainees choosing to engage in online advocacy and knowledge mobilisation activities.

Digital literacies is a relatively new concept in HPE. Historically, the HPE literature on the use of social media has focused on mitigating issues of professionalism, providing dos and don’ts to trainees about how to exhibit appropriate professional behaviour online.5 37–42 Few health professions educators have sufficient grounding in the theories, frameworks and communication strategies required to prepare trainees to use social media to augment their learning, engage in knowledge mobilisation or advocate, particularly given the complexities of how these platforms may affect trainees and their own practices. Digital literacies training should be more than just teaching about online professionalism; it should support health professionals and trainees in communicating such information online in accessible and credible ways and, by extension, support citizens in locating and evaluating trustworthy information.43 At present, little is known about whether and how digital literacies are addressed within HPE. Therefore, the aim of this study is to conduct a critical scoping review of the extant literature to understand what, if any, digital literacies are being explicitly taught and how these interventions prepare trainees to engage in social media-based advocacy and knowledge mobilisation activities. Our critical approach to this scoping review will involve identifying gaps in the literature while also adopting a critical perspective and theoretical framework.44 By doing so, we aim to both bolster digital literacies training and challenge commonly held beliefs and perspectives related to social media use for advocacy and knowledge mobilisation.45 We have successfully used this approach before in Giroux et al20

Theoretical framework

We will use Engeström’s46 47 activity theory to explore how training in digital literacies influences trainees’ online knowledge-sharing and advocacy activities, if at all. According to activity theory, human activity creates its own context, which is in constant historical and interactive movement. Activity theory expands on social learning theories by exploring complex webs of interactions between human and non-human actors; that is, humans, their environments, the tools they use, the associated rules and the division of labour among community members within the system. Activity theory also provides a structure for thinking about and analysing these interactions in the form of an Activity System.46 48 49 Activity theory is an ideal framework for exploring technology, given its focus on mediation and tools. It enables an examination of the reasons and ways in which health professions educators engage—or choose not to engage—with social media, while situating this inquiry within the broader complexities of knowledge sharing, including algorithms, online power dynamics, the hidden curriculum and the development of digital literacies. figure 1 depicts an Activity System.46 49

Methods

This scoping review follows Arksey and O’Malley’s framework,50 with updates by Levac et al51 and Peters et al.52 53 We will report our study according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR).54

Step two: identify relevant studies and other important information

The anticipated start date for the search is March 2026, with an anticipated study completion date of February 2027.

Database searching

The following databases were searched by a health sciences librarian (LS): Medline and Medline in Process via Ovid, Embase Classic+Embase via Ovid, APA PsycINFO via Ovid, CINAHL via EBSCO, Education Source via EBSCO and ERIC via Ovid. A search strategy was developed in Medline, and then translated into the other databases, as appropriate (see online supplemental appendix A). All databases were searched from 2015 to 30 June 2026. The Medline search was peer reviewed using the PRESS tool by another librarian (Patrick R Labelle). There were no other publication limits. All references were entered into Covidence for deduplication and screening.

Supplemental searching

We will hand-search the references of key papers to identify additional sources. Depending on the number of returns from the database search, we may engage in forward-backward citation searching to enhance our findings. Additionally, we will explore dissertations, theses and conference publications.

Step five: collate, summarise and report the results

Our search strategy results will be displayed using a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram. Activity theory will be used as an overarching analytic framework for interpreting digital tools, rules, community, division of labour, power relations, tensions and contradictions. We will conduct two types of analyses. The first is a numerical analysis on all bibliographic information, which will consist of calculating descriptive statistics using Microsoft Excel to describe such information as year of publication, location, study design and discipline. The second type of analysis is a directed content analysis57 using activity theory to produce a critical, qualitative synthesis of how digital literacies are taught in HPE programmes. The goal of a directed content analysis is to conceptually extend a theoretical framework or theory.57 It is guided by a structured process using existing theory, in this case activity theory, whereby preliminary coding structures are derived from key theoretical concepts or variables. Operational definitions for each category are determined using the theory. Coding occurs in a predominantly deductive manner, with any data segments that do not fit the coding framework being inductively assigned new codes.57 A directed content analysis supports a critical scoping review since the theory is used to guide the discussion of findings; any newly identified categories either offer a contradictory view of the phenomenon or may serve to extend or enrich activity theory.57 This analysis will be discussed by all team members to extend activity theory as it relates to the digital literacies required for social media-based advocacy and knowledge mobilisation activities.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required for this scoping review, since no human participants will be involved. All research data will be securely stored on the Principal Investigator (PI)’s password protected Microsoft drive on the University of Ottawa secure server. Findings will be disseminated in peer-reviewed publications, academic conferences and through knowledge mobilisation workshops and presentations at institutional research rounds.
