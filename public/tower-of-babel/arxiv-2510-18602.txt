Defect Landscape of Orthorhombic Ba 2 ​ In 2 ​ O 5 \text{Ba}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{In}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{5}}} from First-Principles Calculations: The Role of Oxygen Interstitials Preprint: APS/123-QED
Rachele Sciotto, Karsten Albe

Abstract
The brownmillerite-type oxide Ba 2 ​ In 2 ​ O 5 \text{Ba}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{In}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{5}}} (BIO) is a potential candidate as an electrolyte material for mixed ionic-electronic conduction in solid oxide fuel cells. Despite its structural relation to perovskite oxides, the defect chemistry of BIO has remained largely unexplored. Using Density Functional Theory within the generalized gradient approximation, complemented by selected hybrid-functional calculations, we evaluate the formation energies, charge transition levels, and concentrations as a function of oxygen partial pressure of vacancies, oxygen interstitials, Frenkel pairs, and substitutional Cr doping. Our results reveal that oxygen vacancies and interstitials dominate the intrinsic defect landscape. Among the interstitials, we identify stable dumbbell configurations that remain neutral across the entire band gap. Other interstitial configurations show charged states and become the prevailing compensating defect at high oxygen partial pressures. For extrinsic doping, we find that Cr preferentially substitutes at the tetrahedral In site and behaves as a donor. Its negative formation energies suggest the formation of secondary phases, possibly the tetragonal Cr-doped BIO. These results provide a first picture of the thermodynamics of intrinsic and extrinsic defects in BIO and set the stage for future investigations into the tetragonal phase and the diffusion dynamics of oxygen vacancies and interstitials.



I
INTRODUCTION

Mixed ionic electronic conductors (MIECs) are a class of materials used in electrochemical energy conversion
Riess 2003
. Due to their ability to conduct both ions and electronic charge carriers simultaneously, they have been extensively studied for applications in energy storage, sensors, gas permeation membranes, and, most notably, solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs)
Schmidt et al. 2001
;
Shao et al. 2001
;
Wang et al. 2005
;
Zhang et al. 2008
.
A fuel cell directly converts the chemical energy of a fuel gas into electrical energy, bypassing the intermediate combustion step found in conventional power generation. This process results in higher conversion efficiency compared to thermomechanical methods. While the electrochemical mechanism resembles that of a battery, fuel cells do not require recharging, as they continuously generate electricity as long as fuel and oxidant are supplied
Stambouli and Traversa 2002
.
Goodenough
et al.

Goodenough 1990
first identified
\text{Ba}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{In}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{5}}}
(BIO) as a promising fast oxide-ion conductor due to its unique brownmillerite-type structure. This oxygen-deficient perovskite-related compound features a high concentration of oxygen vacancies, with one out of every six oxygen sites missing. However, these vacancies are not randomly distributed; instead, they adopt a specific ordering that alternates between layers of
\text{In}\text{\hskip 1.29167pt--\hskip 1.29167pt}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}
octahedra and
\text{In}\text{\hskip 1.29167pt--\hskip 1.29167pt}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
tetrahedra, which restricts their mobility: At temperatures above
925\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, BIO undergoes an order-disorder transition, where the previously ordered oxygen vacancies begin to randomize, leading to a sharp increase in ionic conductivity. This transition is accompanied by a structural phase change, first from orthorhombic (
Ibm2
, space group No. 46-2) to a tetragonal phase (
I4cm
, space group No. 108) between
925\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
and
1040\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, and then to a cubic perovskite phase (
Pm\overline{3}m
, space group No. 221) above
1040\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
, in which the oxygen vacancies are fully disordered
Speakman et al. 2002
;
Lee and Morgan 2006
. Computational studies by Mohn
et al.
have shown that the energetically preferred arrangement of oxygen vacancies in BIO is a staggered configuration rather than a stacked alignment
Mohn et al. 2004
;
Mohn et al. 2005
.
The limited mobility of oxygen vacancies in BIO is reminiscent of that observed in bixbyite
\text{In}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
(IO)
Ágoston and Albe 2010
, suggesting that similar defect mechanisms might be at play in both materials.
A recent photoelectron spectroscopy study on the defect chemistry of IO and
Sn
-doped
\text{In}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
(ITO) by Klein
et al.

Klein et al. 2024
found that cathodic polarization of the material leads to oxygen removal and an increase in the Fermi level, reaching a maximum value of
E_{\textrm{F}}-E_{\textrm{VB}}=$3.85\pm 10\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$
for ITO and
E_{\textrm{F}}-E_{\textrm{VB}}=$3.35\pm 10\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}$
for IO. Further increasing the applied voltage leads to the reduction of
\text{Sn}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{4+}}
to
\text{Sn}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
, suggesting that the ultimate limitation of the carrier concentration in ITO is determined by dopant reduction rather than the self-compensating formation of oxygen interstitials.
While the defect chemistry of IO is relatively well understood, much less is known about BIO, particularly regarding the range of Fermi levels that can be accessed through donor doping. In BIO, an upper limit to the Fermi level could potentially be set by the formation of oxygen interstitials, a possibility that remains largely unexplored. In moderately reducing environments, oxygen interstitials might also act as compensating defects, similar to their role in IO
Ágoston and Albe 2010
;
Klein et al. 2024
.
This work aims to clarify the role of intrinsic and extrinsic defects in determining the electronic and thermodynamic properties of orthorhombic BIO from first principles. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that investigates the defect formation energies and charge transition energies of vacancies, oxygen interstitials, Frenkel pairs, and substitutional Cr doping using density functional theory. By analyzing the defect thermodynamics across different chemical potential conditions and experimental oxygen partial pressures, we establish how these defects govern the accessible Fermi level range and the charge compensation mechanisms in BIO. This provides new insights into the defect chemistry of BIO, offering guidance for future studies on the defect and doping engineering of brownmillerite-type materials.
This paper is structured as follows: Section
II
outlines the computational methodology, Section
III
discusses intrinsic and extrinsic defects, and Section
IV
presents the conclusions and prospects for future work.

II.2
Defect thermodynamics

The formation energy
\Delta E_{D}^{f}
of a defect
D
with charge
q
can be written as
Freysoldt et al. 2014

\Delta E_{D}^{f}=E_{D}-E_{P}+q(E_{\textrm{VBM}}+\mu_{e})+E_{\textrm{corr}}-\sum_{i}\Delta n_{i}\mu_{i},

(1)

where
E_{D}
is the total energy of the system with defect
D
and charge
q
, and
E_{P}
is the energy of the pristine reference cell. The third term describes the dependence of the formation energy on the electron chemical potential
\mu_{e}
, while the position of the valence band maximum (VBM)
E_{\textrm{VBM}}
is taken as its reference.
E_{\textrm{corr}}
describes the energy corrections to the spurious electrostatic interactions between periodic images of charged defects, which arise due to the finite size of the supercell. In this work, the correction scheme proposed by Kumagai
et al.

Kumagai and Oba 2014
was employed. Finally, the last term accounts for the dependence of the formation energy on the chemical potentials of the constituent elements. Here,
\Delta n_{i}
is the difference between the number of atoms of species
i
in the defective and bulk systems. The chemical potential
\mu_{i}
of element
i
can be rewritten as

\mu_{i}=\mu_{i}^{\textrm{ref}}+\Delta\mu_{i},

(2)

where
\mu_{i}^{\textrm{ref}}
is the chemical potential of the element
i
in its stable elemental phase.
The DFT data used to generate the phase stability diagram for the orthorhombic brownmillerite BIO were obtained from the Materials Project (MP) database (
pymatgen
implementation, version 2025.5.2)
Jain et al. 2013
;
Ong et al. 2008
;
Ong et al. 2010
, which uses consistent computational parameters with respect to our defect calculations.
A charge transition level (CTL) is the electron chemical potential at which the formation energy of a given defect in two different charge states (
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
) becomes equal

\varepsilon=-\frac{\Delta E_{D}^{F}(q_{1},\mu_{e}=0)-\Delta E_{D}^{F}(q_{2},\mu_{e}=0)}{q_{1}-q_{2}},

(3)

where
\Delta E_{D}^{F}(q_{1},\mu_{e}=0)
and
\Delta E_{D}^{F}(q_{2},\mu_{e}=0)
are the defect formation energies at charge states
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
respectively, when the electron chemical potential
\mu_{e}
is at the VBM.
At thermodynamic equilibrium, the concentration of a defect
D
in charge state
q
is given by

[D_{q}]=N_{\textrm{sites}}\exp{-\frac{\Delta E_{q,D}^{F}(\mu_{e})}{k_{B}T}},

(4)

where
q
represents the defect charge,
N_{\textrm{sites}}
the concentration of the corresponding lattice sites, and
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant.
It is important to underline that the electron chemical potential
\mu_{e}
is not a free parameter. It is rather determined by the charge neutrality condition

n_{e}+n_{A}=n_{h}+n_{D},

(5)

where
n_{e}
and
n_{h}
are the electron and hole carrier concentrations, respectively, while
n_{D}
and
n_{A}
are the donor and acceptor concentrations, respectively.
Considering all defect species
D
in their different charge states
q
, Equation
5
can be reformulated as

\sum_{q,D}{q\cdot[D_{q}]}-n_{e}+n_{h}=0.

(6)

Intrinsic carrier concentrations are computed by integrating the number of unoccupied states up to the VBM (
E_{\textrm{VBM}}
) for holes,

n_{h}=\int_{-\infty}^{E_{\textrm{VBM}}}{D(\varepsilon)[1-f(\varepsilon)]\textrm{d}\varepsilon},

(7)

and the number of occupied states from the conduction band minimum (CBM) (
E_{\textrm{CBM}}
) for electrons,

n_{e}=\int_{E_{\textrm{CBM}}}^{+\infty}{D(\varepsilon)f(\varepsilon)\textrm{d}\varepsilon},

(8)

where
f(\varepsilon,\mu_{e})=[\exp(\varepsilon-\mu_{e})/k_{B}T]^{-1}
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution.

In this study, the integrals in Equation
7
and
8
were computed as Riemann sums over the energies in the calculated density of states, following the method delineated by
pymatgen

Ong et al. 2008
;
Ong et al. 2010
. Subsequently, the equilibrium electron chemical potential
\mu_{e}
was determined self-consistently by solving the charge neutrality condition for
\mu_{e}
at a given temperature.

II.3
Oxygen chemical potential

Defect formation energies depend on the chemical potential of the reservoir. For oxygen,
\mu_{{}\mathrm{O}}
varies with its partial pressure
p_{{}\mathrm{O}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}
and can be expressed via the ideal gas law as

\mu_{{}\mathrm{O}}(T,p_{{}\mathrm{O}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}})=\mu_{{}\mathrm{O}}(T,p^{0})+\frac{1}{2}k_{B}T\ln\left(\frac{p_{{}\mathrm{O}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}}{p^{0}}\right).

(9)

Here,
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant,
T
the temperature,
\mu_{{}\mathrm{O}}
the oxygen chemical potential, and
p^{0}
the reference pressure.
The computed energy of the
{}\mathrm{O}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}
molecule at 0 K
\mu_{{}\mathrm{O}}(\textrm{0 K},p^{0})
serves as the reference. The temperature dependence
\mu_{{}\mathrm{O}}(T,p^{0})
was calculated following Reuter and Scheffler’s methodology
Reuter and Scheffler 2001
, using the thermochemical tables in
Stull and Prophet 1971
. This procedure enables a direct comparison between
ab initio
and experimental results, since the oxygen chemical potential can be calculated from experimental temperature and partial pressure. Consequently, the defect concentration and the electron chemical potential as functions of the oxygen partial pressure are accessible within the same theoretical framework.

This hybrid functional gives a gap of 2.21 eV, which is closer to the experimental value. Thus, our results should be viewed as quantitatively approximate but qualitatively robust, consistent with the established practice in first-principles defect studies

III.3
Defect formation energies

In this study, we have computed defect formation energies according to Equation
1
, in the different regions of the stability diagram reported in Figure
3
and Table
2
. Due to the finite size of the supercell, we have corrected the unphysical electrostatic interactions between charged defect replicas using the method proposed by Kumagai et al.
Kumagai and Oba 2014
.
We evaluated multiple charge states for each defect to determine the thermodynamically stable charge configurations and the associated charge transition levels (CTLs), as described by Equation
3
, to properly describe the contribution of each defect species to the charge neutrality condition. As far as intrinsic defects are concerned, we have studied the formation energies of Ba, In, and O vacancies (
V_{{}\mathrm{Ba}}
,
V_{{}\mathrm{In}}
,
V_{{}\mathrm{O}}
), O interstitials (
O_{{i}}
), and the complex formed by an O interstitial and an O vacancy, also called a Frenkel pair (
O_{{i}}-V_{{}\mathrm{O}}
).
Regarding extrinsic defects, we have evaluated the substitution of Cr at the In sites (
{}\mathrm{Cr}_{{}\mathrm{In}}
).
BIO contains three distinct crystallographic oxygen sites: O(1) in the equatorial plane of the
\text{In}\text{\hskip 1.29167pt--\hskip 1.29167pt}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}
octahedra, O(2) at the apical positions of the octahedra, and O(3) in the
\text{In}\text{\hskip 1.29167pt--\hskip 1.29167pt}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
tetrahedra that are not shared with the octahedra. There are also two crystallographically distinct indium sites: In(1) in octahedral coordination, and In(2) in tetrahedral coordination. Barium atoms occupy a single crystallographic site.
Among the O and In vacancies, those at O(1) and In(2) exhibit the lowest formation energies (see Figure S2 and S3 in the SM

IV
CONCLUSIONS

In this work, we have investigated the defect chemistry of
\text{Ba}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{In}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{5}}}
by means of Density Functional Theory, with particular focus on vacancies, oxygen interstitials, Frenkel pairs, and substitutional Cr doping. Our results suggest that the intrinsic defects landscape is governed by oxygen vacancies and interstitials, whereas Ba and In vacancies present high formation energies and are therefore unlikely to contribute significantly to the defect thermodynamics.
Among the oxygen interstitials, we observe the formation of dumbbell structures that remain neutral across the band gap. This behavior has been previously observed for oxygen interstitials in
ZnO
and
\text{In}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. Other configurations, such as
O_{i}(04)
, are charge carriers and become increasingly favorable under
n
-type conditions. At high oxygen partial pressures,
O_{i}(04)
dominates the defect population, suggesting that this interstitial plays a central role in the compensation mechanisms in BIO, in contrast to many other oxides where cationic vacancies usually provide charge compensation.
Furthermore, we find that chromium preferentially substitutes at the tetrahedral indium site and behaves as a donor. However, the negative formation energies observed under all chemical potential conditions point to the formation of a secondary phase, most likely tetragonal Cr-doped BIO.
On this note, future work should extend this analysis to the high-temperature tetragonal phase of BIO, enabling a direct comparison of Cr doping in both the orthorhombic and tetragonal structures. Additionally, diffusion studies of oxygen interstitials and vacancies would provide deeper insights into migration barriers and diffusivities for better comparison with experimental studies. Such investigations will help to give a more complete picture of the defect chemistry of BIO and its potential for defect and doping engineering in real-world applications.
