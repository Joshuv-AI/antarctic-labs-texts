ABSTRACT
Over the past 80 years, biotechnology has advanced agriculture, health care, and economic development by harnessing biological processes from the organism inward, i.e., from the organ system to the molecular scale. Today’s global challenges, including biodiversity loss, climate change, and pollution, demand a complementary technological expansion inspired by processes operating from the organism outward, i.e., at the levels of populations, communities, ecosystems, and the biosphere. Here, we present the components of this technological expansion through ecosystem technology, or ecotech. We propose a framework for ecotech to integrate elements of ecology, engineering, and earth science and to function as a practical and conceptual convergence accelerator. Ecotech will advance critical environmental solutions by uniting independently evolving technologies, generating diverse fields of inquiry (e.g., ecomimicry, ecosystem materials science, ecosystem sensing and signaling), and inspiring innovation. To harness this innovation to improve nature restoration, carbon storage, water quality, ecosystem energy, and infrastructure resistance to disasters, ecotech is guided by cross-cutting actions to ensure scalability, equity, and accountability. When integrated into strategies across nongovernmental organizations, business, and governments, ecotech offers a pathway to advance climate adaptation, biodiversity recovery, and economic diversification and growth. By uniting ecology, engineering, and earth sciences at scale, ecotech transforms technology into a tool to confront humanity’s most urgent existential needs and secure a livable future.

INTRODUCTION

Human-driven environmental change now manifests in severe and unpredictable events, where heat waves, storms, droughts, wildfires, flash floods, harmful algal blooms, and rapid cryosphere loss are catching societies unprepared (1–3). Both climate and nonclimate human effects are eroding biodiversity, socioeconomic systems, and public health. These declines in biospheric conditions result in food and energy shortages, lack of clean water and air, declining human well-being, climate refugees, and, inevitably, conflict (4–7). Addressing the interwoven challenges of climate change, pollution, and biodiversity loss requires innovative, science-based solutions that operate across scales.

To accelerate the development of solutions to these challenges, we propose a new, interdisciplinary technological science: ecosystem technology, or ecotech. Ecotech is an emerging branch of applied bioscience that produces technology inspired by the processes and derivatives generated by biological systems from the organism outward. Whereas biotechnology leverages molecular and cellular processes, ecosystem technology draws inspiration from the functions and feedbacks generated from populations, communities, ecosystems, and the biosphere (Fig. 1). Ecotech innovation is guided by an overarching goal to produce solutions that optimize the development of climate and economic resilience and reverse biodiversity loss to help both people and nature.

Ecotech extends and advances biotech.

Biotech (blue) emerges as a function of the processes and derivatives of the body inward, from organismal to molecular scales. By contrast, ecotech (green) emerges as a function of increasing levels of biological organization, from organism to biosphere. (Table S1 outlines examples of ecotech across each level of biological organization.) Ecotech is an outward-expanding temporal and conceptual progression from past and present biotech, and each component of ecotech is interconnected with explicit interactions between physical, chemical, and biological environmental layers (green arrows to and from physiochemical environment). Synergy between ecotech and biotech is essential, as many ecotech applications are closely linked to and supported by biotech categories. For example, cells are often first responders to changes in the physical environment and are critical in natural environmental cycling that fuels ecosystem-level water/habitat provisioning. Similarly, cell- and tissue-based biotech is critical for biosphere-level carbon capture, ecosystem-level environmental remediation strategies, community-level genome editing, population-level cloning, and/or organism-level transgenic crops.

Here, we introduce a cohesive framework to unite approaches from engineering, ecology, and earth sciences under ecotech to create this strategically focused branch of applied science. Our framework seeks to empower the field of ecotech to rapidly attract investment, foster innovation, facilitate business opportunities, and expand workforce development to enhance ecosystem production across all levels of human impact. We draw lessons from biotech history, define eight emerging foundational areas of ecotech inquiry, and highlight several cross-cutting actions key to ecotech’s successful evolution. We expect that ethical and equitable applications of ecotech can synergistically develop new technologies; create more efficient infrastructure, renewable energy, and agriculture; expand workforces; grow small businesses; and advance conservation and governmental initiatives.

ECOTECH IS A CONCEPTUALLY BASED INTERDISCIPLINARY ACCELERATOR

Convergence accelerators merge discovery and innovation from diverse fields to deliver real-world impact. Ecosystem technology is such an accelerator: a new field that unifies specialized disciplines through technological advances explicitly inspired by the processes, interactions, and properties shaping population, community, ecosystem, and biosphere dynamics. Although “ecotechnology” has been used since the 1970s to describe various environmental-technological practices, its meaning has remained diffuse. Past uses of ecotechnology equate it with ecological engineering [the design of sustainable ecosystems that integrate human society with its natural environment for the benefit of both; (8)] and treat it as a general buzzword, or in reference to technologies perceived as environmentally beneficial (9). Haddaway et al. (9) refined it into an impact-oriented framework similar to the biotech subfield “environmental biotechnology” (10), with a primary focus on minimizing environmental harm while providing societal value (e.g., “climate tech” or “clean tech”).

Ecotech is distinct from impact-oriented fields, habitat-focused efforts (e.g., forest tech), or sector-specific domains such as bluetech, autotech, and agrotech. Instead, it is conceptually based and rooted in system-level, ecological principles that serve as a generative foundation for innovation. Ecotech merges ecology and bioscience, earth sciences, and engineering (Fig. 2): Ecology and bioscience describe feedback-rich behavior of living systems, earth sciences define the geophysical and geochemical boundaries they inhabit, and engineering translates that coupled insight into scalable materials, devices, and infrastructure. Ecotech does not need to start de novo because many of its principles already exist in practice, grounded in nature-based processes (11). Following the biotech blueprint, ecotech can be woven through diverse sectors and issues, boosting technological performance while minimizing social and environmental impacts.

Ecotech as a convergence accelerator.

Ecosystem technology emerges from the conceptual overlap of ecology and bioscience, engineering, and earth sciences and listed, but non-exhaustive, subfields such as population ecology, environmental chemistry, and civil engineering. As each discipline is currently advancing foundational areas of inquiry (Fig. 4) independently, explicitly uniting these fields through collaborations and partnerships is a fundamental goal for successful ecotech at scale.

BIOTECH HISTORY, AND HOW ECOTECH CAN LEARN FROM ITS SUCCESSES AND MISTAKES

With ancient roots in fermentation, selective breeding, and domestication, biotechnology first referred to the interaction between biology and technology for converting raw biological materials into useful products (12, 13). Biotech flourished with milestones like the smallpox vaccine, the discovery of penicillin, and breakthroughs in DNA research. Biotech innovations, from vaccines and biopharmaceuticals to high-yield crops and bioplastics, have strengthened global health and food security (14, 15). The continually growing economic activity of biotech is profound; the biotech sector reached a global value of US $1.55 trillion in 2023, with 14% annual growth projected (16).

However, biotech benefits have often come with social and environmental costs because of the absence of an ethical and equitable guiding framework, misuse, misapplication, and high cost of some applications. Cultivating large-scale monocultures of genetically uniform crop varieties has reduced agricultural biodiversity and heightened vulnerability to diseases, pests, or droughts (17, 18). Glyphosate-resistant crops (e.g., “Roundup Ready” crops), while reducing labor and boosting yields, encouraged herbicide-resistant weeds and subsequently more toxic chemical use that degrades soil, decimates pollinators, and runs off into aquatic systems (19, 20). Large-scale production of biofuel crops like genetically modified oilseeds seek to reduce reliance on fossil fuels; however, the expansion of biofuel plantations has offset climate benefits through deforestation (i.e., releasing of carbon stores), habitat loss, and displacement of native species in tropical regions (21). In health care, weak biopharmaceutical regulation has contributed to global public health emergencies (22), overuse of antibiotics in animal agriculture is promoting antibiotic resistance in humans (23), and pharmaceutical pollution in rivers remains alarmingly high, especially in China and India (24). Ecotech must heed these lessons from the harmful effects of biotech to avoid similar pitfalls and ensure sustainable and equitable advancements.

Biotech’s recurring flaw has been the upscaling of technologies without fully considering long-term ecological or societal consequences. Many of the biotechnology breakthroughs now criticized, such as pesticides, synthesis of inorganic fertilizers, glyphosate-tolerant cereals, high-yield monocultures, and rapid-turnover biofuel feedstocks, still hold immense potential. Ecotech provides that missing governance layer. Framed within an ecotech architecture, the same genetic toolkits would have been deployed inside biodiverse, feedback-rich agro-ecosystems: cultivar mosaics that dilute pathogen pressure, rotation schedules that blunt herbicide resistance, and life-cycle accounting that internalizes downstream pollution costs. Ecotech can act as the top-down systems guide that channels bottom-up biotech ingenuity toward outcomes that safeguard biodiversity, ecosystem function, and social equity rather than eroding them.

Building on this systems-level corrective, ecotech requires a conceptual and practical backbone. Conceptually, clear principles and standards will help guide ecotech through the complexity of integrating technological advances into living ecosystems, catalyzing transdisciplinary collaboration, and leveraging biotech’s profit-driven strategies. Our proposed Guiding Principles (Fig. 3) emphasize equity and environmental benefit enabled by convergence, measurable outcomes, and scalable, community-led action. To meet society’s demand for integrated solutions, ecotech must adopt novel, transdisciplinary models of evaluation, ethics, and design, rather than rely on historical metrics. Like biotech, silviculture, and aquaculture, ecosystem technology must evolve quickly while embedding ecological and social knowledge from the start.

Guiding Principles of Ecosystem Technology.

Ecotech is guided by three tiers of principles: Foundational Values (blue, base), Strategic Enablers (green, middle), and Implementation Principles (yellow, top). Foundational Values (Do No Harm and Equitable Access and Workforce Training) ensure that ecotech benefits the environment and local communities. Strategic Enablers (Scientific Alignment, Societal Integration, and Adaptive Reference Systems) provide the frameworks for integrating ecological, technological, and societal knowledge. Last, Implementation Principles (Utilize Adaptive Technology, Measurable Outcomes, and Scalable Community-Level Action) focus on the practical application and scaling of ecotech, ensuring that local efforts lay the groundwork for broader, impactful solutions.

Practically, factoring in environmental and societal costs and benefits early in the development process will help ecotech foster public trust and avoid unintended consequences. Learning from biotech’s pitfalls, ecotech will embed ethics, thorough testing, and stepwise upscaling into its development pipeline. Similar to rigorous testing procedures in medicine, ecotech must implement iterative evaluations that balance the urgency of innovation with the need for precaution. In addition, meaningful codevelopment, coproduction, and consultation from diverse stakeholders (e.g., policymakers, local communities, scientists, and industry leaders) should guide the prioritization and design of ecotech to ensure relevance and accessibility. Furthermore, ecotech must adopt life cycle assessments, a systematic tool that evaluates the full implications of a solution from inception to disposal, before making large-scale investments. By incorporating these safeguards and processes, ecotech can learn from biotech’s successes while charting a more sustainable and equitable path forward.

FOUNDATIONAL AREAS OF ECOTECH INQUIRY

Akin to biotech being a collection of fields that intersect biology and technology, ecotech encompasses diverse, emerging areas of inquiry at the nexus of ecology, engineering, and earth sciences. Successful ecotech integrates multiple emerging areas (Fig. 4), and we provide recent examples of innovation in each. These areas of inquiry are not all-encompassing but represent ongoing or beta-tested examples of ecotech that show how its basis and potential stem from unifying disciplines, sectors, and scales. Combining these fields raises substantial scientific, ethical, and governance challenges, even as they provide powerful tools for accelerating planetary resilience. Ecological systems are complex, with interactions and feedbacks that may produce unintended or irreversible outcomes if new techniques or strategies are introduced without robust safeguards. Avoiding ecological displacement of native species and improving public acceptance of interventions that directly manipulate living systems are a fundamental task for ecotech. Overall, these challenges require rigorous risk assessment, long-term monitoring, transparent stakeholder engagement, and precautionary frameworks to ensure that ecological benefits are realized without compromising biodiversity, ecosystem function, or social trust.

Nature-based solutions

Traditional “gray” approaches such as sea walls, fire suppression, and shoreline straightening can mitigate flooding, fire, or land loss but have negative ecological consequences (25–27). In contrast, nature-based solutions harness ecological processes to protect infrastructure while delivering economic, environmental, and social benefits (28). Within ecotech, nature-based solutions show how ecosystems themselves can be engineered into resilient infrastructure.

Several established aquatic and terrestrial nature-based solutions represent prototypical ecotech strategies. Planting coastal vegetation and reconstructing wetlands can outperform built defenses by attenuating waves, mitigating flood risk, and reducing sea-wall breach-depth (29, 30). Constructed reef structures restore complex underwater habitat while supporting fisheries and recreation (31). Planting trees to restore slope forests stabilizes soils and reduces avalanche severity (32), tropical reforestation and afforestation is a low-cost method to lower CO2 concentration (33), and native plants can be used to create pollinator corridors that sustain biodiversity and agricultural productivity (34). Cross-cutting approaches such as predator rewilding, re-establishing top-down control by reintroducing or protecting top predators, can boost ecosystem productivity and restoration success (35–37). Rewilding can not only increase global photosynthesis, but also scale up to reduce atmospheric CO2 levels and mitigate climate change effects (38, 39). Similarly, rewilding of lower trophic levels, such as depleted detritivore and scavenger populations, re-establishes natural patterns of energy flow and nutrient cycling and suppresses wildlife diseases that can spread to humans (40, 41).

Nature-based solutions also integrate with other ecotech areas when retrofitting engineered structures to support re-establishment of native species and interactions that have been lost (42), especially when combined with ecosystem mimicry and ecosystem materials science and engineering (table S1). For example, habitat restoration success, scale, and extreme event resilience can be strongly enhanced by prioritizing positive interactions like forest mycorrhizal fungal mutualisms (43) or leveraging facultative and obligate group benefits between coastal plants and bivalves (44, 45).

Nature-based solutions do face field-specific risks, as poorly designed or oversimplified projects can devolve into greenwashing (42). Embedding rigorous monitoring and adaptive design into ecotech keeps nature-based solutions accountable to both ecosystems and people.

Ecosystem cultivation

Ecosystem cultivation is the science and practice of building ecosystems through a coordinated, interdisciplinary and integrated systems approach (46). Ecocultivation combines cutting-edge research on the spatial organization of ecosystems with community-driven goals and technological innovation to create and manage habitats that benefit both humans and nature. Drawing inspiration from agricultural, silvicultural, horticultural, and aquacultural sciences, this field informs scalable ecosystem construction while seeking to minimize the negative environmental and social impacts that have accompanied other cultivation practices.

Ecocultivation is ecotech’s intensive ecosystem-building arm and requires ecotech innovation to expand restoration into a global strategy that addresses the challenges of the Anthropocene. Successful recent examples of ecosystem cultivation using ecotech show this synergy. The sub-Saharan “Great Green Wall” combats desertification through large-scale engineered mimicry of landscape processes. Local communities dig semicircular pits (water bunds) that simulate the natural spatial organization to reconstruct microtopography across the sloping desert. Turning hydrological processes into infrastructure cools the local environment; captured rainwater that would otherwise wash over dry soil enhances nearby water retention and promotes native plant growth (47). In Europe, the Marker Wadden project constructed seven islands and a 1000-ha archipelago from dredged sediment. With careful siting and planning for gradual land-water transitions and wetland habitats, the project transforms sediment management into a tool for large-scale ecosystem creation that is stimulating ecotourism and enhancing biodiversity, water clarity, and freshwater provisioning (48). In terrestrial wetlands, paludiculture (i.e., rewetting drained peatlands for wet cultivation) is a scalable agricultural land use that simultaneously produces specialized wetland crops for harvest, reduces greenhouse gas emissions, stops land subsidence, and supports water-purifying processes (49). Coupled climate mitigation and ecosystem recovery also supports local economies by producing raw materials and creating new, sustainable business models (49). Silvofishery systems in Southeast Asia simultaneously rehabilitate degraded mangrove forests alongside shrimp and fish aquaculture. By planting native mangroves in and around fish or shrimp ponds, these actions blend natural filtration and shoreline protection while boosting biodiversity and maintaining aquaculture yields (50).

To rebuild climate-ready ecosystems at scale across different levels of human impacts, ecosystem cultivation requires ecotech innovation for the mass production of living materials. Thus, ecotech tools such as automated seagrass propagation systems, precision forestry techniques for tree sapling production, and mechanized native grass seed production for prairie restoration allow for efficient scaling to match the magnitude of environmental degradation. Overall, ecotech can ensure ecocultivation projects have access to (i) the biological and hybrid materials necessary to rapidly and effectively restore ecosystem functions, enhance biodiversity, and build resilience to future environmental challenges (see the “Ecosystems mimicry” and “Ecosystem materials science and engineering” sections), and (ii) monitoring technology to assess effects of cultivated ecosystems and inform adaptive strategies (e.g., using drones to generate habitat digital twins to assess geological changes due to sediment nourishment). These actions will help avoid an overreliance on donor stock and the risk of monocultures, while maximizing community engagement and co- and adaptive management.

Ecosystem genetics and engineering

Ecosystem genetics extends beyond the study of genetic variation within populations; genetic traits can influence species interactions, keystone genes exist that have disproportionally large ecological effects, and variability in gene expression can shape entire communities and ecosystems (51–54). Within ecotech, this field harnesses genetic variation and engineered traits to discover community and ecosystem phenotypes that enhance ecological interactions, restore biodiversity, and build ecosystem resilience at scales larger than a single species. Scaling genetic and bioengineering tools from organisms to ecosystems represents a promising step in ecotech innovation.

Ecotech-inspired examples of how ecosystem genetics can foster genetically based synergies may include engineering traits of foundation species or by restoring multiple modified populations in tandem. In forests, heritable traits such as tree architecture influence the nesting behavior of birds, while genotypic variation in phytochemical defenses structures entire arthropod communities (54). Thus, when replanting a Populus forest, targeted modification of keystone traits could expand habitat for wildlife and promote diverse, resilient food webs. In drought-threatened coastal wetlands, a genetically informed restoration project could focus on coplanting saltmarsh cordgrass genetically modified to produce more drought-shock proteins with mutualistic ribbed mussels modified to produce more acetylcholinesterase and heat-shock proteins (55, 56). To enhance wave attenuation and cross-ecosystem energy transfer benefits adjacent to marsh grasses, managers could then simultaneously outplant wild, diploid oysters to enhance larval supply with modified triploid oysters for fast-growth and no risk of sexually transmitted diseases (57, 58). Outside of restoration settings, genetic engineering could be used to amplify phenotypic plasticity to improve speed of community and ecosystem responses to changing conditions (59).

Ecosystem genetics also includes the emerging monitoring field focused on environmental DNA (eDNA), where air or water samples can compare diversity and abundance of genetic markers shed from different local organisms. eDNA monitoring is already creating opportunities for genetic ecotech applications that enhance biodiversity conservation, carbon markets, and pollution, disease, and endangered species monitoring (60–62). eDNA provides a noninvasive, system-wide genetic fingerprint that enables rapid tracking of species presence and genetic traits relevant to ecosystem function, service, and design (see the “Ecosystem sensing and signaling” section).

To address challenges that exceed natural evolutionary rates, carefully implemented ecosystem genetics is urgently needed. Risks surrounding gene flow, unforeseen ecological interactions, and impacts on endemic species may demand more safeguards than conventional restoration or conservation practices. Ecotech treats genetic engineering not as a default but as a targeted tool, applied only where risks are carefully evaluated, containment strategies are in place, and societal consent is earned.

Ecosystem mimicry

Similar to how biomimicry emulates specific traits of organisms from molecular or microscales, ecomimicry focuses on design principles inspired by populations, communities, and ecosystems (63). By replicating structural patterns and process dynamics, ecomimicry channels ecological logic into ecotech innovation.

Ecomimicry is already being applied in both natural and hybrid systems to enhance ecosystem functions and reduce demand for restoration donor material. Temporary structures that mimic key population-level traits of habitat-forming species can kickstart density-dependent positive interactions (11). For example, salt marsh shoots have been successfully mimicked to mitigate wave stress, allowing protected small transplants inside the temporary simulated patch to establish and gradually take over self-facilitating functions (64). Similarly, seagrass root systems have been replicated to improve sediment stability; attachment substrate and auditory or settlement cues have been mimicked to accelerate oyster, mussel, and sponge reef establishment; and floating peat mats have facilitated pool revegetation in raised bogs (11, 65–67). Ecomimicry can also focus on creative ways to recreate species interactions. Predator mimics (e.g., sounds, shapes, and movement) long used to protect crop plants can be further developed in other ecosystems and integrated with other technology (e.g., drones, sensors, and ecosystem energy) to limit grazing, increase plant productivity, and amplify CO2 fixation and storage. Last, ecosystem mimicry can optimize other complex ecotech systems by developing integrated frameworks inspired by species interactions and community patterns. Applying population- and community-inspired designs in construction and sustainable development, termite mound ventilation has inspired passive cooling designs that regulate airflow in large buildings with minimal energy inputs (68). Ecomimicry can even shape economies; industrial symbiosis networks mimic symbioses by promoting resource sharing among industries to reduce waste and emissions while fostering economic growth (69).

Ecomimicry often overlaps with other ecotech areas of inquiry such as ecosystem materials science (eco-concretes and bioenhancing substrates), ecocultivation (designing planting mosaics), and nature-based solutions (inventing green or green-gray materials). These synergies allow built systems to perform like ecosystems rather than isolated technologies and emphasize how ecomimicry provides a design logic that extends across sectors.

Ecosystem materials science and engineering

This foundational field focuses on developing sustainable, multifunctional materials inspired by the structure and function of higher levels of ecological organization. Drawing from ecology, engineering, and product design, this emerging field applies principles like circularity and biodiversity-multifunctionality relationships to create materials suited for a circular economy (70, 71). Ecosystem materials science and engineering develops materials that are infused with ecological processes, recreate foundational habitat structure, and/or mimic ecosystem functions.

Examples of ecosystem materials science and ecomimicry together show how transdisciplinary design frameworks can be activated to produce hybrid, bioinspired materials (11). Vertical botanical infrastructure, such as “supertrees” in Singapore’s Gardens by the Bay, can simultaneously reduce air temperature by 5°C, enhance local biodiversity, and greatly elevate aesthetic value (72). Three-dimensionally printed bionic corals replicate the structure of natural corals to host dense populations of microalgal symbionts and improve light efficiency (73). Composites inspired by tree bark’s layered structure offer biodegradable packaging and insulation (74). Other solutions include biodegradable polymers that mimic population-level facilitation traits (64, 75) and bio- and eco-inspired concretes and artificial seawalls that replicate reef chemistry and structure to enhance biodiversity and ecosystem recovery in coastal systems (76, 77).

To complement the prevailing focus on engineering strong/hard materials as structural foundations, the field of soft matter provides new opportunities for ecotech. For example, an ecotech framework can unify advances across disciplines to solve long-standing challenges in engineering extremely soft, polymer-laden pseudofeces of the ribbed mussel Geukensia demissa that drives sediment accretion and sea level rise-resilience (78). Emerging understanding of collective eukaryotic and prokaryotic cell behavior in granular materials (79), shear thickening of granular suspensions (80), and biopolymer-granular material composites (81) can together help replicate polymer-laden pseudofeces and reverse salt marsh erosion. Other innovations from this field could include soft network composite materials for tissue engineering or biomonitoring in wildlife rehabilitation systems (82). Thus, ecotech can help guide cutting-edge ecosystem materials science, where soft matter and ecology converge to develop soft-material solutions.

Eco-geoengineering

To limit global warming, regional, national, and international governments and organizations must implement effective CO2 removal strategies that rely on ecological methods and processes (83). Eco-geoengineering centers around biogeochemical engineering actions that leverage ecosystems and ecosystem processes to enhance large-scale atmospheric CO2 removal.

On land, reforestation and afforestation increase the number of plants that absorb CO2 through photosynthesis, while soil management practices (e.g., rewetting carbon-dense peatlands, no-till farming, biochar application, and use of cover crops) amplify both soil carbon sequestration and storage (84). While certain climate engineering methods do not currently fall under ecotech, they could be integrated with ecosystem restoration efforts to enhance effectiveness. For example, methods such as spraying chemicals in the atmosphere to stimulate cloud formation is not ecotech in its current form but could become ecotech when smartly combined with the restoration of carbon-sequestering or plant-cooling ecosystems in arid bioregions. In the ocean, carbon storage could be increased by promoting biomass growth and sinking [e.g., through iron fertilization; (85)], along with protecting and cultivating carbon sink ecosystems such as mangroves, seagrasses, and kelp forests (86). Similarly, mineral carbonization, enhanced rock and/or silicate weathering that stimulates the natural processes of rocks slowly dissolving calcium and magnesium from rainwater to form carbonate minerals, could be integrated into dredging operations, land reclamation, and beach nourishment or dune restorations (87, 88).

Ethical concerns around ecological risk, social justice, and governance center around the possibility that large-scale interventions can trigger unintended consequences if deployed without precaution (e.g., food-web disruption in iron-fertilized oceans or biodiversity loss from ill-sited afforestation). Ecotech frames eco-geoengineering as a complement to emission reductions, not a substitute, and requires extra protocols for rigorous risk assessment, transparent international oversight, and protection of vulnerable communities. Safeguards such as phased pilot testing, ecological baselines, genetic and biogeochemical containment strategies, adaptive monitoring, and reversibility planning must be embedded to reduce adverse surprises and build public trust. As with other ecotech areas of inquiry, precautionary ethics must be integrated from design to deployment, ensuring that eco-geoengineering advances climate mitigation without compromising ecosystem integrity or societal equity.

Ecosystem energy

Ecosystem energy is the development and application of sustainable energy forms that leverage power that exists in present-day ecosystems as the main source, instead of energy stored in fossil fuels (89). With ecotech, ecosystems are framed not as resources to be consumed, but as cogenerators of sustainable energy. Current ecosystem energy sources include harnessing energy from wind, water, the sun, geothermal heat, and burning biomass (90). Plant-microbial fuel cell technology generates electricity in wetlands, with research showing plant species–specific differences in power generation (91). Ecotech can also measure the output and performance of ecosystem energy, as well as the environmental impacts and positive effects on the energy grid (77).

Ecosystem materials science can enhance the development of ecosystem energy structures that support target species in projects, further contributing to human health and economic development and drastically decreasing negative environmental impacts. For example, an ecotech-designed turbine farm can be designed to facilitate, rather than deter, marine organisms, mirroring ecosystem structure, functions, and services of natural reefs (77, 92). Developing turbine-support-base materials suitable for settlement of target sessile foundation species can enhance turbine food webs, while amplifying soundscapes from healthy habitats can boost recruitment during restoration (66). On land, solar farms can be situated on carbon-capturing wetlands with improved solar panel designs so built structures shade plants and provide refuge from drought (93).

Ecotherapy

Ecotech provides a community interaction framework to guide emerging micro- and macrobiome-based interventions aimed at restoring and enhancing physiological resilience of both humans and wildlife (94). While the Earth’s microbiome assemblage lies at the intersection of the host and the environment, microbiome interventions have primarily focused on the individual host (95). Microbial community-targeted products (e.g., common prebiotics and probiotics) can restore the microbiotic community structure that supports habitat forming populations, thereby supporting biodiversity and ecosystem function at a macroscale (96, 97). Ecotherapy mechanisms have been used for immune regulation in honey bee populations, to treat fungal disease in amphibians and white nose syndrome in bats, bioremediate oil spill effects on coral reefs, and increase coral resistance to bleaching (98–102). Given the ethical considerations, risks, costs, benefits, and opportunities within environmental microbiome management, a convergence framework like ecotech is essential for synthesizing the evidence needed to advance the concept of ecotherapy and its long-term impacts.

On ecosystem scales, the value of diverse, long, and regular nature experiences for human physical and mental well-being is increasingly recognized as a key ecosystem service and aligns with ecotech’s goals to harness natural systems for human benefit (103). For example, living in or near nature can improve cognitive function by reducing stress, anxiety, and depression (104). As urbanization causes declines in human contact with nature, expanding ecosystem service assessments to include mental health outcomes provides a framework for recognizing the therapeutic value of ecosystems. If outcomes are positive at scale and with a more diverse ecosystem, then ecosystem cultivation can be used to better design urban landscapes to increase human mental health. This perspective supports ecotherapy as a key component of ecotech, emphasizing the psychological and social resilience ecosystems provide alongside their physical and ecological functions.

CROSS-CUTTING ACTIONS ACROSS FOUNDATIONAL FIELDS AND AREAS OF INQUIRY

While the foundational areas define what ecotech can achieve, cross-cutting actions establish how it evolves responsibly (Fig. 4). These standards and workflows of ecotech should set ethical guardrails, shape design and evaluation standards, enable continuous monitoring and adaptive innovation and management, and translate outcomes into societal value. These actions progress from ensuring equity and transparency, to filtering ideas through rigorous design criteria, to tracking ecological performance in real time, and, last, to quantifying ecosystem services that connect innovation with policy, business, and community goals. Together, these four cross-cutting actions keep ecotech accountable to ecosystems and societies. They ensure that innovation scales with integrity, embedding transparency and adaptability so that ecotech delivers real and equitable benefits across generations.

Environmental ethics and equity

Technology innovation has historically overlooked ethics, inclusivity, and equity. When planning and design processes lack diverse perspectives or fail to account for the needs of communities, unchecked advancement contributes to significant environmental and societal impacts, wealth concentration in urban centers, and limited resilience in vulnerable rural, coastal, and developing regions. To address these gaps, ethical and equitable ecotech development requires prioritizing decisions that benefit both human communities and ecosystems (105). Specifically, equitable processes should consider distributional equity, who gets what and how much (e.g., ensuring offshore wind projects share revenue and jobs locally); recognitional equity, whose values determine what matters and why (e.g., integrating Indigenous knowledge into biodiversity monitoring); procedural equity, who decides and how (e.g., through participatory governance in carbon removal initiatives); and restorative equity, how is harm repaired and prevented (e.g., via projects that repair historic harms) (106–109).

Explicit ethical criteria to guide the design and deployment of emerging ecotech should ensure that innovation benefits both ecosystems and human communities while avoiding historical patterns of exclusion. For example, Indigenous data sovereignty can be upheld through CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics) that require formal agreements for Indigenous community control over environmental data and for coauthorship on resulting research (110). To address ecological and social uncertainties for carbon removal approaches (e.g., ocean alkalinity enhancement), projects should include structured consent processes, local advisory boards, and transparent reporting (111, 112). Similarly, offshore renewable infrastructure can operationalize equity by guaranteeing local hiring quotas, revenue-sharing agreements with coastal municipalities, and tribal partnerships for ecological monitoring (113). Biodiversity and eDNA monitoring initiatives can also involve local leadership via community-driven sampling protocols, equitable data-sharing agreements, and integration of traditional ecological knowledge into study design and interpretation (114). Beyond governance, equitable innovation also demands direct involvement of local business leaders and social scientists, particularly within underserved communities. Practical models exist, such as community-led mangrove restoration projects that pair traditional fishing cooperatives with small businesses producing biobased erosion control materials. Similarly, codesigning precision agriculture tools with rural stakeholders creates products that better reflect smallholder realities (115). By embedding these principles and practices, developers can foster trust, address risk perceptions, and ensure that technological progress aligns with social and ecological resilience.

Design and performance criteria and evaluation

To support the successful deployment and scaling of ecotechnologies, defining clear, measurable design and performance criteria will guide evaluation throughout the entire life cycle, from conceptualization and permitting to implementation, monitoring, and eventual adaptation or decommissioning. First, projects must quantify the current state of the environment in which the solution is intended to operate with a quantitative assessment of baseline environmental conditions in the target ecosystem. Next, key features of the system, product, or process, such as the minimum viable product (MVP) or process, must be identified. Last, using the convergence approach crucial for ecotech design and development, a transdisciplinary team of scientists and end-users should assess the quality, efficiency, and efficacy of both the cocreation process and the final product. We recommend eight performance indicators: (i) Applicability: Does the solution visibly and measurably address the identified problem? (ii) Unintended side effects: What are any unintended effects on people or ecosystems? (iii) Urgency: Does the problem require immediate action, and is a rapid solution necessary? (iv) Spin-up time: How quickly does the product or process achieve full functionality? (v) Environmental stress tolerance: Can the product or process remain functional and effective despite environmental stressors? (vi) Scalability: What is the potential to implement the solution on larger scales or in different contexts (e.g., soil restoration can be adapted for different soil types and climate background)? (vii) Longevity and durability: How long will the solution remain functional and effective without requiring significant maintenance? (viii) Resource efficiency: How efficiently the solution uses natural resources such as water, energy, or raw materials.

Examples of currently implemented quantitative assessments are limited, as standards for design and performance criteria are being developed now or, in many cases, not used at all. While this is an addressable research and practice gap, we outline an example from recently established assessments for living shoreline performance evaluation (116–118). First, a living shoreline designed to enhance coastal habitat and reduce erosion must quantify the current state of shoreline dynamics and ecological function. Measuring erosion rates (e.g., centimeters per year to define erosion stress), tidal range (e.g., range in meters to estimate daily change in conditions), wind wave height (e.g., thresholds that define low-energy or high-energy environments), and soil bearing capacity (e.g., minimum pounds per square foot for substrate stability) are all recommended. Next, to assess the habitat enhancement goal, ecological baselines (e.g., vegetation cover, biodiversity indices, or sunlight exposure) must be established to quantify changes over time. Then, the MVP or intervention must be identified in functional ecological terms. This example could consist of a bioengineered marsh sill constructed from natural materials like coir logs, shell-filled gabions, or planted dunes. Effectiveness can then be evaluated based on how the living shoreline stabilizes the coastal edge, attenuates wave energy, and supports native marsh vegetation. Key performance targets could include reduction in shoreline retreat, successful establishment of vegetation across a defined slope, or sediment accretion rates that match or exceed local sea level rise. Last, performance criteria should be selected to reflect both the technical function of the intervention and the ecological requirements of the site. For example, if a living shoreline is intended to protect upland infrastructure, the design might be optimized to reduce overtopping during storm events with return interval of 25 to 50 years, while also maintaining conditions suitable for intertidal habitat development. Site characteristics (e.g., width, offshore depth, and nearshore slope) can each influence the performance envelope and should be explicitly factored into both design and evaluation. By embedding these types of quantitative parameters into the assessment of ecotechnologies, researchers and practitioners can align ecological restoration goals with engineering feasibility. This enables more consistent comparisons across projects, facilitates adaptive management, and enhances the credibility of living shorelines as scalable, evidence-based coastal protection solutions.

Ecosystem sensing and signaling

Ecotech leverages both active and passive monitoring systems to detect, interpret, and respond to changes in ecosystem state. Recent advances in materials and miniaturized electronics now enable low-power, autonomous environmental sensors to be deployed across vast landscapes and sensitive habitats, offering scalable and dynamic monitoring capabilities (119). Active tools like eDNA and remote sensing can track biodiversity shifts, contamination events, and habitat health in real time. For example, eDNA is being used to reveal pollution-induced changes in aquatic biodiversity and can quantify airborne fungal and pollen identity and diversity with greater accuracy and resolution than traditional methods (60). This form of barcoding can also support conservation efforts, such as accurately and efficiently tracing pollen sources critical for bee populations—a step toward reversing pollinator population declines with implications for global food systems (62).

For environments that are dangerous or difficult to access (e.g., deep seas, glacial peaks, geothermal lakes, or fragile wetlands), autonomous aerial or aquatic vehicles are increasingly integrated with distributed environmental sensors. Recent developments include biodegradable origami microfliers, helicopter seed–inspired gliders, and soft-bodied aquatic robots capable of passive or self-actuated deployment (120). These platforms can carry electrochemical, spectroscopic, or colorimetric sensors, enabling in situ detection of a wide range of targets including nitrates, heavy metals, pH, salinity, and reactive oxygen species (121, 122). Critically, many of these sensing systems are now made with transient and eco-resorbable materials such as polylactic-co-glycolic acid, magnesium, silicon nanoribbons, and cellulose-based substrates, allowing them to degrade safely after their monitoring life cycle (121, 123).

Passive monitoring integrates sensor networks, remote sensing, and artificial intelligence (AI) to support early warning systems, postdisturbance assessment, and site-specific intervention. For example, continuous flood management in dynamic coastal zones increasingly depends on biodegradable water quality sensors that monitor salinity and turbidity in real time (122) while physics-informed neural networks are being developed to create resolution-invariant and geometry-adaptive flood modeling and forecasting frameworks (124). Early detection of fire (smoke and heat sensors widely deployed in ecosystems), storm, or heatwave risks and long-term assessments of recovery can rely on optimized placement of sensing nodes across terrain. Remote sensing approaches such as hyperspectral imaging, light detection and ranging, and solar-induced fluorescence deployed via drones or satellites complement ground-based sensors by providing high-resolution data on methane hot spots, vegetation diversity and health, carbon uptake or storage, and landscape structure (125–128).

The expanding complexity and volume of environmental data from these platforms is increasingly managed with deep learning and AI-based ecological modeling. These systems combine onsite sensor data with satellite observations and historical environmental parameters to support prediction, anomaly detection, and ecosystem modeling (123, 129, 130). As AI models evolve, they are expected to interface with increasingly diverse sensor arrays to enable faster detection of emergent patterns, improved ecological forecasting, and more responsive environmental management. In this way, AI operates as the connective tissue of the cross cutting actions of ecotech sensing and signaling, amplifying its capacity to perceive and respond.

Ecotech also draws on the signaling systems used by organisms, populations, and communities to regulate interactions and maintain ecological balance. Auditory, chemical, and visual signaling can be harnessed to shape behaviors such as recruitment, migration, and stress response (131). For example, marine organisms use “infochemicals” like pheromones, allelochemicals, and microbial cues to find mates and food, avoid predators, and identify settlement zones (132). These infochemicals could be harnessed and amplified through ecotech strategies such as nature-based solutions, ecosystem mimicry, and biointegrative design to improve recruitment, reinforce population stability, and enhance ecosystem performance.

CULTIVATING THE POWER OF ECOTECH TO SPUR ECONOMIC GROWTH

While a century of R&D and commercialization has demonstrated the massive economic [i.e., projected US $3.54 trillion market size by 2033; (16)] and societal value of biotech for human existence, ecotech is embryonic and lacks a proven pathway for economic prosperity and human well-being. This crucial distinction will inform the commercial development of ecotechnologies into the future because ecotech currently encompasses a range of solutions at varying stages of development. Regardless of maturity, certain mechanisms can foster the growth of these emerging technologies and the communities in which they operate. To cultivate the power of ecotech to spur economic growth, we must advance the following mechanisms: maintaining strong intellectual property (IP) protection, enacting supportive policies at both local and federal levels, and creating clear pathways from pilot projects to sustained market demand. This foundational infrastructure also aids in creating the right investment environment for private capital regardless of which foundational field a solution emerges from or where the solution is intended to act. As these ecosystem technologies mature, they not only address environmental challenges but also contribute to economic development by generating industries, jobs, and long-term societal benefits. Therefore, building a framework for ecotechnology growth can catalyze both environmental and economic transformations that deliver wide-ranging societal gains.

Maintaining strong IP protection

IP protection is critical to determining the investability of the competitive landscape for emerging technologies around the globe (133). Although misusing IP protections can sometimes lead to negative societal impacts (e.g., epi-pen price inflation), these protections foster early-stage innovation, particularly from start-ups (134). For instance, patents on newly approved drugs or other innovations generally provide 20 years of protection from competition, allowing time for cost recovery from extensive and expensive R&D and approval processes. With a mean estimate of US $1.1 billion cost of bringing a drug to market (135), a lack of strong IP protections to guarantee investors an opportunity for returns would create major challenges in financing such high-risk, long-term projects. Similarly, ecotech needs these protections because rebuilding nature requires significant investment to quantify benefits or risks to human and ecosystem health and, similar to biotech, has extended time horizons and high costs that may deter capital providers.

Unlike biotech or pharmaceuticals, where IP aligns with products that deliver clear and immediate value to individuals, ecotech innovations often generate collective benefits that accrue diffusely across communities and over long timescales, such as cleaner water, healthier soils, or reduced climate risk. This mismatch makes conventional IP protections less effective at incentivizing private investment, since the pathway from invention to monetizable return is indirect. Addressing this challenge will require hybrid models that pair IP with public funding, market incentives for ecosystem services, or cooperative ownership structures that ensure both community access and investor confidence.

Enacting supportive policies and regulatory pathways to bolster ecotech development

Policy plays a vital role in shaping the market landscape with both incentives and regulation needed to catalyze the adoption of technologies (136). For example, Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) schemes in industries such as plastics and textiles require producers to bear the costs of their product’s life cycle, driving innovation in sustainable R&D by assigning penalties to negative outcomes (e.g., nonrecyclable packaging that contributes to pollution) (137). For ecotech, policies should address unintended environmental consequences that these solutions aim to mitigate, like biodiversity loss. Policies like EPR encourage entrenched systems to change, potentially pushing funding and opportunities for scaling toward ecotech innovations.

Regulatory streamlining is also necessary for ecotech to succeed because current restrictive environmental and restoration permitting processes will slow down product validation and limit investment interest in unproven technologies (138). By working with local, state, and federal governments to simplify these processes, ecotech projects can more readily achieve the scale needed to impactfully demonstrate success. Policy interventions can also lower financial barriers by providing nondilutive funding for early-stage technologies and communities adopting them. For example, the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 allocated over US $369 billion to climate initiatives and offered grants and tax credits. The Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund (GGRF) dedicates US $27 billion to mobilize financing for greenhouse gas and pollution reduction projects across the US, focusing on disadvantaged communities. While enacting these policies increased investment in the clean energy sector by 71% over 2 years prior to the legislation (139), success can rise and fall based on instability in the political climate (i.e., GGRF funding has been pulled back). Similar policy efforts could support additional objectives, like habitat restoration, to accelerate ecotech adoption.

Establish a clean pathway from pilot to sustained demand

For early-stage ecotech solutions to attract investment and funding, showing clear demand is essential. Nondilutive capital options like grants can support early development, but funding scarcity often becomes a challenge as companies attempt to commercialize first-of-a-kind technologies. This hurdle, known as the “valley of death,” is a common failure point. Mechanisms like offtakes, a contractual approach to guarantee future demand, can bridge this gap. In renewable, ecosystem energy, for instance, long-term offtake agreements help de-risk high capital projects by securing demand once the project is operational. Offtakes can range from nonbinding agreements to binding, bankable contracts that align with the technology readiness level. For ecotech, similar offtake agreements could secure demand for ecosystem restoration services, carbon offsets, or environmental monitoring solutions, ensuring a smoother transition from pilot to market adoption. Recent funding innovations such as focused research organizations (FROs) offer potential avenues for scaling ecotech solutions. FROs create 5 to 10 years of dedicated funding to develop foundational technologies that fall outside the scope of venture capital or traditional academic funding cycles (140). While FROs have primarily targeted biotechnology, ecotech presents a logical next frontier.

Creating the right conditions for private investment

As ecotech has the potential to affect human and ecosystem health profoundly, much like biotechnology, the economic benefits of ecotech development must be considered. Ecotechnologies are costly and time-intensive to bring to market, and adopting biotechnology’s blueprint, supportive policies, clear development pathways, and investor-friendly environments, is essential. The speed of ecotech development, implementation, and viability depends on creating attractive investment conditions. Therefore, interventions like strong IP protection, supportive policies, and market demand demonstration are all essential for fostering a robust economic ecotech ecosystem.

ECOTECH’S ROLE IN CLIMATE AND SOCIOECONOMIC RESILIENCE FROM LAND TO SEA

Extending ecotech into real-world practice demands coordinated action across science, policy and industry, with each sector performing a distinct and mutually reinforcing role. Science drives discovery, develops early stage technologies, generates ecological baselines and evaluates system-wide impacts. Policy creates the enabling environment by establishing regulatory pathways, setting environmental performance standards, enabling field trials, ensuring data stewardship, and integrating community priorities through participatory governance. Effective policy frameworks (e.g., permitting for nature-based carbon removal, standards for sensor interoperability, or coastal resilience mandates) can de-risk innovation and align technological deployment with environmental protection. Industry converts scientific advances into deployable ecotech solutions by providing engineering capacity, manufacturing pathways, operational scale, and long-term implementation.

Recent cross-sector collaborations between research institutions, public agencies, and private companies demonstrate how complementary strengths can accelerate ecotech innovation through bridging of science, policy, and industry. To rapidly upscale unmanned surface vehicle observing systems, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration–Saildrone partnership uses a cooperative R&D agreement framework that enables private-sector agility to pair with federal scientific rigor, operational capacity, and data standards (141, 142). To support Singapore’s commitments to the United Nations Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction Agreement, a partnership between OceanX and the National University of Singapore (NUS) launched the country’s first deep-sea scientific expedition (143). By combining OceanX’s deep-sea infrastructure and media capacity with NUS’s regional scientific expertise, research infrastructure, and specimen stewardship, this partnership is generating publicly shared biodiversity baselines from largely unexplored seafloor regions. Testbed consortia such as the Alliance for Coastal Technologies further illustrate the value of coordinated evaluation and standardization by bringing together universities, managers, companies, and agencies to verify performance and facilitate community adoption of new coastal sensors and platforms (144). Beyond ocean-based partnerships, the CarbonSAFE project between the University of Wyoming, Frontier Carbon Solutions LLC, and the US Department of Energy illustrates how large-scale, multisector collaborations can create durable pathways for climate-critical technology deployment (145). By uniting academia, federal agencies, and private industry around the development of commercial-scale CO2 storage infrastructure, the partnership demonstrates how aligned governance, shared risk, and long-term investment frameworks can accelerate translation from research to real-world implementation.

However, despite these successful models, most partnerships remain project-specific efforts limited by fragmented governance, lack of persistent infrastructure, absence of interoperable frameworks, and misalignment between short-term project incentives and long-term climate goals. Without durable structures, such as standing governance bodies, shared data commons, sustained funding mechanisms, and unified ocean-climate technology networks, these collaborations cannot scale to meet systemic needs in carbon monitoring, ecosystem restoration, renewable ocean energy, or continuous ocean health surveillance. To move forward, ecotech must seek to create an enduring, coordinated platform that integrates governance, funding, data standards, and institutional stewardship to support multiple technologies and sustained deployment. Without this, even the most successful collaborations risk dissipating once their limited project scope ends.

Because the global climate crisis is increasingly straining both human and natural systems, we require urgent action to build cross-ecosystem resilience against escalating threats. Overall, the role of ecotech is to create a multidisciplinary integration of technologies and ecological principles designed to bridge terrestrial and marine ecosystems across levels of human impact (Fig. 5). By merging ecology, engineering, and earth sciences, ecotech seeks to deliver solutions that simultaneously foster economic growth, community wellbeing, and environmental sustainability. This systems-level approach is particularly transformative in rural areas, where ecotech can leverage abundant natural resources and expansive landscapes to test applications at the scale ecosystems actually operate. In urban areas, ecotech delivers deployment-ready solutions that reduce climate risks and improve quality of life. Ecotech also plays an essential role in tackling the defining challenges of climate change, from CO2 removal and biodiversity recovery to water purification and risk management for floods, droughts, heatwaves, and wildfires. As a top-down guide for bottom-up biotech, ecotech ensures that advances in genetic or molecular technologies enhance rather than erode ecosystem integrity. Above all, ecotech embeds ethics, equity, and community participation into its foundation, treating ecosystems as engines of innovation rather than passive backdrops for extraction.

Ecotech in action, from land to sea.

Various ecotechnologies, including remote sensing and multispectral signals, enable comprehensive ecosystem monitoring and data collection from space to ground. On land, sustainable practices such as living shorelines, paludiculture, and hybrid infrastructure combine natural and engineered elements to protect coastal areas while fostering biodiversity. Offshore, multispecies aquaculture and bioengineered reefs promote sustainable food production and habitat restoration. Wind energy exemplifies renewable ecosystem energy development, while innovations such as eDNA analysis, chemical signals, and acoustic signals illustrate the role of ecological sensing in understanding and managing ecosystem interactions. Together, these technologies demonstrate how ecotech can harmonize human development and ecosystem resilience across landscapes and seascapes.

By positioning ecological systems as active infrastructure for survival, ecotech is no longer optional—it is imperative for safeguarding both human society and the planet. The task ahead is to move ecotech from concept to practice at the speed demanded by accelerating environmental crises. Doing so will require unprecedented collaboration across science, policy, and industry, alongside bold investment to test and scale solutions across diverse ecosystems. By acting decisively now, societies can ensure that ecotech fulfills its promise as a convergence accelerator, delivering technologies that protect biodiversity, build resilience, and secure a livable future.
