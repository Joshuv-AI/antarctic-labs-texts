Synergistic Fe and polyimide dual-modification of g-C3N4 for enhanced photocatalytic degradation of tetracycline
Authors: Jing Chen, Yadong Li, Minghua Yang, Zhiang Fu, Hongjiao Zhag, Anqi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pristine g-C3N4 typically suffers from rapid recombination of photogenerated charge carriers and a narrow visible light absorption range, severely restricting its practical photocatalytic applications. In this work, a one-step pyrolysis strategy that co-incorporates Fe and polyimide (PI) into g-C3N4 as a dual-modification approach is proposed. The resulting Fe/PI co-doped g-C3N4 composites (FPICN) displayed a significantly enhanced specific surface area, extended visible light absorption, and improved charge separation. These enhancements are attributed to the electron-deficient PMDA units and the coordination of Fe within the g-C3N4 framework. Structural and spectroscopic analyses confirmed the successful incorporation of Fe and PI, the partial exfoliation of g-C3N4 layers, and favorable modulation of electronic structure. FPICN demonstrated outstanding photocatalytic performance, degrading 86.98% of tetracycline within 80 min under simulated solar light irradiation. The apparent rate constant was approximately 8.7 times higher than that of pristine g-C3N4. Moreover, FPICN retained over 80% of its initial activity after four cycles, demonstrating excellent stability. Radical trapping experiments and band structure analyses revealed that photogenerated holes (h+) and superoxide radicals (˙O2− were the primary reactive species in the degradation pathway. This work provides a rational design strategy for high-performance photocatalysts based on synergistic transition metal doping and conjugated polymer modification, and offers insights into the efficient visible light-driven degradation of pollutants.

Introduction

Persistent contamination of water resources by organic pollutants seriously threatens ecological security and the stability of aquatic ecosystems. Pharmaceuticals, pesticides, and industrial chemicals persist in the environment, readily bioaccumulate, and cause long-term adverse effects.1,2 Therefore, the remediation of these recalcitrant contaminants requires efficient, sustainable, and cost-effective water treatment technologies. Among various approaches, solar-driven photocatalysis has emerged as a highly promising strategy because it utilizes renewable energy and can achieve complete mineralization of pollutants.3 Over the past decade, diverse semiconductors, including oxides, sulfides, and nitrides, have been extensively investigated for environmental remediation.4

Among widely reported photocatalysts, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) has attracted considerable attention as a metal-free, visible light responsive material that can be easily synthesized. Since Wang et al. first reported g-C3N4 for H2 evolution in 2009,5 g-C3N4 has been widely applied in various photocatalytic oxidation and reduction reactions, such as pollutant degradation,6,7 photocatalytic water splitting,8 and CO2 reduction.9 However, the practical application of pristine g-C3N4 is hindered by several intrinsic drawbacks: rapid recombination of photogenerated charge carriers, limited visible light absorption, low specific surface area, and poor electrical conductivity.10,11 To overcome these drawbacks, various strategies such as doping, heterojunction construction, and morphology optimization have been developed to improve the visible light driven photoactivity of g-C3N4.12

Among these strategies, transition metal doping has proven particularly effective in tailoring the electronic structure and band configuration of g-C3N4. Metal ions can coordinate with the tri-s-triazine units of g-C3N4, thereby modulating its electronic properties without disrupting the host framework.13,14 For instance, Nan et al. reported that Fe atoms coordinated with pyridinic N sites in g-C3N4, which immobilized the metal centers, suppressed Fe leaching, and facilitated electron transport via Fe–N bridging pathways.15 Wang et al. fabricated an efficient P/Fe co-doped g-C3N4 catalyst for tetracycline hydrochloride (TC) degradation, with strong chelation and acid–base interactions between precursors leading to significantly enhanced performance relative to singly doped samples.16 In addition to elemental doping, molecular doping is another effective strategy for modulating the structure of g-C3N4 to enhance its photocatalytic performance. Ye et al.17 grafted ferrocene units on g-C3N4 surface to introduce metal-to-ligand charge-transfer transitions and promote interfacial electron delocalization, thereby significantly enhancing the photocatalytic efficiency. Zhang et al.18 incorporated a D–π–A type triphenylamine dye into g-C3N4, which extended the π-conjugated system, broadened the visible light absorption range, and facilitated intramolecular charge transfer, leading to a markedly improved photocatalytic hydrogen evolution rate.

Nevertheless, these molecular doping examples primarily rely on a single functional component, which may limit the achievable structural modulation and overall activity enhancement. Moreover, most molecular dopants are organic dyes, which require cumbersome preparation and may cause secondary pollution. Furthermore, previous studies have mainly focused on synthesizing new complexes to harvest visible light, while the specific interactions between dopants and the g-C3N4 framework have been largely neglected.

Herein, we propose a rational design strategy for the simultaneous incorporation of pyromellitic dianhydride (PMDA) and Fe into the g-C3N4 framework via a one-step calcination process. Fe coordinates with the terminal amino groups of g-C3N4, facilitating the in situ formation of polyimide (PI). This approach promotes the redistribution of electron density, generating an internal gradient that enhances charge separation at the heterojunction interface. The photocatalytic performance of the as-prepared composites was evaluated through the degradation of TC under visible light irradiation. This work provides a novel perspective for designing high-performance photocatalysts that enhance the transport, separation, and utilization of photoexcited charge carriers.

Experimental section

Reagents and chemicals

Melamine, ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), pyromellitic dianhydride (PMDA), tetracycline hydrochloride (TC), sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na), isopropanol (IPA), and benzoquinone (BQ) were used. All chemicals were analytically grade and purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (Shanghai, China). All reagents were used as received without further purification. Deionized water was used throughout all experiments.

Synthesis of photocatalysts

Pristine g-C3N4, denoted as CN, was synthesized according to a previously reported procedure.5 Briefly, melamine was heated in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 4 h in air with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1.

The Fe/PI co-doped g-C3N4 composite (FPICN) was prepared via a one-step pyrolysis method. In a typical synthesis, 10 g of melamine, 17 g of PMDA (melamine: PMDA molar ratio = 1 : 1), and 50 mL FeCl3 aqueous solution (1 g L−1) were mixed in a 250 mL beaker. The resulting suspension was vigorously stirred and evaporated to dryness at 80 °C. The solid intermediate was further dried at 100 °C for 2 h, thoroughly ground into a fine powder, and transferred to a covered crucible. Subsequently, the crucible was placed in a muffle furnace, heated to 550 °C at a rate of 5 °C min−1, and maintained at this temperature for 4 h in air. After cooling naturally to room temperature, the product was washed repeatedly with deionized water until neutral pH, and then dried at 60 °C overnight. The final yellowish powder was obtained after grinding. For comparison, Fe-doped g-C3N4 (FCN) was prepared via an identical procedure except for the omission of PMDA.

Characterization

The crystal structures of the samples were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 ADVANCE diffractometer equipped with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Data were collected over the 2θ range of 5–80° at a scanning rate of 1° min−1. Morphological observations were conducted using a Hitachi S-4800 field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM) operating at an acceleration voltage of 3 kV. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy spectra were obtained by a PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 FT-IR Spectrometer in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with samples prepared as KBr pellets. Prior to measurement, all samples including the KBr were dried at 120 °C for 24 h. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured using a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 analyzer at 77 K. Before measurement, the sample was vacuum-pretreated at 120 °C for 3 h. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on a Thermo Scientific Escalab 250Xi spectrometer to determine the surface chemical states and valence band (VB) positions and calibrated by the C 1s peak located at 284.8 eV. UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra (UV-vis DRS) of the samples were recorded using BaSO4 as a reference on a UV-2650 spectrophotometer. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded at room temperature on a Hitachi F-4500 spectrofluorometer with an excitation wavelength of 350 nm and a slit width of 3 nm, over the emission range of 350 to 700 nm.

Electrochemical measurements, including photocurrent response and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), were carried out on a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation (CH Instruments, China) using a standard three-electrode system. An Ag/AgCl electrode and a platinum wire served as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively. The working electrode was prepared by coating a homogeneous catalyst slurry onto indium tin oxide (ITO) conductive glass. A quartz cell filled with 0.5 mol L−1 Na2SO4 (pH ≈ 6.8) aqueous solution was used as electrolyte. For EIS measurements, a sinusoidal potential perturbation of 10 mV amplitude was applied over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz.

Photocatalytic activity evaluation

The photocatalytic activities of the as-prepared samples were systematically evaluated through the degradation of TC under UV-vis light irradiation. In a typical experiment, 20 mg of the photocatalyst was dispersed into 100 mL of TC aqueous solution (10 mg L−1). The suspension was first stirred in the darkness for 30 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium. The TC concentration measured after this dark period was used as the initial concentration (C0) for the kinetic analysis, thereby excluding the contribution of adsorption. Subsequently, the system was irradiated using a 300 W xenon lamp (PLS-SXE300UV) equipped with a cut-off filter (λ >360 nm). The lamp was placed at a fixed distance of 14 cm from the liquid surface, and the lamp current was controlled at 13 A, corresponding to a measured irradiance of 1200 mW cm−2. During irradiation, the reaction temperature was maintained at 25 ± 2 °C by a circulating water system. At predetermined time intervals, approximately 1 mL of the suspension was sampled and filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane filter to remove the catalyst particles.

The concentration of TC in the filtrate was monitored by measuring its characteristic absorbance at 358 nm using a Shimadzu UV-2450 UV-vis spectrophotometer. The degradation percentage was calculated using the following equation:1η (%) = (1 − Ct/C0) × 100%where η represent the degradation percentage, Ct and C0 represent the TC concentration at time t and initially, respectively.

To quantitatively evaluate the reaction kinetics, the pseudo-first-order kinetic eqn (2) was used to fit the TC photodegradation reaction data:2−ln(Ct/C0) = ktwhere C0 (mg L−1) and Ct (mg L−1) are the initial and remaining concentrations of TC during the reaction process, k (min−1) and t (min) were the apparent rate constant and reaction time, respectively.

To assess the reusability of the photocatalyst, recycling tests were performed. After each photocatalytic cycle, the catalyst was recovered by centrifugation at 10 000 rpm for 15 min. The collected solid was washed three times with deionized water carefully to thoroughly remove residual TC molecules and any adsorbed degradation intermediates. The washed catalyst was then dried at 60 °C overnight and directly reused in the subsequent cycle without any further treatment. The effects of environmental factors on the photocatalytic degradation were also investigated. The initial pH of the TC solution was adjusted using dilute HCl and NaOH solutions. Furthermore, the influence of common coexisting ions was studied by separately adding 1 mol L−1 NaCl, MgCl2, CaCl2 and NH4Cl into the reaction system prior to illumination.

Results and discussion

Characterizations of materials

The morphologies of CN, FCN, and FPICN were investigated by SEM. As shown in Fig. 1a, pristine CN displayed large, irregular lamellar aggregates with smooth surfaces, characteristic of bulk g-C3N4. (ref. 19) After Fe doping (Fig. 1b), FCN displayed a more exfoliated and fragmented morphology, which resulted from the influence of Fe on precursor polymerization during high-temperature pyrolysis.16 In the case of FPICN (Fig. 1c), the stacked lamellae were further fragmented into smaller nanoparticles, likely due to the in situ formation of PI. which suppressed vertical stacking and crystal growth of the g-C3N4 framework. EDS elemental mapping confirmed a uniform distribution of Fe and O throughout the FPICN matrix (Fig. 1d), which verified the successful incorporation of both Fe and PI into the CN framework. Moreover, quantitative analysis indicated that the elemental contents of Fe and O were 0.35% and 7.06%, respectively.

SEM image of (a) CN, (b) FCN, (c) FPICN, and (d) EDS-mapping of FPICN.

The XRD patterns of the synthesized samples are shown in Fig. 2a. All samples exhibited two distinct diffraction peaks at 13.0° and 27.5°. The characteristic diffraction peak at 13.0° corresponded to the in-plane structural packing of tri-s-triazine units, indexed as the (100) plane, whereas the strong peak at 27.5° was attributed to the interlayer stacking of aromatic systems, indexed as the (002) plane. These two peaks were consistent with the standard hexagonal phase of g-C3N4 (JCPDS No. 87-1526).20 In FPICN, additional diffraction peaks at 19.0°, 27.4°, and 29.6° were assigned to face-to-face stacking interactions of PI-PI, melem–melem, and melem-PI units, respectively.21 Notably, the diffraction peak at 27.5° shifted to lower 2θ angles in both FCN and FPICN patterns, accompanied by a decrease in intensity. This shift indicated an expansion of the interlayer distance, likely resulting from the interstitial incorporation of Fe and PI into the framework.22 The reduced peak intensity suggested that doping caused partial fragmentation of the planar structure into smaller crystallites, which agreed well with the SEM observations.

To evaluate the textural properties and porosity changes induced by the modification, N2 adsorption–desorption measurements were conducted. Fig. 2c shows the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of CN, FCN, and FPICN. All samples exhibited type-IV isotherms with H3-type hysteresis loops according to the IUPAC classification, implying mesoporous structures composed of slit-like pores formed by layered aggregates.26 Notably, the hysteresis loop area increased significantly for the doped samples, reflecting the enhanced pore volume resulting from the modification. The pore size distribution curves (Fig. 2d) were derived by the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method applied to the adsorption branch. The pore diameter distributions of the three samples were in the ranged from 20 to 100 nm, suggesting the coexistence of mesoporous and macroporous structures. The specific surface areas and pore parameters of each catalyst sample were summarized in Table 1. CN possessed a relatively low specific surface area (20.5 m2 g−1), which was attributed to the dense stacking of its bulk layered structure. In contrast, the BET surface areas of FCN and FPICN increased significantly to 34.98 and 71.18 m2 g−1, respectively. The enhanced surface area was ascribed to the smaller graphitic plane sizes, consistent with SEM results, which exposed more active sites and facilitated mass transfer, thereby contributing to the enhanced photocatalytic activity.19

The elemental composition and surface electronic states of the samples were investigated using XPS. All binding energies were calibrated against the adventitious C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. The survey spectra (Fig. 3a) confirmed the presence of C, N, and O in all samples. In the high-resolution C 1s spectra (Fig. 3b), pristine CN and FCN exhibited two main peaks at 285.1 eV and 288.2 eV, corresponding to C–N and sp2-hybridized NC–N carbon in the tri-s-triazine aromatic ring,27,28 respectively. A new peak emerged at 286.2 eV in FPICN, which is characteristic of OC–N bonds and confirms the successful formation of the imide linkage.7 The O 1s spectrum (Fig. 3c) displayed two peaks at 530.6 eV and 532.4 eV, corresponding to CO and O–H. The significantly higher relative intensity of the 530.6 eV component in FPICN is attributed to the carbonyl groups introduced by the PI moiety.29

XPS spectra for (a) survey, (b) C 1s, (c) O 1s , (d) N 1s, and (e) Fe 2p of as-prepared samples. (f) A scheme of the synthesis of FPICN.

The N 1s peaks (Fig. 3d) were deconvoluted into three peaks at 398.3 eV, 399.5 eV and 400.7 eV, which were attributed to sp2-hybridized pyridinic nitrogen (C–NC), tertiary nitrogen (N–(C)3) groups in the tri-s-triazine rings, and terminal amino functional groups (–NH2), respectively.30 Compared with pristine CN, the pyridinic nitrogen peak in FCN and FPICN shifted to higher binding energy. This positive shift can be attributed to electron donation from nitrogen to Fe upon Fe–N bond formation. To further confirm this coordination, high-resolution Fe 2p spectra were recorded. The Fe 2p peak (Fig. 3e) could be deconvoluted into two main peaks at 710.5 eV and 723.4 eV, corresponding to Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2, (ref. 20) respectively. No signal of zero-valent Fe (∼706.7 eV) or distinct shake-up satellites (∼719 eV) was detected, further indicating that Fe is predominantly atomically dispersed and chemically bonded to pyridinic N atoms rather than present as segregated oxide clusters.15 The resulting Fe-Nx sites are expected to act as charge-trapping centers and facilitate interfacial electron transfer, thereby contributing to the enhanced charge separation and photocatalytic performance. Based on the above results, a synthesis scheme for FPICN is proposed (Fig. 3e).

Optical and electronic properties

To elucidate the charge-separation and transfer mechanisms responsible for the enhanced photocatalytic performance, photoelectrochemical measurements were performed. As shown in Fig. 4a, the photocurrent density of the FPICN electrode was significantly enhanced compared to those of pristine CN and FCN. This improvement indicated that integration of the PI moiety effectively promoted the migration and separation of photogenerated charge carriers. The enhancement was likely driven by the strong electron-withdrawing effect of PI, which acted as an efficient electron acceptor and facilitated charge separation.6,31

(a) Transient photocurrents response, (b) electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, (c) steady-state photoluminescence spectra, and (d) time-resolved photoluminescence decay spectra of the samples.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to investigate the interfacial charge transfer resistance. As depicted in Fig. 4b, the Nyquist plots showed that FPICN exhibited the smallest arc radius among the three samples. A smaller arc radius corresponds to lower charge-transfer resistance, indicating more efficient separation of electron–hole pairs and faster interfacial charge-transfer kinetics.6 Combined with the photocurrent results, these EIS data indicate that the PI component serves as a charge-transfer mediator, which is consistent with the strong electron-withdrawing character of the imide units.

To further probe the recombination behavior of photogenerated carriers, steady-state photoluminescence (PL) were recorded. As shown in Fig. 4c, pristine CN displayed a strong emission peak centered at approximately 470 nm, indicating a high recombination rate of charge carriers. For FCN and FPICN, the peak intensity of emission significantly decreased and the center of the emission peak was blue-shifted to approximately 450 nm. Such blue-shift is widely ascribed to the quantum confinement effect induced by a reduction in the lateral size of the conjugated domains and the disruption of interlayer stacking.32 This interpretation is consistent with the SEM and XRD observations. To further assess the recombination behavior of photogenerated carriers, time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) decay spectra were recorded (Fig. 4d). The TRPL decay curves were fitted with a double-exponential decay function to determine the carrier lifetimes. The lifetime components (τ1 and τ2), represent radiative and non-radiative decay processes, respectively.22 The calculated average PL lifetimes decreased from 4.38 ns (CN) to 3.18 ns (FCN) and 2.15 ns (FPICN). The shortened lifetimes, consistent with the PL quenching trend, confirmed that radiative recombination was effectively suppressed in the modified samples.

The optical absorption properties of the synthesized photocatalysts were investigated by UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS). Fig. 5a, pristine CN exhibited a characteristic absorption edge at approximately 460 nm. Relative to CN, both FCN and FPICN exhibited red-shift of the absorption edge. In particular, the FPICN composite exhibited a significant red-shift of the absorption edge to 567 nm, accompanied by a distinct color change from light yellow to deep brown. This red-shift, along with an enhanced absorption tail spanning the visible region, is ascribed to the extended π-conjugation in the PI moiety and the charge-transfer transitions involving the highly dispersed Fe species.33,34 The Tauc plots for indirect allowed transitions, (αhν)1/2versus hν, are shown in Fig. 5b, where α is the absorption coefficient. The optical band gap energies (Eg) were estimated by extrapolating the linear portion of the curve to the energy axis. The estimated optical Eg decreased from 2.70 eV (CN) to 2.52 eV (FCN) and 2.29 eV (FPICN). The monotonic narrowing of band gap is fully consistent with the red-shift of the absorption edge and confirms that the dual-modification strategy effectively narrows the band gap, thereby extending the photo-response further into the visible range.

(a) UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra, (b) plots of the (αhv)1/2vs. photon energy (hv), (c) valence band XPS spectra, and (d) band alignments of the as-prepared samples.

To elucidate the electronic structure evolution, the valence band (VB) potentials were determined via XPS valence band spectra. As shown in Fig. 5c, the VB edge of pristine CN was located at approximately 1.59 eV (vs. NHE). After modification, the VB edges of FCN and FPICN shifted to more positive values of +1.73 V and +1.84 V, respectively. This systematic shift toward more positive potentials indicates an enhanced thermodynamic driving force for hole-mediated oxidation reactions. The positive shift of the VB maximum can be rationalized by the electron-withdrawing effect of the PMDA moiety.

By combining the optical bandgap (Eg) and the VB potential (EVB), the conduction band (CB) potentials (ECB) were calculated (ECB = EVB − Eg). The calculated ECB value for CN, FCN, and FPICN were −1.11, −0.78 and −0.45 V, respectively. The resulting band alignment diagram was depicted in Fig. 5d. Compared to CN, both FCN and FPICN exhibited narrower band gaps and more positive VB positions, while the CB edge shifts to less negative potentials. This finding is in good agreement with computational results for the HOMO and LUMO energies reported in the literature.31 The electron-withdrawing imide units lower the HOMO energy, which corresponds to the more positive VB potentials observed experimentally. This electronic structure modulation alters the intrinsic band structure of CN, enabling spatial separation of photogenerated electron–hole pairs and thereby enhancing the photocatalytic efficiency.

Photocatalytic degradation of TC

The photocatalytic performance of the synthesized samples was evaluated by monitoring the degradation of TC under irradiation from a 300 W Xe lamp equipped with a cutoff filter (λ > 360 nm). Fig. 6a presents the changes in TC concentration during the photocatalytic process. Pristine CN exhibited negligible photocatalytic activity, with only 20.10% degradation achieved within 80 min. In contrast, the removal efficiencies for FCN and FPICN were significantly higher, reaching 51.68% and 86.98%, respectively. The influence of composition on performance was systematically investigated by varying the content of Fe and PMDA (see Fig. S1). Control experiments confirmed that the presence of trace amount of Fe was indispensable. This enhancement was attributed to the coordination of Fe within the CN matrix, which electronically activates the terminal –NH2 groups, thereby enhancing their nucleophilicity toward the PMDA monomers.8,35

To distinguish the contributions of the enlarged specific surface area and intrinsic electronic activity, we calculated the BET-surface-area-normalized apparent rate constants (knorm = k/SBET), which represent the activity per unit surface area. As shown in Table S1, the knorm of FPICN is 2.5 times higher than that of pristine CN, while its surface area was 3.5 times that of pristine CN. This result indicates that, although the enlarged surface area contributes positively to the overall activity, the improved electronic properties play an equally important.

To evaluate the practical applicability for water treatment, the impact of pH on TC decomposition was investigated. As illustrated in Fig. 6b, the FPICN composite exhibited relatively high activity for TC degradation over a broad pH range of 3 to 11. Specifically, the degradation efficiency at pH 3.0 was 58.16%, while the highest value of 88.68% was achieved at pH 11.0. This observation aligns with previous reports indicating that the TC molecule is unstable and more prone to photolysis under alkaline conditions.36,37

Additionally, the impact of coexisting inorganic cations on TC degradation was assessed. As shown in Fig. 6c, the inhibition strength followed the order: NH4+ > Mg2+ > Na+ > Ca2+. This phenomenon could be rationalized by the interplay of several factors. The strongest inhibition by NH4+ could be attributed to its well-known ability to scavenge both hydroxyl radicals and photogenerated holes. Metal cations could form complexes with TC, thereby inhibiting its photo-reactivity.38 Generally, divalent metal ions exhibit higher complexation constants than monovalent ions. However, Ca2+, due to its unique bridging effect, partially alleviates the inhibitory effect, resulting in a slightly stronger net inhibition by Na+ compared to Ca2+.39

To evaluate photostability and recyclability, consecutive recycling experiments were performed. After each cycle, the photocatalyst was recovered by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with deionized water to remove adsorbed TC molecules, and dried at 60 °C overnight before being reused in the next cycle. As illustrated in Fig. 6d, the photocatalytic performance of FPICN remained above 81% after 4 successive cycles, demonstrating that FPICN could be efficiently recycled. Furthermore, the XRD patterns of the FPICN before and after the photoreaction remained unchanged (Fig. S2), implying that bulk crystalline structure is preserved under the reaction conditions. Photocatalytic mechanism

To elucidate the photocatalytic mechanism, radical trapping experiments were carried out to identify the reactive species involved. Several scavengers, including benzoquinone (BQ), isopropanol (IPA) and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na) were added during the photocatalytic degradation process of TC to trap superoxide radical (˙O2−), hydroxyl radical (˙OH) and holes (h+),47 respectively. As shown in Fig. 7a, the addition of BQ, IPA and EDTA-2Na led to varying degrees of inhibition of the photocatalytic degradation efficiency of FPICN. The order of inhibition was EDTA-2Na > BQ > IPA, suggesting that both h+ and ˙O2− play the primary roles in the photocatalytic decomposition of TC.

(a) The photodegradation of TC over FPICN in the presence of different radical scavengers; (b) schematic illustration of photocatalytic mechanism of FPICN.

Based on the above experimental results, a synergistic photocatalytic mechanism was proposed, as illustrated in Fig. 7b. Under illumination, photogenerated electrons in FPICN were excited to the CB, leaving the holes in the VB. During the photocatalytic degradation process, h+ in the VB of FPICN directly attack TC molecules for mineralization, as was confirmed by the trapping experiments. Meanwhile, ˙O2− is generated via the reduction of dissolved O2 in the solution by photoexcited electrons. It should be noted that ˙OH played a relatively minor role in the photocatalytic degradation of TC. The ˙OH may originate from the slow conversion of ˙O2−.

Conclusion

In this work, a dual-modification strategy was successfully developed by co-incorporating Fe and PI into g-C3N4via a one-step pyrolysis process. The resulting FPICN composite exhibited a substantially enlarged specific surface area, extended visible-light absorption, and significantly improved charge separation efficiency. The enhanced performance is attributed to the synergistic combination of morphological and electronic effects. The FPICN exhibited outstanding photocatalytic activity, achieving 86.98% degradation of TC within 80 min under UV-vis light irradiation, with an apparent rate constant ∼8.7 times higher than pristine CN, and maintained over 80% activity after four consecutive cycles. Band structure analysis and radical trapping experiments revealed that h+ and ˙O2− were the primary reactive species. This study provides a rational design strategy for high-performance photocatalysts through the synergistic integration of transition metal and conjugated polymer modification, offering insights for efficient photocatalytic water remediation.
