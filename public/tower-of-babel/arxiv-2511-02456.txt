On Convergence Rates of Spiked Eigenvalue Estimates: A General Study of Global and Local Laws in Sample Covariance Matrices
Bing-Yi Jing, Weiming Li, Jiahui Xie, Yangchun Zhang, Wang Zhou

Abstract
This paper investigates global and local laws for sample covariance matrices with general growth rates of dimensions. The sample size N N and population dimension M M can have the same order in logarithm, which implies that their ratio M / N M/N can approach zero, a constant, or infinity. These theories are utilized to determine the convergence rate of spiked eigenvalue estimates.



Keywords: Local laws; Global laws; General growth rates; Rate of spiked eigenvalue estimates

This paper investigates the spectral behaviors of sample covariance matrices with a general correlation structure. The dimension-to-sample size ratio
is allowed to tend towards zero, converge to a positive constant, or diverge to infinity.
By analyzing these diverse cases, one can understand the spectral evolution of covariance matrices across different asymptotic scenarios, thereby providing comprehensive insights for applications.
To be specific,
let
T
be an
M\times M
deterministic matrix and

\displaystyle X=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N})=(x_{ij})_{M,N}

be a collection of
M\times N
independent and normalized real or complex variables.
We will consider the following random matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}:=TXX^{*}T^{*}.

(1.1)

This is the classical sample covariance matrix with
\Sigma:=TT^{*}
describing population components correlation up to a scaling factor.
Our main assumptions on this model are listed below.

Assumption (A1).
The dimensions
M
and
N
tend to infinity in such a way that

\displaystyle N\to\infty,\quad M=M_{N}\to\infty,\quad N^{b}\lesssim M\lesssim N^{a},

(1.2)

for some positive constants
b\leq a
.

Assumption (A2).

The entries of
X
satisfy the following moment conditions

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}x_{ij}=0,\quad\mathbb{E}|x_{ij}|^{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}},\quad\mathbb{E}|(MN)^{1/4}x_{ij}|^{q}<C_{q}.

(1.3)

for some positive constant
C_{q}
and any integer
q\in\mathbb{N}
.

Assumption (A3).

The empirical spectral distribution
\pi
of the matrix
\Sigma

satisfies

\pi\left([0,\tau]\right)\leqslant 1-\tau\quad\text{ and }\quad\pi\left([0,\tau^{-1}]\right)=1

(1.4)

for some small enough constant
\tau>0
. In addition, we assume
\pi
has finite bulk components.

Assumptions (A1)-(A2)-(A3) are commonly employed in Random Matrix Theory. Assumption (A1) illustrates our asymptotic regime, where the dimensions
M
and
N
can be logarithmically proportional, i.e.,
\log M\sim\log N
. Consequently, their ratio

\phi:=\frac{M}{N}

may have a limit
\phi_{\infty}
taking values in the interval
[0,\infty]
. In Assumption (A2), we normalize the entries of
X
by the factor
(MN)^{1/4}
and assume the existence of moments of all orders. This standardization allows us to interpret the matrix
\mathcal{W}
as a rescaled sample covariance matrix, streamlining our analysis when addressing various growth rates of the ratio
\phi
. Assumption (A3) states that the spectrum of
\Sigma
is bounded and does not concentrate at zero. A stronger requirement

\displaystyle T=T^{*}=\Sigma^{1/2}>0

(1.5)

will be employed to simplify the expressions in technical proofs. Note that (
1.5
) can always be substituted with (
1.4
).

This paper offers a threefold contribution: establishing global laws for
\mathcal{W}
, deriving local laws for
\mathcal{W}
, and applying these findings to the estimation of spiked eigenvalues.
These findings are derived through analyzing the Stieltjes transform
m_{\mathcal{W}}(z)
of the matrix
\mathcal{W}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle m_{\mathcal{W}}(z)=\frac{1}{M}{\rm tr}\mathcal{G}(z)\quad\text{ with }\quad\mathcal{G}(z):=\left(\mathcal{W}-z\right)^{-1}

(1.6)

and

z=E+\mathrm{i}\eta,\quad\eta>0

denoting the spectral parameter, a complex number in the open upper-half plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}
. The imaginary part
\eta
of
z
is called the
spectral resolution
.

Global laws.

A global law describes the convergence of
m_{\mathcal{W}}(z)
with the spectral parameter
z
independent of
N
and the spectral resolution
\eta
of order one.
This type of global law provides tools for determining the limiting spectral distribution of
\mathcal{W}
and has been well-established in the regime
\phi\to\phi_{\infty}\in(0,\infty)
, see
[
26
,
36
,
32
,
31
,
13
]
.
There are instances where the centralized sample covariance matrix is under consideration,
say,

\mathcal{W}-{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{W}=TXX^{*}T^{*}-\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma,

which pulls back the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{W}
towards the origin. For references, one can refer to
[
8
,
11
,
15
,
16
]
when
\phi\to\phi_{\infty}=0
or
\infty
.

This paper will first establish global laws for both the sample covariance matrix
\mathcal{W}
and its centralized version
\mathcal{W}-{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{W}
within the asymptotic framework defined by Assumption (A1). These laws depend on
(M,N)
but not on a specific limit
\phi_{\infty}
and can, therefore, accommodate a broad range of scenarios where
\phi
approaches
\phi_{\infty}\in[0,\infty]
. In this way, our findings unify all the previously mentioned results regarding the limiting spectral distribution of
\mathcal{W}
.

Local laws.

A local law quantifies the deviation of
m_{\mathcal{W}}(z)
from its
(M,N)
-dependent non-random approximate, denoted as
m_{0}(z)
, for all
z
with an imaginary part
\eta\gg N^{-1}
. This implies that the local law applies to the spectral parameter
z
, dependent on
N
, allowing the spectral resolution
\eta
to be significantly smaller than the global scale of 1. The local law is a foundation for establishing a universality theorem, which is similar to the central limit theorem. Universality allows us to determine the asymptotic distribution of eigenvalues of random matrices without imposing strict assumptions on the distribution of entries. For instance, Wigner has observed a physical phenomenon that the eigenvalue gap distribution in a large and complex system can illustrate. This distribution is independent of other intricate structures and solely depends on the symmetry class of the physical system. Essentially, the distribution is universal.
Most studies on the local law for
\mathcal{W}
in the literature have primarily focused on scenarios where
\phi
approaches a positive constant
\phi_{\infty}\in(0,\infty)
. See
[
29
,
12
,
23
,
17
,
35
,
33
]
. As far as we know, the only existing work addressing the general case where
\phi
can approach zero or infinity is
[
3
]
, established for the case of
\Sigma=I
.

Therefore, our second objective is to establish local laws for the sample covariance matrix
\mathcal{W}
with general covariance matrix
\Sigma
and dimension-to-sample size ratio
\phi\to\phi_{\infty}\in[0,\infty]
.

Convergence rate of spiked eigenvalue estimates.

The spiked covariance model for
\Sigma
, originally introduced by
[
21
]
, illustrates that a small number of eigenvalues of
\Sigma
are clearly separated from the bulk
and often carry significant information about the population.
Estimating these spikes is an essential statistical inference task, often initiated by the Stieltjes transform
m_{\mathcal{W}}(z)
. This has been discussed by
[
27
]
and
[
4
]
.

As detailed in the paper, we can derive a more precise bound on the difference between
m_{\mathcal{W}}(z)
and its limit by establishing local laws outside the spectrum. This enables us to determine a convergence rate for the estimation.

2
Global laws

2.1
Global laws for sample covariance matrices

Our first result is on global laws for the sample covariance matrix
\mathcal{W}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions (A1)-(A2)-(A3) hold. Then, there exists a deterministic function
m_{0}(z)
such that

m_{\mathcal{W}}(z)-m_{0}(z)\xrightarrow{a.s.}0,\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

In particular, the function
m=m_{0}(z)
is the unique solution to

\displaystyle m=\int\frac{1}{x(\phi^{-1/2}-\phi^{1/2}-\phi^{1/2}zm)-z}\pi(\mathrm{d}x)

(2.1)

on the set
\{z:z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},-(1-\phi)/z+\phi m(z)\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Theorem
2.1
establishes the strong consistency of the Stieltjes transform

m_{\mathcal{W}}(z)
.
The function
m_{0}(z)
is an approximate of the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}m_{\mathcal{W}}(z)
and is uniquely determined by the equation (
2.1
). An alternative representation of this equation is

\frac{1}{m_{1}}=-z+\phi^{1/2}\int\frac{x}{1+\phi^{-1/2}m_{1}x}\pi(\mathrm{d}x),

(2.2)

where
m_{1}=-(1-\phi)/z+\phi m_{0}
approximates the companion Stieltjes transform of
\mathcal{W}
, see
[
32
]
.

Remark 2.3
.

Theorem
2.1
presents a Marcěnko-Pastur law
[
26
]
for the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{W}
, extending the original results to encompass a broader range of the ratio
\phi
. By specializing the limit of this ratio, we can recover several well-established limiting spectral distributions from equations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
).

I.

The standard case where
\phi\to\phi_{\infty}\in(0,\infty)
. Equation (
2.1
) converges to that from
[
26
]
up to a scaling factor
\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

II.

The degenerate case where
\phi\to\phi_{\infty}=0
with
\Sigma=I_{M}
.
[
8
,
15
]
showed that the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of the centralized sample covariance matrix, i.e.,

\mathcal{W}-\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}I_{M},

converges to the standard semicircle law. This can be recovered from (
2.1
) by using the replacement
z\to z+\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and then taking the limit as
\phi\to 0
, which yields
m_{0}(z)\to m=m(z)
satisfying

\displaystyle m+\frac{1}{m}+z=0,\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

(2.3)

III.

The degenerate case where
\phi\to\phi_{\infty}=\infty
. In this case, the matrix
\mathcal{W}
only has
N
nonzero eigenvalues. It is thus convenient to analyze its companion matrix
W:=X^{*}\Sigma X
with the following normalization:

\frac{W-\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}a_{p}I_{N}}{\sqrt{b_{p}}}\quad\text{ where }\quad a_{p}=\frac{1}{M}{\rm tr}(\Sigma),\quad b_{p}=\frac{1}{M}{\rm tr}(\Sigma^{2}).

[
30
]
showed that the ESD of this renormalized matrix converges to the standard semicircle law.
To recover this result, we apply the transforms

m_{1}\to\frac{m}{\sqrt{b_{p}}}\quad\text{and}\quad z\to\sqrt{b_{p}}\cdot z+\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}a_{p}

to (
2.2
), which gives

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{b_{p}}}{m}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{b_{p}}z-\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}a_{p}+\phi^{1/2}\int\frac{x\sqrt{b_{p}}}{\sqrt{b_{p}}+\phi^{-1/2}mx}\pi(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{b_{p}}z-\int\frac{x^{2}m}{\sqrt{b_{p}}+\phi^{-1/2}mx}\pi(\mathrm{d}x).

Then, by taking the limit as
\phi\to\infty
, we get the equation (
2.3
) that defines the semicircle law.

To understand the eigenvalue behaviors of the centralized sample covariance matrix
\mathcal{W}-{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{W}
, we cannot rely on Theorem
2.1
if the covariance matrix
\Sigma
does not have a spherical shape (i.e.,
\Sigma\neq a_{p}I_{M}
). Consequently, we introduce a new result to address this problem.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose that Assumptions (A1)-(A2)-(A3) hold. Let
\tilde{m}_{\mathcal{W}}(z)
be the Stieltjes transform of
\mathcal{W}-{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{W}
.
Then, there exists a deterministic function
\tilde{m}_{0}(z)
such that

\tilde{m}_{\mathcal{W}}(z)-\tilde{m}_{0}(z)\xrightarrow{a.s.}0,\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

In particular, the function
m=\tilde{m}_{0}(z)
is the unique solution to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\displaystyle 1+zm=-\frac{g^{2}}{1+\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}g},\\
&\displaystyle g=-\int\frac{x}{xg/[1+\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}g]+z}\pi(\mathrm{d}x).\end{cases}

(2.4)

on the set
\{z:z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},g\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\}
.

Remark 2.5
.

Theorem
2.4
describes the global eigenvalue distribution of the centralized sample covariance matrix. The auxiliary complex function
g=g(z)
approximates the following
random quantity

g_{n}(z):=\frac{1}{M}{\rm tr}\left(\mathcal{W}-{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{W}-z\right)^{-1}\Sigma

satisfying
g_{n}(z)-g(z)\to 0
, almost surely. It’s evident that if the ratio
\phi\to\phi_{\infty}=0
, the system of equations in (
2.4
) reduces to

\displaystyle 1+zm+g^{2}=0,\quad g=\int\frac{x}{-z-xg}\pi(\mathrm{d}x),

(2.5)

which defines a generalized semicircle law; see
[
9
,
11
]
. If, in addition,

\Sigma=I_{M}
,
it reduces to (
2.3
) that defines the semicircle law.

2.2
Proof of Theorems
2.1
and
2.4
.

This section is devoted to proving Theorem
2.1
and Theorem
2.4
. Let

\displaystyle B=\mathcal{W}-\theta{\mathbb{E}}\mathcal{W},\quad D(z)=B-zI,\quad m_{B}(z)=\frac{1}{M}{\rm tr}D^{-1}(z),

where the parameter
\theta\in\{0,1\}
. As demonstrated below, we will establish a general lemma to prove the two theorems.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that Assumptions (A1)-(A2)-(A3) hold.
Then, there exists a deterministic function
m_{b}(z)
such that

m_{B}(z)-m_{b}(z)\xrightarrow{a.s.}0,\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

In particular, the function
m=m_{b}(z)
is the unique solution to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\displaystyle 1+zm=g\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[\frac{1}{1+\phi^{1/2}g}-\theta\right],\\
&\displaystyle g=\int\frac{x}{x\phi^{-1/2}\big[(1+\phi^{1/2}g)^{-1}-\theta\big]-z}\pi(\mathrm{d}x).\end{cases}

(2.6)

on the set
\{z:z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},g\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\}
.

Theorem
2.4
is a direct consequence of this lemma by taking
\theta=1
. To obtain Theorem
2.1
, we set

\theta=0\quad\text{and}\quad 1+\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}g=b^{-1},

which gives

\displaystyle m_{b}=\int\frac{1}{x\phi^{-1/2}b-z}\pi(\mathrm{d}x)=\int\frac{1}{x\phi^{-1/2}(1-\phi-\phi zm_{b})-z}\pi(\mathrm{d}x).

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

We shall prove this lemma under finite
(4+\delta)
th moments of
\{(MN)^{\frac{1}{4}}x_{ij}\}
for some
\delta>0
. This proof involves five steps:

1.

For any fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},m_{B}(z)-{\mathbb{E}}m_{B}(z)\rightarrow 0
, a.s.;

2.

For any fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},{\mathbb{E}}m_{B}(z)-m_{b}(z)\rightarrow 0
;

3.

Except for a null set,
m_{B}(z)-m_{b}(z)\rightarrow 0
for every
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
;

4.

Uniqueness of the solution to (
2.6
).

We will concentrate only on the first two steps, as the third step only involves standard arguments in Random Matrix Theory
[
6
]
, and the final step follows a similar procedure for obtaining uniqueness for (
2.5
) as described in
[
9
]
.
Below, we list some notation that will be used throughout the proof. For
j=1,\ldots,N
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}_{j}=T{\mathbf{x}}_{j},\quad D_{j}(z)=D(z)-{\mathbf{y}}_{j}{\mathbf{y}}^{*}_{j},\quad\beta_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{1+{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}(z){\mathbf{y}}_{j}},

\displaystyle b_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{1+\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}\Sigma D_{j}^{-1}(z)},\quad\gamma_{j}(z)={\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}(z){\mathbf{y}}_{j}-\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{M}{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}\Sigma D_{j}^{-1}(z),

\displaystyle\underline{m}_{B}(z)=-\frac{1-\phi}{z}+\phi m_{B}(z),\quad\underline{m}_{b}(z)=-\frac{1-\phi}{z}+\phi m_{b}(z),

\displaystyle b_{N}(z)=\frac{1}{1+\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}\Sigma D^{-1}(z)},\quad V(z)=zI_{M}-\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}(b_{N}(z)-\theta)\Sigma.

We denote by
C
some constant appearing in inequalities, which may take on different values from one expression to the next.

Step 1. Almost sure convergence of the random part.

Let
{\mathbb{E}}_{0}(\cdot)
denote expectation and
{\mathbb{E}}_{j}(\cdot)
denote conditional expectation with respect to the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\}
for
j=1,\ldots,N
.
Then, by the matrix formula

\left({A}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\right)^{-1}={A}^{-1}-\frac{{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}{A}^{-1}}{1+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}},

we can obtain a martingale decomposition of
m_{B}(z)
as

\displaystyle m_{B}(z)-{\mathbb{E}}m_{B}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}({\mathbb{E}}_{j}-{\mathbb{E}}_{j-1}){\rm tr}\left[D^{-1}(z)-D_{j}^{-1}(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}({\mathbb{E}}_{j}-{\mathbb{E}}_{j-1})\frac{-{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-2}(z){\mathbf{y}}_{j}}{1+{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}(z){\mathbf{y}}_{j}}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}d_{j}(z).

For any
z=E+{\rm i}\eta
with
\eta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-2}(z){\mathbf{y}}_{j}}{1+{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}(z){\mathbf{y}}_{j}}\right|\leqslant\frac{{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}\left(D_{j}^{2}(E)+\eta^{2}I_{M}\right)^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}}{\operatorname{Im}\left(1+{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}(z){\mathbf{y}}_{j}\right)}=\frac{1}{\eta}.

Therefore,
\{d_{j}\}
forms a sequence of bounded martingale differences.
In addition, from Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-\ell}(z){\mathbf{y}}_{j}\right|^{2k}\leqslant

\displaystyle C|\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{-1}{\rm tr}D_{j}^{-\ell}(z)\Sigma|^{2k}+C{\mathbb{E}}\left|{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-\ell}(z){\mathbf{y}}_{j}-\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{-1}{\rm tr}D_{j}^{-\ell}(z)\Sigma\right|^{2k}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\phi^{k}

for
\ell=1,2
and any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. This implies that if
\phi
tends to zero, the martingale differences
\{d_{j}\}
can be bounded by
|\phi|^{\frac{1}{2}}
with high probability.
Therefore, by Burkhölder’s inequality, for any
k\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|m_{B}(z)-{\mathbb{E}}m_{B}(z)\right|^{2k}\leqslant\frac{C}{M^{2k}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|d_{j}(z)\right|^{2}\right)^{k}\leqslant\frac{CN^{k}}{M^{2k}}\max_{j}{\mathbb{E}}\left|d_{j}(z)\right|^{2k}=\mathrm{O}(M^{-k}).

This, together with the Borel-Cantelli lemma, implies the almost sure convergence.

Step 2. Mean convergence.

For simplicity of notation, we suppress the expression
z
when it serves as an independent variable of some functions.
Recall the quantities
V
,
b_{N}
,
\underline{m}_{B}
, and
\underline{m}_{b}
defined at the beginning of our proof.
We write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}m_{B}-m_{b}

\displaystyle=\left[{\mathbb{E}}m_{B}+\frac{1}{M}{\rm tr}V^{-1}\right]-\left[\frac{1}{M}{\rm tr}V^{-1}+m_{b}\right]:=S_{N}-T_{N}

\displaystyle=\phi^{-1}\left[{\mathbb{E}}\underline{m}_{B}+\frac{b_{N}+\theta(b_{N}^{-1}-1)}{z}\right]-\phi^{-1}\left[\frac{b_{N}+\theta(b_{N}^{-1}-1)}{z}+\underline{m}_{b}\right]:=\underline{S}_{N}-\underline{T}_{N}.

We first show that
S_{N}
and
\underline{S}_{N}
converge to zero. Using the identities

{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D^{-1}={\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}\beta_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{j}=b_{j}-b_{j}\beta_{j}\gamma_{j},

we have

\displaystyle S_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}(D^{-1}+V^{-1})=\frac{1}{M}{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}\left[V^{-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}-\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}b_{N}\Sigma\right)D^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}\beta_{j}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}V^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}-\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}b_{N}\frac{1}{M}{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}\Sigma D^{-1}V^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}b_{j}\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{1}{M}{\rm tr}D_{j}^{-1}V^{-1}\Sigma-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}b_{j}\beta_{j}\gamma_{j}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}V^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}

\displaystyle\quad-\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}b_{N}\frac{1}{M}{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}\Sigma D^{-1}V^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\frac{b_{j}}{M}{\rm tr}D_{j}^{-1}V^{-1}\Sigma-\frac{b_{N}}{M}{\rm tr}\Sigma D^{-1}V^{-1}\right)-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}b_{j}\beta_{j}\gamma_{j}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}V^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}.

Since
\max\left\{|\beta_{j}|,|b_{j}|,|b_{N}|\right\}\leqslant|z|/\eta
and, for any non-random matrix
A
,

\frac{1}{M}\left|{\rm tr}D^{-1}A\right|\leqslant\frac{\|A\|}{v},\quad\left|{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}D_{j}^{-1}A-{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}D^{-1}A\right|\leqslant\frac{\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}\|A\|}{v},

we get

\displaystyle\left|S_{N}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\gamma_{j}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}V^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\right|+\mathrm{o}(1)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|\gamma_{j}\right|^{2}{\mathbb{E}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}V^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\right|^{2}+\mathrm{o}(1).

From Lemma
3.2
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}|\gamma_{j}|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\left|{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}(z){\mathbf{y}}_{j}-\phi^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{M}{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}\Sigma D_{j}^{-1}(z)\right|^{2}\leqslant\frac{C}{N}

and

{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{*}D_{j}^{-1}V^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}|^{2}\leqslant C\phi
.
We thus obtain
S_{N}\to 0
.
For the term
\underline{S}_{n}
, using the identities

M+z{\rm tr}D^{-1}={\rm tr}BD^{-1}=N-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\beta_{j}-\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\theta{\rm tr}\Sigma D^{-1},

we get

z\underline{m}_{B}=-N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\beta_{j}-\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\theta N^{-1}{\rm tr}\Sigma D^{-1}

and thus

\displaystyle\left|\underline{S}_{N}\right|=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}(\beta_{j}-b_{N})}{z\phi}\right|\leqslant C\phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{M}{\mathbb{E}}\left|{\rm tr}\Sigma D_{j}^{-1}-{\mathbb{E}}{\rm tr}\Sigma D^{-1}\right|+\mathrm{o}(1)\to 0.

We next show that

.
Let

. The system (

) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\displaystyle 1+zm_{b}=\phi^{-1}[1-b-\theta(b^{-1}-1)],\\
&\displaystyle m_{b}=\int\frac{1}{x\phi^{-1/2}\big[b-\theta\big]-z}\pi(\mathrm{d}x).\end{cases}

Then, we get

\displaystyle T_{N}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\pi(\mathrm{d}x)}{z-\phi^{-1/2}(b_{N}-\theta)x}-\int\frac{\pi(\mathrm{d}x)}{z-x\phi^{-1/2}(b-\theta)}

\displaystyle=(b_{N}-b)\int\frac{\phi^{-1/2}x\pi(\mathrm{d}x)}{\{z-\phi^{-1/2}(b_{N}-\theta)x\}\{z-x\phi^{-1/2}(b-\theta)\}},

(2.7)

\displaystyle\underline{T}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{N}+\theta(b_{N}^{-1}-1)}{\phi z}+\frac{\underline{m}_{b}}{\phi}=\frac{b_{N}-b+\theta(b_{N}^{-1}-b^{-1})}{\phi z}.

(2.8)

With the fact that

\displaystyle\underline{T}_{N}-T_{N}=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\pi(\mathrm{d}x)}{z-\phi^{-1/2}(b_{N}-\theta)x}-\frac{b_{N}-1+\theta(b_{N}^{-1}-1)}{\phi z}-\frac{1}{z}

\displaystyle-\left\{\int\frac{\pi(\mathrm{d}x)}{z-x\phi^{-1/2}(b-\theta)}-\frac{b-1+\theta(b^{-1}-1)}{\phi z}-\frac{1}{z}\right\}=\mathrm{o}(1).

(2.9)

By the uniqueness of the solution to the system of equations in (

) and (

\max\{|T_{N}|,|\underline{T}_{N}|\}\to 0\quad\text{and thus}\quad{\mathbb{E}}m_{B}(z)-m_{b}(z)\to 0.

The proof of the lemma is complete.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose Assumption

The proof is similar to the one in

]
; we focus on the main difference here. Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}(\Xi)|G_{ij}|\leq\mathbf{1}(\Xi)|z||G_{ii}G_{jj}^{(i)}||\mathbf{y}_{i}^{*}G^{(ij)}\mathbf{y}_{j}|\prec\mathbf{1}(\Xi)|z||G_{ii}G_{jj}^{(i)}|\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\|\Sigma\|\|\mathcal{G}^{(ij)}\|_{F}.

Lemma 4.2

(4.11)

where we used Lemmas

,

{\cal G}-(-z\phi^{-1/2}m_{W}\Sigma-zI)^{-1}=\sum_{i\in{\cal I}}\frac{(\phi^{-1/2}m_{W}\Sigma+I)^{-1}}{z(1+\mathbf{y}_{i}^{*}{\cal G}^{(i)}\mathbf{y}_{i})}(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}^{*}{\cal G}^{(i)}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\Sigma{\cal G}),

(4.12)

one may easily obtain that

\begin{split}&\mathbf{1}(\Xi)|\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\big(\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{G}\Sigma)-\operatorname{tr}(-z\phi^{-1/2}m_{W}\Sigma-zI)^{-1}\Sigma)\big)|\\

(4.13)

From statement

\mathbf{1}(\Xi)|\phi^{-1/2}m_{W}\sigma_{i}+1|\geq\mathbf{1}(\Xi)(|\phi^{-1/2}m_{1}\sigma_{i}+1|-|\phi^{-1/2}(m_{1}-m_{W})\sigma_{i}|)\geq\tau^{\prime}>0.

(4.15)

Now, we are ready to obtain our first result, the weak local law.

Lemma 4.4

(Weak local law)
.

Suppose Assumption
3.11
holds. Then we have
\Lambda(z)\prec(N\eta)^{-1/4}
uniformly for
z\in\mathbf{D}

Proof.

We first observe from Lemma
4.3
that

\begin{split}&\{\mathbf{1}(\Xi)+\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{1}{G_{ii}}-\frac{1}{m_{W}}\Big)\\
&=\{\mathbf{1}(\Xi)+\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in{\cal I}}\Big(-\frac{G_{ii}-m_{W}}{m_{W}^{2}}+\frac{(G_{ii}-m_{W})^{2}}{G_{ii}m_{W}^{2}}\Big)\\
&=\{\mathbf{1}(\Xi)+\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in{\cal I}}\frac{(G_{ii}-m_{W})^{2}}{G_{ii}m_{W}^{2}}\\
&\prec\Psi^{2}.\end{split}

(4.17)

It then follows from Lemmas
3.3
,
4.1
and
4.2
that

\begin{split}&\{\mathbf{1}(\Xi)+\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\}\frac{1}{m_{W}}=\{\mathbf{1}(\Xi)+\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{1}{G_{ii}}+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}_{\prec}(\Psi^{2})\\
&=\{\mathbf{1}(\Xi)+\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\}\Big(-z-\frac{z}{\sqrt{MN}}\operatorname{tr}\big((-z\phi^{-1/2}m_{W}\Sigma-zI)^{-1}\Sigma\big)\\
&+\frac{z}{\sqrt{MN}}\operatorname{tr}\big((-z\phi^{-1/2}m_{W}\Sigma-zI)^{-1}\Sigma\big)-\frac{z}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{G}^{(i)}\Sigma)\Big)+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}_{\prec}(\Psi^{2})\\
&=\{\mathbf{1}(\Xi)+\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\}\Big(-z-\frac{z}{\sqrt{MN}}\operatorname{tr}\big((-z\phi^{-1/2}m_{W}\Sigma-zI)^{-1}\Sigma\big)\Big)+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}_{\prec}(\Psi).\end{split}

(4.18)

Since

\operatorname{tr}\big((-z\phi^{-1/2}m_{W}\Sigma-zI)^{-1}\Sigma\big)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{-z\phi^{-1/2}m_{W}\sigma_{i}-z},

we have

\begin{split}&\{\mathbf{1}(\Xi)+\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\}\Big(\frac{1}{m_{W}}+z-\frac{\phi^{1/2}}{M}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{1+\phi^{-1/2}m_{W}\sigma_{i}}\Big)\prec\Psi\\
&\Rightarrow\{\mathbf{1}(\Xi)+\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\}\{f(\phi^{-1/2}m_{W})-\phi^{1/2}z\}\prec\phi^{1/2}\Psi.\end{split}

(4.19)

The following proposition gives the stability of
f(m)
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose Assumption
3.11
holds. Suppose a
z
-dependent function
\delta
satisfying
N^{-1}\leq\delta(z)\leq\log^{-1}N
for

is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant

. Suppose moreover that for each fixed

|\mu_{0}-m_{1}(z)|\leq\frac{C\phi^{-1/2}\delta}{\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}+\sqrt{\phi^{-1/2}\delta}}.

Applying the above proposition, we have

\{\mathbf{1}(\Xi)+\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\}|m_{W}(z)-m_{1}(z)|\prec\frac{\Psi}{\sqrt{\kappa+\eta}+\sqrt{\Psi}}\prec\Psi^{1/2}.

(4.20)

Therefore, it follows from Lemmas
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3

\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\Lambda(z)\leq\mathbf{1}(\eta\geq 1)\Big(\max_{i}|G_{ii}-m_{W}|+|m_{W}-m_{1}|+\tilde{\Lambda}\Big)\prec N^{-1/2}.

(4.21)

The rest of the proof follows from a standard bootstrapping step, which can be found in
[
33
]
; we omit further details here.

Proof.

6.1
Estimation of spiked eigenvalues

The spiked covariance model, originally introduced by
[
21
]
, assumes that the spectrum of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
forms several separate groups, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\rm Spec}(\Sigma)=\bigg(\underbrace{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{1}}_{q_{1}},\ldots,\underbrace{\alpha_{L},\ldots,\alpha_{L}}_{q_{L}},\ \underbrace{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{M-\mathcal{L}}}_{M-\mathcal{L}}\bigg),\quad\mathcal{L}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{\ell}.

(6.1)

In this spectrum, the eigenvalues
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>\cdots>\alpha_{L}
are referred to as
spiked eigenvalues
with multiplicities
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{L}\}
, and the remaining ones
\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{M-\mathcal{L}}\}
are called
bulk eigenvalues
. To simplify the notation, we assume that the spikes are larger than the bulk eigenvalues, which can be easily extended to general situations.

Estimating the spiked eigenvalues
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{L}\}
is one of the central inferential tasks for this model, which requires a spectrum separation condition described below.

Assumption (A4).
For the spiked covariance model (
6.1
), we assume that

\liminf_{N}\min_{1\leqslant s\neq t\leqslant L}|\alpha_{s}-\alpha_{t}|>0\quad\text{and}\quad\liminf_{N}\min_{1\leqslant\ell\leqslant L}\psi_{\phi}^{\prime}(\alpha_{\ell})>0,

where

\psi_{\phi}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\phi}}x+\sqrt{\phi}x\int\frac{t}{x-t}d\pi_{L+1}(t),\ \pi_{L+1}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M-\mathcal{L}}\delta_{\beta_{i}}\text{ and }x\notin{\rm Supp}(\pi_{L+1}).

This assumption states that the spikes
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{L}\}
must be distinguishable and be distant from the bulk eigenvalues, which we refer to as
distance spikes
.
In this context, the
L
spikes of
\Sigma
give rise to
L
separate clusters of sample eigenvalues, forming a one-to-one correspondence, and they are also isolated from the bulk sample eigenvalues. See
[
6
]
for more details.

. We set the spectrum of

(400,400)
, and

\phi

, (b)

(40000,400)
.

In the work of
[
4
]
, the authors introduced a method to estimate the spikes
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{L}
, relying on the following identity:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\ell}=-\frac{\sqrt{\phi}}{m_{1}[\psi_{\phi}(\alpha_{\ell})]}\quad\text{for}~\ell=1,\ldots,L.

By substituting the empirical counterparts of
m_{1}(z)
and
\psi_{\phi}(\alpha_{\ell})
, the authors proposed a set of estimates
\{\hat{\alpha}_{B}(\lambda_{j}),\lambda_{j}\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\}
for
\alpha_{\ell}
, defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{B}(\lambda_{j})=-\sqrt{\phi}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\lambda_{k}\notin\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}-\lambda}_{j}\right]^{-1},\quad\lambda_{j}\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell},

where
\{\lambda_{j},j=1,\ldots,N\}
are the
N
eigenvalues of the matrix
W
, sorted in descending order,

\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
denotes the
\ell
th cluster of sample spiked eigenvalues, and
\lambda_{j}
can be any spikes within this cluster.
Nevertheless, there is currently no criterion for selecting
\lambda_{j}
’s. To address this concern, we observe that the random vector

\left\{\sqrt{N}(\hat{\alpha}_{B}(\lambda_{j})-\alpha):\lambda_{j}\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\right\}

will converge in distribution to the eigenvalues of a zero-mean Gaussian matrix under certain conditions, see
[
7
]
. This suggests that an individual estimate
\hat{\alpha}_{B}(\lambda_{j})
may be potentially biased, but the average of these estimates can avoid such an issue. We thus consider an averaged estimate of
\alpha_{\ell}
, i.e.,

\hat{\alpha}_{B,\ell}=\frac{1}{q_{\ell}}\sum_{\lambda_{j}\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}\hat{\alpha}_{B}(\lambda_{j}).

Using local laws, we can establish the convergence rate for the estimate
\hat{\alpha}_{B,\ell}
in general asymptotic regimes.

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions (A1)-(A2)-(A3)-(A4) hold. Then, we have

\displaystyle|\hat{\alpha}_{B,\ell}-\alpha_{\ell}|\prec N^{-\frac{1}{2}}

for
\ell=1,\ldots,L
.

There is an alternative estimate of
\alpha_{\ell}
introduced in
[
27
]
, which is formulated using contour intergation as

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{M,\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{-N\sqrt{\phi}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}~q_{\ell}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}\frac{z}{m_{W}(z)}dm_{W}(z)=\frac{N\sqrt{\phi}}{q_{\ell}}\sum_{\lambda_{\ell}\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}\left(\lambda_{\ell}-\mu_{\ell}\right),\quad\ell=1,\ldots,L.

(6.2)

In this formula,
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
is a simple contour, counterclockwise orientated, enclosing only the cluster
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
of sample spikes, and
\{\mu_{\ell}:\mu_{\ell}\in(\lambda_{\ell+1},\lambda_{\ell}),~m_{W}(\mu_{\ell})=0\}
is a set of poles contained inside the contour. One limitation of this estimation lies in the absence of a theoretical guarantee of consistency when
q_{\ell}=\mathrm{O}(1)
.

When the spike
\alpha_{\ell}
is simple, i.e.,
q_{\ell}=1
, Mestre’s estimation is closely related to Bai-Ding’s method. In particular, we have

\displaystyle m_{W}(\mu_{\ell})=\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\ell}-\mu_{\ell}}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\neq\ell}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}-\mu_{\ell}}=0

and
\lambda_{\ell}-\mu_{\ell}=o_{a.s.}(1)
, which give

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{M,\ell}=\hat{\alpha}_{B,\ell}+\mathrm{O}_{a.s.}\left(\sqrt{\phi}(\lambda_{\ell}-\mu_{\ell})\right).

Therefore, the two estimates have the same convergence rate from Theorem
6.1
.

6.2
Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Let
\Sigma^{0}
denote a new covariance matrix obtained by removing all the spiked eigenvalues from
\Sigma
. We define
W=X^{*}\Sigma X
and
W^{0}=X^{*}\Sigma^{0}X
, along with their ESDs denoted by
\rho_{W}
and
\rho_{W^{0}}
, and their Stieltjes transforms denoted by
m_{W}
and
m_{W^{0}}
.

We prove the theorem by showing two lemmas.
The first lemma gives the eigenvalue sticking in the bulk eigenvalues of
W
and
W^{0}
. The second one shows the exact location for the spiked eigenvalues of
W
.

Lemma 6.2
.

For the spiked covariance model
\Sigma
in (
6.1
) and
\Sigma^{0}
described above. Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is a finite number. Then

|m_{W}(z)-m_{W^{0}}(z)|=\mathrm{O}(\frac{1}{N\eta}).

(6.3)

Proof.

By the Cauchy interlacing property, we know that

\lambda_{\mathcal{L}+k}(W)\leq\lambda_{k}(W^{0})\leq\lambda_{k}(W),\quad k=1,\cdots,N-\mathcal{L}.

This implies that

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{R}}|\rho_{W}(x)-\rho_{W^{0}}(x)|\leq\frac{\mathcal{L}}{N}

and therefore,

|m_{W}(z)-m_{W^{0}}(z)|\leq\int\frac{1}{|x-z|}\mathrm{d}(\rho_{W}(x)-\rho_{W^{0}}(x))\leq\eta^{-1}N^{-1}\int\frac{\mathcal{L}\eta}{(x-E)^{2}+\eta^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{\pi\mathcal{L}}{N\eta}.

∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose that Assumption (A4) holds for the spiked covariance model in (
6.1
). Then, we have

|\lambda_{j}-\psi_{\phi}(\alpha_{\ell})|\prec K^{-1/2},\quad\forall\lambda_{j}\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell},

(6.4)

for
\ell=1,\dots,L
.

Proof.

The proof of this lemma is a perturbative one and similar to those in
[
14
, Theorem 2.3]
especially for the case
\phi\gtrsim 1
. In the sequel, we only investigate the case where
K=1
,
q_{1}=1
with
\phi\ll 1
. We give the estimation for the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1,1}
of
W
, while the other cases can be handled similarly. Denote the interval
I_{0}:=[\psi_{\phi}(\alpha_{1,1})-M^{-1/2+\epsilon},\psi_{\phi}(\alpha_{1,1})+M^{-1/2+\epsilon}]
. Then, it is easy to see that if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
W
lying in
I_{0}
, then
\lambda
can not be the eigenvalue of
W^{0}
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{det}(\lambda I-X^{*}(\Sigma^{0}+\alpha_{1,1}\mathbf{u}_{1}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{*})X)=0

(6.5)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 1+\alpha_{1,1}\mathbf{u}_{1}^{*}X(X^{*}\Sigma^{0}X-\lambda I)^{-1}X^{*}\mathbf{u}_{1}=0,

(6.6)

where
\mathbf{u}_{1}
is the corresponding eigenvector of
\Sigma
corresponding to
\alpha_{1,1}
. Since
\mathbf{u}_{1}
is orthogonal to the eigenspace of
\Sigma^{0}
, one has from large deviation bounds for the elements in
X
that

\mathbf{u}_{1}^{*}X(X^{*}\Sigma^{0}X-\lambda I)^{-1}X^{*}\mathbf{u}_{1}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\operatorname{tr}(X^{*}\Sigma^{0}X-\lambda I)^{-1}\prec\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{tr}G^{0}(\lambda)}{\eta}},

where
G^{0}(z):=(X^{*}\Sigma^{0}X-zI)
. On the other hand, we have from Theorem
3.12

|\frac{1}{\sqrt{MN}}\operatorname{tr}G^{0}(\lambda)-\phi^{-1/2}m_{1}^{0}(\lambda)|\prec\frac{\phi^{-1/2}}{K(\kappa+\eta)^{2}}.

Recall that for
\phi\gg 1
, one has
\operatorname{Im}m_{1}^{0}(z)\sim\eta
for
z
outside spectrum with
(\kappa+\eta)\sim 1
. On the other hand, one should notice that for
z
around locates the spiked eigenvalue (outside spectrum), we have

\operatorname{Im}m_{1}^{0}(z)=\operatorname{Im}\int\frac{1}{x-z}\rho^{0}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int\frac{\eta}{(x-E+\eta)^{2}}\rho^{0}(\mathrm{d}x)\sim\phi\eta,

where
\rho^{0}
is the limiting density of
\rho_{W^{0}}
and we also used the fact that
(\kappa+\eta)\sim\phi^{-1/2}
. It gives that for
\phi\ll 1
,

\displaystyle 1+\alpha_{1,1}\phi^{-1/2}m_{1}^{0}(\lambda)+\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(\frac{\phi^{1/2}}{\sqrt{M}})=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\frac{1}{m_{1}^{0}(\lambda)}+\phi^{-1/2}\alpha_{1,1}=\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}),

where we used the fact that
|m_{1}^{0}(\lambda)|\sim\phi^{1/2}
. Besides, recall that
m_{1}^{0}(\lambda)
satisfies

\frac{1}{m_{1}^{0}(\lambda)}=-\lambda+\phi^{1/2}\int\frac{x}{1+\phi^{-1/2}m_{1}^{0}(\lambda)x}\pi^{0}(\mathrm{d}x).

Consequently, we may observe that

\lambda=\phi^{-1/2}\alpha_{1,1}+\phi^{1/2}\alpha_{1,1}\int\frac{x}{\alpha_{1,1}-x}\pi^{0}(\mathrm{d}x)+\mathrm{O}_{\prec}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}).

(6.7)

It implies that
|\lambda-\psi_{\phi}(\alpha_{1,1})|\prec M^{-1/2}
. This completes the proof.∎
