On the Independence Numbers of the Cyclic Van der Waerden Hypergraphs * * * This work was supported by NSF DMS grant no. 1950563.


Abstract
Building upon the work of Berglund (2018), we establish a method for constructing subsets B ⊆ ℤ m ​ k B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk} such that B B does not contain any k k -term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod m ​ k mk , where m , k ∈ ℤ + m,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+} with k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . This construction thereby provides concrete lower bounds for the maximum size of such subsets. Additionally, it allows us to tightly bound specific chromatic numbers χ ⁡ ( m ​ k , k ) \chi(mk,k) of ℤ m ​ k \mathbb{Z}_{mk} and helps increase the lower bounds of certain cyclic Van der Waerden numbers W c ​ ( k , r ) W_{c}(k,r) , originally introduced by Burkert and Johnson (2011) as a way of bounding the standard Van der Waerden numbers W ⁡ ( k , r ) W(k,r) from below for r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 .

1.1
Notation and Terminology

A
hypergraph
is a pair
(V,E)=H
, where
V
is a nonempty set and
E
is a set of subsets of
V
. The elements

are called the
vertices
of the hypergraph, and the elements

are called the
hyperedges
(henceforth just
edges
) of the hypergraph.

A set

is

in

if and only if
S
contains no edges
e\in E
. Correspondingly, the
independence number
of
H
is the size of the largest independent set in
H
.

A
j-coloring
of a hypergraph

is a map
c:V\to\{1,2,\ldots,j\}
. An edge
e\in E
is
monochromatic
, with respect to coloring

, if there exists color
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,j\}
such that

for all

. That is, all vertices
v\in E
are assigned the same color. A
j
-coloring of

is said to be

if there exist no monochromatic edges of cardinality at least two. Correspondingly, the
chromatic number

\chi(H)
of

is defined to be the smallest number of colors necessary to define a proper coloring of
H
. Note that with a proper coloring of
H
, all vertices assigned the same color form an independent set. Hence, properly coloring the vertices in

is equivalent to partitioning
V
into independent sets.

1.2
Cyclic Van der Waerden Hypergraphs & Numbers

Similar to a coloring of a hypergraph, a
j
-coloring
of an arbitrary set
S
is a map
c:S\to\{1,\cdots,j\}
. A subset
T\subseteq S
is
monochromatic
, with respect to coloring
c
, if there exists
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,j\}
such that
c(t)=i
for all
t\in T
. That is, all elements
t\in T
are assigned the same color.

We say that an arbitrary set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}

contains
a
k
-term (cyclic) arithmetic progression
if there exist
k
elements in
S
that form a
k
-term progression. For reasons that will become clear momentarily, this can be rephrased as follows:
S

contains
a
k
-term (cyclic) arithmetic progression if there exists a
k
-element subset
T\subseteq S
such that the elements of
T
form a
k
-term progression.

For
k,r\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
k\geq 3
and
r\geq 2
, the
Van der Waerden Number

W(k,r)
is defined to be the smallest positive integer

such that, for any coloring of
\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
with
r
or fewer colors, there exists a monochromatic
k
-term arithmetic progression in
\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
. That is, for any coloring of
\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
with
r
or fewer colors, there exists a
k
-element monochromatic subset of
\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
whose elements form a
k
-term arithmetic progression.

Van der Waerden
[
4
]
showed that
W(k,r)
always exists, but finding
W(k,r)
for various integers
k
and
r
is difficult. As a strategy for handling this problem, an extension of
W(k,r)
called the
cyclic Van der Waerden Numbers

W_{c}(k,r)
was introduced by Burkert and Johnson
[
2
]
.
W_{c}(k,r)
is defined to be the smallest modulus

such that for all positive integers

, if
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
is
r
-colored, then there exists a monochromatic
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
M
in
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
. That is, if
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
is
r
-colored, then there exists a
k
-term monochromatic subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
whose elements form a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
M
.

Correspondingly, the
chromatic number

\chi(N,k)
of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, for
k,N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, is the minimum number of subsets necessary to partition
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
such that no subset contains any
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
N
. It is clear from the definitions if
\chi(N,k)=r
, then

.

As described by Johnson and Yang
[
3
]
, the
cyclic Van der Waerden hypergraph

H_{N,k}=(V_{N},E_{N,k})
has vertex set
V_{N}=\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}=\mathbb{Z}_{N}
and edge set

E_{N,k}=\{k\text{-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod }N\}
.

Using notation from
[
1
]
, let
b(N,k)
denote the maximum size of a subset
B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{N}
such that
B
contains no
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
N
. In
[
2
]
, finding values of

was considered to be of possible use bounding the numbers
W_{c}(k,r)
from below, since
b(N,k)
is the independence number of

.

1.3
Main Goal

Let
D(N,k)
be the set of possible values of
\gcd(d,k)
, where
d
ranges over all possible common differences of
k
-term arithmetic progressions mod
N
. Berglund
[
1
]
proved the following two results:

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
m>0
. Then,
b(mk,k)\leq mk-m
. If
D(mk,k)=\{1\}
, then
b(mk,k)=mk-m
.

Proposition 1.2
.

b(2k,k)=\begin{cases}2k-2&\text{if $D(2k,k)=\{1\}$}\\
2k-3&\text{if $D(2k,k)=\{1,2\}$}.\end{cases}

Remark
.

As we shall see with Lemma
2.3
,
D(2k,k)=\{1\}
simply means that
k
is odd while
D(2k,k)=\{1,2\}
means that
k
is even.

In this work, we extend Berglund’s methods, which resolved the case
m\leq 2
, to study
b(mk,k)
for arbitrary
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
; equivalently, we investigate
b(N,k)
when
N
is a multiple of
k
.

While Berglund’s results for
m\leq 2
yield exact formulas for
b(mk,k)
, the situation for
m>2
is more involved, and only tight upper and lower bounds are attainable. In this work, we aim to prove the following result for
m,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},k\geq 3
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose
D(mk,k)=\{d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{j}\}
for some
j\geq 0
, and let
d_{-1}:=0
. Then

mk-m\geq b(mk,k)\geq mk-\sum_{i=0}^{j}(d_{i}-d_{i-1})(m-d_{i}+1).

To prove Theorem
1.3
, we will construct specific subsets
B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
that contain no
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
mk
. Consequently, the sizes
|B|
provide lower bounds for
b(mk,k)
across various values of
m
and
k
.

In Section
2
, we demonstrate how to restrict the number of progressions that must be avoided in
B
, thereby reducing the complexity of bounding
b(mk,k)
. In Section
3
, we describe how to construct such sets
B
– more precisely, subsets
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
so that
B:=\mathbb{Z}_{mk}\setminus F
– and use these constructions to prove Theorem
1.3
. Then, in Section
4
, we use our bounds of
b(mk,k)
to obtain tight upper and lower bounds on the chromatic numbers
\chi(mk,k)
, which in turn yield new lower bounds for the cyclic Van der Waerden numbers
W_{c}(k,r)
for various
r\geq 2
. Finally, in Section
5
, we highlight possible directions for future work.

2
Common Differences to Consider

To bound
b(mk,k)
, it suffices to construct a set
B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
that contains no
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
mk
. To do so, we first establish a method for checking if some arbitrary set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
contains any such progressions.
Trivially, we can list out all such progressions and see if
S
contains any of them. However, this is time-consuming and inefficient, especially for large
m
and
k
. Rather, we can limit the amount of progressions to check for based on common differences.

2.1
Some General Lemmas

For
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}\setminus\{0\}
, where
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, let
\langle d\rangle
denote the subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
generated by
d
. The following is well-known, but we include a proof of it for the convenience of the reader:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
|\langle d\rangle|=\frac{N}{gcd(d,N)}
.

Proof.

Let
g:=\gcd(d,N)
. Then there exists
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\lambda d+\mu N=g
. So,
\lambda d\equiv g\mod N
. Hence,
g\in\langle d\rangle\implies\langle g\rangle\subseteq\langle d\rangle
. On the other hand,
g
divides
d
in
\mathbb{Z}
, so
\langle d\rangle\subseteq\langle g\rangle
in
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. Therefore,
\langle g\rangle=\langle d\rangle
. Since
g
divides
N
in
\mathbb{Z}
, we have
|\langle d\rangle|=|\langle g\rangle|=\frac{N}{g}=\frac{N}{\gcd(d,N)}
.
∎

We are now able to prove the following useful lemma. The forward direction was already proven in
[
1
]
but will be proven again here along with the backward direction:

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
k,N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
k\geq 3
, and there exists a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
N
with common difference
d
. Then
k\leq\frac{N}{gcd(N,d)}
. Conversely, if
k,N,d\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},d<\frac{N}{2}
, and
3\leq k\leq\frac{N}{gcd(N,d)}
, then there exists a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
N
with common difference
d
.

Proof.

Let
k,N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
k\geq 3
, and suppose

A=(t,t+d,\ldots,t+(k-1)d)
be a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
N
with common difference

. Then A is a translation by
t
, in
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, of
(0,d,\ldots,(k-1)d)
; and
(0,d,\ldots,(k-1)d)
is a
k
-element subset of
\langle d\rangle
. Hence,
|A|=k\leq|\langle d\rangle|=\frac{N}{\gcd(N,d)}
by Lemma
2.1
.

On the other hand, let
k,N,d\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
d<\frac{N}{2}
, and suppose
3\leq k\leq\frac{N}{\gcd(N,d)}
. Then
(0,d,\ldots,(k-1)d)
is a
k
-element subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
and also a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
N
with common difference
d
.
∎

Recall that
D(N,k)
is defined to be the set of possible values of
\gcd(d,k)
, where
d
ranges over all possible common differences of
k
-term arithmetic progressions mod
N
. In order to check if an arbitrary set

-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod

shows it suffices to check only for progressions with common differences

2.2
Specific Case of
N=mk

We are ready to focus our attention to the case of
N=mk
for
m,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
k\geq 3
. We have the following:

Lemma 2.3
.

D(mk,k)=\{g\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}:g\leq m,\ g\mid k\}

Proof.

First, consider a positive integer

such that
g\leq m
and
g\mid k
. Then

are all distinct mod
mk
and thus form a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
with common difference

. So,
\text{gcd}(g,k)=g\in D(mk,k)
.

On the other hand, suppose

is the common difference of some
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
labeled A. Let
\langle d\rangle
denote the cyclic subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
generated by
d
. Since
A
is a translate of
k
distinct elements of
\langle d\rangle
,
k\leq|\langle d\rangle|=\frac{mk}{\text{gcd}(mk,d)}
. Hence,
g:=\text{gcd}(k,d)\leq\text{gcd}(mk,d)\leq\frac{mk}{k}=m
. Thus,
g
, an arbitrary member of
D(mk,k)
, is a positive integer satisfying
g\leq m
and
g\mid k
.
∎

Remark
.

For integers
N,k
satisfying
3\leq k\leq N
, we note that
(0,1,\ldots,k-1)
is a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
N
. Hence, we always have

. This conclusion also follows from Lemma
2.3
, where

is a multiple of

. On the other hand, the five-term cyclic arithmetic progression
(2,4,6,8,10)
in
\mathbb{Z}_{12}
shows that when

is not a multiple of

, there may be elements of

which do not divide

.

Recall that the following was proven in
[
1
]
:

Proposition 2.4
.

b(mk,k)\leq mk-m
. If
D(mk,k)=\{1\}
, then
b(mk,k)=mk-m
.

Going forth, we focus on constructing sets
B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
such that
B
lacks any
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
mk
. For ease of notation, we set
F:=\mathbb{Z}_{mk}\setminus B
.

Using methods similar to those of Berglund
[
1
]
, we have the following result:

Lemma 2.5
.

If
|D(mk,k)|>1
, then
b(mk,k)<mk-m
.

Proof.

Suppose
|D(mk,k)|>1
and let
B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
such that
B
contains no
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
. We can suppose that
|B|=b(mk,k)
. Then, by Proposition
2.4
,
|B|=b(mk,k)\leq mk-k
.

Suppose towards contradiction that
b(mk,k)=mk-m
. Since any translate of
B
will also avoid all
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
mk
, without loss of generality, suppose that
0\not\in B
. Let
\mathbb{Z}_{mk}\setminus B=F=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\}
, where
0=f_{1}<f_{2}<\ldots<f_{m}\leq mk-1
. Note that, for
1\leq j\leq m-1
, we must have
f_{j+1}-f_{j}\leq k
, since otherwise
(f_{j}+1,f_{j}+2,\ldots,f_{j}+k)
would be a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
contained in
B
. However, if
f_{j+1}-f_{j}<k
for some
j
,
1\leq j\leq m-1
, then
f_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(f_{j+1}-f_{j})<k(m-1)=mk-k
, meaning
(f_{m}+1,\ldots,f_{m}+k)
would be a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
with common difference

contained in
B
. So, we must have
f_{j+1}-f_{j}=k
for
1\leq j\leq m-1
. Thus,
F=\{0,k,2k,\ldots,(m-1)k\}
.

Now, since
|D(mk,k)|>1
and
1\in D(mk,k)
, Lemma
2.3
says there exists
d\in D(mk,k)
such that
d>1
and
d|k
. We have that
A:=(1,1+d,1+2d,\ldots,1+(k-1)d)
is a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
. But no element of
A
equals
jk\mod mk
for any integer
0\leq j<m
. Hence,
F
does not contain any elements of
A
. So,
A
is a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
contained in
B
, a contradiction. Hence, we have
b(mk,k)<mk-k
. ∎

Suppose

,

, and

. Then

if and only of each divisor of

.

The conclusion follows from Lemmas

, and Proposition

.

3
Bounds for
b(mk,k)

In this section, we generalize Berglund’s findings to bound
b(mk,k)
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Fix
m,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
k\geq 3
. In order to prove Theorem
1.3
, we will construct
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
such that
B=\mathbb{Z}_{mk}\setminus F
lacks at least one element from every possible
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
. Thus,
|B|
will become a lower bound for
b(mk,k)
.

3.1
Important Lemmas

For
d\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, let
[x]_{d}:=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\equiv x\mod d\}
. The following lemmas are crucial in the proof of Theorem
1.3
:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d,N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
d\mid N
, and suppose
A
is a cyclic arithmetic progression mod
N
with common difference
d
. Then all residue classes of the elements of
A
lie in a single congruence class of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.

Proof.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{N}
be a cyclic arithmetic progression with common difference
d
, and let
a\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
be a representative of some element in
A
. Fix an arbitrary element
x\in A
. Then we can write
x\equiv a+jd\pmod{N}
for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence, there exists
t\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x=a+jd+tN
. Since
d
divides
N
, this yields
x\equiv a+jd+tN\equiv a\pmod{d}
. So,
x\in[a]_{d}
. Since
x\in A
was arbitrary, we see that
A\subseteq[a]_{d}
. That is, all residue classes of the elements of
A
lie in a single congruence class of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
d\in D(mk,k)
, and suppose
A
is a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
with common difference
d
. Let
\beta\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
such that
A\subseteq[\beta]_{d}
‡
‡

‡

The existence of such a
\beta
is guaranteed by Lemma
3.1
.
. Set
\alpha:=d-\beta
, and define

S:=\{k-\alpha,2k-\alpha,\ldots,mk-\alpha\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}.

Then
A
contains
d
consecutive elements of
S
; that is, there exists an index
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that

\{jk-\alpha,(j+1)k-\alpha,\ldots,(j+d-1)k-\alpha\}\pmod{mk}\subseteq A,

where the indices
j,j+1,\ldots,j+d-1
are taken cyclically modulo
m
.

Proof.

First, since
d\in D(mk,k)=\{g\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}:g\leq m,g\mid k\}
, we have that
d
divides
k
. So,
k\equiv 0\pmod{d}
. Hence, for every
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we have

jk-\alpha\equiv jk-(d-\beta)\equiv-d+\beta\equiv\beta\pmod{d}.

So,
S\subseteq[\beta]_{d}
. On a similar note, since
A\subseteq[\beta]_{d}
, we can write
A=(a,a+d,a+2d,\ldots,a+(k-1)d)\pmod{mk}
for some initial term
a\equiv\beta\pmod{d}
.

Now, let us show that an arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
lies in
S
if and only if
x\equiv-\alpha\pmod{k}
. First, suppose
x\in S
. Then
x\equiv jk-\alpha\pmod{mk}
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Reducing mod
k
yields
x\equiv-\alpha\pmod{k}
. Conversely, suppose
x\equiv-\alpha\pmod{k}
. Then there exists an integer
q
such that
x+\alpha=qk
. Take
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that
j\equiv q\pmod{m}
(if
q\equiv 0\pmod{m}
, then take
j=m
). Writing
q=j+lm
for some integer
l
, we have

x+\alpha=qk=(j+lm)k=jk+l(mk).

So,
x\equiv jk-\alpha\pmod{mk}
. Hence,
x\in S
. So, we see that
x\in S
if and only if
x\equiv-\alpha\pmod{k}
.

Next, consider the following equation:

dr\equiv-(\alpha+a)\pmod{k}.

(1)

Since
d
divides
k
, we can write
k=td
for some
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. So, we can rewrite (
1
) as

dr\equiv-(\alpha+a)\pmod{td}.

(2)

Since
a\equiv\beta\pmod{d}
and
\alpha=d-\beta
, we have that

\alpha+a\equiv\beta+(d-\beta)\equiv d\equiv 0\pmod{d}.

Hence, (
2
) is solvable in terms of
r
.

Now, suppose
r_{0}
is one such solution to (
2
). Then all solutions modulo
td
are
r_{0},r_{0}+t,r_{0}+2t,\ldots,r_{0}+(d-1)t
. Indeed, for each
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
,

d(r+jt)\equiv dr+j(dt)\equiv dr\equiv-(\alpha+a)\pmod{td}.

Moreover, suppose
r_{0}+lt\equiv r_{0}+l^{\prime}t\pmod{td}
for some integers
0\leq l,l^{\prime}\leq d-1
. Then

(l-l^{\prime})t\equiv 0\pmod{td}\implies l-l^{\prime}\equiv 0\pmod{d}.

Since
|l-l^{\prime}|<d
, it follows that
l-l^{\prime}=0
, i.e.
l=l^{\prime}
. Hence, the solutions
r_{0},r_{0}+t,r_{0}+2t,\ldots,r_{0}+(d-1)t
are distinct modulo
td
. That is, there are
d
distinct solutions to (
2
) modulo
td
.

Finally, for such a solution
r_{0}
, consider the corresponding elements of
A
:

A^{\prime}:=\{a+dr_{0},a+d(r_{0}+t),a+d(r_{0}+2t),\ldots,a+d(r_{0}+(d-1)t)\}\pmod{mk}.

By construction, the first element of
A^{\prime}
satisfies

a+dr_{0}\equiv a-(\alpha+a)\equiv-\alpha\pmod{k}.

So,
a+dr_{0}\in S
. Hence, we can write
a+dr_{0}\equiv jk-\alpha\pmod{mk}
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Each subsequent element in
A^{\prime}
differs from the previous one by
dt=k
. So, reducing
A^{\prime}
modulo
mk
yields

jk-\alpha,(j+1)k-\alpha,\ldots,(j+d-1)k-\alpha\pmod{mk},

with indices taken cyclically modulo
m
. Hence, there exists an index
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that

\{jk-\alpha,(j+1)k-\alpha,\ldots,(j+d-1)k-\alpha\}\pmod{mk}\subseteq A,

where the indices
j,j+1,\ldots,j+d-1
are taken cyclically modulo
m
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
d,N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
d\leq N
. For each
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, define
X_{i}:=\{i,i+1,\ldots,i+d-1\}\pmod{N}
. Let
C=\{d-1,d,d+1,\ldots,N-1\}
. Then
X_{i}\cap C\neq\emptyset
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
.

Proof.

Suppose towards contradiction there exists
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
such that
X_{i}\cap C=\emptyset
. Then
X_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{N}\setminus C
. Hence,
|X_{i}|\leq|\mathbb{Z}_{N}\setminus C|
. But
|X_{i}|=|\{i,i+1,\ldots,i+d-1\}|=d
and
|\mathbb{Z}_{N}\setminus C|=|\{0,1,2,\ldots,d-2\}|=d-1
. So,
d\leq d-1
, a contradiction. Hence,
X_{i}\cap C\neq\emptyset
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
.
∎

3.2
Construction of
F

Let us now construct
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
as follows:

Construction 3.4
.

Let
D(mk,k)=\{d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{j}\}
,
d_{0}<d_{1}<\cdots<d_{j}
, for some
j\geq 0
, and take
d_{-1}:=0
. For each
i\in\{0,\ldots,j\}
, define

F_{i}:=\bigcup_{\alpha=d_{i-1}+1}^{d_{i}}\{d_{i}k-\alpha,(d_{i}+1)k-\alpha,\ldots,mk-\alpha\}.

Then take
F:=\bigcup_{i=0}^{j}F_{i}
.

Example
.

Consider
D(3k,k)=\{1,3\}
(and suppose
k=15
). We have

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{1}\{k-\alpha,2k-\alpha,3k-\alpha\}=\{k-1,2k-1,3k-1\}

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\alpha=2}^{3}\{3k-\alpha\}=\{3k-2,3k-3\}.

So,

\displaystyle F=F_{0}\cup F_{1}=\{k-1,2k-1,3k-3,3k-2,3k-1\}.

Plugging in
k=15
yields
F=\{14,29,42,43,44\}
.

Example
.

Consider
D(10k,k)=\{1,3,9\}
(and suppose
k=9
). We have

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{1}\{k-\alpha,2k-\alpha,\ldots,10k-\alpha\}=\{k-1,2k-1,\ldots,10k-1\}

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\alpha=2}^{3}\{3k-\alpha,4k-\alpha,\ldots,10k-\alpha\}=\{3k-2,\ldots,10k-2\}\cup\{3k-3,\ldots,10k-3\}

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\alpha=4}^{9}\{9k-\alpha,10k-\alpha\}=\{9k-4,10k-4\}\cup\cdots\cup\{9k-9,10k-9\}.

So,
F=F_{0}\cup F_{1}\cup F_{2}
. Plugging in
k=9
yields
F=\{8,17,24,25,26,33,34,35,42,43,44,51,52,

53,60,61,62,69,70,71,72,73,74,75,76,77,78,79,80,81,82,83,84,85,86,87,88,89\}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Using the same notation as Construction
3.4
, we have that
F_{i_{1}}\cap F_{i_{2}}=\emptyset
for all
i_{1},i_{2}\in\{0,1,\ldots,j\}
such that
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
.

Proof.

Fix
i_{1},i_{2}\in\{0,1,\ldots,j\}
, and without loss of generality, assume
i_{1}<i_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle F_{i_{1}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\alpha=d_{i_{1}-1}+1}^{d_{i_{1}}}\{d_{i_{1}}k-\alpha,(d_{i_{1}}+1)k-\alpha,\ldots,mk-\alpha\}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle F_{i_{2}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\alpha=d_{i_{2}-1}+1}^{d_{i_{2}}}\{d_{i_{2}}k-\alpha,(d_{i_{2}}+1)k-\alpha,\ldots,mk-\alpha\},

where
d_{i_{1}-1},d_{i_{1}},d_{i_{2}-1},d_{i_{2}}\in D(mk,k)
.

Let
x_{1}\in F_{i_{1}}
and
x_{2}\in F_{i_{2}}
. Then there exists
\alpha_{1}\in\{d_{{i_{1}}-1}+1,\ldots,d_{i_{1}}\}
,
\alpha_{2}\in\{d_{{i_{2}}-1}+1,\ldots,d_{i_{2}}\}
,
t_{1}\in\{0,\ldots,m-d_{i_{1}}\}
, and
t_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,m-d_{i_{2}}\}
such that

x=(d_{i_{1}}+t_{1})k-\alpha_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad y=(d_{i_{2}}+t_{2})k-\alpha_{2}.

Suppose towards contradiction that
x_{1}=x_{2}
. Then

(d_{i_{1}}+t_{1})k-\alpha_{1}=(d_{i_{2}}+t_{2})k-\alpha_{2},

implying that

\Big((d_{i_{2}}+t_{2})-(d_{i_{1}}+t_{1})\Big)k=\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}.

(3)

Now, the left-hand side of (
3
) is divisible by
k
. On the other hand, since
i_{1}<i_{2}
, we have that
\alpha_{1}\leq d_{i_{1}}<d_{i_{2}-1}+1\leq\alpha_{2}
, so that
\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}\geq(d_{{i_{2}}-1}+1)-d_{i_{1}}\geq 1
. Similarly, we have that
\alpha_{2}\leq d_{i_{2}}
and
\alpha_{1}\geq d_{i_{1}-1}+1
, so that
\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}\leq d_{i_{2}}-(d_{i_{1}-1}+1)=d_{i_{2}}-d_{i_{1}-1}-1\leq k-1
, since
d_{i_{1}},d_{i_{2}}\in D(mk,k)\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
. Hence,

1\leq\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}\leq k-1,

so the right-hand side of (
3
) is not divisible by
k
, a contradiction. Hence,
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
. Since
x_{1}\in F_{i_{1}}
and
x_{2}\in F_{i_{2}}
were arbitrary, we see that
F_{i_{1}}\cap F_{i_{2}}=\emptyset
.
∎

Now, we have the following:

Proposition 3.6
.

Construct
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
via Construction
3.4
. Using the same notation as above, we have

\displaystyle|F|=\sum_{i=0}^{j}(d_{i}-d_{i-1})(m-d_{i}+1).

(4)

Proof.

Fix
d_{i}\in D(mk,k)
for some
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,j\}
. Clearly,

\{d_{i}k-\alpha_{1},(d_{i}+1)k-\alpha_{1},\ldots,mk-\alpha_{1}\}\cap\{d_{i}k-\alpha_{2},(d_{i}+1)k-\alpha_{2},\ldots,mk-\alpha_{2}\}=\emptyset

for any
d_{i-1}<\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\leq d_{i}
such that
\alpha_{1}\neq\alpha_{2}
. So,

\displaystyle|F_{i}|

\displaystyle=\Big|\bigcup_{\alpha=d_{i-1}+1}^{d_{i}}\{d_{i}k-\alpha,(d_{i}+1)k-\alpha,\ldots,mk-\alpha\}\Big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=d_{i-1}+1}^{d_{i}}|\{d_{i}k-\alpha,(d_{i}+1)k-\alpha,\ldots,mk-\alpha\}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=d_{i-1}+1}^{d_{i}}(m-d_{i}+1)=(d_{i}-d_{i-1})(m-d_{i}+1).

Finally,
F_{i_{1}}\cap F_{i_{2}}=\emptyset
for all
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
by Lemma
3.5
. Hence,
|F|=\sum_{i=0}^{j}(d_{i}-d_{i-1})(m-d_{i}+1)
.
∎

3.3
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We are now able to prove Theorem
1.3
. For ease of notation, let
[x,y]:=\{x,x+1,\ldots,y\}
for integers
x,y
such that
x<y
, and let
[x,y]\pmod{N}:=\{x,x+1,\ldots,y\}\pmod{N}
for integers
x,y
and
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

First, Proposition
2.4
yields
mk-m\geq b(mk,k)
. On the other hand, let
D(mk,k)=\{d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{j}\}
,
d_{0}<d_{1}<\cdots<d_{j}
, for some
j\geq 0
, and let
d_{-1}:=0
. We will show that if we construct
F
via Construction
3.4
, then
B:=\mathbb{Z}_{mk}\setminus F
will not contain any
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
mk
. Then, the definition of
b(mk,k)
and Proposition
3.6
will yield

b(mk,k)\geq|B|=|\mathbb{Z}_{mk}\setminus F|=mk-\sum_{i=0}^{j}(d_{i}-d_{i-1})(m-d_{i}+1).

Fix
i\in[0,j]
, and suppose
A
is a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
with common difference
d_{i}
. Modulus
d_{i}
admits exactly
d_{i}
congruence classes, namely
[0]_{d_{i}},[1]_{d_{i}},\ldots,[d_{i}-1]_{d_{i}}
. By Lemma
3.1
, there exists a unique
\beta\in[0,d_{i}-1]
such that
A\subseteq[\beta]_{d_{i}}
. Consider this
\beta
.

By Lemma
3.2
,
A
contains exactly
d_{i}
consecutive elements of
S:=\{k-\alpha_{\beta},2k-\alpha_{\beta},\ldots,mk-\alpha_{\beta}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
, where
\alpha_{\beta}:=d_{i}-\beta
. Let
S_{A}\subseteq S
denote this consecutive subset. That is, there exists an index
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that

S_{A}:=\{jk-\alpha_{\beta},(j+1)k-\alpha_{\beta},\ldots,(j+d_{i}-1)k-\alpha_{\beta}\}\pmod{mk}.

Now, there is a clear bijection between
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and
S
, namely

\Phi:\mathbb{Z}_{m}\to S,\quad\Phi(j)=(j+1)k-\alpha_{\beta}.

So,
S_{A}=\Phi([j-1,j+d_{i}-2]\pmod{m})
. Put in another way,
S_{A}=\Phi([l,l+d_{i}-1]\pmod{m})
for
l=j-1
.

Let
C:=\Phi([d_{i}-1,m-1])=\{d_{i}k-\alpha_{\beta},(d_{i}+1)k-\alpha_{\beta},\ldots,mk-\alpha_{\beta}\}
. Then Lemma
3.3
yields that
C\cap S_{A}\neq\emptyset
. That is,
C
contains at least one term of
A
(specifically,
C
contains at least one element of
A
no matter what
j\in[1,m]
is). So, if
C\subseteq F
, then
B
cannot contain all elements of
A
and hence does not contain
A
.

Now,
\alpha_{\beta}=d_{i}-\beta\in[1,d_{i}]
, since
\beta\in[0,d_{i}-1]
. Since
[1,d_{i}]=\bigcup_{q=0}^{j}[d_{q-1}+1,d_{q}]
, there exists a unique
q\in[0,i]
such that
\alpha_{\beta}\in[d_{q-1}+1,d_{q}]
. And since
d_{0}<d_{1}<\cdots<d_{j}
, we have that
d_{q}\leq d_{i}
. So,

\displaystyle C\subseteq\bigcup_{\alpha=d_{q-1}+1}^{d_{q}}\{d_{q}k-\alpha,(d_{q}+1)k-\alpha,\ldots,mk-\alpha\}=F_{q}\subseteq F.

Hence,
B
does not contain all elements of
A
, so
B
does not contain
A
. Since
\beta\in[0,d_{i}-1]
was arbitrary, a similar argument applied to any
\beta\in[0,d_{i}-1]
shows that
B
does not contain any progressions contained in any congruence class of
d_{i}
. So,
B
does not contain any
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
mk
with common difference
d_{i}
. Finally, since
i\in[0,j]
was arbitrary, a similar argument applied to any
i\in[0,j]
shows that
B
does not contain any
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
mk
with any common difference in
D(mk,k)
. So, by Lemma
2.2
, we have that
B
does not contain any
k
-term arithmetic progressions mod
mk
. Hence,
b(mk,k)\geq|B|
and our theorem is proven.
∎

4
Implications of our Bounds

Recall from Section
1.2
that
b(mk,k)
is the independence number of the cyclic Van der Waerden hypergraph
H_{mk,k}
, where
m,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
k\geq 3
.

In this section, we use our constructions of
B,F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
to tightly bound chromatic number
\chi(mk,k)
, thereby providing a lower bound of the cyclic Van der Waerden number
W_{c}(k,r)
for various
r\geq 2
.

4.1
Chromatic Number
\chi(mk,k)

For integers
m,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
k\geq 3
, recall that the
chromatic number

\chi(mk,k)
of
\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
is the minimum number of subsets necessary to partition
\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
such that no subset contains any
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
mk
. In this section, we tightly bound
\chi(mk,k)
in various cases based on the relationship between
m
and
k
.

First, for
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, let us define the
cyclic difference
between two elements
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
,
\alpha<\beta
, to be
(\alpha-\beta)\pmod{N}
. For example, the cyclic difference between
2
and
7
in
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
is
3
.

Also, for an arbitrary progression
(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{j})\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, let
\sigma(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{j})=(a_{\sigma(0)},a_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,a_{\sigma(j)})
be the permutation such that
a_{\sigma(0)}<a_{\sigma(1)}<\cdots<a_{\sigma(j)}
, with the order
<
being taken in
\mathbb{Z}
. We say that the progression
(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{j})

cycles
if
\sigma
is not the identity permutation, i.e. there is some
i
such that
\sigma(i)\neq i
.

Example
.

In
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
,
\sigma(1,3,5)=(1,3,5)
and
\sigma(5,7,1)=(1,5,7)
. So,
(1,3,5)
does not cycle while
(5,7,1)
does.

We are now ready for our first proposition:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. If
k>m
, then
\chi(mk,k)=2
.

Proof.

Suppose
k>m
, and construct
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
via Construction
3.4
. Let
B:=\mathbb{Z}_{mk}\setminus F
. From the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we know that
B
does not contain any
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
mk
. Our goal is to show that
F
does not either. If
k>|F|
, then we are clearly done, so we may assume that
k\leq F
.

First, let us consider possible progressions that do not cycle. We start with

F_{0}:=(k-1,2k-1,\ldots,mk-1)\subseteq F
, since
d_{0}:=1\in D(mk,k)
always holds.
F_{0}
is an
m
-term arithmetic progression mod
mk
, but since
m<k
, this is not a progression we must worry about. Now, suppose
D(mk,k)=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
. Then

\displaystyle(\min(F_{0}),\min(F_{1}),\ldots,\min(F_{m-1}))

\displaystyle=(k-1,2k-2,\ldots,mk-m)\qquad\ \ \ \text{and}

\displaystyle(\max(F_{0}),\max(F_{1}),\ldots,\max(F_{m-1}))

\displaystyle=(mk-1,mk-2,\ldots,mk-m)

are two
m
-term arithmetic progressions mod
mk
contained in
F
. But again, since
m<k
, we need not worry about these progressions. And finally, any other progressions that do not cycle in
F
are shorter than these three by construction (no matter what
D(mk,k)
actually is), so can conclude that
F
does not contain any such progressions that do not cycle.

Now, let us consider possible progressions that do cycle. Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
tell us that we must consider any such progression with common difference at most
m
, by definition of
D(mk,k)
. Now,
\min(F)=k-1
and
\max(F)=mk-1
, since
1\in D(mk,k)
always holds. The cyclic difference between these two elements is
(k-1)-(mk-1)\pmod{mk}=k
. So, any
k
-term progression that does cycle has common difference at least
k
. But since
m<k
, we need not worry about such progression either. Hence,
F
also does not contain any
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progressions mod
mk
that do cycle.

And so, we can partition
\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
into subsets
B
and
F
such that neither subset contains any
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
. So,
\chi(mk,k)=2
.
∎

As seen in the above proof, Construction
3.4
yields
F_{0}:=(k-1,2k-1,\ldots,mk-1)\subseteq F
, which is a
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progression mod
mk
with common difference
k
. So, if
k\leq m
, then
k\in D(mk,k)
and
F
contains such a
k
-term progression that we want to avoid. So, we cannot simply partition
\mathbb{Z}_{mk}
into subsets
B
and
F
like the case where
k>m
. Instead, we must adjust our construction slightly.

Let us first consider the case where
m=k
. Below is an important lemma which we will use to bound
\chi(mk,k)=\chi(k^{2},k)
:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
m=k\geq 3
, and construct
F
via Construction
3.4
. Arrange the elements of
F
in increasing order (viewed in
\mathbb{Z}
). Partition
F
into two subsets
F^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime\prime}
as follows: place the
\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor
smallest elements of
F
into
F^{\prime}
, the next
\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor
elements into
F^{\prime\prime}
, and then continue alternating in this way until
F=F^{\prime}\cup F^{\prime\prime}
. Then, neither
F^{\prime}
nor
F^{\prime\prime}
contain all elements of
F_{0}:=\{k-1,2k-1,\ldots,k^{2}-1\}
.

Remark
.

For convenience, let us refer to a group of consecutive elements in
F
as a
consecutive segment
, and let the
length
of the segment be the number of elements it contains. Hence, we are alternating consecutive segments of length (at most)
\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor
between
F^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof.

First, suppose
k
is prime. Then
D(k^{2},k)=\{1,k\}
. So, the
k
smallest elements of
F
are
k-1,2k-1,\ldots,(k-1)k-1
, and
k^{2}-k
. Note that
\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil k-1\leq k^{2}-k
, so
\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil k-1
is one of the
k
smallest elements of
F
. Hence, constructing
F^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime\prime}
as above yields
k-1\in F^{\prime}
and
\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil k-1\in F^{\prime\prime}
. So, neither
F^{\prime}
nor
F^{\prime\prime}
contain all elements of
F_{0}
.

Next, suppose
k
is not prime. Denote

Proposition 4.3
.

Let

Proof.

Clearly,

(with

4.2
Lower Bounds for Cyclic Van der Waerden Numbers

Using the bounds of

for various

k

In each of the following propositions, let

.

Proposition 4.6
.

W_{c}(k,2)>k(k-1)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
,
\chi(mk,k)=2
for all
m\leq k-1
. So,
W_{c}(k,2)>k(k-1)
.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

W_{c}(k,3)>k^{2}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.3
,
\chi(k^{2},k)\leq 3
. Since we can partition
\mathbb{Z}_{k^{2}}
into three disjoint subsets, none of which contain any
k
-term cyclic arithmetic progresions mod

, we have

.
∎

Proposition 4.8
.

For all

W_{c}(k,3+\lceil\frac{(m-k)\cdot k}{k-1}\rceil)>mk

The result follows from Proposition
