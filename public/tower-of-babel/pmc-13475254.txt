Changes in ocular blood flow and haemodynamic waveform parameters following trabeculectomy
Authors: Shian Mae, Kazunori Hirasawa, Tomohiko Yanagita, Nobuyuki Shoji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate postoperative changes in ocular blood flow velocity and waveform parameters measured by laser speckle flowgraphy (LSFG) following trabeculectomy (TLE) in patients with glaucoma.Methods and analysisThis retrospective study included 80 eyes of 80 patients with glaucoma who underwent TLE and underwent LSFG examinations before and at 1, 3 and 6 months after surgery. LSFG-derived blood flow velocity parameters (mean blur rate (MBR) in MBR of vascular area (MV) and MBR of tissue area (MT), area ratio of blood speed and vascular cloudiness) and waveform parameters (blowout time, blowout score (BOS), skew, acceleration time index, rising rate, falling rate, flow acceleration index (FAI), resistivity index (RI) and fluctuation) were longitudinally evaluated using linear mixed-effects models.ResultsIntraocular pressure (IOP) significantly decreased from 18.3 mm Hg preoperatively to 10.1, 10.0 and 9.9 mm Hg at 1, 3 and 6 months after TLE, respectively (all p<0.01). MV significantly decreased at all postoperative time points (all p<0.01), whereas MT showed a mild decrease only at 6 months (p=0.023). Among waveform parameters, BOS significantly increased postoperatively (all p<0.05), whereas FAI, RI and fluctuation significantly decreased at all postoperative time points (all p<0.01). IOP measured at each visit was independently associated with FAI and fluctuation, whereas age, mean deviation (MD) and axial length (AL) were associated with selected blood flow velocity and waveform parameters.ConclusionThese findings suggest that postoperative changes in ocular haemodynamics reflect the combined influence of postoperative IOP, patient age, MD and AL.Keywords: Glaucoma

These findings suggest that surgical intraocular pressure reduction may improve optic nerve head haemodynamic stability and reduce vascular pulsatility beyond changes in blood flow velocity alone.

Introduction

Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, and reduction of intraocular pressure (IOP) remains the only proven treatment to slow disease progression.1–4 However, the progression of normal-tension glaucoma despite statistically normal IOP levels suggests that factors other than IOP, particularly vascular factors, may play an important role in the pathogenesis of the disease.5 6

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed towards the relationship between ocular blood flow and glaucoma. Laser speckle flowgraphy (LSFG) enables non-invasive evaluation of blood flow in the retina and optic nerve head (ONH) using the mean blur rate (MBR) as a relative index of blood flow velocity.7 8 Previous studies have demonstrated that ONH blood flow is reduced in glaucoma and is associated with disease severity and visual field progression.9 10 In addition, vascular dysregulation, unstable ocular perfusion and impaired autoregulation have been proposed as important mechanisms contributing to glaucomatous optic neuropathy.7

Ocular blood flow is determined by the balance between ocular perfusion pressure and vascular resistance. Because ocular perfusion pressure is influenced by IOP, surgical IOP reduction may alter ocular haemodynamics. However, previous studies evaluating changes in ocular blood flow after trabeculectomy (TLE) have yielded inconsistent results, with some reporting increased blood flow and others showing no significant change.11–16 These discrepancies may partly reflect differences in glaucoma severity, baseline vascular status and vascular autoregulatory capacity among study populations.

Importantly, most previous studies focused primarily on changes in blood flow magnitude. However, recent LSFG studies have increasingly emphasised waveform parameters reflecting vascular resistance, pulsatility and haemodynamic stability.14 17–20 Previous studies have suggested that waveform analysis may provide additional information regarding vascular dysregulation and postoperative haemodynamic changes beyond static blood flow measurements.14 17–20

Therefore, the clinical question of whether TLE induces not only quantitative changes in blood flow velocity but also qualitative changes in ONH haemodynamics remains unresolved. In this study, we evaluated both MBR-derived blood flow velocity indices and waveform parameters using LSFG in patients with progressive glaucoma undergoing TLE, with the aim of clarifying postoperative haemodynamic changes in the ONH.

Methods

The data for this retrospective study were collected at Kitasato University.

The study included 80 eyes of 80 patients who underwent TLE at Kitasato University Hospital between March 2020 and June 2022. Baseline examinations, including refractive error, visual acuity, IOP, axial length (AL), LSFG measurements and Humphrey visual field 10–2 SITA-Standard testing, were performed preoperatively. Patients were included if LSFG measurements were obtained at baseline and at least one postoperative follow-up period (1, 3 or 6 months after TLE).

LSFG) measurement

Ocular blood flow was measured using LSFG (LSFG-NAVI; Softcare Co, Ltd, Fukuoka, Japan) and analysed using LSFG Analyzer software (V.2.4). In our glaucoma clinic, the pupil was dilated with tropicamide eye drops prior to LSFG measurement. After pupil dilation was confirmed, patients were seated for 5–10 min in a darkened room to stabilise blood flow before measurement. LSFG measurements were performed multiple times, and the most reliable dataset was selected for analysis. Although LSFG does not provide a quantitative image quality index as in Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) imaging, image quality is indicated by a colour bar; only measurements with a green bar were included in the analysis. Previous studies have demonstrated good repeatability and reproducibility of LSFG measurements under standardised measurement conditions.21 As LSFG parameters have been described in detail in previous studies,14 only a brief description is provided here.

Statistical analysis

Because repeated measurements were obtained from the same patients at multiple follow-up periods (before and at 1, 3 and 6 months after TLE), and not all patients had complete data, linear mixed-effects models were used to account for within-patient correlation and unbalanced longitudinal data.

For the analysis of longitudinal changes in LSFG parameters, follow-up period was included as a fixed effect, and patient was included as a random effect. Estimated marginal means were calculated, and pairwise comparisons between each postoperative follow-up period (1, 3 and 6 months) and baseline were performed with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons.

To further evaluate factors associated with longitudinal changes in LSFG parameters that demonstrated significant postoperative changes, additional linear mixed-effects models were constructed. In these models, follow-up period, surgical procedure (TLE+IOL vs TLE alone), IOP measured at each visit, baseline age, baseline mean deviation (MD) and baseline AL were included as fixed effects, and patient was included as a random effect. The significance of fixed effects was evaluated within the linear mixed-effects model framework and regression coefficients (β) were estimated to assess the direction and magnitude of associations.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD unless otherwise specified.

Discussion

In this study, TLE achieved substantial IOP reduction while BCVA and MD remained stable throughout the postoperative period. MV decreased significantly at all postoperative time points, whereas MT showed only a modest reduction at 6 months. In contrast, waveform parameters demonstrated consistent postoperative changes, including increased BOS and decreased FAI, RI and fluctuation. Furthermore, linear mixed-effects analyses demonstrated that IOP measured at each visit was independently associated with FAI and fluctuation, whereas age, MD and AL showed parameter-specific associations with several waveform and blood flow indices.

Previous studies evaluating ocular blood flow after TLE have reported inconsistent findings.11–16 Earlier studies primarily focused on quantitative blood flow measurements and investigated whether IOP reduction directly increased ONH circulation. Tamaki et al13 demonstrated that ONH blood flow measured by LSFG remained largely unchanged despite substantial postoperative IOP reduction and increased ocular perfusion pressure, suggesting the presence of autoregulation within the ONH microcirculation. Similarly, Cantor15 reported minimal changes in retrobulbar blood flow parameters after TLE despite marked IOP reduction, further supporting the concept that ocular blood flow may be maintained within a relatively stable autoregulatory range. In contrast, other studies reported increased ocular blood flow following TLE, particularly in eyes with markedly reduced postoperative IOP or impaired baseline circulation.12 16

These discrepancies may partly reflect differences in glaucoma severity and vascular autoregulatory capacity among study populations. In moderate glaucoma, autoregulation may remain relatively preserved, thereby maintaining ONH blood flow despite substantial changes in ocular perfusion pressure. In contrast, eyes with advanced glaucoma may exhibit impaired vascular autoregulation and abnormal vascular dynamics, resulting in different postoperative haemodynamic responses. In the present study, the relatively advanced disease severity may explain why waveform parameters showed more pronounced postoperative changes than MBR-derived velocity indices.

Importantly, the postoperative reduction in MBR-derived velocity indices should not necessarily be interpreted as impaired ocular perfusion. LSFG-derived MBR represents a relative index of blood flow velocity rather than an absolute measure of blood flow volume.8 Sugiyama et al8 emphasised that LSFG is most suitable for longitudinal within-eye evaluation rather than direct intersubject quantitative comparison because LSFG values are expressed in arbitrary units. Previous studies have demonstrated that ONH blood flow values are already reduced in glaucomatous eyes and further decrease with disease progression.9 10 22 In the present study, both preoperative MV and MT were lower than previously reported values in healthy subjects.23 24 Structural loss of neural and microvascular tissue within the ONH in advanced glaucoma may therefore contribute to lower baseline MBR values.

Recent LSFG studies have increasingly focused not only on quantitative blood flow measurements but also on qualitative waveform analyses reflecting vascular dynamics and haemodynamic stability.12 14 18 19 Shiga et al19 demonstrated that NTG eyes exhibit altered LSFG waveform characteristics, including delayed and flattened blood flow waveforms, which may reflect endothelial dysfunction, vascular dysregulation and impaired autoregulation. Similarly, Masai et al18 reported that TLE improved waveform parameters such as BOS and fluctuation despite minimal changes in MBR, suggesting stabilisation of ONH microcirculation rather than simple increases in blood flow volume. Takeshima et al14 also demonstrated postoperative improvement in waveform parameters with relatively preserved MBR values, further supporting the concept that waveform dynamics may reflect vascular stability more sensitively than static blood flow measurements.

In the present study, postoperative increases in BOS together with decreases in FAI, RI and fluctuation may therefore reflect reduced vascular pulsatility and stabilisation of ocular haemodynamics rather than circulatory deterioration. BOS is considered to reflect the stability of blood flow during the cardiac cycle and is inversely related to vascular resistance, whereas RI and fluctuation are associated with pulsatile vascular instability.14 18 19 In contrast, BOT did not show significant postoperative changes, consistent with previous studies evaluating LSFG waveform parameters after TLE.14 18 Unlike BOS, BOT represents the duration for which blood flow is maintained at a relatively high level during the cardiac cycle. These findings suggest that BOS and BOT may reflect different aspects of postoperative ocular haemodynamics. Gardiner et al17 20 recently proposed that abnormal pulsatile haemodynamics and increased vascular stiffness may contribute to glaucomatous progression and that waveform parameters may reflect chronic vascular stress on the ONH microcirculation. In this context, the postoperative decrease in FAI and fluctuation together with increased BOS observed in the present study may indicate attenuation of excessive pulsatile vascular stress and restoration of more stable ocular haemodynamics following TLE.

This interpretation is also consistent with the vascular theory of glaucoma proposed by Flammer et al,7 25 which suggests that vascular dysregulation and unstable ocular perfusion contribute to glaucomatous optic neuropathy. The Early Manifest Glaucoma Trial further demonstrated that low ocular perfusion pressure and systemic vascular factors are associated with glaucoma progression, supporting the importance of vascular mechanisms beyond IOP alone.5 Fan et al26 additionally proposed that unstable ocular blood flow and repeated mild reperfusion injury may play important roles in glaucoma progression. Taken together, these findings suggest that glaucomatous eyes may be exposed to chronic vascular stress and unstable pulsatile circulation before surgery and that surgical IOP reduction may alleviate mechanical and vascular stress on the ONH microcirculation, resulting in more stable blood flow dynamics after TLE.

An additional finding of the present study was that IOP measured at each visit was independently associated with FAI and fluctuation, whereas age, MD and AL showed significant associations with several waveform and blood flow indices. These findings suggest that postoperative ONH haemodynamics are influenced by postoperative IOP as well as patient age, glaucoma severity and AL. The association between IOP measured at each visit and FAI and fluctuation suggests that postoperative IOP may influence pulsatile ocular haemodynamics independently of other clinical factors. Age-related vascular stiffness may contribute to altered vascular compliance and pulsatility, whereas advanced visual field damage may reflect cumulative vascular and structural impairment within the ONH. In addition, axial elongation may influence ocular haemodynamics through mechanical stretching of ocular tissues and vascular attenuation. Previous studies have demonstrated that myopic elongation alters retinal nerve fibre trajectories and posterior pole vascular structures.27 28 Such structural alterations may increase vulnerability of the ONH microvasculature and contribute to interindividual differences in postoperative haemodynamic responses.

Kiyota et al10 demonstrated that impaired vascular reactivity assessed by oxygen inhalation testing predicted future glaucomatous visual field progression and suggested that dynamic vascular responsiveness may be more important than static blood flow measurements in glaucoma pathophysiology. These findings further support the hypothesis that postoperative waveform changes observed in the present study may reflect improved vascular regulation and haemodynamic stabilisation rather than simple alterations in blood flow quantity alone.

Nevertheless, these postoperative changes in LSFG waveform parameters should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence of improved clinical outcomes. Although the observed changes may indicate more stable ocular haemodynamics, severe postoperative visual loss (the ‘wipe-out phenomenon’) has occasionally been reported after TLE,29 particularly in eyes with advanced glaucoma. Therefore, the clinical significance of postoperative waveform changes remains uncertain, and further studies are needed to determine whether these haemodynamic changes are associated with long-term visual outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-centre study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. In addition, pupil diameter and systemic haemodynamic parameters, including heart rate, blood pressure, pulse pressure and ocular perfusion pressure, were not prospectively recorded at the time of each LSFG examination. Therefore, the potential influence of these factors on LSFG measurements and pulse waveform parameters cannot be completely excluded. However, review of the medical records revealed no documented cases of inadequate pupil dilation or marked perioperative haemodynamic instability. Furthermore, because this was a retrospective study based on routine clinical practice, longitudinal LSFG measurements of the fellow eyes were not available in most patients. Therefore, we could not use the fellow eyes as an internal control to further distinguish local ocular changes from systemic influences. Second, the follow-up period was limited to 6 months, and longer-term postoperative haemodynamic changes remain unclear. Third, LSFG provides relative indices of blood flow velocity rather than direct measurements of absolute blood flow volume.8 Therefore, interpretation of postoperative changes requires caution. Finally, because LSFG measurements were obtained at discrete postoperative time points, short-term haemodynamic fluctuations could not be assessed.

In conclusion, postoperative changes in ONH haemodynamics following TLE appear to reflect the combined influence of postoperative IOP, patient age, MD and AL. The observed waveform changes indicate reduced vascular pulsatility and improved haemodynamic stability within the ONH microcirculation after surgery, highlighting the importance of evaluating LSFG-derived waveform parameters alongside conventional blood flow velocity indices in glaucoma.
