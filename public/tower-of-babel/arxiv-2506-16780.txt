Large solutions for subordinate spectral Laplacian
Ivan Biočić and Vanja Wagner

Abstract
. We find a large solution to a semilinear Dirichlet problem in a bounded C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} domain for a non-local operator ϕ ⁡ ( − Δ | D ) \phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D}) , an extension of the infinitesimal generator of a subordinate killed Brownian motion. The setting covers and extends the case of the spectral fractional Laplacian. The upper bound for the explosion rate of the large solution is obtained and is given in terms of the renewal function, distance to the boundary, and the Keller-Osserman-type transformation of the semilinearity. Additionally, we prove interior higher regularity results for this operator.

1.
Introduction

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, be a
C^{1,1}
bounded domain and
f:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
. In this article, we find a solution to the following semilinear problem

(1.1)

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u&=&-f(u)&\quad\text{in }D,\\
\frac{u}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma}&=&\infty&\quad\text{on }\partial D.\end{array}

Here the operator
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
is an extension of the infinitesimal generator of a subordinate killed Brownian motion, where the subordinator has the Laplace exponent
\phi
, and
P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma
is a reference function defined as the Poisson potential of the
d-1
dimensional Hausdorff measure
\sigma
on
\partial D
for the subordinate killed Brownian motion. The solution to (
1.1
) is called a large solution since it cannot be uniformly bounded by a nonnegative
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
-harmonic function.

The operator
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
is a non-local operator of the elliptic type, and for a suitably regular function
u
can be written as the principal value integral

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u(x)=\textrm{P.V.}\int_{D}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)J_{D}(x,y)dy+\kappa(x)u(x),\quad x\in D,

where the singular jumping kernel
J_{D}
and the so-called killing function
\kappa
are completely determined by
\phi
, see (
2.23
). Also,
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
can be written in the spectral form

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\phi(\lambda_{j})\widehat{u}_{j}\varphi_{j},

where
(\varphi_{j},\lambda_{j})_{j}
are the eigenpairs of
-\left.\Delta\right|_{D}
– the Dirichlet Laplacian in
D
.

A classical example of an operator of this form is the spectral fractional Laplacian
(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})^{\alpha/2}
, where
\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha/2}
, for
\alpha\in(0,2)
, i.e. the subordinator is the
\alpha
-stable subordinator.

In this article we make the first step towards the general theory of non-local operators, moving beyond the spectral fractional Laplacian, by considering functions
\phi
which are complete Bernstein functions satisfying the weak scaling condition at infinity: There exist
a_{1},a_{2}>0
and
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
satisfying

(WSC)

\displaystyle a_{1}\lambda^{\delta_{1}}\phi(t)\leq\phi(\lambda t)\leq a_{2}\lambda^{\delta_{2}}\phi(t),\quad t\geq 1,\,\lambda\geq 1.

This assumption covers a large class of subordinators and extends the classical stable, tempered stable, regularly varying classes (for a list of half a dozen examples we refer to
[
44
, p. 41]
), and it is a standard assumption in many state-of-the-art results covering a wide range of general non-local theory, e.g.
[
11
,
13
,
14
,
22
,
23
,
36
,
45
]
and the references therein.

The large problem (
1.1
) is solved both in the distributional and the pointwise sense, where the boundary condition
\frac{u}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma}=\infty
stands for the limit at the boundary of
u/P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma
both in the pointwise as well as in the weak
L^{1}
sense, described in (
2.27
) and (
2.28
). It is a characteristic of non-local settings that boundary blow-up of solutions is possible even for linear equations. For example, the
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
-harmonic in
D
reference function
P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma
explodes at the boundary
\partial D
. Therefore, in the non-local setting, the interpretation of a large solution is that it exhibits a greater blow-up at the boundary than any harmonic function for
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
. In fractional settings, the blow-up rates are written as the fractional powers of the distance to the boundary. In this article, the corresponding blow-up rate can be expressed in terms of the Laplace exponent
\phi
and the corresponding renewal function.

The analysis of non-local operators and semilinear equations has attracted much attention in recent years, particularly in the case of the fractional Laplacian. Research has predominantly been focused on moderate solutions to semilinear equations for the fractional Laplacian, which are solutions bounded by some nonnegative harmonic function, see
[
1
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
18
,
21
,
30
]
. However, there is also great interest in studying large solutions to semilinear equations, i.e. the solutions that explode at the boundary with a higher rate than harmonic functions. In the case of fractional Laplacian, such solutions were explored in
[
2
,
6
,
21
]
. In recent years, there has been further development in the theory of linear and semilinear problems for more general non-local operators, here we refer the reader to
[
16
,
13
,
14
,
32
,
35
,
39
]
for results on linear equations, and to
[
11
,
12
,
20
,
38
,
51
]
for moderate solutions to semilinear problems. To the best of our knowledge, the article
[
24
]
is the first to deal with large solutions for non-local operators that generalise the fractional Laplacian.

On the other hand, an interesting modification of the standard semilinear problem involving the fractional Laplacian is to consider other types of stable operators - such as the spectral fractional Laplacian and other regional or spectral-type non-local operators. Here we mention
[
28
,
34
,
56
]
where homogeneous equations were studied in the case of the spectral fractional Laplacian. Furthermore, we highlight
[
3
]
, where the authors introduced the nonhomogeneous boundary condition for the case of the spectral fractional Laplacian and where both moderate and large solutions to semilinear equations were studied.

Motivated by
[
3
]
, the case of moderate solutions to nonhomogeneous equations for more general non-local operators of the spectral type was studied in
[
10
]
, which serves as a technical cornerstone for this article. The setting here is equivalent to that in
[
10
]
, with the only difference that the results in the present work do not cover the case
d=2
. This is due to some constraints in potential-theoretical techniques applied in the paper, which we further comment on below.

Let us now describe the main results of the article. In Section
4
we present the central result - the existence of a large solution to (
1.1
), Theorem
4.9
. For the nonlinearity
f
we assume that
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
f(0)=0
,
f(t)>0
for
t>0
, and

(F)
.

There exist
0<m\leq M<\infty
such that

(1+m)f(t)\leq tf^{\prime}(t)\leq(1+M)f(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

The novelty here is that this class of functions somewhat extends the class of power functions
f(t)=t^{p}
,
p>1
, for which a large solution for the spectral Laplacian was constructed in
[
3
]
. In the non-fractional setting of this paper, this construction happens to be more technically difficult, and we obtain it by building on the results in
[
3
,
24
,
2
]
. First, motivated by the potential-theoretic properties of the renewal function of the corresponding subordinate Brownian motion, we construct a supersolution to (
1.1
). This is done by establishing the following Keller-Osserman-type condition:

\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{\phi^{-1}(\varphi(t)^{-2})}\lesssim\frac{r}{\phi^{-1}(\varphi(r)^{-2})},\qquad r\geq 1,

which is sufficient for this construction. Here the function
\varphi
completely depends on
f
, so in other words the Keller-Osserman-type condition gives a relation between
f
and
\phi
under which a large solution to (
1.1
) exists, for details see Subsection
4.1
. Then, under the additional assumption which ensures the right boundary behaviour of our supersolution, see (
4.15
), we find a large solution to (
1.1
) by constructing a sequence of approximating moderate solutions. At the end of the section, we prove two notable results. In Theorem
4.11
we prove that the obtained large solution is the minimal solution to (
1.1
), and in Theorem
4.12
, we prove that for every supersolution to (
1.1
), there exists the maximal solution dominated by it.

In order to show the desired boundary blow-up of the constructed large solution to (
1.1
), we also prove a higher regularity theorem in Hölder spaces for distributional solutions for the operator
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
, as well as for the operator
\phi(-\Delta)
. The latter is the infinitesimal generator of the subordinate Brownian motion and is a direct generalisation of the fractional Laplacian. In Theorem
3.6
we prove that for
f\in C^{\alpha}(D)
, every distributional solution to

\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u=f\text{ in }D

is in
C^{\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(D)
and the usual inequality between the appropriate norms of
f
and
u
holds. The detailed proof of this result generalizes the analogous higher regularity approach for the spectral fractional Laplacian given in
[
3
, Lemma 21]
, as well as emphasizes the dimension constraint
d\geq 3
. This limitation seems to be also present, although not explicitly stated in
[
3
,
57
]
, and is necessary for the applied potential-theoretical approach. For details, we refer to Remarks
3.5
and
3.7
. Consequently, the large solution results obtained in this paper and
[
3
]
hold only for
d\geq 3
.

One of the most important ingredients in the proof of Theorem
3.6
is a higher regularity theorem for
\phi(-\Delta)
, proved in Theorem
3.1
: if
f\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
, then every distributional solution to
\phi(-\Delta)u=f
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is in
C^{\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(\mathbb{R})
. The proof of this theorem is motivated by the celebrated article
[
57
]
. The approach in the proof of the higher regularity theorem for the fractional Laplacian,
[
57
, Proposition 2.8]
, again exhibits the dimensional constraint
d\geq 3
, which we highlight in the proof of Theorem
3.1
. However, this problem was overcome in the fractional setting, see e.g.
[
54
]
, by using an alternative method on a larger class of operators.

There are many generalisations of
[
57
, Proposition 2.8]
considering generalised Hölder spaces which are more suitable for the development of regularity theory for non-local operators more general than the fractional Laplacian, e.g. in
[
35
,
54
,
39
,
40
]
. The downside of these approaches is that these regularity results usually have an index gap. The major contribution of Theorem
3.1
is that the regularity, although obtained in classical Hölder spaces, holds for all Matuszewska indices
0<\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{2}<1
. We point out that similar results to ours exist in generalised Hölder spaces in
[
4
,
40
]
but they do not cover the whole span of indices
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
, since their techniques require that indices
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
stay close together. It is worth mentioning that, under this assumption on the Matuszewska indices, one can prove a higher regularity claim for
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
in terms of the generalised Hölder spaces, similar to the one in Theorem
3.6
. Nevertheless, our primary objective is to find a large solution for the operator
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
for the whole span of Matuszewska indices, and the classical Hölder regularity in Theorem
3.6
suits this purpose well.

The article is organized as follows. At the end of this introductory section, we cover the basic notation used throughout the article. Section
2
is the preliminary section that deals with the consequences of the assumption (

we prove that a solution of (

) is not bounded by any nonnegative harmonic function. In addition, we note the definition and properties of the renewal function

, the prominent function in the study of boundary behaviour in

and
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})

and
3.6
. In the last part, Section

1.1
). First, we build an auxiliary sequence of solutions to moderate problems. Then, we construct a large supersolution in Subsection

).

2.
Preliminaries

Let
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, with the characteristic exponent
\xi\mapsto|\xi|^{2}
,
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
D
be a non-empty open set, and
\tau_{D}\coloneqq\inf\{t>0:W_{t}\notin D\}
the first exit time from the set
D
. We define the killed process
W^{D}
upon exiting the set
D
by

\displaystyle W^{D}_{t}\coloneqq\begin{cases}W_{t},&t<\tau_{D},\\
\partial,&t\geq\tau_{D},\end{cases}

where
\partial
is an additional point added to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
called the cemetery.

Let
S
be a subordinator independent of
W
, i.e.
S
is an increasing Lévy process such that
S_{0}=0
, with the Laplace exponent

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lambda\mapsto\phi(\lambda)=b\lambda+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda\,t})\mu(dt),

where
b\geq 0
and the measure
\mu
satisfies
\int_{0}^{\infty}(1\wedge t)\mu(dt)<\infty
. The measure
\mu
is called the Lévy measure and
b
is the drift of the subordinator.

The process
X=((X_{t})_{t\geq 0},(\mathbb{P}_{x})_{x\in D})
defined by
X_{t}\coloneqq W^{D}_{S_{t}}
is called the subordinate killed Brownian motion. Here
\mathbb{P}_{x}
denotes the probability under which the process
X
starts from
x\in D
, and by
\mathbb{E}_{x}
we denote the corresponding expectation. As we will explain in Subsection
2.3
, the operator
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
can be viewed as the infinitesimal generator of
X
.

An additional important process for studying equations related to
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
is the process
Y=((Y_{t})_{t\geq 0},(\mathbb{P}_{x})_{x\in D})
defined by
Y_{t}\coloneqq W_{S_{t}}
, called the subordinate Brownian motion. In other words, the process
W
is not killed upon exiting the set
D
before the subordination takes place. By using this close connection of the processes
X
and

Y
, later in the article we obtain the regularity of distributional solutions for
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
.

2.1.
Assumptions

Throughout the article, we always assume that
D
is a bounded
C^{1,1}
domain, although some auxiliary results are also valid for general open sets.

Before we introduce the assumption on the Laplace exponent
\phi
, i.e. on the subordinator
S
, we note that a function of the form (
2.1
) is called a Bernstein function, and such functions characterize subordinators, see
[
55
, Chapter 5]
.
The following assumption on
\phi
is imposed throughout the article.

(WSC)
.

The function
\phi
is a complete Bernstein function, i.e. the Lévy measure
\mu(dt)
has a completely monotone density
\mu(t)
, and
\phi
satisfies the following weak scaling condition at infinity: There exist
a_{1},a_{2}>0
and
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
satisfying

(2.2)

\displaystyle a_{1}\lambda^{\delta_{1}}\phi(t)\leq\phi(\lambda t)\leq a_{2}\lambda^{\delta_{2}}\phi(t),\quad t\geq 1,\lambda\geq 1.

The most notable subordinator with the property
(WSC)
is the
\alpha
-stable subordinator where
\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha/2}
, for some
\alpha\in(0,2)
. It satisfies the exact and even global scaling condition (
2.2
), and the process
Y
, in this case, is the isotropic
\alpha
-stable process. However, many other interesting subordinators fall within our setting, such as a sum of two stable subordinators, relativistic stable subordinators, tempered stable subordinators, and many more, see e.g.
[
47
, p. 409]
and
[
44
, p. 41]
.

By the assumption
(WSC)
, the function

(2.3)

\phi^{*}(\lambda)\coloneqq\frac{\lambda}{\phi(\lambda)}

is a complete Bernstein function, too, see
[
55
, Proposition 7.1]
, and is called the conjugate Bernstein function of
\phi
. The relation (
2.2
) also holds for
\phi^{*}
but with different constants: i.e. it holds that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{1}}\lambda^{1-\delta_{2}}\phi^{*}(t)\leq\phi^{*}(\lambda t)\leq\frac{1}{a_{2}}\lambda^{1-\delta_{1}}\phi^{*}(t),\quad t\geq 1,\lambda\geq 1.

Thus,
(WSC)
also holds for
\phi^{*}
and by
\nu(dt)=\nu(t)dt
we denote the Lévy measure of
\phi^{*}
.

In the next few paragraphs, we discuss properties of
\phi
. Since
\phi^{*}
also satisfies the condition
(WSC)
, the same will hold for the counterparts of
\phi^{*}
.
By a simple calculation, the scaling condition (
2.2
) implies that

in (
2.1
) and the well-known bound

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(\lambda)\asymp\frac{\phi(\lambda)}{\lambda},\quad\lambda\geq 1.

Here, the lower bound follows from (

) and the upper bound holds for every Bernstein function and every
\lambda>0
. The Lévy measure
\mu(dt)
is infinite, see
[
55
, p. 160]
, and the density
\mu(t)
cannot decrease too fast, i.e. there is

such that

\mu(t)\leq c\mu(t+1),\quad t\geq 1,

see
[

, Lemma 2.1]
. Furthermore, the density
\mu
admits a sharp bound expressed via the function

, see
[

and
[

, Proposition 3.3]
, but we will not directly use this bound.

The potential measure

of the subordinator

is defined by
U(A)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(S_{t}\in A)dt
,

, and it has a decreasing density
{\swabfamily u}
for which it holds that

, see
[

, Theorem 11.3]
. The density
{\swabfamily u}
also admits a sharp bound expressed via

, see
[

and
[

, Proposition 3.4]
, but in the article, they are used only in some results that we quote later. The potential density of

will be denoted by

.

While not all Bernstein functions satisfy (

), a general Bernstein function satisfies a version of a global scaling condition:

\displaystyle 1\wedge\lambda\leq\frac{\phi(\lambda\,t)}{\phi(t)}\leq 1\vee\lambda,\quad\lambda>0,t>0,

which we get directly from (

Under the imposed assumptions of this article, important aspects of the potential theory of the process

[

,

,
49
,
50
,

]

These results include the scale invariant Harnack principle and the boundary Harnack principle, sharp estimates on the Green function and the jumping kernel, as well as the representation of harmonic functions. Although we do not use most of these properties directly, they are heavily used in the proofs of cited results and were essential for obtaining the results we build on.

2.2.
Green functions

The transition density of the Brownian motion
W
we denote by

(2.7)

\displaystyle p(t,x,y)=(4\pi t)^{-d/2}e^{-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{4t}},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,t>0.

It follows that the transition density of the killed Brownian motion
W^{D}
is given, for all

, by

(2.8)

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)=p(t,x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{x}[p(t-\tau_{D},W_{\tau_{D}},y)\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}<t\}}]

It is well known that
p_{D}(t,\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric and differentiable up to the boundary, i.e.
p_{D}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)\in C^{1}((0,\infty)\times\overline{D}\times\overline{D})
, since
D
is a
C^{1,1}
open domain, see
[
10
, Lemma A.7]
. In addition, we have the following heat kernel estimate: There exist constants
T_{0}=T_{0}(D)>0
,
c_{1}=c_{1}(T_{0},D)>0
,
c_{2}=c_{2}(T_{0},D)>0
,
c_{3}=c_{3}(D)>0
, and
c_{4}=c_{4}(D)>0
such that for all

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left[\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\wedge 1\right]\frac{1}{c_{1}t^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{c_{2}t}}\\
&\hskip 60.00009pt\leq p_{D}(t,x,y)\leq\left[\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{t}\wedge 1\right]\frac{c_{3}}{t^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{c_{4}|x-y|^{2}}{t}}.\end{split}

In fact, the right-hand side inequality in (

. For details, refer to

The semigroup

. The potential kernel of

(or the Green function of

\displaystyle G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t,x,y)dt=|x-y|^{-d+2},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

since we consider only dimensions

is the density of the mean occupation time for

, i.e. for

The semigroup
(P_{t}^{D})_{t\geq 0}
of the killed Brownian motion

is for

\displaystyle P_{t}^{D}f(x)=\int\limits_{D}p_{D}(t,x,y)f(y)dy=\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(W_{t});t<\tau_{D}]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(W^{D}_{t})],

where we set

for all Borel functions on

by convention. Since

is

, it is well known that the semigroup

The potential kernel of

\displaystyle G_{D}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{D}(t,x,y)dt,\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

.
The process

\displaystyle G^{\phi}_{D}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{D}(t,x,y)dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{D}(t,x,y){\swabfamily u}(t)dt,\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The Poisson kernel of

\displaystyle P^{\phi}_{D}(x,z)\coloneqq-\frac{\partial}{\partial{\mathbf{n}}}G^{\phi}_{D}(x,z)\asymp\frac{\delta_{D}(x)}{|x-z|^{d+2}\phi(|x-z|^{-2})},\quad x\in D,z\in\partial D,

where the sharp bound for the Poisson integral above was proved in

\displaystyle\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptj(r)

, see e.g.
[

.

Since

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G^{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x-y)f(y)dy=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}f(Y_{t})dt\right],\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

\displaystyle C^{-1}\frac{1}{|x|^{d}\phi(|x|^{-2})}\leq G^{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x)\leq C\frac{1}{|x|^{d}\phi(|x|^{-2})},\quad|x|\leq M.

2.3.
Operators
\phi(-\Delta|_{D})
and
\phi(-\Delta)

Let
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

be a Hilbert basis of
L^{2}(D)
consisting of eigenfunctions
of the Dirichlet Laplacian
-\Delta|_{D}
,
associated to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.
\varphi_{j}\in H^{1}_{0}(D)\cap C^{\infty}(D)\cap C^{1,1}(\overline{D})
and

(2.17)

\displaystyle-\Delta|_{D}\varphi_{j}=\lambda_{j}\varphi_{j},\quad\text{in $D$},

see
[
19
, Theorem 9.31]
and
[
33
, Section 8.11]
. The relation (
2.17
) can be viewed as a distributional or a pointwise relation. In addition,
\Delta|_{D}
in (
2.17
) can be viewed as the
L^{2}(D)
-infinitesimal generator of the semigroup
(P_{t}^{D})_{t}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\left.\Delta\right|_{D}u=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{P_{t}^{D}u-u}{t},\quad u\in\mathcal{D}(\left.\Delta\right|_{D}).

Here
\mathcal{D}(\Delta|_{D})
denotes the domain of the generator
\Delta|_{D}
and the above limit is taken with respect to
L^{2}(D)
norm. Since
\varphi_{j}
is an eigenfunction, we note that

(2.18)

\displaystyle P_{t}^{D}\varphi_{j}=e^{-\lambda_{j}t}\varphi_{j},\quad\text{in $D$}.

For the future reference, it is worth noting that the space
\mathcal{D}(\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
is a class of functions
f\in H_{0}^{1}(D)
such that
\Delta f
exists in the weak distributional sense and belongs to
L^{2}(D)
, see
[
25
, Theorem 2.13]
. In particular,
C_{c}^{2}(D)\subset\mathcal{D}(\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
. For more details on the operator
\left.\Delta\right|_{D}
, refer to
[
25
, Chapter 2]
and
[
19
, Chapter 9]
.

By the assumption that
D
is
C^{1,1}
, it follows that
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\dots
, and the Weyl’s asymptotic law holds:

(2.19)

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}\asymp j^{2/d},\quad j\in\mathbb{N}.

We may also choose the basis
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\varphi_{1}>0
in
D
, see
[
19
, Chapter 9]
, so that very important sharp estimate for
\varphi_{1}
holds:

(2.20)

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}(x)\asymp\delta_{D}(x),\quad x\in D.

The interior estimate is trivial and the boundary estimate follows from Hopf’s lemma. We note that this estimate is closely connected to the boundary behaviour of the heat kernel
p_{D}
, as one can see in (
2.9
), and in the choice of the domain for the distributional operators in
D
, as we will see in a moment.

Consider the Hilbert space

\displaystyle H_{D}(\phi)\coloneqq\left\{v=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\widehat{v}_{j}\varphi_{j}\in L^{2}(D):\|v\|_{H_{D}(\phi)}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi(\lambda_{j})^{2}|\widehat{v}_{j}|^{2}<\infty\right\}.

The spectral operator
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D}):H_{D}(\phi)\to L^{2}(D)
is defined as

(2.21)

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\phi(\lambda_{j})\widehat{u}_{j}\varphi_{j},\quad u\in H_{D}(\phi).

In the simple case where
\phi(t)=t^{s}
, the operator
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})=(-\Delta_{|D})^{s}
is called the spectral fractional Laplacian.
It was shown in
[
10
, Section 2]
that the operator
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
is the
L^{2}(D)
-infinitesimal generator of the process
X
. Furthermore, it was also shown that
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
has a pointwise representation: For
u\in C^{1,1}(D)\cap\mathcal{D}(\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
and for a.e.
x\in D
we have

(2.22)

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u(x)=\textrm{P.V.}\int\limits_{D}[u(x)-u(y)]J_{D}(x,y)dy+\kappa(x)u(x),

where

(2.23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{D}(x,y)&\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{D}(t,x,y)\mu(t)dt,\\
\kappa(x)&\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\int_{D}p_{D}(t,x,y)dy\right)\mu(t)dt.\end{split}

For the jumping kernel
J_{D}(x,y)
, the following sharp estimate holds

(2.24)

J_{D}(x,y)\asymp\left(\frac{\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)}{|x-y|^{2}}\wedge 1\right)j(|x-y|),\quad x,y\in D,

see
[
48
, Proposition 3.5]
and
[
10
, Remark 2.5]
.

We also define a distributional version of
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
for all
u\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
, which we denote by
\phi_{d}(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u
, by the following relation

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{d}(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u,\psi\rangle\coloneqq\langle u,\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\psi\rangle\coloneqq\int_{D}u(x)\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\psi(x)dx,

for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
. The space
L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
is the appropriate one for the distributional operator because
|\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\psi|\leq\delta_{D}
in
D
, and
-\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\psi\asymp\delta_{D}
near
\partial D
for
\psi\geq 0
, as shown in
[
10
, Lemma 2.11]
. For additional details on
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
and
\phi_{d}(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
, please refer to
[
10
, Section 2]
and the references therein.

Remark 2.2
.

By analyzing the proofs of
[
10
, Lemma 2.4]
and
[
10
, Remark 2.6]
, one can easily see that the expression (
2.22
) makes sense for all
C^{2\delta_{2}+\varepsilon}(D)\cap L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
functions, where
\varepsilon>0
. This is because
(WSC)
implies

J_{D}(x,y)\lesssim j(|x-y|)\lesssim|x-y|^{-d-2\delta_{2}},\quad x,y\in D.

Thus, we extend the definition of
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u
by (
2.22
) whenever the principal value integral in (
2.22
) exists.
Furthermore, if

then
\phi_{d}(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u=\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u
as distributions in

.

Now we turn to the definition of the operator
\phi(-\Delta)
. We define the operator
-\phi(-\Delta)
as the infinitesimal generator of the
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-semigroup generated by the Lévy process
Y
. It is well known that the space
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is contained in the domain of this generator, and for
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
it holds that

\displaystyle-\phi(-\Delta)u(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(u(y)-u(x)-\nabla u(x)\cdot(y-x)\mathbf{1}_{\{|y-x|\leq 1\}}\right)j(|y-x|)\,dy

(2.25)

\displaystyle=\textrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(u(y)-u(x)\right)j(|y-x|)dy

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{|y-x|>\epsilon}\left(u(y)-u(x)\right)j(|y-x|)\,dy

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{|h|>\epsilon}\left(u(x+h)-u(x)\right)j(|h|)\,dh.

In the familiar case of the isotropic stable process, where
\phi(t)=t^{s}
, the operator
-\phi(-\Delta)
is the fractional Laplacian
-(-\Delta)^{s}
.

We extend the definition of
\phi(-\Delta)u(x)
by (
2.25
) whenever the limit in (
2.25
) exists. This is true e.g. for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
when
u\in C^{2\delta_{2}+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},(1\wedge j(|x|))dx)
, where
\varepsilon>0
.
For functions
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},(1\wedge j(|x|))dx)
we also define a distributional version of
\phi(-\Delta)
, denoted by
\phi_{d}(-\Delta)
, by the relation

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{d}(-\Delta)u,\varphi\rangle\coloneqq\langle u,\phi(-\Delta)\varphi\rangle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x)\phi(-\Delta)\varphi(x)dx,\quad\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

It is easy to show that if
u\in C^{2\delta_{2}+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},(1\wedge j(|x|))dx)
then
\phi_{d}(-\Delta)u=\phi(-\Delta)u
as distributions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, when dealing with
\phi_{d}(-\Delta)u
we sometimes just simply say that we look at
\phi(-\Delta)u
in the distributional sense.

2.4.
Different types of solutions and boundary conditions for the Dirichlet problem

In the article, we deal with three types of solutions to the Dirichlet problem involving the operator
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
, and we also have two types of the boundary condition.

We say that
u:D\to\mathbb{R}
is a pointwise solution to

(2.26)

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u&=&f&\quad\text{in }D,\\
\frac{u}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma}&=&g&\quad\text{on }\partial D,\end{array}

if
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u=f
in
D
in the sense of (
2.22
) and if

(2.27)

\displaystyle\lim_{D\ni x\to z}\frac{u(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=g(z),\quad z\in\partial D.

On the other hand, the function
u:D\to\mathbb{R}
is a distributional solution to (
2.26
) if
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u=f
in
D
in the distributional sense. Additionally, for the boundary condition, we can either look at the pointwise limit as in (
2.27
) or we can look at the so-called weak
L^{1}
boundary condition:

(2.28)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)\leq t\}}\frac{u(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}\varphi(x)dx=\int_{\partial D}\varphi(z)g(z)\sigma(dz),

for all
\varphi\in C(\overline{D})
. It is easy to see that if the boundary limit (
2.27
) exists and it is finite (or infinite everywhere), then the weak
L^{1}
boundary condition (
2.28
) also holds with the same limiting function.
However, in Section
3
the boundary condition in (
2.26
) is left out which means that we only demand that
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u=f
holds in the distributional sense.

Finally, we say that
u\in L^{1}_{loc}(D)
is a weak solution to (
2.26
) if for every
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
it holds that

(2.29)

\displaystyle\int_{D}u(x)\xi(x)dx=\int_{D}G^{\phi}_{D}\xi(x)f(x)dx-\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{n}}G^{\phi}_{D}\xi(z)g(z)\sigma(dz).

Equivalently, by writing
\psi=G^{\phi}_{D}\xi
, the relation (
2.29
) says

(2.30)

\displaystyle\int_{D}u(x)\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\psi(x)dx=\int_{D}\psi(x)f(x)dx-\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{n}}\psi(z)g(z)\sigma(dz).

We refer to
[
10
]
for a detailed study of weak solutions and their relation to pointwise and distributional solutions.

The following lemma explains why a solution to (
1.1
), the so-called large solution, is the one that is not uniformly bounded by a nonnegative harmonic function.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
u:D\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

(2.31)

\displaystyle\lim_{D\ni x\to z}\frac{|u(x)|}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=\infty,\quad z\in\partial D,

then
u
is not uniformly bounded in
D
by any nonnegative harmonic function with respect to
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
.

Proof.

Since
\partial D
is compact, by the standard
3\varepsilon
argument, we easily obtain uniformness of the convergence in (
2.31
).

Suppose that the claim of the lemma does not hold, i.e. there exists
h
, a nonnegative harmonic function with respect to
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
, such that
|u(x)|\leq h(x)
,
x\in D
. By the representation of nonnegative harmonic functions, see
[
10
, Theorem 2.23]
, there exists a finite measure
\zeta
on
\partial D
such that
h(x)=P^{\phi}_{D}\zeta(x)
,
x\in D
. Further, we have

(2.32)

\displaystyle\lim_{D\ni x\to z}\frac{P^{\phi}_{D}\zeta(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=\lim_{D\ni x\to z}\frac{P^{\phi}_{D}\zeta(x)}{|u(x)|}\frac{|u(x)|}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=\infty,\quad z\in\partial D,

and the convergence is uniform by the same argument as in the beginning of the proof.

However, by the proof of
[
10
, Proposition 3.5]
, we get that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)<t\}}\frac{P^{\phi}_{D}\zeta(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}dx

\displaystyle=\limsup_{t\to 0}\int_{\partial D}\left(\frac{1}{t}\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)<t\}}\frac{P^{\phi}_{D}(x,z)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}dx\right)\zeta(dz)

\displaystyle\leq c\int_{\partial D}\zeta(dz)=c\,\zeta(\partial D)<\infty,

which is a contradiction with (
2.32
) which says that
\frac{P^{\phi}_{D}\zeta(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}
is arbitrarily and uniformly large near the boundary.
∎

To conclude this subsection, we would like to make a few remarks. When dealing with distributional and weak solutions, it is possible to consider Radon measures
\mu
and
\zeta
instead of the functions
f
and
g
as the boundary data, provided that they have the necessary integrability properties. Further, every weak solution is also a distributional one with the weak
L^{1}
boundary condition, as proved in
[
10
, Proposition 4.6]
. However, it is important to keep in mind that a weak solution may not have a pointwise boundary limit in the sense of (
2.28
). On the other hand, if a distributional or weak solution
u
satisfies (
2.27
) and has sufficient regularity, it is also a pointwise solution, see Remark
2.2
.

2.5.
Renewal function

We end the section with a short introduction of the renewal function
V
. For details, see
[
8
, Chapter VI]
. The renewal function
V
plays a prominent role in studying boundary behaviour in
C^{1,1}
domains. We refer the interested reader to
[
46
,
52
,
24
,
11
]
.

Let
Z=(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a one-dimensional subordinate Brownian motion with the characteristic exponent
\phi(\theta^{2})
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. E.g.
Z
can be thought of as one of the components of the process
Y
. Let
M_{t}:=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}Z_{s}
be the supremum process of
Z
, and let
L=(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the local time of
M_{t}-Z_{t}
at zero. The inverse local time
L_{t}^{-1}:=\inf\{s>0:L_{s}>t\}
is called the ascending ladder time process of
Z
. Define the ascending ladder height process
H=(H_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of
Z
by
H_{t}:=M_{L_{t}^{-1}}=Z_{L_{t}^{-1}}
if
L_{t}^{-1}<\infty
, and
H_{t}=\infty
otherwise. We define the renewal function
V
of the process
H
by

(2.33)

V(t):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(H_{s}\leq t)\,ds,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

It holds that
V
is strictly increasing,
V(t)=0
for
t<0
,
V(0)=0
, and
V(\infty)=\infty
. The most notable property of the renewal function
V
is that
V_{|(0,\infty)}
is harmonic with respect to the killed process
Z^{(0,\infty)}
, which was first used in
[
46
]
to obtain the precise decay rate of harmonic functions of a
d
-dimensional subordinate Brownian motion.

In the case of the isotropic
\alpha
-stable process, we have
V(t)=t^{\alpha/2}
, but in general, the function
V
is not known explicitly. However, under
(WSC)
it follows from
[
39
, Lemma 2.4, Lemma 2.5]
, cf.
[
46
]
, that
V\in C^{2}
and that for every
R>0
:

(2.34)

\displaystyle V(t)

\displaystyle\asymp\Phi(t):=\phi(t^{-2})^{-1/2}\,,\quad 0<t\leq R,

(2.35)

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{V(t)}{t}\,,\quad 0<t\leq R,

(2.36)

\displaystyle|V^{\prime\prime}(t)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{V^{\prime}(t)}{t}\,,\quad 0<t\leq R.

3.
Regularity of distributional solutions

In this section, we prove two higher regularity theorems for distributional solutions to linear non-local problems associated with operators
\phi(-\Delta)
and
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
. Analogs of these results were already established in the fractional setting in
[
57
]
and
[
3
]
. Our generalisations of these results follow the approach of the fractional setting, with necessary modifications due to the lack of exact scaling. The results of this section are self-contained and fill in some minor gaps in the original proofs. As we already pointed out in the introduction, the choice to work with classical Hölder spaces, instead of generalised Hölder spaces corresponding to
\phi
as in
[
4
]
and
[
40
]
, was made in order to obtain regularity results for all ranges of Matuszewska indices in (
2.6
). These results are essential in the construction of the large solution by an approximating sequence in Lemma
4.8
.

To simplify notation and to make our results more readable, we write
C^{k+\alpha}
instead of
C^{k,\alpha}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Or, more generally, for
\beta\in(0,\infty)
,
C^{\beta}
means
C^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor,\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}
. However, in cases where more clarity is needed, we stick with the notation of
C^{k,\alpha}
-type.

Further, when dealing with distributional solutions which are defined almost everywhere, we say that a solution has a certain regularity if there exists a modification (with respect to the Lebesgue measure) of this function with the stated regularity.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\in C^{k+\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
so that
k+\alpha+2\delta_{1}\notin\mathbb{N}
, and let
u
be a distributional solution to

(3.1)

\displaystyle\phi(-\Delta)u=f,\quad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

Then
u\in C^{k+\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and there exists a constant
C=C(d,k,\alpha,\phi)>0
such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle||u||_{C^{k+\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C\,||f||_{C^{k+\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

On the other hand, if
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
u\in C^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
\beta\in(0,2\delta_{1})
and there exists a constant
C=C(d,\phi)>0
such that

(3.3)

\displaystyle||u||_{C^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C\,||f||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

In essence, the previous theorem says that, given some regularity on
f
, the Hölder regularity of a solution to (
3.1
) increases by (at least)
2\delta_{1}
. In the fractional Laplacian case (where
\delta_{1}=s
), this result has been proven in
[
57
, Section 2.1]
.

In order to prove Theorem
3.1
, we show a few auxiliary results. The first lemma is a generalisation of
[
57
, Proposition 2.5]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and suppose
\alpha>2\delta_{2}
. If
u\in C^{k,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then
\phi(-\Delta)u\in C^{k,\alpha-2\delta_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and it holds that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|\phi(-\Delta)u\|_{C^{k,\alpha-2\delta_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq c(d,k,\alpha,\phi)\|u\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Proof.

Take
k=0
. First, we prove that
\phi(-\Delta)u
is well defined. Note that, by using (
2.15
) and
(WSC)
, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle j(r)\lesssim r^{-d-2\delta_{2}},\quad r\leq 1,

and the constant of comparability depends only on
d
and
\phi
.
Since
u\in C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\alpha>2\delta_{2}
we have for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u(x+h)-u(x))j(|h|)dh\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d})}|u(x+h)-u(x)|\left(1_{B(0,1)}+1_{B(0,1)^{c}}\right)j(|h|)dh

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{B(0,1)}[u]_{0,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}|h|^{\alpha}\frac{1}{|h|^{d+2\delta_{2}}}dh+2\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\int_{B(0,1)^{c}}j(|h|)dh

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\|u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty,

for some
c_{1}=c_{1}(d,\alpha,\phi)>0
, i.e.
\phi(-\Delta)u
is well defined and

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|\phi(-\Delta)u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq c_{1}\|u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

To show the desired regularity of
\phi(-\Delta)
take
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r<1
and note that

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt|\phi(-\Delta)u(x_{1})-\phi(-\Delta)u(x_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B(0,r)}(|u(x_{1}+h)-u(x_{1})|+|u(x_{2}+h)-u(x_{2})|)j(|h|)dh

\displaystyle+\int_{B(0,r)^{c}}(|u(x_{1}+h)-u(x_{2}+h)|+|u(x_{2})-u(x_{1})|)j(|h|)dh

\displaystyle\eqqcolon I_{1}+I_{2}.

Since
|u(x)-u(y)|\leq[u]_{0,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{\alpha}
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by applying (
3.5
) we get that

(3.7)

\displaystyle I_{1}\lesssim\int_{B(0,r)}[u]_{0,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}|h|^{\alpha}\frac{1}{|h|^{d+2\delta_{2}}}dh\leq c_{2}[u]_{0,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}r^{\alpha-2\delta_{2}},

for some
c_{2}=c_{2}(d,\alpha,\phi)>0
. Similarly, for some
c_{3}=c_{3}(d,\alpha,\phi)>0
we have

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{B(0,r)^{c}}[u]_{0,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}j(|h|)dh

\displaystyle=2\int_{B(0,r)^{c}}[u]_{0,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}(1_{B(0,1)\setminus B(0,r)}+1_{B(0,1)^{c}})j(|h|)dh

\displaystyle\lesssim[u]_{0,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}\int_{B(0,1)\setminus B(0,r)}|h|^{-d-2\delta_{2}}dh

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt+[u]_{0,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}\int_{B(0,1)^{c}}j(|h|)dh

(3.8)

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}[u]_{0,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}r^{-2\delta_{2}}+|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}\big).

If
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\leq 1
, then
|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}\leq|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha-2\delta_{2}}
, so by choosing
r=|x_{1}-x_{2}|
in (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{|\phi(-\Delta)u(x_{1})-\phi(-\Delta)u(x_{2})|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha-2\delta_{2}}}\leq(c_{2}+c_{3})[u]_{0,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

If
|x_{1}-x_{2}|>1
, then by (
3.6
)

\displaystyle\frac{|\phi(-\Delta)u(x_{1})-\phi(-\Delta)u(x_{2})|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha-2\delta_{2}}}\leq 2c_{1}\|u\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

which, together with the previous inequality, implies (
3.4
) for
k=0
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
first note that the derivative and the operator
\phi(-\Delta)
commute, i.e.

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}\phi(-\Delta)u(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{t}(u(x+te_{i}+h)-u(x+te_{i})-u(x+h)+u(x))j(|h|)dh

\displaystyle=\phi(-\Delta)(\partial_{x_{i}}u)(x).

Indeed, similarly as before
we note that
|\nabla u(x)-\nabla u(y)|\lesssim|x-y|^{\alpha}
,

, the result follows by the dominated convergence theorem. Now the desired result for
k\in\mathbb{N}
follows by the iteration of this argument and the result for
k=0
.
∎

Together with the previous lemma, the following result generalises
[
57
, Proposition 2.6]
. However, here we slightly deviate from the ideas in the proof in
[

]
, as we apply a more direct approach without using the Riesz transform.

Let

Next, for

\displaystyle|\phi(-\Delta)u(x_{1})-\phi(-\Delta)u(x_{2})|\leq I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

where

(3.11)

\displaystyle I_{1}\lesssim[u]_{1,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{r}h^{\alpha-2\delta_{2}}dh=c_{2}(d,\alpha,\phi)[u]_{1,\alpha,\mathbb{R}^{d}}r^{1+\alpha-2\delta_{2}}.

(3.12)

\displaystyle u(x)=u(y)+\nabla u(y)\cdot(y-x)+H^{1,\alpha}_{x,y}(y-x),

\displaystyle I_{2}

Proof of Theorem

.

Let

,

.

Similarly as in
[
57
]
, pick a nonnegative function

such that

,
\eta\equiv 1
in

B(0,2)^{c}
, and define

\displaystyle u_{0}\coloneqq G^{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\eta f\big).

Obviously,
\eta f\in C_{c}^{k+\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Also, it is easy to see that
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that we have

(3.22)

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|G^{\phi}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\mathbf{1}_{B(0,2)}\big)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq c(d,\phi)\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

We further have that
u-u_{0}
is harmonic in
B(0,1)
with respect to
\phi(-\Delta)
, since

\phi(-\Delta)(u-u_{0})=(1-\eta)f=0,\qquad\text{in }B(0,1),

see e.g.
[
9
, Proposition 2.5]
. By
[
37
, Theorem 1.7]
we have that
u-u_{0}
is smooth in
B(0,1)
and there exists
c_{1}=c_{1}(d,k,\alpha,\phi)>0
such that

(3.23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|u-u_{0}\|_{C^{k+\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(B(0,1/2))}&\leq c_{1}\|u-u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
\overset{\eqref{eq:u0 Linf norm}}{}&{\leq}c_{1}\big(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\big).\end{split}

In order to estimate the
C^{k+\alpha+2\delta_{1}}
norm of
u_{0}
define
v_{0}\coloneqq G^{\phi^{*}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{0}
, where
\phi^{*}
is the conjugate of
\phi
defined in (
2.3
). By Lemma
2.1
we have that
v_{0}=G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\eta f)
so the regularity of solutions to the classical Poisson equation implies
v_{0}\in C^{k+2+\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Since
\phi^{*}(-\Delta)v_{0}=u_{0}
, and since
\phi^{*}
satisfies
(WSC)
with the exponents
1-\delta_{2}
and
1-\delta_{1}
, see (
2.4
), by either Lemma
3.2
,
3.3
or
3.4
, we have
u_{0}\in C^{k+\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{C^{k+\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq c_{2}(d,k,\alpha,\phi)\|v_{0}\|_{C^{k+2+\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq c_{3}(d,k,\alpha,\phi)\|f\|_{C^{k+\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

The case when
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
follows analogously, using the fact that
v_{0}\in C^{1,\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every
\gamma\in(0,1)
and hence
u_{0}\in C^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every
\beta\in(0,2\delta_{1})
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The assumption
d\geq 3
from the beginning of this article is essential in the proof of Theorem
3.1
to conclude that the function
v_{0}=G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\eta f)
is well defined and finite. Indeed, if
d\leq 2
and
f\geq c1_{B}
for some ball
B\subset B(0,1)
and
c>0
, then by the calculations in
[
55
, Propsition 14.2]
we have

\displaystyle v_{0}(x)=G^{\phi^{*}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{0}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}(\eta f)(x)dt=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\eta(W_{t})f(W_{t})dt\right]\equiv\infty,

where the last equality comes from the recurrence of the Brownian motion in
d\leq 2
, see e.g.
[
53
, Theorem 3.27]
. Therefore, the presented approach, used as well in the proof of
[
57
, Proposition 2.8]
, cannot be applied in the case of
d\leq 2
.

Now we prove a regularity theorem for
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
by applying the same approach as in
[
3
, Lemma 21]
. As the calculations are highly non-trivial, we present the complete proof for clarity, to fill in some gaps, as well as to fix a minor error at the end of the proof of
[
3
, Lemma 21]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
k+\alpha+2\delta_{1}\notin\mathbb{N}
, and let
f\in C^{k+\alpha}(D)
. If
u\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
solves

(3.24)

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u=f\quad\text{in $D$}

in the distributional sense, then
u\in C^{k+\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(D)
. Furthermore, for any given sets
K\subset\subset K^{\prime}\subset\subset D
, there exists a constant
C=C(d,\alpha,K,K^{\prime},D,\phi)>0
such that

(3.25)

\displaystyle||u||_{C^{k+\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(K)}\leq C\left(||f||_{C^{k+\alpha}(K^{\prime})}+||u||_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}\right).

Moreover, if
f\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(D)
and
\beta\in(0,2\delta_{1})
, then

(3.26)

\displaystyle||u||_{C^{\beta}(K)}\leq C\left(||f||_{L^{\infty}(K^{\prime})}+||u||_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}\right).

In particular, if
f=0
, i.e.
u
is
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
-harmonic, then
u\in C^{\infty}(D)
and the equality
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u(x)=0
holds at every point
x\in D
.

Proof.

The main idea of the proof is to compare a distributional solution
u\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
to (
3.24
) to a specific solution to

\phi(-\Delta)\overline{u}=\overline{f}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for a suitable modification
\overline{f}
of the extension of
f
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let
f\in C^{\alpha}(D)
and take
K\subset\subset K^{\prime}\subset\subset D
. By
[
10
, Corollary 2.9]
we have that
v\coloneqq G^{\phi^{*}}_{D}u
satisfies
v\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
and

(3.27)

\displaystyle||v||_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}\leq c_{1}(d,D,\phi)||u||_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}.

Furthermore, it holds that
-\Delta v=f
in the distributional sense in
D
. Indeed, for
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
, by Fubini’s theorem we get

\displaystyle\int_{D}v(x)(-\Delta)\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{D}G^{\phi^{*}}_{D}u(x)(-\Delta)\psi(x)dx=\int_{D}u(x)G^{\phi^{*}}_{D}\big((-\Delta)\psi\big)(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{D}u(x)\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\psi(x)dx,

where the last equality follows from
[
10
, Lemma 2.7 & Proposition 2.2]
. By the classical elliptic regularity, this means that
v\in C^{2,\alpha}(D)
, see
[
31
, Section 2.2]
.

Next, we show that

(3.28)

u=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(v-P_{t}^{D}v\big)\nu(t)dt.

Recall that for
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
by
[
10
, Eq. (2.30)]
we have

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\psi(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\psi(x)-P_{t}^{D}\psi(x)\big)\nu(t)dt,

since
C_{c}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{D}(\left.\Delta\right|_{D})
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{D}u(x)\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{D}v(x)\phi^{*}(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\psi(x)

(3.29)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}v(x)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\psi(x)-P_{t}^{D}\psi(x)\big)\nu(t)dt\right)dx.

Note that we can change the order of integration in (
3.29
), since
v\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
and

(3.30)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\big|\psi(x)-P_{t}^{D}\psi(x)\big|\nu(t)dt\lesssim\delta_{D}(x),\ \ x\in D.

Indeed, by
[
10
, Eq. (2.26)]
we have that
\psi=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{\psi}_{j}\varphi_{j}
where
|\widetilde{\psi}_{j}|\leq c_{2}(\psi,m,D)\lambda_{j}^{-m}
, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, by
[
10
, Eq. (A.35) and the display after]
and (
2.18
), we get that

\displaystyle|\psi-P_{t}^{D}\psi|

\displaystyle=\big|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{\psi}_{j}\varphi_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{\psi}_{j}P_{t}^{D}\varphi_{j}\big|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\widetilde{\psi}_{j}||\varphi_{j}|(1-e^{-\lambda_{j}t})

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}(\psi,m,D)\delta_{D}(x)\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{-m+d/4+1}(1-e^{-\lambda_{j}t}).

Now (
3.30
) follows by choosing
m
large enough and noting that by (
2.19
) we have that

\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda_{j}t})\nu(t)dt=\phi^{*}(\lambda_{j})\leq\lambda_{j}\asymp j^{2/d}.

Applying Fubini’s theorem to (
3.29
) in the first line, and symmetry of
P_{t}^{D}
in the second, we get that

\displaystyle\int_{D}u(x)\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{D}v(x)\big(\psi(x)-P_{t}^{D}\psi(x)\big)dx\right)\nu(t)dt

(3.31)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{D}\psi(x)\big(v(x)-P_{t}^{D}v(x)\big)dx\right)\nu(t)dt.

Here
P_{t}^{D}v(x)
is well defined because of (
2.9
) and the fact that
v\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
. Now we want to change the order of integration in (
3.31
). To do so, let
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
such that
0\leq\eta\leq 1
,
\eta\equiv 1
in
\{x:\textrm{dist}(x,\supp\psi)<\textrm{dist}(\supp\psi,\partial D)/2\}
, and write

\displaystyle\psi(x)\big(v(x)-P_{t}^{D}v(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\psi(x)\big(\eta(x)v(x)-P_{t}^{D}[\eta\,v](x)\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt+\psi(x)P_{t}^{D}[(1-\eta)v](x).

This way
\eta v\in C^{2+\alpha}_{c}(D)
, hence

(3.32)

\displaystyle||\eta v-P_{t}^{D}[\eta\,v]||_{L^{\infty}(D)}\leq\min\{t||\Delta(\eta v)||_{L^{\infty}(D)},2\,||\eta\,v||_{L^{\infty}(D)}\}\lesssim 1\wedge t,

see e.g.
[
55
, Eq. (13.3)]
. Further, for
x\in\supp\psi
by (
2.9
) we have

(3.33)

\displaystyle|\psi(x)P_{t}^{D}[(1-\eta)v](x)|\lesssim\int_{D}\frac{\delta_{D}(y)}{t^{d/2+1}}e^{-\frac{c_{3}}{t}}|v(y)|dy\lesssim 1\wedge t,

where
c_{3}=c_{3}(\textrm{dist}(\supp\psi,\partial D),D)>0
and the constant of comparability is independent of
x\in\supp\psi
. Since
\int_{0}^{\infty}(1\wedge t)\nu(t)dt<\infty
, by (
3.32
) and (
3.33
), we can change the order of integration in (
3.31
) to get

\displaystyle\int_{D}u(x)\psi(x)dx=\int_{D}\psi(x)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(v(x)-P_{t}^{D}v(x)\big)\nu(t)dt\right)dx.

Hence (
3.28
) holds a.e. in
D
. Moreover, the right-hand side in (
3.28
) is continuous in
D
since we can apply the dominated convergence theorem due to (
3.32
) and (
3.33
). Therefore, there exists a version of
u
which is continuous in
D
.

Take now
\overline{f}\in C_{c}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\overline{f}=f
in a neighbourhood of
K
,
\supp\overline{f}\subset\subset K^{\prime}
, and such that
||\overline{f}||_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq c_{4}(K,K^{\prime},D)||f||_{C^{\alpha}(K^{\prime})}
. Define
\overline{u}=G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\phi}\overline{f}
, and note that
\overline{u}
solves
\phi(-\Delta)u=\overline{f}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the distributional sense by
[
9
, Proposition 2.5]
. Hence, by Theorem
3.1
we get that
\overline{u}\in C^{\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that

(3.34)

\displaystyle||\overline{u}||_{C^{\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim||\overline{f}||_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq c_{5}||f||_{C^{\alpha}(K^{\prime})},

where
c_{5}=c_{5}(d,\alpha,K^{\prime},D,\phi)>0
.

It remains to estimate the difference
u-\overline{u}
and, as a first step, we prove a representation for
\overline{u}
analogous to (
3.28
). Define
\overline{v}\coloneqq G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\phi^{*}}\overline{u}
and note that
\overline{v}
is well defined, since
\overline{v}=G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{f}
by Lemma
2.1
, and by a simple calculation it holds that

(3.35)

\displaystyle|\overline{v}(x)|\leq\int\limits_{\supp\overline{f}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d-2}}\,|\overline{f}(y)|dy\leq c_{6}\|\overline{f}\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}(1\wedge|x|^{-d+2}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
c_{6}=c_{6}(d,K^{\prime},D)>0
.
Further, we get that
(-\Delta)\overline{v}=\overline{f}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the distributional sense. Indeed, for
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{v}(x)(-\Delta)\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{f}(x)(-\Delta)\psi(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{f}(x)G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big((-\Delta)\psi\big)(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{f}(x)\psi(x)dx,

where the last equality follows from
[
25
, Proposition 2.8]
since
d\geq 3
. Hence
\overline{v}\in C^{2+\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, see e.g.
[
31
, Corollary 2.17]
and notice that, in the same way as in Theorem
3.1
, the bound for
\overline{v}
in
B(x,\frac{1}{2})
in
[
31
, Corollary 2.17]
does not depend on
x
since
\overline{f}\in C_{c}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. Now, similarly as in (
3.28
), we prove that

\displaystyle\overline{u}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\overline{v}-P_{t}\overline{v}\big)\nu(t)dt.

To this end, similarly as before, let
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{u}(x)\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{v}(x)\phi^{*}(-\Delta)\psi(x)

(3.36)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{v}(x)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\psi(x)-P_{t}\psi(x)\big)\nu(t)dt\right)dx.

Again, we want to change the order of integration in (
3.36
).
First, note that
\overline{v}
is bounded on the bounded set
U\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\textrm{dist}(x,\supp\psi)<2\textrm{diam}(\supp\psi)\}\supset\supp\psi
. Also,

\displaystyle||\psi-P_{t}\psi||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\min\{t||\Delta\psi||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},2\,||\psi||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\}\lesssim 1\wedge t.

Hence,

(3.37)

\displaystyle\int_{U}|\overline{v}(x)|\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\big|\psi(x)-P_{t}\psi(x)\big|\nu(t)dt\right)dx<\infty.

Second, on
U^{c}
we have
\psi=0
, and for
x\in U^{c}
and for
y\in\supp\psi
it holds that

(3.38)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c_{7}}(1\wedge|x|)\leq|x-y|\leq c_{7}(1\wedge|x|),

where
c_{7}=c_{7}(\supp\psi)>0
. Hence,

\displaystyle\hskip-40.00006pt\int_{U^{c}}|\overline{v}(x)|\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\big|\psi(x)-P_{t}\psi(x)\big|\nu(t)dt\right)dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{U^{c}}|\overline{v}(x)|\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{t}|\psi|(x)\nu(t)dt\right)dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{U^{c}}|\overline{v}(x)|\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{(4\pi t)^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{4t}}|\psi(y)|dy\right)\nu(t)dt\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{U^{c}}|\overline{v}(x)|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\psi(y)|j^{*}(|x-y|)dy\,dx

(3.39)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{U^{c}}|\overline{v}(x)|\big(1\wedge j^{*}(|x|)\big)dx<\infty,

where in the second to last line we used the definition of the jumping kernel
j^{*}
, and in the last line the properties (
3.38
), (
2.14
), and the fact that
\overline{v}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},(1\wedge j^{*}|x|)dx)
, see
[
9
, Lemma 2.4]
.

Finiteness of the integrals in (
3.37
) and (
3
) implies that we can change the order of integration in (
3.36
) to get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{u}(x)\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\overline{v}(x)\psi(x)-\overline{v}(x)P_{t}\psi(x)\big)dx\,\nu(t)dt

(3.40)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\psi(x)\overline{v}(x)-\psi(x)P_{t}\overline{v}(x)\big)dx\,\nu(t)dt.

We now change the order of integration in (
3
). This can be justified by repeating the same trick with a bump function
\eta
when we changed the order of integration in (
3.31
), see also (
3.32
) and (
3.33
). Thus, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{u}(x)\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\psi(x)\overline{v}(x)-\psi(x)P_{t}\overline{v}(x)\big)\nu(t)dt\,dx,

i.e.
\overline{u}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\overline{v}-P_{t}\overline{v}\big)\nu(t)dt
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Now we estimate
u-\overline{u}
. In what follows, we fix a minor error from
[
3
]
caused by a miscalculation in the last displayed equation on page 450 in
[
3
]
. Note that

\displaystyle u(x)-\overline{u}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(v(x)-P_{t}^{D}v(x))\nu(t)dt-\int_{0}^{\infty}(\overline{v}(x)-P_{t}\overline{v}(x))\nu(t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(v(x)-\overline{v}(x)-P_{t}^{D}(v-\overline{v})(x)\big)\nu(t)dt

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt+\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(P_{t}\overline{v}(x)-P_{t}^{D}\overline{v}(x)\big)\nu(t)dt

\displaystyle\eqqcolon I_{1}(x)+I_{2}(x),

where, as a consequence of the following calculations, both
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are well defined.

To estimate the first term
I_{1}
note that
w(x,t)\coloneqq P_{t}^{D}(v-\overline{v})(x)
solves the heat equation in
D
with a boundary condition
w(x,0)=(v-\overline{v})\in C^{2+\alpha}(D)
. Also,
v-\overline{v}
is harmonic in a neighborhood of
K
in the classical sense, hence smooth in a neighborhood of
K
. However,
v-\overline{v}\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
can explode at the boundary so we need to be careful if we want to apply the classical parabolic regularity theory to
w(x,t)
.

Take
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
such that
0\leq\eta\leq 1
,
\eta\equiv 1
in a neighbourhood of
K
, and
\supp\eta\subset K^{\prime}
. We have for all
x\in K

\displaystyle v(x)-\overline{v}(x)-P_{t}^{D}(v-\overline{v})(x)

\displaystyle=\left(v(x)-\overline{v}(x)-P_{t}^{D}\big[\eta(v-\overline{v})\big](x)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt-P_{t}^{D}\big[(1-\eta)(v-\overline{v})\big](x)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon J^{(1)}_{1}(x,t)-J^{(1)}_{2}(x,t).

Since
h\coloneqq\eta(v-\overline{v})\in C^{2+\alpha}_{c}(D)
we have for
x\in K

\displaystyle J^{(1)}_{1}(x,t)

\displaystyle=h(x)-P_{t}^{D}h(x)

\displaystyle=h(x)-P_{t}h(x)+\int_{D}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[p(t-\tau_{D},W_{\tau_{D}},x)\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}<t\}}\right]h(y)dy

\displaystyle=h(x)-P_{t}h(x)+\widetilde{P_{t}}^{D}h(x).

Here, we set
\widetilde{P_{t}}^{D}h(x)=\int_{D}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[p(t-\tau_{D},W_{\tau_{D}},x)\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}<t\}}\right]h(y)dy
which is well defined since
h\in L^{\infty}(D)
.
Note that
P_{t}
and partial derivatives commute. Also, for
x\in K
, we have
\textrm{diam}D\geq|x-W_{\tau_{D}}|\geq\textrm{dist}(K,\partial D)>0
, so every partial derivative with respect to
x
satisfies

(3.41)

\displaystyle|\partial^{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[p(t-\tau_{D},x,W_{\tau_{D}})\mathbf{1}_{\{t>\tau_{D}\}}\right]|\leq c(d,\beta,K,D)(1\wedge t).

Hence, by
[
55
, (13.3)]
and (
3.41
) we get

\displaystyle\|J^{(1)}_{1}(\cdot,t)\|_{C^{2+\alpha}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq\|h-P_{t}h\|_{C^{2+\alpha}(K)}+\|\widetilde{P_{t}}^{D}h\|_{C^{2+\alpha}(K)}

(3.42)

\displaystyle\leq c(d,K,D)(1\wedge t)\left(\|h\|_{C^{4+\alpha}(K)}+\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\right),

where
\|h\|_{C^{4+\alpha}(K)}
is finite since
h
is (classically) harmonic in a neighborhood of
K
, hence smooth.
Also, by the elliptic regularity, it follows that

(3.43)

\displaystyle\|h\|_{C^{4+\alpha}(K)}\leq c\|h\|_{L^{1}(K^{\prime})}\leq c(d,K,K^{\prime})\|v-\overline{v}\|_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)},

Recall that
\overline{v}=G_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{f}
, so

\displaystyle||v-\overline{v}||_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}

\displaystyle\leq||v||_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}+\|\overline{v}\|_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}

\displaystyle\leq c||u||_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}+\int_{D}\int_{D}G_{R^{d}}(x,y)|\overline{f}(y)|\delta_{D}(x)dx\,dy

(3.44)

\displaystyle\leq c(d,K,K^{\prime},D,\phi)\big(||u||_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}+||f||_{C^{\alpha}(K^{\prime})}\big).

Thus, by combining (
3
), (
3.43
) and (
3.44
), we get

\displaystyle\|J^{(1)}_{1}(\cdot,t)\|_{C^{2+\alpha}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq c(d,K,K^{\prime},D,\phi)(1\wedge t)\big(||u||_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}+||f||_{C^{\alpha}(K^{\prime})}\big).

For the term
J^{(1)}_{2}
, first note that
J^{(1)}_{2}\in C^{\infty}(D\times(0,\infty))
. Indeed, since
(1-\eta)(v-\overline{v})\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
, the upper bound in (
2.9
) implies that
\|J^{(1)}_{2}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\leq c(t)
, so by the semigroup property we have

\displaystyle J^{(1)}_{2}(x,t)

\displaystyle=P_{t-\varepsilon}^{D}\left(P_{\varepsilon}^{D}\big[(1-\eta)(v-\overline{v})\big]\right)

\displaystyle=P_{t-\varepsilon}(J^{(1)}_{2}(\cdot,\varepsilon))-\widetilde{P}_{t-\varepsilon}^{D}(J^{(1)}_{2}(\cdot,\varepsilon)).

Since

Further, since

Hence,

\displaystyle\leq c(d,K,K^{\prime},D)(1\wedge t)(\|u\|_{L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)}+\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}({K^{\prime}})}\big).

In order to estimate

\displaystyle P_{t}\overline{v}(x)

4.
Large solutions

In this section we solve the following problem

(4.1)

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u&=&-f(u)&\quad\text{in }D,\\
\frac{u}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma}&=&\infty&\quad\text{on }\partial D,\end{array}

in the distributional and in the pointwise sense, where
f
is a nonnegative
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
function such that
f(0)=0
,
f(t)>0
for
t>0
, and
f
satisfies the following condition:

(F)
.

There exist
0<m\leq M<\infty
such that

(1+m)f(t)\leq tf^{\prime}(t)\leq(1+M)f(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that, in essence, we are looking at generalisations of the classical superlinearity
f(t)=t^{p}
,
p>1
. Further, the condition
(F)
implies that
f
is nondecreasing, and that the functions
t\mapsto f(t)t^{-(1+M)}
and
t\mapsto f(t)t^{-(1+m)}
are nonincreasing and nondecreasing, respectively. The assumption
f(x)>0
,
x>0
, is not essential. More precisely, we can instead assume
f\not\equiv 0
and apply minimal technical changes at the beginning of the Subsection
4.1
, in particular, to the definition of the function
\varphi
near the origin.

In order to obtain the solution, we mimic the approach for the spectral fractional Laplacian and the power nonlinearity in
[
3
]
.
The main idea of the construction of a large solution is to build an auxiliary increasing sequence of weak solutions to (
4.1
) but with boundary conditions that are finite and increasing, and to find a supersolution to (
4.1
) (with an infinite boundary condition) which dominates the aforementioned auxiliary sequence of weak solutions. The goal is then to show that the auxiliary sequence converges to a solution to (
4.1
).

With this objective in mind, consider the sequence of semilinear problems:

(4.2)

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u_{j}&=&-f(u_{j})&\quad\text{in }D,\\
\frac{u_{j}}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma}&=&j&\quad\text{on }\partial D,\end{array}

where
j\in\mathbb{N}
and where we assume that the nonlinearity
f
satisfies the condition

(4.3)

\int_{0}^{1}f\left(\frac{1}{s^{2}\phi(s^{-2})}\right)s\,ds=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}f\left(\frac{1}{s\phi(s^{-1})}\right)\,ds<\infty.

Since the nonlinearity
f
is a nondecreasing function, the condition (
4.3
), together with (
2.12
) and
[
10
, Theorem 5.10]
, implies that the problems (
4.2
) have unique weak solutions

\displaystyle u_{j}=-G^{\phi}_{D}f(u_{j})+jP^{\phi}_{D}\sigma,\quad j\in\mathbb{N},

which are also continuous in
D
and nonnegative. In the following lemma, we prove that
(u_{j})_{j}
is an increasing sequence of functions. Note that this claim does not immediately follow by the argument in
[
3
, Chapter 7]
based on the increasing boundary conditions in (
4.2
). This is due to the fact that Kato’s inequality
[
10
, Proposition 5.4]
, cf.
[
3
, Lemma 29]
, holds only for the zero boundary condition problem.

Lemma 4.1
.

The sequence
(u_{j})_{j}
increases as
j\to\infty
.

Proof.

To prove the monotonicity of the sequence
(u_{j})_{j}
we first present the construction of the solution to (
4.2
) from
[
10
, Theorem 5.10]
. The idea is to approximate the problem (
4.2
) with a sequence of auxiliary zero boundary Dirichlet problems. Let
(\widetilde{f}_{k})_{k}
be a sequence of nonnegative, continuous and bounded functions on
D
such that
G^{\phi}_{D}\widetilde{f}_{k}\uparrow P^{\phi}_{D}(\sigma)
and let
u_{j}^{(k)}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, be a solution to the following linear problem

(4.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u_{j}^{(k)}=-f\big(u_{j}^{(k)}\big)+j\widetilde{f}_{k},&\textrm{in $D$},\\
\frac{u_{j}^{(k)}}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma}=0,&\textrm{on $\partial D$}.\end{cases}

Then, by using the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, we can find an appropriate subsequence of
(u_{j}^{(k)})_{k}
that converges to the solution
u_{j}
to (
4.2
). For details we refer to
[
10
, Theorem 5.10]
. Next, note that the function
w\coloneqq u_{j}^{(k)}-u_{j+1}^{(k)}
is a weak solution to the linear problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})w=f\big(u_{j+1}^{(k)}\big)-f\big(u_{j}^{(k)}\big)-\widetilde{f}_{k},&\textrm{in $D$},\\
\frac{w}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma}=0,&\textrm{on $\partial D$}.\end{cases}

Since
f
is nondecreasing and
\widetilde{f}_{k}
is nonnegative, by Kato’s inequality
[
10
, Proposition 5.4]
, we have

\displaystyle w^{+}=\big(u_{j}^{(k)}-u_{j+1}^{(k)}\big)^{+}

\displaystyle\leq G^{\phi}_{D}\left(\big(f\big(u_{j+1}^{(k)}\big)-f\big(u_{j}^{(k)}\big)-\widetilde{f}_{k}\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{u_{j}^{(k)}\geq u_{j+1}^{(k)}\}}\right)\leq 0,

which implies that
u_{j}^{(k)}\leq u_{j+1}^{(k)}
in
D
.

Now we use Cantor’s diagonal argument to show that
u_{j}\leq u_{j+1}
in
D
. By the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, see the proof of
[
10
, Theorem 5.10]
, there is a subsequence
\big(u_{1}^{(k_{n}^{(1)})}\big)_{n}
of
\big(u_{1}^{(k)}\big)_{k}
which converges to
u_{1}
. We have shown that for every
k_{n}^{(1)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that
u_{j}^{(k_{n}^{(1)})}\leq u_{j+1}^{(k_{n}^{(1)})}
, for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Analogously, there is a subsequence
\big(u_{2}^{(k_{n}^{(2)})}\big)_{n}
of
\big(u_{2}^{(k_{n}^{(1)})}\big)_{n}
which converges to
u_{2}
. By continuing this procedure we arrive to a subsequence
(k_{n}^{(n)})_{n}
for which we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{j}^{(k_{n}^{(n)})}

\displaystyle=u_{j},\quad j\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle u_{j}^{(k_{n}^{(n)})}

\displaystyle\leq u_{j+1}^{(k_{n}^{(n)})},\quad j\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence,
u_{j}\leq u_{j+1}
in
D
.
∎

The following result justifies the consideration of the pointwise boundary condition in (
4.2
). Additionally, we consider further regularity of the solution when
f\in C^{\alpha}
. Recall that the reason for working in the setting of classical Hölder regularity, instead of generalised Hölder spaces corresponding to
\phi
as in
[
4
]
and
[
40
]
, is that we want to obtain regularity results for all ranges of Matuszewska indices in (
2.6
).

Lemma 4.2
.

It holds that
\lim\limits_{x\to\partial D}\frac{u_{j}(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=j
. Further, if
f\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
, for some
\alpha>0
, and
\alpha+2\delta_{1}\notin\mathbb{N}
, then
u_{j}\in C^{\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(D)
. In particular, if
\alpha>{2\delta_{2}-2\delta_{1}}
then
u_{j}
is a pointwise solution to (
4.2
).

Proof.

Since
u_{j}\leq jP^{\phi}_{D}\sigma
, by monotonicity of
f
, the condition
(F)
and (
2.12
), it follows that

\displaystyle 0\leq G^{\phi}_{D}\left(f(u_{j})\right)\leq G^{\phi}_{D}(f(jP^{\phi}_{D}\sigma))\leq c(d,D,M,j)G^{\phi}_{D}\left(f(\delta_{D}^{-2}\phi(\delta_{D}^{-2})^{-1})\right).

Since the condition
(F)
implies that
t\mapsto f(t)t^{-(1+M)}
is nonincreasing, one can easily check that the function
t\mapsto f(t^{-2}\phi(t^{-2})^{-1})
satisfies the conditions
(U1)-(U4)
from
[
10
, Theorem 3.6]
. This in turn implies that

\displaystyle 0\leq\lim_{x\to\partial D}\frac{G^{\phi}_{D}\left(f(u_{j})\right)(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}\lesssim\lim_{x\to\partial D}\frac{G^{\phi}_{D}\left(f(\delta_{D}^{-2}\phi(\delta_{D}^{-2})^{-1})\right)(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=0.

Therefore,

\lim_{x\to\partial D}\frac{u_{j}(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=\lim_{x\to\partial D}\frac{P^{\phi}_{D}(j\sigma)(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=j.

For the second part of the lemma, recall that by
[
10
, Theorem 5.10]

u_{j}
is continuous, hence locally bounded. Also, recall that a weak solution is also a distributional one. Since
f\in C^{\alpha}
, we have
f(u_{j})\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(D)
, so Theorem
3.6
implies
u_{j}\in C^{\beta}(D)
for every
\beta\in(0,2\delta_{1})
. By bootstrapping this argument, we obtain that
u_{j}\in C^{\alpha+2\delta_{1}}(D)
. In particular, if
f\in C^{2\delta_{2}-2\delta_{1}+\varepsilon}
, for some
\varepsilon>0
, then
u_{j}\in C^{2\delta_{2}+\varepsilon}(D)
so
u_{j}
is a pointwise solution to (
4.2
) by Remark
2.2
.
∎

4.1.
Construction of a supersolution

A key step in obtaining a solution to (
4.1
) is the construction of a supersolution to the same problem. In this subsection, we find a Keller-Osserman-type condition that guarantees that such a supersolution exists. Let us first introduce several functions that are used to construct this supersolution. Recall that the function
f\in C^{1}
satisfies the condition
(F)
. Define the antiderivative of
f
by

\displaystyle F(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(s)ds,\quad t>0,

and set
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
as

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{F(s)}},\quad t>0.

The function
\varphi
is monotone decreasing so it possesses a decreasing inverse

(4.5)

\psi=\varphi^{-1,}

which also satisfies

\lim\limits_{t\downarrow 0}\psi(t)=\infty,\ \lim\limits_{t\uparrow\infty}\psi(t)=0.

Note that
(F)
implies that

(4.6)

\varphi(t)\asymp\sqrt{\frac{t}{f(t)}},\qquad t>0,

and

(4.7)

\frac{t^{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(t)}{\psi(t)}\asymp\frac{t^{2}\psi^{\prime}(t)^{2}}{\psi(t)^{2}}\asymp 1,\qquad t>0.

Furthermore, the assumption
(F)
can be also written in terms of
\varphi
or
\psi
, that is

(4.8)

\frac{m}{2}\frac{\varphi(t)}{t}\leq|\varphi^{\prime}(t)|\leq\frac{M}{2}\frac{\varphi(t)}{t},\qquad t>0,

and

(4.9)

\frac{2}{M}\frac{\psi(t)}{t}\leq|\psi^{\prime}(t)|\leq\frac{2}{m}\frac{\psi(t)}{t},\qquad t>0,

see
[
2
, Remark 1.1]
.

Recall that
V:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is the renewal function defined in (
2.33
). In the following, we construct a supersolution to (
4.1
) with the boundary behaviour comparable to the function

(4.10)

U(x)=\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x))),\qquad x\in D.

Similarly as in
[
24
, Lemma 3.1]
the following Keller-Osserman-type condition implies the required integrability condition for the supersolution.

Lemma 4.3
.

The function
U
from (
4.10
) satisfies
U\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
if and only if the following condition holds:

(4.11)

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{\phi^{-1}(\varphi(t)^{-2})}<\infty.

Proof.

For
\eta>0
set
D_{\eta}=\{x\in D:\ \delta_{D}(x)<\eta\}
.
Since
\psi,V\in L_{loc}^{\infty}(0,\infty)
and
\delta\in L^{\infty}(D)
, it follows that
U\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(D)
. Therefore, it is enough to show that (
4.11
) holds if and only if
U\in L^{1}(D_{\eta_{0}},\delta_{D})
, for some small
\eta_{0}>0
. By applying the coarea formula in the first equality and using (
2.34
) several times, we arrive to

\displaystyle\int_{D_{\eta_{0}}}U(x)\delta_{D}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\eta_{0}}dt\int_{\{x\in D:\delta(x)=t\}}\psi(V(t))t\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(dx)\asymp\int_{0}^{\eta_{0}}\psi(\Phi(t))t\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\psi(\Phi(\eta_{0}))}^{\infty}s\,\Phi^{-1}(\varphi(s))(\Phi^{-1})^{\prime}(\varphi(s))|\varphi^{\prime}(s)|ds

\displaystyle{\asymp}\int_{\psi(\Phi(\eta_{0}))}^{\infty}\Phi^{-1}(\varphi(s))(\Phi^{-1})^{\prime}(\varphi(s))\varphi(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\psi(\Phi(\eta_{0}))}^{\infty}\frac{\Phi^{-1}(\varphi(s))\varphi(s)}{\Phi^{\prime}(\Phi^{-1}(\varphi(s)))}ds\asymp\int_{\psi(\Phi(\eta_{0}))}^{\infty}(\Phi^{-1}(\varphi(s)))^{2}ds

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{\psi(\Phi(\eta_{0}))}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{{\phi^{-1}(\varphi(s)^{-2}))}},

where in the second to last approximate equality we used
\Phi^{\prime}(t)\asymp\frac{\Phi(t)}{t}
, for
0<t<1
, see (
2.5
). The result now follows by (
4.11
).
∎

Next, we prove that, under a slightly stronger Keller-Osserman-type condition,
U
has a certain behaviour near the boundary of
D
suitable for the construction of a supersolution.

Lemma 4.4
.

If

(4.12)

\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{\phi^{-1}(\varphi(t)^{-2})}\lesssim\frac{r}{\phi^{-1}(\varphi(r)^{-2})},\qquad r\geq 1,

then there exist constants
C>0
and
\eta>0
such that

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})U(x)\geq-Cf(U(x)),\quad x\in D_{\eta}.

Proof.

Let us fix
\eta>0
and
x\in D_{\eta}
, where
D_{\eta}
is defined as in Lemma
4.3
. By following the approach in
[
24
, Proposition 3.2]
, we decompose
D=\cup_{i=1}^{3}D_{i}
such that

\displaystyle D_{1}=\left\{y\in D:\delta_{D}(y)>\tfrac{3}{2}\delta_{D}(x)\right\},

\displaystyle D_{2}=\left\{y\in D:\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{D}(x)\leq\delta_{D}(y)\leq\tfrac{3}{2}\delta_{D}(x)\right\},

\displaystyle D_{3}=\left\{y\in D:\delta_{D}(y)<\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{D}(x)\right\}.

Note that
U\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
by (
4.12
) and Lemma
4.3
. Furthermore,
U\in C^{1,1}(D)
, since
\psi
and
V
are twice differentiable by
(F)
and by
[
39
, Lemma 2.5]
, respectively, and that
\delta_{D}
is a
C^{1,1}(\overline{D})
function by
[
27
, Theorem 8.4 in Chapter 7]
. Therefore, by (
2.22
) and the fact that
t\mapsto\psi(V(t))
is nonincreasing we have that

\displaystyle-\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})U(x)

\displaystyle=\textrm{P.V.}\int_{D}(U(y)-U(x))J_{D}(x,y)dy-\kappa(x)U(x)

\displaystyle\leq\textrm{P.V.}\int_{D_{2}}(U(y)-U(x))J_{D}(x,y)dy+\int_{D_{3}}(U(y)-U(x))J_{D}(x,y)dy

\displaystyle=:J_{2}+J_{3}.

First, split
J_{2}
into two parts

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle=\textrm{P.V.}\int\limits_{B_{x}}(U(y)-U(x))J_{D}(x,y)dy+\!\!\int\limits_{D_{2}\setminus B_{x}}(U(y)-U(x))J_{D}(x,y)dy\eqqcolon J^{1}_{2}+J^{2}_{2},

where
B_{x}\coloneqq B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)
. By symmetry, we have

\textrm{P.V.}\int_{B_{x}}\nabla U(x)\cdot(y-x)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t,x,y)\mu(t)dt\right)dy=0,

which, together with (
2.23
) and (
2.8
), implies that

\displaystyle J^{1}_{2}

\displaystyle=\textrm{P.V.}\int_{B_{x}}\big(U(y)-U(x)-\nabla U(x)\cdot(y-x)\big)J_{D}(x,y)dy

\displaystyle\qquad+\textrm{P.V.}\int_{B_{x}}\nabla U(x)\cdot(y-x)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}[p(t-\tau_{D},y,W_{\tau_{D}})\mathbf{1}_{\{t>\tau_{D}\}}]\mu(t)dt\right)dy.

Since
J_{D}(x,y)\lesssim j(|x-y|)
by (
2.24
), the first term in
J^{1}_{2}
can be dealt with in the same way as in
[
24
]
in order to obtain that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B_{x}}\big(U(y)-U(x)-\nabla U(x)\cdot(y-x)\big)J_{D}(x,y)dy\right|\lesssim\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x)))V(\delta_{D}(x))^{-2}.

For the second term in
J^{1}_{2}
, note that
\nabla U=\psi^{\prime}(V(\delta_{D}))V^{\prime}(\delta_{D})\nabla\delta_{D}
, and

\displaystyle\max_{t\in(0,\infty)}\frac{p(t,y,W_{\tau_{D}})}{1\wedge t}\leq c(d)\max_{t\in(0,\infty)}\frac{t^{-d/2}e^{-\delta_{D}(x)^{2}/8t}}{1\wedge t}\leq c(d,D)\delta_{D}(x)^{d+2},

since
|y-W_{\tau_{D}}|\geq\delta_{D}(y)\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{D}(x)
. This, together with (
4.8
) and (
2.35
), implies that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{B_{x}}\nabla U(x)\cdot(y-x)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}[p(t-\tau_{D},y,W_{\tau_{D}}\mathbf{1}_{\{t>\tau_{D}\}}]\mu(t)dt\right)dy\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x)))\delta_{D}(x)^{-1}\int_{0}^{\delta_{D}(x)/2}\frac{h^{d}}{\delta_{D}(x)^{d+2}}\asymp\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x)))\delta_{D}(x)^{-2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x)))V(\delta_{D}(x))^{-2}.

Thus,
J^{1}_{2}\lesssim\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x)))V(\delta_{D}(x))^{-2}
. Since
J_{D}(x,y)\lesssim j(|x-y|)
, by applying
[
24
, Eq. (3.6)]
, we obtain the same upper bound for
J^{2}_{2}
, and therefore
J_{2}
.

For
J_{3}
we repeat the analogous calculation from
[
24
, Proposition 3.2]
. Let
Q\in\partial D
be the projection of
x
on
\partial D
, that is
x=Q+\delta(x)\nabla\delta(x)
, and
\gamma=\gamma_{Q}
the
C^{1,1}
function such that

B(Q,R)\cap D=\{y=(\widetilde{y},y_{d})\in B(Q,R)\text{ in }CS_{Q}:y_{d}>\gamma(\widetilde{y})\},

where
CS_{Q}
denotes the coordinate system with
Q=0
and
\nabla\delta(x)=e_{d}
. Recall that the pair
(R,\Lambda)
, where
\Lambda
is such that
|\nabla\gamma_{Q}(\widetilde{x})-\nabla\gamma_{Q}(\widetilde{y})|\leq\Lambda|\widetilde{x}-\widetilde{y}|
, is called the characteristics of the
C^{1,1}
set
D
. Set

\omega=\{y\in D:y=Q_{y}+\delta(y)\nabla\delta(y),\ Q_{y}\in B(0,R)\cap\partial D\}

and

J_{3}=\int_{D_{3}\setminus\omega}(U(y)-U(x))J_{D}(x,y)dy+\int_{D_{3}\cap\omega}(U(y)-U(x))J_{D}(x,y)dy=:J_{3}^{1}+J_{3}^{2}.

First note that, for
\eta
small enough (depending only on
D
), we have that
|y-x|>\delta_{D}(x)\vee\delta_{D}(y)
for every
y\in D_{3}\setminus\omega
and, therefore, (
2.24
) implies that
J_{D}(x,y)\lesssim\delta_{D}(x)\delta_{D}(y)
. Since
U
explodes at the boundary, it follows that

(4.13)

\displaystyle J_{3}^{1}\lesssim\delta_{D}(x)\int_{D_{3}\setminus\omega}U(y)\delta_{D}(y)dy\leq\delta_{D}(x)||U||_{L^{1}(D_{\eta},\delta_{D}(x)dx)}\lesssim f(U(x)).

Similarly, again by choosing
\eta
small enough, one can show that there exists a constant
C=C(\Lambda)
such that

(4.14)

|y-x|\geq C(|\delta(x)-\delta(y)|+|\overline{Q_{y}}|),\ \ y\in D_{3}\cap\omega,

see
[
24
, Eq. (3.8)]
. Therefore, by (
2.24
) we have that

\displaystyle J_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{D_{3}\cap w}U(y)J_{D}(x,y)dy\lesssim\delta_{D}(x)\int_{D_{3}\cap w}U(y)j(|y-x|)\delta_{D}(y)dy

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta_{D}(x)\int_{D_{3}\cap w}\psi(V(\delta(y)))\frac{\phi((|\delta(x)-\delta(y)|+|\overline{Q_{y}}|)^{-2})}{(|\delta(x)-\delta(y)|+|\overline{Q_{y}}|)^{d+2}}\delta_{D}(y)dy

\displaystyle\asymp\delta_{D}(x)\int_{0}^{\delta(x)/2}\psi(V(t))t\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\phi((|\delta(x)-t|+s)^{-2})}{(|\delta(x)-t|+s)^{d+2}}s^{d-2}dsdt

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta_{D}(x)\int_{0}^{\delta(x)/2}\psi(V(t))t\frac{\phi((\delta(x)-t)^{-2})}{|\delta(x)-t|^{3}}dt\asymp\frac{\phi(\delta(x)^{-2})}{\delta(x)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\delta(x)/2}\psi(V(t))tdt,

where in the second line we used (
4.14
), (
2.15
) and the fact that
j
is decreasing, and in the first term in the fourth line we used
[
10
, Eq. (A.10) & (A.15)]
.
By a change of variable as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, we get that

\displaystyle J_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\phi(\delta(x)^{-2})}{\delta(x)^{2}}\int_{\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x)/2)))}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{{\phi^{-1}(\varphi(s)^{-2}))}}

\displaystyle{\lesssim}\frac{\phi(\delta(x)^{-2})}{\delta(x)^{2}}\frac{\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x))))}{{\phi^{-1}(\varphi(\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x))))^{-2})}}

\displaystyle\asymp\phi(\delta(x)^{-2})\psi(V(\delta(x)))\overset{\eqref{e:V-phi}}{\asymp}\psi(V(\delta(x)))V(\delta(x))^{-2}.

The result now follows by applying (
4.6
), since

\frac{\psi(V(\delta(x)))}{V(\delta(x))^{2}}=\frac{U(x)}{\varphi(U(x))^{2}}\asymp f(U(x)).

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume that a function
u\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
satisfies
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(D)
and

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u(x)\geq-Cf(u(x)),\quad\delta_{D}(x)<\eta,

for some
C>0
and
\eta>0
. Then, there exists a function
\overline{u}\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
such that

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\overline{u}(x)\geq-f(\overline{u}(x)),\quad\text{in $D$.}

Proof.

Let
D_{\eta}\coloneqq\{x\in D:\delta_{D}(x)<\eta\}
. Take
\lambda>0
, hence

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})(\lambda u)=\lambda\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u\geq-\lambda Cf(u),\quad\text{in $D_{\eta}$}.

Recall that, by
(F)
,
t\mapsto f(t)t^{-1-m}
is nondecreasing, so by choosing
\lambda
large enough we get
f(\lambda u)\geq\lambda Cf(u)
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})(\lambda u)\geq-f(\lambda u),\quad\text{in $D_{\eta}$}.

Let now
\mu\coloneqq\lambda||\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u||_{L^{\infty}(D\setminus D_{\eta})}
and define
\overline{u}\coloneqq\mu G^{\phi}_{D}\mathbf{1}+\lambda u
. By
[
10
, Proposition 2.14]
and by Theorem
3.6
we have
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})G^{\phi}_{D}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
in
D
in the distributional and in the pointwise sense. Hence, on
D_{\eta}
we have

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\overline{u}=\mu+\lambda\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u\geq\lambda\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u\geq-f(\lambda u)\geq-f(\overline{u}),

and on
D\setminus D_{\eta}
we have

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\overline{u}=\mu+\lambda\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u\geq 0\geq-f(\overline{u}).

In other words,
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\overline{u}\geq-f(\overline{u})
in
D
.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
f
satisfy
(F)
and (
4.12
). Then there is a function
\overline{u}\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)\cap C^{1,1}(D)
such that

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\overline{u}\geq-f(\overline{u}),\quad\text{in $D$},

both in the distributional and pointwise sense. Furthermore, if the function
\psi
from (
4.5
) satisfies

(4.15)

\lim_{s\to 0+}\frac{\psi(s)}{s^{2}\phi^{-1}(s^{-2})}=\infty,

then

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\partial D}\frac{\overline{u}(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=\infty.

Proof.

By Lemmas
4.4
and
4.5
we have that
\overline{u}
is of the form
\overline{u}=\mu G^{\phi}_{D}\mathbf{1}+\lambda\psi(V(\delta_{D}))
.
The boundary behaviour follows directly from (
2.34
), (
2.12
) and (
4.15
) by substituting
s=V(\delta(x))
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Note that in the standard fractional setting, i.e. for
\phi(t)=t^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
,
\alpha\in(0,2)
, and for
f(t)=t^{p}
, the Keller-Osserman-type conditions (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) are equivalent to
p>1+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}
. Further, conditions (
4.3
) and (
4.15
) are equivalent to
p<\tfrac{2}{2-\alpha}
. In the more general setting of this article, the conditions (
4.11
), (
4.12
), (
4.3
), and (
4.15
) are independent.

4.2.
Existence of a large solution

In what follows we assume conditions
(F)
, (
4.3
), (
4.12
) and (
4.15
) on the functions
f
and
\phi
, as well as
f\in C^{1\vee(2\delta_{2}-2\delta_{1}+\varepsilon)}
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Let us recall the sequence
(u_{j})_{j}
, where
u_{j}=-G^{\phi}_{D}f(u_{j})+jP^{\phi}_{D}\sigma
is the weak solution to the approximated problem (
4.2
). Note that due to Lemma
4.2
it is also a solution in the pointwise sense. Further, by Lemma
4.1

(u_{j})_{j}
increases so the limit

(4.16)

\displaystyle u(x)=\lim_{j\to\infty}u_{j}(x),\quad x\in D,

so the function
u
on
D
is well defined but possibly infinite. However, due to the following uniform upper bound on
(u_{j})_{j}
in terms of the supersolution
\overline{u}=\mu G^{\phi}_{D}\mathbf{1}+\lambda\psi(V(\delta_{D}))
from Corollary
4.6
,
u
is locally bounded on
D
.

Lemma 4.8
.

For every
j\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that
0\leq u_{j}\leq\overline{u}
in
D
. In particular,
0\leq u\leq\overline{u}
and
u\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)\cap L^{\infty}_{loc}(D)
.

Proof.

Fix
j\in\mathbb{N}
. By Lemma
4.2
and by Corollary
4.6
we have that
u_{j}\leq\overline{u}
on
D_{\eta}
for some
\eta=\eta(j)>0
small enough and

(4.17)

\displaystyle\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})(\overline{u}-u_{j})\geq f(u_{j})-f(\overline{u}),

pointwisely in
D
. Since
u_{j}-\overline{u}\in C(D)
and
\lim_{x\to\partial D}u_{j}-\overline{u}=-\infty
, there exists a
x_{0}\in D
such that
\max_{x\in D}\big(u_{j}(x)-\overline{u}(x)\big)=u_{j}(x_{0})-\overline{u}(x_{0})\eqqcolon m
. Suppose that
m>0
. Then, since
x_{0}
is the point of maximum of
u_{j}-\overline{u}
, by (
4.17
) and the monotonicity of
f
, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle>\textrm{{P.V.}}\int_{D}\big(u_{j}(x_{0})-\overline{u}(x_{0})-u_{j}(y)+\overline{u}(y)\big)J_{D}(x_{0},y)+\kappa(x_{0})\big(u_{j}(x_{0})-\overline{u}(x_{0})\big)

\displaystyle=\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\big(u_{j}-\overline{u}\big)(x_{0})\geq f(u_{j}(x_{0}))-f(\overline{u}(x_{0}))\geq 0,

which is a contradiction. Hence,
m\leq 0
, i.e.
u_{j}\leq\overline{u}
in
D
. By passing to the limit, we get
u\leq\overline{u}
in
D
. Since
\overline{u}\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)\cap C^{1,1}(D)
, we also have
u\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)\cap L^{\infty}_{loc}(D)
.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

The function
u\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
defined by (
4.16
) is a distributional and a pointwise solution to the semilinear problem

(4.18)

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u&=&-f(u)&\quad\text{in }D,\\
\frac{u}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma}&=&\infty&\quad\text{on }\partial D.\end{array}

Proof.

Recall that the function
u_{j}
is a weak solution to (
4.2
), hence also a solution in the distributional sense, so

\displaystyle\int_{D}u_{j}(x)\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\xi(x)dx=\int_{D}f(u_{j}(x))\xi(x)dx,\quad\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D),\,j\in\mathbb{N}.

The fact that
u
is the solution in the distributional sense of (
4.18
) easily follows from the dominated convergence theorem. Specifically, since
0\leq u_{j}\leq u\leq\overline{u}
we have that

(4.19)

\displaystyle\int_{D}u_{j}(x)\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\xi(x)dx\overset{j\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\int_{D}u(x)\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\xi(x)dx,

where we used that
|\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\xi|\lesssim\delta_{D}
by
[
10
, Lemma 2.11]
and
\overline{u}\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
by Corollary
4.6
. On the other hand, the convergence

(4.20)

\displaystyle\int_{D}f(u_{j}(x))\xi(x)dx\overset{j\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\int_{D}f(u(x))\xi(x)dx

follows from the monotonicity of
f
and local boundedness of
\overline{u}
. Since
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u=-f(u)
in
D
in the distributional sense and
f(u)\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(D)
by Lemma
4.8
, by a bootstrap of a Theorem
3.6
we get that
u\in C^{2\delta_{2}+\varepsilon}(D)
. Hence,
u
solves
\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})u=-f(u)
also in the pointwise sense. The required pointwise boundary behaviour of
u
follows directly from

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to\partial D}\frac{u(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}\geq\liminf_{x\to\partial D}\frac{u_{j}(x)}{P^{\phi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=j,\qquad\forall\,j\in\mathbb{N}.

The pointwise boundary behaviour implies the weak
L^{1}
boundary behaviour in the sense of (
2.28
).
∎

Remark 4.10
.

Note that for the obtained solution
u
, it does not hold that
f(u)\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
. Indeed, since
u_{j}+G^{\phi}_{D}\big(f({u_{j}})\big)=jP^{\phi}_{D}\sigma
, and
\uparrow\lim_{j}u_{j}=u<\infty
and
\lim_{j}jP^{\phi}_{D}\sigma=\infty
, by the monotonicity of
u_{j}
we have
\lim_{j}G^{\phi}_{D}\big(f({u_{j}})\big)=G^{\phi}_{D}\big(f({u})\big)=\infty
. Hence, by
[
10
, Lemma 2.8]
it follows
f(u)\not\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
.

Our obtained large solution is the minimal solution to (
4.18
) as we prove in the next theorem.

Theorem 4.11
.

The solution
u
from Theorem
4.9
is the minimal locally bounded distributional and pointwise solution to the problem (
4.18
) such that the boundary condition on
\partial D
holds pointwisely.

Proof.

Let
U
be any locally bounded distributional (and by bootstrapping Theorem
3.6
a pointwise) solution to (
4.18
). By repeating the proof of Lemma
4.8
we get that
u_{j}\leq U
in
D
, hence by taking the limit we have
u\leq U
.
∎

In the next theorem, we prove that for every supersolution, there exists a maximal solution that is smaller than the supersolution. However, this does not prove that there are many large solutions, i.e. the important questions of the uniqueness of the solution to (
4.18
), as well as the true maximality (i.e. the existence of a solution which is larger than any other solution) remain open.

Theorem 4.12
.

For every locally bounded pointwise and distributional supersolution
\overline{v}
of the problem (
4.18
), there exist the maximal solution of (
4.18
) smaller then
\overline{v}
.

Proof.

The key ingredient in the proof is Zorn’s lemma. Let
\overline{v}\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
be a locally bounded pointwise and distributional supersolution of (
4.18
) and

\mathcal{K}=\{v\in L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx):v\leq\overline{v}\text{ and $v$ solves \eqref{eq:large problem in thm} distributionally}\}.

Note that by the bootstrap argument every element of
\mathcal{K}
is also a pointwise solution to (
4.18
). Also,
\mathcal{K}
is nonempty since
u\in\mathcal{K}
. Indeed, we can repeat the proof of Lemma
4.8
to get that
u_{j}\leq\overline{v}
, hence
u\leq\overline{v}
.

Let
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a totally ordered subset of
\mathcal{K}
. We will show that this set has a supremum. Define
v\coloneqq\sup_{i\in I}v_{i}
and note that
v\leq\overline{v}
. Hence,
v
is locally bounded and in
L^{1}(D,\delta_{D}(x)dx)
. Further, note that we can choose an increasing sequence
(v_{n})_{n}
in
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
such that
v=\lim_{n}v_{n}
in
D
by the exhaustion of
D
argument.

Each
v_{n}
solves (
4.18
) distributionally so

\displaystyle\int_{D}v_{n}(x)\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\xi(x)dx=\int_{D}f(v_{n}(x))\xi(x)dx,\quad\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D),\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

By letting
n\to\infty
in the previous inequality, since we may use the dominated convergence theorem, we get that

\displaystyle\int_{D}v(x)\phi(-\left.\Delta\right|_{D})\xi(x)dx=\int_{D}f(v(x))\xi(x)dx,\quad\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D),

i.e.
v
is a distributional solution to (
4.18
), and since it also bounded by
\overline{v}
, by bootstrapping Theorem
3.6
, we have that
v
is also a pointwise solution. Hence, by Zorn’s lemma, there exists the maximal element in
\mathcal{K}
.
∎
