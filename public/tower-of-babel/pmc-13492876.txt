Exploring the cycle: “Perceptions, perspectives, and practices of substance use in young adults”
Authors: Jayanti Semwal, Vidisha Vallabh, Deepti Nagrath, Akanksha Uniyal, Ashok K. Srivastava, Rajeev Prasad Bijalwan, Deep Shikha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Substance abuse among adolescents and young people is a growing public health concern, with significant implications for both individuals and society. This study sought to investigate the Perceptions, Perspectives, and Practices (3Ps) related to substance use, as well as explore contextual factors influencing drug and alcohol engagement among youth in the Dehradun district of Uttarakhand, India.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted in semi-urban and rural areas of Dehradun, screening 14,203 participants aged 10–24 years through multistage stratified random sampling. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires, which included the standardized and locally-adapted CRAFT and ASSIST screening instruments.RESULTS:Among the 3290 participants with complete socio-demographic data, 77.0% of males and 41.5% of females reported a family history of drug abuse (P < 0.0001). About 85.4% of participants in the 20–24 age group reported such a history. Regarding perceptions, 70.8% identified cannabis as a highly addictive substance (n = 1959). Knowledge of side effects was limited: less than one-third recognized key physical harms (n = 1493). Analysis of attitudes (n = 3236) revealed that 30.1% to 34.8% believed some drugs like cannabis are not addictive, while 60.6% agreed that even first-time use can lead to addiction. Curiosity, peer pressure, and family history emerged as the most common reasons for initiation, especially among males.CONCLUSION:These findings emphasize the need for targeted educational interventions—particularly about long-term consequences—and for strengthening protective factors in families and peer groups. Systematic, context-aware prevention strategies are essential to reduce substance abuse and its health and social impact among young people in India.

Introduction

Substance abuse is a global behaviour problem that affects many countries and has serious concern for society at large. It’s a growing global crisis among young people with profound consequences. Factors like peer pressure, mental health issues, academic stress, and family environment contribute to its onset.[1] The glamorization of substance use on social media further exacerbates the problem. Once initiated, substance abuse can lead to severe health issues, academic failure, and social isolation.[2]

According to World Health Organization, 147 million people, 2.5% of the world population consume cannabis (annual prevalence) compared with 0.2% cocaine, and 0.2% opiates.[3] The Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study, 2019 estimates alcohol-attributable burden (3.9% to 69.8%), as the second-highest risk factor among all those that are affect by Disability-Adjusted Living Years in adolescents and young adults (10–24 years of age).[4] Over 3 million deaths globally in 2016 were caused by alcohol use, representing about one in 20 deaths. To elaborate, an estimated 585,000 deaths in 2017 were estimated to drug use.[4]

India too is experiencing a rise in substance use, particularly in alcohol and tobacco, driven by economic growth and changing social norms India is undergoing a rapid development, and has seen a major rise in alcohol and tobacco consumption.[5] Understanding these patterns and impact of substance use during adolescence is critical for developing effective interventions and policies to moderate these adverse outcomes.

To expand, according to National Family Health Survey (NFHS5) (2019–2021),[6] 18.8% of men and 1.3% of women drink alcohol, whereas 38% of men and 8.9% of women consume tobacco. In India, according to a nationwide survey, 31 million people (2.8% of total population) reported using cannabis in 2018, and 0.25% showed signs of cannabis dependence.[5] A recent study of 2023 in India, showed the anticipated prevalence of substance use in Uttarakhand was found to be 32%–37%. The most common substance use was alcohol (61.7%) and Tobacco Smoking (15.6%) and followed by (1.2%) of Cannabis.[7]

Research indicates that alcohol and tobacco are the most commonly used substances, but there is a growing concern about the increasing use of cannabis and other drugs which are often ignored and concealed.[8] Additionally, the reasons behind the habit vary among individuals, with factors such as curiosity, peer pressure, and self-medication playing significant roles. By gaining a deeper understanding of these patterns, tailored prevention and intervention programs can be developed to address the specific needs of young people.

There is limited data available on the factors exploring substance abuse Uttarakhand, highlighting the need for an updated and comprehensive research. We thus aim to explore the Perceptions, Perspectives, and Practices of adolescents and youth aged 10–24 years in Dehradun district, Uttarakhand, India.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study among adolescents and young adults (aged 10–24) residing in the peri urban area-Doiwala and rural area -Sahaspur of Dehradun district, Uttarakhand, India. Data collection spanned two years from December 2021 to 23.

Study participants and sampling

Study participants

A total of 14,203 adolescents and young adults (10–24 years) from Doiwala (n = 6023) and Sahaspur (n = 8180) blocks participated in the study [Table 1].

A multistage stratified random sampling technique was employed to select the study areas (blocks). Two blocks were randomly selected from Dehradun district—one representing a semi-urban area and the other a rural area. From each selected block, one primary health center/Sub enters, two intermediate colleges, and one degree college were randomly chosen. This sampling yielded a total study population of 14,203 individuals (6023 in Doiwala and 8180 in Sahaspur). A comprehensive explanation of the sampling technique is provided in a previous manuscript.[9]

Data collection tool and technique

Interviewers administered self-reported questions to collect data. Respondents from Sahaspur and Doiwala blocks, as well as those from the intermediate schools and degree colleges, participated. The Care, Relax, Alone, Forget, Friends, Trouble (CRAFT) (for less than 18 years) questionnaire and the Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST) (more than 18 years) were used to evaluate substance use and associated variables.

Before commencing the main survey, a pilot study was carried out in both selected regions to assess the practicality, clarity, and reliability of the data collection instruments. During this initial phase, two standardized primary screening tools—the CRAFT and ASSIST questionnaires—were reviewed and adapted to suit the local context. The CRAFT tool, intended to evaluate substance use among adolescents, showed strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87, suggesting that its items consistently measured the target concept. Meanwhile, the ASSIST questionnaire, which assesses a wider spectrum of substance use behaviours, yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.79, indicating satisfactory reliability for the intended population. These outcomes support the use of both tools in the main study and help ensure data accuracy and consistency. In summary, the pilot testing results, along with the socio-demographic insights, enhance the overall trustworthiness of the research by confirming that robust and validated tools were employed.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from Swami Rama Himalayan University (SRHU/HIMS/E-1/2021/37) and school/college principals. Written informed consent/assent was secured from all participants, including parental consent for minors. Participants who declined consent were excluded from the study.

Males were significantly more likely than females to report a family history of drug abuse. Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into substance use and its perceptions among adolescents and young adults (10–24 years old) in Uttarakhand, India. A lack of perception about long-term effects of drug abuse among youth is distressing. There is discrepancy in views on addiction, with many participants believing cannabis isn’t addictive, despite acknowledging the risk of addiction from first-time use. This misconception may be rooted in recent public exposure, media attention, or cultural shifts, where cannabis use has become more visible or stigmatized in Indian society, while awareness of harms associated with legal substances like tobacco or alcohol remains relatively low.[10] Misconceptions about cannabis may also reflect the lack of comprehensive substance education in local curricula.

Interestingly, some participants identified ibuprofen—a common, over-the-counter non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug—as an addictive substance. This suggests confusion between physical dependence, medical use, and addiction among adolescents, highlighting the urgent need for targeted health education that clarifies these differences. Similar cognitive gaps have been observed in studies from other Indian regions, where adolescents have confused prescription drug use with abuse or addiction.

Though self-reported data may be subject to underreporting, our findings reveal, in alignment with recent all-India surveys, a worrying upward trend in substance use, particularly alcohol and tobacco.[10] Curiosity, stress, and seeking pleasure are the most common reasons for initial use, particularly among younger participants. Other factors like lack of confidence, lack of entertainment facilities, easy availability, and peer pressure also play a dominating role.[11] Studies have also depicted the above factors as a primary factor in the early initiation of usage.[12] In a qualitative study done on 13 juvenile males in India in 2024, peer pressure appeared to be as the main cause of substance abuse and anti-social behavior.[13]

Our study’s findings are also consistent on the relationship between substance use and family history, emphasizing the family’s influence on children’s lifestyles and habits. A complexity of other undocumented factors such as advertisements, programs and movies featuring “role models” also promote the use of tobacco and alcohol and can be attributed to this rising epidemic.[14] The feeling of “being cool” and the desperateness of being “included” are also big contributors.[15]

The study also highlights the importance of gender-specific approaches, as males and females displayed some differences in perceptions and reasons for drug use. Males are more likely to initiate this habit, which are consistent with other studies.[16] However, the lack of numbers of female substance abusers can be credited to concealment of information due to taboo.[17]

Gaps in intervention approaches

This study also exposes critical gaps in current approaches to prevention and intervention. Existing strategies in India mainly emphasize on the management of physical withdrawal symptoms in specialized centers, often overlooking psychosocial dimensions such as motivation, coping skills, and family dynamics.[18] The school system, incorporating teachers and parents, represents a key face for prevention and early intervention and must expand its role in providing comprehensive education and monitoring during this vulnerable developmental window.

The young demographic is particularly vulnerable to substance abuse initiation and experimentation. Implementation of educational programs to debunk myths about drug addiction, highlighting the consequences of drug abuse and emphasizing its long-term effects is the need of the hour.

Considered to be a combination of physical, environmental, social and mental factors. Interventions in both research studies and government programs in India are largely focused on specialized addiction centers; however, this requires a multi-consistent approach for its management. Interventions on the ground are mainly focused on the management of physical symptoms and immediate relief resulting from cessation and overdose of drug use; addressing broader issues of motivation, lifestyle adjustment, peer pressure, family issues and risk behaviors, are generally overlooked. The school educational system (teachers, school-authorities, and parents) need to improve comprehensive care and monitoring about such substances as this is the key age of initiation.

Strength and limitations

The strength of the study lies in the large sample size, and the in the use of validated questionnaires and in providing adequate training to the field staff prior to collecting data. A large number of experts involved in the study also adds to its strength. The study also explores the types of drugs used beyond alcohol and tobacco.

The study suffers inherently from the biases of the cross-sectional study, the study only surveyed two blocks of Dehradun District in Uttarakhand and may not representative of the entire state. Participants who use substances are generally a hidden population, thus self-reported drug use data can also be unreliable.

Conclusion

This research provides a foundation for designing effective prevention and intervention strategies to reduce substance use among youth in Uttarakhand. By addressing knowledge gaps, underlying causes, and promoting healthy alternatives, we can contribute to a healthier future for young people.
