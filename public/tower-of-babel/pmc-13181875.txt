The power, potential of real-world data in randomized controlled trials: proceedings from a multistakeholder think tank
Authors: Nina Nouhravesh, Jennifer G. Jackman, Adrian F. Hernandez, Charles Lee, Christoph P. Hornik, Emily Zacherle, Joanne Waldstreicher, Noelle Cocoros, Samuel Brown, Tor Biering-Sorensen, Karen Chiswell, Lisa Wruck, Stefan K. James
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remain the gold standard for evaluating medical interventions, but they often face challenges related to patient recruitment, cost, and efficiency. Real-world data (RWD) has emerged as a valuable tool to enhance trial design, improve patient identification, and support regulatory decision-making. However, integrating RWD into RCTs presents methodological, regulatory, and operational challenges. To address these issues, a think tank was convened in May 2024 at the Duke Clinical Research Institute, bringing together experts from academia, industry, healthcare systems, regulatory agencies, and patient advocacy groups. Discussions focused on three key areas: optimizing patient identification and outcome assessment, leveraging RWD for safety assessments, and using RWD in RCTs supporting regulatory approval. RWD has the potential to simplify eligibility criteria, enhance recruitment through artificial intelligence, and provide practical endpoints for evaluating treatment effects. The think tank underscored the need for collaboration across stakeholders to address challenges, such as data inconsistencies, privacy concerns, and infrastructure limitations. The event concluded with actionable recommendations, including the following: (1) standardizing RWD sources to ensure consistency and improve interoperability across healthcare systems, (2) developing regulatory frameworks that define acceptable use cases for RWD in clinical trials, (3) enhancing data quality through robust validation methodologies and real-time monitoring, (4) investing in artificial intelligence–driven patient identification tools to streamline recruitment, and (5) fostering multi-stakeholder collaboration to align expectations and share best practices. Moving forward, implementing these strategies will be critical to fully harness the potential of RWD in clinical research and improve trial efficiency.

Background

The integration of real-world data (RWD), including data from electronic health records (EHRs), insurance claims, patient registries, and wearable devices, into clinical trials continues to garner attention due to the need to improve the efficiency and applicability of clinical research [1–4]. Traditional randomized controlled trials (RCTs) face numerous challenges, including high costs, low event rates, slow recruitment processes, and significant burdens placed on physicians, clinical sites, and patients [4–7]. These factors often limit the speed and scalability of clinical trials, particularly when investigating long-term outcomes or rare events [8]. By incorporating RWD into clinical trials, researchers can mitigate some of these issues and provide insights into patient care and treatment patterns [6, 9]. Utilizing these diverse sources of data can enhance the generalizability of trial results [7]. Approaches for incorporating RWD into RCTs include hybrid, pragmatic, and decentralized trials (Table 1), each offering unique methods for integrating real-world insights. However, these approaches have limitations, such as data quality issues, lack of data completeness, and regulatory complexities [10, 11]. To explore the future of RWD in clinical research, the Duke Clinical Research Institute (DCRI) convened a think tank. The group aimed to share experiences, identify barriers, and propose solutions for effectively integrating RWD into clinical trials.

Preparatory activities and overview of the think tank format

Over a 5-month period, a group of experts, including trialists, biostatisticians, and experienced think tank organizers, outlined the topic areas, selected speakers, and identified moderators for the event. Based on these preparations, a 2-day think tank was held in May 2024. To address the chosen discussion areas, a multidisciplinary group of experts was invited from academia, industry, government, healthcare systems, non-profits, patient representatives, regulatory bodies from Europe and the United States (USA), and payer organizations.

The think tank followed a workshop format, consisting of three sessions that covered the following topics: identifying the correct patients, data, and outcomes to assess benefits; leveraging RWD in regulatory-enabling randomized clinical trials; ascertaining data needs; and using RWD to streamline safety assessments in randomized clinical trials. In each session, key experts presented relevant perspectives. After the presentations, in-depth moderated discussions took place, encouraging active participation from all attendees to foster collaboration and exchange of ideas.

Identifying the correct patients

Identifying patients for RCTs is often a burdensome process for trial sites and recruiting clinicians. Integrating RWD sources into trials presents an opportunity to expedite patient identification, as seen in multiple RWD-embedded RCTs [12–15]. This approach requires simplified inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure feasibility within RWD sources, which in turn will foster less restrictive trials. Importantly, the use of RWD for patient identification may also improve the inclusivity and representativeness of trial populations by enabling the identification of individuals who are often underrepresented via traditional recruitment pathways, including people with disabilities, individuals from diverse ethnic and socioeconomic backgrounds, and pregnant people. By leveraging routinely collected data rather than relying solely on physician referral or site-based recruitment, this approach may help reduce structural and awareness-related barriers to participation and facilitate engagement with patient groups who may otherwise not be identified or approached for trial enrollment.

Artificial intelligence (AI) can leverage RWD sources to simplify patient identification with the potential to improve recruitment speed [16]. Furthermore, if patients can be identified in the early planning stages via RWD sources (such as EHR screening), a “find the patients, then the sites” strategy can be used, allowing trial sites to be selected based on eligible patients’ locations. This approach can optimize resources, focusing recruitment efforts where eligible participants are more likely to be found, further capturing a broader range of patient demographics and conditions, making trial results more generalizable. While this strategy can improve efficiency by matching sites to patient availability, it does not remove all operational challenges. Achieving broader generalizability may require activating sites with limited trial experience and providing extra support, which can be resource-intensive but allows for the inclusion of more representative populations. This challenge is less pronounced in systems with nationwide registries and established research infrastructure, where patient identification and trial conduct are more seamless across all institution types.

Moreover, the availability of RWD for patient identification often depends on engagement with healthcare systems, which can be costly and difficult to navigate in early trial design stages. Additionally, uncertainties surrounding International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes and variability in coding practices across institutions and physicians can introduce inconsistencies in patient data. A lack of data standardization across systems, clinics, and even within and between countries further complicates the effective scalability of RWD trial design. Moreover, while increasing generalizability is a key advantage of RWD integration, it can also broaden trial populations too much, potentially weakening the statistical power for identifying differences in trial endpoints. Further, the availability of certain data elements in RWD sources, such as social determinants of health, may limit generalizability. While these challenges are not new, RWD clinical trials continue to grapple with these issues.

Identifying the correct data and outcomes to assess benefits

To be suitable for clinical research, RWD must meet the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Good Clinical Practice E6 criteria—accurate, complete, legible, and timely [17]. Due to the types of data available in RWD sources, RCTs using RWD require special consideration of suitable endpoints. For example, clinical events, which are often registered in RWD sources using standardized coding systems, such as ICD-10 codes, may provide more practical measures of treatment effectiveness than baseline changes in biomarkers or tumor size, which are often cumbersome to register, prone to missingness, or unavailable since these data are not routinely collected in clinical practice [6, 18]. As such, it is important to design a protocol that is informed by the RWD source used in the trial. Additionally, RWD sources with a thorough description of variables, including validated ICD-10 codes with reported sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive values, and negative predictive values, provide essential information for the design of a protocol.

Some trials have successfully combined traditional trial-specific data collection with RWD, enabling hybrid endpoint strategies (e.g., using patient-reported outcomes or other trial-collected measures as primary or secondary endpoints) combined with outcomes, such as hospitalizations and mortality obtained through linked RWD sources. Such hybrid designs require more complex consent procedures to permit linkage between trial data and individual-level RWD but may result in particularly rich and comprehensive datasets. Trials, such as ADAPTABLE, DAPA-MI, and ASCEND, illustrate the feasibility of this approach while other trials, including TASTE, DETO2X–SWEDEHEART, and DANFLUE-1, demonstrate the successful use of endpoints derived solely from national population-based quality registries and nationwide administrative health registries [12, 19–23].

A key difference between RWD-embedded RCTs and traditional RCTs lies in the adjudication of clinical events. Adjudication is a common practice in traditional RCTs; however, it is not available when using RWD. Misclassification of events in RWD can occur for various reasons, including entry errors, coding inconsistencies, insurance claims, time lags, and patient self-report.

However, it is essential to trust the process of randomization and to ensure that it performs its intended role supporting comparable treatment groups throughout the study to reduce bias. Moreover, adjudication in traditional RCTs carries the risk of bias from the adjudicating physician [24, 25], although it is broadly accepted that any misclassification is equally distributed between randomized groups. There is no reason to believe that the same notion does not apply to RWD-embedded trials.

A common concern when using RWD is the potential imprecision of the data (e.g., due to misclassification) [11]. However, this concern is often mitigated by the larger sample sizes typically available through RWD sources. The data may be less detailed; however, the volume of information can compensate for some of the inaccuracies, particularly in statistical analyses where large datasets help identify trends and reduce random errors. This trade-off between data precision and sample size has been increasingly accepted, especially when RWD has been used for large-scale analyses [12, 20, 26, 27]. However, quantity does not always override quality concerns. Systematic biases, such as selection bias or incomplete data capture, can still skew results. Thus, the threshold for missing data remains a significant challenge, particularly in the US, where linkage of patient data across multiple providers does not routinely occur and can be problematic, leaving gaps in patient history [11]. As such, data source validation and study design considerations remain critical regardless of sample size.

Another challenge with RWD is the delayed nature of some data sources (e.g., insurance claims, lagging EHR data downloads, Medicare claims) compared with traditional clinical trials, where data are collected and available in real-time. The delay in data capture depends widely on the source. For example, insurance claims processing often takes weeks or months due to billing cycles and claim approvals while EHR data downloads may lag because of differences in update frequencies across healthcare systems. Additionally, data privacy regulations can introduce further lags, as de-identification and compliance checks must be completed before data sharing. These delays highlight the importance of understanding data availability when designing RWD-based studies, which can impact the reliability and timeliness of findings [28].

Despite these challenges, RWD can offer the significant advantage of enabling long-term follow-up [11]. For example, population-based registries, such as the Myocardial Ischaemia National Audit Project (MINAP) and the Swedish Web-system for Enhancement and Development of Evidence-based care in Heart disease Evaluated According to Recommended Therapies (SWEDEHEART), have successfully enabled long-term patient follow-up in the United Kingdom and Sweden [29, 30]. Patient follow-up can be complicated in the US due to a fragmented healthcare system; however, there are successful examples. For instance, the Functional Assessment Standardized Items system created by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) demonstrates how financial incentives can drive consistent data collection while underscoring the need for safeguards to avoid coercive effects [31]. Under this system, healthcare providers must submit specific data to receive reimbursement, highlighting how economic enforcement can lead to more standardized data collection in the US. Additionally, European countries have achieved success in providing long-term follow-up through RWD systems, such as the DAPA-MI trial [20].

Leveraging RWD in regulatory-enabling randomized controlled trials

Regulatory alignment is an important consideration when integrating RWD into RCTs [4]. Establishing an early and continuous dialogue between the sponsor or investigator and regulatory entities, such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA), is crucial to ensure that the RWD strategy aligns with regulatory expectations [4]. Early interactions allow sponsors and regulators to agree on trial design, data sources, and methodologies, thereby reducing potential uncertainties and facilitating smoother regulatory approval processes. For example, the DAPA-MI study was an international registry-based, randomized, double-blind trial to evaluate the effects of dapagliflozin in reducing the risk of adverse cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes in patients without prior diabetes or chronic heart failure who presented with acute myocardial infarction and impaired left ventricular systolic function [20]. As a registry-based RCT, DAPA-MI combined the advantages of a traditional RCT, namely randomization, causal inference, and blinded treatment, with the advantages of the clinical quality registry, namely a broad patient population and a resource-effective framework integrated into normal health care. A key component of the study was the availability of a stable, high-quality registry that covered the target population and facilitated prospectively collected, structured, and regularly validated data entries. Together, these elements enabled the DAPA-MI study to meet regulatory standards as evidenced by its Fast Track Designation status and Special Protocol Assessment agreement from the FDA [32]. Furthermore, the use of regulatory guidance, such as the ICH E19 guidelines on optimizing safety data collection, provides a framework to streamline a subset of data collection activities [33]. The guidelines outline factors that, after careful evaluation, may warrant reducing the amount of specific data collected in a clinical trial. The guidelines specify the types of data suitable for selective safety data collection and safety data that should be gathered. Following these guidelines can help sponsors and investigators streamline data collection while maintaining quality to support a regulatory submission. This is, of course, more suitable for drugs with established safety profiles, whereas novel compounds are better evaluated in hybrid or traditional trials with adjudication to ensure comprehensive safety data.

Leveraging established data infrastructures, such as the Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics (OHDSI) network, the FDA’s Sentinel Initiative, and PCORnet, presents a valuable opportunity to accelerate trial processes with available RWD sources [28]. By tapping into these existing networks and their validated computable diagnosis, procedure and laboratory code lists (e.g., ICD-10, CPT, LOINC) that are maintained in network specific repositories, researchers can avoid the time-consuming and resource-intensive work to create new RWD infrastructures.

Using RWD to streamline safety assessments in RCTS

RWD offers the potential to streamline safety assessments in RCTs by reducing or limiting the scope of safety data collection for drugs with an already well-established safety profile [33]. Collecting extensive safety data in such cases can be uninformative, as much of the information is already available, and it often leads to unnecessary costs. Additionally, non-serious adverse events and detailed safety narratives, which are frequently not captured in RWD, present additional burdens to sites when adhering to traditional safety reporting standards. Importantly, while the use of RWD may reduce the level of detail available for safety assessments, it does not imply a reduction in the amount of meaningful safety data collected. Additionally, the use of RWD can minimize duplication of data collection by utilizing data collected as part of routine clinical care, altogether alleviating site and patient burden by reducing the number of required visits.

Regulatory alignment on safety data collection, such as the ICH E19 guidelines, provides guidance around key safety outcomes while limiting excessive and uninformative safety data, making eligible trials more efficient without compromising patient safety.

Actionable themes for better use of RWD in RCTS

Identify challenges in the acceptability of RWD and develop an educational process to overcome these challenges

To successfully integrate RWD into RCTs, it is essential to identify the challenges associated with its acceptability across all stakeholders and develop a comprehensive educational process to overcome these barriers. All key stakeholders, including regulators, payers, and sponsors, may have different concerns or levels of skepticism regarding RWD. Collaboration across these sectors is therefore essential to reach a consensus on the key barriers, strategies to overcome these, and next steps to align on standards for the appropriate and effective use of RWD in clinical research. Cost can also be a barrier and varies by country. For example, access to RWD datasets can be costly in the UK, whereas national health registries in Denmark are freely accessible. Collaborative efforts involving academic leaders and funding bodies may help mitigate cost-related barriers where they exist.

Crucially, patients are important stakeholders in this process. A primary obstacle to using RWD is securing patient consent for use of their health data and ensuring data privacy. Building trust between researchers and patients is critical to achieve patients’ willingness to share health information. Patient education regarding the importance of health data in advancing medical research, ensuring transparency about how their data will be used, and implementing stringent data privacy measures to protect their information is key to increase patients’ interest and willingness to share health data. Such educational efforts could take the form of publicly accessible, plain-language resources developed in collaboration with patient advocacy groups, similar to existing initiatives, such as Understanding Patient Data and HDR UK. These resources could be integrated into clinical care pathways, consent processes, and patient-facing digital platforms, ensuring they reach patients at the point where decisions about data sharing are made (Fig. 1). Simplify clinical trial design and data collection to facilitate use of RWD sources

The use of RWD in clinical trials is not an “all or nothing” approach; it can be strategically integrated into different parts of a trial. One simple step is to use RWD to inform the clinical trial design process. Reviewing RWD can inform sponsors of readily available data and the location of patients, which can be used to inform protocol development and site selection, potentially resulting in improved recruitment, increased site engagement, and a reduction in protocol amendments [7, 34]. One of the most accessible strategies for leveraging RWD is to simplify eligibility criteria using elements that are available in RWD sources [7]. Simplifying eligibility criteria has many benefits, such as reducing the burden on sites and physicians, enrolling a more representative population, enhancing the generalizability of the results, and making it feasible to leverage RWD for patient identification [7].

Regulatory requirements and the perception of those requirements often create a barrier to the use of RWD, some of which are unintentional and/or outdated. Regulatory frameworks, such as ICH E19, provide valuable guidance on streamlining various clinical research activities, which may ultimately support the use of RWD. The FDA provides additional guidance, which outlines standards to ensure data quality, reliability, and consistency in regulatory submissions while addressing data privacy and integrity. These pertain to the use and integration of EHRs and medical claims data to support drug evaluations [35–37].

Engaging in early, proactive dialogue with regulatory authorities on specific uses of RWD for regulatory-enabling trials allows sponsors to align on more efficient data collection strategies, ensuring that safety and efficacy outcomes are relevant and not overly burdensome without purpose (Fig. 1).

Support collaboration, cooperation, and partnership between sponsors and regulatory authorities

By building and providing access to infrastructure that supports multiple trials, researchers can use existing data and operational frameworks, reducing redundancy and accelerating the clinical research process. This kind of system requires partnerships across healthcare communities, systems, EHRs, claims databases, Health Economic Outcomes Research databases, and sponsors and regulatory authorities both in the US and Europe. Such partnerships can streamline clinical trials using platform trials or hybrid trial design. These examples allow for multiple interventions to be tested simultaneously or in succession re-using the same trial infrastructure, thereby creating a more efficient research environment where studies can be conducted faster and at a lower cost, benefiting both patients and researchers. Notably, this is a challenge across different industry-sponsored trials due to competition in the pharmaceutical industry and ownership of data. As such, early steps in common data models and clinical research infrastructure are most successfully driven by government and non-profit–funded initiatives.

Particularly in the US where healthcare data are fragmented across various systems, meaningful collaboration among large data curators, such as the Veterans Affairs Administration, CMS, insurance claim systems, and wide-reaching patient registries, has the substantial potential to improve the utility of RWD for RCTs. The most impactful changes could include the following: (1) alignment of core data elements and outcome definitions (e.g., harmonized coding for clinical events, medications, and procedures); (2) development of interoperable data models that allow queries to be executed across multiple data sources without requiring full data centralization; and (3) streamlined, standardized governance and application processes for data access. Collectively, these efforts could enable a “single-entry” analytical framework in which trial endpoints, safety events, and follow-up data are consistently defined and retrieved across systems, thereby reducing operational burden, minimizing heterogeneity in outcome ascertainment, and increasing the feasibility of embedding RCTs within routine clinical care (Fig 1).

Summary, conclusions, and outlook

Trial designs that leverage RWD represent a necessary advancement in modernizing traditional RCTs. However, the successful integration of RWD will require early and continuous collaboration among leadership from a wide range of stakeholders, including academia, industry, government agencies, payer groups, regulatory bodies, among others. This cooperation is essential for transforming RWD into a broadly usable form of data, which also necessitates aligning key data elements to ensure seamless integration. Additionally, expanding access to large datasets is crucial to maximize the potential of RWD in clinical research. As such, RWD can be strategically applied to targeted steps of a trial and throughout a trial.

The ability to harness RWD requires simplification of clinical trial protocols, including eligibility criteria and outcomes designed with available RWD as a consideration. However, these efforts do not come without challenges. Addressing data privacy concerns associated with linkage of data sources is critical to increase patient participation, recruitment, and compliance with medical privacy legislation. Additionally, investing in RWD infrastructure and governance, including data sharing practices, is necessary to support the use and development of accessible RWD datasets. Collaborative efforts are essential for the efficient use of RWD in clinical research, which will ultimately inform its widespread adoption and updates to regulatory guidance.
