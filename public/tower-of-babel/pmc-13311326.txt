Higher-order multiple primary cancers and persistent cross-site risk across human papillomavirus-related anatomical sites in the United States: a population-based Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results study
Authors: Tessho Maruyama, Makoto Kaneko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSurvivors of cancers at human papillomavirus (HPV)-related sites are at increased risk of subsequent malignancies, but the burden and patterns of higher-order (≥3 primaries) multiple primary cancers (MPCs) and long-term cross-site risk patterns remain poorly understood. This study characterized these patterns and evaluated implications for cancer prevention and early detection.MethodsThis population-based retrospective cohort study analyzed data from the United States Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)-8, SEER-17, and SEER-21 registries from 1975 to 2022. We evaluated the incidence and distribution of MPCs as well as latency-specific standardized incidence ratios and cross-site associations across HPV-related sites.ResultsAmong 15.9 million cancer cases, 2.8 million (17.6%) were MPCs. Higher-order MPCs accounted for 4.1%–4.6% of diagnoses. Most HPV-related sites showed significantly elevated risk of higher-order MPCs, and the risk of MPCs persisted for ≥10 years of latency. Bidirectional cross-site associations included anus-to-tonsil and tongue-to-vulva associations at latency intervals of ≥10 years.ConclusionsHigher-order MPCs represent a substantial and underrecognized component of overall cancer burden. Survivors of cancers at HPV-related sites carry persistent, long-term cross-site risks. Current organ-specific follow-up strategies may be insufficient and suggest the need for more integrated, long-term surveillance and prevention strategies in this population.

Introduction

Multiple primary cancers (MPCs) refer to the occurrence of two or more biologically distinct primary malignancies in the same individual (Sung et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024), excluding recurrence or metastasis, according to the criteria proposed by Warren and Gates and later refined by Moertel (Warren and Gates, 1932; Moertel et al., 1961). The incidence of MPCs is increasing, posing a growing challenge in cancer survivorship (Vogt et al., 2017; Tanaka et al., 2021), and opportunities for prevention and early detection in high-risk survivors remain incompletely defined.

Patients with cancers arising at human papillomavirus (HPV)-related anatomical sites carry an increased risk of developing subsequent primary cancers. This pattern may partly reflect shared etiologic mechanisms, including possible HPV-related carcinogenesis in various sites in the body. Many of these sites are also accessible to clinical examination, offering opportunities for earlier detection (Dahlstrom et al., 2021; Malagón et al., 2024; Suk et al., 2018).

Prior Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)-based studies have primarily focused on second primary cancers (Suk et al., 2018) and have not adequately addressed higher-order MPCs (i.e., ≥3 primary cancers) or long-term cross-site risk patterns (Almasan and Piciu, 2023). In the present study, we focused on subsequent primary cancers developing after HPV-related site index cancers, particularly higher-order MPCs occurring over extended follow-up periods. Some patients develop higher-order MPCs over many years, often after repeated treatment and long-term follow-up. These patients may represent a distinct survivorship population characterized by cumulative treatment exposure, prolonged surveillance, and persistent carcinogenic risk. Such patterns may reflect shared carcinogenic factors and prolonged cancer survivorship (Vogt et al., 2017), including possible HPV-related mechanisms. In clinical practice, MPCs arising at HPV-related sites are often managed separately, which may lead to missed opportunities for early detection. Late-occurring cancers may also be detected at more advanced stages. This underscores the need for long-term surveillance (Tanaka et al., 2021). Overall, higher-order MPCs and their cross-site risk patterns for HPV-related site cancers remain insufficiently characterized.

In this study, we conducted a large population-based analysis of higher-order MPCs and their long-term cross-site risk patterns of subsequent primary cancers following HPV-related site index cancers to characterize these patterns and evaluate their implications for cancer prevention and early detection. Oral cavity, testicular, and prostate cancers were separately evaluated as non-HPV-related comparator sites, based on established classifications. Methods

Study design and population

This population-based retrospective cohort study used SEER data and SEER*Stat software (RRID: SCR_025808, version 9.0.40; National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, USA) to identify and quantify higher-order MPCs (Surveillance Research Program and National Cancer Institute, 2025). Data were obtained from the SEER-8, SEER-17, and SEER-21 registries (November 2024 submission), covering approximately 8.3%, 26.5%, and 45.8% of the United States population, respectively. Analyses were conducted using predefined SEER*Stat modules and standard multiple primary rules. Because this study used population-based registry data, a priori sample size calculation was not applicable. Therefore, we included all eligible cases within the SEER datasets during the study period. We included invasive cancers classified by anatomic site according to the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3). Patients with unknown age or identified only at autopsy or death certificate were excluded. The study population was identified from the SEER-8, SEER-17, and SEER-21 registries described above. SEER-8 and SEER-21 were used for frequency analyses, and SEER-17 for multiple primary–standardized incidence ratios (MP-SIR) analyses. Analyses were conducted between July 8 and 22, 2025, and were restricted to patients diagnosed within the registry periods (SEER-8, 1975–2022; SEER-17, 2000–2021; and SEER-21, 2000–2022). SEER-8 was used for long-term temporal trend analyses because of its extended historical coverage. SEER-17 was used for MP-SIR analyses because the required multiple-primary session datasets were available for this registry configuration, whereas SEER-21 was used to improve geographic representativeness for descriptive analyses.

Measures

Variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, tumor site, and sequence number, as defined in SEER. Cancer sites were categorized using the SEER “Site recode ICD-O-3/WHO 2008” variable.

Cancers arising at anatomical sites associated with HPV were defined according to consensus definitions from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, International Agency for Research on Cancer, and National Cancer Institute (de Martel et al., 2017; Malagón et al., 2024). In analyses of subsequent primary cancers, these HPV-related site cancers were treated as index cancers and included the oropharynx (tonsil and base of the tongue), cervix, vagina, vulva, anus/anal canal, penis, and head and neck cancer of unknown primary (HNCUP). Certain anatomical sub-sites (e.g., base of the tongue and HNCUP) could not be analyzed separately due to SEER variable limitations. Oral cavity, prostate, and testicular cancers were treated as comparator sites to provide contrasts for evaluating cross-site patterns involving HPV-related cancers. The oral cavity was also included because it is readily accessible for routine clinical examination.

We evaluated the frequency of MPCs involving oropharyngeal and genital/anal sites (the primary outcome) using SEER-21 and SEER-8 datasets to reduce potential bias. Frequencies were calculated for invasive cancers with known age. Sequence numbers were used to classify first primary cancers and MPCs.

MP-SIR analyses were performed using SEER-17 (2000−2021) to evaluate the latency-related risk of subsequent primary cancers. Subsequent primaries were defined according to SEER rules with a ≥6-month interval to reduce misclassification of synchronous tumors. Standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

We assessed the proportion of higher-order MPCs (≥3 primary cancers) and patterns of subsequent primary cancers following HPV-related index cancers across selected cancer sites. Eleven cancer sites showing significantly elevated patterns of subsequent primary cancers were included (tonsil, oropharynx, anus/anal canal/anorectum, vagina, vulva, penis, lip, tongue, floor of mouth, gum and other mouth parts, and testis), as described in Table 1A, Table 1B. Analyses were performed at the diagnosis level rather than at the patient level. Cancer sequence numbers were used to classify diagnoses, which were classified as first/second primaries or higher-order MPCs based on the sequence number. Analyses were stratified by age group. Proportions were compared using the Pearson chi-squared test. Age-adjusted comparisons were performed using the Mantel–Haenszel method. These analyses were separately performed for each of the 11 selected cancer sites as described above (Supplementary Tables S1–2). Analyses used SEER-21 data (2000−2022) restricted to invasive cancers with known age.

To evaluate latency effects, SIRs were stratified into four predefined intervals consistent with previous studies (Feller et al., 2020; Li et al., 2018): 6–11, 12–59, 60–119, and ≥120 months to reduce detection bias related to incidental findings at the time of the index diagnosis. In the present study, cancers diagnosed within 6 months of the index cancer were excluded from latency analyses to reduce overlap with synchronous presentations. Cohorts were defined according to the cancer site, subsequent primary site, and latency interval. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided Poisson exact tests with Bonferroni correction. Each subsequent primary cancer was treated as a separate event.

Selected cross-site combinations identified from the MP-SIR analysis and listed in Table 1A, Table 1B were evaluated using latency-stratified SIRs. Analyses were restricted to combinations with >15 observed cases. Statistical significance was assessed using Poisson exact tests with Bonferroni correction.

Statistical analyses

SIRs were calculated using a Poisson model. Additional statistical analyses, including two-sided Poisson exact tests, Bonferroni correction, Pearson chi-squared tests, and Mantel–Haenszel tests, were performed. The Mantel–Haenszel test, stratified according to age group, was used to compare age-adjusted proportions of higher-order MPCs across cancer sites. Bonferroni correction was applied across latency intervals. A two-sided significance level of 0.05 was used. Statistical analyses were performed using SEER*Stat version 9.0.40 and R version 3.6.2 (RRID:SCR_001905; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical considerations

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (von Elm et al., 2008) and was conducted under the SEER Data Use Agreement. This study used publicly available de-identified registry data and was therefore exempt from institutional review board review in accordance with institutional policies. All data were publicly available and de-identified; hence, informed consent was not required. Documentation of this exemption is maintained at the corresponding author's institution. The study was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

The frequency of MPCs has increased over time

Between 2000 and 2022, 15.9 million cancer cases were recorded in the SEER-21 registry (National Cancer Institute, n.d.). Among these, 13,091,032 (82.4%) cases were first primary cancers, and 2,791,520 (17.6%) were MPCs. In comparison, the SEER-8 registry recorded 4,793,660 cancer cases, including 4,011,831 (83.7%) first primary cancers and 781,829 (16.3%) MPCs. Panels a and b of Supplementary Fig. S1 show the annual number of cancer cases recorded in SEER-21 and SEER-8, respectively. Over time, MPCs approached approximately 20% of all recorded cancer diagnoses (Fig. 2a, b). SIRs are significantly increased for many cancers at HPV-related sites

Table 1A, Table 1B show SIRs for subsequent cancers in the SEER-17 analysis. Most of these cancer types, excluding cervical and prostate cancers, showed significantly elevated risks of subsequent primary cancers among survivors (e.g., all sites-to-oropharyngeal cancers, SIR = 1.39 [95% CI = 1.30, 1.48]; all sites-to-vulvar cancers, SIR = 1.86 [95% CI = 1.79, 1.93]). Oral cavity cancers were analyzed separately as non-HPV-related sites, and intra-site combinations that could reflect recurrence or field cancerization, in which carcinogenic exposure may affect adjacent tissues over time (Slaughter et al., 1953), were excluded to minimize bias. Therefore, we focused on cross-site associations between oropharyngeal and genital/anal cancer sites in both directions. Many of these site combinations occurred with sufficient frequency to permit stable SIR estimation (Table 1A, Table 1B). These analyses were based on anatomical site classifications rather than confirmed HPV-positive tumor status.

Higher-order MPCs were observed across multiple cancer sites

Higher-order MPCs accounted for 4.1%–4.6% of all recorded cancer diagnoses (Fig. 2a, b). Using the predefined criteria, we analyzed whether cancers arising at HPV-related sites showed a tendency to occur as higher-order MPCs. After age adjustment, most HPV-related sites (Supplementary Tables S1) demonstrated significant involvement in higher-order MPCs, except for tonsil cancer. Non-HPV-related oral cavity sites showed a similar pattern, whereas testicular cancer occurred significantly less often as a higher-order MPC (Supplementary Tables S2).

Cancers at HPV-related sites show elevated risks across extended latency intervals

Latency analyses evaluated the timing of subsequent MPCs following an index cancer diagnosis across all sites (Table 2). Cancer sites were classified as showing significantly increased or decreased SIRs, with decreased risks observed only for cervical and prostate cancers. Statistically significant elevations in SIRs were most evident between 12 and 119 months after the index diagnosis (Table 2). For most sites with increased risk, excluding penile cancer, elevated SIRs persisted beyond 120 months. Notably, cancers of the tonsil, vagina, and vulva showed significantly elevated SIRs across all latency intervals. Similar patterns were observed for non-HPV-related comparator sites, including the tongue, floor of the mouth, gum, other oral cavity subsites, and testis. Conversely, prostate cancer displayed significantly reduced SIRs at each latency interval (Table 2). Discussion

In this study of 15.9 million cancer cases, higher-order MPCs accounted for 4.1%–4.6% of all cancer diagnoses, a notably high proportion suggesting that their burden has been substantially underestimated in prior studies (Ali et al., 2022; Borja et al., 2024; Kato et al., 2015; Kjaer et al., 2024; Kwon et al., 2024; Murphy et al., 2018). The results also suggest that higher-order MPCs occur across multiple anatomical sites, including several HPV-related sites. To our knowledge, this is among the first large population-based studies to quantify higher-order MPCs and to demonstrate bidirectional, long-term cross-site risk across HPV-related sites. Tonsil cancer did not show a significant association with higher-order MPCs, possibly due to limited statistical power. MPC frequency increased over time. This increase observed in Fig. 2 may partly reflect cohort accumulation effects, with longer follow-up increasing opportunities for the detection of subsequent primary cancers.

The persistence of these patterns across anatomically distinct HPV-related sites raises the possibility of shared long-term carcinogenic mechanisms extending beyond the initial cancer diagnosis and treatment. However, long-term cross-site MPC patterns across HPV-related anatomical sites remain incompletely understood (Rose Ragin and Taioli, 2008; Sharav et al., 2025). In this study, we observed consistent associations across anatomically distinct HPV-related sites, with elevated risks persisting beyond 10 years and across extended latency intervals. Such findings extend well beyond the initial treatment period and may reflect shared etiologic mechanisms, including possible HPV-related carcinogenesis (Nelson and Lai, 2017; Sharav et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2020). They may be driven, in part, by persistent HPV infection, shared behavioral risk factors, and field cancerization. Collectively, these mechanisms may lead to the development of subsequent cancers across multiple anatomical sites over time.

Follow-up after cancer remains largely organ-specific, which may fail to capture ongoing risk across anatomically distinct but etiologically related sites. Our findings suggest that more integrated, long-term surveillance strategies may be warranted. Patients with a history of cancers at HPV-related anatomical sites may benefit from increased awareness of symptoms and, where appropriate, clinical evaluation across multiple sites. This approach could enable earlier detection and improve prevention strategies in this high-risk population.

This study has some limitations. Causal inference is limited by the observational design, and SEER does not include HPV status or detailed clinical and behavioral data, which limits more detailed analyses of these factors. Surveillance bias may also have influenced the findings despite the use of multiple SEER datasets and exclusion of site combinations likely to reflect intensive follow-up. Not all cancers arising at HPV-related sites are necessarily HPV-driven, and the contribution of HPV likely differs across anatomical sites and tumor types; therefore, some misclassification is possible (Borja et al., 2024; Surveillance Research Program and National Cancer Institute, 2025). Further studies with more precise HPV attribution may be needed. Moreover, residual confounding may have influenced the results. Because this study was based on United States registry data, the findings may not be fully generalizable to populations with different healthcare systems, cancer epidemiology, or HPV prevalence patterns. Survivor bias and competing mortality risks may also have influenced long-latency findings. Additionally, external validation in independent datasets was not performed. More detailed clinical data and molecular HPV confirmation may help clarify the biological mechanisms underlying these associations in future studies. Patterns of higher-order MPCs may also change in HPV vaccination-era cohorts as the epidemiology of HPV-related cancers continues to evolve (Lei et al., 2020; Malagón et al., 2024). Moreover, analysis of specialized SEER datasets that include tumor HPV status, such as the Oropharyngeal Cancer with HPV Status Database, may further improve understanding of HPV-specific associations. Additional validation in independent datasets will also be important to confirm the reproducibility of these findings. Despite these limitations, the observed cross-site and long-term patterns point to areas where surveillance could be improved.

Conclusions

Higher-order MPCs represent a substantial and underrecognized component of the overall cancer burden. Most HPV-related sites demonstrated significant involvement in higher-order MPCs. Survivors of cancers at HPV-related anatomical sites face persistent, long-term risks of subsequent malignancies across multiple sites. Current organ-specific follow-up strategies may not fully capture this ongoing risk. Taken together, these findings point to the need for more integrated, long-term surveillance strategies to improve early detection and guide prevention efforts in this high-risk population.
