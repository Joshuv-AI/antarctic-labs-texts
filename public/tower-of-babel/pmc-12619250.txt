Association between coronary heart disease and cardiometabolic index: a study from NHANES 1999–2018
Authors: Chao Yang, Xiaobo Wang, Youjin Kong, Xiao Liu, Qiuli Sun, Xingxiao Huang, Beibei Gao, Jinyu Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe cardiometabolic index (CMI), a sex-specific marker combining lipid profiles with anthropometric measures, may reflect visceral adiposity dysfunction. Its association with coronary heart disease (CHD) risk remains inadequately characterized in large populations.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 20,888 adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999–2018. The association between CMI and CHD was assessed using weighted multivariable logistic regression. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) and threshold effect analyses explored non-linearity. A predictive model was developed and evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.ResultsAfter full adjustment, a significant positive association was observed between CMI and CHD risk (OR = 1.17, 95%CI:1.11–1.23). Participants in the highest CMI quartile had a 58% greater risk than those in the lowest (OR = 1.58, 95%CI:1.06–2.37). RCS analysis revealed a non-linear relationship (P-nonlinearity < 0.001), with a threshold identified at CMI = 1.175. Below this point, each unit increase in CMI was associated with a 133% elevation in CHD risk (OR = 2.33, 95%CI:1.83–2.97). The final predictive model, incorporating CMI and key covariates, demonstrated robust discrimination (AUC = 0.861, 95%CI:0.851–0.872).ConclusionsCMI is independently and non-linearly associated with CHD prevalence, with a heightened effect below a threshold of 1.175. This index shows promise as a practical tool for improving CHD risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05302-6.Keywords: Cross-sectional study, Cardiometabolic index, Coronary heart disease, NHANES

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases represent the leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, imposing a dual burden of public health crisis and a substantial socioeconomic burden on affected individuals and healthcare systems [1, 2]. In the United States, coronary heart disease accounts for one in four annual deaths according to American Heart Association statistics, solidifying its position as the predominant contributor to nationwide mortality [3]. Coronary heart disease (CHD) remains a preeminent global health threat, driven by the convergence of modifiable risk factors such as unhealthy lifestyles, hypertension, and diabetes. The economic burden is substantial; in the United States, CHD generates annual costs exceeding $320 billion from direct medical expenses and productivity loss. By 2030, these costs are projected to rise to $818 billion in direct expenditures and $275 billion in lost productivity [4]. While traditional risk factors—hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia—have been extensively investigated, gaps persist in comprehensive metabolic risk assessment. The cardiometabolic index (CMI), a novel composite metric that integrates lipid profiles with the waist-to-height ratio, has emerged as a potential tool for evaluating visceral adiposity dysfunction and metabolic risk [5]. However, its epidemiological association with CHD and predictive utility remain inadequately characterized in large-scale population cohorts. The CMI, integrating the triglycerides-to-HDL cholesterol ratio with the waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), reflects synergistic interactions between lipid metabolism and central adiposity [5]. While existing studies primarily focus on CMI’s association with metabolic syndrome, its clinical utility for CHD risk stratification remains underexplored [6]. Importantly, the non-linear dynamics and threshold effects of CMI-CHD relationships remain unelucidated, and insufficient adjustment for multivariate confounders has limited their translational potential.

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database, renowned for its nationally representative sampling, longitudinal follow-up, and multidimensional data integration (encompassing physiological, biochemical, and behavioral metrics), offers an unparalleled platform to explore the CMI-CHD association [7]. Leveraging 1999–2018 NHANES data, this study seeks to delineate the independent prognostic value of CMI in CHD risk prediction, characterize its non-linear dose-response relationship through threshold dynamics, and develop a CMI-enhanced clinical prediction model to address critical evidence gaps in metabolic risk stratification.

This investigation represents the first large-scale epidemiological study systematically evaluating both linear and non-linear associations between CMI and CHD while integrating threshold effect analysis with machine learning-enhanced feature selection (LASSO regression) to develop a high-accuracy predictive algorithm. By elucidating CMI’s distinct role in CHD risk stratification, our findings establish an evidence-based framework for precision prevention strategies targeting high-risk populations through early metabolic profiling.

Materials and methods

Data source

NHANES, a nationally representative epidemiological program conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics under the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, operates with institutional review board approval and requires written informed consent from all participants [8]. This cross-sectional survey systematically collects five core domains: demographics, physiological measurements, laboratory biomarkers, health questionnaires, and nutritional assessments [9]. The NHANES database is publicly accessible to researchers worldwide for health-related investigations and policy development. The data used in this study are publicly available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.

Study participants

The present study employed a cross-sectional design to investigate the association between CMI and CHD prevalence within the NHANES cycles from 1999 to 2018, while evaluating the predictive utility of CMI for CHD progression. From an initial pool of 101,306 individuals, we excluded participants aged < 20 years (n = 47,883). Subsequent exclusion criteria included missing CMI data (n = 32,433), incomplete CHD documentation (n = 100), and lacking key covariates: education level (n = 45), marital status (n = 221), or weight calculations (n = 1,414). The final analytical cohort comprised 20,888 eligible participants. A detailed flowchart of the selection process is provided in Fig. 1.

Assessment of cardiometabolic index

CMI was calculated based on anthropometric and biochemical data, including height, waist circumference (WC), triglyceride (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). The units for height and waist circumference (WC)were expressed in centimeters (cm), while those for TG and HDL-C were in milligrams per deciliter (mmol/L), integrating four physiological parameters [10]. In this study, CMI was analyzed both as a continuous and a categorical variable based on quartiles for group comparisons. The quartiles were defined as follows: Quartile 1 (CMI < 0.29), Quartile 2 (CMI 0.29–0.50), Quartile 3 (CMI 0.51–0.93), and Quartile 4 (CMI > 0.93).

Assessment of coronary heart disease

Consistent with prior NHANES-based studies, CHD diagnosis was ascertained through self-reported medical history [11]. Specifically, participants responded to the standardized question: “Has a doctor or health professional ever told you that you had coronary heart disease?” administered by certified professionals using validated protocols to ensure data consistency. Affirmative responses (“Yes”) served as the diagnostic criterion for CHD case identification. Nevertheless, validation studies have suggested that self-reported major cardiovascular conditions, such as CHD, have high specificity, meaning that false positives are relatively rare [11].

Covariates

Potential confounding variables were selected based on established epidemiological criteria [12, 13], encompassing three domains: (1) demographic characteristics (sex, age categorized as < 40, 40–60, ≥ 60 years; race/ethnicity: Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other; education: below high school, high school graduate, college or above; marital status: married/cohabiting, divorced/separated/widowed, never married; poverty-income ratio [PIR]: <1.3, 1.3–3.5, > 3.5) [14]; (2) biomedical parameters (body mass index [BMI, kg/m²] classified per WHO criteria: normal ≤ 25, overweight 25–30, obese ≥ 30; total cholesterol [TC, mg/dl]); and (3) health-related factors (smoking status: never [< 100 lifetime cigarettes], former [≥ 100 cigarettes with cessation] [15], current [≥ 100 cigarettes with ongoing use]; alcohol consumption: non-drinker [< 12 drinks/year] vs. drinker; physical activity defined as engaging in moderate work-related exertion causing mild cardiopulmonary elevation within 30 days) [13]. Disease definitions included: hypertension (systolic ≥ 140 mmHg/diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg, antihypertensive use, or self-report) [15]; stroke (self-reported diagnosis via MCQ questionnaire); diabetes (meeting ≥ 1 criterion: self-report, hypoglycemic medication use, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L); and prediabetes (HbA1c 5.7–6.4% or fasting glucose 5.6–6.9 mmol/L) [16]. Detailed protocols are accessible through the NHANES official portal (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/).

Statistical methods

In alignment with NHANES’ complex survey design, we applied sampling weights (WTSAF2YR/10) and addressed missing covariates through multiple imputations via chained equations [17]. We generated 20 imputed datasets. The imputation model included all analysis variables presented in Table 1, specifically: age, sex, race, education, marital status, poverty-income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, total cholesterol, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and physical activity. The cardiometabolic index (CMI) and the outcome (CHD status) were included in the imputation model as predictors to preserve their relationships with the covariates, but were not themselves imputed, as their values were complete for the analytical cohort. Analyses were performed separately on each imputed dataset, and the results were pooled using Rubin’s rules.

Participants were stratified by CMI quartiles, with continuous variables expressed as means ± standard errors (SE) and categorical variables as proportions. Analyses were performed using weighted t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Three multivariable logistic regression models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for sex, age, and race), and Model 3 (fully adjusted). Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots examined non-linear associations, while subgroup analyses with interaction terms assessed heterogeneity across strata. Feature selection for predictive modeling employed LASSO regression complemented by univariate/multivariate logistic regression. Model performance was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (AUC reported), decision curve analysis (DCA), and calibration plots, with final risk stratification visualized via a nomogram. All analyses used R4.3.3, with statistical significance set at two-tailed P < 0.05. To test the hypothesis that the CMI-CHD relationship is not linear, we employed RCS with four knots. Upon confirmation of significant non-linearity (P < 0.001), we proceeded to identify an objective inflection point using segmented regression analysis. To develop a parsimonious model for clinical risk visualization, feature selection was performed using LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation to mitigate the risk of overfitting.

Results

Population characteristics

The analytical cohort comprised 20,888 participants, among whom 833 (4.0%) were diagnosed with CHD (Table 1, Appendix at the end of the article). Stratification by CMI quartiles revealed significant demographic and clinical gradients: ascending CMI levels correlated with elevated mean age and TC concentrations. Participants in higher CMI quartiles exhibited predominant male sex, obesity, and smoking prevalence, alongside increased comorbidities including diabetes (with prediabetes), hypertension, stroke, and CHD. Significant disparities emerged across CMI strata regarding gender distribution, racial composition, educational attainment, marital status, and PIR. Anthropometric and health indicators, particularly BMI, demonstrated marked heterogeneity among quartile groups.

Association between CMI and CHD

Weighted logistic regression analyses revealed a consistent positive association between CMI and CHD risk across progressively adjusted models (Table 2). In the unadjusted model, each unit increment in CMI corresponded to a 16% elevation in CHD risk (OR = 1.16, 95%CI:1.11–1.20, P < 0.001). Adjustment for demographic confounders (age, sex, race) strengthened this association (OR = 1.19, 95%CI:1.13–1.24, P < 0.001), with the persistence of significance after full covariate adjustment (OR = 1.17, 95%CI:1.11–1.23, P < 0.001). The quartile-based analysis demonstrated a 58% increased CHD risk in the highest versus the lowest CMI quartile (OR = 1.58, 95%CI:1.06–2.37, P = 0.027). A significant dose-response gradient was observed, with each ascending quartile associated with 23% greater CHD likelihood (OR = 1.23 per quartile, 95%CI:1.07–1.41, P for trend = 0.005).

Nonlinear CMI-CHD dose-response with threshold dynamics

RCS analysis with full covariate adjustment revealed a significant non-linear association between CMI and CHD risk (P for non-linear < 0.001; Fig. 2). This finding prompted threshold analysis using segmented logistic regression, identifying an inflection point at CMI = 1.175. Below this threshold, each unit increase in CMI elevated CHD risk by 133.1% (OR = 2.331, 95%CI:1.830–2.971, P < 0.001), whereas no significant association was observed above this cutoff (OR = 1.070, 95%CI:0.977–1.150, P = 0.107). Likelihood ratio testing confirmed superior model fit for the two-segment model versus linear regression (P < 0.001). Comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders ensured analytical robustness (Table 3).

Subgroup heterogeneity analysis

Stratified analyses across age, sex, race, education, PIR, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, diabetes, and hypertension subgroups revealed consistent CMI-CHD associations (all P for interaction > 0.05). Notably, multivariable-adjusted models demonstrated no significant effect modification between baseline CMI levels and stratification variables (Fig. 3), confirming the robustness of this metabolic risk relationship across diverse demographic and clinical profiles

Feature selection via LASSO regression

LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was employed for covariate selection in predictive modeling. As depicted in Fig. 4A-B, the optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined at one standard error above the minimum cross-validated error (λ1se; log(λ1se)=−5.2127). This approach identified eight clinically relevant predictors for nomogram construction: sex, age, race, smoking status, TC, hypertension, diabetes, and stroke history (Supplementary Table S1).

Refinement of predictive features via logistic regression

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were sequentially applied to validate LASSO-derived predictors. As detailed in Fig. 5, six independent risk factors for CHD progression were ultimately identified: male sex, advanced age, current smoking status, elevated total cholesterol, hypertension, and prior stroke history.

Nomogram construction for risk stratification

Integrating CMI with predictors identified through LASSO and logistic regression analyses, we developed a comprehensive nomogram for CHD risk prediction (Fig. 6A). Each variable was assigned weighted points proportional to its effect size, enabling individualized risk estimation through cumulative scoring. For instance, the nomogram predicted a 26.4% CHD probability for a 65-year-old female smoker (total cholesterol = 6.7 mmol/L, CMI = 2) without hypertension or stroke history, as illustrated by the red datapoint in Fig. 6A.

CHD Risk Nomogram. A Nomogram model based on the characteristic variable screened by LASSO and logistic regression; B ROC curve was used to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of CMI and the nomogram model

Predictive model validation

The nomogram demonstrated superior discriminative capacity compared to standalone CMI metrics, achieving an AUC of 0.861 (95%CI:0.851–0.872) with 84.4% sensitivity and 73.4% specificity (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Table S2). Decision curve analysis revealed substantial clinical net benefit across threshold probabilities (Supplementary Figure S1), while calibration curves showed close alignment between predicted and observed CHD risks (Supplementary Figure S2). These findings collectively validate the model’s robust predictive capacity for CHD identification.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study represents the first comprehensive investigation of CMI-CHD associations in the U.S. population, which aimed to identify a significant association and evaluate the predictive utility of CMI for CHD risk stratification within a large, representative population, thereby generating hypotheses for future causal investigation. Analyzing data from 20,888 NHANES participants over 20 years, we observed significantly elevated CMI levels in CHD patients compared to non-CHD controls (adjusted OR = 1.58, 95%CI:1.06–2.37). Multivariable regression confirmed a positive CMI-CHD dose-response relationship, with the highest quartile (Q4) demonstrating 58% greater CHD risk. Notably, RCS analysis revealed substantial non-linearity (P for non-linearity < 0.001), challenging the adequacy of conventional linear models in risk quantification Our findings that CMI is independently and positively associated with CHD prevalence align with and extend the growing body of evidence on this novel index. For instance, a cohort study by Mo DG et al. [18] conducted in a China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database also reported a significant association between elevated CMI and an increased risk of cardiovascular events, reinforcing the potential of CMI as a cross-population risk marker. Similarly, Wu X et al. [19] demonstrated that CMI was superior to traditional anthropometric indices in predicting coronary artery calcification, which supports the rationale for integrating lipid and adiposity measures as done in CMI However, this study achieved several key breakthroughs compared to previous reports. Our threshold effect analysis revealed a critical inflection point at a CMI of 1.175. Below this value, each unit increase in CMI was associated with a substantially elevated risk of CHD (OR = 2.331, 95% CI: 1.830–2.971). However, the association attenuated significantly above this threshold, which may indicate risk saturation or the activation of compensatory metabolic mechanisms in high-exposure individuals [20]. These findings underscore the cost-efficiency of implementing early interventions—including lifestyle modifications or lipid-lowering therapies—for individuals with CMI values ≤ 1.175 In an exploratory analysis, we incorporated CMI alongside other significant predictors to create a predictive model. While the LASSO-optimized model was derived from cross-sectional data and requires external validation, it demonstrated promising discriminative capacity (AUC = 0.861) [21]. The resulting nomogram should primarily be viewed as a hypothesis-generating tool that visually integrates these factors and illustrates their combined effect. This could inform the design of future prospective risk prediction studies. This multivariate framework incorporated conventional risk determinants such as age, smoking status, and hypertension alongside CMI, with nomogram visualization enabling rapid clinical risk stratification [22]. Decision curve analysis confirmed substantial net benefit across probability thresholds [23], reflecting enhanced precision in CHD risk prediction through the synergistic integration of metabolic indices and clinical variables relative to existing models Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent associations between cardiometabolic index and coronary heart disease across all demographic and clinical strata, with no significant interaction effects observed by age, sex, or baseline metabolic status. This robust generalizability supports CMI’s utility as a population-agnostic screening tool, particularly valuable for primary prevention in resource-constrained settings where advanced diagnostics remain inaccessible The association between CMI and CHD is supported by well-established pathophysiological pathways involving its core components [24].CMI incorporates measures of visceral adiposity (WHtR) and atherogenic dyslipidemia (TG/HDL-C ratio), both of which are key factors in cardiometabolic dysfunction [25]. Firstly, dysfunction of visceral adipose tissue is known to promote a pro-inflammatory state and increase the flux of free fatty acids, thereby contributing to insulin resistance and the overproduction of atherogenic lipoproteins by the liver [26, 27]. Secondly, an elevated TG/HDL-C ratio [28] is a hallmark of atherogenic dyslipidemia, characterized by an abundance of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and small, dense LDL particles [29]. These particles are more prone to vascular infiltration and oxidative modification alongside impaired HDL-mediated reverse cholesterol transport [30]. Together, these processes — chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and resulting endothelial dysfunction — provide a plausible biological framework linking higher CMI values to the increased risk of coronary atherosclerosis observed in our study From a clinical perspective, CMI emerges as a superior risk stratification tool that detects subclinical atherosclerosis through early quantification of visceral adiposity and lipid-inflammatory interplay, preceding conventional risk markers such as elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [31]. Its prognostic capacity enables cost-effective primordial prevention through population-level screening [5]. Precision intervention strategies targeting CMI components—including lifestyle-mediated waist circumference reduction and pharmacotherapeutic optimization of triglyceride-to-HDL cholesterol ratio—demonstrate enhanced efficacy in cardiovascular risk mitigation [32]. Future investigations must validate CMI’s generalizability across heterogeneous populations including individuals with prediabetes and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, while establishing its dynamic responsiveness to therapeutic modulation, thereby catalyzing the evolution of personalized cardiometabolic care paradigms To translate this potential into clinical utility, future research should pursue two critical pathways. First, prospective cohort studies are needed to validate CMI as a predictor of incident CHD events and to confirm its dynamic predictive value as CMI levels change over time. Second, and most importantly, randomized controlled trials should investigate whether CMI serves as a responsive and modifiable treatment target. Such interventional studies could determine if reductions in CMI—achieved through lifestyle modifications, pharmacotherapy (e.g., targeting its components like triglycerides with omega-3 [33] fatty acids or fibrates [34]), or bariatric surgery—are associated with a corresponding decrease in the progression of subclinical atherosclerosis or the ultimate incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events. Establishing CMI as a treatable risk trait would pave the way for its integration into personalized prevention and management strategies, ultimately helping to mitigate the global burden of cardiovascular disease It is important to note that this study is subject to several important limitations. Most critically, the cross-sectional design of the NHANES survey fundamentally prevents the establishment of causal relationships between CMI and CHD. Secondly, the reliance on self-reported CHD diagnoses may introduce misclassification. As previously indicated, this may result in an underestimation of the true CHD prevalence due to probable low sensitivity (i.e., individuals forgetting or being unaware of their diagnosis). However, given that this misclassification is likely non-differential with respect to CMI exposure (i.e., the probability of misreporting CHD is similar across different CMI levels), it typically biases the observed odds ratios towards the null. Consequently, the significant association that was detected may be regarded as a conservative estimate, and the true strength of the relationship may be, in fact, stronger. Additionally, excluding emerging inflammatory biomarkers such as interleukin-6 [35] and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [36] may limit the model’s comprehensiveness. Future prospective cohort studies are recommended to validate CMI’s longitudinal predictive utility. Furthermore, future studies could investigate the effect of established cardiovascular medications on CMI and its metabolic correlates. For example, research has shown that different beta-blockers can affect metabolic parameters such as gamma-glutamyltransferase and uric acid levels differently [37], and these levels may interact indirectly with the pathways reflected by CMI. Investigating such interactions could provide a more nuanced understanding of metabolic management in high-risk patients. These studies should also explore integrating CMI with multi-omics data, including genomic risk scores and coronary calcium imaging. This would advance next-generation cardiovascular risk assessment frameworks.

Conclusion

To conclude, CMI demonstrated an independent positive association with coronary heart disease prevalence (adjusted OR = 1.17, 95%CI:1.11–1.23), persisting after comprehensive multivariable adjustment. As a novel anthropometric composite reflecting cardiometabolic derangements, CMI exhibits a significant discriminative capacity for CHD prediction (AUC = 0.861), positioning it as a pragmatic clinical tool for primordial prevention strategies.
