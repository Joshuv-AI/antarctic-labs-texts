Mean field social optimization: feedback person-by-person optimality and the dynamic programming equation
Minyi Huang, Shuenn-Jyi Sheu, Li-Hsien Sun

Abstract
. We consider mean field social optimization in nonlinear diffusion models. By dynamic programming with a representative agent employing cooperative optimizer selection, we derive a new Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation to be called the master equation of the value function. Under some regularity conditions, we establish ϵ \epsilon -person-by-person optimality of the master equation-based control laws, which may be viewed as a necessary condition for nearly attaining the social optimum. A major challenge in the analysis is to obtain tight estimates, within an error of O ⁡ ( 1 / N ) O(1/N) , of the social cost having order O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) . This will be accomplished by multi-scale analysis via constructing two auxiliary master equations. We illustrate explicit solutions of the master equations for the linear-quadratic (LQ) case, and give an application to systemic risk.

[

]
. The reader is referred to
[

,

,

,

,

]
for the analysis of social optima in a linear-quadratic (LQ) framework.
McKean–Vlasov optimal control has been studied in

,

,
13
,
19
,
30
,
32
,
37
,
41
]
,
and may be heuristically interpreted as a limit form of large population optimal control. Cooperative mean field control has applications in

[

]
, collective choice problems
[
34
]
, multi-agent flocking
[
33
]
,
and power systems
[

]
. The notion of social optima is also useful
for measuring (in)efficiency of mean field games

,

In this paper we analyze social optimization for a class of nonlinear models.

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{i}=\

1.1.
Method, analytical challenges, and contributions

One may attempt to
minimize the social cost by directly solving an optimal control problem. This approach, however, becomes infeasible when
N
is large.
Instead, we develop our solution by studying the optimizing behavior of a representative agent.
We exploit a simple but useful idea in team decision theory called person-by-person (PbP) optimality. The reader is referred to
[
29
,
35
,
39
]
for its characterization and to
[
12
]
for its feedback form in decentralized stochastic control. To explain the idea, let
{\mathcal{J}}(u^{1},\cdots,u^{N})
be the team cost of
N
agents with strategies
u^{i}
. Under a given information structure, if the team attains its optimum by a joint strategy
(\hat{u}^{1},\cdots,\hat{u}^{N})
, then no agent can unilaterally take a new strategy to improve for the team. PbP optimality is a necessary condition for team optimality.

Our PbP optimality-based approach studies the value function of a representative agent and applies dynamic programming in an extended state space including an individual state
x
and a measure
\mu
describing the mean field.
This method enables the agent to choose its optimizer in a feedback form
\hat{u}^{i}=\phi(t,x,\mu)
, which differs from
[
36
]
, where the control perturbation is
a non-anticipative process. In the end we obtain a special Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation, which will be called the master equation for the value function; some heuristic derivation has appeared in
[
27
]
without performance analysis. Master equations have been widely studied in mean field games
[
6
,
9
,
14
]
and also mean field optimal control
[
5
,
6
,
19
,
32
]
.

In the setting of social optimization (or large population optimal control), our approach above differs from some existing works, which approximate the
N
-agent optimal control problem by a McKean–Vlasov optimal control problem (see e.g.
[
19
,
20
,
30
,
41
]
). As a result, their value function takes the form
{V}_{\rm mv}(t,\mu)
, only characterizing the performance of the population as a whole. However, in practical cooperative decision scenarios, each constituent agent still desires to know its own performance; our approach responds to this need and specifies an individual’s value function
V(t,x,\mu)
.
The authors in
[
5
]
formulate an optimal control problem combing a representative agent’s state equation with the McKean–Vlasov dynamics, both controlled by the same process. They prove a dynamic programming principle without common noise, and the drift term in their master equation is structured differently from ours.

When the control laws
(\hat{u}^{1},\cdots,\hat{u}^{N})
of
N
agents have been determined from the master equation, an issue of central importance is their performance.
For certain classes of models, the value function of the controlled McKean–Vlasov dynamics has been shown to be the limit of the value function of the
N
-agent optimal control problem as
N\to\infty

[
8
,
19
]
. However,
to our best knowledge, except for LQ cases
[
21
,
25
,
26
,
40
]
, there has existed no past work on social optimization addressing the performance of the infinite population-based control laws when they are applied by finite populations. As it turns out, our PbP optimality-based approach provides a tractable framework for such performance analysis.
We will establish approximate PbP optimality for the master equation-based control laws in optimizing
J^{(N)}_{\rm soc}
, which may be viewed as a necessary condition for achieving near social optimality. Naturally, it is desirable to bound the gap between the attained performance and exact PbP optimality as tightly as possible. A loose bound would not tell much about performance.

PbP optimality may be viewed as a special case of a Nash equilibrium where all agents take the social cost as their individual costs. For this reason, our performance estimate in terms of approximate PbP optimality is similar to the
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium analysis in mean field games
[
7
]
. However, we confront a far more difficult problem, especially under model nonlinearity. We may think of
J^{(N)}_{\rm soc}
as a quantity of order
O(N)
while expecting the agent in question to minimize it up to an accuracy of
o(1)
.
The estimate of the performance loss becomes very intricate since we face quantities of such drastically different scales as
O(N)
and
o(1)
. In contrast, the
\epsilon
-Nash equilibrium analysis involves quantities of scales
O(1)
and
o(1)
, for an agent’s cost and its performance loss, respectively.
Under some regularity conditions pertaining to the solution of the master equation, we prove that

\hat{u}^{i}
is nearly PbP optimal, with an inadequacy (or regret) of at most
O(1/N)
for optimizing
J^{(N)}_{\rm soc}
.

Our performance analysis depends on exploiting the multi-scale feature when an individual attempts to optimize the social cost, where the key idea is to decompose the social cost roughly as the sum of a much larger macroscopic term and another term that carries information on the representative agent and so will be called the instrumental value function. Meanwhile, we need to introduce two auxiliary master equations. Such combined usage of functions describing phenomena of different scales, together with the construction of their equations, has got much of its inspiration from the physics literature on computing tiny quantities which are superposed to very large ones
[
1
, p. 199-209]

[
18
, p. 195-198]

and also from perturbation methods of dynamical systems
[
23
, ch. 3]
.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces the master equation of the value function. To prepare for performance analysis, Section
3
introduces the instrumental value function and two auxiliary master equations.
The asymptotic PbP optimality theorem is proved in Section
4
. Section
5
illustrates explicit solutions of the master equations by linear-quadratic (LQ) models and a systemic risk example.

5.4.

In the system of inter-bank lending and borrowing,
the state processes of
N

with the initial value

,
where
W_{t}^{0}
,
W^{1}_{t}
,
\cdots

The

J^{(N)}_{\rm soc}({\mathbf{u}}(\cdot))=\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{i}({\mathbf{u}}(\cdot)),\quad{\mathbf{u}}\coloneqq(u^{1},\cdots,u^{N}),

where

J_{i}({\mathbf{u}}(\cdot))={\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{T}L(X_{t}^{i},u_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{(-i)})dt+{\mathbb{E}}g(X^{i}_{T},X_{T}^{(-i)})

with
X_{t}^{(-i)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}X_{t}^{j}
, running cost

L(x^{i},u^{i},z)=(u^{i})^{2}+2qu^{i}(x^{i}-z)+{\epsilon_{0}}(x^{i}-z)^{2},\quad q^{2}\leq\epsilon_{0},

and terminal cost

g(x^{i},z)={c}(x^{i}-z)^{2}.

The parameters
q
,
\epsilon_{0}
and
c
are positive with
q^{2}\leq\epsilon_{0}
to ensure convexity of the running cost
L(x^{i},u^{i},z)
.
See
[
15
]
for an interpretation of
q
in terms of incentive for lending/borrowing.

5.4.1.
The direct solution

Denote

{\mathbf{x}}=(x^{1},\cdots,x^{N})^{T}
. The value function is

\displaystyle U^{N}_{\rm soc}(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\inf_{{\mathbf{u}(\cdot)}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{t}^{T}L(X^{i}_{s},u^{i}_{s},X_{s}^{(-i)})ds+g(X^{i}_{T},X_{T}^{(-i)})\right].

where
(t,{\mathbf{x}})\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.
By solving the HJB equation of

, we obtain

\displaystyle U^{N}_{\rm soc}(t,{\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{x}}^{T}{\mathbf{P}}(t){\mathbf{x}}+r(t),

where
{\mathbf{P}}(t)
has
N

\pi_{1}
and off-diagonal entries all equal to
\pi_{2}
, with

\displaystyle 0=\dot{\pi}_{1}-(\pi_{1}+q)^{2}-\Big(\pi_{2}-\frac{q}{N-1}\Big)^{2}(N-1)+\frac{\epsilon_{0}N}{N-1},

\displaystyle 0=\dot{\pi}_{2}-2(\pi_{1}+q)\Big(\pi_{2}-\frac{q}{N-1}\Big)-\Big(\pi_{2}-\frac{q}{N-1}\Big)^{2}(N-2)-\frac{\epsilon_{0}N}{(N-1)^{2}},

where
\pi_{1}(T)=cN/(N-1)
and
\pi_{2}(T)=-cN/(N-1)^{2}
.
The optimal control law of the
i
-th bank is

(5.18)

\displaystyle\hat{u}^{N,i}(t,{\mathbf{x}})=-(\pi_{1}+q)x^{i}-\Big(\pi_{2}-\frac{q}{N-1}\Big)\sum_{k\neq i,k=1}^{N}x^{k}.

Denote the ODE

(5.19)

\displaystyle 0=\dot{P}_{d}-(P_{d}+q)^{2}+\epsilon_{0},\quad P_{d}(T)=c,

which has a unique solution on
[0,T]
. We can further show
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\pi_{1}(t)-P_{d}(t)|=O(1/N)
. By checking the ODE of
(N-1)\pi_{2}
, we obtain
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|(N-1)\pi_{2}(t)+P_{d}(t)|=O(1/N)
.
As
N\to\infty
, the optimal control law (
5.18
) takes the limiting form

(5.20)

\hat{u}^{i}(t,x^{i},\mu)=[P_{d}(t)+q](\bar{x}-x^{i}),

where
\bar{x}\coloneqq\langle\mu,y\rangle
.
Comparing (
5.20
) with the open-loop and closed-loop Nash equilibria proposed in
[
15
]
, the social optimum has the same solution as the mean field game has in the limit with
N\rightarrow\infty
.

5.4.2.
The master equation-based control

By (
2.6
), we obtain the minimizer

(5.21)

\displaystyle\hat{u}^{i}=-\frac{1}{2}V_{x}(t,x,\mu)-q(x-\bar{x})-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mu(dy),\partial_{x}\delta_{\mu}V(t,y,\mu;x)\rangle,

where
\bar{x}\coloneqq\langle\mu,y\rangle
.
We take the ansatz
V(t,x,\mu)=P(t)x^{2}+\Lambda(t)\bar{x}^{2}+2H(t)x\bar{x}+r(t)
and derive

\displaystyle 0=\dot{P}-(P+q)^{2}+\epsilon_{0},

\displaystyle 0=\dot{\Lambda}+(\Lambda+H)^{2}-(H-q)^{2}-2\Lambda(P+\Lambda+2H)+\epsilon_{0},

\displaystyle 0=\dot{H}-(P+q)(H-q)-H(P+\Lambda+2H)-\epsilon_{0},

\displaystyle 0=\dot{r}+\sigma^{2}P+\sigma^{2}\rho^{2}(\Lambda+2H),

where
P(T)=c
,
\Lambda(T)=c
,
H(T)=-c
,
r(T)=0
.
Clearly,
P=P_{d}
on
[0,T]
.
Denote
Z_{1}\coloneqq\Lambda+2H
, and we use the equations of
\dot{\Lambda}
and
\dot{H}
to write the ODE:

(5.22)

\displaystyle 0=\dot{Z}_{1}+(Z_{1}-q)^{2}-2(P+Z_{1})(Z_{1}-q)-\epsilon_{0},\quad Z_{1}(T)=-c,

for which we can show that
Z_{1}=-P
is a solution. By uniqueness of the solution of (
5.22
), it follows that
\Lambda+2H=-P
on
[0,T]
. Subsequently, we determine
H
from a linear equation using the equation of
\dot{H}
after setting
\Lambda+2H
as
-P
; this in turn determines
\Lambda
.
The above solution
(P,\Lambda,H)
is unique.
The control law (
5.21
) now becomes

\displaystyle\hat{u}^{i}(t,x,\mu)

\displaystyle=-\{[P(t)+q]x+[\Lambda(t)+2H(t)-q]\bar{x}\}

\displaystyle=[P(t)+q](\bar{x}-x),

which agrees with (
5.20
).

6.
Concluding remarks

Our performance estimate via multi-scale analysis has been based on existence of sufficiently well behaved solutions of two master equations for
V
and
M
. For future work, it will be of interest to develop existence results following such methods as in
[
9
,
16
]
for more specific models.

Appendix A: A formal derivation of the master equation of
V

This appendix considers a more general model with diffusion coefficients
\sigma(X_{t}^{i},u_{t}^{i},\mu_{t}^{-i})
and

\sigma_{0}(X_{t}^{i},u_{t}^{i},\mu_{t}^{-i})
before specializing to the form in (
1.1
). This will give us more insights into the dynamic programming method. Below we accordingly denote
\Sigma(x,u,\mu)\coloneqq(\sigma\sigma^{T})(x,u,\mu)

and

\Sigma_{0}(x,u,\mu)\coloneqq(\sigma_{0}\sigma_{0}^{T})(x,u,\mu)
.

Now we take initial time
t\in[0,T)
and initial states
(x^{1},\cdots,x^{N})
. For the feedback control law
\phi
, denote the controlled state processes
for agents
{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
,
j\neq i
, as

(A.1)

\displaystyle dX_{s}^{j}=

\displaystyle f(X_{s}^{j},\phi(s,X_{s}^{j},\mu_{s}^{-j}),\mu_{s}^{-j})ds+\sigma(X_{s}^{j},\phi(s,X_{s}^{j},\mu_{s}^{-j}),\mu^{-j}_{s})dW_{s}^{j}

\displaystyle+\sigma_{0}(X_{s}^{j},\phi(s,X_{s}^{j},\mu_{s}^{-j}),\mu_{s}^{-j})dW_{s}^{0},\quad s\geq t.

For agent
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
, we have

(A.2)

\displaystyle dX_{s}^{i}=\

\displaystyle f(X_{s}^{i},u_{s}^{i},\mu_{s}^{-i})+\sigma(X_{s}^{i},u_{s}^{i},\mu^{-i}_{s})dW_{s}^{i}+\sigma_{0}(X_{s}^{i},u_{s}^{i},\mu_{s}^{-i})dW_{s}^{0},\quad s\geq t,

where we take
u_{s}^{i}\equiv u^{i}\in\mathbb{U}
for
s\in[t,t+\epsilon)
and
u_{s}^{i}=\phi(s,X_{s}^{i},\mu_{s}^{-i})
on
[t+\epsilon,T]
.

A.1.
The control perturbation of
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}

Note that

V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu)
is defined on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\mathcal{P}}^{N-1}_{\rm em}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, where
{\mathcal{P}}^{N-1}_{\rm em}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is only a subset of
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We still formally denote the derivative
\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu;y)
, which is interpreted
in the following sense: for any
\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm em}^{N-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,

\displaystyle V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\nu)-V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu)=\int_{y}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu;y)(\nu-\mu)(dy)+o(W_{2}(\nu,\mu)).

We give an example to illustrate.

Example A.1
.

Suppose
h(t,x^{i},\mu^{{-i}})=x^{iT}\Pi_{1}(t)x^{i}+\bar{x}^{(-i)T}\Pi_{2}(t)\bar{x}^{(-i)}+2x^{iT}\Pi_{12}(t)\bar{x}^{(-i)}
, where
\bar{x}^{(-i)}\coloneqq\langle y\rangle_{\mu^{-i}}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}x^{j}
and
\Pi_{1}(t)
and
\Pi_{2}(t)
are symmetric. Then

\delta_{\mu}h(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i};y)=2y^{T}\Pi_{2}(t)\langle y\rangle_{\mu^{-i}}+2x^{iT}\Pi_{12}(t)y+\chi(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i}),

where
\chi(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i})
is a normalizing term.

We further formally denote the second order derivative
\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu;y,z)
.
We make formal usage of partial derivatives

\partial_{t}V^{\phi}
,
\partial_{x^{i}}V^{\phi}
,
\partial_{x^{i}}^{2}V^{\phi}
,

\partial_{x^{i}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu;y)
,
\partial_{x^{i}y}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu;y)
,
\partial_{y}^{2}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu;y)
,
\partial_{yz}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu;y,z)
.

A.2.
Cost estimate of
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}

Under (
A.1
)–(
A.2
),
let
J_{i}
be given by (
2.3
).
Taking a fixed constant control
u^{i}
(given the initial condition
(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i})
) on the small interval

[t,t+\epsilon]
,
we have the approximation

(A.3)

\displaystyle J_{i}=\

\displaystyle L(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})\epsilon+V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i})

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i})\epsilon+\partial_{x^{i}}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i})f(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})\epsilon

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\partial_{x^{i}}^{2}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i})\Sigma(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})]\epsilon

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\partial_{x^{i}}^{2}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i})\Sigma_{0}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})]\epsilon

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}\langle\Delta\mu^{-i}(dy),\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i};y)\rangle

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}\langle\Delta\mu^{-i}(dy),\partial_{x^{i}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i};y)\xi_{t}^{i}\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\int_{z}\int_{y}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i};y,z)\Delta\mu^{-i}(dy)\Delta\mu^{-i}(dz)

\displaystyle+o(\epsilon),

where
\Delta\mu^{-i}\coloneqq\mu_{t+\epsilon}^{-i}-\mu^{-i}
and

\xi_{t}^{i}\coloneqq\sigma(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})(W_{t+\epsilon}^{i}-W_{t}^{i})+\sigma_{0}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})(W_{t+\epsilon}^{0}-W_{t}^{0}).

The three expectations in (
A.3
) are needed since the empirical distribution
\mu_{t+\epsilon}^{-i}
is random.
Within
J_{i}
, denote

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{1}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i},u^{i})\coloneqq\

\displaystyle L(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})+\partial_{x_{i}}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i})f(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\partial_{x^{i}}^{2}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i})\Sigma(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\partial^{2}_{x^{i}}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i})\Sigma_{0}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})].

The sum
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
explicitly depends on
u^{i}
.
The remaining components in
J_{i}
receive either no or negligible impact from
u^{i}
.
The optimal choice of
u^{i}
, however, is not to simply minimize
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
.
Instead, one should take into account the impact of
u^{i}
on all other agents, which we call the social impact.

We check the double integral term in
J_{i}
.
As

, we obtain the approximation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\int_{z}\int_{y}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu^{-i};y,z)\Delta\mu^{-i}(dy)\Delta\mu^{-i}(dz)

\displaystyle\qquad\approx\frac{\epsilon}{2}\int_{z}\int_{y}{\rm Tr}\Big[\partial_{yz}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{i},\mu;y,z)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\sigma_{0}(z,\phi(t,z,\mu),\mu)\sigma_{0}^{T}(y,\phi(t,y,\mu),\mu)\Big]\mu(dy)\mu(dz),

where
\mu^{-i}
has been approximated by
\mu
.

A.3.
Cost estimate for all other agents

We check how
u_{t}^{i}\equiv u^{i}
on

affects the cost of
agent
{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
,
j\neq i
. By symmetry of the dynamics of the
N
agents on
[t+\epsilon,T]
, the cost of

{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
on
[t,T]
may be written as

(A.4)

\displaystyle J_{j}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j},{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot))

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{t}^{t+\epsilon}L(X_{s}^{j},u_{s}^{j},\mu_{s}^{-j})ds+V^{\phi}({t+\epsilon},X^{j}_{t+\epsilon},\mu^{-j}_{t+\epsilon})\Big],

where
u_{s}^{j}=\phi(s,X_{s}^{j},\mu_{s}^{-j})

on the whole interval
[t,T]
. In general
J_{j}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j},{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot))\neq V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j})
since
u^{i}_{s}
may differ from
\phi(s,X_{s}^{i},\mu_{s}^{-i})
on
[t,t+\epsilon]
.
Then we take the expansion of (
A.4
) to formally obtain

\displaystyle J_{j}=\

\displaystyle L(x^{j},\phi(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j}),\mu^{-j})\epsilon+V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j})

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j})\epsilon+\partial_{x^{j}}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j})f(x^{j},\phi(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j}),\mu^{-j})\epsilon

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\partial^{2}_{x^{j}}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j})\Sigma(x^{j},\phi(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j}),\mu^{-j})]\epsilon

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\partial^{2}_{x^{j}}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j})\Sigma_{0}(x^{j},\phi(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j}),\mu^{-j})]\epsilon

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}\langle\Delta\mu^{-j}(dy),\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};y)\rangle

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}\langle\Delta\mu^{-j}(dy),\partial_{x^{j}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};y)\xi_{t}^{j}\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\int_{z}\int_{y}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};y,z)\Delta\mu^{-j}(dy)\Delta\mu^{-j}(dz)+o(\epsilon)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon

\displaystyle J_{j,1}+\cdots+J_{j,9}+o(\epsilon),

where

\xi_{t}^{j}\coloneqq\sigma(x^{j},u^{j},\mu^{-j})(W_{t+\epsilon}^{j}-W_{t}^{j})+\sigma_{0}(x^{j},u^{j},\mu^{-j})(W_{t+\epsilon}^{0}-W_{t}^{0})
with
u^{j}=\phi(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j})

\partial_{yz}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu;y,z)|_{y=x^{k},z=x^{i}}

We have

\displaystyle J_{j,7}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ {\mathbb{E}}\langle(\mu_{t+\epsilon}^{-j}-\mu^{j})(dy),\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};y)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}-\{i,j\}}{\mathbb{E}}[\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};X^{k}_{t+\epsilon})-\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{k})]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};X^{i}_{t+\epsilon})-\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{i})],

where the second to last line has a much smaller dependence

u^{i}
than the last line has.
Specifically, when
u^{i}
has a change of magnitude
O(1)
, it results in an
O(\epsilon)
change of
X_{t+\epsilon}^{i}
, which in turn causes an

X_{t+\epsilon}^{j}

For
J_{j,7}

\displaystyle J_{j,7,i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{N-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};X^{i}_{t+\epsilon})-\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{i})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\Big\{\partial_{x^{i}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{i})f(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})\epsilon

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\partial^{2}_{x^{i}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{i})\Sigma(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})]\epsilon

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\partial^{2}_{x^{i}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{i})\Sigma_{0}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})]\epsilon+o(\epsilon)\Big\}.

Next we have

\displaystyle J_{j,8}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ {\mathbb{E}}\langle\Delta\mu^{-j}(dy),\partial_{x^{j}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};y)\xi_{t}^{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N-1}{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}_{-j}}[\partial_{x^{j}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};X_{t+\epsilon}^{k})-\partial_{x^{j}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{k})]\xi_{t}^{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}_{-j}}{\rm Tr}\Big[\partial_{x^{j}x^{k}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{k})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\sigma_{0}(x^{k},u^{k},\mu^{-k})\sigma_{0}^{T}(x^{j},u^{j},\mu^{-j})\Big]\epsilon+o(\epsilon).

In the last summation
u^{l}=\phi(t,x^{l},\mu^{-l})
for all
l\in{\mathcal{N}}_{-i}
.
Within the above sum for
J_{j,8}
, denote its component with

, which depends on
u^{i}

\displaystyle J_{j,8,i}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \frac{\epsilon}{N-1}{\rm Tr}[\partial_{x^{j}x^{i}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{i})\cdot\sigma_{0}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})\sigma_{0}^{T}(x^{j},u^{j},\mu^{-j})].

To analyze

denote

\displaystyle\xi_{kl}\coloneqq\

\displaystyle\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};X_{t+\epsilon}^{k},X_{t+\epsilon}^{l})-\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{k},X_{t+\epsilon}^{l})

\displaystyle-\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};X_{t+\epsilon}^{k},x^{l})+\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{k},x^{l}).

We have

\displaystyle J_{j,9}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\int_{z}\int_{y}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};y,z)\Delta\mu^{-j}(dy)\Delta\mu^{-j}(dz)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(N-1)^{2}}\sum_{k,l\in{\mathcal{N}}_{-j}}{\mathbb{E}}\xi_{kl}

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(N-1)^{2}}\sum_{k\neq l;k,l\in{\mathcal{N}}_{-j}}\Big\{{\rm Tr}\Big[\partial_{yz}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{k},x^{l})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\sigma_{0}(x^{l},u^{l},\mu^{-l})\sigma_{0}^{T}(x^{k},u^{k},\mu^{-k})\Big]\epsilon+o(\epsilon)\Big\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2(N-1)^{2}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}_{-j}}^{N}\Big\{{\rm Tr}\Big[\partial_{yz}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{k},x^{k})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot(\Sigma+\Sigma_{0})(x^{k},u^{k},\mu^{-k})\Big]\epsilon+o(\epsilon)\Big\},

where
u^{l}=\phi(t,x^{l},\mu^{-l})
for each

.
Subsequently, within the expression of

,
we have the following

dependent components:

\displaystyle J_{j,9,i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\epsilon}{2(N-1)^{2}}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{N}}-\{i,j\}}{\rm Tr}\Big[\partial_{yz}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{k},x^{i})

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\cdot\sigma_{0}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})\sigma_{0}^{T}(x^{k},\phi(t,x^{k},\mu^{-k}),\mu^{-k})\Big]

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon}{2(N-1)^{2}}\sum_{l\in{\mathcal{N}}-\{i,j\}}{\rm Tr}\Big[\partial_{yz}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{i},x^{l})

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\cdot\sigma_{0}(x^{l},\phi(t,x^{l},\mu^{-l}),\mu^{-l})\sigma_{0}^{T}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})\Big]

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon}{2(N-1)^{2}}{\rm Tr}[\partial_{yz}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu^{-j};x^{i},x^{i})\cdot(\Sigma_{0}+\Sigma)(x^{i},u^{i},\mu^{-i})].

A.4.
Approximation

For large

, all the empirical distributions
\mu^{-j}
,

, may be approximated by a common
\mu\in P_{\rm em}^{N-1}
.
For

\displaystyle\Gamma_{7}(t,y,\mu,x^{i},u^{i})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \partial_{x^{i}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,y,\mu;x^{i})f(x^{i},u^{i},\mu)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\partial^{2}_{x^{i}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,y,\mu;x^{i})\Sigma(x^{i},u^{i},\mu)]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}[\partial^{2}_{x^{i}}\delta_{\mu}V^{\phi}(t,y,\mu;x^{i})\Sigma_{0}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu)].

We take the approximation

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{-i}}J_{j,7,i}

\displaystyle\approx\epsilon\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\Gamma_{7}(t,y,\mu,x^{i},u^{i})\mu(dy)\eqqcolon{\mathcal{K}}_{2}(t,x^{i},u^{i},\mu)\epsilon,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{-i}}J_{j,9,i}

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{2N}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{y}{\rm Tr}[\partial_{yx^{i}}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu;y,x^{i})\cdot\sigma_{0}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu)\sigma_{0}^{T}(y,\phi(t,y,\mu),\mu)]\mu(dy)

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon}{2N}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{z}{\rm Tr}[\partial_{x^{i}z}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,x^{j},\mu;x^{i},z)\cdot\sigma_{0}(z,\phi(t,z,\mu),\mu)\sigma_{0}^{T}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu)]\mu(dz)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{2}\int_{w}\int_{y}{\rm Tr}[\partial_{yx^{i}}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,w,\mu;y,x^{i})\cdot\sigma_{0}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu)\sigma^{T}_{0}(y,\phi(t,y,\mu),\mu)]\mu(dy)\mu(dw)

\displaystyle+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\int_{w}\int_{z}{\rm Tr}[\partial_{x^{i}z}\delta_{\mu\mu}V^{\phi}(t,w,\mu;x^{i},z)\cdot\sigma_{0}(z,\phi(t,z,\mu),\mu)\sigma_{0}^{T}(x^{i},u^{i},\mu)]\mu(dz)\mu(dw)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{4}(t,x^{i},u^{i},\mu)\epsilon.

A.5.
Cooperative optimizer selection

Within the mean field limit, we consider
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and the control law
\phi(t,x,\mu)
.
The function

Lemma B.1
