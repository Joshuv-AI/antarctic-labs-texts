Synergistic Inhibition of Spinal TRPV1 Signaling by Combined Eye Acupuncture and Electroacupuncture Alleviates Inflammatory Pain in a Rat Model of Knee Osteoarthritis
Authors: Shan Chen, Yinyan He, Bin Que, Zhipeng Liang, Xin Chen, Sheng Li, Rong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic pain caused by knee osteoarthritis (KOA) remains inadequately managed by safe, non-pharmacological therapies. Although acupuncture is recommended for KOA pain by clinical guidelines, the efficacy of single-modality acupuncture remains modest, and the synergistic mechanisms of combining different acupuncture modalities are poorly understood.ObjectiveTo determine whether combining eye acupuncture (EAp) with electroacupuncture (EA) produces greater analgesia in a rat KOA model via regulation of TRPV1 signaling in the spinal dorsal horn (SDH) and dorsal root ganglia (DRG).MethodsThirty-six Sprague–Dawley rats (19 males, 17 females) were randomized into sham, model, EA, and combination (EA + EAp) groups (n = 9 per group, each containing 4–5 rats of each sex). KOA was induced by anterior cruciate ligament transection combined with medial meniscectomy. After 7 days of intervention, mechanical and thermal pain thresholds were assessed. Serum concentrations of PGE2, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were quantified by ELISA. TRPV1 expression in SDH and DRG was detected by immunofluorescence and Western blot.ResultsCombination treatment significantly elevated pain thresholds and was associated with reduced levels of all four cytokines compared to the model and EA groups (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). On day 7, the combination group showed a 2.2-fold increase in MWT compared to the model group (18.7 ± 2.6 g vs 8.5 ± 1.9 g, P < 0.001) and a 31.7% increase compared to the EA group (P < 0.01). Immunofluorescence showed the lowest TRPV1/DAPI fluorescence intensity ratio in the combination group in both DRG and SDH (P < 0.01 vs model; P < 0.05 vs EA). Western blot showed that combination therapy was associated with markedly lower total TRPV1 and phosphorylated TRPV1 (p-TRPV1) protein levels compared with the model and EA groups, with consistent trends in male and female rats.ConclusionCombined EAp and EA exert synergistic anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects in KOA rats, which are associated with downregulation of TRPV1 signaling in the SDH and DRG.Keywords: knee osteoarthritis, eye acupuncture, electroacupuncture, TRPV1, spinal dorsal horn, dorsal root ganglion

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a leading global cause of chronic pain and disability, characterized by progressive joint degeneration, synovial inflammation, and central sensitization.1 Pain—the most disabling symptom of KOA—substantially impairs quality of life and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden.2 Knee osteoarthritis represents a major public health burden in China. According to data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, the number of KOA patients in China reached approximately 10.96 million in 2021, reflecting an increase of 157.15% compared to 1992.3 Current first-line pharmacotherapies for KOA pain, including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and analgesics, are limited by considerable adverse effects such as gastrointestinal injury, cardiovascular risks, and hepatorenal toxicity.4 The World Health Organization recommends acupuncture as a non-pharmacological treatment option for chronic pain management. Electroacupuncture (EA), which combines traditional needling with modern electrical stimulation, has been shown to modulate endogenous analgesic systems and suppress inflammatory responses.5 Recent high-quality meta-analyses confirmed that EA produces large effects on pain and function in KOA patients.6 However, the efficacy of single-modality acupuncture is modest.

Eye acupuncture (EAp), a micro-acupuncture technique based on traditional Chinese medicine holism theory, treats systemic diseases by stimulating specific peri-orbital acupoints. Although clinical observations suggest synergistic effects when combined with body acupuncture, the underlying neural mechanisms—potentially involving trigeminal nerve projections to spinal circuits—have not been investigated7. According to TCM theory, the liver governs the sinews and the knee is the “residence of the sinews”; the peri-orbital liver zone is believed to regulate liver qi and blood, thereby influencing the sinews. This provides a theoretical rationale for combining eye acupuncture (systemic regulation) with local meridian points (local dredging). Eye acupuncture is hypothesized to activate trigeminal afferents that project to spinal pain modulatory circuits, but direct evidence is lacking.

Transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1), a non-selective cation channel, plays a pivotal role in nociceptive transduction and sensitization to inflammatory pain.8 Sensitization of TRPV1 channels in the spinal dorsal horn represents a key mechanism underlying chronic pain maintenance.9 Notably, inhibiting spinal TRPV1 signaling significantly alleviates neuropathic pain,10 and EA has been shown to regulate TRPV1 in fibromyalgia models.11 Moreover, emerging evidence indicates that TRPV1 is expressed not only in sensory neurons but also in synovial macrophages and subchondral osteoblasts, making it a promising therapeutic target for KOA.12 However, the regulatory effects of combination acupuncture on spinal TRPV1 signaling remain unreported.

The present study focuses on the early inflammatory phase of post-traumatic KOA, a stage characterized by acute pain and rapid molecular changes in spinal pain processing pathways, which is particularly suitable for investigating the acute modulation of TRPV1 phosphorylation by acupuncture interventions. We therefore hypothesized that combining EAp with EA would produce greater analgesia than either modality alone by synergistically inhibiting the TRPV1 pathway in the SDH and DRG, a hypothesis supported by both TCM theory and preliminary clinical observations.7 Validating this hypothesis would not only provide a mechanistic rationale for combination acupuncture but also identify spinal TRPV1 phosphorylation as a potential molecular target for optimizing clinical analgesic protocols. To test this, we established a KOA rat model and evaluated the therapeutic effects of combination therapy using behavioral, biochemical, and immunohistochemical assessments.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals and Ethics; Randomization, Blinding, Sample Size, and Ethics. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th Edition). Rats were anesthetized with 10% chloral hydrate (300 mg/kg, i.p) for surgical procedures. At the endpoint, rats were deeply anesthetized with an overdose of 10% chloral hydrate (500 mg/kg, i.p) and then decapitated for tissue collection. All procedures were performed in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 Edition).

Thirty-six specific pathogen-free Sprague-Dawley rats (19 males and 17 females; body weight 200 ± 20 g) were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (License No. SCXK (Beijing) 2024–0003). Animals were housed under standard conditions (22 ± 2°C, 55 ± 5% humidity, 12 h/12 h light-dark cycle) with free access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (Approval No: 202410–0954) and followed international guidelines for laboratory animal care.

Rats were randomly assigned to four groups (sham, model, EA, combination; n = 9 per group) using a random number table generated by an independent statistician. The exact sex distribution per group was: sham (5 males, 4 females), model (4 males, 5 females), EA (5 males, 4 females), combination (4 males, 5 females). Allocation concealment was maintained with sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The investigator performing behavioral tests and the technician analyzing Western blot and immunofluorescence images were blinded to group assignment. Acupuncture interventions were delivered by a separate practitioner who was not involved in any outcome assessment.

Sample size was estimated based on a pilot experiment (n = 3 per group) measuring mechanical withdrawal threshold on day 7. Assuming an effect size of 1.2 (difference between EA and combination groups), α = 0.05, β = 0.2, a minimum of 8 rats per group was required. To account for potential dropouts, 9 rats were included per group.

Because the sample size per sex (n = 4–5 per group) was considered moderate for formal subgroup analysis, data from male and female rats were pooled after confirming similar trends by descriptive inspection. No statistical comparisons between sexes were performed.

Experimental Grouping and Model Establishment

Rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 9/group) using a random number table:

Sham group: The left knee joint cavity was exposed and immediately sutured.

KOA model group: KOA was induced by anterior cruciate ligament transection combined with medial meniscectomy.

Electroacupuncture (EA) group: EA intervention after modeling.

Combination therapy (EA + EAp) group: EAp followed by EA after modeling.

Model establishment was performed according to Glasson et al13 with modifications. Rats were anesthetized with 10% chloral hydrate (300 mg/kg, i. p)., a dose established as a standard protocol for rodent anesthesia.14 A medial incision was made on the left knee to expose the joint cavity. The anterior cruciate ligament was transected, and the medial meniscus was completely removed, followed by layered suturing. In the sham group, only the skin was incised and the joint cavity exposed before suturing. All rats received intraperitoneal penicillin for 3 days post-surgery to prevent infection. No formal sex comparisons were performed.

Intervention Protocols

All interventions began 24 h after modeling and were performed once daily for seven consecutive days. The 7-day intervention window was selected to capture acute changes in TRPV1 phosphorylation, a key early event in central sensitization, while minimizing the confounding effects of secondary structural joint damage that typically emerge in later stages of KOA.

EA group: Bilateral “Zusanli” (ST36) and “Kunlun” (BL60) acupoints were selected. Zusanli (ST36) and Kunlun (BL60) were selected based on: (1) their traditional indications for knee pain and sinew disorders according to TCM meridian theory—ST36 is the He-Sea point of the Stomach Meridian and a powerful tonification point, while BL60 is the Jing-River point of the Bladder Meridian, traditionally indicated for low back and lower extremity pain; (2) their documented analgesic effects in animal models of inflammatory pain;15,16 and (3) their accessible anatomical locations on the hindlimb, which allow consistent, reproducible electrical stimulation without interfering with behavioral testing. This combination of a tonification point (ST36) and a collateral-dredging point (BL60) is commonly used in experimental acupuncture research and provides a stable baseline for evaluating the add-on effect of eye acupuncture. After needle insertion and deqi sensation, needles were connected to an electronic acupuncture apparatus (dense-disperse wave, 2/15 Hz, 0.5 mA, 30 min).

Combination group: Eye acupuncture was performed first. Bilateral liver zone and lower Jiao zone acupoints were selected using the peri-orbital transverse insertion method, with needles retained for 20 min. After removing EAp needles, EA was administered immediately following the same protocol as the EA group.

Model and sham groups: Rats were immobilized under the same conditions for the same duration but without any acupuncture intervention.

Behavioral Assessments

Mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT) and thermal withdrawal latency (TWL) were measured at baseline (1 day before modeling), 24 h after modeling (to confirm success), and on days 1, 3, and 7 after treatment initiation.

MWT: Von Frey filaments were applied to the center of the left hind paw with increasing force. The minimum force eliciting paw withdrawal was recorded. Measurements were repeated three times at 5-min intervals.

TWL: A thermal radiation apparatus was used to irradiate the left hind paw. The latency to paw withdrawal was recorded (cut-off 20s). Measurements were repeated three times at 10-min intervals.

Sample Collection and Processing

After the final behavioral test, rats were deeply anesthetized with an overdose of 10% chloral hydrate. Blood was collected via left ventricular puncture, allowed to clot at 4°C for 2 h, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min to obtain serum. Rats were then transcardially perfused with 4°C saline. The L4–L6 spinal cord segments were harvested, and the dorsal horn tissues were isolated on ice. Each sample was divided: one portion was stored at −80°C for protein extraction, the other fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h for paraffin embedding and sectioning. L4–L6 DRGs were also harvested bilaterally and processed identically.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and PGE2 were determined using commercial ELISA kits (Lianke Bio, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M5). Cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves. PGE2 required an additional pre-incubation step with Assay Diluent at 37°C for 60 min before the standard protocol, as per kit instructions. PGE2 concentrations were fitted with a four-parameter logistic (4PL) curve, while linear regression was used for the other cytokines. Blood samples were collected from all 36 rats. After centrifugation, serum from one rat in the model group, one rat in the EA group, and one rat in the combination group was hemolyzed (visible pink coloration) and excluded from analysis. Thus, ELISA analyses were performed on n = 8 per group for these three groups, and n = 9 for the sham group. No other samples were excluded.

Western Blot Analysis

Approximately 30 mg of spinal dorsal horn tissue was homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 15 min, 4°C), the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined by the BCA assay. 40 μg of protein per sample was separated by 12% SDS-PAGE (80 V for 20 min, then 120 V for 60 min) and transferred to PVDF membranes using a wet transfer system (200 mA, 60–90 min, on ice). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti-TRPV1 (1:2000, Abcam), anti-p-TRPV1 (Ser800, 1:2000, Thermo Fisher), and anti-β-actin (1:20000, Proteintech). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:50000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Amersham Imager 600). For each group, tissues from 3 male and 3 female rats were analyzed separately, and the densitometric data were pooled (n = 6 per group). Representative blots from three independent experiments (separately for male and female rats) are shown.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Fixed spinal cord and DRG tissues were cryoprotected in sucrose and sectioned at 14 μm. Sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, blocked with 5% donkey serum, and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-TRPV1 (1:500) and anti-NeuN (1:1000). The next day, sections were incubated with Cy3-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit (1:1000) and FITC-conjugated donkey anti-mouse (1:1000) secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 980). The TRPV1/DAPI fluorescence intensity ratio in SDH laminae I–II and DRG neurons was quantified using ImageJ. For immunofluorescence, three rats per group (randomly selected, including at least one male and one female) were used (n = 3 per group).

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was checked by the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances by Levene’s test. One-way ANOVA was performed, followed by post-hoc tests as appropriate: Bonferroni for behavioral data (equal variances assumed), Tukey’s HSD for immunofluorescence, and for ELISA data, LSD was used when variances were equal, and Games-Howell was used when they were unequal (Levene’s test, P < 0.05). Given the exploratory nature of this study, no additional correction for multiple comparisons was applied; findings should be interpreted conservatively. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0.

Results

Combination Therapy Was Associated with Improved Pain Behaviors

At 24 h post-modeling, both MWT and TWL were significantly lower in the model, EA, and combination groups compared to the sham group (all P < 0.001), confirming successful KOA pain induction. After treatment, both the EA and combination groups showed time-dependent recovery.

MWT: On day 3, the combination group showed higher MWT than the EA group (P < 0.05). By day 7, the combination group exhibited the greatest recovery, showing a 2.2-fold increase compared to the model group (18.7 ± 2.6 g vs 8.5 ± 1.9 g, P < 0.001) and a 31.7% increase compared to the EA group (P < 0.01).

TWL: Similar trends were observed. The combination group was superior to the EA group from day 3, reaching significance on day 7 (14.9 ± 1.6 s vs 11.5 ± 1.8 s in the EA group, P < 0.01; vs 7.8 ± 1.3 s in the model group, P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study provides the first experimental evidence, to our knowledge, that combining eye acupuncture (EAp) with electroacupuncture (EA) produces a synergistic analgesic effect in a rat model of knee osteoarthritis (KOA). This synergy is closely associated with the concurrent downregulation of TRPV1 expression and phosphorylation in both the spinal dorsal horn (SDH) and dorsal root ganglia (DRG). Notably, the combination regimen significantly outperformed EA monotherapy in elevating pain thresholds and reducing systemic pro-inflammatory cytokines (PGE2, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α). These findings not only validate the clinical practice of combining different acupuncture modalities but also suggest spinal TRPV1 signaling as a key molecular substrate for this synergy, offering important mechanistic insights for optimizing clinical acupuncture analgesia. However, the present correlational data do not establish causality; whether TRPV1 inhibition is mechanistically required for the synergistic analgesia requires future interventional studies (eg, intrathecal TRPV1 antagonist or siRNA).

Dual-Site Regulation of Peripheral and Central Sensitization

KOA pain is sustained by a self-perpetuating cycle of peripheral joint inflammation and central sensitization in the spinal cord1,2 Our ELISA data showed that combination therapy induced a robust reduction in serum PGE2, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α compared to EA alone. Given that PGE2 directly sensitizes peripheral nociceptors via EP receptors,6 the marked reduction in this lipid mediator likely contributes to reduced peripheral input into the spinal cord. However, the behavioral advantage of combination therapy was detectable as early as day 3, whereas cytokines were measured only at the endpoint (day 7). This temporal pattern raises the possibility that central analgesic mechanisms could operate partially independently of peripheral cytokine changes, but whether they temporally precede peripheral changes remains unknown. Time-course sampling of both serum and spinal tissue is needed to address this question.

Spinal TRPV1 as a Synergistic Molecular Hub

TRPV1 is a polymodal nociceptor channel expressed at both peripheral and central terminals of primary afferents. Beyond its well-established role in thermal and chemical nociception, accumulating evidence indicates that phosphorylation-dependent sensitization of spinal TRPV1 is a critical driver of central sensitization in chronic inflammatory pain.9,17 Our study extends this concept by demonstrating that while EA monotherapy was associated with a moderate reduction in total TRPV1 protein, the combination of EA + EAp was associated with a significantly greater reduction in both total TRPV1 and its phosphorylated form (p-TRPV1 at Ser800). The p-TRPV1/total TRPV1 ratio—an index of channel sensitization—was normalized to sham levels only in the combination group. This finding is clinically relevant because p-TRPV1, rather than total TRPV1, directly correlates with channel open probability and nociceptor hyperexcitability. Therefore, the combination therapy may have a unique capacity to reverse established central sensitization, a feature particularly valuable for treating chronic KOA pain. Moreover, recent studies have shown that TRPV1 is also expressed in synovial macrophages and subchondral osteoblasts, suggesting that TRPV1 inhibition might simultaneously target both joint pathology and spinal sensitization.12 Descriptively, the synergistic effect was observed in both male and female rats, and no significant sex differences were detected in any outcome measure (all P > 0.05). However, given the limited sample size per sex (n = 4–5 per group for behavior, n = 3 per sex for Western blot), these findings should be considered exploratory and require confirmation in larger sex-balanced cohorts.

Potential Neural Circuits Underlying Eye Acupuncture’s Spinal Effect

How does peri-orbital stimulation (EAp) remotely inhibit spinal TRPV1? We propose two non-mutually exclusive mechanisms. First, EAp may activate trigeminal afferents that project to the trigeminal spinal subnucleus caudalis, which sends direct descending projections to the superficial laminae of the cervical and thoracic spinal cord. Such trigemino-spinal pathways have been implicated in heterotopic acupuncture analgesia.18 Second, EAp may engage supraspinal modulatory centers— particularly the periaqueductal gray (PAG) and the rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM)—which are known to exert descending inhibition of spinal nociceptive transmission. Since EA at ST36 and BL60 also activates the PAG-RVM descending pathway,19 the convergence of two distinct inputs onto the same spinal modulatory network could explain the synergistic downregulation of TRPV1 phosphorylation. ST36 afferents primarily project to the thoracolumbar spinal segments (T13–L2),20 while BL60 afferents are associated with the sacral segments (S1–S2).16 Both acupoints have been shown to activate the PAG and RVM via spinoparabrachial and spinothalamic tracts.18,19,21 The convergence of these two distinct segmental inputs onto the same supraspinal descending modulatory network provides an anatomical basis for the “network convergence” hypothesis. Future studies using retrograde tract-tracing (eg, cholera toxin B subunit) from the spinal dorsal horn to the trigeminal subnucleus caudalis, combined with chemogenetic (hM4Di) silencing of this projection during eye acupuncture, could establish a causal link between peri-orbital stimulation and spinal TRPV1 modulation. A recent review also proposed a three-level spatial framework (peripheral input, spinal response, and brain integration) for acupuncture analgesia, which further supports the plausibility of such hierarchical interactions.22

Integration with Traditional Chinese Medicine Theory

From a TCM perspective, the acupoint combination of ST36 (Zusanli, a he-sea point of the stomach meridian), BL60 (Kunlun, a point on the bladder meridian), and the peri-orbital liver and lower Jiao zones is believed to harmonize systemic qi and blood while locally dispersing stasis in the knee region. Our molecular data provide a biological correlate for this holistic effect: the combination therapy simultaneously reduced peripheral inflammatory mediators (TCM: “clearing heat and resolving dampness”) and suppressed spinal TRPV1 phosphorylation (TCM: “calming the liver and extinguishing wind”). Thus, the current study offers a translational bridge between TCM theory and modern pain neuroscience.

Clinical and Translational Implications

Current pharmacotherapies for KOA pain are limited by gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, and renal adverse effects.4 The present findings suggest that a combination of two non-pharmacological, low-risk interventions—eye acupuncture and electroacupuncture—could produce clinically meaningful analgesia by targeting a specific molecular pathway (spinal TRPV1 phosphorylation) that is not directly addressed by NSAIDs or opioids. If replicated in human trials and confirmed using TRPV1-specific antagonists or PET imaging of spinal TRPV1 availability, this combination regimen could provide a rationale for future clinical investigation. However, direct clinical translation requires confirmation in well-designed human studies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while we observed a strong association between TRPV1 downregulation and analgesia, causality has not been established. This is the most critical unresolved question of the present study. To establish causality, future studies should employ intrathecal administration of TRPV1-specific antagonists (eg, AMG9810) or TRPV1 siRNA knockdown in the spinal dorsal horn, combined with behavioral and molecular assessments, to determine whether TRPV1 inhibition is mechanistically required for the synergistic analgesic effect. Second, the observation period was limited to 7 days. The 7-day window was chosen to capture the acute inflammatory pain phase, which is relevant to early post-traumatic OA and allows mechanistic investigation of TRPV1 phosphorylation. However, this limits extrapolation to chronic KOA pain. Long-term studies (>4 weeks) are warranted to assess durability and potential disease-modifying effects on cartilage degeneration. Third, we did not include a sham acupuncture control (eg, superficial needling at non-acupoints), which would help rule out non-specific needling effects and placebo-like responses. Fourth, the sample size per sex (n=4–5 per group for behavior; n=3 per sex for Western blot) was insufficient for formal sex-stratified analysis; although trends appeared similar between males and females. These findings should be regarded as exploratory, and confirmation in a larger sex-balanced cohort is required to definitively address sex differences in TRPV1-mediated acupuncture analgesia. Fifth, immunofluorescence analyses were based on n = 3 per group; these results should be confirmed in larger samples. The small sample size limits the statistical power of these qualitative observations, and they should be considered supportive rather than definitive evidence for quantitative conclusions regarding TRPV1 localization. Sixth, cytokine levels were measured only at the endpoint; time-course sampling is needed to dissect the temporal relationship between peripheral inflammation and central TRPV1 modulation. Seventh, ELISA data were analyzed without correction for multiple comparisons (four cytokines and multiple pairwise tests), which may increase the risk of type I error. Confirmatory studies should apply stricter correction (eg, Bonferroni or false discovery rate). Eighth, we acknowledge that chloral hydrate, the anesthetic used in this study, may have immunomodulatory effects that could influence inflammatory cytokine measurements. Although the dose used (300 mg/kg, i.p) is a standard protocol for rodent anesthesia,14 and TNF-α expression appears unaffected by chloral hydrate,23 potential effects on other cytokines (eg, IL-1β and IL-6) cannot be excluded. Future investigations should consider using pentobarbital sodium or inhalation anesthetics (eg, isoflurane) to exclude this potential confound.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that the combination of eye acupuncture and electroacupuncture is associated with synergistic alleviation of inflammatory pain in a rat KOA model. This effect correlates with reduced TRPV1 expression and phosphorylation in the spinal dorsal horn and DRG. These findings suggest, but do not prove, that TRPV1 signaling may be a molecular substrate for this synergy. Causal validation using TRPV1-specific antagonists is warranted in future interventional studies.
