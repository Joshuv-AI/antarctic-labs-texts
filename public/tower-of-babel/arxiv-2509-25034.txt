MARLIN: Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning with Murmuration Intelligence and LLM Guidance for Reservoir Management Thanks: * Equal contribution
Heming Fu 1 * , Guojun Xiong 2 * , Jian Li 1 , Shan Lin 1 1 Stony Brook University, 2 Harvard University {heming.fu, jian.li.3, shan.lin}@stonybrook.edu, gjxiong@g.harvard.edu

Abstract
. As climate change intensifies extreme weather events, water disasters pose growing threats to global communities, making adaptive reservoir management critical for protecting vulnerable populations and ensuring water security. Modern water resource management faces unprecedented challenges from cascading uncertainties propagating through interconnected reservoir networks. These uncertainties, rooted in physical water transfer losses and environmental variability, make precise control difficult. For example, sending 10 tons downstream may yield only 8-12 tons due to evaporation and seepage. Traditional centralized optimization approaches suffer from exponential computational complexity and cannot effectively handle such real-world uncertainties, while existing multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) methods fail to achieve effective coordination under uncertainty. To address these challenges, we present MARLIN , a decentralized reservoir management framework inspired by starling murmurations intelligence. Integrating bio-inspired alignment , separation , and cohesion rules with MARL, MARLIN enables individual reservoirs to make local decisions while achieving emergent global coordination. In addition, a LLM provides real-time reward shaping signals, guiding agents to adapt to environmental changes and human-defined preferences. Experiments on real-world USGS data show that MARLIN improves uncertainty handling by 23%, cuts computation by 35%, and accelerates flood response by 68%, exhibiting super-linear coordination, with complexity scaling 5.4× from 400 to 10,000 nodes. These results demonstrate MARLIN ’s potential for disaster prevention and protecting communities through intelligent, scalable water resource management.

Together, they highlight the deeper risks posed by fragmented dam oversight and insufficient hydrological response mechanisms in the U.S.

These failures reveal two key limitations of current approaches. First, as reservoir networks grow, computational complexity scales as
O(n^{3})
(due to pairwise interactions and constraint coupling in optimization), making real-time control impractical, especially since advanced Model predictive control (MPC) systems cannot respond quickly to fast-changing conditions
(
Koo et al. 2025
;
Lin et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2020
)
. Second, cascading uncertainty amplifies prediction errors in centralized models. We identify a dual-layer structure:
Level 1
uncertainty arises from the water transfer process itself (e.g., sending 10 tons downstream may yield 8–12 tons due to evaporation, seepage, and variable channel conditions);
Level 2
stems from variable environmental factors like rainfall and soil saturation. Small measurement errors (5–10% in flow sensors and level gauges) at individual nodes can compound through network propagation. Assuming independent error propagation with 7% average measurement uncertainty, the cumulative uncertainty grows as
\sigma_{cumulative}=\sigma_{base}\sqrt{n}
, reaching approximately 22% after 10 cascaded links, which can exceed 40% under correlated environmental conditions, rendering centralized control unreliable.

Zhang et al. 2020

;
Rashid et al. 2018
;
Yu et al. 2021
)

He et al. 2023
)
.
In contrast, nature demonstrates robust coordination through simple local rules. Starling murmurations (Figure

(
Reynolds 1987
;
Hildenbrandt et al. 2010
;
Cavagna et al. 2010
)
. These principles support rapid, resilient coordination even in noisy and uncertain environments.

Inspired by these natural mechanisms, we introduce
MARLIN
, a decentralized framework that integrates bio-inspired coordination with reinforcement learning for managing complex water systems under dual-layer uncertainty.
MARLIN
features three key components:
(1) murmuration-inspired rules that map alignment, separation, and cohesion to reservoir control actions, specifically designed to handle Level 1 water transfer uncertainties while fostering local responsiveness and global safety;
(2) a specialized MARL architecture that incorporates these coordination signals into policy gradients for stable multi-agent learning; and
(3) To address Level 2 environmental uncertainties, which involve complex interactions between weather patterns, regulatory policies, and stakeholder priorities that cannot be easily encoded in mathematical models, we integrate LLM that can process diverse textual information sources (weather forecasts, regulatory documents, stakeholder communications) and translate this heterogeneous knowledge into appropriate reward shaping, enabling the system to adapt to changing environmental and social contexts that would be difficult to capture through traditional optimization approaches.

Experiments on real-world USGS watershed data demonstrate that
MARLIN
improves uncertainty handling by 23%, reduces computation by 35%, speeds up flood response by 68%, and improves regional water balance by 42% compared to state-of-the-art baselines.

In summary, our main contributions are:

\triangleright

We propose the first bio-inspired MARL framework that applies starling murmuration principles to decentralized water resource management, where complex system-wide coordination emerges naturally from simple local interactions, scaling effectively to thousands of agents.

\triangleright

We design a dual-layer uncertainty handling mechanism, where local coordination rules (alignment, separation, cohesion) address water transfer uncertainty, and LLM-guided reward shaping adapts to environmental fluctuations and human preferences.

\triangleright

We validate our approach on real-world USGS data, demonstrating 23% improvement in uncertainty handling, 35% reduction in computation, and super-linear scalability with increasing system size and coordination complexity, with significant potential for protecting vulnerable communities from water-related disasters.

Related Work

Water Management and Multi-Agent Systems.

Classical water management relied on deterministic optimization like dynamic programming
(
Yakowitz 1982
;
Li et al. 2014
)
and network flow models
(
Tahiri et al. 2022
)
, suffering from exponential complexity and static assumptions. MPC became dominant in operational systems but demonstrates critical limitations during rapid changes. The 2011 Mississippi floods exemplified delayed recalibration contributing to $2.8 billion avoidable damage
(
Sainz 2013
)
.
MARL emerged as a distributed alternative. MADDPG
(
Lowe et al. 2017
)
uses centralized critics with decentralized actors, QMIX
(
Rashid et al. 2018
)
employs value decomposition with monotonic mixing, and MAPPO
(
Yu et al. 2021
)
adapts PPO to multi-agent settings. However, these methods face training instability under non-stationary environments and assume coordination emerges naturally from individual optimization, overlooking explicit coordination mechanisms needed for uncertainty handling
(
Fu et al. 2025
)
.

Bio-Inspired Coordination.

Reynolds formalized flocking behavior into alignment, separation, and cohesion rules
(
Reynolds 1987
)
. Theoretical analysis revealed phase transitions between ordered and disordered states
(
Chaté and Muñoz 2014
;
Vicsek et al. 1995
)
, while empirical studies confirmed scale-free correlations and rapid information propagation
(
Cavagna et al. 2010
)
. These inspired algorithms like Particle Swarm Optimization
(
Kennedy and Eberhart 1995
)
and practical swarm robotics applications
(
Rubenstein et al. 2014
;
Poli 2007
)
. However, most focus on continuous motion rather than discrete decision-making required in infrastructure control.

LLMs for Adaptive Control.

Recent work explores LLMs for reward design and adaptation
(
Tec et al. 2025
;
Xiong and Tambe 2025
)
. Eureka
(
Ma et al. 2023
)
uses GPT-4 for automated reward generation, while L2R
(
Goyal et al. 2019
;
Goyal et al. 2021
)
enables natural language reward specification. However, these approaches primarily focus on single-agent settings or assume full observability, limiting their applicability in decentralized multi-agent systems.

3.
Problem Formulation

Modern water resource management systems face unprecedented challenges from cascading uncertainties that propagate through interconnected reservoir networks. We formulate this as a distributed coordination problem under dual-layer uncertainty, where traditional centralized approaches fail due to exponential computational complexity and compounding prediction errors.

3.1.
Network Model and Stochastic Dynamics

We model the reservoir network as a time-varying stochastic graph
\mathcal{G}(t)=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\boldsymbol{\Theta}(t))
, where
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}
represents the set of
n
reservoir nodes;
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
denotes water flow channels between reservoirs; and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}(t)=\{\theta_{1}(t),\theta_{2}(t),\ldots,\theta_{m}(t)\}
captures time-varying environmental parameters.
Each reservoir
i
maintains state vector
\mathbf{s}_{i}(t)=[h_{i}(t),{q}_{\text{in},i}(t),{q}_{\text{out},i}(t),\mathbf{w}_{i}(t),\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}(t)]^{\intercal}
, where
h_{i}(t)
is water level,
{q}_{\text{in/out},i}(t)
represent flows,
\mathbf{w}_{i}(t)
captures weather, and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}(t)
represents demand patterns.
The water level dynamics follow a stochastic differential equation:

(1)

\displaystyle\hskip-7.11317pt\frac{dh_{i}(t)}{dt}\!=\!\frac{1}{C_{i}}\!\!\left[\!\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{in}}(i)}\!\!\!\!\!f_{ji}(t)\!-\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{out}}(i)}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!f_{ik}(t)\!+\!q_{\text{ext},i}(t)\!+\!\eta_{i}(t)\!\right],

where
C_{i}
is reservoir capacity,
f_{ij}(t)
represents actual water transfer (subject to uncertainty),
q_{\text{ext},i}(t)
captures external inflows, and
\eta_{i}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\eta,i})
models stochastic noise.

3.2.
Dual-Layer Uncertainty Framework

The critical challenge lies in cascading uncertainty amplification. We identify two distinct sources:

Level 1: Water Transfer Uncertainty
arises from physical losses and cascading effects during water transport. When reservoir
i
releases volume
a_{i}(t)
, the actual received volume follows:

\displaystyle f_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{ij}(t),\sigma^{2}_{ij}(t)),

\displaystyle\mu_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{ij}(t)\cdot g_{ij}(a_{i}(t),h_{i}(t),h_{j}(t)),

where
\alpha_{ij}(t)\in[0,1]
represents channel efficiency (accounting for evaporation, seepage) and
g_{ij}(\cdot)
is the nominal flow function.

Level 2: Environmental Parameter Uncertainty
captures how environmental conditions affect system parameters:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle\sim\text{Gamma}(\kappa_{ij},\theta_{ij}(t)),

\displaystyle\theta_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle=\theta_{\text{base},ij}+\beta_{\text{environment}}\|\mathbf{w}_{i}(t)-\mathbf{w}_{j}(t)\|_{2},

meaning uncertainty increases with enviromental changes caused by weather or human activities between connected reservoirs, reflecting how environmental variability affects transfer reliability.

3.3.
Multi-Objective Control Problem

Water management requires balancing competing objectives across spatial and temporal scales.
We define:

\displaystyle J_{\text{safety}}({\pi})

\displaystyle\!=\!-\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{\text{flood},i}\max\big(0,h_{i}(t)\!-\!h_{\text{safe},i}\big)^{2}\right],

\displaystyle J_{\text{supply}}({\pi})

\displaystyle\!=\!\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}_{i}}\mathbb{I}\big[q_{\text{supply},d}(t)\!\geq\!q_{\text{demand},d}(t)\big]\right],

\displaystyle J_{\text{ecology}}({\pi})

\displaystyle\!=\!\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\min\left(1,\frac{Q_{\text{actual}}(t)}{Q_{\text{eco}}(t)}\right)\right],

\displaystyle J_{\text{efficiency}}({\pi})

\displaystyle\!=\!-\mathbb{E}_{{\pi}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(c_{\text{op},i}a_{i}(t)\!+\!c_{\text{dev},i}|h_{i}(t)\!-\!h_{\text{target},i}|^{2}\right)\right],

where
J_{\text{safety}}
penalizes flood risk (water levels exceeding safe thresholds),
J_{\text{supply}}
measures demand satisfaction rate across all demand points,
J_{\text{ecology}}
ensures ecological flow requirements, and
J_{\text{efficiency}}
minimizes operational costs and deviations from target levels.
Q
denotes the flow amounts and
c
is constant weight.
The goal is to find a policy
\pi
for the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{{\pi}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}({\pi})\!=\![J_{\text{safety}}({\pi}),J_{\text{supply}}({\pi}),J_{\text{ecology}}({\pi}),J_{\text{efficiency}}({\pi})]^{\top}

s.t.

\displaystyle h_{\min,i}\leq h_{i}(t)\leq h_{\max,i},\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{V},t,

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[h_{i}(t)>h_{\text{flood},i}]\leq p_{\text{risk}},

subject to physical capacity constraints and probabilistic flood risk limits.

3.4.
Distributed Control Challenge

Each agent
i
operates under partial observability with local information:

(3)

\mathcal{O}_{i}(t)=\{\mathbf{s}_{i}(t),\{\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{j}(t-\tau_{j})\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}},\mathbf{w}_{\text{forecast},i}(t{:}t+H)\},

including delayed neighbor states and weather forecasts. The challenge is finding decentralized policies
\{\pi_{i}:\mathcal{O}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
that achieve global coordination under local constraints.

\mathbf{w}_{\text{forecast},i}(t{:}t+H)
provides weather forecasts for the next
H
hours.

4.1.
Murmuration-Inspired Coordination Layer

Drawing from starling murmuration, we adapt three coordination rules to reservoir management, where each rule addresses specific coordination challenges:

Adaptive Alignment Rule
encourages coordinated release strategies among neighboring reservoirs:

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{align},i}(t)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}w_{ij}(t)\cdot\|a_{i}-\bar{a}_{ij}\|^{2},

where
\mathcal{N}_{i}
is the set of neighboring reservoirs that can communicate with reservoir
i
,
a_{i}
is reservoir
i
’s current release action,
\bar{a}_{ij}
is the average release action between reservoirs
i
and
j
, and
w_{ij}(t)
are time-varying weights computed as:

w_{ij}(t)=\frac{\exp(-\beta_{d}d_{ij}-\beta_{e}\|\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{w}_{j}\|_{2})}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\exp(-\beta_{d}d_{ik}-\beta_{e}\|\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{w}_{k}\|_{2})},

Here
d_{ij}
is the geographic distance between reservoirs,
\mathbf{w}_{i}
represents local weather conditions at reservoir
i
, and
\beta_{d},\beta_{e}
are scaling parameters that control the influence of distance and weather similarity.
This rule mimics how starlings align their flight direction with nearby birds. In reservoir management, when California’s Central Valley faces drought, this rule ensures upstream reservoirs (like Shasta and Oroville) coordinate their releases to maintain consistent downstream supply rather than acting independently. The weights
w_{ij}(t)
prioritize coordination with geographically closer neighbors, so reservoirs in the same watershed coordinate more strongly than distant ones.

Strategic Separation Rule
maintains beneficial diversity to prevent system-wide failures:

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{sep},i}(t)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\phi_{\text{sep}}(\|a_{i}-a_{j}\|_{2};\tau_{i}(t),\sigma_{i}(t)),

where
\phi_{\text{sep}}(\cdot)
is a penalty function that decreases as the difference
\|a_{i}-a_{j}\|_{2}
increases (encouraging diversity),
\tau_{i}(t)
is an adaptive threshold, and
\sigma_{i}(t)
controls the penalty strength. The threshold adapts based on local uncertainty:

\tau_{i}(t)=\tau_{\text{base}}\cdot(1+\alpha_{\tau}\cdot\text{CV}[\{h_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}]),

where
\text{CV}[\cdot]
is the coefficient of variation of water levels
h_{j}
among neighbors, encouraging more diversity when local conditions are highly variable.
Like starlings avoiding collisions by maintaining distance, this rule prevents all reservoirs from making identical decisions. For example, during Texas winter storms, while some reservoirs focus on immediate flood control, others maintain emergency reserves for potential power grid failures. The parameter
\tau_{i}(t)
adapts based on local uncertainty—when water levels are highly variable, the system encourages more diverse strategies to avoid cascading failures.

Ecological Cohesion Rule
aligns local actions with system-wide ecological requirements:

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{coh},i}(t)=\lambda_{\text{eco},i}(t)\cdot\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}q_{\text{release},j}(t)-Q_{\text{eco},i}(t)\|^{2},

where
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is the set of reservoirs in the same ecological region as
i
,
q_{\text{release},j}(t)
is the actual release flow from reservoir
j
,
Q_{\text{eco},i}(t)
is the required ecological flow for region
i
, and
\lambda_{\text{eco},i}(t)
is a time-varying weight that increases during critical ecological periods (e.g., fish migration seasons).
Similar to how starlings maintain flock cohesion, this rule ensures collective reservoir behavior serves global objectives. During salmon migration in the Columbia River, upstream reservoirs coordinate to maintain specific downstream flow patterns despite local agricultural demands, balancing individual reservoir needs with ecosystem requirements.

4.2.
MARL with Coordination Integration

Each agent uses enhanced state representation combining multiple information sources:

\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\text{MARL}}(t)\!\!=\!\!\left[\begin{aligned} &\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\text{local}}(t),\ \text{GNN}_{\theta}(\{\mathbf{s}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{ij}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}),\\
&\text{LSTM}_{\phi}(\{\mathbf{s}_{i}(\tau)\}_{\tau=t-K}^{t}),\ \mathbf{w}_{\text{forecast},i}(t{:}t\!+\!H)\end{aligned}\right]^{\top}.

Each component serves a specific purpose:

1)
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\text{local}}(t)=[h_{i}(t),q_{\text{in},i}(t),q_{\text{out},i}(t)]
contains local reservoir state (water level, inflows, outflows).

2)
\text{GNN}_{\theta}(\{\mathbf{s}_{j},\mathbf{e}_{ij}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}})
processes neighbor information, where
\mathbf{s}_{j}
are neighbor states and
\mathbf{e}_{ij}
are edge features (e.g., channel capacity, flow delay between reservoirs
i
and
j
).

3)
\text{LSTM}_{\phi}(\{\mathbf{s}_{i}(\tau)\}_{\tau=t-K}^{t})
captures historical patterns over past
K
time steps to identify trends and seasonal patterns.

4)
\mathbf{w}_{\text{forecast},i}(t{:}t+H)
provides weather forecasts for the next
H
hours, enabling anticipatory decision-making.

This architecture mimics how starlings process local conditions, observe nearby birds, and anticipate environmental changes. For reservoirs, this means combining local water levels, neighbor reservoir states through GNNs, and weather forecasts to make informed decisions.
The policy network integrates murmuration coordination signals:

\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(2)}\!=\!\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(1)}\!+\!\alpha_{\text{mur}}\cdot\text{MLP}([\nabla_{a_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}},\nabla_{a_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{sep}},\nabla_{a_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{coh}}]^{\top}),

where
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(1)}
is the standard policy network hidden state,
\nabla_{a_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
are gradients of coordination losses with respect to action
a_{i}
, and
\alpha_{\text{mur}}
controls the influence of bio-inspired coordination. These gradients provide directional guidance: alignment gradients push toward neighbor actions, separation gradients push away from identical decisions, and cohesion gradients pull toward global objectives.
Such gradients modify the policy’s hidden representation, enabling bio-inspired coordination to emerge naturally during learning without requiring explicit coordination protocols.

We employ modified PPO that balances individual performance with coordination:

\displaystyle J_{i}^{\text{PPO}}=\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\Big[\min\big(r_{t}(\theta_{i})A_{t},\ \text{clip}(r_{t}(\theta_{i}),1{-}\epsilon,\ 1{+}\epsilon)A_{t}\big)\Big]

(7)

\displaystyle-\beta_{\text{mur}}\sum_{\ell\in\{\text{align},\text{sep},\text{coh}\}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell,i},

where
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is the experience batch for agent
i
,
r_{t}(\theta_{i})=\frac{\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{t}|\mathbf{s}_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{i}^{\text{old}}}(a_{t}|\mathbf{s}_{t})}
is the probability ratio,
A_{t}
is the advantage function, and
\beta_{\text{mur}}
dynamically weights coordination importance.
This formulation balances standard PPO performance optimization with murmuration coordination penalties.
\beta_{\text{mur}}
controls how strongly the bio-inspired rules influence learning. During emergencies,
\beta_{\text{mur}}
increases to prioritize coordination over individual performance, while in stable periods, it decreases to allow more individual optimization.

4.3.
LLM-Guided Adaptive Reward Shaping

To address Level 2 environmental uncertainties involving complex contextual information (weather patterns, regulations, stakeholder preferences), we integrate LLM that process diverse textual information sources like weather reports, regulatory documents, stakeholder communications, and translate this heterogeneous knowledge into appropriate parameter adjustments.
We decompose rewards as:

R_{i}=R_{\text{base},i}+R_{\text{shaped},i}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)),

where
R_{\text{base},i}
captures standard objectives (safety, supply, efficiency) and
R_{\text{shaped},i}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(t))
provides context-aware adjustments based on LLM-processed parameters
\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)
.

The system operates three common coordination modes by adjusting the relative weights of alignment (
\alpha
), separation (
\beta
), and cohesion (
\gamma
) rules. The final coordination signal is computed as:

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{total},i}(t)=\alpha\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{align},i}(t)\!+\!\beta\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{sep},i}(t)\!+\!\gamma\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{coh},i}(t),

where
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1
and the weights are dynamically adjusted based on context:

SAC-Alignment (Coordination-First)

\alpha=0.6,\beta=0.1,\gamma=0.3
: Prioritizes coordination for stable periods. During California’s predictable dry season, all Central Valley reservoirs synchronize releases to maintain agricultural water supply while preventing individual depletion.

SAC-Separation (Diversity-First)

\alpha=0.2,\beta=0.6,\gamma=0.2
: Emphasizes diverse strategies for emergency response. During unpredictable Texas winter storms, some reservoirs prioritize immediate flood control while others maintain emergency reserves for potential infrastructure failures.

SAC-Cohesion (Goal-Oriented)
(
\alpha=0.3,\beta=0.2,\gamma=0.5
: Focuses on global optimization for ecological protection. During Pacific Northwest salmon migration, upstream reservoirs coordinate to maintain specific flow patterns despite varying local demands.

The LLM processes context at multiple temporal scales:

\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)\!=\!\begin{cases}\text{LLM}(\mathcal{C}_{\text{daily}},\mathcal{P}_{\text{strategic}})&\text{Strategic (24h)}\\
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\text{strategic}}\!\!+\!\!\Delta\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\text{LLM}}(\mathcal{C}_{\text{hourly}})&\text{Tactical (4h)}\\
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\text{emergency}}&\text{Operational (10min)}\end{cases}

Each temporal scale serves distinct functions:

1)
Strategic (24h)
: Processes daily weather reports, seasonal forecasts, and long-term regulatory updates (
\mathcal{C}_{\text{daily}}
) along with strategic policies (
\mathcal{P}_{\text{strategic}}
) to set baseline coordination weights for planned operations.

2)
Tactical (4h)
: Adds hourly updates (
\mathcal{C}_{\text{hourly}}
) such as refined weather forecasts and stakeholder communications to adjust coordination strategy as conditions evolve.

3)
Operational (10min)
: Switches to pre-computed emergency parameters (
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\text{emergency}}
) during critical events when rapid response is essential and there is no time for LLM.

This hierarchical approach enables adaptation from long-term strategic planning (seasonal water allocation) to rapid emergency response (flash flood), matching adaptive coordination strategy to the temporal urgency of the situation.

5.
Evaluation

We conduct two comprehensive experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of MARLIN: (1) assessing the emergence of coordinated behaviors driven by murmuration-inspired mechanisms under uncertainty, and (2) demonstrating the adaptive advantages of LLM-guided reward shaping in dynamic, real-world scenarios.

5.1.
Experiment 1: Validation of Murmuration-

Based Coordination

Setup:
We assess the emergence of coordinated behaviors on both the California Central Valley watershed (comprising 25 major reservoirs managing 6.2 million acres of agricultural land) and synthetic networks ranging from
20\times 20
to
100\times 100
nodes, under dual-layer uncertainty (
\sigma_{\text{base}}=0.05
).

Evaluation Metrics:
We assess performance across six dimensions:
Coordination Quality
: Measured as the normalized mutual information between agent actions and optimal collective behavior, computed as
Q_{c}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{I(a_{i};\mathbf{a}_{-i})}{\log n}
, where
I(\cdot;\cdot)
is mutual information and
\mathbf{a}_{-i}
represents actions of other agents.

Adaptation Speed
: Time to reach 95% of steady-state performance after environmental changes.

Uncertainty Resilience
: Performance retention under varying noise levels (5-15%).

Scalability
: Computational complexity scaling with network size.

Safety
: Percentage of time steps maintaining water levels within safe bounds.

Interpretability
: Rated by practitioners on decision clarity and reasoning transparency.

Models Compared:

6.
Discussion and Future Work

Pathways to Real-World Implementation and Societal Adoption.

The pathway to
MARLIN
’s real-world implementation begins with its proven technical feasibility, as the system exhibits linear complexity and can manage a 10,000-node network with a decision time of just 1.85 seconds. This efficiency, combined with memory usage low enough for deployment on standard infrastructure, paves the way for adoption. Successful integration into existing workflows hinges on the LLM’s ability to process real-world regulations and stakeholder preferences.
To further build operator trust, visualization tools for the system’s emergent coordination patterns are proposed. Overcoming key deployment barriers involves specific, targeted solutions: addressing potential LLM latency with low-latency models or hybrid edge-cloud deployments and tackling data scarcity in new regions by using transfer learning to adapt existing policies. Beyond water management, these principles establish a foundation for broader applications in domains such as power grid regulation, transportation, and supply chain coordination.

Conclusion.

MARLIN
tackles uncertainty problems in reservoir management by combining murmuration, MARL, and LLM-guided reward shaping. This shift away from centralized control enables more adaptive and scalable decision-making.
Beyond water systems,
MARLIN
offers a general foundation for resilient infrastructure control under uncertainty. Its ability to generate emergent coordination and adapt to environmental change holds promise for broader applications in disaster mitigation and response, offering a new path toward safer, smarter, and more autonomous infrastructure in the face of global uncertainty.

h_{\min,i}\leq h_{i}(t)\leq h_{\max,i}
for all reservoirs simultaneously.

Scalability Analysis:
Decision time and memory usage measured across network sizes from 100 to 10,000 nodes. Linear complexity confirmed through least-squares regression on log-log plots with

.

Statistical Analysis

All results are reported with 95% confidence intervals computed using bootstrap sampling with 1000 replicates. Statistical significance is assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes are reported using Cohen’s d for continuous variables and Cramér’s V for categorical outcomes.

Theoretical Analysis of Murmuration Coordination

Problem Formulation and Assumptions

Consider a network of
n
reservoirs represented by graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
. Each reservoir
i\in\mathcal{V}
selects action
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}
(water release rate) to minimize a local cost function while coordinating with neighbors.

Assumption 1 (Local Convexity):
Each agent’s local cost function
c_{i}(a_{i},\mathbf{a}_{-i})
is convex in
a_{i}
and has Lipschitz continuous gradients with constant
L_{i}
.

Assumption 2 (Network Connectivity):
The communication graph
\mathcal{G}
is connected and undirected, ensuring information can propagate across the network.

Assumption 3 (Bounded Actions):
The action space
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is compact and convex for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
.

Convergence Analysis of Alignment Rule

The alignment rule encourages agents to coordinate their actions with neighbors:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{align},i}(t)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}w_{ij}(t)\cdot\|a_{i}-\bar{a}_{ij}\|^{2}

Theorem 1 (Alignment Convergence):

Under Assumptions 1-3, if the weight matrix
\mathbf{W}(t)=[w_{ij}(t)]
is doubly stochastic and the step size satisfies
\alpha_{t}=O(1/t)
, then the alignment dynamics converge to the average consensus:

\lim_{t\to\infty}a_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(0),\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{V}

Proof Sketch:
Consider the Lyapunov function
V(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}(t)-\bar{a}\|^{2}
, where
\bar{a}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(0)
. The alignment update can be written as:

a_{i}(t+1)=a_{i}(t)-\alpha_{t}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}w_{ij}(t)(a_{i}(t)-a_{j}(t))

Taking the time derivative of
V(t)
:

\frac{dV}{dt}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}(t)-\bar{a})^{\intercal}\dot{a}_{i}(t)=

-\alpha_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}w_{ij}(t)(a_{i}(t)-a_{j}(t))^{2}\leq 0

Since
V(t)
is non-increasing and bounded below, convergence follows from the doubly stochastic property of
\mathbf{W}(t)
and connectivity of
\mathcal{G}
.
\square

Optimality Analysis of Separation Rule

The separation rule maintains beneficial diversity to prevent local minima:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{sep},i}(t)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\phi_{\text{sep}}(\|a_{i}-a_{j}\|_{2};\tau_{i}(t),\sigma_{i}(t))

Theorem 2 (Separation Optimality):

Let
\phi_{\text{sep}}(d;\tau,\sigma)=\sigma\max(0,\tau-d)^{2}
be the separation penalty function. Under high environmental uncertainty (when
\text{CV}[\{h_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}]>\delta
for threshold
\delta>0
), the separation rule prevents convergence to suboptimal uniform strategies.

Proof Sketch:
Consider the case where all agents adopt identical actions
a_{i}=a^{*}
for all
i
. The separation penalty becomes zero, but the system is vulnerable to correlated failures.

Define the robustness measure
R(\mathbf{a})=\mathbb{E}[\min_{i}U_{i}(\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\xi})]
where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
represents environmental uncertainty. When uncertainty is high (
\text{Var}[\boldsymbol{\xi}]>\sigma_{\text{thresh}}^{2}
), we can show:

R(\mathbf{a}_{\text{diverse}})>R(\mathbf{a}_{\text{uniform}})+\epsilon

for some
\epsilon>0
, where
\mathbf{a}_{\text{diverse}}
satisfies
\min_{i,j}\|a_{i}-a_{j}\|\geq\tau_{\min}
.

The adaptive threshold
\tau_{i}(t)=\tau_{\text{base}}\cdot(1+\alpha_{\tau}\cdot\text{CV}[\{h_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}])
ensures that when local uncertainty increases, the system automatically encourages more diversity, preventing convergence to brittle uniform solutions.
\square

Global Optimality of Cohesion Rule

The cohesion rule aligns local actions with global objectives:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{coh},i}(t)=\lambda_{\text{eco},i}(t)\cdot\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}q_{\text{release},j}(t)-Q_{\text{eco},i}(t)\|^{2}

Theorem 3 (Cohesion Optimality):

Consider the global optimization problem:

\min_{\mathbf{a}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(a_{i})+\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{r}}a_{j}-Q_{r}^{*}\|^{2}

where
\mathcal{P}_{r}
are regional partitions and
Q_{r}^{*}
are regional targets. The cohesion rule with appropriate weights
\lambda_{\text{eco},i}(t)
converges to the global optimum.

Proof:
The Lagrangian of the global problem is:

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\mu})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(a_{i})+\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{r}}a_{j}-Q_{r}^{*}\|^{2}

The KKT conditions give:

\nabla_{a_{i}}c_{i}(a_{i}^{*})+\sum_{r:i\in\mathcal{P}_{r}}2\lambda_{r}(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{r}}a_{j}^{*}-Q_{r}^{*})=0

This exactly matches the first-order conditions of the distributed cohesion rule when
\lambda_{\text{eco},i}(t)=\lambda_{r}
for
i\in\mathcal{P}_{r}
. Under convexity (Assumption 1), these conditions are sufficient for global optimality.
\square

Combined System Analysis

Theorem 4 (Multi-Objective Convergence):

Consider the combined murmuration system:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total},i}(t)=\alpha\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{align},i}(t)+\beta\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{sep},i}(t)+\gamma\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{coh},i}(t)

with
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1
. Under appropriate weight scheduling
\{\alpha(t),\beta(t),\gamma(t)\}
, the system converges to a
(1-\epsilon)
-optimal solution of the global multi-objective problem, where
\epsilon
depends on the uncertainty level and network topology.

Proof Sketch:
The combined system can be analyzed using multi-objective optimization theory. Define the Pareto-optimal set
\mathcal{P}^{*}
for the multi-objective problem.

The convergence analysis proceeds in three phases:

Phase 1 (Exploration):
High
\beta
encourages diversity, ensuring the system explores different regions of the action space, preventing premature convergence to local minima.

Phase 2 (Coordination):
Increasing
\alpha
promotes consensus among agents, reducing the solution space to a consensus manifold.

Phase 3 (Optimization):
High
\gamma
drives the system toward global objectives, ensuring convergence to the Pareto frontier.

The key insight is that the three rules operate on different timescales and serve complementary purposes:
- Alignment provides **convergence** (reduces variance)
- Separation provides **robustness** (prevents local minima)
- Cohesion provides **optimality** (achieves global objectives)

The approximation error
\epsilon
can be bounded as:

\epsilon\leq C_{1}\cdot\sigma_{\text{uncertainty}}+C_{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{L})}+C_{3}\cdot\frac{\beta_{\text{final}}}{\gamma_{\text{final}}}

where
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
are problem-dependent constants,
\sigma_{\text{uncertainty}}
is the system uncertainty level, and
\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{L})
is the smallest eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian.
\square

Robustness Analysis

Theorem 5 (Uncertainty Resilience):

Under dual-layer uncertainty with bounded noise
\|\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\|\leq\sigma_{\max}
, the murmuration system maintains performance within
\delta
of the noise-free optimum with probability at least
1-\epsilon
, where:

\delta\leq\frac{L\cdot\sigma_{\max}}{\mu}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\gamma}\right)

and
L,\mu
are the Lipschitz and strong convexity constants, respectively.

This result shows that the separation parameter
\beta
provides a knob to trade off between coordination efficiency and robustness to uncertainty.

Computational Complexity

Theorem 6 (Convergence Rate):

The murmuration algorithm achieves
\epsilon
-optimality in
O(\frac{1}{\epsilon})
iterations for strongly convex problems, matching the optimal rate for distributed optimization. The per-iteration complexity is
O(|\mathcal{E}|)
, linear in the number of network edges.

Practical Implications

The theoretical analysis provides several key insights for practical deployment:

(1)

Weight Selection:
The weights
\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}
should be adapted based on environmental conditions:
- High uncertainty
\Rightarrow
increase
\beta
(separation)
- Emergency situations
\Rightarrow
increase
\alpha
(alignment)
- Normal operations
\Rightarrow
increase
\gamma
(cohesion)

(2)

Network Design:
Better connected networks (higher
\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{L})
) achieve faster convergence and better performance.

(3)

Robustness-Performance Trade-off:
The separation parameter
\beta
provides a principled way to balance coordination efficiency with robustness to failures.

Corollary 1:

For practical water reservoir networks with typical connectivity patterns (
|\mathcal{E}|=O(n)
) and moderate uncertainty levels (
\sigma_{\max}=O(\sqrt{n})
), the murmuration algorithm achieves near-optimal performance (
\epsilon<0.05
) within
O(n\log n)
iterations.

This theoretical foundation demonstrates that the bio-inspired murmuration rules are not merely heuristic, but provide mathematically principled coordination with provable convergence and optimality guarantees under realistic conditions.
