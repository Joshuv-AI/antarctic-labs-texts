Overview of Patient-Reported Outcomes in Haemophilia Gene Therapy
Authors: Wolfgang Miesbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThree adeno-associated virus-based gene therapies for haemophilia have received regulatory approval in Western countries since 2022, yet commercial uptake has been slow. Traditional clinical endpoints – annualised bleeding rate and factor activity levels – document haemostatic efficacy, but fail to capture what matters most to patients. Both the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) increasingly recognise patient-reported outcomes (PROs) as meaningful endpoints for regulatory and health technology assessment decisions.SummaryNarrative review of peer-reviewed literature in PubMed and Web of Science (2017–2026) covering PRO instruments, clinical trial evidence, cost-effectiveness analyses, and qualitative patient data in haemophilia gene therapy. Six trials have reported PRO data using a total of 12 different instruments, of which five principal measures of which five measures are central to the quality-of-life evidence: the disease-specific Haem-A-QoL and Haemo-QoL-A, the generic EQ-5D-5L, and the gene therapy-specific coreHEM Mental Health Outlook (coreHEM-MHO). Across all studies, gene therapy produced clinically meaningful improvements in quality of life. Total disease-specific quality-of-life scores improved by 6–15 points across trials – exceeding established clinically important difference thresholds – with the largest gains in the Treatment and Dealing with Haemophilia domains. EQ-5D-5L utility indices increased significantly in multiple trials. In the SPK-9001 long-term follow-up, durable Haem-A-QoL gains were confirmed through 156 weeks. The coreHEM-MHO, developed to capture psychological dimensions unique to gene therapy such as durability anxiety and disease identity adjustment, has been piloted in BENEGENE-2. Qualitative interviews documented profound reductions in treatment burden and improved daily functioning.Key MessagesPRO evidence consistently demonstrates that haemophilia gene therapy delivers quality-of-life benefits that extend well beyond what traditional clinical endpoints capture. These PRO-derived improvements are also the principal driver of gene therapy’s economic value since cost-effectiveness models show substantial gains in health-related quality of life but negligible gains in life expectancy. Priorities include standardised coreHEM measurement, long-term registries, and ensuring certified haemophilia centres are equipped for systematic PRO follow-up.

Introduction

Haemophilia A and B are X-linked bleeding disorders caused by deficiencies in coagulation factors VIII (FVIII) and IX (FIX), affecting approximately 1 in 5,000 and 1 in 30,000 male births, respectively [1, 2]. Treatment has evolved from episodic factor replacement through prophylactic regimens with standard and extended half-life concentrates to non-factor therapies such as emicizumab, substantially reducing bleeding frequency and improving joint health [3]. Despite these advances, residual bleeding risk, progressive arthropathy in 25–30% of patients on prophylaxis, and restrictions on education, employment, and physical activity remain important concerns [3].

Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene therapy has changed the treatment landscape for haemophilia [4]. By delivering functional FVIII or FIX transgenes to hepatocytes, a single intravenous infusion can enable endogenous factor production sufficient to convert severe disease to a mild phenotype. Proof-of-concept for haemophilia B was established by Nathwani and colleagues [5], with Reiss, Davidoff, Nathwani, and co-workers subsequently confirming durable clinical benefit over more than a decade of follow-up [6]. The AMT-060 phase I/II trial demonstrated for a period of 9 years stable FIX expression with durable reductions in bleeding and factor consumption [7, 8], and the SPK-9001 phase I/IIa study reported sustained FIX activity in the mild to normal range over 6 years [9].

An important step in vector design has been the adoption of the FIX-Padua, a naturally occurring variant of FIX that exhibits approximately 5–8-fold higher specific activity than wild-type FIX, making it the preferred transgene in current AAV-mediated gene therapy vectors for hemophilia B [10]. It allows therapeutic factor levels to be achieved at lower vector doses, thereby reducing immunogenicity and hepatotoxicity [11].

These advances led to two pivotal phase III trials in haemophilia B: HOPE-B (n = 54), which showed a 64% reduction in annualised bleeding rate (ABR) with 94% of patients discontinuing prophylaxis [12], and BENEGENE-2 (n = 45), which demonstrated a 71% ABR reduction with 64.4% of participants achieving bleed-free status [13]. For haemophilia A, the phase III trial GENEr8-1 (n = 134) showed that valoctocogene roxaparvovec reduced treated bleeds by 82.9% and FVIII usage by 96.8% [14].

Three products have since received regulatory approval in Western countries: etranacogene dezaparvovec (Hemgenix®, FDA November 2022), valoctocogene roxaparvovec (Roctavian®, EMA conditional approval August 2022; FDA June 2023), and fidanacogene elaparvovec (Beqvez®, FDA April 2024). In China, BBM-H901 (dalnacogene ponparvovec), employing the same FIX-Padua transgene in a novel AAV843 capsid, was approved in April 2025 after achieving a mean FIX activity of 55 IU/dL at 52 weeks in its phase 3 trial [15].

However, real-world uptake in Western markets has remained limited. Pfizer discontinued Beqvez in February 2025 without a single commercial patient dosed, and BioMarin announced the voluntary withdrawal of Roctavian in February 2026, with availability ceasing at the end of May 2026.

These setbacks highlight a disconnect between trial efficacy and clinical adoption – one that patient-reported outcome (PRO) evidence could help address by showing patients and physicians what gene therapy means for everyday life. Effective communication about gene therapy – including honest discussion of evidence gaps, eligibility, and realistic expectations – is a prerequisite for informed decision-making [16]. This review synthesises PRO evidence from gene therapy trials, cost-effectiveness findings, and qualitative patient data, with the aim of identifying priorities for future research and implementation.

Methods

PubMed and Web of Science were searched for English-language, peer-reviewed articles published between 2017 and February 2026 using the terms “patient-reported outcomes,” “quality of life,” “haemophilia gene therapy,” “PRO instruments,” and “core outcome set for gene therapy in haemophilia” (coreHEM). Data extraction covered four domains: (i) PRO instrument validation and clinically important difference (CID) thresholds; (ii) PRO outcomes from clinical trials and long-term follow-up studies; (iii) cost-effectiveness analyses incorporating PRO-derived utility data; and (iv) qualitative patient perspectives. Evidence was synthesised narratively.

Results

The results are organised into two sections: validated PRO instruments and the coreHEM initiative, followed by clinical trial evidence on quality-of-life outcomes.

Validated PRO Instruments

Across six trials, a total of 12 different PRO instruments were deployed, of which five principal measures of which five measures are central to the quality-of-life evidence (Table 1). Two distinct disease-specific measures must be distinguished [17], the Haemophilia Quality of Life Questionnaire for Adults (Haem-A-QoL, 46 items, 10 domains) and the Haemophilia-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire for Adults (Haemo-QoL-A, 41 items, 7 domains), which differ in structure und psychometric properties.

For the Haem-A-QoL, CID thresholds of ≥10 points for the Physical Health and Sports and Leisure domains and ≥7 points for the total score have been established [18, 19]. For the Haemo-QoL-A, CID thresholds of ≥6 points for domain scores and ≥5.5 points for the total score have been reported [21], and psychometric validation in gene therapy-treated populations has confirmed that instrument’s fitness for purpose. The Haem-A-QoL was used in HOPE-B, BENEGENE-2, and SPK-9001, while the Haemo-QoL-A was used in GENEr8-1 and BMN 270.

The EQ-5D-5L provides generic health utility values (0 = dead, 1 = full health) required for cost-effectiveness modelling and health technology assessment (HTA) decisions [23]. Its visual analogue scale (VAS) component has shown convergent validity with disease-specific measures.

To address heterogeneous outcome selection across trials, the coreHEM initiative used a modified Delphi process to achieve consensus on six mandatory outcomes beyond adverse events, including mental health [25]. This prompted the development of the coreHEM Mental Health Outlook (coreHEM-MHO), designed to capture durability anxiety, identity adjustment, and treatment expectations – dimensions that neither Haem-A-QoL, the Haemo-QoL-A, nor EQ-5D-5L addresses [24]. The coreHEM-MHO has been piloted in BENEGENE-2 and is currently undergoing further validation.

It should be noted that formal psychometric validation in gene therapy-treated populations has been established only for the Haemo-QoL-A [22]. The Haem-A-QoL has well-documented measurement properties and CID thresholds derived from haemophilia populations but has not been formally revalidated in gene therapy cohorts specifically. The EQ-5D-5L and WPAI-GH are applied on the basis of their general psychometric track record across many disease areas, and their CID thresholds (EQ-5D-5L: +0.04 index, +3 points VAS) were derived in general populations rather than haemophilia-specific samples. Developing haemophilia-specific CID values for the EQ-5D-5L and other instruments – alongside those already proposed for the coreHEM-MHO – would substantially strengthen the interpretability of PRO improvements in future gene therapy studies.

Clinical Trial Evidence

Six trials have published quality-of-life data following haemophilia gene therapy (Table 2): four in haemophilia B (HOPE-B, BENEGENE-2, SPK-9001 phase I/IIa, AMT-060) and two in haemophilia A (GENEr8-1, BMN 270 phase I/II).

Haemophilia B

In HOPE-B (n = 54), etranacogene dezaparvovec improved the Haem-A-QoL total score by −6.0 points at 24 months (p < 0.0001), exceeding the CID, with the treatment domain showing the largest gain (−13.9 points). The EQ-5D-5L utility index rose by 0.04 (p = 0.013) [12, 13, 26].

BENEGENE-2 (n = 45), investigating fidanacogene elaparvovec, provided the broadest PRO assessment to date, deploying five instruments including the novel Patient Global Impression of Change – Haemophilia (PGIC) and the Hemophilia Life Impacts Questionnaire (HeLIQ) [13, 20]. The Haem-A-QoL total score improved by −11.2 points (p < 0.001), nearly double the CID, and 56.1% of participants reported that life with haemophilia had “greatly improved” on the PGIC [20].

In the earlier phase I/II study, the SPK-9001 phase I/IIa long-term follow-up (n = 15) provides the longest published PRO data in haemophilia gene therapy. At week 156, the Haem-A-QoL total score had improved by −15.2 points, with 67% of evaluable participants sustaining improvements of ≥7 points throughout the observation period [27, 28].

The AMT-060 phase I/II trial (n = 10) contributed qualitative rather than quantitative PRO data. All three German participants who were interviewed described a profound reduction in treatment burden, freedom from regular injections, improved participation in daily activities, and psychological relief from no longer planning life around infusion schedules [7, 29].

Haemophilia A

In GENEr8-1 (n = 134), four Haem-Qol-A domains (consequences of bleeding, treatment concern, worry, role functioning) exceeded the 6-point CID at week 104 [30, 31]. EQ-5D-5L utility improvements surpassed the CID at week 52 but attenuated by week 104, consistent with response shift theory [30]. The companion BMN 270 phase I/II study provided the basis for psychometric validation of the Haemo-QoL-A, confirming the instrument’s fitness for purpose in gene therapy populations [22].

The multiyear follow-up of the SPK-8011 phase I/II trial [32] demonstrated sustained factor VIII expression over several years following AAV gene transfer in patients with haemophilia A, with maintained bleeding protection and stable transduction. However, patient-reported quality-of-life outcomes from this cohort have not been published to date.

Discussion

Traditional clinical endpoints – ABR, factor activity levels, joint health scores – demonstrate the impact of gene therapy on bleeding. However, PROs add a critical dimension by capturing what matters to patients beyond haemostatic control: treatment burden, psychological well-being, functional participation, and overall quality of life [17, 33]. It is precisely these dimensions that determine whether patients perceive gene therapy as transformative, and they often fail to be differentiated by traditional endpoints alone when therapies achieve comparable haemostatic efficacy. Both the FDA and EMA increasingly recognise PROs as meaningful endpoints for regulatory decision-making, with the FDA providing formal guidance on their use to support labelling claims [34] and the EMA incorporating health-related quality-of-life data into European Public Assessment Reports for gene therapy products [33].

Across six trials, PRO data consistently demonstrate clinically meaningful benefit. The disease-specific QoL instruments have proved sensitive to the aspects of gene therapy that matter most to patients – freedom from prophylactic schedules, reduced bleeding anxiety, broader participation in work and physical activity – and the psychometric validity of the Haemo-Qol-A in gene therapy populations is established [22], while the Haem-A-QoL has been used extensively in haemophilia B trials (HOPE-B, BENEGENE-2, SPK-9001). The SPK-9001 long-term follow-up offers the first evidence that these gains persist beyond 3 years [28], and the qualitative interviews from AMT-060 document how profoundly the removal of treatment burden reshapes daily life [29]. The coreHEM-MHO instrument merits particular attention for future research [24, 25]. Designed specifically to capture psychological dimensions unique to the gene therapy experience – including durability anxiety, disease identity adjustment, and treatment expectations – the coreHEM-MHO addresses a measurement gap that neither the Haem-A-QoL, the Haemo-QoL-A, nor the EQ-5D-5L can fill. As gene therapy follow-up extends and patients confront uncertainty about factor level durability, these psychological dimensions are likely to become increasingly important determinants of perceived treatment value. Standardised incorporation of the coreHEM-MHO into future trials and registries, as recommended by the coreHEM initiative [24], will be essential for capturing the full spectrum of patient-perceived benefit and burden. Establishing CID thresholds for the coreHEM-MHO should be a priority for future psychometric work.

Economic Implications

The economic case for gene therapy rests almost entirely on PRO-derived utility: cost-effectiveness analyses show modelled quality-adjusted life-year gains of 0.13–1.71 for haemophilia A and 1.47–11.19 for haemophilia B [35, 36], with negligible life-year gains, and these estimates are highly sensitive to durability assumptions. Outcomes-based reimbursement frameworks adopted in several countries ultimately depend on reliable, validated PRO data to define treatment success [37].

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations deserve attention. Durability remains the most consequential unknown: clinical follow-up extends 5–13 years, but cost-effectiveness models assume decades of factor expression [38]. If factor levels decline substantially, PRO gains may follow. Importantly, CID thresholds for the EQ-5D-5L [25] and other instruments were derived from general population and may not fully capture meaningful change in persons with haemophilia. In contrast, the coreHEM-MHO was specifically developed and validated for this patient population, offering greater disease-specific sensitivity [24].

Registries such as the WFH Gene Therapy Registry (WFH GTR) [39, 40] and the European Association for Haemophilia and Allied Disorders haemophilia gene therapy clinical outcome database (EAHAD-GTD) [41] will be essential for resolving this question. Broader evidence gaps, including re-dosing limitations and immunological responses to AAV, have also been highlighted [42].

The open-label, single-arm designs typical of gene therapy trials raise concerns about expectation bias in self-reported assessments. It has been shown in other indications that delayed-start designs, ecological momentary assessment all merit exploration [34].

However, the strength of PRO conclusions is also constrained by instrument limitations. The Brief Pain Inventory and Haemophilia Activities List were added to the SPK-9001 protocol after most participants had been dosed, precluding pre-gene therapy comparisons [28]. More fundamentally, both the Haem-A-QoL and the Haemo-Qol-A were developed 2 decades ago, when effective gene therapy was not envisioned [19, 21]. For example, the Haem-A-Qol dealing with haemophilia domain – which asks participants to rate their ability to recognise and control bleeds – paradoxically penalises patients who no longer bleed because a “not applicable” response option does not exist. This ceiling effect likely underestimates improvements and highlights the need for gene therapy-appropriate quality-of-life instruments. Future trials, including ongoing long-term extensions, should incorporate validated tools that capture the psychosocial dimensions of a life freed from prophylactic infusions, including relief from treatment burden, anxiety about potential factor decline, and the complex identity shifts that accompany a transition away from chronic disease management.

Finally, delivering gene therapy and conducting long-term PRO follow-up imposes considerable demands on centre infrastructure. Treatment should be restricted to certified haemophilia centres with multidisciplinary expertise [43, 44] and hepatology support [45], and these centres must also be equipped to collect, analyse, and report outcome data as part of outcomes-based reimbursement agreements.

Conclusion

PROs are central to the case for haemophilia gene therapy. Across six trials, 12 different PRO instruments were deployed, of which five principal measures – the Haem-A-QoL, the Haemo-QoL-A, the EQ-5D-5L, and the emerging coreHEM-MHO – capture treatment effects that traditional clinical endpoints cannot. Evidence from six trials confirms clinically meaningful improvements in quality of life, and where longer term data are available, these gains appear durable. Cost-effectiveness analyses make clear that gene therapy’s economic value rests on these PRO-derived utility gains. Key priorities going forward include standardised measurement through the coreHEM framework, long-term registries such as the WFH GTR and the EAHAD-GTD, trial designs that strengthen PRO evidence, electronic diaries for routine monitoring, and ensuring that certified haemophilia centres are equipped for comprehensive follow-up.
