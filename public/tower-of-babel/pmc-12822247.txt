Discovery of globally rare CYP51 mutations associated with azole resistance in Iranian Zymoseptoria tritici isolates
Authors: Azar Esfehani, Debabrata Dutta, Guido Puccetti, Pablo Antonio Chong Aguirre, Ehssan Torabi, Naser Mohammadi, Daniel Croll, Angela Feechan, Amir Mirzadi Gohari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeptoria tritici blotch, caused by Zymoseptoria tritici, is a major wheat disease worldwide. Demethylation inhibitor (DMI) fungicides, which target the sterol 14α-demethylase enzyme encoded by the CYP51 gene, remain central to disease control. In Iran, propiconazole is extensively applied, raising concerns about the evolution of resistance. This study investigated potential CYP51-mediated resistance mechanisms in Iranian Z. tritici populations.ResultsTwenty-eight isolates collected from six major wheat-producing provinces were assessed for propiconazole sensitivity using a microdilution assay and were classified as sensitive, tolerant, or resistant based on IC₅₀ values. Sequencing of the CYP51 coding region in representative isolates revealed several amino acid substitutions. Two novel mutations (G450R and G516D) were identified, together with rare variants previously reported at low global frequencies (E454K and L4V) and well-established resistance-associated changes such as Y461S and ΔY459/G460. These mutations defined seven haplotypes with variable resistance phenotypes. To validate these findings, we also performed whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on representative Iranian isolates. The WGS results were fully consistent with targeted sequencing, confirming the robustness of CYP51 mutation detection. Gene expression analysis showed inducible CYP51 upregulation in the most resistant isolate. Structural modelling using both homology-based and AlphaFold2 predictions indicated that the novel substitutions may alter surface electrostatics or cavity properties of the enzyme, potentially affecting fungicide binding.ConclusionsThis study documents the emergence of novel resistance-associated mutations in Iranian Z. tritici populations, expanding the spectrum of known CYP51 variants. The findings highlight the interplay between coding mutations, regulatory changes, and structural flexibility in shaping fungicide resistance. An integrated approach combining phenotypic assays, sequencing, expression analysis, and structural modelling provides a robust framework for monitoring resistance and informing sustainable fungicide use in wheat disease management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07936-2.Keywords: Zymoseptoria tritici, Azole resistance, CYP51 mutations, Fungicide resistance, Propiconazole, Haplotypes, Structural modelling

Introduction

Zymoseptoria tritici, the causal agent of Septoria tritici blotch (STB), is among the most economically destructive wheat diseases globally [1, 2]. The Middle East, particularly Iran, is recognised as major centre of its genetic diversity [3]. STB management relies on resistant cultivars and fungicides applications [4], but the pathogen’s frequent sexual recombination rapidly undermines both strategies [5]. Although gene-for-gene interactions such as Stb6–AvrStb6 contribute to resistance [6, 7], resistance breakdown highlights continued dependence on fungicides. This ongoing evolution stresses the need for effective chemical strategies. Azoles, a class of Demethylation Inhibitor (DMI) fungicides [8] introduced in the late 1970 s, have historically provided effective STB control [9]. These fungicides inhibit cytochrome P450 sterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51), disrupting ergosterol biosynthesis Copy number [10–12]. However, since the early 2000 s, resistance to DMIs has emerged in field populations of Z. tritici, reducing their long-term efficacy [13, 14]. Several studies report reduced sensitivity to DMIs [15–20], particularly older azoles such as cyproconazole, propiconazole, and tebuconazole [9]. Klink et al. (2021) reported sustained laboratory sensitivity to mefentrifluconazole from 1999–2020; however, as the compound was not commercially available during most of this period, these results reflect baseline sensitivity prior to field use. Recent work by Kildea et al. (2024) indicates that reduced sensitivity has now emerged across European populations, affecting both older and newer azoles [21, 22]. Azole sensitivity decline in Z. tritici is primarily linked to CYP51 amino acid substitutions, often generating diverse haplotypes [16]. Reduced sensitivity is not limited to DMIs: western European populations also show resistance to SDHIs [23] and strobilurins, with the G143A mutation specifically linked to QoI (strobilurin) resistance [24]. Glaab et al. (2024) identified widespread variation in CYP51 genotypes and expression, with more complex haplotypes, often containing multiple amino acid substitutions such as S524T, being prevalent in the western Europe, while simpler haplotypes with fewer mutations dominated in eastern and southern regions [25]. Recent sequencing advances have expanded insights into resistance evolution. Oreiro et al. (2025) showed that fungicide selection pressure affects both resistance evolution and population structure [26]. Additionally, copy number variation and heteroallelism in CYP51, which influence gene expression, have also been linked to resistance [27]. Similar patterns of reduced sensitivity to fungicides have been observed in other fungal genera, including Fusarium, Aspergillus, Rhynchosporium, and Blumeria [28–30]. In B. graminis, increased CYP51 copies and heteroallelism correlated with elevated azole resistance [27], though these features have not yet been reported in Z. tritici. It is documented that azole resistance is polygenic and quantitative, evolving gradually [31–33]. Predominantly, three major DMI resistance mechanisms have been identified in Z. tritici [28]: (I) point mutations in CYP51; (II) gene overexpression; and (III) increased efflux pumps activity. The species’ high genetic diversity, sexual reproduction, large population size, and gene flow promote rapid adaptation to fungicide selection and host resistance [34]. In Iran, azoles, particularly propiconazole, are widely used for STB management, yet no systematic studies have explored resistance in Z. tritici from this region. Early detection of resistant isolates is essential to limit their spread. This study represents the first investigation of azole resistance in Iranian Z. tritici isolates and to contrast resistance gains with the global pathogen diversity. We combined phenotypic assessment of propiconazole sensitivity with molecular characterisation of CYP51 mutations. Additionally, we employed structural modelling to predict the impact of amino acid substitutions on CYP51 function and fungicide interaction. This research will fill a critical knowledge gap and support more sustainable STB management strategies in the region.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and fungal isolation

Wheat leaves showing typical STB symptoms were collected from 14 field locations across six Iranian provinces, East Azerbaijan, Ardabil, Alborz, Golestan, Khuzestan, and Lorestan (Fig. 1; Table 1), representing diverse climatic zones: northern (temperate/humid), northwestern (cold), southern (tropical), central (temperate/dry), and western (temperate/cold). Z. tritici isolation followed a modified direct method described previously [35]. Leaf segments with pycnidia were placed on sterile glass slides inside Petri dishes lined with moistened filter paper. After 24 h incubation at 18 °C in darkness, pycnidiospores were transferred with a sterile needle to Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA; Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany). After 5–7 days at 18 °C, pink colonies typical of Z. tritici were selected and purified on Yeast Malt Sucrose Agar (YMSA) using the streak plate method. For long-term storage, 30% (v/v) glycerol stocks were prepared and kept at –80°C.

Fungicide sensitivity assessment

Sensitivity of Z. tritici isolates to propiconazole was assessed using a microtiter plate assay based on McDonald et al. (2019) with minor modifications. [36]. A logarithmic range of concentrations (0.02–39.6 mg/L) was prepared using technical-grade propiconazole (95% purity; Tayf Sabz Products Company, Karaj, Iran) dissolved in ethanol. 100 μL aliquots were dispensed into sterile 96-well plates (Nest, China), and ethanol was evaporated overnight under sterile conditions. A spore suspension was prepared from 5-day-old colonies incubated at 18 °C, adjusted to 10⁶ spores/mL using a hemocytometer, and diluted in liquid YMS medium (4 g/L yeast extract, 4 g/L malt extract, 4 g/L sucrose). Each well received 100 μL of inoculum. Controls included: medium only (blank), medium + spores (no fungicide), and medium + fungicide (no spores). IPO323 served as the sensitive reference isolate. Treatments were run in triplicate, and the entire assay was repeated three times. Because the three independent biological experiments yielded nearly identical inhibition curves, IC₅₀ values were calculated from a single representative biological dataset. The complete raw OD405 measurements for this dataset are provided in Table S1. Plates were sealed with parafilm, wrapped in aluminium foil to reduce evaporation and photodegradation, and incubated at 18 °C in a shaking incubator (120 rpm) for 5 days. Fungal growth was quantified by OD measurement at 405 nm using a microplate reader.

Analysis of fungicide sensitivity data

Variation in propiconazole sensitivity among Z. tritici isolates was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), based on a minimum of three independent replicates per treatment. Statistical analyses were conducted in SAS software, and mean comparisons were performed using the least significant difference (LSD) test at a 1% significance level. IC₅₀ values (the concentration required to inhibit 50% of fungal growth) were calculated for each isolate using KyPlot 6.0 (KyensLab Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Molecular analyses were performed on seven representative isolates selected to capture the full range of IC₅₀ values and growth responses: three resistant (KNA3, NR6, NM10.a), one tolerant (RDZ1.a), one sensitive (SC9.b), and one (AA2.b) with a complex genotype and atypical phenotype (Table 2). The reference isolate IPO323 [37] was included for comparative purposes.

Molecular analyses

Fungal spores were harvested after 5–7 days of growth in liquid YMS medium at 18 °C and freeze-dried for 16 h. Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB protocol adapted from Fagundes et al. (2020), omitting mercaptoethanol and replacing phenol with a 1:1 chloroform–isoamyl alcohol mix. Other steps followed the standard CTAB method [38]. The main region of the CYP51 gene (Protein ID: 60974), including promoter and terminator, was amplified by PCR using six primer pairs (Fig. S1; Table S2) designed with Primer3Plus and synthesized by Metabion GmbH (Germany). Each 25 μL reaction included 22 μL DreamTaq Master Mix, 1 μL of each primer (10 μM), and 1 μL template DNA (15 ng). Cycling: 95 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 45 s, 58–64°C annealing for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min; final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Products were purified and sequenced (Macrogen). Sanger-derived sequences were BLASTed and aligned in Geneious Prime® 2025.0.2 to identify SNPs. Promoters (~ 500 bp upstream) were scanned for fungal TF motifs using JASPAR (Table S3). An 80% threshold and a score cut-off of 5 were applied [39]. Targeted Sanger sequencing was first performed to identify CYP51 variants, and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was subsequently used for an independent validation step. For isolates where both datasets were available (H1–H5), Sanger and WGS results were fully concordant. Two isolates belonging to haplotype H7 (AA1 and W3.a) repeatedly failed to produce PCR amplicons of sufficient quality for Sanger sequencing; however, their WGS assemblies provided complete CYP51 coverage, enabling reliable haplotype assignment. In contrast, isolate H6 (KNA3) produced high-quality Sanger data but could not be sequenced by WGS due to insufficient DNA quality. Therefore, its CYP51 allele was confirmed only through targeted Sanger sequencing. For WGS, DNA libraries were prepared and sequenced at BGI Tech Solutions (Hong Kong) using the DNBseq platform with paired-end 150 bp reads, generating ~ 1 Gb clean data per sample. Raw reads were delivered in FASTQ format and analysed in-house by mapping against the IPO323 reference genome. WGS results were fully consistent with targeted sequencing, confirming the robustness and accuracy of CYP51 variant detection.

Gene expression analysis

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted to assess CYP51 expression in Z. tritici isolates IPO323 (sensitive) and KNA3 (resistant, high IC₅₀). Fungal biomass was collected from YMS cultures grown ± 5 µg/mL propiconazole, each with three biological replicates. Total RNA was extracted (RNeasy Plant Mini Kit, Qiagen), and genomic DNA removed (DNA-free Kit, Ambion). First-strand cDNA (~ 2 µg RNA) was synthesised using SuperScript III with oligo(dT) primers. qRT-PCR (25 µL) used 1 µL cDNA and QuantiTect SYBR Green Master Mix, run on an ABI 7500 system. Gene-specific primers targeted CYP51 and beta-tubulin [40] were used (Table S4). Relative expression levels were normalised to beta-tubulin and calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method [41]. All reactions were performed in technical triplicate for each biological replicate.

Structural modelling and validation of CYP51 protein

To characterize CYP51 from Z. tritici IPO323, we calculated its molecular weight and isoelectric point using ExPASy’s Compute pI/Mw tool, based on its 544-amino acid sequence. Structural models were generated via both homology and deep learning approaches. For homology modelling, the sequence was submitted to SWISS-MODEL using default settings. The best-fit model was selected by QMEAN score and validated using a Ramachandran plot (RAMPLOT) [42]. Additional model quality was assessed using PROSA-web [43], providing Z-scores and residue-level energy profiles. In parallel, structure prediction was conducted via ColabFold (v1.5.5), integrating AlphaFold2 with MMseqs2 [44], using Amber refinement, PDB100 template mode, and 48 recycles. From the five predicted models, the one with the highest mean Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score was selected and refined using DeepRefiner [45]. Models were validated with RAMPLOT and PROSA-web. SC9.b and RDZ1.a, identical to IPO323, were excluded. SNP-containing isolates (NR6, NM10.a, AA2.b, KNA3) were modelled via AlphaFold2 and compared to IPO323 using UCSF Chimera’s MatchMaker (Needleman-Wunsch, BLOSUM-62), focusing on SNP-related deviations [46],

Ligand binding site prediction and molecular docking

To identify potential ligand binding sites and infer CYP51 function in Z. tritici IPO323, we used the I-TASSER server [47]. The full-length CYP51 sequence was submitted to I-TASSER for structure-based annotation. Ligand-binding sites were predicted via COACH (COFACTOR, TM-SITE, S-SIT). The resulting PDB model was visualized in UCSF Chimera [46] to inspect key protein–ligand interactions, including van der Waals contacts. Propiconazole (PubChem CID: 43,234) was selected as the ligand. Its 3D structure was retrieved from PubChem in SDF format, then converted to MOL2 using Open Babel v3.1.1. Docking was performed in SwissDock [48], using the I-TASSER model as the receptor. The docking grid was centred at coordinates (84, 85, 81 Å) with dimensions of 20 × 20 × 20 Å, based on the active site of human CYP51 [49]. Four random initial conditions (RICs) were used. From nine top-ranked models, the pose with the lowest binding free energy was selected. The final complex was inspected in Chimera, and residues within 4 Å of the ligand were annotated as potential interaction partners.

Conservation analysis of amino acid residues and phylogenetic tree of CYP51

To assess evolutionary conservation of Z. tritici CYP51, the IPO323 sequence was submitted to the ConSurf server (https://consurf.tau.ac.il/consurf_index.php) [50]. Homologues were retrieved from UniRef90 using default settings, yielding 150 non-redundant hits. Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was performed using the MAFFT algorithm. Bayesian scores classified residues as variable (1–4), intermediate (5–6), or highly conserved (7–9). Residue exposure was inferred from the structural model, and amino acid frequencies were extracted from the MSA. CYP51 sequences were imported into Geneious Prime® 2025.0.2 for phylogenetic analysis using the Neighbor-Joining method and Jukes–Cantor model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates (seed = 659,903). Our aim was taxonomic placement rather than inference of evolutionary direction: hence, no outgroup was defined. Pairwise sequence identities were calculated relative to IPO323. To confirm the robustness of the clustering, we additionally generated an UPGMA tree using the same sequence set and parameters (random seed 575,298; 1,000 bootstrap replicates). The final tree was exported in.nexus format and visualized using iTOL v7 (https://itol.embl.de/).

In Silico prediction of structural and functional impact of CYP51 mutations

To evaluate the impact of amino acid substitutions in Z. tritici isolates NR6, NM10.a, AA2.b, and KNA3, two complementary in silico tools, I-Mutant3.0 and HOPE, were used. Thermodynamic stability changes (ΔΔG) were predicted using the I-Mutant3.0 server (https://folding.biofold.org/i-mutant/i-mutant2.0.html) [51], with CYP51 sequences submitted in FASTA format under default parameters. Negative ΔΔG values indicate destabilization; positive values suggest enhanced stability. Structural consequences were assessed using HOPE server (https://www3.cmbi.umcn.nl/hope/) [52], which integrates data on sequence conservation, 3D structure, and physicochemical properties to predict the functional impact of mutations. Qualitative outputs were examined to interpret mutation-induced conformational changes.

Global CYP51 polymorphism analyses

To place our findings in a broader evolutionary and epidemiological context, we next examined CYP51 variation at a global scale. For this, we leveraged a previously assembled worldwide collection of 2,156 Z. tritici isolates spanning Europe, the Middle East, North America, South America, Oceania, and Africa, which had been whole-genome sequenced as part of earlier population genomics efforts [33]. This dataset provides a unique opportunity to compare the frequency and geographic distribution of key resistance-associated mutations across major wheat-growing regions. Illumina sequencing data previously generated for 2156 strains were processed by trimming low-quality reads and alignment to the Z. tritici reference genome IPO323. SNP variants were then called using GATK v.4.0.1.2 and jointly analysed as previously described [33] Polymorphism matching the CYP51 coding region was extracted using vcftools v. 0.1.16 and filtered [33, 53]. The resulting VCF file was then annotated with SnpEff to predict the functional impact of the genetic variants [54]. We focused on four specific mutations of interest (G450R, L4V, Y461S, ΔY459/G460), since E454K, and G516D were not identified. The proportion of each genotype (reference, alternative, or missing) per continent was assessed and plotted using the R package ggplot2 [55].

Results

Fungal isolation

In this study, 28 Z. tritici isolates were recovered from individual pycnidia of STB lesions collected from wheat leaves across 14 field locations in six Iranian provinces (Ardabil, Khuzestan, East Azerbaijan, Alborz, Lorestan, and Golestan) (Fig. 1). These provinces represent diverse climatic and agroecological zones, important for assessing nationwide fungicide sensitivity. Details of the isolates, including half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values, are presented in Table 1.

IC₅₀ calculation

Sensitivity testing of the 28 Iranian Z. tritici isolates, along with the wild-type reference IPO323 [37], revealed substantial variation in propiconazole response, based on IC₅₀ values (Table 1). Isolates were grouped as sensitive (IC₅₀: 0.01–1 μg/mL), tolerant (IC₅₀: 1–10 μg/mL), or resistant (IC₅₀ > 10 μg/mL), reflecting underlying genetic diversity. To represent the full IC₅₀ spectrum and distinct growth profiles, we selected seven isolates for further characterization: three resistant (KNA3, NR6, and NM10.a), one tolerant isolate (RDZ1.a), one highly sensitive isolate (SC9.b), and AA2.b, an isolate with a complex response profile and unusual phenotype despite a low IC₅₀ (0.23 μg/ml) (Table 1). Notably, AA2.b exhibited a triphasic growth pattern in response to increasing propiconazole concentrations: stimulation at low levels (0.02–0.04 μg/mL), moderate inhibition at mid-range (1.25–39.6 μg/mL), and partial recovery at the highest dose (125 μg/mL), with OD = 0.382 (~ 81% of fungicide-free control). This pattern was consistent across three replicates. In contrast, SC9.b displayed a typical dose-dependent decline. Statistically significant differences between AA2.b and SC9.b were observed at all concentrations (P < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 2. These findings suggest AA2.b’s resistance profile extends beyond standard IC₅₀ thresholds and may involve physiological adaptations to fungicide stress.

Identification of CYP51 mutations

Seven representative isolates were selected to capture the full IC₅₀ spectrum and phenotypic diversity. Comparative analysis of CYP51 sequences between Iranian isolates and IPO323 revealed multiple point mutations correlated with elevated IC₅₀ values (Fig. 3; Fig. S2). All translated proteins comprised 544 amino acids, consistent with previous reports [37]. Alignments in Geneious Prime identified multiple substitutions (Fig. 3; Table 2), including two novel substitutions, G450R and G516D, not previously reported in Z. tritici or other plant pathogens (Table 2) [28, 56]. Known mutations, L4V, E454K, N513K, Y461S, ΔY459/G460, L50S, and S188N, were also detected in agreement with prior studies [14, 33, 57–59]. The ΔY459/G460, present in AA2.b, eliminates two residues in the α−14 demethylase domain and is linked to reduce DMI sensitivity [58]. Based on these mutations, seven CYP51 haplotypes (H1–H7) were defined (Table 2), illustrating allelic diversity and potential adaptive responses to fungicide selection. Notably, H7 was represented by two isolates (AA1 and W3.a) that carried identical mutations but displayed different IC₅₀ values. To investigate regulatory variation, a 495 bp upstream region of the ZtCYP51 was sequenced. In AA2.b, this promoter region contained multiple SNPs and an 8 bp insertion (positions 398–405) absent in all other isolates (Fig. S3). This may affect transcription factors binding like NRG1 (a glucose-responsive repressor) and EDS1 which may be also involved in glucose mediated repression/activation (Table S3) [60, 61]. SC9.b, RDZ1.a, NR6, and NM10 shared a 6 bp deletion (positions 302–307), potentially disrupting CHA4 binding, a transcription factor responsive to amino acid levels. In addition, a unique mutation at 450 bp, found only in NR6 and NM10.a, may impact AFT2, involved in iron homeostasis (Table S3) [39]. Attempts to amplify this promoter region in isolate KNA3 repeatedly failed, suggesting high divergence or structural variation at this locus. To validate these findings, we also performed whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on seven Iranian isolates (H1–H5) and H7 containing two additional genomes, AA1, and W3.a (Table 2). CYP51 variants identified by targeted sequencing were fully consistent with those recovered from the genome assemblies. The two additional genomes (AA1, and W3.a) revealed only previously reported resistance-associated mutations (L50S, S188N, ΔY459/G460, and N513K). Isolate H6 could not be sequenced due to difficulties in obtaining sufficient DNA. These results demonstrate that targeted sequencing and WGS yielded congruent outcomes for CYP51 variation in Iranian isolates. To place these haplotypes in a broader context, we compared Iranian CYP51 variants with a global genome panel of 2,156 strains [33]. Frequencies of G450R, L4V, Y461S, ΔY459/G460, E454K, and G516D mutations were analyzed across different continents to assess their global distribution. We found E454K, L4V, Y461S, and ΔY459/G460 substitutions also in the global genome panel of 2156 strains [33]. The results showed variable frequencies of these mutations, with ΔY459/G having the highest frequency globally, and G450R and G516D being absent. ΔY459/G mutation was most prevalent in Europe, where the resistant allele was observed at a frequency of 40%. In contrast, the Middle East population showed a complete absence of ΔY459/G mutation. The E454K and L4V mutations were rare, with resistant alleles for both found at low frequencies only in North America and the Middle East, respectively (Fig. 4; Table S5).

ZtCYP51 expression in response to propiconazole exposure

To assess whether ZtCYP51 overexpression contributes to fungicide resistance, gene expression levels were quantified in the reference isolate IPO323 and the resistant isolate KNA3 (highest IC₅₀ value, Tables 1 and 2), under both untreated conditions and following exposure to 5 µg/mL propiconazole. Under untreated conditions, ZtCYP51 expression was similar in both isolates. Following propiconazole treatment, IPO323 showed no significant change in ZtCYP51 expression, whereas KNA3 exhibited an approximately twofold increase relative to its untreated state. These results suggest that inducible overexpression of ZtCYP51 may play a role in the resistance mechanism of this isolate (Fig. 5).

Relative expression of ZtCYP51 in IPO323 and KNA3, untreated and exposed to 5 µg/mL propiconazole. Under control conditions, expression was similar. Upon treatment, KNA3 showed ~ 2 × expression increase, suggesting inducible overexpression. Letters = significant differences (P < 0.05). Error bars = SD from three replicates

Structural characterization and comparative modelling of ZtCYP51 and its variants

The ZtCYP51 IPO323 is composed of 544 amino acids, with a molecular weight of 61,474.41 Da and an isoelectric point (pI) of 6.75. Homology modelling using SWISS-MODEL employed the crystal structure of Aspergillus fumigatus CYP51B bound to voriconazole (PDB ID: 4UYM, chain A), showing 67.5% identity and 89.7% coverage (Ser51–Arg537). The resulting model had a GMQE score of 0.77 and QMEANDisCo Global score of 0.83 ± 0.05 and contained 22 α-helices and 7 β-sheets (Fig. S4a). Secondary structure predictions indicated 30.9% helices, 20.6% β-sheets, and 48.5% coils. A hydrophobic patch was observed near α 20–21 region (Fig. S4b). Ramachandran plot analysis indicated 95.04% of residues were in favoured regions, 3.72% in allowed regions, and only 1.24% in disallowed regions based on the phi (φ) and psi (ψ) angles of the residues in the fold model (Fig. S4c). To address incomplete N- and C-terminal coverage, we used AlphaFold2 for full-length structural prediction. This model displayed 23 α-helices and 8 β-sheets, with higher helical (36.6%) and loop (53.6%) content (Fig. 6a). A prominent extended N-terminal domain was observed. Electrostatic surface revealed a distinct positively charged patch (blue) and several negatively charged regions (red), suggesting potential roles in molecular interaction (Fig. 6b). A solvent-accessible cavity potentially relevant for substrate access and protein interaction was also identified (Fig. 6c). Model confidence was further assessed using per-residue pLDDT scores, which exceeded 60 across > 95% of residues, with local dips mainly around position 450 (Fig. S5a). Ramachandran plot showed 96.7% of residues in favoured regions, 2.95% in allowed regions, and 0.37% in disallowed regions (Fig. S5b). PROSA analysis yielded a Z-score of –9.04, (Fig. S4c) fits within existing experimentally derived x-ray and NMR results for similar size proteins from the PDB database. In the plot of residue energies (Fig. S5d), positive values correspond to problematic or erroneous parts of the input structure. The plot of energy revealed that most of the calculated values in the ZtCYP51 IPO323 were negative, imparting overall good structure predictions (Fig. S5d). To extend the analysis, AlphaFold2 models were also generated for field isolates, NR6, NM10.a, AA2.b, and KNA3 (Table 2; Fig. 5d-g). All models had over 95% residues above pLDDT 60, with low- confidence regions restricted to loops and termini (Fig. S5e–q). Ramachandran plot analyses of these variants further validated structural integrity, with favoured residues ranging from 96.68% to 97.23% and disallowed residues below 1% (Table S6). To further validate the model quality, PROSA PROSA Z-scores for these variants (NR6: –9.11, NM10.a: –9.21, AA2.b: –9.45, KNA3: –9.28) also indicated thermodynamically plausible and stable folds. To investigate structural alterations associated with CYP51 variants from different isolates, full-length AlphaFold2 models of NR6, NM10.a, AA2.b, and KNA3 were aligned with the reference IPO323 CYP51 structure using the MatchMaker tool in UCSF Chimera. Superimposition revealed high overall structural conservation, indicating that most amino acid substitutions do not induce large-scale conformational changes. However, localized deviations were observed near mutation sites. In NR6, the G450R substitution caused a subtle distortion in the loop between α17 and β12, while a novel N513K substitution within α21 induced mild reorientation of the adjacent helical turn (Fig. 6d). The NM10.a variant harboured the Y461S mutation and L4V substitution near the N-terminal α1 helix, with L4V introducing a localized kink potentially affecting protein targeting or interactions (Fig. 6e). AA2.b contained three substitutions: S188N in the α7–β5 loop causing minor loop rearrangement, E454K within α17 leading to a clear shift in helix orientation near the active site entrance, and N513K producing conformational effects similar to NR6 (Fig. 6f). In KNA3, G516D substitution altered the β8 and α21 junction, causing pronounced distortion in the local β-strand (Fig. 6g).

Functional prediction of ZtCYP51 using I-TASSER and Swiss Dock: Ligand binding site and cofactor analysis

To assess ZtCYP51’s functional architecture, we used I-TASSER for ligand prediction and SwissDock for docking stimulations. The I-TASSER model had a high-confidence C-score of 0.99 and strong convergence (cluster size = 1146). Ligand-binding residues were predicted by aligning to the cytochrome P450 template 4nkvA, identifying conserved positions across the protein. Key residues (positions 119, 136, 144, 148, 308, 311–316, 319, 375, 380–384, 386, 474–476, 480–484, 487–488, and 492) clustered around the cofactor HEM (heme) group, consistent with CYP51's sterol demethylation function. Figure 7 a–b illustrates the overall topology of ZtCYP51 and the spatial orientation of the HEM group (shown in red), deeply embedded in the active site cleft. The binding pocket was conserved, surrounded by polar and hydrophobic residues forming stabilizing hydrogen bonds (e.g., GLY 312, LEU 308 and CYS 482–PHE 475; Fig. 7b; Table 3).

Evolutionary conservation of residues in ZtCYP51: ConSurf analysis

To place Z. tritici CYP51 (ZtCYP51) in an evolutionary context a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed based on fungal CYP51 sequences. The obtained phylogeny (Fig. 8a) shows that ZtCYP51 clustered closely with other Dothideomycetes, including Cercospora spp. (82.1% identity), Neohortaea acidophila (73.8%), and Meristemomyces frigidus (71.2%), confirming a strong taxonomic signal within this class. In the reconstructed tree, the ZtCYP51 sequence formed a well-supported clade with other members of the order Capnodiales, reflecting their shared evolutionary origin and conserved sterol biosynthesis machinery. In contrast, CYP51 homologues from Basidiomycota, such as Candida and Schizophyllum spp., showed markedly lower identity (44.4–48.6%), emphasizing the deep phylogenetic divergence between fungal phyla. As expected, CYP51-like sequences from non-fungal eukaryotic outgroups (e.g., Littorina, Batoidea) shared minimal similarity (< 40%), consistent with the fungal lineage-specific specialization of CYP51 (Fig. 8a). An additional tree generated with the UPGMA algorithm using the same sequence set also produced clear separation of the major taxa, supporting the robustness of the phylogenetic clustering generated with the Neighbor-Joining method (Fig. S6). To evaluate the evolutionary constraint acting on ligand-interacting residues of Zymoseptoria tritici CYP51 (ZtCYP51), we performed a site-specific conservation analysis using ConSurf. The analysis revealed that several key residues involved in ligand coordination exhibited relatively low conservation across diverse fungal homologues. Notably, the novel and low frequency mutations found in this study G450, E454, G516, and L4 (Table 2) displayed ConSurf conservation scores of 2, 3, 3, and 4, respectively (Fig. 8b. Among these, G450, E454, and G516 are predicted to be solvent-exposed and variable, whereas L4 was classified as buried but evolutionarily variable (Fig. 7b). Amino acid frequency profiles derived from multiple sequence alignments support this interpretation. Leucine (L) was present at position 4 in 70% of aligned sequences, glutamate (E) at position 450 in 33%, glycine (G) at position 454 in 31%, and glycine at position 516 in 25% (Fig. S7). These observations indicate that while the identified residues are not among the most conserved, their substitutions are not rare among fungal CYP51 homologues, pointing toward moderate evolutionary flexibility.

Functional impact of amino acid substitutions predicted by I-Mutant and HOPE

To evaluate how amino acid substitutions affect ZtCYP51 stability and function, we used I-Mutant and HOPE servers. I-Mutant predicted changes in Gibbs free energy (ΔΔG), with negative values indicating destabilization. All two novel and low frequency mutations, L4V, G450R, E454K, and G516D, were predicted to reduce stability (Table S9). G450R showed the strongest destabilizing effect (ΔΔG = –1.84 kcal/mol), followed by G516D (–1.19 kcal/mol), L4V (–1.06 kcal/mol), and E454K (–0.37 kcal/mol). The HOPE provided complementary structural insights, revealing that these mutations could compromise protein architecture in multiple ways. The L4V substitution, though conservative, was predicted to disrupt terminal α-helix formation due to subtle changes in residue size and hydrophobicity. The G516D introduced a negatively charged, bulkier side chain that likely reduced local flexibility and increased steric hindrance. The G450R introduced a larger, positively charged residue, potentially disrupting electrostatic balance and interfering with ligand binding. The E454K reversed the residue’s charge and increased side-chain volume, possibly perturbing the electrostatic landscape near the substrate-binding site. Collectively, the predictions suggest these substitutions may destabilize the protein, distort local structure, and hinder ligand interactions. Experimental validation via biochemical or crystallographic approaches is needed to confirm these effects.

Discussion

Resistance to DMI fungicides in Z. tritici remains a major challenge for effective STB control. Geographic variation in resistance profiles has been widely reported and is likely driven by regional disease pressure, agronomic practices, and fungicide usage patterns [18, 23]. In this study, we examined six Iranian Z. tritici isolates from wheat-growing areas with frequent STB outbreaks to identify molecular mechanisms underpinning azole resistance. CYP51 sequence analysis revealed seven distinct haplotypes (H1–H7), reflecting notable allelic diversity despite the small sample size. We identified two novel substitutions, G450R and G516D, previously unreported in Z. tritici, and two low frequency (L4V and E454K), expanding the known resistance-associated mutation spectrum. Importantly, these results were independently validated by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) in six Iranian isolates, which confirmed the CYP51 variants detected by targeted sequencing and demonstrated the robustness of our mutation calls. The ongoing emergence of novel CYP51 mutations highlights the pathogen’s continued adaptive potential under fungicide selection pressure. At a global scale, this underscores the need for coordinated, long-term resistance monitoring to detect early shifts in allele frequencies that may threaten the sustained efficacy of azole fungicides worldwide. Although our dataset is limited, detecting novel haplotypes in a region of high genetic diversity such as Iran provides an early indication of how resistance traits may originate or spread across broader agro-ecological zones. To evaluate this further, we analysed the frequencies of G450R, L4V, Y461S, ΔY459/G460, E454K, and G516D across continents in a panel of 2,156 strains [33]. This revealed that while G450R and G516D were not detected, E454K and L4V were rare, observed only at low frequencies in North America and the Middle East, respectively. Gene expression analysis showed ~ twofold ZtCYP51 upregulation in isolate KNA3 following 5 µg/mL propiconazole exposure, compared to the sensitive SC9.b, possibly suggesting that both coding and regulatory changes may jointly confer reduced sensitivity. While the limited sample size precludes broad conclusions, the presence of diverse haplotypes and variable expression indicates ongoing local adaptation under fungicide pressure. Given the limited number of isolates, our findings should be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory, providing an initial framework rather than a comprehensive assessment of resistance dynamics in Iran. The inclusion of WGS data strengthens confidence in our findings by validating haplotype assignments across multiple isolates, although the absence of sequence data for isolate H6 remains a limitation.. To better understand these adaptations, we performed isolate-specific analyses integrating mutation profiles, phenotypic data, and structural protein modelling. This allowed detailed insight into how CYP51 variation may alter protein conformation and azole binding.

The SC9.b isolate was used as a local sensitive control due to its IC₅₀ value (0.014 µg/mL), closely matching the reference isolate IPO323 (Table 2) [36]. Complete CYP51 sequence identity with IPO323 confirmed its use as a baseline for resistance evaluation. SC9.b shared several promoter mutations with RDZ1.a, NR6, and NM10, including a 6 bp deletion (positions 302–307) that may affect the CHA4 transcription factor binding site (Fig. S2) (Sandelin et al., 2004). However, these changes appear unrelated to resistance, as SC9.b remained fully sensitive. Using a native sensitive isolate allows more context-specific comparisons and strengthens the phenotyping framework.

Isolate NR6 displayed high resistance (IC₅₀ = 69.88 µg/mL), representing a ~ 4,650-fold increase relative to IPO323 (Table 2) [36]. Two amino acid substitutions were identified: the frequently reported N513K [14] and the novel G450R. The G450R mutation, reported here for the first time in Z. tritici, resides within a region associated with azole binding (residues 438–463) [59]. A similar G450E mutation has been implicated in fluconazole resistance in Candida albicans [62]. Structural modelling revealed that G450R alters the electrostatic surface near the CYP51 entry channel, potentially affecting azole access. Substituting glycine (small, neutral) with arginine (bulky, charged) likely modifies local folding or ligand interaction via electrostatic effects [63]. As most Z. tritici CYP51 mutations are N-terminal [64], this C-terminal mutation is noteworthy. The extreme resistance phenotype, mutation location, and predicted structural effects support a key role for G450R in NR6 resistance. In addition, a mutation in the ZtCYP51 promoter (450 bp) was only found in resistant isolates NR6 and NM10.a. This could impact transcription factor binding such as AFT2 which regulates iron responsive genes. Previous studies in Aspergillus fumigatus indicate disrupting iron sensing regulatory motifs can increase CYP51 expression and azole resistance [65].

NM10.a exhibited high propiconazole resistance (IC₅₀ = 43.12 µg/mL) and carried a unique mutation combination (Table 2): the previously reported Y461S and the globally rare L4V change near the N-terminus of ZtCYP51. The Y461S change is known to reduce azole sensitivity in European populations [14, 57], while the L4V, lies within a region critical for folding and stabilisation [64]. Although both leucine and valine are hydrophobic, substitution at early structural positions may affect folding or protein dynamics [66]. AlphaFold2 modelling indicated that L4V introduces a localized kink at the tip of the α1 helix, a region protruding from the protein core and may be involved in membrane targeting or intermolecular interactions (Fig. 5). While no major conformational changes were observed, the location of L4V suggests it could affect protein stability or cellular localisation. Whether L4V enhances the effect of Y461S or functions independently remains unclear. The high IC₅₀ observed supports possible synergism, suggesting a novel resistance strategy under fungicide pressure. This case emphasizes the functional relevance of poorly studied N-terminal variants and highlights the need for mutagenesis and broader population screening to define their roles in azole resistance.

Isolate AA2.b displayed a distinct phenotypic and genotypic profile. Despite its low IC₅₀ value (0.23 µg/mL), typically indicative of sensitivity, AA2.b showed a non-linear growth response to fungicide (Fig. 2). Growth increased at low concentrations (0.02–0.04 µg/mL), declined at intermediate levels (1.25–39.6 µg/mL), and recovered at 125 µg/mL, reaching ~ 81% of growth observed without fungicide. This contrasts with the steady inhibition seen in sensitive isolates like SC9.2 [67], suggesting that IC₅₀ alone may not reflect true resistance. The unusual response may stem from fitness trade-offs linked to AA2.b’s extensive CYP51 mutation profile. Resistance mutations can reduce fitness without fungicide but offer advantages at high doses via compensatory mechanisms [67]. AA2.b harboured the most diverse mutation spectrum (Table 2), consistent with sustained local azole exposure, which can drive accumulation of target-site mutations and complex resistance [68]. Genotypic analysis revealed the known ΔY459/G460 deletion and a novel E454K substitution. The identification of the ΔY459/G460 deletion in Iranian isolates, previously reported primarily in European [58], raises the question of whether this mutation arose locally or was introduced. Determining its origin would require additional genetic analyses, such as comparative haplotype reconstruction or genome-wide population structure analyses, which are beyond the scope of this study. E454K, located near the binding pocket, introduces a positive charge, and may disrupt fungicide interaction, similar to the independently impactful I381V [59]. AA2.b’s full mutation set, L50S, S188N, ΔY459/G460, N513K, shows ~ 90% sequence similarity with a New Zealand variant [59], but E454K may reflect regional adaptation. Additionally, promoter analysis revealed an 8 bp insertion (398–405 bp) unique to AA2.b (Fig. S2), potentially affecting binding of glucose-responsive transcription factors such as NRG1 and EDS1 [60, 61]. Together, these findings reveal that low IC₅₀ may obscure underlying resistance, especially in isolates with complex mutations and atypical responses. AA2.b’s profile suggests compensatory or regulatory mechanisms that sustain fitness at high fungicide levels. Further structural and functional work is needed to clarify E454K’s role and its interaction with co-occurring mutations. This case highlights the need for integrated phenotypic and molecular analyses to track emerging resistance in Z. tritici.

Isolate RDZ1.a exhibited an intermediate IC₅₀ value of 8.60 µg/mL, consistent with a tolerant phenotype between sensitive and highly resistant isolates (Table 2). Its growth remained relatively stable across fungicide concentrations, maintaining ~ 33% of control-level growth even at 125 µg/mL. This sustained viability suggests physiological tolerance not readily explained by classical CYP51-mediated resistance. ZtCYP51 sequencing revealed only a synonymous mutation and minor promoter polymorphisms (Fig. S2), also present in SC9.b, NR6, and NM10, indicating that target-site variation is unlikely to underlie the observed tolerance. AlphaFold2-based modelling confirmed near-complete identity with the sensitive isolate at the amino acid level, with no disruptions in azole-binding regions or overall fold. This supports the inference that CYP51 alterations are not responsible for RDZ1.a’s phenotype. Instead, its growth dynamics are consistent with non-target-site mechanisms, particularly efflux-mediated resistance. Previous studies have shown that upregulation of efflux transporters MFS1 and ABC family proteins can reduce intracellular azole concentrations and confer tolerance [10, 69]. Additionally, promoter mutations in MFS1 can enhance expression and drive resistance [70]. Although efflux activity was not measured here, RDZ1.a’s growth profile closely matches this mechanism. The absence of CYP51-related changes, coupled with its tolerance at high fungicide concentrations, emphasises the need to look beyond target-site analysis when diagnosing resistance. Future studies should integrate transcriptomic or efflux-based functional assays to validate this hypothesis.

Isolate KNA3 exhibited the highest resistance to propiconazole among all Iranian isolates, with an IC₅₀ of 93.94 µg/mL (Table 2). CYP51 sequencing revealed a novel amino acid substitution, G516D, not previously reported in Z. tritici. AlphaFold2-based modelling predicted that G516D (Fig. 5) lies near the enzyme’s C-terminus, introducing a negatively charged aspartic acid in place of a small neutral glycine. While no major structural rearrangements were observed, local perturbations in helix architecture and electrostatic surface potential were noted. These could influence azole binding directly via changes to the ligand pocket or indirectly through allosteric effects, as similar distal mutations have done in other fungal CYP51 homologs. Despite repeated genome walking attempts, amplification of the ~ 500 bp upstream CYP51 promoter failed, preventing sequence analysis. However, qRT-PCR revealed a twofold increase in CYP51 expression in KNA3 relative to IPO323 under both control and 5 µg/mL propiconazole conditions, indicating transcriptional upregulation (Fig. 4). Although the promoter architecture remains unresolved, previous studies have linked upstream insertions (e.g. 120 bp, 860 bp) to enhanced expression and azole resistance [19, 70, 71]. The concurrent presence of a novel coding mutation and elevated expression in KNA3 suggests a multifactorial resistance mechanism. Functional validation of G516D via heterologous expression in S. cerevisiae or targeted Z. tritici mutants could further clarify its role [72]. These findings emphasize the need to integrate structural and regulatory analyses in resistance diagnostics.

To better understand the molecular basis of resistance phenotypes, especially those involving non-classical or novel mutations, we conducted in silico structural modelling of ZtCYP51 and its Iranian field variants. These analyses provide mechanistic insights into how amino acid changes may affect enzyme structure, ligand accessibility, and azole binding. High-confidence models from AlphaFold2 and SWISS-MODEL (Fig. 5 and Fig. S3) revealed a conserved cytochrome P450 fold, consistent with CYP51’s role in ergosterol biosynthesis. The core catalytic motif (Cys–Gly–X–His) was preserved across all isolates, as previously observed in fungal pathogens such as Candida albicans and Fusarium graminearum [73, 74]. Docking simulations using I-TASSER and SwissDock (Fig. 6) confirmed the heme-binding cavity remained conserved, with propiconazole occupying a hydrophobic cleft and coordinating with the heme iron via its azole nitrogen, as shown in Aspergillus fumigatus [75]. Electrostatic surface renderings highlighted a solvent-accessible entry tunnel where residues Leu308, Ala311, and Cys482 stabilized ligand and cofactor binding [75, 76]. Variants such as G450R, E454K, and G516D introduced localized structural changes near the access tunnel or active site. While these were structurally tolerated, they may alter access or retention of azoles by modifying tunnel topology or electrostatics. For instance, E454K in AA2.b repositioned helix α17, potentially restricting fungicide entry, a mechanism paralleling in Botrytis cinerea and Blumeria graminis [77, 78].

ConSurf analysis showed moderate conservation for residues G450, E454, G516, L4 across fungal CYP51 homologues, indicating evolutionary flexibility and their role as mutational hotspots (Fig. 7 a and b) [70, 79]. This supports previous studies where adaptive substitutions reduce sensitivity without impairing function [29, 64]. In silico predictions using I-Mutant and HOPE suggested that mutations like G450R and G516D destabilize local structure through charge shifts or steric clashes near the heme.

The structural modelling corresponded with phenotypes: resistant isolates carried mutations affecting either the active site (e.g., Y461S, ΔY459/G460) or access tunnel (e.g., G450R, E454K), consistent with resistance alleles [28, 58]. AA2.b showed triphasic growth and a novel 8-bp promoter insertion, mirroring mechanisms of CYP51 upregulation and MDR transporter activation [70, 71].

Here we used an integrated framework to investigate azole resistance in Z. tritici by combining phenotypic assays, targeted sequencing, and WGS. This study suggests that CYP51 substrate access-site plasticity, conserved catalytic architecture, and promoter variation may collectively shape resistance beyond canonical mutations. Such integrated approaches will strengthen diagnostics and inform next-generation antifungal design.
