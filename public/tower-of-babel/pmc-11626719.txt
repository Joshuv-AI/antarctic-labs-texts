Human mitochondrial peroxiredoxin Prdx3 is dually localized in the intermembrane space and matrix subcompartments
Authors: Fernando Gomes, Helena Turano, Luciana A Haddad, LuisES Netto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeroxiredoxin 3 (Prdx3) is the major sink for H2O2 and other hydroperoxides within mitochondria, yet the mechanisms guiding the import of its cytosolic precursor into mitochondrial sub-compartments remain elusive. Prdx3 is synthesized in the cytosol as a precursor with an N-terminal cleavable presequence, which is frequently proposed to target the protein exclusively to the mitochondrial matrix. Here, we present a comprehensive analysis of the human Prdx3 biogenesis, using highly purified mitochondria from HEK293T cells. Subfractionation and probing for specific mitochondrial markers confirmed Prdx3 localization in the matrix, while unexpectedly revealed its presence in the mitochondrial intermembrane space (IMS). Both matrix and IMS isoforms were found to be soluble proteins, as demonstrated by alkaline carbonate extraction. By combining in silico analysis, in organello import assays and heterologous expression in yeast, we found that Prdx3 undergoes sequential proteolytic processing steps by mitochondrial processing peptidase (MPP) and mitochondrial intermediate peptidase (MIP) during its import into the matrix. Additionally, heterologous expression of Prdx3 in yeast revealed that its sorting to the IMS is dependent on the inner membrane peptidase (IMP) complex. Collectively, these findings uncover a complex submitochondrial distribution of Prdx3, supporting its multifaceted role in mitochondrial H2O2 metabolism.Keywords: Mitochondria, Peroxiredoxin, Prdx3, Intermembrane space (IMS), Matrix

Introduction

Mitochondria are the major source for superoxide and H2O2 generated in two aqueous subcompartments: the matrix and the intermembrane space (IMS) [1,2]. These species, commonly termed mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mROS), play dual roles in cellular signaling and oxidative damage. The steady state levels of mitochondrial H2O2 are primarily controlled by thiol-based systems [3], which can result in either physiological balance or pathological conditions, depending on the context. However, the biogenesis of mitochondrial thiol peroxidases remains poorly understood.

The human mitochondrial proteome is composed of approximately 1136 different proteins and the majority of them (99 %) are encoded by nuclear genes and synthesized on cytosolic ribosomes as precursors containing distinct mitochondrial target signals [4]. Most of these precursors are then post-translationally targeted into the proper mitochondrial subcompartments by specialized mitochondrial protein import machineries [5,6]. The presequence is a classical type of mitochondrial targeting signal that has been extensively studied in different organisms. This pathway is responsible for the import of the vast majority of matrix proteins and a smaller fraction of inner-membrane proteins [6]. In a few cases, the proteins that were initially targeted to the inner-membrane may eventually be released into the IMS after undergoing cleavage by proteases residing in the inner membrane [7].

Presequences are present in the N-terminal extensions of the precursor proteins, which form positively charged amphipathic α-helices [8]. During import, the elements of the presequence are sequentially recognized by receptors and other import components of the TOM and TIM23 complexes localized in the outer and inner mitochondrial membranes, respectively [7,9]. Upon mitochondrial import, most presequences are proteolytically removed from the precursors by the action of the highly conserved matrix-localized mitochondrial processing peptidase (MPP), resulting in mitochondrial mature proteins [8,10]. However, a subset of proteins undergoes additional processing steps [8,[11], [12], [13], [14]]. In this case, the metalloproteases localized in the mitochondrial matrix termed mitochondrial intermediate peptidase (MIP in human, Oct1 in yeast) and X-prolyl aminopeptidase 3 (XPNPEP3 in human, Icp55 in yeast) cleave off either eight or a single amino acid from the N-termini previously processed by MPP, respectively. Although the functional role of this two-step processing remains poorly understood, evidence indicates that the fully processed mature proteins are more stable than their intermediate forms [8,[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Possibly, this two-step processing represents a mitochondrial protein quality control system, which is consistent with the observation that the malfunctioning of their components compromise mitochondrial functions [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]].

Peroxiredoxins (Prxs) are key enzymes in the control of hydroperoxide levels in mitochondria [[24], [25], [26]]. This is achieved by a conserved cysteine residue designated peroxidatic cysteine (CP) which shows exceptionally high reactivity towards H2O2 [27]. For 2-Cys Prx group, which contains two highly conserved cysteine residues, the CP initially reacts with H2O2 to form sulfenic acid (CP–SOH) with release of water. In the next step, this unstable sulfenic acid reacts with a second cysteine residue designated resolving cysteine (CR), thereby resulting in a disulfide bond formation. This disulfide is subsequently reduced by thioredoxin (Trx) through two thiol‐disulfide exchange reactions, which turnover the enzyme [28].

The human mitochondria contain a specific Trx, named Trx2, which can reduce Prdx3 and Prdx5, two mitochondrial Prxs [3,24]. In this process, Trx2 is oxidized to a disulfide that is subsequently recycled back to the dithiol form by the mitochondrial selenoenzyme TrxR2 (thioredoxin reductase 2), which utilizes NADPH as electron donor [[29], [30], [31]]. Acting together, this set of proteins constitutes a thiol-based antioxidant system (Trx-Prx system) that controls the intramitochondrial H2O2 levels, thereby protecting the organelle against oxidative damage [3,24]. Moreover, by controlling the flux of H2O2 to specific targets and or transmitting equivalent oxidizing from H2O2 to other target proteins, this system can also act for signaling purposes [24,[32], [33], [34], [35]]. While Prdx3 is present exclusively in the mitochondria, Prdx5 shows a wide subcellular distribution, being found in mitochondria, peroxisome, cytoplasm, and nucleus [24,36]. Despite their function in H2O2 metabolism has been widely characterized, the mechanisms that govern the mitochondrial import, maturation and half-life of these enzymes into mitochondria remain largely unclear. Sim and coworkers [37] demonstrated that the maturation of mitochondrial Prdx5 from mouse involves sequential cleavages catalyzed by MPP and MIP proteases. Importantly, conditions that increase mitochondrial H2O2 production seem to inhibit the MIP activity eliciting an accumulation of the intermediate unprocessed form of Prdx5 [37]. The physiological consequences of this phenomenon remain uncharacterized.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing the biogenesis of mitochondrial Prdx3. We discovered that Prdx3 is targeted to both the matrix and the IMS subcompartments. During its import into the mitochondrial matrix, Prdx3 undergoes a sequential processing by MPP and MIP proteases. Conversely, its sorting to the IMS likely involves cleavage by the inner membrane protease (IMP). These findings reveal a complex submitochondrial distribution and maturation process for human Prdx3, underscoring its central role in maintaining mitochondrial H2O2 levels and potentially influencing redox signaling.

Results

Human Prdx3 is dually localized in the intermembrane space and matrix subcompartments

To investigate the submitochondrial localization of the human Prdx3, we first isolated mitochondria from HEK293T with a high degree of purity and integrity. By combining differential centrifugation with discontinuous Percoll gradient ultracentrifugation protocols, we successfully obtained a highly purified mitochondrial fraction, with minimal cytosolic and ER contamination, as indicated by the reduced levels of actin and protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) in the mitochondrial fraction compared to the cytosolic fraction (Fig. 1, compare lanes 3 and 4). The purity and integrity of isolated organelles were further confirmed by the significant enrichment of the mitochondrial marker Tim23 in the mitochondrial fraction (Fig. 1, lane 3 and 4).

Nuclei and unbroken cells were removed by centrifugation, and the resulting supernatant was further centrifuged to separate the crude mitochondria fraction (lane 3) from the cytosolic fraction (lane 2). A highly purified mitochondria fraction (lane 4) was then obtained by subjecting the crude mitochondria to a Percoll density gradient centrifugation. Equal amounts of protein from each fraction were separated by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting. Tim23, PDI and actin were used as markers for the mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and cytosol, respectively. These blots are representative of at least two independent biological replicates, each one in two technical replicates.

As anticipated, mammalian Prdx3 and Prdx5 exhibited a fractionation profile similar to the mitochondrial marker Tim23, confirming their mitochondrial localization. As a control, Prdx5 was also detected in the cytosolic fraction, in agreement with previous reports [38,39]. Unexpectedly, significant levels of Prdx4, a peroxiredoxin typically associated with the ER [40,41], were detected in the purified mitochondrial fraction. Although this fraction contained residual traces of PDI (an ER-marker), the Prdx4 levels were significantly higher (Fig. 1). Importantly, the other human Prxs, (Prdx1, Prdx2 and Prdx6) were absent in the mitochondrial fraction (data not shown).

Next, we assessed the submitochondrial localization of Prxs of the highly purified mitochondria fraction by a protease accessibility assay [42]. To this end, mitochondria were initially converted into mitoplasts by swelling in the presence or absence of proteinase K. In this process, the outer membrane is selectively disrupted by osmotic unbalance and the IMS content is released to the supernatant. Subsequently, both mitochondria and mitoplasts were collected by centrifugation, and the protein contents of the different fractions (pellet and supernatant) were analyzed by immunoblotting (Fig. 2A).

Mitochondria were efficiently converted to mitoplasts as confirmed by the substantial decrease of the soluble IMS marker (Sod1) in the pellet of mitoplasts (Fig. 2B, lanes 3 and 4) and its consequent appearance in the mitoplast supernatant (Fig. 2B, lanes 7). Sod1 was also observed in the mitoplast supernatant treated with proteinase K (Fig. 2B, lane 8), indicating that this enzyme was only partially digested.

This finding was also supported by the selective degradation of the protein Tim23 by proteinase K only in mitoplasts (Fig. 2B, lane 4). Tim23 is an inner-membrane protein that became accessible to externally added protease after disruption of the outer membrane. In contrast, Tom20, an outer-membrane protein, was completely degraded by proteinase K in both mitochondria and mitoplast fractions (Fig. 2B, lane 2 and 4). Finally, as expected for mitochondrial matrix proteins, Sod2, Tfam and Sirt3 were all protected against proteinase K digestion in both mitochondria and mitoplasts (Fig. 2B, lane 2 and 4). Importantly, the absence of Sod2, Tfam and Sirt3 proteins in the supernatant of mitoplasts indicated the integrity of the inner membrane in mitoplast preparations.

Similar to mitochondrial matrix makers, Prdx3 and Prdx5 were also protected from the digestion by proteinase K in both mitochondria and mitoplast, indicating their matrix compartment localization (Fig. 2B, lanes 2 and 4). Surprisingly, Prdx3 was additionally detected in the supernatant of the mitoplasts (Fig. 2B, lane 7), indicating that this protein is also localized in the IMS. Notably, the yeast mitochondrial protein (ScPrx1) displays a similar fractionation profile which is consistent with a dual localization: IMS and matrix [43]. As showed below, the mechanisms controlling this double mitochondrial localization of Prxs seems to be conserved in yeast and human cells.

Finally, Prdx4 exhibited a profile resembling a soluble IMS marker, i.e., it was detected in the supernatant of mitoplasts (Fig. 2B, lane 7). However, unlike Sod1, significant levels of Prdx4 were also detected in the mitoplast pellets (Fig. 2B, lanes 3 and 4). Possibly, Prdx4 is also present in the IMS and matrix. Prdx4 is traditionally viewed as an ER protein. As mitochondria and ER are tightly associated to each other, we cannot exclude that the presence of Prdx4 in the IMS and matrix fractions were due to ER contamination.

To further investigate the mitochondrial solubility of Prxs, we incubated the highly purified mitochondria with alkaline carbonate solution to extract soluble proteins and those that are peripherally attached to the membrane [42]. After ultracentrifugation, the integral inner membrane marker protein Tim23 remained in the membrane fraction (Fig. 2C, lane 2), whereas the matrix-located soluble marker protein Tfam was present in the soluble fraction. Similarly to the protein Tfam, both Prdx3 and Prdx5 remained in the carbonate supernatant (Fig. 2C, lane 3), demonstrating that they are soluble proteins.

The Prdx3 presequence contains the R-10 motif recognized by MPP and MIP proteases

We next aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the biogenesis of mitochondrial Prdx3. Our initial approach focused on identifying potential consensus cleavage motifs for mitochondrial proteases within Prdx3 presequence. One of the most recognized mitochondrial protease cleavage sites is the yeast sequence (RX(↓)F/L/I)XX(T/S/G)XXXX(↓)X, where the arrows indicate MPP and Oct1/MIP cleavage sites, respectively). This motif, also called R-10 motif, features three characteristic amino acid residues: a highly conserved arginine at position −10 relative to the mature N-termini, a bulky hydrophobic residue at position −8 and a small uncharged residue at position −5 [11,13,14,44].

It is important to mention that Oct1/MIP recognizes and cleaves their substrates only after initial processing by MPP protease, suggesting that the octapeptide must be present at the amino terminus of the target protein to be recognized by Oct1/MIP. The −10 Arg residue is important for MPP recognition of the presequence. After MPP cleavage, this Arg residue is removed, leaving the new N-termini with a bulky hydrophobic residue (mainly Phe, Leu, Ile or Val) at position −8 of the presequence and a small uncharged residue at position −5 [[45], [46], [47]].

As mentioned above, yeast Oct1 and its human orthologue MIP are highly conserved and seems to cleave their substrates in a very similar manner [17]. However, whereas the Oct1 cleavage site has been identified in a large number of yeast precursor proteins [8,11,14], only a small subset of mammalian preproteins has been experimentally shown to be cleaved by MIP [[46], [47], [48], [49]], imposing uncertainties to the prediction of the exact MIP cleavage site [12,45].

To overcome this limitation, we created a comprehensive list of human, mouse and rat proteins containing the R-10 motif, using the UniProt database and datasets provided by high-throughput analyses of mature mitochondrial N-termini of mouse and human cells [50,51] (Fig. S1). The alignment of these putative MIP cleavage sites allowed us to estimate the amino acid frequency at each position of the R-10 motif, producing a pictogram for each one of the three mammalian species (Fig. 3A). Notably, the predicted mammalian MIP cleavage site motifs were very similar to the one experimentally determined for Oct1 in yeast cells [14]. In the three mammalian organisms evaluated, an arginine residue was highly enriched at position −10, while a bulky hydrophobic residue (Phe, Leu, Ile or Val) was commonly found at position −8. A small, uncharged residue (Ser and Thr) was preferentially distributed across positions −7, −6 and −5, a feature present in the Oct1 cleavage site motif. Finally, we noted a slight preference for serine and alanine at position +1, which represents the N-terminus of the mature proteins.

We then examined the presequences of mammalian proteins within the Trx-Prx system, focusing on Prdx3. Previous studies have identified four different amino acids for the N-terminus of Prdx3 [[50], [51], [52], [53]], with slight variations in the precise position where the mature protein begins (Fig. S2). The most frequently reported cleavage positions are Ala 62 for human Prdx3 and Ala 63 for bovine Prdx3, suggesting that the MIP cleavage site lies between His61 and Ala62 in the human orthologue. This closely resembles the cleavage sites of other well-characterized MIP substrates (Fig. 3B), with the key distinction being a lysine at position −10, replacing the more typical arginine.

Interestingly, in our list of human mitochondrial proteins containing the R-10 motif, only two other proteins exhibit a variation at this position. In both cases, the replacement is with another positively charged residue (Mrpl14-His and Gpd2-Lys) (Fig. S1). The requirement for a positively charged residue at position −2 relative to the MPP cleavage site became evident by the atomic resolution structure of MPP in complex with synthetic substrate peptides derived from the presequences of two Oct1 substrates, Cox4 and Mdh1 [10]. Curiously, unlike human Prdx3, mouse, rat and bovine Prdx3 orthologues possess a similar lysine, but at the −11 position (discussed below).

The experimentally determined human Prdx5 N-terminus (Fig. S2) also exhibits features consistent with the MIP cleavage site recognition motif identified in this study (Fig. 3B). Supporting this prediction, mouse Prdx5 has been shown to undergo sequential cleavage by MPP and MIP [37]. Unlike the variations in positively charged residues found at position −10 for Prdx3, all analyzed Prdx5 orthologues displayed an invariable arginine residue at this position.

Finally, using this same approach, we also observed that mammalian proteins belonging to the mitochondrial Trx system (Trx2 and TR2) also display the canonical MIP cleavage site (Fig. 3B). The only deviation from the consensus was the position −8 in TxrR2, which lacks a bulky hydrophobic residue. Altogether, these findings indicate that all components of the mammalian mitochondrial Trx/Prx system are likely substrates for MPP and MIP.

The R-10 motif arginine position differs among Prdx3 orthologues

Given that the Prdx3 presequences from human, mouse and rat display some variation in relation to the consensus of the R-10 motif (Fig. 3B), we conducted an in-depth analysis of Prdx3 presequences across different metazoan species. We initially defined the amino acid frequencies of the predicted MIP cleavage sites in Prdx3 orthologues from 185 species (Fig. 4, upper panel). Despite some variability, position −8 consistently showed a prevalence for bulky hydrophobic residues, while the position −5 was preferentially occupied by small uncharged residues, specifically serine, alanine, and glycine. Both features align with the structural requirements of MIP cleavage site described above.

The three highly conserved positions within the MIP cleavage site (−2, −8 and −5) are highlighted by blue, black and green boxes, respectively. However, in certain cases, the positively charged residue was found at position −11 instead of −10. This led us to hypothesize that in these instances, MPP might cleave the precursor between residues at positions −10 and −9. To investigate this, we selected 45 metazoan species and performed a sequence alignment of their Prdx3 presequences (Fig. 4, lower panel). Interestingly, the position of the positively charged residue was highly conserved across metazoan orthologues. While in primates, birds and invertebrates this residue is present at position −10, in the remainder species analyzed, it was present at position −11.

This raises the possibility that in certain metazoan groups, Prdx3 could be cleaved by MPP between the amino acids at positions −10 and −9, resulting in a C-terminal peptide containing nine residues instead of the typical octapeptide found in most MIP substrates. However, this hypothesis requires experimental validation.

The mitochondrial import of human Prdx3 into the matrix compartment involves distinct cleavages catalyzed by MPP and MIP proteases

To investigate whether MIP cleaves Prdx3 during its import into the matrix, we attempted to generate a knockout (MIP−/−) in HEK293T cells by CRISPR/Cas9. However, these efforts were unsuccessful, suggesting that MIP may be essential for cell viability, at least in our cellular model. As an alternative, we employed shRNA-mediated knockdown to decrease MIP levels, which was confirmed by immunoblotting (Fig. S3A). Despite this knockdown, Prdx3 migration on SDS-PAGE was not altered (Fig. S3A), likely because residual amounts of MIP protease were sufficient for Prdx3 processing.

To further investigate, we employed a yeast model system expressing both Prdx3 and MIP in an OCT1 deletion yeast strain. However, MIP was absent in yeast mitochondria, while it was detected in whole cell extracts (Fig. S3D), suggesting a defect in the translocation of the mammalian MIP precursor into yeast mitochondria. Consequently, Prdx3 mobility in SDS-PAGE remained unchanged in the distinct strains (Fig. S3B). This issue was not caused by reduced inner membrane potential in the OCT1 mutant [14], as the same defect was observed in the wild-type strain (Fig. S3E). Nevertheless, as we previously showed [43], and confirmed here (Fig. S3C), human Prdx3 is efficiently imported into yeast mitochondria and is sequentially cleaved by MPP and Oct1 (yeast orthologue of MIP). This finding was supported by the retardation in the migration of the band “m” in the null mutant strain for Oct1 protease (Fig. S3C), which aligns with the accumulation of an intermediate (band “i”) form of Prdx3 that was not processed by this protease and retained the octapeptide.

To address these experimental challenges, we investigated the Prdx3 mitochondrial import pathway through an in organello import assay with [35S] radiolabeled Prdx3 precursor proteins. To enhance the protein signal in the autoradiography, we tested two Prdx3 versions: [35S]Prdx3, which includes five additional methionine residues at its C-terminus, and [35S]Prdx3-DHFRMUT, a Prdx3 fused at its C-terminus with an unfolded mutant variant of mouse dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) [54]. This DHFR mutant contains three point mutations that destabilize the precursor protein, thereby enhancing its import rate and efficiency into mitochondria. Moreover, DHFRMUT contains seven methionine residues, further amplifying the protein signal in autoradiography. Both isoforms, especially [35S] Prdx3-DHFRMUT, were imported into mitochondria isolated from HEK293T cells in a time- and membrane potential-dependent manner (Fig. 5A and B). Initially, a single lower molecular weight band resistant to proteinase K digestion was observed, confirming the intramitochondrial localization of Prdx3. This shift (“p” to “m”) indicates cleavage of the Prdx3 precursor by MPP.

Further analysis on longer SDS-PAGE gel revealed one additional band of [35S]Prdx3 (Fig. 5C), indicating two proteolytic steps (“p” to “i” and “i” to “m”), likely catalyzed by MPP and MIP proteases. In an attempt to further boost the Prdx3 import efficiency, we included some mitochondrial energy-regenerating components (like ATP, NADH and malate) in the import reaction, but unfortunately we did not observe any increment in the import efficiency (Fig. 5C).

In summary, the sequence alignment (Fig. 3, Fig. 4), the heterologous expression of Prdx3 in yeast (Fig. S3C and ref 43) and the in organello import assay (Fig. 5C) support the notion that this peroxiredoxin is processed sequentially by MPP and MIP (Oct1) during its import into the matrix.We then explored whether the octapeptide in the intermediate form of Prdx3 influences its structural and biochemical properties. For this end, we generated Prdx3 recombinant proteins corresponding the mature (m-Prdx3) and intermediate (i-Prdx3) forms. The enzymatic activities of both isoforms were measured by a coupling assay using the yeast non-selenium thioredoxin reductase 1(Trr1) and thioredoxin 1 (Trx1) as previously described [55]. Yeast Trr1 was used instead of mammalian TrxR, which requires multiple purification steps from animal tissues. No significant difference in the rates of NADPH oxidation in the reactions catalyzed by the two Prdx3 isoforms were observed, indicating that the octapeptide does not affect peroxidase activity (Fig. S4).

The mitochondrial import of human Prdx3 into the IMS compartment requires the activity of the inner membrane peptidase (IMP complex)

Previously, we demonstrated that the mitochondrial import of yeast Prx1 into IMS relies on the inner membrane peptidase (IMP) complex [43]. To explore whether the mammalian IMP also plays a role in the import of Prdx3, we expressed the human PRDX3 gene under the control of the constitutive TEF1 promoter in both wild-type and ΔIMP2 (a catalytic subunit of the IMP protease) yeast cells. Heterologous expression of human Prdx3 did not lead in any noticeable growth defects, as assessed by the spot test assay (Fig. 6A).

To investigate the involvement of the IMP complex in mitochondrial import of Prdx3, we subfractionated yeast mitochondria isolated from Prdx3-expressing WT and ΔIMP2 cells. As expected, the soluble IMS marker Cyt.b2 was detected in the pellet fraction of mitochondria (Fig. 6B and C, lanes 1 and 2) and in the supernatant fraction of the mitoplasts that were not treated with proteinase K (Fig. 6B and C, lane 7), confirming the successful conversation of mitochondria into mitoplasts. Furthermore, the integrity of the inner membrane in the mitoplasts was validated by the protection of the matrix-marker α-KGD from proteinase K degradation (Fig. 6B and C, lane 4).

Next, we analyzed the fractionation pattern of Prdx3 in these samples. In wild-type cells, Prdx3 was detected in similar amounts in both the pellet and the supernatant fractions from mitoplasts (Fig. 6B, lanes 3, 4, 7 and 8), suggesting a dual mitochondrial localization: matrix and IMS. These findings were, therefore, consistent with observations in human mitochondria (Fig. 2). A residual amount of Prdx3 was also found in the supernatant fraction of intact mitochondria (Fig. 6B, lane 5 and 6), which could be attributed either to Prdx3 intermediates in transit through the import machinery or to mitochondrial rupture during purification. However, unlike Cyt.b2 that was completely degraded by proteinase K in the supernatant fraction, Prdx3 remained resistant to degradation (Fig. 6B and C, lane 8). Interestingly, yeast mitochondrial Prx1 is similarly resistant to proteinase K digestion [43], suggesting a common structural feature shared by peroxiredoxins confers this resistance.

Remarkably, while Prdx3 levels in the pellet fraction of ΔIMP2 mitochondria remained unaltered, the levels in the supernatant fractions were reduced compared to wild-type mitochondria (Fig. 6B and C). The bands corresponding to Prdx3 in Fig. 6B and C were subjected to densitometry. Densitometric analysis of the Prdx3 bands in these figures revealed that Prdx3 levels in the IMS (Fraction 7) were notably higher in wild-type mitochondria than in ΔIMP2 mitochondria (Fig. 6D). These data indicated that in the absence of IMP complex, Prdx3 is not efficiently targeted to the IMS.

Discussion

Prxs are the primary scavengers for mitochondrial H2O2 and other hydroperoxides [3,[24], [25], [26],56], with Prdx3 being responsible for degrading approximately 90 % of the H2O2 generated in the mitochondrial matrix [24,57]. Despite this well-established role of Prdx3 in H2O2 metabolism, the mechanisms governing its import into mitochondria remain unclear as well as their localization in the mitochondrial subcompartments. Our findings reveal that Prdx3 exhibits dual mitochondrial localization: matrix and IMS. The presence of Prdx3 in the IMS, but not Prdx5, aligns with the IMS proteome of HEK293 cells, which also reported the presence of Prdx4 in this compartment [58]. Thus, although Prdx3 had been consistently considered to be targeted exclusively to the mitochondrial matrix, this study also clearly confirms its presence into mitochondrial IMS.

To explore the mechanisms behind Prx biogenesis, we conducted in silico analysis, taking advantage of the available mitochondrial proteomics data [8,50,51]. Notably, we identified for the first time a consensus site for the sequential cleavage by MPP and MIP (Fig. 3A). Additionally, we showed that all components of the mammalian mitochondrial Trx/Prx system possess the R-10 motif (Fig. 3B), indicating they undergo sequential processing by MPP and MIP during maturation. These findings were supported by in organello import assays showing that Prdx3 undergoes two proteolytic cleavages during mitochondrial import (Fig. 5). The fact that the mouse Prdx5 is also processed by MPP and MIP [37] suggests that the mammalian mitochondrial Prxs targeted to the matrix compartment share a common maturation pathway.

To date, the main function attributed to the two-step cleavage by yeast MPP/Oct1 seems to be the removal of N-terminal amino acids that could cause protein instability [8,[13], [14], [15], [16]]. However, recombinant Prdx3 with (i-Prdx3) or without (m-Prdx3) the octapeptide showed similar peroxidase activity (Fig. S4). Further studies are required to understand the physiological roles of the octapeptide. Noteworthy, human diseases are associated with MIPEP (gene symbol coding for MIP protease) pathogenic variants [17,48].

We also aimed to gain insights on the mechanism behind Prdx3 import into the IMS. Through heterologous expression of human Prdx3 in yeast, we showed that the mitochondrial import of Prdx3 into IMS depended on the IMP complex (Fig. 6). In yeast, this protease consists of two catalytic subunits, Imp1 and Imp2, along with the auxiliary protein Som1 arranged into a hetero-oligomeric structure in the inner membrane with its active site facing the IMS [13]. One of its function is to cleave precursor proteins that are laterally released into the lipid bilayer of the inner membrane during their translocation through the TIM23 complex, producing the mature proteins into the IMS [59]. Mammalian mitochondria also contain an IMP-like inner membrane protease composed by the IMMP1l and IMMP2L subunits, but their substrates remains largely unknown [22,60]. While the IMP complex is involved in Prdx3 sorting, the exact cleavage site remains unknown. Identifying more IMP substrates could provide further insights in this direction. Importantly, mutations in the IMMP2L gene, which codes for Imp2 (one of the subunits of the IMP complex) are associated with the Tourette syndrome [61,62]. As we showed previously [43], the yeast mitochondrial peroxiredoxin Prx1 is also localized into IMS and matrix subcompartments by mitochondrial targeting pathways that have been evolutionary conserved. However, notable differences exist between the yeast and the mammalian systems. Yeast Prx1 is a 1-Cys Prx, while the mammalian Prdx3 is a 2-Cys Prx. In addition, while yeast mitochondria contain only one Prx isoform (Prx1), mammalian cells contain at least two isoforms(Prdx3 and Prdx5) in mitochondria [24].

The dual localization of Prdx3 in the matrix and in the IMS raises questions about its roles in each compartment. Its antioxidant function is consistent with mROS generation in both sites [1]. While mROS signaling is often discussed, its role remains debated [63]. A key issue is identifying the systems responsible for reducing mitochondrial H₂O₂ in these distinct sub-compartments. Physiological responses and pathologies linked to mROS production in the mitochondrial matrix are well-documented (reviewed by Ref. [64]).

However, the role of Prdx3 in the IMS remains less clear. One key process in the IMS is oxidative protein folding, which under certain conditions can generate H2O2 within this compartment [[65], [66], [67]]. Prdx3 may reduce this H2O2, which could in turn oxidize CHCHD4 (in yeast Mia40), an oxidoreductase involved in oxidative protein folding and thereby enhancing the import of proteins to IMS. Interestingly, in yeast, besides de mitochondrial Prx (Prx1), a glutathione peroxidase (Gpx3) is imported to IMS by an unconventional way and interacts with Mia40 [68]. A critical point in the mammalian redox biology is the identification of reductants in the IMS, as no Trx has been identified in this compartment so far [58].

Understanding mROS signaling requires insight into their diffusion from mitochondria to other subcellular compartments. Studies employing Hyper7, an ultrasensitive genetically-encoded probe, revealed that IMS acts as a barrier to mitochondrial H2O2 diffusion to the cytosol [69]. Notably, pre-incubation of cells with auranofin, a thioredoxin reductase inhibitor, triggered immediate release of mitochondrial H2O2 to the cytosol, indicating that this barrier is dependent on Trx system. Therefore, IMS-resident Prdx3 is a likely key player in limiting H2O2 diffusion from mitochondria to cytosol.

Material and Methods

Yeast strains, growth media, and plasmids

The genotypes and sources of yeast strains used in this study are listed in Table 1. The wild-type and the null mutant yeast strains were purchased from Life Technologies. Prior to use, the deletion of the gene of interest was confirmed by PCR with specific primers. Yeast cells were grown at 30 °C on YPD (1 % (w/v) yeast extract, 2 % (w/v) peptone, 2 % (w/v) glucose) or YPGal medium (1 % (w/v) yeast extract, 2 % (w/v) peptone, 2 % (w/v) galactose). Cells were transformed with the expression plasmids by lithium acetate method as previously described in Ref. [70]. The yeast strains transformed with the expression plasmids were grown in selective minimal medium (0.67 % (w/v) yeast nitrogen base without amino acids, 0.13 % (w/v) amino acid mix minus the amino acid required for selects the plasmid) containing glucose or galactose as carbon source.

The construction of yeast strains expressing human Prdx3 under the control of the TEF1 constitutive promoter was performed as described. The Prdx3 coding sequence was commercially synthesized by GenScript USA, Inc., and cloned into the yeast centromeric plasmid pRS415 using the BamHI and SalI restriction sites. The resulting recombinant plasmid was used to transform the different yeast strains.

The obtention of yeast cells that co-express the human Prdx3 and MIP from the same vector was performed as described. Initially, the Prdx3 coding sequence was amplified from the pRS413/PRX3 [43] with the primers hPRX3-F 5′ TCAACTAGTATGGCAGCAGCAGTTGGTAGAT 3′ and hPRX3-R 5′ TCAGAGCTCTTATTGGTTGACCTTTTGGAAGTA 3’. The purified PCR product with 789 bp was digested with SpeI and SacI and cloned into pESC-HIS (Agilent Technologies, Catalog #217451) to yield pESCHISGAL10-Prdx3. Then, the cDNA sequence of MIP was amplified from a human cDNA library using the primers hMIP-F 5′-TTAGTCGACATGCTGTGCGTCGGAAGGCT-3′ and hMIP-R 5′-GGCTACCTCGAGTTATTCAGAATCCATGAGGAAAG-3’. The purified PCR product with 2163 bp was digested with SalI and XhoI and cloned into pESCHISGAL10-Prdx3 previously digested with the same enzymes. In this recombinant plasmid, called pESCHISGAL10-PRDX3-GAL1-MIP, both genes are expressed under the control of the GAL promoter.

Cloning, expression and purification of recombinant proteins

To produce a C-terminally his-tagged mature Prdx3 isoform starting at Ala62 (m-Prdx3), the respective coding sequence was amplified from the pRS413/PRX3 [43] with the primers m-Prdx3-F 5′ TATGTACATATGGCACCTGCTGTCACCCAGCATGC 3′ and m-Prdx3-R 5′TCAGGTACCCTGATTTACCTTCTGAAAGTAC 3′ and cloned into the NdeI and KpnI sites of pET29b (Novagen). To produce a C-terminally his-tagged intermediate Prdx3 isoform starting at Phe54 (i-Prdx3), the coding sequence was amplified from the pRS413/PRX3 with the primers i-Prdx3-F 5′TATGTACATATGTTTTCTACATCCAGTTCTTGTCATGCACCTGCTGTCACCCAGCATGC 3′ and m-Prdx3-R 5′TCAGGTACCCTGATTTACCTTCTGAAAGTAC 3′ and cloned into pET29b as described above. The 312-bp DNA fragment encoding the cytosolic thioredoxin 1 from yeast (Trx1) was amplified by PCR from genomic DNA and cloned into the NdeI and BamHI sites of pET15b (Novagen). The recombinant plasmid encoding the cytosolic thioredoxin reductase 1 from yeast (Trr1) was constructed as previously described in Ref. [71].

All recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells. Overnight cultures were diluted in 0.5 L of LB broth to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.2, and cells were grown at 37 °C in a shaking incubator to an OD600 of 0.6–0.8. The protein expression was induced by the addition of 1 mM isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for a period of 3 h. The recombinant proteins were purified as described in Ref. [72] with minor modifications. The crude protein extract was initially loaded on a 5-ml HiTrap chelating HP column (GE Healthcare) pre-equilibrated with start buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate, 500 mM NaCl, pH 8.0). The column was then connected into an AKTA fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) system (Amersham Biosciences, GE Healthcare) and washed with 75 ml of start buffer containing 20 mM imidazole and then with 40 ml of start buffer containing 70 mM imidazole to remove unbound protein. The bound protein was eluted with a linear imidazole gradient (20–500 mM) in 50 ml of start buffer. The purified protein was further dialyzed against TN200 buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl) and subsequently purified by gel filtration chromatography on a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 column (GE Healthcare). Protein concentrations were determined by absorbance at 280 nm using the following extinction coefficients: m-Prdx3 (ɛ280 = 20065 M−1 cm−1), i-Prdx3 (ɛ280 = 20190 M−1 cm−1), Trr1 (ɛ280 = 24410 M−1 cm−1) and Trx1 (ɛ280 = 9970 M−1 cm−1) that were obtained using the ProtParam tool.

Peroxidase activity assay

The peroxidase activities of both Prdx3 isoforms (i-Prdx3 and m-Prdx3) were spectrophotometrically determined by the thioredoxin system coupled assay, following the disappearance of the NADPH absorbance at 340 nm. The standard reaction mixture contained 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 200 μM NADPH, 2 μM Trr1, 5 μM Trx3, 2 μM Prdx3, and 100 μM diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA). The reactions were initiated by addition of H2O2 to a final concentration of 50 μM.

Human cell line

The cell line used in this study was the human embryonic kidney cell line (HEK293T). Cells were cultured in DMEM (GIBCO) with high glucose content (4500 mg/L), supplemented with 10 % (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GIBCO), 2 mM l-glutamine (GIBCO), 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 1 % (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (GIBCO) at 37 °C under a 5 % CO2 humidified atmosphere.

Generation of cell lines stably expressing shRNA

The expression of MIP in HEK293T cells was stably knocked down (KD) using lentiviral vectors carrying specific short-hairpin RNA (shRNA) against MIPEP gene. Three distinct lentiviral vectors with the following shRNA sequences: (1) CCGGCCCAACAAGATTGAGAAGCATCTCGAGATGCTTCTCAATCTTGTTGGGTTTTTG (NM_005932.1–750s1c1), (2) CCGGGCTGGTCAATTGAAATGTTTACTCGAGTAAACATTTCAATTGACCAGCTTTTTG (NM_005932.1–900s1c1) and (3) CCGGCTTCTGTTGATGACTTCGTAACTCGAGTTACGAAGTCATCAACAGAAGTTTTTG (NM_005932.1–2137s1c1) were purchased from the Mission shRNA Library (Sigma-Aldrich). The plasmid pLKO.1-puro encoding a scramble non-mammalian shRNA (SHC002 - Sigma-Aldrich) was used as a control for knockdown events. For lentivirus production, HEK293FT were transfected with each of the MIP shRNA plasmids together with pMD2.G and psPAX2 plasmids (second generation lentiviral system). After 72 h transfection, viral supernatants were collated, filtered-sterilized and then used to infect fresh HEK293T cells. Transduced cells were selected with 0.3 μg/mL of puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 6 days, expanded and used for further experiments.

Isolation of pure mitochondria

Human mitochondria were isolated as previously described [73] with minor modifications. Briefly, cells were seeded into twenty 150 cm2 cell culture dishes and cultured until they become fully confluent. Next, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed with 10 ml of ice-cold PBS. Cells from each plate were collected with 10 ml of ice-cold PBS and harvested by centrifugation at 800×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended with 20 ml of ice-cold isolation buffer (220 mM mannitol, 70 mM sucrose, 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.4), 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 0.5 mM PMSF and subsequently homogenized by 20 strokes using a glass potter homogenizer. Samples, representing the whole cell fraction, were centrifuged twice at 800×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove nuclei, unbroken cells and cell debris fraction. The supernatant, representing the post-nuclear supernatant (PNS) fraction was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged at 12 000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant, representing the cytosolic fraction, was carefully collected and the pellet, representing the crude mitochondrial fraction, was suspended in 1 mL of ice-cold isolation buffer. Protein content was quantified using the Bradford assay.

To increase mitochondrial purity, the crude mitochondria fraction was submitted to a Percoll density gradient purification as described previously [73] with minor modifications. Briefly, the crude mitochondria fraction was carefully loaded on the top of a three-step Percoll density gradient consisting of 2 ml of 80 %, 3 ml of 52 %, and 1 ml of 26 % Percoll prepared in isolation buffer in a Beckman Ultra-Clear centrifuge tube. The sample was centrifuged at 44 000×g for 45 min at 4 °C and a highly pure mitochondria fraction were collected from the 52 %–26 % interface. The mitochondria were diluted 25-fold with ice-cold isolation buffer lacking PMSF and centrifuged at 12 000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Finally, the pellet was suspended in 300–500 μL of ice-cold isolation buffer and the protein content was again determined by Bradford assay.

Yeast mitochondria were isolated and subfractionated essentially as described previously [42].

Submitochondrial fractionation of human mitochondria

For mitochondrial sub-fractionation experiments highly purified mitochondria (320 μg of mitochondrial protein) were centrifuged at 12 000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Mitochondrial pellets were either suspended in isolation buffer lacking PMSF, to osmotically stabilize mitochondria, or in swelling buffer (10 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.4), to rupture the outer mitochondrial membrane. Samples were incubated on ice for 20 min in the presence or absence of proteinase K (62.5 μg/ml). After incubation, proteinase K activity was inhibited by addition of 1 mM PMSF. The samples were centrifuged at 16 000×g for 1 h at 4 °C. The pellets representing mitochondria or mitoplasts were resuspended in Laemmli buffer. The supernatant protein content was precipitated with 5 % (wt/vol) TCA and suspended in Laemmli buffer. The samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

For sodium carbonate extraction assay, highly purified mitochondria (200 μg) were resuspended in 420 μL of ice-cold freshly prepared 0.1 M sodium carbonate solution and incubated for 40 min on ice with occasional mixing. Samples were subsequently centrifuged at 123 000×g for 40 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant fraction was collected and precipitated with 10 % w/v TCA. The pellets from both TCA precipitation and ultracentrifugation were resuspended in Laemmli Buffer and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

In organello import of radiolabeled precursor proteins

The protein import assay was performed as described in Ref. [74] with minor modifications. Briefly, the open reading frames (ORFs) encoding the two Prdx3 isoforms (a C-terminally 5xMet-tagged Prdx3 and a C-terminally DHFRMUT-tagged Prdx3) were cloned into pGEM4Z (Promega) downstream of the SP6 promoter. Radiolabeled precursor proteins were synthesized in vitro in the presence of [35S] methionine (PerkinElmer) using the TNT SP6 Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation Kit (Promega). Freshly isolated mitochondria (30 μg of mitochondrial protein) were mixed with 4 μl [35S]-labeled precursor lysate in 1x M buffer (220 mM mannitol, 70 mM sucrose, 1 mM EGTA, 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.4), supplemented with 1 mM DTT. To deplete the membrane potential (Δψ), 1 μL of 20 mM CCCP (Sigma-Aldrich) solution was added and incubated on ice prior to the import reaction. The import reactions were incubated at 30 °C while shaking at 600 rpm and stopped at different time points by putting on ice. The samples were directly centrifuged at 8000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. Then, the mitochondrial pellets were resuspended in 400 μl of 1 × M buffer in the presence or absence of proteinase K (PK) (20 μg/ml). The incubation with PK, which promotes the digestion of nonimported precursor proteins, proceeded on ice for 20 min. The PK activity was stopped by adding 2 μl of 200 mM PMSF (1 mM final). Finally, mitochondria were re-isolated by centrifugation at 10 000×g for 5 min at 4 °C and the pellets were resuspended in 40 μl Laemmli Buffer containing 1 mM PMSF and 50 mM DTT. The samples were boiled at 95 °C for 5 min and analyzed by SDS/PAGE and autoradiography.

Whole-cell extracts

Whole-cell yeast extracts were obtained by post-alkaline extraction method. Whole-HEK293T cell extracts were obtained by cell lysis in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1 % SDS, 1 % IGEPAL (Sigma CA-630), 0.5 % sodium deoxycholate) followed by centrifugation at 16.000×g at 4 °C for 10 min.

Immunoblotting

SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting analysis were performed according to standard protocols. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE on different concentrations of polyacrylamide gels and then transferred to a Amersham™ Protran® Premium 0.45 μM Nitrocellulose Membrane (GE Healthcare) by semidry blotting. Membranes were blocked with 5 % Blotting Grade Blocker Non-Fat Dry Milk (BioRad) overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were then incubated with primary commercial antibodies (Table 2), diluted in TBST, for 1 h at RT. Membranes were washed at RT 3 times in TBST for 10 min and probed with secondary antibodies coupled to HRP (Table 2), for 1 h at RT. The signals from the antigen-antibody complexes were developed on X-ray films using the enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (GE Healthcare).
