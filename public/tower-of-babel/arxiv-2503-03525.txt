Discretization error analysis for a radially symmetric harmonic map heat flow problem
Nam Anh Nguyen, Arnold Reusken

Abstract
In this paper we study the harmonic map heat flow problem for a radially symmetric case. The corresponding partial dfferential equation plays a key role in many analyses of harmonic map heat flow problems. We consider a basic discretization method for this problem, namely a second order finite difference discretization in space combined with a semi-implicit Euler method in time. The semi-implicit Euler method results in a linear problem in each time step. We restrict to the regime of smooth solutions of the continuous problem and present an error analysis of this discretization method. This results in optimal order discretization error bounds (apart from a logarithmic term). We also present discrete energy estimates that mimic the decrease of the energy of the continuous solution.



keywords
Harmonic map heat flow problem, discrete stability analysis, discretization error bounds, discrete energy estimates.

Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N=2,3
, be a Lipschitz domain and
S^{2}
the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The harmonic map heat flow (HMHF) problem is as follows. Given an initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{0}:\Omega\to S^{2}
, determine
\mathbf{u}(\cdot,t):\Omega\to S^{2}
such that

\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}=\Delta\mathbf{u}+\rvert\nabla\mathbf{u}\lvert^{2}\mathbf{u},\quad\mathbf{u}(\cdot,0)=\mathbf{u}_{0},\quad\mathbf{u}(\cdot,t)_{|\partial\Omega}=(\mathbf{u}_{0})_{|\partial\Omega},\quad t\in(0,T].

(1)

This problem is obtained as the
L^{2}
gradient flow of the Dirichlet energy
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mathbf{v}|^{2}\,dx
on vector fields
\mathbf{v}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
that satisfy a pointwise unit length constraint. Unit length minimizers of this Dirichlet energy are called harmonic maps. The HMHF problem is closely related to the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation. The HMHF equation can be considered as the limit of the LLG equation where the precessional term vanishes and only damping is left
[
26
]
.
Harmonic maps, HMHF and LLG equations have numerous applications, for example, in the modeling of ferromagnetic materials or of liquid crystals, cf. e.g.
[
24
,
25
,
28
,
21
]
.
We refer to
[
32
, Chapter III, Section 6]
and references therein for a further introduction to HMHF.

There is extensive mathematical literature in which topics related to well-posedness, weak formulations, regularity, blow-up phenomena and convergence of solutions of the HMHF problem to harmonic maps are studied, cf. e.g
[
31
,
32
,
13
,
19
,
23
,
15
,
12
,
35
]
.

Early work on the development and analysis of numerical methods for HMHF or LLG problems is found in
[
8
,
7
,
28
,
9
,
2
,
3
]
. In recent years, there has been a renewed interest in the numerical analysis of methods for this problem class
[
1
,
6
,
17
,
5
]
.

In this paper we study the HMHF problem for a specific
radially symmetric
case. Assume
\Omega
is the unit disk in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and assume the solution
\mathbf{u}
to be radially symmetric. Using polar coordinates on the disk a special type of solution of (
1
) is given by

\mathbf{u}(r,\psi,t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\psi}\sin{u(r,t)}\\
\sin{\psi}\sin{u(r,t)}\\
\cos{u(r,t)}\end{pmatrix},

(2)

and the partial differential equation in (
1
) can be reduced to

u_{t}=u_{xx}+\frac{1}{x}u_{x}-\frac{\sin(2u)}{2x^{2}}\quad\text{on}~(0,1]\times[0,T],\\

(3)

for the function
u(x,t)=u(r,t)
on
[0,1]\times[0,T]
, as is shown in
[
30
, Section 2.1]
. The result in
[
11
, Lemma 2.2]
proves that if the solution
\mathbf{u}
to (
1
) is of the form (
2
), then
u
solves (
5
).
Note that due to the structure of the solution
\mathbf{u}
in (
2
) the unit length constraint is satisfied. The
partial differential equation in (
3
) has a strong nonlinearity due to the term
\sin(2u)
and has a singular behavior for
x\downarrow 0
.
The Dirichlet energy in terms of the function
\mathbf{u}
is given by

\displaystyle E(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

which by transformation into polar coordinates and using radial symmetry yields

E(\mathbf{u})=\pi\int_{0}^{1}\big(u_{x}^{2}+\frac{\sin^{2}u}{x^{2}})x\,dx=:\pi\mathcal{E}(u).

(4)

Equation (
3
) has been used in models of nematic liquid crystals
[
35
]
. More importantly,
the radially symmetric case (
3
) plays a fundamental role in the analysis of HMHF.
In the seminal works
[
12
,
11
]
the authors study finite time singularities of (
1
)
for the two-dimensional case
N=2
. In these studies the radially symmetric case (
3
)
plays a crucial role. It is shown that for this problem with initial data
u_{0}
with
|u_{0}|\leq\pi
a unique global smooth solution exists, whereas for the case with
|u_{0}(1)|>\pi
the solution blows up in finite time. The latter means that for the solution
u
the derivative at
x=0
becomes arbitrary large:
\lim_{x\downarrow 0}|u_{x}(x,t)|\to\infty
for
t\uparrow T_{\rm crit}
. In these analyses of the problem (
3
) the maximum principle is an important tool.
The work
[
12
]
has motivated further investigations of the blow-up behavior, e.g.,
[
10
,
33
]
where infinitely many solutions of (
3
) are constructed whose energy is bounded by the initial energy for all times
t
, but can increase at some
t>0
, even for a smooth initial condition.
A further related topic is the analysis of the blow up rate. Several theoretical aspects of blow up rates of solutions of (
3
) are studied in
[
34
,
4
,
29
]
.
In a series of works, Gustafson
et al.

[
16
,
18
]
investigate the well-posedness and regularity of the
m
-equivariant version of (
3
). Hocquet
[
22
]
has studied well-posedness and regularity of a stochastic version of (
3
).

There are only very few papers in which numerical aspects of (
3
) are treated. In
[
36
]
an adaptive method is presented, based on finite element discretization in space combined with a stiff ODE solver. The paper
[
20
]
treats a moving mesh ansatz for the discretization of (
3
), using finite differences in space in combination with an ODE solver for stiff systems. In both works, the authors are interested in capturing the blow up behavior of the solution with their numerical method.

We are not aware of any literature in which for a discretization of (
3
), even in the regime of smooth solutions, a rigorous error analysis is presented. Such an error analysis is the main contribution of this paper. We consider a very basic discretization of (
3
), namely a second order finite difference discretization in space combined with a semi-implicit Euler method in time. The semi-implicit Euler method results in a linear problem in each time step. We restrict to the regime of smooth solutions of the continuous problem and present an analysis based on the classical “stability plus consistency” framework. Note, however, that in the analysis of the consistency error we also have to bound the linearization error. The analyses of blow up behaviour of the continuous problem (
3
) show that there is a critical dependence of stability properties of the problem on the size of the initial condition (
|u_{0}|\leq\pi
: globally smooth solution;
|u_{0}(1)|>\pi
: finite time blow up). In view of this, it is not surprising that the key difficulty in the error analysis of the discrete scheme lies in the derivation of satisfactory stability estimates. Our discrete stability analysis uses
M
-matrix theory, which in a certain sense mimics the maximum principle arguments used in the analysis of the continuous problem, cf.
[
11
]
. A key special ingredient in our stability analysis is that besides stability estimates
\|\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}
, where
\mathbf{u}^{n}
denotes the vector of discrete approximations after
n
semi-implicit Euler time steps, we also derive estimates of the form
\|D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}
, with
D
a diagonal scaling matrix, cf. Section
3
. The latter estimates are a discrete analogon of the control of the derivative of the continuous solution at
x=0
. Based on these stability results we derive a discretization error bound that is of optimal order with respect to both the time step and the spatial mesh size, apart from a logarithmic term in the spatial mesh size. We also present discrete energy estimates that mimic the decrease of the energy (
4
) of the continuous solution.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we formulate the continuous problem and its discretization. For this discrete problem a stability analysis is presented in Section
3
. In Section
4
the stability estimates are combined with an analysis of consistency (including linearization) errors, resulting in an (almost) optimal discretization error bound, cf. Theorem
8
. In Section
5
discrete energy dissipation estimates are derived. We validate our theoretical findings in Section
6
with numerical examples which demonstrate convergence rates, stability properties and discrete energy dissipation.

2
A radially symmetric HMHF problem and its discretization

Let
I:=[0,1]
and given
u_{0}:I\to\mathbb{R}
with
u_{0}(0)=u_{0}(1)=0
, we are interested in solutions
u:I\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
, (
u=u(x,t)
) of

\begin{split}u_{t}&=u_{xx}+\frac{1}{x}u_{x}-\frac{\sin(2u)}{2x^{2}}\quad\text{on}~I\times[0,T]\\
u(\cdot,0)&=u_{0}\quad\text{on}~I,\quad u(0,t)=u(1,t)=0\quad\text{for}~t\in[0,T].\end{split}

(5)

Remark 2.1
.

In analyses of HMHF problems the case with an
in
homogeneous boundary condition
u_{0}(1)=b\neq 0
is often treated. To simplify the presentation we restrict to the case of a homogeneous boundary condition
u_{0}(1)=0
. With rather straightforward modifications our analysis can also be applied to the inhomogeneous case. In a finite difference discretization of the inhomogeneous boundary condition, the boundary data is shifted to the right hand side and the remaining discrete operator corresponds to a homogeneous boundary condition, to which our error analysis can be applied.

The linear part of the spatial differential operator in (
5
) is denoted by
Lu=-u_{xx}-\frac{1}{x}u_{x}
.
The
L^{2}
scalar product on
I
is denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)
, and we use a weighted
L^{2}
-scalar product
(f,g)_{w}:=\int_{0}^{1}xf(x)g(x)\,dx
. Note that for
v\in H^{1}_{0}(I)
:

(Lu,v)_{w}=(-u_{xx}-\frac{1}{x}u_{x},v)_{w}=(-xu_{xx}-u_{x},v)=(xu_{x},v_{x})=(u_{x},v_{x})_{w},

(6)

hence
L
is symmetric positive definite with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)_{w}
.
We introduce a basic discretization of the problem (
5
). First we explain the discretization in space, which uses standard finite differences. We use a uniform grid with mesh size
h
such that
(N+1)h=1
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
and mesh points
x_{i}=ih
,
0\leq i\leq N+1
. The second derivative
-\partial_{xx}u(x_{i})
is approximated with the finite difference
\tfrac{1}{h^{2}}\left(-u(x_{i-1})+2u(x_{i})-u(x_{i+1})\right)
and the first derivative
\partial_{x}u(x_{i})
by the central difference
\tfrac{1}{2h}\left(-u(x_{i-1}+u(x_{i+1})\right)
. We introduce the
N\times N
matrices

A:=\frac{1}{h^{2}}{\rm tridiag}(-1,2,-1),\quad B:=\frac{1}{2h}{\rm tridiag}(-1,0,1),\quad D:={\rm diag}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),

and
C:=A-D^{-1}B
. Note that
C
represents a discretization of the linear differential operator
L
.
For the nonlinear term we introduce, for given
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
the diagonal matrix

G(\mathbf{u}):={\rm diag}\Big(\frac{\sin(2u_{1})}{2u_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\sin(2u_{N})}{2u_{N}}\Big).

For discretization in time of we use a fixed time step
\Delta t=\frac{T}{M}
,
M\in\mathbb{N}
. The nonlinear term in (
5
) can be written as
\frac{\sin(2u)}{2x^{2}}=\frac{\sin(2u)}{2u}\frac{u}{x^{2}}
. For the first factor, which is nonlinear, we have
\left|\frac{\sin(2u)}{2u}\right|\in[0,1]
, and the second factor, which is linear, may blow up for
x\downarrow 0
. Due to the parabolic nature of the PDE it is natural to use an implicit time stepping scheme and the two factor splitting of the nonlinear term suggests an semi-implicit linearization.
This motivates the following basic discretization method for (
5
):

\frac{\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}}{\Delta t}=-C\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2}\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\quad 0\leq n\leq M-1,

(7)

with
\mathbf{u}^{0}:=(u_{0}(x_{1}),\ldots u_{0}(x_{N}))^{T}
. Note that we use a simple first order linearization for the nonlinear term in (
5
).
Well-posedness of this scheme will be discussed below.

3
Stability estimates

A key point in the analysis are discrete stability bounds, not only for the approximation
\mathbf{u}^{n}
of
u(\cdot,t_{n})
but also for
D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{n}
with
\alpha\in(0,1]
, which are approximations of
x^{-\alpha}u(\cdot,t_{n})
.

Remark 3.1
.

In a certain sense boundedness of
\|D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}
, uniformly in
h
and
n
, mimics the “smoothness” of the solution
u(0,t_{n})
of the continuous problem. This smoothness in particular means that
|u_{x}(0,t)|
is bounded (“the derivative at 0 does not blow up”). Using
u(0,t)=0
we have
u_{x}(0,t)=\frac{1}{x}u(x,t)+\mathcal{O}(x)
(
x\to 0
). Hence boundednes of
|\frac{1}{x}u(x,t)|
close to 0 is equivalent to boundedness of
|u_{x}(0,t)|
. The discrete analogon of this is the boundedness of
\|D^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}
. For

\alpha\in(0,1)
we have a discrete analogon of Hölder continuity (with exponent
\alpha
) at
x=0
.

The stability analysis is based on
M
-matrix theory. We recall a few basic results from that theory. Define

Z_{N}:=\left\{\,A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}~|~a_{ij}\leq 0\quad\text{for all}~i\neq j\,\right\}.

A matrix
A
is called an
M
-matrix if
A\in Z_{n}
and
\det(A)\neq 0
,
A^{-1}\geq 0
(inequality elementwise).
A matrix
A
is called weakly diagonally dominant if
\sum_{j\neq i}|a_{ij}|\leq|a_{ii}|
for all
i
. We use the notation
\mathbf{1}:=(1,\ldots,1)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Furthermore, for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
|\mathbf{x}|:=(|x_{1}|,\ldots,|x_{N}|)^{T}
. From the literature we have the following result (Fiedler Thm. 5.1).

Lemma 1
.

A matrix
A\in Z_{N}
is an
M
-matrix if and only if there exists
\mathbf{x}>0
such that
A\mathbf{x}>0
.

Using this we obtain the following.

Lemma 2
.

If
A\in Z_{N}
is weakly diagonally dominant and
a_{ii}\geq 0
for all
i
, then for all
\delta\geq 0
we have
\det(I+\delta A)\neq 0
and

\|(I+\delta A)^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

Proof.

If
A\in Z_{N}
, then
B:=I+\delta A\in Z_{N}
. From the weak diagonal dominance and
a_{ii}\geq 0
we get
1+\delta\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}\geq 1
for all
i
. Hence,
B\mathbf{1}\geq\mathbf{1}
holds. From Lemma
1
we conclude that
B
is an
M
-matrix, hence
B
invertible and
B^{-1}\geq 0
, which implies that
\|B^{-1}\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|(B^{-1})_{ij}\right|=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(B^{-1})_{ij}=\|B^{-1}\mathbf{1}\|_{\infty}
holds. Finally note that
0\leq B^{-1}\mathbf{1}\leq B^{-1}B\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
holds.
∎

Using this result a stability analysis of the scheme (
7
) is very straightforward. Note that for the matrix
C
we have

\displaystyle-C

\displaystyle={\rm tridiag}(\gamma_{i},\delta_{i},\beta_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}\quad\text{with}

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{h^{2}}(1-\tfrac{1}{2i}),\quad 2\leq i\leq N,

\displaystyle\delta_{i}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{2}{h^{2}},\quad 1\leq i\leq N,

\displaystyle\beta_{i}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{h^{2}}(1+\tfrac{1}{2i}),\quad 1\leq i\leq N-1.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathbf{u}^{0}
be such that
\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
holds. Then the scheme (
7
) is well-defined and
\|\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}
for all
n
holds.

Proof.

We use induction. Assumme
\|\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
holds. The matrix
C
is weakly diagonally dominant,
c_{ii}\geq 0
for all
i
and
C\in Z_{N}
. The matrix
G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2}
is diagonal with positive diagonal entries. It follows that the matrix
M:=C+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2}
is weakly diagonally dominant,
m_{ii}\geq 0
for all
i
and
M\in Z_{N}
. From Lemma
2
it follows that
I+\Delta tM
is invertible and
\|(I+\Delta tM)^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
holds. Thus in (
7
) we have

\|\mathbf{u}^{n+1}\|_{\infty}=\|(I+\Delta tM)^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

We need the condition
\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, whereas for the continuous problem, for existence of a smooth solution one needs
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq\pi
. We were not able to bridge the gap between
\frac{\pi}{2}
and
\pi
.

Assumption 3.1
.

In the remainder we assume that
\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
holds.

Clearly, for the discretization error analysis we need to control the nonlinearity in the term
G(\mathbf{u}^{n})
. Note that in (
7
) this term is multiplied by
D^{-2}
(with
\|D^{-2}\|_{\infty}=h^{-2}
). It turns out, that for satisfactory error bounds we need control of
\|D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}
, with
\alpha>\tfrac{1}{2}
.

For the control of
\|D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}
we use a stability estimate for the matrix
D^{-\alpha}CD^{\alpha}
. For this we first note the following. The matrix
C
is a discretization of the differential operator
Lu:=-u_{xx}-\tfrac{1}{x}u_{x}
. As simple computation yields

x^{2-\alpha}L(x^{\alpha})=-\alpha^{2}\quad\text{for all}~\alpha\in[0,1].

(8)

A discrete analogon of this property also holds.

Lemma 4
.

The following holds:

D^{2-\alpha}CD^{\alpha}\mathbf{1}\geq-\alpha^{2}\mathbf{1}\quad\text{for all}~\alpha\in[0,1].

Proof.

Using
D^{\alpha}\mathbf{1}=(x_{1}^{\alpha},\ldots,x_{N}^{\alpha})^{T}\geq 0
,
-C={\rm tridiag}(\gamma_{i},\delta_{i},\beta_{i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
and with
\gamma_{1}:=\frac{1}{h^{2}}(1-\frac{1}{2})
,
\beta_{N}:=\frac{1}{h^{2}}(1+\frac{1}{2N})
we obtain, for
1\leq i\leq N
:

\begin{split}\big(-D^{2-\alpha}CD^{\alpha}\mathbf{1}\big)_{i}&\leq x_{i}^{2-\alpha}\big(\gamma_{i}x_{i-1}^{\alpha}+\delta_{i}x_{i}^{\alpha}+\beta_{i}x_{i+1}^{\alpha}\big)\\
&=x_{i}^{2}\big(\gamma_{i}(\frac{x_{i-1}}{x_{i}})^{\alpha}+\delta_{i}+\beta_{i}(\frac{x_{i+1}}{x_{i}})^{\alpha}\big)\\
&=i^{2}\big((1-\tfrac{1}{2i})(1-\tfrac{1}{i})^{\alpha}-2+(1+\tfrac{1}{2i})(1+\tfrac{1}{i})^{\alpha}\big)=f(\tfrac{1}{i}),\end{split}

(9)

with

\begin{split}f(y)&:=\tfrac{1}{y^{2}}\big((1-\tfrac{1}{2}y)(1-y)^{\alpha}-2+(1+\tfrac{1}{2}y)(1+y)^{\alpha}\big)\\
&=\tfrac{1}{y^{2}}\big((1+y)^{\alpha}+(1-y)^{\alpha}-2+\tfrac{1}{2}y((1+y)^{\alpha}-(1-y)^{\alpha})\big),\quad y\in(0,1].\end{split}

(10)

For
|y|<1
we have the convergent series

\displaystyle(1+y)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=1+\alpha y+\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2!}y^{2}+\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)}{3!}y^{3}+\ldots

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}y^{j},\quad c_{0}=1,\quad c_{j}=\tfrac{1}{j!}\Pi_{m=0}^{j-1}(\alpha-m)\quad j\geq 1.

This yields

\begin{split}(1+y)^{\alpha}+(1-y)^{\alpha}-2&=2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{2j}y^{2j}=2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{2j+2}y^{2j+2}\\
\tfrac{1}{2}y\big((1+y)^{\alpha}-(1-y)^{\alpha}\big)&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{2j+1}y^{2j+2}.\end{split}

Using this in (
10
) we obtain

f(y)=\frac{1}{y^{2}}\left(2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{2j+2}y^{2j+2}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{2j+1}y^{2j+2}\right)=\alpha^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(2c_{2j+2}+c_{2j+1})y^{2j}.

Now note, using
\alpha\in[0,1]
,

2c_{2j+2}+c_{2j+1}=\frac{1}{(2j+1)!}\frac{\alpha-j}{j+1}\Pi_{m=0}^{2j}(\alpha-m)\leq 0\quad\text{for all}~j\geq 1,

which implies
f(y)\leq\alpha^{2}
for all
y\in(0,1)
. Due to continuity this also holds for
y=1
. Using this in (
9
) completes the proof.
∎

We introduce the notation
\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{n}:=D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{n}
.
From (
7
) we obtain

(I+\Delta t(D^{-\alpha}CD^{\alpha}+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2}))\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{n+1}=\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{n},\quad n\geq 0,~\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{0}=D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{0}.

(11)

The matrix
D^{-\alpha}CD^{\alpha}
is an
M
-matrix, but
not
weakly diagonally dominant. We use the property of Lemma
4
to derive a stability result for
(\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{n})_{n\geq 0}
.

Lemma 5
.

Take
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Let
c_{\alpha}\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]
be such that
\frac{\sin(2c_{\alpha})}{2c_{\alpha}}=\alpha^{2}
. Assume
\mathbf{u}^{0}
satisfies
\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}\leq c_{\alpha}
. Then the following holds:

\|\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{0}\|_{\infty}\quad\text{for all}~n\geq 0.

Proof.

We use similar arguments as in the proof of Lemma
3
. Take
\mathbf{u}^{0}
such that
\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}\leq c_{\alpha}
. From Lemma
3
we then have
\|\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq c_{\alpha}
for all
n
. Since
\frac{\sin(2y)}{2y}\geq\alpha^{2}
for all
y\in[-c_{\alpha},c_{\alpha}]
it follows that

G(\mathbf{u}^{n})\geq\alpha^{2}I
. Hence,
M_{\alpha}:=D^{-\alpha}CD^{\alpha}+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2}\geq D^{-\alpha}CD^{\alpha}+\alpha^{2}D^{-2}
. Note that
M_{\alpha}\in Z_{N}
and
(M_{\alpha})_{ii}\geq 0
holds. Using Lemma
4
we get
M_{\alpha}\mathbf{1}\geq D^{-2}(D^{2-\alpha}CD^{\alpha}\mathbf{1}+\alpha^{2}\mathbf{1})\geq 0
, and thus
M_{\alpha}
is weakly diagonally dominant. From Lemma
2
it follows that
\|(I+\Delta tM_{\alpha})^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
holds. Thus in (
11
) we have

\|\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{n+1}\|_{\infty}=\|(I+\Delta tM_{\alpha})^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}^{0}\|_{\infty}.

∎

Note that the result above can not be extended to
\alpha=1
.

Besides the maximum norm we use two other vector norms in the error analysis below. The Euclidean scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the corresponding scaled norm by
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2,h}^{2}:=h\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle=h\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}^{2}
. We also use the diagonally scaled Euclidean norm
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{D,h}:=\|D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{v}\|_{2,h}
, i.e.,
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{D,h}^{2}=h\langle D\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}\rangle
. The matrix norm corresponding to
\|\cdot\|_{2,h}
is denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. Note that the diagonally weighted scalar product is natural in the sense that
C
is symmetric with respect to this scalar product. It is the discrete analogon of the weighted
L^{2}
scalar product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{w}
used in (
6
).

One easily checks that the matrix
DC
is symmetric, which is the discrete analogon of the symmetry, in the
L^{2}
scalar product of
u\to-xu_{xx}-u_{x}
. We finally derive a stability result in the Euclidean norm.

Lemma 6
.

The following holds

\|\big(I+\Delta t(D^{\frac{1}{2}}CD^{-\frac{1}{2}}+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2})\big)^{-1}\|_{2}\leq 1.

(12)

Proof.

The matrix
C+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2}
is an
M
-matrix, cf. proof of Lemma
3
. From this and the symmetry of
DC
we get that
D\big(C+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2})=DC+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-1}
is a symmetric
M
-matrix. Thus this matrix is positive definite, cf.

[
14
, Theorem 5.1]
.
Hence
D^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(DC+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-1}\big)D^{-\frac{1}{2}}=D^{\frac{1}{2}}CD^{-\frac{1}{2}}+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2}
is symmetric positive definite. From this it follows that
\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbf{x}\neq 0}\frac{\langle(I+\Delta t(D^{\frac{1}{2}}CD^{-\frac{1}{2}}+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2}))\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle}\geq 1
holds, which implies the estimate (
12
).
∎

4
Discretization error bounds

For linearization we use the following elementary estimate.

Lemma 7
.

Define
g(y):=\frac{\sin(2y)}{2y}
,
y\neq 0
,
g(0)=1
. The following holds:

|g(y)-g(z)|\leq\tfrac{4}{3}\max\{|y|,|z|\}|y-z|\quad\text{for all}~y,z\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

Elementary computation yields
|g^{\prime}(y)|\leq\tfrac{4}{3}|y|
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus we obtain

\begin{split}|g(y)-g(z)|&=\left|\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(z+t(y-z))\,dt(y-z)\right|\leq\tfrac{4}{3}\int_{0}^{1}|z+t(y-z)|\,dt|y-z|\\
&\leq\tfrac{4}{3}\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)|z|+t|y|\,dt|y-z|\leq\tfrac{4}{3}\max\{|y|,|z|\}|y-z|.\end{split}

∎

For a sufficiently smooth function
v(x,t)
,
x\in I
,
t\in[0,T]
we introduce a corresponding grid function
\mathbf{v}(t):=\left(v(x_{1},t),\ldots,v(x_{N},t)\right)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The grid functions for
v_{t}(\cdot,t)
,
v_{x}(\cdot,t)
,
v_{xx}(\cdot,t)
are denoted by
\mathbf{v}_{t}(t)
,
\mathbf{v}_{x}(t)
and
\mathbf{v}_{xx}(t)
, respectively. Let
u
be the solution of (
5
), which is assumed to be sufficiently smooth. From Taylor expansion we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{t}(t_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})-\mathbf{u}(t_{n})}{\Delta t}+\Delta t\,\mathbf{e}_{\partial t}(t_{n+1}),\quad\mathbf{e}_{\partial t}(t_{n+1})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{tt}(x_{i},\xi_{n+1})\right)_{1\leq i\leq N},

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{x}(t_{n+1})

\displaystyle=B\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})+h^{2}\mathbf{e}_{\partial x}(t_{n+1}),\quad\mathbf{e}_{\partial x}(t_{n+1})=-\tfrac{1}{6}\left(\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial x^{3}}(\xi_{i},t_{n+1})\right)_{1\leq i\leq N},

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{xx}(t_{n+1})

\displaystyle=-A\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})+h^{2}\mathbf{e}_{\partial^{2}x}(t_{n+1}),~\mathbf{e}_{\partial^{2}x}(t_{n+1})=-\tfrac{1}{12}\left(\frac{\partial^{4}u}{\partial x^{4}}(\eta_{i},t_{n+1})\right)_{1\leq i\leq N}.

Thus we get

\mathbf{u}_{t}(t_{n+1})=\mathbf{u}_{xx}(t_{n+1})+D^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{x}(t_{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1}))D^{-2}\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})

and

\begin{split}\frac{\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})-\mathbf{u}(t_{n})}{\Delta t}&=-C\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1}))D^{-2}\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})\\
&\quad-\Delta t\,\mathbf{e}_{\partial t}(t_{n+1})+h^{2}\mathbf{e}_{\partial^{2}x}(t_{n+1})+h^{2}D^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{\partial x}(t_{n+1}).\end{split}

(13)

The discretization error is denoted by
\mathbf{e}^{n}:=\mathbf{u}(t_{n})-\mathbf{u}^{n}
, with
\mathbf{u}^{n}
the solution of (
7
). We then obtain the following recursion for the discretization error, with
\mathbf{e}^{0}:=0
:

\begin{split}\frac{\mathbf{e}^{n+1}-\mathbf{e}^{n}}{\Delta t}&=-C\mathbf{e}^{n+1}-\left(G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1}))D^{-2}\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2}\mathbf{u}^{n+1}\right)\\
&\quad-\Delta t\,\mathbf{e}_{\partial t}(t_{n+1})+h^{2}\mathbf{e}_{\partial^{2}x}(t_{n+1})+h^{2}D^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{\partial x}(t_{n+1}),\quad 0\leq n\leq M-1.\end{split}

(14)

In
[
11
]
it is shown that if the initial condition satisfies
u_{0}\in C^{1+\gamma}(I)
for some
\gamma\geq 0
and
\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\pi
, then the solution
u
of (
5
) has regularity
u\in C^{\gamma}(I\times[0,T)])\cap C^{2+\gamma}(I\times(0,T))
. In the main theorem below, we assume the initial condition
u_{0}
to be smooth enough such that the
L^{\infty}
norms of derivatives of
u
that appear are finite.

Theorem 8
.

Take
\alpha\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
and assume
\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}\leq c_{\alpha}
, with
c_{\alpha}
as in Lemma
5
.
Define
d_{\alpha}:=\|D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}
.
For the error
\mathbf{e}^{n}
the following holds:

\|\mathbf{e}^{n}\|_{D,h}\leq c\big(\Delta t+h^{2}|\ln h|^{\frac{1}{2}}),\quad 1\leq n\leq M,

(15)

with a constant
c
that depends only on
\|\frac{\partial^{j}u}{\partial x^{j}}\|_{L^{\infty}(I\times[0,T])}
,
0\leq j\leq 4
,
\|\frac{\partial^{j}u}{\partial t^{j}}\|_{L^{\infty}(I\times[0,T])}
,
0\leq j\leq 2
,
T
,
\alpha
,
c_{\alpha}
and
d_{\alpha}
. The dependence of
c
on these quantities can be deduced from the proof.

Proof.

We use
c
for a (varying) constant that depends only on the quantities mentioned above.
From (
14
) we obtain, after multiplication with
D^{\frac{1}{2}}

\begin{split}&(I+\Delta tD^{\frac{1}{2}}CD^{-\frac{1}{2}})D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}^{n+1}\\
&=D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}^{n}-\Delta t\,E-(\Delta t)^{2}\,D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial t}(t_{n+1})+\Delta t\,h^{2}D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial^{2}x}(t_{n+1})+\Delta t\,h^{2}D^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial x}(t_{n+1}),\\
&E:=\left(G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1}))D^{-\frac{3}{2}}\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-\frac{3}{2}}\mathbf{u}^{n+1}\right).\end{split}

For linearization of the term
E
we use the splitting

\begin{split}E&=G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1}))D^{-\frac{3}{2}}\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n}))D^{-\frac{3}{2}}\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})\\
&+G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n}))D^{-\frac{3}{2}}\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-\frac{3}{2}}\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})\\
&+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-\frac{3}{2}}\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-\frac{3}{2}}\mathbf{u}^{n+1}=:E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3}.\end{split}

(16)

The term
E_{3}
is shifted to the left hand side. We thus get

\begin{split}&\big(I+\Delta t(D^{\frac{1}{2}}CD^{-\frac{1}{2}}+G(\mathbf{u}^{n})D^{-2})\big)D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}^{n+1}\\
&=D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}^{n}-\Delta t\,(E_{1}+E_{2})\\
&-(\Delta t)^{2}\,D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial t}(t_{n+1})+\Delta t\,h^{2}D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial^{2}x}(t_{n+1})+\Delta t\,h^{2}D^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial x}(t_{n+1}).\end{split}

(17)

We consider the term
E_{1}
. Note that the
\|\cdot\|_{2,h}
matrix norm is scaling invariant. Replacing it by
\|\cdot\|_{2}
we obtain

\|E_{1}\|_{2,h}\leq\|\big(G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1}))-G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n}))\big)D^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}\|D^{-1}\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1})\|_{2,h}.

Note that
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{D,h}\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2,h}\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}
holds for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
For the continuous solution
\mathbf{u}(t_{n})
we have

\begin{split}\|D^{-1}\mathbf{u}(t_{n})\|_{\infty}&=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\left|\frac{u(x_{i},t_{n})}{x_{i}}\right|\\
&=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{1}{x_{i}}\left|\int_{0}^{x_{i}}u_{x}(s,t_{n})\,ds\right|\leq\|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\|_{L^{\infty}(I\times[0,T])}=c_{1}.\end{split}

(18)

Using
x_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\leq x_{i}^{-1}
, Lemma
7
and (
18
) we obtain

\begin{split}&\|\big(G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n+1}))-G(\mathbf{u}(t_{n}))\big)D^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\frac{1}{x_{i}}|g(u(x_{i},t_{n+1}))-g(u(x_{i},t_{n}))|\\
&\leq\tfrac{4}{3}c_{1}\max_{1\leq i\leq N}|u(x_{i},t_{n+1})-u(x_{i},t_{n})|\\
&\leq\tfrac{4}{3}c_{1}\Delta t\|u_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(I\times[0,T])}=c\Delta t.\end{split}

Using this and (
18
) yields

\|E_{1}\|_{2,h}\leq c\,\Delta t.

(19)

For the term
E_{2}
we note, for
1\leq i\leq N
,

\begin{split}|(E_{2})_{i}|&=\left|\big(g(u(x_{i},t_{n}))-g(u_{i}^{n})\big)\big)x_{i}^{-\frac{3}{2}}u(x_{i},t_{n+1})\right|\\
&\leq\tfrac{4}{3}\max\{|u(x_{i},t_{n})|,|u_{i}^{n}|\}|e_{i}^{n}|x_{i}^{-\frac{3}{2}}|u(x_{i},t_{n+1})|\\
&=\tfrac{4}{3}\max\{|u(x_{i},t_{n})|,|u_{i}^{n}|\}x_{i}^{-\alpha}|e_{i}^{n}|x_{i}^{\alpha-\frac{3}{2}}|u(x_{i},t_{n+1})|\end{split}

Using
x_{i}^{-\alpha}|u(x_{i},t_{n})|\leq x_{i}^{-1}|u(x_{i},t_{n})|\leq c_{1}
and

x_{i}^{-\alpha}|u_{i}^{n}|\leq\|D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}=d_{\alpha},

(20)

cf. Lemma
5
, this yields

|(E_{2})_{i}|\leq\tfrac{4}{3}\max\{c_{1},d_{\alpha}\}|x_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}e_{i}^{n}||x_{i}^{\alpha-2}u(x_{i},t_{n+1})|.

With Cauchy-Schwarz we thus get

\|E_{2}\|_{2,h}\leq\tfrac{4}{3}\max\{c_{1},d_{\alpha}\}\|\mathbf{e}^{n}\|_{D,h}\left(h\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2\alpha-4}u(x_{i},t_{n+1})^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(21)

Using
\max_{i}|\frac{u(x_{i},t_{n+1})}{x_{i}}|\leq c_{1}
and
x_{i}=ih
we obtain for the expression above

\displaystyle\left(h\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2\alpha-4}u(x_{i},t_{n+1})^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq c_{1}\left(h\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2(\alpha-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=c_{1}\left(h^{2\alpha-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}i^{2(\alpha-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\left(h^{2\alpha-1}\big(1+\int_{1}^{N}z^{2(\alpha-1)}\,dz\big)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{2\alpha-1}}.

Summarizin*g we obtain

\|E_{2}\|_{2,h}\leq c\|\mathbf{e}^{n}\|_{D,h},\quad\text{with}~c=\tfrac{4}{3}\max\{c_{1},d_{\alpha}\}\frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{2\alpha-1}}.

(22)

Using the results (
19
) and (
22
) in (
17
) and with the stability estimate of Lemma
6
we thus get

\begin{split}&\|\mathbf{e}^{n+1}\|_{D,h}\leq(1+c\Delta t)\|\mathbf{e}^{n}\|_{D,h}+c\,(\Delta t)^{2}\\
&+(\Delta t)^{2}\|D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial t}(t_{n+1})\|_{2,h}+\Delta t\,h^{2}\|D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial^{2}x}(t_{n+1})\|_{2,h}+\Delta t\,h^{2}\|D^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial x}(t_{n+1})\|_{2,h}.\end{split}

(23)

We finally estimate the three terms in the second line of (
23
). We have

\|D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial t}(t_{n+1})\|_{2,h}\leq\|\mathbf{e}_{\partial t}(t_{n+1})\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}\|_{L^{\infty}(I\times[0,T])},

and

\|D^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial^{2}x}(t_{n+1})\|_{2,h}\leq\|\mathbf{e}_{\partial^{2}x}(t_{n+1})\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{12}\|\frac{\partial^{4}u}{\partial x^{4}}\|_{L^{\infty}(I\times[0,T])}.

In the third term there is a scaling with
D^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. For this term we obtain, using
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{i}\leq 1+\int_{1}^{N}\frac{1}{x}\,dx=1+\ln N\leq 1+|\ln h|\leq c|\ln h|
:

\begin{split}\|D^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{e}_{\partial x}(t_{n+1})\|_{2,h}&=\frac{1}{6}\left(h\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{x_{i}}\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial x^{3}}(\xi_{i},t_{n+1})^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{6}\|\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial x^{3}}\|_{L^{\infty}(I\times[0,T])}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{i}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq c\|\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial x^{3}}\|_{L^{\infty}(I\times[0,T])}|\ln h|^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

Using these estimates in (
23
) we obtain

\|\mathbf{e}^{n+1}\|_{D,h}\leq(1+c\Delta t)\|\mathbf{e}^{n}\|_{D,h}+c\,\Delta t\big(\Delta t+h^{2}|\ln h|^{\frac{1}{2}}\big).

A standard recursive argument, using
(1+c\Delta t)^{M}\leq e^{cT}
and
\mathbf{e}^{0}=0
completes the proof.
∎

We comment on the result presented in Theorem
8
. First note that
d_{\alpha}=\|D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}x_{i}^{-\alpha}|u_{0}(x_{i})|
essentially measures the smoothness of
u_{0}
close to 0, cf. Remark
3.1
.
Furthermore, if
u_{0}\in C^{1}(I)
then
d_{\alpha}
is uniformly bounded in
\alpha
:

d_{\alpha}\leq\|(u_{0})_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\quad\text{for all}~\alpha\in[0,1].

(24)

The bound in (
15
) is optimal with respect to the order of convergence in
\Delta t
and in
h
, apart from the factor
|\ln h|^{\frac{1}{2}}
. To derive this bound it is essential (in our analysis) to take
\alpha>\tfrac{1}{2}
. For an explanation of this, consider the error propagation relation (
17
). For deriving the estimates for the local truncation errors in the last line of (
17
) we do
not
need
\alpha>\tfrac{1}{2}
to hold. The remaining two terms
E_{1}
,
E_{2}
in (
17
) are part of the linearization error. For deriving the bound (
19
) for the term
E_{1}
we do
not
need
\alpha>\tfrac{1}{2}
to hold. To be able to control the term
E_{2}
, however, we do need the condition
\alpha>\tfrac{1}{2}
to be satisfied, cf. (
22
). With respect to the choice of
\alpha\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
we note the following. The constant
c
in (

) becomes smaller if we take a larger
\alpha
, cf. (

). The function
\alpha\to c_{\alpha}
is monotonically decreasing on

with

and thus the condition
\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}\leq c_{\alpha}
becomes more severe for increasing

.

5
Discrete energy estimates

Recall that the Dirichlet energy of the HMHF problem in terms of
u
is given by, cf. (
4
) and (
6
),

\mathcal{E}(u)=(Lu,u)_{w}+\big(\frac{\sin u}{x},\sin u\big)=(xLu,u)+\big(\frac{\sin u}{x},\sin u\big).

The matrix
C
is the finite difference approximation of the differential operator
L
. The matrix
DC
is symmetric positive definite, which is the discrete analogon of the fact that
L
is symmetric positive definite w.r.t
(\cdot,\cdot)_{w}
, cf. (
6
). We introduce the
discrete Dirichlet energy
functional

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}_{h}(\mathbf{u})&:=h\langle DC\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\rangle+h\langle D^{-1}F(\mathbf{u}),F(\mathbf{u})\rangle\\
\text{with}~~F(\mathbf{u})&:=\left(\sin(u_{1}),\sin(u_{2}),\ldots,\sin(u_{N})\right)^{T}.\end{split}

(25)

In this section we analyze energy dissipation of the discrete scheme (
7
) with respect to
\mathcal{E}_{h}(\mathbf{u})
.
For this it is convenient to rewrite (
7
) as

\frac{\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}}{\Delta t}=-C\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})D^{-2}\mathbf{u}^{n+1}+\left(G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})\right)D^{-2}\mathbf{u}^{n+1}.

(26)

Lemma 9
.

Take
\alpha\in[0,1)
and assume
\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\min\{\frac{\pi}{4},c_{\alpha}\}
, with
c_{\alpha}
as in Lemma
5
.
Define
d_{\alpha}:=\|D^{-\alpha}\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}
.
Let
(\mathbf{u}^{n})_{0\leq n\leq M}
solve (
7
). The follwing holds for
0\leq n\leq M-1
, and with
c
the constant in the estimate (
15
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{h}(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{h}(\mathbf{u}^{n}),\quad\text{if}~~\Delta t\leq\tfrac{3}{4}d_{\alpha}^{-2}h^{2(1-\alpha)}~\text{holds.}

(27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{h}(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{h}(\mathbf{u}^{n})+\tfrac{64}{3}c^{2}d_{\alpha}^{2}\big(h^{2(\alpha-1)}(\Delta t)^{2}+h^{2(\alpha+1)}|\ln h|\big),\quad\text{if}~~\alpha>\tfrac{1}{2}~\text{holds.}

(28)

Proof.

We take the
h
-weighted scalar product of (
26
) with
D(\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n})
. This yields

\begin{split}&\frac{h}{\Delta t}\langle D(\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}),\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle+h\langle DC\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle\\
&+h\langle D^{-1}G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle\\
&=h\langle\left(G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})\right)D^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle.\end{split}

Using

\displaystyle\langle DC\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\langle DC\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}\rangle-\langle DC\mathbf{u}^{n},\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}\langle DC(\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}),\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta t}\|\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{D,h}^{2}+\frac{h}{2}\langle DC\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}\rangle+h\langle D^{-1}G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h}{2}\langle DC\mathbf{u}^{n},\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle+h\langle\left(G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})\right)D^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle.

(29)

To control the term
\langle D^{-1}G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle
we use a convexity argument.
The function
f(y)=\sin^{2}(y)
is convex on
[-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}]
. Hence, for all
x,y\in[-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}]
we have
f(x)\geq f(y)+f^{\prime}(y)(x-y)
.
From
\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\pi/4
and Lemma
3
, we have
\|\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\pi/4
for all
n=0,\ldots,M
. Thus we get

\sin^{2}(u^{n}_{i})\geq\sin^{2}(u^{n+1}_{i})+\sin(2u_{i}^{n+1})\left(u_{i}^{n}-u_{i}^{n+1}\right),

and

\displaystyle\langle D^{-1}G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{x_{i}}\frac{\sin(2u_{i}^{n+1})}{2}(u_{i}^{n+1}-u_{i}^{n})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2x_{i}}\left(\sin^{2}(u_{i}^{n+1})-\sin^{2}(u_{i}^{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\langle D^{-1}F(\mathbf{u}^{n+1}),F(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})\rangle-\langle D^{-1}F(\mathbf{u}^{n}),F(\mathbf{u}^{n})\rangle\right).

Using this in (
29
) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\Delta t}\|\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{D,h}^{2}+\mathcal{E}_{h}(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{h}(\mathbf{u}^{n})+2h\langle\left(G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})\right)D^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle.

(30)

It remains to control the last term in (
30
). Note:

\displaystyle h\langle\left(G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})\right)D^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=h\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(g(u_{i}^{n+1})-g(u_{i}^{n})\big)x_{i}^{-1}u_{i}^{n+1}(u_{i}^{n+1}-u_{i}^{n}).

Recall that
x_{i}^{-\alpha}|u_{i}^{n}|\leq d_{\alpha}
holds for all
n
, cf. (
20
). Using this, Lemma
7
and
x_{i}^{2(\alpha-1)}\leq h^{2(\alpha-1)}
we obtain

\displaystyle h\langle\left(G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})\right)D^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle\leq\tfrac{4}{3}d_{\alpha}^{2}h\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2\alpha-1}|u_{i}^{n+1}-u_{i}^{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{4}{3}d_{\alpha}^{2}h\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2(\alpha-1)}x_{i}|u_{i}^{n+1}-u_{i}^{n}|^{2}\leq\tfrac{4}{3}d_{\alpha}^{2}h^{2(\alpha-1)}\|\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{D,h}^{2}.

We consider two ways to proceed. In the first approach we assume that
\frac{4}{3}d_{\alpha}^{2}h^{2(\alpha-1)}\leq\frac{1}{\Delta t}
holds. Using this we observe that the last term in (
30
) can be absorbed in the term on the left hand side in (
30
). This proves the result (
27
). The second possibility is to assume
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
and use the result of Theorem
8
, i.e.,
\|\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\|_{D,h}\leq 2c(\Delta t+h^{2}|\ln h|^{\frac{1}{2}})
and thus

h\langle\left(G(\mathbf{u}^{n+1})-G(\mathbf{u}^{n})\right)D^{-1}\mathbf{u}^{n+1},\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\rangle\leq\tfrac{32}{3}d_{\alpha}^{2}h^{2(\alpha-1)}c^{2}\big((\Delta t)^{2}+h^{4}|\ln h|\big).

Using this in (
30
) we obtain the estimate in (
28
).
∎

We discuss the results (
27
) and (
28
). In (
27
) we have monotone discrete energy dissipation under the condition
\Delta t\leq\tfrac{3}{4}d_{\alpha}^{-2}h^{2(1-\alpha)}
. We make the reasonable assumption that
d_{\alpha}
is uniformly bounded, cf. (
24
). Hence, the condition is of the form
\Delta t\leq Ch^{2(1-\alpha)}
. Assume a scaling property
\Delta t=h^{\beta}
with
\beta>0
. Then this condition is satisfied iff
\beta>2(1-\alpha)
. For example, for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
we require (only)
\Delta t\sim h^{\beta}
with
\beta>1
. In (
28
) we have a perturbed energy dissipation. The perturbation is of the form
C\big(h^{2(\alpha-1)}(\Delta t)^{2}+h^{2(\alpha+1)}|\ln h|\big)
. The term
h^{2(\alpha+1)}|\ln h|
tends to
0
for
h\downarrow 0
. For the other term to go to zero we need a condition that bounds
\Delta t
in terms of
h
. Consider again the scaling
\Delta t=h^{\beta}
. Then
h^{2(\alpha-1)}(\Delta t)^{2}\to 0
for
h\downarrow 0
iff
\beta>1-\alpha
, which is a weaker condition than the one used in (
27
).

Finally note that for
\alpha=0
, i.e., under the weakest assumption on the initial condition, namely
\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}\leq c_{0}=\frac{\pi}{2}
, we still have a monotone discrete energy dissipation as in (
27
), provided
\Delta t\leq\tfrac{3}{4}\|\mathbf{u}^{0}\|_{\infty}^{-2}h^{2}
is satisfied.
