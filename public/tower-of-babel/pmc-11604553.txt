Staged Fixation with Respect to Soft Tissue in Tibial Plateau Fractures with Acute Compartment Syndrome: Correlation Analysis of Complications
Authors: Yong-Cheol Yoon, Ye Joon Kim, Chang-Wug Oh, Hee-June Kim, Seung-Bo Sim, Sang-Woo Son, Joon-Woo Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroudStaged operations are commonly employed in the management of high-energy tibial plateau fractures (TPF) complicated by acute compartment syndrome (ACS); however, complications, such as nonunion, deep wound infection, and traumatic arthritis, often occur due to severe bone and soft-tissue damage. We aimed to report the radiological and clinical outcomes of staged surgical interventions performed following complete closure of the fasciotomy wound for the treatment of TPF complicated by ACS. Additionally, we analyzed factors associated with complications arising from these procedures.MethodsThirty patients with TPF and ACS were included (23 men and 7 women; average age, 59.7 years). The mean follow-up period was 33.2 months (range, 12–85 months). An external fixator was initially applied with emergency fasciotomy, and open reduction and plate fixation were performed after complete closure of the fasciotomy wound and soft-tissue stabilization (mean, 31 days; range, 9–55 days). Radiological evaluation of bone union and alignment was conducted, functional evaluation of the knee and ankle joints was performed using the Knee Society and American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) scores, and complications and related factors were analyzed.ResultsPrimary bone union was achieved in 29 of the 30 cases (96.7%) at an average of 20.8 weeks (range, 12–35 weeks). Malalignment was not observed in any case. At the final follow-up examination, the mean Knee Society and AOFAS scores were 92.5 (range, 65–100) and 95.5 (range, 74–100), respectively. Complications included 1 case of nonunion (3.3%), 2 cases of deep wound infection (6.7%), and 5 cases of traumatic arthritis (16.7%). A statistically significant correlation was noted between complications and patients who underwent dual approaches for the fixation of bicondylar TPFs.ConclusionsA staged operation coupled with sufficient soft-tissue healing can achieve excellent bone union and functional outcomes in patients with TPF and ACS. However, complications may occur more often in patients undergoing dual approaches for bicondylar TPFs, necessitating vigilant monitoring and management.Keywords: Tibial plateau fracture, Compartment syndrome, Soft tissue injuries, External fixator, Complications

Tibial plateau fracture (TPF) entails injury to the articular surface of the knee joint, occurring in various forms depending on the external force exerted during trauma.1) Prompt diagnosis and treatment are essential due to the potential damage inflicted on the surrounding soft tissue and intra-articular structures. Treatment strategies and prognoses vary according to the classification of fractures, ranging from simple to severely comminuted fractures. The most critical treatment principle is to restore knee joint function through anatomical reduction and fixation of the articular surface.2)

High-energy fractures often result in increased comminution and damage to the articular surface, leading to soft-tissue injury and increased intracompartmental pressure due to hemorrhage, eventually culminating in acute compartment syndrome (ACS).3) Gamulin et al.4) reported an ACS incidence of 10.4% among patients with TPF. Staged surgeries are generally performed for patients with TPF or ACS. Initial fasciotomy is performed to alleviate compartmental pressure, followed by temporary external fixation to restore alignment using ligamentotaxis. Subsequently, definitive surgery involving open reduction and plate fixation is conducted following soft-tissue healing.5)

A previous study reported infection rates of up to 25% in patients presenting with TPF and ACS, with a particularly dismal prognosis associated with delayed suturing via fasciotomy.6) Before definitive surgery, fasciotomy and external fixation should be performed. The high-energy impact associated with these injuries often leads to significant fracture comminution and substantial soft-tissue trauma, rendering reduction and fixation particularly challenging during definitive surgery. Following definitive surgery, infections, nonunion, and traumatic arthritis can occur. Therefore, a TPF with ACS is a complex and difficult-to-treat fracture.2)

However, definitive surgery was conducted subsequently to complete closure of the fasciotomy wound and soft-tissue recovery. Therefore, we aimed to explore the clinical and radiological outcomes of staged operations involving soft-tissue stabilization in patients with TPF with ACS and to analyze the complications and contributing factors.

METHODS

The Institutional Review Board of the Human Experimental and Ethics Committee of Kyungpook National University Hospital approved the study design and data collection (IRB No. KNUH 2021-04-026-003). Informed consent was obtained from the patients and/or their families after explaining that their case data would be submitted for publication.

Demographics

A retrospective review was conducted on patients diagnosed with TPF and concurrent ACS, who underwent treatment using a staged fixation protocol at a single Level 1 trauma center between December 2011 and November 2020. Patients with trauma aged ≥ 20 years with acute TPF (classified according to the AO/OTA [Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopedic Trauma Association] classification system as 41) who underwent fasciotomy for ACS were included.7) Patients with chronic or pathological fractures, active malignancy, AO/OTA classification of 33 or 42 fractures, a history of knee or ankle degenerative joint disease, < 1-year follow-up, and age < 20 years were excluded to ensure inclusion of patients with skeletal maturity.

TPF was confirmed through plain radiographs, while ACS diagnosis was established based on the following clinical examination findings: (1) disproportionate pain to the injury, (2) pain upon passive stretching of the compartment muscles, and/or (3) severe compartmental swelling.8) Compartment pressures were measured in patients who were unable to participate in clinical examination (i.e., unconscious) because of head injuries or severe polytrauma.4)

The final cohort consisted of 30 patients (23 men and 7 women; average age, 59.7 years), with a median follow-up duration of 33.2 months (range, 12–85 months) (Table 1). Motor vehicle accidents (19 / 30), falls (7 / 30), and crush injuries (4 / 30) were the most common causes of injury. Fractures were classified according to the AO/OTA classification (41-C1: 2 / 30, 41-C2: 5 / 30, and 41-C3: 23 / 30), Schatzker classification (V: 11 / 30 and VI: 19 / 30), and Gustilo–Anderson Classification (I: 1, IIIc: 1).9)

Surgical Methods

Fasciotomies were performed using dual incisions and an appropriate incision length to achieve maximum decompression of all 4 compartments.10) Notably, the incision sites for fasciotomy were carefully chosen preoperatively to avoid overlap with sites intended for subsequent surgical fixation of the fracture. Subsequently, a knee-bridging external fixator was used to treat TPF, with Schanz pins (5 mm) inserted bicortically into the distal femur and the shaft of the tibia.1) Elastic retention sutures were used to prevent tension and widening of the myofascial incision, complemented by negative-pressure wound therapy dressing (Fig. 1). Subsequent irrigation and debridement in the operating room were performed at 3–4-day intervals until satisfactory soft-tissue conditions were achieved, followed by sequential closure of the incision from both ends. Split-thickness skin grafting (STSG) was implemented when primary coverage was deemed unsuitable because of adhesions.11)

Regular cleansing with normal saline was adopted to mitigate infection risk at external pin sites, a practice recognized for its safety and effectiveness. This practice was complemented by the application of sterile, nonadherent dressings to minimize disruption to the healing site.12) Dressing changes were conducted daily during the initial postoperative week, with frequency adjusted thereafter based on signs of healing and inflammation. Rigorous monitoring for infection symptoms was critical, along with patient and caregiver education regarding hygiene and recognition of infection signs. In cases of worsening pin-site infections, intravenous antibiotics were administered, and further debridement was performed to manage the infection.

The patients underwent open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) following primary closure or STSG of the fasciotomy incision sites, ensuring adequate re-epithelialization of skin wrinkles and fracture blisters. A meticulous preoperative plan was established by reviewing computed tomography scans to achieve anatomical reduction of the fracture and depressed joint surfaces. An anterolateral fasciotomy was intentionally positioned more posteriorly than usual, while for cases requiring a posteromedial approach, the fasciotomy was made slightly shorter proximally. This approach aimed to minimize overlap with the incision for definitive fixation. If deemed necessary during definitive fixation, the fasciotomy wound was extended proximally, with utmost care taken to minimize soft-tissue damage (Fig. 2). Following surgical fixation, patients were immobilized with a splint for 3 days, after which the splint was removed and physical therapy commenced. Physical therapy included active or continuous passive movement of the knee joint and quadriceps-strengthening exercises. Patients were advised to initiate weight-bearing with crutches 4 weeks postoperatively, with progression to limited weight-bearing contingent upon the presence of bony calluses observed on plain radiographs.

Evaluation

Demographic data (age and sex), comorbidities (diabetes and antiplatelet therapy), injury characteristics (mechanism, fracture classification, open fracture, tibial shaft fracture, fibular fracture, and polytrauma), time from fasciotomy to definitive fixation, pin-site infection (classified according to the Dahl classification), and outcomes (infection, posttraumatic arthritis, nonunion, and time to union) were collected.13)

Radiographical follow-up was conducted at 4-week intervals with union defined as the trabecular bone crossing the fracture line and disappearance of the fracture interval in 3 out of 4 cortices on anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs, accompanied by the absence of abnormal clinical findings, such as pain or limping with weight-bearing.14) Malalignment was defined as ≥ 5° angulation, ≥ 14° malrotation, and/or a leg length discrepancy of ≥ 2 cm.15) Nonunion was defined as the absence of complete bone union at ≥ 6 months after the index surgery or no radiographical advancement of healing over 3 months with associated pain and limping.16)

In determining infection, our study adhered to the definitions of Fracture-Related Infection set forth by Govaert et al.,17) which include confirmatory criteria, such as the existence of a fistula or sinus, purulent drainage, identification of the same pathogens from at least 2 separate deep tissue specimens, and the presence of microorganisms in deep tissue confirmed by histopathology. Additionally, suggestive criteria cover clinical signs, such as pain and redness; radiological signs, such as bone lysis; and elevated serum inflammatory markers. Posttraumatic arthritis was assessed on AP radiographs taken at 1 year after the index surgery and graded according to the Kellgren and Lawrence (K&L) classification.18)

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included postoperative complications, defined as deep infection, nonunion, malalignment, and grade ≥ 3 posttraumatic arthritis. Secondary outcomes included the Knee Society and American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) scores measured at 1 year postoperatively.19) The AOFAS score was used to evaluate the effect of bilateral fasciotomy on ankle joint function.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using JMP Pro statistical software (version 16; SAS Institute) following standardized guidelines. For all sample groups, the distribution was initially assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk W test. Parametric statistical tests were applied to normally distributed datasets. Two-sample t-tests were used to compare continuous parametric variables, which are presented as mean differences with 95% CIs. Nonparametric statistical tests were used for non-normally distributed data. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare nonparametric continuous data between the groups, and the Hodges-Lehmann estimator was used to present the median differences and 95% CIs. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests (n < 5) were used to compare the categorical variables. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 391 patients with TPF were identified over a 10-year period from December 2011 to November 2020. After applying the exclusion criteria, 30 patients (7.7%, 30 / 391) with acute Schatzker type V/VI TPF or concurrent ACS were included in the final analysis (Table 1). Overall, 96.7% patients (29 / 30) achieved primary union at a median time of 18.0 weeks (interquartile range [IQR], 15.0–25.0 weeks). The remaining patients achieved union after autologous bone grafting and plate augmentation. No cases of malalignment were noted. The average range of motion measured at the time of final follow-up was 124° (100°–135°), with a median Knee Society Score of 90.0 (IQR, 90.0–100.0) and a median AOFAS score of 96.0 (IQR, 93.0–100.0).

The average time from fasciotomy to definitive ORIF was 31 days (standard deviation, 12.5 days). STSG coverage was performed in 6.7% of the patients (2 / 30). The patients were graded according to the Dahl classification at the time of definitive ORIF as follows: 6.7% (2 / 30) were grade 0, 40.0% (12 / 30) were grade 1, 30.0% (9 / 30) were grade 2, and 23.3% (7 / 30) were grade 3.17,18) Dual approaches (anterolateral, posteromedial, anterolateral, and posterior) were performed because of bicondylar TPFs in 63.3% of the patients (18 / 30).

The rate of deep infection was 6.7% (2 / 30), which occurred at 1 and 2 years after achieving primary union, and comprised methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus and methicillin-resistant S. aureus, respectively, all of which were grade 3 according to the Dahl classification. Both patients underwent irrigation, debridement, implant removal, and intravenous antibiotic administration. The occurrence of posttraumatic arthritis, graded as K&L grade ≥ 3, was noted in 16.7% of cases (5 / 30). These instances were attributed to challenges in achieving accurate reduction, primarily due to initial fracture severity and the extent of displacement.

The overall complication rate was 26.7% (8 / 30 patients). Upon analysis, postoperative complications were found to be associated with dual approaches (proportional difference, 50.0%; 95% CI, 12.7%–67.3%; p = 0.01) and a low Knee Society Score measured at 1-year follow-up (mean difference, −10.0; 95% CI, −15.0 to 0.0; p = 0.003) (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

Compared to the previously reported infection rate of 25% in patients with TPF accompanied by ACS, in our study, the infection rate in patients with TPF accompanied by ACS who were treated with our protocol was significantly lower (6.7%).4,5,6) This improvement is believed to be attributed to the complete closure of the fasciotomy site and the implementation of separate incisions during definitive surgery to avoid fasciotomy wounds. Moreover, our research identified associated factors that could contribute to complications, such as nonunion, infection, and osteoarthritis. Notably, patients who underwent a dual approach for bicondylar fractures had a significantly higher likelihood of developing complications. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the clinical and radiological outcomes of staged operations involving soft-tissue stabilization in patients with TPF with ACS and to analyze the complications and contributing factors.

The low infection rate observed in our study can be attributed to several factors. First, the final plate fixation was conducted after the complete healing of the wound, characterized by the recovery of skin wrinkles and reepithelialization of fracture blisters, which facilitated the minimization of additional soft-tissue damage as edema subsided (Fig. 1).20) Zura et al.7) also argued that the timing of definitive fixation is independent of the infection rate and that complete stabilization of soft tissue is a crucial risk factor for infection. Second, we employed separate incision sites for fasciotomy and plate fixation to avoid contamination (Fig 2). Blair et al.21) reported an infection rate of approximately 20% when ORIF was performed through fasciotomy wounds. However, in this study, fasciotomy was performed by avoiding the incision site of the definitive surgery where possible through a preoperative plan. This precautionary measure likely contributed to the reduced infection rate by mitigating potential complications associated with wound spreading and edema post-fasciotomy. Third, the fasciotomy wounds were irrigated and debrided by sterile draping of the entire homonymous leg in the operating room under adequate anesthesia. Fasciotomy wounds are carefully undermined during debridement to promote early soft-tissue healing and avoid adhesion between the muscle and soft tissues.22) Finally, we sought to minimize soft tissue damage by meticulously handling the soft tissue during the definitive surgery.11)

Nevertheless, deep infections were observed in 2 cases (6.7%), both of which were accompanied by grade 3 pin-site infections. Maurer et al.23) also reported that with pin-site infection, the rate of intramedullary infection increased by 6%–70% while converting to internal fixation after external fixation. Moreover, Parameswaran et al.24) reported that superficial pin infections could potentially lead to up to 20% more cases of osteomyelitis. Notably, periarticular soft-tissue mobility is greater with periarticular pin placement compared to pin insertion into the diaphyseal area, increasing the rate of pin-site infection. Therefore, patients who have experienced pin-site infection and are undergoing internal fixator should be informed concerning the increased risk of infection. During surgery, thorough debridement of the pin-site and meticulous handling throughout the reduction and fixation procedures are necessary.

This study focused on patients with TPF accompanied with ACS who were followed up for at least 1 year postoperatively. Although the sample size was small, the patient cohort was compiled over a span of 10 years under a consistent protocol, reflecting the rarity of such cases. Considering that comparable datasets in other studies typically comprised approximately 30 patients, our study group was considered substantial.4,25) Although exercising caution in drawing conclusions from a dataset of 30 patients is important, the distinctive approach and notable outcomes achieved in this study warrant reporting.

In this study, an average of 1 month was allotted for soft-tissue healing from the initial external fixator application to definitive surgery, which was a relatively longer time compared to the interval used in other studies.2,3,5) During this period, consider the potential increase in infection rates associated with external fixators, as well as the impact of ease of reduction during definitive surgery on bone union. In this study, the condition of the external fixator pins was more relevant than the duration of external fixator placement. The challenges encountered during reduction and fixation, particularly in relation to the degree of fracture comminution, appeared to be more significant than the timing of definitive surgery, with the duration of external fixator fixation showing no significant influence on bone union. These results are consistent with those of a study conducted by Thabet et al.,25) which found that ACS and nonunions were not directly correlated.

The main distinction between this study and previous research lies in identifying factors associated with complications of ACS in patients with TPF.9,25) We investigated the variables related to these complications and performed statistical analyses (Table 2). The defined complications included deep infection (2 cases), nonunion (1 case), and grade ≥ 3 posttraumatic arthritis (5 cases). Coelho et al.26) identified the risk factors for infection in TPF as open fractures, Schatzker type V and VI fractures, use of external fixation, and prolonged intraoperative ischemia time. Similarly, Tian et al.27) reported that factors, such as high-energy fractures, open fractures, Gustilo-Anderson grade IIIB or IIIC, AO/OTA classification type C, and open reduction, could lead to nonunion. In this study, the patients with TPF and ACS comprised 23 cases of AO/OTA type 41-C3, 11 cases of Schatzker grade V fractures, and 19 cases of Schatzker grade VI fractures; most cases were of high-energy intra-articular comminuted fractures. We also observed numerous complications when dual approaches were used to fix bicondylar TPF owing to severe intra-articular comminuted fractures. As traumatic arthritis is a common complication, most complications arose from challenging reduction and fixation procedures. Ruffolo et al.28) reported high complication rates when dual approaches were used in TPF surgeries.

The limitations of this study include the absence of controls and the lack of consideration for patient compliance, which makes determining the complete clinical outcomes challenging. Nonetheless, given the 33 months of median follow-up period and the early manifestation of severe complications of typical TPF cases, the efficacy of this treatment is well evidenced.29) Finally, this retrospective study evaluated the efficacy of staged TPF treatment based on clinical and radiological results. Further prospective, large-scale studies are warranted.

In summary, owing to the severe damage to the soft tissue and bones in patients with TPF and ACS, the soft tissue should be sufficiently stabilized through staged operations, and anatomical reduction and stable fixation must be achieved through a thorough preoperative plan. Careful observation of patients undergoing dual approaches for comminuted bone fragments is necessary because complications can occur easily.
