Abstract
This paper studies the verification of almost-sure Büchi objectives in MDPs with a known, compositional structure based on string diagrams. In particular, we ask whether there is a strategy that ensures that a Büchi objective is almost-surely satisfied. We first show that proper exit sets — the sets of exits that can be reached within a component without losing locally — together with the reachability of a Büchi state are a sufficient and necessary statistic for the compositional verification of almost-sure Büchi objectives. The number of proper exit sets may grow exponentially in the number of exits. We define two algorithms: (1) A straightforward bottom-up algorithm that computes this statistic in a recursive manner to obtain the verification result of the entire string diagram and (2) a polynomial-time iterative algorithm which avoids computing all proper exit sets by performing iterative strategy refinement.



1
Introduction

Markov decision processes (MDPs) are a ubiquitous model to describe systems with uncertain action outcomes. Their efficient verification is hindered by typical state space explosion problems, see e.g.,
[
1
,
12
]
.
To overcome this state space explosion, a promising research direction to overcome scalability concerns is to explicitly capture and utilize the (compositional) structure of the MDP
[
5
,
13
,
16
]
.

Concretely, we consider sequentially composed MDPs (potentially with loops), that is, we consider MDPs whose state space is partitioned into
components
.
Specifically, we study the setting in which these components and their interaction is specified as string diagrams
[
19
]
:
That is, components are MDPs with entrance and exit states and their composition is defined by a number of operations that glue these components together to obtain the
monolithic MDP
(see e.g.,
Fig.

1
).
The key concept in compositional verification is then to verify objectives without constructing the monolithic MDP, but rather, by (iteratively or exhaustively) analyzing the components and combining the result of their analysis.
This has been done for quantitative reachability in
[
19
,
22
]
, but not for almost-sure Büchi objectives.
Although the analysis of Büchi objectives on a monolithic MDP can be done in polynomial time in the number of states of the MDP, the size of the MDPs is often prohibitively large.
Compositional approaches avoid the necessity to handle the monolithic MDP.

The key question this paper answers affirmatively is whether such a compositional approach can work for almost-sure Büchi objectives.
We study MDPs with a set of marked states, called the
Büchi states
, and algorithmically answer whether given an entrance it is possible to
win
the almost-sure Büchi objective, that is, can we visit a Büchi state infinitely often with probability one.

In this paper, we first discuss what information is necessary to sufficiently describe the behavior of the components
exhaustively
.
We call this information the
solution
. The solution should include for each entrance the set of exits that can be reached using strategies that we call
no-lose
, that is, a strategy which avoid surely violating the Büchi objective in the component.
We call the set of reachable exits the
proper exit set
.
Intuitively, after playing a no-lose strategy, we can reach a proper exit which is connected to an entrance of another component, after which we play another no-lose strategy.
However, constantly playing no-lose strategies is not sufficient to satisfy the Büchi objective.
In the solution, beside the proper exit set we must therefore also include whether there is a positive probability of reaching a Büchi state.
If for a given entrance, there exists a no-lose strategy that can reach a Büchi state, then reaching this entrance infinitely often implies satisfaction of the Büchi objective.
We call the combination of the proper exit set and the positive reachability of a Büchi state the
effect
of the strategy and it is
the
essential information for our
compositional solution
.

The characterization of the problem in terms of a compositional solution gives rise to a natural (exhaustive) algorithm in the spirit of
[
19
,
21
]
:
Consider the abstract syntax tree of the string diagram, first analyze the leafs of this tree exhaustively by computing its solution, and then compose the analysis results.
The exhaustive analysis is exponential in the number of exits, but polynomial in the number of components.
Compared to the quantitative reachability case
[
19
,
21
]
, this is already good news, because in the quantitative case, the computation can be superpolynomial even with a constant number of exits per component.

Our second (iterative) algorithm avoids computing all possible effects by taking the context of each component into account, inspired by a compositional formulation of value iteration in
[
22
]
.
In contrast to existing work, this algorithm can limit the amount of iterations necessary by determining that certain entrances may not be reached by any winning strategy. In particular, we start with a strategy that reaches as many entrances as possible, and refine this strategy by pruning entrances from which we cannot avoid losing.
Our iterative algorithm mimics the
classical Büchi algorithm

[
7
,
6
,
4
]
on the level of components.
We show that the sets of possible effects computed by the exhaustive algorithm exhibit a join semilattice structure, which the iterative algorithm uses to prevent computing all effects, yielding a polynomial time algorithm.

In conclusion,
the contributions of this work are a characterization of the necessary and sufficient information about the behavior of a component in order to prove satisfaction of a Büchi objective globally,
an exponential-time bottom-up algorithm that computes this information on individual components and composes them according to the structure of the string diagram,
and an iterative approach that avoids the exponential influence of the number of exits.

2
Preliminaries

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
[n]
denotes the set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.
We denote the disjoint union by
\uplus
.
We use
\nu
and
\mu
to denote the greatest and least fixpoint operators, respectively.

A (directed)
graph

\mathcal{G}=\langle V,E\rangle
is a tuple with finitely many vertices
V
and edges
E\subseteq V\times V
.

The successors
of
x\in V
are
\mathrm{Post}(x)\vcentcolon=\{y\in V\mid\langle x,y\rangle\in E\}
. Similarly,
\mathrm{Pre}(y)\vcentcolon=\{x\in V\mid\langle x,y\rangle\in E\}
are the
predecessors
of
y\in V
.
We lift
\mathrm{Post}
and
\mathrm{Pre}
to sets:
\mathrm{Post}(X)\vcentcolon=\bigcup_{x\in X}\mathrm{Post}(x)
,
\mathrm{Pre}(X)\vcentcolon=\bigcup_{x\in X}\mathrm{Pre}(x)
.

We are interested in
almost-sure acceptance
in
Markov decision processes

[
2
,
11
]
(MDPs).
Then, the amplitude of probabilities is irrelevant—a non-zero probability, however small it is, can break almost-sure acceptance. Therefore, we study the following abstraction of MDPs (see, e.g.
[
3
]
), which preserves all results.

Definition 1
(MDP graph and strategy)

An
MDP graph

{{M}}\vcentcolon=\langle V_{1},V_{P},E\rangle
is a graph
\langle V,E\rangle
where
V\vcentcolon=V_{1}\uplus V_{P}
.
V_{1}
are the
player-1 vertices
and
V_{P}
are the
probabilistic vertices
.
A
strategy

\sigma\colon V_{1}\to\mathcal{P}\left\lparen V_{P}\right\rparen
maps player-1 vertices to successor vertices, such that for all vertices
v\in V_{1}
:
\sigma(v)\subseteq\mathrm{Post}(v)
.

In the following we simply refer to MDP graphs as MDPs.
We denote the pointwise union of strategies
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
by
\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2}
, with
(\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2})(v)\vcentcolon=\sigma_{1}(v)\cup\sigma_{2}(v)
for all
v\in V_{1}
.
We note that our strategies are memoryless randomized strategies. These are sufficient for satisfying almost-sure Büchi objectives
[
7
]
.
We assume w.l.o.g. that
V_{1}
and
V_{P}
vertices
alternate
, i.e.,
V_{1}
vertices are only reachable from
V_{P}
vertices and vice versa.

Definition 2
(reachable vertices)

Let
\mathrm{Post}_{\sigma}(x)
be equal to
\sigma(x)
if
x\in V_{1}
and
\mathrm{Post}(x)
otherwise.
We define the
vertices reachable from
X\subseteq V
under
\sigma
as

\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}(X)\vcentcolon=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}X_{n}
, where
X_{0}\vcentcolon=X
and
X_{n+1}\vcentcolon=X_{n}\cup\mathrm{Post}_{\sigma}(X_{n})
.

The general notion of Büchi acceptance is over
infinite traces
.
When one restricts to almost-sure acceptance (as we do), there is an alternative characterization
[
2
, Thm. 10.29, Cor. 10.30]
, namely that a vertex
i
is
almost-sure accepting
iff there exists a strategy
\sigma
such that for every vertex
v
reachable from
i
under
\sigma
, a Büchi vertex
v^{\prime}
is reachable from
v
under
\sigma
.

This characterization justifies the following definition.

Definition 3
(Büchi objective)

A strategy
\sigma

satisfies the Büchi objective

B\subseteq V_{P}
for vertex
i\in V
if
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}(v)\cap B\neq\emptyset
for all
v
in
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}(i)
.
We denote
i,\sigma\models\square\lozenge B
or simply
i\models\square\lozenge B
if such a strategy exists.
We denote the
winning region
by
\mathrm{Win}_{\square\lozenge B}\vcentcolon=\{v\in V_{1}\mid v\models\square\lozenge B\}
.

For ease of presentation, we assume w.l.o.g. that all Büchi vertices are probabilistic vertices.
We briefly recall the
classical Büchi algorithm

[
7
,
6
,
4
]
.

Definition 4
(Büchi operator)

Let
F(X,Y)\vcentcolon=\{v\in V_{1}\mid\exists v^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Post}(v)\colon\\
\mathrm{Post}(v^{\prime})\subseteq Y\text{ and }v^{\prime}\in B\vee v^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Pre}(X)\}
, the
Büchi operator
.

We can compute the winning region
\mathrm{Win}_{\square\lozenge B}
using the Büchi operator:

Lemma 1

\nu Y.\,\mu X.\,F(X,Y)=\mathrm{Win}_{\square\lozenge B}

For a fixed
Y
, a vertex
v
is in the ‘inner fixpoint’
\mu X.\,F(X,Y)
iff it can reach a Büchi vertex while staying in
Y
.
Then, a vertex
v
is in the ‘outer fixpoint’
\nu Y.\,\mu X.\,F(X,Y)
iff all vertices reachable from
v
can reach a Büchi vertex, which is exactly the winning region for Büchi acceptance.
The classical Büchi algorithm computes the above fixpoint using Kleene iteration. Initially, we fix
Y
to
V_{1}
and compute the least fixpoint of
F
by iteratively applying
F
to
\emptyset
, i.e., we compute the sequence
F(\emptyset,Y),F(F(\emptyset,Y),Y),\cdots
.
The obtained fixpoint is the new value of
Y
.
The process is repeated until we obtain a fixpoint of
Y
, in which case
Y
is equal to the winning region
\mathrm{Win}_{\square\lozenge B}
.

All proofs are given in the appendix.

2.1
Compositional MDPs

We define
rightward open MDP
which have
open ends
via which they compose.

Definition 5
(roMDP)

A
rightward open MDP

\mathcal{A}=\langle{{M}},\mathsf{IO}\rangle
is a pair consisting of an MDP
{{M}}
with
open ends

\mathsf{IO}=\langle\mathsf{I},\mathsf{O}\rangle
, where

\mathsf{I},\mathsf{O}\subseteq V_{1}
are pairwise disjoint and totally ordered.
The vertices in
\mathsf{I}
and
\mathsf{O}
are the
entrances
and
exits
respectively.
We require that
\mathrm{Pre}(\mathsf{I})=\mathrm{Post}(\mathsf{O})=\emptyset
.

We denote the
arity
of
\mathcal{A}
as
\mathrm{arity}(\mathcal{A})\colon m\to n
meaning that
\lvert I\rvert=m
and
\lvert O\rvert=n
.
In the following, we will fix arbitrary roMDPs
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
.
We often use superscript to refer to elements of an roMDP
\mathcal{A}
, e.g.,
\mathsf{I}^{\mathcal{A}}
denotes the entrances of
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 6
(string diagram)

A
string diagram
{\mathbb{D}}
of roMDPs
is a term adhering to
{\mathbb{D}}\vcentcolon=\mathsf{c}_{\mathcal{A}}\mid{\mathbb{D}}\fatsemi{\mathbb{D}}\mid{\mathbb{D}}\oplus{\mathbb{D}}\mid\mathrm{tr}({\mathbb{D}})
, where
\mathsf{c}_{\mathcal{A}}
designates an roMDP
\mathcal{A}
.

In the definition of a string diagram,
\fatsemi
denotes
sequential composition
,
\oplus
denotes
sum composition
and
\mathrm{tr}
denotes
trace
.
In the following we fix
{\mathbb{D}}
to be an arbitrary string diagram of roMDPs.

Figure

1
demonstrates the graphical intuition behind trace and sequential composition.
In the figure, the clouds represent a large number of vertices and transitions.
We now define these operations.

Definition 7
(
\mathcal{A}\fatsemi\mathcal{B}
)

Let
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
be roMDPs such that
\mathrm{arity}(\mathcal{A})\colon m\to l
and
\mathrm{arity}(\mathcal{B})\colon l\to n
.
Their
sequential composition

\mathcal{A}\fatsemi\mathcal{B}
is the roMDP
\langle{{M}},\mathsf{IO}\rangle
where

\mathsf{IO}\vcentcolon=\langle\mathsf{I}^{\mathcal{A}},\mathsf{O}^{\mathcal{B}}\rangle
,

{{M}}\vcentcolon=\left\langle(V_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}\uplus V_{1}^{\mathcal{B}})\setminus\mathsf{O}^{\mathcal{A}},V_{P}^{\mathcal{A}}\uplus V_{P}^{\mathcal{B}},E\right\rangle

and
E
satisfies the following:
\langle v,v^{\prime}\rangle\in E
iff either:
(1)
\langle v,v^{\prime}\rangle\in E^{\mathcal{A}}\wedge v^{\prime}\in V^{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\mathsf{O}^{\mathcal{A}}
,
(2)
\langle v,\mathsf{o}_{i}^{\mathcal{A}}\rangle\in E^{\mathcal{A}}\wedge v^{\prime}=\mathsf{i}_{i}^{\mathcal{B}}
for some
i\in[l]
,
(3)
\langle v,v^{\prime}\rangle\in E^{\mathcal{B}}
.

Definition 8
(
\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{B}
)

The
sum

\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{B}
is the roMDP
\langle{{M}},\mathsf{IO}\rangle
where

\mathsf{IO}\vcentcolon=\langle\mathsf{I}^{\mathcal{A}}\uplus\mathsf{I}^{\mathcal{B}},\mathsf{O}^{\mathcal{A}}\uplus\mathsf{O}^{\mathcal{B}}\rangle
,

{{M}}\vcentcolon=\langle V_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}\uplus V_{1}^{\mathcal{B}},V_{P}^{\mathcal{A}}\uplus V_{P}^{\mathcal{B}},E\rangle
,
and
E
satisfies

\mathrm{Post}(v)=\mathrm{Post}^{\mathcal{D}}(v)
for
\mathcal{D}\in\{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\}
if
v\in V^{\mathcal{D}}
.

Definition 9
(
\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{A})
)

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an roMDP such that
\mathrm{arity}(\mathcal{A})\colon m+1\to n+1
.
The
trace

\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{A})
is the roMDP
\langle{{M}},\mathsf{IO}\rangle
where

\mathsf{IO}\vcentcolon=\left\langle\mathsf{I}^{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\left\{\mathsf{i}_{m+1}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\},\mathsf{O}^{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\left\{\mathsf{o}_{n+1}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\}\right\rangle
,

{{M}}\vcentcolon=\left\langle V_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},V_{P}^{\mathcal{A}}\uplus\{*\},E\right\rangle
and

E\vcentcolon=E^{\mathcal{A}}\cup\left\{\left\langle\mathsf{o}_{n+1}^{\mathcal{A}},*\right\rangle,\left\langle*,\mathsf{i}_{m+1}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\rangle\right\}
.

The trace operation introduces two edges that create a loop from the last exit to the last entrance. The ‘
*
’ vertex is added to maintain vertex alternation.

Definition 10
(operational semantics
\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket

The
operational semantics

\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket
is the roMDP inductively defined by
Definitions

7

8
and

Main Problem Statement:
Given entrance
i\in\mathsf{I}^{\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket}
, does
i\models^{\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket}\square\lozenge B
?

3
Compositional Solution

A natural idea, following
[
19
,
21
]
, is to define a compositional algorithm which takes the string diagram, computes a solution for every leaf of the string diagram, and then composes these solutions into an answer on the complete string diagram.
This section develops exactly such an approach.
We first define a (local) solution:
This solution captures the necessary and sufficient information that needs to be extracted from an roMDP.
Next, we define the compositional operations on these solutions so that they can be composed into a final answer.

We continue by introducing the necessary definitions for our compositional solution, which are
no-lose strategies
and their
effect
.
Intuitively, no-lose strategies represent
local strategies
that can be part of a
globally winning strategy
in
\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket
.

Definition 11
(no-lose strategy)

Let
\sigma
be a strategy in
\mathcal{A}
.
We say that
\sigma
is a
no-lose strategy
from
i\in V
if
for every
v\in\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}(i)
either
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}(v)\cap\mathsf{O}\neq\emptyset
or
v,\sigma\models\square\lozenge B
.
Additionally we require that
\sigma
is
i
-local
, that is,
\sigma(v)=\emptyset
for
v\not\in\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}(i)
.
We denote the set of no-lose strategies from
i
by
\Sigma_{\mathit{NL}}[i]
.

The intuition of a no-lose strategy is as follows.
When playing such a strategy, we almost-surely either (1) reach some exit or (2) satisfy the Büchi condition.
A strategy that is not no-lose is
losing
.
We will use the following characterization:

Lemma 2

Strategy
\sigma
is no-lose from
i\in V
iff for all
v
in
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}(i)
we have that
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}(v)\cap(\mathsf{O}\cup B)\neq\emptyset
.

The lemma implies that no-lose strategies can be computed as strategies that satisfy the Büchi objective
\square\lozenge(\mathsf{O}\cup B)
, using off-the-shelf algorithms (e.g.,
[
7
,
4
]
).

Towards determining whether a no-lose strategy
\sigma
is indeed part of a globally winning strategy, we consider its
proper exit set
and
effect
.

Definition 12
(proper exit sets and effects)

Let
\sigma
be a no-lose strategy from vertex
v\in V
.
We denote the
proper exit set
of
\sigma
as
\mathrm{Exits}_{\sigma}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\vcentcolon=\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}(v)\cap\mathsf{O}
.
We define the
effect
of
\sigma
as

\mathrm{Effect}(v,\sigma)\vcentcolon=\left\langle\mathrm{Exits}_{\sigma}\left\lparen v\right\rparen,\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}\left\lparen v\right\rparen\cap B\neq\emptyset\right\rangle.

We use
\langle T_{1},b_{1}\rangle\sqcup\langle T_{2},b_{2}\rangle
to denote the pointwise join of effects, that is,
\langle T_{1}\cup T_{2},b_{1}\vee b_{2}\rangle
.
By definition,
\langle\emptyset,\bot\rangle
cannot be the effect of a no-lose strategy
\sigma
: it would imply that for all vertices
v
we have that
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}(v)\cap(\mathsf{O}\cup B)=\emptyset
, contradicting that
\sigma
is no-lose.

3.1
Local Solution

We first introduce the solution for a given roMDP and then define the composition of such solutions.
Finally, we prove the compositionality in
Theorem

3.1
.
Our solution contains all possible effects of no-lose strategies.

Definition 13
(local solution)

The
local solution
of roMDP
\mathcal{A}
is the function
\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}\colon\mathsf{I}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathcal{P}\left\lparen\mathcal{P}\left\lparen\mathsf{O}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\rparen\times\{\top,\bot\}\right\rparen
s.t.

\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}\left\lparen\mathsf{i}_{i}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\rparen\vcentcolon=\bigl\{\mathrm{Effect}^{\mathcal{A}}\left\lparen\mathsf{i}_{i}^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma\right\rparen\bigm|\sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathit{NL}}\left[\mathsf{i}_{i}^{\mathcal{A}}\right]\bigr\}

We can replace every entrance and exit in the above definition by their index to get
\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}\colon[m]\to\mathcal{P}\left\lparen\mathcal{P}\left\lparen[n]\right\rparen\times\{\top,\bot\}\right\rparen

For brevity, we (sometimes) implicitly convert between entrances/exits and their index, e.g., we denote
i
for
\mathsf{i}_{i}^{\mathcal{A}}
and vice versa.
We define
\mathrm{arity}(\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}})
as
\mathrm{arity}(\mathcal{A})
.
As a special case of local solutions, we can derive whether one can win within an roMDP without leaving it.

Lemma 3

For all
i\in\mathsf{I}^{\mathcal{A}}

\langle\emptyset,\top\rangle\in\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i)\text{ iff }i\models^{\llbracket\mathcal{A}\rrbracket}\square\lozenge B

The information in a local solution suffices to compositionally compute the solution of a string diagram.

(solution is compositional)

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}\fatsemi\mathcal{B}}=\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}\fatsemi\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{B}},\qquad\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{B}}=\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}\oplus\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{B}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{A})}=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}).

3.3
One-shot Bottom-up Algorithm

Theorem

3.1
gives rise to a natural, bottom-up computation.
We provide pseudocode for the bottom-up algorithm in Appendix
0.B.3
.
Its correctness follows directly from
Theorems

3.1
and
3
.
Because our solution is compositional, we can make use of
solution sharing
.

Corollary 1
(solution sharing)

Let
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
be roMDPs.
If
\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{B}}
then

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}\fatsemi\mathcal{C}}=\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{B}\fatsemi\mathcal{C}},\\
\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{C}\fatsemi\mathcal{A}}=\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{C}\fatsemi\mathcal{B}},\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}\oplus\mathcal{C}}=\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{B}\oplus\mathcal{C}},\\
\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{C}\oplus\mathcal{A}}=\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{C}\oplus\mathcal{B}},\end{array}\qquad\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{A})}=\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{B})}.

Thus, an efficient implementation of the bottom-up algorithm only needs to compute the solution of each unique subtree of
{\mathbb{D}}
(up to solution equality).
If we limit the number of exits each roMDP can have to a constant, the maximum number of effects that a solution can have is also a constant, yielding a
polynomial-time
algorithm.
However, in general, the running time of the algorithm grows exponentially in the number of exits.

4
Strategy Refinement Algorithm

The bottom-up algorithm above computes the set of all effects, which can grow exponentially in the number of exits.
Below, we present an algorithm for solving almost-sure Büchi by iteratively reasoning about at most one effect per entrance.

First, we introduce the
shortcut graph
, adapting the shortcut MDP from
[
21
]
.
This graph contains all entrances of
{\mathbb{D}}
, connected by transitions which represent the effect of some local strategy (the
shortcuts
).
We show that an entrance in this shortcut graph is winning iff it is winning in
\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket
.

Next, we show that for each entrance the set of all effects forms a
join semilattice
.
This allows us to define the
maximum effect
given some subset of ‘allowed exits’ (akin to the role of
Y
in the classical Büchi operator).
We define a specialized
refinement Büchi operator
that uses maximum effects to prevent constructing the entire shortcut graph, yielding a polynomial time algorithm.

4.1
Shortcut Graph

We need some additional definitions to reason about string diagrams.

Definition 17

The set of

\mathrm{CPI}(\mathcal{A})\vcentcolon=\left\{\mathsf{I}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\}
,

\mathrm{CPI}(\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B})\vcentcolon=\mathrm{CPI}(\mathcal{A})\uplus\mathrm{CPI}(\mathcal{B})
for

\mathrm{CPI}(\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{A}))\vcentcolon=\mathrm{CPI}(\mathcal{A})
.

We define a

for each roMDP
\mathcal{A}
, which maps a set of (local) exits

to the connected component entrances in
{\mathbb{D}}
(exits that are not connected to any entrance do not map to any entrance). A precise definition of
\mathrm{Conn}^{{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mathcal{A}}
straightforwardly follows by induction on the construction of
\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket

(shortcut graph)

The

4.2
Strategy Refinement Algorithm

In our novel strategy refinement algorithm, we exploit the fact that the effects of a given entrance are a
join semilattice
.
In short, the reason is that no-lose strategies are closed under union and
\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i)
is finite.

Lemma 9
(no-lose strategy union)

Let
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
be no-lose strategies from entrance
i
.
We have that
\mathrm{Effect}(i,\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2})=\mathrm{Effect}(i,\sigma_{1})\sqcup\mathrm{Effect}(i,\sigma_{2})
.

Lemma 10

\langle\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i),\sqsubseteq\rangle
is a
join semilattice
where
\sqsubseteq
is the lexicographical order, that is,
\langle T_{1},b_{1}\rangle\sqsubseteq\langle T_{2},b_{2}\rangle\text{ iff }T_{1}\subset T_{2}\vee[T_{1}\subseteq T_{2}\wedge b_{1}\leq b_{2}]
.

Using the join semilattice structure of effects, we now define
maximum effects
.

Definition 19
(maximum effect)

The
maximum effect for
i
restricted to

E\subseteq[n]
is
\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i,E)\vcentcolon=\max\{\langle E^{\prime},b\rangle\in\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i)\mid E^{\prime}\subseteq E\}
where
\max\emptyset\vcentcolon=\langle\emptyset,\bot\rangle
.

Note that the maximum effect restricted to
E
is equal to the join of all effects that have a proper exit set that is a subset of
E
, and therefore always exists.
Using this notion of maximum effect, we define the
refinement Büchi operator
which is specialized for shortcut graphs.

Definition 20
(refinement Büchi operator)

Let
X,Y\subseteq V_{1}^{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}
, we define

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(X,Y)\vcentcolon=\left\{\mathsf{i}_{i}^{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathrm{CPI}({\mathbb{D}})\middle|\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{let}&\langle T,b\rangle\vcentcolon=\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i,Y)\\
\text{s.t.}&\langle T,b\rangle\neq\langle\emptyset,\bot\rangle\text{ and }b\vee\mathrm{Conn}^{{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mathcal{A}}(T)\cap X\neq\emptyset\end{array}\right\}.

This new operator closely follows the classic Büchi operator defined in
Section

2
.
We will now show that the new refinement Büchi operator matches the classic Büchi operator when applied to the shortcut graph.

Theorem 4.2
(refinement)

On
{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}
,
F^{\prime}(X,Y)=F(X,Y)
for all
X,Y\subseteq V_{1}
.

Proof

Starting with the definition of
F
(
Definition

4

F^{\prime}
.

\displaystyle F(X,Y)\vcentcolon=\left\{v\in V_{1}\;\middle|\;\exists v^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Post}(v)\colon\mathrm{Post}(v^{\prime})\subseteq Y\text{ and }v^{\prime}\in B\vee v^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Pre}(X)\right\}

(*
expand shortcut graph definition

\displaystyle{}=\Bigl\{\mathsf{i}_{i}^{\mathcal{A}}\in V_{1}\mid\exists\langle T,b\rangle\in\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i)\colon{}\mathrm{Conn}^{{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mathcal{A}}(T)\subseteq Y\text{ and }b\vee\mathrm{Conn}^{{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mathcal{A}}(T)\cap X\neq\emptyset\Bigr\}

(*
an effect satisfies the previous condition iff the maximum effect does

\displaystyle{}=\left\{\mathsf{i}_{i}^{\mathcal{A}}\in V_{1}\middle|\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{let}&\langle T,b\rangle\vcentcolon=\max\left\{\langle T^{\prime},b^{\prime}\rangle\in\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i)\mid T\subseteq Y\right\}\\

Computation of Maximum Effect.

We briefly sketch how to efficiently compute the maximum effect for entrance

Caching.

The main computation of the refinement algorithm is that of maximum effects, which we try to cache.
As an initial cache, we simply store the maximum effects and the inputs that were used to obtain it.
For example, if at some point during the refinement algorithm we compute that
\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}(i,E)=E^{\prime}

\mathcal{A}_{2}

Here we assume that

We can improve our caching by observing that the maximum effect is

for a fixed entrance

If

then
\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i,E)\sqsubseteq\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i,E^{\prime})

We can use this monotonicity to cache the maximum effect of some exit set that is ‘in between’ two previous computations, formalized by the following lemma:

Lemma 12

Let
\langle Y^{\prime},b\rangle\vcentcolon=\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i,Y)
.
If
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y^{\prime\prime}\subseteq Y
then
\mathrm{S}_{\mathcal{A}}(i,Y^{\prime\prime})=\langle Y^{\prime},b\rangle
.

Related Work

Compositional model checking has been an active area of research for several decades, originating with the seminal work of Clarke et al.
[
5
]
.
In this context, we focus on two closely related lines of inquiry: compositional probabilistic model checking and compositional algorithms for two-player infinite games.
Other forms of compositions, such as parallel compositions, have also been studied
[
9
,
10
]
.

Sequential Compositional Probabilistic Model Checking.

Compositional probabilistic model checking using string diagrams of MDPs has been developed in a series of works
[
22
,
19
,
21
]
.
These studies focus on quantitative properties such as reachability probabilities and rewards.
Likewise, model checking of hierarchical MDPs
[
13
]
is also closely related to our approach.
In particular, recent works
[
15
,
16
]
incorporate parameter synthesis techniques into hierarchical model checking algorithms.
None of the works above considers repeated reachability.
Sequentially composed MDPs have also been studied in the context of learning-based approaches, such as in
[
14
,
8
]
.

Compositional Algorithms for Two-Player Infinite Games.

The works
[
18
,
20
]
introduce string diagrammatic frameworks for parity games and mean-payoff games, respectively.
The compositional algorithm proposed in
[
20
]
, applicable to both types of games, relies on enumerating positional strategies for each subsystem.
In contrast,
[
17
]
presents a compositional algorithm for string diagrams of parity games that avoids such enumeration, in the spirit of
[
21
]
.
While its worst-case complexity is exponential in the number of exits, our strategy refinement algorithm operates in polynomial time.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented two approaches for the verification of almost-sure Büchi objectives in sequentially composed MDPs, expressed using string diagrams.
The first approach is a bottom-up algorithm which computes a
compositional solution
for each leaf of the string diagram and combines them to obtain a solution of the entire string diagram.
The second approach is an iterative algorithm that closely resembles the classical Büchi algorithm, but reasons locally wherever possible.
Natural directions for future work are extensions to parity objectives and to quantitative variants of the problem.

i,\sigma_{G}\models^{\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket}\square\lozenge B
.
First, we define local strategies
\sigma_{t}
for each transition
t\vcentcolon=\langle i,\langle T,b\rangle\rangle
picked by
\sigma
(i.e.,
t\in\sigma(i)
) such that
\mathrm{Effect}^{\mathcal{A}}\left\lparen i,\sigma_{t}\right\rparen=\langle T,b\rangle
.

We sketch the construction of
\sigma_{G}
.
For the initial vertex
i
, we randomly play a strategy according to the transitions of in
\sigma(i)
, say
\sigma_{t}
.
As
\sigma_{t}
is necessarily a no-lose strategy in the roMDP containing entrance
i
, we will either (1) satisfy the Büchi condition locally, or (2) reach an exit
j
, possibly reaching a Büchi vertex along the way.
We perform the same strategy selection for the entrance connected to
j
as for
i
.
As
\sigma
is winning in
{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}
, this implies that we will eventually see a transition such that the Büchi bit is set, and hence play a local strategy in some component such that we can reach a Büchi vertex in that component.
Therefore, playing
\sigma_{G}
is winning for
i
in
\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket
.

The strategy
\sigma_{G}
requires memory to keep track of the entrance that was taken in the current roMDP.
As memoryless strategies are sufficient, there must be a memoryless strategy that is also winning. Hence, we claim that
i\models^{\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket}\square\lozenge B
.
∎

Lemma 22
(completeness of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}
)

Let
i\in\mathrm{CPI}({\mathbb{D}})
be a vertex.
We have that

i\models^{\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket}\square\lozenge B\text{ implies }i\models^{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}\square\lozenge B_{\mathbb{D}}.

Proof

Let
\sigma
be a strategy such that
i,\sigma\models^{\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket}\square\lozenge B
, which exists by definition.
Next, we can split
\sigma
into local strategies based on each entrance:
Let

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}}}(v)\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}\sigma(v)&\text{if }v\in\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{A}}(j)\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Then every entrance
\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}}
that is reachable under
\sigma
when starting in
\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}}
has a strategy
\sigma_{\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}}}
.

\sigma_{\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}}}
is a no-lose strategy: if
\sigma_{\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}}}
were a losing strategy, it would imply that
\sigma
is losing, which is a contradiction.

Next, we construct a strategy
\sigma^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}
:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}\left\lparen\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}}\right\rparen\vcentcolon=\left\{\left\langle\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}},\mathrm{Effect}^{\mathcal{A}}(\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma_{\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}}})\right\rangle\right\}\text{ for }\mathsf{i}_{j}^{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}^{\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket}(i)\cap\mathrm{CPI}({\mathbb{D}}).

We claim that
i,\sigma^{\prime}\models^{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}\square\lozenge B_{\mathbb{D}}
.
We have to show that for all
v
in
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}(i)
we have that
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}(v)\cap B_{\mathbb{D}}\neq\emptyset
.
By definition of
\sigma^{\prime}
, we have that
v
in
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}(i)
implies that
v
is in
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma}^{\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket}(i)
.
As
\sigma
is winning, this implies there is a shortest path
\pi\vcentcolon=\pi_{1}\ \pi_{2}\ \dots\ \pi_{n}\in V^{*}
from
v
to
b\in B
in
\llbracket{\mathbb{D}}\rrbracket
.
Let
\mathit{Verts}(\pi)\vcentcolon=\left\{\pi_{k}\mid k\in[n]\right\}
denote the set of vertices appearing in
\pi
.
If
\mathit{Verts}(\pi)\subseteq V^{\mathcal{A}}
then,
\mathrm{Reach}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\sigma}(v)\cap B\neq\emptyset
and thus
\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}(v)\cap B_{\mathbb{D}}\neq\emptyset
as required.
Otherwise, if
\mathit{Verts}(\pi)\not\subseteq V^{\mathcal{A}}
, then let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{k}
be the roMDPs ‘containing’
\pi
and let
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\vcentcolon=\mathit{Verts}(\pi)\cap\mathrm{CPI}({\mathbb{D}})
be the entrance vertices visited in
\pi
.
By definition of the shortcut graph, it follows that
v_{1}=v
and for all
1\leq l<k
we have that
v_{l+1}\in T_{l}
where
\langle T_{l},\cdot\rangle=\mathrm{Effect}(v_{l},\sigma_{v_{l}})
and thus
\langle v_{l},v_{l+1}\rangle\in E^{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}
and hence
v_{k}\in\mathrm{Reach}_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{D}}}(v)
.
Finally, it is clear that
v_{k}
is contained within
V^{\mathcal{A}_{k}}
, and hence we can apply the same logic as before to obtain that
B_{\mathbb{D}}
is reached.
∎
