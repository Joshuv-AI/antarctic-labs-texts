Under-5 child mortality rate and associated factors in Somalia: an analysis of the 2020 Somali Health and Demographic Survey
Authors: Hamze G Dahir, Ahmed Tahir Ahmed, Mohamed Arab Abdilahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo estimate the national under-5 mortality rate (U5MR) in Somalia and identify maternal and child-level determinants of child survival using data from the 2020 Somali Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS).DesignSecondary analysis of a nationally representative, community-based, cross-sectional survey.SettingSomalia, including urban, rural and nomadic populations across all regions.ParticipantsA total of 52 198 children under 5 years of age (3161 deaths and 49 037 survivors) born to women of reproductive age (15–49 years) who participated in the 2020 SHDS.Main outcome measuresUnder-5 mortality, defined as the probability of a child dying before reaching the age of 5 years (0–59 months).ResultsThe U5MR in Somalia was 65 per 1000 live births (95% CI 62.3 to 66.7). Multivariable logistic regression indicated that children residing in rural or nomadic settings had significantly higher odds of mortality (adjusted OR (AOR)=2.03; 95% CI 1.15 to 3.61) compared with urban residents. Multiple births were associated with a fourfold increase in the odds of death (AOR=4.35; 95% CI 1.94 to 9.73). Longer birth intervals were protective; a preceding interval of 19–23 months (AOR=0.49; 95% CI 0.26 to 0.94) and a succeeding interval of ≥20 months (AOR=0.44; 95% CI 0.29 to 0.66) were associated with lower odds of mortality compared with shorter intervals. Lack of maternal health facility visits showed a marginal, non-significant association with higher odds of mortality (AOR=1.57; 95% CI 0.97 to 2.53; p=0.066).ConclusionsSomalia’s U5MR remains critically high and far from the 2030 Sustainable Development Goal target of 25 per 1000 live births. Identified risk factors are largely preventable. Public health policies should prioritise expanding healthcare infrastructure in rural areas and promoting family planning services to support optimal birth spacing, enhancing child survival

Introduction

The study of child survival is theoretically anchored in the seminal framework developed by Mosley and Chen,1 which posits that under-5 mortality is not merely a biological event but the result of a complex interplay between distal socioeconomic factors and proximate biological determinants. This multidisciplinary approach recognises that maternal characteristics, environmental conditions and nutrient availability act through intermediate variables—such as injury, infection and maternal health behaviours—to influence the probability of child survival. In low-resource settings, this conceptual synergy is critical, as it underscores that clinical interventions alone are insufficient without addressing the underlying structural and behavioural drivers of health.

The under-5 mortality rate (U5MR) serves as a definitive litmus test for a nation’s developmental progress, health equity, and the robustness of its social safety nets. Beyond its statistical utility, U5MR represents a profound human rights imperative; every preventable child death signifies a systemic failure in the provision of basic healthcare and social protection. In the global health discourse, reducing child mortality is a cornerstone of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), specifically Target 3.2. However, the persistence of high mortality rates in fragile states suggests a widening chasm in health outcomes, where the most vulnerable populations are systematically left behind by global progress.

Historically, the global trajectory of child mortality has seen a dramatic decline, falling by nearly 60% since 1990 due to the scaling of high-impact interventions.2 3 Despite this global success, the burden remains disproportionately concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, which continues to record the highest regional mortality rates in the world.4 5 Within the East African and Horn of Africa regions, while nations like Ethiopia and Kenya have made significant strides toward their targets,1 6 Somalia has remained a tragic outlier. For decades, Somalia’s mortality trends have been stagnated by a ‘perfect storm’ of protracted conflict, environmental shocks, and the collapse of institutional health infrastructure, leaving its U5MR among the highest globally for over thirty years.7 8

Currently, Somalia exists in a state of precarious transition. While there are emerging efforts towards state-building, the national health landscape is defined by extreme fragility. The 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (SHDS) reveals a reality where pervasive poverty, weak governance, and a fragmented healthcare system—largely dependent on international aid—limit the reach of life-saving services.9 10 Furthermore, the country faces a critical ‘zero-dose’ crisis, where a significant proportion of children have never received a single routine vaccination, particularly in nomadic and conflict-affected rural areas.11 These realities create a unique survival context where traditional health models often fail to penetrate the most marginalised strata of society.

Despite the severity of the crisis, empirical evidence regarding the specific drivers of child mortality in Somalia has remained remarkably scarce, often relying on outdated estimates or generalised regional proxies. This study addresses this critical research gap by providing the first comprehensive secondary analysis of the 2020 SHDS, the most robust national dataset available in decades. The novelty of this research lies in its ability to move beyond descriptive statistics to identify the specific maternal and child-level determinants—such as birth spacing and rural-nomadic residency—that dictate survival in a fragile state. By providing context-specific, evidence-based insights, this study offers a primary scientific contribution to the global health literature and a strategic roadmap for the Somali Ministry of Health to transition from emergency response to sustainable, data-driven health programming.

Methods

Data source, study design and period

The SHDS is a community-based, cross-sectional and nationally representative survey. The SHDS used multistage stratified cluster sampling. Urban and rural areas employed a three-stage stratified cluster design, whereas nomadic areas used a two-stage design. The survey used 55 sampling strata, categorising each region as urban, rural or nomadic, except for Banadir, which is completely urban. Security concerns led to the exclusion of three strata from Lower Shabelle, three from Middle Juba and two unnamed strata, resulting in 47 sample strata. Women of reproductive age (15–49 years old) who had a history of birth prior to the survey and for whom important determinant variables had complete data were considered in this study’s analysis.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this secondary data analysis study. The study used existing, anonymised data from the 2020 Somalia Demographic and Health Survey (SHDS), for which the original data collection involved extensive community engagement and ethical approvals.

Sample size

A total of 52 198 children under-5 years of age (3161 deaths and 49 037 survivors) from the 2020 SHDS were included in this study.

Sample and sampling procedure

The 2020 SHDS used a multistage stratified cluster sampling methodology. The national sample frame consisted of 58 400 households, from which a representative sample of 11 220 households was selected. Ultimately, 10 900 households were successfully interviewed (97.1% response rate), and 14 780 eligible women aged 15–49 years were included (97.2% individual response rate). Security concerns led to the exclusion of eight clusters in Lower Shabelle and Middle Juba. For this secondary analysis, the final analytical sample comprised 52 198 children under 5 years of age (0–59 months) for whom complete data on all key determinant variables were available.

Variables

The outcome variable of interest is child mortality before the age of 5 years, categorised as dead (code 1) and alive (code 0). The selection of potential risk factors for childhood mortality was based on the framework developed by Mosley and Chen1 to describe factors impacting child survival in developing nations. The maternal-level variables considered in this study are: place of residence (urban vs rural, including nomadic), level of education (no formal education, primary, secondary and higher), wealth index score (lowest, second, middle, fourth and highest), and maternal visit at health facilities in the past year before the survey (yes, no). The child characteristics considered in the study are: birth type (singleton or multiple), sex of the child (male or female), preceding birth interval (defined as short if <19 months vs 19–36 months and more), succeeding birth interval (defined as short if <20 months vs 20 months and above), and birth order (2, 3 and ≥4).

The household wealth index is measured by collecting information on dwelling and household characteristics, consumer goods and assets. Each household asset for which information is collected is assigned a ‘weight’ or ‘factor score’ generated through principal component analysis. The household wealth index variable was categorised into five quintiles (lowest, second, middle, fourth and highest).

Data analysis

Data were extracted from the 2020 SHDS and analysed using Stata V.16. To account for the multistage stratified cluster sampling design, the ‘svy’ command was employed to adjust for sampling weights, strata and primary sampling units. We used a complete case analysis approach; cases with missing data on key determinant variables (representing less than 2% of the total sample) were excluded from the multivariable model. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) on the unweighted data, as standard VIF estimations are not directly applicable to survey-weighted models. All actual VIF values ranged from 1.013 to 1.049, well below the threshold of 2.5, indicating no significant collinearity (see online supplemental appendix 1).

Sampling weights were applied to both the descriptive bivariable analysis and the multivariable logistic regression models to account for unequal selection probabilities and non-response. In the descriptive tables, we report the unweighted frequencies (n) alongside the weighted percentages (%) to ensure transparency and accurately reflect the population-level estimates.

Results

Magnitude of the U5MR

The primary outcome of this study, the U5MR in Somalia, was estimated at 65 per 1000 live births (95% CI 62.3 to 66.7). This indicates that 65 children die before reaching their fifth birthday for every 1000 live births. This rate is significantly higher than the 2022 global estimate of 37 per 1000 live births and the SDG target of 25 per 1000 live births by 2030. Establishing this national baseline provides the necessary context for the subsequent analysis of maternal and child-level determinants.

Maternal and child characteristics as determinants of child mortality in bivariable logistic regression

Important maternal and child characteristics were included to assess the determinants of child mortality. Households with the lowest wealth index score had a higher proportion of child deaths (41.4%) compared with those with higher scores: second (18.9%), middle (17.0%), fourth (12.9%) and highest (9.8%). Among the 3161 children who died, 21.3% were born to mothers who visited health facilities in the 12 months prior to the survey, compared with 78.7% whose mothers did not visit health facilities. A greater proportion (61.4%) of deceased children lived in rural areas than in urban areas (38.6%). Higher mortality (54.0%) was observed for children with a short preceding birth interval (less than 19 months) compared with children with longer birth intervals (19–23 months: 14.1%; 24–35 months: 21.6%; and ≥36 months: 10.3%). Children with short succeeding birth intervals (less than 20 months) accounted for a greater proportion of mortality (55.6%) compared with those with longer intervals (44.4%). Multiple births were associated with a higher proportion of child deaths (1.9% of all deaths were from multiple births), compared with singletons (98.1% of all deaths). Similarly, being a female child (42.5% of all deaths were female) and having a birth order of 3 or above were also linked to an increased odds of child mortality (table 1).

Discussion

The estimated U5MR of 65 per 1000 live births in Somalia represents a critical public health challenge, standing nearly three times higher than the SDG 2030 target of 25 per 1000. While this reflects a marginal improvement from historical estimates, the findings underscore a persistent survival gap driven by a triad of systemic, reproductive, and biological vulnerabilities.

The estimated U5MR of 65 per 1000 live births in this study is lower than modelled estimates reported by other global sources (eg, UN IGME estimates of approximately 114 per 1000 live births for 2020). This discrepancy is primarily attributable to the SHDS’s reliance on retrospective maternal birth histories, which are inherently susceptible to recall bias and the cultural reluctance to report deceased children, often leading to the underreporting of neonatal deaths. Furthermore, the disproportionate distribution of the sample across wealth quintiles reflects the unique asset-based principal component analysis weighting in a fragile, highly impoverished setting where standard consumer assets are uniformly scarce.

Our study reveals a profound inverse standard of care in Somalia, where populations with the greatest health needs—rural and nomadic communities—have the least access to life-saving services. The finding that rural-nomadic residency doubles the odds of child mortality (AOR=2.03) reflects the urban-centric nature of the current health infrastructure. This disparity is likely compounded by the zero-dose vaccination crisis; recent evidence suggests that children in remote areas are significantly more likely to miss all routine immunisations, leaving them vulnerable to preventable diseases.11 Furthermore, the marginal significance of maternal health facility visits (p=0.066) suggests that even when contact with the health system occurs, it may be too infrequent or of insufficient quality to provide a protective effect. Unlike more stable East African contexts where community health extension programmes have bridged this gap,4 12 Somalia’s fragmented system remains heavily reliant on fixed urban facilities that are inaccessible to much of the population.10 13

Our study has also identified birth spacing as an important associated factor of child survival in Somalia. The protective effect of longer birth intervals—demonstrated by a 0.49 to 0.44 times reduction in odds of child mortality for intervals exceeding 19–20 months—supports the maternal depletion hypothesis. Adequate spacing allows for maternal nutritional recovery and reduces the risk of preterm birth and low birth weight, which are critical in environments where neonatal intensive care is unavailable.14 15 Conversely, the fourfold increase in mortality odds for multiple births (AOR=4.35) highlights a biological vulnerability that the current health system is not equipped to manage. In the absence of specialised neonatal care and nutritional support for high-risk pregnancies, multiple births in Somalia often result in preventable loss.16 17

The novelty of this research lies in its contextualisation of child mortality within Somalia’s state-building transition. The drivers of mortality identified here—short birth intervals, rural isolation, and multiple births—are not merely biological events but reflect structural fragility within the social safety net. The transition from emergency humanitarian response to sustainable, data-driven health programming requires a shift toward a no missed opportunities approach. Every maternal contact for antenatal or postnatal care should be used to deliver a bundled package of services, including healthy timing and spacing of pregnancy counselling and nutritional screening.

The primary strength of this study is the use of the 2020 SHDS, providing the most representative and robust national estimate available in decades. By incorporating complex survey design effects, we have provided a statistically rigorous baseline for the Ministry of Health. However, the cross-sectional nature of the data precludes definitive causal inferences. Additionally, mortality data rely on maternal recall, which may introduce bias regarding the exact timing of deaths. Future research should use prospective longitudinal designs and qualitative inquiries to explore the sociocultural barriers to healthcare utilisation among nomadic populations.

In conclusion, child survival in Somalia is contingent on bridging the gap between urban-centred healthcare and remote rural-nomadic populations. Addressing the unmet need for family planning to optimise birth spacing and decentralising maternal and child health services are not merely programme priorities; they are fundamental human rights imperatives necessary to meet the 2030 global targets.

Conclusions

This study provides a rigorous national estimation of under-5 mortality in Somalia, revealing a rate of 65 per 1000 live births. While this reflects a marginal improvement from historical estimates, it remains a stark indictment of the health inequities persisting in the country, standing nearly three times higher than the SDG 2030 target of 25 per 1000. The analytical depth of this research highlights that child mortality in Somalia is driven by a triad of systemic vulnerabilities: geographical marginalisation, reproductive health gaps and biological risk.

The significant association between rural-nomadic residency and mortality underscores a profound ‘inverse care law’, where those with the greatest health needs have the least access to services. Furthermore, the protective effect of optimal birth spacing—demonstrated by 0.44 times lower odds of mortality for longer intervals—identifies birth spacing not merely as a reproductive choice but as a critical life-saving intervention. Ultimately, the findings synthesise a clear narrative: child survival in Somalia is contingent on bridging the gap between urban-centred healthcare and the remote rural-nomadic populations while simultaneously addressing the unmet need for family planning to mitigate the risks associated with high-frequency childbearing and multiple births.

Policy implications

The findings of this study necessitate a strategic shift in Somalia’s public health policy moving towards evidence-informed actionable interventions that address the identified determinants:

Universal health coverage for rural and nomadic populations: The Ministry of Health must prioritise the decentralisation of healthcare. This should involve the deployment of ‘Nomadic Health Teams’ and mobile clinics equipped with a basic package of maternal and child health services. Strengthening the community health worker programme is essential to provide point-of-care services such as immunisations and nutritional screening in areas where fixed facilities are non-existent.

Limitations and future research directions

The primary strength of this study is its use of a large, nationally representative dataset from the 2020 SHDS which allows for the generalisation of its findings to the entire country. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of the survey means that causality cannot be inferred; the study identifies associations but cannot prove that the identified factors directly cause child mortality. Second, the data relies on maternal self-reporting for variables such as birth history, which may be subject to recall bias. Third, the analysis is limited to the variables collected in the DHS and does not include other potentially important factors such as the quality of healthcare services, specific causes of death or local environmental conditions.

Future research should aim to address these gaps. Qualitative studies are needed to explore the community-level sociocultural and economic barriers that prevent families in rural areas from accessing healthcare. Furthermore, prospective cohort studies could provide stronger evidence on the causal pathways linking birth intervals and other risk factors to child mortality. Finally, health facility-based research is required to assess the quality of care and the health system’s capacity to deliver life-saving interventions for children.
