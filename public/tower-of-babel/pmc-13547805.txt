Reasons for Surgical Delay in HIP Fracture: What Can We Do From the Emergency Room?
Authors: Magali González-Colaço Harmand, Alicia Tejera Concepción, Noemi Elizabeth Alonso Morell, Manuel Enrique Fuentes Ferrer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo identify the causes of surgical delay in patients with hip fracture, as recognized in the Emergency Department, and to assess their impact on mortality and hospital length of stay.MethodsA retrospective longitudinal study was conducted in a tertiary care Spanish hospital between January and June 2023, including 182 patients aged 65 years or older with hip fracture (76.9% women; mean age 836 ± 7.6 years). The reasons for surgical delay were analyzed, along with hospital length of stay and mortality during hospitalization and 10 months post-discharge.ResultsDementia was present in 41.2% of cases, and the mean Barthel Index was 75.4 points. The median time to surgery was 4 days (IQR: 3–6). Surgery was performed within 72 hours in 41.8% of patients. The most frequent causes of surgical delay were admission during weekends or holidays (90.6%), lack of operating room availability (19.8%), severe renal failure (31.1%), severe infection (24.6%), neurological instability (23.6%), antiplatelet therapy (21.7%), and absence of accompanying family members (16%). Overall mortality was 18.8%. In multivariate analysis, patients operated within 72 hours showed no significant difference in mortality. However, those operated after 72 hours had a significantly longer hospital stay.ConclusionsSurgical delay in hip fractures is influenced by clinical, social, and institutional factors. Familiarity with clinical practice guidelines may help reduce avoidable delays and improve overall hospital length of stay.Keywords: hip fracture, osteoporosis, surgical delay causes and timing, hospital length of stay, mortality, emergency department

Introduction

Hip fracture represents an acute, severe injury affecting approximately 1.6 million individuals globally each year. 1 In patients aged 65 years or older, delaying surgical intervention beyond 48 hours or 72 hours when prior stabilization of comorbidities is clinically mandatory, 2 correlates with higher short- and long-term mortality.2-7 Procrastination of surgery also elevates the risk of postoperative complications and associated comorbidities.2-4 Conversely, prompt operative fixation reduces nosocomial respiratory and urinary infections, decreases thromboembolic events, shortens the hospital length of stay, lowers readmission rates, and accelerates bone healing.8-10

The vast majority of hip fractures are initially diagnosed within emergency departments (EDs), 11 with only a small percentage presenting via outpatient clinics due to delayed diagnosis or secondary to inpatient falls in other hospital wards. Consequently, the initial management provided upon ED admission is a determinant of survival, long-term functional recovery, and subsequent quality of life. Prior research demonstrates that an ED stay exceeding 5 hours is associated with a higher incidence of adverse cardiac events and total hospitalizations lasting two weeks or more. 12 Furthermore, prolonged wait times in the ED following diagnosis significantly postpone definitive surgical care. 13

Early emergency management establishes the framework for subsequent orthogeriatric care. Delayed clinical risk stratification, prolonged turnaround times for laboratory or imaging reports, and insufficient initial stabilization within the ED collectively prolong the time to surgery. A precise identification of the clinical, administrative, or social barriers that arise during this initial emergency window is necessary to implement structured multidisciplinary pathways, including fast-track systems designed to eliminate avoidable delays and optimize hospital resource utilization. While previous literature has analyzed general lengths of stay in emergency settings, there is scarce data breaking down the specific causes of surgical delay that originate or are first identified during ED care, such as the absence of integrated orthogeriatric care models 14 or dedicated fast-track optimization protocols. 15

This study aims to evaluate the clinical, institutional, and social factors that prevent patients with hip fractures from undergoing surgery within the timelines recommended by current guidelines.2,16-18 Additionally, we analyze the relationship between these specific delay factors, survival outcomes, and total hospital length of stay in our patient cohort.

Materials and Method

This is an observational, descriptive, retrospective study of patients with osteoporotic hip fractures, treated in the emergency department of a tertiary hospital between January and June 2023. Patients aged 65 or older were included, as well as those younger than this age but with high comorbidity and/or multiple pathologies, who underwent surgery. Those who were not candidates for surgery due to their underlying condition, or required only comfort care, and those who were readmitted during the study period were excluded. Patients were followed up for 10 months after their hospital admission, with data collected from the outpatient consultations of Traumatology.

Variables and Data Collection

Sociodemographic variables (age, sex, social situation at admission) and clinical variables (history of dementia, Charlson comorbidity index, previous functional status measured by the Barthel index, type of fracture, and type of surgery and delirium at admission) were collected, as well as clinical intervals which were calculated in days (time from fracture to surgery, time from admission to surgery, and time in emergency or hospitalization ward until surgery). For the purposes of analysis, reasons for surgical postponement were reviewed and classified into clinical, hospital-related, or social categories based on current available guidelines2,18-20 and internal institutional protocols. Documented reasons for surgical delay were grouped into predefined categories within three domains (social domain, clinical reasons and hospital-related reasons). Social reasons were grouped into three categories: (1) social support limitations, including absence of a caregiver in patients with mental disability; (2) consent-related issues, including delays in obtaining informed consent from the patient or legal representative or refusal to sign consent; and (3) delayed access to care, including delayed presentation to the emergency department.

Clinical reasons were grouped into six categories: (1) hemorrhagic risk factors, including anticoagulant therapy (acenocoumarol or warfarin), direct oral anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, coagulopathy, or thrombocytopenia; (2) acute medical instability, including severe infection, acute respiratory failure, hypercapnia, unstable ischemic heart disease, acute or decompensated heart failure, significant cardiac arrhythmias, or gastrointestinal bleeding; (3) neurological conditions, including delirium, intracranial hemorrhage, suspected acute cerebrovascular events, or traumatic brain injury; (4) metabolic or laboratory abnormalities; (5) chronic organ dysfunction requiring optimization; and (6) delays related to preoperative evaluation or interconsultations, including requests for specialist assessment or additional preoperative testing. Hospital-related reasons were grouped into three categories: (1) surgical capacity limitations, (2) organizational delays, and (3) geographical or logistical factors, including patient transfer from other islands or remote areas.

The outcome variables were hospital stay (time from surgery to clinical discharge in days) and in-hospital mortality and mortality during follow-up.

Statistical Analysis of the Data

The categorical variables are presented with their absolute and relative frequency distribution. Continuous variables that fit a normal distribution are summarized with the mean and standard deviation (SD), and with the median and interquartile range (IQR) if they do not fit this distribution. The distribution of the baseline characteristics of the subjects (at admission) between the two study groups was evaluated based on whether they underwent surgery within 72 hours or not. The comparison of categorical variables was performed using the chi-square test, while the comparison of means was conducted using the Student’s T test or the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test for asymmetric variables. For the outcome variable mortality, a logistic regression model was adjusted to identify the effect of those operated outside of 72 hours. The adjustment variables were those that showed significant differences between the two groups and/or were clinically relevant. For the quantitative outcome variables time from admission to discharge and time from surgery to discharge, a multiple linear regression model was used. Since the distribution of these two variables does not fit a normal distribution, a logarithmic transformation was performed before inclusion in the models. For all hypothesis tests, the null hypothesis was rejected with a bilateral significance level of 5%. The data processing and analysis were performed using the statistical package IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. IBM Corp (Armonk, New York, USA).

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol obtained formal approval from the HUNSC-SCS Research Ethics Committee on December 21, 2023, under registration code CHUNS_2023_119 (CADEM study). This research adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with local regulatory requirements regarding patient data data protection and confidentiality.

Hospital-Related Reasons for Surgical Delay

Among social reasons, the only one referenced in current clinical guidelines was the absence of a caregiver for patients with mental disability, which was also the most frequent cause in this category. However, other factors not included in the guidelines, such as delays in obtaining consent from relatives or legal guardians in patients with dementia, or delayed diagnosis due to not attending the emergency department, were also relevant, accounting for 9.4% of delays.

Among clinical causes supported by guidelines, the most common were severe renal failure (31.1%), neurological instability (23.6%), and severe infection (24.5%). Other causes not specified in the guidelines but still frequent in our sample included treatment with aspirin or triflusal (21.7%) and referrals for interconsultation with other specialties (10.4%).

Some guideline-supported reasons were interpreted loosely, contributing to surgical delay despite not meeting strict clinical criteria. For example, although 13.2% of patients were delayed due to anemia, only 5.7% had a post-transfusion hemoglobin level below 8 mg/dL; of the 8.4% on new oral anticoagulants, 7.5% had taken a dose within the previous five days; only 1.9% of patients with electrolyte disturbances actually had severe imbalances; and none of the patients delayed for cytopenia had platelet counts below 50,000/mm3.

Hospital structural delays are not described in the guidelines as valid reasons for surgical postponement. In our sample, the most common hospital-related causes were delays during weekends or holidays (90.6%), lack of operating room availability (19.8%), and, due to the geographic characteristics of our setting, patient transfers from smaller islands or remote areas (7.5%).

Among the 160 patients with follow-up data, 30 patients died (18.8%), 7 during hospitalization and 23 during follow-up. Mortality incidence was slightly lower in patients operated on within 72 hours compared to those operated on later, though this difference was not statistically significant (17.6% vs. 19.6%, respectively; p = 0.759; OR: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.39–1.98).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis, adjusted for age, Charlson index, Barthel Index, and acute confusional state at admission, showed no significant differences in mortality between patients operated on within 72 hours and those operated later (adjusted OR: 1.1; 95% CI: 0.4–2.8; p = 0.863).

Patients operated after 72 hours had a significantly longer median hospital stay from admission to discharge and from surgery to discharge compared to those operated within 72 hours (15 days [IQR: 12–19.2] vs. 10 days [IQR: 8–14]; p < 0.001, and 9 days [IQR: 7–13] vs. 7 days [IQR: 6–11.7]; p = 0.048, respectively).

In the multiple linear regression analysis, adjusted for age, Charlson index, Barthel Index, and acute confusional state at admission, only the time from admission to discharge was significantly longer for the group operated after 72 hours (adjusted β: 0.37; 95% CI: 0.21–0.53; p < 0.001). The time from surgery to discharge was also longer in this group, though not significantly (adjusted β: 0.17; 95% CI: –0.06–0.33; p = 0.166).

An additional analysis was performed comparing patients operated within 48 hours, with similar results for follow-up mortality and hospital stay from admission and from surgery, comparable to those operated within 72 hours ( Suplemental material available online Tables S1 and S2).

A subgroup analysis restricted to patients with delayed surgery (>72 hours) was performed to explore the association between categorized delay factors and clinical outcomes (Table 6). Among the 106 patients with delayed surgery (>72 hours), follow-up information for mortality was available in 92 patients. Within this subgroup, 18 patients died (19.6%), including 3 in-hospital deaths and 15 deaths during follow-up.

Delays related to acute medical instability were significantly associated with increased mortality (OR 5.7; 95% CI 1.9–17.1), whereas no significant associations were observed for other categories.

Regarding length of hospital stay, delays related to acute medical instability and preoperative evaluation were associated with a longer time from admission to discharge. In contrast, delayed access to care was associated with a shorter length of stay, both from admission to discharge and from surgery to discharge. Preoperative evaluation-related delays were also associated with increased postoperative length of stay.

Discussion

In this retrospective study of older adults with hip fracture, we identified that surgical delay is a multifactorial phenomenon influenced by clinical, hospital-related, and social factors. The most frequent contributors to delay were organizational issues such as weekend or holiday admission, along with clinical conditions requiring stabilization, including severe renal failure and infection. Although surgical delay beyond 72 hours was not associated with increased mortality after adjustment for confounding factors, it was significantly associated with longer hospital length of stay. Among patients who underwent surgery after a delay of more than 72 hours, acute clinical instability was associated with higher mortality and a longer length of stay from admission to discharge, a finding also influenced by the need for preoperative evaluations.

Summarizing the results, we found firstly, the sociodemographic characteristics of our sample are consistent with those reported in the literature, predominantly older women with high comorbidity and pre-fracture functional impairment, with or without associated cognitive decline,20-22 and a higher prevalence of intracapsular (38.5%) and extracapsular (58.9%) fractures, the latter also being more frequent in other studies. 6

In Spanish series of patients with hip fractures, 44% undergo surgery within the first 48 hours. 23 Our results are still below the desired target, given that quality indicators recommend at least 63% of patients be operated on within that timeframe, but is important to emphasize that the time patients spend in the emergency department (median of 1 day, with an interquartile median of 1.8 days) offers a window of opportunity to optimize them for surgery. The most common clinical causes of delay in our sample (which are also consistent with those described in the literature24,25), severe renal failure, severe infection, and neurological instability, are all conditions for which early intervention would facilitate stabilization and expedite surgical intervention.

Clinical reasons are not the only ones that could be addressed in the emergency department. Another key reason for delay is the time spent on interconsultations, whether with Traumatology or other specialties, which delay both clinical decision-making and surgery. This raises the question of what model of clinical care might be most effective in minimizing such delays. Fast-track models in hip fracture surgery have proven especially useful in this regard. Aimed at achieving early recovery and discharge, these models standardize care to prevent delays in the emergency department, expedite imaging tests, and facilitate early admission to trauma wards, all of which have been shown to reduce delirium rates in these patients. However, fast-track pathways have not been successful in reducing length of stay, time to surgery, or mortality. 23

Another possible solution would be to implement more comprehensive training for emergency specialists who treat hip fractures, so that preoperative management in the emergency department becomes standardized and optimized from the moment of hospital arrival. Given the multidimensional and specific nature of orthogeriatric patient care, training specialized nurses to serve as case managers or coordinators from the point of emergency admission has already proven effective inhospital protocols.

In our study, some causes of surgical delay were not included in current guidelines, for example, surgery being postponed despite hemoglobin levels above 8 g/dL or platelet counts above 50,000/mm3. The same was true for patients treated with vitamin K antagonists who had normal coagulation values. For this reason, it would be particularly important to improve the training of the entire healthcare team involved in orthogeriatric care. Familiarity with, and internalization of, updated management guidelines and perioperative quality indicators in hip fracture care could help optimize treatment for these patients.

The role of national and international registries, such as the Spanish Hip Fracture Registry, 23 is essential in disseminating clinical quality standards and promoting adherence to them.

Regarding mortality, our overall mortality rate was lower than that reported in other studies2,3,6 We found no significant difference between patients operated on within or beyond 72 hours after adjusting for classical predictive factors. Although uncommon, this finding has been reported in the literature. 26 The most important finding of our study is that mortality does not appear to be determined by the timing of surgery, but rather by the clinical instability patients present before the procedure. Patients’ survival could depend on the fact that these patients, who have greater baseline comorbidity and a higher need for additional tests, are in a poorer general condition, which ultimately leads to lower survival. Naturally, this delay in surgery results in a longer average hospital stay. On the other hand, the study highlights a possible paradox: delayed access to care is associated with a faster discharge. This can be explained by the fact that these patients tend to be less complex, require less early follow-up, and therefore are discharged more quickly. However, although identifying risk factors for mortality was not an objective of this retrospective study, we did observe several factors that in the literature are associated with it, such as the existence of previous dementia or a high comorbidity.27,28

In any case, even if no higher mortality is observed, it is important to consider several key aspects: the absence of increased mortality does not mean that patients are not exposed to other adverse outcomes related to surgical delay. In our study, longer hospital stays were associated with later surgery. The literature links longer hospitalizations with higher risk of disorientation, increased risk of pressure ulcers, especially due to prolonged preoperative immobilization, and, most importantly, worse functional recovery, which in itself represents the greatest failure in any geriatric therapeutic process. 29

Although our study did not assess functional recovery, numerous previous publications have shown that surgical delay and recovery are inversely related. Prolonged stays also increase the risk of nosocomial infections. 30

It is also worth noting that while patients in our study underwent surgery later than recommended, they received specialized care from a multidisciplinary team that addressed their clinical and social needs, as well as care continuity, from the time of hospital admission through to discharge planning. This undoubtedly contributed to the relatively low overall mortality observed in our sample.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, its retrospective and single-center design limits the external validity of the findings. In particular, the relative importance of specific causes of surgical delay observed in our institution may not be directly generalizable to other hospitals, as these factors are partly determined by local organizational structures, available resources, and established care pathways. Second, although the sample reflects real-world clinical practice in an orthogeriatric population, the overall sample size was moderate. This may have limited the ability to detect smaller differences in some secondary outcomes. Third, follow-up data for mortality were not available for all included patients, which could have introduced some degree of selection bias. However, given the relatively small proportion of patients without follow-up information, we believe the impact of this limitation on the main results is likely limited. An additional limitation relates to the assessment of causes of surgical delay. Reasons for delay were specifically recorded only in patients who experienced delayed surgery, which precludes the identification of independent predictors of surgical delay in the overall cohort. Consequently, our findings are descriptive regarding the causes of delay and exploratory with respect to their association with mortality and hospital length of stay among patients with delayed surgery. Finally, as data were obtained through review of electronic health records, some clinically relevant variables in this context, such as frailty, functional recovery, or other patient-centered outcomes, were not systematically collected and could not be analyzed.

Conclusion

Current orthogeriatric guidelines recommend performing hip-fracture surgery within 72 hours. While this is essential to minimize clinical complications, this study highlights that achieving clinical stabilization prior to surgery is also a key factor for survival in older adults with hip fractures. The time patients spend in the emergency department provides an ideal therapeutic window for optimizing those who require it. Ideally, unnecessary dispersion of diagnostic tests and multiple consultations with different specialists should be minimized, centralizing care within a single therapeutic team with the necessary competencies to avoid avoidable delays.
