A ROS-responsive core–shell microneedle platform integrating sonodynamic gas antibacterial therapy and modulating immunity for diabetic wound healing
Authors: Pinkai Wang, Fanrong Ai, Guanfeng Huang, Yunfeng Shen, Fengyang Cui, Hui Deng, Chengzhi Liang, Jiajun Xie, Jiawei Kang, Yudan Zhu, Weixiang Xiong, Jun Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic diabetic wounds complicated by biofilm infections resist healing because persistent bacterial colonization coexists with microenvironmental imbalances, including oxidative stress and inflammation. Conventional pharmacotherapy and wound care are limited by impaired local perfusion, which hinders both effective biofilm eradication and reversal of the pathological wound microenvironment. We developed an ultrasound-activated core–shell microneedle platform (CCA&Lut@MN) that combines sonodynamic gas cascade antimicrobial therapy with a ROS-responsive microenvironment modulation strategy. Upon ultrasonic stimulation, L-arginine–modified copper–cysteine nanoparticles (CCA) in the microneedle shell generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and catalyze NO release from L-Arg, producing synergistic oxidative and nitrative stress that yields rapid bactericidal activity and effective biofilm eradication. The microneedle core consists of an ROS-responsive PVA-HP-luteolin hydrogel that degrades in response to elevated ROS in the microenvironment, releasing luteolin. Luteolin scavenges excess ROS and promotes macrophage polarization to the M2 phenotype, thereby enhancing angiogenesis and cell migration. In a diabetic rat wound model infected with MRSA, CCA&Lut@MN markedly reduced wound bacterial load, alleviated local inflammation and improved blood supply, and thereby accelerated wound healing. This microneedle platform produces synergistic antibacterial effects via sonodynamic therapy (SDT)–gas cascade reactions, modulates the pathological microenvironment, and improves local blood supply, offering a promising strategy for treating complex and refractory wounds.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04422-1.

Introduction

Diabetes is a widespread metabolic disorder globally, and its incidence continues to increase. Projections suggest that by 2035, the number of individuals with diabetes will surpass 592 million [1, 2]. Diabetic wounds (DW) disrupt the local microenvironment of the wound due to sustained hyperglycemia, which is marked by elevated oxidative stress, dysregulated immune responses, and microcirculatory disturbances, ultimately resulting in delayed wound healing [3]. Chronic non-healing wounds heighten susceptibility to bacterial colonization, and infections in diabetic wounds frequently involve multiple species and the formation of biofilms [4]. The dense extracellular polymers (EPS) within biological membranes not only shield pathogens from host immune responses and conventional antibiotic treatments but also facilitate the horizontal transfer of resistance determinants, a process driven by quorum sensing [5]. This frequently results in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes with traditional antibiotics, heightening the risk of drug-resistant bacteria and rendering infections uncontrollable [6, 7]. This situation exacerbates inflammation and significantly impairs tissue regeneration [8]. In clinical practice, the primary strategies for managing diabetic infected wounds include local debridement and the use of conventional dressings [9]. However, conventional dressings often struggle to address the local imbalanced microenvironment of the wound, contributing to a detrimental cycle characterized by “infection - microenvironment imbalance - infection“ [10]. Therefore, it is crucial to provide a treatment strategy that not only has powerful antibacterial and anti-biofilm effects but also can regulate the local microenvironment of diabetic wounds.

Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) employs ultrasound (US) to activate sonosensitizers, resulting in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) with bactericidal properties [11, 12]. This approach has emerged as a highly promising non-antibiotic treatment. Its excellent tissue penetration and spatiotemporal controllability enable localized bacterial eradication as needed, without the necessity for invasive procedures [13]. In this context, the novel copper-cysteine complex (Cu-Cy), Cu₃Cl (SR)₂ (R = CH₂CH₂NH₂), represents a promising functional nanomaterial that demonstrates significant antibacterial activity by responding to ultraviolet light, ultrasound, and X-rays to generate ROS [14–19]. Cu-Cy nanoparticles, functioning as copper-based sonosensitizers, hold considerable potential for applications in ultrasonic antibacterial technology. First, copper ions are naturally occurring trace elements in the human body, and the copper ions released from Cu-Cy nanoparticles can be safely metabolized through normal physiological pathways [20]. Second, prior research has indicated that copper-based nanomaterials possess the capability to target biofilm infections [21]. The above characteristics give Cu-Cy nanoparticles unique advantages in the treatment of infected wounds, and their abundant free functional groups on the surface provide them with the potential for further functionalization [22]. However, the extremely short half-life and limited diffusion distance of ROS may result in incomplete bacterial clearance [23, 24], potentially leading to infection recurrence. Furthermore, the rapid generation of ROS can inflict collateral oxidative damage on adjacent healthy tissues, exacerbating inflammation and impeding subsequent wound healing [25]. Given the aforementioned limitations of ROS, it is necessary to combine it with other antimicrobial treatment strategies to further enhance the therapeutic effect of SDT on tissue infections.

Gas-based treatment methods have garnered significant interest in the realm of antibacterial therapy [26]. Nitric oxide (NO), a bioactive gas molecule, is pivotal in various pathological conditions and demonstrates potent antibacterial properties [27]. Its extended diffusion distance and half-life enhance its efficacy and sustainability in sterilization [28]. Recent studies have expanded this concept, evolving from traditional NO donors to stimulus-responsive platforms that allow spatiotemporal control of NO release or generation. For example, Cheng et al. reported a red-light–triggered NO release system to combat drug-resistant bacterial infections [29]. Meanwhile, recent advances in NO-generating nanomedicines have highlighted the value of controlled NO production in antibacterial therapy and tissue repair [30]. In addition, emerging research indicates that interactions between NO and ROS generate reactive nitrogen species (RNS), thereby further enhancing antibacterial effects [31, 32]. This characteristic compensates for the limitations of the antibacterial efficacy of individual ROS and enhances the antibacterial capacity of SDT. L-arginine (L-Arg), a naturally occurring amino acid, exhibits excellent biological safety and serves as a natural NO donor [33]. Previous research has demonstrated that ROS can catalyze the conversion of L-Arg into nitric oxide gas [34]. Modifying Cu-Cy nanoparticles with sonosensitive properties using L-Arg holds promise as a strategy to combine ultrasound-triggered ROS generation with NO gas therapy, thereby enhancing antibacterial applications based on SDT. The dense biofilm at the wound site and the limited permeability of the skin barrier significantly hinder the local efficacy of therapeutic components [35]. Furthermore, timely regulation of the local wound microenvironment is essential post-effective antibacterial treatment [36]. Hence, an efficient local drug delivery system is imperative to integrate these functionalities.

Hydrogel microneedles represent an innovative approach to transdermal drug delivery [37]. Unlike traditional dressings, the distinctive structure of microneedles allows for physical penetration of both the skin barrier and dense bacterial biofilms [38]. This method facilitates precise, targeted drug delivery while minimizing the required dosage and reducing the potential for side effects [39]. Furthermore, hydrogel microneedles featuring a core-shell structure and responsive properties enable programmed drug delivery that aligns with the various stages of tissue repair [40]. In recent years, hydrogels composed of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and phenylboronic acid-modified hyaluronic acid (HAPBA) have garnered significant attention for their capacity to release drugs in response to ROS [41]. Furthermore, the phenylboronate ester bonds present in HAPBA can interact with the o-diphenolic hydroxyl groups of flavonoids, enhancing their solubility and imparting ROS-responsive characteristics [42]. Luteolin (Lut) is a natural flavonoid compound known for its distinctive phenolic hydroxyl structure, which enables it to effectively scavenge ROS and makes it a promising candidate for modulating redox balance [43]. To capitalize on these properties, a hydrogel (PVA-HAPBA-Lut) was developed by integrating these three components through dynamic phenylboronate ester bonds. In response to elevated ROS levels, the phenylboronate ester bonds undergo cleavage, liberating the encapsulated Lut to precisely counteract ROS, thereby mitigating local tissue oxidative stress and regulating the wound microenvironment. Here, hydrogel microneedles were utilized as carriers to combine the aforementioned functions, resulting in the creation of an ultrasound-activated ROS-responsive core-shell microneedle patch named Cu-Cy-Arg & luteolin@microneedles (CCA&Lut@MN) (Scheme 1). The microneedles’ shell layer comprises soluble hyaluronic acid (HA) and contains copper-cysteine nanoparticles Cu-Cy-Arg (CCA) modified with L-Arg synthesized via amidation, while the core is made up of a PVA-HAPBA-Lut hydrogel with ROS-responsive properties. When applied to infected diabetic wounds, core-shell microneedles effectively disrupt bacterial biofilms due to their unique physical structure. The outer shell dissolves rapidly, releasing CCA nanoparticles into the biofilm and surrounding deep tissue. Ultrasound activates the CCA nanoparticles’ sonodynamic–gas cascade, producing ROS and NO to kill bacteria. Subsequently, the microneedle core undergoes responsive degradation in the presence of local ROS within diabetic wounds, continuously releasing Lut to mitigate tissue oxidative stress. This process polarizes macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, enhances the local microenvironment, promotes angiogenesis and re-epithelialization, and ultimately accelerates wound healing. Compared with the recently reported core-shell microneedle systems [44, 45], we utilized the structure of core-shell microneedles to functically couple the early cascade antibacterial effect of ultrasound-activated SDT-NO with the late-stage antioxidant repair in response to ROS, establishing a multifunctional platform that can actively trigger treatment, which has significant potential in promoting programmed repair of infectious diabetic wounds.

Materials and methods

Materials

Poly (vinyl alcohol) 1788 (PVA, 87–90% hydrolyze), sodium hyaluronate (HA, MW = 200–400 kDa), NaOH, polyethylene glycol 4000 (PEG-4000), growth factor-reduced Matrigel, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Luteolin (Lut, purity: 98%) and 3-aminobenzeneboronic acid (3-APBA) were purchased from Aladdin (Beijing, China). L-Arg, sodium nitrite, and streptozotocin (STZ, purity: 98%) were obtained from YEASEN (Shanghai, China). The PDMS mold for microneedle (the type code was TM-8) was purchased from Henan Micro-Nano BenTeng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) and 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC·HCl) were purchased from J&K Scientific Ltd (Beijing, China). Copper chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O), cysteamine hydrochloride (Cys, 98%), methylene blue (MB) and Griess reagent were purchased from Sigma (USA). Bacterial peroxynitrosoanion (ONOO-) detection kit were purchased from BestBio (Shanghai, China). ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA) supplied E. coli (Escherichia coli, ATCC 25922) and MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, ATCC43300). Bacterial live/dead staining kit was obtained from Bestbio (Shanghai, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Dulbecco’s minimum essential medium (DMEM) were purchased from GIBCO (Waltham, MA, USA). The CCK-8 assay kit, Live/Dead staining kit, 2,7-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), DAPI staining solution, and iFluor 488 phalloidin were obtained from Beyotime (Jiangsu, China). Anti-mouse CD206, Anti-human CD86, and Anti-mouse CD86 antibodies were purchased from BD (Pharmingen, USA). Anti-mouse/human CD206 antibody was purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, CHN). The qPCR SYBR Green master mix was supplied by Yeasen (Shanghai, China), and the PrimeScript RT premix was procured from Takara Biomedical Technology (Beijing, China). All reagents were of analytical grade and were employed without any additional purification. All the tests were controlled by the homologous isotype control antibodies.

Synthesis of Cu-Cy-Arg (CCA) nanoparticles

Cu-Cy nanoparticles were prepared following a straightforward synthetic protocol described in a recent publication [46]. To synthesize the Cu-Cy particles, CuCl₂·2 H₂O was added to deionized water and stirred at room temperature until fully dissolved. After complete dissolution, cysteamine hydrochloride and PEG-4000 were added under inert conditions. The reaction mixture was then adjusted to pH 7. Following pH adjustment, the inert protection was briefly lifted, and the solution was stirred for roughly 5 min until a distinct dark-violet color developed. The system was subsequently reheated to 100 °C for 5 min with vigorous magnetic agitation while reestablishing the inert environment. After cooling to ambient temperature, the resulting suspension was centrifuged and subjected to three sequential washes with deionized water and ethanol to purify the Cu-Cy product. The final sample was dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 40 °C. The preparation of Cu-Cy-Arg mainly involves an amide condensation reaction between the amino group on Cu-Cy and the carboxyl group on L-arginine. First, 174.2 mg of L-arginine, 191 mg of EDC, and 115 mg of NHS were mixed in 10 mL of deionized water and stirred for 30 min. Then, 100 mg (0.26 mmol) of Cu-Cy NPs were added, and the mixture was stirred for another 20 h. The resulting mixture was then centrifuged and washed three times with deionized water (12000 rpm, 10 min), lyophilized, and stored at −20℃.

Characterization of CCA nanoparticles

A transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-F200) operated at 200 kV was used to visualize the morphology and map the elemental distribution of the samples. Chemical composition was further evaluated through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy on a Thermo Escalab 250Xi instrument. The hydrodynamic particle size and the zeta potential were precisely measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 analyzer. In addition, the release kinetics of CCA were determined using a YOKE T3202 ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometer, and crystal structure information was obtained from XRD measurements performed on an MSAL XD-2 diffractometer.Copper ion release from CCA nanoparticles was measured over 24 h in PBS at different pH values ​​(7.4 and 5.5). pH 5.5 was set to simulate the low pH environment during bacterial infection. The experiments were divided into four groups: CCA + pH 7.4, CCA + pH 5.5, CCA + US + pH 7.4, and CCA + US + pH 5.5. The CCA concentration was 100 µg/mL, and the US group was subjected to ultrasonic irradiation (1 W/cm², 1 MHz, 8 min). The mixtures were then centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 720ES) was used to detect the free copper leached from the CCA.

ROS generation

Ultrasound excitation triggered ROS production in the samples, which was subsequently assessed by ESR spectroscopy. For •OH generation detection, CCA was added to 30 µL of DMPO (100 mM in DI water). The mixture was treated with US for 8 min and evaluated by an ESR spectrometer (A300, Bruker). For ¹O₂ detection, CCA was added to 30 µL of TEMP (100 mM in DI water). The mixture was treated under ultrasound for 8 min, and then immediately subjected to ESR analysis. To evaluate the production of ROS, the samples were mixed with a solution containing MB and subjected to US stimulation with different powers or durations. The variation in MB absorbance at 664 nm before and after ultrasound exposure was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (YOKE, T3202, Shanghai).

NO gas generation

To assess the NO generation capability of CCA, we employed the Griess reagent to measure NO release under ultrasonic stimulation. A standard curve was established by measuring sodium nitrite at concentrations of 0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 µM to quantify NO release. CCA samples were dispersed in PBS and prepared at concentrations of 25 µg/mL, 50 µg/mL, 100 µg/mL, and 200 µg/mL. These solutions were then subjected to ultrasound treatment (1 MHz, 1 W/cm²). The supernatant from treated samples was mixed with Griess reagent in equal volumes at different time points, and the absorbance at 540 nm was then determined with a microplate reader (Tecan Trading AG, Switzerland). To verify whether NO generation depended on ultrasound-triggered activation of CCA, four groups were established, namely Cu-Cy, CCA, Cu-Cy + US, and CCA + US, and the Griess assay was used to comparatively analyze the NO production levels of the different groups after ultrasound stimulation. Furthermore, to evaluate NO generation under alternating ultrasound stimulation, on/off ultrasound interval stimulation was applied at predetermined times, and NO generation was measured using the aforementioned method. Each sample was tested in triplicate.

Synthesis of HA-PBA

HA (1 g) was first dissolved in 100 mL of deionized water at ambient temperature, and the pH of the solution was adjusted to 6 by adding hydrochloric acid. Subsequently, 0.57 g of NHS, 0.77 g of EDC·HCl, and 0.67 g of 3-APBA were introduced into the HA solution, and the reaction mixture was continuously stirred at room temperature for 48 h. The resulting product was dialyzed against deionized water for 2 days and then lyophilized to obtain the HA-PBA polymer. The chemical composition of the synthesized polymer was quantified using 1H NMR spectroscopy (BRUKER AVANCE 400, Germany) in D₂O, with spectra acquired at 25 °C.

Preparation and characterization of PVA-HP-Lut gel

The synthesis process is similar to the above, with slight changes [42]. HAPBA (Hp, 1 wt % in H2O, 1 mL) and luteolin (Lut, 20 µg) were mixed first, and the mixtures were stirred at 40 °C for 24 h, followed by the addition of PVA (2 wt % in H2O, 1mL) to fabricate PVA-HP-Lut gel. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra analyses were performed on an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet 5700, Nicolet, USA). Spectra in the range of 4000–600 cm⁻¹ were examined using potassium bromide (KBr) and ambient air as the background.

Fabrication of microneedle patch

The ROS-responsive core–shell hydrogel microneedles (CCA&Lut@MN) were fabricated by a three-step casting protocol. First, CCA and hyaluronic acid (HA) were mixed to produce an HA solution containing 100 µg/mL CCA, and the solution was cast into a PDMS microneedle mold. The mold was then vacuum-treated to ensure complete filling of the needle tips, excess solution was scraped away, and the mold was dried. In step two, 300 µL of the PVA-HP-Lut gel precursor was added to the dried mold, and negative pressure was applied three times to remove air bubbles and allow the gel to occupy the mold cavities fully. Finally, a 15 wt% PVA aqueous solution was poured on top to form the backing layer, and the assembled mold was dried in an oven at 35 °C for 12 h. After careful demolding, the ROS-responsive core–shell hydrogel microneedles CCA&Lut@MN were obtained. Microneedle morphology and structural features were characterized by scanning electron microscopy combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS; ZEISS Sigma 300), optical microscopy (Nikon TE2000-E, Japan), and confocal laser scanning microscopy (Olympus FLUOVIEW FV4000MPE).

Mechanical strength test

The mechanical performance of the CCA&Lut@MN patch, shell microneedles (HA-CCA-MN) and core microneedles (PVA-HP-Lut-MN) was evaluated using a universal testing instrument (CMT4202, Xinsansi, China). During testing, the patch was fixed onto a rigid platform with the microneedle tips facing upward, and the probe of the machine descended toward the tips at a controlled rate of 10 μm/s. Once the probe made contact with the microneedles and continued to compress them, the system automatically recorded the applied force together with the corresponding displacement values. The generated force–displacement profile was subsequently processed using the built-in analytical software to determine the force exerted on each microneedle.

Skin insertion of MN patches

Place the CCA&Lut@MN patch on the back skin of the mouse and leave it for 5 min before taking it out. The exposed skin area was stained with rhodamine for 5 min and then observed under a microscope. To verify whether the microneedle tips successfully traversed the epidermal barrier, the collected skin samples were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, allowing evaluation of epidermal integrity.

In vitro drug release and degradation of CCA&Lut@MN

To assess CCA release, HA microneedle shells loaded with CCA were immersed in PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated on an orbital shaker (100 rpm) at 37 °C. At predetermined time points, the supernatant was collected, centrifuged, and its absorbance measured with a UV–visible spectrophotometer. The cumulative CCA release was then calculated. To evaluate the luteolin (Lut) release behavior of the microneedle patches, the patches were separately immersed in PBS containing 0, 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mM H₂O₂. Samples were collected at predetermined time points of 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. The absorbance of each sample was measured using a UV–vis spectrophotometer, and the cumulative release of Lut was subsequently calculated. To determine the stimulus-responsive degradation rate of CCA&Lut@MN in vitro, samples were incubated separately in PBS containing 0, 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mM H₂O₂ at 37 °C. At predetermined time points, samples were removed and their mass measured after freeze-drying.

In vitro antibacterial activity

The in vitro antibacterial activity of CCA at different ultrasonic powers was assessed by live/dead fluorescence staining. CCA was mixed with 1 mL bacterial suspensions (10⁵ CFU/mL) of MRSA (ATCC 43300) and E. coli (ATCC 25922), then exposed to ultrasound at 0.5 W/cm², 1 W/cm², or 2 W/cm² for 8 min. The US control group received ultrasound for 8 min without CCA. After treatment, bacteria were collected, washed with PBS (pH 7.4) at 3000 rpm, incubated with live/dead staining solution for 15 min, and imaged by inverted fluorescence microscopy (Nikon TE2000 - E, Japan). The same procedure was also used to evaluate the antibacterial activities of Cu-Cy and CCA under ultrasound irradiation (1 W/cm², 8 min). To detect peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻) generation, a bacterial peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻) detection kit was used to assess ONOO⁻ presence in MRSA and E. coli. The bacterial suspensions were divided into five groups: Control, Cu-Cy, CCA, Cu-Cy + US, and CCA + US. After co-incubation for 30 min, the ultrasound-treated groups were exposed to US for 8 min, followed by fluorescence observation using confocal laser scanning microscopy. The in vitro antibacterial activity of CCA&Lut@MN was assessed by plate counting and by live/dead fluorescence staining. Briefly, 1 mL of bacterial suspension (10⁵ CFU/mL) of MRSA (ATCC 43300) or E. coli (ATCC 25922) was treated with normal saline, blank microneedles (MN), Lut@MN, CCA@MN, or CCA&Lut@MN and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Samples in the ultrasound-assisted group additionally received 8 min of ultrasound (1 W/cm², 1 MHz). After each treatment, bacteria were collected by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 5 min), diluted 100-fold with normal saline, and 100 µL of the diluted suspension was spread evenly on LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h until colonies formed. The live/dead fluorescence staining evaluation followed the same procedure.

ROS generation in biofilms

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production within bacterial biofilms was evaluated by monitoring the fluorescence variation of DCFH-DA. Briefly, 1 mL of MRSA suspension (10⁷ CFU/mL) was dispensed into 24-well plates containing cell crawlers and cultured at 37 °C for 48 h, with fresh LB added every 24 h to allow the formation of mature biofilms. The biofilms were then exposed to saline, CCA solution (100 µg/mL), Lut@MN, or CCA&Lut@MN and incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. After treatment, the biofilms were stained with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 30 min, rinsed with PBS, and subsequently subjected to ultrasound irradiation (1 W/cm², 1 MHz, 1 min) or left untreated. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (Olympus FLUOVIEW FV4000MPE) was then employed to capture fluorescence images. ROS generation in E.coli biofilms was evaluated in the same manner. To evaluate the duration of ROS activity within bacterial biofilms, mature MRSA biofilms were exposed to CCA@MN and divided into five groups according to the post-treatment incubation time (0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 min). The biofilms were stained with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 30 min, rinsed with PBS buffer, and then subjected to ultrasound irradiation (1 W/cm², 1 MHz, 1 min). After ultrasound treatment, the samples were incubated in the dark for 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 min before fluorescence imaging.

Antibiofilm activity of CCA&Lut@MN

To evaluate the biofilm-disrupting capability of CCA&Lut@MN, 1 mL of MRSA suspension (10⁷ CFU/mL) was added to 24-well plates containing cell crawlers and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, with the medium replaced by fresh LB every 24 h to allow mature biofilm formation. Afterward, the biofilms were exposed to saline, CCA solution (100 µg/mL), Lut@MN, or CCA&Lut@MN and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Samples belonging to the ultrasound-assisted group received an additional 8-min ultrasound treatment (1 W/cm², 1 MHz). Following the treatments, stained with a live/dead bacterial fluorescence kit for 15 min, and visualized using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Olympus FLUOVIEW FV4000MPE). For SEM observation, treated biofilms were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight, and gradually dehydrated in increasing ethanol concentrations (20%, 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%; the 100% step was performed twice). After drying, imaged using a scanning electron microscope (ZEISS Sigma 300). To quantify biomass, biofilms subjected to the above treatments were incubated with anhydrous methanol for 10 min, stained with 300 µL of 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, washed three times with PBS, and destained in 500 µL of 95% ethanol. The absorbance at 570 nm was then measured using a microplate reader (Tecan Trading AG, Switzerland). The antibiofilm efficacy of CCA&Lut@MN against E.coli biofilms was assessed using the same experimental procedures.

Transcriptomic analysis

MRSA cultures in the exponential growth phase were treated with CCA&Lut@MN while subjected to ultrasound stimulation (1 W/cm², 1 MHz, 8 min). Untreated MRSA served as the negative control. After incubation, bacterial cells were collected, and total RNA was extracted using the HiPure Bacterial RNA Kit (Magen, China). Transcriptomic sequencing was carried out by Guangdong Biotechnology Corporation using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in PE150 mode. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with the DESeq2 software package (version 1.20.0), and the resulting DEG dataset was subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler toolkit (version 3.8.1). Each group included three independent biological replicates.

Cell viability assay

The CCK-8 assay was used to assess the cytocompatibility of CCA nanoparticles, luteolin (Lut), and various microneedle formulations. L929 fibroblasts and RAW264.7 cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured under standard conditions for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with medium containing luteolin at 0, 10, 20, 40, or 80 µg/mL. After 6 and 12 h, CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by a 2-h incubation. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm to determine cell viability. Additionally, L929 cells were incubated directly with CCA nanoparticles at 0, 25, 50, 100, or 200 µg/mL, and metabolic activity was quantified using the same CCK-8 protocol. For fluorescence-based viability assays, calcein AM and propidium iodide (PI) dissolved in PBS were used as indicators. After co-culturing L929 cells with the different microneedle groups in Transwell chambers for 1 day or 3 days, 100 µL of the staining solution was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Fluorescence images of live cells (green) and dead cells (red) were acquired with an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon TE2000-E, Japan), and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ. Biocompatibility of the microneedle groups with RAW 264.7 macrophages was assessed using the same procedure.

Cell morphology assay

L929 fibroblasts were stained with iFluor 488-phalloidin and DAPI to evaluate cell morphology. Following treatment, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde after removing the culture medium and rinsing with PBS. Subsequently, incubation with iFluor 488-phalloidin at 37 °C for 4 h was conducted, followed by a 5-min nuclear counterstain with DAPI. Excess dye was removed through three PBS washes. Fluorescence images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Olympus FLUOVIEW FV4000MPE) and analyzed using ImageJ software (v1.53).

Antioxidant and ROS scavenging assays

For the cell-based ROS scavenging assay, L929 and RAW264.7 cells were co-cultured with microneedles from each group for 24 h, then exposed to 0.1 mM H₂O₂ for 30 min. Cells were next incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA in DMEM at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ for 20 min. Fluorescence images were captured on an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon TE2000 - E, Japan). The control group did not receive microneedle treatment or H₂O₂ stimulation. Intracellular ROS fluorescence intensity was quantified using Image J.

Flow cytometry in vitro

To assess whether CCA&Lut@MN microneedles modulate macrophage polarization, RAW 264.7 cells were treated with PBS, 0.1 mM H₂O₂, or microneedles from the indicated groups, then exposed to ultrasonic irradiation (1 W/cm², 1 MHz) for 8 min. After 24 h of co-culture, cells were incubated with anti-CD86 antibody for 30 min to label proinflammatory macrophages. Samples were then fixed, permeabilized, and stained with anti-CD206 antibody to identify the antiinflammatory phenotype. Labeled cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA).

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Cultured RAW 264.7 macrophages in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum. Seeded cells in six-well plates, allowed overnight adhesion, then replaced medium with DMEM containing 0.1 mM hydrogen peroxide. Incubated with various microneedle groups for 24 h. Extracted total RNA from cells using TRIzol reagent following the protocol, assessed RNA concentration and purity with Nanodrop 2000 ultramicro spectrophotometer. Conducted reverse transcription into complementary DNA with PrimeScript RT kit. Performed quantitative real-time PCR with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II on a LightCycler 96 Real-Time PCR System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) to measure TNF-α, iNOS, Arg-1, and IL-10 expression. Primer sequences for target genes and β-Actin housekeeping gene provided in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

Scratch and tube formation assay

After treatment with 0.1 mM H₂O₂, RAW 264.7 cells were washed, then treated according to the pre - determined groups, and cultured in fresh DMEM medium for 48 h. After removing the microneedles, the cells were further cultured in fresh medium for 24 h. The supernatant was combined with complete DMEM medium at a 1:1 ratio to produce macrophage-conditioned medium. L929 fibroblasts were plated in 24-well dishes at a concentration of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and grown until a consistent cell monolayer was achieved. Subsequently, a single scratch was created at the center of each well using a 200 µL pipette tip. The scratched cell layers were cultured in macrophage - conditioned medium of different groups or fresh DMEM medium respectively. At 0, 24, and 48 h, cell migration was observed using an optical microscope (Nikon TE2000 - E), and the wound - healing percentage was quantified using ImageJ software. For the angiogenesis assay, Matrigel with low growth factor content served as the extracellular matrix. Approximately 1× 10⁴ HUVECs were seeded into each Matrigel-coated well of a 96-well plate and incubated with either macrophage-conditioned media or fresh DMEM. After 12 h of culture, cells were stained with calcein AM. Capillary-like structure formation was visualized by inverted fluorescence microscopy (Nikon TE2000-E), and the extent of angiogenesis was quantified.

Diabetic SD rats wound model

Animal experiments followed the National Institutes of Health guidelines for Laboratory Animal Care and were authorized by the Nanchang University Ethics Committee (Approval No.: NCULAE-20250411002). Five-week-old male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from GemPharmatech Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China). Before starting the experiments, the rats were maintained under standard housing conditions for one week to allow full acclimatization. A total of 49 male SD rats were enrolled, each weighing roughly 200 g. Type-1 diabetes was induced using a well-established protocol involving intraperitoneal administration of streptozotocin (STZ) at 50–60 mg per kg of body weight [47]. The diabetic rat model was considered successfully established when blood glucose levels exceeded 16.7 mmol/L for seven consecutive days.

Animal procedures and surgeries

Following diabetes induction, the 49 SD rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 3% pentobarbital at a dose of 100 µL per 100 g body weight. After shaving and disinfecting the dorsal skin, two circular full-thickness skin wounds (1.0 cm in diameter) were generated symmetrically on each rat using a biopsy punch. The wounds were then inoculated with 100 µL of MRSA suspension (1 × 107 CFU/mL). After incubation for 48 h, the wounds developed a translucent mucous layer along with noticeable swelling of the surrounding tissue, confirming successful establishment of a biofilm-infected wound model. The 49 animals were randomly assigned to six treatment groups: (1) Control; (2) Saline (100 µL) + ultrasound; (3) CCA solution (100 µg/mL, 100 µL); (4) CCA&Lut@MN; (5) CCA@MN+ultrasound; (6) CCA&Lut@MN+ultrasound; (7) Atrauman® Ag+ dressing. Ultrasound (US) irradiation was applied for 8 min (1 W/cm², 1 MHz). Wounds were photographed on days 0, 1, 3, 7, and 14, and the wound areas were quantified. On day 3, wound exudates were collected using sterile swabs, which were eluted in 1 mL PBS to obtain bacterial suspensions. After 100-fold dilution, 30 µL of the diluted solution was evenly spread onto LB agar plates for colony enumeration, and the resulting CFU counts were used to evaluate bacterial burden in the infected wounds.

Histologic examination

The rats were euthanized at scheduled intervals, and their wound tissues were collected for histological examination. The samples were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Tissue regeneration and collagen deposition were assessed using Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining, respectively. Bacterial load within the skin tissue was examined using Giemsa staining, while the level of ROS in the wound areas was determined by DHE staining. Angiogenesis was assessed through CD31 and α-SMA immunostaining, and macrophage polarization was examined using CD206 and iNOS markers. In addition, immunohistochemical staining of HIF-1α, VEGF-A, TGF-β, IL-10, and TNF-α was performed to evaluate inflammation- and angiogenesis-associated molecular responses. At the end of the study, major organs (spleen, heart, kidneys, liver, and lungs) were collected and subjected to H&E staining to determine the in vivo biocompatibility of the microneedle patch.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses utilized GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments, with a minimum of three replicates per condition in each experiment, and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis for multiple-group comparisons was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple-comparison test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ns for no significant difference.

Results and discussion

Characterization of materials

The synthesis route for Cu-Cy-Arg nanoparticles (CCA) is illustrated in Fig. 1a. Cysteine molecules coordinate with copper ions through thiol and amino groups, forming stable Cu-S bonds and resulting in uniformly dispersed copper-cysteine nanoparticles (Cu-Cy) [14]. Subsequently, L-arginine (L-Arg) is covalently grafted onto the surface of Cu-Cy nanoparticles via an EDC/NHS-mediated coupling reaction [48]. This process involves the interaction between the carboxyl group of L-Arg and the amino group on the Cu-Cy surface, leading to the formation of Cu-Cy-Arg (CCA). This mild post-modification strategy effectively incorporates arginine without disrupting the inherent coordination structure of Cu-Cy, thereby preserving the sonosensitive properties of the Cu-Cy nanoparticles. The morphology and elemental composition of CCA nanoparticles were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). As illustrated in Fig. 1b, the synthesized CCA nanoparticles displayed a uniform particle morphology, suggesting that the modification with L-Arg did not compromise the structural integrity of the Cu-Cy framework. Elemental mapping analysis indicated a uniform distribution of Cu and S within the nanoparticles, thereby confirming the coordination of copper ions with cysteine to establish a stable copper-thiolate network. Furthermore, the characteristic signals of C and N were distinctly observable and uniformly distributed across the surface of the nanoparticles. Given that the original Cu-Cy framework contained no nitrogen apart from the amino groups derived from cysteine, the extensive N signal strongly corroborated the successful grafting of L-Arg. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis (Fig. 1c; Figure S1, Supplementary Information) revealed that the original Cu-Cy nanoparticles exhibited a narrow particle size distribution, with an average hydrodynamic diameter of around 55.32 ± 0.29 nm. Subsequent conjugation with L-Arg led to an increase in the average particle size of the resulting CCA nanoparticles to approximately 82.28 ± 0.99 nm. These alterations are ascribed to the effective attachment of L-Arg molecules onto the Cu-Cy nanoparticle surface, creating an organic layer abundant in guanidyl groups. This process enhanced inter-particle interactions via hydrogen bonding and electrostatic effects. Despite the size increase, the CCA nanoparticles remained within the optimal size range for efficient penetration of deep tissues by nanomedicines [49]. Zeta potential analysis revealed that the initial potential of Cu-Cy nanoparticles was −23.42 mV, which shifted to −18.46 mV post L-Arg modification, the increase in potential is attributed to the success of L-Arg modification [50] (Fig. 1d). The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum depicted in Fig. 1e displayed distinctive features. A comparison between the FTIR spectra of pristine Cu-Cy nanoparticles and CCA nanoparticles unveiled novel absorption peaks at 1656 cm− 1 and 1678 cm− 1. These peaks likely stem from the C = O stretching vibration of the newly formed amide bond and the C = N stretching vibration of the guanidino group in L-Arg [51]. XPS spectra (Fig. 1f) revealed that both Cu-Cy and CCA exhibited characteristic signals corresponding to Cu 2p, S 2p, C 1 s, and Cl 2p, thereby confirming the retention of the Cu-Cy nanoparticle framework following surface modification. To further elucidate the chemical state of nitrogen, we conducted a detailed analysis of the high-resolution N 1 s spectra (Fig. 1g). Notably, in comparison to pristine Cu-Cy, CCA displayed a significantly enhanced N 1 s peak at approximately 399–401 eV. This peak can be attributed to the presence of amide nitrogen (–CONH–) resulting from coupling and guanidyl nitrogen derived from L-Arg, indicating a substantial enrichment of nitrogen-containing species following L-Arg coupling [52]. These findings confirm the successful coupling of L-Arg to Cu-Cy nanoparticles, thereby demonstrating the effective preparation of CCA nanoparticles.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was utilized to examine the crystal structures of Cu-Cy nanoparticles both prior to and following modification with L-Arg. As illustrated in Fig. 1h, the unmodified Cu-Cy displayed a series of distinct diffraction peaks in the low- and medium-angle regions. These peaks are characteristic of the copper-cysteamine coordination nanostructure, aligning with previously reported findings on Cu-Cy nanoparticles [14]. Following the grafting of L-Arg, the XRD pattern of CCA closely resembled that of the pristine Cu-Cy, with no significant peak disappearance, peak shift, or emergence of new crystalline phases observed. This suggests that the modification process with L-Arg did not alter the intrinsic lattice or coordination structure of Cu-Cy, thereby preserving the structural integrity of Cu-Cy nanoparticles as a sonosensitizer. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was utilized, employing 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) and 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) as spin-trapping agents for hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and singlet oxygen (¹O₂), respectively, to further confirm the sonodynamic generation of ROS by CCA. As illustrated in Fig. 1i, CCA subjected to ultrasonic irradiation displayed characteristic four-peak EPR signals with an intensity ratio of 1:2:2:1, which are typical of the DMPO-·OH adduct. Figure 1j shows that CCA under ultrasonic irradiation produced distinct three-line EPR signals with an intensity ratio of 1:1:1, indicative of the TEMP-¹O₂ adduct. In contrast, when ultrasonic stimulation was absent (CCA + non-US) or only ultrasound, only negligible background signals were observed. We further verified CCA’s activation threshold under ultrasonic stimulation and its reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation efficiency using a methyl blue (MB) degradation assay. As shown in Figure S2, Supporting Information, CCA is activated at relatively low ultrasonic settings (0.5 W/cm², 1 MHz), and the amount of ROS produced increases with both ultrasonic power and exposure time. This ultrasound-dependent ROS generation indicates that introducing L-arginine does not impair the intrinsic acoustic sensitization of Cu-Cy nanoparticles, and that SDT efficiency can be modulated by adjusting the ultrasonic parameters.

Previous studies have demonstrated that ROS can catalyze the conversion of L-Arg to nitric oxide (NO) (Fig. 1m) [33, 53]. L-arginine is widely present in living organisms, and due to its unique guanidino group structure, it can generate NO in the presence of ROS. Leveraging this property, ultrasound stimulation of CCA nanoparticles can harness ROS to initiate the generation of NO, thereby offering an intelligent and controllable strategy for on-demand gas therapy. Nitric oxide (NO) production was indirectly quantified using the Griess method, which measures nitrite, a stable oxidation product of NO. This method relies on the diazotization coupling reaction between sulfanilamide and N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine, leading to the formation of an azo dye that exhibits characteristic absorption at 540 nm. The generated NO was quantified using a standard NO detection curve (Figure S3, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure S4, Supporting Information, NO production in the CCA + US group was significantly higher than in the Cu-Cy + US group. In the absence of L-Arg, no NO signal was detected in the Cu-Cy + US group, indicating that the NO generation observed in this study was closely related to the introduction of L-Arg. As illustrated in Fig. 1k, under 8 min of ultrasound stimulation at 1 W/cm², CCA nanoparticles produced 2.28 µM, 3.51 µM, 7.28 µM, and 11.04 µM of NO at concentrations of 25 µg/mL, 50 µg/mL, 100 µg/mL, and 200 µg/mL, respectively. CCA demonstrated a significant increase in NO production over time during ultrasound irradiation, with NO production closely correlated to the concentration of CCA. The ultrasound response and switchable NO release behavior of CCA were further examined by alternately activating and deactivating ultrasonic stimulation during the Griess assay. As illustrated in Fig. 1l, the NO concentration exhibited a rapid and significant increase under ultrasonic irradiation. When the ultrasonic irradiation ceased, the NO generation rate markedly declined, approaching baseline levels; however, upon reinitiating the ultrasonic irradiation, NO generation quickly resumed. This pronounced on-off behavior demonstrates that NO release from CCA is strictly regulated by ultrasonic stimulation and is highly reversible. As shown in Figure S5, Supporting Information, under various experimental conditions there was no significant difference in the amount of copper ions released by CCA within 24 h, and the released amount was far below the concentration required for antibacterial effects [54]. Antibacterial experiments further confirmed this conclusion (Figure S6, Supporting Information). Therefore, the antibacterial effect of copper ions released by CCA was not the focus of our study. To evaluate the biocompatibility of CCA, we systematically assessed its compatibility with L929 cells using the CCK-8 assay. As illustrated in the Figure S7, Supplementary Information, a concentration of 200 µg/mL of CCA reduced the viability of L929 cells to below 90% at both 6 and 12 h. In contrast, the viability of L929 cells co-cultured with 25–100 µg/mL of CCA was comparable to that of the control group. To achieve an optimal balance between biosafety and therapeutic efficacy, we recommend selecting 100 µg/mL as the safe concentration for subsequent experiments.

Preparation and characterization of CCA&Lut@MN patch

Luteolin (Lut) is a natural flavonoid compound known for its unique phenolic hydroxyl structure, which enables efficient scavenging of ROS [43]. Nevertheless, the poor aqueous solubility of Lut significantly hampers its biological availability [55]. The phenylboronic acid groups within phenylboronic acid-modified hyaluronic acid (HAPBA) specifically bind to the ortho-diphenol hydroxyl groups of Lut [42]. This interaction enhances Lut’s solubility and confers responsiveness to ROS. By incorporating PVA to establish reversible borate ester bonds with HAPBA, a three-dimensional polymer network hydrogel (PVA-HP-Lut) is synthesized (Fig. 2a). As illustrated in Fig. 2b, the modification of hyaluronic acid (HA) with phenylboronic acid (PBA) resulted in the emergence of additional resonance peaks in the ¹H NMR spectrum of HAPBA, specifically in the range of 7.0–8.0 ppm. These peaks, which were not present in the spectrum of the original HA, correspond to the aromatic protons of the phenylboronic acid moiety. This observation directly confirms the successful grafting of PBA onto the HA backbone. Lut was added to the deionized water and the HAPBA solution, respectively. A clear yellow solution was obtained when Lut was dissolved in the HAPBA solution, whereas only a suspension formed in deionized water (Figure S8, Supplementary Information). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy revealed (Fig. 2c) a broad -OH stretching vibration absorption band centered at 3423 cm− 1, along with characteristic absorption peaks of the benzene ring at 1620 cm− 1 and 1453 cm− 1 [56], confirming the presence of HA and phenylboronic acid groups. Following complexation with Lut, the -OH stretching vibration absorption band of HP - Lut broadened and slightly shifted, indicating the successful incorporation of Lut into the HP matrix. Upon further introduction of PVA, the FTIR spectrum of PVA–HP–Lut exhibited an enhanced and broadened -OH stretching vibration absorption band at 3423 cm− 1 [57], confirming the successful integration of PVA into the HP–Lut system.

To deliver CCA effectively to the biofilm and integrate Lut into the same system, we employed a three-step casting technique to fabricate CCA&Lut@MN with a core-shell structure responsive to ROS (Fig. 2d). Initially, CCA nanoparticles were incorporated into a hyaluronic acid (HA) matrix, and a microneedle shell layer was created using vacuum-assisted filling and drying. Subsequently, a PVA-HP-Lut hydrogel was introduced as the core layer into the microneedle cavity through a second vacuum filling process, utilizing a 20% PVA solution as the foundation. Ultimately, following drying and demolding, a CCA&Lut@MN microneedle patch with a core-shell structure was produced. Morphological characterization was conducted using both an optical microscope and a scanning electron microscope. The prepared CCA&Lut@MN exhibited an 11 × 11 square array, featuring conical tips, a base diameter of 290 μm, a height of 600 μm, and a needle spacing of 580 μm (Fig. 2e-f). Importantly, the optical microscope images revealed that the microneedles appeared pale yellow, which indicates the successful loading of luteolin. Elemental mapping scans were conducted on copper (Cu), chlorine (Cl), sulfur (S), nitrogen (N), and carbon (C) on the surface of the microneedles (Fig. 2i). The distributions of Cu, S, and N demonstrate a uniform presence of CCA in the outer shell of the microneedles. To characterize the core-shell structure, the HA-based shell was labeled with rhodamine B (RB, red), while the PVA-HP core was labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC, green). As illustrated in Fig. 2g, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images revealed a prominent red fluorescent ring at the periphery, whereas the intense green fluorescence was localized to the central region. The merged image distinctly illustrated the core-shell structure. Continuous optical scanning images of the HA shell layer, collected at various depths, further confirm the successful construction of the core-shell microneedle structure (Figs. 2h). Figure 2j indicates that the mechanical strength of the shell microneedles was 0.33 N per needle, whereas that of the core microneedles reached 1.31 N per needle. Compared with the mechanical strength of the core–shell microneedles (1.33 N per needle), the core microneedles exhibited a comparable strength, demonstrating that the core layer provides robust mechanical support for the shell layer, thereby enabling the microneedles to penetrate biofilms and skin without fracture [58]. These structural and mechanical findings collectively validate the feasibility of this device for transdermal drug delivery and deep tissue administration. To assess the insertion performance of the microneedles in skin applications, we applied CCA&Lut@MN to rat skin. Subsequently, a clear and complete array of puncture marks, corresponding precisely to the puncture sites, was observed on the skin (Fig. 2j). Histological analysis (Fig. 2k) confirmed the microneedle’s ability to penetrate the stratum corneum and extend into the dermis, indicating its capability to overcome the surface barrier and reach deep wound tissue due to the sharp conical tip and sufficient length of each needle. DHE staining was used to assess the depth of SDT effects generated by CCA&Lut@MN after transdermal penetration (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Compared with the group without ultrasound stimulation, the ultrasound-treated group exhibited extensive red ROS signals in the skin tissue, further demonstrating that CCA&Lut@MN possesses penetration-enabled activation capability and can achieve a therapeutic depth sufficient to act on the deeper layers of the skin. Moreover, we examined the in vitro drug release behavior of CCA&Lut@MN. The CCA nanoparticles in the shell layer exhibited a significant burst release effect in PBS solution, with a cumulative release amount of 92.3% within 30 min (Fig. 2l). To mimic the degradation rate of microneedles in the chronic wound microenvironment, CCA&Lut@MN was immersed in PBS-based hydrogen peroxide solutions at concentrations of 0, 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mM [59]. As shown in Fig. 2m, the degradation rate of CCA&Lut@MN increased with rising hydrogen peroxide concentration, leading to complete degradation within 24 days, 14 days, 8 days, and 4 days, respectively. As shown in Figure S10, Supporting Information, Lut release positively correlates with H2O2 concentration. At the 0.5 h time point, however, cumulative Lut release rates in the groups differed little: 0.73 ± 0.13%, 1.07 ± 0.21%, 1.14 ± 0.18%, and 1.36 ± 0.27%, respectively. This observation may be attributed to events during the first 0.5 h after microneedle application, when the hyaluronic acid shell layer that covers the surface primarily dissolves and releases. As time progressed, the groups’ cumulative Lut release rates diverged significantly, indicating that, after shell dissolution, the microneedle core began to degrade in response to the endogenous high-ROS environment and release the drug. These results demonstrate different release timing between the core and shell layers and provide strong evidence supporting the concept of microneedle programmed therapy.

In vitro assessment of antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy of CCA&Lut@MN

The antibacterial and anti-biofilm properties of CCA&Lut@MN were comprehensively evaluated. This study used methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) as representatives of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, respectively, because they are common bacteria involved in biofilm infections [60, 61]. We initially assessed the in vitro antibacterial efficacy of CCA nanoparticles under ultrasound stimulation through live (green)/dead (red) bacterial staining (Fig. 3a – b; Figure S11, Supplementary Information). Fluorescence images revealed that both MRSA and E. coli in the control group and the ultrasound-only group predominantly displayed green fluorescence, indicating a minimal bactericidal effect in the absence of CCA activation. In contrast, the introduction of CCA in conjunction with ultrasound stimulation resulted in a pronounced power-dependent antibacterial effect. At a low ultrasound power density of 0.5 W/cm2, significant inactivation of certain bacteria was observed, with survival rates for MRSA and E. coli decreasing to 46.33 ± 3.81% and 49.38 ± 1.86%, respectively. Increasing the ultrasonic power to 1 W/cm2 and 2 W/cm2 resulted in a significant reduction in the survival rates of both bacterial strains. In the 1 W/cm2 ultrasound group, the survival rates of MRSA and E. coli decreased to 4.39 ± 1.87% and 6.38 ± 2.11%, respectively. In the 2 W/cm2 ultrasound group, the survival rates of MRSA and E. coli further declined to 3.1 ± 0.85% and 5.17 ± 2.28%, respectively. Given that comparable antibacterial effects were noted at both power levels, and considering that high-power ultrasound treatment increases the risk of tissue damage [62], a power setting of 1 W/cm2 was chosen for subsequent experiments. As shown in Figure S12, Supplementary Information, the antibacterial capacities of the Cu-Cy group without ultrasonic stimulation and the CCA group are similar to those of the Control group, indicating that copper ions potentially released from the nanoparticles are not the primary contributor to antibacterial activity. The CCA + US group exhibited greater antibacterial efficacy than the Cu-Cy + US group, suggesting that L-Arg–mediated NO generation made a significant contribution to the enhanced antibacterial effect rather than the effect being solely determined by ROS or copper-related mechanisms. Previous studies show that interactions between ROS and NO generate RNS such as peroxynitrite. Therefore, we used a fluorescent probe to assess whether RNS were produced on the bacterial surface. As shown in Figure S13, Supplementary Information, the CCA + US group exhibited pronounced green fluorescence compared with the other groups, confirming RNS formation on the bacterial surface. Consistent with recent studies on the ROS/NO synergistic antibacterial system [32], the detection of peroxynitrite in bacteria in our work further supports the involvement of NO-derived reactive nitrogen species in the enhanced bactericidal process.

After encapsulating the drugs in microneedles, the in vitro antibacterial activity of CCA&Lut@MN was assessed using live/dead bacterial staining and colony counting methods. As illustrated in Fig. 3c and Figure S14 in the Supplementary Information, both the control group and the blank microneedle (MN) group predominantly exhibited green fluorescence and dense colonies, indicating minimal antibacterial activity. Similarly, the Lut@MN group demonstrated only a slight reduction in bacterial survival rates, suggesting that luteolin does not significantly contribute to bactericidal effects within the microneedle system. In contrast, the red fluorescence intensity of the CCA@MN group and CCA&Lut@MN was markedly increased. The survival rates of MRSA and E. coli decreased to 5.48 ± 1.66% and 5.32 ± 1.46%, as well as 4.7 ± 2.16% and 4.47 ± 2.07%, respectively. Concurrently, the colonies of MRSA and E. coli on the agar plates nearly vanished, with colony counts recorded at 5.49 ± 0.57 and 5.41 ± 0.51, as well as 5.26 ± 0.47 and 5.36 ± 0.70, respectively. These findings confirm that CCA, when loaded into the microneedle system, retains potent ultrasound-activated antibacterial properties. Compared with commercial silver-ion dressings and sustained-release antibacterial systems [44], CCA&Lut@MN exhibited rapid bactericidal activity (Figure S15, Supplementary Information).

To further assess the efficacy of CCA&Lut@MN in eradicating biofilms, this study developed biofilm models of E.coli and MRSA and employed confocal laser scanning microscopy to examine ROS generation in biofilms stained with the indicator DCFH-DA. ROS oxidize this nonfluorescent precursor to DCF, which gives a bright green fluorescence. As illustrated in Fig. 3d, under ultrasonic treatment, biofilms of E. coli and MRSA treated solely with CCA nanoparticles exhibited fluorescence only on the surface, suggesting that the CCA nanoparticle solution could not penetrate fully into the biofilm matrix. In contrast, biofilms of E. coli and MRSA treated with CCA&Lut@MN displayed bright green fluorescence throughout the entire biofilm structure, indicating that this composite material effectively penetrated the biofilms and generated ROS under ultrasonic treatment. We further investigated the retention time of generated reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the biofilm. As shown in Figure S16, Supplementary Information, at 0 min and 1 min, the MRSA biofilm displayed bright green fluorescence throughout. At 2 min, the green fluorescence intensity decreased markedly, and by 4 min the green fluorescence had disappeared completely. These observations indicate that the ROS decayed naturally within 4 min after generation. Confocal laser scanning microscopy was employed to examine biofilms following live/dead bacterial staining. As illustrated in Fig. 3e, in the absence of ultrasound, the MRSA and E. coli biofilms across all groups predominantly exhibited extensive green fluorescence, signifying a dense and largely intact biofilm structure. Following ultrasound stimulation, red fluorescence emerged within the MRSA and E. coli biofilm matrices in the CCA group, accompanied by observable partial dispersion of the biofilms. This phenomenon suggests bacterial death and partial biofilm disruption. Notably, the CCA&Lut@MN group demonstrated the most pronounced anti-biofilm effect, characterized by a robust red fluorescence signal and a significantly diminished biofilm density, indicating near-complete biofilm clearance. These findings suggest that dense biofilms severely impede the penetration of CCA nanoparticles; however, CCA nanoparticles delivered via microneedles can effectively penetrate the biofilms, resulting in remarkable biofilm clearance. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations revealed that following sonication, the residual amounts of MRSA and E. coli biofilms in the CCA&Lut@MN group were markedly low (Fig. 3f), with bacterial morphology exhibiting a shrunken appearance. The biofilms across various treatment groups were quantitatively assessed using the crystal violet staining method (Fig. 3g - j). The absorbance values for MRSA and E. coli biofilms in the control group, both with and without sonication, were 1.64 ± 0.19, 1.71 ± 0.11, and 1.62 ± 0.14, 1.45 ± 0.09, respectively. In the CCA&Lut@MN group without sonication, the absorbance values for MRSA and E. coli biofilms were 1.65 ± 0.17 and 1.58 ± 0.20, respectively. After sonication, these values significantly decreased to 0.21 ± 0.12 and 0.25 ± 0.06. Compared to the control group, the combination of CCA&Lut@MN and sonication achieved the removal of over 90% of mature biofilms, demonstrating exceptional biofilm clearance efficacy. This further substantiates the effectiveness of ROS combined with NO gas therapy mediated by microneedles in sterilization and biofilm eradication.

Antibacterial mechanism of CCA&Lut@MN

To investigate the antibacterial mechanism of CCA&Lut@MN under ultrasound, we performed a transcriptome analysis on MRSA bacteria, comparing the ultrasound-treated group to an untreated control group. The identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) employed thresholds of absolute log2 (fold change) ≥ 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05. We promptly assessed the transcriptomic alterations between the treatment and control groups, evaluating their significance through a volcano plot (Fig. 4a). In comparison to the control group, the CCA&Lut@MN treatment combined with ultrasound resulted in 286 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 140 upregulated genes and 146 downregulated genes. Preliminary research results indicate that the combined treatment of CCA&Lut@MN with ultrasound significantly affects the expression of specific genes in MRSA. Heatmap analysis reveals that the upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in treated MRSA are associated with ion transport and metabolism (modA, copZ). Conve~rsely, the downregulated DEGs primarily participate in several biological processes: cell membrane biosynthesis (aaa, atl), amino acid metabolism (arcA, arcC, arcD), nucleotide metabolism (queC, nrdD), translation (infC, gatC), and energy production (pflB, ald, nirB) (Fig. 4b). These findings suggest that the ROS and NO gas generated by the combined treatment of CCA&Lut@MN with ultrasound can effectively disrupt and inhibit MRSA cell membrane synthesis while enhancing antibacterial activity by interfering with metabolic processes and energy production. Notably, once the integrity of the cell membrane is compromised, copper ions can enter MRSA cells more readily. In response to this stress, bacteria upregulate the expression of the copper-handling gene copZ. This observation indicates a synergistic antibacterial effect of the copper ions present in CCA.

To clarify the functions of genes with varying expression levels, we performed a Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, classifying them into three GO categories: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF)(Fig. 4c). Within the BP category, we observed significant enrichment of functions such as cell killing, cell killing of other organisms, and cytolysis. This finding confirms at the transcriptomic level that CCA&Lut@MN under ultrasound treatment induces bactericidal effects rather than bacteriostatic ones. These processes exemplify lethal oxidative and nitrosative stress, wherein membrane damage caused by ROS and intracellular toxicity facilitated by NO act synergistically, resulting in irreversible bacterial death [63]. Notably, both the arginine metabolic and catabolic processes were significantly enriched. Since arginine serves as the direct biochemical precursor for NO production, the disruption of arginine metabolism strongly indicates that arginine-derived NO and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) play a role in mediating antibacterial activity [64]. In the CC category, the genes that are significantly affected are primarily linked to the cell membrane, extracellular region, and extracellular space, indicating a disruption of membrane-centered cellular functions. ROS-induced lipid peroxidation directly compromises membrane integrity [65], whereas NO easily diffuses across the membrane, targeting membrane-associated respiratory enzymes and transport systems [66]. At the MF level, the notable enrichment of oxidoreductase activity, toxin activity, and molecular function regulator activity suggests a general disruption of redox-sensitive enzymes. ROS are known to oxidize catalytic residues and cofactors [67], while NO and RNS can induce nitrosylation and nitration of enzyme active sites. Consequently, the concurrent enrichment of these molecular functions indicates a synergistic inactivation of enzymes mediated by ROS and NO, ultimately resulting in the failure of metabolic cascades.

Analysis using KEGG pathways (Fig. 4d) indicated significant perturbations in nitrogen metabolism and arginine biosynthesis, confirming the central role of the nitrogen metabolism network in the transcriptional response. This perturbation typifies NO-induced nitrosative stress, disrupting nitrogen flux, enzyme activity, and redox balance. Additionally, changes in purine metabolism, the pentose phosphate pathway, and COA biosynthesis pathways suggest the presence of redox imbalance and reducing equivalent depletion, common outcomes of excessive ROS exposure [68]. Ribosome pathway enrichment indicates impaired protein synthesis, suggesting that MRSA is unable to sustain translational capacity under persistent oxidative and nitrosative stress. Furthermore, the disruption of the two-component system, quorum sensing, and cationic antimicrobial peptide (CAMP) resistance pathways implies a failure in bacterial stress adaptation and defense signaling, which further heightens their susceptibility to ROS and NO-mediated bactericidal effects.

The results above collectively demonstrate that the ultrasound-activated CCA&Lut@MN microneedles’ efficient antibacterial mechanism relies on inducing synergistic oxidative and nitrosative antibacterial reactions. ROS primarily interfere with cell membrane integrity, redox balance, and energy metabolism, while NO and RNS disrupt arginine/nitrogen metabolism, inhibit redox-sensitive enzymes, and impede membrane-related transport and respiration. The combined impact of these stress patterns causes metabolic collapse, translational arrest, and the failure of adaptive responses, ultimately leading to the irreversible death of MRSA.

The biocompatibility examination of CCA&Lut@MN

Skin fibroblasts (L929) play a crucial role in supporting the extracellular matrix structure and facilitating tissue repair, while macrophages (RAW 264.7) are essential for regulating inflammation and regeneration [69]. Consequently, an optimal wound microneedle patch should foster a microenvironment that promotes the proliferation of both cell types, thereby enhancing wound healing. This study assessed the cytocompatibility of the CCA&Lut@MN microneedle patch with L929 fibroblasts and RAW 264.7 macrophages. Cytotoxicity tests indicated that when the concentration of luteolin (Lut) in the microneedles did not exceed 40 µg/mL (Figure S17a, Supplementary Information), the biological activity, as measured by cell viability, of L929 fibroblasts remained intact. A similar concentration gradient applied to RAW 264.7 macrophages yielded comparable results (Figure S17b, Supplementary Information). Therefore, a Lut concentration of 40 µg/mL was selected for the subsequent preparation of the microneedles. Cell viability was assessed using the live/dead cell staining method, where live cells displayed green fluorescence and dead cells exhibited red fluorescence. The results demonstrated that all microneedle patch treatment groups maintained high survival rates for L929 and RAW 264.7 on both Day 1 and Day 3. Specifically, the survival rates for the two cell types in the CCA&Lut@MN group on Day 3 were 94.97 ± 2.38% and 94.81 ± 1.77%, respectively, showing no significant difference compared to the control group, which recorded survival rates of 95.12 ± 1.14% and 95.35 ± 1.86% for the same cell types (Fig. 5a - c). Furthermore, during the three-day culture period, the numbers of both cell types increased significantly. The population of L929 cells rose by an average of 2.13-fold, while the RAW 264.7 cells increased by an average of 3.55-fold. The cytoskeletal morphology of L929 cells was examined through F-actin staining (Fig. 5d-e). In all treatment groups, L929 cells displayed a rhomboid morphology and exhibited rapid spreading. A distinct cytoskeletal network was apparent within the field of view. The average spreading area increased to approximately 1.61–2.19 times the initial value within three days, indicating that the microneedle patch did not hinder cell growth. No significant differences in spreading area were observed among the groups, which aligned with the results of the staining observations. These findings suggest that the CCA&Lut@MN microneedles possess favorable biocompatibility.

CCA&Lut@MN enhances ROS scavenging, M2 macrophage polarization, and tissue repair-associated cell behavior

Macrophages play a crucial role in the wound healing process [70]. During the initial phase, they facilitate the inflammatory response and eliminate pathogens, wound debris, and apoptotic cells. As healing progresses, macrophages undergo phenotypic changes that suppress inflammation and enhance tissue regeneration. However, the elevated levels of ROS in diabetic wounds can induce oxidative stress in macrophages, thereby perpetuating a pro-inflammatory state and delaying the healing process [71]. To simulate these pathological conditions, we exogenously introduced H₂O₂ (0.1 mM) into the culture medium of L929 and RAW264.7 cells. As illustrated in Figs. 6a-b, the two cell types in the group treated solely with H₂O₂ displayed elevated ROS fluorescence intensities of 85.34 ± 3.04 and 82.62 ± 3.78, respectively. In contrast, the CCA@MN, Lut@MN, and CCA&Lut@MN groups significantly decreased ROS levels in both cell types, yielding values of 50.04 ± 2.66 and 51.33 ± 2.77; 31.18 ± 2.09 and 30.55 ± 3.11; and 28.22 ± 2.91 and 22.88 ± 1.85, respectively. Importantly, the fluorescence intensities of the two cell types in the control group were 25.42 ± 2.33 and 20.84 ± 1.55, respectively, which were comparable to the ROS fluorescence intensities observed in the CCA&Lut@MN group. This finding indicates that the combined application of CCA and Lut effectively scavenges excessive ROS, thereby restoring a physiological environment.

The prompt removal of excessive ROS mitigates oxidative stress damage to cells, thereby reducing cellular inflammation. This protective mechanism can influence the phenotypic transformation of macrophages, a process that plays a crucial role in skin tissue healing [72]. Subsequently, we examined the effects of CCA&Lut@MN on the phenotypic polarization of macrophages. The flow cytometry analysis results in Fig. 6c revealed crucial insights. Treatment of RAW264.7 cells with H₂O₂ (0.1 mM) induced an inflammatory response, leading to the emergence of M1-type macrophages distinguished by high CD86 inducible marker expression (70.6%) and low CD206 M2 marker expression (1.30%). Conversely, treatment with CCA@MN and Lut@MN significantly decreased CD86 expression in RAW264.7 cells (48.4% and 44.3%, respectively) while increasing CD206 expression (17.6% and 19.6%, respectively). Remarkably, in RAW264.7 macrophages treated with CCA&Lut@MN, CD86 levels substantially dropped (8.16%), accompanied by a rise in CD206-positive cells (40.3%). This is attributed to the synergistic effect of Lut’s ability to scavenge ROS and NO gas’s role in regulating the immune microenvironment, which promotes M2 polarization of macrophages [73]. Consequently, CCA&Lut@MN more effectively promoted the transition of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the pro-reparative M2 phenotype. To further validate this conclusion, we assessed the transcriptional levels of inflammation-related cytokines in macrophages subjected to various treatment conditions (Fig. 6d-g). M1 macrophages are characterized by the expression of the surface marker iNOS and enhance the inflammatory response through the secretion of cytokines such as TNF-α. In contrast, M2 macrophages express the surface marker Arg-1 and facilitate tissue repair by releasing cytokines such as IL-10 [74]. Compared to the control group, the H₂O₂ group exhibited a significant increase in the expression levels of pro-inflammatory genes (TNF-α and iNOS) in RAW264.7 cells, along with a notable decrease in the expression levels of anti-inflammatory genes (ARG-1 and IL-10). This finding aligns with the results obtained from flow cytometry, indicating that elevated ROS levels activate the inflammatory response in macrophages and promote their transition to the M1 phenotype. Conversely, the CCA&Lut@MN treatment group significantly downregulated the expression levels of inflammatory genes while upregulating the expression of anti-inflammatory genes. These results suggest that CCA&Lut@MN combined with sonodynamic therapy can mitigate oxidative stress damage in macrophages, reshape their phenotypic transformation, and promote polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype.

Wound healing represents a complex interplay between endothelial cells and fibroblasts, a process orchestrated by macrophages through the precise regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration [75]. We subsequently investigated whether the remodeling of macrophage phenotypes induced by CCA&Lut@MN is essential for initiating and facilitating tissue repair. To this end, we prepared macrophage-conditioned medium (MCM) by combining the culture supernatants of macrophages from various treatment groups with fresh DMEM medium at a 1:1 ratio. This customized MCM was then utilized to culture fibroblasts (L929) and vascular endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Fig. 6h). To assess the impact of CCA&Lut@MN on the chemotactic migration of fibroblasts, we cultured L929 cells in either MCM or fresh DMEM medium. After 48 h, the scratch healing rates for the control group and the CCA&Lut@MN group were 78.38 ± 3.24% and 80.17 ± 2.81%, respectively. In comparison to the scratch healing rates of the H2O2 group, CCA group, and Lut@MN group (29.67 ± 1.74%, 55.35 ± 4.17%, 60.62 ± 3.84%), the migration rate of L929 cells in the CCA&Lut@MN group surpassed that of the other groups and was comparable to that of the control group (Fig. 6j, k). These findings suggest that CCA&Lut@MN enhances fibroblast motility, thereby promoting accelerated wound healing.

Establishing a well-developed vascular network is essential for tissue regeneration and repair, as it ensures the continuous delivery of oxygen and nutrients necessary for tissue metabolism [76]. By quantifying the total length of vascular formation and the number of main segments (Fig. 6i, l, m), we observed that the number of main segments and the total length of tube formation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) cultured with macrophage-conditioned medium (MCM) from the H₂O₂ group were 31.26 ± 3.34 and 34.67 ± 4.28 mm, respectively. In contrast, the CCA&Lut@MN group exhibited values of 151.64 ± 11.15 and 131 ± 8.69 mm, indicating enhanced tube-forming activity and the capacity for tubular structure formation. Notably, the CCA@MN group demonstrated a tubular structure formation ability comparable to that of the CCA&Lut@MN group, suggesting that the effect of NO gas may promote tube formation [77]. These results indicate that, under ultrasonic treatment, CCA&Lut@MN induces antioxidant stress in macrophages, leading to their polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype. Coupled with the synergistic effect of NO gas, this process facilitates the reconstruction of a functional vascular network.

CCA&Lut@MN hastens repair of bacterially infected diabetic wounds in a rat model

Based on its established in vitro antibiofilm, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and tissue repair-promoting properties, we subsequently assessed the therapeutic efficacy of CCA&Lut@MN in a biofilm-infected wound model. Following the procedure outlined in Fig. 7a, we developed a full-thickness skin-defect diabetic rat model infected with MRSA biofilms. After administering various treatments, we monitored the wounds and conducted histological analyses. The rats were randomly assigned to seven groups: (1) Control group (G1); (2) Normal saline + ultrasound (G2); (3) CCA + ultrasound (G3); (4) CCA&Lut@MN (G4); (5) CCA@MN + ultrasound (G5); (6) CCA&Lut@MN + ultrasound (G6); (7) Atrauman® Ag+ dressing group (G7). Wound sizes across all groups were imaged and quantitatively analyzed on days 0, 1, 3, 7, and 14 (Fig. 7b-d). On day 14, the relative wound area of the G6 group decreased to 2.2%. In comparison to the relative wound areas of G1, G2, G3, G4, G5, and G7 (41.9%, 37.5%, 26.3%, 24.2%, 13.7%, 27.1%, respectively), the G6 group demonstrated the most rapid wound healing rate. This outcome was attributed to the synergistic effect of sonodynamic nitric oxide gas therapy and luteolin delivered via the microneedle patch. To further evaluate the clinical potential of CCA&Lut@MN, we compared its healing efficiency with other recently reported core-shell microneedle platforms for diabetic wounds. In a similar biofilm-infected full-thickness skin defect model, core-shell microneedles with quaternary ammonium salts as the antibacterial component had a mean residual wound area of ​​38.53% on day 10 [45], while our platform had a mean residual wound area of ​​22.06% on day 7. This significant improvement is attributed to the thorough removal of biofilms by sonodynamically triggered gas therapy, which is more effective in initiating the healing process than sustained-release systems that rely solely on chemical diffusion. On day 3, exudate samples were collected from the dorsal wounds, and the bacterial load at the wound site was assessed using the plate-spreading counting method (Fig. 7e, f). Under ultrasound treatment, CCA@MN and CCA&Lut@MN significantly reduced wound bacteria, with MRSA survival rates of 11.60 ± 3.65% and 7.76 ± 2.96%, respectively. These rates were substantially lower than those observed in the G1 group (96.68 ± 3.06%), the G2 group (95.84 ± 2.93%), and the G7 group (61.8 ± 2.74%). The results of Giemsa staining revealed a substantial presence of MRSA in the wound tissues of the control group, Group G1, and Group G2 on both the 3rd and 7th days (Fig. 7g, h). In contrast, the wounds of Group G5 and Group G6 exhibited a near absence of bacteria. Notably, bacteria were also detected in the deep tissue of the commercially available antibacterial dressing group (G7), indicating that in biofilm-associated infections, relying solely on the penetration of antibacterial agents is insufficient to effectively eradicate bacteria residing in deeper tissue layers. Rats in Groups G1, G2, G3, G4, and G7 demonstrated reduced food intake and significant depletion of glycogen stores, yet they continued to meet the diagnostic criteria for diabetes. These observations suggest that Groups G5 and G6 exert local regulatory effects, implying that effective management of local infections may enhance systemic nutritional status (Figure S18, Supplementary Information). These findings further illustrate that the CCA&Lut@MN microneedle patch can effectively deliver CCA nanoparticles into the interior of biofilms, facilitating the sonodynamic generation of ROS and nitric oxide gas for effective antibacterial therapy.

We conducted a quantitative analysis of ROS at the wound site using dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescence staining to investigate the regulation of the local microenvironment in vivo. As illustrated in Fig. 8a and d, both Group G1 and Group G2 displayed strong red fluorescence signals, indicating a heightened accumulation of ROS at the wound site, a hallmark of prolonged inflammation and oxidative stress. In contrast, Group G5 exhibited significantly lower ROS fluorescence intensity compared to Group G3 and Group G7, suggesting that the presence of biofilms affects the penetration of antibacterial components, and comprehensive eradication of bacteria within the biofilm through microneedle drug delivery can partially mitigate oxidative stress. Nonetheless, the intrinsic high-ROS environment characteristic of diabetic wounds, along with ROS generated by the SDT, continues to impact local tissues. Importantly, Group G6 demonstrated the weakest DHE fluorescence signal among all treatment groups, signifying the most effective ROS scavenging at the wound site, which can be attributed to the synergistic effect produced by the sequential treatment mechanism involving the combined antibacterial activity of CCA and the ROS-scavenging of luteolin.

To further investigate the efficacy of CCA&Lut@MN microneedles in facilitating tissue repair, we employed the H&E staining method to accurately assess wound length and evaluate tissue morphology. By day 7, the wound lengths in Group G5 and Group G6 were significantly shorter than those in the control group, which had an average wound length of 8.43 mm (Fig. 8b, e, f). Notably, Group G6 exhibited the shortest average wound length at only 5.53 mm. By day 14, Groups G1 and G2 still exhibited epidermal discontinuities at the wound sites, accompanied by pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, Group G6 demonstrated the most effective repair, with complete regeneration of the epidermis. Magnified images further revealed that Group G6 possessed denser granulation tissue and a clearer epidermis-dermis interface, indicating a significant acceleration in the tissue reconstruction process. The recently reported core-shell microneedle platform with Ag as the main antibacterial component requires 18 days to reach the same healing endpoint [44], further demonstrating that the synergistic effect of sonodynamic nitric oxide gas therapy and luteolin delivered by microneedle patches can effectively promote the healing speed. On day 14, we also utilized Masson’s trichrome staining method to quantitatively analyze collagen deposition at the wound sites (Fig. 8c, g). Compared to the other groups, Group G6 displayed a stronger and more uniform blue collagen staining signal (81.17 ± 4.16%), with collagen fibers arranged more densely and orderly, resulting in a significant increase in the percentage of collagen deposition. Compared to recently reported core-shell microneedle platforms using MXene-based materials as the main antibacterial component (with approximately 60% collagen volume fraction in the wound skin on day 14) [78], this platform exhibits greater collagen deposition. This is attributed to accelerating the wound’s remodeling and repair phase after active antibacterial treatment, which, combined with subsequent Lut therapy, is beneficial for improving wound healing quality.

In vivo assessment of immunomodulation and angiogenesis

The immunomodulatory effects of the microneedle patch were evaluated in vivo by analyzing wound tissues on day 7. Immunofluorescence staining for CD86 (M1-type macrophage marker) and CD206 (M2-type macrophage marker) was performed (Figure. 9a, c, d). On day 7, the expression levels of CD86 in groups G1 and G2 were significantly elevated compared to those in the other groups, whereas the expression level of CD206 remained the lowest during this period. These findings indicate that the polarization of pro-inflammatory M1-type macrophages predominated. This model demonstrates that in diabetic infected wounds, macrophages predominantly exhibit M1-type polarization, which correlates with ongoing inflammatory activity. Following treatment in the G6 group, there was a significant decrease in CD86 expression, indicating that the microneedle system effectively suppressed M1-type macrophage polarization. Additionally, the G6 treatment group showed elevated CD206 expression compared to the control group. These results imply that ultrasound-activated CCA&Lut@MN can inhibit the tendency of macrophages to polarize toward the M1 type while promoting their polarization toward the reparative M2 type by enhancing the local microenvironment. This mechanism may facilitate the transition of wound healing from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase.

The formation of new blood vessels in the skin can mitigate scar formation and enhance overall repair quality [79]. To assess the capacity of microneedles to promote angiogenesis in vivo, we conducted immunofluorescence staining of CD31 and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) on wound tissues (Fig. 9b, e, f). CD31, a marker for endothelial cells, was employed to visualize newly formed blood vessels, while α-SMA labels vascular smooth muscle cells, facilitating the identification of mature blood vessels [80]. On day 7, both the number of new and mature blood vessels was lowest in Groups G1 and G2, indicating that angiogenesis was significantly inhibited in the context of diabetes and infection. Conversely, Group G6 displayed a higher density of CD31-positive microvascular structures, along with significantly enhanced α-SMA colocalization signals. This suggests not only an increase in the number of new blood vessels but also a tendency toward maturity and stability. Importantly, the quantity of new and mature blood vessels in Group G6 surpassed that in Group G5, indicating a stronger pro-angiogenic capacity, which may explain the faster wound healing rate observed in Group G6 compared to Group G5.

We subsequently analyzed the expression levels of inflammation- and angiogenesis-related factors in the wounds after 7 days of treatment using immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 9g-l). The results from Groups G1 and G2 indicated that in the diabetic infected wounds, the pro-inflammatory factor TNF-α exhibited strong positive staining, while the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 showed weak expression. Notably, among all treatment groups, Group G6 demonstrated the most significant upregulation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, accompanied by the greatest reduction in the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α. Furthermore, we observed that treatment in Group G6 significantly upregulated the expression of HIF-α, VEGF-A, and TGF-β. This suggests that, after controlling the infection, the copper-based nitric oxide donor CCA can stabilize HIF-α, thereby promoting the expression of VEGF-A and TGF-β [81]. These findings suggest that under ultrasound activation, CCA&Lut@MN can enhance angiogenesis and vascular maturation in infected diabetic wounds. Additionally, histological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) of key organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from the rats in each group after treatment (Fig. S19, Supplementary Information) revealed no significant differences among the groups, indicating negligible systemic toxicity. In summary, ultrasound-activated CCA&Lut@MN can not only actively and rapidly combat biofilm-associated infections, but also modulate the local microenvironment by reducing oxidative stress, thereby promoting tissue regeneration and angiogenesis and ultimately accelerating wound healing.

This study still has certain limitations. From a transformation application perspective, although the CCA&Lut@MN platform has demonstrated excellent therapeutic effects, some practical challenges still need to be considered. Compared to simpler passive release or glucose-responsive systems that are less costly and require less patient intervention, ultrasound-triggered therapy is more complex in terms of device availability and treatment standardization [82]. Currently, the accuracy of ultrasound therapy is affected by individual differences in tissue density and wound location. However, the unique advantage of our system lies in its “on-demand” controllability and the mechanochemical energy required to penetrate deep, refractory biofilms, which passive systems often struggle to achieve. With the continued development of portable and wearable ultrasound transducers, barriers related to device cost and patient compliance are expected to decrease, making this high-precision therapy more suitable for personalized chronic wound management.

Conclusion

In this work, we developed an ultrasound-activated ROS-responsive core-shell structured hydrogel microneedle system (CCA&Lut@MN), which integrates sonodynamic therapy, nitric oxide (NO) gas therapy, and microenvironment regulation. Experiments demonstrated that after the system penetrates the biofilm, CCA in the outer shell generates ROS under ultrasound stimulation and catalyzes the release of arginine-derived NO, inducing synergistic oxidative stress and nitrosative stress, disrupting the bacterial cell membrane and metabolic homeostasis, thereby rapidly eliminating bacteria and clearing the biofilm. Moreover, these antibacterial mechanisms synergize with the inherent ability of the PVA-HP-Lut core to alleviate oxidative stress, inhibit the polarization of macrophages towards the M1 phenotype, promote their polarization towards the M2 phenotype, improve the local immune microenvironment, and facilitate angiogenesis and cell migration. This dynamic multi-signal therapeutic strategy successfully promotes the repair of infected diabetic wounds, offering a promising approach for treating complex non-healing wounds.
