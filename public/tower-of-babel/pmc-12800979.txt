Accessory Biceps Femoris Tenotomy and Tenodesis to Biceps Femoris Tendon for Symptomatic Lateral Knee Snapping in Athletes
Authors: Dustin J. Kress, Patrick Waldron, Amber N. Lopez, Anderson Lee, Lucas Voyvodic, Nicolas A. Artz, Kyle Cantave, Patrick McGahan, Ajith Malige, James L. Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Snapping biceps femoris tendon represents a rare but clinically significant cause of lateral knee pain in young athletes. Anomalous tendon insertions can create pathologic subluxation across the fibular head during knee motion, resulting in mechanical symptoms resistant to conservative treatment. We describe a surgical technique for managing this condition. A 20-year-old male athlete presented with lateral knee snapping caused by accessory biceps femoris insertion on the anterosuperior fibular head. The surgical technique involves release of the accessory tendon band from its aberrant insertion and tenodesis to the native biceps femoris insertion on the posterior fibular head using figure-of-8 suture configuration. This approach preserves tendon function while eliminating pathologic snapping through anatomic restoration of the tendon vector. Biceps femoris accessory tendon tenodesis offers an effective treatment option that maintains biomechanical function while addressing the underlying pathology.

An uncommon yet clinically significant cause of lateral knee pain involves pathologic movement of the distal biceps femoris tendon across the fibular head during knee motion.1,2 The literature contains limited documentation of biceps femoris tendon−related snapping, with cases scattered throughout clinical reports rather than larger case series, reflecting the condition's rarity.3, 4, 5

Several etiologic patterns explain this pathologic tendon excursion. Most frequently, anatomical variations in tendon insertion sites predominate, particularly anomalous tibial insertions or atypical fibular head attachments.6, 7, 8 Less commonly, cases arise from tendon subluxation without insertional anomalies, morphologic abnormalities of the fibular head, or develop secondary to trauma.1,5,9

Initial management follows conservative principles, including the use of targeted physical therapy and anti-inflammatory medications.10 However, persistent symptoms often necessitate surgical intervention, particularly in young, active patients.3,8 Developmental variations in tendon anatomy create conditions in which tendon excursion generates abnormal friction against the fibular prominence.11,12 Diagnosis relies on clinical evaluation with confirmatory evidence from dynamic ultrasound assessment.13 We present a systematic approach to the evaluation and surgical management of this posterolateral knee condition, highlighting a tenodesis technique that preserves function while eliminating pathologic snapping.

Surgical Technique

Indications

Accessory biceps femoris tendon tenodesis is indicated for patients with lateral knee pain and snapping caused by anomalous accessory tendon insertion.3,8 Patients typically present with chronic lateral knee pain, particularly with activities requiring deep knee flexion.6,14 Findings on standard radiographs may be unremarkable, whereas magnetic resonance imaging may identify the anatomical variant, although clinical examination remains the cornerstone of diagnosis (Fig 1).6,13Fig 1T2-weighted axial magnetic resonance imaging of the right knee demonstrating anomalous anterior insertion of the accessory biceps femoris tendon (BF) on the anterosuperior fibular head (FH), contributing to mechanical subluxation of the proximal tibiofibular joint. Preoperative Evaluation

Patients presenting with snapping accessory biceps femoris tendon typically describe a painful, audible, or palpable snapping sensation over the lateral knee. These symptoms often are exacerbated by repetitive knee flexion, squatting, or athletic activities. During clinical examination, mechanical symptoms may be reproduced with resisted knee flexion at 90°, particularly when external rotation or posterior tendon translation is accentuated.

Patient Positioning

The patient is positioned supine with the operative thigh elevated on a bump to optimize exposure of the lateral knee structures. After the administration of general anesthesia and a supplemental peripheral nerve block, the operative extremity is prepared and draped to allow full range of motion during the procedure. All relevant anatomical landmarks, including the fibular head and lateral joint line, are marked before incision.

Surgical Approach

A 10-cm curvilinear incision is created over the lateral aspect of the proximal fibula, centered over the site of maximal clinical symptoms (Fig 2). Meticulous dissection is performed through subcutaneous tissues using Metzenbaum scissors and electrocautery. Self-retaining retractors are positioned to optimize exposure while minimizing tissue trauma. The posterior border of the iliotibial band is identified and retracted anteriorly to expose the underlying biceps femoris tendon (Fig 3). Anatomical Assessment and Dynamic Testing

After adequate exposure, systematic inspection of the biceps femoris tendon complex reveals an anomalous thick band inserting on the anterosuperior fibular head, distinct from the primary tendon insertion on the posterior fibular head. With the knee positioned at 90° flexion, mechanical snapping is reproduced, confirming this accessory tendon as the pathological source (Video 1). Dynamic assessment is performed by cycling the knee through a full range of motion, allowing direct visualization of the accessory tendon excursion that produces the characteristic snapping phenomenon.

Tendon Release and Tenodesis

The accessory tendon is meticulously dissected from its aberrant insertion using a combination of sharp and blunt dissection techniques (Fig 4A). After tendon release, the knee is cycled through a complete range of motion to confirm elimination of the pathologic snapping phenomenon. Care is taken to preserve maximum tendon length for the subsequent repair. The released tendon is then redirected posteriorly to create a more anatomic vector of pull. Using No. 2 ETHIBOND suture (Ethicon), the accessory tendon is secured to the native biceps femoris tendon at its insertion on the posterior fibular head (Fig 4B). A figure-of-8 suture configuration is employed to ensure robust fixation. Confirmation and Closure

After completion of the tenodesis, the knee is cycled through a complete range of motion to confirm resolution of the snapping phenomenon (Fig 5A). Dynamic testing reveals elimination of pathologic snapping with knee flexion-extension cycling, confirming that the accessory tendon no longer subluxates across the fibular head (Fig 5B). The surgical site is thoroughly irrigated, and meticulous hemostasis is achieved. Layered closure is performed with absorbable sutures for deeper layers and interrupted nylon sutures for the skin. The incision is dressed with Xeroform, sterile gauze, and compression dressing. Postoperative Management

The rehabilitation protocol after accessory biceps femoris tendon tenodesis progresses through 4 phases over 16 weeks. Initial management (0-4 weeks) includes protected weight-bearing with knee immobilizer and passive range of motion limited to 90°. Weeks 4 to 8 allow full weight-bearing with progressive strengthening exercises. Functional rehabilitation begins at weeks 8 to 12, with sport-specific movements and running. Return to full athletic participation typically occurs by 16 weeks. Potential complications include common peroneal nerve injury, recurrent subluxation, and posterolateral instability.

Discussion

The described accessory biceps femoris tendon tenodesis technique offers several advantages over previously reported approaches, such as preserving tendon function while maintaining proper tension in the posterolateral complex (Table 1). The literature most commonly describes 3 main surgical approaches for treating lateral knee snapping. One is complete tendon resection, which involves removing the aberrant tendon slip entirely and is most suitable for isolated accessory bands.7 Although this method provides good symptom relief, it may weaken knee flexion if substantial tendon tissue is removed. Another technique is partial fibular head resection. It is commonly reserved for when prominent bone is the source of impingement.3 This technique is rarely used as first-line treatment and risks lateral collateral ligament instability and peroneal nerve injury.3 The third technique is the most widely reported approach, involving tendon rerouting through bone tunnels.8,15,16 The tendon is released from its abnormal insertion and reattached to the fibular head using bone tunnels with interference screws or suture anchors. Advantages and Disadvantages of Accessory Biceps Femoris Tendon Tenodesis

In contrast to these established techniques, the described tenodesis technique preserves the accessory tendon through direct soft tissue repair to the native biceps femoris insertion. This approach maintains biomechanical function while eliminating pathologic snapping without requiring bone tunnels or specialized fixation devices. The technique simplifies the surgical procedure and reduces potential complications related to hardware fixation. Specifically, compared with complete resection, this technique preserves tendon function and avoids potential weakness. In addition, unlike fibular head resection, it maintains normal bony anatomy. In the case of our 20-year-old athlete, this approach shows complete resolution of symptoms and return to sports by 16 weeks postoperatively.

Regardless of the technique chosen, technical challenges include accurate identification of the accessory tendon band, protection of the common peroneal nerve, and achieving appropriate tension during the tenodesis (Table 2). Tensioning the repair with the knee in 30° of flexion prevents overconstraining the joint while maintaining sufficient tension to eliminate snapping. Pearls and Pitfalls

Snapping biceps femoris tendon is a relatively rare but clinically significant cause of lateral knee pain in young, active individuals.3,14,17 Standard imaging studies may fail to identify the anatomical variants responsible, making clinical examination essential for accurate diagnosis.6,13 Conservative management should be attempted initially but often provides limited relief.6,10 When conservative management fails to eliminate mechanical impingement, surgical intervention becomes warranted.3,8 A recent study reported significantly improved patient clinical outcome scores when treated with isolated biceps femoris repair.18 Future research should focus on longer-term outcomes with larger patient cohorts and more sensitive imaging protocols to better identify these anatomical variants preoperatively.

Biceps femoris accessory tendon tenodesis represents an effective treatment option for patients with snapping lateral knee pain due to anomalous biceps femoris insertions. The procedure is technically straightforward and can be performed as an outpatient procedure with minimal morbidity. Attention to the pearls and pitfalls can minimize the risk of complications and optimize outcomes. This technique should be considered in the surgical management algorithm for this uncommon but functionally limiting condition.
