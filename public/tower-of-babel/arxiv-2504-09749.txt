An exploration of low crossing and chiral cosmetic bands with grid diagrams
Agnese Barbensi, Daniele Celoria, Christopher Ktenidis with an appendix by Kazuhiro Ichihara, In Dae Jong, Masakazu Teragaito

Abstract
. We computationally explore non-coherent band attachments between low crossing number knots, using grid diagrams. We significantly improve the current H ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{H}(2) -distance table. In particular, we find two new distance one pairs with at most seven crossings: one between 3 1 ​ # ​ 3 1 3_{1}\#3_{1} and 7 4 ​ m 7_{4}m , and a chirally cosmetic one for 7 3 7_{3} . We further determine a total of 33 previously unknown H ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{H}(2) -distance one pairs for knots with up to 8 8 crossings. The appendix by Kazuhiro Ichihara, In Dae Jong and Masakazu Teragaito contains a construction explaining the existence of chirally cosmetic bands for an infinite family of knots, including 5 1 , 7 3 5_{1},\,7_{3} and 8 8 8_{8} .



1.
Introduction

Given a knot
K\subset S^{3}
, one can obtain a new one by a local move called
non-coherent band attachment

[
21
,
13
]
or
\mathrm{H}(2)
-move

[
7
]
. Similar to crossing changes, these moves are unknotting operations and therefore induce a well-defined metric on knots, referred to as
\mathrm{H}(2)
-distance
[
14
]
.

The first effort to systematically study
\mathrm{H}(2)
-moves is due to Lickorish, who produced the first table of knots with
\mathrm{H}(2)
-distance
1
from the unknot
[
16
]
. Since then, several methods to obstruct low distance have been found (see
e.g.

[
11
,
12
,
13
,
10
,
21
]
). Over the past decade, non-coherent band attachments have gained significant attention, partially due to their relevance in biological applications
[
21
]
.

In this paper, we perform a computational exploration of
\mathrm{H}(2)
-moves for low crossing number knots, using grid diagrams. Grid diagrams are a versatile discrete analogue of knot diagrams. They are widely used throughout low-dimensional topology and contact geometry
[
3
,
23
]
, and are the starting point to define a combinatorial counterpart to knot Floer homology
[
25
]
.

A deep result by Dynnikov
[
5
]
implies that grid diagrams for the unknot are theoretically guaranteed to simplify
monotonically
,
i.e.
without increasing complexity. While this result fails to extend to other knot types, in practice, the same method applies in general: performing local simplification moves on a grid is overwhelmingly likely to result in a diagram minimising the grid size within its knot type. This experimentally observed behaviour makes knot recognition more manageable on average than in other computational knot models. Furthermore, grid diagrams are extremely easily randomised. These two characteristics make them a computationally convenient model to investigate the behaviour of random knots and operations on them
[
2
,
1
]
.

Here, we leverage on these features to look for
\mathrm{H}(2)
-moves between knots with small crossing number. Specifically, we focus on knots with up to
8
crossings; we generate random diagrams for each of these knots, perform all available
\mathrm{H}(2)
-moves on the resulting configurations, and identify the knots obtained. As a result, we find previously unknown bands between pairs of knots, thus improving the current
\mathrm{H}(2)
-distances table. The state of the art for such a table for knots with at most
7
crossings was compiled by Kanenobu
[
13
]
. Therein, new results by Zeković
[
29
]
are included, such as single
\mathrm{H}(2)
moves connecting
7_{1}
to
5_{2}m,7_{5}m,7_{7}m
, and between
6_{2}
and
7_{2}
. Here, and in the rest of the paper, the
m
denotes mirrors, consistently with KnotInfo’s notation
[
19
]
.

We find a total of
322
distance one pairs (including the
43
cosmetic ones). These include two previously unknown between knots with fewer than
7
crossings:
7_{4}\leftrightarrow 3_{1}m\#3_{1}m
, for which it was previously only known that the distance was between 1 and 3, and
7_{3}\leftrightarrow 7_{3}m
. Some –but not all– of the ones involving knots with
8
crossings are mentioned in Zeković’s PhD thesis
[
30
]
, but different conventions and language barriers made it hard to properly compare our datasets. In our computations, we find a total of
33
pairs that were not found by Zeković.

Arguably, Zeković’s main result in
[
29
]
was the discovery of a
chirally cosmetic
band attachment for
5_{1}
. This is a
\mathrm{H}(2)
move taking one knot type into its mirror. Such band attachments are of interest due to their connections with chirally cosmetic surgeries, via the Montesinos trick
[
20
]
. To date, the only known chirally cosmetic crossings on knots with fewer than
9
crossings are the aforementioned
5_{1}
(Zeković
[
29
]
) and
8_{8},8_{20}
(Moore-Vazquez
[
21
]
). As mentioned above, here we find a single
\mathrm{H}(2)
move connecting
7_{3}
to its mirror.

Finally, it was noted in
[
21
]
that the occurrence of chiral cosmetic band attachments is extremely low, as a fraction of the sampled population. While this is still the case for our grid-based model, we note that the relative probabilities are substantially higher.

The appendix to this paper contains a way to illustrate the chirally cosmetic banding on
7_{3}
described above. Furthermore, it generalises this construction to an infinite family of knots –including, in particular, the knots
8_{8}
and
5_{1}
– providing a broader framework for understanding chirally cosmetic bandings in these cases.

2.
Grid diagrams and band attachments

A
grid diagram of size
n
is an
n\times n
square grid, divided into
n^{2}
small squares. Two sets of
n

markings
, usually denoted by
X
and
O
, are placed within the grid, so that no two markings of the same kind are in the same row or column, and each small square contains at most one marking (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1.
A grid diagram of size
9
, representing the knot
7_{3}
.

A knot diagram is then obtained by connecting the markings on the same row and column, and resolving each crossing as an overpass for the vertical strand. The size of the grid is also referred to as the grid number. An orientation of the link is encoded by following the convention dictating that the arcs go from
O
to
X
markings horizontally, see
e.g.

[
25
,
1
]
for more details. Just as for Reidemeister moves and regular diagrams, there is a finite set of
grid moves
between two grid diagrams if and only if they represent the same knot type. These moves are known collectively as
Cromwell moves
.

Figure 2.
(a)
Depicts a single portion of a grid diagram (on the left) being changed in
each of the five possible ways (on the right). The top row shows that the link diagram on the left changes by a planar isotopy if both pairs of markings are moved. The mid row shows that swapping markings of the same type in the left diagram results in a coherent band attachment. Note that the two possibilities produce equivalent diagrams. The bottom row shows that swapping two markings of different types in the left diagram results in a non-coherent band attachment. Note that in this case the two possibilities might produce non-equivalent links.
(b)
The two moves on the left diagram correspond to non-coherent band attachments yielding equivalent links.

A
band attachment
(also known as band surgery or
\mathrm{H}(2)
move) on a link
L\subset S^{3}
is the following operation: given a smooth embedding of the square
\iota\colon[0,1]^{2}\hookrightarrow S^{3}
such that
L\cap\iota([0,1]^{2})=\iota([0,1]\times\{0,1\})
, a new link
L_{\iota}
is obtained as

L_{\iota}=(L\setminus\iota([0,1]\times\{0,1\}))\cup\iota(\{0,1\}\times[0,1]).

A band attachment is called
coherent
if the two links it relates have different numbers of components, and
non-coherent
otherwise.

There are combinatorial local moves that carry out (non-)coherent band attachments on grid diagrams. While these are known to experts (see
e.g.

[
25
]
), we include an explicit description here, as they can’t be readily found in the literature.

Consider two consecutive rows in a grid (the case for columns works in the same way);
each contains one
X
and one
O
marking. We will assume for simplicity (but without loss of generality) that the markings on the lower row are not adjacent to any marking on the upper row.

We say that two consecutive rows are
interleaved
if the arcs spanned by the markings partially overlap (see the left diagram in Figure
2
(b)), and
non-interleaved
otherwise.

We can non-trivially move pairs of markings vertically within the two rows in five possible ways, while still producing a valid grid diagram. The first possibility is to move one connected XO pair of markings upwards and the other pair downwards. The effect on the link is either a crossing change or equivalent to a planar isotopy on the underlying knot diagram. These two alternatives depend on whether the two markings were interleaved or not, see the top row of Figure
2
(a) for the non-interleaving case. The second possibility is to move a single marking upward and another non-connected single marking
downward and leave the other two in the same position. In this case the effect of the move depends on whether we move two markings of the same kind (
e.g.
two
O
s), or not. In the first case, the move corresponds to a coherent band attachment, and in the second case to a non-coherent one, see the mid and bottom row in Figure
2
(a). Note that in the latter case the result is an invalid grid
G
as the link orientation is not preserved by the move. However, it is possible to exhibit a valid grid
G^{\prime}
whose unlabelled markings are the same as
G
. In our computations, non-coherent band attachments are achieved using built-in functions in
GridPym
, see
[
1
]
and Section
5
for further details and documentation. Depending on whether the rows/columns are interleaved or not, and whether the corresponding arcs are oriented in the same direction or not, exchanging one pair
X\leftrightarrow O
among the two rows can yield non-equivalent band attachments, see Figure
2
(a) mid and bottom row, and Figure
2
(b) for an example. Note that this can occur in the coherent and non-coherent case alike.

3.
Methods and results

Our computational exploration was achieved by using software in
[
15
]
, based on GridPyM
[
1
]
, see Software and data availability (Section
5
).

Here we focus on the
43
knots with crossing numbers
\leq 8
(up to mirroring, and including connected sums). Note that all these knots have grid number
\leq 10
.

Figure 3.
Distribution of crossing number and grid number for grids generated by a standard randomisation run:
1000
random moves and
400
distinct randomisations per seed diagram.

For every chiral representative (up to orientation) of the knot types considered, we select grid diagrams minimising the grid number. We then randomise each of these seed configurations by applying a customisable amount of random grid moves. This is achieved by using the
scramble_grid
function on GridPyM. This function takes as input a grid diagram
G
and a positive integer
m
. The output is a new grid diagram, representing the same knot type as
G
, obtained by applying a sequence of
m
random Cromwell moves. For a detailed description of the function and for GridPym’s documentation see
[
1
]
.

In a standard run, for each seed configuration
G
, we compute
400
different randomisations by applying the
scramble_grid
function with
m=1000
. This yields a collection of
400
complex and high-grid number diagrams, see Figure
3
.
Once this collection is created, we search for all the possible non-coherent band attachments in each of the
400
generated diagrams. We do this by following the built-in methodology explained in Section
2
, using GridPym’s functions, see Section
5
, and
[
15
,
1
]
for a complete description and further documentation. On average, this results in performing over
32,000
band attachments per seed configuration, per run. The number of runs we performed varied depending on the knot type.

Sage’s
get_knotinfo()
functionality
[
27
]
is then used to determine the resulting knot types (following KnotInfo’s conventions
[
19
]
). More details are available in the code repository
[
15
]
, see also Section
5
.

While it is often difficult to effectively simplify classical knot diagrams (see
e.g.

[
6
]
), this task is often easier in the grid diagrams model. The software GridPyM implements a simplification function that applies random row/column commutations, correspoonding to planar isotopies or Reidemeister moves, and, whenever possible
destabilisation
moves that decrease the grid’s size
[
1
]
. Although monotonic simplification is not guaranteed for non-trivial knots, in practice, this method is likely to result in a minimal grid diagram. Knot recognition is performed after multiple rounds of simplification, thus substantially decreasing computational time.

Figure 4.
Top:
A non-coherent band attachment from
7_{4}
to
3_{1}m\#3_{1}m
.
Bottom:
an explicit example of the band attachment realised in the grids model found by our computations. The band (in red) is attached on row 28.

The combinatorial nature of grid diagrams, along with their randomisation and simplification properties, makes them a simple yet effective tool for computational explorations. Note that all of our computations were performed on standard laptops and are easily parallelisable, thus making similar analysis or extensions fully accessible.

4.
Chirally cosmetic band attachments

Recall that a single non-coherent band attachment converting a knot into its mirror is called
chirally cosmetic
. Such band attachments are related to important open questions in low-dimensional topology, such as the cosmetic surgery conjecture
[
8
,
18
]
. An application of Montesinos’ trick (see
e.g.

[
26
, Sec. 6D]
) implies that if two knots are related by a single
\mathrm{H}(2)
move, then their double branched covers are related by a distance one surgery. In the chirally cosmetic case, the surgery connects the filled manifold with its mirror. As mentioned in the introduction, Zeković exhibited a chirally cosmetic band attachment for
5_{1}
. Note that in
[
22
]
Moore-Vazquez constructed obstructions to the existence of chirally cosmetic bands for alternating torus knots
T(2,n)
(with
n
square-free), using a very elegant argument involving the mapping cone formula in HFK (see also
[
17
]
). The square-free condition was later dropped by Livingston
[
18
]
, except for
n=9
(see also
[
28
]
).

Figure 5.
A chiral cosmetic band attachment on
7_{3}m

Previously, for knots up to
8
crossing, besides Zeković’s
5_{1}
example, only
8_{8}
and
8_{20}
were known
[
21
]
to admit cosmetic smoothings. Using our code, we show the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

The knot
7_{3}
admits a chirally cosmetic band attachment, displayed in Figure
5
.

Peculiarly, all these small examples (including ours) have been produced through different flavours of computational explorations; so far theory has only been used to obstruct these low-crossing examples, while producing infinitely many examples with at least
9
crossings
[
8
]
.

Remark 4.2
.

A result by Livingston
[
18
, Thm. 1]
, together with Proposition
4.1
, implies that
7_{3}\#7_{3}
is the boundary of a properly embedded Möbius band in
D^{4}
.

The double branched cover of
7_{3}
is the lens space
L(13,3)
. By following the recipe beautifully explained in
[
24
]
, it is possible to explicitly exhibit the knot in
L(13,3)
providing the lift of the core of the band shown in Figure
5
. What follows is a brief description of the procedure.

The unknotting number of
7_{3}
is
2
; therefore, we can turn it into the unknot by switching two suitable crossings. This can be done
e.g.
by performing
(+1)
-framed surgeries over two unknots linking these crossings. We then lift the core of the band and the surgery information to the double branched cover. This produces the mixed surgery diagram shown in Figure
6
. Using SnapPy
[
4
]
, we can reliably identify this as the census manifold
s463
. Filling the cusp with slopes
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
, produce
L(13,3)
and
L(13,10)
respectively. Note that these slopes are related by an orientation reversing isometry of
s463
. Additionally, one can explicitly identify the knot in
L(13,3)
to be (the mirror of)
(13,4)
-surgery on the unknotted component of
L11n219
.
