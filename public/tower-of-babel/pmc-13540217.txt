Generation of a highly versatile TCR bispecific scaffold optimized for targeting tumor-specific peptide-HLA antigens
Authors: Martin Hofmann, Felix Unverdorben, M Gabriele Pszolla, Nadine Aschmoneit, Meike Hutt, Timo Manz, Heiko Schuster, Jens Hukelmann, Andreas Acs, Clara Tenkerian, Sarah Missel, Michael Mølhøj, Christoph Schräder, Claudia Wagner, Dominik Maurer, Sebastian Bunk, Carsten Reinhardt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRedirection of T lymphocytes toward cancer cells has been one of the most promising treatment concepts in oncology developed over the past years and leading to multiple regulatory approvals for both adoptive cell therapy (ACT) and T-cell-engaging bispecific (TEB) molecules. However, progress has been achieved predominantly in hematological indications by aiming at so-called lineage antigens (i.e. CD19, CD20, BCMA). Targeting of peptide-HLA antigens (pHLA) has been proposed as a strategy to overcome the paucity of suitable surface antigens in solid cancers, yet the technical hurdle to specifically address this class of low copy and highly promiscuous antigens has so far hampered its use in a broader patient population. We here describe the successful development of TCER (T-Cell-Engaging Receptor), a novel and highly versatile class of T-cell receptor (TCR)-based TEB with optimized in vivo efficacy, tolerability, plasma half-life, and stability characteristics for a broad use targeting pHLA in oncology and beyond.KEYWORDS: (TCR-based) T-cell-engaging bispecifics/(TCR-based) bispecifics, pHLA targets, platform, solid tumors

Introduction

Over the past years, immuno-oncology has become a major treatment paradigm for cancer patients with checkpoint inhibitors (CPI) being the frontrunner of this development.1 While CPI reactivate preexisting tumor-specific T cells in responding patients, many cancer indications have a low mutational burden and typically do not respond to CPI treatment.2 Specific redirection of immune cells to antigens expressed on malignant cells has become an established treatment option with adoptive T-cell therapies (ACT) and T-cell-engaging bispecific (TEB) antibodies being the most promising approaches.3–5 Many distinct TEB formats are currently investigated in clinical trials6,7 and even more are tested preclinically.8 In general, TEBs are composed of recruiting and targeting domains enabling simultaneous binding toward effector and target cells, respectively. Thereby, the majority of TEBs are making use of anti-CD3 recruiters to engage T cells, which, irrespective of their intrinsic specificity, induce anti-tumor cytotoxicity upon simultaneous TEB binding to the tumor target. TEB-mediated tumor cell killing involves formation of artificial immunological synapses9 resulting in lysis of target cells,10,11 which is driven by both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.12,13 Furthermore, targeted killing by TEBs can induce bystander killing of adjacent target-negative cells.14 This feature may especially be beneficial for targeting solid tumors, which are known to contain a heterogeneous expression pattern of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs).15

For hematological malignancies, TEBs targeting cell surface antigens, such as blinatumomab targeting CD19,3,16,17 glofitamab targeting CD20,18 and elranatamab targeting B‑cell maturation antigen (BCMA),19 have already been proven to be effective and have gained approval. However, targeting solid tumors has remained challenging in the past years due to various reasons, such as lack of suitable TAAs20 and limitations of tested molecular formats, partially resulting in suboptimal tolerability, lack of clinical activity, immunogenicity, manufacturing issues, and/or limited half-life.21–24 More recently, encouraging results were obtained in solid tumor indications such as with tarlatamab, a delta-like ligand 3-targeting TEB, achieving an objective response rate (ORR) of 40% and well manageable tolerability when small‑cell lung cancer (SCLC) patients received infusions every other week.25 Furthermore, xaluritamig targeting STEAP-1 showed promising initial efficacy with 48% ORR in the treatment of patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer.26

A critical step for developing safe and efficient immunotherapies targeting solid tumors is the identification of suitable tumor targets. Classical bispecific formats, such as BiTEs, making use of antibody-derived targeting domains, are limited to binding antigens expressed on the surface of malignant cells. Few, if any, of such antigens have been shown to be truly cancer-specific and finding potential therapeutic windows to target, e.g., HER2 or CEA, has been challenging when applying potent immunologic mechanisms of action.27,28 In contrast to rather few addressable surface proteins, the majority of potential TAAs derive from (non-surface) intracellular proteins,29–31 including viral, non-coding, mutated or cancer/testis antigens.32–34 Accessing this pool of potential tumor-specific or tumor-associated antigens is possible by targeting peptides presented at the cell surface in the context of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules (pHLA). Identifying pHLA targets which are ideally homogeneously presented on tumor cells at relevant copy numbers usually requires the integration of multidimensional datasets to aid target identification and/or clinical biomarker development.35 Preferentially expressed antigen in melanoma (PRAME) is a well-described intracellular cancer-testis antigen overexpressed in many different solid tumors and hematological malignancies36–38 and served as a model antigen within the scope of this study.

Using antibody (mAb)-based pHLA binders for TEBs has so far not shown significant clinical results as indicated by the termination of exemplary programs against both MAGE-A4 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05129280) and WT1,39 or are still in early evaluation with no clinical responses reported so far.40 On the other hand, high‑affinity and high‑specificity pHLA binders can be generated by making use of T-cell receptor (TCR)-derived targeting domains,6 which can efficiently target pHLA molecules even if presented at low copy numbers per cell.39,41 Proof of concept for pHLA targeting using TCR-based compounds in solid tumors was recently generated by the approval of tebentafusp targeting an HLA-A *02-restricted glycoprotein 100 (gp100) peptide.6 In a phase 3 trial, weekly tebentafusp treatments were able to prolong overall survival of uveal melanoma patients. However, tumor responses (ORR) were limited leaving potential room for additional optimization of pHLA-targeting TEBs.23

In this study, we describe the optimization of a TEB format designed to efficiently target pHLA-derived tumor antigens. Based on a PRAME-specific TCR-derived targeting domain, we designed various molecular scaffolds differing in valency of the recruiting and targeting domains and molecular size. The goal for an optimized molecular scaffold was to identify and combine critical elements like the T-cell-recruiting antibody domain, stability and developability (i.e. CMC, chemistry, manufacturing, and control) properties, in vivo half-life, as well as overall potency and safety characteristics. Non-clinical assessment and validation of 22 different molecules revealed that the T-cell-engaging receptor (TCER) format was superior to all other tested formats regarding the assessed elements, as outlined in this manuscript.

Results

PRAME is a suitable pan-cancer target for the generation of TCR-based therapies

Aiming to establish a TEB format optimized for targeting of pHLA-derived antigens, PRAME was chosen as a model target due to its high prevalence on various solid tumors38 alongside very low expression, if any, on normal tissues. Using mass spectrometry (MS)-based immunopeptidomics in over 750 tumor tissues of different etiology and 750 healthy tissue samples from across the human body, a highly specific and broadly presented PRAME-derived and HLA-A *02:01-presented peptide (out of more than 40 detectable PRAME epitopes) was identified as a preferred target for a TEB approach (Figure S1A). The absolute quantification of the PRAME pHLA in selected tumor indications revealed a typical presentation at over 100 copies per cell (CpC) with an interquartile range of 50 to 300 CpC measured on patient tumor samples (Figure S1B). Furthermore, a correlation analysis of RNA sequencing (RNAseq) and MS-based peptidomics data was performed to establish an mRNA expression threshold which allows the prediction of PRAME pHLA presence on tumor cells of patients.35 Applying this threshold to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database revealed a ubiquitous and highly prevalent PRAME pHLA expression in a broad range of solid tumors (Figure S1C).

Taken together, these data show that the identified PRAME pHLA is a suitable target for TCR-based immunotherapies as it is highly cancer-specific and presented in the context of HLA-A *02:01 at relevant target peptide levels on tumor cells across several indications.

Design and production of a variety of different TCR bispecific formats reveals superior stability of the TCER scaffold

In contrast to classical cell-surface tumor antigens, different pHLA antigens share nearly identical characteristics in terms of cell membrane proximity and target geometry. Based on these unique features, we aimed for developing a universally applicable TCR bispecific format capable of targeting potentially all pHLA with the highest potency and safety while exhibiting optimal developability characteristics. We generated and evaluated in detail seven distinct (prototypic) TCR bispecific scaffolds based on an affinity-matured, single-chain variable domain TCR (scTv) targeting the identified PRAME pHLA. The scTv was identified in the course of consecutive TCR discovery and engineering campaigns resulting in two affinity-matured scTv (more than 1,000-fold matured affinity vs wildtype TCR) of which the one with higher PRAME pHLA binding specificity was selected for TCR bispecific generation (Figure 1).

Incorporation of the scTv into the seven bispecific scaffolds allowed for monovalent or bivalent PRAME pHLA binding and T-cell binding with four, two, and one formats representing a 1 + 1, 2 + 1, and 2 + 2 design, respectively (Figure 2A). Besides valency differences, the bispecific scaffolds were designed with different distances between the two binding sites and with the absence or presence of effector function-silenced immunoglobulin (Ig)G1-derived Fc domains for half-life prolongation. In case of hetero-dimeric proteins, well‑established knob-into-hole mutations and a stabilizing interchain disulfide bond were introduced into the constant heavy chain region (CH)3-domains.42 To investigate the impact of T-cell recruitment, all scaffolds were tested in combination with variable domains from three different T-cell-recruiting antibodies, binding to different epitopes of the TCR/CD3 complex. We used humanized variants of an SP34-similar antibody (with remediated deamidation site) and UCHT1 antibody (variant 9)43–45 binding to a linear epitope on the N-terminus and a conformational epitope of human CD3ε, respectively. To the best of our knowledge, the epitopes of the SP34-similar antibody and UCHT1 antibody (that is largely overlapping with the OKT3 antibody epitope), are identical or very similar to the majority of CD3 epitopes targeted with current T-cell-engaging bispecifics.46 In addition, we generated a newly humanized variant (binding-optimized variant termed BMhanced) of the BMA031 antibody43 binding to an epitope formed by the constant regions of αβ TCRs and the CD3 chains.

Protein yields for the different scaffolds varied in a range of 2 to 75 mg per liter of cell culture supernatant. The monomer content of the purified proteins, as assessed by analytical size-exclusion high-performance liquid chromatography (SE-HPLC), was ≥90% indicating a successful production (Table 1), which was also further corroborated by non-reducing sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis showing corresponding bands at the expected molecular weights for all protein preparations (Figure S2A).

We next investigated scaffold-dependent stability differences by analyzing the content of monomers and high-molecular-weight species (HMWS) in preparations subjected to accelerated stress conditions at 40°C for 14 d. Notably, the analysis revealed that the TCER format exhibited the highest stress stability among all different bispecific scaffolds and regardless of the recruiting antibody used, resulting in ≥90% of TCER monomer content and less than 10% HMWS content (Table 1). Compared to TCER, all six alternative formats showed a reduced stability performance across the three different recruiting domains as indicated by a twofold or higher increased amount of HMWS species detected after the stress period. Lowest stability post‑stress was observed for the 87 kDa bispecific format with only 14% to 17% monomer content and 67% to 72% HMWS content. The uniformity of the data across three different T-cell recruiters suggests that the TCER design has a positive impact on the stability of the matured TCR, whose intrinsic stability characteristics are rather poor given its natural role as membrane-bound receptor. To confirm the positive impact of the TCER design on TCR stability, a human immunodeficient virus (HIV)-specific TCR (868Z11) was incorporated into the format.47 In this independent dataset, the TCER format rescued potency and functional behavior of the TEB molecule during 40°C heat stress for 1 week, while other formats, such as the 75 kDa, 87 kDa, 150 kDa, and 200 kDa scaffolds, showed heat-stress-mediated loss of potency and/or specificity (Figure S2B).

TCER format mediates highest cytotoxic potency against PRAME-positive tumor cells

To evaluate functional properties of the different bispecific molecules, we next assessed the T-cell-dependent cytotoxic activity (in vitro lactate dehydrogenase [LDH] release assay) in the context of tumor cell lines presenting the target PRAME pHLA at different levels. As cells present a multitude of different peptides on their surface, it is essential to confirm the target-specificity of our matured TCR by determining the binding properties to the PRAME peptide versus 10 sequence-similar peptides, all presented in the context of HLA-A *02:01 (Table S1). These peptides were a predefined, sequence-homology-based panel used during the initial TCR discovery and engineering campaign. Human 9-mer peptide candidates were ranked primarily according to their sequence similarity to the PRAME target peptide SLLQHLIGL and were subsequently prioritized based on compatibility with the HLA-A *02:01 binding motif and, where available, evidence of natural presentation and normal-tissue relevance. Nine of ten sequence-similar peptides plus one irrelevant peptide did not show reactivity in the BLI measurement, only one peptide, SP8, demonstrated minor recognition with a KD window toward PRAME of more than 50-fold. T2 cells loaded with the PRAME peptide showed efficient target cell killing, whereas T2 cells loaded with SP8 peptide did not elicit measurable cytotoxicity, precluding determination of an EC50 (Table S1). Importantly, the 10 peptides were not intended to represent the complete specificity or safety assessment. To further investigate specificity of the binding moiety, BLI measurements were performed against a broad panel of peptides (>100) presented in the context of HLA-A *02:01, without substantial reactivity against any of them (data not shown).

For the assessment of the cytotoxic activity, we used Hs695T and A375 tumor cells presenting 400–550 CpC and 50 CpC, respectively, representing the higher (PRAMEhigh) and lower (PRAMElow) physiological range of PRAME pHLA expression (Figure S1B). Furthermore, we used netMHCpan4.0,48 to predict the binding of the PRAME peptide SLLQHLIGL to the HLA class I alleles expressed by the cell lines used for the cytotoxicity assays. Other than HLA-A *02:01, this specific PRAME epitope is predicted to not strongly bind to any other allele, and hence the observed cytotoxicity can be attributed to the interaction between the TCER and PRAME pHLA presented in the context of HLA-A *02:01 only. We also confirmed the very limited or absent binding of the PRAME peptide SLLQHLIGL to other HLA-alleles by cytotoxicity assays including PRAME-positive, but HLA-A *02:01 negative cell lines (data not shown). In conditions with higher PRAME pHLA expression (Hs695T cells) we observed pronounced differences in the potency of the bispecific scaffolds with the TCER and fragment antigen-binding (Fab)-scTv (75 kDa) formats exhibiting the highest potency regardless of which T-cell-recruiting antibody was used (Figure 2B). While the 2 + 2 format (200 kDa) also showed high cytotoxic potency against Hs695T cells, this format exhibited pronounced cytotoxicity against PRAME-negative T98G cells suggesting a relevant unspecific activity of this format. Interestingly, under conditions with lower PRAME pHLA expression (A375 cells), the TCER format uniformly exhibited the highest cytotoxic activity (Figure 2C), while its activity against PRAME-negative T98G cells was among the lowest of the tested bispecific scaffolds (Figure 2D). Consequently, in a direct comparison with the other six formats, the TCER format seemed to be the only format providing a therapeutic window for targeting tumor cells with low PRAME pHLA expression without inducing similar activity on PRAME-negative T98G cells arguing for highly potent and specific TCER interaction with PRAME pHLA and human T cells. Notably, T98G ranks among the top 20% of HLA-A expressing cell lines.49 These high levels are mirrored by a correspondingly deep immunopeptidomics coverage,50 providing a comprehensive HLA-A *02 peptidomics background including potentially cross-reactive peptides. The higher potency of the TCER format is potentially attributed to the short distance between antigen-binding sites that, according to related antibody structures, is in the range of 30 Å51 corresponding to the size of only a single Ig variable domain52 (Figure 2A). In line with the assumption that a short distance between antigen‑binding sites has a positive impact on potency, we observed a potency decrease among different 1 + 1 formats when the distance of the binding sites was increased from three Ig domains (75 kDa) to four Ig domains (87 kDa and 125 kDa) (Figure 2B).

To gain more data concerning this assumption, an additional array of molecules was designed and generated. Here, instead of the cross-over design of the TCER architecture, the variable domains derived from the TCR and the recruiting antibody BMhanced, respectively, were included as single-chain Tv and single-chain Fv (Figure 3(A), see also Table S2 for more details). After purification and quality control including confirmation of comparable affinity to pHLA, the resulting proteins were evaluated and compared to the respective TCER in LDH-release assays to quantify its T-cell-dependent cytotoxic activity. The TCER demonstrated superior cytotoxic potency, whereas remarkably the scFv-scTv-Fc molecules only showed marginal T-cell-dependent lysis of the highly PRAME-positive target cells (Figure 3B), which is not explained by stronger binding of the TCER toward T cells compared to the scFv-scTv-molecules as determined by flow cytometry (Figure 3C). Further analysis by SE-HPLC (size exclusion) revealed later elution of the TCER compared to all scFv-scTv-Fc-molecules, despite comparable molecular weight (data not shown) confirmed a smaller hydrodynamic radius which further supports the hypothesis of distance and rigidity between the two binding sites are relevant for the superior potency of this molecular scaffold.

BMhanced, a novel T-cell recruiter, binds with low affinity to T cells and promotes their anti-tumor functions beyond cytotoxicity

Based on its high cytotoxic potency, the TCER format was selected for further studies investigating the impact of the recruiting moiety on the functional behavior. For an optimal comparison of recruiters, we used a modified UCHT1 variant (V20 with slightly lower CD3 binding affinity [Table S3]) to achieve an equipotent cytotoxic activity among TCER molecules with BMhanced, UCHT1(V20) and SP34-similar recruiting antibodies. As demonstrated with PRAMEhigh Hs695T tumor cells, the three TCER molecules with different recruiters exhibited very similar cytotoxic activity in vitro (Figure 4A). Interestingly, despite their similar potency, we observed pronounced differences in binding to human CD3+ T cells among the recruiter-modified TCER as assessed by flow cytometry. While TCER with UCHT1 (V20) and SP34-similar recruiter exhibited strong and intermediate binding, respectively, to human primary T cells (Figure 4B) and Jurkat T cells (Figure S3), TCER molecules with the BMhanced recruiter demonstrated only marginal T-cell binding. This low binding activity could, at least partially, be attributed to the TCER architecture as the constructed scFv-scTv-Fc molecules showed significantly stronger binding toward T cells (Figure 3). Driven by the unexpected finding that such a low affinity T-cell binding of the BMhanced recruiter still allows for exhibiting high cytotoxic potency, we further explored the functional characteristics of the different TCER molecules. Notably, using a nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) reporter assay based on human Jurkat T cells, we found that TCER designed with the BMhanced recruiter mediated strongly increased NFAT activity in response to PRAME-positive tumor cell lines, while TCER with UCHT1 and SP34-similar recruiting antibodies induced much lower NFAT activity (Figure 4C). We next assessed if an increased NFAT activation detected in BMhanced-TCER-recruited T cells would also translate into augmented anti-tumor functional responses apart from cytotoxicity, such as cytokine release and proliferation. We observed that, in comparison to TCER with UCHT1 and SP34-similar recruiter, human primary T cells recruited to PRAMEhigh Hs695T tumor cells with BMhanced-TCER secreted significantly higher levels of cytotoxic mediators and effector cytokines, such as granzyme B, interferon (IFN)-y, and interleukin (IL)-2 while the release of IL-6 was not as pronounced (Figure 4D). In line with these observations, we also detected an increased proliferation among CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in response to PRAMEhigh Hs695T tumor cells when compared to TCER based on UCHT1 or SP34-similar recruiting antibodies (Figure 4E). Taken together, our data suggest that TCER molecules designed with the BMhanced recruiter not only confer low‑affinity T-cell binding but also have the potential to augment anti-tumor responses via promoting T-cell effector functions beyond cytotoxic activity.

TCER with BMhanced recruiter exhibit improved control of tumor xenografts in mice

Based on our in vitro observations of increased T-cell functionality, activation, and proliferation induced by TCER molecules incorporating the BMhanced recruiter, in relation to those with UCHT1 and SP34-similar recruiters, we asked if these effects would translate into improved in vivo tumor control. We, therefore, established a Hs695T (PRAMEhigh) tumor xenograft model in immunodeficient NOG mice in which human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) are used as effector cells. Upon three intravenous treatments of established Hs695T tumors with a dose of 0.025 mg/kg of TCER molecules containing either BMhanced, UCHT1, or SP34-similar recruiters, we observed pronounced differences in tumor size at the study end. While five out of six mice treated with BMhanced-TCER were in tumor remission including one complete remission, TCER molecules using SP34-similar and UCHT1 recruiters resulted in weak tumor control with none out of six and one out of six mice in remission, respectively (Figure 5A–B). The improved tumor control of the BMhanced-TCER is presumably driven by the effector function-promoting feature of the BMhanced recruiter. In addition, the less affine T-cell binding of the BMhanced TCER may have resulted in improved biodistribution with TCER enrichment at tumor site and reduced trapping in T‑cell-rich tissues as demonstrated in studies comparing T-cell recruiters with high and low CD3 affinity.53–55

Low‑affinity BMhanced recruiter reduces risk of TCER for unspecific T-cell activation

The high binding affinity for T cells of first and second generation T-cell engagers has been discussed as being causative for the prohibitively narrow therapeutic windows of these drugs due to provoking dose-limiting toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS).56,57 We thus analyzed if the BMhanced recruiter with its low T-cell binding affinity would confer an improved safety profile under conditions where the target pHLA is not present. We used human whole blood of six HLA-A *02-positive and two HLA-A *02-negative donors and assessed at which TCER dose levels target (PRAME pHLA)-independent cytokine release is induced. As shown in Figure S4 and Table 2, an increased induction of cytokines was observed for TCER molecules with intermediate/strong T-cell binding (SP34-similar and UCHT1) compared to the low affinity-binding BMhanced. Among the most sensitively induced cytokines, we found IFN-y, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and IL-8, which are rapidly released from TEB-activated T cells as well as monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1), macrophage inflammatory protein 1 (MIP1)-α, and IL-6, secondarily induced from the myeloid cell compartment58 with IL-6 representing a main driver of CRS symptoms in TEB-treated patients. Along with the increasing T-cell binding affinity for the recruiter variants BMhanced < SP34-similar < UCHT1, we observed an increasing number of blood donors with detectable induction of cytokine release. This recruiter affinity-driven cytokine release in human whole blood was confirmed for different cytokines and chemokines as depicted in Table 2. While the strong and intermediate T-cell-binding recruiters UCHT1(V20) and SP34-similar led to detectable release of three or more cytokines at TCER concentrations of 0.4 µg/mL and 2.0 µg/mL, respectively, the low‑affinity T-cell-binding recruiter BMhanced showed such a cytokine release only at 5–25-fold higher TCER concentrations of 10 µg/mL. Interestingly, among the two HLA-A *02-negative donors, no cytokine induction was detected with the BMhanced-TCER while TCER based on SP34-similar and UCHT1 recruiters led to a limited and distinct whole blood cytokine release, respectively, suggesting that high‑affinity recruiting arms are sufficient to provoke whole blood cytokine release independent from a pHLA-driven mechanism.

Discussion

T-cell-engaging bispecifics (TEBs) based on antibody moieties directed against surface targets have shown substantial activity in different hematological malignancies and are now transforming the treatment of “liquid” tumors, i.e. hemato-oncology indications.59 Development of TEBs is slowly progressing also into solid tumor indications, but therapeutic developments are complicated by the paucity of tumor-specific target molecules expressed on the cell surface.59 Targeting of intracellular tumor antigens presented as pHLA complexes on the cell surface has been postulated to bear great potential to overcome these limitations, which is underlined by the approval of Tebentafusp,6 a bispecific molecule binding to human CD3 and to a peptide fragment of gp100 presented on HLA-A *02:01 molecules. Tebentafusp incorporates an affinity-engineered TCR, whose natural ability to discriminate different HLA-presented peptides was retained during affinity-engineering. The highly specific pHLA recognition of TCRs and the inability of antibodies in mimicking such pHLA interactions60 led to an increased interest in generating TCR-based TEBs, and several new molecules are in clinical development.6 Despite this interest, there is limited knowledge regarding an optimal molecular design of TCR-based bispecifics representing an obstacle in the field. In this work, we aimed to determine the most optimal bispecific scaffold for TCR-based TEBs by exploiting various molecular designs on the basis of an affinity-engineered PRAME-specific TCR. Of particular interest is the fact that, in contrast to classical surface proteins with a variety of proximal and/or distal binding domains, different pHLA targets would have almost identical three-dimensional properties which would allow the use of a potential optimal scaffold for all relevant pHLA targets. A key challenge for the generation of potential development candidates lies in the finding that, compared to classical, surface-exposed tumor antigens, pHLA complexes are presented at significantly lower copies per tumor cell. Absolute quantification studies report abundances ranging from only a few copies per cell for mutant neoantigens to hundreds or occasionally more than 1,000 copies per cell for selected tumor-associated pHLA targets.61–63 The observed median abundance of approximately 100 PRAME pHLA copies per cell, therefore, positions PRAME well within the established range of therapeutically relevant pHLA targets. Yet, even these comparatively abundant pHLA complexes remain substantially less numerous than conventional surface protein antigens, making very high potency critical for TCR-based TEB development. The three major players affinity, avidity, and functional avidity are governing the pHLA recognition of a TCR and subsequent T-cell activation. TCRs with high affinity and long half-life of TCR-pHLA binding kinetics have been shown to sense lower peptide-MHC densities.64 However, it has been shown that there is a certain threshold of affinity and avidity above which further affinity enhancement or selection of supraphysiological avidities in T-cell clones would not translate into favorable in vivo responses.65 Most naturally occurring TCRs have affinities ranging from 1–100 µM,66,67 which can be improved up into the pM range.68–70 Although affinity-enhanced TCRs demonstrate increased recognition of tumor cells with low epitope density,71 they are more likely to demonstrate cross-reactivity to similar peptides68,72,73 or attenuation of biological activity when reaching supraphysiologic affinities.74 Potency and selectivity of TCR bispecifics not only depend on the binding affinity of the TCR for the pHLA but also on the affinity of the CD3 recruiter and the mode of action thereof. It has been shown that unwanted effects due to off-target recognition and interaction with the HLA backbone of a high‑affinity TCR decrease with lower affinity of the CD3 moiety, resulting in improved selectivity.75 Thus, antigen sensitivity and specificity is determined by the balance of affinities of the TCR for the target peptide and the T-cell recruiter. No formal comparison of antigen sensitivity has been done of (affinity-matured, single‑digit nM affinity) TCER to (wild-type, single‑digit uM affinity) TCR-T; however. we have been able to show that TCER are able to activate T cells when co-incubated with target cells in the low/medium double-digit copy range.

In our search for an optimal scaffold, we designed various formats covering different target to recruiter distances and rigidities as well as different valencies of target binding. Three molecules were designed for bivalent recognition of pHLA following the rationale that increased valency may positively impact potency behavior. Within the set of three bivalent TCR bispecifics, we did not observe a potency-enhancing effect of an additional TCR binding domain for the 125(+25) kDa bivalent design. The two other bivalent formats (150 kDa and 200 kDa) differed strongly in terms of potency. Using two (200 kDa) instead of one Fab arm (150 kDa) for T-cell recruitment seemed to markedly enhance cytotoxic activity. However, the potency gain of the 200 kDa format was associated with high unspecific activity against PRAME-negative T98G cells, potentially due to the bivalent binding to T cells and thus the nonspecific activation. This effect may have contributed to the increased potency seen with target-positive cells. A missing beneficial effect of the bivalence of the target‑binding moiety might be attributed to the low PRAME pHLA density on the target cell and, consequently, to the lack of an avidity effect, which would be expected for densities in the range of thousands of target molecules.

Among the seven tested bispecific scaffolds, the TCER format exhibited the highest cytotoxic potency independent of the type of T-cell-recruiting antibody used. Notably, under conditions with low copy PRAME pHLA presentation (A375 cells with ~50 CpC), only the TCER format was capable of inducing target-specific tumor cell lysis, while the other six formats demonstrated no A375-directed cytotoxicity beyond the background activity specified with PRAME-negative T98G tumor cells; in those cases of low target copies resembling physiological conditions of tumor presentation, an avidity effect may also not be expected even if molecules are designed with more binding sites due to the low likelihood of dual antigen encounter.

The superior cytotoxic potency of the TCER format can presumably be attributed to its short distance and high rigidity between the two antigen‑binding sites as recently described for antibody-based TEBs with related architecture.51,52 Based on our selection of bispecific scaffolds, we could not systematically explore the relevance of antigen‑binding sites distances. However, there was a trend that increased distances, estimated by the number of intervening immunoglobulin domains (75 kDa scaffold versus 87 kDa scaffold and 125 kDa scaffold) led to a reduction of cytotoxic potency in line with previously published observations.52,76,77 Also, in line with prior reports,76,78 the superior potency of the TCER scaffold over respective scFv-scTv-Fc constructs further supported the assumption that rigidity of the conjunction of targeting and effector domains is an important parameter for TEBs. Taken together, we found that minimizing the distance and at the same time having a rigid combination of pHLA-targeting moiety and T-cell recruiter results in the most potent TEB.

In addition to the bispecific scaffold design, the characteristics of the T-cell-recruiting Ab are increasingly recognized as a major determinant for optimal TEB performance. Lowering the affinity of T-cell-recruiting Abs seems to be a focus of current attempts to improve TEB design for optimizing function. TEBs with low‑affinity recruiting Abs have been linked with a more beneficial in vivo biodistribution that is driven by reduced trapping of TEBs in T‑cell-rich tissue and facilitated enrichment at the tumor site.78,79 Recruiters with low affinity have also been shown to enable uncoupling of T cells’ cytotoxic activity and cytokine release to some extent,78,79 thereby reducing the risk for cytokine release syndrome generally associated with TEB treatment80 albeit this is controversially discussed.75 Furthermore, benefits of low-affinity recruiters have been attributed to the establishment of an optimal bridging time between tumor cells and T cells, while high-affinity recruiters are linked with a prolonged, non-optimal bridging time, and thus reduced activity.75 Detrimental effects of high recruiter affinity and associated prolonged engagement may also be caused by a reduced capability of T cells to elicit serial tumor cell killing, which has rather been described as a mechanism for TEBs with relatively low recruiter affinity.11,75 Despite these observations, the vast majority of TEB molecules in clinical use to date rely on the T-cell-recruiting antibodies UCHT1 and SP34 or derivates thereof, all with relatively high affinity.46

Here, we investigated the functional implications of different T-cell-recruiting antibody usage, with the goal of finding a recruiter conferring TCER molecules with very high-specific activity against this class of low-copy pHLA targets in combination with a low unspecific immune activation for improved safety. Therefore, all bispecific scaffolds were evaluated using three different T-cell-recruiting antibodies: BMhanced, UCHT1, and an SP34-similar antibody. Within the TCER format, BMhanced, a humanized and optimized derivative of the mouse monoclonal antibody BMA031,43 demonstrated superior in vitro and in vivo anti-tumor activity. To further investigate the underlying mechanisms, three TCER variants incorporating each one of these recruiters were designed to achieve comparable in vitro cytotoxicity against PRAME-positive tumor cells.

This design allowed a direct comparison of different functional parameters, such as T-cell binding, T-cell activation and proliferation, as well as in vivo pharmacodynamics, based on the assumption that all molecules are equally potent in vitro. Rather unexpectedly, we observed very distinct functional profiles of recruiter-modified TCER molecules. Despite their intended equipotent behavior, the TCER molecules differed significantly in binding to human CD3+ T cells with strong to intermediate binding for UCHT1(V20) and SP34-similar recruiter reaching half-maximal T-cell binding at low nM concentrations, while binding of BMhanced was extremely weak and only detectable at concentrations of 100 nM or higher. Further functional assessment revealed a more pronounced T-cell activation signature for TCER with BMhanced recruiter in comparison to UCHT1(V20) and SP34-similar recruiters, as demonstrated by increased signaling of NFAT reporter T cells, increased T-cell-mediated release of granzyme B, IFN-γ and IL-2, and augmented proliferation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Driven by these enhanced functional anti-tumor signatures, we assessed if TCER molecules with BMhanced recruiter would enable an improved control of tumor xenografts in mice. Indeed, when using an Hs695T tumor xenograft model in NOG mice, BMhanced-TCER exhibited a significantly improved in vivo tumor control with five out of six mice in remission at study day 21 compared to one and zero mice in remission for TCER based on UCHT1(V20) and SP34-similar recruiter, respectively. This superior in vivo activity of the BMhanced-TCER is specifically remarkable given that the in vitro potency against the same Hs695T tumor cell line was similar among the three different recruiter-modified TCER variants. Potential explanations for this rather unexpected finding include improved biodistribution toward the tumor vs peripheral T-cell binding as well as facilitation of serial killing at the site of the target by the low‑affinity BMhanced. Given the distinct epitopes recognized by the recruiters (i.e. SP34: linear epitope at N-terminus of CD3epsilon, UCHT1: conformational epitope formed by CD3delta/epsilon, BMA031: conformational epitope formed by the constant domains of αβTCR and CD3), we speculate that the unexpected potency of BMhanced is resulting from a more “natural” activation involving mechanical forces directed toward the TCR.81

To shed more light on these potential mechanisms of improved in vivo activities, additional investigations beyond the scope of the project presented here would be needed and might include formal biodistribution studies as well as full analyses of the respective intracellular signaling cascades.

As described in previous studies,53–56 TEBs with high-affinity T-cell recruiters have a tendency for enrichment at body sites with high CD3+ T-cell densities. Latter enrichment of molecules may bear the potential risk of unspecific, target-independent T-cell activation that could promote CRS symptoms in patients. To investigate unspecific T-cell activation in the absence of target-positive cells, we subjected the three recruiter-modified TCER variants to human whole blood and measured recruiter-driven cytokine release. In line with our assumptions, we observed a correlation between T-cell-binding strength of recruiting Abs and their tendency to induce IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-8, known to be released from T cells,58 as well as MCP-1, MIP1-α, and IL-6, secondarily induced in myeloid cells58 and with IL-6 known as a main driver of CRS.

Therefore, in contrast to its enhanced specific activity, target-independent, unspecific T-cell activation of BMhanced-based TCER was reduced in comparison to classically used recruiting antibodies with stronger T-cell binding.

Besides the important role of antigen-binding site design configuration, the effector-function silenced Fc part of the TCER scaffold plays an essential role for other functional parameters such as heterodimer formation and terminal plasma half-life. The CH3-domains of the TCER Fc part contain well-characterized knob-into-hole mutations42 thereby facilitating the heterodimerization process of the two TCER polypeptide chains. We could confirm the very efficient heterodimerization process since intact-MS analysis of BMhanced-TCER produced at large-scale in a stably transfected CHO cell line and purified on Protein A (capturing hetero- and homodimers) revealed no homodimeric side products (Figure S5). When compared to the two other asymmetric scaffolds with identical Fc region, 125 kDa format and 125 (+25) kDa format, the TCER exhibited a higher heat-stress stability (see Table 1), which may be driven by the cross-over design of TCR- and Ig-derived variable domains, thereby supporting the formation of a compact, robust, and heterodimeric molecule. Favorable expressability of TCER molecules was shown by successful large-scale productions in stably transfected CHO-cells resulting in titers up to 6 g/L and purifications using an mAb-like down-stream process. Albeit the TCER Fc part is modified with effector function-silencing mutations, the binding to the neonatal Fc receptor remains fully intact conferring TCER molecules with an antibody-like pharmacokinetic profile. Latter could be confirmed in mice, in which the PRAME-specific BMhanced-TCER exhibited a terminal plasma half-life of 8–11 d (Figure S6).

Taken together, our study provides valuable insight into design–function relationships of different TCR-based bispecific molecules with the intention to develop an optimal scaffold for targeting tumor-specific pHLA antigens.

Our data indicate that a 1 + 1 design of pHLA-targeting TEBs can elicit sufficient cytotoxic activity, even at low pHLA target copies, if subjected to further format optimization, such as shortening the binding‑site distance and, presumably, increasing the rigidity of the TCR/effector domain conjunction like in the TCER format. It should be noted that the beneficial design–function relationship of the TCER format was specifically observed in the context of various pHLA-targeting TEBs, and other target antigens may require different bispecific format configuration.82 Our finding of superior in vitro and in vivo functionality of the TCER format was made in the context of different T-cell-recruiting antibodies and different TCR domains arguing for a design–function relationship, which can be translated to the class of pHLA antigens in general, as shown for peptides from MAGEA4/8 or from HIV83 (Figure S7).

We believe the outcome of this study has the potential to significantly advance the field of pHLA-targeting bispecifics and TEBs in general, with regard to optimizing therapeutic efficacy and tolerability in solid cancer patients. Based on the TCER platform, two compounds, IMA401 targeting MAGEA4/8 and IMA402 targeting PRAME, have been moved into clinical development. Early data reported from these ongoing dose-escalating phase 1 studies indicated clinical activity including significant tumor regressions when TCER were given as single-agent in metastatic solid cancer patients with no available treatment option left.84,85

Limitations of the study

The scope of the presented study was the systematic identification of an optimal molecular scaffold for the generation of bispecific T-cell engagers targeting pHLA complexes based on engineered T-cell receptors. A variety of preclinical evidence is presented showing that the TCER format can be considered superior to other (well-known) formats tested, a finding which is most pronounced for low‑copy‑target conditions typically observed in cancer patients. However, the exact biophysical and molecular mechanisms underlying these findings could not be completely elucidated. While biochemical data presented strongly suggests that the distance between the two binding sites (and potentially the flexibility or rigidity of structures connecting them) explains these observations, more detailed analyses of intracellular signaling cascades, T-cell activation and anergy/apoptosis profiles, biodistribution studies, and structure-solving experiments should be addressed in future studies.

Materials

Antibodies were purchased from BioLegend (PE/Cyanine7 anti-human CD3, 300,420; PerCP anti-human CD4, 317,432; APC/Cyanine7 anti-human CD8a, 301,016), Thermo Fisher Scientific (eBioscience™ Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 506, 65-0866-18) or Jackson ImmunoResearch (Donkey F(ab´)2 anti-human IgG (Fcγ) PE, 709–116-098). The human Luminex discovery assay was obtained from R&D Systems (LXAHM-06) including a customized panel of 6-plex beads (granzyme B, IFN-γ, IL-10, IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α). CellTrace CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit (C34554) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. SK-MEL-24, Hs695T, A375, and T98G were obtained from ATCC and cultured in EMEM (M2279-500 ML, Merck) + 10% FCS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10,270,106) + 1× MEM-NEAA + 1× GlutaMax + 1× Na-Pyruvat); DMEM, high glucose, GlutaMAX™ Supplement, pyruvate (31966021, Thermo Fisher Scientific) + 10% FCS and DMEM, high glucose, GlutaMAX™ Supplement, pyruvate (31966021, Thermo Fisher Scientific) + 1× MEM-NEAA +10% FCS, respectively. All cell lines were incubated in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37°C. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from DRK Mannheim with written informed consents and with appropriate ethics committee approvals. TCR/CD3 Effector Cells (NFAT) cells included in the T-cell activation bioassay kit were obtained from Promega.

Tissue acquisition and processing

Primary human tissue samples were extracted surgically or postmortem from HLA-A *02-positive patients with cancer or HLA-A *02-positive normal tissue donors. All specimens were procured by Immatics Biotechnologies GmbH with written informed consents and with appropriate ethics committee approvals. The resulting sample set covered 38 different organs and 24 tumor types. Tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen after excision and stored until isolation at −80°C for subsequent RNA and pHLA analyses. Tissue samples were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin (FFPE) for histological assessment.

HLA peptide isolation and relative quantification of PRAME pHLA

After tissue homogenization and lysis, pHLA complexes were isolated by immunoprecipitation using BB7.2 (Department of Immunology, University of Tübingen, Germany) coupled to cyanogen bromide-activated Sepharose resin (GE Healthcare Europe). Peptides were eluted from antibody-resin by acid treatment and purified by ultrafiltration. HLA peptidomics was performed using the XPRESIDENT platform as previously described.35 Briefly, peptidome samples were separated by reversed-phase ultraperformance LC (UPLC) (nanoAcquity Waters) using ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 columns (75 μm × 250 mm, Waters) and a gradient ranging from 1 to 34.5% acetonitrile over the course of 70 or 190 min. MS was performed on online‑coupled Orbitrap mass spectrometers Fusion, Velos, and Linear trap quadrupole (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in data-dependent acquisition mode. Samples were analyzed in at least three replicate runs, acquiring MS/MS data in collision-induced dissociation (CID) and higher collisional energy dissociation mode. Data processing was performed using a proprietary pipeline, which combines database search, spectral clustering, feature detection, retention time alignment, and global normalization for the generation of population-scale, relative-quantitative peptide presentation profiles.

Absolute quantitation of PRAME pHLA

PRAME pHLA copy numbers in cell lines and tumor tissue samples were determined using the AbsQuant method (US10545154B2). In brief, the number of HLA-presented peptide copies per cell is calculated using the following parameters: the number of cells within the investigated sample, the total amount of the isolated PRAME pHLA, and the isolation efficiency of the investigated PRAME pHLA. Hereby, each of these three parameters was determined experimentally.

For tissue samples, cell numbers were estimated based on total DNA content using the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity (HS) Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), whereas cultured cells were quantified by manual counting.

For LC/MS-based analysis, a series of targeted nanoLC-MS/MS measurements were performed on an Orbitrap Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an ultraperformance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (nanoAcquity Waters).

Two differently isotopically labeled peptide equivalents were synthesized. One of the isotopically labeled equivalents was used as an absolute quantity reference and was spiked into retention vials of each sample, which was used for absolute quantitation. The other isotopically labeled equivalent was used to generate a peptide-specific standard curve with at least four technical replicates. Thereby, one of the isotopically labeled equivalents was titrated, and the other one was used as mentioned before as a constant absolute quantity reference. Targeted acquisition of MS/MS spectra was performed in the ion trap using scheduled parallel reaction monitoring (sPRM) restricting to labeled peptide masses for the analysis of standard curves and labeled and endogenous peptide masses for the analysis of PRAME pHLA in tumor and cell line samples. Samples were analyzed with a minimum of two technical replicates.

The signals of selected fragment ions were extracted using Skyline 4.2.0 and translated into an absolute peptide amount using the peptide-specific standard curve. The overall efficiency of PRAME pHLA isolation was assessed by spiking of refolded PRAME pHLA monomeric complex into the protein lysate prior to the immunoprecipitation of pHLA (n = 8 biological replicates) and subsequent detection and quantitation of the isolated peptide by nanoLC-MS/MS as described above for quantitation of the endogenously presented peptide.

Generation and purification of molecules

Sequences encoding the respective polypeptide chains were synthesized (ThermoFisher Scientific) and cloned into proprietary expression vectors. Plasmids, prepared as midipreps (Macherey Nagel) were used to co-transfect CHO-S cells (Life Technologies) utilizing either Freestyle MAX reagent (Life Technologies) or a Maxcyte STX-2 system, respectively. Cells were cultured in TransFx-C or ActiPro medium (Cytiva), respectively, at 36.6°C, 5% CO2, 95 rpm in shake flasks. Transient expression of proteins was enhanced by temperature shift (32°C), addition of sodium-butyrate (final conc. 1 mM) and routinely feeding of cells (Cellboost 7a and 7b, Cytiva).

Filtrated supernatants were purified on either Protein L (Tosoh Bioscience), Protein A (Cytiva) or KappaSelect (Cytiva) columns, respectively, followed by direct application onto a Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva) installed into Äkta pure 25 systems. Fractions containing monomeric protein were pooled, and concentration was adjusted to 1 mg/mL (measured on a NanoDrop device, ThermoFisher) by dilution with PBS or by concentration using Amicons (Merck-Millipore), respectively. Final protein preparations were filtered through a 0.2 µm filter and stored at 2–8°C.

For some experiments, BMhanced-based TCER material produced by a stable cell line at larger scale was used. Briefly, CHO-K1-derived host cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding a single polypeptide chain. A stable cell line was generated by single-cell cloning. The BMhanced-based TCER was produced during a fed-batch process and purified by Protein A affinity chromatography followed by viral inactivation, two chromatographic polishing steps and viral filtration. Extensive characterization of this material showed equivalency to material derived from transient transfections.

Biophysical characterization

Purified proteins as well as heat-stressed samples were analyzed for purity and high-molecular weight species by SE-HPLC using a MabPac SEC-1 (4×300 mm) column installed in a Vanquish UHPLC system (both ThermoFisher). Separation was performed in 50 mM sodium-phosphate 300 mM NaCl, pH 6.8 at 0.2 mL/min. Eluting proteins were detected at 214 nm. For column calibration, a mixture of marker proteins (Gel Filtration Standard, BioRad) was analyzed under identical conditions. Proteins were also analyzed for purity and integrity by SDS-PAGE under non-reducing conditions. Therefore, 1–2 µg of protein, diluted in NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (Life Technologies) as well as Novex Sharp Pre-Stained Protein Standard, were loaded onto NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris Protein Gels in NuPAGE MOPS SDS Running Buffer (all Life Technologies) and separated for 50 min at 200 V. Gels were stained with Coomassie G-250 and documented in a ChemiDoc Imaging System (BioRad).

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

Previously seeded target cells (10,000 cells/well) were incubated with a serial dilution of TCR-based bispecific in RPMI 1640 without phenol-red supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated human serum, 1× GlutaMax, 1× pen/strep, and 10 U/mL IL-2 before adding rested PBMCs (100,000 cells/well) as effector cells in a 10:1 E:T ratio. In the corresponding assays, peptide-loading of suspension T2 cells was performed on the day of the assay at a concentration of 1.25 × 10^6 cells/ml with 0.00333 µg/ml of peptide in the respective culture medium for 2 h at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 80% humidity in Falcon tubes. The coculture was incubated for 48 h at 37°C, 5% CO2 and 80% humidity before cell-free supernatants were harvested, and LDH was determined using the CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The percentage of cytotoxicity was calculated as follows: (experimental LDH release – spontaneous effector LDH release – spontaneous target LDH release)/(maximum target LDH release – spontaneous target LDH release) × 100; assay medium only or medium with lysis buffer was subtracted as background.

T-cell-binding assay

CD3+-enriched T cells (500,000 cells/well) were incubated with a serial dilution of TCER in phosphate-buffered saline-fetal bovine serum-EDTA-sodium azide buffer (PFEA, PBS, 2% FCS, 2 mM EDTA, 0.01% (w/v) sodium azide) at 4°C for 1 h. A PE-conjugated donkey F(ab‘)2 anti-human IgG (Fcγ) (Jackson ImmunoResearch) was used for detection of bound TCER molecules. Incubation and washing steps were performed using PFEA. Fluorescence signal was determined using the MACSQuant X (Miltenyi Biotech), and data were analyzed using FlowJo (Tree star). The relative median fluorescence intensities (relative MFI) was calculated as follows: relative MFI = ((MFIsample − (MFIdetection − MFIcells))/MFIcells).

T-cell activity assay

Jurkat NFAT-RE cells (100,000 cells/well) (Promega, supplied as T-cell activation Bioassay) were co-cultured with SK-MEL-24 target cells (20,000 cells/well) in the presence of a serial dilution of TCERs. Luminescence was quantified after 16 h of incubation at 37°C, 5% CO2 and 80% humidity using a SpectraMax i3x (molecular devices).

Cytokine release assay

A co-culture of Hs695T target cells (10,000 cells/well) and human PBMCs (100,000 cells/well) was incubated in the presence of a serial dilution of TCERs. After 48 h of incubation at 37°C, 5% CO2 and 80% humidity, cell-free supernatants from triplicate samples were pooled and analyzed in duplicates using the human Luminex discovery assay (R&D sys) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Data were acquired using the Luminex™ FLEXMAP 3D™ system.

T-cell proliferation

PBMCs were labeled with carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Hs695T target cells (10,000 cells/well) were then cocultured with 100,000 CFSE-labeled PBMCs/well. After 6 d at 37°C, 5% CO2 and 80% humidity, immune cells of interest were labeled using fluorescence-conjugated antibodies directed against the respective surface antigens, and proliferation was determined by flow cytometry using the MACSQuant X (Miltenyi Biotech).

Whole blood cytokine release assay

Whole blood cytokine release assays were performed by HOT Screen (Germany). Fresh whole blood from healthy HLA-A *02 positive and negative donors was incubated with serial dilutions of TCER molecules and controls. After incubation for 48 h cell-free supernatants were analyzed as single determination of four pooled technical replicates using the human cytokine MAP A assay by measuring fifteen different cytokines and chemokines (GM-CSF, IFN gamma, IL-2, IL-3, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-10, IL-18, MCP-1, MIP-1 alpha, TNF-α, and TNF-β). A lower limit of quantification was determined for every mediator.

Xenograft mouse tumor model

In vivo mouse studies were performed by Charles River Laboratories (Freiburg, Germany). Animal housing and experimental procedures were realized according to the German Regulations. All procedures using animals were submitted to and agreed by the RP Freiburg (G-20/163). The animal facility is authorized by RP Freiburg, Germany (35–9185.64/1.1). Animals were housed under SOPF (specific opportunistic and pathogen-free) conditions in individually ventilated cages (TECNIPLAST Sealsafe-IVC-System, Germany). They were kept under a 14 L:10D artificial light cycle. The temperature inside the cages was maintained at 22–26°C with a relative humidity of 45–65% and 60–65 air changes/hour in the cage.

In vivo efficacy was studied in the subcutaneous, human, amelanotic melanoma Hs695T cell line (CLS, Germany) derived xenograft model in female NOG (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid IL2rgtm1Sug/JicTac, Taconic) mice. At day −24 Hs695T cells (1 × 107 cells/site) were transplanted subcutaneously into NOG mice aged 6–8 weeks. Mice were injected intravenously with human PBMC (1 × 107 cells/mouse) from donor 1 or 2 at day −1 and distributed into treatment groups based on a stratified randomization according to their individual tumor volume (mean tumor volume range 80–84 mm3 at day −1). Treatment started at day 1 by intravenous injection of the different TCER molecules at a dose of 0.025 mg/kg body weight (5 mL/kg body weight), once weekly for 3 weeks (n = 6 mice/group). Individual tumor volume was measured twice weekly by caliper.

ELISA assay for pharmacokinetic plasma samples

The bioanalytical quantification of TCERs in the mouse plasma samples was achieved with two different sandwich ELISA setups. Both approaches used the target pHLA complex to capture TCERs, and either protein L-HRP was used for detection via the VL domain of the antibody part (pHLA – a VL assay) or an HRP-conjugated AffiniPure F(ab’)2 fragment goat anti-human IgG was used for detection of TCERs via the Fc part (pHLA – aFc assay).

For both assays, streptavidin was coated. After washing, plates were incubated with biotinylated PRAME pHLA, washed again and blocked. Dilution series of TCERs were prepared as standard. Standard and plasma samples were analyzed in duplicates and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, the plates were incubated with protein L-HRP or HRP-conjugated AffiniPure F(ab’)2 fragment goat anti-human IgG, washed again, and incubated with TMB substrate. The reaction was stopped by adding 1 M H2SO4. Optical density of the samples on ELISA plates was measured at 450 nm and 570 nm on a Synergy HTX.

Stability and affinity maturation of natural TCRs

The discovery of scTvs used for the development of TCER molecules is based on the XCEPTOR® platform, starting with the isolation of antigen-specific T cells from blood of healthy donors. The scTvs of promising TCRs based on initial efficacy and safety screening assays are further optimized for increased stability and enhanced affinity. This is achieved by combining mutational insertion into the framework region and the CDR, respectively, with subsequent selection using yeast display technology (Figure 1). Finally, scTvs exhibiting a superior affinity, while retaining target specificity, are further selected to be incorporated into soluble immune-stimulatory molecules. Here, we introduce our TCER format by presenting the key steps performed to optimize this format. Furthermore, the characterization of a TCER targeting PRAME will be shown exemplarily.

Real-time binding kinetics via BLI

Binding kinetics of TCR-based bispecifics in various orientations toward HLA-A *02:01-presented PRAME peptides or human CD3-Fc protein were assessed using biolayer interferometry (BLI) on an Octet RED384 or HTX system. Experiments were performed at 30°C with agitation at 1000 rpm in PBS supplemented with 0.05% Tween-20 and 0.1% BSA. Streptavidin (SA) biosensors were hydrated in assay buffer for at least 10 minutes prior to use. PRAME pHLA monomers were immobilized on streptavidin biosensors at 10 µg/mL for 120 seconds prior to incubation with serial dilutions of the TCR-based bispecifics. HIS1K biosensors were hydrated in assay buffer for at least 10 minutes and loaded with 10 µg/mL His-tagged human CD3-Fc-protein for 120 s. Sensorgrams were reference subtracted and analyzed using the Octet Data Analysis software, and kinetic parameters were calculated using a 1:1 binding.
