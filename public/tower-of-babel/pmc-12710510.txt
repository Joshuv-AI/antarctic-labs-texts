Efficacy of Liposomal Amphotericin B in Treating Pulmonary Aspergillus flavus Infection in Nonneutropenic Adults: Cases Report and Literature Review
Authors: Xianqiu Chen, Ling Wang, Jianxiong Li, Yuan Zhang, Chan Liu, Jian Yang, Jingyun Shi, Shuo Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLiposomal amphotericin B (L‐AmB), as a modified formulation of traditional amphotericin B, have better targeting and lower toxicity. However, clinicians have limited experience with the use of L‐AmB for the treatment of Aspergillus flavus ( A. flavus ) at present. We described two cases of immunocompetent adults; despite undergoing various antibacterial treatments, the symptoms were persistent. Based on the clinical characteristics and results of metagenomic next‐generation sequencing and (or) pathology, pulmonary A. flavus infection was confirmed. Subsequently, L‐AmB was added to the treatment. Following the initiation of antifungal therapy, the patients' symptoms and images were significantly improved. In conclusion, even in immunocompetent individuals, pathogen detection and pathological examination are crucial when treatment is not effective. In pulmonary A. flavus infection, L‐AmB may deserve greater attention and confidence in clinic.

Introduction

Invasive aspergillosis is one of the most frequent invasive fungal infections, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 250,000 cases each year [1]. It was acknowledged recently by the World Health Organization that the most common cause of invasive aspergillosis: Aspergillus fumigatus ( A. fumigatus ) was one of the top four priority fungal pathogens to guide research, development, and public health action [2].

Aspergillus flavus ( A. flavus ) is the second most common causative agent of aspergillosis, following A. fumigatus [3]. Amphotericin B exhibits potent broad‐spectrum antifungal activity, particularly against drug‐resistant fungal infections. However, its clinical application is limited due to significant toxic side effects. Liposomal amphotericin B (L‐AmB) offers improved safety compared to conventional amphotericin B, providing a safer and more effective treatment option for clinical use. It has gradually become a first‐line treatment for various invasive fungal diseases (IFD) [4].

Nevertheless, the clinical use of L‐AmB remains relatively limited, and clinicians often lack a systematic understanding of its application. Currently, the limited published literature primarily focuses on its use in treating IFD in immunocompromised patients or animal models. There were no reports about its use in treating pulmonary A. flavus infections in nonimmunocompromised adults. This article presents such cases and provides a review of the clinical application of L‐AmB in treating pulmonary A. flavus infections.

Cases Report

Case 1

A 65‐year‐old male, working as a construction site laborer, was admitted to the hospital with the chief complaints of fever, cough, and chest pain for over 20 days.

The patient suddenly experienced left‐sided chest pain without any obvious cause on July 18, 2024, accompanied by persistent high fever with a temperature exceeding 39°C. He got emergency treatment at a secondary hospital in Shanghai, where a chest computerized tomography (CT) scan (July 18, 2024) indicated bilateral pneumonia. He was treated with latamoxef and levofloxacin for 3 days, but the fever persisted with a temperature above 38°C. On July 22, 2024, he was admitted to the respiratory department of a local hospital with a diagnosis of “severe pneumonia, septic shock, and electrolyte imbalance.” A chest CT scan (July 22, 2024) showed progression of infection in both lungs compared to the previous CT scan (July 18, 2024), with little to moderate amounts of pleural effusion, some fibrous streaks, and emphysema. He was treated with cefotaxime sodium and tazobactam for 3 days, during which his temperature dropped to 37.5°C. However, follow‐up imaging indicated an increase in left pleural effusion, and the treatment was then switched to imipenem and cilastatin. On July 31, 2024, a left thoracentesis was performed to drain the pleural effusion. Acid‐fast bacilli smear was negative, and pleural fluid analysis showed a total cell count of 1074 × 106 and a positive Rivalta test. No cancer cells were found in the pleural fluid. A repeat chest CT scan on August 4, 2024, showed slight absorption of the lesions, but the patient continued to experience intermittent high fever with no significant improvement in symptoms. He was then discharged and referred to a higher‐level hospital for further treatment.

On August 9, 2024, the patient was admitted to the emergency department of Songjiang Branch of Shanghai First People's Hospital and was treated with fluoroquinolones for 3 days. However, his symptoms did not improve significantly, and his temperature rose to 38.2°C. A chest CT scan (August 13, 2024) revealed left pneumonia, a small amount of left pleural effusion, and bilateral emphysema. The patient returned to the original secondary hospital for emergency treatment, where he continued to receive latamoxef and levofloxacin for 2 days, but the fever persisted with temperatures fluctuating around 38°C. On August 16, 2024, he was admitted to the department of respiratory medicine of our hospital for further treatment.

Since the onset of the illness, the patient's mental state has been fair, with normal appetite, sleep, bowel movements, and urination, and no significant weight loss.

He has no history of underlying diseases, but has a 50‐year smoking history, averaging 20 cigarettes per day, and a history of appendectomy. He denies any drug allergies or family history of genetic diseases.

On admission, physical examination showed clear consciousness, coarse breath sounds in both lungs with slightly reduced breath sounds on the left side, and no dry or wet rales in the lungs. Heart rate was 96 beats per minute, regular, with no murmurs. The abdomen was soft, with no tenderness or rebound tenderness, and the liver and spleen were not palpable below the costal margin. The jugular vein reflux sign was negative, and there was no pitting edema in the lower limbs.

After admission, relevant tests were completed, and the patient was treated with latamoxef and netilmicin for antiinfection, as well as symptomatic treatments such as cough suppressants and expectorants. The patient still experienced recurrent fever. COVID‐19 and influenza A/B virus nucleic acid tests were negative. Blood tests on admission showed a white blood cell count of 11.08 × 109/L (elevated), neutrophil percentage of 76.9% (elevated), lymphocyte percentage of 9.3% (decreased), monocyte percentage of 9.8% (elevated), and absolute neutrophil count of 8.52 × 109/L (elevated). Tests for 1,3‐β‐D‐glucan (G) and galactomannan (GM) were both negative. The percentages and absolute counts of %CD3, %CD4 (helper T cells), %CD8 (suppressor T cells), and the CD4/CD8 ratio of T cells were all in the normal range. Metagenomic next‐generation sequencing (mNGS) of the sputum sample revealed Enterococcus faecalis with a sequence of 10,334; Enterococcus faecium with a sequence of 334; Candida albicans with a sequence of 76,414; and A. flavus complex with a sequence of 4. The treatment was adjusted to ceftazidime‐avibactam 2.5 g twice daily and netilmicin 0.2 g once daily intravenously. As the patient had elevated blood eosinophils and an acute exacerbation of bronchial asthma, for which betamethasone 4 mg once daily was administered for antiinflammatory treatment, along with lansoprazole for gastric protection. Given the persistent fever for over a month and poor response to repeated antibacterial treatments, along with the detection of A. flavus in the sputum, the possibility of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis was considered. Starting from August 20, 2024, L‐AmB was added to the treatment (starting at 50 mg for the first day, with a cumulative dose of 900 mg), along with beclomethasone for prophylactic antiallergy treatment, prophylactic anticoagulation, nutritional support, electrolyte correction, and improvement of cardiac function. Then, the patient's symptoms significantly improved, and his body temperature returned to normal. Follow‐up inflammatory markers showed a significant decrease (Figure 1), the chest X‐rays (Figure 2) and chest CT scans indicated improvement in the lesions (Figure 3). The patient was discharged on August 27, 2024, completed the treatment of L‐AmB for 7 days and continued oral isavuconazole for sequential antifungal treatment, linezolid for antibacterial treatment, and a gradual reduction of prednisone, with close outpatient follow‐up. The total course of antifungal treatment lasted for 6 months. During the antifungal treatment, the patient's symptoms improved and no obvious adverse reactions were reported. A 3 months follow‐up after stopping the medication, the patient's symptoms and chest CT were both stable.

Case 2

A 64‐year‐old female patient has been experiencing recurrent coughing for the past 7 years, primarily dry coughs that worsen at night, accompanied by throat itchiness and discomfort. She denies any history of hemoptysis or shortness of breath after physical activity. The condition was not taken seriously initially. On October 8, 2024, a chest CT scan at an external hospital revealed bronchiectasis in the left lung and a bronchial cutoff sign. She was admitted to the respiratory department on October 31 for further diagnosis and treatment. The patient has no significant past medical history. Upon admission, physical examination showed no dry or wet rales in both lungs, and the rest of the examination was unremarkable. Subsequent tests including white blood cell count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, G and GM tests, and lymphocyte subset analysis were all normal. A chest CT angiography showed: (1) Bronchiectasis with infection in the lingular segment of the left upper lobe (Figure 4A,C). (2) Several nodules in both lungs. (3) Enlarged lymph nodes in the mediastinum. To further clarify the diagnosis, an endobronchial ultrasound was performed, which revealed a homogeneous hypoechoic area in the left upper lobe with intact boundaries and no blood flow. A transbronchial biopsy was conducted using a guide sheath, and three tissue samples were collected for pathological examination. Brushings and lavage were sent for cytological and pathogenetic testing. Pathological examination revealed necrotic tissue and epithelial cells with fungal hyphae (Figure 5A), likely Aspergillus. Special stains were negative for acid‐fast bacilli but positive for Grocott's methenamine silver (GMS), fungal rapid stain, and Periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS) (Figure 5B). The tuberculosis DNA sequencing result was negative. mNGS of the lavage fluid showed Haemophilus influenzae with 18,056 sequences, and A. flavus complex with one sequence. Then the patient was treated with L‐AmB for antifungal therapy, along with beclomethasone for prophylactic antiallergy treatment and lansoprazole for gastric protection. L‐AmB treatment lasted for a total of 6 days (100 mg for the first day, with a cumulative dose of 850 mg). As the patient's symptoms improved, she was discharged with a prescription for oral posaconazole for continued antifungal therapy. A follow‐up chest CT 1 month after discharge showed significant improvement in the lesions in the left upper lung (Figure 4B,D). The total course of antifungal treatment lasted for 3 months, and no obvious adverse reactions were reported. The patient's symptoms were improving, but after stopping the medication, the patient did not come for follow‐up.

Discussion

As the second most common pathogenic species causing invasive Aspergillus infections, A. flavus accounts for approximately 15%–20% of cases [5, 6]. Invasive Aspergillus infection usually occurs in conditions involving significant defects in cellular phagocytosis, such as severe and prolonged neutropenia (e.g., after hematopoietic stem‐cell transplantation or aplastic anemia), and in patients undergoing high‐dose glucocorticoid therapy or those with hematologic malignancies or chronic granulomatous disease [5]. Invasive aspergillosis primarily affects the lungs, typically presenting with symptoms such as fever, chest pain, and hemoptysis, along with chest CT findings of patchy infiltrates, nodules that may cavitate, macronodules, or patchy consolidation [7]. Reviewing articles published in recent years on pulmonary A. flavus infections, the majority of cases reported involve immunocompromised adults and children [8, 9, 10], with limited studies conducted in animal models [11, 12]. There were no reports about its use in treating pulmonary A. flavus infections in nonimmunocompromised adults.

The first case describes a case of an immunocompetent adult patient who experienced recurrent fever. Ultimately, A. flavus was detected through the mNGS test of sputum. Based on the clinical characteristics and examination results, the possibility of combined IFD was considered, and L‐AmB was added to the treatment. Following the initiation of antifungal therapy, the patient's symptoms improved and a follow‐up chest CT also demonstrated significant resolution of the lesions. In case two, a woman with a repeated cough was diagnosed with bronchial truncation on CT of her lungs. To make a clear diagnosis, histopathology was obtained by ultrasound tracheoscopy biopsy, suspicious aspergillus mycelium was observed, and the infection of A. flavus was finally confirmed by mNGS. Through the treatment of L‐AmB and posaconazole subsequently, the lesions were well absorbed.

The two cases highlight that it is imperative to consider fungal infection or co‐infection in immunocompetent patients who fail to respond to a prolonged course of empiric antibiotic therapy. Currently, the diagnosis of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) still depends on a combination of clinical features, imaging findings, microbiological evidence [13]. Culture remains the gold standard for pathogen identification, yet it is time‐consuming and has limited sensitivity [14]. Compared to the traditional methods, the advantages of mNGS include higher sensitivity [15]. Early suspicion and appropriate diagnostic evaluation are crucial for timely and effective management of such infections.

Polyene drugs (such as amphotericin B) and azole drugs (such as voriconazole) are both recommended as first‐line antifungal treatments in multiple fungal guidelines. L‐AmB, as a modified formulation of traditional amphotericin B, is an unilamellar liposomal formulation. Its preferential distribution to the spleen and liver thus reduces its nephrotoxicity. In clinical practice, L‐AmB exhibits favorable efficacy and safety profiles. Compared to other amphotericin B formulations, it maintains comparable clinical effectiveness while significantly lowering the incidence of adverse reactions such as nephrotoxicity and infusion‐related reactions [16].

The European guidelines for aspergillosis recommend L‐AmB with a higher priority over another amphotericin B lipid formulation, amphotericin B lipid complex (ABLC) [17, 18]. Some studies indicated that the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of A. flavus to AmB is higher than that of A. fumigatus [18, 19, 20]. There have been some cases reported that AmB has not been effective in the treatment of A. flavus [21, 22], which led to the lack of confidence among clinicians in choosing L‐AmB as a standalone treatment for aspergillosis. Currently, large‐scale clinical reports on its use for treating A. flavus are rare. The two cases are presented to reassure clinicians that with the improved diagnostic efficiency for invasive fungal diseases (IFD) via mNGS methods, L‐AmB may hold clinical value in the treatment of pulmonary A. flavus infection, with a favorable safety profile. However, since the cases are from a single center, further multicenter and large‐scale clinical studies are needed to confirm its clinical efficacy.

Conclusion

In summary, the cases involved the diagnosis of pulmonary A. flavus in nonimmunocompromised patients through mNGS and (or) pathology, and the timely administration of L‐AmB led to a significant improvement in patients' symptoms and demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes. L‐AmB, as a crucial therapeutic agent for pulmonary aspergillosis, exhibited both efficacy and safety. Its unique pharmacokinetic properties confer advantages in treating pulmonary infections, while its significantly reduced toxicity broadens its applicability. With the development and application of novel diagnostic technologies such as mNGS, the diagnostic efficiency for aspergillosis has been continuously enhanced, potentially providing more opportunities and clinical experience for the use of L‐AmB in treating pulmonary aspergillosis. In the future, large‐scale clinical studies will be necessary to further optimize its dosing regimens, explore resistance mechanisms, and investigate synergistic effects with other antifungal agents to improve the therapeutic outcomes and prognosis for IFD.
