Unravelling the link between extreme rainfall and tropical wave activity over the west coast of India
Authors: Prajwal Koovekallu, Ajil Kottayil, Prince Xavier, Aurélien Podglajen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examines the influence of convectively coupled equatorial waves (CCEWs) on extreme rainfall over the west coast of India across all seasons. The results show that CCEWs substantially amplify extreme rainfall, with Rossby waves exerting the strongest impact, followed by Mixed Rossby–Gravity (MRG) and Kevin waves. Overall, these waves enhance land rainfall extremes by about 20–60 %, while amplification over adjacent oceanic regions can exceed 150 %. Wave activity associated with Kelvin and Rossby waves remains relatively uniform throughout the year, whereas MRG waves exhibit pronounced seasonality, peaking during June–September. During summer, the enhancement in extreme rainfall linked to MRG waves is comparable to that of Rossby waves, while in winter the influence of all wave types is largely confined to the southernmost part of India. The amplification of rainfall primarily arises from wave-induced increases in moisture convergence and the subsequent development of deep convective systems, further supported by strong interactions between the dynamical waves and the orography of the Western Ghats. The waves are not only instrumental in magnifying extreme rainfall but also in increasing its probability, with Rossby and MRG waves playing a major role in this process, while Kelvin wave’s effect is insignificant. The study observes that the devastating extreme rainfall events over the southwest coast in 2018, 2019, and 2024 are linked to strong wave activity. These findings provide new insight into the mechanisms that intensify rainfall extremes over the west coast of India and highlight the potential for improving extreme rainfall forecasts using the predictable nature of equatorial wave activity.

Introduction

The tropical atmosphere is a dynamically active and highly complex component of the Earth’s climate system. A wide range of weather systems evolves within this region, from small-scale convection to planetary-scale equatorially trapped waves. Among these, Equatorial Waves (EWs) are particularly significant, as they can modulate weather and climate systems within and around the tropics. EWs also play a crucial role in the zonal transport of momentum and energy1–5 through their direction of propagation. The zonally propagating wave motions in the equatorial region were first discussed by Matsuno2 using a divergent barotropic model and by solving linearized equations for small perturbations. Since then, numerous studies have investigated these waves through analyses of satellite observations and reanalysis products, including Outgoing Longwave Radiation (OLR)1,3,6–8, Brightness Temperature (Tb)9,10, wind fields11, and other atmospheric parameters. Wheeler and Kiladis1,6 investigated different convectively coupled waves such as Kelvin, n = 1 Equatorial Rossby (ER), Mixed Rossby-Gravity, n = 0 Eastward Inertio-Gravity (EIG), n = 1 Westward Inertio-Gravity (WIG) and n = 2 WIG waves by transforming the OLR from the time-longitude domain to the frequency-wavenumber domain. They identified distinct spectral peaks corresponding to Matsuno’s2 dispersion curves for equivalent depths ranging from 12–50 m. In addition to these equatorial waves, two other modes, namely, the Madden–Julian Oscillation (MJO) and Tropical Disturbances (TD) are observed in the power spectrum. These phenomena are convectively coupled but are not considered true wave modes (Wave modes explained by Matsuno’s shallow water theory).

Studies have shown that a considerable portion of tropical convection variance is controlled by convectively coupled equatorial waves (CCEWs). Lubis et al.12 investigated the influence of CCEWs on tropical rainfall variability and found that they contribute about 16–20 % to the total tropical precipitation. When excited in the atmosphere, these CCEWs can generate severe weather patterns, sometimes resulting in floods. Evidence of the modulation of daily rainfall extremes by CCEWs over Java Island was reported by Lubis et al.13. They showed that CCEWs govern moisture flux convergence, leading to enhanced local convection over the region. In addition, they inferred that positive (negative) interference among CCEWs leads to an amplification (suppression) of extreme rainfall probability. Ferrett et al.14 highlighted the role of equatorial waves in organizing tropical convection. They examined the statistical relationship between heavy precipitation over Southeast Asia and high-amplitude equatorial waves and found strong evidence of the influence of tropical waves on the probability of heavy rainfall. Latos et al.15 demonstrated that CCEWs such as Kelvin and Rossby waves embedded within the MJO envelope can enhance the probability of extreme weather events. They also found that the number of organized convective systems is greater during the active phases of convectively coupled Kelvin and Rossby waves. Cheng et al.16 reported that Mesoscale Convective Systems (MCSs) are the fundamental building blocks of CCEWs. They further showed that MCSs occur more frequently, produce extreme rainfall events, and grow larger in size when formed in association with CCEWs.

The extreme rainfall events in the tropics are closely linked to intraseasonal variability, such as the MJO17–19, equatorial waves13–15, Boreal Summer Intra-Seasonal Oscillation (BSISO)20–22, and tropical cyclones23–25. These modes of variability can act independently or in combination, producing intense rainfall events across the tropics. Among them, equatorial waves contribute significantly to both mean rainfall and extreme rainfall events. Understanding the factors that control extreme rainfall is essential for improving the predictability of such events.

Among the many impacts of global warming, one of the most notable is the increase in the frequency of extreme rainfall events around the world, and such severe weather phenomena are expected to become more common in the future26–32. Recent extreme precipitation events along the west coast of India have led to severe floods and widespread landslides, causing substantial loss of life and property. The increasing trend in extreme rainfall events over this region can be largely attributed to rising sea surface temperatures over the Arabian Sea, which favor more vigorous convection33,34. The west coast is geographically favorable for organized convection and heavy rainfall35,36. The large-scale processes, such as cyclones, depressions, and intraseasonal oscillations, are known to influence heavy rainfall over the west coast37,38. The impact of tropical waves on the modulation of extreme rainfall over the west coast is relatively unexplored and poorly understood. In this paper, we investigate this aspect in detail for the first time. This study demonstrates that convectively coupled equatorial waves contribute to the amplification of rainfall extremes over the west coast during all seasons. The study provides key insights to improve the prediction of such events while emphasizing the necessity of accounting for wave-driven dynamics in operational forecasts.

The Student’s t-test has been employed to assess the statistical significance of the variables used in this study.

CCEWs and extreme events

The spatial distribution of extreme rainfall, both with and without CCEWs, is studied to understand their scale of impact. The Figure 3 shows the composite of wave extremes and Non-wave extremes for different seasons. The regions where the means differ statistically from the climatology are shaded in the figure. In all seasons, the daily mean precipitation associated with extreme events over the west coast is intensified in the presence of waves compared to Non-wave extremes. Among the seasons analyzed, the peak monsoon season (JJA) shows the highest intensity of extremes, with a 2–3 fold increase in amplitude compared to other seasons. The modulation of waves during JJA is noteworthy, as it significantly enhances the spatial distribution and amplitude of extreme precipitation. This is primarily due to the interaction of CCEWs with large-scale monsoon systems. Several studies have reported that CCEWs interact with monsoon systems through background flow and moisture convergence, leading to the development of large-scale deep convective systems48–50. Although the precipitation associated with wave activity is higher, Rossby and MRG waves have a greater impact compared to Kelvin waves during both MAM and JJA. In SON, all waves have a similar influence on the magnitude of extreme rainfall. During the DJF season, mean rainfall is very low compared to other seasons, and the impact of waves is seen predominantly over the southernmost part of the subcontinent.

To determine the role of waves in extreme rainfall, we analyzed the temporal evolution of wave filtered OLR within the ± 15 days of extreme rainfall events, as shown in Figure S4. The figure validates the existence of wave activity in association with extreme events. Kelvin waves exhibit coherent eastward propagation, with weaker signals during SON and DJF and stronger activity during MAM and JJA. The westward propagating Rossby waves display large-scale, slow moving convective anomalies that persist for several days around extreme rainfall events. In contrast, MRG waves show more complex propagation characteristics, marked by alternating convective and suppressed phases on shorter timescales (∼3–5 days). Their activity is relatively stronger during MAM and JJA compared to other seasons.

The above observations undoubtedly demonstrate that tropical waves play a pivotal role in amplifying extreme rainfall over the west coast of India. We further investigated the background conditions that are conducive to rainfall enhancement in the presence of strong wave activity. The dynamical forcing of convection associated with wave activity is analyzed using the VIMF divergence. This approach provides insights into the dynamical processes governing the development of deep convection and the occurrence of extreme rainfall. We composited the VIMF divergence for both wave-induced and Non-wave extreme events (Figure 6). Note that negative values of VIMF divergence indicate convergence (VIMFC). Associated with the extremes, an increase in VIMFC is observed over the oceanic region near the southwest coast of India (10–15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}N, 70–77\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}E). A key feature during MAM season is that the VIMFC is limited to the southwest coast of India. This process helps trigger deep convection, eventually leading to extreme precipitation over the southwest coastal region. Compared to Non-wave extremes, the Kelvin wave shows increased convergence along the southwest coast and in the Eastern equatorial Indian Ocean. This implies the eastward propagating dynamical structure of convectively coupled Kelvin waves within the vicinity of the equator is capable of enhancing convection and precipitation over the southwest coast. The Rossby waves sustain for a longer period compared to the Kelvin and MRG waves (see Figure S4), which have higher VIMFC following a larger area of deep convection. In JJA, all the waves have increased VIMFC compared to Non-wave extremes. The SON season marks the transition period from the southwest monsoon to the northeast monsoon. During this season, the southwest coast receives intermittent precipitation events and even thunderstorms. The extreme precipitation events that occur during this season are similar in strength to extremes in the MAM season. The VIMFC extends over a larger area of the west coast and is also observed on the east coast of India on active wave days. In DJF, there is no strong background wind. The convection in this season is generally localised convection (Figure 3). The major contributor to the seasonal extremes is Rossby waves. An increase in VIMFC is observed offshore of the west coast, occurring within the envelope of Rossby waves. In winter, small localised convergence of moisture is also observed in the Arabian Sea during the passage of MRG waves.

Discussion

In recent times, the west coast of India has experienced extreme precipitation, leading to landslides and devastating floods33,36,53. The prediction of such extreme rainfall events remains a challenge because of the limited understanding of their dynamics. The complexity of extremes arises from the interaction of multiple large-scale systems that fuel the development of deep convection. In this study, we attempted to investigate how CCEWs enhance extreme precipitation during different seasons. Kartheek et al46 reported the role of MRG waves in modulating extreme events over Southern peninsular India during the Northeast Monsoon. Subudhi et al54 studied the probability of extreme events over the Indian subcontinent during June-September under the influence of wet and dry phases of different CCEWs. They reported that ER, MRG and MJO have the highest probability of extreme rainfall occurrence, while the Kelvin has the least. The study of Paul et al55 reported that concurrent cyclogenesis over the Indian Ocean is associated with CCEWs, which can advance into extreme rainfall events. The Kelvin, Equatorial Rossby, and MRG waves are strongly coupled with convection, whereas the EIG and WIG waves exhibit weaker coupling with convection1,3,6,8,9,56. The Kelvin wave variance is observed symmetric along the equator, Rossby and MRG variance are observed off to the equator1. These have already been reported by many while studying the horizontal structure and characteristics of CCEWs. There are also studies reporting seasonal variation in peak variance with respect to ITCZ7,57,58. A strong variance of CCEWs is observed over the Indian Ocean region, indicating that CCEWs can modulate extreme rainfall along the west coast of India.

We investigated the frequency of occurrence of each wave, particularly Kelvin, Rossby, and MRG, over the region \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$60^\circ$$\end{document}–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$80^\circ$$\end{document}E, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0^\circ$$\end{document}–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$15^\circ$$\end{document}N, where the variance is strongest. It is observed that Kelvin and Rossby wave activity intensifies during the MAM season, whereas MRG wave activity increases during the JJA season8. Suhas et. al59. reported 50 % of MRG waves occur during August-November. The observations indicate that the west coast experiences a significant increase in rainfall during wave passages. Study of Lubis et. al13. reported CCEWs, such as Kelvin, equatorial Rossby, and MRG waves, control rainfall extremes over Java Island. They also reported that the Kelvin waves in their active phase have the highest influence on the extreme rainfall probability over Java Island. Peyrillé et. al60. showed that the co-occurrence of tropical waves increases the probability of the occurrences of extreme precipitation events in the Central Sahel. In MAM and JJA, Rossby waves bring extreme precipitation, followed by MRG and Kelvin waves over the study region. During MAM, convection over southern India is primarily driven by intense surface heating and pre-monsoon moisture accumulation, resulting in isolated thunderstorms and deep convective clouds. The occurrence of convectively coupled equatorial waves in this season further modulates the development of deep convective clouds by enhancing moisture convergence and convection. The onset of the southwest monsoon (JJA) brings a strong background flow that supports widespread and organized convection. The tropical waves interacting with the monsoon circulation generate intraseasonal variability and episodes of intensified rainfall, particularly along the west coast and adjoining regions, with their influence being more pronounced in this season due to abundant moisture and strong monsoon winds that foster effective wave–convection coupling and amplify rainfall variability.

To ascertain whether wave activity can increase the probability of extreme rainfall, we compared the percentage contribution of different equatorial waves during extreme rainfall days against non-extreme rainfall days. The results are shown in Figure 8. The contribution of large-scale equatorial waves shows notable differences between non-extreme and extreme rainfall days. Non-wave conditions dominate during non-extreme rainfall days, accounting for 62.7 %, but this contribution drops to 50.79 % during extreme rainfall events, indicating that extreme rainfall is less likely to occur in the absence of wave activity. Among the wave types, ER waves show the strongest enhancement, increasing from 10.63 % during non-extreme days to 15.68 % during extreme days, an increase of approximately 48 %. MRG waves also exhibit a substantial increase from 9.65 % to 13.53 % (∼40 % relative increase), further suggesting their influence on extreme rainfall. In contrast, the contribution from Kelvin waves remains nearly unchanged (10.73 % to 9.64 %), indicating a comparatively weaker impact on extreme rainfall. Additionally, multi wave interactions become considerably more prominent during extreme events, with combinations involving ER and MRG more than doubling (from 1.89 % to 4.46 %) and those involving Kelvin, ER, and MRG together increasing nearly threefold (from 0.38 % to 1.15 %). This demonstrates that the presence of equatorial waves substantially increases the probability of extreme rainfall.

The vertically integrated flux convergence and the frequency of occurrence of deep convection associated with wave activity are analyzed. In general, wave activity can significantly enhance lower tropospheric moisture convergence, which aids in the formation of deep convective cloud systems over the west coast and can amplify the magnitude of extreme rainfall. The lower tropospheric moisture convergence is strong during the JJA season because of the westerly winds and wave interaction, helping in the vertical development of deep convection. Extremes that occur during MAM show that only the Rossby and MRG waves produce a large region of moisture convergence along the coast, leading to deep convection. Nakamura and Takayabu61,62reported that Rossby waves are often associated with the simultaneous development of deep convection, while Kelvin waves exhibit a progression from shallow convection to deep convection and finally to organized mesoscale convective systems. Cheng et. al16. suggested that CCEWs enhance the organisation of MCS but do not alter the dynamics. Our results are consistent with previous observations, but this study provides the first detailed quantification of wave impacts on extreme rainfall over the west coast.

The localized extreme rainfall events over land along the west coast result from the organization of convection over the adjacent Arabian Sea. For example, the extreme rainfall that occurred along the southwest coast in 2018, 2019, and 2024, leading to severe loss of life and property, was associated with the formation of MCS over nearby oceanic regions53,63. Thus, any enhancement of convection near coastal areas by waves, as shown in our study, clearly indicates a higher probability of localized extreme rainfall. Furthermore, we observe that the major flood events of 2018, 2019, and 2024 were associated with strong wave activity, as shown in the Figure 9. The 2018 and 2019 events were linked to high amplitude Rossby waves, whereas the 2024 extreme rainfall that triggered major landslides in Wayanad, located in northern Kerala and resulting in around 300 casualties, was associated with intense Kelvin wave activity. In addition, a strong TD type disturbance is also observed during the 2019 extreme event. The presence of wave activity during the major flood events of 2018 and 2019 was reported in a study by Kiran64. Conclusions

The west coast of India has become increasingly vulnerable to extreme rainfall events, particularly during the southwest monsoon season since 2018. Although the warming of the Indian Ocean and changes in deep convective cloud patterns enhance the intensity of these events, subseasonal variability and equatorial wave activity also play crucial roles in triggering them. This study presents, for the first time, a comprehensive analysis of the influence of convectively coupled equatorial waves on rainfall extremes along the west coast of India.

The study shows that convectively coupled equatorial waves amplify extreme rainfall over the west coast of India across all seasons. Among the CCEWs, Rossby waves enhance extreme rainfall more significantly than Kelvin and Mixed Rossby–Gravity waves. Overall, these waves amplify extreme rainfall over land regions by about 20–60 % compared to events occurring without wave influence, while the amplification can exceed 150 % over adjacent oceanic regions. The frequency of wave activity due to Kelvin and Rossby waves remains relatively uniform throughout the year, whereas MRG wave activity exhibits clear seasonal variation, peaking during June to September. The enhancement in extreme rainfall associated with MRG waves during summer is comparable to that caused by Rossby waves. During winter, the influence of wave activity on rainfall extremes is confined to the southernmost part of the Indian subcontinent. These waves amplify rainfall primarily through an increase in moisture convergence, followed by the development of intense deep convective cloud systems. The strong wave influence over this region arises mainly from the interaction between dynamical waves and the orography of the Western Ghats (mountain ranges). The CCEWs not only amplify extreme precipitation but also increase its occurrence. The presence of Equatorial Rossby and MRG waves significantly enhances the probability of extreme rainfall over the west coast. The major extreme rainfall events of 2018, 2019, and 2024 over the south coast bear the signatures of strong equatorial wave activity.

The findings of the present study hold high contemporary scientific relevance as they enhance our understanding of the factors that intensify rainfall extremes over the west coast of India. Since wave dynamics can be monitored and predicted a few days in advance, this knowledge can significantly improve the forecasting skill of extreme rainfall events in the region. While the present work provides a climatological assessment of the wave influence on extremes, future research will focus on quantifying the impact of these waves on circulation, precipitation, and cloud characteristics at diurnal timescales.
