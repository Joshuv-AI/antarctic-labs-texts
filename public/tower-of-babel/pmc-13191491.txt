Peak2Patch: High-Fidelity Functional Group Identification through Attention-Based Fusion of Infrared and Mass Spectra
Authors: Philip Jacobson, Suhas Kumar, Raga Krishnakumar, Jerilyn A. Timlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Identifying molecular structure based on spectroscopic readings is a key task in a variety of chemical and biological applications. Common spectroscopy techniques, such as Infrared (IR) Spectroscopy and Mass Spectrometry (MS), provide detailed information on the structure of molecular compounds but nonetheless require expert-level knowledge to decode. Machine learning has emerged as a potential solution for automating structure prediction from chemical spectra; however, current approaches generally focus on single sensor modalities, neglecting to leverage the complementary information contained within differing spectra. In this paper, we introduce Peak2Patch, a novel approach to fusion-enhanced prediction of functional groups from IR and mass spectra. First, we perform a detailed comparison of backbone networks for encoding both sparse mass spectra and dense IR spectra and demonstrate the superior performance of transformer neural networks over current state-of-the-art convolutional neural networks. Second, we evaluate three broad categories of fusion: early (raw feature), middle (deep feature), and late (decision) fusion, demonstrating the potential of a deep feature fusion-based approach. Lastly, we present Peak2Patch, our attention-based fusion scheme, which leverages cross-attention to mix features between encoded tokens of the two modalities. We validate our approach on a publicly available multimodal spectroscopic data set of 790k simulated molecules, demonstrating a large improvement in functional group prediction over both the previous state-of-the-art and our own strong single-modal baselines.

Introduction

The elucidation of molecular structure using a variety of spectroscopic techniques is a foundational pillar of modern chemistry. The most common techniques, including mass spectrometry, infrared and Raman spectroscopy, and 1H and 13C NMR constitute staples in modern laboratories, each of which views the chemical sample through a unique lens. Although a critical function, the interpretation of the spectra generated through these techniques is still largely relegated to human experts with deep knowledge of the domain. As recent advancements in deep learning have begun to find utility in a variety of roles within chemical applications, − so too have emerged many novel machine learning (ML) approaches with the goal of automating spectroscopic interpretation. −

As ML models continue to scale toward tackling more complex problems, multimodality has emerged as a core functionality within the most performant models. This is particularly apparent within applications such as autonomous driving, − remote sensing, − and semantic-visual understanding, − where fusing features from multiple modes of data is essential for enabling key model capabilities. Although sensor fusion has gained significant mainstream traction, works leveraging fusion in the context of chemical spectroscopy are sparse. Previously, limitations on the availability of data have hampered progress in this direction; however, with the recent release of the large-scale (794,403 molecules) multimodal (IR, MS/MS, 1H and 13C NMR) spectroscopic data set, a new avenue for the exploration of chemical sensor fusion has been opened.

In this paper, we focus our study of fusion on two primary modalities: infrared spectroscopy (IR) and mass spectrometry (MS). Infrared (IR) spectroscopy is a powerful analytical technique widely used for identifying functional groups present in molecules based on the interaction of their characteristic vibrational modes with infrared light. However, certain functional groups, such as hydroxyl (−OH), amine (−NH2), and carbonyl (CO) groups, exhibit inherent ambiguities in their spectral signatures due to overlapping absorption bands and variations in hydrogen bonding which shift absorption bands. These ambiguities can complicate the interpretation of IR spectra, particularly in complex mixtures. Mass spectrometry (MS) provides complementary information by analyzing the fragmentation patterns of molecules induced during ionization through bombardment with an electron beam (or other means), after which differing ion fragments are separated according to their mass-to-charge ratio and patterns emerge that correspond to structural information about the molecules. Sensitivities to the fragmentation mechanism (e.g., collision induced, electron impact, photoionization, chemical ionization, etc.) can lead to challenges in the identification of specific functional groups as certain bonds may cleave preferentially under different ionization conditions. Given the complementary information they provide, the absorption of infrared light is determined by the vibrational modes of present bonding (i.e., indicating presence of particular functional groups) whereas ion fragments generated in mass spectrometry consist of larger pieces of the molecule, and provide a better indication of molecular weight, it is not surprising that the spectral structure of the data is also different. There is a significant difference in spectral density between the two types of spectra. IR spectra contain dense features, as overlapping and adjacent peaks fill in the bulk of the spectrum. In contrast, mass spectra exhibit sparse features, containing only discrete peaks at mass/charge ratios corresponding to present ion fragments. To leverage these two modalities in a sensor fusion approach, we need to both account for the differing spectral structure while also extracting complementary information contained within each, combining this information in a way that emphasizes overlaps and resolves differences.

To build the foundation of our fusion model, we first investigate the two modalities independently to determine the most powerful strategies for feature learning. IR spectroscopy is widely adopted within chemical applications for the specific task of functional group identification, and as such many previous works attempt to automate this recognition process using machine learning. Early works focused on leveraging techniques from signal processing to transform spectra into more interpretable forms, such as through smoothing (e.g., Savitzky-Golay/Gaussian filters), , denoising (e.g., wavelet transforms) and decomposition (e.g., Fourier transforms, principal component analysis). Concurrently, learning-based approaches also emerged, primarily focusing on classical machine learning approaches. ,− Michel et al. evaluates several classical machine learning approaches (e.g., k nearest neighbor, linear discriminant analysis, support vector machine) to identify different plastic types contained within marine debris from IR spectra. Wang et al. consider the functional group identification task, training a support vector machine model capable of identifying 16 functional groups within a data set of 800 spectra.

More recent works have increased model complexity beyond classical ML approaches, finding substantial improvements in model performance by adopting deep learning models for IR spectra classification. − Given the presence of broadly similar spatially correlated features in both RGB image pixel space and IR spectra frequency space, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) (the well-ridden workhorse of computer vision) have become the de facto approach to deep learning on IR spectra. Instead of directly ingesting the IR spectra, Enders et al. proposes using a CNN to classify images containing graphical representations of the spectra. Wang et al. performs a dimensionality reduction of the IR spectra to produce 2D feature maps, which are then encoded using a standard CNN model. Jung et al. develops a 1D CNN model which performs convolutions on the 1D vectorized representation of the spectra, training a model capable of the identification of 37 different functional groups. A few recent works have proposed using transformer models as alternatives to CNNs on IR spectra. , Fcg-former takes the approach of using a patch-based transformer as the backbone model for IR spectra, demonstrating comparable performance to CNN baselines. Alberts et al. propose treating the IR spectra as a sequence input to the transformer, which outputs a SMILES string corresponding to the predicted molecular structure. Lacking in all of these previous works is a rigorous comparison against competing network architectures on large-scale data sets; on the large-scale multimodal spectroscopic data set used here, only the Jung CNN is tested as a benchmark.

Although deep learning approaches for IR spectra have borrowed heavily from image classification techniques, we note that IR spectra differ from images in three key ways: the contiguity of semantic concepts, translational invariance, and the presence of multiscale feature hierarchies. All of these assumptions align with the inductive biases present in CNNs, induced through their shared convolution kernels and multiscale features achieved by downsampling layers. Given the absence of these features in IR spectra, we hypothesize that the all-to-all single-scale attention mechanism contained within transformers is better suited for the structure of IR spectra. Based on this, we adopt the patch-based transformer model as our main IR backbone.

Mass spectrometry, while not directly probing the chemical bonds corresponding to functional groups as is done in IR spectra, provides detailed information regarding a molecule’s fragmentation pattern when exposed to a charged ion beam. Most methods used for the automated interpretation of mass spectra leverage large databases to match the target spectra against known compounds (e.g., library matching), , however these approaches are limited by the fact that only analytes already contained within the database can be successfully recalled. Within this vein, many works focus on finding a suitable way of measuring similarity between differing MS spectra. − Spec2Vec takes inspiration from language processing techniques (i.e., Word2Vec), pretraining a learnable peak embedding model that aims to construct a latent space that respects the natural co-occurrence of peaks within experimental spectra. MS2Deep trains a siamese neural network to jointly predict the similarity score from two input spectra. As an alternative to directly computing similarity between reference spectra, machine learning-based approaches have similarly emerged for MS as with IR data. ,− SIFTER uses a random forest to classify mass spectra into a taxonomy of chemical kingdom, super class, class and subclass. MassGenie and Spec2Mol both employ the strategy of inputting MS peaks as a sequence to a transformer model, which decodes the peaks into the SMILES string of the predicted molecule. , MEDUSA first uses a random forest to perform deisotoping (removal of noninformative spectral peaks), after which a long short-term memory (LSTM) encodes the reduced peaks to predict the presence of periodic elements. DreaMS leverages self-supervised pretraining to learn meaningful representations from MS/MS spectra, which can then be transferred to downstream tasks.

In contrast to IR, the sparse nature of MS data makes patch-based encoding challenging, as each patch consists of largely zero signal. Instead, we follow the popular literature strategy of treating individual peaks as a sequence and likewise adopt a peak-token transformer as our MS backbone.

Despite the interest in applying machine learning to chemical spectra, there has been minimal prior work investigating sensor fusion in the context of molecular identification. The prior work with the most similar experimental setting to that of ours is Fine et al., which also investigates the fusion of IR and MS data for the task of functional group classification. However, their work is limited by the scale of available data (approximately 8000 spectra), and they omit any detailed study of differing fusion mechanisms, instead employing only a feature concatenation scheme in which preprocessed MS and IR spectra are concatenated and encoded via an autoencoder network. Other works have investigated different sensor modalities for fusion, or employ fusion for a different purpose. A fusion baseline which concatenates 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra peaks into a single token sequence to be decoded via transformer was included in the release of the multimodal spectroscopic dataset. DeepSPInN uses a message passing neural network to fuse IR and 13C NMR spectra in order to identify molecular structure. In contrast to the standard supervised setting, MolSpectra employs fusion for the unsupervised pretraining task, proposing a method which uses a masked patch reconstruction objective for representation learning of IR, Raman, and UV–vis spectra. TransG-Net develops a fusion approach for predicting molecular properties, encoding mass spectra with a transformer backbone and the molecular graph with a graph neural network before concatenating the feature representations to predict the molecule’s properties.

Leveraging both our insights in single-modal feature extraction and an improved fusion mechanism, we propose Peak2Patch, a novel scheme for functional group identification from joint IR and MS spectral data. Peak2Patch adopts the aforementioned peak and patch token transformer as backbones for embedding the raw spectra. Within the fusion layer, Peak2Patch uses a custom cross-attention module (inspired by the original transformer decoder block) in which queries are generated from the IR patch tokens, and keys and values are generated from the MS peak tokens. Intuitively, the cross-attention approach can be conceptualized as a decoding of the MS peaks (whose functional group indicators are less straightforward to interpret) using the IR features as complementary sensor context. We argue that this approach better models the complex interactions between distinct features in the spectrum which correspond to different functional groups, as opposed to the rudimentary concatenation strategy which simply adjoins the two modalities in a nondeliberate way. After undergoing multihead dot-product attention, the output is fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) detection head, which produces the final functional group predictions.

Peak2Patch achieves state-of-the-art performance on the multimodal spectroscopic data set, significantly outperforming the previous best-in-class CNN IR model by +0.151 F1 score points. Comparing against our own strong single-modal baselines, we find that Peak2Patch outperforms the single-modal IR transformer by +0.031 F1 score points, demonstrating that performance enhancement can be achieved through fusion.

Methods

Preliminaries

We perform all experiments in this paper on the multimodal spectroscopic data set, which contains a total of 794,403 unique molecules and their associated spectra. The mass spectra contained within are MS/MS spectra, containing three ionization energies: 10, 20, and 40 eV. Both positive and negative mode data is included, however we restrict ourselves to only using the positive mode data for simplicity. As in the original paper, we use a 90/10 split between training/test data. We evaluate all our methods on the functional group prediction task, consisting of 37 unique functional groups to identify. We use three metrics to measure the performance of our models: class-balanced F1 score (preferable to the unbalanced metric used in the multimodal spectroscopic benchmarks as the functional group distribution is highly unbalanced), exact per-sample accuracy (the percentage of molecules whose exact functional group signature is correctly predicted), and average precision. The F1 score and exact per-sample accuracy are computed using a detection threshold of 0.5.

Images vs Spectra: A Discussion of Transformers and CNNs

Before consideration of multimodal fusion, we first discuss the neural network models we employ for single-modality feature extraction. Given the general orientation of the literature on machine learning for IR spectra, we ask the question: are CNNs the preferable class of model for deep learning on chemical spectra? Among image-based deep learning methods, both CNN and transformer backbones are employed widely, with CNNs maintaining a slight edge in performance across most tasks. However, we posit that the structure of chemical spectra differ in ways that can lessen the advantages CNNs offer over transformers. Specifically, we consider three key structural differences between images and chemical spectra: contiguous semantic concepts, translational invariance, and multiscale feature hierarchies. To help illustrate this point, we include a visual comparison in Figure . To the first point, we note that images are generally comprised of semantic objects which are contiguous (e.g., the cat and dog in the figure). Meanwhile, in the case of IR spectra, a given functional group signature generally cannot be traced to a single contiguous region of the spectra. A common example of such a functional group is carboxylic acid, which can be most easily identified by the presence of two peaks: the O–H stretch located in the broad band from 2800 to 3500 cm–1, and the CO stretch, located at around 1710 cm–1 (illustrated in Figure ). The second key difference, translational invariance, arises naturally in images as objects in a scene hold the same identity regardless of their location in pixel space. However, this does not hold true for IR spectra: the interpretation of a peak is irrevocably tied to its location in wavelength space. Even in the case of peak shifts due to molecular environment sch as hydrogen bonding, the peak position change is indicative of that specific molecular arrangement and encodes information about the environment of the functional group. The last key difference, multiscale feature hierarchies, represents a natural understanding of objects in images as a sum of its many parts. For example, two key features which aid in identifying the dog in the image are the texture of its fur and the shape of its body; whereas the fur is a fine-grained feature of the image, the shape is a coarse-grained, large-scale feature. In contrast, peaks in the spectra are do not have nearly as wide a variance in scale; while broader and narrower peaks do occur, the maximum peak width is constrained by fundamental chemical properties Figure .

The presence of these three aforementioned properties in images motivates the key inductive bias in CNNs: hierarchical feature learning guided by a gradually increasing effective receptive field with weights shared across the feature space. Without the same concentrated presence of multiscale features in chemical spectra, the hierarchical learning present in CNNs becomes a more marginal benefit. Instead, all-to-all interactions between spectral features, as is done in transformers, provide a logical manner of feature interaction between noncontiguous peaks. Thus, we adopt the patch-based transformer as our backbone feature extractor for IR spectra.

The breakdowns of CNNs are further exacerbated for MS data, whose sparse underlying spectral structure is a significant departure from dense RGB images. Furthermore, patch-token transformers are also a poor fit: a patch-based encoding unnecessarily obscures the key peak information within the large regions of zero spectral measurement. Instead, we directly extract the peaks from the spectra and treat them as a token sequence, which we again encode using the all-to-all interactions contained within a transformer.

Peak2Patch: Attention-Based Fusion of IR and MS Spectra

To perform sensor fusion between IR and MS data with enhanced intermodal information transfer, we developed Peak2Patch, a novel approach for IR/MS fusion. Based on our analysis in the previous section, we use a peak token transformer and patch token transformer as backbones to extract deep feature representations for the IR/MS modalities, respectively. For fusion, we design a cross-attention layer to mix tokens between the two modalities before passage to the MLP head. A full schematic of Peak2Patch is shown in Figure .

Overview of Peak2Patch: First, IR and MS spectra are converted to patch tokens and peak tokens, respectively. After encoding with the respective transformer backbones, the embedded tokens enter the fusion module and undergo cross-attention followed by a position-wise feedforward network (denoted as FFN). The preprended classification token is then extracted from the sequence and processed by the linear head to generate the final predictions.

The key element within our Peak2Patch framework is the cross-attention fusion module. As output from the backbone networks, we receive IR patch tokens {piIR}i=0NIR and MS peak tokens {piMS}i=0NMS . To perform cross-attention, we select IR tokens as queries and MS tokens as keys and values, generated through a linear embedding as shown below: 1 qiIR=hq(piIR) 2 kiMS=hk(piMS) 3 viMS=hv(piMS) where h q , h k , and h v are linear layers for embedding queries, keys and values, respectively. Next, selective attention is computed between the qiIR and kiMS (i.e., we restrict feature interactions to only intermodal, and not intramodal), after which the attention scores are used to compute a weighted sum of the viMS . We note here the dual significance of the choice to use IR tokens as queries, and MS tokens as keys and values. First, the IR spectra capture peaks directly corresponding to the presence of a particular functional group, while the MS spectra reflect patterns molecular ionization which can be a result of functional group identity, but also could be influenced by ionization method. Second, given the MS-only model performs significantly worse than the IR-only model, we surmise that the relevant chemical features are much more challenging to extract from the MS peaks. Therefore, we leverage the cross-attention fusion layer as a vehicle for enhancing the MS tokens with context from the IR tokens, such that the model can better decode the information contained within the peaks. We further justify this choice empirically in the following section. After undergoing fusion, the single classification token, originally prepended to the MS sequence, is extracted as the final feature representation and is afterward encoded via the final MLP head to generate functional group predictions.

Results

Implementation Details

As previously discussed, we evaluate Peak2Patch on the multimodal spectroscopic data set’s functional group prediction task. Unlike the multimodal spectroscopic benchmarks, we refrain from downsampling the IR spectra, instead inputting the full 1800-length spectra into our model to be split into patches. We tokenize the MS peaks by generating a sequence of peaks with three features each: peak height, peak width, and peak energy (corresponding to the ionization energy the peak is generated from). We note that if additional information was of interest, additional token features (such as mass difference between key peaks and parent daughter relationships) could be straightforwardly included. As the peak location is already encoded in the token feature, we refrain from adding a positional embedding to the MS peaks, although we do use the standard learnable positional embedding for the IR patches. To form batches with the length-varying MS sequences, we zero pad each sequence to the maximum length within the batch, as is standard in transformer training. We train our entire model in an end-to-end manner, using only a loss on the functional group predictions as supervision. We train Peak2Patch for 80 epochs using a one cycle learning rate with maxing learning rate of 3 × 10–4 and batch size of 64. During training we apply a dropout rate of 0.1 to both the attention operation and the position-wise feedforward. All training is conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. Single-Modal Results

Standard Backbone Models vs Bespoke Architectures

The current trend within the literature for encoding IR spectra using neural networks is to design custom architectures based on the specifics of the setting. The previous state-of-the-art on the multimodal spectroscopic data set, the Jung CNN, uses a custom 6-layer architecture consisting of two 1D convolutional layers and four fully connected layers. However, the authors neglect to scientifically motivate the need for custom architectures (as opposed to using more standard architectures widely employed in vision) in the context of IR spectra, which makes such approaches hard to build on. To address this, we perform experimental comparisons with two modern CNN backbone architectures: ResNet and ConvNeXt. , To modify these models to be compatible with 1D spectra, we simply replace each 2D convolutional layer with a 1D layer, otherwise leaving the model topology unchanged. A summary of our experiments in shown in Table . Immediately, we see that every configuration of the standard ResNet and ConvNeXt architectures outperform the Jung CNN across all metrics of the functional group prediction task. Most dramatically, the ConvNeXt-pico model, at one-eighth the parameter count, is able to achieve a +0.062 improvement to F1 score. The most performant model, the ConvNeXt-Base, outperforms the Jung CNN by +8.9. With this result, we argue that customizing the backbone architecture for the domain is unneeded, and we adopt these standard configurations in the rest of our experiments.

Comparing Transformer vs CNN Backbones for IR Spectra

We note that previous works have also proposed applying transformers to dense IR spectra; the multimodal spectroscopic data set includes as one of its benchmarks a transformer which individually tokenizes each element in the IR spectra. Fcg-former patchifies the IR spectra which is then passed through the transformer backbone. However, neither paper demonstrates a clear advantage of transformers over CNNs; the original transformer benchmark on the multimodal spectroscopic data set slightly underperforms the Jung CNN, whereas fcg-former only performs experiments on a small-scale data set, and omits any comparison with modern CNN backbones. In order to validate the advantage of a transformer-based approach, we conduct an experimental comparison between our highest-scoring ConvNeXt models and transformers on the IR modality. Noting that the baseline ConvNeXt model can be significantly improved by using a larger convolution kernel size (as it allows for an increased receptive field), we select a number of different kernel sizes for comparison. We additionally consider increasing the receptive field using dilated convolutions, and train a similar ConvNeXt model with a dilation factor of 1, 2, 4, and 8 for the four convolutional blocks, respectively. Similar to fcg-former, we adopt a vision transformer (ViT)-style model which splits spectra into patches which are then embedded using a transformer encoder backbone. As in ViTs, we prepend a single classification token which is extracted after the attention layers and sent to the final MLP classification head. In all of our main experimental results, we adopt a 66 million parameter model whose architecture is described in Table . Since the number of patches is treated as a hyperparameter, we first perform ablation studies on the number of patches per spectra, show in Table . From these experiments, we determine 90 patches per spectra (corresponding to 20 features per patch) achieves optimal performance; we use this hyperparameter setting for the rest of the experiments in this paper. For fair comparisons between our selected transformer and CNN models, we choose configurations roughly equivalent in memory footprint (ConvNeXt-Base and ViT-Base); notably, the ViT-Base backbone has an increased parameter count of 85 million, in contrast to the 66 million transformer used in our subsequent experiments (although the 85 million parameter model marginally improves the single-modal performance, this backbone overfits when performing fusion, hence the adaptation of the smaller 66 million parameter transformer). We summarize our experimental comparison in Table . By all three detection metrics, our patch transformer model outperforms all of the large-kernel and dilated convolution CNN models. Based on this, we conclude that even compared to highly optimized modern CNN architectures, the all-to-all attention used by transformers is a more appropriate match for the nature of IR spectral data.

Comparing Sparse vs Dense Learning Strategies

Although the dense and sparse format of IR and MS data respectively naturally lend themselves to differing strategies for deep embedding, we also consider the alternatives: can we sparsify IR data and densify MS data and apply the complementary approaches? For a dense-to-sparse conversion of IR spectra, we apply a peak extraction algorithm as part of the preprocessing stage to extract peak features which are then tokenized. For peaks from both the IR and MS modalities, we employ the same transformer used in the original multimodal spectroscopic benchmark. Meanwhile, for a sparse-to-dense conversion, we simply scatter the peaks into a spectra vector which we use as a dense input to be patchified. We compare these variations in Table . The results indicate that the strategy matching the native format of the data performs best; both sparsifying IR data and densifying MS data significantly degrade performance in comparison to the unchanged spectra format. Thus, we conclude that patches and peaks are the preferred tokenization strategies for IR and MS, respectively.

Additionally, we include results for each functional group class in Table . We note that the improvement achieved through fusion varies significantly between functional groups, although nearly all (with the exception of isocyanate, hydrazine, thiol, and azo compound) exhibit an improved F1 score from fusion. Three functional groups exhibit a dramatic improvement from fusion: thial, phosphine, and sulfoxide, all with more than a 10 point improvement to F1. Only six instances of thial exist in the test set, so we attribute the large fusion gain seen here to noisy statistics. The vast majority of functional groups exhibit a moderate improvement, ranging from +0.001 to +0.066. Notably, even functional groups which are very accurately identified by the single-modal models, such as alcohol and alkane, still see non-negligible performance increases from fusion. Among the functional groups with no or negative improvement, we observe that the MS-only model performs poorly on all of these classes, constituting four of the worst seven functional groups by F1 score. Interestingly, the other three functional groups in this group of seven, phosphine, hydrazone, and acid anhydride, are all significantly improved in the fusion model, suggesting that the poor MS-only performance does not necessarily suggest that fusion will fail.

Ablation Studies

Feature Fusion Motivation: Comparison of Baselines

To validate the feature fusion scheme used in Peak2Patch, we considered possibilities for different stages of fusion, namely early (raw data), middle (feature), or late (decision) fusion. For each of these, we develop a simple baseline approach that can be used to experimentally test the merits of each. As our early fusion baseline, we scatter the MS peaks and interpolate the resulting vector to match the dimension of the IR spectra, and concatenate these two along the channel dimension (we note this baseline most closely reflects the method proposed in Fine et al.). For middle fusion, we encode the IR and MS spectra with their respective peaks and concatenate the resulting feature vectors. Our late fusion baseline generates predictions from each modality independently, after which a weighted average (using learnable weights) is computed between the two. As backbones we employ the ConvNeXt-Tiny and ViT peak transformer for IR and MS, respectively, whereas for early fusion we encode the concatenated spectra with a ConvNeXt-Tiny. An experimental comparison between these baselines is shown in Table . Although all three methods for fusion improve over the single-modal baselines, the feature (middle) fusion approach scores highest across all three metrics. Intuitively, fusing earlier in the pipeline ensures greater interaction between modalities, hence the poorer performance of decision fusion. However, the vastly different densities of information contained with the raw IR spectra and the interpolated MS spectra make direct concatenation of the raw spectra nonideal. Instead, the information-rich deep features provide the best opportunity for fusion, and as such we adopt this approach for Peak2Patch.

Cross vs Self-Attention Fusion Ablation

We conduct ablation studies on the fusion component of Peak2Patch to assess impact of different fusion methods. First, we consider the three different possibilities for the exact attention mechanism: self-attention (i.e., keys, queries and values are generated from both MS and IR tokens), cross-attention using IR queries, and cross-attention using MS queries. The experimental results, displayed in Table , indicate that cross-attention using IR queries achieves the best performance of the three options. Self-attention, although the most straightforward of the three options, is hampered due to the inclusion of both intermodal and intramodal feature interactions; as intramodal interactions are already accounted for within the single-modal transformer backbones, the added complexity in the fusion layer has a deleterious effect. The question of IR vs MS queries in cross-attention comes down to which modality undergoes feature enhancement through leveraging the complementary context of the other; as the IR features are already more information-rich in regards to functional groups, the added context from MS tokens does less to enhance the decoding, hence the worse performance of MS-query attention.

Early and Sequential Fusion Ablation

Beyond the exact mechanism of our proposed attention module, we also ask the question: can fusion be performed at an earlier stage in the model pipeline such that the number of intermodal feature interactions is effectively increased? Noting that our previous experiments demonstrate that fusing directly at the raw spectra level is nonideal, we consider another three variations of fusion at early stage. First, we propose an intuitive idea: after undergoing their respective linear projections, simply concatenating all IR patch and MS peak tokens such that intermodal information transfer happens in every single self-attention layer in the transformer backbone. Additionally, we also consider an approach more in-line with the spirit of our cross-attention module: sequential fusion, in which we first generate functional group predictions from a single modality, and append those as a token to the raw tokens of the complementary modality, such that intermodal interactions take-place across many layers, but between tokens of differing information densities. We compare these two approaches with our original deep feature fusion mechanism in Table . Interestingly, we see that the early token fusion scheme performs quite poorly, only marginally improving over the single-modal baseline, emphasizing that successful fusion requires the deeply encoded rich features extracted in the later stages of the model. Meanwhile, both sequential fusion schemes outperform the former strategy, but nonetheless still cannot match the performance of the deep token fusion, indicating that fusion between information-rich deep features and information-sparse raw features is similarly nonoptimal.

As Peak2Patch utilizes two independent backbone models for encoding IR/MS modalities, the parameter count of our fusion model is significantly increased from that of the single-modal baselines. Even against the scaled-up single-modal baseline, Peak2Patch still performs best; in fact, the large IR transformer (126 million parameters) significantly overfits, performing worse than the smaller 66 million parameter IR transformer. As an extension to the fully end-to-end Peak2Patch model, we also consider the usage of pretrained encodings to produce MS tokens with a richer, unified feature representation. Data Set Balancing

Noting that the multimodal spectroscopic data set is highly unbalanced with respect to the functional group classes, we also examine if a data set balancing scheme can be used to improve performance on classes with fewer instances. Given how extreme the difference in frequency is between the most- and least-frequent classes (686000 vs 42), perfectly balancing the data set is not feasible. Instead, we adopt the Class-Balanced Grouping and Sampling (CBGS) sampling scheme, a method widely used with autonomous driving data sets in which data is resampled per-class according to the inverse frequency of the class (i.e., more frequently occurring classes are sampled less, and less frequently occurring classes are sampled more). We perform experiments on a single-modal IR ConvNeXt-pico model, comparing the unbalanced and balanced approaches, shown in Table . Interestingly, we observe that balancing using CBGS does not improve model performance, but rather decreases it dramatically; this suggests that samples containing rare functional group classes degrade learning for the more common classes. Developing a scheme for more-principled balancing of rare classes remains an open question for future work.

Discussion

In this paper, we introduce our novel scheme for infrared and mass spectra fusion: Peak2Patch, bringing modern techniques on large-scale data sets to the chemical spectra setting. Through extensive experiments evaluating a variety of models on the single-modal settings, we first develop an understanding of optimal feature extraction strategies for both modalities: namely patch-based transformers for IR spectra, and peak-based transformers for MS spectra. We then evaluate broad strategies of fusion between the classic early, middle, and late strategies, concluding that a feature fusion approach contains the most promise. Using these insights, we develop a deep fusion approach which fuses highly processed features from both modalities using a cross-attention module whereby IR queries are used to decode the information contained within MS features. Peak2Patch has superior performance in comparison to both the prior art (+15.1 F1), and our own strengthened single-modal baselines (+3.1 F1).

Beyond the specific setting of IR/MS spectral fusion, Peak2Patch provides insight broadly regarding the fusion of diverse data. A key result of our work is an understanding of fusing data with drastically differing information densities, and at what stage in our model is this information best exchanged. Particularly, the failure of various flavors of early fusion indicate that lightly processed features, when exhibiting such drastic differences in information content, fail to transfer well to different modalities. Leveraging significant intramodal encoding to map data into a favorable latent space allows us to generate richer features with information more easily digested by a different modality. Furthermore, the powerful attention mechanism, which has found broad application, is successful at exchanging complementary information in an intermodal manner.

Several unsolved challenges still remain following our work. To first order, fusion still fails for a handful of functional group classes- understanding how to improve feature extraction from MS data as well as increasing the number of instances of underrepresented functional groups will be key to overcoming this hurdle. A broader limitation is that our current experimental evaluation is limited to a simulated data set, which does not capture experimental noise/distortions that is inherent to experimental data. When we apply corruptions to this data simulating realistic conditions and/or test on a small set of experimental data, we observe degraded performance, suggesting that the classification of noisy spectra still represents a significant challenge to be addressed in future work. The generalizability of Peak2Patch to new tasks (such as analyte classification) and new sensor modalities (e.g., Raman, NMR) is also an open question. We hope that Peak2Patch will serve as a strong benchmark for using attention-based mechanisms for chemical spectra fusion, and encourage further research into this direction.
