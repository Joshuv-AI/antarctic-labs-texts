RE: Comment on: “Implementing a no–drain policy for extraperitoneal colorectal anastomosis in a real–life setting: analysis of outcomes and surgeons’ adherence”
Authors: Jacopo Crippa, Michele Carvello, Antonino Spinelli
Published: 2024

We would like to express our gratitude to M. Bhatia and T. Singhal for their interest in our manuscript discussing surgeons’ adherence following the implementation of a no-drain policy in rectal surgery [1]. Their recent commentary [2] highlights a wealth of existing evidence regarding the use of abdominal drains (AD) in colorectal surgery, which clearly does not support routine AD placement. The authors reference the insightful study by Willis et al. [3], which emphasizes the resistance among surgeons to adopt the latest ERAS® protocols [4] that discourage the routine use of drains in colorectal procedures. Although selecting patients based on risk might seem like a reasonable strategy for deciding when to use an AD post-surgery, no studies have demonstrated a benefit—not even within high-risk subgroups—that justifies the use of drains.

Our study not only reports the main postoperative outcomes following low anterior resection for rectal cancer in patients from two separate centers, with or without an AD after implementing a no-drain policy, but also offers new insights into surgeons’ adherence to this policy. By the end of the study period, adherence was notably high; however, drains were still used in approximately 20% of cases. Several factors might explain this, including:

The relatively recent introduction of the no-drain policy (3 years), alongside a steady decrease in its application over time

The relatively recent introduction of the no-drain policy (3 years), alongside a steady decrease in its application over time

The firm, albeit unproven, belief held by a minority of surgeons in the potential benefits of drains

The firm, albeit unproven, belief held by a minority of surgeons in the potential benefits of drains

A perception among some surgeons that high-risk patients could face complications, and the possible—though unverified—role of a drain in early diagnosis or intervention

A perception among some surgeons that high-risk patients could face complications, and the possible—though unverified—role of a drain in early diagnosis or intervention

Admittedly, our study is not the first to explore surgeons’ compliance during a shift in clinical practice. Enhanced recovery programs have often met with substantial resistance, and in some instances, compliance remains low [5], despite a significant body of evidence demonstrating the short-term [6, 7] and long-term [8] advantages of these protocols.

This resistance may stem from the well-intentioned aptitude of surgeons who strive to provide the best outcomes for their patients. Changing a deeply ingrained habit, supported by years of practice, is understandably challenging.

We hope our study sheds new light on the decision-making processes of surgeons regarding AD use in colorectal surgery and similar practices. Additionally, we hope it encourages further research into the underlying reasons for non-compliance. Such efforts could mark a significant step toward moving away from tradition-based practices and embracing evidence-based medicine.
