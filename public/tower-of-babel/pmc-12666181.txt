Pain management and its challenges in Iranian cancer patients: a mixed-method study
Authors: Maryam Esmaeili, Maryam Rassouli, Maryam Karami, Samira Beiranvand, Fatemeh Hajibabaee, Hadis Ashrafizadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify and explain the challenges of effective pain management in patients with cancer in Iran.DesignA convergent mixed-methods study.SettingOncology departments and palliative care units across multiple healthcare institutions in Iran.ParticipantsQuantitative phase: 320 healthcare providers, including anaesthesiologists, general practitioners, oncologists, nurses and pharmacists, selected via convenience sampling. Qualitative phase: 10 stakeholders, including patients, caregivers, policy makers and clinicians.MethodsQuantitative data were collected using a psychometrically validated 23-item questionnaire assessing knowledge, attitudes and perceived barriers to cancer pain management. Qualitative data were obtained through semistructured interviews and analysed using Graneheim and Lundman’s content analysis method with MaxQDA software. Integration was performed using a side-by-side approach.ResultsQuantitative data showed that over 65% of providers did not routinely assess pain, and only 29.1% believed pharmacological treatments were effective. Qualitative analysis identified 13 barriers across three domains—professional, patient and organisational—spanning physical, psychological, social and spiritual dimensions. Integrated findings revealed consistent patterns of underassessment, legal and cultural resistance and lack of interdisciplinary collaboration. These converging challenges highlight the need for holistic, system-level reform.ConclusionsThe convergence of quantitative and qualitative data reveals a multilayered system of barriers, professional, patient-related and organisational—rooted in physical, psychological, social and spiritual dimensions. These interlinked challenges contribute to fragmented pain management and limited interdisciplinary coordination. Addressing them requires a holistic reform strategy that integrates structural, cultural and clinical solutions.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major public health challenge, ranking as the second leading cause of death and a significant barrier to enhancing life expectancy worldwide.1 Despite recent advancements in cancer treatment, which have led to recovery for approximately one-third of patients, cancer continues to have profound impacts on patients and their families, often causing significant psychological stress.2 Among the most common and distressing symptoms experienced by persons living with cancer is pain.3 Pain in patients with cancer not only brings about adverse individual outcomes but is also considered a substantial burden on healthcare systems.4 Globally, health systems are under growing pressure owing to the rising incidence of cancer and its complications. As the global burden of cancer continues to rise, its associated complications, particularly pain, have placed an increasing burden on health systems, making cancer not only a clinical concern but also a pressing public health issue.5 The WHO has identified cancer-related pain as a critical issue requiring attention and comprehensive care.6 High prevalence rates of pain have been documented at diagnosis, during treatment and in the final months of life.7 Research indicates that moderate to severe pain affects approximately 38% of patients, ranging from 31% post-treatment to 45% in advanced stages of the disease.8 9 In a systematic review and meta-analysis, van den Beuken-van et al (2016) reported pain prevalence rates of 50.7% across all cancer stages, 39.3% following curative treatment, 55% during anticancer treatment and 66.4% in advanced, metastatic or terminal stages.9

Pain is a multidimensional experience encompassing physical, emotional, psychological, social and spiritual dimensions. In this study, pain is operationalised as a subjective and layered phenomenon, ascertained through both clinical indicators and lived experiences, with attention to sociocultural interpretations.10 Unrelieved pain can significantly impair patients’ physical, social and psychological functioning. It may hinder recovery, increase complications, cause anxiety and sleep disturbances and lower quality of life, ultimately affecting patients’ willingness to participate in their treatment process.11 Comprehensive cancer care should prioritise safe, effective and evidence-based management of cancer pain. Effective pain management can significantly enhance quality of life, ensure comfort and improve functional outcomes for individuals living with cancer.12 It is estimated that cancer pain can be effectively managed in up to 90% of patients with appropriate therapeutic approaches combined with accurate pain assessment techniques.13 Nevertheless, only about half of these patients receive adequate palliative care. Pain management remains one of the most significant challenges faced by patients and their families, with the persistent undertreatment of cancer pain being a widely recognised issue.14 Unfortunately, pain control is often inadequate, especially among elderly persons living with cancer15 and those receiving hospice or palliative care.16 17 Thinh et al reported that despite the availability of pain management guidelines and effective analgesics, a considerable number of patients still reported insufficient pain relief, negatively affecting their sleep and overall quality of life.18

Effective cancer pain control requires a specialised and multidisciplinary approach. Pain management typically involves pharmacological interventions (eg, opioids, adjuvants), non-pharmacological strategies (eg, psychological support, physical therapy) and spiritual care. The composition of pain management teams varies but ideally involves physicians, nurses, mental health professionals, social workers and spiritual counsellors working collaboratively.19 Typically, pain relief consists of a team of palliative care professionals, including physicians, nurses, mental health experts, social workers, pharmacists and spiritual counsellors.20 While hospitalised patients generally receive appropriate pain treatment from qualified professionals, such as palliative care or anaesthesiology specialists, challenges frequently arise on discharge. In the community setting, healthcare providers seldom have adequate training, experience and skills necessary for effective medication management.21 Research highlights several key barriers to effective cancer pain management, including regulatory constraints, knowledge deficits, reliance on religious and cultural coping strategies, inadequate pain assessment, patient reluctance to report pain or use opioids, physician hesitancy to prescribe opioids, as well as insufficient nursing staff knowledge or willingness to administer opioids.22 23 Nevertheless, there remains a lack of comprehensive data regarding the obstacles to pain relief and strategies to enhance the quality of life for patients and their families.21 Given the sociocultural dimensions of the issue, findings from studies conducted in other countries may not be directly applicable. Therefore, it is essential to conduct research within the context of local cultural and social frameworks.24

Understanding the lived experiences of persons living with cancer and other stakeholders, considering current pain management practices and challenges within the context of the specific sociocultural setting, can enable healthcare teams to more effectively identify, evaluate and address this problem. This knowledge can also guide the delivery of care and educational interventions for patients.25 26 Since effective pain control remains a significant challenge for both patients and their families, it requires greater attention and evaluation by healthcare providers. Clearly, providers need to be aware of current practices and challenges to develop effective educational programmes and supportive services aimed at improving patients’ and families’ quality of life.27 Accordingly, comprehensive initial assessments and personalised management plans are crucial in achieving optimal cancer pain control. Pain management strategies should adhere to established guidelines, incorporate advanced drug formulations, be aligned with patients’ needs and ensure close monitoring of pain outcomes. As all aspects of pain—its perception, manifestation and treatment—are shaped by the surrounding sociocultural context,28 it is essential to identify the current state of pain management within this context for effective care. Thus, the present study aims to identify and explore the challenges of effective cancer pain management in Iran through a mixed-methods approach.

Conceptual framework

This study is grounded in the Total Pain Theory developed by Dame Cicely Saunders; it defines pain as a multidimensional experience encompassing physical, emotional, social and spiritual suffering.29 30 In palliative care, this framework emphasises that pain is not merely a biological symptom but rather a complex phenomenon shaped by personal meaning, relationships and cultural context. In Iran, where family-centred care, religious coping and sociocultural norms heavily influence health experiences, the Total Pain Theory offers a relevant lens for understanding how persons living with cancer perceive and respond to pain. It also supports the exploration of systemic and interpersonal obstacles to pain relief, such as communication gaps, stigma around opioid use and lack of interdisciplinary collaboration.

By applying this framework, the study integrates both quantitative and qualitative data to capture the full spectrum of pain-related challenges. It enables a holistic interpretation of findings while also informing recommendations for culturally sensitive interventions in cancer pain management.

Methods

The present research used a convergent mixed-methods design, based on the pragmatist paradigm, which combines both quantitative and qualitative approaches to gain a deeper understanding of the studied phenomenon. Each approach carries inherent limitations that can be addressed by the other; thus, the mixed-methods approach enhances the breadth of methodological perspectives, enhances the overall validity of findings and brings the researcher closer to the reality under investigation.31 In this single-phase design, both quantitative and qualitative components were undertaken simultaneously. While data for each component were collected and analysed separately, the integration occurred during the interpretation phase to generate a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon32 (figure 1). The study has been reported in accordance with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool Version 2018 guidelines.

Study step: convergent mixed-methods design (one-phase design) based on Creswell et al (2018).

The quantitative phase

Study design and participants

The quantitative phase was a descriptive study. Participants included physicians and nurses working in oncology wards of hospitals as well as affiliated clinics of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (located in the capital of Iran), along with pharmacists working in hospital-affiliated pharmacies. Participants were selected through convenience sampling owing to limited access to centralised cancer registries and logistical constraints across multiple care settings. This approach allowed timely recruitment of eligible professionals actively engaged in cancer care along the study period,33 based on the inclusion criteria of having at least 1 year of work experience in oncology or other departments where persons living with cancer are admitted. The minimum sample size was estimated to be 200 based on the formula (n=Z1−a/22×p×(1−p)d2) with p=0.5, α=0.05 and d=0.05.

Study instruments

Data were collected using two instruments: a demographic questionnaire (seven items) and the Cancer Pain Management Knowledge Questionnaire (23 items). This questionnaire, developed by the Middle East Cancer Consortium, has been used in 56 countries across six WHO regions to evaluate cancer pain management practices. Items 1–9 assess knowledge, items 10–15 address attitudes and item 16, which includes seven subquestions, examines perceived barriers from the healthcare providers’ perspective. A combined scoring system was applied, with attitudinal items reported as percentages.20 The full version of the questionnaire used in this study is available in the online supplemental material (see online supplemental 1). The tool has been psychometrically validated globally and localised for each country. The instrument’s Cronbach’s alpha was reported as 0.91, and the intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.89.20

Study process

Data were collected using an online questionnaire developed via the internal ‘Porsline’ platform, conducted between May and September 2023 by trained research assistants under the supervision of the principal investigator. The surveys were administered in person during work hours, ensuring confidentiality and voluntary participation. The researcher explained the study purpose, data confidentiality and participants’ rights, including voluntary participation as well as the ability to withdraw at any time. After obtaining informed consent, the questionnaire link was distributed via WhatsApp. The design required participants to answer all questions prior to submission. Data were securely stored in a researcher-accessible profile on submission.

Study analysis

Data analysis was undertaken using SPSS V.22. Normality was ascertained using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Descriptive statistics (percentages, means and SDs) were employed to describe participant characteristics and questionnaire responses.

The qualitative phase

Study design and participants

This phase adopted a conventional content analysis approach. Participants included nurses, physicians, policy makers, persons living with cancer and family caregivers. Healthcare providers and policy makers were involved in various clinical, administrative, research and policy-making roles associated with cancer pain. Inclusion criteria for caregivers included fluency in Persian, age ≥18, at least 1 year of experience of direct caregiving, no communication or psychiatric disorders and willingness to participate. Patient inclusion criteria were fluency in Persian, age ≥18, confirmed cancer diagnosis by an oncologist, no communication or psychiatric disorders and consent to participate. Participants were recruited via purposeful sampling and snowball sampling techniques.

Data collection

Data were collected through semistructured interviews, continuing until data saturation was reached. Data saturation is defined as the point at which no new codes or themes would emerge from three consecutive interviews. The stopping criterion was thematic redundancy, indicating that additional data would not contribute further conceptual depth.34 Sample interview questions included: how would you describe the current status of cancer pain management? What are the existing methods for relieving cancer pain? What has been your experience using these methods? How would you describe your overall experience of cancer pain? What approaches do you use to manage pain? What are the pros and cons of these approaches? The semistructured interview guide used in this study is provided in the online supplemental material (see online supplemental 2).

Study process

Interviews were conducted in quiet settings chosen by the participants. Probing questions were applied when necessary. Interviews lasted between 20 min and 50 min and were audio recorded with participants’ consent. Transcriptions were analysed using MaxQDA software following Graneheim and Lundman’s conventional content analysis method in three steps: preparation, organisation and reporting.35 In the preparation phase, interviews and notes were transcribed verbatim and imported into MaxQDA. Texts were read multiple times to gain an overall understanding. In the organisation phase, the texts were coded line-by-line. The codes were derived inductively and grouped into subcategories, which were then organised into main categories based on similarities.

Rigour

To ensure trustworthiness, the Lincoln and Guba criteria were employed:36 credibility: achieved through prolonged engagement, persistent observation, peer checks, researcher triangulation and member checking. Member checking was performed with six participants who reviewed their interview transcripts and the preliminary thematic interpretations. Their feedback confirmed the accuracy and relevance of the findings, whereby no major revisions were required. Confirmability: achieved through audit trails and external review by a qualitative research expert who ascertained the data collection and analysis process, coding structure and category development. The audit trail consisted of documented interview protocols, raw transcripts, coding matrices, analytic memos and theme refinement logs. These materials were systematically archived and made available for external review to ensure transparency and traceability of the analytic process.

The findings of the qualitative phase

Participants in this phase included 10 individuals (eight women and two men), consisting of two patients, one family caregiver, two policy makers, one physician and four nurses. The participants’ ages ranged from 25 to 52 years. Data analysis yielded three main categories and 13 subcategories. Table 7 has been included as online supplemental material 3. Healthcare professional-related barriers

The first group of barriers from the participants’ perspective was barriers linked to care providers, which consisted of four subcategories, including knowledge deficit, negative attitudes, concerns about addiction/side effects and misuse, as well as neglect of using non-pharmacological methods of pain management.

Knowledge deficit

A key issue highlighted by participants was a lack of knowledge about cancer pain management among professionals and other stakeholders. There was a continued reliance on outdated approaches, a lack of attention to holistic concepts such as ‘total pain’, and an overemphasis on pharmacological methods without a programme for managing chronic pain.

Negative attitudes

Some participants noted that healthcare providers sometimes questioned the authenticity of patients’ reported pain and occasionally used unethical approaches such as using placebos. There was also limited adherence to pain management guidelines and insufficient respect for non-pharmacological or integrative oncology methods.

Concerns about addiction/side effects and misuse

Participants expressed concern about side effects of opioids, legal issues, drug toxicity and tolerance, which would hinder optimal pain relief.

Neglecting to use non-pharmacological methods of pain management

In spite of patient interest in traditional or complementary therapies, healthcare providers often rejected these approaches owing to doubts about efficacy. Patients also noted being discouraged from undergoing such methods, which were only available in a few expensive and unregulated centres.

Patient-related barriers

The second group of barriers was patient-related barriers, which consisted of four subcategories, including cultural beliefs, disease-related factors, fear of physiological dependence and drug side effects and poor communication with pain.

Cultural beliefs

The existence of certain cultural and social beliefs among participants was another obstacle to optimal pain relief. Cultural and social context largely shapes the interpretation and expression of the pain experience while also creating preferences for how to manage pain.

Disease-related factors

Patients reported cancer and cancer-related pain as difficult conditions requiring constant effort and work to manage. Pain is the most distressing symptom in patients with cancer, affecting both physical and mental health. Pain management was only one domain of work for patients and caregivers and was described in the context of overall cancer management, along with the tasks necessary to continue living and participating in worthwhile activities despite pain and cancer.

Fear of physiological dependence and drug side effects

Fear of addiction and adverse effects of pain medications was frequently cited by patients as major barriers.

Poor pain communication

One of the most important barriers to pain management in this study, as noted by the participants, was language barriers in communicating with the medical staff, which resulted in reluctance to report pain. Further, the lack of patient and family participation in the process of pain assessment and relief was also mentioned by the stakeholders.

Organisational-related barriers

Organisational barriers in this study consisted of five subcategories, including strict unnecessary legislations, organisational culture and policies, inadequate/insufficient assessment and management, lack of interdisciplinary collaboration and inequality in the distribution of resources.

Strict unnecessary legislations

Participants expressed their views on the influence of current health regulations on cancer care and pain management. Indeed, the lack of reliable national policies on palliative care and legal restrictions on the use of opioid analgesics have been identified as major barriers to effective cancer pain management.

Inequality in the distribution of resources (insufficient allocation of resources)

Participants in this study recognised the inequities in resource allocation and the insufficient distribution of services. This includes a lack of palliative care centres and complementary medicine services, limited access to narcotics and painkillers, insufficient patient-to-nurse ratios, a shortage of specialised physicians—particularly in pain management—and a disconnect between the availability of narcotics and patient needs.

Lack of interdisciplinary collaboration

In this study, participants reported that challenges in interdisciplinary collaboration are to be expected, since the complexity of chronic pain requires interdisciplinary collaboration for treatment and care.

Organisational culture and policies

This group covered the lack of supportive policies, including oversight of various systems, referral system, access to some forms of medication (lack of oral morphine), communication between specialists in the field of cancer management and other fields in pain management (integrative oncology), and alignment of orders between complementary medicine specialists and specialist physicians, together with conflicting opinions among physicians.

Inadequate/insufficient assessment and management

According to the participants’ experiences, pain assessment and documentation of persons living with cancer were not performed consistently and systematically. According to specialists and care providers, the lack of unified guidelines and instructions in treatment units would prevent accurate patient assessment. Also, most care providers in this study pointed to the lack of uniformity in treatment protocols, unreliability and instability in pain management protocols and non-use of global and regional standards for pain relief.

The step of combination of qualitative and quantitative phase

Quantitative and qualitative data were integrated using a side-by-side comparison approach,37 enabling direct juxtaposition and thematic synthesis. This process led to the identification of three overarching categories of barriers—healthcare professional-related, patient-related and organisational—each encompassing physical, psychological, social and spiritual dimensions. The integration revealed how statistical gaps aligned with stakeholder narratives, offering a more comprehensive understanding of systemic challenges. Discussion

The aim of this study was to identify and explain the multifaceted challenges of effectively managing cancer pain in Iran. Guided by Dame Cicely Saunders’ Total Pain Theory, our findings reveal that the barriers to effective management are deeply interwoven across the physical, psychological, social and spiritual dimensions of pain. By converging quantitative and qualitative data, we were able to categorise these barriers into three interconnected domains: those related to healthcare providers, patients and the organisational system. Crucially, each of these domains impedes the holistic management of ‘total pain’, thereby perpetuating patient suffering.

Healthcare professional-related barriers

In this study, barriers related to healthcare professionals emerged not as individual deficiencies but as consequences of an inefficient educational and structural system that directly disrupts the management of the four dimensions of total pain (physical, psychological, social and spiritual).

Physical dimension: failure to identify and assess pain

Quantitative and qualitative findings converged to show that the lack of routine pain screening and inadequate assessment of its intensity and type may stem from a deficiency in structured awareness and the absence of mandatory protocols. This operational gap directly undermines the physical dimension of total pain, as without accurate pain identification, any intervention (even pharmacological) will be ineffective. This finding aligns with Iranian studies; for instance, Rezaei et al showed that <30% of doctors and nurses are familiar with the WHO’s pain ladder, and its use as a guide for pain reduction or relief is not common. This situation indicates a failure to integrate pain education into curricula, which is reflected in clinical practice.38 Therefore, designing and implementing pain screening and assessment protocols across all areas of cancer care is essential, not just as a recommendation but as a vital care standard.39 Additionally, integrating educational topics related to pain management into training curricula and continuing professional education is of particular importance.40

Psychological dimension: legal fear and conservative approach

Widespread concerns about opioid medications, confirmed in both quantitative and qualitative sections, indicate that the main problem is not the ‘fear of prescribing consequences’ but rather ambiguity in laws and the lack of organisational support for clinical decision-making. This fear, extending beyond the physical dimension, impacts the psychological aspect of total pain by creating anxiety and adopting a conservative approach among service providers, depriving patients of pain relief. A study in Iran also revealed that over two-thirds of physicians associate morphine prescription with a ‘heavy legal responsibility’, confirming that without legal structural reform, even specialised training will be ineffective.41 Therefore, the main solution here is to reform laws to provide explicit legal support for prescribers acting according to standard guidelines, rather than merely increasing education.42 This systematic change will enhance access to analgesics and potentially reduce providers’ anxiety, thereby improving the psychological dimension of total pain and the therapeutic relationship.

Psychological and social dimensions: neglect of non-pharmacological methods

The neglect of non-pharmacological methods, despite an 82.8% report of using pharmacological interventions, indicates a cultural-structural gap. Qualitative data showed that these methods have ‘no real place’ and ‘are not part of the care culture’. This attitude directly overlooks the psychological and social dimensions of total pain. This issue is not only a ‘belief deficiency’ but can also be a direct consequence of the hierarchical and centralised decision-making structure in Iran’s healthcare system. Studies conducted in Iran point out that even when care providers are aware of methods like relaxation, they do not have the ‘permission to implement them independently’.43 44 This finding supports our argument that the physician-centred care culture is one of the main barriers to integrating comprehensive approaches in pain management.45 In this context, even international studies emphasising ‘lack of education’ cannot accurately explain the situation in Iran, as the primary issue here is the lack of necessary ‘autonomy’. Therefore, the main solution is to grant clinical authority to nurses and other team members to implement non-pharmacological methods (under standard protocols), rather than simply increasing awareness.46 This structural change will not only cover the psychological and social dimensions of total pain but will also modify the physician-centred care culture by transforming pain management into a team-based process.

Patient-related barriers

The patient-related barriers in this study were interpreted not as individual resistance but as consequences of a cultural and caregiving system that normalises suffering. This system directly affects the spiritual, psychological and social dimensions of total pain.

Spiritual dimension: fatalism and sanctification of suffering

Quantitative and qualitative findings indicated that spiritual and cultural beliefs about pain, including viewing it as a ‘divine test’ or ‘an opportunity for patience and strengthening faith’, play a complex and multifaceted role in the pain experiences of cancer patients in Iran. These beliefs often serve as a powerful source of resilience and meaning, helping patients cope with their difficult circumstances.47 48 However, in some situations, the healthcare system’s neglect of these spiritual dimensions and the misalignment of therapeutic narratives with patients’ spiritual beliefs can lead to patients remaining silent about their pain, not due to weakness, but out of a desire to maintain dignity and align with their internal beliefs.49 While another finding shows that over 70% of cancer patients in Iran use traditional and complementary methods (such as poppy extract, herbal infusions, or prayers and chants) for pain relief,50 this behaviour is not a resistance to modern medicine but an effort to create harmony between the formal healthcare system and their personal spiritual-cultural system. When the healthcare system ignores these beliefs or challenges them without adequate understanding, patients may turn to informal methods or self-treatment instead of seeking pain relief, sometimes resulting in delays in receiving effective care.51 Therefore, the main challenge lies not in the existence of spiritual beliefs but in the failure to integrate these beliefs into comprehensive pain management. Thus, the care team must understand and incorporate these beliefs into the treatment plan with respect and cultural sensitivity.

Psychological dimension: fear of addiction and side effects

The fear of addiction and drug side effects, expressed by both patients and their families, must also be interpreted within the context of patient involvement in decision-making. A qualitative quote stating, “Patients and families do not even know they have the right to be free from pain,” suggests that this fear is a result of the paternalistic care model in Iran.52 This model directly impacts the psychological aspect of total pain by reinforcing feelings of helplessness and fear through the exclusion of patients from the decision-making process.52 Many healthcare providers, despite having theoretical knowledge, resort to pharmacological approaches not out of belief but due to time pressures and workload.53 Therefore, even if a patient wishes to participate, the system, due to organisational pressures, leaves no space for meaningful dialogue. Reducing or eliminating this fear requires a shift from a paternalistic model to a participatory model, where the patient is recognised not as a passive recipient but as an active partner in decision-making regarding their pain relief.54

Social dimension: forced silence and ignoring the patient’s voice

Quantitative findings, such as ‘the patient’s willingness to use narcotics’, alongside qualitative quotes like ‘Patients do not even report their pain because they think it is part of the disease’, indicate that a lack of trust in the system and fear of judgement compel patients to silence. This silence directly disrupts the social dimension of total pain because unreported pain not only goes unrelieved but also poisons the patient’s relationships with family and the treatment team. This phenomenon has been reported in studies where patients refrain from requesting morphine due to fear of being labelled as ‘addicts’, even when their pain is unbearable.55 56 Therefore, the failure to create an environment of trust and psychological safety within the healthcare system not only suppresses pain reporting but also destroys the therapeutic relationship. The solution to this issue is to cultivate a culture where the request for pain relief is viewed not as a weakness but as a legitimate human right.57

Organisational-related barriers

Organisational barriers are at the core of the failure to manage total pain in Iran. These barriers systematically affect all four dimensions of total pain.

Physical dimension: burdensome regulations and unequal access

Quantitative findings such as ‘overly strict regulations’ and qualitative observations like ‘the nurse is not even allowed to prescribe acetaminophen suppositories’ indicate that the centralisation of narcotics control within the Ministry of Health directly disrupts the physical dimension of total pain. This issue arises not from a lack of resources but from a system design that primarily restricts access. This challenge is not unique to Iran; in many counties, patients must wait hours in line to receive morphine.58 This directly exacerbates physical suffering and represents a deliberately restrictive policy aimed at ‘preventing misuse’, which hinders patients’ easy access to pain relief medications.59 Therefore, it may be argued that the only effective solution for holistic pain relief is to reassess centralised narcotics control policies to ensure patients’ right to access medications and pain relief without bureaucratic obstacles.60

Psychological and social dimensions: lack of interdisciplinary collaboration

The lack of interdisciplinary collaboration, which emerged in qualitative data as ‘everyone does their own thing’, should also be interpreted as a failure in managing the multifaceted aspects of total pain. Total pain, by definition, requires a team that includes a physician, nurse, psychologist, social worker and spiritual counsellor.61 The absence of this team in Iran means that each aspect of pain is managed in a fragmented and incomplete manner. This finding aligns directly with Iranian studies emphasising ‘rigid hierarchies’ and ‘disrespect for the role of nurses’.62 When a nurse cannot express her opinion about a patient’s pain, or when a spiritual counsellor is absent from the treatment team, the patient’s pain is viewed in a partial and incomplete manner, directly affecting their psychological and social dimensions. Establishing formal structures for interdisciplinary collaboration with clearly defined roles and operational authorities for each member of the healthcare team is the only practical way to comprehensively manage the psychological and social aspects of pain within a total pain approach in any healthcare system.63

Study limitation

One of the key limitations of the present study was the lack of interviews with other specialists involved in cancer care, such as pharmacists and other healthcare providers. Therefore, it is recommended that future studies conduct interviews with specialists from various fields regarding the barriers to pain management. Given that policy makers in pain management are considered key stakeholders, understanding their perspectives and experiences is vital. It is suggested that a qualitative study be conducted to explore their perceptions.

Conclusion

The findings of this research indicate that the challenges of effective pain management in cancer patients in Iran arise not from individual deficiencies or unidimensional factors but from a complex interplay of structural, cultural and organisational factors that are misaligned with the principles of the total pain theoretical framework at all levels of the healthcare system (from basic and clinical education to national policy-making and caregiving culture). Converging quantitative and qualitative data revealed that existing barriers in the physical (lack of systematic screening and assessment), psychological (structural fear of prescribing opioids), social (neglect of non-pharmacological methods and paternalistic care models) and spiritual (normalisation of suffering and cultural fatalism) dimensions are interconnected in a chain and stem from centralised, ambiguous and unsupported structures. These barriers operate systematically: burdensome regulations limit clinical decision-making; a physician-centred culture reduces the participation of nurses and patients; and the lack of interdisciplinary collaboration makes comprehensive and coordinated pain management difficult or impossible. In such a context, scattered or merely educational solutions, while necessary, are insufficient. Sustainable change requires systematic and multilevel reforms: revising laws to provide legal support for prescribers according to standard protocols, granting clinical authority to nurses within defined guidelines, subtly integrating patients’ spiritual beliefs into treatment plans and creating treatment teams with defined and participatory roles. Only in such an environment can the Iranian healthcare system be expected to address not only physical pain relief but also the overall suffering of the patient across all physical, psychological, social and spiritual dimensions.
