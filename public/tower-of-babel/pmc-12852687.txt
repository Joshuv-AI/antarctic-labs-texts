Investigating the source of acoustic anisotropy in the Asmari Formation, a fractured carbonate reservoir, using a well in the Southwest Iran
Authors: Saeed Yarmohammadi, Abdolrahim Javaherian, Mohammad Sharifi, Mohammad Reza Saberi, Hadi Mahdavi Basir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcoustic wave anisotropy in fractured carbonate reservoirs remains a key challenge for accurate reservoir evaluation and well placement. The Asmari Formation, one of Iran’s most productive reservoirs, is strongly affected by complex fracture networks and heterogeneity, significantly distorting its acoustic log responses. This study applies an integrated workflow combining facies analysis, borehole image interpretation, borehole geometry assessment, and advanced dipole sonic processing across five reservoir zones in a well in a Southwest Iranian oilfield. Geological controls were constrained through petrography and facies descriptions, while textural and structural features, fracture sets, and in-situ stresses were identified from borehole image logs. Dipole sonic data were processed to evaluate shear-wave splitting, azimuthal velocity variations, and Stoneley wave reflection coefficients, with fracture density used to assess connectivity and transmissivity. Results show that anisotropy is predominantly governed by fracture intensity and connectivity rather than facies variability. Zone 1 records the strongest anisotropy, while Zone 3 remains nearly isotropic. In contrast, Zone 4 demonstrates poor fracture connectivity reduces anisotropy despite higher fracture counts. Borehole effects in Zone 5 generate apparent anisotropy unrelated to the actual reservoir properties. Overall, anisotropy decreases systematically from Zone 1 to Zone 3, with connectivity and borehole artifacts as decisive modifiers. The above workflow highlights the necessity of integrating geological and geophysical datasets to separate genuine reservoir anisotropy from borehole-related effects, enabling more reliable prediction of fracture permeability and improved reservoir development strategies.Keywords: Acoustic anisotropy, Fractured carbonate reservoirs, Asmari Formation, Dipole sonic logging, Borehole image logsSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Fractured carbonate reservoirs contain many of the world’s hydrocarbons, but their strong heterogeneity and anisotropy make them difficult to characterize. These challenges directly affect production planning and reservoir management. Understanding carbonate reservoir performance requires precise formation evaluation, supported by conventional and advanced petrophysical techniques. Many sedimentary formations are anisotropic, so elastic properties, wave velocities, and fluid flow vary with direction. In the acoustic case, horizontal layering (e.g., shales) commonly produces a vertically transverse isotropic (VTI) medium, whereas aligned vertical to subvertical fractures give rise to a horizontally transverse isotropic (HTI) medium with azimuthal variations of the elastic properties1. One of the main difficulties is acoustic anisotropy, where elastic wave velocities vary with direction. This property reduces the reliability of sonic logs and complicates the estimation of elastic properties, stress directions, and fracture networks1,2. Anisotropy is caused by a combination of intrinsic rock properties, such as mineral texture and bedding, and external factors, including aligned open fractures, stress-related cracks, and borehole geometry effects3.

To explain these complexities, theoretical models were developed to link geological features with borehole acoustic responses. Earlier studies used linear slip theory to describe how fractures affect wave propagation4. These ideas were expanded with effective-medium theories and weak-anisotropy parameterizations, which helped explain shear-wave splitting and azimuthal velocity variations5,6. Practical applications followed, with techniques such as cross-dipole sonic analysis, acoustic imaging, and Stoneley-wave interpretation enabling direct evaluation of fracture orientation, density, and connectivity7–9. More recently, numerical simulations and advanced data-processing workflows have improved the reliability of anisotropy assessment in complex carbonate reservoirs10,11.

Recent advances in data processing have significantly improved the analysis of borehole acoustic measurements. Earlier works focused on enhancing mode separation for compressional, shear (fast and slow), and Stoneley waves through semblance picking, polarization analysis, and frequency optimization12 Subsequent developments introduced inverse modeling, translating azimuthal anisotropy into fracture compliance, density, and strike estimates. More recent studies have expanded this approach by integrating multi-frequency dipole data with Stoneley-wave attributes to assess permeability and transmissivity in the fractured reservoirs9,13. Despite these advances, most investigations continue to treat geological and geophysical aspects separately, and only limited efforts have developed fully integrated workflows that combine facies analysis, structural interpretation, borehole-condition correction, and advanced sonic-log processing14–16.

The Asmari Formation in southwest Iran exemplifies the challenges of fractured carbonate reservoirs, where heterogeneous lithofacies, complex diagenesis, and structurally induced fracture networks generate strong local anisotropy17,18. In this study, we applied a comprehensive, multidisciplinary workflow to five distinct reservoir zones in a fractured carbonate well in southwest Iran. The geological component involved lithofacies analysis based on core descriptions and thin-section petrography. The structural framework included fault and fracture characterization, and in-situ stress interpretation from image logs. Borehole geometry effects, such as breakouts and washouts, were assessed using caliper, image, and trajectory logs. Advanced dipole sonic processing was also performed to extract high-quality compressional, shear, and Stoneley waveforms for anisotropy analysis. Additionally, we statistically evaluated Stoneley-wave reflection coefficients (RC) and fracture density (FD) across reservoir zones to quantify fracture-related anisotropy in acoustic behavior and reservoir quality, and computed Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients between FD and RC. The novelty of this study lies in an explicitly cause-oriented workflow for explaining acoustic anisotropy. We systematically evaluate sedimentological facies, structural features, petrophysical properties and wellbore conditions, and directly compare them with DSI-derived anisotropy attributes (shear-wave splitting, fast-shear azimuth, Stoneley RC) and FD from image logs. In the Asmari case study, this separation of fracture, fabric and borehole effects fills a gap in earlier image-log/DSI studies and provides new insight into elastic-wave behavior in complex carbonate reservoirs.

Geological setting

Regional geology

The studied field is located along the northern margin of the Dezful Embayment, within the central Zagros Fold–Thrust Belt of southwestern Iran. Regionally, the embayment is recognized as one of the most prolific hydrocarbon provinces in the Middle East. The structural framework of the embayment is influenced by several major faults that separate it from the adjacent Fars Platform and Lorestan Zone (Fig. 1a). Structurally, the northern limit of the embayment is controlled by the Bala-Rud Fault adjoining the Lorestan Zone, while its eastern margin is bounded by the Kazeroon Fault, separating it from the Fars Platform19,20. These structural boundaries strongly influenced subsidence and sediment accumulation. The Fars Platform and Lorestan Zone preserve relatively thinner sedimentary successions because accommodation space was limited. In contrast, the Dezful Embayment contains a much thicker stratigraphic column. This difference reflects the effect of prolonged tectonic subsidence in the embayment21.

(a) Structural map of the Zagros fold–thrust belt in southwest Iran, showing tectonic subdivisions, major faults, and anticline axes, modified after23. (b) Stratigraphic column illustrating the Asmari Formation within the Cenozoic stratigraphic section of the Zagros Basin24.

The studied wells reveal a stratigraphic succession of, from youngest to oldest, the Aghajari, Gachsaran, Asmari, Upper Pabdeh, Shahbazan, Khashakan, Lower Pabdeh, Gourpi, Ilam, and Sarvak formations (Fig. 1b). The Oligocene–early Miocene Asmari Formation is the principal reservoir within this sequence. It is composed mainly of limestones and dolomites with shale and minor anhydrite interbeds, conformably overlying the Pabdeh Formation and overlain by the Gachsaran Formation22.

Structural geology

The field exhibits a near east–west trend in alignment with the Bala-Rud Fault system25. Its axis contains two culminations. The structural dip averages ~ 5°, increasing locally to a maximum of 25° on the flanks. Three longitudinal faults segment the field. They include two thrust faults and one strike–slip fault. Together they divide the structure into three compartments. The thrust fault bounds tail of the reservoir to the south while the strike–slip fault separate the northern part from the rest of the reservoir.26. The interaction of this tectonic configuration with the field’s marginal position within the embayment increases fracturing intensity and encourages structural compartmentalization. Such structural attributes firmly control the development and magnitude of the acoustic wave anisotropy.

Facies study

The Asmari Formation was deposited in a carbonate ramp system. This depositional setting produced systematic lateral and vertical facies changes. High-energy, grain-supported shoal deposits grade into low-energy, mud-supported outer-ramp facies. Petrographic analysis identified 23 microfacies that were grouped into eight facies associations. Of these, five to seven represent deposition during the Eocene–Miocene interval, corresponding to the reservoir interval of the studied field (Fig. 2). These facies associations reflect the characteristic architecture of the carbonate ramp system27. Porosity and pore type variations are the characteristics of these environments. Well-sorted grainstones in shoal settings exhibit high interparticle porosity and good connectivity, which reduces compressional and shear velocities. Mud-supported facies, in contrast, tend to retain finer, more isolated pore networks, resulting in higher velocities. Directional pore alignment—caused by grain orientation, lamination, or dissolution—can impart measurable anisotropy to acoustic wave propagation.

In the Asmari Formation, depositional porosity is further modified by diagenesis. Dolomitization, cementation, and stylolitization strongly influence rock fabrics. Shoal facies may enhance anisotropy through directional frameworks. Stylolitic layering and dissolution-enhanced pathways in finer facies also contribute to anisotropy by aligning pore structures. Integration of facies classification, porosity distribution, and borehole acoustic responses allows a distinction between depositional–diagenetic anisotropy and anisotropy associated with tectonic features such as fractures and stress-aligned cracks.

Methodology

This study employs an integrated, multi-step workflow to characterize acoustic wave anisotropy in fractured carbonate reservoirs to identify its underlying causes. The approach combines geological, structural, and borehole geophysical analyses to determine the primary factors influencing anisotropy and to distinguish formation-related effects from borehole artifacts.

The workflow begins with geological characterization, including lithofacies and petrographic analyses, to establish the depositional and diagenetic framework. Based on image logs and well trajectory data, we used structural interpretation to define fracture orientation, density, and in-situ stress regimes. Here, borehole geometry analysis identifies breakouts, washouts, and tensile fractures that may bias acoustic measurements. Advanced dipole sonic log processing is subsequently applied to extract high-quality compressional, shear, and Stoneley waveforms, which form the basis for anisotropy evaluation. Shear-wave splitting and azimuthal velocity variations are analyzed to quantify anisotropy, while RC derived from Stoneley waves are combined with FD measurements for further statistical analysis. Finally, the results are integrated to assess fracture connectivity and fluid transmissivity to build a robust foundation for enhanced reservoir characterization and development planning (Fig. 3).

Textural and structural features

Comprehensive reservoir characterization is achieved by combining the ultrasonic borehole image (UBI) tool, which delivers high-resolution imaging in difficult conditions, with the oil-based mud image (OBMI) tool, which provides detailed microresistivity data in oil-based mud. The integrated analysis of facies and structural data allows separation of fabric-induced anisotropy, arising from depositional and diagenetic features, from fracture-induced anisotropy associated with stress-controlled jointing. This distinction is essential for interpreting cross-dipole sonic logs and predicting the fractured carbonate reservoirs’ directional permeability and fluid-flow behavior.

Well integrity evaluation

Wellbore image analysis was employed to assess borehole integrity, where breakouts were observed in both OBMI and UBI dynamic images (Fig. 7a). Thirty-three breakout events were identified, distributed across two main depth clusters: 3270–3300 m (10 events) and 3300–3323 m (15 events). Stereonet analysis indicates that these breakouts exhibit a consistent mean azimuth of 144° with a dip of 90° (strike 54°–234°) (Fig. 7b). In stress interpretation, breakout orientations are typically aligned parallel to the minimum horizontal stress axis (Shmin), meaning that the maximum horizontal stress (SHmax) is oriented perpendicular to this trend. Based on the breakout geometry, Shmin is interpreted at ~ 144° and SHmax at ~ 54° (Table 1).

(a) Breakouts and deformation of borehole shapes in the reservoir to detect minimum and maximum horizontal stresses. (b) The stereonet illustrates the trend of breakouts, representing the minimum horizontal stress at 33 degrees northeast.

From an anisotropy perspective, breakout development can influence acoustic measurements by distorting wave travel paths and introducing directional bias in slowness estimates. In cross-dipole sonic logging, this effect may manifest as apparent anisotropy unrelated to the intrinsic rock fabric or fracture network. Therefore, accurate identification and orientation mapping of breakouts is essential for distinguishing stress-induced wellbore deformation from true formation anisotropy. In this study, breakout orientations provided a means to quantify the in-situ stress regime and served as a control dataset against which fracture-induced anisotropy interpretations could be calibrated.

Dipole sonic log evaluation

The dipole shear sonic imager (DSI) was employed to obtain high-resolution monopole and cross-dipole acoustic measurements for formation evaluation, fracture characterization, and anisotropy analysis in the carbonate reservoir under study. The tool consists of a monopole piezoelectric transmitter positioned above two orthogonally oriented dipole transmitters, an array of eight wideband piezoelectric hydrophone receivers spaced at 6-inch intervals, and an electronics section that controls acquisition and processing28. The monopole source operates at low frequencies to generate Stoneley waves and at higher frequencies to produce compressional (P) and shear (S) body waves. The dipole transmitters generate low-frequency flexural waves in two perpendicular azimuths, enabling in-situ shear-wave velocity measurements in orthogonal directions and thus allowing azimuthal anisotropy assessment2,29.

In this study, quality control was performed before slowness extraction using the caliper and borehole image logs to assess wellbore conditions. The caliper data indicated minor caving in the upper logged interval and more severe breakouts toward the base. Nevertheless, these enlargements did not significantly degrade the dipole acoustic data, and the waveforms retained sufficient quality for advanced analysis. After Full Waveform Sonic (FWS) processing, compressional slowness was derived from high-frequency monopole data, shear slowness from dipole and monopole modes, and Stoneley slowness from low-frequency monopole data. Fast and slow shear components were obtained from mode X measurements. Then, pre-processing included time-average filtering to suppress coherent noise, band-pass filtering (3–9 kHz) to remove unwanted frequencies, and slowness-time coherency (STC) analysis to determine arrival times and phase velocities based on semblance calculations30 were performed. Furthermore, Chevron pattern analysis was applied to Stoneley waveforms to assess fracture transmissivity qualitatively. When a Stoneley wave encounters a transmissive fracture, part of the drilling fluid is forced into the fracture. Subsequently, it returns to the borehole, generating a weaker secondary Stoneley wave in the reverse direction. This interaction produces a characteristic Chevron pattern in the time–distance display7,31. The reflected wave component was isolated by applying time-domain cleaning, frequency filtering, and frequency–wavenumber (f-k) separation. Reflection coefficients, computed as the ratio of Chevron energy to residual waveform energy, served as qualitative indicators of fracture connectivity, although interpretation was performed with caution due to potential influences from borehole breakouts and lithological variations. Anisotropy analysis utilized the mode X cross-dipole dataset, which provided four waveform sets (XX, XY, YY, YX) at each depth for a total of 32 complete waveforms per station4,32. In anisotropic formations, the fast shear wave is polarized parallel to the open fracture strike and maximum horizontal stress, while the slow shear wave is polarized perpendicular to it. Maximum and minimum energy curves were computed, with greater separation indicating stronger anisotropy. The azimuth of the fast shear polarization (AZIM_FAST) was taken to the maximum horizontal stress direction. In this study, the DSI-derived fast shear azimuths were consistent with breakout orientations interpreted from ultrasonic borehole images, confirming the reliability of the stress orientation estimates.

Integrating monopole and cross-dipole sonic measurements, Chevron pattern analysis, and borehole image data provided a robust framework for differentiating true formation anisotropy from borehole-related artifacts, mapping fracture networks, and constraining the in-situ stress regime. These results are essential for accurate elastic property estimation, geomechanical modeling, and well placement in fractured carbonate reservoirs33–35.

Results and discussion

The interpretation of acoustic anisotropy in the Asmari Formation was developed through a stepwise workflow that integrated facies and petrographic studies, borehole image logs, borehole geometry assessment, and dipole sonic processing. Facies analysis provided the depositional and diagenetic framework, while image logs captured structural and textural attributes such as bedding, lithological boundaries, stylolites, and conductive fractures. The most decisive constraints on anisotropy were obtained from advanced dipole sonic processing, which quantified shear-wave splitting and azimuthal velocity variations, and from Stoneley-wave reflection coefficients, which served as direct indicators of fracture connectivity and fluid transmissivity. Combining these datasets and accounting for borehole geometry effects, made it possible to distinguish true formation anisotropy from borehole-related artifacts.

This study shows that facies variability exerts only a weak, background influence on acoustic anisotropy (Fig. 8). In the upper reservoir, Zones 1 and 2 contain rapid alternations between shoal grainstone/packstone facies and muddier ramp deposits, which introduce heterogeneity but mainly enhance the expression of fracture-related anisotropy where fractures are present. Zone 3 consists mainly of a laterally homogeneous interval dominated by a single crystalline carbonate facies, characterized by relatively uniform porosity–permeability, and exhibiting only weak anisotropy. Zone 4 again comprises fractured facies alternations, where the fracture network primarily controls anisotropy. Zone 5 consists of tighter, mixed facies with weak reservoir quality, and the anisotropic response there is interpreted to be controlled mainly by borehole breakouts rather than by the formation fabric. Overall, facies type and variability chiefly affect reservoir quality, whereas fracture presence and connectivity remain the primary controls on acoustic anisotropy.

In Zone 1, the highest average FD (~ 2.50 m⁻1) and the largest mean RC (~ 0.075) occur together, with a weak negative correlation (Pearson ≈ − 0.15, Spearman ≈ − 0.30). The borehole is slightly irregular, and some local artefacts are possible. Anisotropy is therefore weak–moderate and of mixed origin, with fractures as the main contributor but with possible borehole influence (Tables 3 and 4, Figs. 9 and 10). Zone 2 shows moderately high FD (~ 1.49 m⁻1), intermediate RC (~ 0.031) and a weak positive correlation (Pearson ≈ 0.14, Spearman ≈ 0.23). The borehole is close to gauge with no major breakouts. Anisotropy is clear and relatively strong, dominated by an intense, connected fracture network and consistent, characterized by fracture-related HTI (Tables 3 and 4, Figs. 9 and 10). Zone 3 has very low FD (~ 0.08 m⁻1), near-baseline RC (~ 0.026) and almost no correlation (Pearson ≈ − 0.02, Spearman ≈ − 0.01). The borehole is regular, and anisotropy is weak. This is consistent with a matrix- or facies-dominated interval with limited fracture or borehole effects (Tables 3 and 4, Figs. 9 and 10). Zone 4 has a moderate FD (~ 0.72 m⁻1), low RC (~ 0.025) and a moderate positive correlation (Pearson ≈ 0.24, Spearman ≈ 0.37). The borehole is generally regular. Anisotropy is moderate and mainly fracture-related, enhanced where fracture density is higher (Tables 3 and 4, Figs. 9 and 10). Zone 5 exhibits moderate FD (~ 0.59 m⁻1) but relatively high RC (~ 0.053) and a moderate negative correlation (Pearson ≈ − 0.24, Spearman ≈ − 0.44). The borehole is irregular with local breakouts and collapse, and the anisotropy is partly strong but not consistent with FD alone. This pattern indicates that the apparent anisotropy in Zone 5 is significantly affected by borehole breakouts/collapse rather than by fracture density alone (Tables 3 and 4, Figs. 9 and 10).

A more detailed zone-by-zone evaluation confirms these statistical observations. Zone 1 displays the strongest anisotropic response, with abundant, stress-aligned fractures and elevated RC values supported by lithological impedance contrasts created by interbedded dolomite and thin anhydrite. Zone 2 shows a similar mechanism, but diagenetic alteration likely reduces fracture connectivity and transmissivity. Zone 3 is essentially isotropic, reflecting the absence of significant fractures and minimal acoustic anisotropy apart from weak transverse isotropy imparted by layering of dolomite and calcite. Zone 4 contains several steeply dipping fractures aligned with the stress regime, but their poor connectivity limits anisotropic development despite structural evidence. Zone 5, in contrast, demonstrates how borehole artifacts can dominate anisotropic responses, as breakouts and irregularities lead to strong fast–slow shear separation and elevated RC values unrelated to true formation properties (Figs. 11, 12, 13, 14 and 15).

Overall, this interval is best described as anisotropy-poor, with weak layering-related transverse isotropy and negligible fracture-controlled anisotropy.

Integrated analysis of Zone 4 (3133–3248 m) in the Asmari Formation. The borehole profile is smooth and near-gauge, and image logs indicate a moderate number of steeply dipping conductive fractures, grouped in three depth intervals (13, 22, and 25 dips). Stereonet plots indicate E–W to SSW fracture orientations consistent with the regional stress regime, and dipole sonic data display moderate fast–slow shear separation with fast-shear azimuths aligned with fracture strike, pointing to structural control on anisotropy. Persistently low Stoneley reflection coefficients, however, suggest poor fracture transmissivity and a relatively muted anisotropic response.

Integrated analysis of Zone 5 (3229–3307 m) in the Asmari Formation. The borehole profile exhibits clear enlargements and irregularities toward the base, consistent with breakouts and wellbore instability observed in the dynamic image. Only a few conductive fractures are identified, and stereonet plots indicate sparse, scattered orientations compared with the fracture-rich zones. Cross-dipole sonic data nonetheless display clear fast–slow shear separation, with fast-shear azimuths broadly aligned with SHmax. Overall, the anisotropic response in Zone 5 is interpreted as being primarily controlled by borehole geometry artefacts rather than by the intrinsic formation fabric or fracture system.

Beyond fractures, fabric-related features observed along the well also contribute to anisotropy. Nearly horizontal bedding planes and lithological boundaries generate weak transverse isotropy, crossbedding introduces depositional fabrics with preferred grain alignment that enhance directional velocity differences, and stylolites formed under compression create aligned microcracks and insoluble seams that reduce stiffness perpendicular to their orientation. Although weaker than fracture-induced anisotropy, these features form a persistent background control on wave propagation.

Overall, the results demonstrate that anisotropy in the Asmari Formation is multi-factorial, governed by fracture abundance, fracture connectivity, lithological heterogeneity, stress orientation, and borehole geometry. Zones 1 and 2 highlight the role of well-connected fracture systems, Zone 3 confirms the near absence of anisotropy, Zone 4 illustrates the importance of fracture connectivity, and Zone 5 shows how borehole instability can generate spurious anisotropy if not recognized. These findings emphasize the necessity of an integrated workflow to correctly attribute anisotropy signatures to their true geological or borehole origins, thereby providing a reliable basis for reservoir characterization.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the value of an integrated workflow combining facies analysis, borehole image logs, borehole geometry assessment, and advanced dipole sonic processing to unravel the causes of acoustic anisotropy in the Asmari Formation. The results show that depositional facies and diagenetic fabrics establish a weak background anisotropy, while the dominant control on anisotropy expression is the abundance, orientation, and connectivity of fractures. The reservoir was subdivided into five zones, and zone-by-zone analysis revealed distinct quantitative differences. Zone 1 exhibits the strongest anisotropy, with the highest fracture density (FD ≈ 2.5 m⁻1) and the largest RC values (≈ 0.075), together with a weak negative FD–RC correlation (Pearson ≈ − 0.15, Spearman ≈ − 0.30) and slightly irregular borehole conditions, indicating that the apparent anisotropic response is mainly fracture-driven but locally amplified by borehole-related effects. Zone 2 also shows relatively strong anisotropy (FD ≈ 1.5 m⁻1; RC ≈ 0.031) and a weak positive FD–RC correlation (Pearson ≈ 0.14, Spearman ≈ 0.23), reflecting dense fracture networks with measurable acoustic expression. Zone 3, dominated by crystalline dolosparite, remains nearly isotropic (FD ≈ 0.08 m⁻1; RC ≈ 0.026) with essentially no FD–RC correlation (Pearson ≈ − 0.02, Spearman ≈ − 0.01), consistent with only one detected fracture. Zone 4 contains more fractures (FD ≈ 0.72 m⁻1) but shows equally low RC values (≈ 0.025) and a moderate positive FD–RC correlation (Pearson ≈ 0.24, Spearman ≈ 0.37), demonstrating that limited connectivity yields only moderate anisotropy even in fractured intervals. Zone 5, with mixed facies, records moderate RC values (≈ 0.053) despite lower FD (≈ 0.59 m⁻1) and a moderate negative FD–RC correlation (Pearson ≈ − 0.24, Spearman ≈ − 0.44); in this interval, borehole integrity issues and breakouts produce apparent anisotropy that does not represent true reservoir properties.

Overall, anisotropy in the Asmari reservoir decreases systematically from Zone 1 to Zone 3, with Zone 4 illustrating the importance of fracture connectivity, and Zone 5 highlighting how wellbore artifacts can be misinterpreted as formation anisotropy. The findings confirm that while facies variability provides subtle elastic heterogeneity, anisotropy is predominantly governed by fractures. This distinction highlights the importance of integrating geological and geophysical datasets to distinguish genuine reservoir anisotropy from borehole-related effects, thereby improving predictions of fracture permeability and supporting more reliable reservoir characterization.
