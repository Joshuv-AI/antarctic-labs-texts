Effect of Lidocaine Spray on the Sedative Dose of Remimazolam During Gastroscopy in Adults: A Prospective, Randomized Dose-Response Trial Using an Up-Down Sequential Allocation Method
Authors: Xiuxia Gu, Weiling Guo, Huan Zhang, Min Fu, Chengcheng Xue, Fanceng Ji, Yongbo Song, Hongyi Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the effect of oropharyngeal topical lidocaine spray on the sedative dose of remimazolam required for gastroscopy in adults.Patients and MethodsPatients undergoing painless gastroscopy at our hospital were selected and randomly divided into a lidocaine spray group (Group L) and a control group (Group C) using a random number table method, with 25 patients enrolled in Group L and 24 patients enrolled in Group C. Patients in Group L received oropharyngeal spray containing lidocaine aerosol (approximately 16 mg of lidocaine per spray), with two sprays administered. Patients in Group C received oropharyngeal spray containing normal saline aerosol. At the start of sedation, alfentanil was intravenously injected at a dose of 3 μg/kg, followed by remimazolam administration using a sequential method. The initial intravenous dose of remimazolam was 0.2 mg/kg, with a dose adjustment gradient of 0.02 mg/kg. The median effective dose (ED50) of remimazolam for successful first-dose sedation during gastroscopy was calculated using probit regression analysis.ResultsThe ED50 (95% confidence interval) of remimazolam in Group L was 0.149 (0.125–0.167) mg/kg, while that in Group C was 0.191 (0.147–0.217) mg/kg. The relative median potency between the two groups was 0.783 (95% CI: 0.381–0.959). In addition, no statistically significant differences were observed in hemodynamic changes or related adverse events between the two groups (P > 0.05).ConclusionLidocaine spray can be used in combination with remimazolam for gastroscopic examination to reduce the sedative dose of remimazolam during gastroscopy in adults.

Introduction

Gastroscopy is a commonly used minimally invasive diagnostic and therapeutic procedure for digestive system disorders.1 In recent years, as the concept of comfort care has been increasingly promoted in clinical practice, painless gastroscopy has gradually become the mainstream clinical choice.2 This approach primarily employs anesthetic medications to allow patients to undergo the entire examination in a pain-free, unconscious, and relaxed state. It not only ensures the smooth performance of endoscopic procedures but also significantly improves patients’ medical experience. Clinical practice has revealed that using a single sedative or anesthetic agent often makes it difficult to achieve the desired depth of sedation while keeping the risk of adverse effects low. A rational combination drug regimen, through synergistic effects among medications, can enhance sedative and analgesic efficacy, reduce the required dose of each individual drug, and lower the incidence of adverse reactions associated with excessive dosing.3

Remimazolam has been recommended for use in gastrointestinal endoscopy owing to its properties of hemodynamic stability and mild respiratory depression.4 The current clinical recommendation for the initial dose of remimazolam in adult patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy is 0.15–0.2 mg/kg. Nevertheless, a multicenter study including 1883 patients demonstrated that the success rate of the first sedative dose of 0.2 mg/kg remimazolam was only 72.2%,5 necessitating additional doses to complete the procedure. The need for supplemental dosing may, to a certain degree, increase the risk of adverse events such as hypotension and respiratory depression.

Lidocaine, a widely used amide local anesthetic, can be delivered as an aerosol spray to the oropharyngeal mucosa to achieve rapid topical anesthesia.6 It has an onset of 1–2 minutes and a duration of 15–20 minutes, effectively reducing pharyngolaryngeal reflex sensitivity and the irritative response to gastroscope insertion.7 A previous meta-analysis demonstrated that intravenous or topical lidocaine can improve the quality of propofol sedation in patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopic procedures, and that intravenous lidocaine infusion can also reduce the dose of propofol required during the procedure.8 Therefore, this study hypothesizes that the combined use of lidocaine spray can reduce the sedative dose of remimazolam required for gastroscopy in adults, thereby enhancing its sedative efficacy. Given that no studies have yet investigated the combination of these two agents for gastroscopy, this study aims to evaluate the clinical effects of this regimen, with the goal of providing a safer and more rational basis for combination pharmacotherapy in painless gastroscopy.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Enrollment

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Weifang People’s Hospital (KYLL20260214-4) and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (available at www.chictr.org.cn; Registration No. ChiCTR2600120898, dated March 22, 2026). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The study is reported in line with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Patients who underwent painless gastroscopy at our hospital between March 2026 and May 2026 were enrolled. Inclusion criteria were: age 18–65 years, body mass index 18~25 kg/m2, and ASA grade I or II. Exclusion criteria were: pharyngeal or palatopharyngeal reflex hypersensitivity, pharyngitis, history of severe cardiopulmonary disease, history of psychiatric or neurological disorders, known allergy to any study drugs, and long-term use of opioids or other analgesics or sedatives.

In this study, the random sequence was generated using a random number table by an independent statistician who was not involved in the conduct of the study. Patients were randomly assigned to either the lidocaine spray group (Group L) or the control group (Group C). Group L received lidocaine aerosol (approximately 16 mg per spray), while Group C received normal saline aerosol via the same device (Figure 1). The results of the random allocation were sealed in opaque envelopes. Ten minutes before gastroscopy, an independent operating room nurse performed oropharyngeal spray anesthesia according to the randomization scheme, administering two sprays at a 5-minute interval, each lasting approximately one second. Patients were blinded to group allocation. The examination was then performed by a designated anesthesiologist and endoscopist, both of whom were also blinded to group assignment. Anesthetic drugs were prepared by another anesthesia nurse based on each patient’s body weight.Figure 1Spray device.Illustration of a spray device and its application in the oropharynx.

Anesthesia Management

Patients underwent routine preoperative fasting and fluid restriction. Oropharyngeal spray was administered according to group allocation before operating room entry. After entering the room, an intravenous line was placed in the upper limb, and lactated Ringer’s solution was infused. Non-invasive blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), electrocardiogram (ECG), and pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2) were monitored throughout the procedure. Patients were placed in the left lateral position. Preoxygenation (3 L/min) was given before anesthesia. Alfentanil (3 μg/kg) was administered intravenously, followed by remimazolam intravenously 1 minute later. The starting induction dose of remimazolam was selected as 0.2 mg/kg. A sequential (up-and-down) method was adopted for the trial, with dose adjustments made according to the success or failure of sedation, and the dose step size was 0.02 mg/kg. Successful sedation was defined as a modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score≤1, loss of eyelash reflex within 1 minute, and no body movement, coughing, or retching during jaw lift and endoscope insertion. For a successful outcome, the next patient received a dose reduced by 0.02 mg/kg; for a failed outcome, the dose was increased by 0.02 mg/kg. The study was completed after seven alternating failed–successful pairs were observed.

If anesthesia failed, a rescue dose of remimazolam (2 mg) was given, with a 1-minute interval between each rescue dose. Additional 2 mg doses were repeated as needed until successful anesthesia was achieved. Respiratory depression (SpO2 < 95%) was managed with a head-tilt chin-lift maneuver and increased oxygen flow; facemask ventilation was provided if no improvement occurred, and endotracheal intubation was performed when indicated. For persistent hypotension or systolic BP < 90 mmHg, ephedrine (6 mg) was administered. Atropine (0.25 mg) was given intravenously for heart rate < 50 beats/min.

Assessment of Primary and Secondary Outcomes

Primary outcome measure: the median effective dose (ED50) of remimazolam for successful first-dose sedation during gastroscopy.

Secondary outcome measures included: rescue dose, number of rescue doses per patient, time to loss of consciousness, hemodynamic changes, and the incidence of related adverse events, including injection pain, hiccups, and respiratory depression. Rescue dose was defined as the cumulative total rescue medication dose per patient (mg), and number of rescue doses per patient was defined as the total number of rescue administrations per patient (n). Respiratory depression was defined as pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2) below 95%. Hemodynamic parameters were monitored at the following time points: T0 (upon patient entry into the operating room), T1 (immediately after remimazolam administration), T2 (immediately after endoscope insertion), and T3 (5 minutes after the start of the examination).

Statistical Analysis

The pre-specified sample size of 25 patients per group in this study was determined using the Dixon sequential up-and-down method, which generally requires 20–40 subjects.9 The enrolled sample size was deemed statistically adequate once the seventh turning point was observed.10 The turning point, defined as a shift from successful sedation to sedation failure, was established based on previous relevant studies.11 Successful sedation was defined as a modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score≤1, loss of eyelash reflex within 1 minute, and no body movement, coughing, or retching during jaw lift and endoscope insertion. Patient enrollment was terminated upon the occurrence of seven turning points.

Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution, and Levene’s test was used to evaluate homogeneity of variances. Continuous variables following a normal distribution, such as age, height, and body weight, were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For continuous variables that met both normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions, comparisons between groups were performed using the independent-samples t-test. Continuous variables that met normality but not homogeneity of variance were analyzed using Welch’s t-test. Continuous variables not conforming to a normal distribution were presented as median (interquartile range) [M(Q1-Q3)], and comparisons between groups were performed using the rank-sum test. Categorical variables, including ASA physical status and sex, were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For the primary outcome measure, probit regression analysis was used to calculate the ED50 and 95% confidence interval of remimazolam for successful first-dose sedation during gastroscopy. The ED50 values of the two groups were compared using the relative median potency method derived from probit regression analysis. A significant difference was indicated when the 95% confidence interval of the potency ratio did not include 1. For repeated continuous variables measured intraoperatively, such as blood pressure and heart rate, a two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (two-way repeated measures ANOVA) was used to evaluate the main effects of group, time, and their interaction. For variables that did not satisfy Mauchly’s test of sphericity, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. For significant interactions, simple effects of group and time were further evaluated. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For multiple comparisons, the P values were adjusted using Bonferroni correction.

Results

In Group L, the mean initial dose of remimazolam for sedation was 9.68 mg, with 14 patients achieving successful sedation and 11 patients experiencing failed sedation requiring rescue sedation. In Group C, the mean initial dose of remimazolam for sedation was 12.32 mg, with 13 patients achieving successful sedation and 11 patients experiencing failed sedation requiring rescue sedation. Discussion

The results of this study show that pretreatment with lidocaine spray reduced the ED50 of remimazolam for successful first-dose sedation during gastroscopy by 22%, implying that the administered dose of remimazolam can be decreased by approximately one-fifth when the two agents are used in combination, which holds potential clinical value in reducing drug accumulation effects. This finding provides important clinical evidence for optimizing sedation regimens for gastroscopy and enhancing the safety and comfort of anesthesia. Furthermore, we also observed hemodynamic changes and related adverse reactions in both groups, and no significant differences were found between the two groups.

Remimazolam has demonstrated favorable efficacy in gastrointestinal endoscopy. Two meta-analyses have shown that,12,13 compared with propofol, patients sedated with remimazolam have lower incidences of intraoperative hypotension and respiratory depression. However, the success rate of sedation with remimazolam is relatively lower, and inadequate sedation may occur. One clinical study compared the efficacy and safety of remimazolam, ciprofol, and propofol in hysteroscopic surgery14 and found that the remimazolam group had a lower incidence of hypotension, but the frequency of body movements was significantly higher than that in the ciprofol and propofol groups. Therefore, it is necessary to combine remimazolam with other drugs to improve the success rate of sedation. Furthermore, the main stimulation during gastroscopy is concentrated in the pharynx and larynx. The mechanical stimulation of the pharyngeal and laryngeal mucosa during endoscope insertion can trigger pharyngeal reflexes, coughing, and agitation.15 This stress state enhances the body’s tolerance to sedative drugs, leading to an increased dose of remimazolam required to achieve effective sedation. Lidocaine spray, as a self-administered topical mucosal anesthetic, is characterized by rapid onset, strong mucosal penetration, and high safety.16 By directly acting on the nerve endings of the pharyngeal and laryngeal mucosa through topical administration, it quickly blocks the conduction of nerve impulses, thereby effectively reducing mechanical stimulation during endoscope insertion and attenuating the stress response. This is the core mechanism by which it reduces the sedative dose of remimazolam.

A randomized controlled trial involving 527 patients demonstrated that spraying lidocaine onto the pharyngeal and laryngeal region 10 minutes before endotracheal intubation significantly reduced postoperative throat pain,17 suggesting that lidocaine spray can attenuate the irritation of the pharyngeal and laryngeal mucosa and reduce pain responses by blocking nerve conduction in advance. In studies on patients undergoing pharyngeal surgery, Zhou et al18 also showed that applying lidocaine aerosol to the surgical site at the end of the procedure produced a good analgesic effect on acute pain after pharyngoplasty. Furthermore, Cheng et al19 reported that preoperative use of lidocaine aerosol effectively lowered the incidence of pharyngeal complications after pediatric tonsillectomy and improved children’s postoperative pharyngeal comfort. All these clinical studies have confirmed the reliable anesthetic and analgesic effects of lidocaine spray in pharyngeal intervention from multiple perspectives. Previous studies have shown that intravenous midazolam combined with topical lidocaine spray can provide adequate comfort and tolerance for diagnostic esophagogastroduodenoscopy,20 indicating that topical pharyngeal anesthesia combined with benzodiazepine sedation has been applied in clinical practice. However, remimazolam, as a novel benzodiazepine, differs significantly from midazolam in terms of onset speed, metabolic pathway, and recovery characteristics.21,22 Its efficacy in combination with lidocaine spray for the purpose of deep sedation has not yet been supported by prospective study data. This study firstly investigated and evaluated the influence of lidocaine spray on the required sedative dose of remimazolam during gastroscopy, which can provide valuable reference for its clinical popularization and application.

The core objective of painless gastroscopy is to eliminate patients’ discomfort, such as pain and nausea, during the diagnostic and therapeutic procedure, and a deep level of sedation is usually required. Based on previous literature on the application doses of remimazolam for painless gastrointestinal endoscopy in adults,5,23 as well as clinical experience with the concomitant use of alfentanil, the starting induction dose of remimazolam in both groups was set at 0.2 mg/kg. In dose-related studies on remimazolam, Sheng et al demonstrated that a single injection of remimazolam tosilate at doses ranging from 0.025 to 0.4 mg/kg exhibited good safety and tolerability.24 Chae et al estimated that the recommended dose of intravenous bolus remimazolam for loss of consciousness ranged from 0.14 to 0.33 mg/kg.25 The ED50 and ED95 values in both groups of our study fell within this recommended dose range; however, the control group (Group C) exhibited a relatively wide confidence interval for ED95, which is attributable to the inherent limitation of the Dixon up-and-down method—namely, that data are mostly concentrated around the ED50, limiting the precision of estimating tail quantiles such as ED95. Additionally, Cui et al compared the efficacy and safety of different doses of remimazolam (0.2 mg/kg, 0.3 mg/kg, and 0.4 mg/kg) for upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, and their findings indicated that higher doses of remimazolam were associated with a higher success rate of sedation. However, with increasing doses, the incidence of adverse events related to remimazolam, such as dizziness and prolonged sedation recovery time, also increased significantly.26 Our study addresses this issue by demonstrating that the concomitant use of lidocaine spray can reduce the sedative dose of remimazolam while ensuring successful sedation, thereby avoiding excessively high doses of remimazolam.

This study still has several limitations that need to be improved in subsequent investigations. First, it was conducted at a single medical center, and large-sample, multicenter trials are required to verify our findings. Second, this is an initial exploration of combining lidocaine spray with sedatives for gastrointestinal endoscopy. The enrolled subjects were all physically healthy adults, and further research is still needed to explore its efficacy in special populations such as obese and elderly patients. Third, and importantly, the study was designed and powered primarily for ED50 estimation. The sample size is relatively small, and therefore the absence of statistically significant differences in respiratory, hemodynamic, or other adverse events should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent safety between the two groups. Larger adequately powered studies focusing on safety endpoints are warranted. Fourth, this study only focused on the ED50 of remimazolam during the anesthesia induction period. We did not conduct follow-up observations on the maintenance sedation dosage or recovery time during the examination. Future research can expand the sample size and prolong the observation period, so as to comprehensively evaluate the actual effect of lidocaine spray on gastroscopy anesthesia. In addition, the pharyngeal numbness caused by lidocaine spray may compromise the integrity of patient blinding.

Conclusion

Lidocaine spray can be used in combination with remimazolam for gastroscopic examination to reduce the sedative dose of remimazolam during gastroscopy in adults.
