Association of primary tumor surgery with survival outcomes in patients with bone metastases across multiple cancer types
Authors: Pei-Hang Xu, Bochong Shi, Tianqi Li, Mingkang Tian, Meng Fang, Mo Cheng, Wending Huang, Fei Ren, Weiluo Cai, Wangjun Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSynchronous bone metastases (BM) have a notable impact on the survival rates of patients across various cancer types. While primary tumor surgery (PTS) has shown potential benefits, its impact across different cancers and patient subgroups needs further clarification. This study aimed to investigate the survival benefits of PTS across various cancer types.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, covering 53,015 patients diagnosed with BM from 2010 to 2019. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to reduce baseline imbalance. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression.ResultsPTS substantially improved overall and cancer-specific survival in patients with lung, kidney, breast and bladder cancers. All surgical modalities benefited breast and renal cancer survival. In prostate cancer, PTS produced no overall survival gain; survival varied by surgery type, being better after prostatectomy and worse with local resection. For liver cancer, PTS improved cancer-specific but not overall survival, and the small matched cohort necessitates circumspect interpretation.ConclusionThe findings indicate that PTS can significantly enhance survival in selected patients with BM, particularly in lung, kidney, breast, and bladder cancers. Tailoring surgical approaches based on individual cancer type and patient characteristics is crucial to optimizing treatment outcomes. Further studies are required to validate these results and expand the understanding of PTS in cancer management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00402-026-06419-9.Keywords: Pancancer, Bone metastases, SEER database, Surgery, Prognosis

Introduction

Bone metastasis (BM) is a significant late-stage manifestation of numerous cancers marked by secondary bone involvement that precipitates severe symptoms and skeletal-related events (SREs), such as pain, spinal cord compression, hypercalcemia, and pathological fractures [1, 2]. Currently, metastatic disease affects more than half of all cancer patients, and the prevalence of BM is notably high among breast (73%), prostate (68%), lung (36%), and kidney (35%) cancers [3, 4].

Advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic techniques have markedly improved treatment outcomes in many patients. Enhanced imaging technologies now allow for earlier detection of disease progression in those afflicted with BM [5, 6]. The introduction of targeted therapies, including bisphosphonates and denosumab, has been crucial for reducing bone degradation and the frequency of SREs [7–9]. Furthermore, surgical interventions at metastatic sites are increasingly recognized as vital for managing complications such as pathological fractures or spinal cord compression [10, 11].

Despite these advancements, limited studies with small sample sizes have suggested that surgeries targeting the primary tumor may offer additional survival benefits [12–14]. Given that BM generally has a more favorable prognosis than other organ metastases, there is a significant opportunity to tailor treatments specifically for patients with BM to potentially enhance their survival rates [15–17].

However, variability in the underlying mechanisms of BM and the recommended intensities of primary tumor surgery (PTS) contribute to prognostic differences across cancer types [12, 18]. This underscores the urgent need to reassess the survival benefits of PTS in patients with exclusive BM. Such re-evaluation is critical for identifying specific patient cohorts and for developing detailed strategies to effectively implement PTS in BM management.

Our research aimed to explore the survival benefits associated with PTS and examine the impacts of different extents of tumor excision and the inclusion of combined metastasectomy on patient prognosis. By conducting these analyses, we sought to identify patients who were most likely to benefit from PTS, thereby aiming to consistently improve their survival prospects and slow the progression of their disease.

Methods

Study subjects

In this study, we used the SEER database, which is managed by the National Cancer Institute and covers approximately 28% of the U.S. population. This extensive cancer registry provides data on BM, demographics, and clinical details at diagnosis. Primary tumor sites and histological types were classified according to the ICD-O3, and TNM staging followed the 7th edition of the AJCC guidelines.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Because the SEER database is open and provides anonymized information, informed consent was not required. The requirement for ethical approval was formally waived by the Medical Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (SCCIRB; Approval No. 2409-N0.001, Version B-16, 2024.08.30) in accordance with Article 32 of the Measures for Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Humans of the National Health Commission of China.

We identified suitable cases from the SEER database adhering to the following specific inclusion criteria: (i) patients with BM originating from primary cancers of the lung, prostate, breast, kidney, bladder, or liver; (ii) individuals whose primary cancer was their first and only diagnosis; (iii) patients aged 18 years or older at the time of their primary cancer diagnosis; (iv) patients diagnosed with BM between 2010 and 2019; and (v) patients with BM at diagnosis without any metastases to the liver, lungs, or brain. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) diagnosis not confirmed microscopically; (ii) cancer diagnosed only at autopsy or on a death certificate; (iii) diagnosis with T0, indicating that no primary tumor was identified, or with non-invasive tumor stages Ta and Tis owing to small sample sizes; (iv) unknown status of PTS; (v) unknown status of metastasectomy (MS); and (vi) incomplete follow-up data.

Study design and propensity score matching analysis

We collected a comprehensive array of variables, including demographic data (age at diagnosis, sex, race), clinical features (pathological subtypes, TNM staging, differentiation grade), and cancer-specific attributes (such as molecular subtypes of breast cancer and serum PSA levels for prostate cancer). The initial local treatment approaches considered were primary tumor resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. We primarily focused on overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) to evaluate the treatment outcomes.

To minimize the selection bias caused by varying baseline characteristics, we employed a 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) technique using the ‘MatchIt’ and ‘Cobalt’ packages in R. This method ensured that patients underwent primary tumor surgery (PTS) and those who did not undergo PTS (No-PTS). After matching, we analyzed the prognosis of patients across different cancer types and investigated the effect of PTS on survival. We also explored how different surgical techniques for PTS and combinations of PTS with resection at metastatic sites influenced patient outcomes.

In the final phase of our analysis, we aimed to present the risk ratios for PTS within specific patient subgroups categorized by diverse demographic and clinical features for each type of cancer. To assess the prognostic significance of these ratios, we generated forest plots using the ‘forestplot’ package in R. Survival analyses were conducted using the ‘survminer’ and ‘survival’ packages, which facilitated the examination of survival data and the visualization of survival curves.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared using Student’s t test, Mann–Whitney U test, Pearson’s chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to reduce baseline imbalance between patients who underwent primary tumor surgery (PTS) and those who did not. Survival outcomes were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis and compared using log-rank tests. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression models.

Additional Cox regression analyses were conducted according to surgical extent in prostate cancer. Multivariable models included age, race, PSA level, T stage, N stage, grade, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy.

The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Restricted mean survival time (RMST) analysis was used as a supplementary survival measure. Subgroup analyses were performed using Cox regression within predefined clinical categories, and interaction terms were evaluated using likelihood ratio tests.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3). All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and propensity score matching

According to the stringent and meticulous inclusion and exclusion criteria depicted in Fig. 1, we ultimately included 53,015 patients who were diagnosed with BM between 2010 and 2019 and had no metastases to other organs. The cohort comprised individuals with BM secondary to various primary cancers, including lung cancer (41.94%), prostate cancer (33.76%), breast cancer (15.90%), kidney cancer (3.8%), liver cancer (2.9%), and bladder cancer (1.7%).

Flowchart of patient selection from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database

In the entire pre-PSM cohort, 12.1% of patients received PTS, with the highest proportion (75.9%) observed among patients with bladder cancer. The proportion of patients who underwent PTS for other cancer types was notably lower (28.8% for breast cancer, 11.5% for prostate cancer, 38.8% for kidney cancer, 2.9% for liver cancer, and 1.9% for lung cancer). Subsequently, through the application of PSM, we standardized the baseline characteristics of these patients.

For each cancer type, a 1:1 PSM was implemented, as detailed in Tables S1–S5, with the specific results for the bladder cancer cohort presented in Table 1. In this cohort, patients who underwent PTS exhibited earlier T and N stages than those in the no-PTS group. After implementing PSM, each cohort was adjusted to 141 individuals to effectively standardize baseline characteristics, including T and N stages, to ensure comparability between the PTS and no-PTS groups. The effectiveness of PSM in aligning these characteristics is visually substantiated by the similar distribution of propensity scores post-PSM, as illustrated in Panels B-E of Figs. S1–S6. Additionally, the absolute mean differences in the clinical and pathological features between the PTS and No-PTS groups, both before and after PSM, are depicted in Panel A of Figs. S1–S6. These visual representations confirmed that the PSM process successfully aligned critical clinicopathological parameters, ensuring that the study groups were directly comparable in terms of their baseline clinical presentations. Adjustments for similar variables in other cancer types led to consistent intergroup trends, underscoring the robustness of our PSM strategy in assessing the survival benefits of PTS across various cancers with isolated BM.

Survival outcomes associated with PTS across different cancer types

After applying PSM, we observed that PTS significantly influenced both OS and CSS in patients with BM from breast, bladder, kidney, and lung cancers, thereby enhancing survival outcomes (Fig. 2A-F) (P < 0.05). For instance, in kidney cancer patients, the 3-year OS rate for patients who underwent PTS was 49.74%, whereas it was only 19.28% for those who did not. Similarly, the 3-year CSS rates in the PTS and non-PTS groups were 54.16% and 20.71%, respectively (Fig. 2D). This indicates that patients with kidney cancer and concurrent BM who undergo PTS may achieve significant survival benefits.

Conversely, overall PTS was not associated with improved OS and CSS in prostate cancer. In liver cancer, PTS was associated with improved CSS but not OS. Because visual overlap of the Kaplan–Meier curves was observed in liver cancer, we formally assessed the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals. No significant violation was detected for either CSS (global P = 0.075) or OS (global P = 0.102) (Fig. S8). However, potential bias from crossing curves could compromise the reliability of conventional survival tests. Therefore, we adopted RMST, a metric robust to time-varying hazards, for complementary assessment. In sensitivity analyses, RMST results were consistent with a potential survival benefit of PTS. For CSS, PTS was associated with longer RMST at 12, 24, and 36 months, with differences of 2.69 months, 3.58 months, and 4.56 months, respectively (Table S6). Despite the observed borderline improvement in CSS with PTS in liver cancer patients, the potential advantages need to be cautiously viewed given the small matched cohort.

Comparison of survival benefits after different PTS surgical approaches in patients with BM

Our detailed examination of various PTS approaches revealed differential survival outcomes across cancer types. For prostate cancer, although overall PTS was not associated with improved survival, analyses stratified by surgical extent revealed marked heterogeneity (Fig. 3B). Compared with patients who did not undergo surgery, local tumor surgery was associated with inferior outcomes, whereas prostatectomy was associated with substantially improved survival (P < 0.0001). In multivariable Cox regression adjusting for age, race, PSA level, T stage, N stage, grade, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, prostatectomy remained associated with improved CSS (adjusted HR = 0.34, 95% CI 0.27–0.44, P < 0.001) and OS (adjusted HR = 0.37, 95% CI 0.30–0.47, P < 0.001), whereas local tumor surgery was associated with poorer CSS (adjusted HR = 1.24, 95% CI 1.13–1.36, P < 0.001) and OS (adjusted HR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.11–1.32, P < 0.001) (Table S7). These findings indicate that the absence of benefit in the overall PTS analysis may reflect heterogeneity across surgical approaches rather than a uniformly neutral effect of surgery. In line with observations for prostate cancer, patients with lung cancer who underwent local tumor resection exhibited the poorest survival outcomes (P < 0.0001). This suggests that, while some surgical interventions enhance survival, the effectiveness of local tumor surgery may be limited, emphasizing the need for prudent selection of surgical methods. For breast and renal cancer patients, all types of PTS approaches confer survival advantages, underscoring the benefits of surgical interventions for these cancers.

Comparative survival outcomes of combined MS and PTS versus singular approaches in diverse cancers

In our extended analysis, we assessed the prognostic impact of combining metastasectomy (MS) with PTS on survival outcomes, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Notably, this combination provided significant survival benefits only in patients with lung cancer (Fig. 4F). For breast and kidney cancer patients with BM, no significant difference was observed in survival outcomes between those who underwent only PTS and those who underwent both PTS and MS (Fig. 4A, D). For other malignancies exemplified by prostate cancer, patients administered PTS alone achieved marginally superior OS and CSS compared with those who received combined PTS and MS (Fig. 4B). Survival profiles were equivalent between the dual-intervention group and the non-surgical group. Across all cancer types in this analysis, standalone MS was linked to inferior survival outcomes versus PTS monotherapy.

These results highlight the complex and variable impact of combining PTS with MS on survival across different cancer types, emphasizing the necessity for personalized treatment strategies that consider the specific characteristics and metastatic behavior of each cancer.

Subgroup analysis of the prognostic value of the PTS across different types of cancer with BM

Our subgroup analyses, designed to elucidate which patient subgroups derive greater benefits from PTS, revealed nuanced outcomes across different cancer types that present with BM. As illustrated in Figs. 5 and S7, PTS yields a wide range of survival benefits, with efficacy dependent on cancer subtypes and patient demographic characteristics.

Subgroup analysis of the association between primary tumor surgery and survival in patients with oligometastatic bone metastases after propensity score matching. A Kidney cancer; B breast cancer; C bladder cancer; D prostate cancer. Hazard ratios were estimated using Cox regression models within predefined subgroups. HR hazard ratio, CI confidence interval

For kidney, breast, and lung cancer patients (Fig. 5A, B, Fig. S7A), the analyses demonstrated that almost all subgroups achieved significant survival advantages from PTS. Conversely, the survival benefits of PTS among patients with bladder cancer exhibited considerable heterogeneity (Fig. 5C). Despite general findings suggesting benefits from PTS in bladder cancer patients (as shown in Fig. 2C), subgroup analyses revealed that these benefits were predominantly observed in specific segments of the population, including patients aged 60–69 years, males, and those with earlier T and N stages (Fig. 5C). To further address concerns regarding statistical power and potential subgroup heterogeneity in bladder cancers, interaction analyses were additionally performed and are summarized in Table S8. Most bladder cancer subgroup effects remained consistent with the overall treatment effect.

For liver cancer, subgroup analyses were limited by the small matched sample size (Figure S7B). Several subgroup-specific estimates, particularly in race, subtype, nodal stage, and grade categories, were not estimable because of sparse data (Table S8). Although interaction testing suggested potential heterogeneity according to chemotherapy status, these results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Furthermore, while PTS yielded no overall survival benefit for prostate cancer patients, subgroup analyses identified favorable outcomes in specific populations. Specifically, patients aged under 60 appeared to derive survival benefits from PTS (Fig. 5D).

PTS requires careful patient selection, with substantial survival gains seen in certain subgroups irrespective of overall treatment effects. A visual summary (Fig. 6) was developed to streamline clinical decision-making and pinpoint PTS-benefiting subgroups. These results advocate personalized therapy and warrant prospective trials for validation. Subgroup data for liver cancer were not included due to small sample size, and larger cohorts are needed for further investigation.

Discussion

Although there is ongoing debate about the efficacy of aggressive surgical intervention for primary tumors in metastatic patients—with skeptics citing the risks of overtreatment and proponents highlighting the potential for improved prognosis—recent studies suggest that PTS can indeed enhance survival outcomes [19–21]. Our study extends this discourse by specifically investigating the impact of PTS on cancer patients with extensive BM. After PSM, PTS was associated with improved survival outcomes in patients with kidney, breast, lung, and bladder cancers. For prostate and liver cancers, the interpretation was more nuanced. In prostate cancer, the overall PTS analysis did not show a survival benefit, but surgery-type analyses revealed that prostatectomy was associated with improved survival, whereas local tumor surgery was associated with poorer outcomes. In liver cancer, PTS showed a potential CSS benefit, supported by Cox regression and RMST sensitivity analyses, although the small matched cohort warrants cautious interpretation.

After meticulous PSM analysis, our study demonstrated significant survival benefits from PTS in patients with kidney, breast, lung, and bladder cancers, whereas prostate and liver cancer did not show similar advantages. This finding challenges the conventional view that metastatic patients should not undergo primary lesion resection. Previous studies have corroborated these benefits, emphasizing that PTS can reduce tumor burden, alleviate symptoms, and enhance survival [19–24]. Notably, our study innovates by employing PSM to balance baseline differences between the PTS and No-PTS groups, thus mitigating selection bias inherent in retrospective research. This adjustment avoids the pitfalls of inappropriate conclusions driven by selection bias, offering a more reliable understanding of the effectiveness of PTSs. Furthermore, our analysis revealed that prostate cancer patients did not derive any significant benefit from PTS. This finding aligns with the results of a recent prospective randomized clinical trial, which demonstrated that local therapy (radiotherapy) failed to improve survival outcomes for patients with bone-metastatic prostate cancer [25].

In assessing the influence of surgical approach and extent of resection on survival outcomes, our findings reveal that the optimal scope of excision varies significantly among cancers, suggesting the need for personalized surgical strategies. For example, in breast cancer, less extensive surgeries such as excisional biopsy or simple mastectomy have been associated with better prognoses, supported by studies favoring breast-conserving surgery over more radical procedures due to fewer complications and better survival rates [26, 27]. In lung cancer, simpler procedures like lobectomy, especially when combined with lymph node dissection, have been shown to offer more favorable outcomes than extensive resections such as bilobectomy or pneumonectomy [28, 29]. The prostate cancer findings highlight the importance of distinguishing overall PTS from specific surgical modalities. When all surgical procedures were combined, PTS was not associated with improved survival. However, stratified and adjusted analyses showed that prostatectomy was associated with substantially better CSS and OS, whereas local tumor surgery was associated with worse outcomes. This suggests that aggregating heterogeneous procedures into a single PTS category may obscure clinically meaningful differences. Although prostatectomy was associated with improved survival in surgical-type analysis, the overall benefit of PTS in prostate cancer was not consistently supported across OS/CSS analyses and subgroup analyses. For prostate cancer patients with oligobone metastasis, our PTS group comprised local resection and radical prostatectomy, two procedures differing greatly in scope and intent. Radical prostatectomy enables radical tumor resection plus lymph node dissection, while local resection mainly aims to alleviate urinary symptoms with limited tumor debulking. Surgeons’ prognostic judgment also guides surgical selection. Since far more patients received local resection than radical prostatectomy in this cohort, combining the two approaches diluted the overall survival benefit of PTS in pooled analyses. Therefore, the potential benefit of prostatectomy should be interpreted cautiously and may apply only to highly selected patients. This underscores the need for careful evaluation of surgical options based on the extent of tumor invasion and the specific cancer type, ensuring that treatment plans are optimally tailored to improve patient outcomes while minimizing adverse effects.

Surgical treatment targeting BM primarily aims to manage skeletal-related events (SREs) and improve quality of life, potentially enhancing survival [30, 31]. However, while notable survival benefits were predominantly observed in lung cancer patients undergoing combined metastasectomy (MS), the impact on prognosis varied across other cancer types, with some showing no significant improvement or even a decline. This variability suggests the importance of a strategic approach, emphasizing primary tumor management and caution in extending surgical intervention to metastatic lesions, which are typically reserved for cases necessitated by complications such as pathological fractures. The differing impacts across cancer types may stem from biological differences in tumor-bone interactions, variations in tumor burden and response to systemic therapies, or disparities in surgical techniques [32, 33]. According to one study, surgical injury may trigger the mobilization and recruitment of bone marrow stem cells, thereby enhancing angiogenesis and accelerating tumor progression [34]. These findings highlight the need for further research to elucidate optimal strategies for BM management in each specific cancer context, suggesting a tailored treatment approach based on the unique characteristics of each cancer type.

We performed cross-cancer subgroup analyses to determine which patient populations benefited most from PTS. These findings were compiled into a concise visual guide, which illustrates personalized treatment approaches and highlights variable PTS efficacy across distinct patient profiles. Caution is required when interpreting subgroup results, especially for bladder and liver cancers. We adopted interaction tests to assess treatment effect heterogeneity. A small number of subgroups presented wide confidence intervals, yet none of these results reached statistical significance (P > 0.05). Also, sparse data after matching made several liver cancer subgroups unestimable. These results are thus exploratory and hypothesis-generating; they inform future research but should not serve as the sole basis for clinical decisions. After all, this visual guide serves as a reference for clinical practice according to subgroup characteristics, consistent with precision medicine that advocates individualized care for patients and their tumors [35, 36]. Applying these findings clinically can maximize survival benefits by administering PTS to responsive patients and avoiding unnecessary interventions for those unlikely to gain advantages.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, residual immortal time bias remained present. Without precise records of surgery timing in SEER, it’s hard to distinguish PTS timing relative to the landmark, and the lack of data on the interval from BM diagnosis to surgery in the SEER database is highly likely to cause the immortal time bias. Therefore, our findings may slightly overestimate treatment effects and should be interpreted as associations, not causal evidence. Second, data on bone metastasis characteristics and skeletal-related events were missing. We enrolled only patients with isolated synchronous bone metastases, but lesion count, location and complications like fractures or cord compression were not documented. These unreported factors may cause residual confounding by indication. Furthermore, while the SEER database provides extensive data, it lacks specific details on quality-of-life metrics and comprehensive information about systemic therapy such as chemotherapy regimens, targeted agents, immunotherapy, and the dose/sequence of radiotherapy. Patients undergoing PTS may receive more aggressive comprehensive systemic therapy, which could confound the survival effect of surgery. Also, future prospective studies combining survival outcomes with quality of life and SRE-related endpoints are warranted. Finally, subgroup analyses for liver and bladder cancers were constrained by limited matched sample sizes. In liver cancer, several subgroup-specific estimates could not be reliably estimated because of sparse data, and interaction findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. These limitations reveal difficulties in applying PTS to clinical regimens. Future large prospective trials and independent studies are required to refine treatment approaches and define PTS utility in different cancer settings.

Conclusions

In summary, PTS showed heterogeneous prognostic implications across cancer types in patients with synchronous BM. Breast, bladder, kidney, and lung cancers demonstrated the most consistent survival benefits from PTS. In prostate cancer, the effect of surgery depended strongly on surgical extent, with prostatectomy showing favorable outcomes and local tumor surgery showing unfavorable outcomes. In liver cancer, PTS was associated with a potential CSS benefit, supported by proportional hazards diagnostics and RMST sensitivity analyses, but this finding requires cautious interpretation because of the small matched cohort. Furthermore, we underscore the pivotal value of combining MS with PTS in patients with lung cancer, elucidating the nuanced interplay between surgical interventions and patient prognosis. These findings underscore the need for tailored therapeutic strategies grounded in a comprehensive understanding of cancer-specific characteristics and patient demographics to optimize treatment efficacy in the complex milieu of synchronous BM management.
