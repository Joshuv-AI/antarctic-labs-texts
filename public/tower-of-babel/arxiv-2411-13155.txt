Lie-Algebraic Bounds on Quantum Control Time via the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff Formula
Go Kato 1, Masaki Owari 2, Koji Maruyama 3,4

Abstract
The time required to implement a desired unitary operation is a central issue in quantum control, especially for many-body systems with limited control access. While controllability theory determines whether a target unitary is reachable in principle, it does not directly quantify the implementation time. Here we derive a Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH)-based inequality that connects these two questions at the operator level. Whenever a target unitary is implemented by the available Hamiltonians, an effective logarithmic generator can be chosen within the corresponding dynamical Lie algebra, and its normalized traceless Hilbert-Schmidt norm is bounded by the time integral of the same norm of the applied Hamiltonian. This induces a global-phase-insensitive distance on the reachable subgroup and yields a protocol-independent lower bound on the control time that explicitly respects Lie-algebraic restrictions. We compare the resulting bound with familiar quantum-speed-limit estimates and show that it refines a stabilizer-based control-time bound for an X ​ Y XY spin chain in the single-excitation subspace. Our result thus provides an algebraic link between controllability and quantitative bounds on unitary implementation time.



I
Introduction

How long does it take to implement a desired unitary operation on a quantum system when only a limited set of Hamiltonians is experimentally accessible? This question is central to quantum control, because coherent operations must be completed within the finite coherence time of the physical system. It is also theoretically subtle. Standard Lie-algebraic controllability theory addresses the reachability question, namely whether a target unitary can be generated in principle, but it does not by itself quantify the control time required to synthesize it.

Quantum speed limits (QSLs) provide a natural framework for discussing lower bounds on evolution time; see, e.g., Refs.
[
1
,
2
]
for reviews. The Mandelstam-Tamm and Margolus-Levitin bounds
[
3
,
4
]
relate the minimum time of quantum evolution to quantities such as the energy uncertainty or the mean energy. Later developments include geometric and operator-based formulations of QSLs and related bounds for unitary operations
[
10
,
11
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
7
]
. Quantum brachistochrone and time-optimal-control formulations treat the implementation of a target state or unitary as a constrained optimization problem
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
. Shortcuts to adiabaticity and counterdiabatic driving provide another important perspective, constructing Hamiltonians that reproduce the outcome of an adiabatic process in finite time
[
21
,
22
]
.

The present work is related in spirit to these approaches, but its aim is different. We do not attempt to solve the full time-optimal control problem, nor do we construct an optimal or shortcut protocol. Instead, we derive a lower bound that any control protocol must satisfy if it implements a prescribed target unitary. The bound is formulated at the operator level and is compatible with the dynamical Lie algebra generated by the available Hamiltonians.

In a typical closed-system control setting, the Hamiltonian takes the form

H(t)=H_{0}+\sum_{m}h_{m}(t)H_{m},

(1)

where
H_{0}
is a drift Hamiltonian and
H_{m}
are directly controllable Hamiltonians. The corresponding dynamical Lie algebra generated by
\{iH_{0},iH_{1},...,iH_{M}\}
determines the reachable unitary group. This Lie-algebraic controllability criterion is powerful, but it is essentially a reachability statement. It does not directly give a lower bound on the time required to implement a specific unitary.

This difficulty is closely tied to the noncommutativity of the available Hamiltonians. Although only a small set of Hamiltonians may be directly accessible, their commutators can generate additional directions and may span a large dynamical Lie algebra
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. Algebraic
structures induced by limited direct access have also been investigated in related settings
[
28
]
. Converting such local algebraic information into global lower bounds on the time required for a specified target unitary remains challenging. Related time-optimal analyses have been carried out in special low-dimensional
settings
[
29
]
.

Our main result is a Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH)-based inequality for effective generators of controlled unitary evolutions.
Roughly speaking, if a target unitary is implemented by a time-dependent Hamiltonian generated by the available controls, then one can choose an effective generator of the target inside the corresponding dynamical Lie algebra. After the identity components are removed, the size of this generator is bounded by the time integral of the size of the applied Hamiltonian. This immediately gives a lower bound of the form

\mathrm{time}\geq\frac{\mathrm{distance\ to\ the\ target\ in\ the\ reachable\ algebra}}{\mathrm{maximum\ available\ speed}}.

(2)

In this sense, the bound is projective: it removes the physically irrelevant global phase of the resulting unitary. This is achieved by using a normalized traceless Hilbert-Schmidt norm, denoted below by
\operatorname{dev}
. In this way, the result provides a direct algebraic connection between controllability and quantitative lower bounds on unitary implementation time.

The BCH formula is relevant because a time-dependent unitary evolution is naturally approximated by a product of short-time exponentials. Since the usual BCH series is guaranteed to converge only near the identity, we apply it to sufficiently small pieces and then take an appropriate limit. The result is an inequality of the form
\mathrm{dev}C\leq\mathrm{dev}A+\mathrm{dev}B
for an effective generator
C
satisfying
e^{C}=e^{A}e^{B}
, which can then be iterated to derive lower bounds on control time.

The scope of this result should be stated carefully. The distance to the target is defined by minimizing not over the full set of Hermitian logarithmic generators
C
satisfying
e^{-iC}=U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
, but only over those satisfying the Lie-algebra constraint
iC\in\mathcal{L}
. The bound therefore respects the algebraic constraint
iC\in\mathcal{L}
on the effective logarithmic generator, but it does not distinguish directions generated directly by available Hamiltonians from those arising only through high-order commutators. When the dynamical Lie algebra is the full
\mathfrak{su}(D)
, the distance appearing in the bound reduces essentially to a standard projective-unitary logarithmic distance. The present result should therefore be regarded as a first algebraic lower bound, rather than a complete solution of the many-body time-optimal control problem.

This point also clarifies the relation to quantum brachistochrone and shortcut-to-adiabaticity approaches. Those approaches typically seek an optimal or accelerated Hamiltonian protocol under given constraints. By contrast, the present work gives a necessary condition that any such protocol must obey, once it is described within the dynamical Lie algebra. In this sense, the result is complementary to, rather than a replacement for, optimal-control or shortcut-construction methods.

We compare the bound with familiar QSL-type estimates using the Choi representation. The purpose is not to claim a universal improvement over QSLs, but to clarify the difference between a state-space distance and a unitary-level projective distance.
We also illustrate the bound using the XY spin-chain example of Lee et al. in the single-excitation subspace
[
30
]
, where the proposed distance refines a stabilizer-based control-time bound.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II

III
, we compare the resulting bound with state-based and unitary-based speed-limit estimates. In Sec.
IV
, we outline the proof based on the BCH formula. In Sec.
V
, we discuss the geometric interpretation of the Hilbert-Schmidt-norm version of the result, summarize the limitations of the present bound, and comment on future directions.

II
Main result

II.1
Control setting and dynamical Lie algebra

We consider dynamics governed by the Schrödinger equation

i\frac{d}{dt}U(t)=H(t)U(t),

(3)

with the initial condition

U(0)=I.

(4)

Here
U(t)
is a unitary operator,
H(t)
is a time-dependent Hamiltonian, and we set
\hbar=1
.

The control Hamiltonian is assumed to have the form

H(t)=H_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}h_{m}(t)H_{m},

(5)

where
H_{0}
is a drift Hamiltonian and
H_{m}
are directly controllable Hamiltonians. The real-valued functions
h_{m}(t)
represent the applied control fields. We assume that
H(t)
is piecewise continuous in time.

With amplitude constraints on the control fields, the allowed instantaneous Hamiltonians form

\mathcal{H}_{\rm exp}=\left\{H=H_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}h_{m}H_{m}\ \middle|\ |h_{m}|\leq h_{m}^{\max}\right\}.

(6)

We denote by

\mathcal{L}=\operatorname{Lie}\{iH_{0},iH_{1},\ldots,iH_{M}\}

(7)

the dynamical Lie algebra generated by the drift and control Hamiltonians, namely the Lie closure under repeated commutators and their linear combinations.

II.2
Deviation norm and removal of global phase

For a
D\times D
operator
A
, define the deviation norm of an operator by

\left(\operatorname{dev}A\right)^{2}:=\frac{1}{D}\operatorname{tr}(A^{\dagger}A)-\left|\frac{1}{D}\operatorname{tr}A\right|^{2}.

(8)

Equivalently,

\operatorname{dev}A=\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\left\|A-\frac{\operatorname{tr}A}{D}I\right\|_{F},

(9)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm,
\left\|A\right\|_{F}:=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}A^{\dagger}A}
, which is often called the Hilbert-Schmidt norm in the literature.
This dev is a legitimate norm for traceless operators, while it is a semi-norm otherwise.
Thus
\operatorname{dev}A
measures the size of
A
after removing its identity component. For Hermitian or anti-Hermitian normal operators, it is the standard deviation of the eigenvalues, up to the corresponding factor of
i
in the anti-Hermitian case.

This definition is independent of any particular quantum state. We use the notation
\operatorname{dev}
to distinguish it from the usual state-dependent uncertainty of an observable

(\Delta A)^{2}=\langle\phi|A^{2}|\phi\rangle-\langle\phi|A|\phi\rangle^{2}.

(10)

Since the trace component is removed, the distance induced by
\operatorname{dev}
naturally lives on a projective unitary group rather than on the full unitary group.

II.3
BCH inequality for two operators

The key algebraic ingredient is the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
A
and
B
be anti-Hermitian operators. Then there exists an anti-Hermitian operator
C
such that

e^{C}=e^{A}e^{B},

(11)

C\in\operatorname{Lie}\{A,B\},

(12)

and

\operatorname{dev}C\leq\operatorname{dev}A+\operatorname{dev}B,

(13)

\operatorname{tr}C=\operatorname{tr}(A+B).

(14)

At first sight, Eq. (
11
) resembles the BCH formula. The point of the lemma, however, is not merely the formal identity
e^{A}e^{B}=e^{C}
. The usual BCH series is guaranteed to converge only when
A
and
B
are sufficiently small.
Lemma
1
asserts the existence of a suitable
C
in the Lie closure
\mathrm{Lie}\{A,B\}
, together with the deviation inequality (
13
), without assuming that the original
A
and
B
are small. The proof is given in Sec.
IV
and in Sec. VIII of the Supplemental Material. The anti-Hermitian assumption is essential for the argument; Sec. I of the Supplemental Material gives a simple example showing that the analogue of Eq. (
12
) need not hold for non-anti-Hermitian operators.

II.4
Main theorem

We now state the main result for a continuously modulated Hamiltonian.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that a target unitary
U^{(\mathrm{target})}
is implemented at time
T
by a solution of Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
), with
H(t)
of the form Eq. (
5
). Then there exists a Hermitian operator
C[T]
such that

e^{-iC[T]}=U^{(\mathrm{target})},

(15)

iC[T]\in\mathcal{L}

(16)

and

\operatorname{dev}C[T]\leq\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{dev}H(t)\,dt,

(17)

\operatorname{tr}C[T]=\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{tr}H(t)\,dt.

(18)

The notation
C[T]
does not imply that
C
is a single-valued continuous function of
T
. It indicates that
C[T]
is an effective generator associated with a particular control protocol that reaches the target at time
T
. For a fixed target unitary, there may be multiple protocols and hence multiple possible effective generators. The possible discontinuity of such a choice of
C[T]
is illustrated by a simple example in Sec. II of the Supplemental Material.

Theorem
1
is obtained by approximating the time-ordered evolution by a product of short-time exponentials and applying Lemma
1
recursively. Taking the continuum limit gives Eqs. (
15
)–(
18
). In this sense, the theorem is an operator-level consequence of the BCH structure of controlled unitary evolution.

II.5
Lower bound on control time

Theorem
1
immediately implies a lower bound on the implementation time. Define

c_{\mathcal{L}}\!\left(U^{(\mathrm{target})}\right):=\min\left\{\operatorname{dev}C\ \middle|\ e^{-iC}=U^{(\mathrm{target})},\ iC\in\mathcal{L}\right\}.

(19)

Then Eq. (
17
) gives

c_{\mathcal{L}}\!\left(U^{(\mathrm{target})}\right)\leq\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{dev}H(t)\,dt\leq T\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\operatorname{dev}H(t),

(20)

and therefore

T\geq\frac{c_{\mathcal{L}}\!\left(U^{(\mathrm{target})}\right)}{\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\operatorname{dev}H(t)}.

(21)

This has the form “distance divided by maximum speed.” The numerator is the minimum projective generator size of the target within the reachable Lie algebra. The denominator is the maximum projective Hamiltonian strength available along the implemented protocol. We denote the right-hand side of Eq. (
21
) by
T_{*}
.

In the special case where
\mathcal{L}=\mathfrak{su}(D)
, the minimization in Eq. (
19
) reduces to the usual minimization over logarithm branches, modulo global phase:

c_{\mathfrak{su}(D)}(U)=\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{dev}\left[i\,\operatorname{Log}\left(Ue^{i\theta}\right)\right],

(22)

where
\operatorname{Log}
denotes the principal logarithm. In this case the bound is essentially a projective-unitary logarithmic distance divided by the available speed. For a proper subalgebra or a nontrivial representation of the reachable algebra, the value of
c_{\mathcal{L}}
can differ from the full-
\mathfrak{su}(D)
value.

Equation (
21
) should be interpreted as a lower bound obtained under the algebraic constraint
iC\in\mathcal{L}
. It does not, however, estimate the time overhead required to synthesize the minimizing generator
C
from the available Hamiltonians
\{H_{0},H_{1},...,H_{M}\}
. Directions that arise only through high-order commutators may therefore require additional control time beyond what is captured by this bound.

The bound is nevertheless saturable in simple cases. Suppose that the Hamiltonian is always proportional to a fixed traceless Hermitian operator
H_{\ast}
with
iH_{\ast}\in\mathcal{L}
:

H(t)=f(t)H_{*}.

(23)

For the target

U^{(\mathrm{target})}=e^{-igH_{\ast}},

(24)

and when the logarithmic branch
C=gH_{\ast}
gives
c_{\mathcal{L}}=|g|\operatorname{dev}H_{\ast}
, the minimum time under the amplitude bound
|f(t)|\leq f_{\max}
is

T_{\min}=\frac{|g|}{f_{\max}}=\frac{c_{\mathcal{L}}}{f_{\max}\operatorname{dev}H_{\ast}}.

(25)

This example shows that the bound can be saturated in simple constant-Hamiltonian cases. It should not be read, however, as a statement that the bound is saturated for generic control systems.

II.6
Projective distance induced by the theorem

Theorem
1
also induces a distance on the reachable subgroup modulo global phases. For two unitaries
U_{A}
and
U_{B}
in the reachable group, define

\widetilde{d}(U_{A},U_{B}):=\min\left\{\operatorname{dev}C\ \middle|\ e^{-iC}=U_{A}U_{B}^{-1},\ iC\in\mathcal{L}\right\}.

(26)

Because
\operatorname{dev}C=0
precisely when
C
is proportional to the identity, this distance identifies unitaries that differ only by a global phase. Lemma
1
implies the triangle inequality,

\widetilde{d}(U_{A},U_{B})\leq\widetilde{d}(U_{A},U_{C})+\widetilde{d}(U_{C},U_{B}),

(27)

and therefore
\widetilde{d}
defines a metric on the corresponding subgroup of the projective unitary group,
PU(D)=U(D)/U(1)
, whenever the relevant minimizers exist within the reachable algebra.

With this notation, Eq. (
19
) can be written as

c_{\mathcal{L}}\!\left(U^{(\mathrm{target})}\right)=\widetilde{d}\!\left(U^{(\mathrm{target})},I\right),

(28)

and the time bound becomes

T\geq\frac{\widetilde{d}\!\left(U^{(\mathrm{target})},I\right)}{\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\operatorname{dev}H(t)}.

(29)

Because the minimization in Eq. (
26
) is constrained by the Lie algebra
\mathcal{L}
, the resulting distance can depend on the chosen dynamical Lie algebra and its representation, even for the same pair of unitaries. A specific example illustrating this algebra dependence is given in Sec. III of the Supplemental Material. When
\mathcal{L}
is the full
\mathfrak{su}(D)
, the distance reduces to the usual projective logarithmic distance. When
\mathcal{L}
is a proper subalgebra, the minimization is constrained by the allowed effective generators, and the resulting distance can be larger or may be undefined for unreachable targets.

The next section compares this projective operator-level bound with speed-limit estimates based on state-space distances and with related unitary-distance bounds.

III
Comparison with quantum speed limits and unitary-distance bounds

III.1
The Mandelstam-Tamm bound

We now compare our bound, denoted by
T_{*}
, with those implied by well-known discussions on quantum speed limits. Although the bound obtained by Margolus and Levitin (ML) is a major speed limit for time-independent Hamiltonians, there is no widely accepted extension to time-dependent closed-system dynamics
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
.
In fact, Hörnedal and Sönnerborn constructed explicit counterexamples showing that an ML-type estimate based on the time-averaged normalized mean energy can be violated
[
35
]
.
Thus, we focus on the speed limit by Mandelstam and Tamm (MT)
[
3
]
, whose original derivation was based on the uncertainty relationship between energy and time.

There is a subtle difference in the formulations of their bounds and ours: While the MT bound measures the speed of evolution in terms of the
fidelity
between the initial and final (target) states, we consider the time to obtain the target unitary operation
U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
since our main interest is in the implementation of quantum control operations rather than reaching a specific fidelity with respect to the initial state.
Although the two bounds are formulated for different tasks, it is possible to connect these measures through the Choi representation of quantum operations; i.e., in the operator-form comparison considered below, our projective-distance bound can be sharper than the MT-type state-distance bound.

The setting for the MT bound is similar to the one given by Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
). A quantum state is in
|{\phi(0)}\rangle
at
t=0
, and it evolves according to the Schrödinger equation. Letting
|{\phi(\tau)}\rangle
be the target state, it is summarized as follows.

Mandelstam-Tamm bound:
The fidelity,

|\langle{\phi(0)}|{\phi(\tau)}\rangle|^{2}
, or the Bures angle
[
36
]
, is bounded by

\arccos|\langle{\phi(0)}|{\phi(\tau)}\rangle|\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\Delta H(t),

(30)

where
(\Delta A)^{2}=\langle{\phi(t)}|A^{2}|{\phi(t)}\rangle-\left(\langle{\phi(t)}|A|{\phi(t)}\rangle\right)^{2}
is the deviation of operator
A
. The MT bound is often written as the time needed for a state to become orthogonal to the initial state, i.e.,
\tau\geq\pi/(2\Delta H_{\tau})
, with
\Delta H_{t}=(1/t)\int_{0}^{t}\Delta H(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}
.
A closely related bound, where
\Delta H
is replaced with the time-averaged norm of
H(t)
, has also been derived without referring to specific states. In
[
37
]
, a lower bound for the transition time of the (known) geometric angle between the (unknown) initial and final states is obtained by considering the minimum energy cost.

The MT bound can also be seen as a consequence of geometrical considerations a la Anandan and Aharanov
[
38
]
, as well as Uhlmann
[
39
]
. The length of the path
\mathcal{C}
that a quantum state follows under the Schrödinger equation was derived in
[
38
]
as

\mathrm{length}(\mathcal{C})=2\int_{0}^{T}\Delta H(t)dt,

(31)

where the length is evaluated with the Fubini-Study metric
[
40
]
.
In a similar spirit to Eq. (
31
), Poggi has obtained inequalities for quantum speed limits for unitary transformations
[
8
]
, which we look at in the next subsection.

We compare Eq. (
30
) with Eq.
(
17
) by rewriting them in terms of operators through the Choi representation. To do this, suppose that the Hilbert space in which the quantum states reside is a tensor product of two
D
-dimensional spaces, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}
. The Hamiltonian
H(t)
is replaced with
I\otimes H(t)
, and the initial state is set to be

|{\phi(0)}\rangle=1/\sqrt{D}\sum_{n}|{n}\rangle|{n}\rangle
. Then, we have the following corollary:

Corollary 1
.

(Operator form of the Mandelstam-Tamm bound) Let the unitary operator
U(T)
be a solution of Eq. (
3
) evaluated at time
T
, with the initial state
|{\phi(0)}\rangle=1/\sqrt{D}\sum_{n}|{n}\rangle|{n}\rangle
. The MT bound, Eq. (
30
), is rewritten as

\arccos\left|D^{-1}\mathrm{tr}U(T)\right|\leq\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{dev}H(t)dt,

(32)

where
(\mathrm{dev}A)^{2}:=D^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(A^{2})-(D^{-1}\mathrm{tr}A)^{2}
.

III.2
Comparison with a specific example

Letting
H(t)=C[T]/T
in Eq. (
32
) of Corollary
1
so that
U(T)=e^{-iC[T]}=U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
, we obtain

\arccos\left|D^{-1}\mathrm{tr}U^{\mathrm{(target)}}\right|\leq\mathrm{dev}C[T].

(33)

The LHS of Eq. (

) is the lower MT bound from Eq. (
32
), and its RHS is the lower bound from our Theorem
1
.
Therefore, in this operator-form comparison, Eq. (

Note that, although there may exist a
C\notin\mathcal{L}
such that
e^{-iC}=U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
for a given target unitary, Eq. (

) still holds.

To see the difference between the bounds of Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
more clearly, let us consider a specific example in which the Hamiltonian

is given as the following, somewhat artificial, function of time for

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}A,&\makebox{when }2m\leq t<2m+1\\

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}A&=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&be^{i\theta}&0\\

B&=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&-be^{-i\theta}&0\\

(36)

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\arctan\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}\tan\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\right).

(37)

The parameters are set as

III.3
Relation to other speed limits

In order to compare our result with other speed limits, let us first prove the following lemma from Lemma
1
.

Lemma 2
.

For two anti-Hermitian operators,
A
and
B
, there is an anti-Hermitian
C
such that

\displaystyle e^{C}

\displaystyle=e^{A}e^{B},

(47)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{L}(\{A,B\}),

(48)

\displaystyle\mathrm{and}\;\;\|C\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|A\|_{F}+\|B\|_{F}.

(49)

Proof.

For any
D\times D
anti-Hermitian matrix
X
, define the traceless matrix
\tilde{X}:=X-(\mathrm{tr}X/D)I
. Then

\|X\|_{F}^{2}=\|\tilde{X}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{|\mathrm{tr}X|^{2}}{D}.

(50)

By Lemma
1
, there exists
C\in\mathcal{L}(\{A,B\})
such that

e^{C}=e^{A}e^{B}
,
\mathrm{dev}C\leq\mathrm{dev}A+\mathrm{dev}B
, and
\mathrm{tr}C=\mathrm{tr}(A+B)
.
Since
\mathrm{dev}\tilde{X}=D^{-1/2}\|\tilde{X}\|_{F}
for traceless anti-Hermitian
\tilde{X}
,

\|\tilde{C}\|_{F}\leq\|\tilde{A}\|_{F}+\|\tilde{B}\|_{F}
holds. Moreover,

|\mathrm{tr}C|=|\mathrm{tr}(A+B)|\leq|\mathrm{tr}A|+|\mathrm{tr}B|
.
So, if we let
v(X):=(\|\tilde{X}\|_{F},|{\rm tr}X|/\sqrt{D})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}
, then

v_{i}(C)\leq v_{i}(A)+v_{i}(B),\;i=\{1,2\}
holds elementwise and thus

\|C\|_{F}=\|v(C)\|_{F}\leq\|v(A)+v(B)\|_{F}\leq\|v(A)\|_{F}+\|v(B)\|_{F}=\|A\|_{F}+\|B\|_{F},

which proves Eq. (
49
).
∎

We now arrive at the following theorem by concatenating Eq. (
47
) and letting the norm of each operator
A,B,...
be infinitesimal.

Theorem 2
.

Given a unitary operator
U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
, which can be obtained as a solution of the Schrödinger equation (
3
) with initial condition (
4
) at some finite time
T\geq 0
with
H(t)
in Eq. (
5
), there exists a Hermitian operator
C[T]

for any
T\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle e^{-iC[T]}

\displaystyle=U^{\mathrm{(target)}}\;\mbox{and}

(51)

\displaystyle iC[T]

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{Lie}\{iH_{0},iH_{1},\ldots,iH_{M}\},

(52)

where
C[T]
satisfies

\|C[T]\|_{F}\leq\int_{0}^{T}dt\|H(t)\|_{F}.

(53)

Equation (
53
) in Theorem
2
allows us to have a phase-sensitive distance (metric) for a unitary group as follows:

\displaystyle d(U_{1},U_{2}):=\min\left\{\|C\|_{F}\left|e^{C}=U_{1}U_{2}^{-1}\land{C\in\mathcal{L}}\right.\right\},

(54)

which is dependent on the algebra
\mathcal{L}
.
A simple physical example, where this algebra-dependence can be seen, is given in Section IV of the Supplemental Material.

Before discussing applications of this metric, Eq. (
54
), here is a brief remark on its geometric aspect. Equation (
53
) also admits a geometric interpretation as an inequality for curve lengths on the unitary group: the right-hand side is the length of the control trajectory from
I
to
U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
, whereas the LHS corresponds to the shortest such length. We return to this viewpoint in Sec.
V
; our emphasis here is that Eq. (
53
) provides an explicit, operationally derived inequality that can be applied directly to obtain computable time lower bounds under realistic control constraints.

Lower bounds on gate complexity and control time have also been explored through inequalities that relate distances between unitary transformations to the Hamiltonians generating them
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
,
[
30
]
. We now show how the metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
of Eq. (
54
) refines one such type of bound. These analyses are based on the inequality

\displaystyle\left\|U_{A}(T)-U_{B}(T)\right\|\leq\int_{0}^{T}dt\left\|H_{A}(t)-H_{B}(t)\right\|

(55)

for the unitary operators
U_{A}(t)
and
U_{B}(t)
that are generated by Hamiltonians
H_{A}(t)
and
H_{B}(t)
, respectively. This inequality holds with any unitarily invariant norm, including the Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
; thus, our result can be compared with it.

It is possible to obtain a similar inequality in terms of the metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
:

\displaystyle d(U_{A}(T),U_{B}(T))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}dt\left\|H_{A}(t)-H_{B}(t)\right\|_{F}.

(56)

The LHS of Eq. (
56
) can be larger than that of Eq. (
55
); that is,
d(\cdot,\cdot)
may yield a larger lower bound for the RHS and thus the control time as well. The proof of Eq. (
56
), together with the unitary invariance of the metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
, is given in Section V of the Supplemental Material, with careful treatment of the series convergence and its conditions for unitary operators. Since Eq. (
55
) implies

\displaystyle\left\|e^{iC}-I\right\|_{F}\leq\left\|C\right\|_{F},

we have

\displaystyle\left\|U_{A}(T)-U_{B}(T)\right\|_{F}=\left\|U_{A}(T)U_{B}(T)^{-1}-I\right\|_{F}\leq d(U_{A}(T)U_{B}(T)^{-1},I)=d(U_{A}(T),U_{B}(T)).

(57)

Since our theorem states that the metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
bounds the RHS of Eq. (
55
) from below, there might be cases in which our bound is larger than those deduced from Eq. (
55
). This can be seen quickly by considering
H_{A}(t)=\sigma_{x}
and
H_{B}(t)=0
, where
\sigma_{x}
is one of the standard Pauli matrices. Then, for
U_{A}(t)=\exp(-i\sigma_{x}t),U_{B}(t)=I
, the LHS of Eq. (
57
) is
2\sqrt{2}\sin(t/2)
, and the RHS is
\sqrt{2}t
, so the inequality holds for
t>0
.

Another interesting example of such bounds resulting from Eq. (
55
) is that of Lee et al.
[
30
]
:

T\geq\max_{V\in\bigcap_{k}\mathrm{Stab}(H_{k})}\frac{\|[U(T),V]\|}{\|[H_{0},V]\|}.

(58)

Here, the total Hamiltonian
H(t)
is assumed to have the same form as Eq. (
5
).
Furthermore, in Eq. (
58
),
\mathrm{Stab}(H_{k})
is a set of stabilizers for
H_{k}
, i.e., unitary operators that commute with
H_{k}
.
We fix the norm
\|\cdot\|
as the Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
to make it comparable with our bound.
We next show how Eq. (
58
) can be refined.
By combining Eqs. (
56
) and
(
57
) and setting
H_{A}=H_{0}
and
H_{B}=VH_{0}V^{\dagger}
with arbitrary
V\in\bigcap_{k}\mathrm{Stab}(H_{k})
, we obtain the following:

\displaystyle\|U(T)-VU(T)V^{\dagger}\|_{F}\leq d\left(U(T),VU(T)V^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}dt\ \|H(t)-VH(t)V^{\dagger}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=T\cdot\|H_{0}-VH_{0}V^{\dagger}\|_{F}.

(59)

Owing to the unitary invariance of the Frobenius norm,
we have:

T\geq\frac{d\left(U(T),VU(T)V^{\dagger}\right)}{\|[H_{0},V]\|_{F}}\geq\frac{\|[U(T),V]\|_{F}}{\|[H_{0},V]\|_{F}}.

(60)

Since
V
is an arbitrary unitary operation in
\bigcap_{k}\mathrm{Stab}(H_{k})
, Eq. (
60
) still naturally holds even if we take the maximum of the two expressions on the right side of Eq. (
60
); thus, the bound obtained with our metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
(the first inequality of Eq. (
60
)) refines Lee et al.’s bound given in Eq. (
58
) with the Frobenius norm.

As an application of Eq. (
60
), we consider a many-body control problem: a one-dimensional chain of
N
spin-
1/2
particles with XY interaction, following Ref.
[
30
]
.
Its drift Hamiltonian is given by

H_{0}=J\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}|{j}\rangle\langle{j+1}|+|{j+1}\rangle\langle{j}|,

(61)

when we restrict our consideration to the single-excitation subspace. Here
|{j}\rangle\;(j=1,2,...,N)
represent the excited state at the
j
-th site. It is known that the system spanned by
\{|{j}\rangle\}_{j=1}^{N}
is fully controllable
by accessing the Hamiltonian element
H_{1}=|{1}\rangle\langle{1}|

[
25
,
26
]
, and
Lee et al. attempted to obtain the minimum necessary time to implement
U(T)=\exp(-i\pi/2(|{1}\rangle\langle{N}|+|{N}\rangle\langle{1}|))
, a swap operator between the first and
N
-th spins
[
30
]
.
The operator
V
in Eq. (
60
) is chosen to be
V=I-2|{1}\rangle\langle{1}|
, which is clearly in
\mathrm{Stab}(H_{1})
.
Then, by letting
H_{0}^{\prime}
be a normalized Hamiltonian
H_{0}^{\prime}:=H_{0}/\|H_{0}\|_{F}=H_{0}/(J\sqrt{2(N-1)})
,

\|H_{0}^{\prime}-VH_{0}^{\prime}V^{\dagger}\|_{F}=\frac{2J}{\|H_{0}\|_{F}}\|(|{1}\rangle\langle{2}|+|{2}\rangle\langle{1}|)\|_{F}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{N-1}}

(62)

and
VU(T)V^{\dagger}=U(T)^{\dagger}
lead to

\frac{\|[U(T),V]\|_{F}}{\|[H_{0}^{\prime},V]\|_{F}}=\frac{\|U(T)-VU(T)V^{\dagger}\|_{F}}{\|H_{0}^{\prime}-VH_{0}^{\prime}V^{\dagger}\|_{F}}=2\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{N-1}}{2}=\sqrt{2(N-1)}.

(63)

Meanwhile,
d(U(T),VU(T)V^{\dagger})
can be evaluated as

\displaystyle d(U(T),VU(T)V^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=d(U(T),U(T)^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\min_{C\in\mathrm{su}(N)}\left\{\|C\|_{F}\left|e^{C}\right.=\exp(-i\pi(|{1}\rangle\langle{N}|+|{N}\rangle\langle{1}|)\right\}

\displaystyle=\|-i\pi(|{1}\rangle\langle{N}|+|{N}\rangle\langle{1}|)\|_{F}=\sqrt{2}\pi.

(64)

Then, Eq. (
60
) is verified as

T\geq\frac{d(U(T),VU(T)V^{\dagger})}{\|[H_{0}^{\prime},V]\|_{F}}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{N-1}>\sqrt{2(N-1)}=\frac{\|[U(T),V]\|_{F}}{\|[H_{0}^{\prime},V]\|_{F}}.

(65)

Thus, this example illustrates a realistic many-body setting in which the present bound gives a sharper estimate.

IV
Outline of proof

Let us delineate the proof of Theorem

1
, while its full details are given in Sections VI to IX of the Supplemental Material. Here, we first prove Lemma

1

via the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH) formula.
To this end, let us define an infinite series for operators
A
and
B
in a Lie algebra

\displaystyle M(A,B):=A+B+\frac{1}{2}[A,B]+\frac{1}{12}([A,[A,B]]+[B,[B,A]])+\cdots,

(66)

whose more precise form with higher-order commutators is shown in Section VI of the Supplemental Material. We also define the operator norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}:Hom(\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}:=\sqrt{\sup_{|{x}\rangle}\frac{\langle{x}|A^{\dagger}A|{x}\rangle}{\langle x|x\rangle}}.

(67)

The BCH formula can be expressed as follows.

Theorem 3
.

(Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula)
If
\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\log 2
, the series in
M(A,B)
converges, and

\displaystyle e^{A}e^{B}

\displaystyle=e^{M(A,B)}

(68)

\displaystyle\mathrm{and}\;\;M(A,B)

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{L}(\{A,B\})

(69)

hold.

When
A
and
B
are both anti-Hermitian operators, i.e.,
A^{\dagger}=-A
, whose Frobenius norms are sufficiently small, we obtain a simple inequality that
\|M(A,B)\|_{F}
should satisfy:

\|M(A,B)\|_{F}^{2}\leq 2\|A\|_{F}^{2}+2\|B\|_{F}^{2}-\|A-B\|_{F}^{2}.

(70)

The proof of this inequality is somewhat lengthy and technical, so it is summarized in Section VII of Supplemental Material, along with the condition on the norm.

We are now ready to describe the proof of Lemma

1

by using Theorem
3
and Eq. (
70
); the full proof is given in Section VIII of the Supplemental Material.
For anti-Hermitian operators
A
and
B
, we choose large values of
m_{a},m_{b}\in\mathbb{N}
so that
(1/m_{a})A
and
(1/m_{b})B
are small enough for Eq. (
70
) to be used, and we let
m_{a}
be odd. Let us define the operators
n=m_{a}+m_{b}
by

\{C_{j}^{(1)}\}:=\{\overbrace{\frac{1}{m_{a}}A,\frac{1}{m_{a}}A,...,\frac{1}{m_{a}}A,}^{m_{a}}\overbrace{\frac{1}{m_{b}}B,\frac{1}{m_{b}}B,...,\frac{1}{m_{b}}B}^{m_{b}}\}

(71)

for
j\in\{1,2,...,n\}
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we recursively define a sequence of operators

\displaystyle C_{j}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}M(C_{j-1}^{(k)},C_{j}^{(k)})&\makebox{ if $j+k$ is odd and $j\neq 1$},\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}M(C_{j}^{(k)},C_{j+1}^{(k)})&\makebox{ if $j+k$ is even and $j\neq n$},\\
C_{1}^{(k)}&\makebox{ if $1+k$ is odd and $j=1$},\\

V
Discussion and conclusion

We have derived BCH-based inequalities for effective generators of controlled unitary evolutions. The main result gives a lower bound on the time required to implement a target unitary in terms of a projective operator distance and the time-integrated strength of the applied Hamiltonian. Since the deviation removes the trace component, the bound is insensitive to global phase and naturally defines a distance on the corresponding projective unitary group.

The bound incorporates the Lie-algebra membership constraint on the effective generator, but not the time overhead required to synthesize the minimizing logarithmic generator from the available Hamiltonians. It therefore rules out algebraically inadmissible shortcuts, while leaving open the finer problem of quantifying synthesis costs within the dynamical Lie algebra. We compared this projective operator-level bound with familiar QSL-type estimates and unitary-distance bounds, and found that it can give a sharper lower bound in the examples considered.

Let us make a remark on a geometric interpretation of the main results. The general terminology and notations in the following can be found in textbooks on geometry, e.g.,
[
45
]
. For a dynamical Lie algebra
\mathcal{L}
, we assume
e^{\mathcal{L}}
is closed on the unitary group
U(D)
, where
D
is a dimension of the quantum system.
A Riemannian metric
g_{I}
on identity
I
can now be defined by a Hilbert-Schmidt inner product:
g_{I}(X,Y)=\mathrm{tr}X^{\dagger}Y
, where
X,Y\in\mathcal{L}
.
Then, it is known that we can extend
g_{I}
to all the Lie group
e^{\mathcal{L}}
so that
the resulting Riemannian metric is bi-invariant. Here, Riemannian metric
g_{U}
is called bi-invariant if for all
U,V\in e^{\mathcal{L}}
and
X,Y\in T_{U}e^{\mathcal{L}}

g_{U}(X,Y)=g_{VU}\big((dL_{V})_{U}X,(dL_{V})_{U}Y\big)=g_{VU}\big((dR_{V})_{U}X,(dR_{V})_{U}Y\big),

(86)

where
T_{U}e^{\mathcal{L}}
is a tangent space of
e^{\mathcal{L}}
at
U
,
(dL_{V})_{U}
and
(dR_{V})_{U}
are differential maps of the left action
L_{V}
and the right action
R_{V}
of
e^{\mathcal{L}}
. Namely,
L_{V}(U)=VU,R_{V}(U)=UV
, and
(dL_{V})_{U}X=VX,(dR_{V})_{U}X=XV
for
X\in T_{U}e^{\mathcal{L}}
.

Now, suppose that a curve
U(t)
is a solution of Schrödinger equation, Eq. (
3
), connecting
U(0)=I
and
U(T)=U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
.
By writing a velocity vector of
U(t)
as
X_{U(t)}:=dU/dt\in T_{U(t)}e^{\mathcal{L}}
, the pull-back of
X_{U(t)}
to the identity is given by

X_{I}(t):=(dL_{U(t)^{\dagger}})_{U(t)}X_{U(t)}=U(t)^{\dagger}\frac{dU}{dt}=-iU(t)^{\dagger}H(t)U(t)\in\mathcal{L}.

(87)

Then, the length of the curve
\{U(t)\}_{t=0}^{T}
is

\displaystyle\mbox{length}(\{U(t)\}_{t=0}^{T})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{g_{U}\big(X_{U(t)},X_{U(t)}\big)}dt=\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{g_{I}\big(X_{I}(t),X_{I}(t)\big)}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\big(X_{I}(t)^{\dagger}X_{I}(t)\big)}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\|H(t)\|_{F}dt.

(88)

This length must be lower-bounded by the length of the minimum geodesic connecting
I
and
U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
.
An important observation is that under this bi-invariant metric
g_{U}
of the compact Lie group
e^{\mathcal{L}}
,
any geodesic through identity
I
is given by
an exponential map
\exp:t\mapsto e^{-itH}
, where
iH\in\mathcal{L}
.
In other words, for a given
U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
, there exists a time-independent Hamiltonian
iH_{0}\in\mathcal{L}
such that the minimum geodesic between
I
and
U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
is given by
U_{0}(t)=e^{-itH_{0}}
. Then, if
U^{\mathrm{(target)}}
is achieved at
t=T
, the length of the geodesic is evaluated by Eq. (
88
) as

\displaystyle\mbox{length}(\{U_{0}(t)\}_{t=0}^{T})=\int_{0}^{T}\|H_{0}\|_{F}dt=T\|H_{0}\|_{F},

(89)

and therefore,

\displaystyle T\|H_{0}\|_{F}\leq\int_{0}^{T}\|H(t)\|_{F}dt.

(90)

Identifying
C[T]=TH_{0}
,
we see Eq. (

) is equivalent to Eq. (
53
).
Thus, Theorem
2
can be understood as a consequence of the geometrical consideration of the space of unitaries under the assumption that
e^{\mathcal{L}}
is closed in
U(D)
.
However, this assumption may not hold, in general. We give an example, where

\mathcal{L}
generates a non-compact group, in Section I of the Supplemental Material.

Despite the difficulties of analyzing quantum control time problems, their importance cannot be overemphasized, and our general arguments and results can be useful in exploring this rich and interesting field. A natural next step is to go beyond Lie-algebra membership and develop a more refined account of the cost of generating different directions within the dynamical Lie algebra. Such refinements may provide sharper lower bounds for genuinely many-body quantum control problems with limited access.

Lemma 3
.

The metric

is invariant under unitary operations

\displaystyle d(U_{1},U_{2})=d(V_{1}U_{1}V_{2},V_{1}U_{2}V_{2})

Proof.

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\|V_{1}^{-1}CV_{1}\|_{F}\left|e^{V_{1}^{-1}CV_{1}}=U_{1}U_{2}^{-1}\right.\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\|C\|_{F}\left|e^{C}=V_{1}U_{1}U_{2}^{-1}V_{1}^{-1}\right.\right\}

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle:=\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\;\max\left(\left\|H_{A}(t)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}},\left\|H_{B}(t)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right),

(S81)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle:=\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\;\max\left(\left\|H_{A}(t)\right\|_{F},\left\|H_{B}(t)\right\|_{F}\right),

(S82)

as well as an integer
N
such that

\displaystyle 6\alpha T

\displaystyle<N,\;\mbox{and}

(S83)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\beta T

\displaystyle<N.

(S84)

We now consider the Schrödinger equation
idU(t)/dt=H(t)U(t)
under the initial condition
U(0)=I
.
The Hamiltonian is given as follows for an interval
t\in[0,2\Delta_{N})
with
\Delta_{N}=T/N
and
n\in\{1,...,N\}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{n}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle H_{A}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}+t\right)&\;\mbox{if}\;\;\displaystyle 0\leq t<\Delta_{N}\\
\displaystyle-H_{B}\left((n+1)\Delta_{N}-t\right)&\;\mbox{if}\;\;\displaystyle\Delta_{N}\leq t<2\Delta_{N}.\end{array}\right.

This Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{n}(t)
evolves the system forward by
H_{A}(t)
for a duration
\Delta_{N}
and then backward by
H_{B}(t)
for the same
\Delta_{N}
. Thus, the overall evolution over the duration
2\Delta_{N}
shows the difference between these two Hamiltonians, and by accumulating such differences over
T
, we intend to prove Eq. (
S80
).

The solution of the Schrödinger equation with the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
V
) is

\displaystyle U^{(n)}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle U_{A}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}+t\right)U_{A}^{-1}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}\right)&\makebox{if}\;\;0\leq t<\Delta_{N}\\
\displaystyle U_{B}\left((n+1)\Delta_{N}-t\right)U_{B}^{-1}\left(n\Delta_{N}\right)U_{A}\left(n\Delta_{N}\right)U_{A}^{-1}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}\right)&\makebox{if}\;\;\Delta_{N}\leq t<2\Delta_{N},\end{array}\right.

so at
t=0
, i.e., at the beginning of each small time interval
\Delta_{N}
,
U(0)=I
.
Therefore, at time
2\Delta_{N}=2T/N
,

U^{(n)}\left(2\Delta_{N}\right)=U_{B}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}\right)U_{B}^{-1}\left(n\Delta_{N}\right)U_{A}\left(n\Delta_{N}\right)U_{A}^{-1}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}\right),

and the assumptions of Lemma
4
are satisfied when
the time
t
in Eqs. (
S65
) and (
S66
) is set to
2\Delta_{N}
.
Therefore, Eq. (
S64
) in Lemma
4
now implies that a
C[\cdot]
exists such that

d\left(U_{B}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}\right)U_{B}^{-1}\left(n\Delta_{N}\right)U_{A}\left(n\Delta_{N}\right)U_{A}^{-1}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}\right),I\right)=\left\|C\left[2\Delta_{N}\right]\right\|_{F},

(S89)

and

\displaystyle\left\|C\left[2\Delta_{N}\right]\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{0}^{2\Delta_{N}}dt\tilde{H}_{n}(t)\right\|_{F}+\left\|C\left[2\Delta_{N}\right]+\int_{0}^{2\Delta_{N}}dt\tilde{H}_{n}(t)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{0}^{2\Delta_{N}}dt\tilde{H}_{n}(t)\right\|_{F}+4\sqrt{D}\left(2\alpha\Delta_{N}\right)^{2},

(S90)

using the triangle inequality and Eq. (
S63
).
The first term in the last line of Eq. (
S90
) can be upper bounded:

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{0}^{2\Delta_{N}}dt\tilde{H}_{n}(t)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{(n-1)\Delta_{N}}^{n\Delta_{N}}dt\left(H_{B}(t)-H_{A}(t)\right)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(n-1)\Delta_{N}}^{n\Delta_{N}}dt\left\|H_{B}(t)-H_{A}(t)\right\|_{F}.

(S91)

Combining these relations, we can evaluate the distance as follows:

\displaystyle d(U_{A}(t),U_{B}(t))

\displaystyle=d(U_{B}^{-1}(t)U_{A}(t),I)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}d\left(U_{B}^{-1}\left(n\Delta_{N}\right)U_{A}\left(n\Delta_{N}\right),\;U_{B}^{-1}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}\right)U_{A}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}d\left(U_{B}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}\right)U_{B}^{-1}\left(n\Delta_{N}\right)U_{A}\left(n\Delta_{N}\right)U_{A}^{-1}\left((n-1)\Delta_{N}\right),I\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\left\|\int_{0}^{2\Delta_{N}}dt\tilde{H}_{n}(t)\right\|_{F}+4\sqrt{D}\left(2\alpha\Delta_{N}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}dt\left\|H_{B}(t)-H_{A}(t)\right\|_{F}+16\sqrt{D}\frac{\alpha^{2}T^{2}}{N}.

(S92)

The first and third lines are due to the unitary invariance of the distance
d(\cdot,\cdot)
shown in Lemma
3
. The second line
again comes from
recursive applications of the triangle inequality. The last two inequalities are a result of Eqs.
(
S90
) and (
S91
), respectively.
By letting
N
tend toward infinity, the second term in the last line of Eq. (
S92
) vanishes; hence, Eq. (
S80
) holds.

∎

VI
Definition of
M(\cdot,\cdot)

The coefficients in the infinite series for
M(\cdot,\cdot)
are defined in the following manner.

Definition 1
.

For
\vec{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}
, the functions
f(\vec{c}),g(\vec{c}),h(\vec{c}):\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are those that satisfy the following equations:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}f(\vec{c})A^{1-c_{1}}B^{c_{1}}A^{1-c_{2}}B^{c_{2}}\cdots A^{1-c_{n}}B^{c_{n}}

\displaystyle:=\left(\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{j}}{j!}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{B^{j}}{j!}\right)-I\right)^{n-1},

(S93)

\displaystyle(n+2)g(\vec{c})

\displaystyle:=(-1)f(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n},1)-(-1)^{n}f(1-c_{1},1-c_{2},\cdots,1-c_{n},1),

(S94)

\displaystyle h(\vec{c})

\displaystyle:=2g(1,c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n})-2g(0,c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-1)^{m}g(c_{m},c_{m-1},\cdots,c_{1})g(c_{m+1},c_{m+2},\cdots,c_{n}).

(S95)

where
A
and
B
are noncommutative algebraic elements.

Additionally, for a vector
\vec{c}
, we define
|\vec{c}|
as

\displaystyle|\vec{c}|

\displaystyle:=\sum_{m=1}^{n}c_{m},

(S96)

and we assume that
\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes 0}}g(\vec{c})=g(\varnothing)
, where
\varnothing
is a null string.

Some of the specific values of these functions are:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{clcclcclccl}f(\varnothing)=1,&&f(0)=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},&&f(1)=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},&&\\
f(0,0)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{12},&&f(0,1)=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{6},&&f(1,0)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{3},&&f(1,1)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{12},\\
g(\varnothing)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},&&g(0)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{12},&&g(1)=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{12},&&h(\varnothing)=-\displaystyle\frac{1}{12},...\end{array}

It is now possible to show that the three function series,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes{n}}}|f(\vec{c})|x^{{n}-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|},

(S100)

\displaystyle\sum_{{n}=0}^{m}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes{n}}}(n+2)|g(\vec{c})|x^{{n}-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|},\quad\mbox{and}

(S101)

\displaystyle\sum_{{n}=0}^{m}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes{n}}}|h(\vec{c})|x^{{n}-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|},

(S102)

locally converge uniformly on

(x,y)\in\Omega
as
m\rightarrow\infty
, where
\Omega:=\{(x,y)|x\geq 0,\;y\geq 0,\;x+y<\log 2\}
.
The locally uniform convergence of these series implies that when
\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\log 2
, changing the order of the terms in the following series is allowed; that is, such a change has no effect on the final result:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}f(\vec{c})A^{1-c_{1}}B^{c_{1}}A^{1-c_{2}}B^{c_{2}}\cdots A^{1-c_{n}}B^{c_{n}},

(S103)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}(n+2)g(\vec{c})A^{1-c_{1}}B^{c_{1}}A^{1-c_{2}}B^{c_{2}}\cdots A^{1-c_{n}}B^{c_{n}},\quad\mbox{and}

(S104)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}h(\vec{c})A^{1-c_{1}}B^{c_{1}}A^{1-c_{2}}B^{c_{2}}\cdots A^{1-c_{n}}B^{c_{n}}.

(S105)

We now prove the locally uniform convergence of the series of (
S100
)–(
S102
). In the following, the pair
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Omega
is chosen in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
so that
0\leq x\leq x_{0},0\leq y\leq y_{0}
, and
x_{0}+y_{0}<\log 2
.

For the first series (
S100
), we can consider the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle>\frac{d^{m}}{dz^{m}}\left.\frac{-\log(2-e^{z})}{e^{z}-1}\right|_{z=x_{0}+y_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{d^{m}}{dz^{m}}\left.a_{n}z^{n}\right|_{z=x+y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\partial_{x,y}^{(m)}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}f_{+}(\vec{c})x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}f_{+}(\vec{c})\partial_{x,y}^{(m)}x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}|f(\vec{c})|\partial_{x,y}^{(m)}x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}.

(S106)

The first inequality holds simply because the function
-\log(2-e^{z})/(e^{z}-1)
is analytic for
z<\log 2
. The second line is a Taylor expansion of the function, where
a_{n}
are all positive coefficients, and the inequality is justified because the convergence radius of the series is
\log 2
. Note that
-\log(2-e^{z})/(e^{z}-1)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1/n)(e^{z}-1)^{n-1}
; then,
f_{+}(\vec{c})
on the third line of Eq. (
S106
) is formally defined by the coefficients in the expansion of the following series so that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left(\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{j}}{j!}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{B^{j}}{j!}\right)-I\right)^{n-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}f_{+}(\vec{c})A^{1-c_{1}}B^{c_{1}}A^{1-c_{2}}B^{c_{2}}\cdots A^{1-c_{n}}B^{c_{n}}

(S107)

holds, where
\partial_{x,y}^{(m)}
is an
m
-th order differential operator. Specifically, this operator should be one of
d^{m}/dx^{m-k}dy^{k}
with
0\leq k\leq m
in the expansion; however, we let it represent all of these for simplicity. Additionally,
\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}f_{+}(\vec{c})x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}=a_{n}(x+y)^{n}
is used in the third line, which can be verified through Eq. (
S107
) by replacing
A
and
B
with
c
-numbers
x
and
y
. The last inequality is due to
|f(\vec{c})|<f_{+}(\vec{c})
.

Equation (
S106
) shows that when
m=0
, the series Eq. (
S100
) converges uniformly on
(x,y)\in\Omega
. Let us define
f_{\infty}(x,y)
by the RHS of Eq. (
S106
) in what follows.

f_{\infty}(x,y):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}|f(\vec{c})|x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}.

(S108)

To show the convergence of the second series (
S101
), we proceed in a similar manner:

\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle>\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f_{\infty}(s,t)\right|_{s=x_{0},t=y_{0}}+\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f_{\infty}(s,t)\right|_{s=y_{0},t=x_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}|\vec{c}|\cdot|f(\vec{c})|\left(x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|-1}+y^{n-|\vec{c}|}x^{|\vec{c}|-1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes(n-1)}}|f(c_{1},c_{2}\cdots,c_{n-1},1)|x^{n-|\vec{c}|-1}y^{|\vec{c}|}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes(n-1)}}|f(1-c_{1},1-c_{2}\cdots,1-c_{n-1},1)|x^{n-|\vec{c}|-1}y^{|\vec{c}|}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}(n+2)|g(\vec{c})|x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}=:g_{\infty}(x,y).

(S109)

The first inequality comes from Eq. (
S106
) by setting
\partial_{x,y}^{(m)}
to
\partial/\partial y
. The second inequality is due to the positivity of the coefficients, as well as to
0\leq x\leq x_{0}
and
0\leq y\leq y_{0}
. The third line is obtained by multiplying each term by a number that is less than or equal to 1 and dropping some positive terms. The last line is obtained from the definition of
g(\vec{c})
in Eq. (
S94
).

The convergence of the third series in Eq. (
S102
) can be shown as follows:

\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle\geq\left.\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}f_{\infty}(s,t)\right|_{s=x_{0},t=y_{0}}+\left.\frac{d^{2}}{dsdt}f_{\infty}(s,t)\right|_{s=y_{0},t=x_{0}}+\left.\frac{d^{2}}{dsdt}f_{\infty}(s,t)\right|_{s=x_{0},t=y_{0}}+\left.\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}f_{\infty}(s,t)\right|_{s=y_{0},t=x_{0}}+g_{\infty}(x_{0},y_{0})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}|f(\vec{c})|\left(|\vec{c}|(|\vec{c}|-1)x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|-2}+(n-|\vec{c}|)|\vec{c}|y^{n-|\vec{c}|-1}x^{|\vec{c}|-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}|f(\vec{c})|\left((n-|\vec{c}|)|\vec{c}|x^{n-|\vec{c}|-1}y^{|\vec{c}|-1}+(n-|\vec{c}|)(n-|\vec{c}|-1)y^{n-|\vec{c}|-2}x^{|\vec{c}|}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}(n+2)|g(\vec{c})|x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}\frac{2}{n+2}\left(|f(1,c_{1},c_{2}\cdots,c_{n},1)|+|f(0,1-c_{1},1-c_{2}\cdots,1-c_{n},1)|\right)x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}\frac{2}{n+2}\left(|f(0,c_{1},c_{2}\cdots,c_{n},1)|+|f(1,1-c_{1},1-c_{2}\cdots,1-c_{n},1)|\right)x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}|g(\vec{c})|x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}2|g(1,c_{1},c_{2}\cdots,c_{n})|x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}2|g(0,c_{1},c_{2}\cdots,c_{n})|x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}|g(\vec{c})|x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}|h(\vec{c})|x^{n-|\vec{c}|}y^{|\vec{c}|}.

(S110)

Here,
\partial_{x,y}^{(m)}
in Eq. (
S106
) is replaced with
d^{2}/dx^{2},d^{2}/dxdy
, and
d^{2}/dy^{2}
, and the remaining changes are almost the same as those in Eq. (
S109
).

Now, we are ready to define
M(\cdot,\cdot)
. In the following, we use the notation
\mathrm{ad}(A)(\cdot)=[A,\cdot]
, and the operator norm of this linear transformation is given by

\left\|\mathrm{ad}(A)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}:=\sup_{B}\frac{\|[A,B]\|_{F}}{\|B\|_{F}},

(S111)

where the supremum is taken over all complex matrices
B
whose Frobenius norms are nonzero,
\|B\|_{F}\neq 0
.

Definition 2
.

When
\|\mathrm{ad}(A)\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\mathrm{ad}(B)\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\log 2
holds,

M(A,B):Hom(\mathbb{C}^{N})\times Hom(\mathbb{C}^{N})\rightarrow Hom(\mathbb{C}^{N})
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle M(A,B)

\displaystyle:=A+B+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}g(\vec{c})\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{\vec{c}}([A,B])

\displaystyle=A+B+\frac{1}{2}[A,B]+\frac{1}{12}\left([A,[A,B]]+[B,[B,A]]\right)+\cdots,

(S112)

where for
\vec{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n})
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{\vec{c}}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{ad}(A)^{1-c_{1}}\circ\mathrm{ad}(B)^{c_{1}}\circ\mathrm{ad}(A)^{1-c_{2}}\circ\mathrm{ad}(B)^{c_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathrm{ad}(A)^{1-c_{n}}\circ\mathrm{ad}(B)^{c_{n}}.

(S113)

For example, if
\vec{c}=(0,1,1)
, then
\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{\vec{c}}=\mathrm{ad}(A)\circ\mathrm{ad}(B)\circ\mathrm{ad}(B)
.

\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{\varnothing}
is considered to be identity as a superoperator.

We can now obtain a relation between the norms of operators.

Lemma 5
.

For any linear operator
A
, the following relation holds:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{ad}(A)\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2\left\|A\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2\left\|A\right\|_{F}.

(S114)

Note that when
A,B
are both anti-Hermitian,
M(A,B)
is also anti-Hermitian. When
A,B
are Hermitian,
iM(iA,iB)\in\mathcal{L}(\{iA,iB\})
.
Although
h(\vec{c})
defined in Eq. (
S95
) is unnecessary for
M(\cdot,\cdot)
, we define it because it will be used in the proof of Eq. (
70
).

VII
Proof of Eq. (
70
)

We now prove Eq. (
70
), i.e.,

\|M(A,B)\|_{F}^{2}\leq 2\|A\|_{F}^{2}+2\|B\|_{F}^{2}-\|A-B\|_{F}^{2}.

(
70
)

Lemma 6
.

When
A
and
B
are anti-Hermitian and
\|A\|_{F},\|B\|_{F}<\Delta
, Eq. (
70
)
holds, where

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle:=\min\left(\frac{\log 2}{6}\delta,\frac{1}{4}\log 2\right),

(S115)

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle:=\min\left(\frac{1}{12}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}|h(\vec{c})|\left(\frac{\log 2}{3}\right)^{n}\right)^{-1},1\right)>0.

(S116)

Note that since
2\times\log 2/3<\log 2
, the infinite series in the definition of
\delta
converges.
Additionally, from Lemma
5
, we can see that the condition for defining
M(A,B)
is met (see Def.
2
):

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{ad}(A)\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\mathrm{ad}(B)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}+2\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2\|A\|_{F}+2\|B\|_{F}<4\Delta\leq\log 2.

(S117)

Proof.

The difference between the left- and right-hand sides of Eq. (
70
) is

\displaystyle-\|M(A,B)\|_{F}^{2}+2\|A\|_{F}^{2}+2\|B\|_{F}^{2}-\|A-B\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}M(A,B)^{2}-2\mathrm{tr}A^{2}-2\mathrm{tr}B^{2}+\mathrm{tr}(A-B)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}2g(\vec{c}){\rm tr}(A+B)\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{\vec{c}}([A,B])

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}\sum_{\vec{c}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes m}}g(\vec{c})g(\vec{c}^{\prime}){\rm tr}\left(\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{\vec{c}}([A,B])\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{\vec{c}^{\prime}}([A,B])\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n-1}}2g(1,c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n-1}){\rm tr}\left([A,B]\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{\vec{c}}([A,B])\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n-1}}2g(0,c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n-1}){\rm tr}\left([A,B]\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{\vec{c}}([A,B])\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}\sum_{\vec{c}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes m}}(-1)^{n}g(\vec{c})g(\vec{c}^{\prime}){\rm tr}\left([A,B]\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{(c_{n},\cdots,c_{2},c_{1},c_{1}^{\prime},c_{2}^{\prime}\cdots,c_{m}^{\prime})}([A,B])\right)

\displaystyle={\rm tr}\left([A,B]\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes n}}h(\vec{c})\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{\vec{c}}\right)([A,B])\right).

(S118)

The second equality is a result of substituting
M
in Eq. (
S112
), and the following four relations (
S119
–
S122
) are used to rewrite the first term of the third line; Eq. (
S123
) is used for the second term:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(A\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{(0,c_{2},c_{3},\cdots,c_{n})}(C)\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(S119)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(B\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{(1,c_{2},c_{3},\cdots,c_{n})}(C)\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(S120)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(A\mathrm{ad}(A,B)^{(1,c_{2},c_{3},\cdots,c_{n})}(C)\right)

{\mathrm{tr}}C_{\infty}=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}{\mathrm{tr}}C_{r_{m}}={\mathrm{tr}}(A+B)
due to Eqs. (
S159
) and (
S162
).

Finally, from Eq. (
S162
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{dev}C_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{dev}C_{r_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\left(\mathrm{dev}A+\mathrm{dev}B+\frac{2\Delta}{r_{m}\sqrt{D}}\right)\left(\mathrm{dev}A+\mathrm{dev}B\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dev}A+\mathrm{dev}B,

(S165)

which means that Eq. (
13
) is also satisfied.
Therefore,
C_{\infty}
is the operator
C
whose existence was to be shown for Lemma
1
.
∎

IX
Proofs of Theorems
1
and
2

Let us first prove Theorem
1
, namely, Eqs. (
15
)–(
18
), from Lemma
1
following the flow shown in
Sec.
IV
of the main text.

Proof of Theorem 1.

Owing to the piecewise continuity of the Hermitian operator
H(t)
, and because
H(t)
is bounded, the generated unitary operator
U(T)
can be written as

\displaystyle U(T)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\searrow 0}\exp\left(-i\delta H\left(\left\lfloor\frac{T}{\delta}\right\rfloor\delta\right)\right)\cdots\exp\left(-i\delta H(2\delta)\right)\exp\left(-i\delta H(\delta)\right).

(S166)

From Lemma
1
, there is an operator
C_{\delta}[T]
such that

\displaystyle\exp\left(-iC_{\delta}[T]\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-i\delta H\left(\left\lfloor\frac{T}{\delta}\right\rfloor\delta\right)\right)\cdots\exp\left(-i\delta H(2\delta)\right)\exp\left(-i\delta H(\delta)\right),

(S167)

\displaystyle iC_{\delta}[T]

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{L}(\{iH(t)\}_{0\leq t\leq T}),

(S168)

\displaystyle{\rm dev}C_{\delta}[T]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor T/\delta\rfloor}\delta{\rm dev}H(n\delta),

(S169)

\displaystyle{\rm tr}C_{\delta}[T]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor T/\delta\rfloor}\delta{\rm tr}H(n\delta).

(S170)

Since
H(t)
is piecewise continuous, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta_{0}
such that
for any
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
,

\begin{split}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor T/\delta\rfloor}\delta{\rm dev}H(n\delta)-\int_{0}^{T}dt{\rm dev}H(t)\right|\leq\varepsilon,\\
\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor T/\delta\rfloor}\delta{\rm tr}H(n\delta)-\int_{0}^{T}dt{\rm tr}H(t)\right|\leq\varepsilon.\end{split}

(S171)

From Eqs. (
S169
-
S171
), it can be seen that, if
\delta<\delta_{0}
,
\mathrm{dev}C_{\delta}[T]
and
\mathrm{tr}C_{\delta}[T]
are upper bounded for a fixed
T
. Thus,
\|C_{\delta}[T]\|_{F}
is also bounded, that is,
C_{\delta}[T]
is included in a compact set.

Then, it is possible to choose a decreasing sequence
\{\delta_{m}\}
of
\delta
for any fixed
T
that converges to zero so that

\displaystyle iC[T]

\displaystyle:=i\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}C_{\delta_{m}}[T],

(S172)

which is naturally in
\mathcal{L}(\{iH(t)\}_{0\leq t\leq T})
. Therefore, from Eq. (
S167
), we have

\displaystyle\exp(-iC[T])

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\exp(-iC_{\delta_{m}}[T])

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\exp\left(-i\delta H\left(\left\lfloor\frac{T}{\delta_{m}}\right\rfloor\delta_{m}\right)\right)\cdots\exp\left(-i\delta H(2\delta_{m})\right)\exp\left(-i\delta H(\delta_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=U(T).

(S173)

Additionally, Eq. (
S169
) leads to

\displaystyle{\rm dev}C[T]

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}{\rm dev}C_{\delta_{m}}[T]

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor T/\delta_{m}\rfloor}\delta_{m}{\rm dev}H(n\delta_{m})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt{\rm dev}H(t)+\epsilon.

(S174)

for any
\epsilon>0
.
Similarly, Eq. (
S170
) implies

\displaystyle\left|{\rm tr}C[T]-\int_{0}^{T}dt{\rm tr}H(t)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}{\rm tr}C_{\delta_{m}}[T]-\int_{0}^{T}dt{\rm tr}H(t)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor T/\delta_{m}\rfloor}\delta_{m}{\rm tr}H(n\delta_{m})-\int_{0}^{T}dt{\rm tr}H(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon.

(S175)

for any
\epsilon
.
Now, Eqs. (
15
)–(
18
) are proven; hence, Theorem
1
holds.
∎

Let us move on to prove Theorem
2
, i.e., Eqs. (
51
)-(
53
), by Lemma
2
. This can be carried out in a similar manner to that of Theorem
1
, so below we depict some points that are different from above.

Proof of Theorem 2.

For a unitary
U(T)
with discretized time, Lemma
2
implies there exists a
\delta
-dependent operator
C_{\delta}[T]
, so that

\displaystyle\|C_{\delta}[T]\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor T/\delta\rfloor}\delta\|H(n\delta)\|_{F}

(S176)

holds, instead of Eqs. (
S169
) and (
S170
).
Further, since
H(t)
is piecewise continuous, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor T/\delta\rfloor}\delta\|H(n\delta)\|_{F}-\int_{0}^{T}dt\|H(t)\|_{F}\right|\leq\varepsilon

(S177)

holds, instead of Eq. (
S171
).
This guarantees that
C_{\delta}[T]
is in a compact space for a fixed
T
. Then, it is possible to choose a decreasing and converging-to-zero sequence
\{\delta_{m}\}
of
\delta
for any fixed
T
so that
iC[T]
in Eq. (
S172
) is in
\mathcal{L}(\{iH(t)\}_{0\leq t\leq T})
. Equation (
S173
) then also holds for a similar reason.

Equation (
S176
) allows us to have, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\|C[T]\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\|C_{\delta_{m}}[T]\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor T/\delta_{m}\rfloor}\delta_{m}\|H(n\delta_{m})\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt\|H(t)\|_{F}+\varepsilon,

(S178)

instead of Eqs. (
S174
) and (
S175
).
Therefore, Eqs. (
51
)-(
53
) are proven; thus, Theorem
2
holds.
∎
