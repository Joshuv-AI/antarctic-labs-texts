Assessing the Discriminative Power of Metabolic Indices for PMOS Detection in Normal-Weight Women
Authors: Alihan Tigli, Yakup Baykus, Rulin Deniz, Muhammet Bora Uzuner, Sefer Ustebay, Nazli Sener, Yasemin Ercan Degirmenci, Guzide Ece Akinci, Erdem Gürkan, Oğuzhan Karakoç, Engin Korkmazer, Mustafa Ata Aydin, Esra Demirocak, Suleyman Aydin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between metabolic indices and Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) in normal-weight women and to evaluate their individual and combined discriminatory performance.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective case-control study included 86 normal-weight women (BMI <25 kg/m2) with PMOS and 88 healthy controls. Electronic medical records were used to calculate the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), Triglyceride–Glucose (TyG) index, Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP), and Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess discriminatory performance, and optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index. Combined analyses were performed according to concordant classification of the component indices relative to their respective cut-off values.ResultsFasting glucose, fasting insulin, triglyceride levels, HOMA-IR, TyG, AIP, and METS-IR were significantly higher in women with PMOS (p<0.05). Among individual indices, TyG showed the highest discriminatory performance (AUC=0.775; 95% CI: 0.706–0.843). HOMA-IR and AIP demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance, whereas METS-IR showed lower performance. Among the combined assessments, HOMA-IR and TyG yielded an AUC of 0.830 (95% CI: 0.747–0.913), with 80.8% sensitivity and 85.2% specificity.ConclusionMetabolic indices, particularly TyG, may be useful for identifying metabolic abnormalities in normal-weight women with PMOS. Combined assessment of complementary metabolic indices may provide additional information for metabolic risk evaluation. These findings support further investigation of such indices as practical tools for metabolic assessment and risk stratification, rather than as independent diagnostic criteria for PMOS.Keywords: insulin resistance, atherogenic index of plasma, METS-IR, PCOS, rotterdam criteria, triglyceride-glucose index

Introduction

Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) is one of the most common endocrine disorders affecting women of reproductive age and is a heterogeneous clinical condition characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology.1,2 In a recent large-scale review, the prevalence of PMOS is reported to be approximately 12% in the general population, although this figure varies depending on the diagnostic criteria applied.3

Nowadays, PMOS is recognized not merely as a condition confined to the reproductive system, but also as a systemic syndrome with significant metabolic components.4 Insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, dyslipidemia, and increased cardiometabolic risk are among its major metabolic features. Insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia promote ovarian androgen production and suppress hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) synthesis, thereby contributing to hyperandrogenism, impaired follicular development, and ovulatory dysfunction.5–7 These metabolic disturbances may also contribute to atherogenic dyslipidemia, characterized particularly by increased triglyceride levels, and to increased long-term cardiometabolic risk.8,9 Accordingly, early identification of metabolic abnormalities in PMOS is important, particularly for recognizing individuals who may be at increased long-term metabolic risk.

PMOS and its metabolic aspects are frequently associated with obesity.10 However, a significant proportion of women with PMOS have a normal body mass index (BMI). In this patient group, the clinical phenotype is often more heterogeneous, which can lead to metabolic risks being overlooked. However, accumulating evidence from recent studies indicates that insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction may also occur in normal-weight women with PMOS.11 This suggests that metabolic risk assessment based solely on BMI may be inadequate, and that there is a need for more sensitive biochemical markers capable of identifying metabolic disorders, particularly in women without excess body weight.

In recent years, various metabolic indices that can be easily calculated from routine biochemical parameters have increasingly been used to assess insulin resistance and cardiometabolic risk. The Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), the Triglyceride–Glucose (TyG) index, the Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) and the Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR) are among the indices developed for this purpose and which can be used to assess metabolic risk in different populations.12–14 Although a growing body of evidence suggests that these indices may be useful for assessing insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and metabolic risk, their discriminatory performance in normal-weight women with PMOS has not yet been fully established.15–18 In particular, evidence regarding whether combining complementary metabolic indices provides additional discriminatory value over individual indices remains limited.

In clinical practice, the diagnosis of PMOS is based on the Rotterdam criteria,19 which primarily reflect reproductive and morphological features and do not directly characterize the metabolic dimension of the syndrome. Therefore, metabolic indices should not be regarded as substitutes for established diagnostic criteria, but rather as potential tools for identifying metabolic abnormalities and stratifying metabolic risk, particularly in normal-weight women with PMOS.20,21

Furthermore, given the multifactorial aetiopathogenesis of PMOS, the combined assessment of metabolic indices reflecting different metabolic pathways may provide additional discriminatory information beyond that obtained from individual indices.22 In this context, evaluating the diagnostic performance of metabolic indices—both individually and in combination—and determining their potential to predict PMOS and its metabolic profile, particularly in normal-weight women, could provide clinically valuable insights. Examining the discriminatory power of these indices may contribute to the identification of practical and accessible biochemical markers that could be utilized in clinical practice.

This study was designed to comprehensively assess the association between HOMA-IR, TyG index, AIP, and METS-IR and PMOS in normal-weight women. In addition, the study evaluates the individual and combined discriminatory performance of these indices and their potential utility in characterizing the metabolic risk profile of the normal-weight PMOS phenotype. By doing so, it aims to clarify whether these readily available metabolic indices may provide complementary information for metabolic assessment and risk stratification alongside established diagnostic criteria.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Sample

This study was designed as a retrospective, observational case-control study. The data for the study were obtained by retrospectively reviewing the electronic medical records of patients who attended the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at Bandırma Onyedi Eylül University Faculty of Medicine Hospital between January 2023 and December 2025.

The study included women of reproductive age aged between 18 and 45. The diagnosis of PMOS was established based on the core Rotterdam criteria framework, which serves as the primary diagnostic foundation in both the 2003 consensus and the updated 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline.1,19 Diagnosis requires the presence of at least two of the following three features:

Ovulatory dysfunction (oligo- and/or anovulation),

Ovulatory dysfunction (oligo- and/or anovulation),

Clinical and/or biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism,

Clinical and/or biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism,

Polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) on ultrasound. Given the retrospective design of our study and the data collection period (January 2023–December 2025) which spanned a transitional phase in clinical guidelines, PCOM was determined based on the standard sonographic evaluations routinely performed at our center at the time of diagnosis.

Polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) on ultrasound. Given the retrospective design of our study and the data collection period (January 2023–December 2025) which spanned a transitional phase in clinical guidelines, PCOM was determined based on the standard sonographic evaluations routinely performed at our center at the time of diagnosis.

Women who met at least two of these three criteria constituted the PMOS group. The control group, meanwhile, was selected from women who attended the outpatient clinic during the same period and did not meet any of the PMOS diagnostic criteria. In line with the aim of the study, only normal-weight women were included in the study to exclude the effect of obesity on metabolic indices. For this reason, women with a BMI value of 25 kg/m2 or higher—calculated using standard formulas defined in the literature based on height and weight measurements—were excluded from both groups.23

To minimize potential confounding factors that could influence metabolic parameters, patients with a history of diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders, Cushing’s syndrome or adrenal disorders, those who were pregnant or breastfeeding, and patients taking medication that could affect lipid metabolism or insulin sensitivity were excluded from the study.

Convenience sampling was used. As this was a retrospective study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. All eligible normal-weight women who met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria and had complete clinical and laboratory data during the study period were included. After applying all inclusion and exclusion criteria, 86 women formed the PMOS group and 88 women formed the control group (Figure 1).

Data Collection

The participants’ demographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements and laboratory parameters were recorded using a structured data collection form prepared by the researchers. The variables collected included age, height, weight, fasting blood glucose (FBG), fasting insulin (FI), TG and HDL-C levels. These laboratory measurements were obtained from routine biochemical analyses performed following a fast of at least 8 hours. Biochemical parameters were measured in serum obtained by centrifugation of blood samples collected in gel-sealed biochemical tubes, using an enzymatic colorimetric method on an automated biochemical analyzer (Cobas 6000, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). All analyses were performed in accordance with daily quality control procedures.

Based on the recorded laboratory parameters, metabolic indices were calculated using standard formulas reported in the literature to assess the metabolic risk12–14 (Figure 2).

Abbreviations: HOMA-IR, Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance; FI, Fasting Insulin; FBG, Fasting Blood Glucose; TyG, Triglyceride-Glucose Index; TG, Triglyceride; AIP, Atherogenic Index of Plasma; HDL-C, High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol; METS-IR, Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance; BMI, Body Mass Index.

For the combined-index analyses, each metabolic index was classified as positive or negative according to its optimal cut-off value determined by the Youden index. Participants in whom all component indices exceeded their respective cut-off values were classified as combination-positive, whereas those in whom all component indices were below their respective cut-off values were classified as combination-negative. Participants with discordant index classifications were excluded from the diagnostic performance analysis of the corresponding combination. The same concordance-based approach was applied to the three-index combination.

All data were recorded anonymously, and personal identifiers have been removed from the dataset prior to statistical analysis to ensure patient confidentiality.

Data Analysis

The data were analyzed using the SPSS 23.0 statistical software package. Descriptive statistics including frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation, median and interquartile range were calculated. The suitability of the variables for a normal distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. In comparing demographic and clinical characteristics according to the presence of PMOS, the Pearson chi-square test was used for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables. In comparing the mean and median values of metabolic indices according to the presence of PMOS, the Student’s t-test and the Mann–Whitney U-test were utilized, depending on whether the distribution conformed to a normal distribution.

The Rotterdam criteria are accepted as the gold standard for assessing sensitivity and specificity. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted for the metabolic indices, and the Area Under the Curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, Positive Likelihood Ratio (LR+), and Negative Likelihood Ratio (LR−) were calculated to assess the ability of these indices to correctly distinguish individuals with PMOS. AUC values were classified as follows: 0.9–1.0 excellent, 0.8–0.89 good, 0.7–0.79 moderate, 0.6–0.69 poor, and values below 0.6 were considered inadequate.24 The optimal cut-off values of the metabolic indices for predicting PMOS were determined based on the Youden index, with the point yielding the highest Youden index value defined as the optimal cut-off point. Pairwise comparisons of the AUCs were performed using the DeLong test.

To assess the agreement of combined metabolic indices in predicting PMOS, Cohen’s Kappa (κ) coefficient was calculated, along with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The Shrout classification was used to interpret the Kappa (κ) values: 0–0.10 was considered almost no agreement; 0.11–0.40 slight agreement; 0.41–0.60 moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80 good agreement; and 0.81–1.00 excellent agreement.25 To evaluate whether the associations between metabolic indices and PMOS were independent of the observed age difference between the study groups, additional binary logistic regression analyses were performed for each metabolic index, adjusting for age. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. In all statistical tests, a significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted.

The Diagnostic Performance of Single Metabolic Indices

Table 3 presents the diagnostic performance of metabolic indices for predicting PMOS. Among the individual indices, the highest discriminatory power was observed for the TyG index (AUC = 0.775; 95% CI: 0.706–0.843; p < 0.001). The optimal cut-off point for the TyG index was 8.191, with a sensitivity of 60.5% and a specificity of 84.1% at this value. The diagnostic performance of the HOMA-IR and AIP indices was found to be similar and of moderate level (AUC 0.735 and 0.738, respectively; p<0.001). The cut-off point for HOMA-IR was 1.243 (sensitivity 76.7%, specificity 59.1%), and for AIP it was 0.036 (sensitivity 79.1%, specificity 58.0%). The discriminatory power of the METS-IR index was found to be lower than that of the other indices (AUC = 0.638; 95% CI: 0.556–0.721; p = 0.002). The optimal cut-off point for this index was 28.857, with a sensitivity of 69.8% and a specificity of 58.0% (Table 3 and Figure 3). Pairwise comparison of ROC curves using the DeLong test showed that the AUC of the TyG index was significantly greater than that of METS-IR (ΔAUC=0.136, p=0.006), whereas its differences from HOMA-IR (p=0.410) and AIP (p=0.109) were not statistically significant. AIP also showed a significantly greater AUC than METS-IR (ΔAUC=0.099, p=0.036). No significant differences were observed between HOMA-IR and AIP (p=0.956) or between HOMA-IR and METS-IR (p=0.065).

The Diagnostic Performance of Combined Metabolic Indices

Table 4 summarizes the diagnostic performance of combined metabolic indices in the prediction of PMOS. According to the results of the ROC analysis, the combination of the HOMA-IR and TyG indices was found to have the highest discriminatory power (AUC = 0.830; 95% CI: 0.747–0.913). This combination demonstrated high diagnostic performance with a sensitivity of 80.8% and a specificity of 85.2% (PPV 84.0%, NPV 82.1%). Furthermore, the positive likelihood ratio (LR+) was calculated as 5.452, the negative likelihood ratio (LR-) as 0.226, and the accuracy rate as 83.0%; the Cohen’s kappa value of 0.66 indicated good agreement.

Discussion

In this study, the diagnostic performance of various metabolic indices in predicting PMOS in normal-weight women were evaluated, and significant clinical evidence regarding the metabolic dimension of the syndrome was presented. Our findings demonstrate that the glucose-insulin axis and lipid metabolism are affected in women with PMOS, even in the absence of overt obesity. The finding that some combinations of indices reflecting different metabolic pathways yielded numerically higher discriminatory performance than individual indices supports the value of a comprehensive metabolic assessment. Our findings suggest that the use of accessible biochemical markers in addition to the Rotterdam criteria in normal-weight women may provide additional information for metabolic assessment in clinical practice; the results obtained are discussed below under subheadings in the context of underlying potential mechanisms and clinical effects. Traditionally, the diagnosis of PCOS has relied heavily on the Rotterdam criteria, which predominantly focus on reproductive and morphological features. However, as strongly emphasized by the 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline,1 the syndrome carries a profound metabolic burden. This paradigm shift has recently sparked debates in the literature proposing the renaming of the condition to PMOS.2 Our findings appear consistent with this emerging PMOS vision, suggesting that metabolic dysfunction may be an important component of the syndrome, even in the absence of an obvious obesity phenotype.

Masked Metabolic Burden in Women with PMOS of Normal Weight

Our findings indicate that PMOS also has metabolic effects on normal-weight women. In our study, fasting blood glucose (FBG), insulin (FI) and triglyceride (TG) levels were found to be significantly higher in the PMOS group compared to the control group. This finding suggests that PMOS may have significant effects on the glucose-insulin axis and lipid metabolism even in individuals of normal weight. In particular, the increase in insulin levels is a key indicator supporting insulin resistance, one of the fundamental pathophysiological components of PMOS.26 As the classic metabolic risk phenotype may not be overt in normal-weight women, early biochemical markers such as FI may provide a more sensitive reflection of underlying metabolic dysfunction. Likewise, the significantly elevated FBG levels observed in the PMOS group point to subclinical disturbances in glucose homeostasis.27 Although this increase does not reach diabetic levels in most cases, it is evident that glycemic control cannot be fully maintained even in cases of PMOS with normal body weight.28 This situation highlights the need for metabolic assessment to be considered not only in the presence of overt hyperglycemia, but also in relation to early-stage changes.29 Another notable finding of our study was the significant elevation in triglyceride levels. Elevated triglyceride concentrations are considered one of the earliest and most prevalent features of dyslipidemia in PMOS.30 This condition is closely associated with insulin resistance and can be regarded as an early indicator of an atherogenic metabolic profile. The fact that elevated triglyceride levels were detected even in the normal-weight sample in our study supports the view that the effect of PMOS on lipid metabolism is not limited to obese cases.31

Conversely, the fact that no statistically significant differences were observed for certain parameters should also be interpreted with caution. In our study, no significant difference was found between the two groups in terms of HDL-C levels. This result can be explained by the fact that, in a population of normal weight and relatively homogeneous composition, not all components of metabolic dysfunction manifest simultaneously or to the same degree.32,33 In other words, while the metabolic effects of PMOS become more apparent in the early stages, particularly in terms of glucose-insulin balance and triglyceride levels, other parameters may not yet be sufficiently distinct in this selected population. This suggests that the variables found to be non-significant are not clinically insignificant, but rather that metabolic effects may be less pronounced in the normal-weight PMOS phenotype.

Discriminatory Performance of the TyG Index

Among the individual indices, the TyG index showed the highest observed AUC; however, pairwise DeLong comparisons indicated that its discriminatory performance was not significantly different from that of HOMA-IR or AIP in normal-weight women with PMOS. The AUC value of 0.775 obtained for the TyG index indicates that this index possesses moderate but clinically meaningful discriminatory power in distinguishing the presence of PMOS. The higher specificity than sensitivity observed at the determined cut-off value further indicates that the TyG index may be especially valuable in confirming the metabolic phenotype associated with PMOS.

In the literature, the TyG index is gaining increasing importance as a practical indicator of insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction. It has been reported that TyG index levels are elevated in women with PMOS and that this index may serve as an early indicator of metabolic risk.18,34 The fact that the TyG index reflects both FBG and TG levels makes it more functional than measures based on a single metabolic component. The fact that metabolic dysfunction in PMOS often manifests as a combined impairment of glucose and lipid metabolism also explains why the TyG index is particularly relevant in this syndrome.35 While our findings are consistent with the literature, the results of our study take on added significance given that our sample consisted solely of normal-weight women. This is because our study demonstrated the discriminatory power of the TyG index in a population that has been as free as possible from the influence of obesity. This suggests that the TyG index could serve as a practical indicator capable of reflecting metabolic effects even in normal-weight PMOS cases.

HOMA-IR and AIP: Roles in Reflecting Metabolic Dysfunction

In our study, the AUC values for HOMA-IR and AIP in distinguishing PMOS in normal-weight women were found to be 0.735 and 0.738, respectively. These findings indicate that, while both indices possess moderate diagnostic power when evaluated individually for distinguishing PMOS, they offer clinically meaningful discriminatory power.

HOMA-IR is one of the most used indicators for assessing insulin resistance in PMOS and is recognized in the literature as a key parameter reflecting hyperinsulinaemia, one of the fundamental pathophysiological components of PMOS.36–38 Although metabolic dysfunction does not always present with obvious clinical symptoms, particularly in cases of PMOS in patients of normal weight, an increase in HOMA-IR—based on fasting insulin and glucose levels—can indicate underlying insulin resistance.39,40 Consistent with previous literature, HOMA-IR was also found to be significantly elevated in the PMOS group in our study. In this regard, HOMA-IR appears to be a useful index for identifying early metabolic alterations in normal-weight women with PMOS.

AIP is an important index reflecting atherogenic changes in lipid metabolism. In PMOS, dyslipidaemia is often associated with elevated triglyceride levels and changes in HDL-C levels.41 For this reason, the AIP, which assesses both TG and HDL-C components, can be considered a complementary marker reflecting the lipid aspect of metabolic risk in PMOS. In our study, AIP was found to be significantly higher in the PMOS group. Although no significant difference was detected in HDL-C levels between the two groups when considered in isolation, it is thought that the higher TG levels reveal smaller changes in lipid metabolism due to the composite nature of AIP.

The fact that both indices were found to be elevated in the PMOS group suggests that, even in individuals of normal weight, PMOS causes a metabolic impact that is not limited to the reproductive system alone but is associated with insulin resistance and atherogenic lipid changes.

The Limited Diagnostic Performance of METS-IR in Normal-Weight PMOS

In our study, although METS-IR was found to be statistically significantly higher in the group of patients with PMOS, its performance in distinguishing PMOS was more limited compared to other indices. The AUC value of 0.638 indicates that this index offers only weak discriminatory power in distinguishing the presence of PMOS in normal-weight women. In the literature, METS-IR is defined as a useful composite index associated with insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome.42 It has been reported that METS-IR is associated with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome and certain metabolic-hormonal disorders in women with PMOS. It has been reported that METS-IR yields successful results in predicting insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome in women with PMOS.15 However, as the relevant study was conducted in a group with high BMI values, it is difficult to directly generalize the findings to normal-weight PMOS patients. Furthermore, in groups where obesity is completely excluded, as in our study, the influence of the obesity component within the METS-IR structure diminishes; in the absence of obesity, the total metabolic burden that the index can reflect is limited, and consequently, its discriminatory power may decrease. This interpretation is consistent with studies indicating that metabolic dysfunction may be present in normal-weight PMOS patients, but that this is not reflected to the same extent by every index.20

Comprehensive Assessment: Discriminatory Performance of Composite Indices

In our study, when examining the individual indices found to be clinically significant in predicting the metabolic aspects of PMOS, it is evident that each reflects a different metabolic component of the syndrome. HOMA-IR reflects insulin resistance, the TyG index reflects glucose homeostasis and the metabolic burden associated with triglycerides, while AIP reflects the atherogenic lipid profile more distinctly.43–45 Consequently, the combined assessment of these complementary indices contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of the heterogeneous metabolic profile of PMOS. Indeed, our findings suggest that the combined assessment of clinically meaningful indices may provide additional discriminatory information beyond that obtained from individual indices, indicating that the metabolic impact of PMOS is not one-dimensional and that the combined use of different biomarkers can enhance diagnostic discriminatory power. The literature also emphasises that combinations of markers reflecting different components of metabolic dysfunction may provide stronger clinical differentiation compared to a single biomarker, thereby supporting our findings.46

The most striking finding of our study was that some combined metabolic index assessments showed numerically higher AUC values than individual indices. In particular, the HOMA-IR + TyG index combination yielded the highest observed AUC among the combined assessments, with an AUC of 0.830, 80.8% sensitivity, 85.2% specificity, 83.0% accuracy, and κ = 0.66. The higher observed discriminatory performance of the HOMA-IR + TyG combination may be clinically and biologically relevant, as the two indices reflect complementary aspects of metabolic dysfunction. While HOMA-IR reflects insulin resistance via FBG and FI, the TyG index combines FBG and TG levels to reflect both the glycaemic and lipidaemic aspects of metabolic dysfunction.22,47 When these two indices are considered together, the metabolic effects of PMOS which do not manifest phenotypically, particularly in cases of normal weight, but persist at the biochemical level become more clearly apparent.

However, other combinations of indices have also yielded noteworthy results. The HOMA-IR + AIP combination demonstrated strong performance in distinguishing PMOS (AUC = 0.804). This finding suggests that the combined assessment of insulin resistance and atherogenic lipid changes is also meaningful. While AIP alone reflects lipid-based risk, when used in conjunction with HOMA-IR, both insulin resistance and the tendency towards dyslipidaemia—the two key axes of metabolic dysfunction—are considered simultaneously. Consequently, the successful performance of the HOMA-IR + AIP combination supports the notion that PMOS constitutes a broader metabolic impairment model that is not limited solely to glucose metabolism.48

Similarly, the fact that the TyG + HOMA-IR + AIP combination also showed a relatively high observed AUC (AUC = 0.811) further supports the potential value of combined metabolic assessment. Although the triple combination is not the approach yielding the highest performance, it is important in reflecting the complex metabolic structure of PMOS because it encompasses multiple areas of metabolic dysfunction simultaneously. However, the fact that the strongest performance in our study was achieved with the HOMA-IR + TyG combination suggests that, in terms of discriminatory performance, it is not merely a matter of increasing the number of indices, but rather of selecting indices that cover different pathways of metabolic dysfunction and complement one another. In other words, the inclusion of additional parameters does not necessarily translate into improved diagnostic performance.49,50 The key determining factor is the extent to which the selected indices complementarily represent the various metabolic components of PMOS.

Clinical Implications

The key clinical implication of this study is that PMOS in normal-weight women should not be regarded as a low-metabolic-risk condition. Although the effect of obesity was excluded in our study, the finding that FBG, FI and TG levels were higher in the PMOS group indicates that metabolic impairment does not arise solely in the presence of excess weight. Therefore, in clinical assessment, metabolic risk should not be overlooked simply because the BMI is within the normal range.

Our findings also suggest that metabolic indices derived from routine biochemical parameters may provide a meaningful contribution to clinical assessment in patients with PMOS of normal weight. They indicate that more careful metabolic screening in this patient group using indices such as TyG, HOMA-IR and AIP which reflect different aspects of metabolic dysfunction may be beneficial.

Finally, the data obtained from our study suggest that these indices could be considered as complementary tools for metabolic assessment and risk stratification in women diagnosed with PMOS according to Rotterdam criteria. In particular, the combined use of these indices may provide additional value to clinical assessment in predicting the metabolic aspect of PMOS in normal-weight women and in identifying cases at higher metabolic risk. However, for this approach to translate into stronger recommendations for routine metabolic risk assessment, it requires support from larger, prospective studies conducted in different populations. Importantly, while sex hormone parameters (such as testosterone, SHBG, and LH/FSH ratios) are central to establishing the classical hyperandrogenic phenotype of PMOS, they may not fully reflect the metabolic dimension of the syndrome. In clinical scenarios where detailed hormonal measurements are unavailable, our results suggest that simple metabolic indices (particularly the combination of HOMA-IR and TyG) may offer complementary information regarding the underlying metabolic risk profile. By helping to outline the underlying metabolic risk profile—or the PMOS dimension—these indices may support metabolic risk assessment alongside established diagnostic criteria.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several strengths that contribute to the literature. By evaluating only normal-weight women, the study provided an opportunity to examine the metabolic effects of PMOS independently of obesity, thereby ruling out the dominant confounding effect of obesity. Furthermore, the use of indices derived from routine biochemical parameters—which are readily available in clinical practice—enhances the study’s clinical applicability. Analysing not only individual indices but also combinations of these indices has provided a more comprehensive assessment of their discriminatory performance. While the selection of the control group from the same center and a similar BMI range strengthens the comparability between groups, women with PMOS were significantly younger than controls. Given that metabolic abnormalities and related index values generally tend to increase with age, the finding that these indices were nevertheless higher in the PMOS group is noteworthy. Moreover, all four metabolic indices remained significantly associated with PMOS after adjustment for age, suggesting that the observed associations were not solely attributable to the between-group age difference.

However, the results of our study should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. While the retrospective and single-centre design of our study limits the establishment of a definitive causal relationship, it also cannot entirely eliminate the risk of selection bias. As the study relied on indirectly calculated indices rather than direct measurements of insulin resistance, the results reflect the relative diagnostic contribution of these metabolic indices and should not be considered directly equivalent to direct measurements of insulin sensitivity. Furthermore, our dataset does not include additional variables that could explain the metabolic heterogeneity of the syndrome, such as androgen levels, inflammatory markers, detailed body composition measurements, or different PMOS phenotypes. The inclusion of these variables could better clarify which subgroup of normal-weight PMOS carries a more pronounced metabolic risk.

In addition, the use of convenience sampling and the inclusion of only participants with complete clinical and laboratory records may have introduced selection bias. These factors, together with the single-center retrospective design, may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations and different clinical settings.

Future Directions

In future studies, it is important to validate these findings in larger, multicenter, prospective cohorts. Furthermore, multivariate models incorporating variables such as age, hormonal profile, androgen levels, waist circumference, body fat distribution and inflammatory biomarkers may provide a clearer picture of the independent contribution of metabolic indices. Moreover, considering the marked heterogeneity of the syndrome, different PMOS phenotypes (eg, the severe classic hyperandrogenic Type A versus the non-hyperandrogenic Type D) may inherently require different optimal cut-off values for these metabolic indices. Future prospective studies powered to conduct detailed subgroup analyses across the specific Rotterdam subtypes are essential to establish phenotype-specific diagnostic thresholds. In normal-weight women with PMOS, the predictive value of metabolic indices over time—for example, their ability to predict glucose intolerance, metabolic syndrome or fertility outcomes—should also be investigated, in addition to their diagnostic discriminatory power. Such studies may further elucidate the role of metabolic indices not only as cross-sectional diagnostic tools, but also as markers for risk stratification and monitoring over time.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that metabolic indices may provide useful discriminatory information in normal-weight women with PMOS. Among the indices examined, the TyG index showed the highest observed AUC, while combined assessment of complementary metabolic indices may provide additional information regarding the metabolic profile of PMOS. These findings suggest that metabolic abnormalities may be detectable even in women without an obesity phenotype and support further investigation of these readily available indices as tools for metabolic assessment and risk stratification. However, their clinical utility should be interpreted cautiously, and external validation in larger, multicenter, prospective studies is required before these approaches can be incorporated into routine clinical practice.
