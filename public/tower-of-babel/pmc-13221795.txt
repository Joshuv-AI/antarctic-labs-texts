Concurrent Patent Foramen Ovale and Pulmonary Arteriovenous Malformation Etiology
Authors: Abdullah Al Qaraghuli, Kalyan R. Chitturi, Beni Rai Verma, Jason Galo, Itsik Ben-Dor, Toby Rogers, Ron Waksman, Lowell F. Satler, Michael C. Slack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatent foramen ovale (PFO) and pulmonary arteriovenous malformations (PAVMs) are congenital vascular abnormalities that can lead to right-to-left shunting, potentially causing paradoxical embolism.Case SummaryWe describe a case of a 39-year-old woman who presented with syncope and left-sided facial numbness; her brain imaging confirmed a subacute lacunar infarct in the left thalamus. A transthoracic echocardiogram showed a PFO with moderate to severe right-to-left shunting. The patient underwent successful PFO closure. Postprocedurally, she developed transient hypotension. Computed tomography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was performed to exclude bleeding. Incidentally, a PAVM in the right middle lobe of the lung was identified, which was then successfully closed with embolization.DiscussionPFO is a well-established cause of paradoxical embolism, but rarer extracardiac shunts such as PAVMs can coexist and contribute to cryptogenic stroke.Take-Home MessageA comprehensive evaluation for multiple shunt sources is essential in younger patients with cryptogenic stroke.

History of Presentation

A 39-year-old woman presented with syncope, a metallic taste, and transient left-sided facial numbness lasting 6 hours. In the emergency department, vital signs were normal, and resting oxygen saturation ranged from 96% to 99% in room air. Physical examination revealed no focal neurologic deficits or cardiac murmurs. Laboratory results were unremarkable. Electrocardiography demonstrated normal sinus rhythm. Computed tomography (CT) of the head showed no acute stroke. CT angiography of the head and neck showed no intracranial arterial occlusion or stenosis. Past Medical History

The patient had a past medical history of prediabetes, hyperlipidemia, asthma, anxiety, and fatty liver. She was on medications such as atorvastatin, montelukast, and ethinyl estradiol/norethindrone.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses for cryptogenic stroke included paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, hypercoagulable states (due to oral contraceptives, antiphospholipid syndrome, malignancy-related thrombosis, and gene mutations), patent foramen ovale (PFO), pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (PAVM), and small-vessel vasculitis.

Investigations

Event monitoring showed no atrial fibrillation. Hypercoagulability work-up including venous Doppler studies of lower extremities were unremarkable. Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography revealed normal left ventricular ejection fraction and a PFO with moderate (grade II-III) right-to-left shunting on microbubble injections (Figure 1B, Video 1). The timing of bubble studies was equivocal, with one demonstrating early bubble appearance, and another indicating delayed. Right heart catheterization measurements (to exclude pulmonary hypertension) were normal: right atrial pressure = 1 mm Hg, right ventricular pressure = 30/10 mm Hg, pulmonary artery pressure = 25/10 mm Hg, mean pulmonary artery pressure = 20 mm Hg, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure = 6 mm Hg.

Management

The patient was started on daily oral aspirin and clopidogrel and underwent percutaneous PFO device closure guided by intracardiac echocardiography (ICE). Initial ICE showed mild to moderate hypermobility of the septum primum at the fossa ovalis without evidence of an atrial septal aneurysm or a significant resting shunt on color Doppler. A 7-F multipurpose end-hole catheter with a wire was unable to cross the PFO despite the use of multiple catheter shapes and both a Teflon J-wire and an angled Glidewire (Terumo Medical Corporation). Even with more aggressive than usual manipulation, the tunnel would not permit catheter entry into the left atrium. Using a Baylis VersaCross radiofrequency transseptal system (Baylis Medical) (Figure 2), the PFO was crossed at a site immediately adjacent to the right side of the fossa, and a 0.035-inch J-tipped Teflon wire was guided into the left upper pulmonary vein. An Amplatzer Talisman PFO Occluder (30/25 mm) (Abbott Structural Heart) was implanted via the 9-F Talisman delivery sheath into the PFO in a standard fashion (Figure 1C). The device position and orientation were evaluated through the wiggle test, 11-o'clock test, and ICE confirming optimal device placement. An hour after the procedure, she became nauseous and diaphoretic. Her blood pressure was 73/48 mm Hg, pulse was 48 beats/min, and oxygen saturation was 92%. Her symptoms and hemodynamics improved after a fluid bolus and a dose of atropine. Her hemoglobin level dropped from 13 to 10 g/dL. Bedside echocardiography showed a circumferential pericardial hematoma measuring 0.6 cm in diameter. To evaluate for alternative bleeding sources, CT imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis demonstrated a small anterior right femoral hematoma measuring 3.6 cm in diameter. Incidentally, an arteriovenous malformation in the right middle lobe of the lung was identified (Figure 3A). She was transferred to the cardiovascular intensive care unit for close monitoring with serial echocardiography. She had indicators of cardiac tamponade for 1 day, which subsequently resolved. Elective percutaneous PAVM closure was recommended after multidisciplinary review. During the second procedure, a National Institutes of Health catheter was used with a deflectable tip wire to enter the right pulmonary artery and left pulmonary artery for pulmonary angiography. A moderate-sized PAVM, measuring around 5 mm in diameter along its course (Supplemental Figure 1), originating from the right pulmonary artery middle lobe subsegmental branch draining into the right upper pulmonary vein was identified (Figure 3B, Video 2). Mild proximal right pulmonary artery dilation was noted (17 mm vs 15.2 mm on the left) (Supplemental Figure 2). A 6-mm Amplatzer Vascular Plug II (Abbott Vascular) was first deployed in the feeding pulmonary artery of the PAVM. A 5 mm × 11 cm AZUR CX coil (Terumo Interventional Systems) was then placed within the malformation, followed by a second 6-mm Amplatzer Vascular Plug II, creating a plug-coil-plug sandwich to ensure durable occlusion. Final subselective right pulmonary angiography confirmed complete closure of the PAVM (Figure 3C, Video 3). The catheter, wire, and sheath were removed, and hemostasis was achieved with a single Perclose ProGlide closure device (Abbott Vascular).

Outcome and Follow-Up

Post-PAVM closure, the patient has remained clinically well without recurrent stroke-like symptoms or other cardiac complaints. Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no evidence of residual right-to-left shunting. Repeat chest CT angiography performed several weeks later showed no residual flow through the treated PAVM, no contrast extravasation, and no evidence of pulmonary vein stenosis or segmental pulmonary infarction in lung regions previously supplied by the PAVM.

Discussion

PFO is a well-established cause of paradoxical embolism and is associated with 25% to 40% of cryptogenic strokes.1 This case highlights the need to consider rarer etiologies, particularly in younger patients.2 A Nationwide Inpatient Sample analysis identified PAVMs in 0.02% of patients hospitalized with acute ischemic stroke between 2010 and 2019, and an untreated PAVM carries an estimated 25% lifetime stroke risk.2 These low-resistance, high-flow conduits allow venous emboli to bypass pulmonary filtration and enter the systemic circulation.3 Although PAVMs are often associated with hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasias, many occur sporadically.4 In this case, the patient had no formal diagnosis of vascular dysplasia but did have an incidental cerebral telangiectasia, raising suspicion for an underlying vascular predisposition. Importantly, resting oxygen saturation was preserved, underscoring that clinically significant right-to-left shunting may be present despite normal baseline oxygenation. Although reduced or borderline resting oxygen saturation can be a clinical clue to the presence of PAVM, normal saturation does not exclude physiologically meaningful shunt or embolic risk.4

The patient's subacute thalamic infarct did not fully explain her transient neurologic symptoms or syncope. In the setting of a high-grade right-to-left shunt, a cardioembolic mechanism was favored because embolic events may produce heterogeneous or transient symptoms not always captured on neuroimaging.5 Stroke risk is strongly correlated with shunt magnitude.6 Current guidelines recommend PFO closure in patients aged 18 to 60 years with cryptogenic stroke attributed to paradoxical embolism, particularly in those with large shunts or atrial septal aneurysms. The Risk of Paradoxical Embolism (RoPE) score aids in estimating the likelihood of PFO-related stroke. A score of 7 or higher identifies patients most likely to benefit from closure. Our patient's RoPE score of 8 corresponded to an estimated 84% probability of PFO-related stroke, supporting the decision for closure.5,7

Contrast echocardiography can help differentiate intracardiac from intrapulmonary shunting based on bubble timing. PFO-related shunting typically produces early bubble appearance within 3 to 5 cardiac cycles, whereas PAVMs are associated with delayed appearance beyond 6 cycles. Conversely, PAVMs are associated with delayed bubble appearance, often beyond 6 cardiac cycles, due to the time required for blood to traverse the pulmonary malformation.8,9 In practice, interpretation may be challenging when both early and delayed bubble appearance are observed, as in our patient. Although echocardiography remains the first-line modality for the evaluation of cryptogenic stroke, PAVM is a rare etiology, and routine CT thorax screening is therefore not recommended. However, in patients who lack high-risk PFO features by PASCAL classification and demonstrate equivocal or delayed bubble timing, consideration of alternative embolic sources is warranted. In such cases, particularly when diagnostic uncertainty persists despite favorable RoPE scoring, selective cross-sectional imaging with CT is appropriate to evaluate for extracardiac shunting such as PAVM. Although earlier CT pulmonary angiography may have identified the PAVM in this case, the presence of a moderate to severe right-to-left shunt and a pathogenic PFO supported proceeding with closure at the time of intervention.

Selective PAVM embolization was performed using Amplatzer vascular plugs with adjunctive detachable coils. Plug-based strategies are generally favored due to shorter procedure times, lower radiation exposure, and reduced risk of nontarget embolization compared with coil-only techniques, which carry an approximately 1% to 2% risk of coil migration and/or persistent residual flow. Surgical resection is rarely required and reserved for complex or refractory cases.4,10 After closure, immediate postprocedural assessment focuses on confirming device position and excluding residual right-to-left shunting using transthoracic echocardiography and CT. Longer-term surveillance targets detection of residual or recurrent PAVM flow and procedure-related complications. Current guidelines recommend repeat chest CT at 6 to 12 months after embolization and every 3 years thereafter.10

Conclusions

Cryptogenic stroke may involve multiple sources of paradoxical embolism, including PFO and PAVM. A combined approach of closure and embolization is reasonable, though supporting data remain limited.
