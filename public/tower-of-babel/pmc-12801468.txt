The Velvet protein vea: a key positive regulator of growth and cordycepin biosynthesis in Cordyceps militaris
Authors: Wenbin Yu, Xiaoyu Zhou, Yufei Zhang, Lihua Yao, Yayi Tu, Bin He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cordyceps militaris (C. militaris) is a prized medicinal fungus and a rich source of bioactive compounds, notably cordycepin. While the cordycepin biosynthetic gene cluster has been identified, the global regulatory networks orchestrating its production remain elusive. Here, we identify the Velvet protein CmVeA as a master regulator that synchronizes fungal development and cordycepin biosynthesis in C. militaris. Overexpression of CmveA significantly enhanced mycelial growth, sporulation, and cordycepin production by 2.3-fold, whereas knockout strains exhibited severe growth defects and a 60% reduction in yield. This enhancement was underpinned by the direct upregulation of the core biosynthetic genes cns1-4 at the transcriptional level. Furthermore, we confirmed that CmVeA physically interacts with CmVelB and CmLaeA to form the conserved Velvet complex. Our findings elucidate a sophisticated regulatory mechanism where CmVeA coordinates cellular development to secondary metabolism, positioning it as a prime engineering target for the construction of high-yield C. militaris cell factories.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02897-5.

Introduction

Cordyceps militaris (C. militaris), a food-sourced fungus, is valued for its potential to enhance physical performance, improve respiratory function, and promote overall well-being. It is particularly recognized for cordycepin, a key bioactive compound [1]. Cordycepin has gained considerable research interest due to its ability to combat tumors, inhibit viral replication, and modulate the immune system, making it a candidate for pharmaceutical applications [2]. In addition to its therapeutic properties, C. militaris is widely used as a dietary and cosmetic supplement [3]. Fungal secondary metabolites, such as cordycepin, are often regulated by complex biosynthetic gene clusters, which play a role in survival functions like self-protection and defense against pathogens [4]. The growth and secondary metabolism of fungi, including C. militaris, are influenced by multiple regulatory genes. One such gene, CmLec3, has been linked to fruiting body development in C. militaris, although the protein was heterologously expressed in Escherichia coli (E. coli) BL21 host cells [5]. Recent genomic analyses of C. militaris have revealed several key regulatory factors. Through CHIP-seq (Chromatin Immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing) analysis, vepN was identified as a key gene that interacts with VeA, influencing fungal development and metabolic pathways. The deletion of vepN significantly reduced aflatoxin production in Aspergillus flavus (A. flavus), suggesting a similar regulatory role in other fungal species [6].

Recent advances in understanding the metabolic regulation of bioactive substances in C. militaris have identified several key regulatory factors [7]. Among global regulatory proteins, the Velvet complex proteins, particularly VeA, have emerged as crucial regulators of fungal secondary metabolism and development [8]. VeA and its associated proteins integrate environmental signals to coordinate fungal differentiation, sporulation, and secondary metabolite production [9]. The regulatory influence of VeA extends across diverse fungal species: in Penicillium citrinum, VeA and LaeA control ML-236B production through the pathway-specific regulator mlcR [10], while in Fusarium verticillioides, the VeA ortholog FvVE1 regulates multiple secondary metabolites including fumonisin and fusarin [11]. Furthermore, VeA may interact with other global regulators such as AreA to influence secondary metabolism and pathogenicity [12]. While VeA has been extensively characterized in other fungal species, its specific role in C. militaris secondary metabolism remains poorly understood. Given the commercial importance of cordycepin and the established role of VeA as a global regulator of secondary metabolism in fungi, elucidating the precise functions and mechanisms of VeA in C. militaris is critical. This study aims to investigate the role of VeA in fungal development and cordycepin production, thereby providing insights for optimizing cordycepin yield through genetic and metabolic engineering..

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis of VeA

The gene sequence of veA (XP_006669742) from C. militaris CM01 was retrieved from the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ National Center for Biotechnology Information). Gene structure analysis was performed using Softberry (https://www.softberry.com/) with default settings. Homologous protein sequence alignment was conducted using BLAST to ensure statistically significant results. The 13 sequences with the highest similarity (i.e., the highest Total Score) were selected for local analysis and downloaded for further bioinformatics evaluation. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MEGA6 with the ClustalW algorithm, optimizing the alignment by setting gap penalties to 10 for opening and 0.2 for extension [13].

The phylogenetic tree was constructed based on neighbor-joining and Jones-Taylor-Thornton (JTT) models to reveal the evolutionary relationships of VeA proteins. Species were selected based on their high ranking in the NCBI database and their close sequence similarity to the VeA protein, with fourteen species included in the phylogenetic analysis: C. militaris, Beauveria brongniartii, Beauveria bassiana, Akanthomyces lecanii, Akanthomyces muscarius, Cordyceps javanica, Aphanocladium album, Tolypocladium paradoxum, Ophiocordyceps sinensis, Ilyonectria robusta, Neonectria ditissima, and Fusarium species. The conserved domain within the veA gene was identified using SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) with default parameters and was visualized with Domain Graph 2.0 [14]. The theoretical isoelectric point of the protein sequence was calculated using “IPC protein” for pKa values through NovoPro (https://www.novopro.cn/), and its transmembrane domains were predicted and visualized with IBS (http://ibs.biocuckoo.org/). The molecular weight of the CmVeA protein was predicted using EMBOSS (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/), while its subcellular localization was forecasted with WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/).

Fungal strains and growth conditions

The wild-type strain CM01 of C. militaris, maintained as a laboratory stock, serves as a standard reference in comparative studies. This monokaryotic strain, with MAT-1 mating type, is a key model system for molecular biological research. A mutant strain lacking the CmpyrG gene is denoted as CmΔpyrG. For spore collection, KA agar medium was used, consisting of 2% glucose, 0.1% yeast extract, 0.1% potassium dihydrogen phosphate, 0.05% potassium chloride, 0.05% magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, and 1.8% agar. To determine conidial count, spores were washed off the agar surface with 10 mL of sterile deionized water containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween-80 by gentle scraping with a sterile loop. The resulting spore suspension was filtered through three layers of sterile cheesecloth to remove mycelial debris. The spores were then enumerated using a hemocytometer under a light microscope. For fungal growth, the DPY agar medium was prepared with 2% glucose, 1% peptone, 0.5% yeast extract, 0.5% potassium dihydrogen phosphate, and 0.05% magnesium sulfate heptahydrate [15]. The medium was sterilized by autoclaving at 115 °C for 15 min. C. militaris was pre-cultured on DPY agar plates at 22 °C for 7 days. Biomass was measured by collecting mycelia from agar plates, followed by freeze-drying to constant weight at -40 °C. For bacterial culture, Escherichia coli was grown on LB agar medium, which contains 1% sodium chloride, 1% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, and 1.8% agar. E. coli DH5α was used for bacterial transformations and plasmid amplification.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription of C. militaris

The mortar is first sterilized with alcohol and stored at -20 °C until needed. The following day, approximately 100 mg of fresh C. militaris mycelium is ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen. This powder is then mixed with 1 mL of RNA-solv reagent and vortexed to ensure proper mixing. Chloroform is added to aid in phase separation, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C for 15 min at 12,000 rpm to separate the aqueous phase from the organic phase. The HiBind RNA spin column is used for RNA purification, with samples transferred and centrifuged to remove the filtrate. RNA Wash Buffer I and II are used sequentially to wash the column, and the process is repeated to ensure purity. The column is then dried and eluted with DEPC water to recover the RNA. For reverse transcription, genomic DNA is first removed using gDNA Eraser treatment. A reaction mixture is prepared on ice, and the reverse transcription reaction is carried out using PrimeScript RT Enzyme Mix and RT Prime MIX. The reaction conditions are optimized to yield high-quality cDNA from the RNA template, which is then stored at -80 °C for future use.

CmVeA knockout and overexpression

The gene sequence for CmveA was retrieved from the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), specifically targeting the C. militaris strain CM01 (XP_006669742). The KO-CmveA (knockout CmveA) plasmid was constructed using the pEX1 vector, which contains the pyrG gene, driven by its native promoter and terminator as a selectable marker. To construct the knockout plasmid, primers pEX1-5’VeA and pEX1-3’VeA were used to amplify approximately 1,500 bp of the upstream and downstream homologous arms of the CmveA gene. These fragments were cloned into the pEX1 vector, flanking the pyrG marker via homologous recombination. The primer pEX1-5’VeA contains an EcoRI restriction site, while pEX1-3’VeA contains SpeI and HindIII restriction sites. For the construction of the OE-CmveA (overexpression of CmveA), pEX1-VeA primers were designed with the cleavage site XhoI and SnaBI. A complete list of primers used is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The binary vector pEX1, marked with pyrG, was used to delete or overexpress the veA gene in the nutritionally deficient CmΔpyrG strain of C. militaris via homologous recombination. PCR was conducted using the C. militaris CM01 genome cDNA as a template, along with the premix Ex Taq enzyme and designed primers. After amplification, the presence of the expected DNA fragment was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis, which verified that the amplified product was of the correct size. The PCR products were then purified and cloned into the pEX1 vector using homologous recombination methods. The knockout plasmid pEX1-KOCmveA was constructed by inserting the amplified fragments upstream and downstream. All the plasmid constructions were generated with a CloneExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (C112-00-AB, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). For transformation, Agrobacterium tumefaciens (A. tumefaciens) AGL-1 was pre-induced with MES and AS to enhance plasmids absorption. Following transformation, DNA was extracted from the transformed strains, and PCR was performed to verify the presence of the plasmid in the transformant.

RT-qPCR analysis of C. militaris strains

The transcript levels of veA and the cordycepin biosynthetic genes cns1–4 in the OE-CmveA strain were quantified by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) using the gene-specific primers listed in Table S1. Spores from various strains were evenly spread on DPY agar medium. Samples from wild type (WT), and OE-CmveA strains of C. militaris were collected to ensure purity and representativeness. Total RNA was extracted from these samples, and cDNA was synthesized using reverse transcriptase and appropriate primers. The quality of the synthesized cDNA was assessed by PCR to confirm its suitability for subsequent RT-qPCR experiments [16]. The cDNA products were either used immediately for PCR reactions or stored at -80 °C. The qRT-PCR was conducted using the CFX Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) with the Bio-Rad CFX Connect Optics Module software. The gene tef1 was used as the endogenous control [17].

Functional characterization and growth assessment of CmVeA

The phenotypic analysis of C. militaris strains (OE-CmveA, KO-CmveA) was conducted to observe growth differences, with the WT strain used as a control. Spores from both the control and experimental groups were adjusted to a concentration of 0.5 × 102 conidia/mL as determined by measuring the OD600 on a 96-well plate. The strains were then spotted onto CD agar plates and cultured at 22 °C for five days to observe colony morphology. The growth diameter was measured using the cross-multiplying method for comparative analysis. To determine conidial count, spores were washed with deionized water and counted using a hemocytometer under a microscope. For biomass assessment, spore suspensions were spread on sterile glass paper over CD agar plates, incubated at 22 °C for ten days, and exposed to light at 1000 lx for three days. The mycelia were then carefully removed, freeze-dried, and weighed to quantify biomass production. This comprehensive assessment provided insights into the growth characteristics and biomass production of the genetically modified strains relative to the WT.

Determination of cordycepin content in C. militaris strains by HPLC

A standard curve was established using cordycepin standard solutions diluted in methanol across concentrations from 38.125 µg/mL to 610 µg/mL. The solutions were analyzed by HPLC, and the peak areas were plotted against the cordycepin concentrations to generate a linear regression equation [18], Y = 0.00003X-6.2744 (R²=0.9997). Spores from both the control and experimental strains were inoculated onto DPY medium and incubated at 22 °C for 10 days in the dark, followed by exposure to light. The harvested mycelium was freeze-dried and ground into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen. The powdered samples (0.2 g) were then extracted with 4 mL of methanol, vortexed, and sonicated. Afterward, the samples were centrifuged, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane for subsequent analysis. The HPLC system was equipped with a C18 column, maintained at 35 °C, and a UV detector set at 260 nm [19]. The mobile phase consisted of 12% methanol and 88% water. After system equilibration and baseline stabilization, the cordycepin content in the samples was quantified by comparing their peak areas with the standard curve, providing an accurate measurement of cordycepin levels in the different strains of C. militaris.

Proteomic analysis of overexpressed and knockout strains of C. militaris

A comprehensive proteomic study was performed on the OE-CmveA, KO-CmveA and the WT. These strains were cultured on DPY medium at 22 °C for seven days, followed by three days of light exposure. The mycelium was harvested, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored in a dry ice container before being sent to Jingjie Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for proteomic analysis. The analysis began by constructing a specific protein database based on the sample origins. This database was used to search against raw data obtained from mass spectrometry. Following the database search, quality control analysis was performed at both the peptide and protein levels to ensure data reliability [20]. Quantitative analysis of proteins was performed to assess their distribution, repeatability, and the quantitative intensity distribution across samples. Identified proteins were annotated for their functional roles. Based on the quantification results, fold changes (FC) and significance values (P-values) were calculated using a T-test between sample groups. Differentially expressed proteins were identified according to preset thresholds. Differential proteins were categorized by their function, using secondary Gene Ontology (GO) classifications and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis [21]. Fisher’s exact test was used for enrichment analysis of differential proteins in functional categories such as GO, KEGG, protein domains, Reactome, and WikiPathways. When multiple experimental groups were involved, functional clustering analysis was performed to compare functional associations under different experimental conditions.

Protein interactions

The study aimed to predict and experimentally validate the interactions of CmVeA, CmVelB and CmLaeA. First, the interactions were predicted using the STRING database, focusing on physical interactions with a low-confidence score threshold of 0.150. The predicted interaction network was then visualized interactively and exported in PNG format for presentation [22]. To validate the predicted interactions, yeast two-hybrid systems were employed, following the methods described by Fields and Song (1989) [23]. Specific primers were designed for PCR amplification of CmlaeA, CmvelB and CmveA genes. These genes were cloned into the pGBKT7 and pGADT7 vectors using NdeI and EcoRI or NdeI and XhoI restriction sites, respectively (BK-CmLaeA, BK-CmVeA, AD-CmVeA, AD-CmVelB). The positive clones were identified through colony PCR, and sequencing was performed to confirm the presence of the correct inserts. Yeast strains were transformed with combinations of the pGBKT7 and pGADT7 plasmids carrying the target genes (BK-CmVeA with AD-CmLaeA, BK-CmLaeA with AD-CmVeA, BK-CmLaeA with AD-CmVelB). Transformants were selected on synthetic dropout media lacking tryptophan, adenine, and histidine to assess protein-protein interactions. Successful interaction between the proteins of interest was indicated by growth on selective media, confirming their ability to form a complex [24].

Data analysis

Data are reported as the mean ± standard error (SE) from three independent replicates. The statistical significance of differences among multiple groups was determined by a one/two-way nested ANOVA, with subsequent pairwise comparisons performed using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test. Differences between two groups were evaluated with a one-tailed Student’s t-test. In all cases, a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical computations were performed using SAS 9.20 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Bioinformatics analysis of CmVeA

Gene structure analysis using SoftBerry revealed that CmveA comprises six exons and five introns (Fig. 1A). The encoded protein, CmVeA (NCBI accession: XP_006669742), was found to contain a conserved Velvet domain (amino acids 365–556) (Fig. 1B). A notable transmembrane region was predicted between amino acids 300 and 400 (Fig. 1C), indicating potential roles in subcellular localization and signal transduction.

Phylogenetic analysis placed CmVeA within a clade containing homologs from related species such as Aphanocladium album and Tolypocladium paradoxum, indicating evolutionary conservation of its function across fungi, particularly in secondary metabolism and development (Fig. 1D). Basic protein characterization predicted a molecular weight of 93.93 kDa and an isoelectric point (pI) of 9.16 (Table 1). Interestingly, subcellular localization predictions indicate potential distribution across multiple cellular compartments with the highest confidence in extracellular localization (credibility score of 12), suggesting a secretory pathway to the extracellular space. This aligns with the presence of a transmembrane region, implying roles in signal transduction or intracellular communication.

CmVeA promotes growth, development and the production of cordycepin

To investigate the function of CmVeA, we successfully constructed OE-CmveA and KO-CmveA strains via homologous recombination and Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. PCR verification and sequencing confirmed that the target gene sequence in the overexpression plasmid CmveA-pEX1 matched 100% with the veA, and that the pyrG marker gene in the knockout vector had correctly replaced the VeA locus. These successful manipulations of the veA underscore the utility of genetic engineering approaches to investigate gene function in C. militaris (Figure S1 and S2). Phenotypic characterization revealed that CmveA overexpression enhanced the growth and reproductive capacity of C. militaris. For example, OE-CmveA strains formed colonies with larger diameters, produced a 3.2-fold higher spore density, and yielded a greater biomass (dry weight) compared to the wild-type (WT) strain. Conversely, the KO-CmveA strains exhibited impaired growth, with approximately half the spore density and a significantly reduced biomass (Fig. 2A-D). Cordycepin content assays revealed that OE-CmveA4 strains had approximately 2.3 times more cordycepin than WT, while KO-CmveA strains had about 2.5 times less. However, the knockout of CmveA did not completely abolish cordycepin synthesis in C. militaris strain CM01, suggesting that while CmVeA plays a crucial regulatory role, other factors may also contribute to cordycepin biosynthesis.

Critically, a strong positive correlation was observed between CmveA expression levels and cordycepin production. As shown in Fig. 2E, OE-CmveA strains accumulated approximately 2.3 times more cordycepin than the WT, whereas KO-CmveA strains produced about 2.5 times less. This dose-dependent effect was underpinned by corresponding changes in the expression of key biosynthetic genes: RT-qPCR analysis revealed that in OE-CmveA strains, cns1 and cns2 were upregulated approximately eightfold relative to WT, while cns3 and cns4 were elevated about fourfold and threefold, respectively (Fig. 2F). Given that cns1–4 encode the core enzymes for cordycepin biosynthesis [25], their coordinated upregulation is a critical determinant of yield. Conversely, cns2 expression in KO-CmveA strains was reduced to 0.6-fold of WT (Figure S3). Variations in cordycepin content among different OE clones are likely attributable to position effects from random T-DNA integration. The retention of basal cordycepin production in KO-CmveA strains confirms that CmVeA is a key positive regulator, but not the sole essential factor, in the pathway.

Proteomic profiling reveals global regulatory role of CmVeA

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms of CmVeA, we conducted a comparative proteomics analysis of OE-CmveA4, KO-CmveA600 and WT strains. The differential protein screening revealed a pronounced imbalance: the KO-CmveA600 vs WT comparison had the highest number of downregulated proteins (1,707), whereas the OE-CmveA4 vs WT comparison showed a substantial number of upregulated proteins (Fig. 3A). A Venn diagram illustrated significant overlap between the two comparisons, alongside unique subsets of proteins specifically regulated in each condition (Fig. 3B). Volcano plot analysis identified key individual proteins with drastically altered expression (Figure S4). In the OE-CmveA4 vs WT comparison, significantly upregulated proteins included an FG-GAP repeat-containing protein (XP_006665620.1) and actin (XP_006670578.1). Conversely, the KO-CmveA600 vs WT comparison featured strong downregulation of orotidine 5’-phosphate decarboxylase (XP_006670464.1) and a putative O-methyltransferase (XP_006671676.1). Notably, CmLaeA (XP_006670602.1) was among the top five upregulated proteins in the OE-CmveA4 strain, suggesting a synergistic role with CmVeA.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis demonstrated that the OE-CmveA600 and KO-CmveA4 strains triggered extensive reprogramming of cellular activities. In the OE-CmveA4 strain, it was characterized by the significant enrichment of 1,077 differentially expressed proteins in cellular metabolic processes and 501 proteins in the regulation of biological processes. These proteins were predominantly localized to organelles (1,237 proteins) and the cytoplasm, and were primarily associated with catalytic activity (547 proteins) and organic cyclic compound binding (405 proteins) (Figs. 3C). Similarly, in the KO-CmveA600 strain, GO analysis revealed a comparable yet more extensive disruption of these core cellular functions, with 1,142 proteins enriched in cellular metabolic processes and 1,379 proteins localized to organelles, further underscoring the profound global impact of CmVeA on the fungal proteome (Fig. 3D). KEGG pathway enrichment further demonstrated that CmVeA orchestrates a fundamental metabolic trade-off (Fig. 3E). OE-CmveA4 strains upregulated pathways related to nucleocytoplasmic transport, DNA replication, and cell cycle, enhancing genomic integrity, but downregulated glycolysis and proteasome activity. In stark contrast, KO-CmveA600 strains activated compensatory catabolic pathways (e.g., amino acid degradation, glycan breakdown) but exhibited severe suppression of ribosome biogenesis, aligning with their growth defects. This systemic analysis positions CmVeA as a master regulator that modulates the balance between primary metabolism, protein synthesis, and secondary metabolite production.

The regulatory mechanisms of CmVeA

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which CmVeA regulates cordycepin biosynthesis, we conducted a detailed analysis of the proteomic data, focusing on key metabolic pathways. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that CmVeA orchestrates a complex reprogramming of central metabolism (Fig. 4). Our pathway analysis revealed distinct regulatory patterns: in carbohydrate metabolism, several glycolytic/gluconeogenic enzymes (e.g., EC 2.7.2.3) were downregulated, potentially redirecting carbon flux away from glycolysis. Concurrently, key enzymes in the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) (e.g., EC 5.4.2.2) were downregulated. These coordinated changes in central carbon metabolism suggest a complex rebalancing of metabolic fluxes under CmVeA regulation. In amino acid metabolism, we observed coordinated upregulation of enzymes involved in glutamate metabolism (EC 1.4.1.4) and histidine metabolism (EC 2.7.6.1), facilitating nitrogen provision and PRPP synthesis for purine nucleotide formation. The regulatory network extended to energy metabolism and transport systems, with upregulation of ABC transporters (EC 7.6.2.4), ensuring adequate energy supply and efficient metabolite transport. In contrast, KO-CmveA strains exhibited predominantly opposite regulation patterns across these pathways. This comprehensive metabolic rewiring demonstrates that CmVeA functions as a global regulator that coordinately enhances the supply of carbon skeletons, nitrogen sources, energy, and transport capacity, collectively creating a favorable metabolic environment for efficient cordycepin biosynthesis.

CmVeA interacts with both CmVelB and CmLaeA

Protein interaction predictions for CmVeA indicated potential partnerships with several proteins, including CmLaeA (CCM_05395) and CmVelB (CCM_06675) (Fig. 5A; detailed information is provided in Table S2). This suggested the formation of a core regulatory complex similar to the conserved Velvet complex characterized in fungi such as Aspergillus nidulans (A. nidulans) [26]. Furthermore, to provide structural insights into these interactions, we performed molecular docking. The predicted models demonstrate that CmVeA can form stable complexes with both CmLaeA and CmVelB. In both complexes, the proteins adopt intertwined, elongated structures and associate through large, complementary binding interfaces (Figure S5). To experimentally validate these interactions, we performed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays. Control experiments confirmed that none of the proteins alone (e.g., AD-CmVeA co-transformed with an empty BD plasmid) activated the reporter genes, ensuring the specificity of the assay (Fig. 5B). The Y2H results demonstrated a direct interaction between CmVeA and CmLaeA, as well as between CmVeA and CmVelB, evidenced by robust growth on selective medium (SD/-Leu/-Trp/-His/-Ade) (Fig. 5C and D). In contrast, no interaction was detected between CmLaeA and CmVelB. This pairwise interaction pattern, where VeA bridges LaeA and VelB, confirms the existence of a heterotrimeric Velvet complex in C. militaris, mirroring its architecture in other fungi.

Discussion

The global regulator VeA is widely recognized as a master coordinator of fungal development and secondary metabolism across diverse fungal species [27]. In C. militaris, a prized medicinal fungus renowned for its production of the bioactive compound cordycepin, understanding the molecular mechanisms governing growth and metabolite synthesis is of paramount importance for industrial applications. While previous studies have highlighted the potential of nutritional supplementation, such as L-alanine [25], to enhance cordycepin yield, the specific regulatory networks involved remain incompletely characterized. Our previous transcriptome data suggested that the transcription factor CmVeA might be a key player in this process, prompting a comprehensive functional investigation. In this study, we systematically dissected the role of CmVeA through an integrated approach combining gene editing, phenotypic analysis, proteomics, and protein-protein interaction studies. Our results provide compelling evidence that CmVeA acts as a central positive regulator, enhancing both fungal growth and cordycepin biosynthesis in C. militaris. This regulatory function is mechanistically executed through its participation in a conserved Velvet complex and, more significantly, by orchestrating a global reprogramming of central metabolic pathways to efficiently redirect carbon and nitrogen flux towards precursor supply for cordycepin synthesis.

Phenotypic analyses offered the first layer of evidence, unequivocally demonstrating that overexpression of CmVeA promoted mycelial growth, sporulation, and biomass yield, whereas gene knockout led to significant impairments in these traits (Fig. 2A-D). The observed growth-promoting role is consistent with findings in certain fungi like A. flavus, where VeA also positively influences conidiation [28]. However, this stands in contrast to its role as a repressor of conidiation in A. nidulans [29], highlighting the context-dependent functional divergence of the Velvet complex across different fungal lineages. This species-specific functionality underscores the importance of empirically characterizing VeA homologs in each organism of interest. More crucially from a biotechnological perspective, we established a strong positive correlation between CmVeA expression levels and cordycepin production (Fig. 2E). The approximately 2.3-fold increase in cordycepin content in OE-CmveA strains, coupled with the coordinated transcriptional upregulation of the core biosynthetic genes cns1-4 (Fig. 2F), provides direct evidence that CmVeA acts upstream to activate the cordycepin biosynthetic gene cluster. It is noteworthy that the knockout of CmVeA did not completely abolish cordycepin production, suggesting that while CmVeA is a major positive regulator, it is not the sole essential factor. This indicates the existence of potential regulatory redundancy or alternative pathways that maintain a baseline level of synthesis, a phenomenon not uncommon in complex metabolic networks [30].

To unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying these phenotypic changes, we conducted a comparative proteomic analysis. The data robustly position CmVeA as a master metabolic regulator, responsible for a fundamental reprogramming of the cell’s metabolic landscape. Our proteomic data revealed a coordinated downregulation in both glycolysis (e.g., EC 2.7.2.3) and the PPP (e.g., EC 5.4.2.2) in OE-CmveA strains (Fig. 4). This seemingly paradoxical regulation suggests a sophisticated rebalancing of central carbon metabolism rather than a simple linear activation [31]. The crucial branch-point enzyme ribose-phosphate pyrophosphokinase (EC 2.7.6.1) was significantly upregulated, creating a targeted pull mechanism that efficiently channels available ribose-5-phosphate toward PRPP synthesis [32]. This strategic regulation prioritizes the production of PRPP—a vital precursor for purine nucleotide synthesis—while simultaneously reducing carbon flux through other carbohydrate metabolic routes. This metabolic rewiring represents an elegant efficiency strategy: by downregulating parallel pathways, the cell potentially concentrates resources toward the most critical nodes in cordycepin precursor synthesis. The enhanced PRPP production, coupled with upregulated glutamate metabolism (EC 1.4.1.4) and histidine metabolism (EC 2.7.6.1), ensures adequate provision of both carbon and nitrogen skeletons for adenosine biosynthesis. This coordinated enhancement of specific precursor pathways, despite broader pathway suppression, constitutes a core mechanism underpinning the elevated cordycepin yield [33].

Beyond restructuring central carbon metabolism, our proteomic data revealed a significant upregulation of the ABC transporter pathway in OE-CmveA strains (Fig. 4). This finding offers a complementary mechanism for increased cordycepin production. Enhanced ABC transporter function likely facilitates the active efflux of cordycepin and potentially its intermediates from the cell. This efflux could serve dual purposes: firstly, it may alleviate potential end-product inhibition of the biosynthetic enzymes, allowing for continuous synthesis [34]; it may function as a detoxification mechanism, reducing the intracellular concentration of a bioactive compound that could be detrimental to the fungus itself at high levels [35]. Moreover, the upregulation of ABC transporters could be interpreted as a component of a coordinated stress response orchestrated by CmVeA. The synthesis of high levels of a secondary metabolite like cordycepin represents a significant metabolic burden and potentially induces cellular stress. The simultaneous enhancement of efflux capacity may be a proactive adaptation to mitigate this self-imposed stress, ensuring cellular homeostasis during metabolic rewiring [36]. This integrated view suggests that CmVeA coordinately regulates both precursor supply and product removal pathways, creating a synergistic effect that maximizes cordycepin yield and secretion.

The question of how CmVeA coordinates these widespread changes leads to the level of transcriptional regulation. Our yeast two-hybrid assays confirmed the formation of a canonical Velvet complex in C. militaris, with CmVeA physically interacting with both CmVelB and CmLaeA, while no direct interaction was detected between CmVelB and CmLaeA (Fig. 5). This interaction pattern is consistent with the well-characterized architecture of the Velvet complex in Aspergillus species, where VeA acts as a central scaffold [26, 29, 37, 38]. It is well-established that this complex, particularly under certain environmental cues like darkness, translocate to the nucleus. There, the LaeA component, which possesses histone methyltransferase activity, is believed to remodel chromatin into a transcriptionally permissive state, thereby activating the expression of secondary metabolite gene clusters [39]. The significant upregulation of pathways related to nucleocytoplasmic transport and DNA replication in our OE-CmveA proteomic data (Fig. 3E) provides supporting evidence for enhanced nuclear activity, which is congruent with the role of VeA as a nuclear transcriptional coordinator. Therefore, we propose that the CmVeA-VelB-LaeA complex directly or indirectly activates the transcription of the cns genes, orchestrating the coordinated response observed.

Finally, the findings from this study mechanistically explain our previous observations where L-alanine supplementation was shown to boost cordycepin yield [25]. The upregulation of CmVeA transcript levels in response to L-alanine likely initiates the precise regulatory and metabolic cascade described here. This establishes a clear link between nutrient sensing, VeA-mediated global regulation, and secondary metabolism in C. militaris, providing a more complete picture of how environmental and genetic factors converge to modulate cordycepin production. In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive functional characterization of CmVeA in C. militaris. We demonstrate that CmVeA is a pivotal global regulator that promotes fungal growth and cordycepin biosynthesis through a dual mechanism: by participating in a conserved Velvet complex to modulate gene expression, and by extensively reprogramming central carbon metabolism.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive functional characterization of the global regulator CmVeA in Cordyceps militaris, revealing its dual role in coordinating fungal development and secondary metabolism. Genetic manipulation demonstrate that CmVeA serves as a positive regulator of both mycelial growth and cordycepin biosynthesis. Overexpression of CmveA resulted in a 2.3-fold increase in cordycepin yield along with enhanced sporulation and biomass accumulation, while knockout strains exhibited severe growth defects and a 60% reduction in metabolite production. Mechanistically, this study reveals that CmVeA exerts its regulatory control through a multifaceted network that includes physically assembling the conserved Velvet complex with CmVelB and CmLaeA and activating the expression of the core biosynthetic genes cns1-4. Furthermore, proteomic analysis uncovered a strategic metabolic reprogramming orchestrated by CmVeA, which optimizes carbon flux and upregulates key anabolic enzymes such as PRPP synthetase to channel essential precursors toward cordycepin biosynthesis. These findings not only advance the understanding of the complex regulatory networks linking cellular growth to secondary metabolism but also identify CmVeA as a pivotal target for metabolic engineering aimed at constructing high-efficiency microbial cell factories.
