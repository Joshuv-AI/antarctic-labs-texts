SVLA: A Unified Speech-Vision-Language Assistant with Multimodal Reasoning and Speech Generation
Ngoc Dung Huynh, Mohamed Reda Bouadjenek, Imran Razzak, Hakim Hacid, Sunil Aryal

Abstract
Large Vision-Language Models have shown impressive capabilities in tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and cross-modal retrieval. However, there are still significant challenges that need to be addressed in order to fully unlock the potential of these models. First, integrating speech, text, and vision into a unified model is particularly difficult for tasks like Spoken Image Captioning and Spoken Visual Question Answering, where the interaction between these modalities introduces additional complexity. Second, existing speech generation approaches differ—some generate speech directly, while others use an intermediate text step—but their impact on fluency, coherence, and accuracy remains unexplored. Third, current speech evaluation relies heavily on Automatic Speech Recognition, which introduces biases and fails to directly assess speech quality. To address these challenges, we propose SVLA , a unified S peech- V ision- L anguage A ssistant based on a decoder-only transformer architecture that seamlessly integrates multimodal inputs and outputs. We enhance model performance with a large-scale speech-text-image dataset containing 38.2 million examples and 64.1 hours of TTS-generated speech. Additionally, we introduce Speech-VQA Accuracy, a novel acoustic evaluation metric for assessing speech-based VQA tasks. Our approach advances multimodal understanding and generation, facilitating more effective integration of speech, text, and vision ( http://github.com/vlm-svla/svla ).

First, these models lack robust reasoning abilities for tasks like image captioning (IC) and visual question answering (VQA), where inputs may be text or speech and outputs may be speech or text, limiting their applicability to complex multimodal interactions.
Second, existing speech generation approaches vary, with some models generating speech directly, while others use text instructions as an intermediary step.

In contrast, direct speech generation bypasses the textual constraint, producing more fluid spoken responses but at the risk of greater variability, pronunciation inconsistencies, or a loss of linguistic structure. Understanding these trade-offs is essential for advancing multimodal reasoning, spoken language generation, and user interaction quality in AI systems.
Finally, existing evaluation metrics for multimodal learning focus mainly on text and vision, offering limited insight into speech-based tasks.

Related Works

Below, we explore key advancements in integrating speech into multimodal LLMs, compare direct and instruction-based speech generation approaches, and examine evaluation metrics for speech in multimodal tasks.

Direct vs. Instruction-Based Speech Generation:

Speech generation in multimodal AI follows two main approaches: direct speech generation and instruction-based speech generation. Direct models generate spoken responses end-to-end without intermediate text, while instruction-based models—such as ChatGPT-4o
[
10

, AnyGPT, and Next-GPT

These models output structured text (e.g., ”The answer is ‘running’.”) before generating speech, ensuring consistency but introducing redundancy and unnatural phrasing. While instruction-based generation aligns text and speech outputs, it is less suited for natural conversations. However, a systematic comparison between these approaches remains unexplored, leaving open questions about their effectiveness for context-aware spoken interactions in multimodal AI.

Speech-Based Evaluation Metrics for Multimodal Tasks:

Evaluating text output is straightforward using established metrics like BLEU

[
41

have been used to measure similarity in speech output tasks, but they do not account for reasoning quality or semantic correctness.
This highlights the need for a dedicated evaluation metric to assessing spoken responses in multimodal reasoning tasks.

4
Model Architecture

Our architecture builds on LLaVA’s design
[
8
]
with additional components for speech processing. Our model integrates speech and vision inputs into an LLM, denoted as
f_{\theta}(x)
, where
\theta
represents the model parameters.
The overall network architecture is depicted in Figure
2
.

4.1
Vision Encoder

Given an input image
X_{v}
, we employ a pretrained VIT-based vision encoder, such as CLIP
[
44
]
, denoted as
g(\cdot)
, to extract high-level visual representations:

Z_{v}=g(X_{v}),\quad Z_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}\times d_{v}}.

(1)

Here,
Z_{v}
is obtained from the final layer of the vision encoder, where
n_{v}
is the number of image patches. Each visual token in
Z_{v}
has a feature dimension of
d_{v}
.

Next, to align visual embeddings with the LLM’s word embedding space, we apply a learnable linear projection layer
W_{v}
:

H_{v}=W_{v}Z_{v},\quad H_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}\times d_{h}}.

(2)

Here,
H_{v}
represents the projected visual tokens, now residing in the same dimensional space
d_{h}
as the LLM’s text embeddings, ensuring compatibility for multimodal fusion.

4.2
Speech Encoder and Tokenization

For speech input
X_{s}
, a pretrained speech encoder
s(\cdot)
generates a sequence of discrete tokens:

Z_{s}=\{q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{T_{s}}\},\quad q_{i}\in\{1,2,\dots,d_{s}\},

where
T_{s}
is the number of tokens and
d_{s}
is the speech vocabulary size. Each token
q_{i}
is mapped to a text-like representation (e.g.,
\langle\!\langle\text{speech\_}i\rangle\!\rangle
) and added to the LLM’s vocabulary, enabling text and speech to be handled uniformly by the tokenizer.

4.3
Multimodal Fusion

Given textual tokens
Z_{t}
from input text
X_{t}
, discrete speech tokens
Z_{s}
, and visual embeddings
H_{v}
, we construct the multimodal token sequence:

X=[bos,\,boi,\,img,\,eoi,\,Z_{t},\,boa,\,Z_{s},\,eoa,\,eos],\quad X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

where
n
is the initial token sequence length. Special tokens (
bos,boi,eoi,boa,eoa,eos
) explicitly mark the beginning and end of the sequence, image tokens, and audio tokens.

After embedding, we replace the placeholder embedding of the token
img
with the visual embeddings
H_{v}
:

E^{\prime}=[E_{bos},\,E_{boi},\,H_{v},\,E_{eoi},\,E_{Z_{t}},\,E_{boa},\,E_{Z_{s}},\,E_{eoa},\,E_{eos}],

\quad E^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}\times d_{h}}
, where
n^{\prime}=n_{v}+n-1
. Finally, the backbone LLM processes
E^{\prime}
to achieve multimodal understanding.

4.4
Speech Decoding

The multimodal LLM outputs a sequence of predicted tokens, from which the speech-related tokens
Z_{s}^{\prime}
are extracted between the special tokens
boa
and
eoa
. A pretrained speech decoder
s^{-1}(\cdot)
then transforms these discrete tokens back into speech waveforms:

X_{s}^{\prime}=s^{-1}(Z_{s}^{\prime}),

where
Z_{s}^{\prime}
represents the predicted discrete speech token sequence, and
X_{s}^{\prime}
is the resulting synthesized speech audio.

4.5
Text-Instruction Outputs

During the pre-train stages (can be found in Appendix
A.2
), the model generate text tokens and speech tokens directly. Similarly, during the SFT stage, the model generates responses directly in textual form. However, for speech output, we use a structured prompting system to guide the model in reasoning and generating coherent spoken responses. Unlike text generation, speech output introduces additional complexities, requiring structured guidance to ensure fluency, coherence, and semantic alignment. For ASR and TTS tasks, we use simple yet effective prompts to map between speech and text. ASR follows
“The transcript of the given speech is:”
to extract text, while TTS uses
“This is how your text sounds in speech:”
to generate speech. For speech-vision tasks, where the model must generate speech from visual inputs, we adopt an instruction-based speech generation approach. Here, textual captions and textual answers serve as intermediate reasoning steps to align the model’s understanding before producing spoken outputs. In image captioning, we explicitly structure the prompt as
“The textual caption is ‘caption’. Therefore, the audio caption is:”
followed by discrete speech tokens. This ensures that the speech output remains faithful to the structured textual representation. Similarly, for VQA tasks, we prompt the model with
“The textual answer is ‘answer’. Therefore, the audio answer is:”
before generating discrete speech tokens. This method enforces a structured reasoning process, ensuring that the model first forms a grounded textual response before converting it into speech.

5.2
Metrics

Text-Output tasks:

For tasks where the model generates text outputs, we use Word Error Rate (WER) for ASR, CIDEr
[
40
]
for image captioning, and accuracy for VQA. These metrics provide a standardized evaluation framework for assessing performance across text-output tasks.

Speech-output tasks:

We evaluate speech generation using two methods: We transcribe model-generated speech with Whisper Medium
[
48
]
and compare the resulting text to human references (as in text-output tasks).
However, ASR models can exhibit biases that introduce transcription errors, thereby distorting the perceived quality of the generated speech. Therefore, we use WavLM-TDNN
[
41
]
to extract speech embeddings from both generated and reference speech and measure their similarity (e.g., via cosine similarity). This directly compares acoustic properties without relying on ASR.
Apart from that, we propose a
Speech-Vqa-Accuracy
that incorporates the above similarity. Given a generated answer
\hat{A}
and
N
human-annotated references

A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{N}
, let the set of unique references be

\{U_{1},U_{2},\dots,U_{n}\}
(where
n\leq N
). We compute the similarity

\mathrm{sim}(\hat{A},U_{j})
for each
U_{j}
, and choose the closest reference:

U_{\mathrm{best}}=\arg\max_{U_{j}}\,\mathrm{sim}(\hat{A},U_{j}).

(3)

Let
\mathrm{count}(U_{\mathrm{best}})
be the number of times
U_{\mathrm{best}}

appears in the original
N
references. We define a weight:

W(U_{\mathrm{best}})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\mathrm{count}(U_{\mathrm{best}})\geq 0.3N,\\
\tfrac{2}{3},&\text{if }\mathrm{count}(U_{\mathrm{best}})=0.2N,\\
\tfrac{1}{3},&\text{if }\mathrm{count}(U_{\mathrm{best}})=0.1N.\end{cases}

(4)

Finally, we define the
Speech-VQA Score
:

\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{VQA}}=I\!\bigl(\mathrm{sim}(\hat{A},U_{\mathrm{best}})\,\geq\,\tau\bigr)\,\cdot\,W(U_{\mathrm{best}}),

(5)

where
\tau
is a similarity threshold, and
I(\cdot)
is an indicator function that
is 1 if the condition is true, and 0 otherwise. In this paper, we set
\tau
to 0.7 based on empirical observations that this threshold best balances precision and recall, effectively distinguishing perceptually similar speech segments from dissimilar ones.

, instruction tuning in SVLA-2B-Text-Ins has minimal impact on text-only performance, evidenced by near-identical CIDEr scores on COCO-2014 (120.0 vs. 120.2) relative to the non-instruction version, SVLA-2B. However, when generating spoken captions, SVLA-2B scores close to zero, whereas SVLA-2B-Text-Ins jumps to around 56—an improvement driven by structured prompts (
as “The textual caption is … Therefore, the audio caption is: …”
) that significantly boost speech accuracy. This advantage is also clear in acoustic similarity metrics: on COCO-2014, SVLA-2B achieves 79.7, while SVLA-2B-Text-Ins rises to 89.3, reflecting more faithful speech outputs (Table
2
). While TMT attains an even higher speech-only result (78.7 of CIDEr scores on COCO) by treating each modality as a separate “language”, it handles only one input modality at once and thus lacks a unified approach to image+text or image+speech inputs.
Moreover, Next-GPT-7B surpasses SVLA-2B in text tasks (158.3 vs. 120 CIDEr) but does so at a larger 7B scale, while SVLA-2B (Qwen2.5-1.5B) maintains parameter efficiency and handles both text and speech.

VQA Performance:

In Table
1
shows that SVLA-2B-Text-Ins stands out in the I+T → T VQA task by delivering competitive performance despite utilizing a much smaller Qwen-1.5B backbone compared to the 7B-sized alternatives. While models like LLaVA-1.5 and Next-GPT-7B achieve strong scores on benchmarks such as VQAv2, OKVQA, and GQA, our SVLA-2B-Text-Ins variant demonstrates impressive results with a VQAv2 score of 69.7 and a VizWiz accuracy of 58.0, rivaling or even surpassing larger models. On the other hand, the model shows a significant drop—by approximately 20% in OKVQA and GQA—when evaluated on the I+S → T VQA task, indicating that the model performs better when the question is provided in text form. Notably, similar to the image captioning task, the instructive output strategy does not substantially impact performance in text-output VQA tasks, as the accuracy difference between non-instruct and instruct configurations is minimal.

The performance of speech outputs is generally worse than that of text outputs by roughly 15–20% on VQA tasks. In the ASR-based evaluation (Table
1
), the SVLA-2B-Text-Ins model operating in the I+T → S configuration achieves a VQAv2 score of 37% and an OKVQA score of 11.6% , compared to 29.4% and 6.08% , respectively, in the I+S → S configuration. This discrepancy indicates that integrating a text input—as opposed to directly processing speech—preserves richer semantic details. The acoustic Speech-VQA accuracy evaluation further highlights this benefit, with the SVLA-2B-Text-Ins model recording a VQAv2 accuracy of 51.7% and an OKVQA accuracy of 69.6% in I+T → S, versus 44.5% and 62.5% in I+S → S. These improvements are likely due to the structured intermediate text-generation stage, which reduces the ambiguity inherent in raw speech processing and results in more semantically faithful responses for complex VQA tasks.

Does Text Guidance Make Speech Smarter?

According to Figure
5
, SVLA-2B-Text-Ins generates more structured and coherent speech than SVLA-2B, enhancing semantic alignment and reasoning. By first generating a structured textual response, the model ensures that speech output follows a well-defined linguistic structure. This intermediate step helps maintain consistency, reduces ambiguity, and improves multimodal alignment, leading to clearer and more contextually relevant spoken responses. In contrast, SVLA-2B, which generates speech directly, tends to produce more random and less structured outputs due to the absence of textual guidance. This approach increases the likelihood of semantic misinterpretation, prosody misalignment, and overall incoherence.

The Limits of ASR-Based Evaluation:

ASR-based evaluation falls short in assessing speech quality because it misinterprets minor phonetic variations as errors, even when the spoken response is nearly correct. As seen in Figure

“kimeroh”
or
“tan”

. These small differences, though perceptually acceptable, lead to unfair penalization since ASR focuses on exact text matching rather than acoustic similarity. This limitation reveals the need for an evaluation method that directly measures speech waveform similarity rather than relying on error-prone transcription.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce SVLA, a unified Speech-Text-Vision Assistant capable of handling both language tasks (ASR, TTS) and vision-language reasoning tasks (image captioning, VQA). To support the community in building similar models, we also release a large-scale tri-modal dataset encompassing speech, text, and vision. Additionally, we analyze two settings for speech generation: directly producing spoken output and using a text prompt to guide speech synthesis. We further propose Speech-VQA-Accuracy, a new metric based on acoustic similarity for evaluating spoken VQA responses. Our experiments show that while the model performs better with text outputs, speech outputs benefit from an instructive text prompt, yielding more coherent.

Our study has several limitations. First, the speech data in our dataset is generated using a TTS model, which may lack the natural emotional and prosodic variations found in real human speech. Second, our architecture relies on a straightforward concatenation-based fusion, whereas more sophisticated approaches—such as Q-Former
[
7
]
or Perceiver Resampler
[
9
]
—could potentially yield better performance. Finally, due to resource constraints, we build on Qwen2.5-1.5B, which limits the model’s capacity. We hypothesize that employing a larger backbone would further enhance speech-related capabilities.

A.2
Training Strategy

Our model is trained in three sequential phases, progressively increasing multimodal complexity to enhance stability, efficiency, and modality integration.

Stage 1 - Text-Speech Pre-Training

The model learns text-speech alignment through ASR and TTS on large-scale paired datasets, establishing a strong linguistic foundation before incorporating vision.

Stage 2 - Vision-Text-Speech Pre-Training

Training expands to include vision-based tasks like VQA and image captioning alongside ASR and TTS, enabling the model to unify vision, text, and speech representations.

Stage 3 - Supervised Fine-Tuning

The model is refined through supervised fine-tuning on benchmark-aligned datasets, focusing on multi-turn conversations, history retention, and multimodal reasoning.
