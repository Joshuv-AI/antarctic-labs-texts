High prevalence and pathogen-specific variations of co-infections in pediatric adenovirus pneumonia: a retrospective epidemiological analysis from Northern China
Authors: Yanmei Lang, Cuijie Gong, Dandan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the characteristics of co-infection pathogen profiles in children with adenovirus pneumonia and provide guidance for clinical diagnosis and rational treatment.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the etiological results of co-infections in children hospitalized with adenovirus pneumonia in the Respiratory Department of Hebei Children’s Hospital from January 1, 2024, to December 31, 2024. Differences in co-infections across genders, age groups, and seasons were analyzed.ResultsAmong 5,373 children hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, 330 cases (6.1%) were diagnosed with adenovirus pneumonia, of which 310 (93.9%) exhibited co-infections. Bacterial co-infections predominated (70.0%, 231/330), with Streptococcus pneumoniae (45.2%), Haemophilus influenzae (40.9%), and Moraxella catarrhalis (2.7%) being among the most frequently detected, followed by Staphylococcus aureus (2.4%), Bordetella pertussis (2.1%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter cloacae, and Streptococcus pyogenes. Viral co-infections were identified in 45.5% (150/330), primarily rhinovirus (26.4%), influenza A/B (7.3%), parainfluenza virus (5.8%), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV, 4.8%), metapneumovirus (4.5%), coronavirus (1.5%), and bocavirus (0.3%). Additionally, Mycoplasma pneumoniae co-infections accounted for 44.5% (147/330). Gender-specific analysis revealed significantly higher RSV co-infection rates in girls than boys (P < 0.05), with no notable gender disparities for other pathogens. Age-related differences showed higher bacterial co-infection rates in infants/toddlers compared to preschool/school-age groups (P < 0.05), where Streptococcus pneumoniae peaked in infants/toddlers, parainfluenza virus was most frequent in infants/toddlers, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae predominated in school-age children. Seasonally, co-infection rates remained consistent year-round, though RSV and influenza A/B peaked in winter, parainfluenza virus in summer, metapneumovirus was least detected in summer, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae exhibited the highest positivity in autumn and the lowest in spring.ConclusionAdenovirus pneumonia in children in this region exhibits a high rate of co-infections, predominantly bacterial (especially Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae), followed by viral (rhinovirus, influenza, parainfluenza, RSV) and Mycoplasma pneumoniae. Co-infection profiles vary by gender, age, and season. Timely identification of co-pathogens is critical for guiding rational antimicrobial use and improving prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-025-02129-x.Keywords: Children, Adenovirus pneumonia, Co-infection

Introduction

Adenovirus is a significant pathogen responsible for pediatric pneumonia, particularly in children aged 6 months to 5 years, and is predominantly transmitted via respiratory droplets [1]. Epidemiologically, adenovirus accounts for 4%–10% of childhood community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) cases globally, with its prevalence rising to 19.4% in severe pneumonia cases [1, 2]. The clinical presentation of adenovirus pneumonia is characterized by high fever, dyspnea, respiratory failure, and multisystem involvement (e.g., circulatory, hematologic, gastrointestinal, and neurologic systems), often leading to long-term pulmonary sequelae and substantial morbidity and mortality, especially among infants [3].

The management of adenovirus pneumonia is challenging, partly due to the absence of widely available and approved targeted antiviral therapies and the virus’s propensity to trigger intense inflammatory responses [4, 5]. Co-infections with other pathogens further exacerbate disease severity, prolong hospitalization, and increase the risk of multiorgan dysfunction [6]. Notably, bacterial or viral co-infections may amplify lung injury through synergistic inflammatory pathways, while Mycoplasma pneumoniae co-infections complicate clinical differentiation and therapeutic decision-making [7]. Current clinical practices often rely on empirical antibiotic regimens, contributing to antimicrobial resistance and increased treatment-related toxicity in children [8].

Despite these challenges, comprehensive data on the co-infection profiles of adenovirus pneumonia remain scarce. Previous studies have primarily focused on singular pathogens or limited geographic regions, with insufficient emphasis on demographic and seasonal variations [9]. This gap hinders the development of region-specific diagnostic and therapeutic protocols.

In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 330 pediatric cases of co-infection patterns in children with adenovirus pneumonia, emphasizing bacterial, viral, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae co-pathogens. By evaluating gender-, age-, and season-related differences, our findings aim to refine clinical strategies for co-infection identification, optimize antimicrobial stewardship, and improve patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study included 330 children diagnosed with adenovirus pneumonia who were hospitalized in the Respiratory Department of Hebei Children’s Hospital between January 1, 2024 and December 31, 2024. The diagnosis of adenovirus pneumonia was confirmed via adenovirus Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) positivity in nasopharyngeal aspirate or sputum specimens, in conjunction with clinical and radiological findings consistent with pneumonia according to established guidelines [10].

General characteristics

These 330 cases of adenovirus pneumonia represented 6.1% of the 5,373 children hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia during the study period. Among the 330 adenovirus pneumonia cases, 310 (93.9%) had co-infections. The cohort comprised 196 males (59.4%) and 134 females (40.6%). Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages (%). Group comparisons utilized the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for small sample sizes. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Gender-specific co-infection patterns

The overall co-infection positivity rates in male and female patients were 92.9% (182/196) and 95.5% (128/134), respectively, with no significant gender-based difference (P > 0.05). Bacterial co-infections were detected in 70.4% (138/196) of males and 61.9% (83/134) of females, viral co-infections in 43.4% (85/196) of males and 48.5% (65/134) of females, and Mycoplasma pneumoniae co-infections in 41.3% (81/196) of males and 49.3% (66/134) of females. None of these differences reached statistical significance (P > 0.05 for all). However, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) co-infection rates were significantly higher in girls (10.4%, 14/134) than boys (1.0%, 2/196) (P < 0.05). No gender disparities were observed for other pathogens (Table 1).

Pathogen co-infection complexity

Among 310 co-infected cases, 102 (32.9%) had single-pathogen co-infections, 115 (37.1%) had dual-pathogen co-infections, 78 (25.2%) had triple-pathogen co-infections, 12 (3.9%) had quadruple-pathogen co-infections, 2 (0.6%) had quintuple-pathogen co-infections, and 1 (0.3%) had sextuple-pathogen co-infections.

Discussion

High co-infection rate and epidemiological context

Adenovirus is a major pathogen associated with severe pediatric pneumonia, with disease severity influenced by viral load, serotype, and host immune responses [1]. Co-infections further exacerbate clinical outcomes by amplifying inflammatory damage and complicating treatment [6]. In this study, 93.9% (310/330) of adenovirus pneumonia cases involved co-infections—a rate substantially higher than global reports, where pre-pandemic co-infection burdens were consistently lower due to seasonal virus intermittency and lacked the hyperendemic transmission dynamics observed in this cohort. Notably, surveillance from Italy and Brazil documented near-zero detection of adenovirus and other respiratory viruses during COVID-19 lockdowns, confirming that co-infection rates >90% are exceptional outside high-transmission settings [11–13]. This discrepancy may reflect regional epidemiological trends in Hebei Province, heightened diagnostic sensitivity through multiplex PCR, or selection bias inherent to hospitalized cohorts; nevertheless, the phenomenon of pathogen co-occurrence is well-documented across various respiratory infections, although reported rates can vary. For example, a literature review on COVID-19, citing a meta-analysis by Musuuzza et al. [14], indicated a viral co-infection rate of 10% (95% CI: 6–14%) in COVID-19 patients, with influenza and respiratory syncytial virus being typical co-pathogens [15]. More broadly, a systematic review by Karakonstantis et al. [16], which analyzed polymicrobial infections based on PRISMA standards and included 290 cases of polymicrobial lung infections, underscored that the presence of two or more pathogens at the same infection site is a recognized feature in conditions like severe pneumonia, after excluding cases of mixed infection sites or colonization. Notably, our study excluded community-managed mild cases, potentially skewing results toward severe co-infected patients. Such high co-infection rates underscore the necessity for comprehensive pathogen screening in adenovirus pneumonia to guide targeted therapy.

Bacterial co-infections: dominance of S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae

Bacterial co-infections predominated (70.0%), with Streptococcus pneumoniae (45.2%) and Haemophilus influenzae (40.9%) as leading pathogens. These findings align with global data identifying S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae as the most common bacterial culprits in pediatric pneumonia [17]. Adenovirus-induced mucosal barrier disruption and immune dysregulation likely facilitate bacterial invasion [18], particularly in infants/toddlers, where S. pneumoniae detection peaked (P < 0.05). Clinically, these results support empirical use of appropriate β-lactam antibiotics (such as amoxicillin-clavulanate, a combination formulation of amoxicillin and the β-lactamase inhibitor clavulanate) in regions with high pneumococcal prevalence, though local antibiotic resistance patterns must be considered.

Moraxella catarrhalis co-infections were detected in 2.7% of cases. While this specific co-infection rate with adenovirus is modest, M. catarrhalis is a common colonizer of the pediatric nasopharynx. For instance, a prospective cohort study by Verhaegh et al. [19] found that in healthy Dutch children, the cumulative nasopharyngeal colonization rate of M. catarrhalis reached as high as 22%−55% by 6 months of age, with colonization peaking in autumn and winter, underscoring its widespread presence as a commensal in early life. Furthermore, M. catarrhalis is frequently implicated in various respiratory tract infections. It is recognized as a leading pathogen in acute otitis media in children, accounting for 15–20% of cases, and often acts as a secondary invader in viral lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs), particularly in children co-infected with respiratory syncytial virus or adenovirus [20]. The increased colonization rates of M. catarrhalis observed following the widespread use of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV), potentially due to altered microbial competition, might also influence its interaction dynamics [20]. This suggests adenovirus may not synergistically promote M. catarrhalis colonization as strongly as might be expected given its prevalence, or that other factors now play a more significant role in its pathogenic emergence in our population. However, vigilance remains warranted in infants due to immature immunity and loss of maternal antibodies. Unexpectedly, Bordetella pertussis was detected in preschool-age children, mirroring global “pertussis resurgence” trends linked to waning vaccine immunity and pathogen adaptation [21]. Co-infection with adenovirus may mask classic pertussis symptoms, necessitating PCR confirmation to avoid misdiagnosis.

Viral co-infections: rhinovirus prevalence and seasonal dynamics

Viral co-infections (45.5%) were led by rhinovirus (26.4%), followed by influenza A/B (7.3%) and parainfluenza virus (5.8%). Rhinovirus, often dismissed as a benign pathogen, is increasingly recognized for severe outcomes in immunocompromised children or those with comorbidities [22]. Its high co-infection rate with adenovirus highlights the need for dual-pathogen screening, even in mild presentations. Influenza A/B showed expected winter seasonality, emphasizing the importance of vaccination and infection control measures during peak months. Optimal timing for influenza vaccination is typically before the onset of the influenza season, usually in early autumn, to allow for development of immunity [23].

Parainfluenza virus co-infections peaked in infants/toddlers during summer, consistent with observations from various regions. For instance, PIV-3, a common serotype in young children, often exhibits activity in warmer months, which some studies suggest could be related to distinct viral subtypes or climate-driven factors like dry seasons in tropical areas [24, 25]. In our study, the average age of PIV-3 infected children and the milder clinical presentation often associated with it align with these broader epidemiological patterns [24, 25]. In contrast, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) co-infections were unexpectedly low (4.8%). This is notably lower than RSV co-infection proportions reported in some multicenter studies, which can be around 40%, and significantly lower than overall RSV detection rates in hospitalized children (e.g., 16.2%−31.1% in various Chinese cohorts) [26]. One potential explanation for this low RSV co-infection rate in the presence of adenovirus could be competitive viral interference. Adenoviruses encode non-coding RNAs (like VA1 RNA) that can modulate host antiviral responses, including RNA interference pathways, potentially inhibiting the replication of other viruses like RSV that rely on specific host factors for efficient infection [27]. Furthermore, the observed female predominance for RSV co-infection in our cohort (10.4% in girls vs. 1.0% in boys) is an interesting finding. While some reports, such as by Gong et al. [28], noted male predominance, the literature on RSV gender differences is varied. For example, a study from Sichuan, China, reported a higher overall viral detection in female children but a higher RSV detection rate in males (7.5% vs. 4.9%) [29], while other studies in infants have shown higher rates of severe RSV lower respiratory tract infection in male infants under 6 months [30]. Factors such as age-specific susceptibility, hormonal influences on immune responses (e.g., NF-κB pathway activation by RSV), and potential immunomodulatory effects from adenovirus co-infection (e.g., via AdV E3 gene products) could contribute to such sex-specific observations [31]. This may reflect adenovirus’s competitive interference with RSV replication or atypical immune interactions warranting further study.

Mycoplasma pneumoniae co-infections: regional outbreak implications

Mycoplasma pneumoniae co-infections reached 44.5%, a rate higher than the overall prevalence of M. pneumoniae (26.60%) reported in retrospective analysis of a single center in Suzhou from 2014 to 2020 [32] and rates from various individual studies in other regions of China prior to widespread outbreaks. This surge correlates with a documented M. pneumoniae outbreak in Hebei Province during 2023, underscoring the pathogen’s cyclical epidemic nature. Seasonal peaks in autumn align with general M. pneumoniae seasonality reported in other studies, including from regions like Shenyang [33], suggesting climate or school reopening may drive transmission. Dual infections with adenovirus and M. pneumoniae necessitate combination therapy (e.g., macrolides for Mycoplasma plus supportive care, or antivirals like cidofovir in select severe adenovirus cases if deemed appropriate, though evidence for the latter is limited), though emerging macrolide resistance in Asia complicates management [34].

Limitations and future directions

This study has limitations inherent to retrospective designs: (1) Selection bias from excluding outpatient cases may overestimate co-infection severity; (2) Incomplete data on antibiotic pretreatment or viral load dynamics; (3) Regional focus limits generalizability. (4) While both nasopharyngeal (NP) swabs and sputum samples were collected, the ease of obtaining NP swabs, especially in younger children, might mean they were more frequently used for PCR testing of certain pathogens; NP swabs may not always perfectly reflect the microbiology of the lower respiratory tract, which could be a constraint, although they are a standard and practical method for pediatric respiratory pathogen detection. (5) The study did not systematically collect or analyze data on disease severity in relation to specific co-infection patterns, which would be a valuable area for future prospective research. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized protocols are needed to validate these findings and to investigate the impact of co-infections on clinical severity and outcomes more thoroughly.

Empirical antibiotic therapy for hospitalized children with adenovirus pneumonia, especially infants and toddlers, should consider coverage for S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae, guided by local antimicrobial susceptibility patterns to ensure efficacy and stewardship.

Conclusion

This study reveals a strikingly high co-infection rate (93.9%) in children with adenovirus pneumonia in Hebei Province, China, dominated by bacterial pathogens (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae), followed by viral (rhinovirus, influenza A/B) and Mycoplasma pneumoniae co-infections. Age-specific trends highlight the vulnerability of infants/toddlers to bacterial and parainfluenza co-infections, while school-age children face higher risks of Mycoplasma pneumoniae co-infection. Seasonal variations further underscore the winter predominance of RSV and influenza, contrasting with autumn peaks for Mycoplasma pneumoniae. These findings emphasize the critical need for comprehensive pathogen screening in adenovirus pneumonia cases, particularly in regions with high co-infection burdens. Clinicians should prioritize targeted antimicrobial therapy against common bacterial co-pathogens like S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae, adapt seasonal testing protocols, and exercise antimicrobial stewardship to mitigate resistance risks. Timely identification of co-pathogens is pivotal for optimizing treatment, reducing complications, and improving outcomes in this vulnerable population.
