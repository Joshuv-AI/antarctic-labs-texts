Public health system capacity of tribal and rural areas in India: a retrospective state-level analysis from 2011 to 2021
Authors: Padmavathy Krishna Kumar, Divya Shrinivas, Suraj Bhor, Siddhesh Zadey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndia’s multi-tiered public health system caters to rural and tribal populations. Previous studies have noted limited access to care among tribal populations. However, a systematic nationwide analysis remains missing. Hence, we retrospectively analyzed the capacities and disparities in public health systems between tribal and non-tribal rural areas.MethodsWe extracted Rural Health Statistics (RHS) data from 2011 to 2021 on the numbers of required and functional SCs, PHCs, and CHCs for states with a tribal population > 25% of their total population. We captured healthcare access using the ratio of functional to required centers for each health facility type, separately for rural and tribal areas. We used tribal-to-rural odds ratios to assess relative differences in tribal-rural healthcare access.ResultsNationally, both the Tribal Health System Capacity Index (C) and the Tribal-Rural Health System Disparity Index (D) decreased from 2011 to 2021. The national disparity of the tribal health system decreased in facility-wise disparity indices for SCs (1.16 to 0.97), PHCs (1.09 to 0.99), and CHCs (1.20 to 1.12). State-level values for facility-wise and overall tribal-rural health system disparity index showed some improvement over the years.ConclusionThere was an overall decline in health system capacity, with limited access to healthcare in tribal areas compared to rural India. Despite some improvement in areas with higher tribal populations, there is still a need to address healthcare access for the country’s tribal population. Incorporating data on resource availability in future studies can provide a more accurate assessment and inform the development of effective solutions.

Background

India has a multitiered public healthcare system comprising Sub-Centres (SCs), Primary Health Centres (PHCs), and Community Health Centres (CHCs) to cater to the population’s primary and secondary healthcare needs [1]. The Government of India launched the National Rural Health Mission (NRHM) in 2005 to strengthen health service delivery and program implementation in rural and tribal areas [2]. The 2011 Census records that 833 million of the 1.2 billion Indians lived in rural areas [3]. The National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) has noted a significant gap in health indicators and disease burdens between rural and urban areas. For example, the rural tuberculosis (TB) prevalence rate was 242 per 100,000 people, compared with 179 per 100,000 in urban areas [4]. Increasing evidence indicates that non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are also rising in rural populations. The large share of the population and disparities in health outcomes make programs like NRHM essential.

The 2011 Census noted about 104 million tribal people belonging to 705 different groups residing in rural areas. The Expert Committee on Tribal Health in India (2018) observed a high burden of communicable diseases, including leprosy, affecting 18.5% of the tribal population [5]. TB prevalence among tribal people was 703 cases per 100,000, significantly higher than that of rural and urban residents [5]. There is an increase in the prevalence of non-communicable diseases, with every 1 out of 4 tribal adults diagnosed with hypertension [5]. In addition, 72% of tribal men consume tobacco, while > 50% consume alcohol, indicating high substance use. Furthermore, nearly 42% of tribal children are underweight. In central India, for every 86 children in the tribal communities, 1 child is afflicted with a severe form of sickle cell disease [5]. There is a stark difference in the disease burden between the tribal and the non-tribal rural populations. A 2012 study in rural Kerala noted that the prevalence of anemia, goiter, and malnutrition was significantly higher among the tribal population than among their non-tribal rural counterparts. Additionally, the study noted that the burden of disease varied among different tribal groups [6]. Thus, it is essential to separately examine the implementation of health programs in tribal and non-tribal rural areas.

Research on tribal healthcare access and health-seeking behavior in India highlights multiple interconnected barriers. Tribal communities often live in remote, hard-to-reach areas with limited infrastructure and weak connectivity, which hampers timely access to services [7]. Simultaneously, low literacy, high poverty, and reliance on traditional healers reduce utilization of public healthcare facilities [7]. Cultural perceptions, such as illness being benign or spiritual, and mistrust of inconsistent and low-quality services, lead to avoidance of and delays in seeking care within the public health system [8]. Even when these social, cultural, economic, and structural factors are available, their interplay creates gaps in demand and in the effective use of the public health system among tribal populations. On the supply side of the problem, healthcare workers are often reluctant to serve in tribal areas due to poor living conditions, inadequate healthcare and educational infrastructure, limited road connectivity, unreliable power and water supplies, and a lack of support for families, among other factors. Language barriers and lack of incentives further discourage them. Additionally, limited exposure and training for tribal health issues in the healthcare curriculum leave professionals unprepared to provide culturally sensitive and effective care [9].

The National Rural Health Mission (NRHM) and its continuation under the National Health Mission (NHM) introduced some initiatives to benefit tribal populations. For instance, state-level Tribal Health Action Plans address the specific needs of Scheduled Tribes, focusing on strengthening infrastructure, deploying the health workforce, and community-level outreach, among other initiatives [10, 11]. To improve access in remote areas, Mobile Medical Units provide diagnostic, curative, and preventive services directly to villages, offering consultations, laboratory services, and medicines on a scheduled basis [9, 10]. Recognizing high neonatal mortality in tribal districts, NRHM has also started facility-based and home-based newborn care, including Newborn Care Corners and Special Newborn Care Units [10, 12]. For Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups, customized interventions such as nutritional supplementation, immunization drives, and mobile outreach have been implemented in states including Odisha, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, and Chhattisgarh [9, 10]. Village Health and Nutrition Days provide regular community-based sessions for maternal and child health, antenatal care, immunization, and health education [2, 11]. There are 15,000 Ayushman Bharat–Health and Wellness Centres in 177 districts, which provide comprehensive primary healthcare services in tribal districts. The Swasthya Portal was launched in 2020 by the Ministry of Tribal Affairs to consolidate health and nutrition data for 177 high-priority tribal districts and support evidence-based program planning [13].

The health system in India is fragmented, with more private facilities concentrated in urban areas and more public facilities in rural areas. The Expert Committee’s findings indicated that half of outpatient visits and about two-thirds of inpatient admissions among tribal care seekers occurred in public health facilities [5]. Previously, cross-sectional studies have identified deficits in public health infrastructure in tribal and rural areas [14]. Limited financing for public health facilities serving tribal communities can result in infrastructure and human resource deficiencies. Data from 2021 to 22 note shortages of 82.6%, 32.6%, and 27.9% among specialists, technicians, and nurses, respectively, at tribal peripheral health facilities [15]. This can partly be attributed to healthcare workers’ hesitancy to work in remote areas due to limited infrastructure [9]. Even when available, the quality of workforce training might be questionable [15]. Where well-trained healthcare workers are present, limited resources or a lack of local political will can impede service delivery and quality of care. Hence, a multitude of interconnected factors determine the availability and quality of healthcare provided to tribal populations. However, all such determinants come into play only after ensuring basic infrastructure, i.e., the presence of healthcare facilities. Hence, investigating the presence of infrastructure is a crucial first step in situational analysis of the public health system capacity.

It is important to specifically investigate changes in the trends of existing public health system capacity within tribal areas as a primary step to improve healthcare access among tribal communities. A mixed-methods evaluation of the Chhattisgarh Rural Medical Corps (CRMC) in tribal areas was conducted, primarily focusing on workforce enhancement from 2009 to 2013, with some emphasis on changes in infrastructure accessibility and capacity [16]. This evaluation was only specific to one state and one particular program focusing on retaining the workforce in tribal, remote, and non-tribal rural areas. Most analyses have focused solely on infrastructure and health outcomes for tribal communities at a single point in time [17]. Our analysis, on the other hand, focuses on national and subnational tribal health facility capacities and the rural-tribal disparities over 10 years. Multi-year analyses of infrastructure change help monitor and evaluate existing policies and programs. Such assessments are intended to support better policy design and implementation in the future. However, there has been no multi-year analysis evaluating the public health system capacity in non-tribal rural and tribal areas of India since the introduction of NRHM. Such a multi-year analysis can be useful for several research, policy, and practice purposes. Here, our goal is to track progress in public health system capacity and investigate disparities.

We conducted a multiyear retrospective analysis of public health system capacity at the primary and secondary levels for tribal and (non-tribal) rural areas. Our study had two aims: (1) To assess public health system capacity in tribal areas in India and its states and union territories (UTs) with a > 25% tribal population from 2011 to 2021, and (2) To identify gaps between tribal and rural health system capacities at national and state levels in the last decade.

Methods

We extracted data from the Rural Health Statistics (RHS) annual reports for the financial years 2011 to 2021 on the number of required and functional (present) SCs, PHCs, and CHCs across 36 states and UTs [18]. The Rural Health Statistics (RHS) are detailed annual reports published by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, outlining data on key public healthcare infrastructure at the national and sub-national levels in India [19]. We based the number of required health facilities on the Indian Public Health Standards (IPHS) norms, which were derived by accounting for population, local disease epidemiology, geographical needs, and community requirements. The IPHS guidelines, last updated in 2022, stipulate standards for infrastructure, workforce, and performance evaluation for public health facilities across urban, rural, and tribal areas in the country. The primary emphasis is on decentralized implementation of the various national health programs introduced by the Government of India. According to the IPHS guidelines, tribal areas must have one SC per 3000 people, one PHC per 20,000 people, and one CHC per 80,000 people [20–22]. A cluster of lower-tier facilities must be linked to one higher-level facility (e.g., one PHC acts as a referral center for six SCs) to manage complex cases [23]. Data extraction was part of the broader data compilation effort currently run at the Association for Socially Applicable Research (ASAR) under the Indian Data Library (IDLi). Per IDLi standard practices, we primarily performed consistency and uniqueness checks to assess data quality. We completed all data profiling, integration, cleaning, and metadata collection from all existing RHS reports for each year. We did not perform any imputation.

For our analyses, we used data from Sect. 3, part 2, of the RHS database, which focuses on the presence of healthcare infrastructure and workforce in the country’s urban, rural, and tribal areas. The national analysis included data from the RHS for all states and UTs at the start and end points, i.e., 2011 and 2021, respectively. For subnational analysis, we focused on the 11 states/UTs with at least a quarter of their population classified as tribal, using 2011 Census data (Table 1). Of these, Lakshadweep had limited/no data for several years, especially at the start and end points. In some early years, the data were unavailable due to a lack of facilities. Hence, we excluded Lakshadweep from the analysis. In addition, the 2021 RHS data for the UTs Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu were combined due to changes in the delimitation.

Ethics approval

The study only used publicly available aggregate-level data. The study did not involve any human participants or animal subjects and therefore did not require ethical review.

Results

Facility-wise tribal health system capacity index

Nationally, the facility-wise tribal health system capacity index decreased from 0.96 in 2011 to 0.79 in 2021 for SCs, 0.89 to 0.79 for PHCs, and 0.79 to 0.78 for CHCs. Notably, the health system capacity index for India was less than adequate (C < 1) across all facility types in both 2011 and 2021 (Fig. 1).

In 2011, for SCs, the states/UTs with adequate health system capacity (C ≥ 1) included Arunachal Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Mizoram, Sikkim, and Dadra and Nagar Haveli. For PHCs, Arunachal Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, and Dadra and Nagar Haveli had adequate health system capacities. In 2021, for SCs, the states/UTs with adequate health system capacity (C ≥ 1) were Mizoram (highest capacity index), Tripura, Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Chhattisgarh, Sikkim, and Dadra & Nagar Haveli, while Meghalaya had the lowest capacity index. For PHCs, Arunachal Pradesh (the highest capacity index), Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, Meghalaya, Manipur, and Chhattisgarh showed adequate capacity, while Jharkhand had the lowest capacity index. Among CHCs, Arunachal Pradesh had the highest capacity index, followed by Mizoram and Nagaland, while Sikkim and Dadra and Nagar Haveli had the lowest capacity indices (Fig. 2).

In 2011, five states/UTs had values for the health system capacity index for SCs higher than the national value, eight states/UTs had values for PHC higher than the national value, and six states/UTs had values for CHC higher than the national value. However, by 2021, more states/UTs had values higher than the national facility-wise tribal health system capacity index. Nine states/UTs had a value for SCs and PHCs higher than the national average, and seven states/UTs had a value for CHCs higher than the national average. This shows that there has been some improvement in facility-level health system capacity across states/UTs over the past decade.

Overall tribal health system capacity index

The overall tribal health system capacity index at the all-India level fell from 0.95 in 2011 to 0.79 in 2021.

In 2011, Arunachal Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, and Dadra and Nagar Haveli had a C > 1, while Jharkhand recorded the lowest value. In 2021, six states, including Arunachal Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, and Tripura, had an overall tribal health system capacity index greater than one, while Meghalaya had the lowest value.

State-wise values for the overall tribal health system capacity index improved compared with the national value. In 2011, six states/UTs had values for health system capacity indices higher than the national value. In 2021, eight states/UTs had an overall tribal health system capacity index value higher than the national value.

Facility-wise tribal-rural health system disparity index

Nationally, the facility-wise tribal-rural health system disparity index (D) decreased for SCs (from 1.16 in 2011 to 0.97 in 2021), PHCs (from 1.09 in 2011 to 0.99 in 2021), and CHCs (from 1.20 in 2011 to 1.12 in 2021) (Fig. 3). This implies that health system disparity increased in the tribal areas.

Overall tribal-rural health system disparity index

Nationally, the overall tribal-rural health system disparity index (D) declined from 1.15 in 2011 to 0.98 in 2021, indicating that overall tribal health system disparity increased relative to the rural health system.

In 2011, Arunachal Pradesh, Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Chhattisgarh, Sikkim, Meghalaya, Nagaland, and Mizoram had D > 1, indicating that the overall tribal health system disparity was lower than the rural health system disparity in these states. Jharkhand recorded the lowest value (D = 0.68), noting the highest overall tribal health system disparity. In 2021, seven states had an overall D > 1, including Jharkhand, Arunachal Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Manipur, and Nagaland, indicating that the overall tribal health system disparity was lower than the overall rural health system disparity in these states. Dadra & Nagar Haveli had the lowest value (D = 0.68), noting the highest overall tribal health system disparity. State-wise values for the overall tribal-rural health system disparity index improved over the years compared with the national value. Three states/UTs had values higher than the national value in 2011. In 2021, seven states/UTs had disparity indices higher than the national value.

Discussion

Nationally, both facility-wise and overall tribal health system capacity indices decreased from 2011 to 2021, indicating fewer functional centers than required over the decade. However, there has been some improvement for certain individual states and UTs compared to the national values. We aimed to develop an initial framework and a more standard, interpretable, and easily accessible methodology for evaluating capacity and disparity (i.e., indices). We focused on descriptive analysis and indices, in part because they can be easily communicated to diverse stakeholders, including policymakers and health officers, who can integrate these methods into programmatic monitoring and evaluation.

Nationally, the facility-wise disparity index decreased from 2011 to 2021, suggesting that health system capacity in tribal areas did not improve relative to their non-tribal rural counterparts. However, state-wise values of the facility-wise disparity index improved relative to the national value. The changing trends in disparity also indicate that tribal health system capacity at the state level is improving. The improvement at the state level can be partly attributed to the nationwide introduction of the NHM. Recent NHM guidelines offer additional benefits to tribal areas, such as upgrading SCs and PHCs to Ayushman Bharat-Health and Wellness Centres, deploying Mobile Medical Units (MMUs) to provide health services in more inaccessible terrain, and providing additional resources to support tribal areas classified as “High Priority Districts” [24, 25]. Independent surveys monitor the impact the NHM and other programs have on disease surveillance and service provision at the community level. One such example is the Odisha Tribal Family Health Survey (OTFHS), which has documented both disease prevalence and the remarkable utilization of public health facilities, especially for maternal and child health services [26]. Besides the NHM, the Ministry of Tribal Affairs has also introduced various initiatives to improve access to health services among tribal communities [11]. In addition, state-level programs focused on enhancing infrastructure, workforce capacity, and service delivery to improve tribal healthcare access may have also contributed significantly to these improvements [9, 27].

Our analysis shows that over the decade, there has been some improvement in strengthening public health systems in states with higher tribal populations. Arunachal Pradesh, with a tribal population of 68.8%, has consistently improved public health capacity in its tribal areas over the past few years, even exceeding the national average. Similarly, in Nagaland, with 86.5% of the population being tribal, the overall capacity increased from 1.04 in 2011 to 1.31 in 2021. On the other hand, Sikkim and Dadra and Nagar Haveli, with smaller tribal populations, continue to face disparities in their public health systems.

Our paper analyzes the state and national capacity of public health infrastructure in areas with higher tribal populations in India. Additionally, the methodology introduced for calculating the capacity index and disparity index in tribal and rural healthcare provides an effective way to assess and monitor the development of health infrastructure. This analysis not only highlights progress in certain states but also identifies states that require more attention. For example, states with a disparity index (D) below 1, such as Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Tripura, should be prioritized for scaling up health infrastructure, as they lag behind other states. Similarly, states like Sikkim, where capacity indices have declined, need focused efforts to prevent any further setbacks. It is essential to acknowledge the heterogeneity across states. Our focus in the state-level analysis was on states with large proportions of tribal populations. For instance, our findings are consistent with previous reports highlighting that certain Northeastern states, such as Arunachal Pradesh, have adequate health system capacity in tribal areas [28]. States with a tribal population of less than 25% may be contributing to the national improvement in health system capacity. This is possible due to sustained investment in the public healthcare system over decades, along with increasing community acceptance of utilizing it [9, 29]. For instance, in Kerala, several review mechanisms were introduced after 2013 to ensure timely access to health services among tribal populations [29]. It is also important to understand that policies are generalized and implemented across India similarly, and the one-size-fits-all approach does not work at the ground level. An exploratory study conducted in a district of Odisha found that tribal populations living in more interior, geographically isolated pockets opted to seek care from informal healers because they lacked the means to arrange transportation to reach the nearest center. In addition, healthcare workers from outside the tribal areas were not adequately trained to provide culturally sensitive care, deterring people from seeking care at public health facilities [30].

Our study has certain limitations. First, our analysis is based on state-level data, which lacks the granularity needed for a more precise understanding of ground-level realities, including, but not limited to, population density and the socioeconomic status of communities near health facilities. Ideally, assessing healthcare capacity at the district level or even lower would provide deeper insights, but such data are currently unavailable to us. Second, we defined functional healthcare facilities solely by their presence. However, the mere existence of a healthcare center does not guarantee it can deliver quality care. Lastly, the Rural Health Statistics has missing data labeled as “Not Applicable” for certain health facilities across various states and UTs during the 10-year period, not just the start and end points. This may have affected our analysis’s ability to truly reflect year-specific changes in capacity and/or disparity indices. In addition, while changes in the geopolitical context, such as the formation of the state of Telangana from Andhra Pradesh in 2014, were accounted for in RHS, no additional information was available to examine how the division affected the distribution of tribal health facilities. This may have also been reflected in our analysis. To complement these findings, we also need qualitative data and implementation science studies to reveal community and health system barriers affecting various aspects of healthcare delivery in tribal areas [31].

Despite these limitations, this study represents a significant first step in evaluating tribal healthcare capacity and lays the groundwork for more detailed investigations in the future. The findings from our study can serve as a framework for analyzing the workforce and infrastructure at public health centers in tribal and rural areas. Future analyses can help comprehensively understand the strengths and weaknesses of operating public health centers for a unique population living in a relatively isolated geographic area, thereby offering policy-level guidelines and measures to ensure accessible, affordable healthcare services and improve overall health outcomes in the area.

Conclusion

India’s tribal public healthcare system capacity and the tribal-rural disparity have shown little improvement at the national level between 2011 and 2021. However, improvements have been observed at the state level over the same period, suggesting that healthcare infrastructure is gradually strengthening in tribal areas. Incorporating relevant sub-national tribal demographic, socioeconomic, and facility-level metrics to assess the true functionality of these facilities can provide more insights into expanding infrastructure to ensure timely, culturally relevant, and equitable provision for all tribal communities across the country.
