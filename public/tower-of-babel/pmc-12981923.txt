Early experience of an integrated ambulatory care program for joint replacement surgery
Authors: Samuel B. Morgan, Matey Juric, Ervis Musa, Ariane Parisien, Stéphane Poitras, Joel Werier, Benjamin Sohmer, Paul E. Beaulé
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:To address the increasing demand and backlog of joint replacements, integrated ambulatory care centres (IACs) are being introduced as a public–private partnership in certain areas of Canada. We sought to assess the safety, effectiveness, and sustainability of an IAC for performing hip, knee, and shoulder replacements and to compare patient outcomes between an IAC and a public hospital.Methods:We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent elective arthroplasty surgery at an IAC between Feb. 1, 2023, and Mar. 28, 2024. We matched patients on age, body mass index (BMI), and sex to patients having outpatient surgery at a partner hospital during that same period. The primary outcome was unexpected emergency department (ED) encounters within 1 week of surgery. Secondary outcomes included failure to discharge on the day of surgery, 90-day reoperation rate, and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), including the Oxford Hip Score (OHS), Oxford Knee Score (OKS), and Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System (PROMIS) scores.Results:We included 600 patients who underwent arthroplasty procedures at an IAC (282 hips, 282 knees, and 36 shoulders). The cohort included 318 females and 282 males, with a mean age of 66 years and a mean BMI of 28.7. Of the 600 patients, 16 (2.7%) had an unexpected ED encounter within 7 days, with 2 (0.3%) patients requiring 24-hour admission. At 90 days, 2 (< 1%) patients required admission for reoperation. In the matched cohort of 234 patients, there were no significant differences in unexpected ED encounters at 7 days (3% for IAC v. 5% for public hospital, p = 0.2). The mean improvement in OHS, OKS, and PROMIS (physical) scores was 17.6 (standard deviation [SD] 10.7), 13.1 (SD 11.2), and 8.8 (SD 7.3), respectively, in the IAC group, and 16.1 (SD 12.7), 8.5 (SD 8.9), and 6.2 (SD 7.0), respectively, in the public hospital group.Conclusion:An IAC for joint replacement is a safe and effective model of improving access for patients requiring joint replacement surgery, with no negative effect on subsequent ED encounters or patient-reported outcomes.

Replacement of major joints such as the hip, knee, and shoulder provide substantial improvements in quality of life worldwide.1–3 The demand for these procedures among patients younger than 65 years is projected to increase by more than 50% by 2030.4,5 Initiatives such as Enhanced Recovery after Surgery and improvements in perioperative care have led to total joint arthroplasty surgeries being performed on an outpatient basis in Canada.2,6–9 One of the driving forces of performing outpatient total joint arthroplasty is the limited bed capacity for nonurgent surgical procedures, as well as the constant rising cost of health care in general.10–15 Even so, the overall prevalence of outpatient surgery in Canada is less than 30 percent, highlighting administrative challenges in shifting this phase of care and its associated resources from the inpatient bed unit to the surgical day care unit.16,17

Wait times for joint replacement were already a serious issue in Canada and are now further compounded by the backlog of surgeries resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic.18 According to the Canadian Institute for Health Information, between April and September 2022, only 50% of patients who underwent total hip arthroplasty (THA) and 57% of patients who underwent total knee arthroplasty (TKA) received treatment within the recommended time frame, with the main underlying cause for delay being that volumes were still not at the prepandemic level.16 One of the key challenges to address Canada’s backlog of surgeries is the workforce shortage. Burnout rates are notably higher than before the pandemic and were the highest they have ever been in 2022, given the amount of health care workers working overtime.19 Unfortunately, certain contractual agreements hinder workforce availability by limiting health care workers from providing care at other centres and departments within a hospital.

In response to the increasing surgical backlog, limited health care resources, and workforce shortages, an integrated ambulatory care centre (IAC) for hip, knee and shoulder replacements was established in partnership with a public hospital (PH). We sought to assess the safety, effectiveness, and sustainability of the IAC for performing hip, knee, and shoulder replacements and to compare its patient outcomes with a comparable patient population treated in a PH, with the primary outcome being unexpected emergency department encounters within 7 days.

Methods

Study design

We followed the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data (RECORD) statement.20 We conducted a retrospective analysis of a prospective cohort study of patients who underwent elective arthroplasty surgery on weekends at an IAC between Feb. 1, 2023, and Mar. 28, 2024. The IAC was established at the beginning of February 2023. It is operated by the Academic Orthopedic Surgical Associates of Ottawa (AOAO), which is a private not-for-profit organization. Included in the study were consecutive patients who underwent elective TKA, THA, or total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) at the IAC over 1 year of sustained activity. We excluded patients who were not medically fit for day surgery, patients without adequate home supports for same-day discharge, and patients with case complexity that would have led to substantially longer surgical times than anticipated (e.g., in situ internal fixation). For patients without these exclusion criteria, the decision on whether surgery was performed at the IAC or the PH was based on their spot on the wait-list.

We matched patients from the IAC cohort on age, body mass index (BMI), and sex to patients who underwent outpatient joint replacement surgery at the partner hospital, The Ottawa Hospital, during the same period. We compared reoperation rates, unexpected encounters in the emergency department (ED), and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) between cohorts.

Study setting

Patients were referred to the individual surgeons at the IAC through a centralized intake program and then scheduled for outpatient surgery using previously published guidance.2 Surgeries were performed by 14 arthroplasty-trained surgeons (11 trained in THA and TKA, and 3 trained in TSA), with a team of 31 anesthesiologists coming from 4 regional hospitals, in addition to the partnered hospital.

All other health care professionals at the IAC were independent contractors who worked under the terms of AOAO, with 46 registered nurses covering the operating room suites, surgical day care unit, and postanesthetic care unit, 19 registered practice nurses working in the operating room suites, 11 allied health professionals (5 imaging technologists, 6 physiotherapists), and 1 clinical manager assigning shifts based on individual preference and availabilities. There was no resident involvement at the IAC.

All care at the IAC was provided under the bundle-care program of the Ministry of Health and Long-Term Care of Ontario.21 In other words, the partner hospital subcontracted part of the bundle payment to the private entity (i.e., AOAO) to cover all costs for the delivery of care, including implants, disposables, site setting, and health care provider salaries. Physicians billed the Ministry of Health as per usual practice.

All patients who underwent elective TKA or THA received a spinal anesthetic with lidocaine, given the reduced ambulation time and time to discharge compared with other anesthetics.22 Less than 10% of patients required conversion to a general anesthetic. All TSA procedures were done under regional anesthesia. Postoperatively, all patients ambulated with physiotherapy in the day care unit to obtain clearance for discharge. All elective arthroplasty patients obtained 1 dose of cefazolin postoperatively. All patients had follow-up appointments at 2 weeks for a wound check, radiography, and clinical examination.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was rate of unexpected ED encounters within 7 days of surgery. Secondary outcomes included postoperative admission, reoperation, and PROMs, which included the Oxford Hip Score (OHS), the Oxford Knee Score (OKS), and Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System (PROMIS) scores.23–25 We collected information from chart reviews and routine clinical follow-ups. We calculated PROMs at preoperative encounters and at 3-month follow-up.

To explore impact of the IAC on the perioperative workforce, we categorized the contracted health care providers as either full-time perioperative staff or supplementary staff. For instance, if a contractor had retired but worked part-time with the IAC or had left the perioperative area to work in other areas within the hospital (i.e., medical records, outpatient clinics), these were categorized as supplementary. All other contractors were categorized as full-time perioperative surgery workers.

We asked all program providers to complete a health care provider survey, which consisted of 5 questions focused on provider satisfaction with the IAC program.

Statistical analysis

The sample was a consecutive series of patients treated over a 1-year period to ensure a representative sample, which we then matched to a patient cohort from a PH over the same period. A matched cohort sample size of 234 patients per group allowed us to detect an odds ratio of 1.643 between groups in hospital readmission rates. We summarized descriptive data for patient baseline demographic and surgical characteristics. We calculated means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables, and total counts and cumulative percentages for categorical variables. We used paired t tests to compare means for the matched cohort analysis. We used the McNemar test to examine the differences between categorical variables. The level of statistical significance was set at a p value less than 0.05.

Results

Over the study period, 600 patients underwent surgery at the IAC, of whom 306 (53%) were female, with a mean age of 66.4 (range 32 to 88) years and a mean BMI of 28.7 (range 16.9–52). Of the 600 patients, 274 had TKAs (45.7%), 254 had THAs (42.3%), and 32 had TSAs (5.3%) (Table 1).

Overall, 900 patients underwent outpatient joint replacement surgery at The Ottawa Hospital during the same period. We matched 234 patients in each of the IAC and PH cohorts for a similar mean age (66 [SD 8.7] yr v. 65 [SD 9.6] yr, p = 0.005), sex (60% female v. 60% female], p = 1.0), and BMI (29.6 [SD 5.8] v. 30.0 [SD 6.0], p = 0.04) (Table 1). There were statistically significant differences in age and BMI between cohorts.

More than 65% of the contractors (50 of 77) came from hospitals other than the partner hospital. Staff were fairly evenly split between full-time and supplementary staff (Table 2).

Unexpected emergency department encounters

Of the 600 patients who underwent surgery at the IAC, 16 (2.7%) had an unexpected ED encounter within 7 days of discharge. Of the 16 encounters, 8 (50.0%) were for pain, swelling, or numbness; 7 (43.8%) were for bleeding, oozing, or wound issues; and 1 (6.2%) was for unrelated issues. None of the unexpected health care encounters required admission.

From the matched cohort analysis, postoperative unexpected ED encounters within 24 hours (IAC: n = 1, 0.4% v. PH: n = 3, 1.3%, p = 0.3) and 7 days (IAC: n = 7, 3.0% v. PH: n = 12, 5.1%, p = 0.2) did not differ between groups (Table 3).

Postoperative admission and 90-day reoperation rates

Of the 600 patients in the IAC cohort, 2 (0.33%) could not be discharged on the same day as their procedures, 1 because of postural hypotension and the other because of arrythmia. Both patients were discharged the next day. Two (0.33%) patients had reoperations within 90 days, 1 for periprosthetic joint infection, for which the patient underwent a débridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) procedure 3 weeks after index THA. The other patient underwent a superficial wound débridement with a primary closure 1.5 weeks after their index THA procedure.

From the matched cohort analysis, the rate of reoperation did not significantly differ between the IAC (n = 2, 0.85%) and PH (n = 8, 3.4%; p = 0.06) groups (Table 3).

Patient-reported outcome measures

For the matched cohort analysis, there was a significantly greater improvement in PROMIS physical scores in the IAC cohort (8.8 [SD 7.3]) than in the PH cohort (6.2 [SD 7.0], p = 0.01). No differences between cohorts were noted for changes in OHS scores, OKS scores, or PROMIS mental health scores (Table 3).

Health care provider experience

Of the health care providers who participated in the survey (n = 66), 59% were female. The average number of years’ work experience was 20 and 57% worked casually or part time outside of the IAC (Figure 1). More than 90% of survey respondents either agreed or strongly agreed with the statements “I am able to meaningfully care for my patients,” “I feel I am good at my job,” “I feel a sense of meaning through my work,” and “I am satisfied with the quality and the amount of communication I have with my manager,” while 80% of respondents agreed or strongly agreed with the statement “I feel the pace or volume of my work is manageable” (Table 4).

Discussion

Long wait times for joint replacement surgeries (i.e., accessibility) have been a substantial challenge faced by Canadians over the last decade and, unfortunately, the COVID-19 pandemic further compounded the backlog of cases.18,26 In Ontario alone, the lingering effects of the pandemic led to a 30% reduction in the case volume of elective THA and TKA procedures from April 2020 to February 2021.27 Although substantial effort has been made to increase the surgical throughput, the lack of health care personnel — many of whom either left the perioperative surgical workplace or health care altogether — has represented an additional barrier.19 To address this, several health authorities in Canada have put forth various initiatives, including subcontracting surgical volumes with out-of-province carriers within both Canada and the United States.28 In that regard, the Ontario Medical Association proposed the introduction of IACs to support and supplement the strong hospital system, emphasizing a collaborative approach with increased efficiency while ensuring high levels of quality.29 In late 2021, our group of surgeons established the AOAO to provide a structure with which to enter into partnerships with hospitals to provide musculoskeletal surgical care. This led to the establishment of an IAC in partnership with their primary public hospital to increase and improve access for patients requiring outpatient joint replacement surgery in the treatment of shoulder, hip, and knee arthritis. Our initial experience of this model of care, novel in the Canadian context, has demonstrated it to be safe and effective, compared with our partner hospital, as well as sustainable within a regional health care local delivery system, while enhancing the health care workforce capacity.

Although ambulatory surgery centres are increasingly used in other countries, many of the surgeries performed in these centres occur in full-service hospitals in Canada.30 Given the unacceptable surgical wait-times experienced by patients in Canada, studies from Canadian centres recently have begun to assess the feasibility of ambulatory surgery centres. Recent studies in urologic, bariatric, and gynecologic surgeries additionally support the implementation of ambulatory surgery models across the country.31–33 In one of the largest publicly funded ambulatory surgery centres in the country, Oxley and colleagues34 reported on 11 728 cases over 2 years and reported a 1.2% unplanned admission rate.

Although our numbers were smaller, this initial experience met those standards for shoulder, hip, and knee replacement surgeries. As this new program was responsible for oversight for quality, staffing, instrumentation, and sterilization, establishing mechanisms and protocols that are safe and reliable was essential. Some of these challenges were mitigated with reduced instrument pans, in accordance with Savings Through Lowering of Instrumentation Mass (SLIM) principles, by having extra sets available, and by ensuring a high level of quality for equipment sterilization.35 In addition, unlike laparoscopic or cystoscopic procedures, joint replacement procedures come with greater risk of adverse events (e.g., need for transfusion, arrythmias, hypotension leading to failures to discharge, requiring medical transfer to a full-service hospital). Despite these potential pitfalls, improved protocols — such as use of tranexamic acid and short-acting spinal anesthetics, with minimal use of narcotics — contributed to our low (< 5%) rates of failure to discharge and of adverse events, consistent with much larger experiences in ambulatory surgery centres.13,36–41 Moreover, surgery performed at an ambulatory centre has been shown to be cost-effective for health care systems.42–44 Habbous and colleagues45 showed that outpatient hip and knee arthroplasty is cost-saving during and beyond the perioperative period in comparison with inpatient arthroplasty for similar patients in Ontario.

It is important to note and understand the variability of wait-times for elective surgeries across the country. In their Ontario study, de Jager and colleagues46 found extreme variability for wait times of common elective nonurgent surgical procedures, including 15-fold differences in median wait-times for TKA between facilities. The authors suggested that only increasing the services would not necessarily improve wait-times but rather, they advocated for a coordinated system that matches supply and demand to assist with reducing wait-times. Consequently, as stated by the Ontario Medical Association, new models of care must be integrated into Canadian health care systems, leveraging the strong pre-existing hospital system, while providing opportunities for increases in efficiencies.

Our program engaged multiple interested parties (e.g., nurses, surgeons) within our region, and all the patients followed the normal process of the centralized intake program. This ensured equity in terms of access, whereby patients with arthritis were able to receive timely care. Furthermore, bundle-care requirements of reporting on PROMs were maintained, supporting the effective care delivery of the IAC. This program highlights the need to increase the number of outpatient surgeries, given that only 30% of patients have undergone outpatient joint replacement in Canada, according to the last report of the Canadian Institute for Health Information.47 This is also consistent with results of a study of the Danish National Register by Jensen and colleagues,48 which found that 48% of 166 730 patients who underwent THA, TKA, or unicompartmental knee arthroplasty and who were admitted were eligible for same-day discharge. These findings highlight some of the barriers facing institutions in establishing these types of programs and underline how IACs provide a new environment to facilitate innovation and enhance health care providers’ experience.17

The Canadian health care workforce is challenged by the shortage of nursing staff and allied health care personnel.49 A 2023 article by Duong and Vogel19 reported that more than 250 000 health care workers reported overtime in 2022 and, in a recent survey by the Canadian Federation of Nurses Union, 45% of 4467 practising nurses reported severe burnout, up from 29% before the COVID-19 pandemic.19 More than half of early- and mid-career nurses reported that they were considering leaving their jobs within the next year, highlighting the challenging working conditions. One concern regarding nonpublicly run health care delivery, such as AOAO, is that it would add further stress to the Canadian health care workforce, raising questions about the sustainability of such models. As current labour and union contracts preclude nursing and other staff from working at other centres, as well as across departments or units within a hospital, the IAC model actually increased the workforce availability by just over 50%. This integrated model attracted back individuals with the right skill sets to the perioperative workplace. More importantly, this model highlights the strengths of truly working within a regional health care model with close to three-quarters of the health care workforce coming from nonpartner hospitals. Our findings suggest that if implemented properly, such a model is an effective way of improving surgical wait-times and patient care in Canada while permitting an expansion of the health care workforce. More importantly, improving the experience of providing care has been identified as one of the goals of the quadruple aim in delivery of high-value care, as a means of supporting a safe and productive work environment, defined as the ability to find joy and meaning within one’s contributions to one’s work and efforts within health care.50 The results from our health care provider survey demonstrate the potential influence of a positive workplace environment, found in an IAC, on the satisfaction of health care workers, which could ultimately contribute to improved patient care.

Limitations

As with most retrospective studies, our results were limited by the quality of data available. Although we performed a matched cohort analysis, there were still baseline factors that differed between cohorts, which could have confounded results. At the IAC, there was no resident involvement, in comparison to the PH, where residents are heavily involved, which could have affected results. Another limitation is the potential racial, socioeconomic, and health disparities associated with ambulatory surgery centres,51,52 which we hope were mitigated by the centralized intake process for joint replacements and by having surgeons from multiple institutions. Finally, the establishment of such a program requires a high level of trust and transparency. This requires substantial effort and a common goal of improving access to care, which may be difficult to achieve in other environments. Further work is needed to develop policies and measures, as well as incentives, that will further facilitate the implementation of such novel models of care.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that total joint arthroplasty performed at an IAC is feasible, safe, and effective for appropriately selected patients. More importantly, when functioning within an integrated model of care, this innovative solution has permitted the increase of the health care workforce within the perioperative environment. Other centres in the country should consider these findings when developing ways to address the backlog of surgeries and unacceptably high wait-times for patients.
