The molecular mechanisms and therapeutic potential of p53 in kidney injury repair: a perspective on the p53alpha/delta133p53alpha signaling axis
Authors: Jiayan Fan, Yitian Guo, Guanhua Cui, Li Qiu, Nieke Zhang, Shuchun Tao, Hang Wu, Guangyuan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTP53 is a gene with complicated functions, which is involved in maintaining genomic stability and regulating biological processes such as apoptosis, senescence, and cell-cycle arrest. In recent years, p53 isoforms have been comprehensively investigated in multiple cancer types including melanoma, ovarian cancer, lung cancer, among others. However, the roles of p53 isoforms in kidney injury and repair remain elusive. The latest research progress indicates that p53alpha is involved in the occurrence of acute kidney injury and the subsequent kidney repair process mainly by regulating apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and autophagy. Studies have shown that the p53 isoform delta133p53alpha primarily functions to inhibit apoptosis and facilitate DNA double-strand break repair as well as impacts the transcriptional activity of p53alpha via forming hetero-oligomeric complex. These processes may play a pivotal role in the repair of kidney injury. However, the specific mechanisms by which delta133p53alpha acts in the process of kidney injury and repair remain unclear. Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of p53alpha (e.g. pifithrin-alpha) attenuates G2/M arrest, while proximal tubule-specific TP53 knockout confers protection against ischemia-reperfusion injury. Given the diverse and extensive functions of p53 isoforms, this review aims to explore the novel therapeutic potential of p53 isoforms in kidney injury and repair treatment.

Introduction

Kidney injury, including acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), has attracted widespread global attention. According to statistics from the World Health Organization, approximately 1.5 million patients die annually from kidney injuries such as AKI and CKD [1]. These conditions impose a significant economic burden and psychological stress on both society and families. Consequently, there is an urgent need to gain a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of kidney injury and to explore more effective therapeutic strategies.

TP53 is a multifunctional gene that plays critical roles in regulating cellular processes, including cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and cellular senescence [2]. To date, TP53 has been shown to express nine distinct mRNA variants, which are subsequently translated into twelve isoforms. These isoforms are classified into four primary groups: FLp53, delta40p53, delta133p53, and delta160p53. Each group can be further subdivided into three subtypes (alpha, beta, and gamma) based on alternative splicing patterns in the C-terminal region, resulting in a total of twelve distinct isoforms: p53alpha, p53beta, p53gamma, delta40p53alpha, delta40p53beta, delta40p53gamma, delta133p53alpha, delta133p53beta, delta133p53gamma, delta160p53alpha, delta160p53beta, and delta160p53gamma [3]. While p53 isoforms have been extensively investigated in the context of cancer, their roles in kidney injury remain largely undefined. For example, in the ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) model, p53alpha exacerbates IRI-induced repair dysfunction and further promotes the progression of fibrosis. In the unilateral ureteral obstruction model, inhibition of Snail1 attenuates the upregulation of p53alpha expression, as well as the induction of cell-cycle arrest and fibrogenesis. Additionally, the mechanisms by which isoform interactions regulate kidney injury repair remain unclear. The functions of p53 isoforms are highly complex and exhibit cell-type-specific differences. For detailed information on their functional diversity, refer to Reference [4]. However, the distinct roles of different p53 isoforms – particularly delta133p53alpha – in kidney injury and repair remain poorly understood. Thus, we summarized recent research advancements regarding p53 and its isoforms, with a particular focus on delta133p53 which is related to anti-apoptotic activity and DNA double-strand break repair [5], these findings may help establish potential correlations between p53 isoforms and kidney injury.

p53 and its isoforms

Different domains of p53 and their functions

Understanding the structure of p53 is fundamental to elucidating its diverse biological functions. As a key tumor suppressor, p53 plays an indispensable role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and genomic integrity. It acts as a central regulator of multiple cellular processes, including apoptosis, DNA damage repair, and cell cycle regulation [5–7]. The functional versatility of p53 is achieved via precise structural modifications that enable it to respond to diverse cellular signals.

Structurally, p53 comprises multiple distinct functional domains: the N-terminal transactivation domain 1/2 (TAD1/2, residues 1-40/residues 41–61), the proline-rich domain (PRD, residues 62–93), the DNA-binding domain (DBD), and the tetramerization domain (TD) [8]. Notably, the DBD, together with the N- and C-terminal domains, exists predominantly in intrinsically disordered conformations. These domains undergo extensive post-translational modifications that dynamically regulate the functional specificity and activity of p53 in response to diverse cellular stimuli. Recent structural studies have demonstrated that the DBD, N-terminal, and C-terminal domains function in a coordinated manner to modulate p53′s transcriptional activity and tumor suppressor functions [9]. This intricate domain organization and inter-domain crosstalk enable p53 to integrate multiple cellular signals and execute appropriate biological responses.

The N-terminal domain of p53 comprises two distinct subdomains, TAD1 and TAD2. Specific point mutations in TAD1/TAD2 are reported to be essential for the transactivation of distinct target genes and signaling pathways. TAD1 plays a pivotal role in mediating G1 cell-cycle arrest and apoptotic processes induced by DNA damage. The integrity of TAD1 is critical for sustaining p53′s transactivation function, and concurrent mutations in both TAD1 and TAD2 can completely abrogate this activity [10].

Adjacent to the TAD is the proline-rich domain (PRD), which spans residues 62–93 within the 393-amino acid tetrameric structure of p53. This domain plays a critical role in human physiology, with key functions including: (i) In human malignancies, the PRD frequently harbors hotspot mutations at codons 72, 73, 82, 84, and 89. These mutational hotspots provide valuable insights into specific residues within the PRD that modulate protein–protein interactions and p53 functionality, thereby informing cancer prevention strategies [11]. (ii) Genetic ablation of the PRD in murine models results in homozygous mice that develop diverse spontaneous tumors during aging, particularly malignancies of the B-cell lineage [12]. (iii) XAF-1 – characterized by seven zinc finger domains [13] – is a well-recognized pro-apoptotic factor. Specifically, the fifth zinc finger of XAF-1 interacts with the PXXP motifs (amino acids 62–92) within the PRD of p53, leading to increased p53 abundance and enhanced p53-mediated cellular apoptosis [14].

Furthermore, extensive research has established that the PRD plays a critical role in orchestrating cellular responses to DNA damage, including the induction of apoptosis, cell-cycle arrest, and the regulation of senescence [15]. These findings underlie the multifaceted importance of the PRD in maintaining genomic stability and tumor suppression.

The C-terminal domain (CTD), a specialized lysine-rich structural domain, functions as a critical regulatory module that modulates p53 activity and stability via diverse post-translational modifications. Hamard et al. employed Trp53deltaCTD/deltaCTD murine models to delineate three distinct mechanistic roles of the CTD across various tissues. In the thymus, the CTD of p53 negatively regulates p53-dependent gene expression by suppressing its transcriptional activity. Deletion of the CTD (p53deltaCTD) relieves this repression, leading to marked upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes (e.g. Bax, Pidd). In the liver, however, p53deltaCTD exhibits impaired protein stability, resulting in significantly reduced expression levels. These findings demonstrate that CTD-mediated regulation of p53 is tissue-specific: it primarily inhibits transcriptional activity in the thymus, while governing protein stability in the liver [16]. Furthermore, in both bone marrow and thymus, the CTD exerts inhibitory effects on p53 activity. Hupp, Ahn, Prives, Anderson, and colleagues demonstrated that this domain – enriched in basic amino acids – negatively regulates p53′s transcriptional activity by interfering with the core domain’s sequence-specific DNA-binding capacity [17–19].

The DNA-binding domain (DBD) exhibits a complex architecture, comprising an immunoglobulin-like beta-sheet core, a loop–helix motif, and two large loops stabilized by tetrahedrally coordinated zinc ions. The beta-sheet core consists of two antiparallel beta-sheets, containing four and five beta-strands, respectively. These two sheets are separated by an extended hydrophobic core – this configuration confers structural stability to other structural elements and provides a supportive framework for the binding of other components. The loop–helix motif is critical for the sequence-specific recognition of DNA. It enables p53 to bind precisely to the regulatory regions of target genes, thereby regulating the transcription of associated genes. The large loops contain conserved regions of p53 and are closely linked to tumor-associated mutations; mutations in these regions disrupt p53′s DNA-binding capacity, leading to the loss of its tumor-suppressive function [20,21]. Beyond its sequence-specific DNA-binding capability, the DBD facilitates protein–protein interactions, forming various DBD/protein complexes (e.g. 53BP1 and 53BP2) [22]. These interactions are critical for DNA damage response and repair mechanisms.

The tetramerization domain (TD, residues 326–356) is structurally defined by beta-strands, tight turns, and an alpha-helix in each monomer. Dimerization occurs via beta-strand interactions, with two dimers subsequently assembling into an antiparallel four-helix bundle to form the complete tetrameric structure [23]. The TD not only modulates p53′s transcriptional processes but also exerts allosteric control over its DNA-binding activity. Furthermore, it regulates p53′s post-translational modifications, thereby governing both the protein’s activity and its tetramerization state [24]. Recent experimental evidence indicates that tetramerization has minimal effects on proteasomal degradation, while numerous proteins interact directly with the TD – suggesting that p53 tetramerization substantially regulates protein–protein interactions [25]. The HD mediates increased conformational flexibility in p53′s tertiary structure [21].

The role and mechanisms of p53alpha in kidney injury

Kidney injury

The kidney is a critical organ responsible for maintaining homeostasis and regulating fluid–electrolyte balance in human physiology [40]. However, this vital organ is vulnerable to diverse pathological insults from both endogenous and exogenous factors, resulting in a range of kidney pathologies, including acute kidney injury AKI and CKD. These distinct kidney pathologies are characterized by unique pathophysiological changes and clinical manifestations.

Aki

AKI is fundamentally characterized by an abrupt reduction or complete loss of kidney function. The mechanisms underlying AKI are broad; examples include alterations in vascular tone, local perfusion deficits, activation and release of inflammatory mediators, increased vascular permeability, acute tubular injury or cell death, and compression of intrarenal veins [41]. Reportedly, the overall mortality associated with AKI in adults is approximately twice that observed in children. Epidemiological data on AKI demonstrate that exogenous infections, sepsis, nephrotoxic agents, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and major surgical procedures are the predominant etiologies of AKI in most countries worldwide, with drug-induced nephrotoxicity as the leading cause [42,43]. Given the multifactorial etiology of AKI and the potential interplay of multiple concurrent pathological mechanisms, a comprehensive account is beyond the scope of this discussion. Instead, specific emphasis is placed on acute tubular necrosis and sepsis-induced AKI as representative paradigms.

Among hospitalized patients, acute tubular necrosis is the predominant cause of intrinsic kidney AKI. Pathogenic mechanisms involving kidney ischemia and nephrotoxic insults induce endothelial and epithelial cell injury, culminating in acute tubular necrosis and subsequent development of AKI [44]. Sepsis is the most prevalent precipitating factor for severe AKI in critically ill patients [45,46]. Contemporary definitions characterize sepsis as “a life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection” [47]. This complex pathophysiology encompasses inflammatory cascades, autophagic processes, macrovascular and microvascular dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, apoptotic pathways, and metabolic reprogramming – all of which may interact synergistically to contribute to sepsis-associated AKI. Furthermore, sepsis induces immune dysregulation and directly or indirectly activates the complement system, thereby exacerbating kidney injury [44,48,49].

Chronic kidney disease (CKD)

CKD is a progressive disorder characterized by kidney structural and functional changes resulting from diverse etiologies, including diabetes mellitus, glomerulonephritis, and hypertension. The global burden of CKD is steadily increasing, with millions of individuals affected by CKD-related morbidities annually. Reportedly, CKD will become one of the top five global causes of death by 2040 [50]. A growing body of retrospective studies has shown that CKD is a key factor contributing to the pathogenesis of AKI, and recovery from AKI may also lead to the development of CKD [51].

Extensive research has increasingly clarified the pathophysiological link between AKI and CKD. Following AKI episodes, a subset of patients fails to achieve complete kidney functional recovery, instead progressing to further kidney deterioration. Proximal tubular epithelial cells undergo impaired repair within a pathological microenvironment characterized by persistent inflammation and progressive fibrosis. This maladaptive repair process can lead to long-term structural changes and functional impairment of kidney tissue [52]. Emerging epidemiological and experimental evidence has delineated the association between incomplete post-AKI kidney repair and the development of persistent tubulointerstitial inflammation, fibroblast proliferation, and excessive extracellular matrix deposition. Additionally, a study by Fu et al. identified another critical p53-interaction network in kidney fibrosis: in the setting of chronic inflammation and impaired kidney repair induced by cisplatin nephrotoxicity, p53 activation suppresses the transcriptional expression of SIRT1; downregulation of SIRT1, in turn, further promotes kidney fibrosis via the NF-kappa B pathway. Conversely, administration of the p53 inhibitor piperlongumine-alpha reverses SIRT1 downregulation, thereby attenuating the fibrotic process. This suggests the p53/SIRT1/NF-kappa B axis may represent a key regulatory node in chronic kidney injury [53]. Furthermore, both genetic knockout and treatment with the p53 inhibitor Pifithrin-alpha effectively suppress TGF-beta1-induced expression of genes (e.g. PAI-1) and reduce the morphological transformation of kidney fibroblasts. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TGF-beta1-induced expression of kidney fibrotic genes requires EGFR activation, p53, and reactive oxygen species [54]. These pathological changes are strongly associated with suboptimal post-AKI kidney recovery and may act as critical mediators in the progression to CKD [55].

The role of p53alpha in acute kidney injury

The classical full-length p53alpha is a well-characterized tumor suppressor that plays a critical role in maintaining genomic stability and mediating stress responses. Beyond its canonical tumor-suppressive functions, nuclear p53alpha contributes to key cellular processes including cell cycle regulation, autophagy, and programmed cell death; in contrast, cytoplasmic p53alpha exerts transcription-independent functions via direct interactions with cytoplasmic proteins [55–57]. Notably, accumulating experimental evidence supports a crucial role of p53 in AKI. According to published studies, CX and DH reduce the expression levels of p-p38/p38 MAPK and p53 in rats with cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury and inhibit renal tubular epithelial cell apoptosis. Additionally, cell cycle arrest induced by p53 activation may further promote current terminology following AKI [56]. Kim et al. demonstrated experimentally that p53 is involved in regulating the expression of Plk2 and Nrf2 in response to cisplatin-induced AKI [58]. Intravenous administration of p53 small interfering RNA (siRNA) has been shown to alleviate tubular injury and protect the kidney against cisplatin-induced acute damage [2]. Furthermore, p53alpha exacerbates AKI by promoting tubular cell apoptosis, driving inflammatory responses, and accelerating the progression of fibrotic transformation [59]. Research by Liu et al. revealed that CUL4B overexpression exerts a protective effect against AKI by inhibiting the p53/PAI-1 signaling pathway [60]. Moreover, the CUL4B complex reduces the pro-apoptotic activity of p53alpha through ubiquitination and degradation of p53alpha. Collectively, these findings indicate that CUL4B mitigates AKI via p53 inhibition. However, no relevant evidence regarding the off-target effects of p53alpha has been identified in the published literature.

In recent years, therapeutic strategies for injury-related diseases targeting p53alpha have primarily achieved therapeutic effects by either targeting p53-associated proteins or interacting with p53alpha-related signaling pathways. Extensive research over the past decades has established a significant association between the p53alpha signaling pathway and kidney pathophysiology, particularly in the context of AKI. AKI is frequently classified into prerenal, intrarenal, and postrenal subtypes. Prerenal and postrenal injuries typically exert their effects via extrarenal pathologies that disrupt the autoregulation of kidney blood flow, thereby impairing glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and kidney function. Notably, sustained prerenal or postrenal insults can precipitate further kidney parenchymal damage. In prerenal AKI, reduced kidney perfusion and kidney blood flow drive the decline in GFR. Hypoperfusion may result from dehydration or hemorrhage, whereas heart failure can lead to insufficient effective circulating volume. In contrast, intrarenal and postrenal AKI exhibit extensive etiological heterogeneity [61]. Considerable attention has focused on their distinct mechanisms, as revealed across diverse models including ischemia-reperfusion injury, unilateral ureteral obstruction, acute kidney injury, and cisplatin-induced injury.

Intrarenal kidney injury

Intrarenal kidney injury is typically triggered by sepsis, ischemia, and intoxication. Common clinical scenarios include ischemia-reperfusion injury and cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury. Notably, a pivotal study by Zhang et al. used proximal tubule-specific Lats2 knockout mice to dissect the role of p53alpha in ischemia-reperfusion injury. Their results showed that targeted ablation of Lats2 in proximal tubules exacerbated maladaptive repair following ischemic AKI and promoted more severe kidney fibrosis, indicating that proximal tubule-specific Lats2 deletion aggravates IRI-induced maladaptive repair via p53alpha upregulation [62]. Furthermore, an international research consortium has provided additional experimental evidence demonstrating that specific deletion of TP53 in kidney proximal tubules confers protection against IRI [63].

Postrenal kidney injury

Reports indicate that in postrenal obstruction, retrograde hydrostatic pressure increases, accompanied by a concurrent rise in intratubular pressure. Obstruction of a single kidney may progress to AKI; however, bilateral kidney involvement is generally required to induce overt AKI [61]. Unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) is a pathological condition caused by partial or complete obstruction of a single ureter. The rodent UUO model is widely recognized as a key approach to mimic human chronic obstructive kidney injury [64]. During UUO progression, inhibition of p53alpha via the drug pifithrin-alpha significantly downregulates the expression of CDK1/cyclin B1/D1 and fibrotic matrix molecules. Conversely, TP53 gene knockout alleviates kidney fibrosis and cell apoptosis in UUO mice. Additionally, studies by Gifford et al. demonstrate that inhibiting Snail1 expression via an shRNA plasmid delivery system attenuates UUO-induced upregulation of kidney p53alpha, G2/M phase arrest, and fibrotic progression [2].

Mechanisms of p53alpha-mediated kidney injury

Cell cycle arrest

As a key regulator of cell cycle progression, p53alpha mediates cell cycle arrest at the G2/M checkpoint to prevent mitosis and proliferation of cells with genomic instability, thereby suppressing tumorigenesis and cellular damage. However, under conditions of irreparable cellular damage, p53alpha can alternatively trigger cellular senescence or programmed cell death pathways [57].

In the context of kidney repair, regulation of the p53alpha signaling pathway can ameliorate ischemic AKI. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-exos), for instance, exhibit specific tropism for proximal tubules in ischemic AKI and demonstrate therapeutic potential by ameliorating ischemic AKI and reducing proximal tubular damage in murine models. Recent studies have shown that MSC-exos modulate the p53alpha signaling pathway via delivery of miR-125b-5p molecules, effectively reversing G2/M phase arrest in tubular epithelial cells (TEC) and promoting their proliferation. This mechanism contributes to the improvement of ischemic AKI and facilitates tubular epithelial repair [58].

Conversely, in pathological conditions associated with chronic damage, p53alpha drives kidney fibrosis via hypoxia-induced pathways. Hypoxia is a key driver of progressive kidney dysfunction and plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of kidney fibrosis [65]. Liu et al. investigated the relationship between HIF-1alpha and p53alpha expression by analyzing profibrotic factor expression in renal tubular epithelial cells and using a UUO model. Their findings demonstrate that HIF-1alpha mediates hypoxia-induced upregulation of p53alpha and its downstream profibrotic effects. Experimental results showed that HIF-1alpha overexpression upregulated both p53alpha mRNA and protein expression under hypoxic conditions, whereas HIF-1alpha knockdown exerted the opposite effect. In hypoxic renal tubular cells, p53alpha upregulation suppressed the expression of CDK1, cyclin B1, and cyclin D1, leading to G2/M phase cell cycle arrest and activation of profibrotic signaling pathways mediated by TGF-beta and CTGF. These molecular events ultimately result in extracellular matrix accumulation and kidney fibrogenesis [59].

Beyond hypoxia, another key regulator intersects with the p53alpha pathway in kidney injury: the transcription factor Snail1. Snail1 – a well-established regulator of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) during embryogenesis and oncogenesis – is significantly upregulated during the progression of kidney pathology in both human and murine models. Snail1 has been shown to induce G2/M phase arrest in renal tubular epithelial cells. Notably, Xu et al. have provided experimental evidence that Snail1 not only modulates specific aspects of the EMT process [60,61], but also orchestrates p53-p21 axis-mediated G2/M arrest in the UUO model of kidney injury [62].

Autophagy

Autophagy is a fundamental cellular process that contributes to diverse physiological responses. It exhibits dual regulatory roles in sepsis-associated acute kidney injury and diabetic kidney disease (DKD) [63,64]. Deacetylation of p53alpha enhances autophagy in renal TEC, thereby mitigating sepsis-associated acute kidney injury. In the context of DKD, autophagy impairment occurs via miR-214-mediated suppression of ULK1. Recent studies have identified Fyn as a potential therapeutic target for acute kidney injury, likely through its involvement in autophagic regulation [66]. Experimental evidence indicates that Tgm2 facilitates p53alpha degradation within autophagosomes; in Tgm2 knockout models, p53alpha expression is reduced in proximal tubular cells during autophagy, suggesting a Fyn-Tgm2-p53 regulatory axis [67].

In cisplatin-induced AKI models, Numb is identified as a key protective factor against renal tubular damage [68]. Wang et al. showed that Numb overexpression in NRK-52E cells enhances autophagic activity and increases p53alpha protein levels, defining Numb’s role in promoting p53alpha-mediated autophagy in cisplatin-induced AKI [69].

Apoptosis

Apoptosis is a fundamental cellular defense mechanism that precisely regulates cellular homeostasis by eliminating superfluous and potentially detrimental cells, thereby maintaining organismal integrity. The p53 tumor suppressor protein plays a pivotal role in initiating apoptotic pathways in numerous cancer cell types; pro-apoptotic proteins can induce p53alpha-mediated apoptosis via intrinsic damage signaling [70].

p53alpha is indispensable for regulating E-cadherin expression – a critical adhesion molecule that maintains REC integrity [65,71]. Downregulation of E-cadherin impairs REC structural integrity and promotes apoptotic cell death [72]. Recent studies show that MM102, a selective inhibitor of the MLL1/WDR5 complex, dephosphorylates p53alpha and preserves E-cadherin expression. Zhang et al. observed upregulation of MLL1, WDR5, and trimethylated H3K4 in renal tubular cells from cisplatin-induced AKI murine models, accompanied by increased p53alpha phosphorylation and reduced E-cadherin expression. Notably, E-cadherin silencing abrogates the protective effects of MM102 without altering p53alpha phosphorylation status. These findings establish that in cisplatin-induced AKI, MLL1 drives renal tubular cell apoptosis via p53alpha-dependent suppression of E-cadherin [73].

Aquaporin 1 (AQP-1) mitigates kidney inflammatory responses and promotes tissue repair. In patients with septic AKI, kidney tissue and peripheral blood exhibit significantly reduced AQP1 expression, alongside markedly elevated p53alpha levels [74]. Lv et al. further demonstrated that during different stages of sepsis-associated AKI, AQP-1 and p53alpha levels initially increase and then gradually decline. AQP-1 overexpression reduces p53alpha expression in LPS-induced AKI models and HK-2 cells, attenuating inflammatory responses, apoptosis, and fibrotic processes – thereby exerting protective regulatory effects in AKI pathogenesis [75]. Delta133p53

The function of delta133p53alpha

With the in-depth development of p53 research, the focus of this field has shifted from elucidating the classic mechanisms of action of full-length p53 to exploring the functional specificity of its various isoforms. Through continuous scientific exploration, more than 12 subtypes have been discovered. The biological significance of these isoforms has gradually been revealed and is being progressively translated into clinical applications. Notably, the delta133p53alpha isoform has gained considerable attention and is currently being extensively studied in various fields, including aging, inflammation, breast cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases. Research has demonstrated that delta133p53alpha plays a crucial role in both antagonizing p53-mediated apoptosis and inhibiting cell cycle arrest [27,76–78]. In response to DNA double-strand break stress, delta133p53alpha not only suppresses apoptosis but also enhances all three major DNA double-strand break (DSB) repair pathways: homologous recombination, non-homologous end joining, and single-strand annealing [5]. This isoform facilitates DNA DSB repair by upregulating key repair genes, including RAD51, LIG4, and RAD52 [79].

Beyond its DNA repair functions, delta133p53alpha exhibits several other biological roles: (1) It enhances DNA DSB repair through upregulation of E2F1 expression [80]; (2) Delta133p53alpha activates NF- kappa B signaling, inhibiting apoptosis and promoting inflammation via induction of NF- kappa B target genes such as IL-6 [81]; (3) P53alpha can induce delta133p53alpha to mediate transient inflammatory responses for damage control [82]; (4) Delta133p53alpha interacts with p53alpha to modulate its transcriptional activity [83]; (5) Under subtoxic oxidative stress conditions, delta133p53alpha promotes cell survival by upregulating antioxidant genes (e.g. SOD1 and SESN1) in coordination with p53alpha [84]; (6) Delta133p53alpha inhibits p53alpha-induced apoptosis through activation of Bcl-2, and its knockout enhances p53alpha-mediated apoptosis under stress conditions [76]. In summary, p53alpha primarily contributes to AKI pathogenesis and subsequent kidney repair by regulating apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and autophagy [85]. As a p53 family isoform, delta133p53alpha exhibits distinct functions in inhibiting apoptosis and facilitating DNA DSB repair [4,5,39,86]. These findings lead us to hypothesize that delta133p53alpha and other p53 family isoforms may play critical roles in kidney injury and its repair mechanisms, presenting promising therapeutic potential for the treatment of kidney injury.

The role of Delta133p53 in kidney injury and potential mechanisms

As research on the p53 family advances, investigation focus has gradually expanded from the mechanisms of action of classical full-length p53 to exploring the functions of its isoforms. As a key member of the p53 family, delta133p53 is widely implicated in tumorigenesis, inflammation, and other related fields. While the role of p53alpha in kidney injury is relatively well defined, the mechanisms by which delta133p53 contributes to kidney injury repair remain unclear. We therefore focused further on the kidney injury microenvironment to investigate how delta133p53 may mediate post-injury repair through its core functions. In cellular models of septic AKI, Zhang et al. confirmed that abnormal upregulation of p53alpha in renal tubular epithelial cells is closely associated with lipopolysaccharide-induced apoptosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. Inhibiting p53 expression via AQP-1 overexpression significantly alleviated injury in HK-2 cells, directly validating the pro-pathological role of p53alpha in human renal tubular cell damage [75,87]. More importantly, our literature review revealed that doxorubicin treatment increases delta133p53 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels in human osteosarcoma cell line and normal human dermal fibroblasts cells [88]. Existing studies show that delta133p53 overexpression in human fibroblasts inhibits p53-induced cellular senescence. Furthermore, delta133p53 may interact physically with p53; their heterodimerization at p53-responsive promoters establishes a dominant-negative mode of transcriptional regulation. However, whether this regulatory pathway is directly involved in kidney fibrosis requires further verification [89]. According to established literature, Lu et al. identified two primary biological functions of delta133p53 in a zebrafish model: anti-apoptotic activity and promotion of DNA DSB repair [5]. Experimental evidence demonstrates that elevated delta133p53 levels antagonize p53alpha-mediated apoptosis via bcl2L activation, whereas genetic ablation of delta133p53 enhances p53alpha-dependent apoptotic responses under stress conditions [76]. Delta133p53 promotes DSB repair by transcriptionally upregulating key repair genes (e.g. RAD51, RAD52, LIG4) [5]. Additionally, research has shown that delta133p53 overexpression in normal fibroblasts impairs p53alpha’s transcriptional activity and its ability to regulate cell cycle arrest in response to DNA damage [90].

Delta133p53 inhibits apoptosis to alleviate kidney injury

Apoptosis is a form of programmed cell death associated with multiple stages of organismal development, including cell turnover, embryonic development, and tissue regeneration [91]. According to the literature, apoptosis is mediated by two distinct pathways: the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways. The intrinsic pathway is initiated within mitochondria and relies on proteins localized to the mitochondrial membrane; its activating signals include DNA damage, reactive oxygen species, and hypoxia. In contrast, the extrinsic pathway is triggered by the activation of cell surface receptors – for example, death receptors such as FAS or tumor necrosis factor can receive apoptotic signals from their respective ligands [91]. Different apoptotic pathways may initiate distinct molecular cascades or alternative forms of programmed cell death [92]. Inhibition of necroptosis has been shown to exert a significant protective effect against AKI. Similarly, inhibiting apoptotic proteins such as Bax/Bak confers an important protective role in kidney ischemia-reperfusion injury [93]. Chen et al. investigated the function of delta113p53 in zebrafish – the ortholog of human delta133p53 in this organism. Their experimental results demonstrate that delta133p53 antagonizes p53alpha-mediated apoptosis via bcl2L activation [76]. Thus, delta133p53 is highly likely to alleviate kidney injury by inhibiting apoptosis.

Delta133p53 suppresses kidney injury by regulating transcriptional activities of p53alpha

Transcriptional regulation is a fundamental process that sustains normal bodily function. A recent study demonstrates that delta133p53 alters p53alpha conformation via heterooligomerization, thereby inhibiting its DNA-binding ability and directly downregulating p53alpha transcriptional activity. Herewith, delta133p53 overexpression modulates p53alpha transcriptional activity, which in turn influences cellular apoptosis and cell cycle arrest. Notably, delta133p53 forms heterotetramers with p53alpha; through direct physical interaction, it alters p53alpha conformational state and thus modulates its activity. Additionally, heterooligomerization induces conformational changes in the p53alpha DNA-binding domain (DBD), preventing key amino acids from binding specific DNA sequences and impairing its DNA-binding capacity. Collectively, delta133p53 heterooligomerizes with p53alpha, which significantly suppresses p53alpha transcriptional activity [90].

Delta133p53 mitigates kidney injury via inhibition of DNA damage

Multiple studies indicate that DNA damage is associated with kidney injury [94]. Park et al. demonstrated that insufficient TRIP13 expression following kidney ischemia-reperfusion injury leads to persistent tubular injury; this phenomenon may relate to increased accumulation of DSBs in non-kidney cells [95]. Recent studies have shown that substantial peroxynitrite is generated after kidney ischemia-reperfusion, and peroxynitrite directly induces DSBs [96]. Given that the delta133p53 isoform promotes DNA double-strand break repair by transcriptionally upregulating key repair genes (e.g. RAD51, RAD52, LIG4) [5], it is reasonable to hypothesize that delta133p53 alleviates kidney injury by reducing DNA damage.

On the other hand, DNA double-strand break repair is closely linked to the expression of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). The kidneys require substantial adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to support their activity during reabsorption; accordingly, they contain abundant mitochondria and mtDNA. Additionally, a significant correlation has been established between reduced mitochondrial DNA copy number, impaired kidney function, and increased risk of adverse outcomes [97]. When mtDNA is damaged and undergoes replication in vivo, the likelihood of developing AKI increases [98]. In contrast to mtDNA, however, nuclear DNA is less susceptible to damage, owing to the protective effects of histones and DNA repair mechanisms [99].

Targeting p53alpha as potential therapy of kidney injury

Literature demonstrates that p53alpha plays a pivotal role in the progression and prognosis of kidney injury. p53alpha’s involvement in pathological responses is evident across diverse conditions, including AKI, CKD, and IRI. With ongoing research advances, investigators have established that agents such as mitotic arrest deficient 2 like 2B (MAD2B), phosphatase and tensin homolog protein (PTEN), aristolochic acid, and cisplatin hold substantial significance for the targeted treatment of kidney injury.

Mad2b

Recent studies demonstrate that varying MAD2B expression levels yield distinct in vivo outcomes. MAD2B knockdown intensifies cisplatin-induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in HK-2 cells. Conversely, MAD2B overexpression reduces p53alpha levels and mitigates cisplatin-induced G1-phase cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. Collectively, these findings identify MAD2B as a novel therapeutic target for ameliorating AKI [100].

PTEN

PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog deleted on chromosome 10) is a phosphatase that negatively regulates the serine/threonine protein kinase Akt [101]. Inhibition of PTEN activity exacerbates cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury and apoptosis [102]. Furthermore, activation and upregulation of p53alpha expression are associated with PTEN loss. Paul et al. demonstrated experimentally that p53alpha is a target of PTEN loss or inactivation [103], highlighting a close relationship between p53alpha, PTEN, and kidney injury.

Aristolochic acid

Aristolochic acid (AA) is a compound found in plants of the Aristolochia genus. It primarily comprises aristolochic acid I (AA1) and aristolochic acid II, with AA1 being the most abundant. AA is the primary etiological agent of aristolochic acid nephropathy. Mateja et al. experimentally demonstrated that p53alpha modulates AA1 activation and cytotoxicity via Nqo1, and exerts a significant regulatory effect on AA1-induced kidney injury. Specifically, Trp53(+/+) mice exhibited the most severe damage, whereas Trp53(-/-) mice showed the least [104].

Cisplatin

Cisplatin, a classic chemotherapeutic agent, is widely used to treat diverse cancers, including reproductive system cancers, lung cancer, and head-and-neck tumors. However, its clinical utility is limited by nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity, with nephrotoxicity being particularly prominent. Clinically, nephrotoxicity frequently leads to AKI and chronic kidney insufficiency [105]. Recent studies demonstrate that cisplatin-induced kidney injury involves multiple complex cellular signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms. Fu et al. focused on p53alpha-mediated chronic kidney pathology in proximal tubules following cisplatin treatment, revealing the pivotal role of p53 in cisplatin-induced chronic kidney injury [106]. Wang et al. emphasized that p53alpha promotes inflammation and fibrosis by inhibiting SIRT1 and activating NF-kappa B [53].

Additionally, via a review of relevant literature, we summarized research progress on p53-targeted interventions in kidney-associated diseases and cell models. This includes diverse p53-related intervention strategies, such as different intervention types (e.g. agents, proteins, and drugs), their corresponding experimental models, and the target proteins modulated by these interventions. Among drug-related examples, our literature review revealed that vitamin D may exhibit nephrotoxicity via interaction with toxic metals [107]. However, vitamin D can exert a protective effect against nephrotoxicity in patients with type 2 diabetes [108] and alleviate doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity [109]. Dimethylthiourea (DMTU) is a potent hydroxyl radical scavenger. Literature reviews showed that DMTU prevents cisplatin-induced acute renal failure in mice. Wei et al. performed hematoxylin-eosin staining on mouse kidneys, observing that cisplatin induced significant kidney damage (e.g. renal tubular dilation and fragmentation). They also assessed the effect of DMTU on cisplatin-induced renal tubular cell apoptosis. Collectively, their experiments demonstrated that DMTU exerts a nephroprotective effect against cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity, ameliorating tissue damage and renal tubular cell apoptosis [109,110]. Conclusions

Emerging evidence suggests that the tumor suppressor protein p53 orchestrates a complex response to kidney injury by coordinating networks governing cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and autophagy. Accumulating evidence further indicates that this p53-mediated regulatory mechanism is consistent across studies. Moreover, we observed that the dual-gene structure of the TP53 gene is highly evolutionarily conserved. Existing studies have confirmed its stability across diverse species, including zebrafish, Drosophila, mice, and humans [21]. This evolutionary conservation provides a foundational basis for extrapolating findings from animal models to future research and strengthens our rationale for further exploring its potential functional relevance in the human kidney.

Extensive research demonstrates that p53 and its related isoforms are critically linked to key cellular processes, including apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, and autophagy. This underscores the potential involvement of the p53 family and its isoforms not only in oncogenesis but also in the pathogenesis of kidney injury. While numerous studies have focused on delta133p53 in oncological contexts – leading to its characterization as a tumor-promoting factor – this perspective is likely context-dependent. Given the functional similarities between delta133p53 and p53, and considering p53′s established role as a “molecular policeman” and “cellular guardian,” the biological functions of these proteins should be evaluated based on their specific cellular contexts rather than generalized assumptions. Current research indicates that delta133p53 exerts significant regulatory roles in DNA damage response and transcriptional regulation, particularly in anti-apoptotic processes and DNA double-strand break repair – both of which are crucial mechanisms in kidney injury and repair.

Moreover, p53 functions vary by its subcellular localization. Transcriptional activation of nuclear p53 contributes to pathways governing cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and autophagy, whereas cytoplasmic p53 exerts effects via direct binding to cytoplasmic proteins [85]. Reports from international research groups indicate that p53 in proximal tubules promotes the development and progression of AKI. By establishing mouse models under diverse conditions, Zhang et al. demonstrated that p53 is widely distributed in the nuclei of renal tubular cells, with proximal tubular p53 playing a critical role in ischemic AKI. Additionally, relevant studies have reported a marked increase in p53 expression in the kidney medulla within 24 h of ischemia-reperfusion [75].

This review aims to establish a theoretical framework for future investigations into the role of delta133p53 in kidney injury and repair. Subsequent studies should focus on elucidating the specific functions of delta133p53 in kidney injury and delineating its molecular mechanisms, thereby facilitating the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. Continued exploration of p53 and its associated isoforms in kidney injury may uncover novel therapeutic targets, advance our understanding of kidney injury and repair mechanisms, and potentially lead to more effective treatment modalities.
