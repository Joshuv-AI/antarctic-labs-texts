Anisometropia, Strabismus, and Amblyopia in Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder, Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder, and Their Comorbidity
Authors: Xiu Nian Chen, Yuzhou Zhang, Xiu Juan Zhang, Charlene Yim, Ka Wai Kam, Mandy PH Ng, Patrick Ip, Ian CK Wong, Wei Zhang, Alvin L Young, Clement C Tham, Chi Pui Pang, Li Jia Chen, Jason C Yam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore the association of anisometropia, strabismus, and amblyopia with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and their comorbidity.DesignPopulation-based cross-sectional study.SubjectsChildren aged 6-10 years in Hong Kong.MethodsParticipants underwent comprehensive ocular examinations. Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and ASD diagnoses were made by qualified physicians using International Classification of Diseases 10 criteria.Main Outcome MeasuresThe prevalence of anisometropia, strabismus and amblyopia.ResultsA total of 10 936 children were included, comprising 153 (1.4%) with ASD only, 413 (3.8%) with ADHD only, 276 (2.5%) with comorbid ASD–ADHD, and 10 094 (92.3%) healthy controls. The prevalence of anisometropia was 9.8%, 8.7%, 15.2%, and 6.0% across the respective groups (P < 0.001). The corresponding prevalence of strabismus was 4.6%, 2.9%, 4.0%, and 1.7% (P < 0.001), and for amblyopia, it was 7.2%, 2.7%, 4.0%, and 2.9% (P = 0.03). Autism spectrum disorder (ASD only plus comorbidity) was associated with higher risks of anisometropia (odds ratio [OR] = 1.51, P = 0.04), strabismus (OR = 2.30, P = 0.01), and amblyopia (OR = 1.91, P = 0.02), whereas ADHD (ADHD only plus comorbidity) was associated with anisometropia (OR = 1.58, P = 0.008) after adjusting for covariates. In subgroup analyses, the ASD-only group showed associations with strabismus (OR = 2.47, P = 0.04) and amblyopia (OR = 2.46, P = 0.02). The comorbid ASD–ADHD group exhibited a higher risk of anisometropia (OR = 2.17, P < 0.001), whereas children with ADHD only showed no association with the aforementioned ocular disorders.ConclusionsChildren with ASD only and ASD–ADHD comorbidity were associated with increased and distinct patterns of anisometropia, strabismus, and amblyopia, while children with ADHD only showed no such association.Financial Disclosure(s)The author has no/the authors have no proprietary or commercial interest in any materials discussed in this article.Keywords: Anisometropia, Strabismus, Amblyopia, ASD, ADHD

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) are both common neurodevelopmental disorders that typically onset in childhood. Autism spectrum disorder is characterized by impaired social communication and interaction, along with repetitive or restrictive patterns of interests and behaviors,1 and affects 1%–3% of children globally.2 Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder is defined by the presence of impairing symptoms of inattention and/or hyperactivity–impulsivity that onset before age 12, are present across ≥2 settings, and cannot be better explained by another condition,1 with a global prevalence of 5%–8% in children.3 Studies have reported significantly higher rates of ophthalmic disorders in children with ASD compared to typically developing peers.4 Regarding anisometropia in ASD, the current evidence is mixed. Some studies found no association,5,6 while another reported a higher risk of anisometropia among ASD.7 Higher prevalence of strabismus and amblyopia in ASD was observed.6,8 Gronlund et al9 reported a higher prevalence of anisometropia in individuals with ADHD, although the difference was not significant. A higher risk of strabismus and amblyopia in children with ADHD was also reported.10,11

Autism spectrum disorder and ADHD both fall within the broader category of neurodevelopmental disorders, with a remarkably high rate of co-occurrence. An estimated 40%–70% of individuals with ASD meet the criteria for ADHD, while 20%–60% of those with ADHD meet the criteria for ASD.12,13 However, previous reports of ophthalmic problems in ASD or ADHD have not controlled for the influence of the other condition; thus, whether the elevated prevalence of ocular disorders is shared by both disorders or is specific to one remains unclear. Furthermore, it has been suggested that comorbidity is not merely an additive combination of the 2 disorders but rather represents a neurofunctionally distinct phenotype.14 Therefore, investigating how ocular disorders manifest in individuals with comorbid ASD–ADHD is of significant clinical interest.

Addressing these questions could assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients and deepen our understanding of the pathophysiological distinctions between ASD only, ADHD only, and their comorbidity. This knowledge could also facilitate the development of ocular biomarkers to differentiate these conditions in the future.

Methods

Study Population

Participants for this cross-sectional study were recruited from the ongoing Hong Kong Children Eye Study, a population-based study on ocular conditions among children aged 6 to 10 years attending primary school,15, 16, 17, 18, 19 using data collected between 2016 and 2023. A flow diagram of the study is shown in Figure 1. Once participants’ consent forms were obtained, they were invited to the Chinese University of Hong Kong Eye Centre for comprehensive ocular examinations and completion of standardized questionnaires according to a unified protocol.15 The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee board of the Chinese University of Hong Kong, and all study procedures adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all children and their parents prior to their participation in the study, including agreement for direct access to the child’s original medical records for verification of clinical trial procedures and/or data.

Study flow diagram. ADHD = attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder; ASD = autism spectrum disorder; BCVA = best-corrected visual acuity; SDQ = Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire; SWAN = Strengths and Weaknesses of ADHD-symptoms and Normal-behaviors Questionnaire.

Identification of Children with ASD or ADHD and Assessment of Symptoms

For children who underwent ocular examinations and whose parents provided informed consent, we extracted their ASD and ADHD diagnoses, along with the corresponding prescription information from the Hong Kong hospital authority electronic medical records system.20 The diagnosis was made by a psychiatrist based on International Classification of Diseases 10 criteria, and all records were ensured to be dated prior to the ocular examination date. Children diagnosed with both ASD and ADHD were included in the comorbidity group, those diagnosed with only one were defined as ASD only or ADHD only, and those without a diagnosis of either were defined as controls. Participants treated with methylphenidate or amphetamine for ≥1 month prior to the ocular examination were defined as having a stimulant treatment history.

The Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire and the Strengths and Weaknesses of ADHD-symptoms and Normal-behaviors Questionnaire were completed by children’s parents. The Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire comprises 25 items, from which the 5-item peer relationship problems subscale was used as a proxy for the ASD symptoms rating scores, with higher sum scores indicating more severe symptoms.21 The 5 items are: “Rather solitary, prefers to play alone,” “Has at least one good friend,” “Generally liked by other children,” “Picked on or bullied by other children,” and “Gets along better with adults than with other children,” The Strengths and Weaknesses of ADHD-symptoms and Normal-behaviors Questionnaire scale consists of 18 items rated on a 7-point scale. The average score across all items provides the ADHD symptoms rating score, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms.22

Ocular Examinations

Cycloplegic autorefraction was performed using an autorefractor (ARK-510A; NIDEK Co, Ltd). Two cycles of cyclopentolate, 1% (Alcon), and tropicamide, 1% (Santen), were administered 10 minutes apart. Five readings, all of which had to be <0.25 diopters (D) apart, were obtained and averaged, at 30 minutes after the last drop of cycloplegic agent. If cycloplegia failed to reach these criteria, the children were excluded from the analysis.15 Certified ophthalmologists performed standardized ocular alignment evaluations employing cover–uncover test with high-contrast fixation stimuli positioned at near (33 cm) and distance (6 m) intervals. Visual acuity was measured both with spectacles and without spectacles using a retro-illuminated logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) chart (Nidek) containing 70 optotypes arranged in 14 lines. Monocular testing was conducted at 5 m, first on the right eye then the left. The LogMAR scale ranged from 1.0 (20/200 Snellen) to –0.3 (20/10 Snellen). When neither unaided nor aided acuity reached 0.1 LogMAR (20/25 Snellen), best-corrected visual acuity was determined through manifest refraction performed by an optometrist using trial frames.

Definition

Spherical equivalent was calculated as the spherical power plus half the cylindrical power. Anisometropia was defined as an absolute interocular spherical equivalent difference of ≥1.0 D. Strabismus was evaluated using the cover–uncover test at both near and distance fixation. The assessments were categorized into 8 possible outcomes: orthotropia (0), exophoria (1), intermittent exotropia (2), constant exotropia (3), esophoria (4), esotropia (5), right hypertropia (6), and left hypertropia (7). Strabismus was defined as the presence of a manifest strabismus (codes 2, 3, 5, 6, or 7) identified at either testing distance. Amblyopia was defined as a best-corrected visual acuity between 0.3 LogMAR (20/40 Snellen) and 1.0 LogMAR (20/200 Snellen) inclusive, in one or both eyes, provided that no underlying organic or structural ocular pathology could account for the visual impairment. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, the analysis focuses on the prevalence of anisometropia, strabismus, and amblyopia among children with ASD, ADHD, and their comorbidity.

Statistical Analyses

The demographic characteristics of the 4 groups (ASD only, ADHD only, comorbidity, and control) were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. The analysis of variance was used to compare the continuous variables between the 4 groups, while the chi-square test was used to compare the categorical variables. Unequal group sizes were addressed by using Welch analysis of variance when Levene test indicated variance heterogeneity and Fisher exact test for low expected cell counts in unadjusted analyses. Logistic regression was used to assess the associations between groups and the prevalence of anisometropia, strabismus, or amblyopia. Twelve covariates were included in regression models in a stepwise manner. Model 1 included age, sex, spherical equivalent, and stimulant treatment history. Model 2 added low family income, low maternal or paternal educational level, advanced maternal age, and maternal smoking or drinking during pregnancy to the covariates from Model 1. Model 3 further added parental-reported developmental delay or epilepsy to the covariates from Model 2. To account for potential mediating pathways among the 3 ocular disorders, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by mutually adjusting for the other coexisting ophthalmic conditions in our multivariable logistic regression models. All statistical analyses were performed using the R version 4.3.0, with a 2-sided P value of <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Population Demographics and Ocular Characteristics

The study included 10 936 children, of whom 429 had ASD and 689 had ADHD. Specifically, 153 (1.4%) were diagnosed with ASD only, 413 (3.8%) with ADHD only, 276 (2.5%) with comorbid ASD–ADHD, and 10 094 (92.3%) served as controls (Table 1). Among children with ADHD (ADHD only plus comorbidity), 30.6% (n = 211) had a stimulant treatment history. The ASD-only, ADHD-only, and comorbid groups were older (8.89 ± 2.71, 8.08 ± 2.08, 8.53 ± 2.60, and 7.33 ± 0.90 years, respectively; P < 0.001) and had a higher proportion of males (79.7%, 72.9%, 82.5%, and 50.4%, respectively; P < 0.001) compared with controls.

Discussion

This study found that ASD (ASD only together with comorbidity) was associated with a higher risk of anisometropia, strabismus, and amblyopia, while ADHD (ADHD only together with comorbidity) was associated with a higher risk of anisometropia. In the subgroup analyses, only children with comorbid ASD–ADHD exhibited a higher risk of anisometropia, and the ASD-only group showed a higher risk of strabismus and amblyopia. In the overall population, greater severity of ASD symptoms was associated with a higher risk of anisometropia and amblyopia, whereas ADHD symptom severity and stimulant treatment history were not associated with these ocular disorders.

This study is one of the first to evaluate anisometropia, strabismus, and amblyopia in patients with comorbid ASD–ADHD. We found that the comorbid group was associated with a twofold higher risk of anisometropia than controls after controlling for covariates. Chantiluke et al14 used functional magnetic resonance imaging to compare prefrontal function in youth with only ASD, only ADHD, comorbid ASD–ADHD, and controls. Compared with the noncomorbid and control groups, the comorbid group exhibited unique and more severe impairments in the lateral and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, ventral striatum, and anterior cingulate cortex. It is possible that the brain's capacity to effectively regulate the symmetrical development of both eyes might be compromised to a greater extent in children with comorbid ASD–ADHD compared with noncomorbid peers and controls. However, this speculation requires further investigation. The prevalence of strabismus and amblyopia in the comorbid ASD–ADHD group fell between that of the ASD-only and ADHD-only groups; further research is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD only plus comorbidity) was associated with a higher risk of anisometropia, strabismus, and amblyopia compared with controls, which is consistent with previous reports.6,8 Patients with ASD showed abnormalities in higher-level visual functions that are strongly associated with strabismus and amblyopia, such as visual search,23 global motion processing,24 and binocular rivalry.25,26 Additionally, many genetic syndromes causing ASD, including FOXP1 disorder,27 Fragile X syndrome,28 and Rett syndrome,29 are known to be associated with strabismus. However, when examining the ASD-only group, a higher risk was observed for strabismus and amblyopia, but not for anisometropia. It is possible that the association between ASD (ASD only plus comorbidity) and anisometropia was mainly driven by comorbidity rather than ASD only. Further studies are needed to explore the underlying mechanisms.

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD only plus comorbidity) was associated with a higher risk of anisometropia, but not with strabismus or amblyopia. However, when examining the ADHD-only group, no significant associations with the aforementioned ocular disorders were observed. Similar to ASD, it is possible that the association between ADHD and anisometropia was mainly driven by comorbidity rather than ADHD only. Previous clinical studies have reported a higher risk of strabismus or amblyopia in ADHD10,11 or a higher risk of ADHD in children with strabismus or amblyopia;30, 31, 32 however, these either lacked adjustment for covariates or used nonstandardized ADHD diagnoses. Mendelian randomization analysis revealed that the genetic liability to ADHD—after excluding any risk single nucleotide polymorphisms related to ASD—was not associated with a higher risk of amblyopia, anisometropia, or strabismus.33 This suggests no causal relationship from ADHD to these ocular disorders at the genetic level.

Although our study reported the prevalence of strabismus, amblyopia, and anisometropia separately among children with ASD and ADHD, the relationships among these conditions are complicated and highly correlated. Anisometropia and strabismus are frequently considered among the leading primary factors associated with pediatric amblyopia.34,35 Anisometropia leads to retinal image defocus, while strabismus causes diplopia or visual confusion; both mechanisms compel the visual cortex to suppress inputs from the weaker eye, ultimately disrupting cortical visual development and leading to amblyopia. Additionally, uncorrected anisometropia can lead to refractive amblyopia, and the resulting monocular visual deprivation can disrupt sensory binocular fusion, eventually manifesting as secondary sensory strabismus.36 In children with neurodevelopmental disorders, this intricate network of ophthalmic conditions may be exacerbated by behavioral and sensory vulnerabilities. To account for these potential mediating pathways, we performed a sensitivity analysis by adjusting for coexisting ophthalmic conditions in our regression models. While the results revealed significant associations among these 3 ocular disorders, the ASD-only group was still associated with strabismus and amblyopia, as was the comorbid ASD–ADHD group with anisometropia.

Several limitations in this study should be acknowledged. First, although stimulant treatment history was not significantly associated with ocular disorders in this study, given that stimulant treatment was observational and nonrandomized, the potential effect of stimulant on these ocular disorders should be examined in longitudinal studies. Second, ASD symptoms were assessed using the “peer relationship problems” subscale of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire. While this captures a key social component, it represents only a subset of core ASD traits rather than providing a comprehensive clinical assessment. This partial measurement may lead to differences between analyses using categorical ASD diagnosis and those based on symptom scores, as the latter may reflect broader but less specific social–behavioral variation across the population. Third, the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes the establishment of causal relationships between neurodevelopmental disorders and the evaluated ophthalmic outcomes. Furthermore, any underlying mechanisms proposed in the discussion require further validation.

In conclusion, ASD (ASD only plus comorbidity) showed a higher risk of anisometropia, strabismus, and amblyopia, while ADHD (ADHD only plus comorbidity) showed a higher risk of anisometropia. In stratified analysis, children with ASD only were associated with higher risks of strabismus and amblyopia. The comorbid ASD–ADHD group exhibited a higher risk of anisometropia, whereas children with ADHD only showed no association with the aforementioned ocular disorders. These findings underscore the need for both psychiatrists and ophthalmologists to screen for these ocular disorders in ASD and ADHD and also provide clues to potential underlying mechanisms.
