Economic evaluation of embryo transfer strategies in in vitro fertilization (IVF): a systematic review
Authors: Maryam Eri, Aziz Rezapour, Somayeh Afshari, Jalal Arabloo, Samaneh Ziyaee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aims to systematically review the economic evaluations of various embryo transfer strategies, including single embryo transfer (SET), double embryo transfer (DET) and others in IVF.MethodsWeb of Science, Scopus, PubMed, Embase, and the NHS Economic Evaluation Database were searched since January 1980 to 1 October 2023. Full economic evaluations comparing embryo transfer strategies were included; while review articles, editorials, abstracts, and non-English articles were excluded. The CHEERS 2022 checklist was used to assess the quality of the included studies.FindingsOut of 4,336 records, 16 studies were included, mostly from Europe (75%), with a few from North America (19%) and Asia (6%). Most studies (94%) assessed the cost-effectiveness of SET versus DET using cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA), one study (6%) used cost-utility analysis (CUA). The Markov model was most common, with some using decision-tree or other modeling approaches. Study perspectives were societal (50%), healthcare system (25%), service provider (12.5%), or insurer (12.5%). The most reported outcomes were live birth rate (50%) and incremental cost per live birth (56%), while other outcomes, such as expected life years or quality-adjusted life years were less frequently reported.ConclusionFindings indicate that SET and SET-based strategies, such as combining fresh and frozen SET cycles or mild stimulation protocols, are often more cost-effective than DET and other strategies, especially among younger women, by reducing maternal and neonatal complications and long-term healthcare costs. Cost-effectiveness, however, varies by maternal age, healthcare system context, modeling approach, patient preferences, and societal willingness-to-pay thresholds. Most evidence comes from high-income countries, but the findings provide insights to guide evidence-based embryo transfer decisions that balance clinical effectiveness, patient preferences, and economic sustainability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01181-x.

Introduction

Infertility is a public health concern that significantly affects both individuals and society [1]. It can result from various factors affecting either the male or female reproductive system [2]. Infertility is a disease of the reproductive system, most often defined as the failure to achieve a clinical pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse [3]. The estimated prevalence of infertility among couples of reproductive age ranges from 12.6% to 17.5% worldwide [4]. The World Health Organization reports that around one in six individuals of reproductive age globally will experience infertility at some point in their lives [2].

Among the available treatments for infertility, in vitro fertilization (IVF) has become one of the most common methods to help patients conceive. Over the past two decades, the success rate of IVF pregnancies has increased significantly [5]. A common approach to improve the chances of successful pregnancy in IVF is the transfer of multiple embryos [6]. In this method, multiple fertilized eggs are transferred to the mother to increase the likelihood of achieving pregnancy. However, transferring more than one embryo during IVF significantly increases the risk of multiple pregnancies, which pose greater health risks compared to singleton pregnancies [7]. These risks include complications for the mother, such as gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, and preterm birth [8], as well as risks for the baby, including low birth weight and prematurity [9–12]. Perinatal mortality is significantly higher in twins compared to singletons [8, 10], and parents of multiples often report increased stress [13].

In addition to health risks, multiple pregnancies substantially increase healthcare costs [10]. The economic burden on healthcare systems is significant. In Australia, for example, the average cost of twin births resulting from Assisted Reproductive Technologies (ART) was three times higher than that of singleton births, and up to 11 times greater for higher-order multiple births [10, 14]. As a result, concerns about the medical, social, and economic impacts of multiple pregnancies in ART [8] have led to a growing trend towards single embryo transfers (SET) to reduce healthcare costs associated with multiple pregnancies [11, 15].

However, the rules regarding the number of embryos transferred vary widely across countries. In some European countries, policies place restrictions on double embryo transfer (DET) and allow it only in certain circumstances, such as advanced maternal age or repeated treatment failures. However, in many other countries, double and multiple embryo transfers are still routinely performed [9]. Although most economic evaluations of embryo transfer have been conducted in high-income countries (HICs), which limits their direct applicability to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), these studies can still provide valuable insights into methodology and cost-effectiveness patterns. This is especially important given the increasing use of ART in LMICs, where clinical guidelines and local economic evidence are still limited. Therefore, understanding the economic consequences associated with SET, DET, and other transfer strategies can help improve clinical decision-making and policy-making in health systems worldwide.

Previous systematic reviews of the economic evaluation of ART [16–19] have focused mainly on methodological aspects [17, 18] or have provided only limited comparisons between SET and DET [16]. The most recent review by Olive et al. (2024) [19], summarized ART-related costs in HICs but did not specifically analyze embryo transfer strategies. To address this gap, the present study systematically reviews economic evaluations of different embryo transfer strategies in IVF—including single, double, and combined approaches—to provide updated insights into their cost-effectiveness and to strengthen evidence-based decision-making in reproductive health policy.

Methods

This systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [20]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024514230).

Identification of the research question

The primary research question was whether different embryo transfer strategies in IVF, including SET, DET, and multiple embryo transfer, differ in terms of cost-effectiveness. This question was formulated based on the PICO framework: Population (P): Infertile couples undergoing IVF; Intervention (I): SET or other embryo transfer strategies; Comparison (C): Alternative embryo transfer strategies (e.g., DET, multiple embryo transfer); and Outcome (O): Economic evaluation outcomes such as incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), cost per live birth, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs).

Search strategy/ Information sources

An initial search was conducted to determine whether there were enough primary studies, previous systematic reviews, and registered protocols. The final comprehensive search was completed on October 1, 2023, covering studies published from January 1980 to October 2023. The search strategy was developed using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and relevant keywords, including “in vitro fertilization,” “IVF,” “embryo transfer,” “single embryo transfer,” “double embryo transfer,” “economic evaluation,” “cost-effectiveness,” “cost-utility,” and “cost-benefit”.

We searched five electronic databases: Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, Embase, and the NHS Economic Evaluation Database. Gray literature was also explored using Google Scholar. Full details of the search strategy are provided in the Supplementary File (see Additional file 1).

Eligibility criteria

In this systematic review, all studies that used a full economic evaluation method, including cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA), cost-benefit analysis (CBA), cost-utility analysis (CUA) and cost-minimization analysis (CMA) to compare embryo transfer strategies in humans, were included. Review articles, editorials, abstracts, non-English articles, and articles without full text available were excluded.

Selection/screening of studies

To select relevant studies, duplicates were identified and removed initially. The first screening stage of title and abstract screening was performed by two independent researchers. Full-text screening based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria was performed after the initial screening of titles and abstracts. Disagreement between the two researchers was resolved by a third reviewer to prevent any bias.

Quality assessment of the studies

The quality of these studies was assessed using the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) checklist (2022), which contains 28 questions categorized under seven sections: title, abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, and other relevant information [21].

In this study, a score of 1 was assigned if a study fully met a checklist requirement (“Yes”), 0.5 if it partially met the requirement (“Partial”), and 0 if it did not meet the requirement (“No”). The highest possible score of 28 represents the most complete report. Items marked as “not applicable (NA)” were excluded from the denominator when calculating the total score. Based on the percentage of total points obtained, studies were categorized as good quality (≥75%), moderate quality (50–74%), or low quality (≤50%). A minimum score of >50% (≥14 out of 28) was set as the inclusion threshold, and studies rated as low quality (≤50%) were excluded. This classification followed a tiered framework commonly used in health economic reviews and is consistent with previous work adopting similar percentage-based quality criteria [22].

Two reviewers (M.E and S.Z) independently assessed the quality of included studies, discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (S.A).

Information extraction

Data extraction was carried out using a researcher-developed data extraction form. This form consisted of two tables: one summarizing the general characteristics of the studies and the other presenting findings related to the economic evaluations. The extracted information included the article title, first author, year of publication, country of the study, population studied, year of costing, strategies compared, type of economic evaluation, study perspective, time horizon, source of cost and effectiveness data, discount rate, type of sensitivity analysis, funding source, cost-effectiveness results, and the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold.

All cost data were adjusted to 2023 US dollars using the Campbell and Cochrane Economic Methods Group’s cost converter, which accounts for both inflation and purchasing power parity (PPP) [23] for cross-study comparability. These adjustments were applied consistently across all included studies and are reflected in Table 1. However, ICERs were retained in their original currency and year, as conversion is not methodologically valid due to their ratio nature.

Data synthesis

Due to potential sources of heterogeneity, such as variations in patient populations, model structures (e.g., Markov, decision-tree), perspectives (societal, healthcare provider, payer), outcomes, and WTP thresholds, a meta-analysis was not conducted. However, we performed structured narrative syntheses organized by economic perspective, Maternal age group and model type. A summary table comparing key modeling assumptions of included studies -such as model type, cycle number, time horizon, perspective, discount rate, twin outcomes, sensitivity analysis- is presented in the Results section (Table 2). It complements Tables 1 and 3 by clarifying methodological differences that may influence cost-effectiveness results.

Results

Overview

A total of 4,335 studies were identified through database searches. After removing duplicates, 2,938 articles entered the screening stage. Based on title and abstract screening, 2,864 articles were excluded, leaving 74 full texts for detailed review. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 16 studies were included in the final analysis (Figs. 1 and 2; Additional file 2). All included studies received an acceptable score on the CHEERS 2022 checklist, with total scores ranging from 14.5 to 26 (53.7%–92.9%), a median of 18, and a mean of 18.7.

Characteristics of included studies

The included studies were geographically concentrated in Europe (Netherlands [11, 24–27], Belgium [28], Spain [29], Scotland [30], Finland [31], Sweden [32], England [5], and a joint study across Denmark, Sweden, and Norway [33]) and North America (United States) [34–36], with one study conducted in Asia (Singapore) [37] (Fig. 3).

No studies from low- or middle-income countries (LMICs) were identified, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Fig. 3Distribution of relevant studies by country

Distribution of relevant studies by country

Most studies focused on the cost-effectiveness of SET compared to DET. Fifteen of the sixteen studies employed CEA, while one study used CUA [30]. The Markov model was the most commonly applied method, with a few studies using decision-tree or other modeling approaches. Study perspectives varied: societal (N = 8), healthcare system (N = 4), service provider (N = 2), and insurer (N = 2). This variability in perspectives affects the interpretation of cost data, as some studies focused on costs to the healthcare system, others on patient out-of-pocket expenses, and others on broader societal costs. Time horizons ranged from 6 weeks to 20 years. Most studies applied discount rates between 3% and 4%, and sensitivity analyses included probabilistic [5, 24, 25, 29], deterministic [30], and one-way approaches [11, 36].

Key characteristics of included studies are summarized in Tables 3and 4. Most studies enrolled women undergoing IVF, often stratified by age groups (< 30, 30–35, 36–39, ≥ 40 years). Cost sources included hospital records, national registries, and published literature.

Characteristics of included studies

Among the included studies, the most frequency reported outcomes were live birth rate (LBR) in 8 studies (50%) [11, 25, 26, 28, 29, 31, 36, 37], and incremental cost per live birth in 9 studies (56%) [5, 11, 25, 26, 31, 33, 35–37]. Other outcomes reported were expected life years (LYs) [24], QALYs for the baby [24], QALYs for women [30], live births without disabilities [30], twin pregnancy rate [30], successful pregnancy [32], and incremental cost per successful pregnancy [27].

Across the included studies, cost-effectiveness was mainly reported as the ICER per live birth. Reported thresholds varied substantially by region: €20,000–50,000 per QALY in Europe, £20,000–30,000 per QALY in the UK, and $5,000–25,000 per live birth in the US. For example, Veleva et al. (2009) reported –€19,889 per term live birth for eSET vs. DET [31], Polinder et al. (2008) €185,000 per live birth for mild SET vs. standard DET [26], and Dixon et al. (2008) £18,463 per additional live birth for women < 30 years [5]. The studies also varied in funding sources and inclusion of personal or societal costs (Tables 1 and 3 ).

Economic evaluation findings

Summary of cost-effectiveness across strategies

Among the 11 studies comparing SET and DET, 10 concluded that SET was more cost-effective. SET consistently reduced multiple pregnancies, preterm births, and neonatal complications, thereby lowering healthcare costs without compromising LBR [31, 32, 37]. Across studies, twin pregnancy rates were consistently lower with SET/eSET than DET. Reported twin rates ranged from 0 to 9% for SET and 7–75% for DET, depending on age, number of cycles, and modeling approach. Some studies stratified by cycles and age showed that three to five consecutive eSET cycles resulted in 4–5% twin births, whereas DET in younger women could reach 20–30% [24, 37]. This reduction in multiple births substantially contributed to fewer preterm deliveries, NICU admissions, and overall healthcare costs.

For example, Wong et al. [37] reported sequential SET saved −1,476 SGD per live birth compared with DET, while fresh eSET was −20,172 SGD per live birth [37]. eSET, particularly when combined with embryo freezing, was both more effective and less costly than DET [31].

However, cost-effectiveness varied by age group. eSET was preferred for women under 36 years due to the higher risk of twin pregnancies and uncertain QALY gains, whereas DET could be more cost-effective in women aged 37–39 years [30]. Although SET may increase out-of-pocket costs of patients, it remained cost-saving from the health system perspective [34, 36].

Broader comparisons: SET, DET, and ≥ 3 embryos

Transferring two or more embryos increased risks of preterm delivery, cesarean section, and low birth weight, resulting in higher costs [35]. Among women under 35, transferring five embryos increased costs by 80% and raised the risk of complications such as cerebral palsy. SET remained the most cost-effective from a societal perspective [36].

Alternative and combined strategies

A Dutch study compared 3–5 cycles of eSET in all patients, standard treatment policy (STP; i.e., eSET for women < 38 years with a good-quality embryo), and DET over 1, 5, and 18 year horizons found eSET most cost-effective short term, while DET became more effective long term [24].Another study evaluating seven IVF strategies (combination of eSET, STP, and DET) found that combining multiple embryo transfer policies was not cost-effective. Instead, selection among single strategies (eSET, STP, or DET) was recommended based on societal WTP [25].A study comparing mild versus standard ovarian stimulation found that the mild strategy (GnRH antagonist + SET) more cost-effective than the standard strategy (GnRH agonist + up to two embryos). Discussion

Summary of key findings

This systematic review synthesized evidence from multiple economic evaluations comparing different embryo transfer strategies in IVF. Overall, most studies indicated that SET is often a more cost-effective option than transferring two or more embryos [11, 27, 28, 31–34, 37], particularly among younger women [11, 33, 34, 37]. SET was associated with lower overall treatment costs [5, 26, 31, 33–37] and fewer maternal and neonatal complications [5, 33, 35], despite requiring more cycles. Strategies combining fresh and frozen SET or mild stimulation protocols further enhanced cost-effectiveness. However, cost-effectiveness varied across populations and contexts. Maternal age, healthcare system perspective [11, 29], and societal WTP thresholds [11, 38] emerged as key modifiers: SET tended to be more favorable in younger women, whereas DET appeared relatively more advantageous among older patients or in settings with lower per-cycle treatment costs [11, 30]. Additionally, patient-level costs may influence preferences, particularly in countries with limited insurance coverage for infertility treatments. Regulatory frameworks and reimbursement policies across countries also affect both practice patterns and the generalizability of findings.

Quality and robustness of evidence

The overall quality of included studies ranged from moderate to high according to the CHEERS 2022 checklist. Most studies clearly described modeling approaches, time horizons, and perspectives. However, substantial heterogeneity existed in outcome measures (e.g., QALY vs. cost per live birth), costing methods, and currency adjustments, which reduced comparability.

The heterogeneity in study designs and perspectives (societal, healthcare system, and patient) also influenced the consistency of findings. Studies adopting a societal or healthcare system perspective generally favored SET due to lower overall costs and better perinatal outcomes, whereas those from a patient perspective highlighted the financial burden of undergoing multiple SET cycles. Furthermore, few studies incorporated a societal perspective, potentially underestimating indirect and long-term costs. While most studies employed robust modeling frameworks and credible clinical data, several lacked comprehensive cost reporting and relied on limited sample sizes.

Potential biases in the review process

Despite a comprehensive search, several potential biases may have influenced the findings. Studies with positive results are more likely to be published, introducing possible publication bias. Excluding non-English studies may have caused language bias, and the inability to formally assess publication bias (e.g., via funnel plots or Egger’s test) is another limitation. Moreover, substantial heterogeneity in study designs and outcomes precluded meta-analysis, limiting the ability to drive pooled quantitative estimates.

Completeness and applicability of evidence

Decisions regarding embryo transfer strategies should consider economic, clinical, and patient-preference factors. While SET is often cost-effective, particularly among younger women, the optimal strategy varies depending on maternal age, healthcare system context, patient preferences, and societal WTP thresholds. The findings are broadly applicable to IVF settings, but contextual factors such as national regulations, insurance coverage, and cultural preferences may affect generalizability. For instance, in many European countries, DET is strictly regulated and permitted only under specific conditions, making SET the default strategy [9]. In contrast, in regions with limited insurance coverage, patients may prefer DET to reduce the number of treatment cycles.

As all included studies were conducted in HICs, future research should generate context-specific evidence in LMICs, adopt broader perspectives (e.g., societal), and, where feasible, apply meta-analytic approaches to provide more precise and generalizable estimates.

Comparison with previous reviews

The findings of this review are broadly consistent with previous systematic reviews evaluating the cost-effectiveness of ART interventions. López-Regalado et al. (2016) reported that eSET reduces overall treatment costs compared with DET, despite a modest decrease in live birth rates, particularly in high-quality studies [16]. Methodological reviews [17, 18] highlighted that most economic evaluations are short-term, clinic-based, and often omit societal costs, indirect expenses, and age-stratified analyses. Olive et al. (2024) found SET among the more cost-effective ART strategies, whereas high-cost interventions such as PGT-A and ICSI for unexplained infertility were generally not cost-effective [19]. Overall, these findings confirm the economic advantage of SET over DET, while emphasizing that cost-effectiveness is context-dependent, influenced by maternal age, healthcare perspective, and methodological and policy factors.

Unlike previous reviews, this study highlights how economic and contextual factors shape cost-effectiveness across settings.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this systematic review is its synthesis of findings from multiple economic evaluations, providing a comprehensive comparison of the cost-effectiveness of different embryo transfer strategies. This integration of diverse study designs and economic perspectives enables a balanced assessment of both the clinical and economic implications of SET, DET, and related strategies. The review thus offers robust evidence to inform clinical decision-making and health policy.

However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged.

First, limitations stem from the nature of the included economic evaluation studies. Many included studies relied on modeled rather than real-world data, used short time horizons, or adopted narrow analytic perspectives (e.g., healthcare or payer perspective only), potentially underestimating indirect or long-term costs. Moreover, heterogeneity in populations, interventions, costing years, and economic models limited direct comparability and synthesis of findings.

Second, the review process itself has inherent constraints. Only studies published in English were included, which may have introduced language bias and excluded relevant evidence from non-English-speaking regions. Although a comprehensive search strategy was employed, the databases searched and the specific keywords used might also have resulted in the omission of eligible studies. Furthermore, publication bias could not be formally assessed, as standard tools are not well developed for economic evaluations, and the limited number of studies further precluded quantitative analysis.

Third, due to heterogeneity in cost measures, perspectives, and outcome definitions, a meta-analysis was not feasible, limiting the ability to produce pooled quantitative estimates.

Finally, all included studies were conducted in HICs. Consequently, numerical results such as ICERs or cost per live birth may not be directly generalizable to LMICs, where IVF access, cost structures, and healthcare systems differ substantially.

Even so, this review provides a clear and structured synthesis of the evidence, points out key methodological gaps, and offers a framework for future research—particularly in LMIC contexts—to support context-specific economic evaluations and inform both policy and clinical decision-making in reproductive health care systems.

Conclusion

The evidence suggests that SET and SET-based strategies are often cost-effective, particularly among younger women, by reducing maternal and neonatal complications and long-term healthcare costs. However, cost-effectiveness varies according to maternal age, healthcare system perspective, modeling approach, and societal WTP thresholds.

While most economic evaluations were conducted in HICs, limiting direct applicability to LMICs, the findings provide methodological insights and general trends that can inform context-specific evaluations.

These results can support clinicians, policymakers, and health economists in developing evidence-based embryo transfer strategies that balance clinical effectiveness, patient preferences, and economic sustainability.
