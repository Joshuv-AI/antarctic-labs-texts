Perfect t-embeddings of uniformly weighted generalized tower graphs
David Keating, Hieu Trung Vu

Abstract
. In this paper, we study sequences of perfect t-embeddings of a uniformly weighted family of graphs we call generalized tower graphs. We show that the embeddings of these graphs satisfy certain technical assumptions, in particular, the rigidity assumption of Berggren-Nicoletti-Russkikh in [ 4 ] . As a result, we confirm the convergence of the gradients of the height function fluctuations of these graphs to those of the Gaussian free field.

1.
Introduction

Let
G=(V\cup E,\nu)
be a edge-weighted, planar, bipartite graph with vertex set
V
, edge set
E
, and edge weights
\nu:E\to{\mathbb{R}}
. A dimer configuration, or dimer cover, on the graph
G
is any subset of
E
such that each vertex belongs to precisely one edge of the subset. We denote the set of all possible dimer configurations on
G
by
\mathcal{M}(G)
. A dimer model on
G
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{M}(G)
, defined for
m\in\mathcal{M}(G)
as

\mathbb{P}(m)=\frac{\prod_{e\in m}\nu(e)}{\sum_{m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(G)}\prod_{e\in m^{\prime}}\nu(e)}.

The denominator in the above is known as the partition function of the model. We refer to
[
39
,
38
,
30
,
25
]
for surveys and expository materials on dimer models and give a brief summary of the features important to the current work below.

The dimer model was introduced in the physics and chemistry communities to study the absorption of diatomic molecules on the surface of a crystal by Fowler and Rushbrooke
[
29
]
in 1937. Later in the 1960’s the model was studied from the mathematical point of view (independently) by Kasteleyn
[
35
]
, Percus
[
47
]
, and Fisher and Temperley
[
58
]
, giving the first enumerative result. A special feature of the dimer model for certain families of graphs
(G_{n})_{n>0}
is that they display the
arctic curve phenomenon

[
33
,
51
]
in the limit
n\to\infty
. For such graphs, there exists phase separation in which regions where local configurations are fixed in one orientation of dimer, called
frozen/solid regions
, are separated from regions where the local configurations are disordered, called
liquid/rough regions
. The boundary separating the different regions approximates a limiting curve known as the
arctic curve
. One of the most well-studied dimer model is the
Aztec diamond

[
28
]
for which, with uniform weights, the arctic curve is known to be a circle
[
34
]
. See Section
3.1
for a precise description of the Aztec diamond. Figure
1
gives examples of domino tilings of the Aztec diamond of various ranks.

Figure 1
.
From left to right, tilings of the Aztec diamond of rank 2, 20, and 200, respectively. Each domino covers two faces of the dual graph to the Aztec diamond graph and corresponds to an edge belonging to the dimer cover. The different colors distinguish the four possible dimer orientations. Note that one can clearly see the arctic curve appearing in the right tiling.

A remarkable feature of dimer models is the
shuffling algorithm
, the general version of which was introduced by Propp
[
50
]
. The shuffling algorithm consists of certain local modifications to the graph
G
along with its edge weights for which you can keep track of the change in the dimer model. A precise description of the shuffling algorithm is given in Section
3
. This algorithm allows us to recursively compute the probability that a randomly-chosen matching of an Aztec diamond will include a particular edge as well as sample a random matching. In
[
40
]
, the shuffling algorithm is shown to be equivalent to certain transformation of the complex planar embedding for dimer models on planar graphs. For more applications of the shuffling algorithm, see
[
45
,
10
,
7
,
1
]
for a non-exhaustive list.

The dimer model can also be viewed as a stepped surface via
Thurston’s height function

[
59
]
. For a finite graph
G
, let
G^{*}
be the planar dual of
G
with vertices corresponding to the faces of
G
. Given any dimer cover
M
of a planar, bipartite graph
G
, the height function
h:V(G^{*})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a function on the set of vertices in the augmented dual
G^{*}
, obtained by fixing a reference matching
M_{0}
of
G
and the boundary values
h(v^{*})=0
for outer vertices of
G^{*}
. For an edge
(bw^{*})\in V(G^{*})
oriented such that the white vertex is on the left, the height function
h(v^{*})-h(u^{*})=-{\mathbf{1}}_{bw\in M}+{\mathbf{1}}_{bw\in M_{0}}
. See Figure
2
for an example.

The height function plays an important role in describing the limiting structure of a dimer model. Namely, the fluctuations of the height function away from its mean were first shown to be related to the Gaussian Free Field (GFF) in
[
36
,
37
]
for the cases of dimer models on Temperleyan domains which, roughly speaking, correspond to domains for which there are no frozen region. These results were later extended to several other cases by Russkikh
[
52
,
53
]
. More generally, it was conjectured by Kenyon and Okounkov in 2007, that the height fluctuations in the liquid regions converge to the Gaussian free field in a non-trivial complex conformal structure
[
42
,
41
]
. For background on the Gaussian free field, we refer the reader to
[
60
,
54
,
2
]
. This has motivated much subsequent research, and the conjecture has been proven in a handful of cases
[
8
,
9
,
27
,
48
,
49
,
11
,
14
,
13
,
44
,
7
,
32
]
. More general results on height function fluctuations can also be found in
[

,
21
]
.

For some 2-dimensional statistical mechanics models, one can consider the underlying objects (graphs, lattices, etc.) embedded in the complex planes. Then, a correct notion of discrete holomorphicity and discretization of the plane can be used to analyze the asymptotic behavior of these models by taking the limit as the mesh size goes to 0. This technique is extremely fruitful, namely, in the study of the critical Ising model on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and isoradial graphs; see, e.g.
[
19
,
20
,
31
,

,

.

Recently, in
[

,

]
, the authors introduced a new embedding of planar graphs in the complex plane called a

\mathcal{T}
as well as a mapping to the plane called the

and extended to the notion of

in the Minkowski space

-point correlation functions converge to those of the standard Gaussian free field in the intrinsic metric of the surface

. Note that this is, however, is a weaker statement than the conjecture by Okounkov and Kenyon in

The main focus of this paper is the study of perfect t-embeddings of a large class of graphs we call
generalized tower graphs
. For a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, satisfying certain constraints given in Section

, we may approximate

by a an

on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. Using a version of the shuffling algorithm, we show how to construct a planar, bipartite graph whose boundary is determined by the up-down path approximating

. By scaling the integer lattice to have step size
\frac{1}{n+1}

we call the

f(x)=1
and the tower graph which is indexed by
f(x)=\frac{1}{3}x+\frac{4}{3}
. Other examples are given in Figure

[

[

(see Rmk.

, this family of graphs also appear as a special case of the graphs of
[
26
]
in the context of octahedron recurrence (see Rmk.
3.7
).

The goal of this paper is to verify the assumptions in

,

]
and confirm that the gradients of

The organization of this paper is the following. In Section

2.1.
t-embeddings and perfect t-embeddings

Consider the graph
G
and add a vertex
v_{\text{out }}
to the graph by connecting it with all vertices of the outer face of
{G}
. Define
augmented dual

{G}^{*}
of
G
as the dual graph of
{G}\cup v_{\text{out }}
.
For the rest of this paper, when we mention
G^{*}
, we will always mean the augmented dual if we do not specify otherwise.
By an embedding of
G
on the complex plane we mean an embedding of the augmented dual graph
{G}^{*}
on the complex plane, that is, an assignment to each vertex of
G^{*}
a complex number. Since we only consider bipartite graphs
G
, we will always consider the faces of the embedding to be colored black or white depending on whether they correspond to black or white vertex of
G
. A
t-embedding
of
G
is an embedding on the complex plane satisfying certain properties we now state.

Figure 4
.
An example of a perfect t-embedding.

Definition 2.1

(
t-embedding
)
.

Given weighted planar bipartite graph
({G},\nu)
, a t-embedding
\mathcal{T}\left({G}^{*}\right)
is a proper embedding of an augmented dual graph
G^{*}
such that the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

The sum of the angles at each inner vertex of
\mathcal{T}\left({G}^{*}\right)
at the corners corresponding to black faces is equal to
\pi
(and similarly for white faces),

\sum_{i}\theta(b_{i},v)=\sum_{j}\theta(w_{j},v)=\pi

(2)

The geometric weights (dual edge lengths)
\left|\mathcal{T}\left(\nu_{1}^{*}\right)-\mathcal{T}\left(\nu_{2}^{*}\right)\right|
are gauge equivalent to
\nu_{e}
, where
v_{1,2}^{*}
are vertices of the dual edge
e^{*}
.

From here on out, we will denote
d\mathcal{T}\left(bw^{*}\right):=\mathcal{T}\left(v^{\prime}\right)-\mathcal{T}(v)
, where
vv^{\prime}
is an oriented edge on the complex plane such that
w
is on the left.

The existence of t-embeddings is guaranteed for some families of graphs.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
40
]
)
.

t-embeddings of the (augmented) dual graph
G^{*}
exist at least in the following case:

(1)

(G,\nu)
is a non-degenerate bipartite finite weighted graph admitting a dimer cover and with outer face of degree
4
.

(2)

(G,\nu)
is a infinite doubly periodic weighted bipartite graph, equipped with an equivalence class of doubly periodic edge weights.

In this paper, we consider a more specific type of t-embedding which is more susceptible to boundary conditions.

Definition 2.3

(
Perfect t-embedding
)
.

A t-embedding is perfect if the outer face of
\mathcal{T}(G^{*})
is a tangential polygon to a circle and all the non-boundary edges adjacent to boundary vertices lie on the bisectors of the corresponding angle. See Figure
4
.

2.2.
Origami Maps

For each oriented edge, let
bw^{*}
be the corresponding edge of
G^{*}
oriented so that
w
is to the left. To each t-embedding
\mathcal{T}(G^{*})
one can associate the
origami map

\mathcal{O}:G^{*}\to{\mathbb{C}}
.

Definition 2.4
.

A function
\eta:V(\mathcal{G})=W\cup B\rightarrow\mathbb{T}
where
\mathbb{T}
denotes the unit circle, is said to be an origami square root function if it satisfies the identity

\bar{\eta}_{b}\bar{\eta}_{w}=\frac{d\mathcal{T}\left(bw^{*}\right)}{\left|d\mathcal{T}\left(bw^{*}\right)\right|}

for all pairs
(w,b)
of white and black neighboring faces of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
.

Definition 2.5
.

The origami map
\mathcal{O}
is the primitive of the piecewise differential form
d\mathcal{O}
defined by

(2.1)

d\mathcal{O}(z)=\begin{cases}\eta_{w}^{2}dz&\textrm{if}\ z\in\mathcal{T}(w)\\
\bar{\eta_{b}}^{2}d\bar{z}&\textrm{if}\ z\in\mathcal{T}(b)\end{cases}.

Let us describe the geometric interpretation for these definitions. One can think of the map
z\mapsto\mathcal{O}(z)
as folding along the edges of
G^{*}
passing through a face path connecting the root face
b_{0}
to some other face corresponding to some vertex
v\in V(G)
. This procedure is locally consistent due to the angle condition in the Def.
2.1
. That is, the order of folding preserves the image of
\mathcal{O}
, see Figure
5
. The primitive of
d\mathcal{O}(z)
is defined up to a global additive constant and thus one can set
\eta_{w_{0}}=1
and
\mathcal{O}(w_{0})=\mathcal{T}(w_{0})
. From this folding procedure, we can see that if
\mathcal{T}(G^{*})
is a perfect t-embedding, then the image of the outer faces of
G^{*}
obtained from the origami map by fixing one outer face as root face coincides.

Figure 5
.
The image of a point under the origami map by folding the faces along adjacent edges.

2.3.
Technical assumptions for perfect t-embeddings

After one shows that the structure of perfect t-embedding and origami map exists, there are several technical assumptions from
[
17
,
16
,
18
]
that one must verify in order to conclude the convergence of the gradients of
k
-point correlation functions to that of the GFF. We first consider the pseudo-Riemannian manifold
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
as
\mathbb{R}^{3}
equipped with the metric
dx^{2}+dy^{2}-d\vartheta^{2}
. We denote by
\Omega_{\mathcal{T}}
the region covered by the union of faces of
\mathcal{T}(G^{*})
. We are now ready to formulate the main theorem of
[
16
]
.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
16
]
)
.

Suppose there exists a sequence of perfect t-embeddings
\mathcal{T}_{n}
of weighted planar bipartite graphs
{G}_{n}
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

The regions
\Omega_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
approximate (in the Hausdorff sense) a simply connected domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

(2)

We have the convergence of origami maps
\mathcal{O}_{n}(z)\rightarrow\vartheta(z)
, uniformly on compact subsets, for some function
\vartheta:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, the limiting graph
S_{\Omega}=\{(z,\vartheta(z))\}_{z\in\Omega}
of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
over
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is a space-like surface with zero mean curvature in
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
.

(3)

There is a sequence of scales
\delta=\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0
, such that the sequence
\mathcal{T}_{n}
satisfies Assumptions
2.8
and
2.9
on compact subsets.

Let
\zeta_{m,i}\in\mathbb{D},m=1,\ldots,k,i=1,2
be points of the disk, and let
v_{m,i}^{(n)}
be vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
which approximate the points
z\left(\zeta_{m,i}\right)
, where
(z(\zeta),\vartheta(\zeta))
is a conformal parameterization of
S_{\Omega}
. Suppose that for
i=1,2
, the points
v_{1,i}^{(n)},\ldots,v_{k,i}^{(n)}
stay uniformly away from each other and from the boundary
\partial\Omega
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{m=1}^{k}\left(\bar{h}_{n}\left(v_{m,2}^{(n)}\right)-\bar{h}_{n}\left(v_{m,1}^{(n)}\right)\right)\right]\rightarrow\sum_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\in\{1,2\}}(-1)^{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}}G_{k}\left(\zeta_{1,r_{1}},\ldots,\zeta_{k,r_{k}}\right)

where
\bar{h}_{n}(v)=h_{n}(v)-\mathbb{E}\left[h_{n}(v)\right]
denotes the mean subtracted height function on
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and
G_{k}
is the
k
-point correlation function of the Gaussian free field on
\mathbb{D}
.

The two assumptions, Assumptions
2.8
and
2.9
, in the theorem are called assumptions
\operatorname{LIP}
and
\operatorname{EXP-FAT}
, respectively, in
[
16
]
. Verifying these assumptions will be the main goal of this paper.

Definition 2.7
.

Given constants
\delta>0
and
\kappa\in(0,1)
, we say that a t-embedding
\mathcal{T}
satisfies the assumption
\operatorname{LIP}(\kappa,\delta)
on a compact subset
K\subset\Omega
if

\left|\mathcal{O}\left(z^{\prime}\right)-\mathcal{O}(z)\right|\leq\kappa\left|z^{\prime}-z\right|

for all
z,z^{\prime}\in K
such that
\left|z^{\prime}-z\right|\geq\delta
.

Assumption 2.8

(LIP assumption,
[
16
]
)
.

Given a sequence
\delta=\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0
, we say that a sequence of t-embeddings
\mathcal{T}_{n}
satisfies the Lipschitz assumption on compact subsets if for each compact
\mathcal{K}\subset\Omega
there exists
\kappa\in(0,1)
such that for
n
large enough
\mathcal{T}_{n}
satisfies
\operatorname{LIP}(\kappa,\delta)
on
\mathcal{K}
.

Intuitively speaking, Def.
2.7
implies that the function does not increase in length with respect to the Euclidean norm at the discrete level. Thus, this assumption implies that the origami map is a 1-Lipschitz function with constant strictly less than 1.

The second assumption is a non-degeneracy condition on the faces of the sequence of t-embeddings, which roughly asserts that for almost every face, the radius of the largest circle which can be inscribed in the face cannot decay exponentially fast as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We say that a face of
\mathcal{T}
is
\rho
-fat if there exists a disk of radius
\rho
contained in the face. To formulate the second assumption, we also need to recall the definition of a splitting of a t-embedding introduced in
[
16
]
. A
splitting

\mathcal{T}_{\text{spl }}^{\circ}
(resp.
\mathcal{T}_{\text{spl }}^{\bullet}
) of
\mathcal{T}
is obtained from
\mathcal{T}
by adding diagonals to each white (resp. black) face of degree larger than 3 such that all of these faces are split into triangles.

Assumption 2.9

(EXP-FAT assumption,
[
16
]
)
.

Given a sequence
\delta=\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0
, we say that a sequence of t-embeddings
\mathcal{T}_{n}
satisfies the assumption EXP-FAT(
\delta
) on compact subsets if for each compact
\mathcal{K}\subset\Omega
, there exists some sequence
\delta^{\prime}=\delta_{n}^{\prime}\rightarrow 0
such that:

•

There exist splittings
\left(\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)_{\text{spl }}^{\bullet}
such that after removing all
\exp\left(-\delta^{\prime}\delta^{-1}\right)
-fat white faces and black triangles, the size of any remaining vertex connected component in
\mathcal{K}
converges to 0 as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

•

There exists splittings
\left(\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)_{\mathrm{spl}}^{\circ}
satisfying the same condition.

In
[
4
]
, the authors provide a stronger but more straightforward condition, called the
rigidity assumption
, that would imply the validity of the two assumptions
\operatorname{LIP}
and
\operatorname{EXP-FAT}
with the scaling
\delta=\delta_{n}=\frac{\log n}{n}
.

Assumption 2.10

(Rigidity Assumption,
[
4
]
)
.

Given a compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\Omega
, there exist positive constants
N_{\mathcal{K}},C_{\mathcal{K}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{K}}
which only depend on
\mathcal{K}
, such that for all pairs of adjacent vertices
v,v^{\prime}
of the dual graph
\mathcal{G}_{n}^{*}
such that both
\mathcal{T}_{n}(v)
and
\mathcal{T}_{n}\left(v^{\prime}\right)
are contained in
\mathcal{K}
we have

\frac{\mu_{n}}{C_{\mathcal{K}}}\leq\left|\mathcal{T}_{n}\left(v^{\prime}\right)-\mathcal{T}_{n}(v)\right|\leq\mu_{n}C_{\mathcal{K}}

for all
n>N_{\mathcal{K}}
. In addition, the angles of the faces of the perfect t-embedding inside
\mathcal{K}
are contained in
\left(\varepsilon_{\mathcal{K}},\pi-\varepsilon_{\mathcal{K}}\right)
for all
n>N_{\mathcal{K}}
.

We refer to Section 6 of
[
4
]
for the detail discussion of the
rigidity assumption
. Let us now give some background and motivation on these technical assumptions LIP and EXP-FAT, which are given in a much more detail in the introduction of
[
16
]
. In
[
36
,
37
]
, Kenyon used the fact that the entries of the inverse Kasteleyn matrix (also known as the coupling function in
[
36
]
) of the dimer model on the square grid satisfy a discrete version of the Cauchy-Riemann equation to prove the convergence of the height fluctuations to the Gaussian Free Field for the dimer models on Temperleyan domains (domains, intuitively speaking, do not possess frozen regions in the scaling limit). However, for other cases, the scaling limits possess a more complicated complex structure, first witnessed in
[
23
]
by Cohn-Kenyon-Propp and later described in detail by Kenyon-Okounkov in
[
42
]
. This is due to the fact that for many cases, the entries of the inverse Kasteleyn matrix are not uniformly bounded and do not converge as the mesh
\delta\to 0
. These assumptions are steps toward developing a framework to remove the exponential growth of the model but still be able to utilize the discrete complex analysis toolbox to study discrete equations arising from the non-trivial complex structures.

Currently, there are several instances where perfect t-embeddings are found satisfying the properties in Thm.
2.6

[
4
,
3
,
5
]
. The goal of the current work is to extend this to a large family of graphs. Include in our family of graphs will be the Aztec diamond and the tower graph, defined by Borodin and Ferrari in
[
8
]
.

2.4.
Convergence of t-embeddings and origami maps

For the discussion in this section, it is more convenient to consider the rotated version of the origami map. Let us assume that a perfect t-embedding
\mathcal{T}_{n}
exists, with boundary contour
C_{\lozenge}=\{z=x+\mathrm{i}y:|x|+|y|=1\}
. For the origami map
\mathcal{O}_{n}
corresponding to folding faces of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, define,

\mathcal{O}_{n}^{\prime}:=\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}\left(\mathcal{O}_{n}-\frac{1+i}{2}\right),

which is a composition of a translation and rotation of the origami map
\mathcal{O}_{n}
defined in Section
4
. We define
S_{\lozenge}
as the maximal surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
, in the sense that
S_{\lozenge}
locally maximizes the area in the Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
with the boundary contour
C_{\lozenge}
. This is the unique space-like surface in
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
with this boundary contour, which has vanishing mean curvature, see
[
43
]
.
The second assumption in Thm.
2.6
is that the sequence of t-embeddings and origami maps
(\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{O}_{n})
converges to
this maximal surface in the Minkowski space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2,1}
.

3.
General Shuffling

In this section, we define the class of graphs that we will consider. These graphs will be constructed by generalizing the shuffling algorithm used to construct the Aztec diamond graph
[
50
]
. Included in our family of graphs will be the Aztec diamond as well as the tower graph studied in
[
4
,
45
,
8
]
. The building block of this construction are certain local transformations applied to the dimer model graph
G
, which preserve the partition function up to an overall factor. In words, these transformations are:

(1)

combining a double edge into a single edge or vice-versa,

(2)

adding or contracting a degree 2 vertex,

(3)

or performing a spider move.

The description of how these operations change the graph and its weights is shown in Figure
6
. We will also need the additional operation of applying a gauge to the weights. Let
W
be the set of white vertices of our graphs and
B
the set of black vertices. We say that two choices of edge weights,
w
and
\widetilde{w}
, are
gauge equivalent
if there exist functions
F:W\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
and
G:W\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
such that for every edge
e=(wb)
we have

\widetilde{w}(e)=w(e)F(w)G(b).

Note that gauge-equivalent weights have the same partition function up to an overall factor depending on the gauge and thus define the same dimer model.

Figure 6
.
Elementary moves on
G
along with their adjusted weights. Note that going from the top row to the bottom the leftmost transformation the partition function is unchanged, for the middle transformation the partition function gains an overall factor of
\nu
, and in the rightmost transformations (spider move) the partition function gains an overall factor of
\Delta^{-1}
.

3.1.
The Aztec Diamond

Here we recall the shuffling algorithm for the Aztec diamond. Consider the square lattice
\left(\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}
where we label the faces by pairs of integers
(j,k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We can assign a bipartite coloring to the vertices by choosing the top-left corner of the face at
(0,0)
to be black. We define the
Aztec diamond
of rank
n
to be the set of faces
(j,k)
such that
|j|+|k|\leq n-1
and denote it by
AD_{n}
. By convention we let
AD_{0}
be the empty graph. Figure
7
shows the Aztec diamond of rank 4. For each
c\in\mathbf{Z}
, we call the set of faces such that
j+k=c
the
c
-th
column
of the graph.

Figure 7
.
Aztec diamond of rank 4. We draw the graph such that the axis are rotated by
\pi/2
so that faces such that
j+k
is constant form vertical columns.

It is shown in
[
50
]
that one can construct
AD_{n}
from
AD_{n-1}
using what is known as the domino shuffling algorithm. By iterating this algorithm, one may start from
AD_{0}
, construct
AD_{1}
, and so on, until they reach
AD_{n}
. In terms of the dimer model, we present the algorithm below.

Shuffling algorithm for the Aztec diamond

(1)

Start at rank
0
, with no faces or edges included in the graph.

(2)

To get from rank
m
to rank
m+1
do the following:

(a)

Decorate the boundary:
Add the edges and vertices around the faces located at
|j|+|k|=m
to the graph. To the newly added vertices of degree
2
, add a new edge connecting to a new vertex of degree
1
. Give all the newly added edges weight 1.

(b)

Shuffle:
Perform a spider move at all faces such that
m-|j|-|k|\mod 2=0
.

(c)

Contract:
Contract all degree-
2
vertices.

(d)

Gauge:
Multiply the weight of the edges adjacent to each white vertex by 2.

This gives an Aztec diamond of rank
m+1
with uniform weights.

(3)

Repeat step 2 until you reach an Aztec diamond of rank
n
.

An example of step 2 can be seen in Figure
8
.

Similar shuffling algorithms can be used to generate other families of graphs. Figure
9
shows such an algorithm for the so-called
tower graph
defined in
[
8
]
(see
[
4
]
).

Definition 3.1

(
Tower graph
)
.

A tower graph of size
n
is a union of
3n-1
columns of faces with
n
columns of hexagonal faces separated by two columns of square faces. The first column contain
n
hexagons, and the last column has
2n
squares. For
p\geq 0
the (
3p+1
)st column contains
n+p
hexagons and it is adjacent to a column of
n+p-1
squares on the left (if
p>0
) and to a column of
n+p+1
squares on the right. See Figure
9
for examples of the rank 2 and 3 tower graphs.

Figure 8
.
Steps in the shuffling algorithm from the Aztec diamond of rank 3 to the Aztec diamond of rank 4. The red edges are those added as decoration to the boundary. The dashed edges have weight
1/2
while all other edges have weight
1
.

Figure 9
.
Generating the rank-3 tower graph from the rank-2 tower graph using a shuffling algorithm. During the shuffle step, a spider move is done at every face of the decorated columns. Unlike the shuffling algorithm for the Aztec diamond, the tower graph requires two rounds of decorating then shuffling.

3.2.
Column-wise Shuffling

We would like to highlight two features of the shuffling algorithm for the Aztec diamond. First, during each step, we always shuffle all faces in a column of the graph. Moreover, the number of faces in a column increases by two after shuffling. Second, we can shuffle non-adjacent faces in any order. In particular, the different columns of the Aztec diamond can be shuffled in any order and the resulting weighted graph would be the same. We will use the above observation to describe an alternative formulation of the shuffling algorithm that will be useful for us in what follows.

For this alternative description, we will consider shuffling only at a single column at a time, and so will need to understand how this affects our graphs. To that end, we say that the
height
of a column is the number of faces of the graph it contains, and we call a column
shufflable
if it is a column of square faces such that its height is less than its neighboring columns. Shuffling at a column takes the form shown in Figure
10
. Note the height of the column increases by two after the shuffle.

Using the single column shuffling one may check that we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Shuffling at a column turns adjacent columns from squares into hexagons and vice-versa.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that our graph will no longer consist of only square faces but may include hexagonal faces as well. This will not effect how we label the faces by elements of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and we will continue to use the same coordinate system.

We will also want to allow shuffling of columns on the boundary of our graph. Let
l,r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and suppose our graph includes all columns such that
-l<j+k<r
where
(j,k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
are the coordinates of the faces in the column. Then we call
boundary columns
the columns satisfying
j+k=r
and
j+k=-l
, obtained by adding faces to the the left and right boundary of the graph as

\vdots

for the right boundary and the horizontal flip for the left boundary, where the added edges are colored red. Note that there is no freedom in the placement of dimers in the boundary columns, and the boundary column will always have height two less than the adjacent non-boundary column. All other columns are called
interior columns
.

We consider boundary columns to always be shufflable by decorating the column with an additional square face at the top and bottom, as well as corresponding adjacent degree one vertices, then doing a spider move at each face, contracting and gauging. This turns the boundary column into an interior column and increases the number of columns in the graph by one.

We may give a slight variation on the shuffling algorithm of the Aztec diamond using column shuffling that highlights the fact that the order of shuffling at non-adjacent columns does not effect the end graph.

Alternate shuffling algorithm for the Aztec diamond

(1)

Start with rank-
0
Aztec diamond.

(2)

Choose a shufflable column whose height is not equal to the height of the same column in
AD_{n}
and perform the single column shuffling.

(3)

Repeat step 2 until you reach an Aztec diamond of rank
n
.

3.3.
Shuffling and Up-Down paths

We would like to consider all graphs that can be obtained by starting with the rank-
0
Aztec diamond and then shuffling the columns in some order until they reach the prescribed heights.
In order to describe these graphs, we first define a certain lattice path that will contain the information of the column heights of our graph.

By an
up-down path
we mean an assignment for every
c\in\mathbb{Z}
of a height
H(c)\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
H(c+1)-H(c)=\pm 1
. We then linearly interpolate between the integer points so that
H:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. By convention, we will represent the graph
AD_{0}
by the up-down path such that for each
c\in\mathbb{Z}
the height
H
of the path at
c
is

H(x)=|x|-1.

We have the following proposition relating these paths to our graphs.

Proposition 3.4
.

There is a bijection between up-down paths and square-hexagon graphs satisfying the following properties:

•

Suppose the graph contains columns
-l<j+k<r
. Then the height of the path at
c\in\{-l+1,-l+2\ldots,r-1\}
is the height of column
c
in the graph.

•

For all
c\leq-l
we have
H(c)=-c-1
and for all
c\geq r
we have
H(c)=c-1
. Note that
H(-l)
and
H(r)
correspond to the height of the boundary columns of the graph.

•

For
c\in\{-l,-l+1,\ldots,r\}
if
H(c)>H(c+1),H(c-1)
or
H(c)<H(c+1),H(c-1)
then column
c
of the graph consists of square faces. If
H(c-1)<H(c)<H(c+1)
or
H(c-1)>H(c)>H(c+1)
then column
c
of the graph consists of hexagonal faces.

•

Shuffling at column
c
corresponds to an upward corner flip of the up-down path at
c
. That is,
H(c)\mapsto H(c)+2
and segments connecting to
H(c\pm 1)
are changed accordingly.

Proof.

Note that starting from
AD_{0}
the only possible column shuffle results in
AD_{1}
. This agrees with the up-down path corresponding to
AD_{0}
having only one possible corner flip which results in the path corresponding to
AD_{1}
. The proposition then follows from induction using Lem.
3.2
.
∎

Instead of first starting with a family of graphs in mind, we may instead start with a curve
f
and use this to define the graphs. To make this precise, consider a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying the conditions

•

f(-1)=1
,

•

there exists a smallest
x_{*}>-1
such that
f(x_{*})=x_{*}
,

•

and
|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq 1
for all
x\in[-1,x_{*}]
.

We then define
\bar{f}
by

(3.1)

\bar{f}(x)=\begin{cases}-x,&x<-1\\
f(x),&x\in[-1,x_{*}]\\
x,&x>x_{*}\end{cases}.

One can put a partial order on the paths according to the rule that for paths
H
and
H^{\prime}
,
H\leq H^{\prime}
if and only if
H(c)\leq H^{\prime}(c)
for all
c\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. We define a sequence of up-down paths
(H_{n}^{f})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}
approximating
f
by letting
H_{n}^{f}
be the largest up-down path in this partial order such that

(3.2)

\frac{1}{n+1}\left(H_{n}^{f}((n+1)x)+1\right)\leq\bar{f}(x)

for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. By Prop.
3.4
, this allows us to define a sequence of graphs whose column heights approximate
f
. We denote these graphs
generalized tower graphs approximating
f
. We call
n
the rank of such a graph. Note that we have

(3.3)

\left|(n+1)\bar{f}(x)-H_{n}^{f}((n+1)x)+1\right|\leq 2

for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Remark 3.7
.

Rather than column heights one can generalize the above to a two-dimensional version in which we consider the up-down surfaces that encode the height
H(j,k)
at each face. For
AD_{0}
, define the corresponding up-down surface heights to be

H_{0}(j,k)=\begin{cases}|j+k|-1,&j,k\geq 0\text{ or }j,k\leq 0\\
|j-k|-1,&j\geq 0,k\leq 0\text{ or }j\leq 0,k\geq 0.\end{cases}

The analogue of corner flips for the surfaces are local moves such that if
H(j,k)=h-1
and
H(j\pm 1,k)=H(j,k\pm 1)=h
, then we may “flip” at
(j,k)
and change the height at that face to from

to
h+1
. This corresponds to shuffling at the face
(j,k)
in the graph. Graphs constructed in this way include the
(r,s,t)
-graphs of
[

. The change in height after a move of shuffling is identical to the change on the stepped initial data surface of the octahedron recurrence. One can then define families of graphs that approximate two-dimensional surfaces
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
similar to what we have for the generalized tower graphs.

The up-down paths and generalized tower graphs correspond to the case for which one always shuffles at every face in a column, so that the two-dimensional surfaces are actual constant in one direction and satisfy
H(j,k)=H(j+k)
. We restrict ourself here to the one-dimensional case for simplicity, but note that the asymptotic analysis in Section
5
will not depend on this fact and applies equally well to the more general case.

Proposition 4.1

(

)
.

Perfect t-embeddings of

{G}
. More precisely,

(1)

by parallel edges with weights
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
corresponds to adding a point dividing the corresponding edge of the t-embedding into two segments with proportion

(2)

Contracting a degree 2 vertex corresponds to removing a degree two face of a t-embedding which can be seen as a diagonal of a face of the t-embedding. Splitting a vertex of degree

to two vertices of degrees

\frac{\left(u_{1}-u\right)\left(u_{3}-u\right)}{\left(u_{2}-u\right)\left(u_{4}-u\right)}=\frac{\left(u_{1}-\tilde{u}\right)\left(u_{3}-\tilde{u}\right)}{\left(u_{2}-\tilde{u}\right)\left(u_{4}-\tilde{u}\right)}

4.2.
Perfect t-embeddings of generalized tower graphs

As the generalized tower graphs are also constructed by shuffling, Rmk.

and Prop.

ensure that perfect t-embeddings also exist, although in general they do not satisfy such a simple recursive formula as that of the Aztec diamond. However, we may relate the t-embeddings of the generalized tower graphs to those of the Aztec diamond through the following lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j,k)
be the t-embedding of the reduced generalized tower graph of rank
n+1
approximating
f
. Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j,k)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{{r(n;j,k)}}(j,k)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}_{n}(j,k)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{{r(n;j,k)}}(j,k)

with
r(n;j,k)+j+k
odd. Moreover,

{r(n;j,k)}=(n+1)\bar{f}\left(\frac{j+k}{n+1}\right)+\epsilon(j,k)

with
|\epsilon(j,k)|\leq 2
.

Proof.

Note that when constructing the t-embedding (or origami map) via the shuffling algorithm, the image of a face only changes when there is local transformation of
(G^{f}_{n})^{\prime}
at that face. In particular, when a column
c
has been shuffled until it reaches its desired height
H_{n}^{f}(c)
, the embedding of the faces in that column no longer change. In light of Rmk.
4.3
, the embedding of faces in column
c
of the
(G^{f}_{n})^{\prime}
will be the same as those of a reduced Aztec diamond whose the height at column
c
is equal to
H_{n}^{f}(c)
. In fact, we have two choices here since, for
r+j+k
odd, both the Aztec diamond of rank
r
and rank
r-1
have the same height at column
j+k
.

Let
{r(n;j,k)}
be the choice of for which
r(n;j,k)+j+k
is odd. We have

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j,k)=\mathcal{T}_{r(n;j,k)}(j,k).

From Eqn. (
3.3
), we have

0\leq(n+1)\bar{f}\left(\frac{j+k}{n+1}\right)-H_{n}^{f}(j+k)\leq 1.

Letting
\epsilon(j,k)=r(n;j,k)-(n+1)\bar{f}\left(\frac{j+k}{n+1}\right)
and noting that
r(n;j,k)
differs from
H_{n}^{f}(j+k)
by at most one, we
have

{r(n;j,k)}=(n+1)\bar{f}\left(\frac{j+k}{n+1}\right)+\epsilon(j,k)

with
|\epsilon(j,k)|\leq 2
, as desired.

Note that the points in the embedding are always from faces in the interior columns of the graph, so we may drop the bar on the

whenever referring to

\begin{aligned} \widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j,k)&\;={\mathcal{T}}_{r(n;j,k)}(j,k)\\
&\;=p_{E}(j,k,r(n;j,k))+\mathrm{i}p_{N}(j,k,r(n;j,k))-p_{W}(j,k,r(n;j,k))-\mathrm{i}p_{S}(j,k,r(n;j,k)).\end{aligned}

∎

Note that we may also express
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j,k)
as

(5.10)

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j,k)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\left(r+1\right)\left(S(w;\frac{j}{r+1},\frac{k}{r+1})-S(z;\frac{j}{r+1},\frac{k}{r+1})\right)}G_{\mathcal{T}}(z,w)\frac{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}w}{z-w}-1

by directly using the contour integral formula for
\mathcal{T}_{n}(j,k)
.

Recall that the image of the limit of the perfect t-embedding lies in
\lozenge:=\{|x|+|y|<1\}
. Denote
G^{f}
and
\mathfrak{L}
are the rescaled generalized tower graph indexed by some function
f
and the interior of the arctic curves (the liquid region) inscribed in

. The action
{\widetilde{S}}(z)
has critical points that satisfy
{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime}(z)=0
and are conjugate pairs

Denote the critical point in the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
by
\displaystyle\xi
and
\theta_{\xi}:=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{arg}({\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi))
.

Remark 5.5
.

The boundary of the liquid region
\mathfrak{L}
can be determined by the critical points of the action
{\widetilde{S}}
. Namely, the arctic curves are determined by
{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime}(z)=0
when

. Then,
\mathfrak{L}
is the domain with boundary contour
f(x,y)^{2}=2(x^{2}+y^{2})
.

Let
j,k,n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\iota,\iota^{\prime}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
be such that
|\iota|+\left|\iota^{\prime}\right|=1
or
{\iota}+{\iota}^{\prime}=0
. We denote the edges in the dual graph
\left(G^{\prime f}_{n}\right)^{*}
by

, where

is the edge between
(j,k)
and
\left(j+\iota,k+\iota^{\prime}\right)
. Note that unlike the Aztec diamond in
[
4

, we include the case
{\iota}+{\iota}^{\prime}=0
corresponding to an edge between two hexagonal faces. We orient the edges so that they have a white vertex to the left. Recall that

d\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}\left(e_{\iota,\iota^{\prime}}(j,k)\right)=\pm\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}\left(j+\iota,k+\iota^{\prime}\right)-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j,k)\right)

where the sign depends on the orientation of the edge. Our goal is to show that the rigidity assumption holds for the perfect t-embedding of the generalized tower graph. We reproduce the assumption below for convenience.

Assumption 5.6
.

Given a compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathfrak{L}
, there exist positive constants
N_{\mathcal{K}},C_{\mathcal{K}}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{K}}
which only depend on

for all

. In addition, for
n>N_{\mathcal{K}}
and
e_{\iota,\iota^{\prime}}(j,k),e_{\tilde{\iota},\tilde{\iota}^{\prime}}(j,k)\subset\mathcal{K}
the angle between two adjacent edges
d\mathcal{T}_{n}\left(e_{\iota,\iota^{\prime}}(j,k)\right)
and

, which are adjacent to a common face, is contained in
\left(\varepsilon_{\mathcal{K}},\pi-\varepsilon_{\mathcal{K}}\right)
. These angles are precisely the angles of the faces of the image of the perfect t-embedding.

5.1.
Steepest descent analysis

To prove that the rigidity assumption holds, we must understand the leading term asymptotic of
d\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}\left(e_{\iota,\iota^{\prime}}(j,k)\right)
as
n\to\infty
. To that end, we make use of Eqn. (
5.8
) and perform a steepest descent analysis.

In order to understand
d\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
we fix an edge
e_{{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime}}(j,k)
. Note that, in the case
|{\iota}|+|{\iota}^{\prime}|=1
in which the edge connects faces in different columns, we may assume the height of column
j+{\iota}+k+{\iota}^{\prime}
is greater than that of column
j+k
. If this is not the case we may swap the roles of
(j,k)
and
(j+{\iota},k+{\iota}^{\prime})
which will only contribute an overall sign to
d\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
. We have the following lemma.

Lemma 5.7
.

For
|{\iota}|+|{\iota}^{\prime}|=1
, we have

(5.11)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j+{\iota},k+{\iota}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\left(r(n;j,k)+1\right)\left(S(w;\frac{j}{r(n;j,k)+1},\frac{k}{r(n;j,k)+1})-S(z;\frac{j}{r(n;j,k)+1},\frac{k}{r(n;j,k)+1})\right)}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{e}^{S(w;{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime})-S(z;{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime})}G_{\mathcal{T}}(z,w)\frac{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}w}{z-w}-1.

For
{\iota}+{\iota}^{\prime}=0
, we have

(5.12)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j+{\iota},k+{\iota}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\left(r(n;j,k)+1\right)\left(S(w;\frac{j}{r(n;j,k)+1},\frac{k}{r(n;j,k)+1})-S(z;\frac{j}{r(n;j,k)+1},\frac{k}{r(n;j,k)+1})\right)}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{(z-1)(w+1)}{(z+1)(w-1)}\right)^{\pm 1}G_{\mathcal{T}}(z,w)\frac{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}w}{z-w}-1

where the sign of the exponent matches the sign of
{\iota}
.

Proof.

Recall that we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j,k)=\mathcal{T}_{r(n;j,k)}(j,k)
with
r(n;j,k)+j+k
odd. Let
r:=r(n;j,k)
.

In the first case, as we assume the face
(j,k)
is in a column whose height is less than the height of the column containing the face
(j+{\iota},k+{\iota}^{\prime})
, we have

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j+{\iota},k+{\iota}^{\prime})=\mathcal{T}_{r}(j+{\iota},k+{\iota}^{\prime})=\mathcal{T}_{r+1}(j+{\iota},k+{\iota}^{\prime})

where the second equality holds for the AD when
r+j+k+{\iota}+{\iota}^{\prime}
is even, as is the case here. Note that
r+1+j+k+{\iota}+{\iota}^{\prime}
is odd so we may use the contour integral formula for
\mathcal{T}_{r+1}(j+{\iota},k+{\iota}^{\prime})
to write

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(j+{\iota},k+{\iota}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\left(r+2\right)\left(S(w;\frac{j+{\iota}}{r+2},\frac{k+{\iota}^{\prime}}{r+2})-S(z;\frac{j+{\iota}}{r+2},\frac{k+{\iota}^{\prime}}{r+2})\right)}G_{\mathcal{T}}(z,w)\frac{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}w}{z-w}-1.

The result then follows from the fact that

(n+2)S(z;\frac{j+{\iota}}{n+2},\frac{k+{\iota}^{\prime}}{n+2})=(n+1)S(z;\frac{j}{n+1},\frac{k}{n+1})+S(z;{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime}).

In the second case, we note that

\displaystyle(n+1)S(z;\frac{j+{\iota}}{n+1},\frac{k+{\iota}^{\prime}}{n+1})=

\displaystyle(n+1)S(z;\frac{j}{n+1},\frac{k}{n+1})

\displaystyle-\frac{{\iota}^{\prime}-{\iota}}{2}\log(z+1)-\frac{{\iota}-{\iota}^{\prime}}{2}\log(z-1).

The result then follows from applying the above to the contour integral formula for
\mathcal{T}_{r}(j+{\iota},k+{\iota}^{\prime})
observing that
{\iota}-{\iota}^{\prime}=\pm 2
.

∎

Using this we obtain a contour integral formula for
d\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}\left(e_{\iota,\iota^{\prime}}(j,k)\right)
.

Corollary 5.8
.

The difference of the t-embedding along an edge can be written as

(5.13)

\displaystyle d\widetilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}\left(e_{\iota,\iota^{\prime}}(j,k)\right)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{(1-\mathrm{i})}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\mathrm{e}^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(w)}\frac{w-\mathrm{i}}{w}\left(\frac{w}{(1+w)(w-1)}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon(j,k)+1}{2}}G_{{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime}}(w)\operatorname{d}w\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{-(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(z)}\frac{z-\mathrm{i}}{(z-1)(z+1)}\left(\frac{(1+z)(z-1)}{z}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon(j,k)+1}{2}}H_{{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime}}(z)\operatorname{d}z\right).

where

\displaystyle G_{1,0}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w-1},\quad G_{0,1}(w)=\frac{-1}{w+1},\quad G_{-1,0}(w)=\frac{1}{w+1},\quad G_{0,-1}(w)=\frac{1}{w-1},

\displaystyle H_{1,0}(z)

\displaystyle=H_{0,1}(z)=\frac{1}{z},,\quad H_{-1,0}(z)=H_{0,-1}(z)=1,

\displaystyle{G_{1,-1}(w)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{w-1},\quad G_{-1,1}(w)=\frac{-2}{w+1},\quad H_{1,-1}(z)=\frac{1}{z+1},\quad\text{and}\quad H_{-1,1}(z)=\frac{1}{z-1}.

Proof.

Using integral expressions in (
5.10
) and (
5.11
), in the case
|{\iota}|+|{\iota}^{\prime}|=1
, we may write
d\widetilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}\left(e_{\iota,\iota^{\prime}}(j,k)\right)
as

\begin{aligned} &\operatorname{d}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(e_{{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime}}(j,k))=\pm\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{(r+1)(S(w;\frac{j}{r+1},\frac{k}{r+1})-S(z;\frac{j}{r+1},\frac{k}{r+1}))}G_{\mathcal{T}}(z,w)\left(\mathrm{e}^{S(w;{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime})-S(z;{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime})}-1\right)\frac{\operatorname{d}z\operatorname{d}w}{z-w}\\
&=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{(n+1)({\widetilde{S}}(w;x,y)-{\widetilde{S}}(z;x,y))}G_{\mathcal{T}}(z,w)\left(\mathrm{e}^{S(w;{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime})-S(z;{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime})}-1\right)\left(\frac{w(1+z)(z-1)}{z(1+w)(w-1)}\right)^{\frac{{\epsilon(j,k)}+1}{2}}\frac{\operatorname{d}z\operatorname{d}w}{z-w}\end{aligned}

where the last equality is obtained by applying (
5.8
). Notice that the term
\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{e}^{S(w;{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime})-S(z;{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime})}-1\right)
simplifies to

\begin{cases}\frac{z-w}{z(w-1)},&{\iota}=1,{\iota}^{\prime}=0\\
\frac{z-w}{w+1},&{\iota}=-1,{\iota}^{\prime}=0\\
-\frac{z-w}{z(w+1)},&{\iota}=0,{\iota}^{\prime}=1\\
\frac{z-w}{w-1},&{\iota}=0,{\iota}^{\prime}=-1\end{cases}

which cancels out the factor of
z-w
in the denominator.

In the case
{\iota}+{\iota}^{\prime}=0
, we have the integral expression

\begin{aligned} &\operatorname{d}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}(e_{{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime}}(j,k))\\
&=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{(n+1)({\widetilde{S}}(w;x,y)-{\widetilde{S}}(z;x,y))}G_{\mathcal{T}}(z,w)\left(\left(\frac{(z-1)(w+1)}{(z+1)(w-1)}\right)^{\pm 1}-1\right)\left(\frac{w(1+z)(z-1)}{z(1+w)(w-1)}\right)^{\frac{{\epsilon(j,k)}+1}{2}}\frac{\operatorname{d}z\operatorname{d}w}{z-w}.\end{aligned}

The term
\left(\frac{(z-1)(w+1)}{(z+1)(w-1)}\right)^{\pm 1}-1
simplifies to

\begin{cases}\frac{2(z-w)}{(z+1)(w-1)},&{\iota}=1,{\iota}^{\prime}=-1\\
\frac{-2(z-w)}{(z-1)(w+1)},&{\iota}=-1,{\iota}^{\prime}=1\end{cases}

where, again, the factor of
z-w
in the denominator cancels.

In all cases, the two integrals factor and we can write the double contour integral formula as the product of two single integrals.
∎

This expression is similar to (6.4) of
[
4
]
up to the error term and thus the verification of the rigidity assumption will proceed similarly. We now perform the steepest descent/saddle point approximation separately for each factor. From here on, we denote
{\widetilde{S}}(z;x,y)={\widetilde{S}}(z)
since there is no more subtlety with the parameter.

Lemma 5.9
.

(5.14)

\displaystyle I_{w}(n):=\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\mathrm{e}^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(w)}\frac{w-\mathrm{i}}{w}\left(\frac{w}{(1+w)(w-1)}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon(j,k)+1}{2}}G_{{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime}}(w)\operatorname{d}w

\displaystyle\sim G(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\xi)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n+1}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{|{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)|}}e^{-i\theta_{\xi}}+G(\bar{\xi})e^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\bar{\xi})}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n+1}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{|{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\xi})|}}e^{-i\theta_{\bar{\xi}}}

where

G(w):=\frac{w-\mathrm{i}}{w}\left(\frac{w}{(1+w)(w-1)}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon(j,k)+1}{2}}G_{{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime}}(w).

Proof.

We perform the steepest descent asymptotic analysis on the integral
I_{w}(m)
. First, recall that the action
{\widetilde{S}}(z)
has two critical points/saddle points that are simple and also conjugate pairs.
And since
{\widetilde{S}}(z)
is analytic in the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
, the paths of steepest ascent and descent are the level lines
of
\operatorname{Im}({\widetilde{S}}(z))=\operatorname{Im}({\widetilde{S}}(\xi))
. These paths can only cross the real line at 0. We now describe
these paths. For the saddle point
\xi
on the upper-half plane, the steepest ascent contour start from
0
, passes through
\xi
and goes to
\infty
. The complete steepest ascent includes the reflection of the mentioned path in the lower half plane. We will denote the steepest ascent contour in
w
as
\gamma_{w}
. Note that the contour
\mathcal{E}_{2}
only has a singularity at
w=1
. Thus, we can deform
\mathcal{E}_{2}
to the steepest descent contour without picking up the residue at
w=1
. The Taylor expansion near the saddle point
\xi
of the action
{\widetilde{S}}(w)
gives,

{\widetilde{S}}(w)={\widetilde{S}}(\xi)+\frac{1}{2}{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)(w-\xi)^{2}+O(w^{3})

since the first derivative vanishes at
\xi
. Writing
{\widetilde{S}}(w)=u(w)+\mathrm{i}v(w)
, then along the steepest descending path, the integral
I_{\xi}(n)
is approximated,

(5.15)

\displaystyle I_{\xi}(n)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\xi)}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}G(w)\mathrm{e}^{(n+1)({\widetilde{S}}(w)-{\widetilde{S}}(\xi))}\operatorname{d}w

\displaystyle\sim G(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\xi)}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(n+1)(v(w)-v(\xi))}\mathrm{e}^{(n+1)(u(w)-u(\xi))}\operatorname{d}w

\displaystyle\sim G(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\xi)}\int_{\gamma_{w}}\mathrm{e}^{(n+1)(u(w)-u(\xi))}\mathrm{d}w

where
\gamma_{w}
is the steepest descent contour. Note that along this contour, the imaginary part of
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
stays constant so
v(w)=v(\xi)
. Let
s^{2}=u(\xi)-u(w)
, then we can rewrite (
5.15
) as

(5.16)

\displaystyle I_{\xi}(n)\sim G(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\xi)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(n+1)s^{2}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}s}\right)\mathrm{d}s.

Note that we have the Gaussian integral
\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(n+1)s^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n+1}}
, so the leading term of the asymptotic expression near the saddle point where
s=0
gives

I_{\xi}(n)\sim G(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\xi)}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}s}\right)_{s=0}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n+1}}.

On the other hand, since
v(w)
is constant along the steepest descent path,
-s^{2}=u(w)-u(\xi)={\widetilde{S}}(w)-{\widetilde{S}}(\xi)
. By taking derivative with respect of
w
twice, we have

-2s\left(\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{{\mathrm{d}w}}\right)={\widetilde{S}}^{\prime}(w),

-2\left(\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{{\mathrm{d}w}}\right)\left(\frac{{\mathrm{d}s}}{{\mathrm{d}w}}\right)-2s\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}s}{{\mathrm{d}w}^{2}}={\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(w)

and so
\displaystyle\left(\frac{{\mathrm{d}w}}{{\mathrm{d}s}}\right)_{s=0}=\left(\frac{-2}{{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)}\right)^{1/2}
.
This square root has some ambiguity in sign. To avoid this issue, denote
\displaystyle\theta_{SDP}:=\operatorname{arg}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}s}\right)_{s=0}
. Then,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{{\mathrm{d}w}}{{\mathrm{d}s}}\right)_{s=0}=\left(\frac{2}{|{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)|}\right)^{1/2}e^{i\theta_{SDP}}
.
On the other hand, recall that we define
\theta_{\xi}:=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{arg}({\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi))
. Let
{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)=R\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{2}i\theta_{\xi}}
and
w-\xi=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{SDP}}
, we have,

{\widetilde{S}}(w)-{\widetilde{S}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)(w-\xi)^{2}+O(w^{3})\sim\frac{1}{2}(Rr^{2})\mathrm{e}^{2i(\theta_{\xi}+\theta_{SDP})}.

But note that since we are on the steepest descent path
\gamma_{w}
,
2(\theta_{\xi}+\theta_{SDP})=\pm\pi
and thus
\theta_{SDP}=-\theta_{\xi}\pm\frac{\pi}{2}
(where the sign is dependent on the direction of the contour) and we have the asymptotic of
I_{\xi}(n)
given by,

(5.17)

I_{\xi}(n)\sim G(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{(n+1)S(\xi)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n+1}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{|S^{\prime\prime}(\xi)|}}\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta_{\xi}}.

Recall that the contour
\mathcal{E}_{2}
has also passes through the other critical point
\bar{\xi}
on the lower half-plane. The analysis is similar and we have

(5.18)

I_{\bar{\xi}}(n)\sim G(\bar{\xi})\mathrm{e}^{(n+1)S(\bar{\xi})}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n+1}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{|S^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\xi})|}}\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta_{\bar{\xi}}}.

Altogether, we have that the leading order asymptotic of the integral
I_{w}(n)
is given by

\displaystyle I_{w}(n)

\displaystyle=I_{\xi}(n)+I_{\bar{\xi}}(n)

\displaystyle\sim G(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\xi)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n+1}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{|{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)|}}\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta_{\xi}}+G(\bar{\xi})\mathrm{e}^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\bar{\xi})}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n+1}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{|{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\xi})|}}\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta_{\bar{\xi}}}.

∎

Similarly, we can also perform the steepest descent analysis for the integral in
z
along the contour
\mathcal{E}_{1}
.

Corollary 5.10
.

I_{z}(n):=\int_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{-(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(z)}\frac{z-\mathrm{i}}{(z-1)(z+1)}\left(\frac{(1+z)(z-1)}{z}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon(j,k)+1}{2}}H_{{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime}}(z)\operatorname{d}z.

The analysis is similar to Lem.
5.9
except for the contour of integration. For the contour integral in

, which passes through

and

Combining Lem.
5.9
and Cor.
5.10
, we have the asymptotic expansion of the edge length.

Lemma 5.11
.

(5.20)

\begin{aligned} d\widetilde{\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}\left(e_{\iota,\iota^{\prime}}(j,k)\right)&\sim\frac{1-\mathrm{i}}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{2}}\left(G(\xi)e^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\xi)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n+1}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{|{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)|}}e^{-i\theta_{\xi}}+G(\bar{\xi})e^{(n+1){\widetilde{S}}(\bar{\xi})}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{n+1}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{|{\widetilde{S}}^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\xi})|}}e^{-i\theta_{\bar{\xi}}}\right)\\

The equality comes from the facts
\displaystyle e^{(n+1)({\widetilde{S}}(\xi)-{\widetilde{S}}(\bar{\xi}))}=e^{2\mathrm{i}(n+1)\operatorname{Im}({\widetilde{S}}(\xi))}

5.2.
Edges and angles bounds - rigidity condition

All proofs and results in this section are identical to those in Section 6.3 of

, with only some differences in notation to accommodate the error terms coming from approximating the function

We would like to use the asymptotic / leading terms in Lem.

to prove the boundedness of the lengths and angles and confirm the rigidity assumption. Recall that

G(w)=\frac{w-\mathrm{i}}{w}\left(\frac{w}{(1+w)(w-1)}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon(j,k)+1}{2}}G_{{\iota},{\iota}^{\prime}}(w),

and

Let

{\alpha}=\frac{1}{e^{2\mathrm{i}\theta_{\xi}}},\qquad\beta=e^{-2\mathrm{i}(n+1)\operatorname{Im({\widetilde{S}}(\xi))}}.

Then

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Im}\left\{{H_{{\iota}_{1},{\iota}^{\prime}_{1}}(\xi)}H_{{\iota}_{2},{\iota}^{\prime}_{2}}(\bar{\xi})\left(|\frac{\xi-{\mathrm{i}}}{\bar{\xi}-{\mathrm{i}}}|^{2}G_{{\iota}_{1},{\iota}^{\prime}_{1}}(\bar{\xi})G_{{\iota}_{2},{\iota}^{\prime}_{2}}(\xi)+G_{{\iota}_{1},{\iota}^{\prime}_{1}}(\xi)G_{{\iota}_{2},{\iota}^{\prime}_{2}}(\bar{\xi})\right)\right\}}{\left|\left(\bar{\alpha}\beta\frac{\bar{\xi}+{\mathrm{i}}}{\xi+{\mathrm{i}}}G_{{\iota}_{1},{\iota}^{\prime}_{1}}(\bar{\xi})\frac{\xi}{\bar{\xi}}\left(\frac{\bar{\xi}(\xi^{2}-1)}{\xi(\bar{\xi}^{2}-1)}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon(j,k)+1}{2}}+G_{{\iota}_{1},{\iota}^{\prime}_{1}}(\xi)\right)\right|\times\left|\left({\alpha}\bar{\beta}\frac{\xi-{\mathrm{i}}}{\bar{\xi}-{\mathrm{i}}}G_{{\iota}_{2},{\iota}^{\prime}_{2}}(\xi)\frac{\bar{\xi}}{\xi}\left(\frac{\xi(\bar{\xi}^{2}-1)}{\bar{\xi}(\xi^{2}-1)}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon(j,k)+1}{2}}+G_{{\iota}_{2},{\iota}^{\prime}_{2}}(\bar{\xi})\right)\right|}

Note that for (
5.31
), in all cases, either one of the factors
G_{{\iota}_{1},{\iota}^{\prime}_{1}}(\xi)G_{{\iota}_{2},{\iota}^{\prime}_{2}}(\bar{\xi})
or
{H_{{\iota}_{1},{\iota}^{\prime}_{1}}(\xi)}H_{{\iota}_{2},{\iota}^{\prime}_{2}}(\bar{\xi})
will be a real number and the other factor will have non-trivial imaginary part bounded away from zero. This finished the proof for the case when
{\iota}+{\iota}^{\prime}=0

∎
