Uncovering the prevalence, risk factors and consequences of school bullying in China’s rural primary schools
Authors: Wenting Liu, Yuyang Xie, Yajie Wang, Bin Tang, Hongyu Guan, Fang Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchool bullying has emerged as a significant global issue with profound implications for the cognitive and non-cognitive development of children. Despite a growing body of research on bullying in urban areas, there is a dearth of comprehensive studies focusing on rural and underdeveloped regions. This study aims to fill this gap by examining the prevalence of bullying, its contributing factors, and its associations between cognitive and non-cognitive abilities of rural primary school students in underdeveloped regions.MethodThe study utilized data from a social-emotional survey conducted among 4134 fourth-grade students from rural primary schools in three cities of Gansu province, China. Bullying victimization was measured using the “Students Bullied at School” (SBS) scale, cognitive ability was assessed through standardized math scores, and non-cognitive ability was evaluated using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ). Socioeconomic data were also collected, encompassing student, family, teacher, and school characteristics. To ensure the robustness of the findings, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to address potential sample selection bias.ResultsThe study revealed a high prevalence of bullying victimization in less developed rural China, with boys, older students, and those from single-parent families particularly vulnerable. It highlighted family factors like lower parental education, single parenthood, and maternal migration as predictors of bullying. A higher student-teacher ratio associated with increased bullying incidents. The research linked bullying to diminished cognitive performance, reflected in lower math scores, and adverse impacts on non-cognitive skills, marked by elevated difficulties and diminished prosocial behavior among affected students.ConclusionsThe findings underscore the critical need for targeted interventions to mitigate school bullying in rural China. Understanding the risk factors and the negative consequences of bullying is essential for developing effective prevention strategies and improving student welfare. The study contributes to the literature by providing insights into bullying in underdeveloped areas and by using internationally standardized measures, which enhances the comparability of the results with global studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25101-0.Keywords: School bullying, Child development, Cognitive and non-cognitive abilities, Bullying prevalence, Risk factors

Background

Bullying in schools has emerged as a global issue since its initial identification in the late 1960s [1], with its widespread occurrence prompting extensive research interest across the globe [2–4]. The Health Behavior in School-aged Children (HBSC) study, which encompassed 35 countries and regions, reported that he respondents had experienced bullying at least once (34%) or two or three times (11%) in the past few months [5]. An additional cross- national survey, focusing on adolescents aged 13 to 15 years across four continents—Africa, America, Asia, and the Middle East—found that the prevalence of bullying victimization varied significantly from 7.8% (Tajikistan, Asia) to 60.9% (Zambia, Africa) [6].

The basic definition of bullying victimization is “consistently subjected to the negative actions of one or more peers over an extended period” [7]. Scholarly research describe it as “intentional, repetitive behaviors that involve a power imbalance” [8]. Studies have identified various risk factors linked to school bullying. Individually, bullying incidents tend to peak at age 13 and then decline significantly between ages 11 and 15 [5]. Ethnic differences also play a role, but conclusions are inconsistent [3, 9]. At the family level, bullying victims often have less communication with their families and are more likely to come from single-parent households [10, 11]. Additional family-related factors, such as lower socio-economic status [12] and lower parental education levels [13], are also associated with increased bullying victimization. In the school environment, a positive school climate [14] and supportive teacher attitudes [15, 16]can reduce bullying behaviors. Conversely, bullied students often experience lower rates of school affiliation, negative peer interactions, and weak problem-solving skills [17, 18].

Being bullying victimization can inhibit children and adolescents development at school and even future labour productivity [19, 20]. Extensive researches demonstrate that students being bullied suffer worse cognition performance (such as math, reading, dictation, the reduction enrollment of college) than their peers [21–23]. Studies from a non- cognition perspective reveal that bullied students tend to have lower creative attitude [21]and self-esteem [24], more depression and anxiety [25], increased feelings of loneliness and tobacco/alcohol abuse [26]. Furthermore, bullying victims demonstrate a 40% decrease in current skills or abilities and are more prone (38%) to be re-victimized [27].

School bullying in China is prevalent, with reported rates ranging widely from 2% to 73%, and there is a significant concentration of research focus on the issue within large cities [28–34]. Studies in these regions have concentrated on identifying bullying risk factors, exploring the psychological aspects of bullying behaviors, and examining the link between bullying and sleep quality. However, comprehensive studies on bullying associated with the correlated factors and overall development of children are scared. Moreover, empirical research on the thorough understanding of bullying in developing regions is limited. Underdeveloped areas, especially rural primary schools, are often more susceptible to bullying due to limited school resources and support services [21, 35, 36], lower economic conditions leading to parental absence, which affect children’s conflict management and social skills [37], ultimately increasing their vulnerability. Given these unique challenges and vulnerabilities, as well as the negative impacts of bullying on the cognitive abilities and non-cognitive abilities of children and adolescents mentioned above, a full insight of bullying in China and other impoverished areas is essential in accumulating human capital and enhancing future labour productivity [19, 20].

The objective of this paper is to conduct a comprehensive analysis of bullying within underdeveloped regions, encompassing the incidence of bullying, the contributing factors, and the consequences experienced by its victims. This paper is structured around three primary objectives. First, we assess the prevalence of bullying within primary schools in these regions. Second, we identify the various factors associated with bullying, encompassing individual, family, and school perspectives. Third, we investigate the relationship between bullying and the cognitive and non- cognitive abilities of affected children To ensure the reliability of our findings, we employ propensity score matching as a methodological approach. This technique will bolster the robustness of our results, thereby providing a more accurate understanding of the bullying phenomenon in the underdeveloped context.

The structure of this paper is organized into five sections. Section 2 is an introduction of methods for the selected samples, the criteria used for assessing bullying, cognition and non-cognition of samples. Section 3 is the main findings of the paper: the prevalence of bullying, the identification of factors correlated with bullying victimization, and the link between bullying victimization and cognitive or non-cognitive abilities of the affected children. Section 4 is the discussions and conclusion of the findings.

Methods

Sample

The data for this study were derived from a social-emotional survey conducted among rural students in three cities (henceforth referred to as Cities A, B, and C) in Gansu province, northwestern China. According to the Gansu Provincial Bureau of Statistics, Gansu’s GDP per capita in 2022 was 44,968 yuan, ranking 27th among 31 provinces in China. Despite the focus on a single province in China, our sample is representative of the general situation in rural areas of northwest China.

To select our sample, we used a two-step sample selection protocol. First, as our study focused on rural areas, we obtained a list of all districts and counties from the three cities and excluded areas entirely within the cities, resulting in a sample of 13 counties.

The second step involved selecting the sample schools and students. Taken cost into consideration, we acquired a list of all primary schools in the selected counties from the education departments, excluding schools located in the county cities, schools without full grade coverage, schools with fewer than 20 fourth-grade students, and schools with more than three fourth-grade classes. Specifically, if a sample school had two or fewer fourth-grade classrooms, all such classes were included. At the same time, the math teachers of the selected classes were included in the study.

In general, following the application of these selection criteria, the final sample consisted of 129 schools and 4543 fourth-grade students from rural primary schools in Gansu Province. Subsequently, after handling missing data and conducting data cleaning procedures, which included logical calibration and the removal of invalid questionnaires (details in Supplementary Table A1), a total of 4134 students were included in the analysis for this study. The schools and students were distributed as follows: 78 schools (60.5%) and 2382 students (57.6%) in City A, 47 schools (36.4%) and 1594 (38.6%) in City B, and 4 schools (3.1%) and 158 students (3.8%) in City C (Supplementary Table A2).

Data collection

All data collection was carried out by Center for Experimental Economics in Education at Shaanxi Normal University in 2023. We collected data in four blocks. The first block collected information on bullying victimization in school. The second block collected data on students’ academic performance to assess their cognitive ability. The third block evaluated students’ non-cognitive ability using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ). Unique identifiers were used to link data across the SDQ, SBS, and academic attainment measures, with the reliability of this linkage enhanced through the use of pilot-tested scales and math tests conducted by trained surveyors. The final block of data collection involved gathering socioeconomic information from multiple sources. Student questionnaires captured details such as age, gender, boarding status, and parental education level. Meanwhile, questionnaires completed by principals and teachers provided data on the number of students and teachers, as well as the gender of the math teacher.

Bullying victimization

Bullying victimization was measured using the “Students Bullied at School” (SBS) scale [38–40]. This scale has been employed to measure the frequency of peer bullying among children in fourth grade in a wide array of 52 countries and regions, spanning diverse developmental and economic levels. For this study, the SBS questionnaire was translated into Mandarin Chinese [21]. The SBS scale has shown high reliability when administered in rural China, with a Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of 0.93.

The SBS scale includes six items asking students how often they experienced specific bullying behaviors: (a) being made fun of or called names, (b)being excluded from games or activities, (c) having lies spread about them, (d) having something stolen, (e) being hit or hurt (e.g., shoving, hitting, kicking), and (f) being forced to do things they did not want to do. Responses were quantified with “at least once a week” = 0, “once or twice a week” = 1, “once or twice a month” = 2, and “never” = 3, resulting in raw scores ranging from 0 (exposure to all six types weekly) to 18 (no exposure). Therefore, a lower SBS score corresponds to a higher level of bullying victimization in school. Subsequently, raw scores were transformed into SBS scaled scores, categorizing of students into three bullying frequency groups: “Almost Never”, “About Monthly”, and “About Weekly” [21]. Transformed scores range from 3 to 13 points with scores above 10.1 indicating “Almost Never” bullied, scores between 8.3 to 10.1 indicating “About Monthly” bullied, and scores below 8.3 indicating “About Weekly” bullied. Students bullied “about weekly” and “about monthly” are classified as “frequently bullied”.

Cognitive ability

To assess cognitive ability, we used standardized math scores as a measure of academic performance. Firstly, The project team first invited teachers experienced in teaching elementary school math in rural areas to create the test, ensuring the questions aligned with the textbooks and the teaching progress of the sample students. The test questions were also piloted multiple times in non-sample schools to ensure their appropriateness for the sample students’ ability level. Secondly, all fourth-grade students from the sample schools used the same math test paper. Lastly, to ensure the accuracy of the test results and eliminate interfering factors, the project team created Test A and Test B (with the same questions in different orders), and members of the research team proctored the exams to ensure that sample students and their deskmates received different versions. Test scores were normalized as z-scores by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation (SD) of the test score distribution, a process crucial for making the results comparable across different groups of students by accounting for variations in ability levels and test difficulty.

Non-cognitive ability

Students’ non-cognitive abilities were measured using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [41]. The SDQ consists of 25 questions divided into five dimensions: (a) emotional problems, (b) conduct problems, (c) peer problems, (d) hyperactivity and (e) prosocial behavior. By summing the scores of the four difficulty subscales (excluding prosocial behavior), an overall difficulty score can be calculated [42]. Responses are rated on a scale from 0 to 2 (0 = not true, 1 = somewhat true, 2 = certainly true) [43]. After adaptation and validation for use in China, the SDQ scale demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.927) and moderate test-retest reliability (Pearson’s correlation = 0.719). Adequate time was provided for students to complete the questionnaire [44].The questionnaire were completed by the students in this survey.

Socioeconomic information

Comprehensive socioeconomic data were collected, including information about students, their families, teachers, and schools (Supplementary Table A3). Students information included gender, age, ethnic, boarding, and whether he is the only kid of the family. Moreover, additional data collected included their parents’ marital status to identify single-parent families, and information on parents’ education levels and migration status. A eleven-item checklist of household assets was used to calculate wealth quartiles via principal components analysis. Subsequently, we segmented the household wealth into four quartiles, with values ranging from 1 (lowest quartile) to 4 (highest quartile). Finally, data on the gender of math teachers, the school’s distance from the local government seat, and the student-teacher ratio were collected.

Analytical approach

This study employed three primary statistical methods. First, we compiled summary statistics of the sample, including variables and the prevalence of bullying. The variables were characteristics such as individual level: gender (1 = boy), age (years), ethnic (1 = minority), boarding (1 = yes), family level: only child (1 = yes), single parent-family (1 = yes), parental education (1 = junior high school and above), parental migration for work (1 = yes), asset value (1 = the lowest 25%), school level: gender of math teacher (1 = boy), distance from local government seat (1 = the closest 25%), and student-teacher ratio (1 = the lowest 25%).

Second, we used T-test measure to identify the vulnerable students being bullied among various groups categorized by individual, family, and school characteristics. Significant differences were also observed within these groups Bullying victims were the students subjecting to bullying at school almost weekly and almost monthly across the six subcategories of the SBS scale. Multivariate ordinary least squares regressions (OLS) models without fixed effectswith county-level fixed effects, and logistic regressions were then applied to examine the relationship between bullying victimization and various characteristics. Specially, unlike the binary variable used in the other three models, bullying victimization was categorized as 0 = never, 1 = monthly, and 2 = weekly in ordinal logistic regression (ologit) model.

Thirdly, T-tests identified significant differences in cognitive ability, non-cognitive ability (total difficulties and prosocial) among groups and analyzed the effect of bullying on cognitive ability, total difficulties, and prosocial behavior. And then, we assessed the correlation between bullying (monthly or weekly) and cognitive ability (measured by math performance) using OLS regression models without fixed effects and with county-fixed effects respectively. We also evaluated the relationship between bullying and non-cognitive abilities (measured by the SDQ scores for total difficulties and prosocial behavior) using OLS regression models with county-fixed effects. To control for potential confounders, we conducted a multivariate analysis with a vector of control variables.

To address the potential sample selection bias and confirm the robustness of our results, we used propensity score matching methods (PSM). PSM is a technique used to create a sample that simulates a randomized trial by matching treated and untreated subjects based on their propensity scores, thereby mitigating endogeneity in sample selection. This approach is widely used for robustness testing in various applications [45]. Given the association factors identified in our previous analysis, the covariates used in creating propensity scores encompassed numerous student characteristics (e.g., gender, boarding, ethnic, parental education, and student-teacher ratios). We used three PSM methods: one-to-one matching, caliper matching, and kernel matching to explore the effects of being bullied on cognitive and non-cognitive ability, to explore the effects of being bullied on cognitive and non-cognitive abilities. All analyses are conducted using Stata 18.0 (Stata Corp., Texas, USA). Two-sided tests are employed, and significance is determined at p < 0.1.

Prevalence of bullying among primary school students in rural China

Table 2 presents the prevalence of different forms of bullying among the sample, measured by the “Students Bullied at School” (SBS) scale. Notably, 68% of students reported that their belongings had been stolen by peers at least a few times in the past year. Additionally, 55% of students indicated that they had been subjected to name-calling or teasing with the same frequency. The other types of bullying behaviors were also prevalent, with comparable percentages. Specifically, 48% of students experienced physical harm such as shoving, hitting, or kicking, while 47% reported being the target of rumors or lies spread by others. Furthermore, 45% of students were excluded from group activities or games by their peers. Alarmingly, 41% of students disclosed that they were coerced into doing things they were unwilling to do by classmates.

These findings indicate that bullying negatively impacts both academic performance and social-emotional well-being.

Particularly, the coefficient for emotional problems was relatively high at 0.344, suggesting that bullying is strongly associated with increased emotional distress. In the context of prosocial behaviors, as indicated in column 5, the negative coefficient of -0.152 suggests that bullied students are less likely to exhibit prosocial behaviors.

Propensity score matching (PSM) results

Recognizing that bullying victimization is not randomly assigned and can be influenced by a multitude of factors, including gender, boarding status, ethnicity, parental education, and student-teacher ratios, there exists the potential for bias in the regression analysis [21, 46, 47]. To address this, we employed three matching protocols within the Propensity Score Matching (PSM) framework: one-to-one matching, caliper matching, and kernel matching. Balance tests for the post-matching samples are detailed in Supplementary Table A4.

Table 8 presents the PSM results. Column 1 presents the association between bullying and cognitive ability. The average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) using one-to-one matching was − 0.123 (p < 0.01), indicating that bullied students tended to have 0.123 lower academic performance scores compared to non-bullied students. This association was consistent across caliper matching and kernel matching, with ATT values of -0.120 (p < 0.01) and − 0.126 (p < 0.01), respectively. Columns 2 and 3 detail the associations between bullying and non-cognitive abilities. For total difficulties, the ATT from one-to-one matching was 0.387 (p < 0.01), indicating increased difficulties among bullied students. In terms of prosocial behaviors, the ATT was − 0.132 (p < 0.01), suggesting decreased prosocial scores for bullied students. Caliper matching and kernel matching provided similar results, with ATT values for total difficulties of 0.410 and 0.415, respectively, and for prosocial behaviors of -0.142 and − 0.137, respectively. The results across all columns in Table 8 are statistically significant at the 1% level, underscoring the robustness of the findings.

Discussions and conclusion

School bullying has become a globally recognized concern nowadays. This study investigates the prevalence of school bullying among 4134 fourth-grade students in rural areas of China. Furthermore, it identifies the factors correlated with bullying behavior and examines the associations between bullying victimization and the overall development of children. Gaining a thorough understanding of bullying is essential as it lays the groundwork for effective strategies to mitigate such behavior in schools.

Our research reveals a concerning high rate of bullying victimization among rural primary school students in the sample area, particularly among those around 11 years old. The incidence rate is similar to that of peers in other areas within China (70%) and abroad (68%) [48, 49]. Consistent with the previous research, our findings reveal that boys, often considered as a more aggressive population, are more prone to victimization by bullying [2]. Students who board outside of their homes are more likely to be involved in school bullying incidents, primarily due to the feelings of physical and emotional insecurity they commonly experience [50]. Contrasting with other findings, our study indicates that ethnic minorities is more likely to be bullied than ethnic majorities. Future research in these areas may place a heightened focus on ethnic minorities to identify the casual relationship.

As the family level, children with lower educated parents, lacking the ability to cope with peer relationship, seem more likely to be the targets of bullying. The reason is that parental educational level indicates general knowledge and problem-solving skills, which in turn affect children’s social skills and capacity to deal with peer relationships [51]. Families characterized by single parenthood, maternal migration, or low income are all correlates with elevated risk of being bullied. One explanation is that financial challenges on these households limit the time and attention available for children, thereby making it harder for children to get timely support and adjust their improper behaviors [37]. Further contributing explanation may include reduced maternal engagement within the family, decreased communication with the children, and the weakening parent-child bonds [11, 13]. As the school level, a higher student-teacher ratio is related to increased incidence of bullying victims, as it results from less individualized attention for each student [21].

Furthermore, our research has identified a negative correlation between these abilities and bullying victimization.

Students who experience bullying on a monthly or weekly basis show a significant decline of 0.148 standard deviations in cognitive ability. Furthermore, bullying victimization is associated with more difficulties, as evidenced by a 0.344 standard deviation increase in emotional problems, a 0.270 standard deviation increase in conduct problems, a 0.312 standard deviation increase in peer relationship issues, and a 0.211 standard deviation increase in hyperactivity among those who are bullied. The study also reveals a concerning decrease of 0.152 standard deviations in prosocial behaviors among students who have been victimized by bullying.

We recognize several limitations in our study that require consideration. First, our study has concentrated on the correlation between bullying victimization and children’s cognitive and non-cognitive abilities, yet it does not look into causal relationships. It is possible, for instance, that the relation between bullying and abilities is bidirectional and that a student’s weak abilities in school may made them vulnerable to bullying. Subsequent research could aim to understand the impact and underlying mechanisms of bullying on children’s overall development, which would be instrumental in devising effective strategies to protect students from the adverse effects of school bullying. Second, the study only examines relations between bullying victimization and their abilities within the same time frame, a thorough examination of the long-term consequences of bullying on children’s overall development is needed, and studying the enduring trends of bullying could aid in a more insightful understanding of bullying. Third, our study collected parents’ education levels from students, potentially causing inaccuracies due to incomplete or imprecise child-reported information. This limitation impacts results on parental education’s impact on bullying and student development. Future research should explore more reliable methods, like surveying parents directly or using administrative records, to improve accuracy.

Despite its limitations, our study offers several key strengths that enhance its contribution to both global and China-specific literature on child development. First, it is among the few studies that focus on the issue of bullying in rural and underdeveloped regions of China, providing insights into its prevalence, influencing factors, and potential consequences. This focus allows for a better understanding of bullying as a social phenomenon in these areas. Second, the use of a large and representative sample increases the robustness of our findings and enhances their relevance to other regions, offering valuable insights for broader applications. Third, by employing internationally standardized scales, including the SBS scale and measures of cognitive and non-cognitive abilities, our results are more comparable with related studies globally, facilitating further research and interventions in this field.

Our study highlights critical risk factors and the negative impact of bullying on student development. These findings offer practical strategies for policymakers to prevent school bullying and improve student welfare. To address bullying in disadvantaged communities (family factors like lower parental education, single parenthood, and maternal migration), it’s essential to establish support groups for sharing experiences and strategies [52], offer after-school programs with social skills training [53], and provide accessible counseling services to to build children’s resilience and mitigate the emotional impacts of bullying on children and parents [54]. By fostering a safer and more supportive school environment, we can enhance the quality of education in developing countries and regions worldwide.
