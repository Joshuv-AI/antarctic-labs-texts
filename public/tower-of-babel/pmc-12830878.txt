The impacts of China’s low-carbon technology trade on modern energy access in Africa: the role of domestic absorptive capacity
Authors: Dinkneh Gebre Borojo, Miao Miao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMotivated by the increase in China’s low-carbon technology product exports to Africa and the ongoing challenges of energy poverty (EP) in African countries (ACs), this paper investigates the effects of China’s low-carbon technology trade (CLTT) on access to modern energy (AE) using China’s low-carbon technology product imports of 42 ACs for the period of 2005–2021. We employ the instrumental variable-generalized methods of moments and Lewbel’s instrumental variable-two-stage least squares methods to control for endogeneity concerns. Additionally, a panel threshold regression model is employed to examine the threshold effect of domestic absorptive capacity, controlling for variation in the size of economic development (ED), control of corruption (CC), and financial development (FD) in ACs. Our results indicate that CLTT significantly improves AE in Africa, implying that harnessing low-carbon technology product trade contributes to improving AE and mitigating EP in Africa. Moreover, ED, CC, and FD positively moderate the relationship between CLTT and AE. Besides, the panel threshold results imply that the promoting effect of CLTT on AE increases with the improvement of ED and the FD levels of ACs and optimization of governance quality. These results contribute to achieving SDG-7 and provide policymakers with valuable insights.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32837-7.Keywords: Low-carbon technology product trade, China-Africa, Access to modern energy, IV approach, Threshold analysisSubject terms: Economics, Economics, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Access to modern energy (AE) is a vital element for achieving global environmental sustainability and development goals1. Energy poverty (EP), which is characterized by inadequate access to clean energy, modern and advanced energy technologies, availability, and affordability constraints, contributes to a lack of AE and continues to be a global concern2. EP in developing economies indicates a lack of AE sources3. Additionally, AE is a crucial element in achieving the SDGs in developing countries and deeply undermines the well-being, disrupts the provision of social services, and hinders economic progress (ED)4. It is aligned with SDG-7, which aims to ensure access to affordable, reliable, sustainable, and modern energy for all5,6.

Most African countries (ACs) suffer energy shortages and are consequently challenged with continuous EP7. For example, nearly 600 million people across Africa still live without electricity8. In addition, an estimated 970 million people do not have access to clean cooking options in Africa8. Specifically, over 70% of people in Sub-Saharan ACs remain without electricity and energy access continues to fall behind the region’s rapid population growth9. Besides, a significant proportion of the population depends on traditional biomass such as firewood, crop residue, dung, and charcoal, which have detrimental health and environmental consequences10. Therefore, achieving universal access to clean cooking fuels and technologies in Africa by 2030 requires a significant shift in current trends, as the number of people lacking access has been progressively increasing8.

The energy sector transition has been given special attention in the low-carbon development agenda to mitigate carbon emissions, aligning with Paris Agreement targets. In this context, curbing carbon emissions to meet targets requires the deployment of low-carbon technologies on a global scale and at the regional level11. Additionally, low-carbon technologies have long been recognized for their ability to support sustained economic growth while preserving environmental integrity12. It encompasses all technological advancements aimed at reducing carbon emissions and is considered a vital way to achieve the SDGs13.

Therefore, the deployment of low-carbon technologies is especially important in developing economies, which are typically not producers of low-carbon technologies11. More specifically, the widespread deployment of low-carbon technologies is crucial for ACs, which currently lack the technological infrastructure needed to meet the SDG targets14. With non-renewable energy sources still dominating, fossil fuels account for a major share of total energy use in ACs15, and growing energy demand continues to increase CO₂ emissions16. However, energy consumption patterns of ACs indicated a gradual shift from traditional fuels to cleaner energy sources15.

Moreover, according to United Nations reports, over the past two decades, trade in environmental goods has expanded markedly, with green energy technologies constituting the largest share of the total trade value12. Similarly, global trade in renewable energy products is estimated to increase by more than 180% between 2000 and 2020, surpassing the annual growth rate of total merchandise trade17. Additionally, trade in low-carbon technologies has steadily increased, accounting for more than 35% of global trade by 202018. Therefore, the diffusion of low-carbon technologies reduces EP by enhancing energy efficiency19. Moreover, trade promotes the long-term adoption of renewable energy by enabling the flow of advanced technologies and the import and export of renewable energy components essential for efficient energy generation20. Therefore, trade in low-carbon technologies is essential, particularly for developing countries where access to advanced technologies remains limited21.

Interestingly, China has become the foremost exporter of low-carbon technologies, rapidly closing the gap with its Western nations22. In addition, China contributes significantly to enhancing access to energy resources and facilitating the transfer of technology23,24. It has also placed strong emphasis on advancing decarbonization technologies, particularly in terms of clean power generation, transportation, and energy storage. Combined exports from these areas totaled $174 billion, representing 68% of CLTT25. This trend underscores China’s strategic commitment to expanding its capabilities in cutting-edge clean technologies, an essential driver of the global energy transition. In addition, China has actively advanced the creation of low-carbon technologies and promoted their spread to developing nations through initiatives focused on climate technology transfer26.

With respect to Africa’s trade, China has become the top export destination and has become the dominant source of imports for ACs, supplying manufactured goods, low-carbon technologies, energy products, and machinery27,28. More specifically, China’s exports to ACs achieved a compound average growth of 16.1% from 2005 to 2021 (Fig. 1). Similarly, China’s low-carbon technology products exported to Africa experienced significant growth, with an average increase of 21% during the same period (Fig. 1). Likewise, access to electricity and clean fuels and technologies for cooking (CFTC) energy have shown upward trends in ACs (Fig. 2). Trade in these goods allows importing nations to access high-efficiency technologies that would be difficult or costly to develop independently29. Therefore, to achieve the goal of universal access to AE services, ACs need to promote access to low-carbon technologies.

Within this framework, this study focuses on an important question that remains unexplored: How does CLTT affect AE in Africa? Hence, this research aims to analyze the role of CLTT in AE in ACs. Conceptually, low-carbon technology trade significantly shapes the energy transition and AE. The theoretical link between low-carbon technology trade and AE can be found in technology diffusion theory, as suggested by Nelson and Phelps30. Trade promotes AE and adversely affects EP by promoting information diffusion, technological innovation, economic scale, foreign capital, and managerial expertise, which increases AE and ultimately mitigates EP31. It also promotes the development of clean energy technologies and energy-efficient products and services17. Trade in technology products forms the primary transmission mechanism through which technologies flow across countries32. Therefore, theoretically, it is contended that the low-carbon technology trade can be among the most vital mechanisms promoting AE. From policy prospects, it is further contended that low-carbon technology diffusion via trade is among the most vital mechanisms promoting AE and mitigating EP, aligned to achieve SDG-717.

Despite the growing body of literature on the determinants of EP in Africa, the role of CLTT has been omitted, although the volume of CLTT to Africa has been increasing at a substantial level. However, a limited number of empirical studies have been conducted on the effects of the China–Africa economic relationship on EP, focusing on the role of Chinese investment and aid in mitigating EP of ACs33–35. Therefore, this study closes the research gaps by conducting a comprehensive analysis of the effects of CLTT on AE in ACs.

Moreover, a key and fundamental question remains unanswered: Do the impacts of CLTT on AE vary on the basis of the domestic absorptive capacity of ACs? Thus, this study further investigates the asymmetrical and nonlinear characteristics of the link between CLTT and AE by examining the moderating and threshold effects via the levels of economic development (ED), financial development (FD), and control of corruption (CC) to further investigate the role of the domestic absorptive capacity of ACs in realizing the positive effects of CLTT on AE in ACs. Further investigation into controlling the domestic absorptive capacity of ACs is worthwhile because, compared with other economies, countries with a larger economy share the most significant proportion of CLTT (Fig. S1). In addition, AE is consistent mainly with economic levels; countries with better economic size have better AE than countries with smaller economic size do36,37, and countries with better FD promote firms to overcome barriers that typically hinder their participation in foreign trade, thereby boosting trade competitiveness by lowering capital costs and increasing productivity38,39. Additionally, corruption, a key factor undermining effective institutions, poses a significant barrier to economic and social progress, adversely affecting the link between trade and AE40.

Therefore, on the basis of the preceding discussion, this study aims to investigate the role of CLTT on AE in 42 ACs from 2005 to 2021, applying robust econometric strategies, such as Instrumental variable–generalized methods of moments (IV-GMM), instrumental variable–two-stage least squares (IV-2SLS) and method of moments quantile regression approaches to address potential endogeneity and distributional heterogeneity concerns. Threshold approaches and moderation analyses are conducted to control for the role of differences in domestic absorptive capacity among ACs, as they are heterogeneous in terms of their ED, governance performance (indicated by the CC), and financial markets that can shape the role of CLTT on AE in Africa.

This study offers several contributions to our understanding of the multifaceted relationship between CLTT and AE in Africa. First, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to examine the effect of CLTT on AE in Africa. Therefore, it represents the first attempt to assess the impact of CLTT on AE in Africa. Thus, this study provides comprehensive insights into the complex relationships between CLTT and EP in Africa, contributing to existing literature and offering policy frameworks that aim to promote the development of practical solutions to mitigate EP and advance SDG-7 in Africa.

Second, for the first time, this study assesses the moderating and threshold effects of the economic level and FD and CC on the influence of CLTT on AE. Consequently, the findings offer valuable insights for countries with varying levels of ED, CC, and FD capacities to design tailored CLTT development strategies that foster the role of low-carbon technology trade dynamics to promote AE in Africa.

Finally, this study employs the IV-GMM methodology alongside Lewbel41 IV-2SLS technique to effectively mitigate concerns related to reverse causality. Furthermore, it applies the MMQR to account for distributional heterogeneity and deviations from normality. Therefore, by rigorously addressing these methodological challenges, this study advances the existing literature and offers a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between CLTT and AE.

Literature review

Trade can promote the improvement of energy infrastructure and the deployment of energy technologies, and promote energy infrastructure development, influencing AE. Lewis42 was the first to introduce the concept of EP, emphasizing that insufficient energy access can negatively impact people’s well-being. Boardman43 quantified this idea by classifying a household as energy-poor when the household’s energy expenditures surpass 10% of its income. In the recent literature, particularly in the context of developing economies, EP primarily refers to the absence of AE44. AE includes the availability of modern fuels and technologies, which are more efficient and less polluting than traditional three-stone fires are45. EP can also be evaluated using indicators such as the proportion of energy consumption derived from renewable sources and the percentage of people with electricity access, as well as access to clean cooking and fuels. Of these, the share of renewable energy represents a shift toward cleaner energy, and electricity access directly reflects a country’s level of energy availability46.

From a theoretical standpoint, two major concepts, the energy ladder and energy stacking, are central to understanding EP in developing countries. The energy ladder model arranges energy sources along a hierarchy, with traditional fuels such as firewood, charcoal, and coal at the lower rungs, and modern options like electricity and natural gas at the higher rungs47. According to this model, nations tend to shift from low-tier to high-tier energy sources as their economies advance. However, in many developing contexts, households continue to rely on lower-tier fuels even when modern alternatives are available. They often combine several types of energy at once, creating what scholars describe as a multi-ladder overlap. This is where the energy stacking theory becomes relevant, explaining the simultaneous use of diverse energy sources48.

More specifically, the theoretical foundation for the assessment of the impact of trade on AE lies in technology diffusion theory30. This theory explains how trade and investment promote technological advancement by transferring knowledge and innovation among trading countries. When applied to AE, the theory provides a strong rationale for how trade flows can improve access to modern, sustainable energy. Trade serves as a channel through which advanced green energy technologies and expertise are transferred from advanced economies to developing economies, supporting their adoption and integration49 and thereby reducing EP. Moreover, trade-driven economic growth can lead to higher income levels, further enhancing AE.

With respect to empirical works on the link between low-carbon technology trade and AE, studies on the effects of CLTT on the AE of ACs are scarce, with most studies focusing on the effects of low-carbon technologies and environmental goods on environmental quality and the ecological footprint25,50–52, green innovation and entrepreneurship53,54. Other studies have focused on the role of aggregate trade on EP55,56. A few studies have been conducted on the impacts of low-carbon technology trade on environmental sustainability. For example, Gallagher et al.25 examined CLTT and its impact on climate change mitigation. The findings indicated that CLTT substantially lowers CO₂ emissions in partner countries. These reductions are notably more pronounced among high-income partner nations.

Additionally, Ha52 linked environmental goods to environmental quality and argued that trade in environmental goods improves environmental sustainability. Can et al.50 argued that trade in environmental goods substantially mitigates the ecological footprint. Similarly, Ahmad et al.51 found that green trade reduces CO2 emissions in BIRCS economies. Also, Cobbold54 investigated the role of importing low-carbon technologies in boosting domestic green innovation in a sample of countries and implied that low-carbon technology product imports increase domestic green innovation. Additionally, Susantiningrum and Rapih53 investigated the influence of low-carbon technology product trade on green entrepreneurship across several countries. The findings highlighted the importance of promoting trade in low-carbon technology products to foster domestic eco-friendly innovation.

Furthermore, with respect to foreign trade and AE, a study by Wang et al.55 found that trade positively contributes to EP in some ACs. A recent study by Zahonogo56 revealed that trade liberalization plays a mitigating role in EP. Using 2SLS-IV and system GMM, Chen et al.57 reported that imports from China are positively associated with renewable energy consumption in Africa. In addition, Wu49 argued that trade freedom positively contributes to the development of clean energy. Suleman et al.58 found that trade balance plays a positive role in promoting clean energy consumption in MINT economies. Additionally, Feng et al.59 reported that energy trade patterns significantly influence renewable energy development. Additionally, Berhe et al.60 found that AC imports from China positively contribute to renewable energy consumption.

With respect to low-carbon technology trade and energy dynamics, employing IV-GMM, Zhao et al.61 examined the role of low-carbon energy technologies, and the findings suggested that they are beneficial for improving energy carrying capacity. Applying IV-GMM and MMQR, Chen et al.26 assessed the role of low-carbon technology trade between China and BRI countries and reported that low-carbon technologies do not significantly reduce energy poverty. Olawuyi14 highlighted the crucial role of climate technology absorption as an integral part of a comprehensive climate technology diffusion strategy for Africa. The study findings underscored that addressing Africa’s limited domestic capacity to implement and sustain imported technologies, along with strengthening the regulatory framework to encourage clean technology adoption, is essential to closing existing climate technology gaps effectively. In light of these foundations, we propose Hypothesis 1.

Hypothesis 1 (H1): CLTT positively contributes to AE in ACs.

Furthermore, the effects of CLTT on the AE of ACs depend on the domestic absorptive capacity of the countries. Specifically, the effect of CLTT on AE in Africa can vary depending on the level of ED. For example, compared with economically small countries, economically better countries such as South Africa, Algeria, Nigeria, Egypt, and other middle-income economies share the most significant proportion of CLTT (see Fig. S1 Supplementary material). In addition, existing empirical works support the argument that AE is consistent primarily with economic levels, with higher income levels associated with lower energy deficits. It has also been argued that countries with higher incomes and advanced economies have lower EP than developing countries do36 and that the effects of trade on facilitating energy transition vary heterogeneously across countries and are subject to their income levels62.

Moreover, differences in the FD of ACs can affect the relationship between CLTT and AE in Africa, as better access to credit and financial services can assist firms in overcoming the barriers that frequently hinder their capacity to engage in trade. It can increase trade and enhance a country’s competitiveness by lowering capital costs and increasing productivity39. Furthermore, the aspects of FD, including depth, access, and efficiency, significantly shape trade relationships38, influencing their impact on AE. Moreover, countries with high FD tend to be net exporters of products characterized by high credit intensity63.

Moreover, improvement in governance quality enhances the role of low-carbon technology trade on AE. For example, Gallagher et al.25 provided evidence that improving the institutional framework and performance of importing countries for China’s low-carbon technology exports is one of the mechanisms through which CLTT affects emissions reductions, implying that differences in countries’ levels of CC affect the relationship between CLTT and AE. Additionally, it has been argued that corruption and a lack of regulatory enforcement can create uncertainties that deter investments in innovative and sustainable practices64, including low-carbon technology trade. The following hypotheses are formulated.

Hypothesis A (H2A): The positive effect of CLTT on AE becomes stronger in ACs with better ED.

Hypothesis B (H2B): ACs with better FD benefit more from increasing CLTT to promote AE.

Hypothesis C (H2C): The beneficial impact of CLTT on AE is more pronounced in ACs with better CC.

Theoretical underpinning of model construction

The role of CLTT in AE can be justified by a number of theoretical factors. For example, the relationship between CLTT and AE can be discussed based on technology diffusion and spillover theory, which asserts that increased trade openness fosters access to advanced energy technologies49, mitigating EP. The theoretical foundation for low-carbon technologies and AE revolves around integrating sustainable energy sources and ensuring equitable access to reliable energy. In addition, through technology transfer, trade flow can reduce EP by making modern and clean energy solutions more available and affordable. Moreover, bilateral trade promotes new technology and goods and increases a society’s income in trading partner countries, lowering energy costs and increasing access65. Therefore, AE in Eq. Data

This study uses CLTT data from 42 ACs from 2005 to 2021 (Table S1 in the Supplementary material). The selection of sample ACs and study period is exclusively based on the availability of balanced panel data.

Dependent variable

AE is the dependent variable. It is an aggregate indicator derived by using principal component analysis (PCA) using data for the share of the total population having access to CFTC, renewable energy consumption per total final energy use, and the proportion of the population with access to electricity46. PCA is a vital technique for reducing the dimensionality of data67. We used the first component because its eigenvalue is greater than one (2.661 > 1), accounting for 88.7% of the overall variation (Table S2 in the Supplementary Material).

To ensure the accuracy of the PCA results, it is useful to perform the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test. It is a widely used strategy to evaluate sampling competence and helps determine whether the dataset is suitable for the PCA dimensionality-reduction approach. It indicates certain variables add little to the underlying data structure, guiding whether they should be retained or removed before running the analysis68. Therefore, the accuracy of the indicator is indicated by the KMO value, which is greater than 0.5 (0.747 > 0.5) (Table S2 in the Supplementary material).

However, in the first component of the PCA, renewable energy has a negative sign. This pattern can be broadly attributed to the fact that access to CFTC and the proportion of the population with access to electricity will increase with the expansion of grid power, or other non-renewables, positively contributing to AE. This is a typical pattern in countries transitioning from traditional to modern energy systems.

Therefore, in contrast to some existing works that proxied EP by the share of the total population with access to CFTC, this study uses an aggregate indicator of AE. Thus, it collectively captures multiple dimensions of EP46. Among these components, the share of renewable energy represents the environmental sustainability of energy consumption, and electricity access reflects the level of energy availability within a country46. We borrowed access to electricity, CFTC (a percentage of the population) and renewable energy use data from the WDI database.

Target variable

The primary target variable is CLTT. It is represented by Chinese low-carbon technology products’ exports to ACs. These products are technology products that generate significantly less pollution than conventional energy alternatives do and are crucial for advancing the shift toward a low-carbon economy. These include devices such as wind turbines, biomass systems, solar panels, and carbon capture equipment11.

Control variables

Variables such as trade, GDP per capita, CC, remittance, energy intensity, FD, and natural resources rent are included in the analysis following existing empirical works31,55,66,69–71. Besides, the inclusion of these variables is founded on stacking and ladder theories66. GDP per capita is included to represent the size of economies. Likewise, aggregate trade openness, remittance, and natural resources rent are included to include income flow from abroad and revenue from selling resources. Method of analysis

IV regression approaches

There will be a possible reverse causality between AE and CLTT. An improvement in CLTT will influence AE, and conversely, an improvement in AE will affect trade. This may lead to simultaneity, which can produce biased ordinary least squares (OLS) coefficients and other traditional panel regression strategies26,72. Therefore, to control for reverse causality issues, two IV techniques are used to address endogeneity issues. First, we use the IV-GMM method. It is a robust approach to control for potential reverse causality73,74. Employing this method mitigates estimation challenges stemming from endogeneity, omitted variable bias and measurement errors, ensuring more robust and reliable results72,73. It also addresses concerns about heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation75.

Moreover, it is a static technique that yields long-run coefficient estimates, offering insights into the sustained effects of the explanatory variables72. It performs effectively in datasets where the cross-sectional units exceed time periods (N > T). Furthermore, even under uncertain heteroscedasticity, the IV-GMM analysis can address omitted-variable bias and yield consistent parameter estimates and robust empirical results26. Therefore, the Baum et al.73 IV-GMM technique is applied to estimate Eq. (2).

Second, for robustness analysis, the alternative Lewbel’s41 IV-2SLS strategy is employed to address concerns about reverse causality. It generates its internal instrument derived from heteroskedasticity to deal with endogeneity41.

Threshold analysis

The threshold analysis is based on the premise that the impact of CLTT on AE varies across ACs, with those possessing stronger absorptive capacity responding differently from those with weaker capacity when leveraging CLTT to enhance AE. Therefore, to assess the nonlinear relationship between CLTT and AE, this study conducts a threshold analysis that accounts for the levels of ED, FD, and CC as threshold variables, as the influence of CLTT on AE may differ on the basis of a country’s level of ED, FD and CC.

The advantage of this strategy is that it determines the threshold value from the sample data, segments it into appropriate intervals, and evaluates the nonlinear relationship between the dependent and independent variables76. In addition, introducing a threshold parameter or change point provides a simple and understandable approach to model certain kinds of nonlinear links between a predictor and an outcome variable77, though the outcomes strongly depend on the precision of the estimated thresholds and minor variations in the threshold can result in different results78. With respect to the control variables, the GDP per capita of the countries has a positive and significant effect on AE, providing evidence that an increase in GDP per capita in ACs positively contributes to AE. This implies that an improvement in GDP per capita adversely affects EP in Africa. These findings are consistent because an increase in ED increases people’s access to energy technologies for their kitchen and home usage and motivates individuals to shift toward more efficient and modern energy consumption practices65,82. Moreover, the total trade, remittance flow, natural resources, and CC of the sample ACs positively impact AE. Conversely, energy intensity has adverse effects on AE in the sample countries. The observed negative relationship between energy intensity and AE is aligned with the outcomes of Mohsin et al.83and Kar et al.84.

With respect to the variables of interest, CLTT positively impacts AE in trade partner ACs. A percentage increase in CLTT results in a 0.029% increase in AE in ACs. Therefore, the results indicate that an increase in CLTT is associated with a greater share of the population gaining access to CFTC and electricity, thereby contributing to the mitigation of EP in Africa. However, as the eigenvector of renewable energy in the AE is negative, the effects of CLTT will adversely affect the renewable energy share. This can be justified by the fact that CLTT will increase the proportion of non-renewable energy consumption initially and the positive role of CLTT in enhancing AE comes by promoting clean cooking fuels and technology and increasing access to electricity. Thus, promoting CLTT contributes to improving AE, alleviating EP in ACs, aligning with our expectations, and confirming Hypothesis 1. Therefore, the findings conclude that increasing the low-carbon technology trade relationship between China and Africa is crucial for mitigating EP by expanding access to electricity and clean and modern cooking solutions.

Discussion

The results of this study offer compelling evidence that CLTT has a robust positive effect on AE in ACs, implying that it can play a significant role in mitigating EP in Africa (Table 6). The positive role of CLTT on AE is quite intuitive, suggesting that trade helps to ensure better provision of public goods, including AE84. Therefore, ACs can access low-cost solar panels and other modern technologies through CLTT, improving AE and mitigating EP in Africa. In addition, CLTT can promote modern energy investment and facilitate the flow of goods and services, including advanced technologies from China to Africa, allowing ACs to access and adopt new energy technologies and promote AE.

From an empirical perspective, the findings validate that the low-carbon technologies trade has a significant effect on AE and an inhibitory effect on EP88. The diffusion of these technologies to Africa through CLTT is crucial for alleviating EP. Specifically, the findings of this study support the arguments of Zhao et al.61, who argued that low-carbon energy technology is beneficial for improving the energy resource carrying capacity. However, these findings contradict the outcomes of Chen et al.26, who found the insignificance of China’s low-carbon technology trade on EP in BRI countries.

From a practical standpoint, these results can be broadly interpreted to mean that CLTT includes the trade of goods and services related to technologies designed to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and promote sustainability and energy transition. These products span a wide range of sectors, including energy-efficient appliances, modern energy, electric vehicles, energy storage systems, smart grid technologies, and eco-friendly construction materials89,90. More specifically, CLTT promotes AE in Africa by promoting Chinese exports of modern and clean energy materials such as solar panels, wind turbines, batteries, and hydropower components to ACs at relatively low cost. The diffusion of clean energy technologies through CLTT can lower the production costs of clean energy, expand its market supply, and enhance the overall structure of energy consumption. The promotion of CLTT contributes to the advancement of clean energy technology and the improvement of clean energy infrastructure, thereby contributing to AE and alleviating EP. It also increases the deployment of clean energy technology products that can directly reduce EP and indirectly alleviate EP by enhancing energy efficiency19.

Additionally, the results are aligned with the practical aspects of China–Africa trade, as China’s low-carbon technology exports to Africa are significantly increasing (see Fig. 1). In addition, the results are logical, as Africa’s solar imports from China significantly increased by 187% year-on-year, primarily because of South Africa’s growth90. Specifically, in Nigeria, Chinese companies supply solar home system kits through trade-linked contracts with local distributors. The large-scale import of solar panels from China into East Africa is rising at a substantial rate. CLTT significantly contributes to cost reduction in the shift toward a sustainable energy sector. Obtaining energy material and components from nations with lower production costs enhances the economic feasibility of modern energy projects, making them more attractive to investors and businesses91,92.

From a theoretical standpoint, the evidence obtained in this study concurs with the principles of technology transfer and diffusion theory, implying that opening economies to trade fosters technological innovation, facilitates the diffusion of knowledge and technology, enhances economies of scale, improves managerial capabilities, and attracts foreign capital. Together, these factors strengthen AE resources and ultimately contribute to the improvement of EP in ACs. Additionally, trading low-carbon technology products often promotes the transfer of energy-efficient technologies and modern energy infrastructure that can significantly promote AE, alleviating EP. ACs engaged in low-carbon technology trade are more likely to access advanced energy technologies from relatively advanced economies, which can increase AE and reduce energy costs. Additionally, it promotes the exchange of goods and services, particularly advanced technologies, enabling countries to access and adopt innovative energy solutions, as trade plays a pivotal role in driving investment in the modern energy62,93.

Moreover, a moderation analysis revealed that economic size (GDP per capita), FD, and CC positively moderate the relationship between CLTT and AE. Hence, the results suggest that the positive impact of CLTT on AE increases with increasing per capita GDP and with increasing FD and CC of ACs. Furthermore, the threshold analysis provided evidence that the domestic absorptive capacity of the countries can influence the benefit of CLTT to enhance AE in ACs. Specifically, the outcomes in Table 12 show that when the level of economic size acts as a threshold variable, we exhibit a nonlinear relationship between CLTT and AE. While the influence of CLTT remains positive across all levels of economic size, the effect becomes markedly stronger once economic size exceeds a certain threshold. These findings are logical because relatively larger ACs with a larger economic size and level of development can better absorb CLTT, as they have a greater fiscal capacity to coinvest in AE projects. These results are congruent with those of Murshed62, who argued that trade has a heterogeneous effect on facilitating energy transition, which is subject to countries’ income level.

The threshold analysis further implied that a higher level of FD and better quality in CC coincide with greater benefits from CLTT to enhance AE in Africa (Table 12). The positive role of CLTT increases from the first regime to the second regime of the FD threshold, implying that ACs with better FD maximize the benefit from CLTT to promote AE. Therefore, ACs with better FD benefit more from CLTT in promoting access to CE. Countries with advanced FD appear to have a more pronounced effect of trade relationships on access to CE, which has a significant effect on trade38. Additionally, the findings are intuitive, as enhanced access to credit and financial services enables firms to overcome trade barriers, thereby increasing their trade participation. Additionally, it reduces capital costs and enhances productivity, thereby strengthening trade competitiveness39.

Moreover, the coefficient of CLTT on AE is positive before and after the threshold values of CC; nonetheless, the magnitude of the coefficients is greater after the threshold values of CC. Therefore, in countries where corruption is effectively controlled, regulatory systems function more transparently, reducing barriers to the import and distribution of clean technologies, which can positively affect AE. This enables efficient allocation of resources and builds trust in public institutions, which are essential for expanding AE. In contrast, high levels of corruption can distort markets, misallocate funds, and hinder the deployment of technology, thereby weakening the impact of low-carbon technology trade. Therefore, strong governance and anticorruption measures enhance the positive effects of CLTT on AE.

Conclusions, policy recommendations, and limitations of the study

Conclusions and policy recommendations

This study provides a comprehensive insight into the multifaceted links between CLTT and AE in Africa, along with the domestic absorptive capacity of ACs. More specifically, drawing on panel data 42 ACs spanning 2005–2021, it assesses the impacts of CLTT on AE. It then examines how economic size, FD, and CC affect the relationship between CLTT and AE. By applying IV-GMM, Lewbel’s IV-2SLS and MMQR approaches, as well as threshold analysis, the outcomes of the paper offer a detailed assessment of how CLTT influences AE in ACs, and the main results are summarized as follows:

First, the study’s outcomes demonstrate that CLTT has a strong positive effect on AE, thereby helping reduce EP in ACs. Second, moderation analyses show that economic size, FD, and CC positively moderate the relationship between CLTT and AE in ACs. Third, threshold analyses reveal that though the effects of CLTT on AE in ACs are positive before and after threshold points of economic size, FD, and CC, the magnitude of CLTT is higher after the inflection points, implying that the benefits of CLTT to improve access to AE intensify with the larger economic size, stronger FD, and improved CC in ACs.

Therefore, the findings of this study have substantial implications for policymakers, business leaders, and governments in the development of targeted policies to promote AE and mitigate EP in ACs. First, promoting AE should be integrated into every phase of policy design, decision-making, and management processes within ACs, aligned with the SDGs. Accordingly, the findings of this study support the strengthening of CLTT as a strategic means to advance SDG-7, which seeks to guarantee universal access to reliable, affordable, modern, and sustainable energy.

Second, CLTT should be promoted to increase AE in Africa. Thus, policymakers should take steps to promote CLTT with coordinated and meticulous policies in relation to the importation of goods. Additionally, focusing on cleaner and low-carbon technology trade and affordable CLTT with an emphasis on greener energy accessibility and material imports from China is worthwhile.

Third, enhancing trade policies, such as cutting nontariff barriers and lowering tariffs, is crucial for fostering CLTT, mainly for products associated with AE and modern energy technologies. These measures can facilitate CLTT integration, green technology transfer, and knowledge exchange, thereby accelerating the promotion of AE and sustainable energy production in ACs. Moreover, ACs should develop prudent trade policies to encourage the importation of CLTT, including tax reductions or waivers and special subsidies on the imports of clean and modern energy products from China.

Fourth, policymakers in ACs should focus on fostering robust financial systems that encourage investments in modern energy technologies to catalyze the positive role of CLTT on access to CE. Improved FD enhances access to credit and financial services that can support firms engaged in trade to overcome trade barriers and increase their trade with China. Additionally, foreign trade is more finance intensive and requires capital for transportation, insurance, and compliance with several trade regulations; governments in ACs should provide financial services to promote CLTT.

Finally, to enhance the benefits of CLTT, ACs should strengthen their domestic absorptive capacity by investing in institutional frameworks. In this context, promoting transparent governance and controlling corruption are essential for ensuring the effective technology transfer of CLTT and expanding AE across ACs. Therefore, ACs should improve the quality of institutions and legal frameworks to control CC to benefit more from CLTT to promote AE. In addition, ACs are heterogeneous in terms of economic size. Therefore, ACs trading with China should develop targeted policies aligned with specific economic conditions while leveraging their own unique advantages to promote CLTT to enhance AE and mitigate EP.

Limitations of the study

The outcomes of this study are limited to the role of CLTT on AE of ACs. While it offers a comprehensive insight into the effects of CLTT on AE, given AC’s domestic absorptive capacity, it has some limitations that future research should address. First, due to limited data availability, this study examines the link between CLTT and AE across ACs for the period 2005–2021. Therefore, future research should aim to improve data depth and extend the time span to enable more detailed long- and short-term analyses whenever long-term data become available.

Second, this study accounted for the effects of ACs’ domestic absorptive capacity in terms of economic size, CC, and FD. However, other socio-economic and political differences among ACs and policy changes should also be considered to accurately evaluate the heterogeneous role of CLTT in AE of ACs. Finally, future research can expand the study by conducting a comparative analysis of the effects of CLTT and Africa’s low-carbon technology trade with other major trade partners on AE of ACs.
