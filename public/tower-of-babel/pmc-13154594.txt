Non-enzymatic sweat sensors for pediatric continuous glucose monitoring: a systematic review of engineering readiness and the cost-accuracy trade-off
Authors: Oluwanifemi Temiloluwa Bolaji, Emmanuel Dominic Ephraim, Olumhense Benedict Adoghe, Deborah Daramola, Emmanuel Francis Kibuebu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) in children with type 1 diabetes is faced with major challenges due to prohibitive price, invasiveness and lack of compliance with insulin of the currently available interstitial devices, which is particularly severe in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). To investigate emerging solutions, we have undertaken a systematic review (2016–2025) of non-invasive, sweat-based glucose sensors and used a structured biomedical engineering model to determine the technological maturity and pediatric and global health usage of the sensors. The review of 25 peer-reviewed articles indicates a significant shift to non-enzymatic sensing schemes based on strong approaches and incorporating highly sensitive nanomaterials like MXene and scalable libraries of fabrications. An analysis of qualitative cost accuracy trade-off, formalized by new, specially developed LMIC Scalability Score, proves that whereas complex and expensive systems would reach the lowest mean absolute relative difference (MARD), simpler and highly scalable devices would reach the same performance with a MARD value under 10.5%, making them possible and low-cost variants. To this end, the essential interventions that can be done to facilitate clinical translation in the short term are the design of advanced physiological time-lag simulations and the implementation of strong, age-focused pediatric clinical trials to bridge the technology innovation and clinical practice gap.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus, especially Type1 Diabetes (T1D), is a persistent condition of metabolic disorder that requires close monitoring of blood glucose. It is especially challenging in pediatric patients, in which compliance challenges, needle aversion and the physiological changes that come with growth pose significant obstacles [1, 2]. Despite the fact that the use of conventional continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems has significantly improved glycemic control, it becomes a crucial hindrance, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), due to its reliance on interstitial fluid parameters and high cost [1]. The fact that sweat is a promising non-invasive biofluid to detect glucose remains secreted and can be collected without skin penetration is an important consideration in encouraging pediatric compliance [3, 4]. The glucose levels in sweat are easily accessible and easily measured in comparison with other biological fluids like saliva or tears and correlate with the blood glucose in the body provided that physiological lag times and changes in the rate of perspiration are taken into account [5, 6].

However, the development of a firm and straightforward connection between sweat glucose and blood glucose is a complicated task. Lack of sweat production and possible contamination are some of the factors that increase this complexity [3]. These complications are also compounded in children, as most of them produce less sweat, have thinner skin layers, and underdeveloped eccrine glands. Besides, the changing hydration status, metabolic rate and thermoregulation with age can change the composition of sweat and the processes of analyte transportation. Because of these shortcomings, there is an urgent requirement of glucose sensors that are sensitive and resilient. The inherent limitations of traditional enzymatic glucose sensors, despite their long-term use, are instability, short shelf life, and exposure to external environmental conditions that can cause the sensor to drift in its signal such as temperature and pH [7–10].

Non-enzymatic glucose sensors on the other hand have become a better option as they are more stable, economical and they have a longer operating life [9, 11]. These systems take advantage of the electrochemical process of oxidation of glucose by using advanced nanomaterials like MXenes, noble metal nanoparticles (e.g., Pt, Cu), and other metal oxides or sulfides to directly catalyze the oxidation reaction [11–17]. These sensors reduce fragility to the use of delicate enzymes, and facilitate fabrication, so production costs can be reduced, which is essential to scalability in resource-limited environments [7, 10]. Recent reports on literature possess some of the most sensitive non-enzymatic platforms including ZnFe(PBA)@Ti3C2Tx nanohybrids [12] and Pt/MXene-based flexible sensors [13] that illustrate possibilities of continuous and accurate monitoring.

The objective of this review is to present a critically minded, engineering-oriented overview of the recent development in sweat based CGM, with special consideration of evaluating the possibility of creating robust and cost effective non-enzymatic CGM applicable to children. In doing so, the paper aims to examine the opportunities and limitations of sweat-based glucose monitoring in children and pays particular attention to the contexts with constrained resources.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted according to the guidelines of Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses, PRISMA 2020 (Fig. 1), and selection was carefully outlined in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Even though this review protocol was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO, the systematic review has been conducted in full compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020. The choice of the studies is carefully outlined in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1) that accompanies it. The literature search was specifically focused at the studies that have been published within the last ten years, thus assuring inclusion of recent advance in the field of wearable glucose sensing; articles in online ahead of publication were included when they were indexed in the selected databases and sorted out by the year in which they are presented online. Previous publications outside the main search window had been intelligently cited as and when required to provide a background information in relation to glucose sensing in the mechanisms and material. Data mining was done by one researcher and stored in a way that could be used to enhance a detailed review of developments in biomedical engineering. The parameters extracted were divided into three main areas, namely the engineering components, key performance indicators, and clinical or deployment factors. Components Engineering included the sensing modality (enzymatic or non-enzymatic), electrode design, fabrication mechanism, microfluidic design, power and communication interface. The important key performance indicators are the Mean Absolute Relative Difference (MARD), sensitivity, limit of detection (LOD), linear detection range, response time and on body stability. Clinical and deployment considerations were concerned with age group of participants, limit on sweat-rate, calibration scales, skin adhesiveness, correlation with blood glucose and expense or scalability issues.

Each of the technical studies was given a Low-Middle-Income-Country (LMIC) Scalability Score based on its potential to support low-resource settings: 1 (Low) to 3 (High). This aggregate measure has been calculated by weighted evaluation of three numbers: cost of material, complexities of fabrication, and compatibility with the infrastructure of the LMIC. Material appraisal was based on the utilization of cheap, local materials (e.g. paper, carbon ink) as opposed to an expensive noble metal or specialized polymer. Considering the necessity of minimizing the complexity of fabrications, more than scalable, low-infrastructure processes such as screen and inkjet printing were preferred over more resource-intensive processes such as MEMS, 3D printing, or clean-room fabrication. Accordance with LMIC organization taken into consideration the buying power in resource-limited settings. The outcome score is the basis of the Cost-Accuracy Trade-off analysis provided in the Results section (Fig. 4). LMIC Scalability Score as used in this study was designed as a relative engineering model that evaluates the translational feasibility and not a relative quantitative measure of the deployment cost. It was a composite measure obtained by weighted combination of three contributory measures:

Scalability score rubric

To critically appraise the quality of the methodology and potential bias that the included studies had, a two-pronged assessment measure was employed. To conduct clinical inquiries, the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS 2) tool was utilized on human subject investigations and clinical validation projects to approximate the risk of bias and generalizability to a pediatric sample. In the case of technical development studies, a tailored Engineering Quality Assessment was used in ensuring the strength of reporting. The evaluation of this was based on three critical criteria, which were selectivity, stability, and scalability. The selectivity was challenged at least with three typical interferents, such as, ascorbic acid, uric acid, and lactate. The criterion of stability was the capability of the sensor to maintain performance at least during seven days. Scalability created a need to discuss or demonstrate the scale-up capability substantively. Articles providing all three requirements were considered of high quality in engineering and those that did not provide stability data or selectivity were considered low. The numbers illustrate that the most desirable MARD - which reflects best accuracy - is the study by Ga@MXene [10], in which a complicated Liquid -Metal/3D -Printing fabrication method results in a MARD of 6.5% and Low Scalability Score (1). Using scalable fabrication devices, the MXene Paper Chip [11] and Screen-Printing [25] devices recorded a High Scalability Score (3) of MARD values of 9.2- and 10.1%, respectively. This contrast proves the current engineering dilemma that to achieve ultra-high performance, it is expected to trade-off cost and scalable manufacturing, but the scalable process is increasingly close to the clinical acceptability standard of MARD less than 10%.

Discussion

The current systematic review credibly argues that non-invasive, sweat-based Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) has improved significantly, transforming its intangible, theoretical, concept into a concrete and practical, almost imminent translation opportunity. We have pointed out two important developments as the analysis of the engineering world, which are a clear shift in sensing architecture towards non-enzymatic, and the development of a trade-off between sensor accuracy and manufacturing scalability that is measurable.

Scalability and performance sensor engineering

The information retrieved in Table 2 marks a drastic change of direction in the field. Initial studies [14] considered studied systems of enzymes. More recent high-impact studies [7, 10] are also beginning to study non-enzymatic mechanisms, frequently with at least one advanced nanomaterial, such as MXene and Layered Double Hydroxides (LDH) [10]. This is a very topical trend in light of the low-cost goal, because non-enzymatic sensors do not require the use of costly, temperature-sensitive enzymes, which represents one of the greatest expenses and stability challenges toward LMIC implementation.

Cost-accuracy trade-off: a translational requirement

The key point that is raised by our quantitative evaluation (Fig. 4) of Mean Absolute Relative Difference (MARD) versus LMIC scalability is that the best accuracy (i.e., lowest MARD) is currently achieved with the sophisticated and expensive fabrication methods (such as Liquid Metal/3D Printing) [10]. However, the key clinical translation discovery relates to the fact that competitive MARD values below the 10.5% mark are attained with highly scalable approaches, including Screen Printing [18, 25] and Photothermal Paper Chips [10]. The implication of this observation is that the field is nearing a performance plateau, where small gains in accuracy provided by complicated manufacturing are offset by significant cost cuts as well as throughput benefits of scalable processes. This trade-off is synonymous in favor of the implementation of scalable, non-enzymatic platforms to the purposes of global health applications, in which the price of the device has proven to be the most significant obstacle to accessibility.

Despite the fact that the LMIC Scalability Score provides an organized framework of evaluating the translational viability of the emergent sweat-based glucose sensors, it needs to be used with the proper methodological care. The scoring system includes variables that include the cost of the materials, complexities of fabrication, as well as infrastructure requirement, which are in their nature, heterogeneous in various technological platforms and manufacturing environments. Although explicit scoring criteria were used to improve consistency, the measure is bound to be restrictive of the ordinal scale of infusing complex economic and engineering considerations. The cost-accuracy relationship presented in Fig. 4 is, therefore, not to be considered as exact quantitative relationship but, above all, as the comparative one. That said, the framework can be useful to establish general engineering principles, most notably the finding that the highly scalable fabrication strategies, including screen printing and paper-based ones, are ever more capable of reaching the clinically relevant level of accuracy, at the same time keeping the complexity of production lower, thus justifying their possible applicability to the low- and middle-income healthcare settings.

Time lag challenge and performance metrics

Physiological time lag modelling

Although MARD values in the first generation sweat sensors (between 6.5 and 11.5%) are close to the clinical acceptability levels of commercial interstitial CGM [1], the largest engineering challenge on the way of clinical feasibility is the physiological time lag. The delay in the blood versus sweat glucose concentration (between 5 and 30 min) is determined by transdermal transport kinetics and local sweat rate [4].

In order to have real-time monitoring, this lag must be mathematically corrected strictly. Implications for pediatrics and LMIC clinical translation

Pediatric readiness and clinical validation

The major aim of this research is to come up with a monitoring device that is user-friendly to the child. Even though the available clinical literature emphasizes on the benefits of continuous glucose techniques in the management of type 1 diabetes in children [2], it concerns mostly interstitial sampling methods. The switching to sweat-based continuous glucose monitoring requires, in addition to the mean absolute relative difference (MARD) being equal with the current interstitial modalities, the indication that it is indeed better in dermal compatibility and very comfortable to the patient.

Most importantly, the pediatric integumentary system marked by a lack of sweat, diminished dermal thickness and continued maturation of the eccrine gland activity (Table 1) makes the extrapolation of sensor metrics tested on adults invalid. The lack of data on pediatric data in the current studies is a significant obstacle. Future clinical validation efforts must therefore use only pediatric subjects, both to ensure that strong correlations are achieved between sweat-borne and corporeal glucose concentrations, and because sensor architecture including microfluidic retrieval mechanisms, adhesive adherence and associated design considerations need to be specific to this biological group.

Roadmap for future research

The translational research roadmap, according to this systematic review, needs to focus on the following:

Dynamic Time-Lag Compensation: A combination of the real-time measurement of the sweat-rate with highly sophisticated mathematical modelling, used to dynamically tune the time constant − 1.Pediatric-Centered validation: Application of age-based clinical trials by using pediatric cohorts and confirm the performance of MARD as well as determine the usability and comfort over a long period.Standardizing Scalability Metrics: Developing a quantitative measure of LMIC applicability incorporating aspects of complexity of production, infrastructure requirement, and stability of the supply-chain alongside cost of materials.Ethical, safety and acceptability need to be taken into the consideration particularly in the highly cultural regions.

Long term safety of nano-material based biosensors

Despite the growing number of advanced nanomaterials, which include MXenes, metal oxides, and layered double hydroxides, making non-enzymatic sweat glucose biosensors increasingly sensitive and stable, biocompatibility and dermatological safety issues should be put into a critical perspective, especially in pediatric users. Children have a thinner epidermal barrier and developing integumentary systems, and this can make them more susceptible to irritation or exposure to nanoparticles over the long-term during sensor wear. Though the majority of the papers that were included in the review primarily discussed electrochemical activity and the integration of the device, very few of them have conducted extensive analysis of the dermal toxicity, the long-term compatibility with the skin, or inflammatory reactions of nanomaterial-based sensing layer. The next-generation research should thus involve the use of standardized tests on biocompatibility, such as cytotoxicity, irritation and long-lasting wear tests to make sure that these materials will not cause any harm to pediatric skin when in constant contact.

Conclusion

The systematic review is based on a strict approach to the biomedical engineering paradigm and confirms the active translational development of sweat-based Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) and outlines a clear pathway towards its application in pediatric and global health conditions. The analysis of the recent decade of the scholarship demonstrates a shift to the necessity of strong and non-enzymatic sensing strategies, thus avoiding the stability and cost limitations of the traditional enzymatic platforms.

The most striking finding is the measurable Cost-Accuracy Trade-off (Fig. 4). Though complex fabrication protocols give the lowest mean relative difference (MARD) values, very scalable, low-cost methods, including screen printing, have become competitive (MARD < 10.5%). This observation highlights that the engineering problem has now developed beyond that it is purely focused on performance to include translational efficiency, thus making scalable, non-enzymatic sensors a practical and cost-effective substitute in resource-limited environments.

In order to fill in the final gap between laboratory innovation and clinical efficacy, any future research should focus on two key areas: first, the creation of dynamic mathematical models of real time physiological time-lag compensation and second, the completion of rigorous age stratified pediatric clinical validation studies. Through these engineering and clinical imperatives, the discipline can be successful in converting these technological breakthroughs into convenient, painless and life changing CGM solutions to children with diabetes across the world.
