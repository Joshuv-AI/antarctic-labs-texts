A massive ocular surface squamous cell carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Horia A Alansi, Butheinah A Al-Sharafi, Sameha A Aleriani, Amin A Ogbah, Ali A Alashwal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSquamous cell carcinoma of the conjunctiva is a rare local malignancy, and there are few reports in literature of massive growth within 7 weeks.Case presentationA 60-year old Yemeni male, complained from heaviness discomfort with 2 weeks fresh bleeding from left orbital big mass. The tumor spell out the entire orbital outlet as pinkish fleshy fine lobes in tumor sides as most of the upper and lower surface was covered by intact skin. There was a history of eye-surface growth excision followed by lids closure 18 months back. The magnetic resonance imaging showed the tumor dimensions (10.8 × 9.8 × 9.4 cm) with the eye in its posterior part. The patient underwent urgent de-bulking by exentration (hemoglobin was 7.0 gm%) with adjuvant radiation after a histopathology diagnosis of well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. The low socioeconomic status and other unknown factors might enhance the tumor (rapid) growth. The patient was healthy for 17 months post management, thereafter got left leg limping. By 24 months, the magnetic resonance imaging with angiography of the whole body showed: irregular lesion in upper temporal part of the left orbit and 9 × 4 × 2 cm mass within the left calf muscle and 2.5 × 2.2 cm and 2 × 1.7 cm in diameters in left and right lungs.ConclusionThis is a rapidly growing with massive size ocular surface squamous cell carcinoma exhibited was also recurrent exhibited systemic manifestations within 4 years of onset, although initially it was isolated and low staging.Keywords: Elderly, Massive squamous cell carcinoma, Rapid growing, Recurrent

Introduction

Squamous cell carcinomas (SCC) occur worldwide and are the second most common malignancy (next to basal cell carcinoma) of the peri-orbital area among elderly adults, mostly male patients are reported [1, 2]. It is a tumor of low malignancy and tends to occur in situ, but may invade nerve endings supplying the orbit and extra-ocular muscles (perineural invasions), SCC can be fatal if it reaches the brain and systemic metastasis occur rarely if it reaches the lymph nodes or nasal sinuses [1–27].

Usually SCC affects fair-skinned individuals and occurs mainly in the peri-equatorial geographical area [3]. The exact mechanism of the pathogenesis is unknown. However, environmental and intrinsic stimuli initiate a process in which cell growth and regulation are lost [3]. Environmental factors induce genetic mutations as cumulative ultraviolet radiation (sun exposure), ionizing radiation, papilloma virus infection, petroleum products, arsenic ingestion, heavy cigarette smokers, psoralen plus ultraviolet A (PUVA) therapy for psoriasis [3]. Intrinsic factors include the autosomal recessive conditions, seroderma pigmentosum, and oculo-cutaneous albinism [3]. In Black patients, chronic skin dermatoses, ulcerations, and scarring can occur [3]. The diagnosis is often from clinical suspicious, but for many other malignant and benign processes can be confused with SCC, the diagnosis needs histopathological confirmation [1–6].

Other risk factors that related to immune system imbalance and fitting genetic predisposition, play through enhance DNA mutations in the epithelial cells might explain cancers that develop in parts of the skin or epithelium not ordinarily exposed to sunlight [1, 2, 4, 5]. There are controversies regarding the contribution of viruses such as as human papilloma virus type 16 and 18, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), or chemical exposure to oil derived products, and tobacco [1–5, 9, 25].

Patients with immunodeficiency virus said to have a more aggressive disease progression and tend to be younger [25]. A study explained dysregulation of several genes associated with epithelia tumerigenesis in the ocular surface through alterations, including up-regulation of MMP-9, MMP-11, and S100A2, as well as down-regulation of clusterin. Future investigations are needed to delineate the molecular mechanisms underlying the development and progression of ocular surface squamous neoplasia [7].

Periocular squamous cell carcinomas are described clinically as small stationary or extended lesions that could be fatal if they spread to the adjacent vascular nasal sinuses, with very rare remote metastases, or spread perineurally to the brain [26].

Massive SCC, that grows rapidly, has rarely been reported in literature, their origin was from epithelium of the eye conjunctiva or lids skin, and they grew circumferentially enclosing the eyeball [1, 2, 4, 5, 9, 22]. Massive SCC are reported mostly among males and require aggressive treatment, as prognoses are not yet known [2].

The incidence rate of all periorbital SCC of a size less than 2.5 cm has been reported to occur in 0.09 and 2.42 cases per 100,000 population and the overall rate of regional lymph node metastases range from 5 to 10% to as high as 20% to 25% [3, 4].

In a recent study published in 2012 an incidence of 37.3 per 10 [6] was reported for all eye cancers and 8.4 per 10 [6] for SCC [26].

SCC is responsible for considerable morbidity and mortality; however, if it is detected early and treated adequately, the prognosis is generally excellent, but recurrence reported [4, 6]. The low socioeconomic status is a factor for all big sized tumors leading to patients delaying seeking medical care [2, 5].

Ocular surface squamous cell neoplasia (OSSN) is a broad term that ranges from conjunctival intraepithelial neoplasia with various grades of dysplasia to invasive squamous cell carcinoma of conjunctiva and cornea. Terminology, such as epithelial plaque, intra-epithelial epithelioma, dyskeratosis, dysplasia, precancerous epithelioma, Bowen's disease and Bowenoid epithelioma. [26] Clinically, OSSN has myriad presentation, described as sessile, fleshy, elevated masses, plaque with no mark whether this OSSN is invasive or not [26]. SCC as periorbital nodule growth usually appears as a painless elevated tissues with or without some ulcerations in the center, at the exposed conjunctiva, medial to the limbus has a worse prognosis than lateral because of its proximity to nasal sinuses. It appears as atypical elevated conjunctiva tissue or as presumed pterygium, so all pterygia should always be sent for histopathology after radical excision [1, 6, 9].

Owing to the potential lethality of SCC, a wider excision than that usually performed for basal cell should be performed with closer postoperative follow up [9].

SCC may arise de novo, or from existing pre-malignant lesions like, actinic keratosis, Bowen’s disease, keratoacanthoma and so on. SCC of the eyelid needs differentiation from benign lesions, such as seborrhoeic keratosis, nevi, and squamous papilloma, and from other malignancies including basal cell carcinoma and sebaceous gland carcinoma. Mixed lesions may also occur. Definitive diagnosis usually done by histopathological examination of the biopsy sample with the use of immune-histochemical markers [5, 16]. Therapy varied from topical chemotherapy to orbital exentration. [1, 2, 4, 25].

To preserve vision and the eye, radical excision of the SCC is performed using electrical cautery, with or without chemotherapy when it is small [1, 2, 4]. A trial of chemotherapy by intra-lesion injection of interferon alpha 2b applied for 3 months or 5 fluorouracil or as topical eye drops [6]. Mitomycin applied for weeks to a year might cure the tumor with follow up. Nonaggressive conjunctiva SCC appear as para limbal heterogeneous thickening of the conjunctiva, atypical whitish irregular pterygium or Bowen’s diseases (carcinoma grade 0 or in-situ) [9–12].

Cemiplimab, prescribed first for skin SCC, is a new immuno-modulator therapy of anti-PD-1 belong to a class of agents known as checkpoint inhibitors [8]. It works by strengthening the body’s immune response to tumors, showing persistent stable disease without any immune related adverse events and might lead to an increased understanding of the aggressive nature of periocular SCC [8].

For big tumors, it is currently advised to perform aggressive intervention through exentration with adjuvant radiotherapy (57.6 Gy over 32 fraction) with or without chemotherapy (7 weekly infusions of Cisplatin 40 mg/m2), cryotherpy and Moh surgery are also described [6, 8].

Case report

He had spent there seven years working with cement in building construction, He presented himself to the Saudi German Hospital in Sana’a, Yemen, in the last week of November 2020. He complained of continuous bleeding from a large left orbital mass that had persisted for two weeks following a biopsy operation in Jazan. The bleeding was controlled with firm pressure, as shown in Fig. 1. He complained of heaviness (of a left sided orbital mass that grew rapidly within 7 weeks). It begun as small mass near lateral canthus and as he said a surgeon remove it with the left eye in April 2019 but abruptly grew to a big size and protruded in the last 7 weeks. He came alone requesting urgent and easily removal of the mass before going to his village, meanwhile he missed all papers related to his condition in the land way from Jazan to Sana’a. The hospital insisted to bring a relative person to support all his needs.

A photo of the patient at time of presentation, showed a big squamous cell carcinoma in his left orbit that had got increasing in size rapidly among 7 weeks as he said. The tumor, as shown, was tightened by a gauze and plaster in trial of pressing and stop bleeding from its lower far end

The patient had brown skin. He looked pale and tired. The tumor was pinkish, hard, filled the left orbit outlet and protruded anteriorly (Fig. 1).

He presented approximately 18 days after undergoing biopsy sampling outside the country. On local examination, the skin over most of the mass appeared normal and stretched, except for a 6 × 3 cm area at the center of the medial and lateral surfaces, where protruding edges of pinkish, fleshy, tightly packed lobes of the tumor were visible. This finding supported and confirmed his history of previous surgical intervention with removal of the eye (as he thought) by a surgical doctor, he stated, “He was non ophthalmic doctor because the mass grow again;” meaning it was recurrent. A confirmation of the nonideal primary carcinoma intervention was the closure of the left eye (seen by the MRI) by upper and lower eyelid suturing (Figs. 1, 2). There was a continuous jet stream of fresh bleeding of 2.5 mm in diameter came from the lower outermost tumor surface. There was no pus or gross color changes covering the stretched skin. There were no palpable submandibular or pre-auricular lymph nodes. The other eye and lid movements were intact and no headache denoting absence of clinical perineural spread was present.

Magnetic resonance imaging with contrast for the head from left side: the tumor was mild contrast enhanced, encouraged on the left eye posteriorly, query involve optic nerve and lacrimal gland but protrude more freely toward anterior surface in diameters exceeded the orbital outlet. See text

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with contrast showed a left orbital mass of 9.5 × 10.8 × 8.8 cm with mild contrast enhancement and areas of breaking down. The lesion involved the left retro-orbital region and extended anteriorly, encasing the optic nerve, lacrimal gland, and left eye. Serial images of the cranium and nasal sinuses showed no bony erosions or lymphadenopathy in the periauricular or submandibular regions (Fig. 2). MRI of the chest and abdomen was also unremarkable.

The preoperative investigations showed that hemoglobin was 7 gm %. The viral profile, hepatitis B, C, and HIV-antibodies were negative. He had 1000 cc fresh blood given preoperatively and 1000 cc postoperatively. Under universal precautions, the patient underwent surgical debulking via orbital exenteration under general anesthesia. The procedure began with encasing the tumor in an 8.0 glove up to its base to contain tumor cells and minimize bleeding during manipulation. Blood filled the glove fingers, helping to keep the surgical field dry. An incision was then made using electrocautery, approximately 4 mm from the tumor base, as a safety margin. Dissection proceeded with posterior undermining to release the firmly adherent tissue at the tumor base, which separated more easily posteriorly. The tumor was completely excised and sent for biopsy.

We used separate sterile instruments to excise the remaining orbital tissues at the orbital apex and walls, which were sent in a separate container for histopathology. There were no frank bony erosions were observed; however, a bony flap was opened while dissecting adherent tissue at the edge of the thin lamina cribrosa in the medial orbital bone and was repositioned without extraction. An impregnated gauze with 0.5% mitomycin was applied at the orbital outlet, where the tumor had been firmly adherent, while a rotational flap was used to cover the anterior 2 cm of bare bone at the frontal, temporal, and zygomatic sites. The hollow orbit was left to heal by granulation, with recurrence follow-up for two years before skin grafting [4, 5, 15, 16].

The histopathology report showed well-differentiated SCC that enclosed the eye posteriorly. Fortunately, the optic nerve stump, as well as the small pieces taken from the orbital apex and from the orbital walls, were free from neoplastic tumor cells. He was sent to oncology department where he underwent 30 fraction sets of adjunctive radiation therapy 2 weeks after surgery [8].

During radiation therapy, the patient experienced three episodes of infection with greenish purulent discharge, which culture confirmed as Pseudomonas aeruginosa sensitive to gentamicin and ceftriaxone. Dressings were continued every third day. Then, 3 months after radiation, the empty orbit appeared dry and stiff with multiple deep fistulas. MRI imaging showed no evidence of systemic disease, orbital involvement, or nasal sinus pathology. The patient was advised to undergo follow-up every three months, including systemic and local MRI with contrast at two years post-treatment, to exclude metastasis, new tumor growth, or recurrence. If these results remain normal, skin grafting will be planned to cover the bare orbit for protection and cosmetic management [2, 4, 7, 10, 11]. The patient was seen 17 months after operation, he was active and healthy; the orbital walls became wet and more pink, and the central fistula closed.

At 20 months-post exentration and radiation, and in preparation for a skin graft to his bared left orbit (Fig. 3), the tumor bed was not dry, the central fistula was closed by granulation tissue with flat white tissue. The whitish area posterior to the inferior rim developed into a scaly, rough patch that progressed centrally. MRI with angiography revealed an irregular flat lesion in the upper temporal part of the left orbit, suggestive of a recurrent mass over granulation tissue, as it appeared isolated and not diffuse to other orbital regions. Additionally, a 9 × 4 × 2 cm mass was identified within the left calf muscle, which had caused limping over the previous four months, and another lesion measuring 2.5 cm in diameter was detected in the upper part of the middle lobe of the right lung [4, 5, 9–28]. Patient was referred to the oncologist for further investigations. These multiple metastases involving also both lungs denoted late stage and the oncologist suggested the need for chemotherapy. Unfortunately, patient did not follow oncology instructions.

A photo, 20 months post treatment, showed that the tumor bed was not dry, central fistula closed by granulation tissue with flat white tissue. The whitish area posterior to the inferior rim had a scaly rough-skin like color and progressed centrally

Patient died in October 2024 after 2 years of limping and weeks of being bed ridden as he developed a bulky mass opposite the vertebral column in the upper (back) cervical region.

Applying the AJCC guidelines for SCC grading, our patient—along with similar cases of rapidly enlarging orbital SCC—showed no tumor extension beyond the orbit for more than 1.5 years. Discussion

The epithelial cellular components of the conjunctiva are diverse: stem cells at the limbus, goblet cells, minor lacrimal secreting cells forming the plica semilunaris and caruncle at the medial canthus, and the non-keratinized conjunctival epithelium. These components give rise to a variety of tumor types and subtypes, most of which are carcinomas [7]. Massive SCC reported from the eyelids and even places in the orbit, where there is no epithelium, described as isolated posterior orbit away from the conjunctiva, are also reported [4, 5, 7]. There were a series of 26 cases described in 1999, with history of rapid growth to 10 times the original size in weeks to 2 years and histologically diagnosed as SCC [2].

Although, SCC is classically seen in 6th to 7th decade, isolated big sized orbital SCC have been described in younger Asian patients [4]. Another orbital SCC was in a younger male aged 45-years-old with reported eyelid carcinoma engulfing the whole eye globe, shaped like a multi-nodular flower, with a size of 6 × 5 cm that grew over 2 years [4]. The onset was by peanut painless nodule at the lower lid, and was treated by orbital exenteration and postoperative radiotherapy [3, 4]; at 20 months follow-up, the patient was free from the disease [4].

A similar tumor has been described in a younger patient, aged 30 years, presenting with infiltrative lobules of soft tissue measuring approximately 85 × 79 × 75 mm. The mass occupied nearly the entire left orbit, engulfing the globe and extraocular muscles, and extended into the lateral extraorbital soft tissues. No regional lymphadenopathy or evidence of distant metastasis was observed [5]. There were multiple sites of bony erosions during exenteration on the orbital roof and medial wall and suggested incomplete tumor clearance surprisingly, the surgical site was healthy and the patient was systemically well 1-month post-surgery [5, 12]. It was reported that incomplete tumor clearance occurred in 38% of total exentration with complications, up to 23.5% [28]. Recurrence after radical exentration was reported within 5 years and then with impossible further re-irradiation and also within the first 2 years post management [28, 29].

Ocular surface squamous neoplasia is generally described as a low-grade, slow-growing malignancy, with only a few reports of death resulting from direct perineural extension with intracranial spread [11].

While the overall 5-year survival of such SCC patients believed to be 55–65%, it believed to be significantly worse in patients with perineural spread [1–5, 24]. Among patients with residual tumor, Bartley GB et al. report “1-year survival of 55% and a 5-year survival of just 25.8%” [12].

Recurrence also was reported in a Hispanic patient of 75 years old, with massive SCC invading the eye and optic nerve, orbit, and lower lid. The patient ultimately died from a massive intracranial tumor in three periods of 8–9 month intervals [9, 10]. Patients with SCC are at a high risk of recurrence or a threefold greater risk of new tumors and hence, they should be on lifelong follow up [2, 4–7, 15, 17–24]. Classically, large SCC is managed by orbital exentration and radiation, and there is no chance of globe preservation [5, 12]. Nevertheless, in small SCC with early presentation, it is preferred to perform a trial of neoadjuvant therapies to decrease the tumor size and try to preserve the eye [6–15]. Studies showed that within 24 months and up to 5 years, the advance of squamous cell carcinoma was delayed after aggressive treatment [29].

Generally, factors of worst prognosis include large lesions, incomplete excision, histo-pathological features, such as poor differentiation, multicentric origin, pagetoid spread, delayed diagnosis, penetration of the orbital septum and/or periosteum, gene mutations, and primitive cell types of origin [1, 2, 8–24].

Our patient remained healthy for 17 months following management. The triggers for the rapid growth and large size of the tumor require further investigation. No regional spread was observed, with neither lymph node involvement, perineural spread, nor bony erosions. Possible explanations include the rapid growth leaving insufficient time for invasion, aggressive treatment, an intact periosteal layer with no bone erosion at surgery, absence of systemic illnesses, and no immune compromise (by history and negative HIV antibody titer). The tumor originated at the temporal conjunctiva and expanded circumferentially outward toward extraorbital structures. MRI imaging showed no extension to the nasal sinuses or distant metastases. The well-differentiated type of SCC, together with the absence of neurological symptoms in the contralateral eye, further supported a favorable outcome [27]. The histopathology report showed that the orbital tissue behind the eye and the optic nerve stump were free from neoplastic cells.

After the 2nd year, our patient either had hematologic spread to the lungs and left calf muscle or had liability to new growths of other malignancies as sarcoma because no biopsy report documented for his new growths. He did not do regular follow up in the oncology department and seen limping from his left calf swelling and had little bleeding from temporal side of his right orbit. Regarding the parameters of SCC spread by increasing perineural invasion as well as individually induce high risk profile of SCC as summarized by Tong et al. [30] as follow: There are 40 gene expression profile-test to predict metastesic risk in localized high risk cutaneous SCC, [31] rather clinically, the parameters of SCC aggressiveness are applied to our patient tumor as follows: The Austeralian Cancer Council 2019 [32] and the American National Comperhensive Cancer Network - version 1 2022 guidelines [33] consider periocular SCC as being carry the highest aggressive SCC by location. Other parameters also applied in our patient as the size (> 2cm), thickness (> 0.6mm) [33] and getting ultimately high AJCC category [27]. The perineural invasions (histopathology signs), that is promoted by multiple surgical excisions, is also blamed to be a parameter in SCC aggression by inhancing further recurrence and growing in big size [34]. Other parameters in acquiring aggressive SCC are the longer duration, post radiation, and a histopathology of moderate to poor differention as well as atypical spindle adenosquamous cell types. Other postulated parameters of chronic immuno-suppression and lymph vascular invasion [35]. In addition to those parameters our patient got a rare OSSC that behave rapidly growing in a huge manner.

Conclusion

This massive and rapidly growing tumor, was presented as an isolated ocular surface SCC is extremely rare and has been reported mainly in middle-aged and elderly patients, with unknown triggering factors. This patient had OSSC with a behavior may represent a kind of a rare variant of SCC that initially presents with low staging, at least within the first two years post-management, Initially showed neither neural nor lymphatic spread. There should be factors for the aggressive growth among which the delayed intervention is very importent Other possible extrinsic influences and/or intrinsic susceptibility leading to the development of this aggressive sort of SCC variant, which can result in eye loss and life-threatening complications. The presence of any risk of malignancy, underscores the importance of early intervention and lifelong follow-up to monitor for potential recurrence, metastasis, or new malignancy predispositions.

Lesson

The study followed a recurrent OSSC. It was aggressive and fatal. The patient was unwell in the last 5 years of his life. He suffered from two recurrences of the carcinoma and ultimately neo-growths targeting vital organs. Inadequate early management and disrupted and inadequate follow-up. Rather, other unclear risks related to genetic mutations, immune imbalance, or possible specific lifestyle andor environmental influences should also be considered.
