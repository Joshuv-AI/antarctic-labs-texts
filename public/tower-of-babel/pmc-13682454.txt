Mechanism-guided endograft explantation eliminates shear-mediated aortic injury in a controlled simulation study
Authors: Zain Moin, Alayne Morrel, Maham Rahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEndograft explantation is associated with high morbidity and mortality, driven in part by uncontrolled shear-mediated aortic injury. In this study, we evaluated whether a mechanism-based explantation device could reduce this injury and improve the procedural performance.MethodsIn a controlled simulation using a multilayered three-dimensional printed aortic model, 25 vascular trainees and surgeons performed endograft explantation using both a conventional syringe technique and a novel device (EndoEx). The outcomes included quantitative intimal injury, procedure time, and validated usability metrics.ResultsThe EndoEx device reduced intimal injury by approximately 90% compared with the syringe technique (0.94% vs 9.56%; P < .001), representing near-elimination of shear-mediated disruption. The procedure time was moderately longer (+32 seconds; P < .05). EndoEx demonstrated superior usability across all domains, including ease of use, safety perception, and overall cognitive load (P < .05).ConclusionsIn this controlled simulation, a mechanism-based explantation strategy (EndoEx) markedly reduced shear-mediated aortic injury while improving operator control. These findings support further in vivo evaluation and establish a mechanistic foundation for a device-based approach to endovascular aneurysm repair explantation that warrants clinical validation.

Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) has transformed the management of abdominal aortic aneurysms, offering reduced perioperative morbidity and mortality compared with open repair.1 Despite these advantages, long-term complications such as endoleaks, graft migration, and infection may necessitate graft explantation.2, 3, 4 Explantation is technically demanding, with the risks of aortic dissection, renal artery injury, and catastrophic bleeding, and it is associated with mortality rates approaching 30%.2 However, few standardized, purpose-built devices are currently available to perform this procedure effectively. The XplantR device recently obtained Food and Drug Administration de novo authorization following a multicenter simulated trial demonstrating atraumatic EVAR explantation, and it among the few notable developments in the field.5 The current explantation strategies range from improvised intraluminal maneuvers—traction, strut cutting, or syringe crimping—to formal open conversion, sacotomy/partial explant techniques, or hybrid endovascular-open approaches; the improvised techniques are often difficult and lead to significant aortic injury, and sacotomy/partial or hybrid strategies avoid full explantation but leave prosthetic material in situ and only address select failure modes, potentially necessitating further intervention.6, 7, 8, 9

Regardless of the approach used, EVAR explant techniques have been documented to induce significant intimal trauma and the “snowplow effect,” in which the graft or extraction device shears displace intimal tissue longitudinally.10 Disruption of the intimal layer can propagate into subintimal dissection planes, increasing the risk of aortic wall destabilization, uncontrolled bleeding, and the need for more extensive proximal and distal control. These events often translate into prolonged aortic clamp times, which are directly associated with higher rates of renal ischemia, visceral malperfusion, and perioperative morbidity.2 Additionally, the snowplow effect can create large areas of denuded endothelium and intimal flap formation, both of which may predispose patients to thrombosis, embolization, visceral aortic branch occlusion, or delayed aortic wall injury.4 Because EVAR extraction has a mortality rate approaching 30%, even incremental reductions in aortic wall injury during explantation may have an outsized effect on operative safety, translating into more controlled dissections, shorter clamp times, and reduced end-organ ischemic complications.2,4,10,11

In response, we developed the EndoEx device, a conical, ball-bearing–assisted, ergonomic instrument specifically engineered for graft explantation.10 By rolling along the aortic wall rather than sliding against it, the device reduces the friction and direct endothelial trauma associated with the syringe technique, in which the syringe makes direct contact with the aorta. The device is designed to accommodate grafts both with and without suprarenal fixation. However, EVAR without suprarenal fixation is intrinsically easier to remove and is less likely to require additional intervention. In previous proof-of-concept studies using hydrogels, bovine aorta, and cadaveric models, EndoEx demonstrated superior control and significantly reduced tissue injury compared with the syringe method. This was assessed grossly, however, without specific quantification of the extent of damage.10

Before clinical translation, however, it is critical to determine whether device-mediated explantation is usable, reproducible, and capable of mitigating known injury mechanisms. Usability testing provides insights into ergonomic challenges, cognitive load, and perceived utility in realistic settings and will further enable device refinement.12 Equally important is the development of a standardized, physiologically relevant test model to enable reproducible assessments across participants and devices. Because the abdominal aorta is composed of distinct layers—the adventitia providing tensile strength, the media contributing elasticity, and the intima serving as a delicate shear-prone lining—a model used for testing should capture these anatomic and biomechanical properties. Diseased aortas further complicate such testing as wall thinning, calcification, and intimal irregularities increase the risk of injury during graft explantation.13

Despite the morbidity and mortality associated with EVAR explantation, the absence of dedicated tools represents a critical gap in the operative strategy. This study evaluated whether a mechanism-based device can mitigate the primary mode of aortic injury—longitudinal shear—during explantation. We present the results for the structured usability testing of the EndoEx device conducted with vascular surgeons and trainees, along with the development of a custom abdominal aorta model. Our objectives were to (1) compare the performance of the EndoEx device against the syringe technique in a simulated environment and (2) identify the usability challenges and opportunities for device refinement.

Methods

Most participants had minimal prior explantation experience, having observed or performed one or two cases, although one vascular fellow reported five prior cases. An aortic model was developed through an iterative four-stage design process to simulate both scraping and snowplow effects during device extraction. The early prototypes used silicone tubing and three-dimensional (3D)-printed constructs; material hardness and layer thickness varied across the iterations to balance structural stability and wall compliance. The finalized abdominal aortic model was printed using Elastic 50A material on the Stratasys J55 3D printer, with an inner diameter of 1.905 cm and a total length of 10 cm, closely approximating the average infrarenal portion of the abdominal aorta in adults (diameter of ∼2.0 cm). After printing, each model was coated with a ∼0.5-mm layer of pink-dyed Dragon Skin 10 silicone to simulate the delicate intimal layer and to facilitate the visual assessment of endothelial scraping and snowplowing. The silicone was intended to serve as a surrogate marker of endothelial disruption rather than histologic injury. To ensure a uniform coating, the models were continuously rotated on a conveyor belt for at least 30 minutes during curing. Once complete, each model was secured to a poster board with zip ties, and a predeployed endograft was compressed into a 1-cm diameter syringe and deployed within the model, leaving the neck inferior to the bifurcation exposed.

Participants performed standardized endograft explantation using the syringe technique, followed by the EndoEx technique, in a controlled, anatomically representative aortic model, with each participant serving as their own control. Before testing, all participants watched a standardized instructional video demonstrating both techniques (Supplementary Fig 1, online only). Each participant then completed both extractions on opposite sides of the same model. Both devices operate on a common principle—encapsulating the endograft within a sheath while a stabilizing instrument holds the graft in place, allowing the fixation struts to collapse and the graft to be progressively withdrawn (Supplementary Figs II and III, online only). The EndoEx device additionally incorporates a ball-bearing system to reduce resistance during sheathing. Trials were recorded using a tripod-mounted camera. After both extractions, the intimal layer of each side was examined through the clear walls of the aortic model; areas of snowplow and scraping damage were quantified using ImageJ (Fig 1). The procedure times were also recorded for each trial. Participants completed a posttesting survey adapted from the NASA Task Load Index and System Usability Scale to assess mental workload, usability, and satisfaction, along with open-ended questions for obtaining qualitative feedback.Fig 1Experimental setup and snowplow effect simulation. The ImageJ-based quantification of intimal injury was conducted by two independent reviewers who were blinded to the extraction technique used. Quantification involved progressively rotating each model a set number of degrees and analyzing the endothelial injury identified in each plane. After a full 360° rotation was completed, the aggregate area of injury was documented. Because implantation itself caused a small, easily identifiable amount of endothelial damage at the strut insertion sites, these areas were excluded from the analysis of the explantation injury. Each reviewer measured the area of endothelial disruption on recorded images, and the values were averaged for analysis. Across all trials, the inter-reviewer differences did not exceed 5%.

Descriptive statistics (mean, median, and standard deviation) were used to summarize demographic data. Explantation times and snowplow areas were compared between devices using paired-samples t tests, as each participant served as their own control (significance threshold: P < .05). Survey scores were averaged and compared across the groups, and qualitative responses were analyzed thematically and summarized by proportion. Finally, a sensitivity analysis was used to assess the order effects between the first- and second-attempt performance, and Cohen’s d was computed for the paired difference in injury area between each participant’s syringe and EndoEx trials.

Twenty-five trials were completed with participants encompassing experienced vascular surgeons, residents, and fellows. On average, participants had 3.4 years of surgical experience and had observed or performed 1.2 prior EVAR explantation cases.

In this simulated environment, the EndoEx device markedly reduced shear-mediated intimal injury, reducing the affected surface area by approximately 90% compared with the syringe technique (Table I). The mean damaged area with EndoEx was 0.066 ± 0.055 in2 (0.94% ± 0.78% of the total traversed area) vs 0.669 ± 0.344 in2 (9.56% ± 4.91% of the total traversed area) with the syringe (P < .001), representing a statistically significant reduction in vessel wall injury associated with EndoEx use (Fig 2, A and B). EndoEx-assisted procedures required moderately longer extraction times compared with the syringe technique (Fig 2, C: 104.5 ± 60.9 seconds vs 71.7 ± 35.9 seconds; P < .05).Fig 2Quantitative experimental metrics comparing the EndoEx technique against the syringe technique. All participants completed a post-procedure usability survey comparing the two techniques (Fig 3; Table I). The EndoEx technique scored significantly higher than the syringe technique across all evaluated domains, including ease of introduction and positioning (7.09 ± 3.14 vs 4.09 ± 2.43), ergonomics and hand feel (7.00 ± 3.10 vs 4.36 ± 2.16), intraoperative visibility (7.36 ± 3.04 vs 4.55 ± 2.50), intuitiveness (7.09 ± 3.02 vs 4.55 ± 2.61), device stability and control (7.00 ± 3.13 vs 4.73 ± 2.57), and safety perception (6.55 ± 3.27 vs 3.64 ± 2.69) (all P < .05). Intraoperative visibility was defined as each participant’s ability to visualize the operating space inside the aortic lumen, specifically whether one device was bulkier and obstructed the visualization of the aortic walls. When asked to self-assess confidence, most participants reported feeling comfortable, with an average level of confidence when using the EndoEx device during this initial simulation.Fig 3Survey results evaluating confidence and overall perception of the EndoEx technique against the syringe technique.

Individualized survey responses most commonly identified the handgrip design and smaller body of the EndoEx device as a friction point when handling the tool during introduction. However, once the EndoEx device was introduced into each model, the majority of participants described handling the EndoEx device to be smoother, more controlled, and much safer than the syringe throughout the remainder of the explantation process.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the dominant mechanism of aortic injury during endograft explantation—longitudinal shear and the intimal snowplow effect—can be substantially eliminated through a mechanism-based device strategy. The observed reduction in intimal injury reflects the suppression of a specific, reproducible biomechanical failure mode rather than a nonspecific improvement in procedural performance. This observation suggests that the encapsulation-based device design represents a fundamentally different operative strategy rather than an incremental refinement of the existing explantation techniques.

Despite the increasing application of EVAR explantation, few purpose-built devices exist to safely disengage fixation while minimizing aortic injury, likely due to the procedure's relative infrequency, technical complexity, and reliance on improved techniques.5 The EndoEx device addresses this gap by introducing a controlled, reproducible, and device-driven approach centered on atraumatic graft encapsulation. For the practicing vascular surgeon, this approach has meaningful implications. EVAR explantation is often marked by poorly defined dissection planes, prolonged clamp times, increased risk of renal or visceral ischemia, and high-risk proximal control. A strategy that eliminates shear-mediated intimal injury could enable more controlled graft disengagement and, if clinically translatable, reduce these key determinants of perioperative mortality.

The EndoEx device reduced intimal injury in our aortic models by approximately 90% compared with the syringe technique while also decreasing the cognitive workload and improving the ease of use—a magnitude of reduction consistent with the elimination of the shear-driven snowplow effect rather than incremental procedural refinement. However, the EndoEx-assisted explantation procedure required moderately longer procedure times (+32 seconds), likely reflecting first-use inefficiency and additional device steps. In practice, the results of our study indicate that at the cost of ∼30 seconds, the EndoEx device is projected to reduce intimal injury by nearly tenfold.

In this study, two participants performed repeat trials to preliminarily evaluate the effect of familiarity on the procedure time. These repeat trials revealed an average 40-second reduction in the EndoEx-based EVAR extraction time, bringing its performance in line with each participant’s corresponding syringe technique trial. These findings suggest a measurable learning curve for the EndoEx device. Although its initial use was associated with moderately longer extraction times compared with the syringe technique, this difference diminished with repetition, indicating rapid gains in efficiency with minimal additional training. This trend has favorable implications for clinical adoption; this observation suggests that the device may be incorporated into surgical practice without a prolonged or burdensome training period. However, because these repeat trials involved only two participants, additional trials will be needed to further examine this phenomenon. Notably, improvements in device control and reductions in intimal injury were observed even during initial use, highlighting an inherent safety advantage independent of operator experience. Future studies incorporating increased trials and broader participant exposure are expected to demonstrate that growing familiarity with the EndoEx device will reduce the procedure time and minimize any remaining efficiency differences between the techniques.

Several methodological considerations warrant mention. A sensitivity analysis revealed no significant order effects between the first- and second-attempt performance, and the post hoc effect size calculation indicated a large treatment effect (Cohen's d > 2.0), supporting the adequacy of statistical power despite the limited sample size. While not histologic, this model isolates shear-induced surface disruption, enabling targeted mechanistic evaluation.

In addition to the present simulation-based findings, previous evaluations of the EndoEx device in cadaveric models further support its translational potential. In these studies, the device demonstrated effective graft encapsulation and controlled explantation within native human aortic tissue, with the preservation of the surrounding structures and minimal evidence of intimal disruption. Importantly, cadaveric testing captures key anatomic and tactile elements that are not fully reproducible in benchtop models, including tissue heterogeneity, fixation variability, and spatial constraints encountered during open conversion. The concordance between cadaveric performance and the current usability results strengthens the validity of the EndoEx approach, and this concordance provides an important bridge between early prototype testing and future in vivo evaluation.

Despite the clear clinical need, innovation in dedicated EVAR explantation tools has been remarkably limited.10 The current open strategies for this procedure generally involve strut cutting or graft crimping, which have been documented to be associated with significantly elevated risks of aortic wall injury and trauma.6 Additionally, hybrid approaches, although limited, leave prosthetic material in situ and thus address only a small subset of failure mechanisms. Our study adds to this limited body of literature by incorporating structured feedback from both practicing surgeons and trainees across varying levels of expertise for the comparison of our device with the current standard syringe technique and for the validation of the EndoEx technique's superiority over the current approaches.

Alongside the limited innovation in endograft explantation, very few studies have detailed the development of a dynamic, mechanically accurate aortic model suitable for evaluating intraluminal devices.14,15 Our methodology addresses this gap by providing a reproducible framework for aortic model construction and device analysis that may serve as a useful reference for future researchers. Our findings suggest that effective models should incorporate a dual-layer approach—with a softer inner layer to replicate the tunica media and a more robust outer layer to approximate the tunica adventitia—to accurately reproduce the surgical feel and structural integrity of the native aortic wall. Additionally, using a thin 0.5-mm silicone layer served as an effective mechanism for recreating the overall fragility and strength of the tunica intima. These techniques allowed us to mimic the compliance of the native infrarenal aorta, which has been reported to range from approximately 5% to 10% diameter change per 100 mmHg, thereby providing a physiologically relevant substrate for device interaction.14,16 While not a perfect replication of the native tissue, this approach aligns with prior validated in vitro aortic models used for endovascular device testing.14,16

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the order of device use was not randomized, introducing the potential for learning or carryover bias that may have influenced procedural efficiency and user perception. Second, this was a simulation-based study, and although the model was designed to approximate the aortic anatomy and compliance, it does not fully replicate the complexity of in vivo conditions, including active bleeding, pulsatile hemodynamics, tissue fragility, and the need for proximal and distal control. The findings may not fully capture the technical challenges encountered during open explantation. Third, the model did not incorporate pathologic features, such as aneurysmal degeneration, calcification, or infection, which may affect the device performance in clinical scenarios. Finally, participants had relatively limited prior experience with EVAR explantation (mean of 1.2 cases), which may limit generalizability to highly experienced surgeons, although this also reflects the reality that explantation is an infrequent but high-risk procedure. As the conclusions in this study were primarily extrapolated from simulations, additional studies incorporating randomized designs, more complex anatomic models, and in vivo validation are essential to confirm these findings and to validate the relationship between the EndoEx device and other predictive clinical metrics, such as surgical cross-clamp times and end-organ ischemia.

Conclusions

The findings of this simulation-based study suggest that EndoEx not only improves operator ergonomics but fundamentally alters the mechanics of endograft explantation. By markedly reducing the shear-driven “snowplow” effect noted with syringe-based techniques, the device minimizes longitudinal intimal displacement—a key driver of aortic dissection and wall injury. This shift from shear-dominant extraction to controlled encapsulation establishes a mechanistic foundation for device-mediated EVAR explantation and supports progression to in vivo validati1on. Clinically, reducing the aortic injury in this manner may translate to shorter clamp times, improved hemostasis, and decreased end-organ ischemia—factors closely linked to perioperative morbidity and mortality.
