Prevalence and risk factors of intestinal schistosomiasis among school-age children living near the Mai-Nigus irrigation dam, Tigray, northern Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Teklay Abrha Yanshet, Gebremedhin Gebrezgabiher, Melese Birmeka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchistosomiasis, a neglected tropical disease (NTD), is caused by the blood flukes of the genus Schistosoma. Irrigation dams create ideal habitats for freshwater snails and increase the risk of schistosomiasis transmission. This study was conducted to assess the prevalence of intestinal schistosomiasis and associated risk factors among school-age children (SAC) living near Mai-Nigus irrigation dam in La’elay Maichew district, Tigray, northern Ethiopia, using Kato-Katz thick smear technique.MethodsA school-based cross-sectional parasitological study was conducted to assess the prevalence, burden, and risk factors of schistosomiasis among SAC residing near Mai-Nigus irrigation dam in June 2024, Tigray, northern Ethiopia. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and binary logistic regression, with test statistics conducted at a 95% confidence level and a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsThis study found a 12.6% prevalence of Schistosoma mansoni infection among SAC near the Mai-Nigus irrigation dam. All of the infections were due to the light intensity of infection. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that those SAC who fetch water from the dam [adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 14.5, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.8–74.7] and wash clothes in the dam [AOR = 6.4, 95% CI: 1.1–35.4] were nearly fifteen and seven times more likely to be infected with S. mansoni compared to their respective counterparts, respectively. Moreover, SAC engaged in irrigation practices faced a 13-fold higher infection rate than those not participating in such activities [AOR = 13.2, 95% CI: 2.4–74.2].ConclusionsThe study reports a 12.6% prevalence of intestinal schistosomiasis among SAC near Mai-Nigus irrigation dam, Tigray region. Although the infection intensity is light, the presence of the disease indicates ongoing transmission needing attention. Key risk factors for S. mansoni infection include activities such as fetching water, washing clothes, and engaging in irrigation, underscoring the need for enhanced public health interventions and community education. Essential strategies include promoting the use of safe water, hygiene education, and routine screenings. Ongoing epidemiological monitoring and community engagement are vital to reducing disease prevalence and safeguarding the future generations’ health in the area.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06354-5.

Background

Schistosomiasis, often called bilharzia, is a neglected tropical disease (NTD) caused by blood flukes of the genus Schistosoma [1, 2]. The majority of infections are due to three species: Schistosoma haematobium, S. japonicum, and S. mansoni [3]. The disease can manifest as either urogenital (S. haematobium) or intestinal (S. mansoni) schistosomiasis [4]. The disease remains a significant public health concern in the tropical and subtropical areas of the world [5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that over 2 million people are infected with schistosomiasis globally [6]. The disease is transmitted through freshwater infested with the larval form of the parasite, which penetrates the skin upon contact with the contaminated water [5]. Inside the body, the parasites mature into adult schistosomes in the blood vessels, producing thousands of eggs and causing various health complications [2]. Symptoms range from mild to severe, including abdominal pain, diarrhea, and anemia. Chronic cases can lead to significant morbidity, affecting growth, cognitive development, and overall quality of life [7, 8]. In 2020, WHO launched a road map for 2021–2030 to end the suffering from NTDs by 2030. The road map specifically targets eliminating schistosomiasis as a public health problem and interrupting its transmission in selected countries by 2030 [2].

Ethiopia bears one of the highest burden of schistosomiasis in Africa [9], with 53.3 million people at risk of infection in 2020 [10]. The disease is widely distributed [11] due to favorable ecological conditions [4, 12] and is endemic in various regions [9], disproportionately affecting school-age children (SAC) [12]. The impact of schistosomiasis extends beyond physical health, affecting children’s school attendance, cognitive development, and overall quality of life. Consequently, since 2015, the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health has implemented a national school-based deworming program to treat SAC. This initiative aims to reduce the prevalence of schistosomiasis to a level where it no longer constitutes a public health problem [13].

Small dam irrigation has been promoted to enhance agricultural productivity and improve food security in Ethiopia [14–17], including the Tigray region, in response to climatic challenges and population growth [14]. While beneficial for improved crop yields and economic development, these dams can impact the transmission of waterborne diseases like schistosomiasis. Research in many parts of Africa indicates that dam irrigation projects increase schistosomiasis transmission [18–23] by providing ideal habitat for the snail intermediate hosts [18, 20, 21, 24–28]. Moreover, several studies conducted globally, including in Ethiopia, have identified various factors that increase the risk of infection. These factors encompass demographic elements such as gender [29] and age [30]; environmental factors like proximity to water bodies and irrigation activities [30–32]; and behavioral factors such as fishing [5, 32], swimming [5, 30, 32], washing clothes and fetching water [12], and the level of knowledge and awareness about the disease and its transmission [33].

The Mai-Nigus irrigation scheme, located in the La’elay Maichew district of the Tigray region, is crucial for enhancing the nearby community’s agricultural productivity and food security. Available literature indicates that little information exists on the impact of irrigation dams on the epidemiology of schistosomiasis in Tigray [21, 28]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has been done on the impact of Mai-Nigus irrigation dam on the epidemiology of schistosomiasis in this specific locale. Thus, this study was conducted to assess the prevalence of schistosomiasis and associated risk factors among SAC around the irrigation dam in Dura kebele of La’elay Maichew district of Tigray region, northern Ethiopia, using the Kato-Katz thick smear technique.

Methods

Study area and selection

The study was undertaken among SAC attending Dura Elementary and Debrebrhan Secondary Schools in Dura Tabiya (Kebele) of La’elai Maichew district, Tigray region, northern Ethiopia (Fig. 1). Kebele, also known as Tabia or Tabiya, is the smallest administrative division in Ethiopia, typically consisting of around 20 smaller villages. Collectively, kebeles form a district, which is a subdivision below the zone and region levels. These two public schools were purposefully chosen for this study because they are close to the Mai-Nigus irrigation dam and have students aged 7 to 21 years old. Many children and adults in the communities surrounding the dam engage in activities — such as irrigation, fetching water, swimming, bathing, and washing clothes — using the dam’s water, which could predispose them to S. mansoni infection. Dura Kebele, located in the La’elay Maichew district of Tigray region, is home to this long-standing man-made irrigational dam, for crop production. Geographically, the site is located at an elevation of 2,131 meters above sea level, between latitude 14o 7’ 5’’ north and longitude 38o 42’ 53’’ east, approximately seven kilometers west of Axum town (the zonal capital of Tigray’s central zone). The Mai-Nigus dam area has a unimodal rainfall pattern, with the primary rainy season lasting from June to August. The remaining months are dry. The average annual rainfall in the area is 662.7 mm. Founded in 1998 by the Commission for Sustainable Agriculture and Environmental Rehabilitation in Tigray, the dam has the capacity to irrigate around 310 hectares. Based on information from the La’elay Maichew district health bureau officials and the directors of the respective schools, it has been confirmed that no preventive chemotherapy is currently being implemented against intestinal schistosomaisis in the study area.

Study design and period

A school-based cross-sectional study was employed to assess the prevalence, burden, and risk factors of schistosomiasis among SAC residing near Mai-Nigus irrigation dam in June 2024.

Study population

The study population comprised SAC aged 7–21 years residing in Dura kebele, including both primary and high school students. This cohort was selected due to their likelihood and easiness of complying with stool collection requirements. Additionally, the prevalence of infection among SAC serves as a valuable proxy for assessing the overall community prevalence.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

The study included participants who met the age criteria between 7 and 21 years who resided in communities within a five=kilometer radius of the Mai-Nigus irrigation scheme for at least a year. These participants were enrolled in the selected schools and were not undergoing treatment for schistosomiasis at the time of the study. Children who voluntarily provided verbal assent and whose parents or legal guardians consented to their participation were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Children younger than seven years or older than 21 years, those who had not resided in the communities near the Mai-Nigus irrigation scheme for at least a year, and children who did not volunteer to participate were excluded from the study. Additionally, children who had received treatment for schistosomiasis (praziquantel) within the last six months, recent arrivals (less than one year) from schistosomiasis-endemic areas in Tigray or elsewhere, and those participants aware they had schistosomiasis infection but did not take the appropriate medication (praziquantel) were also excluded.

Where “n” is the required total sample size, “z” is the level of confidence, “p” is the estimated prevalence of S. mansoni infection, and “d” is the margin of error. Taking an estimated prevalence (p) of 14% [35], Z of 1.96 at a 95% confidence interval (CI), and precision value (d) of 5%, the total sample size was calculated to be 185. Assuming a 10% non-response, the sample size was adjusted to 204. Dura Primary School (Grades 1–8) has a total size of 562 students, while Debrebrhan Secondary School (Grades 9–12) has 191 students, with each grades consisting of a single class. The number of children enrolled in the study from each school was proportional to the total number of students. Accordingly, 52 and 152 SAC were enrolled from Debrebrhan Secondary and Dura Primary Schools, respectively. First, at the school level, students were stratified according to their grade level (class). Then, the sample size of each school was reallocated to each class proportional to their sizes. The sampling interval k was determined by using the formula: k =\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{N}{\text{n}\text{c}}$$\end{document}, where N is the total number of students in each class listed as 1,2,3,….N, and nc is the sample size allocated to each class. The first sudent was selected using systematic sampling from the list 1 to k using the class roster as a sampling frame.

Data collection and processing

A semi-structured questionnaire was developed, pretested on a small group before full-scale implementation, and then administered to collect data on socio-demographic and behavioral practices related to schistosomiasis transmission among children (Supplementary file). The collected data include children’s profiles, including gender, age, grade level, and water contact-associated activities like swimming habit in the dam, washing clothes in the dam, fetching water from the dam, engaging in irrigation practices using the dam’s water, and awareness about the disease. The interview data were reviewed for completeness, and any needed corrections were made before departing the study sites. After instructing on collecting stool specimens without contamination with the soil, urine, or any dirty material, each selected SAC was provided with a clean, dry, and leak-proof stool cap to collect 3 g stool specimens. Then, the samples were transported to and processed at Aksum University Comprehensive Hospital using a one-slide Kato-Katz technique [36]. Eggs were counted, recorded, and later converted to egg per gram (epg) by multiplying with a factor of 24 [37, 38]. The intensity of S. mansoni infection was calculated to determine the worm burden based on the intensity categories set by WHO as light (1–99 epg), moderate (100–399 epg), and heavy (epg >400) infection [39].

To maintain the quality of laboratory procedures, an experienced laboratory technician verified reagents, chemicals, and kits before processing and examining samples. Upon submission, samples were immediately checked for proper labelling. Additionally, 10% of the slides were randomly selected and re-examined by a different laboratory technician in the hospital who had not previously examined them.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Research Ethical Review Committee (RERC) of Hawassa University (reference number: CNCS-REC019/24). Relevant authorities were given a detailed explanation of the study’s objectives, and permission to conduct the study was granted by the La’elay Maichew district health and educational authorities and the directors of the selected schools. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of the participating children before stool specimen collection. Participating children were given a detailed explanation of the study’s objectives and provided their oral consent before the specimen collection. They were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. All the collected information was kept confidential. Children diagnosed with S. mansoni infection were also referred to their nearby health facilities for treatment.

Data analysis

Data were entered into Epidata and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics, including frequency and percentage, were computed to summarize the socio-demographic characteristics of the SAC and the prevalence of schistosome infection. The arithmetic mean intensity of infection was calculated to evaluate the intensity of infection. The arithmetic mean intensity of infection was calculated as:

Results

Socio-demographic and behavioral characteristics of the study participants

Among the 204 SAC enrolled in this study, 190 (93.1%) provided sufficient stool samples. The majority (65.3%) were aged 7–14, with a slight female predominance (53.2%). The highest group (40.5%) had an education level below grade 4. Moreover, 17.9%, 19.5%, 22.6%, and 24.2% of the children had the habit of swimming in the irrigation dam, fetching water directly from the dam, using the dam water for washing clothes, and engaging in irrigation activities using dam water, respectively. Regarding awareness, a slight majority of the study participants (52.6%) were unaware of the disease (Table 1).

The prevalence and intensity of S. mansoni infection

The study found a 12.6% (24 out of 190) prevalence of S. mansoni infection in SAC near the Mai-Nigus irrigation dam, with 83.3% of infected individuals aged 7–14 years. Both genders had an equal prevalence of 50% (n = 12 each). Based on the educational levels, the highest prevalence was observed in students below grade 4, at 50%. Behavioral characteristics showed varying prevalence rates, with 44.1% of infected children habitually swimming in the dam, 45.7% involved in irrigation activities, 45.7% washing clothes using dam water, and 45.9% fetching water from the dam. Discussion

Knowledge of the prevalence, burden, and drivers of schistosomiasis is critical for disease control [40]. This study was conducted to determine the burden of schistosomiasis and risk factors of S. mansoni infection in Dura Kebele of La’elay Maichew district of Tigray region, northern Ethiopia, where the Mai-Nigus irrigation dam is located.

According to this study, 12.6% of the SAC in the study area had S. mansoni infection, indicating a moderate prevalence of infection [2]. Findings from other parts of Ethiopia, such as Tigray [35], Guangua district [30], Sanja health center [40], Wondo district [41], Ejaji town [42], and Abeshge district [43], are largely consistent with this prevalence. The prevalence found in this study, however, was more than that seen in the Medebay Zana district [44], Koga irrigation scheme [45], rural Bahir Dar [46], Jimma town [47], Ambesame [48]. On the other hand, this was less than the reports from other regions in Ethiopia [49–65] and other African nations, like the Tanzania [66] and Gambia [29]. This variance likely reflects differences in water contact behaviour of the SAC, diagnostic methods (e.g. single versus duplicate Kato-Katz), socioeconomic status, environmental sanitation, ecological distribution of snails, local endemicity [67].

In this study, all SAC who tested positive for S. mansoni had only light infection, with no moderate or heavy cases detected. This contrasts with previous studies in other areas of Ethiopia, which reported moderate and heavy infections alongside light infections [55, 57, 59, 68, 69]. These discrepancies may be attributed to geographical and ecological variations, differences in diagnostic methods, environmental sanitation, socioeconomic conditions, and variations in the study period [12].

Identifying the risk factors for schistosomiasis among SAC in specific communities is critical for designing effective preventative efforts. The findings of this study highlight significant associations between various water-related activities in the irrigation dam and the risk of schistosomiasis infection [70]. In our study, SAC who washed their clothes in the dam exhibited a 6.4 times higher infection rate than their peers who did not wash. This corroborates previous research establishing a direct correlation between washing clothes and increased exposure to parasitic larvae [12, 71]. Individuals engaged in this activity often spend prolonged periods in the water, increasing their skin exposure to cercariae, the larval form of the parasite that penetrates the skin. This practice is common due to a lack of other washing facilities. Community-led initiatives to provide alternative water sources for washing and/or fetching could reduce exposure.

For many agricultural communities, including those in our study area, fetching water for household consumption is a daily necessity. Our findings indicate that individuals who fetch water from the Mai-Nigus irrigation dam are at heightened risk for schistosomiasis (AOR = 14.5). Wading into infested waters to collect water exposes individuals to cercariae that can penetrate the skin [72, 73]. This aligns with similar studies indicate that fetching water significantly increases the frequency of water contact [40] and the likelihood of S. mansoni infection, especially without protective measures. Providing clean water could help alleviate this problem. Additionally, SAC who participate in irrigation practices experienced a 13-fold higher infection rate compared to those not participating in such activities, which is in line with previous studies that highlighted agricultural practices involving direct contact with irrigation waters significantly contribute to the transmission of S. mansoni infection [30, 31, 40]. This increased risk of infection is attributed to the daily exposure to contaminated water. Raising awareness about protective measures, such as wearing appropriate protective clothing and footwear, is vital in reducing community incidence rates. In this study, no sociodemographic-related risk factor was observed, suggesting that behavioral and environmental factors significantly impact the dynamics of S. mansoni transmission more than socio-demographic factors. This finding underscores the critical importance of implementing comprehensive control measures that address water contact behaviors, improve sanitation, and regulate environmental conditions to reduce S. mansoni transmission effectively.

Limitation of the study

The findings of this study should be interpreted with the following considerations in mind. First, this research relied on a single-slide Kato-Katz thick smear preparation method per stool sample due to insufficient specimen quantities for duplicate slides. This methodological constraint may reduce the diagnostic sensitivity, as single slide protocols are known to underestimate infection prevalence compared to the WHO-recommended duplicate slide approach. Consequently, the reported prevalence of S. mansoni in this study should be interpreted as a conservative estimate. Future studies should prioritize collecting larger stool samples to facilitate double slide examination, thereby improving diagnostic rigor. Second, the reliability of self-reported behavioural data may be limited by comprehension challenges among younger participants. Children aged 7–10 years, in particular, may have struggled to fully articulate water-contact practices or understand the questions, potentially introducing recall or response bias.

Conclusions

The study reveals a 12.6% prevalence of intestinal schistosomiasis among school-age children living near the Mai-Nigus irrigation dam. The light intensity of infection suggests that while the immediate risk may appear low, the presence of the disease indicates ongoing epidemiological dynamics that require attention.

Our findings highlight several significant risk factors contributing to schistosomiasis transmission among SAC in the study area, notably water-related activities, including fetching water from the dam, washing clothes in the dam, and engaging in irrigation activities. These activities increase exposure to contaminated water and underscore the critical need for improved public health interventions and community education.

It is essential to promote safe water practices, practical hygiene education, and regular screening to mitigate the risk of schistosomiasis. Additionally, continued epidemiological monitoring and community engagement are crucial for safeguarding the health of future generations in the area.
