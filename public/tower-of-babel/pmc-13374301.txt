Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses reveal melatonin-mediated drought response in red raspberry at the squaring stage
Authors: Juanjuan Guo, Siqi Cheng, Ziqian Fu, Qinglong Dong, Peng Jia, Haoan Luan, Xuemei Zhang, Guohui Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRed raspberry (Rubus idaeus L.) cultivation is often constrained by drought stress, which reduces fruit yield and quality. Melatonin (MT) plays an important role in plant stress tolerance. This study examined how exogenous MT improves drought tolerance in red raspberry at the squaring stage. It focused on changes in photosynthetic performance, antioxidant capacity, leaves anatomical structure, gene expression, and hormone homeostasis.ResultsDrought stress significantly reduced photosynthetic performance, antioxidant enzyme activities, and hormone homeostasis in red raspberry, and altered leaves anatomical structure. Exogenous MT treatment, particularly at 150 μmol L⁻1, effectively alleviated these drought-induced effects. MT increased net photosynthetic rate and stomatal conductance, reduced oxidative damage, and promoted osmotic accumulation. At the anatomical level, MT-treated leaves showed more compact spongy tissue, increased palisade tissue and leaves thickness under drought stress. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses further revealed coordinated molecular changes under MT treatment. These changes mainly involved photosynthesis, carbohydrate and starch metabolism, plant hormone signaling, and amino acid metabolism. Genes related to photosynthetic function (PNSL3 and PNSB4), carbohydrate metabolism (glgc and PYG), antioxidant defense (SOD1 and sodC), and auxin signaling (AUX1 and SAUR) showed marked expression changes under MT treatment. Metabolite profiling also revealed changes in sugars, amino acids, phenolic compounds, and hormone-related compounds. Several transcription factor families, including bHLH, MYB-related, NAC, WRKY, and ERF, showed strong responses to MT. These changes were associated with transcriptional regulation and metabolic adjustment under drought stress.ConclusionsExogenous MT significantly improved drought tolerance in red raspberry at the squaring stage, with 150 μmol L⁻1 showing the best overall performance. This protective effect was associated with improved photosynthetic performance, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and better maintenance of leaves structure. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses further revealed changes in hormone-related and metabolic pathways. These findings indicate that MT enhanced drought tolerance in red raspberry through coordinated physiological, anatomical, and molecular responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08974-0.Keywords: Red raspberry, Exogenous melatonin, Drought stress, Squaring stage

Introduction

Red raspberry (Rubus idaeus L.) is a nutrient-rich fruit containing ellagic acid, vitamins, and anthocyanins with recognized health benefits [1]. Its fruits and bioactive leaves are used in a variety of functional products, which has promoted its development as a high-value crop worldwide [2, 3]. The squaring stage is a critical transition from vegetative to reproductive growth. Its physiological status strongly influences subsequent flowering and fruit set rate and final yield. Drought stress can readily lead to pollen abortion, floral organ deformity and flower and fruit drop, resulting in irreversible yield loss [4]. During this period, plants become more sensitive to adverse conditions such as drought stress, temperature extremes and imbalanced nutrient supply. Drought often leads to a decrease in photosynthetic rate, flower bud degeneration, reduced fruit set rate and increased malformed fruit rate, thereby reducing yield and fruit quality [5].

Red raspberries have shallow root systems, require substantial water supply, and are highly sensitive to water deficit. Drought can negatively affect their growth, leading to reduced yield and lower economic returns [6]. Climate change has increased the frequency and severity of drought events worldwide. In response to such stress, plants activate a suite of physiological and biochemical adaptations to maintain cellular turgor and mitigate damage [7]. Plants respond by accumulating soluble proteins, soluble sugars, starch, and proline to maintain osmotic balance and enhance drought tolerance [8]. Furthermore, drought stress induces antioxidant enzyme systems to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and preserve membrane integrity [9]. Under drought stress, abscisic acid (ABA) accumulates rapidly, promoting stomatal closure to minimize transpirational water loss. It also participates in hormonal crosstalk to sustain photosynthetic activity and enhance overall drought tolerance [10].

Melatonin (MT) is an indoleamine that is ubiquitous in organisms. Exogenous MT application has been widely reported to alleviate abiotic stress in plants [11]. MT promotes plant growth and development and regulates key physiological processes. It also enhances tolerance to a range of diverse abiotic stresses [12]. It alleviates oxidative damage by enhancing antioxidant defense systems, thereby increasing tolerance to adverse conditions [13]. MT maintains endogenous hormone homeostasis, maintains photosynthesis activity, increases chlorophyll content, and promotes roots development [14]. It interacts with other hormones, nitric oxide (NO), and ROS to contribute to stress tolerance [15]. MT increases antioxidant enzyme activity and regulates the expression of stress-related genes. It also improves photosynthetic efficiency, promotes ROS scavenging, and affects secondary metabolism [15]. Raspberries are especially sensitive to drought during the shoot growth and squaring stages. Existing studies on raspberry drought stress primarily focus on the seedling stages [16] or on general physiological responses [17]. Molecular response mechanisms at the squaring stage remain poorly understood. This gap highlights the need for further research to clarify stage-specific drought responses in raspberry.

This study examined the effects of MT pretreatment on 'Polka' raspberry under drought stress. Physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses were assessed. Integrated analysis was performed to examine the relationships among differentially expressed genes, metabolites, and phenotypic. This analysis helped identify key genes and metabolic pathways linked to drought tolerance. Although MT has been shown to alleviate drought stress in various plants, specific pathways mediating its action remain incompletely characterized.

Methods

Plant materials

Current-year ‘Polka’ red raspberry with uniform growth and no visible pests or diseases were selected at the squaring stage. The seedlings were collected from Zishui Technology Co., Ltd. in Neiqiu County, Xingtai City, Hebei Province, China (114° 21′ 21″ E, 37° 28′ 67″ N, elevation 90 m). They were then transported to Hebei Agricultural University, Baoding, Hebei Province, China (115° 45′ 40″ E, 39° 23′ 03″ N, elevation 20.4 m) for the experiment. The seedlings were transplanted into plastic pots (15 cm in diameter and 25 cm in height) with drainage holes at the bottom. Each pot was filled with 6 kg of air-dried and sieved experimental soil, and two seedling was planted in per pot.

Experimental design

Thirty-six pots of red raspberry seedlings were placed in a simple greenhouse constructed for this experiment for the experiment. The greenhouse was covered with polyethylene film and had adjustable ventilation openings at the top. During the experiment, the temperature inside the greenhouse was regulated by adjusting the ventilation openings. At night, the greenhouse was covered with an insulation blanket at night. The mean temperature was 28.8 ℃ (range: 25.0–32.7 ℃). The relative humidity was adjusted by ventilation and floor watering, and maintained at about 60.3% (range: 52.5%−68.2%) (Tab. S1). The light intensity was 2000–3000 lx. After seedlings were grown under normally conditions until the squaring stage. Plants with uniform growth and no pests or diseases were selected and randomly divided into six treatment groups (each treatment consisted of six pots, with two plants per pot, and three biological replicates). The treatments were as follows: control group (CK), drought treatment group (D), and drought stress plus MT at 50, 100, 150, and 200 μmol L⁻1, designated DM1, DM2, DM3, and DM4, respectively.

All red raspberry plants were fully irrigated the day before MT pretreatment. The first spraying was carried out at 8:00 p.m. at the squaring period. The CK treatments was sprayed with distilled water containing 0.1% (v/v) ethanol, whereas plants in the D treatments were subjected to continuous drought eceived no spray treatment. The DM1-DM4 treatments were sprayed with MT solution at the corresponding concentration. MT was first dissolved in a minimal volume of absolute ethanol, and then diluted with distilled water to the target concentration. Spraying was then performed once every two days for a total of four applications. Approximately 30 mL of solution was applied to each pot. During the treatment period, soil water content was controlled by the pot-weighing method. Under normal watering, soil water content was maintained at 60%–80% of field capacity, whereas under drought treatment it was maintained at 30%–40% of field capacity. Water was replenished daily between 18:00 and 19:00 to maintain the target soil water content. In addition, pots without plants were included as evaporation controls to correct for evaporation loss. After the first spraying, drought treatment was imposed for 20 d in the D and DM1–DM4 treatments, whereas the CK treatments was maintained under normal watering conditions. At the end of the treatment, leaves, stems and roots were collected from each plants for analysis. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were performed using the same set of biological replicate leaves samples collected at the same sampling time. These samples were then divided for the two omics analyses.

Leaves color and photosynthetic pigments

The photosynthetic pigments were extracted with an acetone-ethanol mixture (Analytical Reagent; China National Pharmaceutical Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). A 0.2 g of fresh leaves tissue was weighed into small pieces and transferred to a stoppered tube containing 10 mL of acetone–ethanol mixture (1:1, v/v). The samples were extracted in the dark for 24 h until the tissue completely turns white.

A colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Japan) was used to measure the color difference of red raspberry leaves after 20 d of treatments. The 3rd, 6th, and 9th fully expanded leaves from the apex were selected and designated as upper leaves (UL), middle leaves (ML), and lower leaves (LL), respectively. Only healthy and undamaged functional leaves were used for measurement. The main vein was avoided during measurement.

Each leaf was measured three times at three symmetrical positions on the leaves tip, middle and base. The color difference (ΔE) was calculated using formula (1). Chromaticity (ΔC) was calculated using formula (2). Hue difference (ΔH) was calculated using formula (3).

Photosynthetic characteristics and chlorophyll fluorescence

Photosynthetic parameters were measured using LI-6400 portable photosynthesis instrument (LI-COR, USA). Measurements were taken between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. on a clear day. The 5th to 6th fully expanded leaves from the apex, with similar size and light exposure, were selected for measurement. The net photosynthetic rate (Pn), transpiration rate (E), stomatal conductance (Gs) and intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci) were measured. The fluorescence parameters of the same leaves were measured using Pocket PEA chlorophyll fluorometer (Hansatech, UK). After dark adaptation for 30 min, the initial fluorescence (Fo), maximum fluorescence (Fm), variable fluorescence (Fv) and maximum quantum yield of photosystem II (Fv/Fm) were measured. Each treatment included three biological replicates, and three leaves per replicate were measured.

Osmotic adjustment substances and antioxidant parameters

Soluble sugars (SS) and starch (ST) were quantified by the anthrone method. A 0.1 g dry sample was extracted with 80% ethanol (analytical grade, China National Pharmaceutical Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and then centrifuged. The supernatant was used for soluble sugar determination, and the residue was extracted in a boiling water bath to measure starch. The extract was mixed with anthrone-sulfuric acid reagent (Huabo Station Bioanalytical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and reacted in a boiling water bath for 10 min. The absorbance was measured at 620 nm, and the content was calculated from a glucose standard curve. Free proline (Pro) was determined by the sulfosalicylic acid method [18]. A 0.5 g fresh sample was ground in sulfosalicylic acid solution (Solaibao Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and extracted in a boiling water bath. The supernatant was mixed with acid ninhydrin (Baima Technology Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) and the reaction product was extracted with toluene (analytical grade, China National Pharmaceutical Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The absorbance was measured at 520 nm. Soluble protein (SP) content was determined by Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 staining [19]. A 1 g fresh sample was ground and homogenized in pre-cooled phosphate buffer and then centrifuged (Anpu Cloud Experimental Supplies Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The supernatant was mixed with Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 solution (Solaibao Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and incubated for 5 min. The absorbance was measured at 595 nm, and the content was calculated from a bovine serum albumin standard curve. Activities of peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) were assayed using commercial kits (Solaibao Technology Co., Ltd., China). Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was measured via the thiobarbituric acid method. 0.5 g of fresh sample was grinded and centrifuged by 10% trichloroacetic acid (McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The supernatant was mixed with thiobarbituric acid (Solaibao Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) in a boiling water bath and then centrifuged. The absorbance was measured at 532 nm, 600 nm and 450 nm, and the content was calculated according to the formula [(A532-A600)—0.056 × (A450-A600)] × extract volume/(1.55 × sample fresh weight).

Hormone determination

The contents of auxin (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonic acid (JA), and melatonin (MT) and zeatin riboside (ZR) were quantified using an HPLC–MS/MS system consisting of an Agilent 1290 HPLC and a QTRAP 6500 mass spectrometer. Samples were extracted with acetonitrile (HPLC grade; McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at low temperature and purified using a C18 solid-phase extraction column (Anpel Laboratory Technologies Inc., Shanghai, China). The purified extracts were dried under nitrogen and reconstituted in methanol (HPLC grade; McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

HPLC conditions were as follows: column temperature 30 °C; gradient elution with methanol and water; flow rate 0.3 mL·min−1; and injection volume 2 μL. Mass spectrometry was performed with the following settings: electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive and negative ion modes, multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, spray voltage of ± 4500 V, nebulizer pressure of 65 psi, and source temperature 400 °C. The standard curve was established using plant hormone standards (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). Each treatment included three biological replicates.

Leaves anatomical structure

Paraffin sections were prepared according to the standard protocol [20]. The samples were washed with water, stained with 1% safranin ethanol solution (Baima Technology Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China), dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and cleared in xylene (McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The tissues were then gradually infiltrated with paraffin wax (Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 50 ℃ and 60 ℃. The sections were sectioned using a KD-20 microtome, the sections were floated and expanded, dewaxed, counterstained with fixed green, dehydrated and cleared, and then mounted, and observed under a DM4000 microscope.

Fruit traits and fruit set

At the mature harvest stage (full red and soft ripe fruits), 30 fruits were randomly selected for each treatment, and the longitudinal and transverse diameters were measured with a digital vernier caliper (accuracy 0.01 mm) (each measurement was repeated three times and the mean value was used). The single-fruit fresh weight was weighed with an electronic balance (accuracy 0.001 g). When approximately 80% of the plants were in flower, five plants were tagged per treatment, and record the total number of flowers (including full and semi-open flowers). After harvest, the number of normally matured fruits was recorded (excluding abscised flowers and undeveloped small fruits).

Fruit set rate (%) = number of fruits set/total number of flowers (fruits with more than 50 drupelets).

Malformed fruit rate (%) = number of deformed fruits/total number of fruits (malformed fruit was defined as fruits with irregular shape and single-fruit weight < 1 g).

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing

Among the tested MT concentrations, DM3 treatmentshowed the best overall performance under drought stress and was therefore selected for transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. Leaves from the CK, D, and DM3 treatments were collected at the squaring stage, the leaves tissues were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the Tiangen RNAprep Pure Polysaccharide Polyphenol Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purification, library preparation and sequencing and sequencing were performed by Shanghai Paison Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Sequencing was carried out using an Illumina-based next-generation sequencing platform.

Raw reads were filtered to remove adapter sequences, reads containing poly-N, and low-quality reads with fastp (v0.23.2). Clean reads were then assembled using Trinity (v2.13.2), and the longest transcript of each locus was defined as the unigene. Functional annotation of unigenes were functionally annotated based on BLAST (v2.13.0) searches against. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.30.1), and genes with |log2(fold change)|≥ 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) [21]. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using GOseq (v1.42.0) and KOBAS (v3.0.3) respectively. cSNP and InDel analyses were carried out using VarScan (v2.4.4).

RT-qPCR validation

To validate the RNA-seq results, leaves samples from CK, D, and DM3 plants at the squaring stage were collected. Three biological replicates were used for each treatment. The sampled tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C prior to RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and concentration were evaluated prior to reverse transcription. First-strand cDNA was synthesised from 1 μg of total RNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, China). Gene-specific primers were designed based on the transcript sequences using Primer 3, and the primer sequences were listed in Tab. S2. RT-qPCR was performed on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). The amplification procedure was as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. A melt-curve analysis was then performed to verify amplification specificity. Actin-2 was used as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were analysed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Metabolomic analysis

The red raspberry at the squaring stage leaves (CK, D and DM3) were sent to Shanghai Paison Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for non-target metabolome analysis. Samples were extracted with methanol containing 4 ppm 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine as the internal standard. The extracts were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before LC–MS analysis. Metabolites were analyzed using a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with an electrospray ionization source (ESI) in both positive and negative ion modes [22]. The capillary temperature was set at 325 °C. Full scans were performed at a resolution of 60,000, followed by HCD fragmentation at a resolution of 15,000. The top four ions were fragmented per cycle with dynamic exclusion applied to minimize redundant MS/MS data [23].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three biological replicates, and the data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were preprocessed using Microsoft Excel 2021 and statistically analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27. Differences among treatments were analysed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’ HSD test for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at three levels: P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001. Graphs were generated using OriginPro 2024.

Results

Phenotypic traits and leaf color

After 20 d of drought stress at the squaring stage, red raspberry plants showed marked leaf wilting and darkening, reduced true leaf formation and reproductive growth, and inhibited lateral root growth (Fig. 1A). Leaves color difference parameters increased under drought stress, with the largest changes observed in the lower leaves (Fig. 1B-D). MT pretreatment alleviated these drought-induced symptoms. Among the treatments, 150 μmol L⁻1 treatment showed the best overall performance, with improved leaves appearance, better maintenance of reproductive growth, and a more stable root system. However, the leaf color parameters in MT-treated plants did not fully recover to the control level, especially in the lower leaves.

Photosynthetic pigments and fruit quality

Chlorophyll a and total chlorophyll contents were significantly higher in CK than in all drought treatments (Fig. 2). Among the drought-treated plants, DM3 maintained the highest chlorophyll a level (P < 0.05).

Carotenoid content increased markedly under drought and was 60.0% higher in D than in CK. The lowest value was recorded in DM2 (0.15 mg·g⁻1). Chlorophyll b showed a similar pattern, with the highest level observed in DM1 (0.90 mg·g⁻1). Drought stress also reduced fruit development, lower fruit length, fruit diameter, single-fruit weight, and fruit set, together with a marked increase in malformed fruit rate (Tab. S3). MT pretreatment alleviated these drought-induced changes. DM3 showed the best overall performance, with fruit-related traits close to those of CK and a much lower malformed fruit rate than D.

Physiological responses to drought and MT pretreatment

Drought stress significantly inhibited the antioxidant enzyme system of red raspberry (Fig. 3A-C). Compared with CK, SOD activity in leaves and roots decreased under D, leaves CAT activity declined, and MDA content in leaves and roots increased significantly (P < 0.05). MT pretreatment modified these responses in an organ-dependent manner. In addition, POD activity in leaves and roots peaked in DM3, increasing by 37.2% and 46.5%, respectively, compared with D (P < 0.001). Leaves MDA content decreased with increasing MT concentration (Fig. 3D).

Osmoregulatory substances also showed clear organ-specific patterns (Fig. 3E–H). Proline and soluble sugar contents were highest in leaves and lowest in stems. Starch content was highest in stems and lowest in roots. Soluble protein content was highest in roots and lowest in stems. After MT pretreatment, proline content in stems reached the highest levels in DM3, with increases of 80.1%, compared to D (P < 0.01).

Photosynthetic characteristics and chlorophyll fluorescence

Photosynthetic parameters in D were significantly lower than those in CK and the MT-pretreated groups, except for Ci (P < 0.05). Under MT pretreatment, net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), and transpiration rate (E) increased first and then decreased with increasing MT concentration. Intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci) showed the opposite trend and reached its lowest value in DM3 (156.71 μmol mol⁻1) (Table 1). For chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, Fo, Fm, and Fv in D were 13.0%, 19.3%, and 5.9% higher than those in CK, respectively (Table 2). After MT pretreatment, Fo decreased, Fm and Fv increased. Fv/Fm showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing, with the highest value recorded in DM3 (0.78).

Endogenous hormones

Under drought stress, hormone contents in red raspberry showed clear organ-specific distribution patterns (Fig. 4A–F). IAA content was highest in leaves and lower in stems and roots (Fig. 4A). Compared with CK, leaves IAA increased in D, whereas IAA levels in stems and roots decreased. After MT pretreatment, IAA levels in stems and roots first increased and then decreased. The highest values were observed in DM3, which were 64.1% and 22.5% higher than those in D, respectively. ABA content was also highest in leaves (Fig. 4B). ABA levels in roots and leaves increased by 30.4% and 180.0%, respectively, compared with CK. Endogenous MT content was highest in leaves and drought further promoted its accumulation (Fig. 4C). Compared with D, endogenous MT content in DM3 increased significantly by 60.8% (P < 0.001).

No significant difference in JA content was detected among organs (Fig. 4D). ZR content was significantly higher in leaves than in roots and stems. After MT pretreatment, ZR continued to decline, whereas root ZR first increased and then decreased (Fig. 4E). In DM3, root ZR was 81.7% higher than that in D (P < 0.05). The IAA/ABA ratio in roots and stems was significantly reduced by drought stress (Fig. 4F). DM3 significantly increased this ratio by 70.7% in roots (P < 0.05) and 30.8% in stems (P < 0.01) compared with D. The leaf IAA/ABA ratio in DM3 was close to that in CK.

Leaves anatomical structure

Based on phenotypic and physiological responses of different organs, leaves showed the strongest response to drought stress at the squaring stage and were therefore selected for anatomical analysis. Compared with CK, drought stress significantly reduced the thickness of the upper epidermis, lower epidermis, palisade tissue, and spongy tissue (Table 3). Under MT pretreatment, all anatomical traits showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with increasing MT concentration. In D, spongy tissue cells were irregularly shaped and loosely arranged, with enlarged intercellular spaces (Fig. 5C).

RT-qPCR validation

To verify the RNA-seq data, 12 DEGs associated with drought tolerance-related were selected. RT-qPCR analysis was performed using leaves samples from the CK, D, and DM3. The expression trends detected by RT-qPCR were largely consistent. These results support the reliability of the RNA-seq data (Fig. S1).

Metabolomic profiling

To evaluate the effects of exogenous MT on metabolic changes in ‘Polka’ raspberry under drought stress, metabolomic profiling was performed. Leaves samples from the CK, D, DM3, and QC groups were analyzed by LC–MS. PCA score plots indicated separation among treatments. This pattern was further supported by OPLS-DA (Fig. S2). Differential metabolites were screened using VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 as the selection criteria. A total of 266, 237, and 256 differential metabolites were detected in CK_vs_D, CK_vs_DM3, and D_vs_DM3, respectively (Tab. S6).

KEGG enrichment analysis showed that differential metabolites in the three comparison groups were mainly enriched in pathways. These included secondary metabolite biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, plant hormone metabolism, and alkaloid biosynthesis. In D_vs_DM3, enriched pathways included α-linolenic acid metabolism, ornithine and lysine biosynthesis, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Fig. 9C). Among the 266 differential metabolites were identified in CK_vs_D, 143 upregulated and 123 downregulated. In CK_vs_DM3, 127 metabolites were upregulated and 110 were downregulated, and in D_vs_DM3, 124 were upregulated and 132 were downregulated (Fig. 9B). Representative differential metabolites included indole, abscisic acid, salicylic acid, glucose, chlorogenic acid, catechin, epicatechin, quercetin, and L-proline.

Discussion

MT improves plant growth and photosynthetic performance under drought stress

As a plant biostimulant, MT significantly promotes root and shoot growth. It also increases leaves area and biomass and enhances flowering and fruiting, thereby improving overall plant development [24]. Physiologically, MT improves water uptake and retention, optimizes stomatal conductance and transpiration, and markedly enhances photosynthetic efficiency [11]. Drought stress markedly reduced chlorophyll contents, impaired light energy utilization, and inhibited growth in red raspberry. These effects were accompanied by leaves curling, wrinkling, yellowing, and impaired true leaves development, thereby compromising photosynthesis [25]. MT alleviated these symptoms and maintaining leaves morphology closer to that of control (Fig. 1). Under drought stress, MT significantly increased roots length, roots surface area, and bud number. This is consistent with its promotive effect on cotton root development [25]. Additionally, MT has been reported to regulate seed germination, flowering, fruiting, roots growth, and leaves senescence [26]. MT pretreatment significantly alleviated drought-induced damage in red raspberry. However, color parameters related to leaves pigments did not fully recover to CK levels, especially under long-term stress LL [27]. This result indicated that MT only partially alleviated the persistent damage to the pigment system caused by long-term drought. At present, there are no reports on the effects of melatonin on color changes at different leaves positions. Meanwhile, melatonin pretreatment has been reported to reduce incidence of malformed fruits and improve fruit traits [28].

Photosystem II (PSII) is highly sensitive to abiotic stress. Drought severely disrupts PSII reaction centers and markedly reduces the maximum quantum yield of PSII (Fv/Fm) in red raspberry [29]. MT application significantly restored Fv/Fm. This indicated that MT may help alleviate PSII damage and maintain electron transport efficiency [30]. MT treatment significantly improved the photosynthetic parameters in red raspberry plants and restored them to levels close to the control. MT may be synthesized in chloroplasts and mitochondria. It can also protect the chloroplasts ultrastructure by maintaining grana stacking and thylakoid membrane integrity [31]. These effects, together with enhanced stomatal conductance [32], may contribute to improved photosynthetic performance under drought. Together, these effects enabled MT to markedly improve photosynthetic performance in drought-stressed red raspberry, consistent with observations in tomato [26]. MT enhances photosynthetic carbon fixation in drought-stressed red raspberry at the squaring stage by modulating key enzyme-encoding genes, including those for RUBISCO, FBA, FBP, SEBP, and RPI [33]. MT upregulated photosynthesis-related genes such as PNSL1, PNSB1, PNSL2, and LPE1. It also regulated tktA expression, which was associated with reduced fructose-6-phosphate accumulation. These transcriptional adjustments were consistent with observations reported in hickory under drought stress [34]. They may have contributed to the recovery of PSII efficiency and overall photosynthetic performance. Collectively, MT alleviated drought-induced inhibition of carbon metabolism and promoted recovery of photosynthetic capacity in red raspberry. These transcriptomic and metabolomic changes together suggested that MT alleviates drought-induced inhibition of photosynthesis. This effect may be associated with improved photosystem function and downstream carbon metabolism.

MT enhances antioxidant defense and maintains ROS homeostasis

MT regulates multiple physiological processes in plants under abiotic stress, including photosynthetic activity, biomass accumulation, osmotic adjustment, circadian regulation, membrane stability, and redox homeostasis [35]. Chloroplasts, the primary site of ROS generation, suffer structural damage from excessive ROS during stress [36]. MT functions as an important antioxidant regulator under stress [37]. It limits ROS accumulation, upregulates antioxidant gene expression, and enhances antioxidant enzyme activities, thereby improving stress tolerance. Excessive ROS under drought increases membrane permeability, disrupts cellular integrity, and promotes cell death [38]. MT further induces endogenous NO production, which activates ROS-scavenging enzymes [39]. SOD converts superoxide into H₂O₂, while CAT, POD, and APX are responsible for H₂O₂ decompose. Under MT treatment, the activities of these enzymes are often elevated, which may help reduce oxidative damage and preserve membrane integrity, as reported in sorghum seedlings [30, 40].

Under drought stress, MT application significantly increased SOD and CAT activities in red raspberry leaves at the squaring stage. At the same time, it suppressed POD activity and MDA accumulation. Comparable MDA reductions occurred in flooded tomato [41]. These changes facilitated effective ROS scavenging. This pattern is consistent with observations in maize [42], grape [43], and kiwifruit [14]. Trypan blue staining in maize further supported the role of MT in reducing cell death, likely through improved ROS removal. MT mitigates oxidative damage by enhancing ROS-scavenging efficiency or directly neutralizing ROS [44]. MT upregulated antioxidant genes including SOD1, sodC, cat-1, and cta1, while downregulating POD regulators PER29 and PER9. This suggests that MT may influence POD regulation through multiple pathways. MDA-associated genes showed no significant differential expression, suggesting additional regulatory layers (Fig. S1). In contrast, kiwifruit displayed pronounced changes in SOD, POD and MDA related genes [14], underscoring species-specific MT responses. Collectively, these results suggest that MT modulates antioxidant gene expression, enhances antioxidant enzyme activity, and contributes to the removal of excess ROS, thereby alleviating drought-induced oxidative stress in red raspberry.

The MT biosynthetic pathway involves several key enzymes. These include tryptophan decarboxylase (TDC), tryptophan 5-hydroxylase (T5H), serotonin N-acetyltransferase (SNAT), N-acetylserotonin methyltransferase (ASMT/HIOMT) and melatonin 2-hydroxylase (M2H) [45, 46]. These enzymes positively regulate stress responses by modulating antioxidant systems and ROS homeostasis [47]. Notably, direct protein interactions have been reported between melatonin synthesis enzymes, such as MeTDC2 and MeASMT2, and ascorbate peroxidase (MeAPX2) in cassava. These interactions activate APX and enhancing redox balance and stress tolerance [48]. Transient overexpression or silencing of these genes alters endogenous melatonin levels [49]. The precise interactions between melatonin biosynthetic and ROS-scavenging enzymes across diverse plant species and stress types remain to be elucidated.

ROS homeostasis depends on the coordinated action of enzymatic antioxidant systems and non-enzymatic systems. The non-enzymatic system helps maintain ROS balance through osmolytes and antioxidant metabolites. Together with enzymatic antioxidants, it constitutes a core defense mechanism against oxidative stress in plants [8]. Carbohydrate metabolism is central to plant adaptation across the life cycle. Under drought stress, plants accumulate compatible osmolytes to lower osmotic potential and maintain cell turgor, despite temporary disruption of normal metabolism [50]. MT modulates sugar metabolism, gluconeogenesis, transient starch catabolism, and transport, sustaining photosynthetic efficiency under stress [51]. In drought-stressed red raspberry at the squaring stage, MT application elevated soluble sugars, soluble proteins, and starch contents, likely through improved redox homeostasis [34].

MT upregulated glgc and PYG genes, which was associated with enhanced ADP-glucose and starch synthesis. At the same time, it downregulating beta-glucosidase, reducing cellobiose hydrolysis. Concurrently, D-Gluconate and 2-Dehydro-3-deoxy-D-gluconate levels increased, suggesting enhanced carbohydrate conversion and transport capacity [52]. Proline accumulation, which was pronounced under drought stress, decreased after MT treatment. This pattern was consistent with responses reported in cherry rootstocks and suggests that MT may regulate proline homeostasis through P5CS/P5CR-related genes and enzymes [53]. These adjustments may improve energy supply and osmotic adjustment. Species-specific differences in MT responses remain evident, as seen in contrasting outcomes in Mongolian grass [50]. Thus, coordinated changes in antioxidant gene expression and osmotic/metabolic intermediates suggest that MT improves drought tolerance through multiple mechanisms. These include enzymatic ROS scavenging as well as broader metabolic support for redox homeostasis and cellular stability.

MT regulates hormone balance and stress-responsive transcription under drought stress

Our results suggest that the protective effect of MT under drought stress is closely associated with coordinated changes in hormone-related pathways [47, 54]. This effect is unlikely to be explained by the action of a single hormone alone. the squaring stage, the transcriptomic data mainly supported the involvement of ABA, IAA, and JA related processes. This pattern indicates that MT reshapes hormonal balance under drought and helps maintain a functional equilibrium between stress defense and growth [55, 56].

Drought stress promoted ABA accumulation, consistent with the well-established role of ABA in water-deficit signaling [57]. However, the effect of MT on ABA should not be interpreted simply as suppression or promotion. Our data suggest that MT may help optimize ABA homeostasis under stress [58]. This may allow ABA-dependent defense responses to be maintained while reducing excessive physiological costs, such as chlorophyll degradation, accelerated senescence, and growth inhibition [15]. In this sense, MT seems to buffer the intensity of drought-induced hormonal disruption rather than merely shifting ABA in one direction. The ABA signaling pathway involves receptors (PYR/PYL/RCAR), SnRK2 kinases, and PP2C phosphatases [59]. Together, these components regulate downstream phosphorylation events and ABA-responsive transcription. In red raspberry, drought stress and exogenous MT both promoted ABA accumulation. However, MT’s effect on ABA homeostasis is context-dependent. In some studies, MT has been reported to suppress ABA biosynthesis and related gene expression under drought [60].

IAA-related regulation represents another important component of the MT response. Because tryptophan is a shared precursor for both MT and IAA, changes in tryptophan allocation may influence auxin metabolism under drought conditions [61]. It is consistent with observations in P. tunicoides [54]. In our dataset, auxin-related genes were closely associated with multiple transcription factor families. In particular, ARG7 and LAX2 showed significant correlations with members of the GRAS, bHLH, E2F/DP, SRS, MYB, NAC, and LBD families. These results suggest that MT may influence auxin transport and signaling through a broader transcriptional regulatory network, rather than through a simple linear pathway. Such regulation is likely important for maintaining developmental plasticity in drought-stressed leaves [54, 61]. The JA-related branch also appears to contribute to this process. Previous studies have shown that MT can interact with JA metabolism. Our results further support the view that hormone adjustment under MT treatment is not limited to ABA and IAA [25]. Instead, ABA IAA, and JA related processes seem to act together to coordinate stress signaling, membrane protection, and growth maintenance under drought conditions [62]. This integrated hormonal response is more consistent with the observed physiological recovery than any single-pathway explanation [24]. The hormone-related transcriptomic response to MT was also integrated into a broader transcriptional framework. MT treatment was associated with changes in several stress-responsive transcription factor families, including NAC, AP2/ERF, bHLH, MYB, HD-Zip, and bZIP [63]. Overexpression of ASMT and SNAT in Arabidopsis upregulated WRKY33 and defense genes (PR1, PR5, PDF1.2) [64]. Similarly, MT induced MiWRKY45 and related defenses in mango [65]. Notably, the close association between auxin-related genes and multiple TF families suggests that hormone signaling under MT treatment is tightly linked broader transcriptional reprogramming. Together, these findings indicate that MT-induced hormonal regulation operates within a broader transcriptional network. This network may connect hormonal adjustment with metabolic reprogramming and structural adaptation under drought stress.

The downstream consequences of this regulatory network were also evident at the anatomical level. Drought stress significantly inhibits the anatomical development of red raspberry leaves. This was reflected in reduced thickness of the upper and lower epidermis, palisade, and sponge tissue, as well as loose arrangement of spongy tissue [66]. Under the present conditions, 150 μmol/L MT pretreatment showed the best concentration-dependent reversal of this trend. This structural recovery is unlikely to result from a single pathway; rather, it likely reflects the combined effects of improved hormone homeostasis, antioxidant defense, and carbon-metabolic regulation. MT has been shown to upregulate key genes involved in photosynthesis, carbon metabolism, antioxidant defense, and hormone signaling [13, 15]. These coordinated molecular adjustments may promote palisade tissue thickening, improve light capture, enhance oxidative stress tolerance, increase spongy tissue compactness, and support osmotic adjustment under drought stress [67]. Comparable cross-pathway effects have also been reported in other species, including maize, tomato, and alfalfa. In these species, MT-mediated drought tolerance involved coordinated regulation of carbon metabolism, antioxidant systems, and ABA/IAA-related pathways [62, 68, 69].

Overall, our results suggest that MT improves drought tolerance in red raspberry through coordinated hormone crosstalk, transcriptional regulation, and leaves anatomical adaptation. ABA-, IAA-, and JA-related pathways appear to play central roles, while multiple transcription factor families help connect hormonal changes with metabolic and structural responses. Because this study focused on the squaring stage, these findings should be interpreted within that developmental context. DM3 was selected for omics analysis because it showed the strongest effect under our conditions. Further studies across growth stages and MT concentrations are needed to test the broader applicability of this regulatory framework.

Conclusions

This study showed that MT treatment improved drought tolerance in red raspberry at the squaring stage. This effect was associated with enhancing photosynthetic performance, promoting osmotic adjustment, strengthening antioxidant capacity, and modulating endogenous hormone homeostasis. These responses were accompanied by altered expression of PNSL3, PNSB4, glgc, PYG, SOD1, and CAT1, as well as changes in ABA, JA, SA, and IAA levels. Together, these physiological and molecular responses contributed to leaves anatomical. They also enhanced drought tolerance under the present water-deficit conditions (Fig. 12). Based on these findings, this study provided a framework for understanding the MT-mediated drought response in red raspberry. These results may offer useful reference information for improving drought tolerance in raspberry and related crops. Future research should combine efficient genetic transformation systems with gene-editing approaches to functionally validate key genes involved in drought tolerance. Such work would facilitate the application of these findings to stress-resistance breeding.
