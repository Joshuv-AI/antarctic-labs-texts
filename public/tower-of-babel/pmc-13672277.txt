Network pharmacology–driven identification of PTP1β and rational design of simufilam-based allosteric inhibitors for neuroprotection in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Vishnu Malakar, Rajeshkumar Raman, Antony Justin, Dhanabal SP, Dhritiman Roy, Chandi C Malakar, Dipen Purohit, Pratap Chand Mali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that predominantly affects elderly people and is characterized by cognitive decline and dementia. Protein tyrosine phosphatase-1B (PTP1B) is known to modulate pro-inflammatory mediators, and its overexpression in the brain has been implicated in AD-associated neuroinflammation. This study explores the mechanism of A20, A26, and co-crystal-designed compound targeting non-competitive allosteric PTP1B enzyme inhibition for AD therapy using network pharmacology and computational studies. Protein-protein interaction was designed for 128 active targets by STRING database and Cytoscape software. Further, active target-associated pathways analysed using ClueGO plugin Cytoscape software and the Reactome online public domain server. The non-competitive allosteric PTP1B inhibition through the design of Simufilam derivatives, with particular emphasis on A20, which is predicted to bind at the non-competitive allosteric site of the PTP1B protein (PDB ID: 1T49), involves α3, α6, and α7 helices that influence the WPD loop orientation. The proposed non-competitive allosteric binding mode is based on computational evidence and for that further experimental validation would be required to conclusively confirm binding of the designed compounds within this regulatory region. Using in-silico approaches, including molecular docking, identified A20 with the most favourable predicted binding energy score (−10.8 kcal/mol) compared to other derivatives. Molecular dynamics simulations further indicated structural stability of the PTP1B-A20, 26, and co-crystal complex, with consistent RMSD, RMSF, H-bond, Rg, and SASA. Principal component analysis suggested that A20 restricts conformational flexibility; hence MM-PBSA analysis displayed its stable binding free energies. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation characterized the polarity, stability, and reactivity of A20 studies supported its favourable electronic configuration. Overall, A20 displayed superior calculated binding energies, trajectory stability, and predicted drug-likeness, positioning it as a promising lead candidate for future empirical validation in AD research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13205-026-05086-2.Keywords: Simufilam derivatives, PTP1B inhibition, MD simulation 1000ns, MM/PBSA, DFT calculations

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is an irreversible neurodegenerative disorder characterised by the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) aggregates, insoluble tau protein-based neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), and associated pathology in the hippocampal regions of the brain (Reddy et al. 2019). The accumulation of aggregated proteins is neurotoxic, leading to AD symptoms including cognitive impairment, dementia, poor judgment, personality changes, memory loss, behavioural changes, and reduced ability to perform self-care (Zhao et al. 2022). Despite the increasing number of AD patients, the condition remains a significant burden on individuals and society. Therefore, there is an urgent need to better understand the pathophysiology of AD and to develop more effective therapeutic agents (Sharallah et al. 2026). Currently, several FDA-approved drugs are available for the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease, including symptomatic therapies and disease-modifying agents (Malakar et al. 2025b). These include acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChEIs) such as donepezil (Agrawal et al. 2018), galantamine (Zhang and Gordon 2018), and rivastigmine (Liu et al. 2019); the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDAR) antagonist memantine (Lu and Nasrallah 2018), displaying potential therapeutic changes in cognitive decline and daily routine by reinstituting neurotransmitter alteration. amyloid-beta (Aβ) inhibitors such as aducanumab (Brockmann et al. 2023); and lecanemab (Brockmann et al. 2023); and GV-971, a gut microbiota modulator approved only in China (Syed 2020). Pharmacotherapies for AD approved drugs mainly divided into two classes: traditional symptomatic therapy and newer disease-modifying treatments (DMTs). Symptomatic therapy anticipates with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) and the NMDA receptor antagonist memantine, which temporarily regulate cognitive activity and daily routines by reinstating neurotransmitter levels (Birks and Harvey 2006). Recently, anti-Aβ mAbs including lecanemab have gained approval, representing that targeted amyloid plaque clearance can halt clinical deficits in early-stage AD (van Dyck et al. 2023). However, practical clinical limitations endure across both classes. Symptomatic agents do not alter the underlying neurodegenerative disease progression, and their efficacy reduces as the disease advances (Birks and Harvey 2006). On the other hand, while anti-Aβ antibodies regulate progression, their clinical benefit is modest rather than curative. Their broader utility is further repressed by the risk of ARIA, expensive, needed intravenous drug administration, and lower BBB penetration (van Dyck et al. 2023). Recent studies evaluated that AD pathogenesis contributes intertwined mechanisms including amyloid accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, synaptic decline, and oxidative stress single-target agents scarcely display comprehensive therapeutic control (Cummings et al. 2023). There is therefore a strong rationale study for exploring alternative therapeutic strategies, specifically multi-target small molecules and drug delivery systems competent to addressing multiple central pathological molecular targets simultaneously. However, the magnitude of their clinical benefit remains limited, as they modestly slow cognitive and functional impairments rather than reversing or halting disease progression. AChEIs show symptomatic cognitive enhancement, improve cognitive function and activities of daily living (ADLs) (Fan et al. 2022). Traditionally, FDA-approved AD treatments—specifically AChEIs and memantine displayed strictly symptomatic treatment without changing the underlying of disease cascade (Birks and Harvey 2006). The therapeutic landscape has since evolved with the FDA approvals of anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies, such as lecanemab and donanemab, which marked the arrival of disease-modifying therapeutic approach capable of clearing Aβ plaques from the AD patients (van Dyck et al. 2023; Sims et al. 2023). However, the clinical impact of these disease-modifying therapies remains a subject of active debate. While phase 3 trials evaluated statistically significant depletions in cognitive decline among early-stage AD patients, the absolute treatment effects are modest (van Dyck et al. 2023; Sims et al. 2023). Additionally, their broader implementation is restricted by safety risks notably ARIA and expensive, requirement for daily basis MRI monitoring, and intravenous delivery demands (Knopman et al. 2021; van Dyck et al. 2023). Thus, while disease modification is now part of clinical practice, there demands immediate attention toward alternative small-molecule and multi-targeted therapeutic approach. The combination of memantine and donepezil provides the greatest benefit in case of moderate to severe AD patients (Singh et al. 2024). Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies may reduce amyloid plaques and prevent disease progression. The major limitation of current anti-Alzheimer’s drugs is that they primarily manage symptoms rather than halt or reverse disease progression (Ortiz-Islas et al. 2025). Therefore, alternative therapeutic strategies remain necessary because, despite decades of research, currently available treatments provide only modest clinical benefits. AChEIs enhance acetylcholine levels at the synapse and are referred to as cognitive enhancers. However, overdose may cause cardiovascular effects such as bradycardia and cardiac muscle abnormalities, as well as other adverse effects, including bronchorrhea, seizures, neuromuscular weakness, and paralysis (Thornton and Crane 2020). Memantine, which has low to moderate affinity for N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDARs), is sometimes considered less effective because it can inhibit physiological glutamate signalling necessary for normal synaptic function (Kaushik et al. 2026a; b). Its reported adverse effects include dizziness, confusion, dyspnea, and headache (Olivares et al. 2012). Aβ inhibitors may induce amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), which include brain edema and microhaemorrhage, as well as other associated symptoms such as delirium, dizziness, confusion, and altered mental status (Ali et al. 2022). GV-971, an oligosaccharide-based drug extracted from marine brown algae, has been reported to improve cognitive performance in select clinical cohorts of patients with mild to moderate AD (Wang et al. 2019). AChEIs remain the first-line symptomatic treatment for mild to moderate Alzheimer’s disease. Clinical trials demonstrated modest but statistically significant therapeutic benefits, with mean ADAS-Cog enhancements of 2–4 points compared to placebo groups and cognitive improvements for 6–12 months. Recent studies have demonstrated slower cognitive deficits (0.2–1.4 points/year MMSE loss vs. 1.07–3.4 points/year untreated) and 27–42% reduction in mortality. These therapeutic drugs are recommended in major clinical guidelines and retain therapeutic relevance because of their identified efficacy, cheaper, and widespread availability. Common adverse effects such as nausea, diarrhea, and vomiting, that is typically dose-dependent and manageable through gradual dose reduction (Gao et al. 2024). Growing evidence reports that brain metabolic dyshomeostasis plays a significant role in AD progression. Alterations in cerebral glucose utilization and glucose uptake may precede cognitive decline by several decades, suggesting that brain hypometabolism plays an important role in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis. Furthermore, systemic metabolic disease, including obesity and type-2 diabetes (Malakar et al. 2026d), are well-established risk factors for AD. These several factors are linked with chronic neuroinflammation, mitochondrial impairment, and insulin resistance, all these parameters may heighten AD progression (Cen et al. 2025).

Comparative analysis indicates that PTP1B represents a more promising therapeutic target for AD than several other molecular targets currently under investigation. BACE1 inhibitors have largely failed in clinical trials due to psychiatric effects, severe cognitive decline, and hepatic toxicities (Egan et al. 2019; Hampel et al. 2021), GSK-3β inhibition carries a narrow therapeutic window and regulate Wnt molecular pathways (Lauretti et al. 2020), CDK5 shows paradoxical effects depending on disease stage (Liu et al. 2016), and both tau- and TREM2-directed approaches remain limited to targeting downstream consequences rather than the upstream drivers of pathology (Krasemann et al. 2017; Congdon et al. 2023), and demonstrate strong blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration. PTP1B acts as negative regulator of IRS-1 and receptor tyrosine kinase receptor (Rahman et al. 2019). Preclinical evidence suggests that PTP1B inhibition offers a multi-target strategy capable of modulating central insulin signaling, reduces microglial Src/NF-κB-driven neuroinflammation, and indirectly normalizes overactive kinases such as GSK-3β, without the toxicity associated with directly knocking out GSK-3β catalytic activity (Combs et al. 2001). Taken together, this positions PTP1B inhibition as a more comprehensive, disease-modifying strategy than single-pathway approaches that have so far struggled to translate into clinical success.

PTP1B, also known as protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 1 (PTPN1), is a member of the protein tyrosine phosphatase (PTP) subfamily and plays a pivotal role in the negative regulation of insulin and leptin signalling pathways (Malakar et al. 2025b). It dephosphorylates insulin receptor substrates and downregulates the JAK2 signalling pathway (Zhao et al. 2022). PTP1B-null mice are resistant to obesity induced by either leptin gene deletion or a high-fat diet. The leptin signalling pathway has also been identified as a potential neuroprotective target in AD (Gomes et al. 2014; Carbone et al. 2024). PTP1B negatively regulates insulin receptor signalling and contributes to insulin resistance. Additionally, PTP1B directly regulates leptin receptors in hippocampal neurons (Cen et al. 2025). PTP1B deletion or genetic inhibition enhances insulin and leptin signalling and regulates glucose homeostasis in obesity and type-2 diabetes model (Malakar et al. 2026b). Moreover, these molecular pathways are associated with the modulation of cognitive impairment, dementia, and synaptic plasticity. PTP1B regulates tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) and nSOC pathways, both of which are linked to synaptic plasticity (Akhtar and Sah 2020). PTP1B also downregulates brain-derived neurotropic factor (BDNF) signalling by dephosphorylating TrkB (Ozek et al. 2014). In AD, PTP1B activity is further increased by proinflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-1β present in cortical microglia (Pei et al. 1994) Fig. 1. Recent studies have revealed that PTP1B may act as a positive modulator of neuroinflammation (Song et al. 2016). PTP1B has emerged as an important positive regulator of neuroinflammation (Malakar et al. 2026d). In activated microglia, increased PTP1B expression stimulates Src kinase activation by dephosphorylation of its inhibitory tyrosine residue, resulting in potentiating NF-κB signalling and increased release of pro-inflammatory mediators including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, iNOS, and COX-2 (Sharallah et al. 2026). Pharmacological inhibition of PTP1B diminished microglial stimulation and neuroinflammatory responses in both cell-based studies and animal models, demonstrating that PTP1B contributes to the progression of AD by inflammatory mechanisms (Song et al. 2016). However, several PTP1B inhibitors have been withdrawn from clinical trials due to limited efficacy, organ toxicities, and the large molecular weight of the compounds (Singh et al. 2022). Simufilam disrupts the pathological interaction between soluble Aβ42 and α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (α7nAChR) by restoring the native conformation of filamin A. Filamin A abnormally connects to α7nAChR and several neuroinflammatory targets in AD, and Simufilam disrupts these pathological interactions (Wang et al. 2023a). Simufilam has also been shown to regulate insulin and leptin receptors signalling, reduce hyperactivation of the mTOR signaling pathway (Wang et al. 2023b). In AD preclinical models, Simufilam disrupts hyperphosphorylation of tau protein, Aβ aggregation, NFT lesions, production of neuroinflammatory cytokines, while restoring neuronal function and reducing synaptic degeneration (Wang et al. 2017). In a Phase I clinical trial, Simufilam was evaluated in healthy volunteers to assess its pharmacokinetic profile and safety. The study also demonstrated restoration of CSF and blood lymphocyte biomarkers (Wang et al. 2020). A succeeding 28-day placebo Phase-II clinical study result outcome in mild to moderate AD patients displayed improvement of CSF biomarker vs. placebo. The molecular design strategy aimed to retain the favorable CNS drug-like properties of Simufilam while introducing structural modifications capable of improving functional group interactions within the PTP1B non-competitive allosteric site, thereby providing a new avenue for the designing of potential disease-modifying Simufilam agents against AD. Additionally, the cell line studies reported that lupeol and betulinic acid reduce the PTP1B function enhanced by TNF-α activity in hippocampal neurons. Growing evidence reported that natural flavonoids isolated form the Broussonetia papyrifera plant demonstrated PTP1B inhibition (Chen et al. 2002). Although, quercetin-3-O-β-D-glucuronide and myricetin-3-O-β-D-glucuronide, extracted from Cyclocarya paliurus leaves displayed significant PTP1B inhibition with significant IC50 value 7.39 µg/mL and 9.47 µg/mL (Zhang et al. 2010).

Computational studies may play a significant role in recognizing potent PTP1B inhibitors for diabetes, cancer, obesity, and AD therapy, hence further research is still needed to validate their therapeutic potential. These data highlight PTP1B as an important therapeutic molecular target in AD due to its contribution to synaptic dysfunction, neuronal loss, and metabolic overload (Patel et al. 2026; Jindal and Rai 2026; Abubakar et al. 2026). Simufilam, a drug developed by Cassava Sciences, was designed to target aberrant filamin A (FLNA) in AD and progressed to Phase III clinical evaluation (NCT05026177). Simufilam Phase III failure does not necessarily invalidate the underlying scaffold. The lack of clinical efficacy may be attributed to several factors including bioavailability limitations, off-target effects, and trial-related challenges rather than the scaffold itself (Srivastava et al. 2026). The trial was ultimately discontinued due to insufficient efficacy despite favourable safety and pharmacokinetic profiles. Based on the reported neuroprotective effects of Simufilam and the therapeutic relevance of PTP1B inhibition in AD, we hypothesized that rational structural modification of the Simufilam scaffold could yield novel allosteric PTP1B inhibitors with enhanced binding affinity, improved non-competitive allosteric-site selectivity, and favorable pharmacokinetic properties. We further anticipated that optimization of key pharmacophoric features would strengthen key molecular interactions with critical residues in the PTP1B non-competitive allosteric site, hypothesized to enhance predicted binding interactions and allosteric inhibition.

Given its compact, brain-penetrant spirocyclic scaffold, Simufilam represents a promising chemical starting point for the development of PTP1B inhibitors. Unlike previously reported PTP1B inhibitors, including the active-site inhibitors TCS401 and CPT157633 and the allosteric inhibitors Ertiprotafib and Trodusquemine (Kumar et al. 2020), Simufilam was originally developed for Alzheimer’s disease and possesses favorable CNS drug-like properties together with a chemically tractable scaffold suitable for structural optimization (Nand Rai 2026). Although active-site inhibitors often face challenges related to selectivity and pharmacokinetic properties, allosteric inhibition offers improved selectivity by targeting less conserved regulatory regions (Liu et al. 2022). These features support the rationale for selecting Simufilam as the parent scaffold for designing novel PTP1B-targeted therapeutics for AD.

In this study, 52 Simufilam derivatives were rationally designed through structural modifications at the R1 and R2 positions and evaluated as potential allosteric PTP1B inhibitors. Network pharmacology was employed to explore the therapeutic relevance of PTP1B, followed by molecular docking against the non-competitive allosteric site (PDB ID: 1T49) to evaluate binding affinity and key residue interactions. The lead compounds (A20, A26, and the co-crystal ligand) were further investigated using ADMET prediction, molecular dynamics simulations, PCA, MM/PBSA, DFT, and NBO analyses to assess their drug-likeness, binding stability, and electronic properties. This integrated computational workflow aimed to identify optimized Simufilam derivatives with improved binding characteristics and favorable pharmacokinetic properties as potential therapeutic candidates for AD.

Materials and methods

Rationale for structure-based drug design

Simufilam analogues designed with higher potency, efficacy, selectivity, and central nervous system (CNS) exposure, a systematic medicinal chemistry strategy was structured on the basic core 1,3,8-triazaspiro [4.5] decan-4-one scaffold (Wang et al. 2012). The central spiro core imposes a rigid 3D geometry that minimizes entropic penalty upon binding, while incorporating targeted steric bulk optimizes hydrophobic contacts within the active site (Carreira and Fessard 2014). The N-benzyl group at position 1 was maintained as an obligatory lipophilic anchor domain to keep baseline π-π hydrophobic amino acid interactions within the active binding pocket (Wang et al. 2012; Carreira and Fessard 2014). Central core optimization mainly focused on modifying the R1 and R2 positions: R1 Modification methods: The basic piperidine nitrogen functional group at R1 forms ionic bridge interact with conserved carboxylate amino acid residues in the active binding pocket (Wang et al. 2012). However, highly basic tertiary amines (pKa > 9.0) often restrict passive BBB permeability and excessive hepatic clearance through cytochrome P450-mediated N-dealkylation (Pajouhesh and Lenz 2005; Wager et al. 2010). Substitutions at R1 moiety were structured to reduced amine basicity, tune lipophilicity (log D7.4 maintained between 2.0 and 3.5), and sterically designed the nitrogen functional group against metabolic degradation (Pajouhesh and Lenz 2005; Meanwell 2011). R2 changes Strategy: Derivatization at the imidazolidinone core R2 nitrogen was engineered to probe an adjacent, under-utilized binding pocket (Wang et al. 2012; Carreira and Fessard 2014). By assorting steric bulk and H-bonding capabilities at R2, we aimed to optimize complementary van der Waals residue contacts, increase molecular target affinity, and enhance selectivity over off-target receptors (Wager et al. 2010; Meanwell 2011).

Screening of design compounds

In this work, a set of 52 Simufilam derivatives (SDs) (Table 1) were designed and evaluated for non-competitive allosteric site of the PTP1B enzyme (PDB ID: 1T49) enzyme. A library of 52 SDs was generated by introducing several substituents at the R₁ and R₂ positions while maintaining the parent compound. These positions were selected to enable structural diversification without altering the parent scaffold. Diverse substituents with varying steric, electronic, and hydrophobic properties, including alkyl, halo-substituted phenyl, methoxy-substituted phenyl, heteroaryl, and H-bond donor or acceptor functional groups, were incorporated to explore their impacts on predicted ligand–protein interactions. The molecular design was guided by computational evaluation by molecular docking and drug-likeness assessment. A rational, structure-based drug design strategy was adopted on target identification, as well as structure- and ligand-based strategies. These 52 derivatives were not arbitrarily selected, but were designed by a rational structure-based approach to explore key chemical variations around the parent compound while maintaining synthetic feasibility and enabling systematic comparison of predicted structure–activity relationship (SAR) trends. This designed library allowed us to balance novelty, chemical diversity, and synthetic feasibility, rather than generating an excessively large and less interpretable compound dataset. Network pharmacology

Network pharmacology (NP) was conducted to analyze potential targets of designed compounds (A20, A26) and co-crystal with protein tyrosine phosphorylation-based targets. Targets with a prediction probability ≥ 0.1 were retained, duplicate entries were removed, and only human (Homo sapiens) proteins were included for further analysis. Protein-drug interactions of the 3 compounds correspond to 233 active targets associated with AD pathogenesis identified by the Swiss Target prediction (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) online server, disease-related targets were downloaded from several open databases including DisGeNET (https://disgenet.com/), GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), and GeDiPNet (https://gedipnet.bicnirrh.res.in) (Grijaldo et al. 2023). The protein-protein interactions (PPI) were analysed by using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) online server, target associated pathways were mapped out by Cytoscape 3.10.4 software. Overlapping targets between compound prediction sets and disease profiles were mapped using Venn diagrams. Top-ranked targets were screened out using node degree (total number of nodes bound with neighbours), betweenness (shortest path distance between node pairs), and closeness (shortest distance between node-node) (Malakar et al. 2025c).

Selection of protein structure

Several researchers have focused on the development of potent drugs or pharmaceutical products for the treatment of AD and the mitigation of AD-related symptoms. Using X-ray crystallography and other tools such as cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM), numerous co-crystal structures of AD-related genes have been identified and deposited in the protein data bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/) with specific PDB IDs (Ghahremanian et al. 2022). PDB ID 1T49 was selected for molecular docking studies because it consists PTP1B in complex with a known allosteric inhibitor, thereby providing a structurally validated non-competitive allosteric site. Compared with other known PTP1B crystal structures, 1T49 captures a ligand-induced conformation of the non-competitive allosteric site which is particularly suitable for the screening and optimization of allosteric inhibitors. The protein with PDB ID: 1T49 was downloaded and prepared using AutoDock software (http://autodock.scripps.edu/) software (Morris et al. 2009).

The preparation process involved the removal of unwanted chains, co-crystallized ligands, water molecules, and metal ions, followed by the addition of polar/non-polar hydrogens and Gasteiger charges. The prepared protein was then analysed with Discovery Studio Visualizer, which was used to identify the active catalytic binding pocket (Baroroh SSi, MBiotek, et al. 2023). For protein–ligand docking, the.pdbqt file was opened in the visualizer, and the ‘Define and Edit Binding Site’ tool was employed to determine the binding pocket. By using AutoDock Vina, molecular docking was performed using a grid box centered on the known non-competitive allosteric site of PTP1B located at the interface of helices α3, α6, and α7. This region, which encompasses the α7 helix regulatory site, was selected because allosteric inhibition of PTP1B has been shown to stabilize inactive conformations and provides improved selectivity compared with targeting the highly conserved catalytic site. The binding site 3-dimensional (3D) coordinates were defined as X = 45.90, Y = 17.90, Z = 15.17 to ensure site-specific docking coordinates. These coordinates corresponded to the active binding region for PTP1B (Malakar et al. 2025a).

Molecular docking studies

Molecular docking analysis was performed using AutoDock software (Eberhardt et al. 2021). Docking was carried out on a library of 52 SDs. Initially, a compound library file was generated; throughout the configuration file containing the required docking variables for complex interactions was prepared (Ni et al. 2024). AutoDock Vina supports Perl scripting for automated docking, which facilitates protein and ligand preparation, molecular docking, dynamics simulation, and result analysis (Sarkar et al. 2024). The docking step was executed using a Vina Perl script, which integrates the configuration file via the command prompt (Aslam Mohd, Malakar, and Nand, 2025). Additionally, protein-ligand 2D interaction maps and docking validation, including the highest binding scores and RMSD values, were analysed using Discovery Studio (Valdés-Tresanco et al. 2020).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2020 (Makarewicz and Kaźmierkiewicz 2013; Abraham et al. 2015) to investigate the physical interactions of the ligand-protein complex in a biophysical environment. Topology files for the protein-ligand complex were generated using the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF), CHARM27, and Swiss Param (http://www.swissparam.ch/). All simulations were carried out in the Ubuntu Linux environment (Malakar et al. 2025c). The system was solvated using the TIP3P water model in a 1.0 nm triclinic box and neutralized with Na+ and Cl− ions (Bjelkmar et al. 2010). Energy minimization was followed by equilibration under NVT and NPT ensembles at 300K and 1 bar, after which production runs were extended to 1000 ns. Simulation trajectories analysis was analysed using the GROMACS package, including root mean square deviation (RMSD) (Malakar et al. 2025a), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), hydrogen bond (H-bond) analysis, and ion pair interactions (Ishak et al. 2017). RMSD was calculated to assess equilibration and structural stability of the protein–ligand complex over 1000 ns (Law et al. 2005). RMSF was assessed to analyze the local complex fluctuations in atomic positions at the active residue level during the molecular simulation (Martínez 2015). Rg was analyzed to evaluate structural rigid compactness, where lower Rg values represent a higher compact complex, and higher values reflect denote increased flexibility (Lobanov et al. 2008). Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) and center of mass (CoM) were also evaluated at 1000 ns.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

The MD simulation trajectory files were initially centered and transformed for PCA analysis using the GROMACS analysis tool. The analysis was conducted in two steps: construction of covariance matrix and diagonalisation of the resulting matrix (Malakar et al. 2025c). The “gmx covar” command was used to compute the covariance matrix and principal vectors, while the “gmx anaeig” command was applied to analyse the eigenvectors and calculate the PCA data. The orientation and dominant motions of the protein-ligand complex were described by the first two eigenvectors (EV1 and EV2), which represents the principal modes of motion (Roy and Anbarasu 2025). PCA was conducted on the Cα atoms of PTP1B using the MD trajectories. Before analysis, all frames were least-squares fitted to the reference structure to decrease overall translational and rotational native or complex orientations. The covariance matrix of Cα atomic positional fluctuations was evaluated to obtain the eigenvectors and eigenvalues demonstrating the collective motions of the native protein and complex. The conformational space during the simulation was evaluated by projecting the trajectories onto the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) axis.

Molecular mechanics poisson-boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) analysis

The MM-PBSA method is a computational approach used to integrate MD simulations with the estimation of free binding energies of protein-ligand complexes. The results provide several energy components, including total binding energies (GTOTAL). The gmx_MMPBSA tool, implemented within the GROMACS package, was used to calculate the binding free energies. MM-PBSA calculations were performed after removing unwanted water molecules and applying periodic boundary conditions for PTP1B in complex with multiple SD-designed molecules. The molecular geometry of the complexes was further optimized through energy minimization using molecular mechanics (Roy and Anbarasu 2025). MM/PBSA calculations were conducted using the g_mmpbsa package. A total of 1000 snapshots extracted from the MD trajectory were used for free-energy calculation. The binding free energy was decomposed into van der Waals, electrostatic, polar solvation, and nonpolar solvation components. Polar solvation energies were computed using the APBS Poisson–Boltzmann solver, while nonpolar contributions were identified from solvent-accessible surface area (SASA). Entropic contributions were not included in the calculations, and the resulting MM-PBSA energies were used for relative comparison of complex binding affinities.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

DFT calculations were performed using Gaussian 16 for ligands exhibiting the lowest binding energy. Geometry optimization and frequency calculations were carried out using the B3LYP method with the 6–311 + G basis set (Malakar et al. 2026c). Geometric symmetry was relaxed, and the quadratically convergent SCF method with default solvation parameters was applied. Additional DFT calculations were conducted at the B3LYP/6–31G* level using geometries optimized at the B3LYP/6–311G* with Gaussian 94 program modules (Medimagh et al. 2023). These calculations provided insights into intramolecular stabilization and molecular interactions that may contribute to the biological activity of the molecules (Shekarkhand et al. 2022).

Molecular docking and its interacting residues

Molecular docking validation was performed using the AutoDock Vina tool Fig. 7. The co-crystallized ligand from PDB ID 1T49 was extracted and re-docked into the non-competitive allosteric site to validate the docking protocol. The superposition of the re-docked pose with the experimentally determined crystallographic pose yielded an RMSD of 0.8 Å, confirming the reliability of the docking methodology for reproducing the native binding orientation. Additional, non-covalent residues interaction patterns were cross-validated using the Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) webserver. PLIP analysis demonstrated that the re-docked complex reproduced all key native non-covalent interactions, such as H-bonding, hydrophobic contacts, π-π stacking, halogen contacts, and water-bridge coordinates within the active binding pocket (Schake et al. 2025). To ensure the computational protocol reliably distinguished true active leads from non-specific binders, hit selection was governed by a multi-tiered consensus strategy. Designed Simufilam derivatives (SDs) (Table 2) were evaluated against the PTP1B non-competitive allosteric site, with calculated Gibbs free energy (ΔG, kcal/mol) reflecting thermodynamic complex stability. Ligand inhibition constants (Ki) were derived via ΔG = RTlnKi, where R = 1.987 cal/Kmol and T = 310.15 K (Jain and Bharti 2025). Among the 52 evaluated SDs, A20 and A26 emerged as the top-scoring leads, displaying favourable binding affinities of −10.8 kcal/mol and −9.5 kcal/mol, respectively (Table 2, Table 3). The significantly lower binding energy of A20 indicates a highly stable protein-ligand complex compared to A26. Both top candidates successfully engaged key residues across the catalytic and adjacent non-competitive allosteric sites, satisfying all consensus criteria and confirming their potential as selective inhibitors. Detailed 2D and 3D interaction diagrams for the co-crystal control (Figure S1A), A20 (Figure S1B), and A26 (Figure S1C) are provided in Figure S1. Given these challenges, recent research has shifted toward exploring both natural and synthetic scaffolds as sources of selective PTP1B inhibitors. Saeed et al. indicated that alkannin, a naturally occurring naphthoquinone compound derived from the Boraginaceae family, possesses PTP1B inhibitory activity with potential therapeutic relevance to diabetes. Computational analysis displayed favourable binding affinity for the complex of alkannin and PTP1B, with a binding energy of −8.1 kcal/mol. Key interacting residues included hydrogen bond contributors (Val297, Ala299, Leu300, as well as Ser302) and hydrophobic residues (Tyr20, Tyr48, Tyr79, Tyr111, Tyr219, Val47, Val297, Phe122, Cys298, Ala299, Leu300, and Leu301), which were identified in the protein-ligand complex. Biological evaluation result outcome showed that alkannin inhibits PTP1B with an IC50 value (19.47 µM). Enzyme kinetics revealed that alkannin exerts PTP1B and aldose reductase (AR) inhibition, denoting that it can bind not only with active residues but also with additional non-competitive allosteric sites of PTP1B (Saeed et al. 2024). Jin et al. identified targeting PTP1B with betulinic acid and lupeol, found that betulinic acid exhibited higher allosteric inhibition, with a binding affinity −12.2 kcal/mol and −10.6 kcal/mol for lupeol. The key interacting residues included hydrophobic amino acids (Ala189, Leu192, Phe196, Phe280, Trp291, and Leu294) and hydrogen bond-forming residues (Asn193, Glu276, and Gln288), which are located at the alpha seven (α7) helix. Biological result showed that lupeol and betulinic acid inhibited PTP1B with IC50 values 5.6 µM and 1.5 µM, respectively (Jin et al. 2016). These data are particularly important, as prior results have identified that the α7 helix as a regulatory non-competitive allosteric site of PTP1B (Choy et al. 2017a). Ligand binding at this region has been shown to regulate conformational alterations that may destabilize the WPD loop, hence suppress enzymatic function while provide higher selectivity compared to inhibitors of the highly conserved binding site. Although, previous reports have recognized the C-site, where compounds including difluoromethylphosphonic (DFMP) acid derivatives interact, as another different non-competitive allosteric site identified adjacent to the catalytic pocket that involves to selective enzymatic inhibition. Zhang et al., reported that DFMP acid derivatives, compounds 7 and 8, reduced PTP1B with binding affinities −11.74 and −9.06 kcal/mol, respectively. Biological assay evaluated potent enzyme inhibition, with an IC50 value 7 nM for compound 7 and 90 nM for compound 8 (Zhang et al. 2017).

Mechanistic insights from protein-ligand interactions

Rationally designed SDs were molecular docked into the catalytic binding pocket of PTP1B (PDB ID: 1T49), revealing interactions that extend into non-competitive allosteric sites linked with the α3, α6, and α7 helices regions. These helices form part of the dynamic network which modulates the WPD loop regions, a structural element necessary for catalysis. The allosteric inhibitors examined in this study do not bind within the catalytic Tyr(P)-binding pocket. Instead, they bind to a separate non-competitive allosteric sites located mainly at the interface of helices α3, α6, and α7. Structural studies have displayed that binding at this catalytic site stabilizes the open, inactive conformation of PTP1B and attenuates the conformational changes required for WPD-loop closure, which is essential for catalytic activity. Thus, inhibition occurs indirectly through conformational regulation rather than through direct occupation of the catalytic site. Consistent with previous identifies by Choy et al. (Choy et al. 2017b), which displayed that conformational coupling within the α-helices and the WPD sites governs enzymatic function, the A20 compound was identified to regulate H-bond binding within this molecular network. Moreover, the H-bond binding of A20 with Asn193 and Glu276 are positioned in the structural network which couples α-helices to WPD sites mobility, rationalizing its identified ability to destabilize active loop closure (Table 3 and Figure S1B). Such changes are likely to differentiate the positioning of the catalytic residue Cys215 and destabilize WPD site closure, while impairing dephosphorylation. Additionally, inhibitor binding was found to restrict α3 helix rotation and alter the α7 helix, halt the formation of stabilizing H-bonds which normally favour a closed WPD loop conformation. In contrast, A26 found comparatively weaker interaction, but still occupied regions overlapping the catalytic and proximal non-competitive allosteric sites. A26 formed additional interactions with Leu192 and Gly277, active residues at the periphery catalytic binding pocket (Aslam Mohd, Malakar, and Nand, 2025), representing a weaker but broader engagement which may still perturb loop dynamics (Table 2 and Figure S1C). This dual involvement suggests that A26, while lesser potent, may retain inhibitory activity by partially perturbing WPD loop changes. Collectively, these outcomes suggest that A20 serves as a potent allosteric modulator by exploiting conformational cross-talk within the catalytic binding pocket and distal helical loop regions of PTP1B, while A26 acts as a supportive candidate with lower stability. These data and mechanistic deeper insights from molecular docking outcomes displayed the basis for carrying out further computational approach on A20 and A26, such as molecular simulations, PCA, MM/PBSA, and DFT analysis to strengthen and deepen the mechanistic insights of their inhibitory activity.

Molecular dynamics simulations

MD is a real-time simulation technique commonly used to evaluate conformational alterations/changes, stability, and interactions within the protein-ligand complexes (Kharwar et al. 2025). Conformational stability up to 1000 ns allows atomic-level alterations in geometry and coordinates to be analysed (Esmaili and Shahlaei 2015). Simulations were carried out for 1T49-Co-crystal, A20 and A26 ligand complexes to assess comparative stability.

The RMSD graph of PTP1B bound with A20, showing favourable binding affinity, is illustrated in Fig. 8A. The RMSD profiles of the Co-crystal and A20 complexes demonstrated high stability, with fluctuations observed with values of 5.6 nm at 75 ns, 2.3 nm at 187 ns, 7.7 nm at 47 ns, and 2.2 nm at 217 ns. Further, the protein-A26 complex revealed higher deviations between 0 and 431 ns, with values of 8.6 nm at 89 ns and 0.4 nm at 431 ns, respectively; moreover, the complex is highly stable at 1000 ns time duration. The RMSF plot revealed the residue-level deviations points across the 1T49-Co-crystal, A20, and A26 complexes, which is illustrated in Fig. 8B. The higher fluctuations in the co-crystal complex identified 0.6378 nm (atom 4588) and 0.038 nm (atom 3709), while the A20 and A26 complexes remained comparatively lower fluctuations (Lemkul 2024). Rg was evaluated for all complexes (1T49-Co-crystal, A20, and A26), illustrated in Fig. 8C. The higher and lower Rg values (nm) were found as follows: 1.905 at 557 ns, 1.84 at 246 ns, 2.02 at 941 ns, 1.93 at 536 ns, 1.95 at 286 ns, and 1.88 at 764 ns. H-bond calculation revealed that a more than three H-bonds were stabled over the 1000 ns simulation (Malakar et al. 2026a), additional supporting the stability of all complexes (Fig. 8D). Further, SASA (Fig. 8E) and CoM (Fig. 8F) results were analyzed to evaluate binding stability and examined the surface area of the complexes. Both SASA and CoM values remained stable over the 1000 ns simulation for the 1T49-Co-crystal, A20, and A26 complexes, revealing consistency with the native protein (1T49).

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA analysis was performed to examine the protein orientations influenced in the presence of ligand, which helps to interpret the confirmation from the dynamic trajectories. This analysis displays in-depth mechanistic insights in case of complexes and how they evolve over time duration and evaluates the most significant orientation patterns. Each point on the PCA graph denotes a snapshot from each simulation trajectories, projected on the first two principal components (eigenvectors) axis, which reveals the most stable orientations.

As displayed in Fig. 9A, the 1T49 occupies a minimal phase space within a molecule cluster, representing limited conformational alterations and higher complex stability without ligand. PCA results outcome further displayed that ligand binding poses enhances the phase space, making the 1T49 more flexible. Despite this increased flexibility, some variations in phase space were still observed across the complexes. For the 1T49-Co-crystal, A20, and A26 complexes, ligand binding significantly expanded the conformational space explored by the protein, as reflected in the broader distribution along the principal components, as shown in Fig. 9B. The A26 complex primarily moved along EV2, reaching a maximum value of 53 nm, whereas A20 complex was oriented along EV1, with a maximum value of 72 nm. The 1T49-A20 complex displayed intermediate behaviour in terms of phase space, structural changes, and stability (Kumari et al. 2021). PCA values calculated over 200 ns intervals for the 1T49-Co-crystal, A20, and A26 complexes, covering the entire 0–1000 ns simulation, are illustrated in Fig. 9C, Figure S2D, and S2E. MM-PBSA calculations were performed to evaluate the binding energetics of protein-ligand complexes 1T49-Co-crystal, A20, and A26. The binding free energy was computed over a 1000 ns MD simulation. The calculations included contributions from VDWAALS, EEL, and the GTOTAL. Its demonstrated ability to penetrate the BBB, together with its clinical advancement and neuroprotective activity, makes Simufilam an attractive lead basic scaffold for the design of potent derivatives. Natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis

The NBO calculation was conducted to gain mechanistic insights into the electronic configuration and bonding nature of the compounds. This calculation requires for studying symmetric and asymmetric molecules nature, as it displays accurate molecular insights on aromatic systems, specifically those consisting heterocyclic ring moieties (Shekarkhand et al. 2022). For the proposed A20 compound, NBO analysis was used to examine the molecular energy gap among the HOMO and LUMO, and to evaluate its residues interactions within the binding pocket, such as Tyr46, Ser216, Ala217, and Thr263. The data of this calculation are depicted in Figure S3.

NBO calculation of A20 displays a molecular network of intramolecular H-bonds (O/N···H distances 1.732–1.772 Å, with a lower contact at 2.107 Å) that pre-organizes the molecule into a compact, H-bond-stabilized orientation; a standard C–Cl bond length (1.719 Å) is also identified. The dominant donor–acceptor residues interactions are n(O/N) σ(N–H/C–H)* hyperconjugation and π-π* delocalization across carbonyl functional groups and aromatic ring fragments, which collectively stabilize the optimized compound geometry and increase electron delocalization. These properties are consistent with the frontier molecular orbital gap depicted for A20 (~5.1–5.2 eV) and its moderate dipole energy. In summary, NBO calculations indicate that intramolecular hydrogen bonding and donor–acceptor delocalization play a significant role in stabilizing A20’s molecular geometry and enhancing electronic delocalization, energetically favouring a pre-organized conformation for site binding. Conclusion

This in silico study revealed that the structural dynamics and binding affinity of designed compounds of Simufilam derivatives (A1–A52) targeting PTP1B as potential computational leads for AD therapy. The PTP1B molecular target analysed using network pharmacology to identify molecular interactions with active biological therapeutic targets. To explore feasible therapeutic benefits in preventing Alzheimer’s disease (AD) symptoms, with a series of Simufilam drug derivatives (A1–A52) was designed. Among these derivatives, A20 revealed the stronger molecular binding affinity (−10.8 kcal/mol) toward the PTP1B enzyme, highlighting its favorable computational ranking relative to other derivatives in the compounds. Molecular docking and simulations further examined that the PTP1B-A20 complex exhibited the highest binding stability at the non-competitive allosteric sites of PTP1B, while most of the other SDs derivatives induced conformational changes within the native protein (1T49). Remarkably, A20 maintained a maximally exposed conformation changes that reinforced its stable binding drug profile. PCA calculation displayed that PTP1B significant conformational flexibility in the absence of A20, enabling traversal of a broad conformational landscape. In contrast, simulated interactions with A20 were associated with reduced conformational flexibility, yielding a more compact calculated ensemble. Importantly, A20 residue interactions were identified to initiate higher conformational re-organization in the protein sequence, representing a profound stabilizing changes at the binding molecular interface. To elucidate the non-competitive inhibition mechanism of compound A20, its binding interactions were contextualized within the established allosteric regulatory framework of PTP1B (Wiesmann et al. 2004; Friedman et al. 2022). Catalytic activity in PTP1B requires dynamic motion of the flexible WPD loop (residues 177–185), which must transition from an “open” state to a “closed” conformation to correctly position catalytic Asp181 relative to active-site P-loop (Cys215–Arg221) for substrate cleavage (Brandão et al. 2012; Friedman et al. 2022). Computational docking demonstrates that compound A20 occupies a hydrophobic C-terminal non-competitive allosteric site situated approximately 20 Å from the active site, delineated by helices α3, α6, and α7 (Wiesmann et al. 2004). The o-chlorophenyl hydrazine tail at R1 position establishes hydrophobic contacts and halogen interactions with Leu192, Ala189, Phe280, and Leu281, while the spiro-hydantoin core anchors the compound within the surrounding helical framework. Occupancy of this site by A20 prevents the C-terminal α7 helix from adopting the packed, ordered conformation required to facilitate closure of the flexible WPD loop (Wiesmann et al. 2004; Krishnan et al. 2014). By locking the WPD loop in its open, inactive state, A20 decouples substrate binding from enzymatic cleavage, leading to a reduction in catalytic rate turnover (Kcat reduction). Because this non-competitive allosteric site is distinct from the highly conserved, positively charged active site, targeting this region provides a structural basis for high isoform selectivity and improved cellular permeability (Tonks 2006; Krishnan et al. 2014). Complementary DFT calculation further examined the electronic configuration of A20, reveals its higher electronic reactivity, stability, and polarity in association with active residues such as Tyr47, Ser216, Asp193, and Glu276. NBO calculation additionally revealed that intramolecular H-bonding and donor–acceptor delocalization involves to A20’s pre-organized and energetically suitable binding geometry. In silico ADMET drug profiling examined that A20 exhibits favorable blood-brain barrier permeability and solubility, it also exhibited certain molecular violations of Lipinski’s rule parameters, warranting further geometry optimization. Although the present study employed an integrated computational method contributing network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular simulations, principal component analysis, MM/PBSA, density functional theory, natural bond orbital, and ADMET prediction, several limitations have been acknowledged. The predicted binding affinities, interaction patterns, and pharmacokinetic properties are based on theoretical models and may not fully reflect biological behavior under physiological conditions. Furthermore, molecular docking and molecular simulations provide insights into protein–ligand interactions but cannot directly establish biological function, target selectivity, or therapeutic efficacy. Future development of A20 relies on systematic lead optimization. Synthetically accessible through a simple and concise two-step synthesis process, A20 targets the non-conserved α3/α6/α7 allosteric sites, providing a scaffold to guide target selectivity over homologous TCPTP in future experimental studies to prevent off-target effects. To improve metabolic half-life and reduce P450-mediated oxidation of the hydrazine cross-linker, bio-isosteric modifications (-CH2NH-, triazoles) will be examined. Additionally, formulating A20 into PLGA nanoparticles will enhance aqueous solubility and facilitate CNS drug delivery for upcoming in vitro and in vivo studies. However, the identified Simufilam derivatives should be considered potential lead compounds rather than confirmed therapeutic agents. Future studies involving in vitro PTP1B inhibition assays, cell-based experiments, pharmacokinetic analyses, and periclinal studies are required to confirm the computational findings and assess the clinical relevance of these compounds. Taken together, these theoretical findings highlight A20 as a computationally promising lead candidate with a strong molecular binding affinity, conformational stabilizing capability, favorable electronic configuration, and drug-likeness property, thereby underscoring its relevance as a potential candidate for therapeutic development against AD therapy.
