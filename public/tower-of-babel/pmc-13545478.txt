A Technique for the Generation of Mucosal and Acral Melanoma Cell Lines
Authors: Ishani Banik, Sugandha Basu, Meng Wang, Austin J. Escobar, Veronica Steri, Juan A. Camara Serrano, Paul Phojanakong, Noel Cruz Pacheco, Katy K. Tsai, Edwin A. Alvarez, Adil Daud, Ivan H. El‐Sayed, Anastasia Samarkina, Joy Xu, Jamie D. Aquino, Min Xiao, Meenhard Herlyn, Iwei Yeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMucosal and acral melanomas are rare subtypes of melanoma with unique clinical and biological features. A dearth of well‐established and characterized melanoma cell lines that reflect rare subtypes impedes research on these lethal diseases. We present a protocol developed by exploring various combinations of established techniques that we used to generate mucosal and acral cell lines from patient tissues and patient derived xenografts.

Introduction

Patients with mucosal and acral melanoma have worse outcomes than those with cutaneous melanoma, likely attributed in part to later stage at diagnosis (Sergi et al. 2023; Cho et al. 2021). Complete resection and wide negative margins are challenging to achieve at mucosal and acral sites, given their multifocal growth pattern and anatomic site constraints, contributing to a high recurrence rate following surgical management (Sergi et al. 2023; Goldemberg et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2024). Mucosal and acral melanomas also demonstrate a lower response rate than cutaneous melanomas to immune checkpoint blockade (Shoushtari et al. 2016; Klemen et al. 2020), potentially due to a lower burden of point mutations (Newell et al. 2019; Buchbinder et al. 2021; Hayward et al. 2017). The median survival of patients with metastatic mucosal melanoma treated with ipilimumab was 6.4 months; after anti‐PD‐1 therapy, it was 12.4 months (Shoushtari et al. 2016; Postow et al. 2013). While melanomas harboring BRAF mutations may respond to BRAF/MEK inhibitors, most acral and mucosal melanomas do not have BRAF mutations. KIT inhibitors have also been used to treat acral and mucosal melanomas due to the presence of activating KIT mutations in 10%–15% of cases, but only a minority of patients respond (Zhang et al. 2024).

There is an urgent clinical unmet need to investigate novel therapies given the poor response to therapy and ultimately poor outcomes for mucosal and acral melanoma patients. Extensive analyses of cutaneous melanoma cell lines have revealed meaningful insights on the underlying mechanism of cancer. There are > 2000 established melanoma cell lines of cutaneous origin making it the most extensively studied cancer (Rebecca et al. 2020). The acral and mucosal melanoma model catalog, freely available from the Melanoma Research Alliance (curemelanoma.org) provides a comprehensive list of existing cell lines and patient derived xenografts (PDXs). There exists a dearth of mucosal melanoma cell lines given that there are only 3 mucosal melanoma cell lines listed. While there are more acral melanoma cell lines available, they do not represent the full spectrum of genomic diversity in acral melanoma (Furney et al. 2012; Rubinfeld et al. 1997; Hu et al. 2021).

Protocols for melanoma cell line derivation commonly begin with mechanical or enzymatic dissociations followed by conditional enrichment steps to reduce fibroblast overgrowth. Early studies using explant and primary melanoma cultures highlighted fibroblast overgrowth as a major barrier to successful melanoma cell line establishment, often necessitating physical or selective approaches to enrich for tumor cells (Carey and Prunieras 1984). Initially, melanocytes were grown in media containing phorbol ester which promoted sustained cellular responses (Eisinger and Marko 1982). However, withdrawal of phorbol ester resulted in fibroblast contamination which was mitigated by geneticin treatment followed by a gradual increase in calcium concentration in the culture medium (Horikawa et al. 1996). Our protocol builds on these concepts but adds additional steps (e.g., low to high attachment transfer, nest‐based enrichment, etc.) to address the high stromal background and slower growth that we often encounter in mucosal and acral melanoma.

We present here a step‐by‐step guide to the establishment of melanoma cell lines from fresh tumor tissue harvested from a patient sample or patient derived xenograft (PDX) that we have used to generate mucosal and acral melanoma cell lines. We present options to culture whole tissue or dissociate the tissue in addition to providing recommendations on the type of culture medium, splitting techniques, passaging times, and verification of pure melanoma cultures. We focus on the developing morphology of melanoma cells, the color of culture medium, and their capacity to detach using either a pipette tip or trypsin to isolate true melanoma cells from non‐melanoma cells. This protocol avoids the use of synthetic basement membrane matrices or proprietary growth factor cocktails, reducing the material cost per generated line compared to 3D organoid protocols.

Step 2. Tissue Dissociation and Plating

In this step, we describe the processing of tumor tissue and seeding cell suspensions in tissue culture plates. Tumor tissue can either be dissociated (option 1) or fragmented before plating (option 2). We recommend the former. While laborious and time consuming, there will be a lower chance of contamination since the time to a pure population of melanoma cells is shorter. If the size of tumor tissue is larger than 2 cm3, both techniques may be performed in parallel.

The duration of option 1 is approximately 3 h.

Results

We successfully used this protocol to develop mucosal and acral melanoma cell lines. Our success rate was 40% from first passage PDX (2 of 5), 25% from well‐established PDX (1 of 4), and 22% from patient tissue (2 of 9), though our sample sizes are small. Failures were often due to fungal contamination. Furthermore, the cell lines we generated using our protocol can withstand repeated cycles of freezing and thawing and retain their morphology for more than 8 passages. We performed DNA sequencing of the cell lines we developed and confirmed the presence of pathogenic mutations that were clinically identified in the corresponding melanoma (Table 2).

Discussion

We describe a protocol for the establishment of melanoma cell lines from mucosal and acral tumors, which are historically underrepresented in existing model systems. Compared to cutaneous melanoma, these subtypes present unique technical challenges, including dense stromal components, slower intrinsic growth, and a higher propensity for fibroblast overgrowth during early culture. Prior studies of primary melanoma cultures have identified stromal contamination as a major barrier to successful cell line establishment, often necessitating selective or mechanical enrichment strategies (Carey and Prunieras 1984; Raaijmakers et al. 2015).

Our protocol builds on these foundational observations but introduces key modifications tailored to mucosal and acral melanoma. In particular, the incorporation of a low‐to‐high attachment transition exploits differences in anchorage dependence to selectively disadvantage stromal fibroblasts while preserving melanoma cells capable of suspension growth. In parallel, the identification and manual isolation of melanoma “nests” provides an orthogonal strategy to conventional differential trypsinization, enabling more precise separation of tumor cells from stromal backgrounds. Together, these approaches offer a practical alternative to traditional enrichment techniques and improve the efficiency of establishing melanoma monocultures from challenging specimens.

Recent advances in three‐dimensional organoid systems have expanded the toolkit for modeling oral mucosal melanoma and enable the study of tumor heterogeneity and therapeutic response in vitro (Sun et al. 2023). However, organoid models often require specialized matrices and growth factor cocktails, increasing cost and limiting accessibility. Furthermore, so far reports have been limited to mucosal melanoma of oral origin. In contrast, the two‐dimensional culture system described here relies on standard tissue culture reagents and instrumentation, facilitating broader adoption. Moreover, established cell lines remain highly tractable for downstream applications, including genetic manipulation, high‐throughput screening, and long‐term propagation through freeze–thaw cycles.

Our results demonstrate moderate success rates in establishing cell lines from both patient‐derived xenografts and primary patient tissues, although variability remains. Failures were most commonly attributable to contamination or insufficient tumor cell outgrowth, highlighting the importance of rapid processing and careful culture monitoring. The ability of derived cell lines to maintain morphology and retain pathogenic mutations from the parental tumor supports their utility as biologically relevant models of mucosal melanoma. We were able to apply our method to acral and mucosal melanoma isolated from various anatomic sites including the nasal cavity, vagina, and anorectum, demonstrating a generalized applicability of our protocol.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this protocol does not address intratumoral heterogeneity, and it is possible that specific subclonal populations are preferentially selected during in vitro culture. Second, prior therapies may influence the fitness and growth characteristics of tumor cells, introducing additional selection biases. Third, the relatively small sample size limits precise estimation of success rates across tumor types and anatomic sites. Despite these limitations, this protocol provides a practical and scalable framework for generating melanoma cell lines from rare and understudied subtypes. By lowering technical and cost barriers, it may facilitate the expansion of available model systems and support further biological and therapeutic investigations in mucosal and acral melanoma.
