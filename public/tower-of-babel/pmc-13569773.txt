Immunometabolic Modulation in Obese Asthma: How GLP-1 Receptor Agonists Could Influence Systemic and Pulmonary Immune Phenotypes
Authors: Helen O’Brien, Adam J. Byrne, Alessandro N. Franciosi, Marcus W. Butler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity-associated asthma is an increasingly prevalent and clinically challenging phenotype, characterized by poorer response to conventional asthma therapies, greater disease burden, and a distinct immunometabolic profile that sets it apart from classical eosinophilic asthma. Given the growing interest in therapies targeting the treatment of obesity, understanding the underlying biological mechanisms that drive inflammatory processes in this cohort is essential. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have emerged as transformative agents in the management of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), with a growing basis of evidence that their utility may extend beyond these indications and exert potent anti-inflammatory effects. In late 2025, the World Health Organization endorsed GLP-1RAs for obesity management, reframing how we contextualize care for patients living with obesity. In this review, we examine the immunopathological mechanisms underpinning obesity-associated asthma, and explore the biology of GLP-1 receptor signaling and the emerging preclinical and observational evidence supporting a direct role for GLP-1RAs in modulating pulmonary inflammation, airway remodeling, and immune cell activation. This could have important implications on future therapeutic directions. Key questions remain regarding whether any respiratory benefits are weight loss dependent or driven by direct immunomodulatory effects, and how GLP-1RAs should be positioned within treatment algorithms. Future prospective trials will be essential to define which patients derive the greatest benefit from these agents. GLP-1RAs represent a compelling and novel therapeutic avenue in obesity-associated asthma and potentially across the broader spectrum of chronic inflammatory airway disease.

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways characterized by marked clinical and biological heterogeneity [1, 2]. Clinically, it is characterized by bronchial hyperresponsiveness leading to smooth muscle contraction, variable airflow limitation, and recurrent respiratory symptoms. Biologically, multiple distinct asthma phenotypes have been described, each influenced by diverse immunological, metabolic, and environmental factors, which ultimately result in variability in disease presentation and response to therapy [3].

Severe asthma is defined as asthma that remains poorly controlled, with persistent symptoms or frequent exacerbations, despite optimal inhaled therapies in accordance with the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines [4]. Patients with severe asthma experience a disproportionate burden of disease, including higher rates of exacerbations, increased systemic corticosteroid use, and greater healthcare use [2, 5, 6]. Within this group, multiple distinct biological phenotypes have been identified [7]. Traditionally, asthma has been broadly classified into type 2 (T2)-high and T2-low inflammatory endotypes [8]. However, growing evidence suggests that asthma exists along a spectrum of overlapping biological processes existing between these two biological entities [9]. These overlapping pathways may contribute to poor disease control and may represent potential therapeutic targets.

One phenotype within this spectrum is obesity-associated asthma. Obesity is a growing global health challenge and its interaction with asthma is complex, involving mechanical, metabolic, and inflammatory mechanisms [10, 11]. With the evolution of targeted obesity treatments in recent years, focus has moved to understanding and addressing the underlying biological mechanisms driving the obese asthma phenotype and whether it should be considered a distinct clinical and pathophysiological entity.

In late 2025, the World Health Organization (WHO) released new guidelines supporting the use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) for the management of obesity [12]. This represents an important shift in the conceptualization of obesity, framing it as a chronic disease akin to hypertension or diabetes that needs lifelong therapy, which may relapse when treatment is discontinued. This paradigm shift may help reduce stigma surrounding obesity management, encouraging open dialogue and earlier medical engagement to improve management of obesity and its comorbidities.

Given their potent metabolic effects and emerging body of evidence as anti-inflammatory mediators, GLP-1RAs may represent a promising future therapeutic agent for the management of asthma, particularly in obesity-associated disease [13]. However, their precise place within the treatment algorithm remains unclear. Important questions remain regarding the role of GLP-1RAs as adjunctive therapy in severe asthma, particularly in those who have failed or do not qualify for biologic therapy, or whether their metabolic effects may benefit a broader group of patients beyond those with severe obesity. In this review, we aim to understand the obese asthma phenotype, the underlying biology of GLP-1RAs and the pathways that influence the inflammatory matrix in obese asthma, and the potential clinical use of these agents in the future.

This is a narrative review. Relevant literature was identified through searches of PubMed/Medline from January 2010 to January 2026, using combinations of the terms “obesity,” “asthma,” “immunometabolism,” “GLP-1,” “GLP-1 receptor agonist,” “semaglutide,” and related terms, and was supplemented by hand searching the reference list of key articles and recent systematic reviews. Preclinical, observational, and mechanistic studies were included given the current absence of randomized controlled trial data with pulmonary endpoints.

Obese Asthma: A Distinct Immunometabolic Phenotype

Obesity is strongly associated with increased asthma incidence and is consistently linked to greater disease severity [14, 15]. Patients with both obesity and asthma frequently report a higher symptom burden, poorer response to conventional therapies, and increased healthcare use [16, 17]. Asthma in the context of obesity does not just represent a distinct clinical phenotype but is likely heterogeneous when obesity associated. Children with immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated asthma have inhaled budesonide responses that are adversely impacted by obesity status [18]. Obesity-associated asthma is classically defined as asthma occurring in adults with a body mass index (BMI) of ≥ 30 kg/m2, in whom disease is frequently characterized by a T2-low, paucigranulocytic inflammatory profile, characterized by less atopy, later age of onset, and persistent symptoms despite optimal treatment [19, 20]. Importantly, individuals with obesity-associated asthma are less likely to demonstrate an optimal response to biologic therapies targeting T2 inflammation [21]. As a result, these patients may experience greater reliance on systemic corticosteroids for disease control, which may further propagate weight gain, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation, perpetuating a cycle of worsening obesity and poorly controlled asthma.

Obesity alters respiratory function through several mechanisms. Mechanical factors due to increased adiposity result in extra thoracic restriction, reducing lung volumes and restricting normal lung mechanics [22]. In addition, comorbidities often associated with obesity, such as gastroesophageal reflux disease, can further aggravate respiratory symptoms and worsen asthma control [23]. Beyond this, dysregulation of immune cells within adipose tissue releases inflammatory cytokines driving systemic inflammation. As such, obesity is considered a chronic systemic inflammatory condition with altered airway immune responses, contributing to poor disease control [24].

The benefits of weight reduction on asthma outcomes in individuals with obesity have been well documented. Prior to the inception of GLP-1RAs, bariatric surgery has been shown to be the most effective method of weight loss with significant implications for outcomes in patients with asthma [25, 26]. Studies have demonstrated that up to half of patients undergoing bariatric surgery were able to reduce or discontinue asthma medications [27]. More recently, observational data have suggested a potential association between GLP-1RA use and reduced asthma incidence among individuals with T2DM or obesity [28]. Although the mechanisms underlying these observations remain incompletely understood, they raise intriguing questions about the potential dual metabolic and anti-inflammatory effects these agents may have. Are improvements in asthma control reflective of improved mechanics due to weight loss or by direct immunometabolic modulation? Central to this is deepening our understanding of the underlying mechanisms of obese asthma and how the niche interplay of immune and metabolic pathways influences inflammation in this cohort.

Insulin Resistance

Insulin resistance, defined as an impaired cellular response to insulin that results in compensatory hyperinsulinemia, is increasingly recognized as an important contributor to asthma pathophysiology and a driver of increased asthma exacerbations [29, 30]. In obesity, adipocyte hypertrophy results in the release of free fatty acids, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and bioactive polypeptide cytokines called adipokines, which disrupt normal insulin signaling pathways, leading to the development of insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, and subsequent hyperglycemia [31].

Hyperinsulinemia promotes airway dysfunction through numerous mechanisms. Experimental mouse models have demonstrated that increased insulin levels can promote airway remodeling characteristic of asthma, such as airway hyperresponsiveness, peribronchial collagen deposition, and increased proliferation of airway smooth muscle cells [32]. These structural and functional changes may exacerbate airway narrowing and worsen respiratory symptoms.

Pharmacological evidence further supports the role of metabolic dysfunction in asthma. Metformin, which enhances peripheral insulin sensitivity, has been shown to reduce asthma exacerbations in patients with glycemic dysfunction, independent of weight loss [33]. This further clarifies that improvements in insulin sensitivity may confer immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory benefits, highlighting the potential role of dysregulated metabolic pathways in asthma, beyond their association with obesity alone.

Together, these findings suggest insulin resistance offers a plausible biological framework to link the metabolic consequences of obesity to airway disease and asthma severity. Insulin resistance and immune dysregulation in obesity are closely interdependent processes with impaired insulin signaling and consequent hyperinsulinemia promoting a pro-inflammatory state. In parallel, inflammation within adipose tissue further exacerbates insulin resistance through cytokine-mediated disruption of insulin signaling, establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of metabolic and immune dysfunction. Understanding these pathways deepens our insight into the complex inflammatory matrix in obesity, strengthening the rationale for GLP-1RA use in this cohort.

Immunopathology of Obesity-Associated Asthma

Obesity-associated asthma has been associated with distinct immunometabolic features including systemic inflammation, altered adipokine signaling, and changes in innate immune response [34]. In obesity, there is a shift to a pro-inflammatory state driven by influx of immune cells to adipose tissue and polarization of immune cells [35]. Adipose tissue represents a major component of immune cell housing, and thus increased adipose tissue mass and remodeling results in heightened immune cell interaction. Inflammatory Shift in Adipocytes in Obesity

Adipose tissue is a dynamic endocrine organ that plays a central role in maintaining inflammatory homeostasis. In the setting of obesity, this tightly regulated balance is disrupted, largely as a result of adipocyte hypertrophy and the consequent downstream upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators [36, 37]. Two key adipokines secreted by adipose tissue that are of particular importance are leptin and adiponectin [38, 39]. Both are widely expressed in adipocytes and act to regulate insulin sensitivity, metabolic homeostasis, and free fatty acid (FFA) oxidation.

Adipocyte hypertrophy and adipose tissue expansion in obesity results in dysregulation of leptin and adiponectin signaling characterized by elevated leptin levels and reduced adiponectin expression (Fig. 1). Leptin, usually implicated in appetite suppression, also exerts important pro-inflammatory effects [38]. Elevated leptin levels in obesity promote a pro-inflammatory immune environment by inducing differentiation of naive T cells to Th1 cells [38]. Th1 cells produce pro-inflammatory cytokines that are necessary for macrophage activation and sustained inflammatory signaling. Conversely, low circulating levels of adiponectin are seen in obesity [40]. Adiponectin typically acts to increase insulin sensitivity and inhibits nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-kB) signaling. Pulmonary Immune Alterations

Macrophages represent one of the dominant immune cell populations within obese adipose tissue and are key mediators of inflammatory signaling [44]. Hypertrophic adipocytes release chemotactic signals that result in macrophage accumulation in adipose tissue. In addition, hypertrophic adipocytes release free fatty acids, promote mitochondrial dysfunction, and induce adipocyte necrosis [45]. These processes further amplify immune cell recruitment and inflammatory signaling.

Altered pulmonary macrophages in obesity are responsible for the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-alpha and IL-6, which are central to maintaining the low-level systemic inflammation state described in obesity [41]. Circulating inflammatory mediators contribute to airway inflammation and altered immune regulation in obese asthma. Beyond increases in macrophage accumulation, altered activation of macrophages also occurs in obesity.

In lean individuals, protective, anti-inflammatory immune cells predominate, including anti-inflammatory polarized macrophages [46]. IL-4, IL-13, and adiponectin dominate in this environment, preserving insulin sensitivity and suppressing pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-kB [47]. Through these mechanisms, adiponectin acts as an important regulator of immune homeostasis and limits excessive inflammation.

In obesity, however, local hypoxia, metabolic stress, and altered cytokine signaling drives a shift in macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype [48]. This supports sustained inflammatory signaling through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and IL-6. Ultimately, this contributes to tissue remodeling, oxidative stress, and cellular injury, promoting systemic dysfunction and insulin resistance [48].

Systemic Inflammation in Obese Asthma

Obese asthma is frequently associated with a T2-low inflammatory profile, typically characterized by neutrophilic airway inflammation and driven by systemic activation of inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-6 and IL-17 [49]. This inflammatory profile reflects broader immunometabolic dysregulation originating within adipose tissue.

Hypoxia as a consequence of adipose tissue hypertrophy represents a key amplifying factor within this inflammatory cascade [50]. As adipose tissue enlarges, inadequate vascularization leads to reduced oxygen availability, activating hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), a transcription factor that functions as a master regulator of cellular response to hypoxia [51]. Activation of HIF-1α promotes the expression of genes involved in fibrosis and inflammation within adipose tissue. The hypoxic environment supports macrophage polarization, further exacerbating tissue inflammation. Evidence from high-fat diet murine models demonstrates that genetic deletion of HIF-1α reduces adipose tissue inflammation and improves insulin sensitivity, highlighting its important role in metabolic dysfunction [52]. In addition, HIF-1α and NF-kB are closely associated, representing a key point of convergence between hypoxia and inflammatory signaling pathways [53]. Under inflammatory conditions, NF-kB enhances HIF-1α transcription, amplifying hypoxia-related responses and sustaining chronic inflammation in adipose tissue, which ultimately contributes to the development of insulin resistance and metabolic disease [52].

Airway smooth muscle (ASM) mass is increased in severe asthma, with obesity attenuating ASM remodeling through various mechanisms. Hyperinsulinemia promotes ASM proliferation and remodeling, while dysregulated adipokine signaling stimulates vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) release, contributing to airway vascular remodeling and further structural disease burden [32, 54, 55]. GLP-1 receptors are expressed on ASM cells, and preclinical evidence from other obstructive lung diseases suggests that GLP-1RA signaling may suppress ASM proliferation, representing a potentially relevant mechanism in obese asthma [56]. Epithelial barrier integrity is similarly compromised in obese asthma, where oxidative stress and leptin-driven downregulation of tight junction proteins increase airway permeability, enhancing exposure to allergens and microbiological insults, amplifying the downstream inflammatory cascade [57].

Fibroblasts also play a key role in obese asthma, contributing to airway remodeling and fibrosis. Pro-inflammatory mediators prevalent in obesity, such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and leptin, promote transition of resident airways fibroblasts to myofibroblasts, a phenotypically distinct population characterized by high contractility and increased deposition of extracellular matrix proteins [58, 59]. This persistence of myofibroblast activity contributes to excess fibrosis and progressive airflow limitation, which is largely corticosteroid resistant.

Collectively, these various mechanisms that drive dysregulated inflammation transform adipose tissue from an immunoregulatory environment into a chronically inflamed organ that actively participates in immune cell cross talk, releasing circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, promoting insulin resistance, and metabolic dysfunction. This interplay between metabolic and immune dysfunction provides a rationale for exploring therapies such as GLP-1RAs, which may target both metabolic and anti-inflammatory pathways.

GLP-1 Biology and Signaling

GLP-1 is an incretin hormone produced and secreted by the intestinal L-cells and acts a key regulator of glucose metabolism [60]. The GLP-1 receptor is expressed predominantly on pancreatic beta cells, where binding of GLP-1 stimulates insulin secretion and suppresses glucagon release, thereby contributing to the maintenance of glucose homeostasis [61]. Beyond its role in glycemic regulation, GLP-1 exerts additional effects to promote satiety and delay gastric emptying, supporting weight regulation, and appetite suppression [62, 63]. Understanding of these combined effects led to the development of GLP-1RAs as pharmacologic therapies designed to mimic the activity of endogenous GLP-1. Initially introduced as potent hypoglycemic agents in the management of T2DM, growing recognition in their efficacy in promoting significant and sustained weight loss has expanded their clinical application to the treatment of obesity.

GLP-1 expression is not restricted to the pancreas. GLP-1 receptors are found in several extra-pancreatic tissues including ASM, the cardiovascular system, central nervous system, renal tract, and beyond [60, 64]. Specifically, GLP-1 expression has been described in airway epithelial cells, alveolar macrophages, and smooth muscle cells of pulmonary vasculature, further supporting the potential influence of GLP-1 signaling on immune responses in the lung [65–67]. Through their influence on systemic GLP-1 receptors, GLP-1RAs have demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects in multiple chronic inflammatory conditions, based predominantly on observational and mechanistic data [68–70]. While evidence to date remains sparse, although prospective clinical trials are ongoing, the broad distribution of the GLP-1 receptor provides a plausible biological basis for the expanding clinical interest in GLP-1RAs as potent anti-inflammatory mediators, beyond their current role as glycemic modulators.

Following binding of GLP-1 to its receptor, downstream activation of G-proteins leads to an increase in intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), which in turn activates protein kinase A (PKA) [71, 72]. This signaling cascade is responsible for promoting synthesis and secretion of insulin while preventing glucagon release [73]. GLP-1 is also implicated in maintaining functionality of pancreatic beta cells through activation of the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway, supporting its therapeutic benefit in T2DM [60, 74].

In an immune context, increased cAMP levels inhibit NF-kB suppressing inflammatory cytokine production [75]. GLP-1 activation promotes polarization of macrophages to an anti-inflammatory phenotype, reducing key cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, independent of changes in baseline weight [76, 77]. These mechanisms provide a contextual link between enhanced GLP-1 signaling and its broader anti-inflammatory role.

GLP-1 is broken down by dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4), resulting in rapid degradation and a short half-life [78]. Conversely, synthetic GLP-1RAs are relatively resistant to DPP-4, resulting in prolonged duration of action and sustained metabolic effects [79]. In addition to their established benefits in glycemic control and weight reduction, emerging clinical evidence reporting the potential role of GLP-1RAs as metabolic modulators and potent anti-inflammatory mediators is a tantalizing prospect. Exploiting these cellular pathways presents an opportunity for the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting immunometabolic dysfunction in a myriad of diseases, including the obese asthma complex.

Currently available GLP-1RAs, including liraglutide, semaglutide, exenatide, and dulaglutide, act as single receptor agonists. Of these, liraglutide and exendin-4 underpin most of the preclinical evidence for GLP-1RA effects on lung inflammation [80–84]. Tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 receptor agonist, acts not only on the GLP-1 receptor but also on the GIP receptor, further perpetuating weight loss and improving insulin sensitivity [85]. In comparison with single receptor agents, it has greater weight loss efficacy and a distinct pharmacological profile [86]. However, direct respiratory evidence for tirzepatide and for other dual/triple agonists now in development, remains considerably more limited than for single GLP-1 agents. As a result, findings should be interpreted with caution, and findings for one agent may not be generalizable to the class as a whole.

Furthermore, the therapeutic landscape of GLP-1 therapies is continuing to evolve with several dual and triple hormone receptor agonists currently undergoing phase 2 and 3 clinical trials [86, 87]. This ongoing development highlights the expanding potential of these agents with an increasing number likely to come to the market in the coming years.

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists in the Lung

Growing interest in GLP-1RAs has prompted investigation into their potential direct effects within the lung. While treating metabolic dysregulation and promoting weight loss is the current primary focus of their treatment potential, the metabolic benefits may contribute substantially to altering chronic disease course in the lung and beyond. GLP-1 receptors are expressed throughout the lungs, along pulmonary vascular smooth muscle, submucosal glands regulating mucus secretion, and alveolar type 2 cells to modulate surfactant production [88, 89]. In addition to their potent systemic anti-inflammatory actions, these agents may exert direct influence in localized pulmonary inflammation and modulate structural airway changes.

Human eosinophils and neutrophils have been shown to express GLP-1 receptors, and in an ex vivo study, exposure of eosinophils isolated from asthmatic subjects to GLP-1 analogs dampened eosinophil activation with reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4, IL-8, and IL-13 [90], supporting a potential role of GLP-1RAs as treatments for attenuating eosinophilic inflammation.

Emerging preclinical evidence supports a direct therapeutic role for GLP-1 receptor activation in pulmonary disease models. In animal studies, GLP-1RAs have been reported to regulate mucus secretion, inhibit collagen formation in fibrosis models, and directly alter immune cell signaling in ways that could possibly attenuate asthma-related inflammation, although these findings have not yet been confirmed in human airway disease [80–82]. In addition, these agents modulate key intracellular signaling pathways, such as cAMP and NF-kB, thereby reducing oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling. In murine models of acute lung injury, GLP-1RAs have been reported to reduce inflammatory cytokine burden and histological lung injury, findings that remain to be tested in human respiratory disease [83].

Regulation of hyperinsulinemia and adipokine imbalance by GLP-1RAs may mediate important structural and inflammatory benefits. By restoring insulin sensitivity and adipokine balance, GLP-1RAs may modulate key drivers of ASM proliferation, preserve epithelial barrier integrity, and reduce downstream inflammatory signaling. Concurrently, at the fibroblast level, reduction in TGF-β activity and leptin signaling may slow subepithelial fibrosis and attenuate corticosteroid-resistant airflow limitation in obese asthma. These observations highlight the potential role of GLP-1RAs beyond their systemic anti-inflammatory and weight reduction effects with direct airway-mediated activity to alter disease course. GLP-1 and Asthma Outcomes: The Evidence

Despite compelling mechanistic and preclinical evidence, prospective data evaluating the long-term impact of GLP-1RAs on asthma outcomes remain limited with much of the data describing improved clinical outcomes with GLP-1RAs limited to observational studies, meaning that extrapolation of these findings to clinical asthma should therefore be regarded as hypothesis generating, rather than as an established effect [91, 92]. Improved asthma control observed with GLP-1RA therapy is frequently reported in conjunction with weight loss. However, the extent to which these benefits extend beyond obesity-related asthma remains uncertain. The clinical benefits observed with GLP-1RA therapy are likely multifactorial, with weight loss, improved lung mechanics, and alterations in localized and systemic inflammatory pathways all playing a crucial role in regulating asthma control.

Beyond asthma, emerging evidence supports a broader role for GLP-1RAs in other respiratory diseases, including lung cancer and interstitial lung disease [84, 93, 94]. While much of this evidence is in the preclinical stage or based on observational studies, the expanding scope of these agents is certainly an exciting and rapidly evolving area of research.

Weight-Loss-Dependent Versus Direct Immunological Effects

A key unresolved question remains whether any respiratory benefits associated with GLP-1RA therapy are primarily driven by weight loss or by weight-independent mechanisms, specifically direct anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects. Current evidence makes it difficult to distinguish between these pathways.

Weight loss, regardless of the method by which it is achieved, is strongly associated with improvements in asthma symptoms and disease control [95]. How weight loss influences T2-inflammation, however, remains unclear. Few studies have directly examined the relationship between weight loss and change in T2 biomarkers such as FeNO and eosinophils, with available results inconsistent. Bariatric surgery and exercise-based weight loss have been associated with reductions in FeNO and sputum eosinophils, reflective of direct T2 inflammatory influences [96, 97]. However, other studies have found no significant change in these markers despite clear improvements in symptoms and lung function [98]. This inconsistency may be partly reflective of lower baseline FeNO expected in obesity or the differing metabolic and mechanical factors that play a role in obesity-associated asthma [49]. How GLP-1RA-induced weight loss specifically alters T2 inflammation has not yet been established. However, the ongoing GATA-3 trial, a placebo-controlled study of semaglutide in obesity-associated asthma, incorporates FeNO among its outcome measures and should help clarify this question (GATA-3; Clinical Trials.gov identifier NCT05254314). To further disentangle weight-loss-dependent from weight-independent effects, further studies examining the effect of GLP-1RAs in nonobese asthma or comparisons against non-GLP-1 weight loss interventions are necessary.

Therapeutic Implications and Future Research Directions

Ultimately, the promising therapeutic potential of GLP-1RAs is increasingly compelling and extends beyond their established role in metabolic disorders. While their application in obesity-associated asthma is of particular interest, the broader immunometabolic effects of GLP-1RAs cannot be underestimated. In diseases characterized by persistent inflammation, targeting shared metabolic and inflammatory pathways may represent a complementary therapeutic strategy beyond conventional therapies.

Safety Considerations

While they propose an interesting therapeutic option in the obesity-associated asthma, the potential side effects of GLP-1RAs cannot be underestimated. Gastrointestinal adverse events, such as nausea, reflux, diarrhea, and delayed gastric emptying, are the most common reason for discontinuing GLP-1RA therapy and warrant specific counseling in patients with asthma, particularly in those in whom reflux already significantly contributes to symptom burden [99, 100]. Delayed gastric emptying carries particular clinical relevance in this population, potentially increasing risk of aspiration events, potentially contributing to poor asthma control and recurrent exacerbations.

Sarcopenia and loss of lean muscle mass are also increasingly recognized alongside fat loss with GLP-1RA therapy [101]. While preclinical data suggest these agents may preserve muscle function in some contexts, clinical cohorts show meaningful reduction in lean mass, raising particular concern for older patients and those with preexisting sarcopenia [102].

Finally, weight regain after discontinuation of treatment is common and often substantial, where any weight-loss-dependent effect could be attenuated in the event treatment is stopped or discontinued [103]. Overall, respiratory-specific safety data for GLP-1RAs remain sparse, and caution is warranted when using these agents in the asthma population until such data become available.

Limitations of Clinical Applicability

A key challenge that will only be further perpetuated by the growing therapeutic potential of these agents is identifying which patients are most likely to derive a meaningful clinical benefit. Currently, this remains difficult to determine. The heterogeneity of asthma further complicates this within the respiratory sphere. As such, identification and development of reliable biomarkers that can stratify patients and identify those who are most likely to respond to treatment is key.

Future research avenues should focus on clarifying the potential role and efficacy of GLP-1RAs in asthma, potentially using a “treatable trait” approach, their optimal positioning within treatment algorithms, and their role as adjuncts or replacements to current therapies. In addition, assessing their long-term impact on disease progression and effects on morbidity and mortality is central to discerning the true impact these agents could have for our patients living with asthma.

Conclusions

Obesity-associated asthma is a complex asthma phenotype, which is only going to increase in prevalence as the global rates of obesity continue to rise. Understanding the underlying immunometabolism mechanisms that underpin the driving inflammatory forces in this condition is central to our understanding and ability to accurately diagnose and treat. Emerging understanding of the role of adipose-driven inflammation resulting in immune cell metabolic reprogramming highlights the possible therapeutic potential of GLP-1RAs as modulators of these inflammatory pathways.

GLP-1RAs represent an exciting avenue of treatment, not only in obesity-associated asthma but also across a range of chronic inflammatory conditions. As our understanding of the role of altered immunometabolism in obesity-associated asthma continues to evolve, GLP-1RAs may represent a novel strategy to address treatment-refractory disease, particularly in patients who respond suboptimally to conventional therapies. Whether any potential clinical benefit of these agents is attributable primarily to weight reduction, to weight-independent immunomodulatory effects, or both acting simultaneously cannot currently be distinguished on the basis of available evidence. Future research should focus on further clarification of their mechanistic effects both systemically and within the pulmonary compartment, defining their potential role within current treatment algorithms, and being able to accurately identify those patients who will benefit most from their use.
