Inverse problems for history-enriched linear model reduction
Arjun Vijaywargiya, George Biros

Abstract
Standard projection-based model reduction for dynamical systems incurs closure error because it only accounts for instantaneous dependence on the resolved state. From the Mori-Zwanzig (MZ) perspective, projecting the full dynamics onto a low-dimensional resolved subspace induces additional noise and memory terms arising from the dynamics of the unresolved component in the orthogonal complement. The memory term makes the resolved dynamics explicitly history dependent. In this work, based on the MZ identity, we derive exact, history-enriched models for the resolved dynamics of linear driven dynamical systems and formulate inverse problems to learn model operators from discrete snapshot data via least-squares regression. We propose a greedy time-marching scheme to solve the inverse problems efficiently and analyze operator identifiability under full and partial observation data availability. For full observation data, we show that, under mild assumptions, the operators are identifiable even when the full-state dynamics are governed by a general time-varying linear operator, whereas with partial observation data the inverse problem has a unique solution only when the full-state operator is time-invariant. To address the resulting non-uniqueness in the time-varying case, we introduce a time-smoothing Tikhonov regularization. Numerical results demonstrate that the operators can be faithfully reconstructed from both full and partial observation data and that the learned history-enriched MZ models yield accurate trajectories of the resolved state.



Keywords
: Mori-Zwanzig formalism, closure modeling, memory closure, model reduction, data driven model, scientific machine learning

Introduction

In many areas of science and engineering, one encounters driven linear dynamical systems of the form

\displaystyle\dot{\mathord{\btensor u}}(t)=\mathord{\btensor A}\mathord{\btensor u}(t)+\bar{\mathord{\btensor g}}(t),\qquad\mathord{\btensor u}(0)=\mathord{\btensor u}_{0},\qquad t\in[0,T],

(1.1)

where
\mathord{\btensor u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the (typically high dimensional) state vector,
\mathord{\btensor A}
is a linear operator,
\bar{\mathord{\btensor g}}(t)
is a prescribed forcing vector, and
T>0
is the time horizon. A classical problem in machine learning is the recovery of an effective operator
\hat{\mathord{\btensor A}}
from discrete snapshot data that best explains the observed dynamics. Widely used methods like Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD)
[
28
]
and Operator Inference (OpInf)
[
26
]
learn such best-fit operators from data via least-squares regression. Conversely, SINDy
[
4
]
fits a nonlinear right-hand-side operator over a dictionary of candidate functions via sparse regression. However, in many applications the large dimensionality of
\mathord{\btensor u}
renders such standard approaches impractical due to high computational and storage costs.

In practice, one is often interested only in the evolution of a few quantities of interest (QoIs), obtained by projecting the state onto a suitably chosen low-dimensional subspace. In low-rank DMD
[
11
]
and OpInf formulations, the QoIs are taken as modal amplitudes associated with a low-rank subspace identified from data, often via Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD)
[
2
,
1
]
. However, neither approach models the closure error arising from mode truncation, leading to inaccurate predictions when the discarded modes exert a non-negligible influence on the reduced dynamics.

From the perspective of the Mori-Zwanzig formalism
[
21
,
37
]
, projecting onto such a low-dimensional subspace generally induces additional non-Markovian (memory) and noise contributions to the reduced dynamics. These contributions are neglected in standard low-rank DMD and OpInf, which approximate the reduced dynamics purely through the action of a time-local linear operator, resulting in an inexact model with closure error. An exact closed evolution equation that makes the additional contributions explicit is provided by the Mori-Zwanzig (MZ) identity. Suppose the state
\mathord{\btensor u}
can be decomposed into
resolved
variables
\bt@\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(QoIs), and
unresolved
variables
\widetilde{\bt@\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\widetilde{d}}
, where
d+\widetilde{d}=N
and
d\ll\widetilde{d}
. Then the MZ identity is a Volterra integro-differential equation (VIDE)
[
33
]

describing the exact dynamics of
\bt@\phi
:

\dot{\bt@\phi}(t)=\mathord{\btensor g}(t)+\underbrace{\mathord{\btensor R}(t)\bt@\phi(t)\vphantom{\int_{0}^{t}\mathord{\btensor K}(t,s)\phi(s)\,ds}}_{\text{Markovian}}+\underbrace{\mathord{\btensor B}(t,0)\widetilde{\bt@\phi}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathord{\btensor B}(t,s)\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor g}}(s)\,ds\vphantom{\int_{0}^{t}\mathord{\btensor K}(t,s)\bt@\phi(s)\,ds}}_{\text{Noise}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}\mathord{\btensor K}(t,s)\phi(s)\,ds\vphantom{\int_{0}^{t}\mathord{\btensor K}(t,s)\bt@\phi(s)\,ds}}_{\text{Non-Markovian}},

(1.2)

Here, the linear operator
\mathord{\btensor R}(t)
plays the role of the instantaneous (or Markovian) contribution that is typically learned in standard approaches, while the noise kernel
\mathord{\btensor B}(t,s)
encodes the influence of unresolved initial conditions and forcing
\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor g}}(t)
, and the memory kernel
\mathord{\btensor K}(t,s)
encodes the history dependence of
\bt@\phi
. Despite the terminology, the “noise” term is deterministic; it is traditionally called noise only because it cannot be determined from
\bt@\phi
alone. Since the dimension of
\widetilde{\bt@\phi}
is typically much larger than that of
\bt@\phi
, (
1.2
) can be viewed as an exact reduced-order model of the original system. However, computing solutions of (
1.2
) is challenging, as the integral terms are often expensive to evaluate, requiring efficient approximate treatments.

In this work, we formulate inverse problems that approximate the operators in (
1.2
) on a prescribed time mesh from trajectory snapshots via least-squares minimization, evaluating the integral terms using numerical quadrature. In our analysis, we adopt the following assumptions:

•

Linearity.
The dynamics are governed by (
1.1
).

•

Ensemble data.
We observe multiple trajectories generated from distinct initial conditions.

•

Time-resolved snapshots.
Data are sampled on a uniform grid with spacing
\Delta t
, sufficiently small to resolve the dynamics; discretization effects are neglected.

•

Exact observations.
Snapshots are free of measurement error, and the dynamics are deterministic (no stochastic forcing).

When
\mathord{\btensor A}
is constant in time, the kernels
\mathord{\btensor B}
and
\mathord{\btensor K}
, are time-translation invariant, that is
\mathord{\btensor B}(t,s)=\mathord{\btensor B}(t-s)
and
\mathord{\btensor K}(t,s)=\mathord{\btensor K}(t-s)
. However, when
\mathord{\btensor A}
varies in time, this is only an approximation. We nevertheless adopt this approximation even for
\mathord{\btensor A}(t)
, since it makes the inverse problems far more tractable by significantly reducing the number of unknowns: the kernels depend only on the time lag instead of separately on
t
and
s
. Viewed from this perspective, the memory and noise contributions act as corrective terms augmenting a standard time-local ROM. We refer to this time-translation invariance property as the
stationarity assumption
in the rest of this paper.

Given
\bt@\Phi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times d}
and
\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times\widetilde{d}}
containing the resolved and unresolved states at time
t_{n}
for
N_{s}
trajectories, we consider the problem of reconstructing
\mathord{\btensor R}
,
\mathord{\btensor K}
, and
\mathord{\btensor B}
when
\mathord{\btensor A}
is unknown. We study
two distinct types of inverse problems
:

1.

Reconstruction from full observation data
: full observations of
\mathord{\btensor u}(t)
are available for
0\leq t_{n}\leq T
, i.e., snapshots
\bt@\Phi_{n}
and
\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{n}
at each
t_{n}
.

2.

Reconstruction from partial observation data
: only partial observations of
\mathord{\btensor u}(t)
are available, i.e., snapshots
\bt@\Phi_{n}
for
0\leq t_{n}\leq T
together with the initial conditions
\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}
.

Full Data
problems typically arise when constructing surrogate models from high-fidelity simulations, where full-state snapshot data are available. In contrast,
Partial Data
problems are common when data come from experiments or from very large-scale simulations, where only the resolved component can be measured (or stored), making identification of the unresolved operator
\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}
practically infeasible.

We propose a greedy, sequential-in-time reconstruction scheme to efficiently solve the global-in-time inverse problems. Within this framework, we show that Full Data problems are well-posed, while Partial Data problems are well-posed only when
\mathord{\btensor A}(t)
is time-invariant. For a time-varying
\mathord{\btensor A}
, the partial data problem becomes ill-posed.

1.2
Contributions

While previous works have primarily emphasized application-specific modeling, problem formulation, and numerical experimentation, the numerical analysis of the inverse problem underlying the reconstruction of the memory kernel has received comparatively little attention. The principal objective of the present work is to address this gap by formulating a generic data-driven framework for learning MZ-based reduced models of driven linear dynamical systems and by investigating assumptions on the dynamics and data availability under which the MZ operators can be uniquely identified from discrete snapshot data. Although we restrict attention to linear systems, we anticipate that the analysis developed here will inform the design of data-driven memory closures for nonlinear systems in future work.

The main results of our work are summarized below:

•

For time-invariant
\mathord{\btensor A}
, we show that the operators
\mathord{\btensor R}
,
\mathord{\btensor B}
, and

\mathord{\btensor K}
are uniquely recoverable within the proposed discrete reconstruction
framework from both full and partial observation data; see

Theorems

3.1
and
3.2
.

•

For time-varying
\mathord{\btensor A}(t)
, we show that the operators remain uniquely
recoverable from full observation data, subject to the conditioning of the
associated linear systems; see
Theorems

3.1
and
3.1
, and we
identify additional assumptions on the forcing function that mitigate ill-conditioning; see

Remark

3.2
.

•

For time-varying
\mathord{\btensor A}(t)
with partial observation data, we show that the greedy
sequential reconstruction scheme is
ill-posed; see
Theorem

3.2
. To address this, we introduce a time-smoothing regularization that stabilizes the reconstruction.

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor P}}\dot{\mathord{\btensor u}}=\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor P}}\mathord{\btensor A}(\mathord{\btensor P}+\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor P}})\mathord{\btensor u}+{\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor P}}\bar{\mathord{\btensor g}}}=\underbrace{(\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor P}}\mathord{\btensor A}\mathord{\btensor P})}_{\operatorname{Diag}(\bt@0,\mathord{\btensor U})}\,\mathord{\btensor P}\mathord{\btensor u}+\underbrace{(\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor P}}\mathord{\btensor A}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor P}})}_{\operatorname{Diag}(\bt@0,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor U}})}\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor P}}\mathord{\btensor u}+\underbrace{\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor P}}\bar{\mathord{\btensor g}}}_{\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor g}}}.

2.2
Inverse problems

We have demonstrated how the Mori-Zwanzig formalism can be applied to linear dynamical systems to obtain an exact dynamical equation for the resolved variables. In this subsection, we formulate inverse problems aimed at inferring the Markovian operator
\mathord{\btensor R}(t)
, noise kernel
\mathord{\btensor B}(t-s)
, and memory kernel
\mathord{\btensor K}(t-s)
directly from snapshot data. We require that the snapshot data be generated for
N_{s}
different initial conditions and use the superscript notation
\bt@\phi^{(j)}(t)
and
\widetilde{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}(t)
to denote the resolved and unresolved states at time
t
for the
j
-th trajectory.

2.2.1
Formulation

Given trajectories
\{\big(\bt@\phi^{(j)}(t),\,\widetilde{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}(t)\big)\}_{j=1}^{N_{s}}
corresponding to
N_{s}
independent initial conditions
\{\big(\bt@\phi^{(j)}_{0},\,\widetilde{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}_{0}\big)\}_{j=1}^{N_{s}}
, and forcings
\{\big(\mathord{\btensor g}^{(j)}(t),\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor g}}^{(j)}(t)\big)\}_{j=1}^{N_{s}}
, we construct two types of inverse problems presented in the definitions that follow.

Definition 2.1
.

(Continuous Full Data inverse problem)
Given full observation data
\{\big(\bt@\phi^{(j)}(t),\widetilde{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}(t)\big)\}_{j=1}^{N_{s}}
and forcings
\{\big(\mathord{\btensor g}^{(j)}(t),\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor g}}^{(j)}(t)\big)\}_{j=1}^{N_{s}}
, the Markovian operator
\mathord{\btensor R}(t)
and
\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}
can be determined by solving

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\mathord{\btensor R},\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}}\;\frac{1}{2N_{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{s}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\dot{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}(t)-\mathord{\btensor g}^{(j)}(t)-\mathord{\btensor R}(t)\bt@\phi^{(j)}(t)-\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}(t)\widetilde{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}(t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\,dt.

(2.5a)

Once
\mathord{\btensor R}(t)
is obtained, the memory kernel
\mathord{\btensor K}(t-s)
and the noise operator
\mathord{\btensor B}(t)
are determined by solving

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\mathord{\btensor K},\mathord{\btensor B}}\;\frac{1}{2N_{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{s}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\mathord{\btensor l}^{(j)}(t)-\mathord{\btensor B}(t)\widetilde{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathord{\btensor K}(t-s)\bt@\phi^{(j)}(s)\,ds-\int_{0}^{t}\mathord{\btensor B}(t-s)\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor g}}^{(j)}(s)\,ds\right\|_{2}^{2}\,dt,

(2.5b)

where
\mathord{\btensor l}^{(j)}(t)=\dot{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}(t)-\mathord{\btensor g}^{(j)}(t)-\mathord{\btensor R}(t)\bt@\phi^{(j)}(t)
.

Definition 2.2
.

(Continuous Partial Data inverse problem)
Given partial observation data
\{\big(\bt@\phi^{(j)}(t),\widetilde{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}_{0}\big)\}_{j=1}^{N_{s}}
and forcings
\{\big(\mathord{\btensor g}^{(j)}(t),\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor g}}^{(j)}(t)\big)\}_{j=1}^{N_{s}}
, the set of operators

\{\mathord{\btensor R}(t),\mathord{\btensor K}(t),\mathord{\btensor B}(t)\}
is determined by solving

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor R},\mathord{\btensor K},\mathord{\btensor B}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{1}{2N_{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{s}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\dot{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}(t)-\mathord{\btensor g}^{(j)}(t)-\mathord{\btensor R}(t)\bt@\phi^{(j)}(t)-\mathord{\btensor B}(t)\widetilde{\bt@\phi}^{(j)}_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathord{\btensor K}(t-s)\bt@\phi^{(j)}(s)\,ds\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hskip-113.81102pt-\int_{0}^{t}\mathord{\btensor B}(t-s)\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor g}}^{(j)}(s)\,ds\right\|_{2}^{2}\,dt.

(2.6)

2.2.2
Discrete formulation based on the Backward Euler rule

We now present a discrete formulation of the inverse problems. For a simplified presentation, we present a first-order accurate time discretization that yields operators evaluated at time points consistent with the implicit Backward Euler forward solve (
2.2.2
). Alternative discretizations compatible with the Forward Euler and Implicit-Midpoint schemes—the latter being second-order accurate—are provided in
Appendix

B
. The theoretical results established in
Section

3
extend naturally to these alternative discretizations due to an analogous structure of the underlying least-squares problems.

Applying the Backward Euler rule to discretize the VIDE (
1.2
), evaluating the
\mathord{\btensor K}
-integral using a composite right-point quadrature and the
\mathord{\btensor B}
-integral using a composite left-point quadrature, and rearranging terms, the following forward update can readily be obtained:

\displaystyle\bt@\phi_{n+1}=\left[\mathord{\btensor I}-\Delta t\,\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}-{\Delta t\,^{2}}\mathord{\btensor K}_{0}\right]^{-1}\left[\bt@\phi_{n}+\Delta t\,\mathord{\btensor g}_{n+1}+\Delta t\,\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}\widetilde{\bt@\phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}\bt@\phi_{k+1}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\left.+\Delta t\,^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathord{\btensor B}_{n-k+1}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor g}}_{k}\right].

(2.7)

Here,
\mathord{\btensor I}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
denotes the identity matrix. The discrete inverse problems are naturally derived from this forward update as described below.

Given initial condition
(\bt@\phi_{0},\widetilde{\bt@\phi}_{0})
, we assume that snapshots
\{\bt@\phi_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
and
\{\widetilde{\bt@\phi}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
can be sampled using an appropriate ODE time integrator at uniformly spaced
N_{T}
time points
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
, where
\bt@\phi_{n}=\bt@\phi(t_{n})
. The data is stored in matrices
\{\bt@\Phi_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
and
\{\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
, where
\bt@\Phi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times d}
and
\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times\widetilde{d}}
. Each row of
\bt@\Phi_{n}
(respectively,
\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{n}
) corresponds to one trajectory, and each column corresponds to a resolved (respectively, unresolved) variable. Thus,
(\bt@\Phi_{n})_{ij}
and
(\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{n})_{ij}
denote the
j
-th resolved and unresolved variables at time
t_{n}
for the
i
-th trajectory. The forcing matrices
\{\mathord{\btensor G}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
and
\{\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
are defined analogously.

Definition 2.3
.

(Discrete Full Data inverse problem)
Given full observation data
\{\big(\bt@\Phi_{n},\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{n}\big)\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
and forcing data
\{(\mathord{\btensor G}_{n},\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{n})\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
, the set of Markovian operators
\{\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}
and
\{\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}
can be reconstructed by solving

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1},\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}_{n+1}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{\Delta t}{2N_{s}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}\left\|\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{n+1}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{n+1}-\bt@\Phi_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}^{\top}-\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{n+1}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}^{\top}_{n+1}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(2.8a)

where
\mathord{\btensor R}^{\top}
denotes the transpose of
\mathord{\btensor R}
. Matrices
\{\mathord{\btensor K}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}
and
\{\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}
can subsequently be reconstructed by solving

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{\Delta t}{2N_{s}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}\left\|\mathord{\btensor L}_{n+1}-\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}{\mathord{\btensor B}}^{\top}_{n+1}-\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}\bt@\Phi_{k+1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}^{\top}-\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{k}\mathord{\btensor B}_{n-k+1}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}\,,

(2.8b)

where
\mathord{\btensor L}_{n+1}=\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{n+1}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{n+1}-\bt@\Phi_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}^{\top}
.

Definition 2.4
.

(Discrete Partial Data inverse problem)
Given partial observation data
\{\bt@\Phi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N_{T}}
and
\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}
, and forcing data
\{(\mathord{\btensor G}_{n},\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{n})\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
, matrices
\{\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1},\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}
can be reconstructed by solving

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1},\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{\Delta t}{2N_{s}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}\left\|\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{n+1}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{n+1}-\bt@\Phi_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}^{\top}-\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}{\mathord{\btensor B}}^{\top}_{n+1}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip-113.81102pt\left.-\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}\bt@\Phi_{k+1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}^{\top}-\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{k}\mathord{\btensor B}_{n-k+1}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(2.9)

Once the entire set
\{\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1},\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}
of operators has been reconstructed, the forward update (
2.2.2
) defines a ROM that can be used to predict trajectories for unseen initial conditions.

3
Reconstruction algorithms

In this section we develop methodology to efficiently solve the discretized inverse problems described in
Section

2
. In the Full Data problem, the minimization step (
2.8a
) for
\{(\mathord{\btensor R}_{n},\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}_{n})\}
decouples over
n
into
N_{T}
separate independent problems. However, the problem (
2.8b
) does not, since the contribution to the objective from time step
n
depends on all past states
\{\mathord{\btensor K}_{j},\mathord{\btensor B}_{j+1}\}_{j\leq n}
of the kernels. In principle, one could recover these kernels by solving a single global least-squares problem that couples all time levels, but this becomes computationally prohibitive as the number of snapshots grows. Instead, we exploit the causal, lower-triangular structure of the objective function and introduce a
greedy time-marching least-squares scheme
, which solves only for the current pair
(\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1})
at each step while treating previously computed operators as fixed. Because past estimates are never revisited, this scheme only recovers an approximation to the true global minimizer. The time-marching technique is also used to solve the global minimization problem (
2.4
) in the partial data problem. Here, the unknowns at each time step are
(\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1},\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1})
.

We show that under mild assumptions, with the greedy time-marching scheme, the Full Data problem is well-posed. In contrast, the Partial Data problem is ill-posed unless the dynamics is governed by a time-invariant full-state operator
\mathord{\btensor A}
. For ill-posed cases, we stabilize reconstruction by employing a time-smoothing Tikhonov regularization for
\mathord{\btensor R}
and
\mathord{\btensor K}
.

For the Full Data problem, we also examine reconstruction and prediction under a finite-memory approximation, in which the memory kernel
\mathord{\btensor K}
is assumed to vanish beyond a prescribed time lag and is thus compactly supported in time. Additionally, we briefly consider reconstruction of a fully non-stationary memory kernel
\mathord{\btensor K}(t,s)
, without the stationarity assumption
\mathord{\btensor K}(t,s)=\mathord{\btensor K}(t-s)
, and show that the resulting inverse problem is fundamentally ill-posed due to rank deficiency of the least-squares operator.

3.1
Reconstruction with full observation data

3.1.1
Reconstruction of
\mathord{\btensor R}

Given full observation data, i.e., snapshots of both the resolved and
unresolved trajectories in time for multiple initial conditions, we first solve the minimization problem
(
2.8a
) to reconstruct the operators
\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}
and

\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}_{n+1}
at each time step. Defining

\mathord{\btensor X}_{n+1}^{R}:=[\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}\;\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}_{n+1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
, where
N=d+\widetilde{d}
and
N_{s}
denotes the number of initial conditions, (
2.8a
) decouples across time and is equivalent to solving
N_{T}
separate minimization problems

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor X}_{n+1}^{R}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\left\|\mathord{\btensor Z}_{n+1}^{R}-\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R}\mathord{\btensor X}_{n+1}^{R}\right\|_{F}^{2},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N_{T}-1,

(3.1)

with the known matrix
\mathord{\btensor Z}_{n+1}^{R}=\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{n+1}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times d}

and least-squares operator
\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R}:=[\bt@\Phi_{n+1}\;\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{n+1}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N}
.
Thus, each time level
t_{n+1}
corresponds to an independent least-squares fit. Assuming a dense factorization, the cost of factoring the least-squares operator is
\mathcal{O}(N_{s}N^{2})
flops per time step.

For time-invariant
\mathord{\btensor A}
, the operators
\mathord{\btensor R}
and
\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}
are also time-invariant. In this case, instead of
N_{T}
separate problems, (
2.8a
) reduces to a single global least-squares problem for
\mathord{\btensor X}^{R}:=[\mathord{\btensor R}\;\;\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor R}}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
:

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor X}^{R}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\left\|\mathord{\btensor Z}^{R}-\mathord{\btensor F}^{R}\mathord{\btensor X}^{R}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(3.2)

with the known matrix and least-squares operator

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor Z}^{R}:=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{1}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{N_{T}}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{N_{T}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}N_{T}\times d},\quad\mathord{\btensor F}^{R}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bt@\Phi_{1}&\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\bt@\Phi_{N_{T}}&\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{N_{T}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}N_{T}\times N},

obtained by stacking the block matrices
\{\mathord{\btensor Z}_{n+1}^{R}\}
and

\{\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R}\}
row-wise. This global formulation uses all time levels simultaneously, improving conditioning. Factoring costs
\mathcal{O}(N_{s}N_{T}N^{2})
flops.

3.1.2
Reconstruction of
\mathord{\btensor K}
and
\mathord{\btensor B}

After reconstructing
\mathord{\btensor R}
at all prescribed time points, we next estimate the kernels
\{\mathord{\btensor K}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}
and
\{\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}
by solving the discrete problem (
2.8b
). Defining

\mathord{\btensor L}^{KB}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathord{\btensor L}_{1}\\[2.0pt]
\vdots\\[2.0pt]
\mathord{\btensor L}_{N_{T}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}N_{T}\times d},\qquad\mathord{\btensor X}^{K}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathord{\btensor K}_{0}^{\top}\\[2.0pt]
\vdots\\[2.0pt]
\mathord{\btensor K}_{N_{T}-1}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN_{T}\times d},\qquad\mathord{\btensor X}^{B}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathord{\btensor B}_{1}^{\top}\\[2.0pt]
\vdots\\[2.0pt]
\mathord{\btensor B}_{N_{T}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\widetilde{d}N_{T}\times d},

(
2.8b
) can be written in the simplified form

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor X}_{K},\mathord{\btensor X}_{B}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{\Delta t}{2N_{s}}\,\big\|\mathord{\btensor L}^{KB}-\mathord{\btensor F}^{K}({\mathord{\btensor X}^{K}})^{\top}-\mathord{\btensor F}^{B}({\mathord{\btensor X}^{B}})^{\top}\big\|_{F}^{2}.

(3.3)

where both

\mathord{\btensor F}_{K}=\Delta t\begin{bmatrix}\bt@\Phi_{1}&\bt@0&\cdots&\bt@0\\[2.0pt]
\bt@\Phi_{2}&\bt@\Phi_{1}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\bt@0\\[2.0pt]
\bt@\Phi_{N_{T}}&\bt@\Phi_{N_{T}-1}&\cdots&\bt@\Phi_{1}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathord{\btensor F}_{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0}&\bt@0&\cdots&\bt@0\\[2.0pt]
\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{1}&\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\bt@0\\[2.0pt]
\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{N_{T}-1}&\cdots&\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{1}&\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0}\end{bmatrix},

have a block lower-triangular Toeplitz structure, with each block being a non-square, tall matrix. The combined least-squares operator
[\mathord{\btensor F}_{K}\ \mathord{\btensor F}_{B}]
has size
N_{s}N_{T}\times NN_{T}
, where
N=d+\widetilde{d}
. Treating this as a dense least-squares problem would require factoring a matrix whose cost scales like
\mathcal{O}(N_{s}N^{2}N_{T}^{3})
flops and
\mathcal{O}(N_{s}NN_{T}^{2})
storage. Since forming and solving this large global system is prohibitively expensive, we exploit the causal, lower-triangular structure of
\mathord{\btensor F}_{K}
and
\mathord{\btensor F}_{B}
and use a greedy but approximate time-marching approach. At time step
n
, we solve only for the unknowns
\mathord{\btensor K}_{n}
and
\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}
through the least-squares problem

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{\Delta t}{2N_{s}}\left\|\mathord{\btensor L}_{n+1}-(\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0}){\mathord{\btensor B}}^{\top}_{n+1}-\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}\bt@\Phi_{k+1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}^{\top}-\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{k}\mathord{\btensor B}_{n-k+1}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(3.4)

keeping the terms
\{\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}\}_{k\geq 1}
that have already been reconstructed at earlier time steps fixed. Similarly, in the second sum all terms depend only on previously computed
\{\mathord{\btensor B}_{n-k+1}\}
, and for
n=0
this sum is empty. Defining
\mathord{\btensor X}^{KB}_{n+1}:=[\Delta t\mathord{\btensor K}_{n}\;\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
, the above problem can be written as

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor X}_{n+1}^{KB}}{\operatorname{argmin}}||{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{n+1}^{KB}-\mathord{\btensor F}^{KB}{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{n+1}^{KB}||_{F}^{2},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N_{T}-1,

(3.5)

with least-squares operator
{\mathord{\btensor F}}^{KB}:=[\bt@\Phi_{1}\;\;(\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0})]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N}
and known matrix

{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{n+1}^{KB}:=\mathord{\btensor L}_{n+1}-\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bt@\Phi_{k+1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}^{\top}-\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{k}\mathord{\btensor B}_{n-k+1}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times d}.

The time-independent least-squares operator
\mathord{\btensor F}^{KB}
can be factored once and reused for all
n
, with a factoring cost of
\mathcal{O}(N_{s}N^{2})
flops. Because past estimates
\{\mathord{\btensor K}_{j},\mathord{\btensor B}_{j+1}\}_{j<n}
are never revisited, this greedy scheme only recovers an approximation to the true minimizer of the global problem

The next theorem establishes the well-posedness of the discrete Full Data inverse problem and provides bounds on the condition numbers of the least-squares operators
\mathord{\btensor F}^{R}_{n}
,
\mathord{\btensor F}^{R}
, and
\mathord{\btensor F}^{KB}
for the zero forcing case.

Theorem 3.1
.

The Full Data inverse problem is well-posed under the following assumptions:

(i)

\mathord{\btensor F}_{0}:=[\bt@\Phi_{0}\;\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}]
has full column rank.

(ii)

\sigma_{\min}(\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0})>0
.

(iii)

\operatorname{range}(\bt@\Phi_{0})\,\cap\,\operatorname{range}(\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0})=\{0\}
.

Moreover, if
\mathord{\btensor G}(t)
and
\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}(t)
are zero matrices, the condition numbers of least-squares operators in (
3.1
), (
3.2
), and (
3.5
) satisfy

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R})\leq\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{0})e^{\int_{0}^{t_{n+1}}\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))-\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds},\quad\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}^{R})\leq\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{0})\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}e^{2\int_{0}^{t_{n+1}}\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds}}{\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}e^{2\int_{0}^{t_{n+1}}\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds}}},

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}({\mathord{\btensor F}^{KB}})\leq\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{0})\frac{\max\{e^{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\bt@\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds},1\}}{\min\{e^{\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\bt@\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds},1\}},

(3.6)

where
\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H})
and
\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H})
are the extremal eigenvalues of the Hermitian part
\mathord{\btensor H}=\frac{\mathord{\btensor A}+\mathord{\btensor A}^{\star}}{2}
of the full-state linear operator
\mathord{\btensor A}
in (
1.1
).

A detailed proof of the theorem is provided in
Appendix

A
. The key idea is to show that the least-squares operators
\mathord{\btensor F}^{R}_{n+1}
in (
3.1
),
\mathord{\btensor F}^{R}
in (
3.2
), and
\mathord{\btensor F}^{KB}
in (
3.5
) have full column rank under assumptions (i)–(iii), so the corresponding least-squares problems admit unique minimizers. Stability under small perturbations of the least-squares operator and known matrix then follows from standard perturbation results for overdetermined least-squares problems. The 2-norm condition number bounds are obtained by applying Grönwall’s inequality to the full-state system and using the resulting growth estimates to bound the extremal singular values of the least-squares operators.

Remark 3.1
.

(Numerical ill-conditioning of
\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R}
)
Although
Theorem

3.1
shows that the Full Data problem is well-posed, in many applications, the least-squares operator
\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R}
may become numerically ill-conditioned as
n
increases. To see this, consider a diffusion equation on
[0,2\pi]
with periodic boundary conditions discretized by the Fourier pseudospectral method with
N
nodes. For the resulting dynamical system,

\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H})=-\tfrac{N^{2}\mu}{4},\qquad\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H})=0,

where
\mu>0
is the diffusion coefficient. The bound for
\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R})
becomes

\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R})\leq\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{0})\,e^{(\frac{\mu N^{2}}{4})t_{n+1}},

which is tight since
\mathord{\btensor H}
and
\mathord{\btensor A}
share eigenvalues. The condition number thus grows exponentially in time with rate
\frac{\mu N^{2}}{4}
. In contrast,
\mathord{\btensor F}^{R}
remains well-conditioned, with the bound on its condition number given by:

\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}^{R})\leq\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{0})\sqrt{N_{T}\frac{1-e^{-\frac{N^{2}\mu\Delta t}{2}}}{e^{-\frac{N^{2}\mu\Delta t}{2}}(1-e^{\frac{N^{2}\mu T}{2}})}}\sim\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{0})\sqrt{\frac{N^{2}\mu T}{2}}\text{ as }\Delta t\to 0.

While this example assumes a constant diffusion coefficient, similar ill-conditioning can arise for a time-dependent
\mu(t)
, making the reconstruction of the time-dependent operator
\mathord{\btensor R}(t)
susceptible to numerical instability.

Remark 3.2
.

(Full column rank requirement of
\mathord{\btensor G}
)
Although
Theorem

3.1
does not explicitly require the forcing matrix
\bar{\mathord{\btensor G}}=[\mathord{\btensor G}\;\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}]
to have full column rank, in problems like the one in the previous remark (with forcing added), this condition must hold to mitigate the ill-conditioning of
\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R}
for large
n
. To see this, take
\bar{\mathord{\btensor G}}
to be constant. Then

\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R}=e^{t_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor A}^{\top}}\mathord{\btensor F}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{n+1}}\bar{\mathord{\btensor G}}\,e^{(t_{n+1}-s)\mathord{\btensor A}^{\top}}ds=e^{t_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor A}^{\top}}\mathord{\btensor F}_{0}+\bar{\mathord{\btensor G}}\,\mathord{\btensor A}^{-\top}(e^{t_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor A}^{\top}}-\mathord{\btensor I}).

As
n
increases,
e^{t_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor A}^{\top}}\to 0
for a stable, invertible time-invariant operator
\mathord{\btensor A}
, and thus
\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}^{R}\sim-\,\bar{\mathord{\btensor G}}\,\mathord{\btensor A}^{-\top}
,
and
\mathrm{rank}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}})\;\mathrm{rank}(\mathord{\btensor A}^{-\top})
.

3.1.3
Reconstruction under a finite-memory approximation

We now consider reconstruction under a finite-memory approximation, in which the memory kernel is assumed to vanish beyond a prescribed time lag so that the memory integrals have truncated support. For simplicity, we set
\bar{\mathord{\btensor g}}=0
. For the continuous problems (
2.5b
) and (
2.2
), this corresponds to replacing the first integral term by
\int_{\bar{t}_{m}}^{t}\mathord{\btensor K}(t-s)\phi^{(j)}(s)ds
where
\bar{t}_{m}=\max\{0,t-t_{m}\}
and
t_{m}>0
. In the discrete setting, the finite-memory approximation leads to

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{\Delta t}{2N_{s}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}\left\|\mathord{\btensor L}_{n+1}-\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}{\mathord{\btensor B}}^{\top}_{n+1}-\Delta t\sum_{k=\bar{m}}^{n}\bt@\Phi_{k+1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(3.7)

where
\bar{m}=\max\{0,n-m\}
with
m>0
. Thus only the discrete lags
0,\dots,m
of the memory kernel sequence
\{\mathord{\btensor K}_{n}\}
are retained, so
\mathord{\btensor K}_{n}=0
for all
n>m
, and the discrete kernel has compact support in the time-lag index. The global unknowns are
\begin{bmatrix}\mathord{\btensor B}_{1}&\cdots&\mathord{\btensor B}_{N_{T}}&\Delta t\mathord{\btensor K}_{0}&\cdots\Delta t\mathord{\btensor K}_{m}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
, and the corresponding least-squares operator is
\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Diag}(\bt@\Phi_{0})&\mathord{\btensor F}^{\text{trunc}}\end{bmatrix}
,
where

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor F}^{\text{trunc}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bt@\Phi_{1}\\
\bt@\Phi_{2}&\bt@\Phi_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\
\bt@\Phi_{m+1}&\bt@\Phi_{m}&\cdots&\bt@\Phi_{1}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\bt@\Phi_{N_{T}}&\bt@\Phi_{N_{T}-1}&\cdots&\bt@\Phi_{N_{T}-m+1}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(3.8)

Due to the block-trapezoidal structure of
\mathord{\btensor F}^{\mathrm{trunc}}
, in contrast to the case for (
2.8b
), a local least-squares solve over
n
neglects the final
N_{T}-(m+1)
rows, leading to biased or incomplete recovery. Accurate recovery thus requires a global solve of (
3.7
). Since forming
\mathord{\btensor F}^{\mathrm{trunc}}
explicitly can be prohibitive in large-scale settings, we instead solve (
3.7
) iteratively using a matrix-free implementation of the corresponding linear operator.

Remark 3.3
.

(Reconstruction of a non-stationary memory kernel)

In this remark, we briefly discuss solving (
2.8b
) after removing the stationarity assumption
\mathord{\btensor K}(t,s)=\mathord{\btensor K}(t-s)
. For simplicity, we set
\bar{\mathord{\btensor g}}=0
so that the second integral term disappears. With the fully non-stationary kernel
\mathord{\btensor K}(t,s)
, (
2.8b
) changes to

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{\Delta t}{2N_{s}}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}\left\|\mathord{\btensor L}_{n+1}-\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}{\mathord{\btensor B}}^{\top}_{n+1}-\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}\bt@\Phi_{k+1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{nk}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}\,,

(3.9a)

where
\mathord{\btensor K}_{nk}=\mathord{\btensor K}(t_{n+1},t_{k+1})
. Since the contributions to the objective are independent over
n
, (
3.9a
) decouples into
N_{T}
separate least-squares problems:

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor X}_{n+1}}{\operatorname{argmin}}||{\mathord{\btensor L}}_{n+1}-\mathord{\btensor F}^{KB}_{n+1}{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{n+1}||_{F}^{2},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N_{T}-1,

(3.9b)

where

3.2
Reconstruction with partial data

For the case where only partial data are available, we use the Type 2 discrete inverse problem (
2.4
) to reconstruct the operators
\{(\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1},\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1})\}_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}
. As in the full-observation setting, we do not solve the globally coupled minimization (
2.4
) directly. Instead, we employ the greedy time-marching least-squares scheme introduced in
Section

3.1
, updating only the current triple
(\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1},\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1})
at each time step while treating the previously reconstructed kernels
\{(\mathord{\btensor K}_{j},\mathord{\btensor B}_{j+1})\}_{j<n}
as fixed. At time step
n
, the minimization problem to be solve is

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1},\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{\Delta t}{2N_{s}}\left\|\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{n+1}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{n+1}-\bt@\Phi_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}^{\top}-(\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0}){\mathord{\btensor B}}^{\top}_{n+1}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip-170.71652pt\left.-\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}\bt@\Phi_{k+1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}^{\top}-\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{k}\mathord{\btensor B}_{n-k+1}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(3.10)

With
{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{n+1}^{RKB}:=[\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}\;\Delta t\,\mathord{\btensor K}_{n}\;\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2d+\widetilde{d})\times d}
, we have the
N_{T}
minimization problems

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{n+1}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;||{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{n+1}^{RKB}-{\mathord{\btensor F}}_{n+1}^{RKB}{\mathord{\btensor X}}_{n+1}^{RKB}||_{F}^{2},\qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N_{T}-1,

(3.11)

where
{\mathord{\btensor Z}}_{n+1}^{RKB}:=\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{n+1}-\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bt@\Phi_{k+1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}^{\top}-\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{k}\mathord{\btensor B}_{n-k+1}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times d}
, and
{\mathord{\btensor F}}_{n+1}^{RKB}:=[\bt@\Phi_{n+1}\;\bt@\Phi_{1}\;(\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0})]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times(2d+\widetilde{d})}
.

Factoring
\mathord{\btensor F}^{RKB}_{n+1}
at each step costs
\mathcal{O}(N_{s}(2d+\widetilde{d})^{2})
flops, so the overall cost of the sequential Type 2 reconstruction scales like
\mathcal{O}(N_{T}N_{s}(2d+\widetilde{d})^{2})
.
Unlike in the full-observation setting, however, the problems (
3.11
), in general, are not uniquely solvable. This is because the first two block columns of
\mathord{\btensor F}^{RKB}_{n+1}
are linearly dependent whenever all three operators
(\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1},\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1})
are independent unknowns. However, when
\mathord{\btensor R}
is treated constant, it can be absorbed into
\mathord{\btensor K}_{0}
by replacing this pair with the single operator
\mathord{\btensor R}+\Delta t\,\mathord{\btensor K}_{0}
. In this case, the block
\bt@\Phi_{1}
drops out of
{\mathord{\btensor F}}_{n+1}^{RKB}
and (
3.11
) becomes uniquely solvable. The following theorem makes this precise.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume the conditions of
Theorem

3.1
hold.

(i)

If
\mathord{\btensor R}
is constant in time, then, after combining
\mathord{\btensor R}
and

\mathord{\btensor K}_{0}
into the single operator
\mathord{\btensor R}+\Delta t\,\mathord{\btensor K}_{0}
at
n=0
, each
local least-squares problem (
3.11
) admits a unique
minimizer.

(ii)

If
\mathord{\btensor R}
is time-varying, the local least-squares problems
(
3.11
) do not admit unique minimizers.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{range}(\bt@\Phi_{n+1})=\operatorname{range}(\bt@\Phi_{1})=\operatorname{range}(\bt@\Phi_{0})
for all
n
, the least-squares operator

{\mathord{\btensor F}}_{n+1}^{RKB}=[\bt@\Phi_{n+1}\;\;\bt@\Phi_{1}\;\;(\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0})]

fails to have full column rank whenever all three blocks are present.
Thus, for time-varying
\mathord{\btensor R}
, each local problem has infinitely many
minimizers. If
\mathord{\btensor R}
is constant, the block
\bt@\Phi_{1}
drops out of

{\mathord{\btensor F}}_{n+1}^{RKB}
and, under assumptions (i)–(iii) of
Theorem

3.1
,
the resulting least-squares operators have full column rank, so the local least-squares problems are uniquely solvable.
∎

3.2.1
Regularized problem

To address the ill-posedness of the sequential solve for the Type 2 discrete inverse problem, we augment the objective in (
3.2
) with time-smoothing Tikhonov penalties on
\mathord{\btensor R}
and
\mathord{\btensor K}
, penalizing the differences
\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}-\mathord{\btensor R}_{n}
and
\mathord{\btensor K}_{n}-\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-1}
. This choice stabilizes the reconstruction and yields a unique, regularized least-squares solution at each time step. Given parameters
\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor R}},\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor K}}>0
, the regularized problem at time step
n\geq 1
reads

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1},\mathord{\btensor K}_{n},\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\frac{\Delta t}{2N_{s}}\left\|\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{n+1}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{n+1}-\bt@\Phi_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}^{\top}-(\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\;\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0}){\mathord{\btensor B}}^{\top}_{n+1}-\Delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}\bt@\Phi_{k+1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}^{\top}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip-369.88582pt\left.-\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{k}\mathord{\btensor B}_{n-k+1}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor R}}}{2}\|\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}-\mathord{\btensor R}_{n}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor K}}}{2}\|\mathord{\btensor K}_{n}-\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-1}\|_{F}^{2},

(3.12)

assuming
\mathord{\btensor R}_{n}
and
\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-1}
have already been computed at step
n-1
. The problem for step
n=0
can be combined with the problem for
n=1
into a
single system, since the regularization intrinsically couples the pairs
(\mathord{\btensor R}_{1},\mathord{\btensor R}_{2})
and
(\mathord{\btensor K}_{0},\mathord{\btensor K}_{1})
. This leads to the following sequence of least-squares problems:

•

For
n=0,1
, define

\mathord{\btensor X}_{1}^{\text{reg}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathord{\btensor R}_{1}&\mathord{\btensor R}_{2}&\Delta t\,\mathord{\btensor K}_{0}&\Delta t\,\mathord{\btensor K}_{1}&\mathord{\btensor B}_{1}&\mathord{\btensor B}_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

and solve

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor X}_{1}^{\text{reg}}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\left\|\mathord{\btensor Z}_{1}^{\text{reg}}-\mathord{\btensor F}_{1}^{\text{reg}}\mathord{\btensor X}_{1}^{\text{reg}}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(3.13a)

with known matrix

\mathord{\btensor Z}_{1}^{\text{reg}}:=\begin{bmatrix}(\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{1}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{1})^{\top}&(\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{2}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{2})^{\top}&\bt@0&\bt@0\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

and least-squares operator

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor F}_{1}^{\text{reg}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bt@\Phi_{1}&0&\bt@\Phi_{1}&\bt@0&\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0}&\bt@0\\
\bt@0&\bt@\Phi_{2}&\bt@\Phi_{2}&\bt@\Phi_{1}&\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{1}&\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0}\\
-\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor R}}}\,\mathord{\btensor I}&\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor R}}}\,\mathord{\btensor I}&\bt@0&\bt@0&\bt@0&\bt@0\\
\bt@0&\bt@0&-\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor K}}}\,\mathord{\btensor I}&\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor K}}}\,\mathord{\btensor I}&\bt@0&\bt@0\end{bmatrix}.

•

For
n>1
, define

\mathord{\btensor X}_{n}^{\text{reg}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathord{\btensor R}_{n+1}&\Delta t\,\mathord{\btensor K}_{n}&\mathord{\btensor B}_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

and solve

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor X}_{n}^{\text{reg}}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\left\|\mathord{\btensor Z}_{n}^{\text{reg}}-\mathord{\btensor F}_{n}^{\text{reg}}\mathord{\btensor X}_{n}^{\text{reg}}\right\|_{F}^{2},

(3.13b)

where

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor Z}_{n}^{\text{reg}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathord{\btensor J}_{n+1}\\
\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor R}}}\mathord{\btensor R}_{n}\\
\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor K}}}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathord{\btensor F}_{n}^{\text{reg}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bt@\Phi_{n+1}&\bt@\Phi_{1}&\widetilde{\bt@\Phi}_{0}+\Delta t\,\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{0}\\
\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor R}}}\,\mathord{\btensor I}&\bt@0&\bt@0\\
\bt@0&\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor K}}}\,\mathord{\btensor I}&\bt@0\end{bmatrix},

and
\mathord{\btensor J}_{n+1}=\dot{\bt@\Phi}_{n+1}-\mathord{\btensor G}_{n+1}-\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bt@\Phi_{k+1}\mathord{\btensor K}_{n-k}^{\top}-\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor G}}_{k}\mathord{\btensor B}_{n-k+1}^{\top}
.

If
\mathord{\btensor F}_{0}
has full column rank, the matrices
{\mathord{\btensor F}_{1}^{\text{reg}}}^{\top}\mathord{\btensor F}_{1}^{\text{reg}}
and

{\mathord{\btensor F}_{n}^{\text{reg}}}^{\top}\mathord{\btensor F}_{n}^{\text{reg}}
are positive definite for
\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor R}},\lambda_{\mathord{\btensor K}}>0
, and thus invertible. Hence, both (
3.13a
) and (
3.13b
) admit unique minimizers.

4
Results

We evaluate the accuracy and performance of the proposed model reduction framework through computational experiments, reconstructing operators in (
1.2
) from synthetically generated data, and predicting resolved dynamics for unseen initial conditions. The experiments focus on linear dynamical systems obtained after spatially discretizing partial differential equations (PDEs). We choose a small subset of the degrees of freedom of the system as the resolved variables. The goal of the experiments is to accurately predict the trajectories of the resolved variables through the MZ model (
1.2
), with operators reconstructed from full and partial observation data.

We consider two parametric PDEs on a one-dimensional periodic domain: a reaction-diffusion-advection equation and a damped wave equation (discussed in
Appendix

D
), and generate multiple test cases by varying the PDE parameters. To ensure that the initial data matrix
\mathord{\btensor F}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N}
has full column rank, we generate
N_{s}\geq N
independent smooth initial conditions for training using the following procedure:

1.

For each
s=1,\ldots,N_{s}
, set

\mathord{\btensor u}^{s}=\sum_{m=0}^{N/2}\frac{a_{m}^{s}}{1+m^{2}}\cos(m\mathord{\btensor x}-\phi_{m}^{s}),\qquad a_{m}^{s}\sim U(0,1),\quad\phi_{m}^{s}\sim U(0,2\pi),

where
\mathord{\btensor x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the vector of spatial grid points.

2.

Stack all
\mathord{\btensor u}^{s}
as rows of
\mathord{\btensor F}_{0}
.

Here,
N
is even, and
U(a,b)
denotes the uniform distribution on
[a,b]
. A low-pass filter is applied to dampen the high-frequency modes by dividing the amplitudes
a_{m}^{s}
by
1+m^{2}
, which yields spatially smooth initial data. The testing data are generated independently using the same procedure. Once the matrices of initial conditions are obtained, the linear dynamical systems are integrated in time using an adaptive high-order Runge-Kutta scheme (the RK45 implementation in SciPy’s
solve_ivp
) with a prescribed solver tolerance
\tau_{\text{sol}}=10^{-12}
for all experiments. After appropriate projections, this yields the datasets
\{\Phi(t_{n})\}_{n}
and
\{\widetilde{\Phi}(t_{n})\}_{n}
needed for operator reconstructions. Operators are reconstructed in both full and partial observation data regimes, with results reported in the latter only for ill-posed cases. For all experiments, we employ the second-order accurate schemes of
Section

B.1
for reconstruction and subsequent trajectory prediction.

For reconstruction with full observation data, we verify the second-order convergence of the
\mathord{\btensor K}
and
\mathord{\btensor R}
operators on a sequence of temporal discretizations. The error metrics considered are

•

Relative Frobenius error in
\mathord{\btensor K}
over
[0,T]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathord{\btensor K}}=\sqrt{\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathord{\btensor K}(t)-\mathord{\btensor K}^{\text{true}}(t)\|_{F}^{2}\,dt}{\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathord{\btensor K}^{\text{true}}(t)\|_{F}^{2}\,dt}},

(4.1)

where
\mathord{\btensor K}
and
\mathord{\btensor K}^{\text{true}}
denote the reconstructed and ground-truth operators, respectively. Since an analytical form of
\mathord{\btensor K}^{\text{true}}
is not available when the PDE parameters are time-varying, we replace
\mathord{\btensor K}^{\text{true}}
by
\mathord{\btensor K}
reconstructed at the finest temporal resolution, yielding a self-convergence error metric.

•

Relative Frobenius error in
\mathord{\btensor R}
over
[0,T]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathord{\btensor R}}=\sqrt{\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathord{\btensor R}(t)-\mathord{\btensor R}^{\text{true}}(t)\|_{F}^{2}\,dt}{\int_{0}^{T}\|\mathord{\btensor R}^{\text{true}}(t)\|_{F}^{2}\,dt}}.

(4.2)

•

Relative Frobenius error in
\bt@\Phi^{\text{pred}}
over
[0,T]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\bt@\Phi^{\text{pred}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\|\bt@\Phi^{\text{pred}}(t)-\bt@\Phi(t)\|_{F}^{2}\,dt}{\int_{0}^{T}\|\bt@\Phi(t)\|_{F}^{2}\,dt}},

(4.3)

where
\bt@\Phi^{\text{pred}}
contains the trajectories of resolved variables for test initial conditions obtained by time-integrating (
1.2
) after reconstructing the operators.

The time integrals in (
4.1
)-(
4.3
) are approximated by the same numerical quadrature used to integrate the noise and memory terms in (
1.2
). For the reconstruction of the memory kernel under a finite-memory approximation, we solve the least-squares problem (
3.7
) matrix-free and iteratively using SciPy’s
lsqr
solver. This solver employs an iterative Krylov subspace method based on the Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization process
[
22
]
and efficiently minimizes the residual and solution norms for large sparse least-squares problems.

We consider the reaction-diffusion-advection equation with periodic boundary conditions:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u(t,x)=v(t)\partial_{x}u(t,x)+\mu(t)\partial_{xx}u(t,x)+a(t)u(t,x)+\bar{g}(t,x),\quad&&t\in[0,T],\quad&&x\in[0,2\pi],\\
&u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad&&x\in[0,2\pi],\\
&u(t,0)=u(t,2\pi),\quad&&t\in[0,T],\end{aligned}\right.

(4.4)

\mu(t)
is the diffusion coefficient,

is the advection velocity,

is the reaction strength, and
\bar{g}(t,x)

2\pi

\{x_{j}=j\Delta x\}_{j=0}^{N-1}

\frac{d\mathord{\btensor u}}{dt}=\left[{\mu}(t)\mathord{\btensor D}^{2}+{v}(t)\mathord{\btensor D}+{a(t)}\mathord{\btensor I}\right]\mathord{\btensor u}+\bar{\mathord{\btensor g}}(t).

(4.5)

Here,
\mathord{\btensor u}=[u_{0}(t),\dots,u_{N-1}(t)]^{\top}
,
\bar{\mathord{\btensor g}}=[\bar{g}(t,x_{0}),\dots,\bar{g}(t,x_{N-1})]^{\top}
,
\mathord{\btensor D}
denotes the spectral differential matrix, and
\mathord{\btensor I}
is the identity. The terminal time is
T=5
. We set
N=30
and define the resolved variables as
\bt@\phi=[u_{5},u_{10},u_{15},u_{20},u_{25}]\in\mathbb{R}^{5}

\mathord{\btensor u}
forming
\widetilde{\bt@\phi}

\mathord{\btensor P}
and
\widetilde{\mathord{\btensor P}}

Table

2
. Cases (a)-(f) have zero forcing; among these, (a)-(d) use time-independent parameters, while (e)-(f) use time-varying parameters.

Conclusions

Using the Mori-Zwanzig formalism, we derived an exact model for the projected reduced dynamics of generic high-dimensional driven linear systems and posed inverse problems to learn the associated model operators from full and partial observation data. By introducing a greedy, sequential-in-time solution strategy, we identified conditions under which these operators are identifiable and the inverse problems are well posed. Numerical experiments on a one-dimensional reaction–diffusion–advection equation and a damped wave equation demonstrate that the resulting data-driven, history-enriched models faithfully reproduce the dynamics of the resolved degrees of freedom.

However, the proposed framework has several limitations. Our analysis is restricted to linear dynamical systems. Extending the formulation and identifiability theory to parametric and nonlinear systems would significantly broaden the range of applications, and we hope the present work will help guide the construction and analysis of inverse problems in those settings. A systematic treatment of noisy and sparse observation data—together with more robust regularization strategies—remains to be developed. These issues lie beyond the scope of this paper but represent natural next steps toward practical, history-enriched reduced models for more complex dynamical systems.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mathord{\btensor X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
solve the least-squares problem

\displaystyle\underset{\mathord{\btensor X}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\|\mathord{\btensor Z}-\mathord{\btensor F}\mathord{\btensor X}\|_{F}^{2},

where
\mathord{\btensor Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
\mathord{\btensor F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has full column rank.

\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}
solves the perturbed problem

\displaystyle\underset{\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\|\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}-\hat{\mathord{\btensor F}}\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}\|_{F}^{2},

where
\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}:=\mathord{\btensor Z}+\Delta\mathord{\btensor Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and

\hat{\mathord{\btensor F}}:=\mathord{\btensor F}+\Delta\mathord{\btensor F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with

\|\Delta\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}<\sigma_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor F})
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\|\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}-\mathord{\btensor X}\|_{F}}{\|\mathord{\btensor X}\|_{F}}\leq\frac{\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F})}{1-\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F})\cdot\frac{\|\Delta\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}}{\|\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}}}\left(\frac{\|\Delta\mathord{\btensor Z}\|_{F}}{\|\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}\|\mathord{\btensor X}\|_{F}}+\frac{\|\Delta\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}}{\|\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}}+\frac{\|\mathord{\btensor O}\|_{F}}{\|\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}\|\mathord{\btensor X}\|_{F}}\right),

(A.1)

where
\mathord{\btensor O}:=\mathord{\btensor Z}-\mathord{\btensor F}\mathord{\btensor X}
.

Proof.

By
[
10
, Corollary 7.3.5]
,

\big|\sigma_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor F})-\sigma_{\min}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor F}})\big|\leq\|\Delta\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}\;\implies\;\sigma_{\min}(\hat{\mathord{\btensor F}})\geq\sigma_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor F})-\|\Delta\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}>0.

Thus,
\mathord{\btensor F}
and
\hat{\mathord{\btensor F}}
have full column rank, and the pseudoinverses

\mathord{\btensor F}^{\dagger}:=(\mathord{\btensor F}^{\top}\mathord{\btensor F})^{-1}\mathord{\btensor F}^{\top}
and

\hat{\mathord{\btensor F}}^{\dagger}:=(\hat{\mathord{\btensor F}}^{\top}\hat{\mathord{\btensor F}})^{-1}\hat{\mathord{\btensor F}}^{\top}
are well defined.

\mathord{\btensor Z}=\mathord{\btensor F}\mathord{\btensor X}+\mathord{\btensor O}
and
\hat{\mathord{\btensor Z}}=\mathord{\btensor Z}+\Delta\mathord{\btensor Z}
, we have

\frac{\|\hat{\mathord{\btensor X}}-\mathord{\btensor X}\|_{F}}{\|\mathord{\btensor X}\|_{F}}\leq\frac{\|\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}}{\sigma_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor F})-\|\Delta\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}}\left(\frac{\|\Delta\mathord{\btensor Z}\|_{F}}{\|\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}\|\mathord{\btensor X}\|_{F}}+\frac{\|\Delta\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}}{\|\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}}+\frac{\|\mathord{\btensor O}\|_{F}}{\|\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}\|\mathord{\btensor X}\|_{F}}\right).

Finally,
\|\mathord{\btensor F}\|_{2}=\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F})\sigma_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor F})
yields (
A.1
).

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}||\mathord{\btensor x}(t)||_{2}^{2}=\frac{d}{dt}\mathord{\btensor x}(t)^{\star}\mathord{\btensor x}(t)=\mathord{\btensor x}(t)^{\star}\mathord{\btensor A}(t)\mathord{\btensor x}(t)+\mathord{\btensor x}(t)^{\star}\mathord{\btensor A}(t)^{\star}\mathord{\btensor x}(t)=2\mathord{\btensor x}(t)^{\star}\mathord{\btensor H}(t)\mathord{\btensor x}(t).

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H}(t))||\mathord{\btensor x}(t)||_{2}^{2}\leq\mathord{\btensor x}(t)^{\star}\mathord{\btensor H}(t)\mathord{\btensor x}(t)\leq\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H}(t))||\mathord{\btensor x}(t)||_{2}^{2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle 2\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H}(t))||\mathord{\btensor x}(t)||_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{d}{dt}\mathord{\btensor x}(t)^{\star}\mathord{\btensor x}(t)\leq 2\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H}(t))||\mathord{\btensor x}(t)||_{2}^{2}.

Applying Grönwall’s inequality, we get, for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle e^{\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds}||\mathord{\btensor x}_{0}||_{2}\leq||\mathord{\btensor x}(t)||_{2}\leq e^{\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds}||\mathord{\btensor x}_{0}||_{2}

(A.2)

\mathord{\btensor x}(0)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\max}(\bt@\Psi(t))\leq e^{\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds},\qquad\sigma_{\min}(\bt@\Psi(t))\geq e^{\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds}.

Hence, the 2-norm condition number of
\bt@\Psi(t)
satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}(\bt@\Psi(t))\leq e^{\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))-\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds}

Using
\mathord{\btensor F}^{R}_{n+1}=\mathord{\btensor F}_{0}\bt@\Psi({t_{n+1}})^{\top}

\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}^{R}_{n+1})
follows. Now for any unit
\mathord{\btensor v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

\displaystyle||\mathord{\btensor F}^{R}_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor v}||_{2}

\displaystyle=||\mathord{\btensor F}_{0}\bt@\Psi(t_{n+1})^{\top}\mathord{\btensor v}||_{2}\leq\sigma_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{0})\sigma_{\max}(\bt@\Psi(t_{n+1}))||\mathord{\btensor v}||_{2}\leq\sigma_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{0})e^{\int_{0}^{t_{n+1}}\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds}

By the variational characterization of singular values,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor F}^{R})^{2}=\min_{||\mathord{\btensor v}||=1}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}||\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor v}||_{2}^{2}\geq\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}\sigma_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{0})^{2}e^{2\int_{0}^{t_{n+1}}\lambda_{\min}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds},

\displaystyle\sigma_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor F}^{R})^{2}=\max_{||\mathord{\btensor v}||=1}\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}||\mathord{\btensor F}_{n+1}\mathord{\btensor v}||_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}\sigma_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor F}_{0})^{2}e^{2\int_{0}^{t_{n+1}}\lambda_{\max}(\mathord{\btensor H}(s))ds},

from which the bound for
\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}^{R})
can be obtained. The bound for
\kappa_{2}(\mathord{\btensor F}^{KB})
follows using a similar argument.

Appendix C
Pseudospectral discretization

We consider spatially
2\pi
-periodic problems and discretize the spatial variable by a Fourier pseudospectral method. Let
N
be an even integer and introduce the equispaced grid points

x_{j}=\frac{2\pi j}{N},\qquad j=0,\dots,N-1.

Given a periodic function
u(t,x)
we denote its nodal values by

u_{j}(t)=u(t,x_{j}),\qquad j=0,\dots,N-1,

and collect them in the vector

\mathord{\btensor u}(t)=(u_{0}(t),\dots,u_{N-1}(t))^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}.

The spatial discretization is formulated in terms of the unitary discrete Fourier transform. Let

K_{N}=\Bigl\{-\tfrac{N}{2}+1,\dots,-1,0,1,\dots,\tfrac{N}{2}\Bigr\}

be a set of
N
distinct integer wavenumbers. For a given vector
\mathbf{u}(t)
we define its discrete Fourier coefficients by

\widehat{u}_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}u_{j}(t)\,e^{-ikx_{j}},\qquad k\in K_{N},

with the inverse relation

u_{j}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k\in K_{N}}\widehat{u}_{k}(t)\,e^{ikx_{j}},\qquad j=0,\dots,N-1.

Let
\mathord{\btensor F}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
denote the unitary discrete Fourier matrix with entries

F_{k,j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}e^{-ikx_{j}},\qquad k\in K_{N},\;j=0,\dots,N-1.

Then

\widehat{\mathord{\btensor u}}(t)=\mathord{\btensor F}\mathord{\btensor u}(t),\qquad\mathord{\btensor u}(t)=\mathord{\btensor F}^{*}\widehat{\mathord{\btensor u}}(t),

where
\mathord{\btensor F}^{*}
denotes the conjugate transpose of
\mathord{\btensor F}
. Differentiation in Fourier space is diagonal. We introduce the diagonal matrices

\bt@\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(ik)_{k\in K_{N}},\qquad\bt@\Lambda^{(2)}=\mathrm{diag}(-k^{2})_{k\in K_{N}},

so that

\widehat{\partial_{x}u}(t)=\Lambda\,\widehat{\mathord{\btensor w}}(t),\qquad\widehat{\partial_{xx}u}(t)=\Lambda^{(2)}\,\widehat{\mathord{\btensor w}}(t).

Transforming back to physical space yields

(\partial_{x}u)(t)=\mathord{\btensor F}^{*}\bt@\Lambda\mathord{\btensor F}\,\mathord{\btensor u}(t),\qquad(\partial_{xx}u)(t)=\mathord{\btensor F}^{*}\bt@\Lambda^{(2)}\mathord{\btensor F}\,\mathord{\btensor u}(t).

This defines the spectral differentiation matrices

\displaystyle\mathord{\btensor D}:=\mathord{\btensor F}^{*}\bt@\Lambda\mathord{\btensor F},\qquad\mathord{\btensor D}^{(2)}:=\mathord{\btensor F}^{*}\bt@\Lambda^{(2)}\mathord{\btensor F},

(C.1)

which represent the pseudospectral approximations of the first and second spatial derivatives at the collocation points.

The discretizations (
4.5
) and (
D.2
) follow by enforcing the PDEs (
4.4
) and (
D.1
) at the collocation points
x_{j}
, with each spatial derivative replaced by its pseudospectral approximation via the differentiation matrices defined in (
C.1
).
