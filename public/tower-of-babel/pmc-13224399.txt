Determinants of time to malaria treatment-seeking behaviour among caregivers of children under five in Uganda: a case study of Apac district
Authors: Benard Odur, Bosco Opio, Olivia Nabulya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria is a significant public health issue in sub-Saharan Africa, especially in Apac District, Uganda, where it greatly affects children under five. Despite its high rates of morbidity and mortality, there is limited understanding of how communities manage malaria and seek treatment. This study aimed to identify the factors that influence treatment-seeking behaviour among caretakers of young children in Apac District.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study was conducted using quantitative methods, with data collected from 240 households through simple random sampling. Caregivers completed structured questionnaires, and Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed in R to examine the socio-economic, demographic, and behavioural factors influencing the time taken by caretakers to seek malaria treatment for their children.ResultsAll caregivers sought treatment for their children and out of the 240 caregivers, (70%) are female. The Cox proportional hazards model identified key factors affecting treatment-seeking time: secondary education significantly increased the likelihood of time for treatment seeking (HR = 1.67, p = 0.044). Significant delays were associated with no medicine at the hospital (HR = 0.61, p = 0.001) and financial constraints (HR = 0.49, p < 0.001).ConclusionTo ensure timely malaria treatment for young children, it is crucial to improve drug supply at hospitals and through Village Health Teams, and to educate caregivers on early treatment. Expanding education financing and promoting NGO involvement in caregiver education are essential. Efficient medicine distribution and caregiver education on prompt treatment are key to improving health outcomes for children under five.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05781-0.Keywords: Treatment seeking, Malaria management, Children, Apac district, Uganda

Background

Malaria is a mosquito-borne infectious disease that affects humans, causing symptoms such as fever, fatigue, vomiting, and headaches. In severe cases, it can lead to jaundice, seizures, coma, or death [1, 2]. Symptoms usually begin 10–15 days after being bitten by an infected mosquito [2]. If not treated properly, people infected with Plasmodium vivax or Plasmodium ovale may experience recurrences of the disease months later due to dormant liver-stage parasites (hypnozoites) [3, 4]. In contrast, Plasmodium falciparum may reappear if inadequately treated, typically within weeks rather than months [5, 6]. For individuals who have recently recovered from malaria, reinfection often leads to milder symptoms due to partial immunity; however, this resistance diminishes over months to years without continued exposure [7].

Globally, malaria remains a significant public health threat, particularly in Africa and several Asian nations, compared to the industrialised world where cases are typically imported from endemic regions [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reported that in 2023 alone, there were 263 million clinical cases of malaria and 597,000 related deaths, most of them among children in Africa [2]. Approximately 95% of malaria deaths occurred in the WHO African Region, where many at risk still lack access to essential prevention, detection, and treatment services. Most victims are children under 5 years old living in Africa [2, 8].

In Uganda, malaria continues to present a substantial burden. The National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) under the Ministry of Health highlights that Uganda has the third highest global burden of malaria cases (5.1%) and the eighth highest level of malaria-related deaths (2.9%) as of 2022 [9]. The country also has the highest proportion of malaria cases in East and Southern Africa, accounting for 23% of the region’s malaria burden [9]. The disease has both direct and indirect impacts, including significant out-of-pocket expenditures for consultations, drugs, transport, and subsistence at health facilities [10]. These costs are estimated to range between USD 0.41 and USD 3.88 per person per month, equivalent to USD 1.88 to USD 26 per household, representing a significant burden, particularly for the poorest households [10].

To combat malaria, Uganda has implemented various interventions, including malaria case management at both private and public health facilities, distribution of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), implementation of Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS), integrated community case management (ICCM) of childhood illnesses, and intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) [9]. According to the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP), Uganda is stratified into three malaria transmission zones: low (parasite prevalence: < 10%), medium (11–50%), and high (> 50%). Apac District is among the high-prevalence zones, a situation that has persisted for over 20 years despite targeted interventions like IRS, LLINs, and IPTp [11].

To align with WHO recommendations, Uganda’s malaria strategic roadmap emphasizes prompt access to appropriate antimalarial treatment within the first 24 h of fever onset to prevent progression from uncomplicated to severe malaria [11]. This approach is supported by evidence showing that delays in seeking care are a risk factor for severe malaria, often associated with longer symptom duration and rapid disease progression [12]. Although studies have been conducted in primary healthcare settings in Uganda, there is still a need to better understand community-level malaria health-seeking behaviours, particularly among caregivers of children under 5 years [13].

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in Apac District, Uganda (see Fig. 1). The Apac District is bordered by Oyam District to the northeast, Kole District to the north-northeast, Lira District to the north-northeast, Dokolo District to the east, Amolatar District to the southeast, Nakasongola District to the southwest, and Kiryandongo District to the west. Apac, the district's largest town, is located approximately 62 kms southwest of Lira, the main city in the Lango sub-region. Apac District has undergone several administrative changes, including its division to create Oyam District in 2006, Kole District in 2010, and Kwania District in 2018, which comprises Chawente, Abongomola, Nambyeiso, Inomo, Aduku, and Aduku Town Council. As of 2017, Apac Town Council was upgraded to Apac Municipal Council. The district population increased from 162,200 in 1991 to 248,700 in 2002, and approximately 349,000 in 2012, reflecting an annual growth rate of 3.5% between 2002 and 2012.

Study design, population and sampling

A cross-sectional analytical study employing quantitative data collection methods was conducted from July to September 2022. Pre-tested structured questionnaires were uploaded onto Android-enabled smartphones using Kobo Collect for data collection. The study focused on children under 5 years old in Apac District who had been diagnosed with malaria and treated with anti-malarial medications, such as chloroquine phosphate, within the two weeks prior to data collection. At the household level, a simple random sampling technique was used to identify participants. Records of eligible children were obtained with the support of the district biostatistician and local public health facility leaders. Community health workers, also known as Village Health Teams (VHTs), assisted in following up with these children at their homes. At the village level, purposive sampling was applied to reach the households, and the consent process was initiated with the caretakers. Once consent was obtained, structured interviews were conducted with the caretakers. Cochran’s modified formula was applied to establish the sample size for this study, resulting in a total of 240 households.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Children under five years old diagnosed with malaria and treated with anti-malarial medication within the past two weeks were included. Children suffering from severe illness or neurocognitive conditions that could affect their ability to participate in the study or provide accurate responses through their caregivers were excluded.

Data collection procedure

The data collection was carried out in predefined phases to ensure accuracy and representativeness. A stratified sampling method was employed to select participants, and specialized data collection tools were developed and rigorously tested. The structured questionnaire captured variables such as the time caregivers took to seek malaria treatment (in hours) and independent variables including treatment-seeking behaviour, age, sex, employment status, marital status, choice of hospital, education level, weekly expenditure, reasons for delay, and household wealth index based on asset ownership. Quality control measures were implemented to address data outliers and missing values, ensuring a reliable dataset for analysis.

Data analysis

Data analysis was done using Stata 18. Frequency tables were used to summarize and display categorical variables, while numerical data were analysed using means and percentages. Survival analysis was conducted by setting "time to treatment" as the time variable and "seeking treatment" as the event, reflecting the nature of the dependent variable, which is the time caregivers took to seek malaria treatment for children under five. A summary table and Kaplan–Meier graph were employed to illustrate the time to treatment as well assessing the hazard function, which accounts for censored observations. The Log-rank test compared treatment times across different categories. Multivariate analysis was performed using Cox proportional hazards regression, with coefficients indicating changes in treatment duration between different variable categories. Independent variables with a p-value of less than 0.05 were considered potential risk factors for malaria treatment-seeking time for children under five.

Ethical considerations

The research project was approved by the Head of the Department of Planning and Applied Statistics at Makerere University. Ethical clearance was given by the College of Business and Management Sciences Research Ethics Committee (CoBAMS-REC-2002–36). Administrative clearance was granted by Apac District Local Government through the Chief Administrative Officer. Caregivers provided informed consent before interviews, and confidentiality of their information was strictly maintained.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the participants

Most of the caregivers were aged 25–34 years (57.9%), followed by those aged 15–24 years (32.1%), and 10.8% were aged 35 years or older. Most caregivers were female (70.0%) and married (81.3%), while 18.8% were unmarried. Most respondents were small-scale farmers (88.8%). Regarding education, 60.0% had attained primary education, 18.8% had secondary education, 6.3% had tertiary education, and 15.0% had no formal education. Caregivers selected health centres mainly due to proximity (45.8%) or unavailability of medicines at the main hospital (54.2%). Reported reasons for delayed treatment included unavailability of medicines at the hospital (45.8%), being occupied in the garden (33.8%), and financial constraints (20.4%). Most participants (81.7%) had weekly expenditures between 0–14.2 USD, 11.3% spent between 14.3 and 28.4 USD, and 7.1% spent above 28.5 USD. In terms of household wealth status, 51.7% were classified as “poorest,” 42.5% as “poor,” and 5.8% as “rich.” Among children under care, 56.3% were aged 25–60 months, followed by those aged 12–24 months (29.2%) and 1–11 months (14.6%) (Table 1).

Time to treatment-seeking

A higher proportion of the children (238/240) in the study received treatment. The median time it took caregivers before taking the children for treatment was 8 (IQR = 18) hours. The earliest time a child was taken for treatment was one hour and the longest time child waited before being taken for treatment was 168 h (7 days) (Table 2). Interestingly after 50 h, most of the children in the study sought treatment with exception of 32 children whose treatment delayed. Only 12 children had not sought care after 100 h (Fig. 2).

Treatment times varied significantly based on caregiver education level, employment status (Fig. 5) and marital status. Caregivers with no or primary education took an average of 9 h to seek treatment, compared to 6 h for those with secondary education and 7 h for those with tertiary education (p = 0.0001, Fig. 3). Married caregivers sought treatment in 8 h on average, whereas unmarried caregivers took 24 h (p = 0.041, Fig. 4). Caregivers who were farmers had a median treatment time of 9 h (p = 0.0139, Fig. 5), while the treatment times of male and female caregivers were similar (p = 0.1169, Fig. 7). Weekly spending did not significantly affect treatment times (p = 0.2242), Fig. 6). Wealth index also had a significant effect on treatment-seeking time, with poorer caregivers having a greater median treatment time of 9 h (p = 0.0478, Fig. 8, 9).

Multivariate analysis of time to treatment seeking

The Cox proportional hazards model reveals several significant factors affecting the time to treatment seeking. Caregivers with secondary education sought treatment 67% faster than those with no formal education (HR = 1.67, 95% CI 1.01–2.75, p = 0.044) (Table 3). Interestingly, tertiary or university education did not show a statistically significant effect on treatment-seeking time (HR = 1.77, 95% CI 0.88–3.56, p = 0.107). Marital status, employment, and wealth index did not show significant effects on treatment times (p > 0.05). Treatment at a facility with no medicine at the main hospital was associated with a significantly longer treatment time (HR = 0.61, 95% CI 0.462–0.806, p = 0.001). Similarly, delays due to "no money" (HR = 0.49, 95% CI 0.341–0.691, p < 0.001) and "no medicine at the main hospital" (HR = 0.510, 95% CI 0.356–0.723, p < 0.001) were also significantly associated with longer treatment seeking/delays.

Discussion

This study assessed caregivers' time to seek malaria treatment for children under five years in Apac District, northern Uganda. The findings revealed that caregivers waited an average of 8 h before seeking treatment, with over 25% of children receiving treatment within 24 h. This rate of timely treatment contrasts with a study in Nigeria where only 18.6% of caregivers sought treatment within 24 h [14], suggesting regional differences in health-seeking behaviour. However, it aligns more closely with Uganda's Malaria Indicator Survey, which reported 57% of caregivers demonstrating appropriate health-seeking behaviour for fever/malaria [15].

The study found that caregivers with secondary education sought treatment 67% faster than those without formal education. This is consistent with findings by Gebregergs and Birhanu [16], who highlighted the positive role of education in improving health-seeking behaviours. After controlling for other factors, the caregiver’s level of education remained a significant predictor, with secondary education proving sufficient to enhance timely access to care in this context. However, it contrasts with Odimbe and Atuhairwe [17], who found no significant relationship between education and treatment-seeking times. These findings underscore the importance of investing in community-based educational programmes to enhance caregivers’ understanding of malaria symptoms, risks, and the urgency of seeking prompt treatment. Targeted awareness campaigns could help bridge knowledge gaps by tailoring messages to be easily understood by all community members, particularly those with lower educational attainment. This could include using simple language, local dialects, visual aids, and practical illustrations to ensure the information is accessible and actionable for populations with limited education.

The study also revealed that medicine shortages delayed treatment-seeking by 39%. This corroborates findings by Miljković et al. [18], who identified medicine stockouts as a critical barrier to timely healthcare access. Ensuring consistent availability of essential medicines in health facilities is paramount. Strengthening supply chain mechanisms and employing robust inventory management systems could mitigate delays caused by medicine shortages.

Financial constraints slowed treatment-seeking by 51%, consistent with studies by Moitra et al. [19] and Alem et al. [20], but this contrasts with Johnson [21], who reported that financial barriers had minimal impact on timely access to care. This variation may reflect differences in healthcare financing policies and socioeconomic contexts. Addressing financial barriers requires policies that promote affordable healthcare access, such as community health insurance schemes and subsidies for vulnerable populations. Additionally, supporting income-generating activities at the household level could reduce economic challenges related to healthcare access.

The findings align with prior research demonstrating the interplay between education, resource availability, and economic factors in shaping health-seeking behaviours. For instance, the Uganda Malaria Indicator Survey reported 57% of caregivers demonstrating appropriate health-seeking behaviours for fever/malaria, mirroring the timely responses observed in this study [15]. However, regional variations, as seen in the Nigeria study, highlight the need for tailored interventions addressing unique local challenges [14].

The disparity in findings across studies highlights the importance of contextual factors in shaping health-seeking behaviours. For instance, in regions such as South-West Ethiopia, differences in malaria prevalence, healthcare infrastructure, cultural practices, and caregiver perceptions may influence treatment-seeking patterns differently than in Apac, which experiences very high malaria transmission. In Apac, frequent malaria episodes, long distances to health facilities, and entrenched local practices may interact with caregiver education and household resources to determine response times. Future research should examine these contextual interactions in greater detail to inform interventions that are tailored to high-burden settings like Apac.

Strength and limitations

This research addresses an important public health issue, malaria treatment-seeking behaviour for children under five, which has significant implications for child health and mortality in the district. Data collection provided valuable insights into the factors influencing treatment-seeking behaviours, contributing to a better understanding of the issue.

Weaknesses

Resource constraints were a serious weakness that hampered the study's scope and depth. These limits had an impact on several areas of the research, including the ability to collect a bigger sample size, conduct more extensive data analysis, and use more complete research methodology. Another problem was the presence of malaria among the data collectors. This health concern could have hampered the data collection procedure, resulting in fewer complete responses or lowering overall data quality. The health and well-being of data collectors was an important factor in the study, and the existence of illnesses among them proved to be an unexpected challenge that impacted the study's reliability and outcomes.

Conclusions and recommendations

Based on the findings, the following conclusions can be drawn from the study: Caregivers in Apac district exhibited a concerning delay in seeking malaria treatment for children under five, with an average wait time of 8 h. This delay indicates a need for improved awareness and education regarding the urgency of malaria treatment. The study highlights the significant impact of education on health-seeking behaviour. Caregivers with secondary education sought treatment 67% faster than those with no education, suggesting that educational interventions may enhance prompt treatment-seeking. The availability of essential medicines at health facilities was a crucial determinant of treatment-seeking behaviour. Caregivers experiencing medicine shortages were 39% slower to seek treatment, emphasizing the need for consistent drug supply to improve timely healthcare access. Finally, Economic barriers were identified as a major factor influencing treatment delays, with caregivers facing financial challenges taking 51% longer to seek care. Addressing these financial constraints is critical for enhancing access to timely malaria treatment.

To improve timely malaria treatment, it is essential to implement community education campaigns that are tailored to the diverse literacy levels, cultural norms, and social contexts of the target communities. Additionally, strengthening school and community programs on malaria awareness, ensuring consistent availability of medicines at health facilities, providing financial support for low-income families, and enhancing the role of community health workers in connecting caregivers with timely care are critical measures.
