Psychometric Validation of the Weight and Emotions Scale (WES) in Adults with Obesity or Overweight
Authors: Chisom Kanu, Hayley Karn, Claudine Clucas, Iris Goetz, Lisa M. Neff, Kristina S. Boye, Miriam Kimel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIndividuals with obesity or overweight experience a substantial socioemotional burden and lower quality of life. Existing patient-reported outcome (PRO) instruments do not adequately measure emotional functioning concepts important to individuals with obesity/overweight. This study describes the psychometric properties of the Weight and Emotions Scale (WES), a new PRO measure to evaluate emotional functioning in adults with obesity or overweight.Methods120 adults completed two web-based surveys (baseline, week 2). Survey 1 comprised the 16-item WES, Control of Eating Questionnaire, Impact of Weight on Quality of Life-Lite Clinical Trials Version, Impact of Weight on Self-Perceptions Questionnaire, and Patient Global Impression of Severity (PGIS) ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight.’ Survey 2 included the WES and PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight.’ Factor structure, reliability, and validity of the WES were assessed.ResultsExploratory factor analysis of the WES supported a two-factor structure as items loaded strongly (≥ 0.59) on factors corresponding to positive and negative feelings. The unidimensional framework was revised to include two WES domains (‘Positive Feelings’ [8 items] and ‘Negative Feelings’ [8 items]). The WES domain and total scores demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha ≥ 0.90) and good test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient: 0.87–0.89). Convergent validity of the WES was demonstrated via large correlations with the Impact of Weight on Quality of Life-Lite Clinical Trials Version ‘Psychosocial’ score and items, Impact of Weight on Self-Perceptions Questionnaire total score, and PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight.’ Known-groups validity was demonstrated via higher WES domain and total scores in participants who reported feeling good about their weight (PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight’).ConclusionThe final version of the WES with 16 items is a reliable and valid PRO measure for assessing emotional functioning in individuals with obesity or overweight, with potential utility in observational studies, clinical trials, and clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03615-2.

Introduction

Obesity is a complex disease, with 59% of adults worldwide living with obesity or overweight in 2022 [1]. Emotional functioning is a critical domain for understanding the lived experience of individuals with obesity and overweight, as the condition is frequently associated with a substantial socioemotional burden [2–5]. Emotional impacts related to obesity and overweight—such as diminished self-confidence, increased worry, frustration, and feelings of loss of control—can influence treatment engagement, treatment adherence, and overall health-related quality of life [2, 4, 6–11]. Measuring emotional functioning allows researchers and clinicians to understand how individuals with obesity or overweight experience their condition, respond to interventions, and undergo psychological change and to develop patient-centered care strategies. Patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures currently used in obesity research or clinical practice that capture emotional/psychological function include the Impact of Weight on Quality of Life-Lite Clinical Trials Version (IWQOL-Lite-CT), the Obesity and Weight Loss Quality of Life (OWLQOL) questionnaire [12], and the Short Form (SF)−36 health survey [13]. However, they do not fully capture emotional impact concepts relevant to individuals with obesity or overweight (e.g., feeling frustrated or emotionally drained or feeling hopeful, happy, or motivated associated with weight or weight reduction) [14–16]. Furthermore, certain concepts assessed by these existing measures, such as worrying about health or being frustrated with shopping for clothes, are not consistently associated with weight [17, 18].

The Weight and Emotions Scale (WES) is a new PRO measure to evaluate the impact of obesity or overweight and benefits of treatment on patients’ emotional functioning [19]. The WES was recently developed in alignment with the US Food and Drug Administration guidance for industry on PRO measures [20, 21] to evaluate weight-related emotional functioning in individuals with obesity or overweight. The WES was developed based on findings from exit interviews with adults living with obesity or overweight and ≥ 1 weight-related complication but without diabetes, enrolled in a phase 2 retatrutide trial; a targeted literature review of existing PRO measures to assess emotional functioning; and feedback from clinicians specializing in obesity medicine [19]. The relevance and content validity of the 16-item WES have been confirmed via cognitive interviews with adults living with obesity or with overweight and obesity-related complications [19]. The objective of this study was to evaluate the psychometric properties of the WES, including assessment of its item characteristics, factor structure, reliability, and validity in adults living with obesity or overweight and ≥ 1 obesity-related complication (i.e., hypertension, dyslipidemia, or cardiovascular disease).

Methods

Study Design

This was a quantitative, observational, web-based survey conducted in the US to assess the psychometric properties of the WES (Fig. 1). The study was conducted between December 2024 and February 2025. The study was approved by Salus IRB (Austin, Texas, USA) and conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices, and applicable national laws. The study did not involve the administration of any treatment. All participants provided electronic informed consent. Participants

Participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 30 kg/m2) or overweight (BMI ≥ 27 to < 30 kg/m2) and ≥ 1 obesity-related complication (i.e., hypertension, dyslipidemia, or cardiovascular disease) living in the US.

Prospective participants were identified through an online healthcare research survey panel via their proprietary database [22] and social media platforms (i.e., Facebook and Instagram). Prospective participants were contacted via email to solicit interest in participating in the study. Individuals interested in participating in the study completed a web-based eligibility screener with questions regarding their age, sex, race/ethnicity, geographical location, height, weight, obesity-related complications (e.g., type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease), and use of obesity management medications (OMMs). Individuals with overweight provided documentation confirming the diagnosis of obesity-related complications. Eligible individuals were asked to provide electronic informed consent before the survey began.

Study Instruments and Procedures

The WES comprises 16 items measuring positive and negative emotions associated with weight. The recall period for each item was the previous 7 days. Each item has five response options ranging from “never” (= 0) to “always” (= 4), with higher scores reflecting better outcomes for positive emotions (Items 1–8) and worse outcomes for negative emotions (Items 9–16). Negative emotion items are reverse-scored, and raw item scores are transformed to a 0 to 100 scale, with higher scores reflecting better emotional outcomes.

Participants completed two web-based surveys (i.e., Survey 1 at baseline and Survey 2 at the 2-week follow-up) (Fig. 1).

Survey 1 comprised a sociodemographic and health questionnaire and all PRO measures included in the study. Participants first completed the sociodemographic and health questionnaire, which included questions on age, education, employment, marital status, and household income, as well as questions regarding their overall health, obesity-related complications, how long they had been living with obesity or overweight, and current and past methods they used to manage their weight (Fig. 1). Participants then completed the PRO measures in the following order: the 16-item WES with 13 concepts pertaining to weight-related emotional functioning [19], the Control of Eating Questionnaire (CoEQ; ‘Positive Mood’ subscale) [23, 24], the IWQOL-Lite-CT [17, 18], Patient Global Impression of Severity (PGIS) ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight’ (a global assessment item developed specifically for this study), and Impact of Weight on Self-Perceptions Questionnaire (IW-SP) [25] (Fig. 1 and Table 1). Participants were asked to complete the survey in one session. Participants who completed Survey 1 were contacted 2 weeks later to complete Survey 2, which comprised the 16-item WES and the PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight’ (Fig. 1 and Table 1). Participants were required to complete Survey 2 within 4 days of receiving the survey link.

Statistical Analysis

A sample size of 120 participants was deemed sufficient to assess the internal and test–retest reliability of the WES, as well as to evaluate convergent and known-groups validity between the WES and other PRO measures included in the study. This sample size was also based on the recommendation of a sample size of ≥ 5 times the number of items in a PRO instrument for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) [26–28].

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS® version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, US). Participants’ sociodemographic and health characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. All PRO measures were scored according to the developers’ guidelines.

The methodology used for the psychometric validation of the WES has been previously reported [29].

Factor Analysis

Given that the unidimensional nature of the WES was based on an assumption, an EFA using weighted least squares mean and a variance-adjusted estimator with Geomin oblique rotation was used to examine the factor structure of items in the WES. The adequacy of model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), standardized mean square residual (SRMR), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). A CFI ≥ 0.90 and SRMR ≤ 0.08 were considered acceptable fits [30]. RMSEA values were deemed as a good fit (< 0.05), an acceptable fit (0.05–0.08), a marginal fit (0.08–0.1), or a poor fit (> 0.1) [31]. An item was considered for exclusion from the WES if data were missing for > 60% of the participants, the item-to-total correlation was < 0.40, or the factor loading for an item was < 0.40 on any factor or > 0.40 on > 1 factor.

Item and Domain Characteristics of the WES

The distributional characteristics of the WES item, domain, and total scores were evaluated using descriptive statistics. WES item responses and domain and total scores were evaluated for floor and ceiling effects (i.e., the percentage of responses at the lowest or highest possible score, respectively, that was selected by ≥ 25% of the participants [32]).

Item-to-item correlations (i.e., the extent to which scores on one item were related to scores on all other items in the WES) were estimated at baseline using Spearman’s correlations. Spearman’s correlations were interpreted as weak (r < 0.30), moderate (r ≥ 0.30 to < 0.70), or strong (r ≥ 0.70 to ≤ 0.90) [33]. Corrected item-total correlations (i.e., correlation between an item and the WES total score with that item removed) were used to assess internal consistency. Moderate or large correlations (i.e., r ≥ 0.30) were considered acceptable [34, 35].

Internal Consistency Reliability

Internal consistency reliability (i.e., the extent to which individual items in the WES were related to one another) was evaluated at baseline. Cronbach’s α coefficients were calculated for the WES total score. Cronbach’s α values were deemed acceptable (> 0.70), good (0.80–0.89), or excellent (≥ 0.90) [36].

Test-retest Reliability

Test-retest reliability was used to assess the stability and reproducibility of the WES scores between baseline and the 2-week follow-up. WES scores from the stable population (i.e., participants with the same score on PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight’ at baseline and the 2-week follow-up) were used to examine test–retest reliability. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were estimated based on absolute agreement and two-way mixed-effects analysis of variance models using time as a fixed effect [37]. ICCs ≥ 0.75 indicated good test-retest reliability [38].

Convergent and Divergent Validity

Convergent validity is demonstrated when a PRO measure correlates well with other PRO measures assessing similar or related concepts [20]. Convergent validity indicates that a PRO measure is effectively capturing the concept it is intended to measure by showing that it aligns well with other established PRO measures assessing similar concepts. Convergent validity was tested by estimating Spearman’s correlations with the CoEQ ‘Positive Mood’ subscale, IWQOL-Lite-CT ‘Psychosocial’ composite (Items 6–7, Items 9–11, and Items 18–20), PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight’, and IW-SP total score. These measures were chosen as they assess similar yet not identical concepts with the WES. For example, although the WES includes components of self-esteem that are measured in the IW-SP (e.g., self-consciousness associated with weight), it also includes other emotions that may influence self-esteem but are not components of self-esteem, such as feeling embarrassed about current weight. Divergent validity is inferred through weaker correlations between unrelated concepts and was tested by estimating Spearman’s correlations between the WES and the IWQOL-Lite-CT ‘Physical’ and ‘Physical Function’ composites (Tables 1 and S1).

Spearman’s correlations between the WES and the selected PRO measures were calculated at baseline. The hypothesized correlations between the WES and other PRO measures are summarized in Table S1. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were deemed small (r ≥ 0.10), moderate (r ≥ 0.30), or large (r ≥ 0.50) [39].

Known-groups Validity

Known-groups validity [20] of the WES—sensitivity to differentiate among groups of participants known to be different on a relevant parameter—was assessed by comparing the WES scores based on PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight.’ Mean scores on the WES were compared between each subgroup using analysis of variance with Scheffé’s correction for post hoc pairwise comparisons [40]. The analysis of variance models included WES scores at baseline as the dependent variable and the known-groups measure at the corresponding time point as the independent variable. Participants who frequently reported feeling good about their current weight overall (i.e., had higher scores on PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight’) were expected to score significantly higher on the WES total score.

Results

Participants

One hundred twenty individuals participated in the study (mean age, 53.0 years; BMI 39.8 kg/m2). Two-thirds (n = 80, 66.7%) were female, and one-third (n = 40, 33.3%) were male. Most participants were white (n = 75, 62.5%) and not Hispanic or Latino (n = 109, 90.8%). Participants reported living with obesity (n = 92, 76.7%) or overweight (n = 28, 23.3%) at the time of the survey (BMI 27.3–93.7 kg/m2). More than half of the participants reported that they had been living with obesity for > 15 years (n = 65, 54.2%). Most participants reported using diet (n = 115, 95.8%) and exercise (n = 100, 83.3%) and less than half reported using medication (n = 50, 41.7%) to manage their weight in the past. At the time of the survey, participants reported that they were using diet (n = 101, 84.2%), exercise (n = 82, 68.3%), and medication (n = 35, 29.2%) to manage their weight. Twenty-nine participants (24.2%) reported that they were receiving treatment with OMMs.

Many participants reported having obesity-related complications, such as hypertension (n = 92, 76.7%), high cholesterol (n = 78, 65.0%), sleep apnea (n = 55, 45.8%), arthritis (n = 53, 44.2%), and type 2 diabetes (n = 48, 40.0%). Participants also reported experiencing anxiety (n = 61, 50.8%), depression (n = 59, 49.2%), and other mental health conditions (n = 23, 19.2%).

All 120 participants completed Survey 1 at baseline. One hundred sixteen participants (96.7%) completed Survey 2 at the 2-week follow-up. Participants required a mean (standard deviation [SD]) time of 20.2 (23.7) min to complete Survey 1 and 6.1 (6.8) min to complete the shorter Survey 2.

Psychometric Validation of the WES

Distributional Characteristics of the WES Items

Participants used the full range of responses (“never,” “rarely,” “sometimes,” “often,” or “always”) for all WES items except Item 2 (‘In control of my current weight’), for which none of the participants selected “always” at baseline (Table S2).

Eight items in the WES showed a floor effect (i.e., ≥ 25% of the sample selected ‘never’) at baseline and the 2-week follow-up. Seven of these items were positive emotion items: Item 1 (‘Happy about my current weight’), Item 2 (‘In control of my current weight’), Item 4 (‘Optimistic about my health because of my current weight’), Item 5 (‘Hopeful about my health because of my current weight’), Item 6 (‘Good about my appearance because of my current weight’), Item 7 (‘Confident in myself because of my current weight’), and Item 8 (‘Confident about being physically intimate because of my current weight’). The eighth item was a negative emotion item (Item 16, ‘Treated poorly by others because of my current weight’). For items showing floor effects, the percentage of participants selecting “never” ranged between 30.8% (Item 4: ‘Optimistic about my health because of my current weight’) and 49.2% at baseline (Item 1: ‘Happy about my current weight’). Similar results were seen at the 2-week follow-up.

None of the positive emotion items (Items 1–8) showed a ceiling effect (i.e., ≥ 25% of the sample selected ‘always’) at baseline or 2-week follow-up. Three negative emotion items showed a ceiling effect at baseline. These items included Item 10 (‘Worried about my health because of my current weight’), Item 11 (‘Frustrated because of my current weight), and Item 13 (‘Embarrassed because of my current weight’). At the 2-week follow-up, only Item 11 (‘Frustrated because of my current weight’) showed a ceiling effect. For items showing ceiling effects, the percentage of participants who endorsed “always” ranged between 26.7% at baseline (Item 10: ‘Worried about my health because of my current weight’) and 35.0% at baseline (Item 11: ‘Frustrated because of my current weight’).

Factor Analysis

An EFA supported a two-factor structure (CFI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.143, SRMR = 0.067), with items loading strongly (≥ 0.59) on factors corresponding to positive and negative feelings about weight (Fig. 2). Items 1–8 (i.e., items corresponding to positive feelings about weight) had higher factor 1 loadings (range: 0.59–0.99), and Items 9–18 (i.e., items corresponding to negative feelings about weight) demonstrated higher factor 2 loadings (range: 0.72–0.96) (inter-factor correlation: 0.61). The two-factor model for the WES had a better fit than the one-factor model (CFI = 0.89, RMSEA = 0.201, SRMR = 0.138) and was supported by CFI, SRMR, and strong factor loadings. The initial unidimensional conceptual framework of the WES was therefore revised to two domains corresponding to positive and negative feelings about weight (Fig. 2). The revised WES included 16 items across the ‘Positive Feelings’ (8 items) and ‘Negative Feelings’ (8 items) domains.

Item-to-item Correlations

Overall, item-to-item correlations were moderate (or close to moderate) to strong between similarly valenced items (i.e., items that reflect the same type of emotional tone, such as positive or negative feelings) in the WES (Table S3). Spearman’s correlations ranged from 0.29 to 0.86 for items reflecting positive feelings and from 0.33 to 0.84 for items reflecting negative feelings. Item-to-item correlations ranged from 0.08 to 0.63 for oppositely valenced items (i.e., items that reflect opposite emotional tones) in the WES, and most correlations were > 0.30.

Corrected Item-Domain and Item-Total Correlations for the WES

Corrected item-domain correlations for the ‘Positive Feelings’ and ‘Negative Feelings’ domains in the WES indicated moderate-to-strong relationships between each item and each domain (r: 0.43–0.83) (Table S4). Corrected item-total correlations were moderate to strong (r: 0.31–0.80) and support internal consistency of the WES domain and total scores (Table S5).

WES Domains

In general, participants used the full range of responses for the items in the ‘Positive Feelings’ and ‘Negative Feelings’ domains of the WES at baseline and the 2-week follow-up. The mean (SD) ‘Positive Feelings’ and ‘Negative Feelings’ domain scores were 28.7 (20.3) (range: 0.0–78.1) and 43.1 (23.2) (range: 0.0–93.8), respectively (Table S6). The mean (SD) total score for the WES was 35.9 (19.5) (range: 0.0–81.3). The WES domain and total scores at the 2-week follow-up were similar to the scores at baseline. No ceiling or floor effects were observed for the WES domain or total scores at either timepoint.

Reliability

Internal Consistency Reliability

Cronbach’s α was 0.90 for the ‘Positive Feelings’ domain, 0.92 for the ‘Negative Feelings’ domain, and 0.93 for the WES total score, indicating excellent internal consistency (Table S7). Cronbach’s α values with items removed for the WES domain and total scores ranged between 0.88 and 0.94, demonstrating good-to-excellent internal consistency reliability.

Test-Retest Reliability

The WES total and domain scores had good test-retest reliability (ICC: 0.87–0.89) (Table S8). The mean differences in scores between baseline and the 2-week follow-up were small (range: – 1.3 to 2.7) and not statistically significant.

Known-Groups Validity

Known-groups validity for the WES was confirmed at baseline based on groups defined by PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight’ (Fig. S1–S3). Overall, the mean total scores for the WES were higher (i.e., reflected better emotional functioning) in participants who reported feeling good about their current weight on the PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight’ (Fig. S1). The ‘Positive Feelings’ and ‘Negative Feelings’ domain scores were also higher (i.e., reflected more frequent positive feelings about current weight or less frequent negative feelings about current weight, respectively) in participants who scored higher on PGIS ‘Overall Emotional Impact of Weight’ (Figs. S2 and S3).

Discussion

Individuals living with obesity or overweight experience considerable socioemotional burden that has a negative impact on their health-related quality of life [2, 11, 41, 42]. Encouragingly, weight reduction through dietary modifications or OMMs has been associated with improvements in patients’ health-related quality of life and emotional functioning (e.g., improved self-esteem, body image, and sexual functioning, decreased depression and anxiety) [2, 43–47]. The WES is a new PRO measure developed to comprehensively assess weight-related emotional functioning in individuals living with obesity or overweight; unlike other measures, it captures the range of emotions (i.e., both positive and negative) associated with weight and weight loss. The measure is easy to complete and score for use in both clinical practice and research [14, 19]. The present study evaluated the factor structure, internal consistency, test-retest reliability, convergent validity, divergent validity, and known-groups validity of the 16-item WES. Participants provided a wide range of responses for the WES at baseline and the 2-week follow-up. Seven positively worded items and one negatively worded item (Items 1, 2, 4–8, and 16) showed a floor effect at both timepoints. Three negatively worded items showed a ceiling effect at baseline (Items 10 and 13) or at baseline and the 2-week follow-up (Item 13). In general, the floor and ceiling effect findings suggest there is room for improvement in these items with treatment. In addition, neither the ‘Positive Feelings’ nor ‘Negative Feelings’ domain demonstrated floor or ceiling effects, suggesting that there is variability in participant ratings across items within each domain (i.e., there was a spread of responses across the 8 items within each domain where “never” was not selected uniformly for all or most items within the domain).

As the EFA results showed a superior fit on a two-factor model, the initial unidimensional framework of the WES was revised to two domains corresponding to positive and negative feelings about weight: a ‘Positive Feelings’ domain (8 items) and a ‘Negative Feelings’ domain (8 items). The WES domain and total scores demonstrated excellent internal consistency, as well as good test-retest reliability and construct validity, demonstrating relationships of expected magnitude with other PROs that measure similar but not identical aspects of emotional functioning. The large correlations between the WES total score and the IWQOL-Lite-CT ‘Physical’ composite, ‘Physical Function’ composite, and ‘total score’ were larger than expected. Validation studies for the IWQOL-Lite-CT showed moderate correlations with the SF-36 Mental Health scale [17, 18]. The strong correlations between these measures may be due to how adults with obesity or overweight think about their emotions in terms of their weight. That is, when thinking about the different emotions they experience associated with weight, participants may consider how their weight affects their physical functioning more than anticipated. Consistent with this, previous literature has reported worsening physical function with obesity and an association between physical and emotional health/quality of life [48–50].

Results from the psychometric analyses show that the 16-item WES is a reliable and valid measure for assessing emotional functioning in observational studies and clinical trials involving patients with obesity or overweight.

Existing PRO measures used to evaluate health-related quality of life in individuals with obesity or overweight do not adequately assess aspects of patients’ emotional functioning [14, 19]. Furthermore, the core set of PRO measures recommended by Dijkhorst et al. for use in clinical practice or obesity research to evaluate the quality of life in individuals with obesity or overweight mainly included instruments with emotional impact concepts, such as patients’ perceptions of self-esteem (IWQOL-Lite), body image (BODY-Q), and psychological function (BODY-Q) [15, 16]. The WES is the first comprehensive PRO measure that can be used to evaluate a broad range of emotional functioning concepts (i.e., both positive and negative feelings associated with obesity or overweight) considered relevant by individuals living with obesity or overweight.

Most participants completed both surveys. Only four (3.3%) of the participants who completed Survey 1 did not complete Survey 2. However, this study used a convenience sample recruited through a proprietary database and via online health platforms. Therefore, the findings from this study may not be representative of the general population and may be limited by digital literacy bias stemming from access to and ability to navigate digital platforms, since those who are not computer-literate may have been inadvertently excluded. Nonetheless, the study sample was diverse in terms of sex [51], race/ethnicity, annual household income, and level of education, and included patients with type 2 diabetes, as well as individuals with obesity or overweight who were receiving treatment with OMMs. The ability of the WES to detect change in emotional functioning with treatment or over time (i.e., responsiveness) can be examined in future research.

Conclusion

Emotional functioning is a critical domain in understanding the socioemotional burden experienced by individuals with obesity or overweight. Results from this psychometric analysis study show that the final 16-item version of the WES is a reliable and valid PRO measure for evaluating emotional functioning in these individuals. These findings suggest the WES may be used in observational studies, clinical trials, and clinical practice to assess the emotional impact of obesity or overweight and its treatment on individuals living with obesity or overweight.
