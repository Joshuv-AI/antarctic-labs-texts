Preliminary adaptation of cognitive behavioral therapy for major depression in the Bangladeshi context: a pilot study
Authors: Jesan Ara, Farah Deeba
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cultural values and standard social rules of communities strongly influence any psychological therapy. Being developed and modified in the Western world, cognitive behavior therapy (CBT) is not anyway different from such influences. In this study, CBT was adapted in Bengali to determine the functional feasibility of the psychological intervention for people with depression in Bangladesh. This is the first pilot study of an adapted CBT manual for psychological intervention for working with depressive clients in Bangladesh. There were three phases of the study. In the first two phases, the standard protocols of CBT were adapted for the Bangladeshi community, as practiced by mainstream practitioners and mental health professionals around the world. The information gathered during the first phase of the standard protocols of CBT was written in Bengali as the first draft. In the second phase, the CBT manual was judged by seven senior clinical psychologists in Bangladesh who were trained in CBT by British clinical psychologists and later provided training to fellow mental health professionals. After the judges’ evaluation, their comments and suggestions were synthesized and the first draft of the adapted intervention was finalized. In the third phase, to evaluate the functional feasibility of the Bengali CBT-based manual, five patients diagnosed with major depression by psychiatrists were tested. Four valid and reliable psychometric measurements were used to measure symptoms of depression and comorbid symptoms of anxiety, psychological distress, and suicidal risk. Assessments were completed at 2 time points (pretest and posttest) via scales. The intervention was delivered within 6 to 10 sessions, and improvements were monitored over the scores of all the questionnaires. The possibilities of Bengali CBTs to reduce depression and appear to be promising treatment options for depression are discussed in this paper.

Introduction

It is a well-understood fact now that culture and communities make a large difference in how an individual perceives others, the world, or themselves and play a significant role in developing psychological problems [1]. Cultural factors also affect the assessment and treatment of psychological disorders. Therefore, any psychological tool or therapy requires adaptation to be appropriate for the population of interest [2–4]. Cultural factors need to be taken into account, as culture is a complex phenomenon and a “dynamic process” rather than a static entity or event, and our understanding of principles might need consideration while adapting psychotherapy, particularly cognitive behavior therapy (CBT) [3]. It is well known that culture plays a significant role in the development of depressive symptoms; for example, in a study of Asian patients, more somatic than psychological symptoms were reported in India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh [4]. In many Asian cultures, mental health issues are stigmatized, deterring individuals from seeking help [3, 4]. In contrast, some indigenous communities integrate mental health with spiritual and communal practices, favoring holistic healing [2–4]. These differences highlight the need for culturally specific interventions.

Depression is the most common multifaceted health issue and a serious condition among various other mental health problems that affect people in all nations and across cultures [5, 6]. The impact of depression is multidimensional, but the vital concerns of depression are suicide and increased risk of mortality [7–9]. There is enough evidence suggesting that CBT is a wide-ranging scientific treatment approach and can be useful in treating depression [10, 11]. CBT has evolved from a specific treatment model into a scientific approach that incorporates a wide variety of disorder-specific interventions and treatment techniques [12]. It is a universally classified psychotherapeutic approach that seeks to solve psychological or neurotic disorders, and behavior problems concerning dysfunctional cognitions, behavior, and emotions through a goal-oriented systematic procedure [13]. Research findings also suggest that CBT might be as effective as medication in treating moderate to severe depressive illness(es), especially in the initial phases of depressive illness [10]. There is also strong evidence for the effectiveness of CBT in the treatment and relapse prevention of depression [2, 14–16].

In the treatment process, cultural factors should be considered because the application of psychotherapy, such as beliefs about the nature of illness and the likely effectiveness of interventions, may vary [14, 15, 17]. Despite this evidence in Western countries, inadequate movement has been made to adapt CBT in low- and middle-income countries. The use of CBTs developed for one culture without modifications for another presents significant challenges and limitations [3, 4]. Researchers suggest that any psychotherapeutic model developed in a different context needs to be used in another context after some modification [18, 19]. CBT often involves assessing and attempting to modify core beliefs and negative automatic thoughts [1, 3]. However, it may not always align with different cultural beliefs and practices, which can reduce its effectiveness and engagement in patients [17, 19]. For example, being developed in Western culture CBT tends to overlook the spiritual and communal aspects that are crucial to mental health in some non-Western cultures [2–4]. This highlights the need for culturally adapted interventions to ensure that treatments are relevant and effective for diverse populations.

Research indicates that individuals with depressive illnesses typically hold unsupportive beliefs about themselves, others, and the world [1, 20]. Additionally, the international literature suggests that adapted CBT within a particular population has been found effective, for instance, American Indians, African Americans, and populations in Iran and Pakistan [3, 21–23]. These findings underscore the importance of the current study in evaluating and improving the cultural relevance of CBT for various groups.

Almost all the physical, psychological, and social functions of depressed individuals are often reduced, and they visit medical centers more often than people with other major chronic medical problems do [24–26]. Unfortunately, even over a very long period, it has been warned by experts about the seriousness of depression [24, 27], and little attention has been given to treating mental health problems effectively in developing countries, such as Bangladesh [24]. The overall prevalence of psychiatric disorders in Bangladesh is 16.50%, and among them, 4.60 − 8.00% suffer from depressive disorders [24, 28, 29]. The social stigmas associated with mental disorders, a lack of resources, a lack of trained professionals, and other factors work as barriers to changing scenarios in the country [30, 31]. In Bangladesh, cultural factors also affect the assessment and treatment of depression [30–32]. As there is no standard format of psychological intervention based on testing, the mental health profession and quality of service delivery are at risk. However, psychologists and mental health professionals are taking training on various Western psychological interventions in Bangladesh by various trainers from other cultures and these interventions are being conducted by practitioners in their own ways [33, 34]. Therefore, an attempt was made to adapt the most effective treatment for depression in Bangladesh.

Methodology

The study was conducted in three phases. The initial phase of this pilot study aimed to develop a CBT treatment protocol in Bengali for people experiencing depression and carefully included cultural issues, psychoeducation, and personal CBT experiences related to depression to prepare them to work collaboratively in a psychotherapeutic alliance. The second phase was for the experts to evaluate the translated adapted version and the third phase involved a pilot study. This study was conducted with five participants diagnosed with major depression to determine the functional feasibility of a Bengali CBT-based treatment manual in wider population settings in Bangladesh.

Intervention and cultural adaptation

The treatment method, while remaining structurally parallel to that given by Beck et al. (1979), aims to incorporate a range of adaptations, as recommended by the adapted CBT manual in non-Western countries, and evaluation by judges as part of the protocol development [20]. Adaptations of the manual specific to the Bangladeshi culture were conducted in three phases following the framework of Barrera and Castro [35].

First phase (information gathering)

A Bengali manual for CBT with depressive clients was initially adopted following the internationally accepted current evidence-based psychological intervention. First, the researcher communicated with the Beck Institution for a standard CBT manual in September 2016 (M. Finkel, personal communication, Sep 18, 2016). According to the Beck Institution the steps and strategies described in the book titled “Cognitive Therapy: Basics and Beyond” is the standard for treating patients with depression, and the researcher therefore reviewed the book of J. Beck [1]. In addition, the researcher reviewed manuals on CBT and psychological interventions that were available for multicultural communities [36–39] and the current literature on CBT adaptation and manuals in developing countries [17, 40, 41]. At the same time, information was gathered from personal experiences of CBT with depressed patients [30, 32], and evidence-based information was gathered about the nature of depressive disorders, relevant demographics, assessments, and interventions in Bangladesh [42–44]. A questionnaire survey of 40 CBT practitioners in Bangladesh revealed various treatment barriers, including practice-oriented, patient-centered factors such as motivation, social system, therapeutic relationship, and specific challenges in implementing CBT for psychological disorders [34]. The information gathered from the above elementary stages, as well as the first author’s own experiences of therapy and clinical practices, was organized to develop a guide to the process of CBT adaptation. At this stage, the first draft of the Bengali CBT manual, drawn from Judith S. Beck’s (1995) “Cognitive Therapy: Basics and Beyond” [1], was written, and a psychoeducation section and motivational interviewing (MI) were added. MI was implemented during initial interviews and assessments. Therapists used open-ended questions, reflective listening, and affirmations to explore participants’ perspectives on their depression and treatment goals, all these Bengali materials are described in another publication of the authors [30]. Specific MI techniques were included in exploring participants’ values and goals, religion, challenges, and metaphors; discussing the pros and cons of change; and addressing any resistance to participating in CBT [30, 32, 33] as per cultural norms. This helped adapt the CBT manual more suited to the individual’s needs and cultural background.

Second phase (professional evaluation)

The first draft of the Bengali CBT manual was given to seven experts for review based on their opinions and experience. Group I included two clinical psychologists who had more than 15 years of CBT experience, were trained by a British psychologist, and had a strong understanding of mental health needs in Bangladesh. In group II, there were three professionals (with more than 10 years of experience with CBT and who had taken training from a first-generation CBT practitioner); in group III, there were two professionals (with more than 5 years of experience with CBT and who had taken training from a second-generation CBT practitioner). The final draft of the adapted manual was prepared according to their suggestions. Their recommendations included adding culturally relevant examples, integrating additional techniques such as psychoeducation, enhancing practitioner training on cultural competence, changing negative thoughts, behavioral activation, problem-solving, and communication skills, and establishing detailed documentation and monitoring guidelines. Implementing these recommendations helped to ensure that the intervention was comprehensive, culturally sensitive, and effective [33].

The content validity ratio (CVR) of the Bengali CBT manual was calculated based on professionals’ ratings. The I-CVR values for individual chapters ranged from 0.7 to 1.0, reflecting different levels of perceived relevance among the professionals. The overall scale CVR (S-CVI/Ave) was determined to be 0.85, indicating that, on average, 85% of the judges found the chapters relevant. This high S-CVI/Ave score suggests that the manual possesses good content validity. Psychoeducation

Educational materials were developed in Bengali and incorporated culturally relevant examples to enhance understanding and engagement. For example, depression is explained via familiar concepts, such as ‘kalo megh’ (dark cloud), ‘jibon ei nodir moton’ (life is like a river, with its ups and downs) affecting daily life and represent the flow of emotions making it easier for individuals to understand and relate to their symptoms.

Behavioral activation

Culturally relevant activities were incorporated by traditional and community activities, such as participating in local festivals or family traditions.

Problem-solving: Culturally familiar problem-solving approaches, such as community-based discussions and collective problem-solving methods, were applied. This approach respected cultural norms and integrated familiar methods for addressing issues, guiding individuals through structured problem-solving steps.

Using metaphor

Local folk tales, stories, and proverbs were used to illustrate CBT concepts. A metaphor for depression, such as ‘cholar pothe onek kada’ (heavy mud in the pathway) illustrates how depression creates obstacles and challenges that slow progress and make daily tasks feel burdensome and unmanageable. The story of ‘bash jhar’ (bamboo bush) was also used to illustrate resilience. Just as the bamboo bends and adapts during a storm rather than breaking, the client can navigate through the ‘mud’ of depression by finding ways to adapt and persist. This story highlights that while the path may be challenging now, resilience and effort can eventually help clear the way, making progress possible. The idea of ‘planting positive thoughts’ or ‘removing weeds of negative thinking’ was also used to illustrate local experiences, especially in rural areas where agriculture is a central part of life.

Religious and spiritual practices

Religion, culture, and spiritual practices have contributed to the development of myths and stigma attached to mental illness. Many people in Bangladesh believe that neglecting religious practices can make people depressed [30]. To address this, local religious and spiritual practices were integrated into the therapy to ensure alignment with clients’ spiritual beliefs. For example, when suicidal thoughts are addressed, a client’s belief that ‘attohotta moha pap’ (suicide is a great sin) is used as alternative thinking for a patient who prioritizes religion as their explanation of depressive thoughts. This belief helps counter feelings of hopelessness by providing a spiritually grounded reason to find hope and seek help.

Respect for hierarchical and gender roles

Adapted the communication styles and therapeutic approaches to express respect and honor as per traditional age related hierarchical and gender roles. For example, in prioritizing elderly individuals, addressing a person in gender-specific norms in treatment plans was cared for and integrated.

Festivals and cultural events

Goals were set or activities were structured around major festivals such as Pohela Boishakh (Bengali New Year) or Eid. For example, the themes of forgiveness and new beginnings, often emphasized during Eid, were integrated to help clients work through past hurts and focus on moving forward.

Use of Bengali Language

Incorporating Bengali terminology and phrases where possible and appropriate, both in general session discussions and in CBT homework forms (e.g., activity schedules, and thought records) were done.

In the third phase (preliminary test of the adapted treatment and adaptation refinement)

The manual was finalized for further randomized control trial. In this phase, the intervention was pilot-tested to evaluate the functional feasibility of the adapted Bengali CBT treatment manual.

Participants

A total of five (three female and two male) participants diagnosed with major depression were selected for the sample in this study who were referred from the two hospitals in Dhaka, Bangladesh. The exclusion criteria included those who had psychotic symptoms, severe mental illness, an intellectual disability, or an inability to comprehend simple instructions. The flowchart of the participants is shown in Fig. 1. The diagnosis of major depression was confirmed by the psychiatrists of the hospitals according to the criteria of the DSM-V (APA, 2013). All participants identified as adequate for the study received a consent letter with information about the research. Written consent from the participants was obtained before starting the intervention with them. The mean age was 25.6 years (SD = 9.0). All but one participant was single, one had completed H.S.C., three had completed their bachelor’s degree, and one had completed postgraduate degree. CBT was provided by the first author.

Procedure

The task of the first final draft of the Bengali CBT adaptation was carried out between October 2016 and August 2018. The intervention was pilot-tested via a pre-post test research design. Ethical approval was received from the Faculty of Biological Science (Ref-49/Biol.Scs./2017–2018), University of Dhaka. Throughout the study, the first author (researcher) received weekly supervision from the second author (supervisor) on conducting the sessions according to the manual so that necessary modifications could be made.

Assessments

The participants were assessed at baseline and 3 months (end of the intervention) after baseline. The following self-rating measures of depression and co-morbid psychiatric problems were used as part of a larger study.

Depression scale (DS)

To measure symptoms of depression, participants were given the DS [45] to complete. The scale is frequently used by researchers and mental health professionals for research [33, 46, 47]. It consists of 30 items with a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (rarely) to 5 (always). The minimum obtained score is 30, and the maximum is 150. The validity of this scale was satisfactory, and the split-half reliability range was 0.76. The construct validity of the scale was satisfactory.

Anxiety scale (AS)

To measure symptoms of anxiety, participants were given the AS [48]. It consists of 36 items with a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very much). The minimum score achieved is 0, and the maximum score achieved is 144. The split-half reliability of the scale was 0.92, and the Cronbach’s alpha reliability was 0.99. The content and construct validity of the scale were found to be satisfactory.

General health questionnaire (GHQ-28)

To measure psychological distress, participants completed the GHQ-28 [49]. The GHQ-28 was translated into Bangla for use with the Bangladeshi population by Banoo (2001). This 28-item checklist ranges from 0 to 3, and the maximum possible scale score is 84. The test-retest reliability was 0.68 [50].

Beck scale for suicidal ideation (BSS)

To measure the risk of suicide in the participants, BSS [51] was used. Bangla BSS [52] was adapted for use with the Bangladeshi population. It consists of 21 items with ratings ranging from 0 to 2, which are rated on a 3-point Likert scale. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability of the Bangla version of the BSS was 0.83. Five types of validity (content, concurrent, construct, discriminant, and factorial) of the scale were found to be satisfactory. The BSS [52] is also frequently used in Bangladesh for multiple studies [32, 33].

Intervention

The fundamental principles of CBT include recognizing the depressive symptoms as part of the overall care, building a supportive alliance with others (significant others or relevant professionals), addressing negative automatic thoughts, establishing realistic and attainable goals, and focusing on sustaining progress. Hence the manual included comprehensive educational materials and guidelines for each of the six to ten sessions, anticipating active participation from attendees. Key components of our adapted Bengali CBT manual include cognitive appraisal, use of metaphors, psychoeducation, problem-solving techniques, crisis management, and promoting effective self-care practices [33].

In each session, homework such as thought records, activity diaries, and positive affirmations was evaluated and discussed. During the first session, the foundational concepts of CBT were introduced within the framework of psychoeducation on psychological distress due to depression and related other problems, e.g. anxiety symptoms. Participants engaged in exercises to identify their thoughts, emotions, physical responses, and behaviors related to distressing situations. They were introduced to the concept of thought records and motivational interviewing was used to encourage them to practice. The second session provided an overview of concepts on depression, while the third session focused on mastering the identification of negative automatic thoughts and cognitive restructuring [33].

Subsequent sessions covered problem-solving, distraction techniques, and other CBT techniques to address depressive symptoms. Participants also learned behavioral strategies, including relaxation and controlled breathing. They were encouraged to maintain activity diaries to reflect on how they spent their time, the enjoyment they derived from various activities, and their sense of accomplishment, as well as to identify potential stressors. Activity diaries were reviewed starting from the second session, with participants motivated to incorporate additional activities into their schedules, where fitting, to boost their sense of mastery and enjoyment. The discussion covered the concepts of negative automatic thoughts and symptoms of depression, along with effective cognitive and behavioral strategies. Patients received psychoeducation aimed at boosting their self-esteem and sense of mastery during the sessions throughout. The final session included a review of the intervention and a summary of the educational topics covered in earlier sessions [33]. Experiences with thought records, activity diaries, relaxation training, and the application of positive affirmations were revisited and discussed among the participants.

Statistical analysis

Since the sample size was too small nonparametric tests were used for all measurements to verify data distribution. Therefore, we computed the mean scores at two time points, and changes from the pretest to all posttests were assessed via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Additionally, within-group effect sizes were calculated at posttest via Cohen’s d = M1 – M2/spooled [53].

Feasibility

The high session attendance indicated the feasibility of the Bengali-CBT manual. All the participants found the adapted version clear and understandable. Significant barriers to participation were continuous attendance, and time overlap with the clinical psychologist made it difficult to arrange a suitable session time. At the end of treatment, participants were asked which treatment components were helpful. Most participants (n = 4) reported that techniques such as the homework, problem-solving, using positive statements, talking about their sadness, and thought challenges were helpful. Additionally, most of the participants also recommended making the intervention accessible to others experiencing similar problems.

Discussion

As far as we are aware this is the first pilot study investigated the feasibility of a culturally adapted Bengali CBT for people with depression in Bangladesh. Our result indicates significant reductions in depression and anxiety, accompanied by improvements in psychological distress and suicidal ideations. These results are consistent with studies on CBT for depressive disorders in non-western countries (e.g., Hwang et al., 2015; Naeem et al., 2015) [17, 41]. It is imperative that the favorable outcomes are due to high session attendance and improvements in outcome measures [17, 54, 55]. It has been suggested that this improvements may be linked to the use of locally relevant metaphors, cultural stories, respecting age-related hierarchical roles, psychoeducation, family involvement, and community-based activities which helped participants to connect emotionally and cognitively with the therapeutic process and change their cognitions and behaviors as per goals of CBT [22, 56–58].

The findings of our study support the efficacy of CBT in reducing depression [14, 16, 57–59], since all the participants in this research included only those who did not take medication, and at post-intervention assessment showed improvement in scores of the measure of depression (see Table 2). The strategies of Bengali-CBT helped them adjust their thoughts to assist adaptive coping, distress, and alleviate depressive symptoms [60]. Our results further showed a good percentage of comorbid anxiety (85.6%) with primary depression, and there was a significant reduction in comorbid symptoms (anxiety, distress and suicidal thoughts) as well [53–55]. This is particularly evident when CBT is tailored to address culturally specific irrational negative thoughts, such as hopelessness, low self-esteem, or suicide, leading to improvements in both depression and anxiety symptoms, ultimately reducing mean scores on measurement scales [3, 61, 62].

Consequently, the GHQ-28 is significantly correlated with depression and anxiety, thus representing somatic symptoms of depression [2, 54, 61]. There is important evidence that CBT can improve health-promoting behaviors by modifying dysfunctional attitudes, changing risky lifestyles, and breaking harmful habits [54, 62]. This becomes particularly relevant when addressing suicide risk in Bengali CBT, which may require incorporating a specific component, focused on suicidal ideation. Additionally, the use of culturally relevant stories, metaphors, and spiritual beliefs (such as ‘attohotta moha pap’), may have played a role in instilling hope reducing hopelessness, and decreasing suicidal thoughts [30, 63].

The present pilot study has several limitations that should be mentioned. The first limitation of this study is the small sample size, absence of follow-up assessment and the uncontrolled pre-post feasibility design. Therefore, detailed analyses and the variables that could influence therapy have not been well studied. Another limitation is that the study was conducted to one geographical area in Bangladesh; hence, these results may not be generalizable to other regions, and external validity is severely threatened. We are planning a larger trial to address these issues [33]. Third, adaptation was not based on systematic qualitative analyses of interviews but rather on the status of the current research, judge evaluation, personal experiences, and data from the interviews with individual participants. Fourth, we did not assess treatment integrity, although weekly supervision was given by the second author, and both authors had several years of experience in CBT. Despite the methodological limitations, the results of this pilot study are encouraging and indicate the functional feasibility of the adapted CBT manual in Bengali.

Conclusion

This culturally adapted CBT in Bangladesh ensures that therapeutic concepts are relatable and effective by incorporating local metaphors, community-based activities, family involvement, religious practices, and culturally relevant stories. It respects hierarchical and gender roles, uses localized psychoeducation materials, encourage community support networks, and align therapy with cultural festivals. This adaptation enhances the relevance of CBT and fosters a supportive environment, offering a comprehensive and culturally sensitive approach to mental health care.
