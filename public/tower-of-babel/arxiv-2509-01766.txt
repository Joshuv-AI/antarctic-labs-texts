Abstract
. In an r r -coloring of edges of the complete graph on n n vertices, how many edges are there in the largest monochromatic connected component? Conlon, Luo, and Tyomkyn proved this conjecture for r = 3 r=3 and Luo proved it for r = 4 r=4 , along with a lower bound of 1 r 2 − r + 5 4 ​ ( n 2 ) \frac{1}{r^{2}-r+\frac{5}{4}}{n\choose 2} for all r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 and n n . 1.
Introduction

A classical observation of Erdős and Rado is that in any 2-coloring of the edges of the complete graph
K_{n}
, one of the two color classes consists of a single connected component on all
n
vertices.
Gyárfás
[
4
]
extended this result, proving that for
r\geq 2
, in any
r
-coloring of the edges of
K_{n}
, there is
a monochromatic connected component with at least
\frac{n}{r-1}
vertices. He also exhibited a construction showing that this bound is tight for
arbitrarily large
n
and infinitely many values of
r
.

Since then, Gyárfás’s result has been further generalized in many different ways. One notable example is due to Bal and DeBiasio
[
1
]
, who showed that a similar result holds when
K_{n}
is replaced by an Erdős-Rényi random graph
G(n,p)
, which is a random graph on
n
vertices where each pair of vertices is adjacent with an independent probability of
p
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

For all
r\geq 2
and sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C
such that for
p\geq\frac{C}{n}
, with high probability every
r
-coloring of
G(n,p)
contains a monochromatic connected component with at least
(1-\varepsilon)\frac{n}{r-1}
vertices.

We can also ask analogous questions about the largest number of
edges
in a monochromatic component.

Question 1.2
.

If the edges of
K_{n}
are colored with
r
colors, what is the largest number of edges that some monochromatic component is guaranteed to have?

This question was first studied by Conlon and Tyomkyn
[
2
]
, who showed that in the case
r=2
, every such coloring contains a monochromatic component with at least
\frac{2}{9}n^{2}+O(n^{3/2})
edges. Later work by Luo
[
5
]
and by Conlon, Luo, and Tyomkyn
[
3
]
resolved the cases
r=3
and
r=4
respectively, showing that when the edges of
K_{n}
are colored with
3
(resp.
4
) colors, there always exists a monochromatic connected component with at least
\frac{1}{6}\binom{n}{2}
(resp.
\frac{1}{12}\binom{n}{2}
) edges, and that these bounds are tight.

The lower bounds in the
r=3
and
r=4
cases match the upper bounds arising from a close examination of Gyárfás’s construction, which shows that for infinitely many values of
r
(in particular, whenever
r-1
is a prime power), there is an
r
-coloring of
K_{n}
where every monochromatic component has at most
(\frac{1}{r(r-1)}+o(1))\binom{n}{2}
edges. Conlon, Luo, and Tyomkyn conjectured that a matching lower bound holds for every value of
r
.

Conjecture 1.3

(
[
3
]
)
.

For any positive integers
n,r
with
r\geq 3
, in every
r
-coloring of the edges of
K_{n}
, there is some monochromatic connected component with at least
\frac{1}{r(r-1)}\binom{n}{2}
edges.

In
[
5
]
, Luo proves a general lower bound that approximates the conjectured lower bound within a factor of
1-O(\frac{1}{r^{2}})
.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
5
]
)
.

In any
r
-coloring of the edges of
K_{n}
for
r\geq 2
, there is a monochromatic connected component with at least
\frac{1}{r^{2}-r+\frac{5}{4}}{n\choose 2}
edges.

In analogy to the generalization of Gyárfás’s original result to
Theorem

1.1
, in this paper we study a generalization of
1.2
for random graphs: In an
r
-coloring of the edges of the random graph
G(n,p)
, what lower bounds
can we obtain (with high probability) for the number of edges in the largest monochromatic component?

The answer to this question naturally depends on our choice of the density
p
. We may ask if there is a
threshold
for values of
p
above which the answer to the above question about
G(n,p)
starts to behave in a certain way with high probability, e.g. when our bounds on the number of edges in the largest monochromatic component start to behave similarly to the analogous bounds in the complete graph setting.

Our main result is an analogue of
Theorem

1.4
in the setting of
G(n,p)
for any
p=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For any fixed integer
r\geq 2
and any
p=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
,

G:=G(n,p)
satisfies the following property with high probability: In every
r
-coloring of the edges of
G
,
there is a monochromatic connected
component with at least
\frac{1}{r^{2}-r+\frac{5}{4}}|E(G)|
edges.

We prove this result by extending the argument used in proving
Theorem

1.4
, including several technical lemmas, to the sparse random graph setting. Applying similar extensions yields a sparse random analogue of the lower bound for
r=3
as well.

The paper proceeds as follows. We begin in
Section

2
by providing some background on previous work on
1.2
for complete graphs, including some technical tools that we will adapt for the random graph setting. We also introduce some relevant results on random graphs that will be used later in the proofs. Then, in
Section

3
, we prove our main results on
G(n,p)
. We follow in
Section

4
by investigating analogues of our questions in the setting of graphs of high minimum degree and discussing how this setting relates to the sparse random setting. We conclude by discussing some potential future directions.

2.
Preliminaries

All graphs studied in this paper are finite and simple.
For a graph
G
, let
E(G)
and
V(G)
denote the sets of edges and vertices of
G
, respectively, and write
e(G)=|E(G)|
and
v(G)=|V(G)|
.
Given subsets of vertices
S,T\subseteq V(G)
, let
G[S]
denote the induced subgraph of
G
on the vertex set
S
(i.e.
G[S]
is the graph on
S
such that two vertices
v,w\in S
are adjacent in
G[S]
if and only if they are adjacent in
G
). Let
E_{G}(S)=E(G[S])
,
e_{G}(S)=|E_{G}(S)|
, and let
e_{G}(S,T)
denote the number of ordered pairs
(s,t)\in S\times T
such that
s,t
are adjacent in
S
. We write
e(S,T)=e_{G}(S,T)
,
E(S)=E_{G}(S)
, and
e(S)=e_{G}(S)
when the choice of
G
is clear from context. Given two graphs
G
and
M
on the same set of vertices
V
, we write
G\cap M
for the graph on vertex set
V
whose edge set is
E(G)\cap E(M)
.

For real-valued functions
f
and
g
, we write
f=o(g)
or
f\ll g
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=0
, and we write
f=\omega(g)
or
f\gg g
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=\infty
. Unless otherwise noted, all such asymptotic notation will be in terms of the variable
n
going to infinity.

To extend results about edge colorings of complete graphs to the random graph setting, we will frequently apply the following form of the Chernoff bound:

Proposition 2.1

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let
X=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}
, where the
X_{i}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables. Let
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]
. Then for any
\delta>0
,

\qquad\mathbb{P}(X\geq(1+\delta)\mu)\leq e^{-\delta^{2}\mu/(2+\delta)},

while for
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}(X\leq(1-\delta)\mu)\leq e^{-\delta^{2}\mu/2}.

In particular, for
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}(|X-\mu|\geq\delta\mu)\leq 2e^{-\delta^{2}\mu/3}.

2.1.
Upper bounds and Gyárfás’s construction

Gyárfás’s construction
[
4
]
gives, for certain values of
r
, an upper bound corresponding to the conjectured lower bound in
1.3
, showing that the conjecture, if true, is tight for infinitely many values of
r
. We summarize the relevant details of the construction below.

The construction depends on the existence of a certain family of objects called affine planes. An affine plane of order
t
consists of a set of
t^{2}
points and
t^{2}+t
lines, where every pair of points shares exactly one line, and the set of lines can be partitioned into
t+1
classes (or “directions”) of parallel lines, such that each pair of lines intersects in one point if they are not parallel (and no points if they are parallel). Affine planes of order
t
are known to exist whenever
t
is a prime power; it is open whether affine planes of any other order exist.

When an affine plane of order
r-1
exists and
n=(r-1)^{2}
, we can construct an
r
-coloring of
K_{n}
as follows:
The vertices are the points of an affine plane of order
r-1
,
and the edge between any pair of vertices is colored based on the direction of the line
between the corresponding points. Examples for
r-1=2
and
r-1=3
are shown below.

To extend this coloring to larger values of
n
, we partition the vertices of
K_{n}
into
(r-1)^{2}
clusters, each corresponding to a point in the affine plane of order
r-1
.
For each edge between two distinct clusters, we color it based on the direction of the line between the two corresponding points in the affine plane.
Within each cluster, we color the edges such that each cluster has a roughly equal number of edges of each color.
Each connected component in a given color
k
then consists of the clusters corresponding to the points on one line in the affine plane with the direction corresponding to
k
. The number of components then equals the number of lines in the affine plane of order
r-1
, which is
r(r-1)
. By symmetry, each component contains roughly the same number of edges, so each component contains roughly
\frac{1}{r^{2}-r}\binom{n}{2}
edges.

We can use the same construction to show an analogous upper bound in the random graph setting, by identifying
G=G(n,p)
as a subgraph of
K_{n}
and taking the coloring induced by the
r
-edge-coloring of
K_{n}
given by Gyárfás’s construction. For fixed
r
and large
n
, if
p
is not too small (e.g.
p=\omega(1/n)
suffices),
each monochromatic connected component in the
r
-edge-coloring of
K_{n}

will have, with high probability, close to a
p
fraction of its edges remain in
G
, meaning that for any constant
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability,
G=G(n,p)
will have the property that for some
r
-coloring of its edges, every monochromatic connected component has at most
(1+\varepsilon)p\frac{1}{r^{2}-r}\binom{n}{2}
edges.

2.2.
Lower bounds in the complete graph setting

An important tool in the proof of
Theorem

1.4
in
[
5
]
is the following inequality, which gives a density version of the relevant coloring results about sizes of connected components in subgraphs. Recall that a
complete
k
-partite graph
is a graph on a vertex set
V=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{k}
, where the
V_{i}
are pairwise disjoint, and a pair of vertices
v\in V_{i}
,
v^{\prime}\in V_{j}
is connected by an edge if and only if
i\neq j
. A graph
M
is called a
complete multipartite graph
if it is a complete
k
-partite graph for some
k\geq 2
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
5
]
)
.

In a subgraph
H
of a complete multipartite graph
M
, there must be a connected component
with at least
\frac{e(H)^{2}}{e(M)}
edges.

This result, in turn, follows from the following even simpler looking (yet still nontrivial) inequality, which is Corollary 6 in
[
5
]
.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
M
be a complete multipartite graph. For any
S,T\subseteq V(M)
, we have

e(S,T)^{2}\geq 4e_{M}(S)e_{M}(T).

In order to prove
Theorem

1.5
, we will need to adapt the proofs of
2.3
and
Theorem

2.2
, in order to extend them to the random graph setting.

3.
Proof of results on random graphs

Our first aim is to prove an analogue of
2.3
for random graphs.
We begin with a series of technical results that are essentially direct applications of the Chernoff bound.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any choice of
p=p_{n}
such that
p=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
and any
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{1}\in(0,1)
, the following holds as
n
goes to infinity.
In
G=G(n,p)
, with high probability, for every graph
H
on
V(G)
satisfying
e(H)\geq\varepsilon\binom{n}{2}
that is either a clique on a subset of vertices or a complete multipartite graph on
V(G)
,

(1-\varepsilon)\mu\leq e(H\cap G)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mu,

where
\mu=\mathbb{E}[e(H\cap G)]=pe(H)
.

Proof.

For any
H\subseteq E(K_{n})
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, the Chernoff bound yields

\mathbb{P}(|e(H\cap G)-\mu|\geq\delta\mu)\leq e^{-\delta^{2}\mu/3}.

When
|H|\geq\varepsilon\binom{n}{2}
and
\delta=\varepsilon
, this yields

\mathbb{P}(|e(H\cap G)-\mu|\geq\varepsilon\mu)\leq\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}p\varepsilon\binom{n}{2}/3)\leq\exp(-(1+o(1))\varepsilon^{3}pn^{2}/6).

Since there are at most
2^{n}+n^{n}\leq n^{2n}
possible choices of
H
, the overall probability that there exists some valid choice of
H
with
|e(H)-\mu|\geq\varepsilon\mu
is then bounded above by

\exp(-(1+o(1))\varepsilon^{3}pn^{2}/6+2n\log n).

For this probability to go to
0
, it suffices to have

\varepsilon^{3}pn^{2}/(12n\log n)>1.

Since
p=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
, for large enough
n
we have
\varepsilon>3(\frac{\log n}{pn})^{1/3}
, which gives the desired inequality.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
c_{0}\in(0,1)
, any choice of
p=p_{n}
such that
p=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
, and any
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{2}\in(0,1)
, the following holds as
n
goes to infinity.
In
G:=G(n,p)
, with probability
1-o(\frac{1}{n})
, every subgraph
H
with average degree
\bar{d}(H)\geq c_{0}p(n-1)
has
v(H)>\bar{d}(H)(1-\varepsilon)\frac{n}{p(n-1)}
.

Proof.

Condition on the high probability event in
3.1
with parameters
p=p_{n}
and
\varepsilon_{1}=\min(\varepsilon,(1-\varepsilon)^{2}c_{0}^{2})
to be determined later.
It suffices to consider the induced subgraphs
H:=G[S]
on vertex sets
S\subseteq V(G)
. Fix some such
S
, and let
s:=|S|
. If
s\geq(1-\varepsilon)c_{0}n
, then
e_{K_{n}}(S)=\binom{s}{2}\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{2}c_{0}^{2}\binom{n}{2}\geq\varepsilon_{1}\binom{n}{2}
. As long as
n
is large enough,
3.1
applied to the clique on
S
yields that
e(H)=e_{G}(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)p\binom{s}{2}
, which means
\bar{d}(H)=\frac{2e(H)}{v(H)}\leq(1+\varepsilon)p(s-1)
. Then
s\geq\frac{\bar{d}(H)}{(1+\varepsilon)p}\frac{s}{s-1}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\bar{d}(H)\frac{n}{p(n-1)}
, as desired.

Thus it suffices to show that with high probability, every
S
with
\bar{d}(G[S])\geq c_{0}p(n-1)
has
s:=|S|\geq(1-\varepsilon)c_{0}n
. Fix some
S
with
s<(1-\varepsilon)c_{0}n
. Note that a subgraph with average degree at least
c_{0}p(n-1)
must have at least
c_{0}p(n-1)+1
vertices, so we can assume
s\geq c_{0}p(n-1)+1\geq c_{0}pn
.
By the Chernoff bound, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\bar{d}(H)\geq c_{0}p(n-1))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(e(H)\geq\frac{1}{2}sc_{0}p(n-1))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(e(H)\geq\frac{c_{0}(n-1)}{s-1}\mathbb{E}[e(H)])

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\left(\frac{c_{0}(n-1)}{s-1}-1\right)^{2}}{\frac{c_{0}(n-1)}{s-1}+1}p\binom{s}{2}}\leq e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2-\varepsilon}\frac{\varepsilon c_{0}n}{s-1}p\binom{s}{2}}\leq e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}(n-1)c_{0}sp}.

For any fixed value of
s
, a union bound over all
S
of size
s
shows that the probability that
G
has a subgraph on
s
vertices with average degree more than
c_{0}p(n-1)
is at most

{n\choose s}e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}(n-1)c_{0}sp}\leq e^{s(1+\log n-\log s)-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}(n-1)c_{0}sp}\leq e^{s(1+\log n-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}(n-1)c_{0}p)}.

Since
p=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
, for
n
large enough such that
p>\frac{8}{\varepsilon^{2}c_{0}}\frac{\log n}{n-1}
and
\log n>1
, this last quantity is maximized when
s
is as small as possible, at a value of
e^{c_{0}pn(1+\log n-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}(n-1)c_{0}p)}
.
Taking a union bound over all possible values of
s<(1-\varepsilon)c_{0}n
, the probability of any such subgraph existing is at most
ne^{c_{0}pn(1+\log n-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}(n-1)c_{0}p)}=e^{\log n+c_{0}pn(1+\log n-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}(n-1)c_{0}p)}
. Since
p=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
, for large enough
n
this upper bound is
e^{-\omega((\log n)^{2})}=o(\frac{1}{n})
, as claimed.
∎

We are now ready to prove the following random graph analogue of
2.3
.

Theorem 3.3
.

For any choice of
p=p_{n}
such that
p=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
, any constant
c\in(0,1)
, and any
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{3}\in(0,1)
, the following holds as
n
goes to infinity. In
G:=G(n,p)
, with probability
1-o(\frac{1}{n})
, the following holds for every complete multipartite graph
M
on the vertices of
G
:

For every pair of subsets
S,T\subseteq V(G)
with
e_{M}(S,T)\geq cn^{2}
, in the graph
G^{\prime}=G\cap M
, we have

e_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)\geq(1-\varepsilon)pe_{M}(S,T),

and furthermore,

e_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)^{2}\geq 4(1-\varepsilon)e_{G^{\prime}}(S)e_{G^{\prime}}(T).

The first inequality is another simple Chernoff bound; we will prove the second inequality by arguing that, without the factor of
1-\varepsilon
, the inequality is true
in expectation, and that with high probability, the actual values of both sides of the equation are close to their expected values.

Proof.

There exists a constant
d
such that the number of ways to choose
M
,
S
, and
T
is at most
e^{dn\log n}
. Fix such a choice of
M
,
S
, and
T
.
As in the proof of
3.1
, by the Chernoff bound, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(e_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)<(1-\varepsilon/4)\mathbb{E}[e_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)])

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}[e_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)]}{32}}\leq e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}pcn^{2}}{32}}.

Since
p=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
, taking a union bound over all choices of
M
,
S
, and
T
gives an upper bound of

e^{dn\log n}e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}pcn^{2}}{32}}=e^{-\omega(n\log n)}=o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),

so that with
1-o(\frac{1}{n})
probability,

e_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)\geq(1-\varepsilon/4)\mathbb{E}[e_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)]=(1-\varepsilon/4)pe_{M}(S,T),

for all choices of
M
,
S
, and
T
such that
e_{M}(S,T)\geq cn^{2}
, which proves the first inequality. By
2.3
,
e_{M}(S,T)^{2}\geq 4e_{M}(S)e_{M}(T)
, so we also have that with
1-o(\frac{1}{n})
probability,

\displaystyle e_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\varepsilon/4)^{2}p^{2}e_{M}(S,T)^{2}\geq(1-\varepsilon/4)^{2}p^{2}4e_{M}(S)e_{M}(T)

\displaystyle=4(1-\varepsilon/4)^{2}\mathbb{E}[e_{G^{\prime}}(S)]\mathbb{E}[e_{G^{\prime}}(T)],

for all such
M
,
S
, and
T
. Note that
e_{M}(S,T)\geq cn^{2}
implies
|S|,|T|\geq cn
, so that

\mathbb{P}(e_{G^{\prime}}(S)>(1+\varepsilon/4)\mathbb{E}[e_{G^{\prime}}(S)])\leq e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}[e_{G^{\prime}}(S)]}{48}}\leq e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}pc^{2}n^{2}}{4}}=e^{-\omega(n\log n)},

and similarly for
e_{G^{\prime}}(T)
.
Therefore, again with
1-o(\frac{1}{n})
probability, we have

e_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)^{2}\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon/4)^{2}}{(1+\varepsilon/4)^{2}}4e_{G^{\prime}}(S)e_{G^{\prime}}(T)\geq 4(1-\varepsilon)e_{G^{\prime}}(S)e_{G^{\prime}}(T),

for all such choices of
M
,
S
, and
T
, as desired.
∎

Next, we prove the following random graph analogue of
Theorem

2.2
.

Theorem 3.4
.

For any choice of
p=p_{n}
such that
p=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
, any constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,1)
, and any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the following holds as
n
goes to infinity. With probability
1-o(\frac{1}{n})
, in
G:=G(n,p)
, the following holds for every complete multipartite graph
M
on
V(G)
with minimum degree at least
c_{1}n
:

Let
G^{\prime}:=G\cap M
. Then every subgraph
H
of
G^{\prime}
with
\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}\geq c_{2}
has a connected component
H^{\prime}
with
e(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{e(H)^{2}}{e(G^{\prime})}(1-\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

Condition on the high probability events of
3.1
,
3.2
, and
Theorem

3.3
, with parameters
c_{0}=\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\frac{c_{1}c_{2}}{3}
,
c=\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\frac{c_{1}^{2}c_{2}}{3}
,
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{3}=\varepsilon/3
. Let
H_{1},\dots,H_{k}
be the connected components of
H
. For any set
S\subset V:=V(G)
, let
f(S)=\frac{1}{2}e_{G^{\prime}}(S,V)
. In particular, we have
f(H_{1})+\cdots+f(H_{k})=f(V)=\frac{1}{2}e_{G^{\prime}}(V,V)=e(G^{\prime})
.
Then, by the generalized mediant inequality, for some
H^{\prime}=H_{\ell}
, we have

\frac{e(H^{\prime})}{f(V(H^{\prime}))}\geq\frac{e(H_{1})+\cdots+e(H_{k})}{f(V(H_{1}))+\cdots+f(V(H_{k}))}=\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}\geq c_{2}.

If
v(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{c}{c_{1}}n
, then since
M
has minimum degree
\delta(M)\geq c_{1}n
, we obtain
e_{M}(V(H^{\prime}),V)\geq cn^{2}
.
Theorem

3.3
then yields

e_{G^{\prime}}(V(H^{\prime}),V)^{2}\geq 4(1-\varepsilon_{3})e_{G^{\prime}}(V(H^{\prime}))e_{G^{\prime}}(V)=4(1-\varepsilon_{3})e(H^{\prime})e(G^{\prime}),

so that

e(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{e(H^{\prime})}{f(V(H^{\prime}))}f(V(H^{\prime}))\geq\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}\sqrt{e(H^{\prime})e(G^{\prime})(1-\varepsilon_{3})},

which rearranges to give

e(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{e(H)^{2}}{e(G^{\prime})}(1-\varepsilon_{3})\geq\frac{e(H)^{2}}{e(G^{\prime})}(1-\varepsilon),

in the high probability event we have conditioned on, as desired. So it suffices to reduce to the case where
v(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{c}{c_{1}}n
for some
H^{\prime}
such that
\frac{e(H^{\prime})}{f(V(H^{\prime}))}
is large.

Consider the vertex set

T=\bigcup_{i:|V(H_{i})|<\frac{c}{c_{1}}n}V(H_{i}).

First suppose that
|T|\geq\frac{c}{c_{1}}n
. In this case, similarly to before, we obtain
e_{M}(T,V)\geq cn^{2}
, so that
e_{G^{\prime}}(T,V))\geq(1-\varepsilon_{3})pe_{M}(T,V)\geq(1-\varepsilon_{3})p|T|c_{1}n
. Then the average degree of the induced subgraph
H[T]
is

\bar{d}_{H}(T)=\frac{2e_{H}(T)}{|T|}\geq\frac{e_{H}(T)}{\frac{1}{2}e_{G^{\prime}}(T,V)}(1-\varepsilon_{3})pc_{1}n.

If
\frac{e_{H}(T)}{f(T)}\geq\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}\geq c_{2}
, then

\bar{d}_{H}(T)\geq(1-\varepsilon_{3})pc_{1}c_{2}n\geq c_{0}pn,

so that
H[T]
contains a connected component
H_{i}
with
\bar{d}(H_{i})\geq c_{0}pn
. By
3.2
, this implies
v(H_{i})\geq\bar{d}(H_{i})(1-\varepsilon_{3})\frac{n}{p(n-1)}\geq(1-\varepsilon_{3})^{2}c_{1}c_{2}n>\frac{c}{c_{1}}n
,
contradicting the definition of
T
. So we must have
\frac{e_{H}(T)}{f(T)}<\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}
. But then there is some connected component
H^{\prime}
with
V(H^{\prime})\subseteq V\setminus T
such that

\frac{e(H^{\prime})}{f(V(H^{\prime}))}\geq\frac{e_{H}(V\setminus T)}{f(V\setminus T)}\geq\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})},

with
v(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{c}{c_{1}}n
, so that we can conclude as before that
e(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{e(H)^{2}}{e(G^{\prime})}(1-\varepsilon)
, as desired.

It remains to consider the case where
|T|<\frac{c}{c_{1}}n
. Since
\frac{c}{c_{1}}\geq(1-\varepsilon_{2})c_{0}
, we must have
\bar{d}_{H}(T)<(1-\varepsilon_{2})^{-1}\frac{c}{c_{1}}p(n-1)
, or else
3.2
leads to a contradiction. So
e(T)<\frac{c^{2}}{2(1-\varepsilon_{2})c_{1}^{2}}pn(n-1)
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{e_{H}(V\setminus T)}{f(V\setminus T)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{e(H)-e_{H}(T)}{f(V)}\geq\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}-\frac{\frac{c^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon_{2})c_{1}^{2}}p\binom{n}{2}}{(1-\varepsilon_{1})p\binom{n}{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{c^{2}}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}c_{1}^{2}c_{2}}\right)\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}\geq(1-\varepsilon/3)\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}.

We can then argue as before that for some connected component
H^{\prime}
of
H
with
v(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{c}{c_{1}}n
, we have
\frac{e(H^{\prime})}{f(V(H^{\prime}))}\geq(1-\varepsilon/3)\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}\geq(1-\varepsilon/3)c_{2}
. obtaining as before that
e_{G^{\prime}}(V(H^{\prime}),V)^{2}\geq 4(1-\varepsilon_{3})e(H^{\prime})e(G^{\prime})
, we conclude that

e(H^{\prime})=\frac{e(H^{\prime})}{f(V(H^{\prime}))}f(V(H^{\prime}))\geq(1-\varepsilon/3)\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}\sqrt{e(H^{\prime})e(G^{\prime})(1-\varepsilon_{3})},

which rearranges to give

e(H^{\prime})\geq(1-\varepsilon/3)^{2}(1-\varepsilon_{3})\frac{e(H)^{2}}{e(G^{\prime})}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{e(H)^{2}}{e(G^{\prime})},

as desired.
Thus in every case, in the high probability event we have conditioned on, we obtain the desired bound.
∎

We finally have all the tools we need to prove our main result,
Theorem

1.5
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Let
z_{0}=\frac{1}{r^{2}-r+\frac{5}{4}}
,
c_{1}=\frac{1}{r+1}
,
c_{2}=\frac{1}{r-1}
, and
c_{0}=\frac{1-\varepsilon_{0}}{r}
, where
\varepsilon_{0}\leq c_{1}(1-c_{1})
is some constant depending on
r
to be chosen later.
For a given choice of
p=p_{n}=\omega(\frac{\log n}{n})
, condition on the high probability events in the conclusions of
3.1
,
3.2
, and
Theorem

3.4
with parameters
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}
as chosen above, and
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}/3
.
In particular, we can assume that
(1-\varepsilon)p\binom{n}{2}\leq e(G)\leq(1+\varepsilon)p\binom{n}{2}
.

Fix an
r
-edge-coloring of
G
. Without loss of generality, let red be the color with the most edges. Let
R
be the subgraph of
G
consisting of all its red edges, so
e(R)\geq\frac{1}{r}e(G)\geq(1-\varepsilon)p\frac{1}{r}\binom{n}{2}
. Let
x=\frac{e(R)}{e(G)}\geq\frac{1}{r}
. Let the red connected components be
R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
, with
v(R_{1})\geq\cdots\geq v(R_{k})
. Let
z
be the fraction of the edges of
G
that are in the monochromatic component with the most edges. It suffices to show that
z\geq z_{0}
, conditioned on the high probability events specified earlier. Note that for some
i\in[1,k]
we have
\bar{d}(R_{i})\geq\bar{d}(R)\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{r}p(n-1)\geq c_{0}p(n-1)
, so that
3.2
yields
v(R_{i})\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\frac{n}{r}
, and thus
z\geq\frac{e(R_{i})}{\binom{n}{2}}\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{3}}{r^{2}}\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon_{0}}{r^{2}}
.

First, suppose that
v(R_{1})\geq(1-\frac{1}{r+1})n
. Then by
3.1
, we have

e(R_{2})+\cdots+e(R_{k})\leq e_{G}(V(G)\setminus V(R_{1}))\leq(1+\varepsilon)p\binom{\frac{1}{r+1}n}{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)p\frac{1}{(r+1)^{2}}\binom{n}{2},

which means

e(R_{1})\geq(1-\varepsilon)p\frac{1}{r}\binom{n}{2}-(1+\varepsilon)p\frac{1}{(r+1)^{2}}\binom{n}{2}\geq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{r^{2}-r+\frac{5}{4}}p\binom{n}{2},

for
r\geq 2
and sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, so that
z\geq z_{0}
as desired.
Thus, we can assume that
v(R_{1})<(1-\frac{1}{r+1})n=(1-c_{1})n
.

For
0\leq j\leq k-1
, let
G_{j}=G[V(R_{j+1})\cup\dots\cup V(R_{k})]
and
H_{j}=G_{j}\cap R
. Let
c=\sqrt{z\frac{1+\varepsilon_{0}}{1-\varepsilon_{0}}}
. Since
z\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon_{0}}{r^{2}}
, we have
c\geq c_{0}
. If any component
R_{i}
has average degree at least
pc(n-1)\geq pc_{0}(n-1)
, then the assumption of
3.2
gives
v(R_{j})>(1-\varepsilon)cn
, so that
e(R_{j})>(1-\varepsilon_{0})c^{2}p\binom{n}{2}=z(1+\varepsilon_{0})p\binom{n}{2}\geq ze(G)
, contradicting our choice of
z
. Thus we can assume every
R_{i}
, and hence every
H_{j}
, has average degree less than
pc(n-1)\leq(1-\varepsilon_{0})^{-1}p\sqrt{z}(n-1)
. In particular,
j=0
gives

xe(G)=e(R)<(1-\varepsilon_{0})^{-1}p\sqrt{z}\binom{n}{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{z}}{(1-\varepsilon_{0})^{2}}e(G),

so that
z\geq(1-\varepsilon_{0})^{4}x^{2}\geq(1-2\varepsilon_{0})^{2}r^{-2}
. Let
\delta=r-\frac{1-2\varepsilon_{0}}{\sqrt{z}}
, so
z=\frac{(1-2\varepsilon_{0})^{2}}{(r-\delta)^{2}}
. If
\delta\geq 1
, then
z\geq z_{0}
as long as we pick
\varepsilon_{0}
small enough in terms of
r
, so we can assume
\delta<1
.
More generally, since
e(R_{i})\leq ze(G)
for
1\leq i\leq k
, we have for every
j
that

e(H_{j})=\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}e(R_{i})\geq(x-jz)e(G),

meaning

(1-\varepsilon_{0})^{-1}p\sqrt{z}(n-1)>\frac{2e(H_{j})}{v(H_{j})}\geq\frac{2(x-jz)e(G)}{n-\sum_{i=1}^{j}v(R_{i})},

which rearranges to give

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j}v(R_{i})

\displaystyle<n-\frac{2(x-jz)e(G)}{(1-\varepsilon_{0})^{-1}p\sqrt{z}(n-1)}

\displaystyle\leq n(1-\frac{(1-\varepsilon_{0})^{2}(x-jz)}{\sqrt{z}})\leq(1+2\varepsilon_{0})(1-x/\sqrt{z}+j\sqrt{z})n.

The same smoothing argument as in the proof of
[
5
, Lemma 3.2]
then yields

\sum_{i=1}^{j}{{v(R_{i})}\choose 2}\leq\frac{n^{2}((1-x/\sqrt{z}+\sqrt{z})^{2}+(x/z-1)z)-n}{2}(1+5\varepsilon_{0}).

Let
M
be the complete
k
-partite graph on
V(G)
with
V(R_{1}),\dots,V(R_{k})
as the independent sets.
Because
G^{\prime}:=M\cap G
has no red edges, one of the other colors (say, blue) has at least
\frac{1}{r-1}e(G^{\prime})
edges in
G^{\prime}
. Let
H
be the subgraph of
G^{\prime}
consisting of all its blue edges, so
\frac{e(H)}{e(G^{\prime})}\geq\frac{1}{r-1}=c_{2}
. Since
v(R_{1})<(1-c_{1})n
, the minimum degree of
M
is at least
c_{1}n
. Then, by
Theorem

3.4
, there is a connected component
H^{\prime}
of
H

with
e(H^{\prime})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{e(H)^{2}}{e(G^{\prime})}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{(r-1)^{2}}e(G^{\prime})
. Since
e(M)\geq c_{1}n(1-c_{1})n\geq\varepsilon_{0}\binom{n}{2}
,
3.1
yields
e(G^{\prime})\geq(1-\varepsilon)pe(M)
, so

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{(r-1)^{2}}\frac{e(G^{\prime})}{e(G)}\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\frac{1}{(r-1)^{2}}p\frac{e(M)}{e(G)}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\frac{1}{(r-1)^{2}}(1-((1-x/\sqrt{z}+\sqrt{z})^{2}+(x/z-1)z)(1+5\varepsilon_{0}))

\displaystyle\geq(1-7\varepsilon_{0})\frac{1}{(r-1)^{2}}(1-(1-x/\sqrt{z}+\sqrt{z})^{2}-(x/z-1)z).

This rearranges to yield

\displaystyle-\frac{(r-1)^{2}z}{1-7\varepsilon_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-x/\sqrt{z}+\sqrt{z})^{2}+(x/z-1)z-1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}x^{2}-(1+2/\sqrt{z})x+2\sqrt{z}.

For
\varepsilon_{0}
small enough in terms of
r
, we have
\frac{z}{2}+\sqrt{z}\geq x\geq\frac{1}{r}
, so the right hand side is at most

\frac{1}{zr^{2}}-(1+2/\sqrt{z})\frac{1}{r}+2\sqrt{z}.

Recall that
z=\frac{(1-2\varepsilon_{0})^{2}}{(r-\delta)^{2}}
for some
\delta\in(0,1)
. Substituting this in yields

\displaystyle-\frac{(1-2\varepsilon_{0})^{2}(r-1)^{2}}{(1-7\varepsilon_{0})(r-\delta)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(r-\delta)^{2}}{(1-2\varepsilon_{0})^{2}r^{2}}-\frac{(1-2\varepsilon_{0})+2(r-\delta)}{(1-2\varepsilon_{0})r}+\frac{2(1-2\varepsilon_{0})}{r-\delta},

which can be rearranged to get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-2\varepsilon_{0})^{4}}{1-7\varepsilon_{0}}r^{2}(r-1)^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(r-\delta)^{4}+2(1-2\varepsilon_{0})^{3}(r-\delta)r^{2}-(1-2\varepsilon_{0})^{2}r(r-\delta)^{2}-2(1-2\varepsilon_{0})r(r-\delta)^{3}

\displaystyle\leq(r-\delta^{2})^{2}-r(r^{2}-\delta^{2})(1-2\delta)+4\varepsilon_{0}r(r-\delta)^{2}(r-\delta+1),

and thus, taking
\varepsilon_{0}\leq(32(r+1))^{-4}
,

1-2\delta\leq\frac{(r-\delta^{2})^{2}}{r(r^{2}-\delta^{2})}+\frac{1}{8r^{3}}<\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{8r^{3}},

so that
\delta>\frac{r-1}{2r}-\frac{1}{16r^{3}}
, and thus

z=\frac{(1-2\varepsilon_{0})^{2}}{(r-\delta)^{2}}\geq\frac{1-32^{-3}r^{-4}}{r^{2}-r+\frac{1}{4}+1-\frac{1}{4r}+\frac{1}{4r^{2}}+2\frac{r}{16r^{3}}}.

For
r\geq 2
, it can be verified that this expression is at least
\frac{1}{r^{2}-r+\frac{5}{4}}=z_{0}
, as desired.
∎

4.
High minimum degree argument

We now turn to the closely related setting of graphs of high minimum degree. Unlike in the problem of finding monochromatic components with many vertices, for the edge version of the problem it suffices to assume a global condition of an edge density close to
1
, rather than a local condition about degrees. We obtain a result that approximates the bound in
Theorem

1.4
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
r\geq 4
be an integer and let
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{r-1})
. Then for any graph
G
on
n
vertices with at least
(1-\beta){n\choose 2}
edges, every
r
-coloring of the edges of
G
contains a monochromatic component with at least

\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{r^{2}-r+\frac{5}{4}}\binom{n}{2}

edges.

Proof.

We adapt the proof of
Theorem

1.4
in
[
5
]
, needing to make only a few adjustments.
Instead of considering an edge coloring of a subgraph of
K_{n}
, we essentially consider an edge coloring of
K_{n}
that leaves up to a
\beta
fraction of edges uncolored. Fix such a partial
r
-edge-coloring, let
z
be the fraction of the edges of
K_{n}
that are in the monochromatic component with the most edges, and let red be the color with the most edges. Let
R
be the subgraph of
K_{n}
consisting of all the edges colored red, and let
x=\frac{e(R)}{e(K_{n})}\geq\frac{1-\beta}{r}
. By
Theorem

2.2
applied trivially with
M=K_{n}
, we have
z\geq x^{2}\geq\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{r^{2}}
.

Let
R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
be the red connected components, with
v(R_{1})\geq\cdots\geq v(R_{k})
. For
j\in[0,k-1]
, let
G_{j}
be the complete graph on
\bigcup_{i={j+1}}^{k}V(R_{i})
, with its (partial) coloring induced by the coloring on
K_{n}
. Let
H_{j}=G_{j}\cap R
.
Applying
Theorem

2.2
with
M=G_{j}
and
H=H_{j}
yields a red component
R_{i}
with
ze(K_{n})\geq e(R_{i})\geq\frac{e(H_{j})^{2}}{e(G_{j})}
. We have

e(H_{j})=\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}e(R_{i})\geq(x-jz)e(K_{n}),

while

e(G_{j})={{n-\sum_{i=1}^{j}v(R_{i})}\choose 2}.

This is the exact same setup as in the proof of
Theorem

1.4
given in
[
5
]
.
Letting
v_{i}=\frac{V(R_{i})}{n}
and solving the optimization via
[
5
, Lemma 3.2]
yields

\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{2}\leq(1-x/\sqrt{z}+\sqrt{z})^{2}+(x/z-1)z,

so that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}{{v(R_{i})}\choose 2}\leq\frac{n^{2}((1-x/\sqrt{z}+\sqrt{z})^{2}+(x/z-1)z)-n}{2}.

Let
M
be the complete multipartite graph obtained by removing all edges within each
V(R_{i})
from
K_{n}
, with its (partial) coloring induced by the coloring on
K_{n}
. Then no edges of
M
are colored red, and at most
\beta e(K_{n})
edges of
M
are uncolored, so by the pigeonhole principle, some color class
H
in
M
has
e(H)\geq\frac{1}{r-1}(e(M)-\beta e(K_{n}))\geq\frac{1}{r-1}e(K_{n})(1-\beta-((1-x/\sqrt{z}+\sqrt{z})^{2}+(x/z-1)z))
. By
Theorem

2.2
, some component
H^{\prime}
of
H
has
e(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{e(H)^{2}}{e(M)}\geq\frac{1}{r-1}e(H)
. Therefore,

z\geq\frac{e(H^{\prime})}{e(K_{n})}\geq\frac{1}{(r-1)^{2}}(1-\beta-((1-x/\sqrt{z}+\sqrt{z})^{2}+(x/z-1)z)).

Applying the same sequence of rearrangements and simplifications as in the proof of
Theorem

1.4
(which are very similar to those in the proof of
Theorem

1.5
), with the distinction being that we only have
x\geq\frac{1-\beta}{r}
in this case, then yields

-(r-1)^{2}z\leq(1-\beta)^{2}\frac{1}{zr^{2}}-(1+2/\sqrt{z})\frac{1-\beta}{r}+2\sqrt{z}+\beta.

Letting
z=\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(r-\delta)^{2}}
and further expanding and simplifying the above then gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq(r-\delta)^{4}+(r-1)^{2}r^{2}(1-\beta)^{2}-(1-\beta)r(r-\delta)^{2}-2(r-\delta)^{3}r+2(1-\beta)(r-\delta)r^{2}+\beta(r-\delta)^{2}r^{2}

\displaystyle=(r-\delta^{2})^{2}-r(r^{2}-\delta^{2})(1-2\delta)+\beta((r-\delta)^{2}r^{2}+r(r-\delta)^{2}-2(r-\delta)r^{2}-(2-\beta)(r-1)^{2}r^{2})

\displaystyle\leq(r-\delta^{2})^{2}-r(r^{2}-\delta^{2})(1-2\delta)+\beta r^{2}((r-\delta)^{2}-2(r-1)^{2}),

where the last step uses the fact that
\beta\leq\frac{1}{r-1}
. Since
r\geq 4
, the last term
\beta r^{2}((r-\delta)^{2}-2(r-1)^{2})
is negative, so we obtain
\delta>\frac{r-1}{2r}
just like in the (fully colored) complete graph setting. Then we have
z\geq\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{(r-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2r})^{2}}\geq\frac{(1-\beta)^{2}}{r^{2}-r+\frac{5}{4}}
, as desired.

∎

The proof of
[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
likewise translates to the following result about the
3
-color case in the high minimum degree setting.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices where every vertex has degree at least
(1-\beta)n-1
, where
\beta\leq\frac{1}{25}
.
Then in every
3
-coloring of the edges of
G
, there is a monochromatic connected component with at least
\frac{1}{6}e(G)
edges.

Proof.

Call the three colors red, green, and blue. Assume for the sake of contradiction that every monochromatic component has less than
\frac{1}{6}e(G)
edges.

First suppose that one of the colors (say, red) consists of exactly one component. Then the number of red edges is at most
\frac{1}{6}e(G)
, so there are at least
\frac{5}{6}e(G)
total green and blue edges. Without loss of generality,
there are at least
\frac{5}{12}e(G)
green edges. By
Theorem

2.2
, there is a green component
H^{\prime}
with

e(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{(\frac{5}{12}e(G))^{2}}{{n\choose 2}}\geq\frac{25}{144}(1-\beta)e(G)\geq\frac{1}{6}e(G),

a contradiction.
So, we can assume there are multiple components of each color.
Let red be the color with the most edges, so there are at least
\frac{1}{3}e(G)
red edges.
Let
H^{\prime}
be the red component with the largest average degree, so

v(H^{\prime})\geq\bar{d}(H^{\prime})+1\geq\frac{1}{3}\bar{d}(G)+1\geq\frac{1}{3}(1-\beta)n.

If
v(H^{\prime})>\frac{n}{2}
, then
e(H^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}v(H^{\prime})\bar{d}(H^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{6}e(G)
, so we can assume that
v(H^{\prime})\leq\frac{n}{2}
.
Let
V_{1}=V(H^{\prime})
and
V_{2}=V(G)\setminus V_{1}
, and let
G^{\prime}
be the bipartite subgraph of
G
consisting of all its edges between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
.

Fix a green component
C_{1}
of
G^{\prime}
. If
|C_{1}\cap V_{1}|\geq|V_{1}|-3\beta n-1
, then the edges of
G^{\prime}
between
V_{1}\cap C_{1}
and
V_{2}\setminus C_{1}
are all blue.
So, all the edges between
V_{1}\cap C_{1}
and
V_{2}
are split into two monochromatic components.
One of those components has a number of edges that is at least

\frac{1}{2}e_{G}(V_{1}\cap C_{1},V_{2})\geq\frac{1}{2}|V_{1}\cap C_{1}||V_{2}|-\beta(n-1)|V_{1}|\geq\frac{2}{9}(1-\beta)\binom{n}{2}-\beta\binom{n}{2}\geq\frac{1}{6}e(G),

a contradiction. So, no green (or blue) component can cover all but at most
3\beta n
vertices of
V_{1}
(or, by symmetry, of
V_{2}
).

Let
A_{1}=C_{1}\cap V_{1}
,
A_{2}=C_{1}\cap V_{2}
,
B_{1}=V_{1}\setminus C_{1}
, and
B_{2}=V_{2}\setminus C_{1}
.
By assumption,
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
each have at least
3\beta n+1
vertices.
Since every vertex in
A_{1}
is connected in blue to all but at most
\beta n
vertices of
B_{2}
, by pigeonhole every pair of vertices in
A_{1}
shares a neighbor in
B_{2}
.
Thus,
A_{1}
is contained in a single blue component. The same is true of
A_{2}
by symmetry.
Let
M_{1}
(resp.
M_{2}
) be the subset of
B_{1}
(resp.
B_{2}
) contained in the same blue component as
A_{2}
(resp.
A_{1}
). Then
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
each have at least
2\beta n+1
vertices, so the bipartite graph between
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
is connected.
If any edge between
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
