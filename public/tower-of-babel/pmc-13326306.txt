A multimodal study on predicting extrathyroidal extension of papillary thyroid carcinoma based on radiopathomics
Authors: Jiao Yao, Di Wang, Kun-Ming Pu, Wei-Han Xiao, Xiao-Min Hu, Jin-Feng Qin, Wei Yuan, Hong-Mei Yuan, Xiao-Ling Liu, Fan-Ding He, Chao-Xue Zhang, Xia-Chuan Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe aimed to develop and validate a radiopathomics model for predicting extrathyroidal extension (ETE) in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC).MethodsThis retrospective study included 388 PTC patients with preoperative ultrasound and 400× cytology images from five medical centers between June 2017 and April 2024. We analyzed ultrasound and cytology images using Python and CellProfiler to extract features. Feature selection was performed using univariate analysis, Spearman correlation, and LASSO regression. The XGBoost algorithm was then used to build radiomics, pathomics, and combined radiopathomics models. The diagnostic performance of the radiopathomics model was compared with that of radiologists in an external validation cohort. Model and radiologist performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The radiopathomics model was visualized and interpreted through SHAP analysis.ResultsThe radiopathomics model selected 21 features for construction. The AUC of the radiopathomics model was 0.887, 0.857, and 0.873 in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively, exceeding those of the single radiomics model (0.824, 0.787, and 0.804) and the pathomics model (0.809, 0.811, and 0.794). Compared with radiologists, the radiopathomics model improved the mean accuracy from 0.661 to 0.821. SHAP analysis showed that radiomics features played a major role in diagnosing ETE, while pathomics features provided additional support.ConclusionThe radiopathomics model serves as a promising auxiliary tool for preoperative ETE risk stratification and can help improve radiologists’ diagnostic performance.Clinical trial numberNot Applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02308-9.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors of the endocrine system. The incidence of thyroid cancer increased significantly worldwide from 1990 to 2019, especially in China, where the incidence increased by 289.6% [1, 2] during this period. Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most common type of thyroid cancer. Patients with stage I or II PTC generally have a favorable prognosis, with 10-year overall survival rates of 86.34% and 66.03%, respectively [3]. By contrast, patients with stage III or IV PTC have a relatively poor prognosis and are prone to disease recurrence and metastasis, a key factor of which is extrathyroidal extension (ETE), which indicates that tumor infiltration has extended beyond the thyroid capsule [4].

According to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Cancer Staging Manual, ETE is classified as either minimal (m-ETE) or gross (g-ETE). m-ETE refers to microscopic invasion into the perithyroidal soft tissue only, whereas g-ETE denotes gross or radiologically evident invasion into surrounding structures such as the strap muscles (sternohyoid, sternothyroid, etc.), trachea, larynx, esophagus, or major blood vessels [5]. The significant association of g-ETE with higher recurrence rates and worse survival has rendered it a well-established adverse prognostic factor. In contrast, the clinical significance of m-ETE remains controversial. For example, according to the AJCC 8th edition Cancer Staging Manual, m-ETE does not affect TNM staging [6, 7]. However, some studies have suggested that m-ETE may be associated with adverse features such as lymph node metastasis. Furthermore, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Clinical Practice Guidelines for Thyroid Carcinoma state that any confirmed ETE is an indication for total thyroidectomy [8, 9]; furthermore, some studies suggest that m-ETE may be associated with adverse features such as lymph node metastasis [10, 11]. Therefore, the accurate preoperative assessment of ETE (including both minimal and gross types) is critical for surgical planning. In this study, we aimed to develop a predictive model as an objective tool for preoperative evaluations.

Although ultrasonography is the preferred modality for thyroid examination [12], the accuracy of an ETE diagnosis by radiologists using this method alone remains relatively low owing to the close anatomical relationship between the thyroid gland and adjacent critical structures. Several studies have shown that the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC) curve for the accuracy of ultrasonography in diagnosing ETE is only approximately 0.608–0.687 [13–15]. Therefore, a simple, accurate, objective, and quantifiable assessment tool is required to evaluate ETE in patients with PTC. Radiomics refers to the high-throughput extraction of a large number of imaging information, thereby transforming medical images into mineable high-dimensional data and allowing for the subsequent quantitative analysis of features that are invisible to the naked eye. It was introduced to medical imaging as a computer-aided system for preoperative diagnosis to support diagnostic decisions [16–18]. Although some researchers have attempted to use ultrasonographic images with radiomics-based machine learning models to preoperatively predict ETE in PTC, the AUC curve values achieved by a single modality is only 0.744–0.760, which limits the utility of the model and renders it unable to satisfy clinical needs [19–21].

According to the 2015 American Thyroid Association (ATA) Management Guidelines [22], and reaffirmed in the 2025 ATA guidelines [23], the primary modality for accurate evaluation of thyroid nodules is cytologic examination of the thyroid by fine-needle aspiration (FNA) biopsy, in addition to performing cervical thyroid US [24]. Importantly, the microscopic morphological information provided by FNA harbors relevant clues about tumor aggressiveness. This is reflected at two levels. At the level of diagnostic classification, for instance, within standardized reporting systems such as the Bethesda System, malignant nodules initially diagnosed as “Atypia of Undetermined Significance” have been shown to exhibit significantly fewer aggressive features (e.g., ETE) on postoperative pathology compared to those categorized as “Suspicious for Malignancy” or “Malignant” [25]. At the level of cytological features, specific subtle morphological indicators (such as nuclear grooves and cell swirling patterns) have also been validated to effectively distinguish PTC histological variants with differing invasive potential [26]. Collectively, this evidence indicates that FNA not only enables discrimination between benign and malignant lesions but also reveals that the cellular morphology and architectural patterns reflected in microscopic images are closely related to the intrinsic aggressive biological behavior of the tumor. A recent study has shown that radiopathomics, which combines radiological information of the whole tumor at the macroscopic scale and pathological information of local lesions at the microscopic scale, can achieve better predictive performance than unimodal models using radiological or pathological data alone [27]. This suggests that integrating multimodal data can complement nodule properties at multiple scales and enhance the predictive power of existing models [28, 29]. However, to the best of our knowledge, the application of US radiomics and cytology pathomics to assess ETE in PTC has not been reported. Therefore, we analyzed ETE in PTC by combining thyroid US and FNA cytology images, aiming to develop and validate an interpretable radiopathomics model for more accurate preoperative assessment of ETE in PTC.

Materials and methods

Study cohorts

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM) [30]. The protocol of this retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee. The Ethics Committee waived the requirement for written informed consent from patients. The study population consisted of patients who underwent thyroid surgical treatment with a pathologic diagnosis of PTC from June 2017 to April 2024 at five hospitals. Inclusion criteria were: (a) receipt of thyroid ultrasound within one week before surgery; (b) receipt of FNA of a thyroid nodule within one week before surgery; (c) lobectomy of the affected side or total thyroidectomy; and (d) pathologic diagnosis of PTC with documentation of whether the thyroid capsule was invaded. Exclusion criteria were: (a) age younger than 18 years; (b) missing or poor-quality US images; (c) missing or poor-quality cytology images; (d) other types of thyroid cancer; and (e) chemotherapy or radiotherapy before surgery.

From four centers, we collected 306 patients who were randomly allocated in a 7:3 ratio into a training cohort (N = 214) and an internal validation cohort (N = 92). From another center, we collected 82 patients as an external test cohort. The reference standard for ETE was postoperative histopathological examination, defined as tumor infiltration beyond the thyroid capsule. Consistent with NCCN guidelines, both minimal and gross ETE were considered positive. Pathological evaluations were performed by experienced pathologists (> 10 years) at each center, and ETE status was extracted from original pathological reports. Given the retrospective design, centralized cross-center pathology review was not performed; however, all centers applied consistent diagnostic criteria. Acquisition of US and cytology images

US machines included Philips Q5, Q7, iU22; GE Logiq E9, Vivid E9; Toshiba Aplio 500; and Mindray Resona7, 7s, 8T, all equipped with 6–15 MHz superficial probes. All patients underwent routine US examinations within one week before surgery, performed by experienced radiologists (> 5 years of experience). Patients were placed in the supine position with pillows removed to lower the head and tilt it slightly, exposing the neck region as much as possible. US examination of the thyroid and neck was performed using longitudinal and horizontal sequential scanning to obtain maximum diameter views of the nodule.

All patients underwent US-guided FNA preoperatively to obtain cellular biopsy material on two or three slides, which were sent to the pathology department for initial diagnosis of diseased nodules. Each slide was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For each patient, all H&E-stained slides were evaluated by a senior pathologist (> 10 years of experience). A slide with the largest tumor-enriched area was selected, and characteristic pictures observed under a microscope (40× objective lens) were transferred to a computer for storage and analysis.

ROI and feature extraction from US images

The radiologist was informed of the location of the surgically confirmed tumor but was unaware of other pathologic findings and clinical information. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually outlined on all two-dimensional US images by one radiologist (5 years of diagnostic US experience) using ITK-SNAP software version 4.0 (http://www.itksnap.org). As this was a retrospective study, we used the maximum diameter section of each thyroid nodule for ROI delineation, because this is the standard view routinely captured and stored by radiologists during clinical practice for diagnostic assessment and measurement. This single section typically provides the most representative view of tumor morphology and invasion features, and using it helps maintain consistency with real-world clinical workflows while reducing segmentation variability.To assess ROI consistency, 50 patients were randomly selected, and the ROIs were outlined again by a second radiologist (6 years of diagnostic US experience). The interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess inter-observer and intra-observer consistency of feature extraction. An ICC > 0.75 was considered excellent [31].

Before feature extraction, all US images were preprocessed to reduce inter-scanner variability: (1) images were resampled to a uniform spatial resolution to eliminate scale differences; (2) Z-score normalization was applied to each image, transforming the intensity distribution to a standard normal distribution (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) to effectively mitigate global intensity biases caused by variations in device gain and contrast settings.

For US image ROIs, the “pyradiomics” package imported from open-source Python software version 3.1.2 (https://www.python.org) was used for feature extraction. Extracted features were categorized into four classes: (1) shape features (e.g., maximum diameter, area, sphericity); (2) first-order statistical features (e.g., energy, entropy, kurtosis); (3) texture features (e.g., gray-level co-occurrence matrix [GLCM], gray-level run-length matrix [GLRLM], gray-level dependence matrix [GLDM]); and (4) wavelet features (mainly extracted by transforming the image using wavelet functions to describe texture and low-order features).

Feature extraction from cytological images

All H&E-stained slides from each patient were evaluated by an experienced pathologist (10 years of diagnostic thyroid cytology). The most characteristic areas of the tumor were selected, transferred to a computer, and the entire image was used as the ROI.

Color normalization of cytology images was performed using the StainTools toolkit for Python software version 3.12 (https://www.python.org), which incorporates the Vahadane algorithm to effectively ensure comparability of FNA images from different centers. Subsequently, we used CellProfiler software version 4.0.7 (https://cellprofiler.org/) to extract quantitative pathomics features from typical pathology screenshots. First, we used the “ColorToGray” module to convert H&E-stained images to grayscale and the “Unmix Colors” module to separate H&E-stained images into hematoxylin-stained and eosin-stained grayscale images. We then extracted general features including colocalization, granularity, image intensity, image quality, and texture. Second, we focused on extracting object features using the separated hematoxylin-stained images. Primary objects and secondary objects were identified, and object intensity, object size/shape, and texture features were extracted. In addition, we calculated the mean, median, and standard deviation of these features. After carefully screening and excluding unnecessary image features, we integrated the overall features with the object features to form a comprehensive set of pathomics features.

Feature selection and model construction

Extracted radiomics features (with ICC ≥ 0.75) and pathomics features were normalized using Z-score transformation. A total of 810 radiomics features and 1,143 pathomics features were initially extracted. All feature selection steps were performed strictly within the training cohort to avoid data leakage. No missing data were present in the extracted features after quality control, as cases with poor-quality ultrasound or cytology images had been excluded during patient selection.

Feature selection was conducted as follows. First, univariate rank-sum test was used to eliminate non-significant features (P > 0.05). After this step, 469 radiomics features and 248 pathomics features remained. Second, Spearman correlation analysis was performed, and features with a correlation coefficient exceeding 0.6 were considered redundant and excluded. This step yielded 27 radiomics features and 22 pathomics features. Third, LASSO regression was applied to select the most predictive features. This process yielded 15 radiomics features and 12 pathomics features.

After combining the selected radiomics and pathomics features, Spearman correlation analysis was again performed with a threshold of 0.5 to remove redundant features, followed by LASSO regression to obtain the final feature set for the radiopathomics model. This process yielded 21 optimal features (10 radiomics and 11 pathomics). The coefficients presented in Table 2 are the LASSO regression coefficients from this final feature selection step.

Three models (radiomics, pathomics, and radiopathomics) were constructed using the Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm. To ensure fair comparison, all models were trained using identical hyperparameters. Hyperparameters were selected and optimized through ten-fold cross-validation based on model performance on the training cohort. The final hyperparameters were set as follows: number of trees = 100, learning rate = 0.05, maximum tree depth = 3, minimum child weight = 5, subsample ratio = 0.7, column sampling ratio per tree = 0.7, L1 regularization term = 1, L2 regularization term = 5, and minimum loss reduction required for a split = 1. These parameter configurations were determined to balance model fitting and generalization performance.

To address the “black box” nature of ML, this study applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to calculate the contribution of each variable to the radiopathomics model. These visualizations are intended to improve clinical understanding and interpretation of model predictions [32, 33].

Diagnostic performance of radiologists

To compare the diagnostic efficacy of ML, we invited nine radiologists to independently evaluate preoperative US images in the external validation cohort without knowledge of pathologic results. The radiologists were categorized into three groups based on thyroid diagnostic experience: junior radiologists (Radiologists 1, 2, 3: <5 years), intermediate radiologists (Radiologists 4, 5, 6: 5–10 years), and senior radiologists (Radiologists 7, 8, 9: >10 years). ETE was diagnosed according to AJCC criteria if either of the following two criteria were met: (1) more than 25% of the lesion circumference was in contact with the thyroid capsule or loss of the peritumoral line where the lesion contacted the thyroid; or (2) a tumor of any size extended beyond the thyroid capsule and invaded subcutaneous soft tissues, larynx, trachea, esophagus, recurrent laryngeal nerve, carotid artery, or mediastinal vessels [34, 35].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software version 4.3.2 (http://www.r-project.org) and SPSS software version 23.0 (http://www.ibm.com/spss). Quantitative data (age and tumor size) were normally distributed and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Qualitative data were expressed as numbers and percentages (N %), with between-group comparisons using the chi-square test. To compare the diagnostic performance of the single-modality models (radiomics and pathomics) and the radiologist group with the radiopathomics model, we assessed predictive performance, calibration ability, and clinical utility using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, AUC, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (PLR), and negative likelihood ratio (NLR) were also calculated. The DeLong test was used to compare the AUCs of different models.

Explanation of model

SHAP analysis was employed to interpret the radiopathomics model. The beeswarm plot (Fig. 5), which visualizes the top 13 contributing features, revealed that radiomics features, particularly those derived from wavelet transformations, played a dominant role in predicting ETE, while pathomics features provided supplementary predictive value. This underscores the validity of our multimodal approach.

Four representative cases from the external validation cohort (Fig. 6) further illustrate model interpretability at the individual level. Patient A, a non-ETE case (46-year-old female, 5.2 mm nodule, TI-RADS 4a), was correctly predicted with a probability of 9.9% and a SHAP value of − 2.206. Patient B, an ETE case (63-year-old female, 12.6 mm nodule, TI-RADS 4c), was correctly predicted with a probability of 79.5% and a SHAP value of 1.355. Patient C, an ETE case (74-year-old female, 15.8 mm nodule, TI-RADS 4c), was correctly predicted with a probability of 72.2% and a SHAP value of 0.954. Patient D, a non-ETE case (49-year-old female, 4.9 mm nodule, TI-RADS 4a), was correctly predicted with a probability of 7.9% and a SHAP value of − 2.459. In all four instances, the model predictions aligned with postoperative pathological outcomes. Patients A and D exhibited low SHAP values with predominantly blue colors, indicating negative predictions consistent with non-ETE pathology, while Patients B and C showed high SHAP values with predominantly red colors, indicating positive predictions consistent with ETE pathology.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed and validated a radiopathomics model based on ultrasound and cytology images for the preoperative prediction of ETE in PTC. The model demonstrated excellent and robust performance across the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, with AUCs of 0.887, 0.857, and 0.873, respectively. Its diagnostic efficacy significantly surpassed that of radiologists’ assessments. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to fuse ultrasound radiomics with cytology pathomics for predicting PTC ETE, offering a novel multimodal solution for accurate preoperative evaluation.

US, as a noninvasive, real-time, reproducible medical imaging test, is the preferred screening method for thyroid nodules [36], However, due to the anatomical location of the nodule, the subjectivity of physician diagnosis, and equipment resolution, radiologists‘ accuracy in preoperative diagnosis of ETE is low, with an accuracy rate of only 56%–65% [37, 38]. In our study, the highest AUC for ETE diagnosis by nine radiologists was 0.685, which is hardly sufficient to meet clinical needs. Over the years, radiomics has been widely used in biomedicine as a method for quantitatively extracting shape, texture, and other features from images, revealing many features undetectable by the human eye. Consequently, numerous studies have investigated ETE in PTC using radiomics. For example, Jiang et al. [39] used a clinical radiomics nomogram based on B-mode and contrast-enhanced ultrasound to predict ETE in PTC, achieving an AUC of 0.792 in the validation cohort for the best model. Similarly, Lu et al. [37] developed a radiomics nomogram based on three-dimensional US to predict ETE in PTC, with the radiomics nomogram achieving an AUC of 0.798 in the validation cohort. However, these studies focused only on macroscopic image features, and unimodal features do not capture the complete tumor heterogeneity.

Previous studies have shown that combining macroscopic image information with microscopic image features can significantly improve the accuracy and performance of prediction models. For instance, Hu et al. [40] constructed a multimodal model for predicting biochemical recurrence of prostate cancer after surgery on the basis of whole-slide imaging technique and two-parameter MRI. The model showed the best predictive efficacy on the test cohort with a C-index as high as 0.860, a value that was significantly higher than the predictions of all unimodal models. Similarly, Yang et al. [41] developed and validated an artificially intelligent radiomics model combining preoperative CT scans with postoperative H&E-stained slide images, which was designed to accurately predict the pathological staging of gastric cancer. The results of the study showed that the model had high area under the AUC values of up to 0.953 and 0.851 for subjects in the training cohort and validation cohort, respectively, a performance that outperformed all other models. Based on the inspiration from the above studies, we attempted to integrate image information representing microscopic features for a comprehensive assessment of PTC ETE in this study. In clinical practice, FNA is usually performed for suspected malignant nodules with an ACR TI-RADS classification of category 3 or higher on US to obtain more accurate diagnostic information [42]. FNA allows direct visualization of lesion cells, including morphology, structure, and nuclear division, providing direct and powerful evidence to accurately determine the nature of the lesion. In this context, the microscopic features revealed by FNA complement the macroscopic features of US well. Therefore, we constructed a more effective ML model to predict ETE in PTC by combining US radiomics with cytology pathomics from a multimodal perspective. Experimental results show that the fusion model exhibits optimal prediction performance in all three cohorts, further validating its robustness and reliability and providing strong support for future clinical diagnosis and treatment.

To overcome the opaque nature of ML models [43], this study used SHAP analysis to visualize the importance of model features. SHAP assigns a value to each feature representing its contribution to the model output. This helps understand the extent to which each feature contributes to the predicted outcome, making ML model predictions more transparent and interpretable. As shown in the SHAP figure (Fig. 5), among the top 13 features, eight are radiomics features and five are pathomics features. This indicates that radiomics features play a major role in diagnosing ETE, while pathomics features provide additional support, further validating the radiopathomics model we developed.

To evaluate the clinical feasibility of the ML model, we invited nine experienced radiologists to assess ETE in PTC patients using preoperative US images. In our current study, even the most experienced senior radiologists achieved a maximum AUC of only 0.685, with an average AUC of 0.672 across the nine radiologists, insufficient to meet clinical requirements for precise treatment planning. In contrast, the radiopathomics model demonstrated superior diagnostic performance, improving diagnostic accuracy by 14.2% compared with the experienced radiologists (69.9%). However, it should be noted that in this comparison, the model integrated both ultrasound and cytology information, whereas the radiologists based their assessments solely on ultrasound images. Therefore, interpretation of these comparative results should be approached with caution. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that machine learning models have potential value in assisting ETE diagnosis. Our ML model may assist in treatment planning and support personalized therapeutic approaches. The model is designed to detect the presence of any ETE, as its identification—regardless of whether minimal or gross—is a key determinant of surgical strategy according to current clinical guidelines. Distinguishing between m-ETE and g-ETE for refined prognostic stratification remains a valuable direction for future research.

The radiopathomics model developed in this study demonstrates not only improved diagnostic performance but also potential clinical translation value. This model could potentially be integrated into routine thyroid nodule diagnostic and management pathways. Specifically, after patients complete preoperative ultrasound and FNA cytology examinations, relevant imaging and cytological features could be extracted to compute a quantitative assessment of ETE risk through the model, which may then be appended as supplementary information to clinical reports.

In clinical decision-making, this model may provide an objective, quantitative auxiliary reference for surgical planning. For PTC patients identified by the model as high-risk for ETE, surgeons may more confidently adhere to NCCN guidelines by opting for total thyroidectomy along with corresponding lymph node dissection, aiming to reduce the risk of residual disease or reoperation due to inadequate preoperative evaluation.

Furthermore, this model may serve as an auxiliary tool to support diagnostic work in radiology and pathology departments. On one hand, it could provide objective risk stratification for less experienced clinicians, helping to reduce subjective variability in diagnosis. On the other hand, its SHAP interpretability analysis results (Figs. 5 and 6) may assist physicians in gaining deeper insights into key imaging and pathological features influencing ETE determination, thereby enhancing diagnostic consistency. Future efforts should focus on developing user-friendly clinical interfaces, integrating the model with hospital Picture Archiving and Communication Systems (PACS) and laboratory information systems, and validating its impact on surgical outcomes and patient prognosis through prospective, multicenter trials.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective study, which may introduce selection bias. Second, the sample size, particularly for the external validation cohort (n = 82), was relatively limited, which may have reduced statistical power to detect significant differences in DeLong’s test. Third, manual segmentation of ROIs on ultrasound images is time-consuming and labor-intensive. Fourth, ultrasound ROIs were delineated with knowledge of the tumor location confirmed by surgery, which may introduce optimistic bias and deviate from the real preoperative scenario. To mitigate this limitation, we performed strict intra- and inter-observer consistency analysis (ICC > 0.75), independent internal and external validation, and ten-fold cross-validation to ensure the robustness of model performance. Future prospective studies with blinded ROI segmentation are warranted. Fifth, for cytology analysis, whole slides were used as ROIs, which may include non-tumor components such as blood or inflammatory cells, potentially affecting the specificity of pathomics features. Finally, although the radiopathomics model demonstrated acceptable calibration in both the internal and external validation cohorts, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test in the external validation cohort approached but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.061). Future multi-center studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to further refine model calibration.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully developed and validated a radiopathomics model for predicting ETE in PTC by fusing preoperative US and FNA cytology images. The model demonstrated excellent discriminative performance across training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts (AUCs: 0.887, 0.857, and 0.873, respectively), with acceptable calibration in the external validation cohort (HL P = 0.061, Brier = 0.195). The radiopathomics model outperformed both single-modality (radiomics or pathomics) models and radiologists‘ assessments, offering a promising auxiliary tool for preoperative risk stratification and individualized clinical evaluation.
