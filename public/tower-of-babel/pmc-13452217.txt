County-Level Geographic Access and Cardiovascular Disease Outcomes in Washington State
Authors: Vladislav Baglaev, Hovan Dermendjian, Serj Dermendjian, Alexander Mammar, Ernest Balezi, Annabella M Hochschild, Jay D Pal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeographic access to specialized cardiac care varies widely, yet how these disparities relate to population-level cardiovascular disease (CVD) outcomes remains poorly understood.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to evaluate county-level disparities in geographic access to specialized cardiac care and their associations with CVD prevalence, hospitalization, and mortality across Washington state.MethodsWe assessed county-level variation in average travel time and distance to facilities offering catheterization laboratory, coronary intervention, cardiac surgery, and emergency services across all 39 Washington state counties using publicly available data and the Google Maps API. Reliance on critical access hospitals (CAHs) was also evaluated. Age-adjusted CVD prevalence, mortality, and hospitalization rates were obtained from the CDC Atlas of Heart Disease and Stroke (2019-2021) and multivariable linear regression were used to assess associations between county-level accessibility metrics and CVD outcomes.ResultsIn adjusted univariable analyses, shorter travel time and distance to catheterization laboratories, coronary intervention, and cardiac surgery facilities were associated with higher cardiovascular and heart disease hospitalization rates (R2 = 0.34-0.50, all adjusted P < 0.05). Conversely, greater rurality and longer travel burden to these specialized cardiac services were associated with higher prevalence of coronary heart disease, hypertension, and stroke (R2 = 0.35-0.56; all adjusted P < 0.05). No county-level predictor was associated with overall heart disease prevalence. For mortality, higher area deprivation index and lower median household income were the only significant predictors, each associated with higher ischemic stroke mortality (R2 = 0.41-0.43; both adjusted P < 0.01). In multivariable analyses, travel burden remained independently associated with hospitalization but not with prevalence or mortality after adjustment for area deprivation, rurality, and age structure. CAH–reliant counties had lower hospitalization rates but higher CVD prevalence compared with non-CAH-reliant counties (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsGreater travel burden to specialized cardiac care was associated with higher CVD prevalence and lower hospitalization but not mortality. Socioeconomic factors most strongly predicted CVD mortality, suggesting that reducing geographic disparities requires addressing both care access and socioeconomic determinants.Key words: cardiovascular disease, critical access hospitals, health care accessibility, rural health, socioeconomic status, travel burden

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death in the United States, accounting for over 700,000 deaths annually.1 In Washington state, heart disease accounted for approximately 11,967 deaths (19.1%) in 2022.2 CVD prevalence and mortality continue to rise, with projections estimating that by 2050, more than 184 million U.S. adults will be living with CVD.3,4 The role of environmental, socioeconomic, and behavioral risk factors as predictors of CVD burden is well established.5, 6, 7, 8 However, these factors vary substantially across regions, contributing to persistent disparities in outcomes. Rural communities are particularly vulnerable, and these disparities persist even after accounting for baseline risk factors.9, 10, 11

Geographic access to cardiac care, proximity to hospitals with catheterization laboratories, and the travel burden to reach definitive care vary substantially by region and may contribute to these inequities.12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Less is known about how differences in geospatial proximity and access to care affect observed regional disparities in CVD burden and outcomes.

We sought to examine how geographic disparities in access to cardiovascular services influence CVD outcomes across Washington’s 39 counties. We evaluated accessibility using average travel times and distances from county population centers to hospitals with varying levels of cardiovascular capability, as well as workforce availability, hospital bed capacity, and baseline access to care. Three categories of county-level factors were hypothesized a priori to explain variation in CVD outcomes: 1) baseline access to care and preventive health use; 2) spatial proximity and travel burden to cardiovascular services; and 3) cardiovascular health care workforce and infrastructure capacity (Supplemental Table 1). Understanding these relationships is important to inform resource allocation, achieve equitable care delivery, and ultimately reduce regional disparities in CVD burden.

Methods

Data sources and ethics

All data used in this study are publicly available and deidentified, and the study was therefore exempt from Institutional Review Board approval by the University of Washington.

Study population and variables

All 39 counties in Washington state (encompassing 7.77 million inhabitants) were included. County-level socioeconomic disadvantage was first characterized using the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), a validated composite measure derived from 17 indicators including education, employment, housing quality, and poverty.17 We assigned county-level ADI by computing the population-weighted median ADI across all census block groups within each county on a national percentile scale of 1 (lowest disadvantage) to 100 (highest disadvantage). The median household income data for 2021 were obtained from the Washington Office of Financial Management. Data on percentage of population without insurance, with delayed care due to cost, with a personal physician, and with a checkup within the past year were obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System for 2016 to 2020. The most recently available percentage of rural population was obtained from the Washington Department of Health based on the 2010 U.S. Census. The full list of predictor variables is provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Geographic proximity and travel burden

Medical facilities were identified using the American Hospital Directory, and the following services were cataloged per each care center: 1) outpatient catheterization laboratory; 2) inpatient coronary intervention; 3) cardiac surgery; and 4) emergency services. Average travel distance (miles) and time (minutes) from each county’s population-weighted geographic center (2020 U.S. Census) to the nearest facility offering each service were calculated using the Google Maps Distance Matrix API, assuming driving at 9 AM on a Wednesday. This service computes the ideal driving route between 2 sites and incorporates real-time traffic estimates to standardize driving durations across counties. Automobile travel at 9 AM on a Wednesday was used to approximate a standard weekday commuting period. critical access hospital (CAH) status was identified for each hospital. CAHs are federally designated small hospitals (≤25 beds) designed to ensure Medicare beneficiaries have access to care in rural areas.18 Counties were classified as “CAH-reliant” if all hospitals within the county held CAH designation.

Physician workforce and infrastructure

County-level physician counts were obtained from the 2022 to 2024 Physician Demographic Census by the Washington Medical Commission. Density of primary care physicians, cardiologists, cardiothoracic surgeons, emergency medicine physicians, critical care specialists, and total cardiovascular providers was calculated per 100,000 residents using 2021 county population estimates by the Washington Office of Financial Management. Hospital bed number and capacity across all county hospitals was derived and calculated per 100,000 residents using American Hospital Directory hospital profiles.

Cardiovascular disease outcomes

County-level CVD mortality, hospitalization, and prevalence data were derived from the CDC Atlas of Heart Disease and Stroke for 2019 to 2021. Mortality rates were based on death certificates from the National Vital Statistics System, classified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes for diseases of the circulatory system (Supplemental Table 2). Prevalence data for heart disease, coronary heart disease, hypertension, and stroke were obtained as percentage of adults with each condition. Hospitalization rates included total cardiovascular, heart disease, and avoidable hospitalizations per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries. All outcome data were age-adjusted by the CDC using the 2000 U.S. Standard Population.

Statistical analysis

Univariable linear regression using ordinary least squares was performed to assess the association between each county-level factor and each CVD outcome. The regression coefficient (β), coefficient of determination (R2), and P value were computed for each model. Welch t-tests were used to compare CVD outcomes between CAH-only and non-CAH-only counties. To address potential confounding among intercorrelated predictors, multivariable hierarchical linear regression models were fit for each primary outcome, with model 1 including ADI and percent rural population, model 2 adding a single travel-burden metric, and model 3 additionally adjusting for percent of population aged ≥65 years. The Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons and statistical significance was defined as Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12; Python Software Foundation).

Results

Washington state’s 39 counties and 7.77 million residents are served by 13,244 general medicine practitioners and 3,575 surgeons, including 554 cardiologists (∼6.9 per 100,000) and 108 cardiothoracic surgeons (∼1.3 per 100,000). In 2021, county-level CVD outcomes varied considerably: median total CVD hospitalizations were 4,230 (IQR: 3,335-4635) per 100,000, median heart disease prevalence was 26.3 (IQR: 24.9-27.7), and median total CVD mortality was 386 (IQR: 359.1-410.2) per 100,000. Average travel distances to the nearest facility were 34.9 ± 35.1 miles for catheterization laboratory, 39.3 ± 34.6 miles for coronary intervention, 47.1 ± 33.4 miles for cardiac surgery, and 9.0 ± 6.6 miles for emergency services. Hospitalization rates

County-level predictors of hospitalization differed across hospitalization types (Table 2, Figure 1). In univariable analyses, higher rates of total cardiovascular hospitalization were most strongly associated with shorter travel time to catheterization laboratories (R2 = 0.50, Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001), shorter distance to catheterization laboratories (R2 = 0.49; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001), shorter distance to coronary intervention facilities (R2 = 0.49; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001), and shorter travel time to coronary intervention facilities (R2 = 0.48; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001), followed by shorter travel time (R2 = 0.36; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.026) and distance (R2 = 0.35; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.033) to cardiac surgery. Greater vascular surgeon density was also independently associated with higher total cardiovascular hospitalization (R2 = 0.34; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.044). For total heart disease hospitalization, shorter distance (R2 = 0.44; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.002) and travel time (R2 = 0.44; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.002) to coronary intervention facilities, shorter travel time (R2 = 0.43; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.003) and distance (R2 = 0.41; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.005) to catheterization laboratories, and shorter travel time to cardiac surgery (R2 = 0.34; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.049) were significantly associated with higher hospitalization rates. For preventable hospitalizations, travel time to coronary intervention facilities was the strongest predictor (R2 = 0.41; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.005), whereas percentage of population with an annual checkup was the only positively associated predictor (R2 = 0.26; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001). No measure of rurality, area deprivation, or median household income was significantly associated with hospitalization rates.

Cardiovascular disease prevalence

Interestingly, predictors of CVD prevalence showed a pattern opposite of hospitalization (Table 3, Figure 2). In univariable analyses, percent rural population was the strongest predictor of coronary heart disease (R2 = 0.56; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001), stroke (R2 = 0.54; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001), and hypertension (R2 = 0.46, Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001) prevalence, with higher rurality associated with higher prevalence. Greater distance and travel time to catheterization laboratories, coronary intervention, and cardiac surgery were consistently associated with higher prevalence of hypertension, coronary heart disease, and stroke. For coronary heart disease prevalence, predictors included distance to cardiac surgery (R2 = 0.47; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001), travel time to cardiac surgery (R2 = 0.46; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.001), travel time to coronary intervention (R2 = 0.41; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.006); distance to coronary intervention (R2 = 0.38; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.014), and travel time to catheterization laboratory (R2 = 0.36; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.026). For stroke prevalence, predictors included distance to cardiac surgery (R2 = 0.47; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001), travel time to cardiac surgery (R2 = 0.45; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.002), travel time to coronary intervention (R2 = 0.38; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.013), and distance to coronary intervention (R2 = 0.37; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.021). For hypertension prevalence, predictors included distance to cardiac surgery (R2 = 0.37; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.019) and travel time to cardiac surgery (R2 = 0.35; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.039). Socioeconomic factors were also significantly associated with CVD prevalence: lower median household income was associated with higher coronary heart disease (R2 = 0.38; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.013) and stroke (R2 = 0.38; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.013) prevalence, and a higher percentage of households without broadband internet access was associated with higher coronary heart disease (R2 = 0.36; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.023) and stroke (R2 = 0.40; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.009) prevalence. No county-level predictor was significantly associated with overall heart disease prevalence.

In multivariable analyses, the apparent associations between travel burden and CVD prevalence were substantially attenuated (Supplemental Table 3). ADI and percent rural population together explained 0.48 to 0.59 of the variance in hypertension, CHD, and stroke prevalence in model. After this adjustment, no travel-burden measure remained independently associated with hypertension, CHD, or stroke prevalence under either Bonferroni or false discovery rate correction. Additional adjustment for percent of population aged ≥65 years (model 3) yielded a substantial increase in explained variance (ΔR2 = 0.16-0.33) and model R2 reaching 0.80 to 0.87 across prevalence outcomes, indicating that county age composition is a major determinant of measured prevalence variation. These results suggest that the univariable associations between geographic access and CVD prevalence were largely confounded by socioeconomic disadvantage, rurality, and age structure rather than reflecting an independent effect of travel burden.

Cardiovascular mortality

In contrast to hospitalization and prevalence, no county-level metrics of care proximity or provider density significantly predicted CVD mortality (Table 4). The only significant predictors of mortality were median ADI (R2 = 0.43; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.003) and median household income (R2 = 0.41; Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.005), each associated with ischemic stroke mortality.

Multivariable analyses confirmed this pattern for the mortality outcomes (Supplemental Table 3). After adjustment for ADI and percent rural population (model 1 R2 = 0.29 for total CVD mortality, 0.32 for heart disease mortality, and 0.43 for ischemic stroke mortality), no measure of travel burden was independently associated with total CVD, all heart disease, coronary heart disease, heart attack, or ischemic stroke mortality. Interestingly, however, longer travel time to cardiac surgery was independently associated with higher all-stroke mortality (Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.040), longer travel time to coronary intervention was associated with higher hemorrhagic stroke mortality (Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.032), and greater distance to emergency services was associated with higher CHD mortality (Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.048).

Critical access hospitals

Among the 39 counties in Washington state, 17 are CAH reliant (43.6%). These CAH-reliant counties demonstrated significantly lower rates of total cardiovascular (33.1 vs 46.8 per 100,000; P < 0.001) and heart disease (23.7 vs 34.3 per 100,000; P < 0.001) hospitalizations compared to non-CAH-reliant counties (Table 5). However, CAH-reliant counties had significantly higher prevalence of coronary heart disease (7.5% vs 6.2%; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.05) and stroke (3.8% vs 3.3%; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.05). CVD mortality did not significantly differ between groups.

Discussion

In this county-level analysis of Washington state, we found that geographic access to specialized cardiac care was differentially associated with CVD prevalence and hospitalization but not mortality (Central Illustration). Multivariable adjustment further revealed that the hospitalization association was independent of underlying socioeconomic and demographic factors, whereas the prevalence association was largely attributable to them. Consistent with prior literature9, 10, 11,19 CVD outcomes varied substantially across counties, with some rural counties experiencing up to 70% higher prevalence rates than the most urban counties.

Travel distance and time to specialized cardiac services were among the strongest predictors of both hospitalization and prevalence but followed opposing directions in univariable analyses. Shorter travel to catheterization laboratories, coronary intervention, and cardiac surgery was associated with higher hospitalization rates but lower CVD prevalence. After adjustment for area deprivation, rurality, and county age structure, travel burden remained an independent predictor of hospitalization but no longer predicted prevalence, suggesting that the apparent geographic effect on prevalence was confounded by underlying socioeconomic disadvantage and demographic composition. The persistent hospitalization association suggests that geographic proximity to specialized services may facilitate care utilization and enable earlier disease recognition and intervention.20, 21, 22 Conversely, greater travel burden may represent a geographic barrier that leads patients to defer seeking care, contributing to higher undiagnosed and undertreated disease burden despite lower hospitalization rates.

Counties with larger rural populations and those served exclusively by CAHs exhibited a concordant pattern marked by lower hospitalization rates but higher disease prevalence. CAHs are small facilities that generally lack advanced cardiac capabilities. The lower hospitalization rates in these counties may reflect limited access to and capacity for specialized services rather than reduced clinical need. Patients in these areas may face longer delays in receiving care and may require transfer to distant higher-capacity centers.16,19

An alternative explanation for the association between proximity to specialist care and higher hospitalization is supply-induced demand, a phenomenon in which greater local availability of specialists and centers generates higher utilization regardless of underlying clinical need. Our ecological analysis cannot distinguish supply-induced demand from earlier disease detection and more aggressive treatment in well-resourced counties. The robustness of the hospitalization-proximity association after adjustment for socioeconomic and demographic factors is compatible with both mechanisms, and patient-level studies will be needed to determine which predominates.

A further consideration when interpreting these opposing outcomes is that the 3 outcome domains are drawn from different data sources. Hospitalization rates were derived from Medicare beneficiaries (age >64 years), whereas prevalence estimates cover all adults, and mortality encompasses all residents. Given substantial variation in county age structure across Washington state, this denominator mismatch may itself contribute to divergent associations. To partially address this, we adjusted for the proportion of residents aged ≥65 years in our multivariable models. Age structure proved to be a major determinant of measured prevalence variation, with model R2 rising from approximately 0.48 to 0.59 to 0.80 to 0.87 across coronary heart disease, hypertension, and stroke prevalence after age adjustment. In contrast, age adjustment produced minimal change in the hospitalization models (ΔR2 ≤0.02), indicating that the travel-burden–hospitalization association is not explained by age distribution.

No county-level predictor was significantly associated with overall heart disease prevalence after correction for multiple comparisons. We interpret this null finding as consistent with the broader pattern that overall heart disease, an aggregate category, is shaped by multiple intersecting determinants whose individual associations may be too modest to detect in a 39-county sample. By contrast, several individual predictors were significantly associated with the more specific prevalence outcomes of coronary heart disease, hypertension, and stroke in univariable analyses, but these were attenuated by adjustment for socioeconomic, rurality, and age covariates. Notably, primary care physician density was not significantly associated with prevalence in either univariable or multivariable analyses, arguing against a purely preventative-care mechanism and suggesting that the observed geographic patterns more plausibly reflect aggregate health-system capacity than specific care pathways.23, 24, 25, 26, 27

Provider density alone did not predict county-level outcomes including mortality, suggesting that system capacity, access pathways, and socioeconomic conditions play a more critical role than specialist availability per se. This is consistent with the broader observation that cardiovascular care delivery requires coordinated infrastructure including specialized anesthesiologists, critical care physicians, and interventional teams alongside surgical capacity, such that any single provider category may serve as a marker of, rather than a driver of, health-system development.

Notably, although geographic access predicted CVD hospitalization, it did not predict mortality after Bonferroni correction. Instead, higher area deprivation and lower median household income were the only significant predictors of CVD mortality, with both showing strong associations with ischemic stroke mortality specifically. In multivariable analyses, 3 exploratory associations did emerge among less common mortality outcomes. Namely, longer travel time to cardiac surgery with higher all-stroke mortality, longer travel time to coronary intervention with higher hemorrhagic stroke mortality, and greater distance to emergency services with higher coronary heart disease mortality. However, these findings warrant cautious interpretation given the small number of counties and modest baseline R2 in these models. The broader divergence between mortality and prevalence/hospitalization may reflect that mortality results from cumulative disease accrual driven by chronic socioeconomic disadvantage24,26, 27, 28, 29 whereas prevalence and hospitalization are more proximal indicators sensitive to care access at a given time point.36, 30, 31, 32, 33 In addition, patients presenting with advanced or acute CVD are often transferred to tertiary centers providing standardized interventions, which may attenuate the effects of baseline geographic access on mortality outcomes.34, 35, 37 Higher percentages of households without broadband internet access, which can serve as a marker of broader infrastructural and socioeconomic disadvantages, were also significantly associated with higher coronary heart disease and stroke prevalence, further underscoring the interplay between socioeconomic determinants and disease burden.

These findings have important policy implications. Analyses that focus solely on socioeconomic, environmental, or lifestyle risk factors fail to account for the modifying effects of geography, provider distribution, and health-system capacity. In CAH-reliant regions, strengthening cardiac infrastructure to support earlier recognition and management of CVD may improve care utilization and reduce disease burden. Strategies such as telemedicine and mobile catheterization laboratories may extend diagnostic and interventional capability to geographically vulnerable populations. However, our multivariable findings underscore that improving geographic access alone is unlikely to reduce CVD mortality without concurrently addressing the socioeconomic conditions that shape disease burden.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. All analyses were performed at the county level, which may not capture within-county or patient-level variation and introduces the possibility of ecological fallacy. Consequently, individual-level inferences cannot be made. Outcomes were also derived from Medicare beneficiaries, limiting generalizability to younger or non-Medicare populations. Unmeasured confounding may influence the observed associations, and any causal relationships cannot be established. The temporal lag between county-level exposures and observed CVD outcomes is unknown. Although we applied Bonferroni correction across hypothesis tests, given the large number of predictor-outcome relationships examined, our analyses should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. County-level data may also be subject to inconsistent reporting across jurisdictions.

With only 39 counties, our analysis has limited statistical power to detect modest associations, particularly for mortality outcomes where effect sizes were smaller than for prevalence or hospitalization. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant relationship between geographic access and mortality should be interpreted as an absence of a detectable association within our sample rather than evidence that no such relationship exists. Analyses extending to multiple states would be better powered to detect whether such a link is present.

Travel burden was estimated based on private automobile travel on a weekday during commuting hours and therefore does not incorporate patients who lack access to a personal vehicle or the effect of seasonal and weather-related road closures, which more frequently affect rural and mountainous counties. Patients in the most isolated counties also tend to have the fewest alternatives for method or route of transportation. Our approach therefore likely underestimates the true travel burden faced by residents of the most vulnerable counties, which might tend to attenuate observed associations and suggest our reported effect sizes are conservative.

We did not incorporate county-level race or ethnicity data as predictors or confounders, although these characteristics are reported descriptively in Table 1. Washington's counties vary considerably in racial and ethnic composition, and racial and ethnic identity has well-documented independent associations with cardiovascular risk and health care access. Because these characteristics may further correlate with geographic, socioeconomic, and access measures examined here, their omission represents a potential source of residual confounding, and our findings should be interpreted with caution.

As noted earlier, our data are drawn from multiple publicly available sources that capture differing time spans, reflecting the most contemporaneous data applicable to our region and a limitation inherent to the use of publicly available data. Notably, our analysis period includes the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted access to preventative care and may have reduced overall health care utilization. Noncontemporaneous measurements may also be affected by intervening economic, pandemic-related, and political trends.

Conclusions

In this county-level analysis of all 39 Washington counties, geographic access to specialized cardiac care was independently associated with cardiovascular hospitalization but not with prevalence or mortality after adjustment for area deprivation, rurality, and population age structure. Apparent univariable associations between travel burden and CVD prevalence were largely attributable to underlying socioeconomic disadvantage and demographic composition, whereas CVD mortality was predicted only by area deprivation and median household income. Together, these findings reframe geographic access as a determinant of where and how cardiovascular care is delivered, rather than a primary driver of who lives or dies from CVD. Reducing geographic inequities may close meaningful gaps in care utilization in rural and CAH-reliant communities but achieving durable reductions in cardiovascular mortality will require addressing the socioeconomic conditions that shape disease burden long before patients reach specialized care.

COMPETENCY IN SYSTEMS-BASED PRACTICE: Proximity to specialized cardiac services, rurality, reliance on CAHs, and physician density significantly influence county-level CVD prevalence and hospitalization in Washington state. Limited geographic access to comprehensive cardiac care may contribute to observed disparities in disease burden and lower rates of care utilization.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Integration of population-level geographic access to comprehensive cardiac care into health policy planning and resource allocation may help identify vulnerable populations and guide efforts to reduce disparities in care access and disease burden.
