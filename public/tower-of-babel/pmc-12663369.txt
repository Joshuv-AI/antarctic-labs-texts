Molecular mechanisms of CO2 mineralization on wetting nanoscale surfaces using molecular simulations and metadynamics
Authors: Xinping Zhu, Yong Tao, Romain Dupuis, Yining Gao, Chi-Sun Poon, Katerina Ioannidou, Roland J-M Pellenq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Carbonatable minerals on earth have significant potential to act as gigatonne-scale CO2 sinks. Many carbon removal managements rely on CO2 mineralization on wetting mineral surfaces. Realizing their carbon removal potential requires a fundamental understanding of the atomic-scale mechanisms of mineral carbonation. This study employs reactive/non-reactive molecular simulations and well-tempered metadynamics to elucidate the complete interfacial CO2 mineralization pathways within a portlandite mesopore adsorbed with a nanometric water film. Here we reveal quantitatively, for the first time, a global CO2 mineralization spectrum describing the local molecular environment and the thermodynamics of the five critical steps: water adsorption, calcium dissolution, CO2 adsorption, CO2 speciation, and CaCO3 ion pairing. We identify kinks as the primary reactive sites for surface dissolution and demonstrate how the water film’s acid-base environment modulates these processes, creating an energetically favorable reaction loop for sustained CO2 mineralization. We uncover that quasi-neutral to slightly basic conditions optimize mineralization efficiency by balancing the opposing effects of pH on calcium dissolution and CO2 speciation.

Introduction

Carbon dioxide (CO2) mineralization is a complex process with significant implications for natural CO2 capture strategies1, construction industry solid waste re-utilization2, environmental remediation3, and geological CO2 storage4. As the second largest industrial CO2 emitter, the cement industry contributes to about 8% of the global anthropogenic CO2 emission5. However, the alkaline and high-calcium nature of cement concrete enables it to serve as a large carbon sink due to natural weathering carbonation6. From 1930 to 2019, 21.02 Gt CO2 was absorbed by cement concrete in various constructions and infrastructures7. Note that the natural weathering carbonation is slow, while it can be accelerated under specific laboratory conditions. Considering the tremendous amount of carbonatable concrete related materials generated each year (3.9 Gt in 2020)8, accelerated carbonation of these materials has a significant potential to offset CO2. The current carbonation technologies of concrete materials only compromise to mitigating 0.8% of the global anthropogenic CO28. It is imperative to understand the fundamental mechanism of CO2 mineralization to accelerate this carbonation process, to propose an efficient and permanent geologic docking of atmospheric CO2.

The CO2 mineralization kinetics relies on (i) the stability of the mineral surfaces in relation to dissolution when in contact with an aqueous solution, (ii) the transport of the CO2 molecule in the electrolyte that ensures both ionic and molecular mobilities, and (iii) the reactivity leading to precipitation of carbonate. The surface stability with regard to an aqueous electrolyte depends on both its hydrophilic/phobic character in terms of the surface density of the OH− groups, which also reflects the surface roughness seen through the presence of kinks or other extended topological defects9. Furthermore, water plays a crucial role in governing ion transport in confined environments, significantly influenced by steric effects as profoundly revealed by Chen et al.10. Meanwhile, it can also act as a reactant in chemical processes11, particularly under varying pH conditions that significantly influence reaction rates. In general, the carbonation rate and degree of minerals are limited in extremely water-poor (dry) conditions, indicating the key role of water in the overall process11. However, carbonation can occur rapidly in a water-limited environment11, thus showing that there should be an optimum water content for maximum carbonation at a relative humidity (RH) less than unity. In mesoporous/macroporous or nonporous systems, this corresponds to the presence of a nanometric water film on all internal (pores) and external surfaces of the considered Mg2+/Ca2+ alkaline earth oxide. This nano-thick aqueous layer can be seen as a nanoreactor because it possesses unique properties that can significantly impact the chemical reactions occurring in this interfacial environment. In fact, it provides an easy way to reach appropriate concentrations for chemical reactions (such as precipitation) to occur. On the molecular scale, the orientations of the water molecules in the thin water film on the surface of a mineral are highly ordered12. Consequently, the water film gains a small dielectric permittivity13, which contributes to ion complexation and stabilizing precipitates14. The ultra confinement conditions provided by this thin water film dramatically increase the probability of encountering dissolved ions that can then react with CO2 (compared to that in an equivalent bulk aqueous solution)15. Another aspect concerns the role of pH and its definition close to a surface, inducing charged surfaces to the proximity of which will then form an electric double layer (EDL) that is known to favor solid dissolution. For all these reasons, the nanometric aqueous film can be the locus for the formation of carbonate (metastable) precipitates that can be amorphous magnesium carbonate (AMC)16, aragonite17, and amorphous calcium carbonate (ACC)18. Previous studies19 revealed that the critical carbonation reactivity thresholds are markedly affected by the water monolayers. The film thickness has a significant impact on the ion dissolution and mass transport. Our recent experimental investigations and molecular simulations20,21 further confirmed this point and demonstrated that the thickness of metastable water films also plays a critical role in modulating CO2 uptake within mesoporous solids. An optimal water-to-solid mass ratio in the range of 0.1 to 0.2 was identified, which facilitates ultrafast carbonation.

In sum, in an aqueous condition, a complete carbonation path of solids containing alkali earth metals involves a series of complex interactions between the mineral surface and the CO2 molecules, including water adsorption, ion dissolution, CO2 solvation, CO2 speciation, ion pair formation, and carbonate nucleation22. However, it is unclear whether CO2 mineralization mechanism remains on the same reaction path in the water-limiting environment compared to the bulk23. One pivotal factor is certainly the initial chemical nature and thermodynamic state of the nanometric electrolytic film adsorbed on the mineral surface. Observations in the aqueous solution show that pH and salinity can significantly change the way of CO2 speciation24 and mineral dissolution25. The acidity/basicity of the nanometric water film can also be expected to be significantly influential on the CO2 mineralization reaction pathway under water-limited conditions26. While several experimental and computational works14,23,27–29 have partially revealed certain mineral carbonation steps, there is a lack of a comprehensive mechanistic picture that allows a global understanding of the interfacial carbonation path of alkali earth-metal minerals in water-limited conditions at the molecular level. Following a systematic research program to deconstruct multiscale carbonation mechanisms within mineral pores, our prior work established the optimal moisture content for efficient carbonation by elucidating the thermodynamics that govern CO2 partitioning into confined porewater. This clarified how porewater saturation controls the reactant reservoir at the gas-liquid-solid interface. We now move beyond these transport phenomena to dissect the subsequent chemical pathways by resolving the elementary steps from the initial solvation of CO2 to the culminating formation of an ion-pair.

In this study, we explore the progressive interfacial carbonation reactions occurring in portlandite mesopores at the optimal RH using reactive molecular simulations and well-tempered metadynamics (WT-MTD), focusing on the role of the nanometric water film adsorbed on the mineral surface working as a carbonation nanoreactor. Portlandite was chosen as a solid source of Ca2+ cations as it is the first to be consumed and is believed to be the main Ca solid phase provider in set cement paste (with the ultimate idea of reusing cement deconstruction waste as carbon sinks through accelerated carbonation)30.

Here we focus on the interfacial carbonation on the basal (001) surface of the portlandite mineral. It is the predominant crystal plane of the lamellar portlandite grains, acting as the primary reaction domain. A series of physicochemical phenomena will be progressively simulated and discussed, including water adsorption on mesopores, ion dissolution, CO2 solvation, CO2 speciation, and CaCO3 ion pairing (see Fig. 1). We show that these reactions markedly depend on the acid-base environments of the film. The successive simulations finally enable us to draw a global picture of the complete interfacial CO2 mineralization pathways. It shows that CaCO3 can be generated under various acid-base conditions, even in the acidic film. However, a neutral to basic film turns out to be the favorable mineralization condition since the carbon mineralization reactions (mainly ion dissolution and CO2 speciation) compete with each other with respect to the acid-base environment. This is further observed in our carbonation experiments. Results

Water adsorption

Carbonation experiments of portlandite show that the hardened cement paste achieves the high carbonation rate at room temperature when the RH is greater than 50%31,32. Recent mesoscale simulations of water adsorption and desorption have shown that at RH greater than 50% all cement hydrate particles are covered with a monolayer of water, in most of the contact points of the particles capillaries have formed, and around some particles a continuous water film has grown (funicular regime)33,34. Our nonreactive Grand Canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulations of a portlandite mesopore of 5 nm in size at room temperature (see Methods for more information on the pore model, the chosen GCMC conditions, and interaction potentials) indicate that a water monolayer film (one monolayer) only forms at RH >30%, and a one-nanometer-thick (around three monolayers) water film is captured at RH ~ 90% (see Fig. 2). Under the RH of 90%, we investigated interfacial CO2 mineralization on a flat and a defective (with an inverse kink, i.e., a cavity) basal surface, as mineral surfaces are usually rough and contain extended defects. Ion dissolution

In the complete carbonation pathway of calcium-bearing minerals, the dissolution of Ca2+ cations in the above-described aqueous film is a critical step for the formation of CaCO3. Here, we first gain atomistic insights on the dissolution process of Ca2+ at the defective and flat portlandite surface sites including acid-base effects using the WT-MTD to calculate free-energy profiles of Ca2+ ions migration from the solid phase to the aqueous film (chosen as the collective variable associating x, y and z moves and relaxing all other species than the chosen Ca). Figure 3a, b shows the free energy surface (FES) of Ca2+ dissolution at the kink and flat sites under a neutral condition, respectively. Note that two OH− neighbors were removed around the kink Ca2+. Four free energy minima are identified on the FESs of both cases along the minimum free energy path (MFEP, black dashed line), suggesting that the dissolution process of an attached ion has two metastable states prior to the dissolved state. The free energy differences between the transition and metastable states extracted from the FESs are summarized in Fig. 3c. Note that the iso-surfaces of the energy wells in Fig. 3a have larger areas than Fig. 3b, showing that the kink Ca2+ cation can explore a much wider phase space than at the intact one. This is mainly because the kink Ca2+ is less restrained than the intact. In the initial state (labeled A), the intact Ca2+ is asymmetrically bonded to six OH− neighbors (see the inset in Fig. 3b), which leads to a narrow dissolution path along the z-axis. To jump out of the initial state, the intact Ca2+ has to overcome the 0.296 eV energy barrier, reaching the metastable state B, wherein no Ca-OH bonds are broken, and the Ca2+ remains attached to the portlandite surface. Still, three H2O molecules attack the first hydration shell of intact Ca2+ at state B, lifting Ca2+ up to the surface for about 2 Å. The free energy difference between states B and A of intact Ca2+ is merely 0.065 eV. In contrast, the kink Ca2+ requires higher activation energy (ΔG = 0.490 eV) to reach the metastable state B, where Ca2+ is driven out of the kink by a distance around 3 Å with two Ca-OH bonds broken, hanging on the defective surface by two left Ca-OH bonds and four attacking H2O molecules. To jump further to state C, the intact and kink systems must cross an energy barrier of 0.409 eV and 0.185 eV, respectively. Although the dissolution distance of both systems in state C is around 4 Å, the Ca2+ complexes are significantly different. In the intact system, one Ca-OH is broken with four H2O molecules attacking the Ca2+, and it is still attached to the surface of the portlandite by two Ca-OH bonds (see Supplementary Fig. 6a). The metastable state C of the intact system is volatile since the backward energy barrier (from C to B) is only 0.023 eV. Regarding the state C of the kink Ca2+, the dissolved Ca2+ is weakly bonded to the kink by a remaining Ca-OH bond, forming a complex (CaOH)+ ⋅ (H2O)5 (see Fig. 3d). Subsequently, the kink Ca2+ can detach completely from the surface by climbing a small energy barrier (ΔG = 0.091 eV), forming a complex (CaOH)+ ⋅ (H2O)5 in the dissolved state D, while the intact Ca2+ must overcome a higher energy barrier (ΔG = 0.374 eV) to break the remaining two Ca-OH bonds, forming a complex Ca(OH)2 ⋅ (H2O)4 (see Supplementary Fig. 6a). Note that our simulations show that the OH− ion cannot dissolve in the water film before the dissolution of Ca2+. Overall the dissolution of intact Ca2+ requires higher activation energy than the kink Ca2+. This activation energy increases with the number of OH neighbors around the central Ca2+35.

Here, we observe significantly different detachment behaviors of Ca2+ between flat and defective systems. It is shown that the dissolution of ions in the kink of many defective minerals (e.g. barite36, alite37, belite38, and tricalcium aluminate39, etc.) goes through four similar states, which are named respectively initial state, bidentate, inner sphere state, and outer sphere state, as we observe in defective portlandite (see Fig. 3d). As shown in Fig. 3c, the intact Ca2+ on the flat portlandite surface must overcome a higher energy barrier than the kink Ca2+ for complete detachment, which means that a rough surface of minerals can dissolve more efficiently than a flat surface. This tendency is consistent with previous experimental observation40 of portlandite dissolution, which demonstrates that the dissolution on the basal surface occurs via the formation and propagation of hexagonal etch pits. The growth velocity of the kink faces is faster than that of the flat ones40. By comparing the free energy difference between the dissolved state D and the initial state A, it shows that the Ca2+ dissolution is a non-spontaneous endothermic process (ΔG = + 0.555 eV) on a flat surface, while it becomes a spontaneous process (ΔG = -0.078 eV) when it occurs at the kink site of a defective surface. For the flat system, the rate-limiting processes for Ca2+ dissolution are the transitions B → C and C → D, where Ca-OH bonds are broken. Whereas, for the kink Ca2+ of a defective surface, breaking the first two Ca-OH bonds at transition B → C is the rate-limiting process.

Regarding the effects of acid-base conditions on Ca2+ dissolution, we restricted ourselves to studying the defective system in four acid-base environments of the nanometric film, namely acidic (1 H+), neutral, basic (1 OH−) and highly basic (2 OH−). As presented in Supplementary Fig. 5, the FESs of the acidic, basic, and highly basic systems are similar to the neutral case in Fig. 3a, showing that the Ca2+ detachment paths under different acid-base conditions are analogous. However, the free energy profiles along the MFEP differ significantly (see Fig. 3c). A complete detachment of Ca2+ in the acidic film is a spontaneous process with ΔG = - 0.329 eV. The acidic system can easily reach the state C by passing small energy barriers. The rate-limiting process occurs at the transition C → D, and the dissolved Ca2+ cation energetically prefers to stay in the liquid film rather than to reversely attach on the portlandite, as the backward energy barrier is high (ΔGbackward = + 0.520 eV). In comparison, Ca2+ dissolution becomes a rate-limiting process in the basic system, which has the highest overall energy barrier among the four defective systems, indicating the lowest Ca2+ detachment rate according to the transition path theory. Interestingly, the highly basic system has the lowest overall energy barrier for Ca2+ dissolution. This can be due to the electrical double-layer effect when increasing the ionic strength (KOH)25. During the dissolution of Ca, an OH− ion is taken away from the portlandite surface, consequently leading to a negative vacancy. The heavy Ca2+ cation has a strong attractive force to the OH− ions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6c, the Ca2+ dissolution in this system is assisted by an OH− anion, which accelerates water dissociation around the dissolving Ca2+. After the Ca2+ detaches from the surface, two OH− ions are coordinated to the Ca2+. However, the basic conditions are still less energetically favorable for the Ca2+ dissolution of portlandite compared to the acid and neutral conditions. As presented in Fig. 3c, Ca2+ dissolution is a nonspontaneous process under both basic and highly basic conditions.

CO2 solvation

Note that the basicity of the water film increases after the Ca2+ dissolution, which can impact the subsequent carbonation reactions. Following the ion dissolution, we focus on the CO2 capture process from the vacuum to the water film. To track the solvation state of the CO2 molecule, we use the water coordination number of CO2 (CC,Ow) and the vertical distance (dzC,surface) to the surface as reaction coordinates. Figure 4a shows the FES of the CO2 capture process in the neutral water film (the FES of the acidic, basic, and highly basic systems can be found in Supplementary Fig. 7). Figure 4b summarizes the free energy profiles of the four different acid-base systems. We can identify five minimum energy states from the FES in which the CO2 molecule remains in vacuum (A), air-water interface (B), inner film (C and D), and portlandite surface (E), respectively (see the snapshots in Fig. 4c). Before adsorption on the portlandite surface, CO2 must pass through around three adsorbed water monolayers. The metastable states in B, C, and D correspond to the water monolayer 3rd, 2nd, and 1st water monolayer, respectively. Consequently, the CO2 molecule is solvated by zero, one, and two water molecules, respectively, in the three states. The free energy profiles show that state A has the lowest energy in all systems, indicating that the CO2 molecule energetically prefers to stay in the vacuum. This is understandable since the nonpolar CO2 molecule is highly hydrophobic. Starting from vacuum (A), the CO2 molecule is adsorbed on the surface of the water film (B) by overcoming an energy barrier ΔG of ~ +0.1 eV. The captured CO2 molecule is energetically favorable to stay at the vacuum-water interface, where the system has low free energy. To pass through the 3rd and 2nd water monolayers, it requires jumping over the energy barrier (0.03–0.1 eV). Note that the system has the highest energy barrier as the CO2 molecule passes through the 1st water monolayer to adsorb on the surface of the portlandite. As presented in the snapshot for solid surface adsorption (see Fig. 4c), oxygen from the CO2 molecule is headed to the portlandite surface, exhibiting a weakly chemisorbed state. Meanwhile, the CO2 molecule in this adsorbed state is solvated by three water molecules (see Fig. 4a). In general, the system must overcome the energy barrier of 0.25–0.4 eV to bring the CO2 molecule to the solid surface. A basic film benefits the diffusion of CO2 to the solid surface. The recovered high-energy barrier for the highly basic condition might be due to the increased ion concentration. The increased hydrated ions slightly slow down the water dynamics (see the mean square displacements in Supplementary Fig. 18e), which may limit the CO2 molecule passing through the water layer.

The microstates of subsequent H2CO3 deprotonation reaction are sampled on the water oxygen coordination number around the two hydrogen atoms of the H2CO3 molecule, i.e., CH1O and CH2O (see Fig. 5c and Supplementary Notes 1). The FESs of the acidic and basic systems are shown in Supplementary Fig. 8. Figure 5d summarizes the full CO2 hydration pathways of the four systems. The acid-base condition profoundly impacts the reaction paths. In neutral and acid systems, the reaction of CO2 hydration for the formation of H2CO3 occurs in concerted fashion, where CO2 reacts concertedly with two spying H2O molecules, ie CO2 + 2H2O ⇌ H2CO3 + H2O (see Fig. 5e, and Supplementary Fig. 10b). Consequently, the FES of the CO2 hydration reaction in the two systems only has two minima near (0,0) and (1,1), corresponding to the reactant and product, respectively (see Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 8a). In contrast, the CO2 hydration reaction in the basic and highly basic systems occurs in a two-step mechanism: CO2 + H2O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightleftharpoons {{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}⇌HCO3− + H3O+⇌ H2CO3 + H2O (see Fig. 5b, e, and Supplementary Fig. 10a). In this scheme, the reaction begins with the formation of hydronium ion (H3O+) and hydroxide (OH−) from the water autolysis; then, the OH− ion attacks the carbon atom, forming \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3−; finally, the H3O+ ion donates one proton to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3−, forming H2CO3. Therefore, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3− is an intermediate species in this reaction path. The forward energy barriers of the CO2 hydration reaction of the four systems are classified as acidic (2.115 eV) > basic (2.089 eV) > neutral (1.860 eV) > highly basic (1.519 eV). To show the effect of the water film, the minimum free energy profile of this CO2 hydration pathway in bulk water is also included in Fig. 5d. It shows that the interfacial CO2 hydration reaction requires a markedly higher activation energy than that in the bulk aqueous (1.144 eV)23,42. It is interesting to observe that the basic system has a higher energy barrier than the neutral system. A plausible reason can be attributed to the fact that the OH− ions are not actively diffusive in the nanometric water film, since they are strongly attracted by the dissolved heavy Ca2+ cation (see Supplementary Fig. 12). In addition, the defective portlandite surface is not locally charge neutral, making OH− ions easily trapped at the defective sites. Resultingly, the OH− ions are inactive in the water autolysis in the systems.

The subsequent H2CO3 deprotonation reaction to forming \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{CO}}}}_{3}}^{2-}$$\end{document}CO32− ion is a two-step reaction: H2CO3 + H2O \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightleftharpoons {{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}⇌HCO3− + H3O\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^{+}\rightleftharpoons {{{{\rm{CO}}}}_{3}}^{2-}$$\end{document}⇌+CO32− + 2H3O+ (see FESs in Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 8, and snapshots in Fig. 5f). Water molecules act as proton acceptors along the whole deprotonation pathway, irrespective of the acid-base environments. The free energy profiles in Fig. 5d show that H2CO3 is more energetically stable than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3− and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{CO}}}}_{3}}^{2-}$$\end{document}CO32−, particularly in the acidic system. Acidic, neutral, basic, and highly basic systems must overcome the energy barrier 1.534 eV, 1.364 eV, 1.094 eV, and 1.414 eV, respectively, to deprotonate the two protons from the H2CO3 molecule. Likewise, the H2CO3 deprotonation reactions in the adsorbed water films require higher activation energy than in the bulk aqueous (0.822 eV)23,42.

For the CO2 hydroxylation pathway, we take the OH− oxygen coordination number around the carbon atom (CC,Oh) and the distance between the two (dC,Oh) as the collective variables for the metadynamics simulations of hydroxylation reactions (see the FES in Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 9), and the water oxygen coordination number around the only proton of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3− (CH1O) for the following \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3− deprotonation reaction (see Fig. 6b). Figure 6c presents the minimum free energy profiles along this CO2 hydroxylation pathway under different water film conditions and in bulk solution23,42. The comparison of free energy profiles in Figs. 5d and 6c reveals that the CO2 hydroxylation reaction exhibits a lower energy barrier than the CO2 hydration reaction in water films, consistent with CO2 speciation behavior in bulk aqueous solution23,42. Note that he energy barriers obtained for the interfacial CO2 hydroxylation reaction in the three systems are all around 1.5 eV, which are significantly higher than in the bulk aqueous (0.556 eV). For dilute bulk aqueous, the microscopic origin of the CO2 hydroxylation energy barrier is primarily responsible for the desolvation of the OH− ion and the solvation of carbon-oxygen43. When the reaction occurs in an adsorbed water film, the interaction between Ca2+ and the OH− is significantly pronounced. As presented in the snapshot in Fig. 6d and e, the OH− ions are strongly coordinated to the dissolved Ca2+ cation. The CO2 molecule actually reacts with the (CaOH)+ ⋅ (H2O)5 or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{Ca}}}{({{\rm{OH}}})}_{2}\cdot {({{{\rm{H}}}}_{2}{{\rm{O}}})}_{4}$$\end{document}Ca(OH)2⋅(H2O)4 hydrates, depending on the basicity. As the CO2 molecule approaches the hydrate, the OH− ion in the hydrate rotates toward the carbon atom and attempts to detach from the coordination shell of the Ca2+ cation (see Fig. 6d, e). According to our calculation, breaking the Ca-OH bond requires a small activation energy of 0.014 eV (see Supplementary Fig. 14). Thus, the main contribution to the energy barrier of the hydroxylation reaction probably originates from the solvation of the oxygen (Oc) of CO2 and the desolvation of the oxygen (Oh) of OH− ion. As the CO2 molecule and the OH− ion are approaching each other, the Oc is getting more solvated (see Supplementary Fig. 13a), while the Oh is less solvated (see Supplementary Fig. 13b). Also, note that the hydroxylation reaction occurs near the portlandite surface (see Fig. 6d and e). The observations indicate that the OH− ion has less affinity for the surface of the air-water and the CO2 molecule must pass through the monolayers of water and solvate in the water film, which also contributes to the barrier with high hydroxylation reaction energy, as indicated in Fig. 4b. All of these distinctive interfacial features account for the markedly high CO2 hydroxylation reaction energy barrier for the adsorbed water film. The energy barriers of the subsequent \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3− deprotonation reaction in the neutral, basic, and highly basic systems are 0.976 eV, 0.829 eV, and 0.797 eV, respectively, which are higher than that in bulk solution (0.515 eV).

It should be noted that the CO2 molecule does not necessarily have to be brought to the portlandite surface for the hydration reaction, although the CO2 molecule is more prone to be solvated by H2O molecules near the solid surface than other sites (see Fig. 4a). Instead, the hydration reaction prefers to take place at the vacuum-water interface (see Fig. 5e). This reaction propensity has been extensively observed in many chemical reaction systems from both experiments and theoretical calculations, such as water microdroplets44 and organic aerosols45. The mechanism of this phenomenon can be derived from the configuration of water molecules in the nanometric film. As we show in Fig. 4b, the energy barriers for CO2 diffusion toward the portlandite surface increase when the CO2 molecule continuously punctures the water monolayers. This preference of the vacuum-water interface for the CO2 hydration reaction is probably due to the particular water orientation in the film. As presented in Supplementary Fig. 18c, the vertical dipole moment (Mz) oscillates at the two vacuum-water interfaces, showing that the water oxygens favor orienting toward the vacuum. The self-arrangements of water molecules trigger the formation of a slightly negatively charged surface water membrane near the vacuum (see Supplementary Fig. 18d), while the region near the water-portlandite interface is positively charged. Consequently, we observe an apparent local electric field gradient in the water film, which can enhance the autolysis of water molecules46.

Discussion

Based on the free energy calculation results, here we propose the thermodynamically favorable interfacial CO2 mineralization paths for portlandite (see Fig. 8d). The mineralization path in the initial stage includes six steps, namely water adsorption, Ca2+ dissolution, CO2 solvation, CO2 speciation, deprotonation, and CaCO3 ion pairing. A complete mineralization path would involve further nucleation and crystallization of CaCO3. One should note that the schematic representation shown in Fig. 8d is an idealized thermodynamically favorable reaction path for CO2 mineralization of portlandite under infinitely dilute conditions. In real concentrated systems, all these steps/reactions can occur simultaneously since mineralization kinetics is a collective behavior that is also significantly influenced by other quantities, such as CO2 partial pressure, local ion concentration, and reactive kink site density50,51. For example, by increasing CO2 concentration, the higher availability of CO2 molecules in confined films may collectively increase the collision events between reactant molecules (e.g., H2O and CO2)22, which therefore may enhance the collective carbon mineralization rate52. In the initial-stage interfacial CO2 mineralization path, the adsorbed water film acts as a carbonation nanoreactor, which forms on the surfaces of mesopores when the RH is higher than 30%. A one-nanometer water film is observed at RH ~90% from our simulations. Once the nanometric water film adsorbs on the portlandite surface, the surface Ca2+ at kink sites is prone to dissolve in the companion of OH− ions under the attack of water molecules, leading to an increase in the concentration of floating Ca2+ cations and the basicity of the water film. Our simulations show that an acidic environment is energetically favorable for this ion dissolution process. This is validated by our kinetic experiments on portlandite dissolution in solutions with different initial pH values (Supplementary Fig. 20). Compared to the Ca2+ dissolution rate at pH=7, the dissolution is an order of magnitude faster at pH=1 and an order of magnitude slower at pH=13. Moreover, by fitting the temperature-dependent rates to the Arrhenius equation, we experimentally determined the apparent activation energies (Ea) for portlandite dissolution under each pH condition. As shown in Fig. 8a, the fitted activation energies for portlandite dissolution at pH 1, 7, and 13 are 0.065 eV, 0.084 eV, and 0.124 eV, respectively. The experimentally observed trend in activation energy (Ea at pH 1 < Ea at pH 7 < Ea at pH 13) matches well the trend we found for the simulated energy barriers. We note that the absolute values of the experimental activation energies are much lower than the simulated energy barriers. This is expected due to the differences between the simulated and experimental conditions: our simulations investigate single kink site dissolution in a thin film with high Ca2+ ionic strength, while our experiments measure the dissolution of realistic portlandite samples with multiple surface defects in a dilute bulk solution. Nevertheless, the fundamental role of pH in regulating the kinetics of Ca2+ dissolution is robustly demonstrated by both our simulations and experiments. Meanwhile, the water film captures CO2 at the air-water interface, which facilitates CO2 speciation in a hydration or a hydroxylation fashion. In the acidic and neutral water film, the CO2 molecule can react with water molecules at the air-water interface, speciating into H2CO3. Regarding the basic film, the CO2 molecule energetically favors diving into the liquid to react with OH− ions that have low affinity to the air-water interface, producing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3−. H2CO3 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3− can be further deprotonated into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{CO}}}}_{3}}^{2-}$$\end{document}CO32− in the basic film. Note that a basic film is favorable for this CO2 speciation process, and the basicity of the water film drops in the formation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3− and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{CO}}}}_{3}}^{2-}$$\end{document}CO32−. Therefore, in the mineralization pathway shown in Fig. 8d, the ion dissolution can enhance the CO2 speciation, whereas limiting further dissolution due to the rise of basicity, and the CO2 speciation in turn boosts the ion dissolution by reducing the basicity. The ion pairing process precipitates the floating Ca2+ and carbonate species generated in the previous steps, which further contributes to the ion dissolution and CO2 speciation. Consequently, these successive reactions establish an energetically favorable cycle that facilitates continuous interfacial CO2 mineralization. This suggests that the extent of mineral carbonation in water-limited environments is primarily governed by water availability. A piece of supportive evidence is that the carbonation extent of portlandite at a low RH can be recovered by raising the RH32. Considering that ion dissolution and CO2 speciation processes compete under varying acid-base conditions, the neutral to basic film is anticipated to be the most favorable for interfacial CO2 mineralization. This deduction is supported by our carbonation experiments on portlandite conducted at various constant pH conditions (Supplementary Fig. 19). As illustrated in Fig. 8b, the extent of portlandite carbonation is maximized when exposed to a neutral mist. It is important to note that the energy barriers for portlandite dissolution are significantly lower than those for CO2 speciation. This suggests that CO2 speciation is the rate-limiting step of interfacial CO2 mineralization rather than the ion dissolution, with the reaction rate of the latter exceeding that of the former. This can explain why the aqueous on the portlandite surface remains basic during carbonation32.

Through the carbon mineralization process, the gaseous CO2 phase is stored as CaCO3 precipitations, which can exist in different polymorphic forms, including calcite, aragonite, and vaterite53. These CaCO3 polymorphs, strongly influenced by the pressure and temperature conditions54, exhibit distinct thermodynamic stabilities, crystallographic structures, and physical properties. Among them, calcite is thermodynamically the most stable phase at ambient temperature and pressure conditions53, making it the ideal target for long-term geological carbon storage to ensure permanent sequestration. Aragonite, while metastable, is denser and mechanically harder than calcite. In particular, its needle-shaped morphology enhances the mechanical properties of carbonated construction materials55. Moreover, aragonite can be the most stable form of CaCO3 in the presence of foreign ions like magnesium56. Vaterite is the least stable and least dense polymorph, often serving as a transient precursor. As CaCO3 is widely present in nature systems and living organisms, and has significant applications in environmental remediation and geological carbon storage, etc., unraveling its crystallization and transformation mechanisms continues to raise scientific interest. It is interesting to see that pH also significantly impacts the polymorphism of the formed CaCO3 (see Fig. 8b and c). Calcite constitutes the vast majority of CaCO3 products at various pH levels, especially under acidic conditions. As we increase the pH, the portion of amorphous calcium carbonate increases. A minor portion of aragonite is also observed in the experiments. The pH dependence of the carbonate polymorph can be ascribed to the various ion pairing pathways. The CaCO3 ion pairing reaction path is strongly dependent on the acid-base condition of the water film. CaCO3 can form from the direct binding of Ca2+ and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{CO}}}}_{3}}^{2-}$$\end{document}CO32− in the highly basic film, while it can also form in the neutral and moderate basic film where the binding of the two counterparts and the deprotonation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{HCO}}}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}HCO3− or H2CO3 occur at the same time. However, CaCO3 can always form irrespective of various acid-base environments. We have shown the diversity of the metastable states of Ca2+-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{CO}}}}_{3}^{2-}$$\end{document}CO32− precursor (see Fig. 7) and the pH dependence of the CaCO3 ion pairing pathways in our simulations, which is already a good starting point for future work to explain the formation of different CaCO3 polymorphisms. An interesting prospect is to examine how these Ca2+-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{CO}}}}_{3}^{2-}$$\end{document}CO32− precursors nucleate, reorganize, and evolve into different polymorphs.

Herein we focus our attention on the interfacial CO2 mineralization of portlandite only. This is mainly because portlandite is one of the most common sources for carbonation in cementitious materials and natural minerals. The experiment reveals that portlandite tends to be carbonated first in hydrated cement pastes30. Our findings in the interfacial CO2 mineralization regime can have broader implications for other CO2 active minerals in built environments and natural geologic systems, such as cementitious calcium silicate, wollastonite, olivine, and serpentine22. The carbon mineralization of these minerals often proceeds under water-limited conditions. The nano-meter-thick thin water films adsorbed on these minerals diverge drastically from bulk water in the water dynamics, water structure, and ion mobility, which play a unique role in mediating the carbon mineralization process22. Although the mineralization process may differ among different minerals depending on many other physicochemical conditions (pressure, temperature, CO2 concentration etc.), they share key mineralization steps like portlandite. We examined the thermodynamics of all critical mineralization steps from water adsorption to CaCO3 ion pairing, which provides a transferable framework for understanding the carbon mineralization of other CO2 active minerals. Future work can be directed to test the generality of our mechanistic insights using silicate surfaces.

Methods

Model construction

The primitive cell structure of portlandite is adopted by Henderson et al.57 with the dimension of a × b × c = 3.5925 Å × 3.5925 Å × 4.905 Å, α = β = 90°, γ = 120°. The primitive cell is replicated to generate a triclinic portlandite supercell of dimension 8a × 6b × 8c, which is then converted to an orthorhombic structure. The layered orthorhombic portlandite structure is cleaved in the middle of the basal (001) surface, creating a slit pore at 5 nm. To investigate the effect of surface roughness on ion dissolution, we prepared two models with defective and flat surfaces, respectively (see Fig. 3a, b). To generate the defective model, we manually removed six Ca(OH)2 units from each surface of the flat model. The number of water molecules in the thin water films associated with RH = 90% is determined according to the GCMC simulation. To investigate the impact of the acid-base environment, we prepared models representing acidic, basic, and highly basic films in addition to the neutral defective model. This involved introducing an H+ ion for acidity, one KOH for basicity, and two KOHs for high basicity. For the acidic model, H+ is taken from the lower layer of the portlandite body to maintain charge neutrality.

Grand canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulation

To simulate the adsorption of water as a function of RH, we perform a GCMC simulation. All interactions are described using the Clay-FF force field, which can accurately describe electrostatic interactions. Regarding the water, we use the SPC/E model, since it can reproduce the mechanics of liquid water better than other water models. GCMC simulations are performed in a μVT ensemble where the system has a constant volume and temperature that is equilibrated with the reservoir of particles at a given chemical potential μ. In the Monte Carlo scheme, one samples the microstates of the system according to the probability distribution. The new microstate is based on the previous microstate through the trials of translation, rotation, insertion, and deletion of existing particles. In practice, we perform a hybrid GCMC/MD scheme in which 100 attempts of insertion/deletion are performed every 50 MD steps. The translation and rotation moves are kept at zero since the displacements of the particles are performed by the MD. For the MD part, we use the Nose-Hoover thermostat to control the temperature at 300 K with a damping factor of 100 fs, and the timestep is set at 1 fs. The rigid SPC/E water model is constrained by the SHAKE algorithm. Herein, we perform a sequence of GCMC/MD simulations to obtain an adsorption isotherm in which the RH ranges from 0 % to 100 %. In practice, we impose fugacities corresponding to the relative humidities instead of the chemical potentials. The relationships of fugacities and relative humidity are determined by Brochard et al.58. Over two million GCMC steps are performed to equilibrate the systems. The simulations are performed using the LAMMPS package (lammps-28Mar2023 version)59.

Portlandite dissolution experiments

To investigate the effects of pH on portlandite dissolution, 1.5 g of analytical grade spheroidal calcium hydroxide particles (2.5-3.0 mm diameter) were dissolved in 300 g of water with initial pH values of 1, 7, and 13, respectively. The initial pH was adjusted with hydrochloric acid (1.0 mol/L) or sodium hydroxide (10.0 mol/L) solutions. The dissolution experiments were conducted in a 500 mL beaker using a magnetic stirring apparatus at constant temperatures of 10 °C, 25 °C, 40 °C, and 55 °C. During the dissolution process, 0.5 mL aliquots were extracted from the solution at intervals of 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5, 10, 15, and 20 min, which were immediately filtered using a 0.22 μm microporous membrane filter. Calcium ion concentrations in the filtrates were quantified using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES) with an Agilent 5110 instrument.

Portlandite carbonation experiments

To evaluate the impact of pH on portlandite carbonation kinetics, we adopted our recently developed ’semi-wet carbonation’ technique, which allows precise control over carbonation conditions, including water content, pH, additives, and temperature. Detailed information about the experimental apparatus can be found in Supplementary Fig. 19 and our previous paper20. In this study, 1 g of analytical grade Ca(OH)2 powder was placed into the carbonation reactor. Solutions of varying pH levels (1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12, and 13) were aerosolized and introduced into the reactor at a flow rate of 0.1 mL/min, alongside a pure CO2 supply at 500 mL/min. After 30 min of carbonation at room temperature and pressure, the samples were promptly freeze-dried for 48 hours to quench the reaction and preserve the phase composition, preventing the transformation of metastable phases like amorphous calcium carbonate (ACC). The phase composition of the carbonated samples was identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Rigaku SmartLab 9 kW diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), operating at 45 kV and 200 mA. The scanning range (2θ) ranged from 5° to 60° with a step size of 0.02° for 2θ, and a time per step of 0.6 seconds, corresponding to a scanning rate of 2°/min in step-scan mode. Phase quantification was performed using the Rietveld method with HighScore software, ensuring a weighted residual (Rwp) of no more than 10%. To quantify both crystalline and amorphous phases, a known quantity (10 wt. %) of an internal standard (ZnO) was added to each sample. Since ACC is the only amorphous phase in the carbonated samples, the crystalline phases (portlandite, calcite, aragonite) were quantified via Rietveld refinement, and the amount of ACC was determined by difference, accounting for the known amount of the standard. The surface morphology of the carbonated samples was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a Tescan VEGA3 instrument equipped with an X-Max N80 precision detector. The electron beam voltage was set at 20 kV with a working distance of 10 mm.
