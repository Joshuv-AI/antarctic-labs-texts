Numerous overlapping programs add complexity, unpredictability to rural hospital finances
Authors: Carolyn San Soucie, Michael E Chernew, Benjamin D Sommers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRural hospitals face unique financial challenges, prompting congressional action including multiple Medicare-specific payment programs. Medicare's programs to enhance rural hospital payment include critical access, sole community, Medicare-dependent, and low-volume hospitals.MethodsWe examined the payments made through these programs and their resulting impact on hospitals' finances for 2942 rural hospitals from 1997 to 2024 using cost report data.ResultsAlmost four-fifths (82.4%) of rural hospitals received payment from one of the programs. From 2015 to 2024, 14.7% of participating hospitals churned between programs. In 34.6% of hospital-years, hospitals received no special payments from programs they were eligible for. Furthermore, payments received by sole community hospitals and Medicare-dependent hospitals fluctuated by more than 50% year-to-year for 16.0% of hospital-years. Financial reliance on these programs was larger in states that have not expanded Medicaid and for hospitals participating in 2 programs simultaneously.ConclusionOverall, many rural hospitals rely on Medicare's special payment programs, but funding unpredictability, complicated and overlapping eligibility rules, and payment formulas based on historic costs create challenges for hospitals' finances. There is a need to streamline Medicare payment and make existing programs more efficient before layering on dozens of differing state-level policies through the recently created Rural Health Transformation Program.

Introduction

Rural hospitals face unique financial challenges that put them at high risk of financial instability and possible closure.1-3 Rural hospitals tend to have lower patient volumes than urban hospitals, as they serve small populations that increasingly travel to urban facilities for care.4,5 This leads to some rural hospitals having higher costs per patient, on average, than facilities with higher volumes.6 A greater share of rural hospital patients are covered by Medicare compared with urban hospitals, and Medicare typically reimburses less for services than commercial insurers.7 Last, rural hospitals tend to provide more uncompensated care than their urban counterparts.8 The confluence of these factors leads many rural hospitals to financial distress.2

In all, 195 rural hospitals have ceased providing inpatient services since 2005, accounting for 6782 fewer beds across the country—approximately 9% of all hospitals and 5% of all beds.9,10 These closures have occurred even as the absolute number of rural residents in the United States has risen by 3% over the same period.11 For rural hospitals that remain open, many have merged with other hospitals or been acquired by health systems, both of which can lead to increased prices and termination of service lines.2,12,13 The federal government has several policies in place to aid rural hospitals directly and ensure that rural communities have access to care; and still, rural health care delivery and access continue to be a perennial issue for policymaking.

The largest source of financial support for rural hospitals comes from the Medicare program.7 Because of perceived inadequacies in Medicare’ Inpatient Prospective Payment System (IPPS) for rural providers, several programs have been created to augment the IPPS or replace it entirely.14 These payments come from the Critical Access Hospital (CAH) program, the Sole Community Hospital (SCH) program, the Medicare-Dependent Hospital (MDH) program, and the Low-Volume Hospital (LVH) program. Importantly, these programs are not the only sources of added federal support for rural hospitals. The Rural Referral Center program, Rural Emergency Hospital program, and the 340B program all provide support as well. See Appendix A1 for more information on these additional programs (to access the Appendix, click on the Details tab of the article online).

In this study, we focus on the CAH, SCH, MDH, and LVH programs for several reasons. First, they provide the largest amounts of financial support to rural hospitals through replacement of or additions to the IPPS. For example, critical access hospitals receive cost-based payments entirely separate from the IPPS (Table 1).15,16 The SCH and MDH programs provide hospitals with supplemental IPPS payments if their current costs through the IPPS are lower than their historic costs from a set list of years (Table 1).17-20 The LVH program provides an additional percentage payment on top of IPPS payments that is dependent on all-payer volume (Table 1).21,22

Second, these programs are all designed with similar eligibility criteria (eg, low volume, isolation, and/or their importance in serving Medicare beneficiaries). Thus, they would be natural programs to harmonize. Specifically, these programs have overlapping eligibility criteria, and hospitals can receive payment from 2 of these programs simultaneously (Table 1).

US Congress created the SCH program in 1983, and hospitals are eligible for the designation through 1 of 4 pathways that define their isolation and role as a primary source of inpatient care for beneficiaries in the community.17,18 The MDH program was created in 1989 to help hospitals that demonstrate that (1) at least 60% of its inpatient days or discharges are attributable to Medicare beneficiaries, (2) it has 100 or fewer beds, and (3) it is rural.19,20 The CAH program was created in 1997 to provide cost-based payment to hospitals with 25 or fewer inpatient beds that are located more than 35 miles from another hospital.15,16 The LVH program was initially introduced in Congress in 2003 and temporarily expanded so that hospitals with less than 3800 all-payer inpatient stays that are more than 15 miles from another IPPS hospital receive payment.21-23 Due to overlapping eligibility, many hospitals receive both the low-volume adjustment as well as payments through either the SCH or MDH program.24

Last, these programs, their eligibility pathways, and their payments have not been robustly studied in combination. Since the LVH program was expanded in 2011, about half of all rural hospitals paid under the IPPS (ie, excluding critical access hospitals) receive the adjustment.25 However, there is not much research studying how the LVH program interacts with the SCH and MDH programs. There is also not extensive information comparing the CAH program with the 3 IPPS programs. It is necessary to understand the current avenues by which the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) provides financial subsidies to these hospitals first in order to reform rural support in the future. Research on the performance of these programs may offer insights that can assist in ensuring that payments to hospitals are well-targeted, empirically justified, and based on financial need.

While not expressly focused on hospitals, the Rural Health Transformation program will provide $50 billion to states across a 5-year period to support rural health care.26 This money was allocated alongside recent legislation that is forecasted to drastically reduce the number of individuals insured by Medicaid.27,28 The funds are set to support rural communities and build on existing state and federal programs. In this context, a better understanding of existing rural programs can help promote well-targeted and streamlined support of rural hospitals at the federal level before state-based initiatives are layered on top.

This study adds to the literature by examining payments made through the CAH, SCH, MDH, and LVH programs and their resulting association with hospitals' finances through 3 main aims: (1) calculating the frequency of hospitals churning through different programs and understanding how often this occurs for individual hospitals, (2) analyzing the fluctuations and stability of payments year-to-year for hospitals, and (3) estimating the share of operating revenues rural hospitals draw from each program annually.

Data and methods

Data and measurement

This study used 30 years of data from CMS' Healthcare Cost Report Information System from 1996 to 2025 and CMS' Provider of Services File (POS). All Medicare-certified hospitals are required to file cost reports, which collect various financial information such as charges, revenues, and costs in addition to information on the provision of care. Hospitals can file multiple cost reports in the same year covering different periods, so cost report information was aggregated to the calendar-year level (see Appendix A2 for more information).29 Some hospitals have incomplete calendar-year coverage in the starting and terminal years of data collection29; as a result, our study period focuses on the years from 1997 to 2024.

Hospitals were included for each year they had cost report information, and we required their CMS Certification Number (CCN) to match to a record in the POS. Consistent with routine practice using Medicare hospital cost report data, we winsorized financial variables to address extreme outliers.30 Values were winsorized to the first and ninety-ninth percentiles.

Hospitals were determined to be rural in a given year if they were located in a nonmetropolitan county (a county lacking an urban area of at least 50 000 people), were located in a zip code with a rural-urban commuting area code of 4 or higher, or had CAH status.2 Because the rurality of areas can change over time, hospitals were included in the sample if their location was rural for at least 1 year of the study period. A hospital may have various CCNs across its lifespan due to ownership changes, consolidated reporting, or conversion to a CAH. We matched multiple CCNs to common hospitals by using geographical address information in the POS. Hospitals were identified by their geographical addresses across CCNs.

Hospitals were determined to be eligible for the SCH, MDH, and LVH programs in a given year by information they recorded in their cost reports (Appendix A3). Critical access hospitals were determined to be eligible for the program based on their CCN. We found our estimates to be more consistent with other government reports on program participation than determining participation through other sources of CMS data. The Institutional Review Board at Harvard University determined that this study was not human subjects research and required no further review.

Outcome measures

For each hospital-year observation, we assessed payments made to hospitals through each of the programs, total inpatient revenue, and expense information (Appendix A3). Our primary eligibility outcome was the frequency at which hospitals were eligible for one program, then another year-to-year. We refer to this change in eligibility as “churning” between programs throughout the article but understand that this is not always an active choice made by hospitals. Changes in eligibility and churning could reflect annual volume changes, closures of a nearby hospital, or changes in payer mix from one year to the next.

Our primary financial outcomes included the following: (1) a hospital's special payment, which is the amount of money they received for participating in the program; (2) a hospital's subsidy ratio, which is their special payment divided by their total inpatient hospital revenue; and (3) their total margin calculated as net income from both patient and non-patient care activities divided by total revenue.2,31,32

Critical access hospitals' special payment includes the entirety of the cost-based payment that these hospitals receive in lieu of IPPS payments. For sole community hospitals, Medicare-dependent hospitals, and low-volume hospitals, special payments include the marginal add-on payment received in addition to their routine IPPS payments. Critical access hospitals were excluded from analyses involving subsidy ratios, because their subsidies are not made as additional payments on top of regular IPPS payments; CAH subsidies replace the IPPS entirely. Similarly, critical access hospitals were excluded from the analyses involving counterfactuals (what they would have received if not participating in the program) because we do not have information on what their IPPS payments would have been if they did not receive CAH payments.

Analysis

We have access to nearly 3 decades of data, but the avenues for rural hospital support have changed greatly over this time period. We therefore present our results over 3 time frames. First, we present some results for the full 28-year study period, because we wanted to understand hospitals' eligibility for the programs and how the financial support affected their profitability over as long a time period as we had data for. Next, we present other results for the most recent 10 years of data (2015 to 2024), as this reflects a time period of more stability for the programs and after the initial implementation of the Affordable Care Act (ACA) Medicaid expansion in approximately half of states. Last, we present the most recent year of data available, 2024, for certain analyses to provide a current snapshot relevant for policymaking.

To assess program participation over the 28-year study period, we first plotted the frequency of hospitals receiving payments from these programs by year. Next, we calculated hospital characteristics by program at the hospital-year level. To understand the stability of participation in each program, we assessed when hospitals were paid through different programs across 2 periods: (1) from 1997 to 2010, which was before the LVH program was expanded, and (2) from 2015 to 2024, which was after the LVH program was expanded. This separation accounted for the large number of hospitals that became eligible for the LVH program when it first expanded in 2010. To account for heterogeneity in the frequency that hospitals churn through different programs, we examined the number of programs a hospital was eligible for and denoted which programs those were.

Finally, to examine how reliant rural hospitals are on these programs for financial stability, we calculated the ratio of a hospitals' total inpatient hospital revenue that comes from special payment programs. We plotted these as a function of Medicare share stratifying by whether states expanded Medicaid or not, due to known benefits of Medicaid expansion on rural hospital finances.27,33 We also modeled hospitals' total margins across the study period calculated as net income from both patient and non-patient care activities excluding special payments divided by total revenue and plotted these against the actual hospital margins. Our approach makes the simplifying assumption that hospitals would not alter their behavior in response to the loss of supplemental payments, in order to provide an approximation of the financial impact of these bonus payments.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed 2 sensitivity analyses: (1) we assessed hospitals' subsidy ratios without any winsorization of cost report financial variables and (2) we conducted our main analyses using hospitals that were rural for at least half of the years they were active during the study period.

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations. The cost report information is entirely based on hospitals' administrative records, which might have reporting inaccuracies. In our analysis of hospitals' margins without special payments, we made the simplifying assumption that cost and utilization would have stayed the same had program rules varied, which is unlikely the case in practice. Additionally, this study is descriptive in nature; we did not examine the impacts of these special payments on rural health care delivery, access to care, or patient outcomes. These are beyond the scope of our analysis, but they represent an area for future work.

Results

In total, 2942 hospitals were located in a rural area and therefore eligible for inclusion in our study. Over four-fifths (82.4%) of rural hospitals were eligible for 1 of the 4 special payment programs for at least 1 year during the 28-year study period (Figure 1). The CAH program has been the largest special payment program for over 2 decades in terms of number of hospitals (Figure 2). Since the LVH program was expanded in 2010, it has had the second most hospitals eligible for payment each year (Figure 2). Most low-volume hospitals also receive payment from the SCH or MDH program as well (Figure 2).

Some hospital characteristics differ notably by special payment program, while others are similar across hospitals (Appendix A4). Sole community hospitals are more likely to be located in the West, critical access hospitals in the Midwest, and low-volume hospitals in the South as compared with other hospitals. Critical access hospitals are more likely to be government-run, and sole community hospitals are more likely to be nonprofit hospitals, while low-volume hospitals are more likely to be for-profit. Only a minority (9.9%) of hospitals participating in any program are affiliated with a medical school. Sole community hospitals are the largest hospitals, both in terms of beds and inpatient discharges, and they also tend to have the highest margins on average.

The CMS administered $113.1 in special payments (inflation adjusted to 2024 dollars) over the 28-year period, broken down by $73.9 billion through the CAH program, $24.5 billion through the SCH program, $4.0 billion through the MDH program, $23.2 billion through the LVH program, $6.1 billion to hospitals in both the SCH and LVH programs, and $1.3 billion to hospitals in both the MDH and LVH programs (Appendix A4). In 2024, special payments totaled almost $3.7 billion, approximately 0.3% of total Medicare spending (data not shown).

Hospitals eligible for the SCH and MDH programs frequently (34.6% of participating years) did not receive any payment from these programs (Appendix A4). This is because the hospitals' historic costs were not higher than current payments through the IPPS. In all, 55.8% of hospitals that were ever a sole community hospital or Medicare-dependent hospital received zero special payments in at least 1 year even after being eligible for one of the programs (Appendix A5). Many of these hospitals started receiving consistent subsidies through the LVH program when they began participating concurrently with the SCH or MDH program (data not shown).

Before 2011 (the first full year of the expanded LVH program), 56.6% of hospitals churned between payment programs (Appendix A6). During that period, 13.2% hospitals churned 2 or more times (Appendix A7). The programs were more stable after the expansion of the LVH program, yet 14.7% of hospitals still churned into or out of payment programs between 2015 and 2024 (Appendix A8). Additionally, 4.5% of hospitals churned 2 or more times between program after 2011 (Appendix A7).

On top of the instability related to participation in the programs, hospitals reported unstable program payments across years. Critical access hospitals and low-volume hospitals were relatively protected from this phenomenon (Appendix A9); sole community hospitals and Medicare-dependent hospitals had their payment amount decrease by half or more from year-to-year while still participating in the same programs in 9.6% of hospital-years (Appendix A9). A slightly smaller share (5.4%) of sole community hospital and Medicare-dependent hospital hospital-years experienced a doubling of their subsidy amounts from year-to-year while still participating in the same program (Appendix A9).

The ratio of special payments to total inpatient hospital revenue varied by program, but it was consistently higher for hospitals in the LVH program plus either the SCH or MDH programs concurrently (Figure 3). This is because LVH payments are made on top of either SCH or MDH payments if hospitals are eligible for 2 programs simultaneously. Reliance on these programs as a share of revenue was larger in states that had not expanded Medicaid (Figure 3). In 2024, the mean subsidy ratio across all hospitals in states that expanded Medicaid was 10.1% and 14.9% in states that did not expand Medicaid (Appendix A10).

Mean subsidy ratio vs Medicare fee-for-service share in expansion and non-expansion states, 2015–2024. Source: Authors' analysis of data from Medicare cost reports. Abbreviations: FFS, fee-for-service; LVH, low-volume hospitals; MDH, Medicare-dependent hospitals; SCH, sole community hospitals.

Across all programs, special payments contributed to higher margins for hospitals, often ensuring that hospitals had positive margins (Appendix A11). The subsidies from these programs were the difference between positive and negative margins for hospitals in 7.9% of hospital-years (Appendix A12). This percentage varied by program.

Our 2 sensitivity analyses elucidated how results differ with varying definitions of our outcomes and sample. When forgoing winsorization, mean subsidy ratios across expansion and non-expansion states looked more similar than with winsorization (Appendix A13). Under 10% of hospitals were not rural in at least half of their active years (data not shown). We excluded these hospitals from the second sensitivity analysis, but our results did not differ that significantly (Appendixes A14–A16).

Discussion

In this national study of rural hospitals over a 28-year period, we had 3 main findings. First, over the most recent decade of data, approximately 15% of hospitals churned between programs with 4.5% of hospitals churning 2 or more times. Although prior research has documented conversion to critical access status, this study, to our knowledge, is the first to highlight churning between IPPS payment programs as well.34,35 Churning adds administrative burdens to both hospitals and CMS. Second, payment volatility was common, with sole community hospitals and Medicare-dependent hospitals receiving zero special payments from eligible programs in over a third of hospital-years and experiencing special payments that either shrunk by at least half or doubled in approximately 16% of years. Our findings complement prior research by Rosko and colleagues,36 highlighting that these programs may not be effective in improving rural hospital operating margins over time and provide explanations as to why. Our analysis builds on prior work by demonstrating the unpredictability year-to-year of these special payment programs, which may partially explain these programs' relative limited effectiveness at improving rural hospital margins over time. Previous work did not track individual hospitals' receipt of subsidies. Given the presumed goal of these government payments to provide stability to rural hospitals, further exploration is needed of why payments can be unreliable year-to-year, and whether revisions such as rolling 3-year averages to eligibility criteria could promote more predictable revenue streams. Third, reliance on these programs as a share of total inpatient revenue was larger in states that have not expanded Medicaid and for hospitals participating in 2 programs simultaneously. Many studies have shown the importance of Medicaid expansion for rural hospital finances.4,33 However, to our knowledge, no research to date has shown the differences by special payment program nor the interaction of participation in 2 programs simultaneously. This complements findings that show that recent hospital closures have clustered in non-expansion states.33,36

Promoting rural health care delivery and access to care continue to be priorities for the federal and many state governments. While these special payment programs support rural hospitals, the numerous and overlapping programs add complexity and unpredictability to rural hospitals' finances. Policymakers should work to streamline rural hospital payments without eligibility cliffs or large shifts in payments from year-to-year. Additionally, there is a tension that the Medicare program must balance between paying for rural care and paying for rural and isolated care. The 4 programs have a combination of volume-based and geography-based criteria that can fracture targeted support. Last, the MDH and LVH programs have to be renewed by US Congress; they are not permanent like the CAH and SCH programs, which can lead to uncertainty for rural providers. Efforts to standardize and simplify payment rules could extend to other rural support programs as well, including the 340B programs.

There have been many changes to federal policy that have impacted rural hospitals' finances over the years. Trends in insurance coverage and the shift away from inpatient care have impacted CMS' ability to support hospitals through the IPPS programs. The increase in Medicare Advantage (MA) enrollment over the past 2 decades and decrease in inpatient volume at rural hospitals have likely decreased these special payments overall.37-39 The role of private plans in Medicare was much smaller when the SCH, MDH, and CAH programs were created, and the growth of the MA program has likely affected financial support to rural hospitals (Appendix A17). As policies enacted under H.R. 1 are expected to decrease Medicaid enrollment by 10.3 million, hospitals will become even more reliant on these special payments to maintain financial stability.40

Conclusion

Rural hospitals’ finances are buoyed by payments through multiple Medicare programs, but the programs are unpredictable and overlapping, adding complexity and risk to their performance. To maintain financial stability for rural hospitals, policymakers should look to simplify and streamline support to providers separate from these payment programs.
