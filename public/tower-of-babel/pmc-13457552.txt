Desflurane versus sevoflurane and postoperative inflammatory biomarkers in older adults undergoing minor- to moderate-risk noncardiac surgery
Authors: Nikolas Adamowitsch, Christian Reiterer, Edith Fleischmann, Katharina Horvath, Nicole Hantakova, Laura Höfer, Magdalena List, Barbara Rossi, Florian W. Zenz, Giulia Zanvettor, Oliver Zotti, Melanie Fraunschiel, Alexandra Graf, Barbara Kabon, Alexander Taschner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Volatile anesthetics modulate inflammatory responses, but it remains unclear whether desflurane and sevoflurane differ in their effects on postoperative inflammation. We evaluated in this secondary analysis of a prospective randomized trial, the effect of desflurane versus sevoflurane anesthesia on postoperative inflammatory biomarkers in older adults undergoing minor- to moderate-risk noncardiac surgery. We included all 190 patients who were enrolled in the main trial in this analysis. We administered desflurane or sevoflurane, aiming for a BIS value of 50 ± 5. C-reactive protein, Interleukin-6, and Procalcitonin concentrations were measured preoperatively, within one hour after surgery, and on the second postoperative day. We observed no significant difference in maximum postoperative Interleukin-6 (8.11 pg ml− 1 [IQR 4.77; 15.7] versus 7.84 pg ml− 1 [IQR 4.67; 12.62]) (p = 0.357), C-Reactive Protein (0.32 mg dl− 1 [IQR 0.13; 0.9] versus 0.26 mg dl− 1 [IQR 0.13; 0.83] (p = 0.433), and Procalcitonin (0.04 ng ml− 1 [IQR 0.03; 0.07] versus 0.04 ng ml− 1 [IQR 0.03; 0.06]) (p = 0.709) between desflurane and sevoflurane anesthesia. We did not find a significant difference in the concentration of postoperative inflammatory biomarkers. While prior studies indicated different effects on inflammation between different types of volatile anesthetics, we cannot observe this in our study. Our findings indicate that in this specific clinical setting, the choice between sevoflurane or desflurane for minor- to moderate-risk noncardiac surgery does not significantly affect the minor postoperative inflammatory response in older patients when volatile anesthetics at low MAC concentrations are administered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55579-6.

Introduction

Surgery is associated with increased postoperative inflammation, specifically in older adults1,2. Several studies showed that inflammatory biomarkers, including interleukins, leukocytes, or C-reactive protein, are significantly increased after noncardiac surgery, whereas surgical technique, hemodynamic perturbations, tissue damage, and the type of anesthesia are known trigger factors2,3. Exacerbated inflammatory reaction after surgery has been shown to be a modulator for the development of postoperative complications, including impaired wound healing, delirium, cognitive dysfunction, and acute kidney injury4–6.

Desflurane and sevoflurane have been shown to be modulators of the inflammatory system7–11. The use of volatile anesthetics during general anesthesia is associated with immunosuppression, impaired monocyte and neutrophil function, leucocyte depression, and an increase of pro-inflammatory cytokines7,12. For example, postoperative pro-inflammatory cytokines, including Interleukin-6, Interleukin-1beta (IL-1β), and Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) were higher after volatile anesthesia as compared to propofol anesthesia8,13. A study in healthy volunteers showed that inflammatory biomarkers increased significantly even after sedation with sevoflurane14. Furthermore, studies indicated that different types of volatile anesthetics exert different effects on the inflammation9,13. For example, a randomized study in patients undergoing tympanoplasty surgery showed that TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly higher after desflurane anesthesia as compared to sevoflurane anesthesia9. Nevertheless, except for the aforementioned study data regarding desflurane versus sevoflurane and the effect on postoperative inflammation, specifically in older adults, are lacking.

To gain further evidence regarding the effects of volatile anesthetics on inflammation, we performed this pre-planned secondary analysis of a prospective randomized clinical trial, in which we compared the effect of desflurane-based anesthesia versus sevoflurane-based anesthesia on postoperative neurocognitive recovery in older adults undergoing minor- to moderate-risk surgery15. In this secondary analysis, we evaluated the effect of desflurane versus sevoflurane anesthesia on postoperative maximum concentrations of C-reactive protein, IL-6, and Procalcitonin within the first two postoperative days.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is a pre-planned secondary analysis of a prospective randomized, observer-blinded, single-center clinical trial that investigated the effect of desflurane versus sevoflurane anesthesia on the speed of postoperative recovery in older adults undergoing minor- to moderate-risk surgery15. The study was conducted at the Medical University of Vienna after approval by the local Institutional Review Board (Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna, Registration number: 1111/2022) and registration at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05331027, Date of registration 15th April 2022) and at the European Clinical Trial Database (EudraCT 2022-000556-11, Date of registration 9th February 2022). The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice, and the authors have followed the applicable CONSORT guidelines. The included biomarkers presented in this manuscript were pre-specified and approved by the local Institutional Review Board within the original protocol. All biomarkers are included in the clinical trials registration as tertiary outcomes.

We obtained written informed consent from all patients on the day before surgery. Patients meeting the following criteria were eligible for inclusion: (1) ≥ 65 years of age; (2) scheduled for elective minor- to moderate-risk noncardiac surgery with expected duration of surgery ≤ 2 h. Patients meeting one of the following criteria were excluded: (a) Emergency surgery; (b) Bariatric surgery; (c) Documented dementia or neurologic disorder; (d) Language, vision, or hearing impairments that may compromise cognitive assessments; (e) Malignant hyperthermia; (f) Structural muscle disease.

Randomization and masking

We randomized patients shortly before induction of anesthesia at a 1:1 ratio using a web-based randomization program (Randomizer, Medical University of Graz, Graz, Austria; https://www.meduniwien.ac.at/randomizer/web) using permutated blocks into a desflurane group and a sevoflurane group, respectively. Patients randomly assigned to the desflurane group received desflurane after induction of anesthesia with an intraoperative goal of bispectral index (BIS) 50 ± 5. Patients randomly assigned to the sevoflurane group received sevoflurane after induction of anesthesia with an intraoperative goal of bispectral index (BIS) 50 ± 5.

The attending anesthesiologist was not blinded to the allocated group. Study personnel responsible for randomization were not involved in outcome assessment. Neither patients nor blinded study personnel were informed about the randomized allocation. Postoperative visits of study participants, as well as phone follow-ups, were only performed by blinded study personnel. They did not have access to electronic anesthesia documentation.

Anesthesia protocol

The detailed anesthesia protocol was published previously15. In summary, we measured BIS before induction of anesthesia and during surgery. For induction of anesthesia, we used a standardized regimen including a bolus of 1 µg.kg− 1.body weight− 1 (BW) remifentanil, 1 mg.kg− 1.BW− 1 propofol, and 0.6 mg.kg− 1.BW− 1 rocuronium. We evaluated BIS values one minute after induction of anesthesia. Intubation was performed if BIS values were lower than 60. In the case of BIS values higher than 60, a second bolus of 0.5 mg.kg− 1.BW− 1 propofol was administered before intubation was performed. All patients received 4 mg of dexamethasone for the prophylaxis of postoperative nausea and vomiting.

Desflurane and sevoflurane concentrations for maintenance of anesthesia were titrated using processed electroencephalography to achieve a BIS (Bispectral Index™, Medtronic©, Minneapolis, United States of America) value of 50 ± 5. Remifentanil with a rate of 0.1 µg.kg− 1.min− 1 was used for maintaining intraoperative analgesia. If the heart rate increased by 20% and no changes in blood pressure or BIS values were observed, the rate of remifentanil infusion was increased by 0.05 µg.kg− 1.min− 1. Intraoperative administration of atropine, scopolamine, and clonidine was avoided. At the end of surgery, all patients received 1000 mg paracetamol, and the administration of the respective volatile anesthetic and remifentanil infusion was stopped. All patients received 200 mg sugammadex for complete reversal of muscle relaxation.

At the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), 0.05 mg.kg− 1.BW− 1 piritramide administration was repeated if the visual analog scale was ≥ 4. VAS scores were assessed every 10 min.

Measurements

We recorded baseline demographic data, including age, sex, weight, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, as well as comorbidities, long-term medication, and type of surgery. We further recorded routine intraoperative data, including duration of anesthesia and surgery, amount of fluid administration, medication, vasopressors, blood pressure at least every three minutes, heart rate, and continuous BIS values. We continuously recorded volatile anesthetic concentrations, converted these to age adjusted minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) equivalents, and expressed them as an age-adjusted fraction. Blinded research personnel drew all study-specific blood samples. C-reactive protein (CRP), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and Procalcitonin (PCT) were measured shortly before induction of anesthesia, within one hour after surgery, and on the second postoperative day in all patients if patients were still in hospital. All laboratory measurements were performed by the Department of Laboratory Medicine at the Medical University of Vienna, Austria.

Data management

Blinded research staff obtained all data. Electronic data were recorded in the data management system “Clincase”, version 2.7.0.12 hosted by IT Systems and Communications, Medical University of Vienna, Vienna, Austria.

Statistical analysis

Patients were analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis according to their randomized group. Continuous variables were summarized using mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range separately for both groups. Categorical variables were summarized using absolute numbers and percentages for both groups.

Maximum concentrations of CRP, IL-6, and PCT were first compared between groups using Wilcoxon-tests. Furthermore, concentrations of inflammatory biomarkers were compared between the groups for each time point separately using Wilcoxon-tests. We further performed univariable median regression models for the randomized group variable as well as for confounding factors: age, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, duration of anesthesia, type of surgery, long-term statin use, Diabetes type II, and the respective baseline value of the inflammatory biomarker. All variables being significant in the univariable models were analyzed in a multivariable median regression model. Note that due to the skewness of data with equal medians in both groups, the regression model for PCT could not be calculated. Median regression models were chosen due to the skew distributions of observed outcome parameters. Note that due to the distributional characteristics of PCT with a high degree of discreteness and many tied observations at very low levels and limited variability caused numerical issues in quantile regression. We acknowledge that the use of univariable screening followed by multivariable inclusion has methodological limitations, however, this approach was intended as a pragmatic exploratory strategy for exploratory analyses of confounding factors. The maximum of IL-6, CRP, and PCT was calculated from all available postoperative measurements for each patient. Due to the large number of patients discharged after postoperative day 1, we additionally performed Wilcoxon-tests as sensitivity analyses restricted to complete cases, in which maximum values were calculated only in patients with complete measurements.

All p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R, release 4.2.2.

Sample size

The sample size of 95 patients per group of this study was based on the primary research question of the original study15. Here we present the results of a secondary analysis. We included all patients of the main trial in this secondary analysis, however, since the sample size was not primarily planned for the presented analyses, the study may be underpowered to detect small differences in inflammatory biomarkers.

Results

Patient characteristics

190 older adults undergoing minor- to moderate-risk noncardiac surgery between May 2022 and April 2023, who have been enrolled, were included in this secondary analysis15. Two patients randomly assigned to the desflurane group received sevoflurane for anesthesia (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics, including age, height, weight, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status, comorbidities, long-term medications, and type of surgery, were similar between groups and have been published previously within the main trial15,16. Except for heart rate, which was significantly higher in the desflurane group (61 beats.min− 1 [56;67] versus. 58 beats.min− 1 [54; 64] p = 0.026), age adjusted minimum alveolar concentration (0.48 [0.37; 0.55] versus 0.58 [0.49; 0.70] p = < 0.001) and intraoperative mean arterial pressure which was significantly higher in the desflurane group (80 mmHg [75; 87] versus 77 mmHg [72; 82] p =0.010), duration of anesthesia, duration of surgery, duration transfer to PACU, intraoperative medications, medications in the PACU, and vital parameters in the PACU were similar between the groups and published previously (Online Supplement eTable 1)15,16.

Interleukin-6

Postoperative maximum IL-6 concentrations did not significantly differ between the desflurane (8.11 pg.ml− 1 [IQR 4.77; 15.7]) or the sevoflurane group (7.84 pg.ml− 1 [IQR 4.67; 12.62]) (Wilcoxon-test: p = 0.357, complete case analysis: p = 0.173). There was no significant difference in the IL-6 concentrations between the desflurane and sevoflurane groups at any time-point of measurement (all p > 0.05); (Fig. 2a) (Table 1).

The univariable regression model did not show a significant effect of the randomization group on postoperative maximum IL-6 concentrations (effect estimate: −0.305 pg.ml− 1 95% CI −2.578– 1.968; p = 0.793).

In the univariable regression model, the type of surgery (gynecologic vs. general surgery: effect estimate: −5.328 pg.ml− 1; 95% CI −10.199– 0.457; p = 0.033) was significantly associated with lower postoperative maximum IL-6 concentrations. In the multivariable median regression analysis, these variables did not remain significant (all p > 0.05). Procalcitonin

Postoperative maximum PCT concentrations did not differ significantly between the desflurane group (0.04 ng.ml− 1 [IQR 0.03; 0.07]) and the sevoflurane group (0.04 ng.ml− 1 [IQR 0.03; 0.06]) (p = 0.709), complete case analyses: p = 0.224. There was no significant difference in PCT concentrations between the desflurane group and the sevoflurane group at any measurement point (all p > 0.05); (Fig. 2c) (Table 1).

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of a prospective randomized controlled trial, we did not observe any significant difference in postoperative maximum CRP, IL-6, and PCT concentrations between older adults receiving desflurane or sevoflurane for general anesthesia undergoing minor- to moderate-risk noncardiac surgery.

A previous randomized controlled trial by Koksal et al. evaluated the effect of sevoflurane versus desflurane on perioperative TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 concentrations in 60 ASA I-II patients undergoing tympanoplasty surgery9. In contrast to our study, they observed a significantly higher release of cytokines in those patients who received desflurane anesthesia9. One explanation for the different results may be the differences in the applied concentrations of volatile anesthetics. In the study by Koksal, a MAC between 1.0 and 1.5 was administered, while in our study, volatile anesthetics were administered goal-directed, aiming for a BIS value between 45 and 55, which resulted in a relatively low MAC of 0.48 for desflurane and 0.58 for sevoflurane. Another reason might be the differences in the duration of surgery, which was approximately 30 min longer in the study by Koksal et al.9A previous study in rats investigated the time-dependent effect of isoflurane, sevoflurane, and desflurane on pulmonary inflammation17. They found that the volatile anesthetics led to a time-dependent inflammatory response in the lungs, measured as Chemokine linker 2 (CCL2), IL-6, TNF-α, and Interleukin-10, in the lung parenchyma17. Thus, due to the shorter time and the lower concentration of administration of desflurane and sevoflurane it seems reasonable that we did not observe a significant effect between the groups.

Previous studies have shown that surgery is associated with a significant increase in postoperative inflammatory biomarkers1. The impact of surgery on postoperative inflammatory processes mainly depends on the extent of surgical tissue trauma18,19., Since longer surgical procedures are usually associated with more extensive tissue trauma, it is challenging to determine the specific influence of volatile anesthetics on the inflammatory response. In our study, we only included patients with a low risk of surgical tissue trauma and thus a low risk for postoperative inflammation. Thus, we can exclude surgical trauma as a possible influencing factor on our measured postoperative inflammatory biomarker concentrations. Interestingly, however, in the aforementioned study by Koksal et al., which only included patients undergoing tympanoplastic surgery, which in fact is associated with a very low risk for tissue trauma, showed that desflurane anesthesia led to significantly higher postoperative inflammatory biomarkers9. In the study by Koksal et al. TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, whereas except for IL-6, which we also measured, we assessed CRP and PCT for determination of the inflammatory response9. Possible explanations for the inconsistent results may be, again, the administered concentration of desflurane and sevoflurane, and the duration of administration of both volatile anesthetics. Nevertheless, although a previous study showed a significant difference between different types of volatile anesthetics, clinical meaningfulness is still questionable.

It has been shown that the administration of desflurane is associated with an inhibition of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), as well as an increased activation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine Interleukin-1α11,20. While sevoflurane has also been shown to decrease IL-1β, studies have shown conflicting results concerning other anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α21–23. Nevertheless, many previous studies were conducted in-vitro or in animals, which significantly limited the generalizability for the clinical setting. In contrast, we evaluated the effect of both anesthetics on clinically established biomarkers to detect a possible clinically meaningful effect in patients undergoing minor- to moderate-risk noncardiac surgery.

This study has some limitations. First, this is a secondary analysis of a prospective randomized trial, which was powered to detect a difference in postoperative recovery between the desflurane and the sevoflurane group15. Therefore, the study may be underpowered to detect small differences in inflammatory biomarkers, and results may be interpreted exploratory only. However, this is still one of the largest studies evaluating the effect of desflurane versus sevoflurane on clinically established inflammatory biomarkers.

Second, since our duration of surgery is relatively short, a longer duration of administration might have resulted in significant differences. To be able to evaluate the isolated effects of volatile anesthetics on inflammation, we focused on low-risk surgery without any considerable tissue trauma. Thus, the impact of different inhalational agents on the postoperative inflammatory process remains still unknown in patients undergoing major noncardiac surgery with excessive tissue trauma.

Third, blood samples were obtained shortly before the induction of anesthesia, within one hour after surgery, and on the second postoperative day. Furthermore, postoperative IL-6, CRP, and PCT peak at different time points, mainly between 2 and 72 h24,25. IL-6 peaks between two and 24 h after surgery, whereas CRP and PCT have similar kinetics, namely they peak at approximately 48 h after surgery24–28. We only included low-risk surgery with a relatively small surgical trauma, thus, we observed a smaller rise in postoperative inflammatory biomarkers as compared to other trials. Furthermore, we only measured biomarkers within the first two postoperative days, thus it might be possible that we have missed the peak concentrations. Nevertheless, as presented in our main trial, none of our patients had postoperative infections or re-surgery15. This would further strengthen our results, that the effect of desflurane versus sevoflurane plays a minor role in our very specified trial population.

Fourth, since we included patients undergoing minor to moderate risk surgery, many patients were discharged on POD1, therefore the sample size is decreased on postoperative day 2, as presented in the results. This could create an imbalance between groups. The sample size decreased to 51 in the desflurane group and 53 in the sevoflurane group. This indicates that missing blood draws were balanced between groups. The remaining missing lab values can be attributed to measurement errors.

Fifth, another limitation for interpreting our results is that all patients received a standardized remifentanil infusion for intraoperative pain management. We used remifentanil because of its rapid onset and offset to minimize the possibility of residual opioid affecting the primary outcome of the original trial, which was postoperative recovery from anesthesia15. It is well known that opioids influence the bodies response to surgical stress, and the innate and adaptive immune system29,30. Specifically, the administration of intraoperative remifentanil was associated with a more pronounced reduction in the response to surgical stress as compared to fentanyl31,32. Animal studies have shown that remifentanil suppresses the production of IL-6 and Interleukin-10 in the lungs and liver of septic rats, and of IL-6 and IL-1β in the lungs of rats with acute lung injury33,34. However, in human umbilical vein endothelial cells stimulated with TNF-α, remifentanil did not influence the production of IL-6 and Interleukin-835. In patients undergoing cardiac surgery, remifentanil was not associated with significant differences in plasma IL-6 or Interleukin-10 levels compared with fentanyl; however, PCT levels were significantly lower in the remifentanil group36. In patients undergoing surgery for cervical cancer, remifentanil resulted in significantly lower IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP levels compared to fentanyl at 24 h postoperatively37. Thus, we cannot completely rule out that remifentanil influenced our results.

Sixth, we used a limited set of inflammatory biomarkers including IL-6, CRP and PCT. Previous studies used various numbers of further inflammatory markers, such as Tumor necrosis factor alpha, IL-1β, Interleukin-8, Interleukin-10, soluble intercellular adhesion molecule, monocyte chemotactic protein 1, matrix metalloproteinase 9, or vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 concentrations8,9,11,38. Based on their faster release kinetics due to inflammatory processes, it might be possible that using those biomarkers, a difference would have been detected immediately after surgery. We evaluated the effect on clinically established biomarkers to detect a possible clinically meaningful effect.

Seventh, all patients received a single dose of 4 mg of dexamethasone for PONV prophylaxis. It has been shown that the administration of 10 mg dexamethasone before and after surgery leads to a reduction in postoperative CRP and IL-6 concentrations39,40. However, in those trials, the administered doses were nearly two to four times higher as compared to our study. Since we only administered a single dose of 4 mg for PONV prophylaxis, a clinically meaningful effect would be unlikely.

Lastly our methodology of using BIS guided administration of volatile agents assured equal hypnotic depth between groups. Desflurane and sevoflurane have shown to produce different levels of hypnotic depth at the same MAC values41,42. Nevertheless, the modulation of inflammatory processes might be dose dependent. Therefore, we have tested the effect of the two volatile anesthetics on inflammation at a low dose with equivalent hypnotic effect. Thus, it is possible that higher doses of desflurane or sevoflurane might have yielded different results.

In summary, under conditions of low inflammatory stimulus and low anesthetic exposure, no measurable difference was observed. Based on our observations, there is no clear indication of meaningful differences in inflammatory processes between BIS-guided administration of desflurane and sevoflurane in older adults undergoing low-risk surgery for up to two hours. Given the exploratory nature of the presented analyses, the inflammatory potential of volatile anesthetics in patients undergoing major surgery for a few hours must still be tested in large scale trials. However, because of the upcoming concerns regarding the environmental impact of volatile anesthetics, the administration of desflurane, specifically for short and low-risk surgery, may be questioned.
