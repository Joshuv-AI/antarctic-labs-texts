Ultrasound-guided tip location and detection of tip migration of neonatal peripherally inserted central catheters: a prospective, multicenter study (DISLOTIP study)
Authors: Fiorentino Grasso, Antonella Capasso, Pasquale Dolce, Gaetano Ausanio, Italo Bernardo, Chiara Poggi, Carlo Dani, Luca Bonadies, Eugenio Baraldi, Martino Landi, Barbara Tomasini, Ferdinando Spagnuolo, Mauro Carpentieri, Vito D’Andrea, Giovanni Vento, Carmen Rodriguez-Perez, Francesco Maria Risso, Giovanni Barone, Gina Ancora, Benjamim Ficial, Paolo Biban, Mauro Pittiruti, Letizia Capasso, Francesco Raimondi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncorrect neonatal peripherally inserted central catheter (n-PICC) tip positioning and secondary migration lead to serious complications. Identifying an accurate and non-invasive tip location method is crucial. This study primarily aimed to evaluate the feasibility of ultrasound-guided n-PICC tip location in a multicenter setting. Secondary aims were to determine the incidence and timing of tip migration and to identify factors associated with migration.MethodsMulticenter, prospective, cross-sectional study conducted at 10 Neonatal Intensive Care Units from January 2024 to January 2025. Ultrasound was used to confirm initial tip placement (T0) and repeated at 60–120 min (T1), 48–72 h (T2), and 6–8 days (T3) while the neonate was maintained in a standardized position. Feasibility was defined as obtaining clear images of all protocol-specified ultrasound views. Migration was defined as a tip displacement ≥ 0.5 cm or as a location in a different vessel. To analyze the factors associated with migration, univariate and multivariable logistic regression models were used.ResultsTwo hundred nineteen n-PICCs in 205 neonates were investigated. Ultrasound tip location was feasible in 212/219 (97%) cases. Tip migration occurred in 90/207 (43%) catheters evaluated for migration. The majority of migrations (79/90, 88%) were towards the heart and frequently occurred within the first 2 h post-insertion. Intracardiac migration was significantly more common when catheters were inserted via the upper extremities or scalp (79/87, 90%) compared to those placed via the lower extremities (0/14, 0%; p = 0.001). While univariate analysis linked migration to lower body weight and insertion site, multivariable analysis confirmed only the insertion site as an independent predictor, with non-upper limb insertions showing significantly lower odds of migration compared to those distal to the elbow.ConclusionsUltrasound is a highly feasible and accurate method for n-PICC tip location. Tip migration is a frequent event, particularly towards the heart in upper limbs/scalp insertions and within the first 2 h. We recommend serial ultrasound monitoring to detect tip migration and potentially reduce catheter-related complications.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06438289. Introduction

Central venous access is often needed in critical patients for the administration of drugs and parenteral nutrition. In the neonatal period, this is often obtained through a peripherally inserted central catheter (neonatal PICC, n-PICC); the latter is a 1–2.7 French catheter positioned by direct venipuncture of superficial veins of the limbs or the scalp to reach the distal third of the superior vena cava (SVC)—preferably at the junction with the right atrium (RA)—or the same segment of the inferior vena cava (IVC) [1–3]. Any n-PICC tip location should be documented during its placement [1, 4]. Incorrect tip positioning is associated with the risk of potentially life-threatening complications such as cardiac arrhythmias, transmural perforation and tamponade, pleural effusion, thrombosis, extravasation [5–10]. In addition to primary malposition, n-PICCs tips may undergo secondary migration from the original position after placement arising the same serious concerns [11, 12].

Neonatal PICC tip location and migrations are currently documented by conventional radiology (chest/abdomen film) [13–19]. This approach is post- and not intra-procedural, implies the exposure to ionizing radiation and the inaccurate reporting of the tip position based on skeletal proxy [20, 21]. These issues can be avoided using ultrasound (US).

In a previous single-center study, we demonstrated the high feasibility (92.3%; 109/118 catheters) of US-guided tip location, even when using the smallest n-PICCs, which are more prone to complications. That study also highlighted the clinical relevance of this technique, as malpositions led to a different clinical course in 1 of 6 cases [22].

The present multicenter investigation was designed to test the wider applicability of our previous results. Specifically, we aimed to validate our findings over time by including a new cohort from our Institution (temporal validation), and across different clinical environments by enrolling several other NICUs (geographical validation). In addition, n-PICC tip migration frequency and timing were investigated by serially scanning the study cohort.

Materials and methods

This was a multicenter, prospective, cross-sectional study conducted in 10 Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICU) (Federico II University Hospital, Naples; Sant’Anna e San Sebastiano Hospital, Caserta; Careggi University Hospital, Florence; Padova University Hospital, Padova; Siena University Hospital, Siena; Luigi Vanvitelli University Hospital, Naples; Gemelli University Hospital, Rome; Spedali Civili di Brescia, Brescia; Infermi Hospital, Rimini; Verona University Hospital, Verona) with consolidated experience in US tip location of n-PICCs from January 2024 through January 2025.

Primary outcome was the multicenter validation of the feasibility of n-PICC US-guided tip location, defined as obtaining clear images of all protocol-specified US views.

Secondary outcomes were:

to assess by US the incidence and the timing of n-PICC tip migration;

to assess by US the incidence and the timing of n-PICC tip migration;

to define factors associated with tip migration.

to define factors associated with tip migration.

Inclusion criteria: neonates who underwent n-PICC placement as determined by the attending physician.

Exclusion criteria: lack of parental consent; major congenital malformations.

All Centers used 1 French/28 Gauge polyurethane catheters (Premicath, Vygon, France), placed with sterile technique. The catheter insertion site was chosen by the implanting physician based on the patient’s venous anatomy. A standardized catheter securement system was implemented across all Centers, involving the use of cyanoacrylate glue (Secureport IV, Vygon) at the insertion point, stabilization with an adhesive sutureless device (Grip-lok, Vygon), and application of a semipermeable transparent membrane (Tegaderm Advanced, Solventum, U.S.A.). Nurse written report on the catheter and dressing status was obtained at every shift.

The choice of the type of ultrasound probe to be used was left to the individual Center.

The following B-mode views were obtained: parasternal longitudinal view of SVC, apical 4-chamber view, left parasternal short axis view and bicaval subcostal view for n-PICCs inserted from the upper extremities; subcostal longitudinal view of IVC and transhepatic IVC view for those inserted from the lower extremities (Fig. 1). The specific utility of each view for tip location was as follows:

For n-PICCs inserted from the upper extremities or scalp, the long-axis SVC view was the primary acoustic window used both to confirm correct tip placement during insertion and to precisely measure the tip distance from the ostium where the SVC drains into the RA at subsequent time points. The apical 4-chamber, parasternal short-axis, and subcostal bicaval views played a role in identifying intracardiac tip positions, particularly when the long-axis SVC view visualized the catheter shaft but not its terminal tip.

A small flush of normal saline (0.5–1.0 mL) was used to gain a better visualization of the catheter tip.

Once the central tip position had been assessed, the US procedure was repeated at 60–120 min (T1), 48–72 h and (T2), and 6–8 days (T3) after placement. Throughout all re-evaluations, the previously described standardized neonate position was maintained. If the n-PICC tip was not visualized within the aforementioned acoustic windows despite a technically feasible examination (defined as obtaining clear images of all protocol-specified US views), the catheter was considered malpositioned. The location of the tip was then determined through a systematic US examination of the brachiocephalic, subclavian, internal jugular, and axillary veins, according to the GAVeCeLT Rapid Central Vein Assessment (RaCeVA) protocol (Additional File 2: Table S2) [23, 24]. n-PICCs were considered migrated when a displacement of 0.5 cm (cm) or greater from the previous time point was observed, or when the tip was located in a different vessel. The magnitude of tip migration was quantified by comparing the tip distance from a single, fixed anatomical reference point across different time points. This landmark was the ostium where the vena cava (superior or inferior) drains into the RA, evaluated in the long-axis SVC view or the subcostal longitudinal view of IVC, respectively. This standardized approach allowed for precise and reproducible measurement. The 0.5 cm threshold was selected as the operational definition of migration, representing the minimum displacement considered clinically relevant in a preterm infant due to the small dimensions of the cardiac chambers and vessels, where such a shift significantly increases the risk of mechanical complications, particularly with inward (intracardiac) advancement.

Primary malpositioned or secondary migrated catheters were not subsequently re-evaluated.

Sample size

A formal power analysis was not performed, as the primary aim of the study was to estimate the feasibility rate of US-guided tip location. Therefore, we adopted a confidence interval–based approach. Based on the results of our previous single-center study [22], which reported a feasibility of 92.3% for US-guided tip location in neonates with 1 French/28 Gauge catheters, we calculated the required sample size using the exact binomial method. Assuming this expected proportion, a sample size of 196 n-PICC placements would allow estimation of the feasibility rate with a 95% confidence interval and a margin of error of ± 4%,

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD), categorical variables as frequencies (number of cases) with percentages. US tip location feasibility and the incidence of n-PICC migration were reported with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To explore potential risk factors for migration, both univariate and multivariable logistic regression models were applied. For the multivariable model, candidate variables were selected based on their potential clinical influence on the dependent variable. To prevent multicollinearity, highly correlated predictors were not included simultaneously. The final model was used to assess the independent association of each variable with tip migration. Statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software R. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided α level of 0.05.

US examinations were performed in 2 Centers using a linear probe (3–13 MHz) and in the other 8 Centers using a sectorial probe (7–8 MHz).

In the entire cohort, the US procedure was feasible in 212 cases, i.e., 0.97 [95% CI 0.93–0.99]. In the 7 cases where US-guided tip location was not feasible, tip location was achieved by radiography, and these catheters were excluded from the study. In all non-feasible cases, the n-PICC had been placed via the upper limbs or scalp; furthermore, in 5 cases the patient was on mechanical ventilation (MV) and in 2 on non-invasive ventilation (NIV).

Analysis of feasibility based on the ultrasound probe type revealed a success rate of 100% for examinations using a linear probe and 96% for those using a sectorial probe. The seven non-feasible examinations were all performed with a sectorial probe, and the failure was consistently attributed by the operator to poor thoracic acoustic windows resulting from diffuse pulmonary hyperechogenicity.

We reported 5 primary malpositions at T0 which we were unable to correct.

Tip migration was documented in 90/207, i.e., 0.43 [95% CI, 0.37–0.50]. Specifically, in the group of 193 catheters inserted via the upper extremities or scalp, 87 migrations occurred (45% of cases), while 3 migrations occurred in the group of 14 catheters placed via the lower extremities (21% of cases).

Table 3 details the frequency, direction, and timing of the migrations. The vast majority of events occurred towards the heart throughout the study (79/90). Specifically, out of the 87 migrations that occurred in the group of catheters placed via the upper extremities or scalp, 79 were intracardiac (90%), while no intracardiac migration was observed among the 3 migrated catheters placed via the lower extremities, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). Furthermore, dislocations within two hours from placement were predominant.

The extent of migration in the entire case series was on average 1 cm (range 0.5–3 cm).

We report only one case of dislodgement attributable to catheter outward migration of approximately 3 cm detected at T3, where the tip dislocated into the homolateral subclavian vein. In all other cases, the external catheter length remained unchanged during the observation period.

Dressing changes were needed for 3 n-PICCs during the study’s observation period: twice due to soiling and once due to membrane detachment. Catheter dislodgement did not occur in any of these three cases.

The presence of factors associated with migration was also investigated (Table 4). Univariate analysis identified lower body weight at catheter positioning and the overall insertion site as factors significantly associated with migration. In the multivariable analysis, which included both variables in the model, the association with body weight was no longer statistically significant (aOR 0.64; 95% CI 0.37–1.05; p = 0.092). Regarding the insertion site, when comparing specific categories, insertion from upper limb proximal to the elbow did not differ significantly from distal insertion (aOR 0.59; 95% CI 0.32–1.08; p = 0.089). However, non-upper limb insertion showed significantly lower odds of migration compared with distal upper limb insertions (aOR 0.24; 95% CI 0.05–0.83; p = 0.037). No significant differences were found in the association with the examined variables among the migrations occurring at different time points.

Six mechanical complications (3 occlusions and 3 phlebitis) were reported during the catheters’ dwell time. No life-threatening complications (such as pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, or arrhythmias) directly attributable to secondary malpositions were observed.

Discussion

This multicenter study validates the high feasibility of US-guided n-PICC tip location.

These findings are a necessary complement to our previous single center observations with a similar aim [22].

The robustness of this methodology is further highlighted by its high feasibility across different clinical settings and equipment. Although all seven non-feasible cases were observed in Centers using sectorial probes, the overall success rate for this group remained exceptionally high at 96%, a rate substantially comparable to the 100% feasibility achieved with linear probes, supporting the real-world applicability of US method. Further previous studies regarding n-PICCs US tip location are available; however, they have reported discordant success rates for the technique, ranging from 59 to 100% [26–33]. Notably, all existing studies present data generated within controlled settings of single-center research protocols. Our study is the first multicenter investigation on this topic, and therefore, it reports feasibility data for n-PICCs US tip location in a more realistic and pragmatic setting of routine NICU clinical practice. Our findings underscore the necessity of adopting US tip location as the standard of care in NICUs, in place of radiography, which several studies have demonstrated to be less accurate [22, 28, 29, 33].

The design of the study also allowed us to investigate the timeframe of tip migration, a clinically relevant topic on which limited evidence was available.

Previous researchers studied n-PICC migration using different reassessment time points and variable-caliber devices. Furthermore, all studies available relied on conventional radiography for tip location. Acun C et al. serially assessed the tip position of 168 n-PICCs (1.4 or 1.9 French, polyurethane or silastic), finding migration in 47 cases (28%), with 60% occurring within 12–24 h and 83% within the first 3 days post-insertion [17]. Gupta R et al. showed that 15/63 n-PICCs (23%) of unspecified caliber were dislocated 1 h after placement and 7 out of 63 (11%) at 24 h [18]. Srinivasan HB et al. observed migration in 45 out of 100 n-PICCs (caliber ranged from 1 to 1.9 Fr) examined 24 h after placement [19].

Using the smallest and most mobile n-PICCs, we found an overall n-PICC tip migration incidence of 43%, mostly occurring between 60 and 120 min post-insertion. We also documented that the majority of migrations occurred towards the heart (88%) for n-PICCs placed via the upper limbs, consistent with previous studies on this topic.

Since migrations away from the heart were only sporadic, we believe that this may downplay the role of limb movement on n-PICC migration.

Explaining tip migration is not easy. We speculate that after placement a polyurethane catheter can be softened by the body temperature and the blood stream may straighten its course overcoming the tortuosity of peripheral vessels. This hypothesis would explain the higher frequency of migration observed in catheters inserted via the upper extremities with an insertion site distal to the elbow compared to those inserted proximal to the elbow or at the antecubital fossa (59% and 41%, respectively), a path with greater venous tortuosity.

Our study has some limitations. First, to provide results homogeneity catheter securement was performed in a standardized manner using the most innovative devices; another securement protocol may result in a different dislodgement frequency. Second, we provide little evidence on catheters placed via the lower limbs with few (3/14), clinically non-significant migrations. This is not in keeping with Tao X et al. who reported in a large series of 1.9 Fr caliber n-PICCs in the lower limbs 66% displacement in flexed position and 76% in straight position one week post insertion [34].

Third, our study was powered for the primary outcome, which was the feasibility of the US technique. Consequently, the analysis of risk factors for migration should be considered exploratory and may be underpowered to detect smaller, yet clinically relevant, effects. This is exemplified by our multivariable model, which did not find a significant difference between proximal and distal upper limb insertion sites; however, a clinically meaningful difference cannot be ruled out and this warrants investigation in a larger, appropriately powered study. Conversely, the statistically significant difference observed between “distal to the elbow” and “non-upper limb” insertions should be interpreted with caution. The “non-upper limb” category is a heterogeneous group combining lower limb and scalp insertions, making this comparison less clinically actionable for guiding placement choices within the upper extremities.

In conclusion, by confirming the feasibility of non-invasive tip location and position monitoring, the results of this multicenter study advocate for a wider adoption of the US technique. We suggest the use of intraprocedural US during catheter placement, an early post-insertion examination at 60–120 min (perhaps before definitive catheter securement) and a later scan at 72 h. This simplified protocol will not only spare a harmful load of ionizing radiation [35] but it would also reduce the risk of catheter-related mechanical complications and improve the quality of care for neonate.
