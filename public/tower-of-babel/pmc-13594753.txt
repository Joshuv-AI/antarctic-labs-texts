Unrepaired bladder exstrophy associated with genital prolapse in an adult: Case report and literature review
Authors: Arsen Mudiangombe Ngoie, Abdou Aziz Mbaye, Mamadou Sissokho, Modou Ndiaye, Ahmed Fall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bladder exstrophy is a rare congenital malformation within the exstrophy–epispadias complex, usually diagnosed in the neonatal period. Untreated cases persisting into adulthood are exceptional and may be associated with major functional disorders, including pelvic organ prolapse. We report the case of a 25-year-old woman with an uncorrected complete bladder exstrophy, revealed by lifelong urinary incontinence and postpartum genital prolapse. Radiological assessment showed pubic symphysis diastasis without involvement of the upper urinary tract. Management requires a multidisciplinary approach aimed at improving functional outcomes and quality of life

Introduction

Bladder exstrophy is a rare congenital malformation resulting from an abnormal development of the cloacal membrane, which forms the infra-umbilical anterior abdominal wall during the first weeks of embryonic life. In females, this condition is characterized by a failure of closure of the anterior bladder wall, malformation of the abdominal wall, separation of the pubic bones, a bifid clitoris, and abnormal pelvic floor musculature. The vagina is shortened and located in an anterior position. The cervix extends deeper into the anterior vaginal wall.1 Due to the altered anatomy, severe genital prolapse may be present in affected women.2

It is usually diagnosed and managed at birth in order to preserve urinary function and reduce long-term complications.3 In adults, untreated bladder exstrophy is rare. The association of genital prolapse with untreated bladder exstrophy is exceptionally reported.4

This case highlights the clinical and radiological presentation of bladder exstrophy associated with genital prolapse in an adult woman and allows discussion of management options according to the literature.

Clinical case

We report the case of a 25-year-old woman, a single mother of a 4-year-old child, born with untreated bladder exstrophy. She complained of permanent urinary incontinence, a sensation of pelvic heaviness that appeared after a vaginal delivery, and peri-vesical skin irritation. On physical examination, the patient presented with complete bladder exstrophy with permanent exposure of the bladder mucosa, an erythematous and irritated peri-vesical mucosa (Fig. 1), as well as genital prolapse, with protrusion of the bladder and uterus during increases in intra-abdominal pressure (Fig. 2). Pelvic ultrasound revealed anatomical alterations of the pelvic floor, while CT urography showed an upper urinary tract without abnormalities associated with pubic symphysis diastasis (Fig. 3). The final diagnosis was complete bladder exstrophy associated with genital prolapse secondary to vaginal delivery in an adult woman. No surgical intervention was performed at this stage, and future management will require a multidisciplinary approach combining urology and reconstructive pelvic surgery in order to optimize continence and the patient's quality of life.

Discussion

Bladder exstrophy remains a rare condition within the exstrophy–epispadias complex. Its incidence is estimated at approximately 3.3 per 100,000 live births, making it a rare pathology requiring early specialized management.5 Epispadias, which is part of the exstrophy–epispadias spectrum, has an estimated incidence of 2.4 per 100,000 live births. Indeed, the prevalence of untreated bladder exstrophy in adults is extremely low, estimated between 1 per 30,000 and 50,000 births.1 Pelvic organ prolapse may represent a difficult and distressing problem in women with bladder exstrophy. Mathews et al. reported a prevalence of 18%, with a mean age of onset of 16 years.6 However, in many regions of the world, particularly in low-resource settings, the true incidence of these anomalies remains difficult to determine due to insufficient congenital anomaly surveillance systems.

The diagnosis of bladder exstrophy is based on non-visualization of the bladder, theoretically possible from the first-trimester ultrasound. However, in most cases, it is confirmed during the second-trimester anomaly scan. Prenatal diagnostic features include absence of bladder filling, low-set umbilicus, widened pubic diastasis, small external genitalia, and a lower abdominal mass.7 The diagnosis is often missed or confused with omphalocele or gastroschisis.

The literature reports diagnoses made in the early postnatal period or during the first years of life,8 while other cases are diagnosed at 15 years of age or older.9 In our case, the diagnosis was established at 24 years of age during consultation for uterovaginal prolapse. Traoré, in his series, reported patients aged 24, 25, and 28 years.10 Although antenatal diagnosis is possible, it is most often made at birth.

In our case, the patient came from a low socioeconomic background. Several authors have noted that delayed diagnosis and management of this malformation are often associated with poor social conditions, characterized by limited access to healthcare facilities, lack of antenatal care and obstetric ultrasound, and sometimes home deliveries.10

Management of this malformation requires a multidisciplinary approach involving urologists, pediatric surgeons, orthopedic surgeons, and rehabilitation specialists in order to optimize functional outcomes, particularly urinary continence, renal function, and quality of life.

Treatment of bladder exstrophy in both newborns and older patients remains surgical. The primary objective is successful primary closure, as it is associated with reduced overall costs, decreased bladder inflammation and fibrosis, improved bladder growth, and reduced need for urinary diversion.11

Since the first description of staged surgical reconstruction of exstrophy by Sweetser et al. in the 1950s, debate has continued regarding the optimal surgical management, timing, and technique of closure.12

Generally, the modern staged approach divides repair into three phases: primary bladder and abdominal wall closure, epispadias repair, and continence procedures. In recent years, primary closure of the bladder and abdominal wall is usually scheduled between 6 and 12 weeks of life.13 Before closure, the infant is managed with frequent saline irrigations of the exstrophic bladder and protective cellophane dressings to protect the bladder mucosa.

In children with mild to moderate vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), ureteral reimplantation is performed at the same time as continence surgery. Children not eligible for bilateral ureteral reimplantation or those with persistent urinary incontinence after surgery may require bladder neck closure, augmentation cystoplasty, and/or catheterizable continent urinary diversion.13

In 1987, Matsuda et al. reported a case of a 24-year-old woman with exstrophy treated with a Koch pouch.14 Ozdiler et al. reported two cases treated with cystectomy and a modified Mainz pouch.15

Patients requiring augmentation cystoplasty generally also require a continent urinary diversion (CUD). Typical options include appendicovesicostomy (Mitrofanoff procedure) or tapered ileal catheterizable channel (Monti procedure). Recent literature reports that approximately half of adults with bladder exstrophy have a continent urinary stoma. About 1 in 5 patients who undergo CUD report stomal leakage.16

Pelvic osteotomy has several advantages in bladder exstrophy closure, including tension-free approximation of the pubic bones, deeper positioning of the bladder and posterior vesicourethral unit within the pelvis, and improved outcomes for subsequent reconstruction.17 Osteotomy is recommended when the pubic diastasis exceeds 4 cm, in patients older than 72 hours, or in children with low pelvic compliance, based on evaluation by a pediatric orthopedic surgeon.18

Various surgical techniques have been proposed for correction of uterine prolapse in these patients. Uterine suspension has shown only modest success, with multiple complications. Some experts recommend uterine fixation.19 Hysterectomy is not an option in women wishing to preserve fertility. Moreover, removal of the uterus without vaginal support does not resolve the underlying problem.

Sexual desire is considered normal in adult women, and most are sexually active. Fertility in women born with bladder exstrophy is generally preserved, with up to 66% of those attempting conception achieving full-term pregnancy. Cervical and uterine prolapse, as well as transient urinary incontinence, are common complications after pregnancy.16

Conclusion

Bladder exstrophy associated with genital prolapse in adults is extremely rare. Delayed diagnosis, often related to socioeconomic factors, complicates management. A multidisciplinary approach is essential to optimize continence, sexual function, and quality of life. Even in adulthood, planned surgical management can significantly improve functional prognosis.
