Impact of COVID-19-related data drift on machine-learning prognostic models predicting 30-day opioid-related emergency department visits, hospitalisation or mortality: a population-level administrative data study in Alberta, Canada
Authors: Vishal Sharma, Weiting Li, Tanya Joon, Cerina Dubois, Darren Lau, Ed Jess, Cliff Lindeman, Nicole Allison Kain, Ming Ye, Michael Semenchuk, Dean T Eurich, Salim Samanani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo develop machine-learning (ML) models during the COVID-19 pandemic and adjacent time periods to evaluate the impact of data drift on model performance.DesignThis prognostic study used population-level administrative health data to develop ML prediction models.SettingAlberta, Canada during 2019–2023.ParticipantsAll patients over 18 who received at least one opioid dispensation from a community pharmacy within the province of Alberta between 2019–2023.ExposureEach opioid dispensation served as the unit-of-analysis.Main outcomes/measuresOpioid-related outcomes were identified from linked health administrative datasets. Light Gradient Boosting-machine models were developed on pre-pandemic, pandemic and endemic data and temporally validated on 2023 data (pre-pandemic model was also validated on 2020–2021 data) to predict the risk of emergency department visit, hospitalisation or mortality within 30-days of an opioid dispensation. We described key feature distributions across the study time period and changes in model prediction performance on the validation sets using relevant metrics.ResultsAmong 1.2 million study participants representing over 13 million opioid dispensations, there were 59 809 (2.1%), 134 402 (2.4%) and 62 143 (2.3%) events reported in the pre-pandemic (2019), pandemic (2020 and 2021) and endemic (2022) time periods, respectively (estimated 2023 validation set pre-test probability of 2.8%). Notable differences in key features were observed in the 2020–2021 model relative to other years. In the 2023 validation set, discrimination performance was highest for the pre-pandemic and endemic models compared with the pandemic model (0.81, 0.83, 0.74, respectively). A similar trend regarding changes from pre-test to post-test probabilities in higher categories of predicted risk (23%, 40%, 16%) was observed. 2020–2021 had the lowest discrimination performance (0.71) and uninformative post-test probabilities (<10%).ConclusionCOVID-19 pandemic health data contributed to significant ML drift. Although ML approaches allow for quick re-training to mitigate drift, health regulators should approach ML prediction with caution when using pandemic-times data.Keywords: Machine Learning, COVID-19, EPIDEMIOLOGY

Introduction

Machine learning (ML) approaches offer novel and innovative methods to predict population-level health outcomes, including opioid-related outcomes.1,3 This is not surprising as the opioid crisis in Canada continues, despite efforts by policy makers and healthcare providers, and the crisis has led to more hospitalisations and deaths than from all other categories of accidental deaths, combined.4 5 Health profession regulators administer prescription monitoring programmes as part of opioid stewardship initiatives; the Tracked Prescription Program Alberta (TPP) is one such programme administered by the College of Physicians & Surgeons of Alberta (CPSA).6

Supervised ML is an approach that uses computer algorithms and large amounts of outcome-labelled data to build clinical prediction models within a well-defined framework.7 8 Because these models are developed on historical data, they can lose their accuracy over time due to changes in data variable distribution called data drift.9 Data drift occurs when the distribution of training data changes over time, a common reality in ML prediction. One approach to prevent model data drift involves regularly retraining models as new data emerges. ML model drift is infrequently studied in clinical prediction studies. To date, we found no studies that explore data drift and model performance when predicting opioid-related outcomes. Indeed, the healthcare environment, including healthcare data distributions (both measured and unmeasured), changed during the COVID-19 pandemic,10 11 which likely contributed to model drift in prediction tools using data derived from the pandemic period.

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, ML methods classifying opioid dispensations to predict risk of opioid-related adverse outcomes12 13 demonstrated promising results. This work included developing ML models using administrative health data available in Alberta, Canada and on models developed on truncated data sets available to the TPP. Overall, the findings suggested ML prediction could potentially assist opioid stewardship programmes administered by health regulators. However, the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in considerable changes in how the public used the health system.

In Alberta, emergency public health measures were implemented in March 2020 to address the COVID-19 threat. Initial phases of the COVID-19 pandemic brought unprecedented social and economic disruption: lockdowns led to job losses, health services were diverted or delayed, and patient access to routine care was constrained. Although the WHO declared the end of the global COVID-19 public health emergency of international concern on 5 May 2023, Alberta declared an end much earlier. Indeed, in Alberta, the government progressively lifted public health restrictions; and by March 2022, all major restrictions were removed, except for health facilities that still maintained some restrictions (eg, masking) that were also subsequently lifted by June 2022. Together, these timelines help anchor our analytic periods in evolving real-world policy and service environments.14 15 Hence, we hypothesise that these changes would have considerable influence on opioid-related ML prediction due to underlying model drift.

Thus, we aimed to explore and compare ML model prediction performance during pre-pandemic, pandemic and endemic periods in the context of opioid-related outcomes and data drift. We developed ML models trained on pre-pandemic (2019), pandemic (2020–2021) and endemic (2022) data. We also compared training data distributions and model performance between these three periods using 2020–2021 and 2023 outcomes as temporal validation.

Methods

This study followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis+Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD+AI) guideline (online supplemental appendix A) and other published guidelines specific to ML projects.7 16 17 The study design, data elements, feature generation, outcomes and measures are similar to our previous work in this field.12

Study design, setting and participants

This prognostic study used a supervised ML approach which trained a Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGB) model on opioid dispensations in Alberta, Canada between January 2019 to September 2023. We used the LGB machine as it was associated with the highest prediction performance based on our previous work12 and because it generates an explainable model. All Albertans aged 18 and over who received an opioid dispensation from a community pharmacy were included in this study.

Data sources

Although a wide range of administrative databases are available in Alberta, few are readily available, if at all, to health profession regulatory organisations, a common issue in many jurisdictions. The TPP is limited to the prescription drug dispensation records that it actively monitors. Thus, we limited the datasets used to train the ML models to those accessible by the CPSA. Health regulators in other jurisdictions may have access to additional data to train ML models and create their own specific ML classifier.

To develop the ML models, we used dispensation data from the Pharmaceutical Information Network (PIN), which has comprehensive information on dispensing records from community pharmacies in Alberta, irrespective of coverage status and age.18 The PIN data was limited using Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification (ATC) codes to only include prescription records for which the TPP has access to.6 The CPSA receives records of opioid, benzodiazepine and antibiotic dispensations in daily updates from Alberta Health.

To label each instance of an opioid dispensation with an outcome, we linked the prescription data with (1) Population and Vital Statistics Data (vs, Alberta Services): sex, age, date of birth, death date, immigration and emigration data within the province, and underlying cause of death according to the WHO algorithm using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes and Related Health Problems19 and (2) Hospitalisations and Emergency Department Visits National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS), Discharge Abstract Database (DAD): all services, length of stay, diagnoses (up to 25 ICD-10 based diagnoses). The ICD is the global standard for diagnostic health information, maintained by the WHO to track mortality and morbidity. For this study, data and coding accuracy are routinely validated both provincially and centrally via the Canadian Institute for Health Information.20

These linked databases represent a labelled dataset used to develop the LGB models. Currently, the CPSA does not have access to outcomes data (eg, DAD, NACRS and vs) nor physician claims data. We labelled data equivalent to the PIN data CPSA would receive with DAD, NACRS and vital statistics data to assess data drift and compare ML prediction performance during and adjacent to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Measures and outcomes

The unit of analysis was at the opioid dispensation level, such that each opioid dispensation served as a potential instance to predict our outcome. This level of analysis was used to be consistent with others2 and to accurately represent clinical use in the real world, in which a health regulator may want to assess the risk at each dispensation of an opioid. Opioid dispensations were identified from the PIN file using ATC codes21 (online supplemental etable).

The main outcome of interest was a composite of a drug-related emergency department visit, hospitalisation or death within 30-days of an opioid dispensation based on ICD-10 codes (online supplemental etable 1).22 Events occurring within 30 days of an exposure of interest are commonly used by health systems for risk assessments23 (online supplemental efigure 1).

Rare outcomes are common in clinical prediction model development, and we also anticipated our training data would be class-imbalanced.24 To address this, we used the frequently used class weight-age method, which does not alter the data distribution and instead, increases the importance of the positive class (ie, instances that led to the outcome).25

Predictors and ML methods

Similar to our previous work, all candidate predictors were obtained from the PIN dataset and were either derived from the literature26 27 or directly incorporated from the data, unmodified (online supplemental etable 2). These features included demographics (ie, age, sex, Forward Sortation Index from postal codes,28 income), drug utilisation (ie, ATC codes, oral morphine equivalents,29 concurrent use with benzodiazepines, number of dispensations, number of unique molecules) and healthcare utilisation (ie, number of opioid prescribers and pharmacies). We used data 30 days before each opioid dispensation to measure each predictor (online supplemental efigure 1).

We used the LGB Machine to develop three ML models. Models 1, 2 and 3 were developed using pre-pandemic data (2019), pandemic data (2020–2021) and endemic data (2022), respectively. Each model’s performance was assessed using temporal validation on 2023 data spanning 1 January to 30 September (online supplemental efigure 1). The pre-pandemic model was also temporally validated on 2020–2021 data to assess model performance during the pandemic, when many health and social measures were in place. We performed k=5 cross fold validation in the development set to tune hyperparameters. To ensure thorough development and validation, the model was trained and cross-validated using five random splits of the training data via an 80%/20% split. By introducing different subsets for training, this technique makes a robust evaluation by reducing the variance associated with a single split of data. Furthermore, an iterative approach was used for hyperparameter optimisation called Randomised Search. Hyperparameters like learning rate, maximum depth, number of estimators and others are adjusted incrementally to determine the optimal configuration for LGB Machine. We anticipated missing data when handling laboratory and healthcare encounter records. The Provincial Laboratory and administrative databases comprehensively capture all completed laboratory tests and recorded healthcare encounters. Accordingly, any ‘missing’ data indicate that a test, healthcare encounter, or diagnosis did not occur, rather than incomplete reporting. As such, missing values were not imputed prior to analysis. LightGBM (LGBM) inherently handles missing data during model training by learning the optimal direction for missing values at each decision split. This allows the algorithm to effectively use all available information without requiring explicit imputation, reducing potential bias from ad hoc data imputation.

Our validation sets were defined from 2020 to 2021 and 2023 dispensations and are not a true external independent set as is typically employed in ML prediction model research literature. Participants in the three development sets could also appear in the validation sets. This was a deliberate decision, as this is the real-world scenario that health regulators operate in, where patient instances can be repeatedly encountered. Indeed, it is ideal to develop ML prediction models trained on data from the population in which they will be deployed. Our analysis was done on these validation sets because health regulators frequently monitor dispensations with repeated encounters, making it impractical to report performance metrics on an external validation set with out-of-sample instances. Separate models were developed at each longitudinal time point. As this represents a dynamic real-world cohort, individual patients could contribute data to multiple time points if they continued to receive opioid prescriptions during later periods. Within each time point, however, training and validation data were partitioned to ensure no overlap.

Analysis and prediction evaluation

Summary statistics are presented for participant characteristics and outcome event rates in the development and validation sets. Descriptive analyses were done at the participant and dispense level and where each participant could be represented by multiple instances of opioid dispensations. To assess data drift in the pre-pandemic (online supplemental Appendix B), pandemic and endemic periods, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values for each of the three ML models were used to present distributions of key ML features. Since ML models do not estimate an interpretable quantity relating predictors to outcomes, it is not appropriate to summarise that relationship with a single parameter. Instead, the impact of individual predictors can be summarised using variable importance’, which is a rank-ordering of variables that are most important for the ML model’s prediction performance30; variable importance does not have a causal or statistical meaning. To address interpretability31 of our ML models, feature importance30 and feature impact using SHAP value plots32 33 are presented (online supplemental Appendix C). The percent change in feature distributions across study time periods is presented visually. To assess potential population drift across study periods, we calculated Population Stability Index (PSI) values comparing model time points (pre-pandemic vs pandemic, pandemic vs endemic and pre-pandemic vs endemic).34

Validation sets were used to evaluate the ML models’ predictive performance using metrics commonly applied to clinical prediction models.35,38 As is done in many ML prediction studies,17 we assessed our LGB machine models’ discrimination performance by estimating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). For binary classification studies like ours, AUROC curves correspond to c-statistics, which are a measure of model discrimination performance, the extent to which a model predicts a higher probability of an outcome among participants who actually had the outcome compared with those who did not.36 Precision-recall curves (PRC) were also included39 (online supplemental efigure 2).

We provided a calibration plot36 for each model; calibration is considered an important property of any prediction model and reflects the extent to which predicted values align with observed values and is most often illustrated by a plot of observed versus predicted35 36 (online supplemental efigure 3).

A prediction threshold analysis is presented to evaluate the potential clinical utility of the three ML models by ranking predicted risks by categorising them into percentiles (eg, deciles).12 13 At each of these category cut-off points, prediction performance metrics are presented (ie, positive likelihood ratios (LR+),37 40 true/false positives, true/false negatives and positive predictive values (PPV, equivalent to post-test probability). These metrics were estimated on our validation sets using the average of daily measurements.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted sensitivity analyses to evaluate model performance across key demographic subgroups and time periods. Specifically, model discrimination and calibration were assessed separately by age group (medium split), sex and income (medium split) within each study phase (pre-pandemic, pandemic and endemic). For each subgroup and time period, we calculated the AUROC, precision and recall examining consistency in predictive performance. AUROC is a performance metric for binary classification models that measures their ability to distinguish between classes (e.g., positive vs. negative) across all possible classification thresholds. These analyses were performed to determine whether model behaviour differed across demographic strata or evolved over time as population characteristics and healthcare utilisation patterns changed.41 42

Patient and public involvement

This research was done without patient involvement. Patients were not invited to comment on the study design and were not consulted to develop patient-relevant outcomes or interpret the results. Patients were not invited to contribute to the writing or editing of this document for readability or accuracy. There are no plans to disseminate the results of the research to study participants.

Data availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from Alberta Health but restrictions apply to the availability of these data, which were used under licence for the current study, and so are not publicly available. However, administrative health data can be accessed from Alberta Health by following defined research protocol and confidentiality agreement approval.

Results

A total of 1 219 591 participants were included in this study (figure 1), representing 13 023 483 opioid dispensations between January 2019 and September 2023; 46% of study participants were men and 54% were women with mean age of 52.8 (SD=16.6) as measured within the 2023 validation set. The 2020–2021 and 2023 validation sets had 5.5 million and 1.9 million opioid dispensations with positive instances (2.4% and 2.8%), representing the pre-test probability of the composite outcome, respectively. Age, sex and outcome distributions were similar across the three development set time periods (table 1).

Discrimination performance for the three models differed when validated on the 2023 set; the endemic model had the highest estimated AUROC at 0.83, with pre-pandemic and pandemic models at 0.81 and 0.74, respectively (table 1). The pre-pandemic model validated on the 2020–2021 dataset had the lowest AUROC at 0.71. Estimated area under the PRC also followed a similar trend within the 2023 validation set, with the endemic model highest at 0.13 followed by the pre-pandemic and pandemic models at 0.10 and 0.06, respectively (online supplemental efigure 2). Area under PRC for the 2020–2021 validation set was also the lowest at 0.05. As is commonly seen with class-imbalanced outcomes (ie, rare outcomes), our findings indicate a PPV-sensitivity trade-off in which PPV decreases as sensitivity increases and vice versa.

The 2020–2021 and 2023 calibration curves overestimated risk across all three ML models to varying degrees. As with AUROC and PRC, calibration was stronger with the endemic model validated on 2023 compared with pre-pandemic and pandemic, while weakest for the pre-pandemic model validated on 2020–2021 data (onlinesupplemental efigure 3 online supplemental efigure 4). Recalibration models such as Platt scaling and isotonic regression were explored, but did not impact model prediction, and were therefore not reported.

In the 2023 validation set threshold analysis (online supplemental etable 3), the pre-pandemic and endemic models were associated with larger LR+at the highest percentiles of predicted risk (top 1–10 percentile of predicted risk). Both pre-pandemic and endemic models had informative LR+>10 at the highest percentile categories of predicted risk while the pandemic model did not (figure 3A).37 This trend also extends to post-test probabilities (PPV), as the pre-pandemic and endemic models have higher PPVs than the pandemic model across all predicted risk categories with the highest predicted risk categories associated with higher post-test probabilities; the pre-pandemic and endemic models provided post-test probabilities as high as 40% (pandemic model ~16%) compared with the pre-test probability (baseline) of 2.8% (figure 3B). Regarding the pre-pandemic model validated on 2020–2021 data, both LR+’s and PPV’s across predicted risk thresholds are the lowest among all estimates, similar to all other metrics we presented (figure 3).

The PSI results (onlinesupplemental appendix A B) indicated no substantial changes in the underlying populations receiving opioids over time. While PSI is a widely used metric to quantify shifts in data distributions, we believe that in our context where most predictors are categorical variables, percentage change in the raw prevalence of key features across periods provide a more clinically interpretable summary of distributional shifts. Therefore, in addition to the PSI analysis, we report percentage changes in feature prevalence across evaluation periods to aid interpretability and to complement the formal PSI results.

Sensitivity analyses examining model performance across demographic subgroups (age, sex and income) (onlinesupplemental efigure 5, online supplemental efigure 6 online supplemental efigure 7undefined) and study periods showed that discrimination (ROC-AUC) remained stable across all groups and time points. In contrast, recall declined markedly during the pandemic across all subgroups, while precision increased during the endemic period - particularly among females and individuals in higher-income quintiles. These findings suggest that while overall model discrimination was consistent, precision and recall fluctuated with changes in healthcare utilisation and prescribing patterns over time.

Discussion

In this study, substantial data drift occurred in ML model performance due to the COVID-19 pandemic in predicting opioid-related adverse outcomes. Comparing ML performance estimates between the 2020–2021 and 2023 validation sets showed that ML prediction performance during the pandemic (2020–2021) was substantially less informative compared with either pre-pandemic or endemic periods. AUROC, area under PRC, calibration and post-test probabilities (PPV) were all estimated lower when measured and validated on the pandemic model and 2020–2021 validation set. Indeed, the pre-pandemic model (trained on 2019 data) reported the weakest prediction performance when validated on 2020–2021 (pandemic) data, despite it being directly before the pandemic period. Further, higher prediction performance on the pre-pandemic and endemic model was observed when measured on the 2023 validation set.

Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that model discrimination, as reflected by AUROC, remained stable across demographic subgroups and pandemic phases, suggesting robust predictive performance. However, precision and recall varied meaningfully across periods. Notably, recall declined sharply during the pandemic, potentially reflecting disruptions in healthcare access and changes in opioid prescribing practices. In the endemic period, overall model performance recovered but revealed new distributional patterns such as improved precision among females and higher-income individuals, indicating potential shifts in population risk profiles and healthcare engagement. These findings highlight how model performance can evolve with broader contextual and behavioural changes, underscoring the importance of temporal validation and subgroup assessment in longitudinal prediction studies.

Some individuals contributed data at multiple longitudinal time points, reflecting the ongoing nature of opioid prescribing and care. This overlap was intentional and represents true longitudinal follow-up rather than data duplication. Each model was developed independently, with mutually exclusive training and validation datasets, ensuring internal validity within each period. Allowing individuals to appear in more than one time point captures the real-world evolution of patient characteristics and outcomes and enables assessment of how model performance and predictor importance change over time. While some correlation across time points may exist, this structure aligns with standard practice in longitudinal prediction modelling and supports understanding of how risk relationships evolve in dynamic clinical settings.

Several reasons may explain the poor ML prediction during pandemic times. Feature importance (SHAP values) was similar between all three models when measured on the 2023 validation set. However, feature importance was different for the pre-pandemic model validated on the 2020–2021 data set with total opioid dose displacing age as the most important variable. This change in feature importance likely reflects the different healthcare utilisation patterns observed during the pandemic. Pandemic times presented a unique environment where public measures influenced social interactions, workplace dynamics and community engagement.43 Healthcare access was impacted by the shift to virtual care, delays in elective procedures and resource reallocation to manage hospital capacity. Subsequently, the impact of the pandemic has been shown to be detrimental to the mental health of Canadians. Indeed, in Alberta there was a notable increase in excess deaths during the pandemic, particularly due to substance misuse.44 Although our model captured only prescribed opioid dispensations, unmeasured use (ie, illicit) and other unmeasured social factors may have contributed to the poor ML prediction during the pandemic. The administrative data sets we used to develop our ML models do not capture these factors. The fact that the pre-pandemic model also performed better than the pandemic model (on the 2023 validation set) also points to the influence of a rapidly changing healthcare and social environment during the pandemic affecting utilisation patterns, feature distributions and ML model performance.

Despite the substantial volume of published studies describing ML health outcome predictions, we are unaware of any other work exploring ML model data drift during the pandemic for predicting the same outcomes over time, let alone opioid-related ones. Monitoring model drift in ML studies is essential to ensure accuracy and reliability. Significant disruptions, such as pandemics or economic crises, rapidly alter data distributions, and shifts in behaviours, policies and demographics can introduce bias, degrade performance and lead to poor decision-making. Indeed, the 3 ML models using distinct data periods around a major world event showed that substantial drift occurred within the data during the pandemic. Without adaptation, models may fail to reflect new societal trends. Although the ML approach allows for quick retraining to accommodate emerging data and different locales as one way to address data drift, our findings indicate that data from the pandemic period to predict opioid-related outcomes is uninformative even with model retraining. Indeed, pandemic data was out of the ordinary, contributing to substantial drift of measured and unmeasured factors.

Although our study used comprehensive, population-based administrative health data, our study is subject to potential unmeasured confounding. Administrative data capture a wide range of clinical encounters, diagnoses and dispensed medications; however, certain important variables are not available. These include measures such as pain intensity, functional status, patient or physician attitudes toward opioid use and some social determinants of health (eg, education, employment, housing stability). While income quintile was included as a proxy for socioeconomic status, residual confounding from unmeasured clinical and contextual factors likely remains. Such factors may influence both opioid prescribing and subsequent outcomes, and their absence should be considered when interpreting model performance and feature importance. Future studies that integrate administrative, clinical, and patient-reported data could help better account for these unmeasured but potentially influential variables.

Additional methodological limitations also need to be acknowledged in this study. First, there may have been an extreme class imbalance and minority overfitting. Administrative health datasets often exhibit extreme class imbalances as adverse opioid outcomes are relatively rare events that occur in comparison to the general population. While the LGB model includes hyperparameters to mitigate these imbalances, it may still overfit the majority class. Second, there may have been limitations related to concept drift and calibration decay. Indeed, as healthcare policies shift, some risk factors may be reassigned as standard care practice, which may confuse the model’s pre-pandemic logic. While LGB can detect shifts in data distribution, it can also lack the causal reasoning to account for external structural breaks. Consequently, the model may suffer from calibration decay, where predicted risk probabilities may not align with actual clinical reality.

While hindsight allowed us to evaluate model performance during the pandemic, a much larger question remains—how should data from disruptive societal events be utilised and integrated into ML models? Although retraining helps maintain model relevance, ensuring that predictions remain informative and aligned with real-world changes is essential. These analyses clearly show that health regulators and clinicians interested in ML assistance to predict negative opioid-related outcomes should approach ML prediction with caution if based on data during the pandemic. In fact, previous period data may be preferable to data collected during periods of disruption. This remains an underexplored area in ML research that requires urgent attention. For real-world implementation, ongoing monitoring for population and model drift is essential to ensure sustained model validity and clinical utility. Drift can arise as patient characteristics, clinical practices, or external factors (eg, healthcare disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic) evolve over time. Implementers should establish continuous performance surveillance pipelines that include periodic recalculation of metrics and monitoring of model discrimination and calibration over time. When meaningful drift is detected, models should undergo revalidation and, if necessary, retraining using updated data to maintain accuracy and fairness. Equally important is transparent documentation of these monitoring processes and thresholds for action. Embedding such governance and maintenance frameworks within health systems helps prevent model degradation, supports clinical trust, and aligns predictive models with evolving real-world populations and care contexts.
