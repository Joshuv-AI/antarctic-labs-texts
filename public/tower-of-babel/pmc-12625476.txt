Interactions between brucellosis and environmental factors: spatiotemporal epidemiology from Xinjiang, China
Authors: Chengjing Wei, Liping Zhang, Bo Shang, Xiaodong Wang, Jiangshan Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrucella is a highly adaptive pathogen, capable of withstanding environmental stress, adapting to multiple hosts, and transmitting through animal-environment interactions.This study investigates the potential impact of environmental factors on the incidence of brucellosis, aiming to identify spatiotemporal patterns between transmission sources and the environment.MethodsWe performed a comprehensive spatiotemporal analysis of brucellosis incidence in Xinjiang (2015–2023) by integrating satellite-derived environmental data, including temperature, vapor pressure deficit (VPD), NDVI, precipitation, and grazing intensity. Our study examined seasonal and regional transmission patterns, assessed lagged cumulative effects of environmental exposures, and and employed ridge regression modeling to address multicollinearity and quantify the potential contributions of these factors to disease incidence.ResultsNorthern Xinjiang (Yili, Changji, Tacheng) and Southern Xinjiang (Kashgar, Aksu) are the main grazing areas for cattle and sheep, accounting for 57.61% of brucellosis cases. Seasonal peaks occur in the second and third quarters (May-August). (2) The potential impact of environmental factors on brucellosis (M ± SD) was as follows: rainfall (0.42 ± 0.91 months lag, 1.95 ± 1.02 months accumulation), temperature (2.47 ± 0.95, 1.51 ± 0.92 ), VDP (0.52 ± 0.96, 1.52 ± 0.86 ), and NDVI (1.04 ± 1.35, 1.15 ± 0.59 ). (3) The incidence of brucellosis showed significant spatial and seasonal heterogeneity (P < 0.05), which may be related to the different local environments. In particular, regions with extreme climate conditions, such as large diurnal temperature fluctuations, heavy rainfall, and risks of sandstorms.ConclusionEnvironmental factors exhibit short-term lag effects, cumulative effects, and periodicity. Moderate climate cycles in spring and winter enhance immunity, while extreme climate cycles in summer and autumn increase physiological stress, thereby elevating the risk of disease transmission.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25415-z.

Introduction

Brucellosis is a globally distributed zoonotic disease caused by Brucella bacteria, with high prevalence in regions such as the Mediterranean Basin, the Middle East, Central Asia, Africa, Asia, and Latin America [1, 2]. Approximately 500,000 cases are reported globally each year [3]Brucella is a highly adaptive pathogen, capable of withstanding environmental stress and adapting to multiple hosts. It is primarily transmitted through direct contact with infected animals or their secretions, ingestion, or inhalation [4]. Brucellosis imposes significant health and economic burdens, especially in regions with low economic development, inadequate public health infrastructure, and limited animal disease control measures [5]. In traditional animal husbandry systems, extensive free-range breeding, low vaccination coverage, and inadequate health management further increase disease transmission risks [6, 7]. Therefore, it is necessary to provide new perspectives to explore more information about the disease.

Brucellosis may be influenced by the interaction of various ecological factors [8, 9].Xinjiang is a major livestock base in China and exhibits a relatively high incidence of brucellosis [10]. The region experiences large diurnal temperature variations, sparse vegetation, frequent dust storms, and adequate precipitation in grassland areas. Humidity distribution varies across different regions [11, 12]. The climatic conditions in this region create a conducive environment for the formation of bioaerosols [13]. Concerning the temporal relationship between climate factors and brucellosis, several studies have employed distributed lag models to assess the temporal effects of temperature, humidity, wind speed, and precipitation on the incidence of brucellosis [14]. In terms of the spatial relationship between human activities, the environment, and brucellosis, some studies have employed traditional geographically weighted regression models to analyze the spatial effects of livestock slaughter, pastoral activities, and the distribution of health resources on brucellosis [15]. The former studies typically analyze the relationship from individual temporal and spatial dimensions, demonstrating the feasibility of using geographic methods to study the link between the environment and diseases. However, the approach of relying on a single dimension or merely adding temporal and spatial analyses still presents inherent limitations [16].

Research in the field of remote sensing offers significant advantages in these areas. Satellite remote sensing provides spatially continuous and temporally consistent observations of surface characteristics, capturing the variability of key environmental determinants [17]. Leveraging the advantages of remote sensing, some studies have used atmospheric trajectory clustering models to simulate the impact of bioaerosols and their trajectories on tuberculosis incidence [18]. Other research has employed machine learning and SHAP interpretable features to conduct spatial analysis and feature mining of migraine and its influencing factors in the GBD database [19]. The time-lag accumulation effect method not only analyzes current spatiotemporal relationships but also investigates the accumulation and lag of climate or environmental factors in the past and future [20]. Additionally, spatial ridge regression can eliminate spatial multicollinearity among factors, providing insight into the shift from unidimensional to multidimensional research on brucellosis. Therefore, with advanced remote sensing technologies, it is possible to enhance the integration of temporal and spatial features in studying brucellosis.

We aim to explore the interactions between environmental factors and brucellosis in Xinjiang from 2015 to 2023 through an in-depth analysis of detailed and rich monthly-level geographic remote sensing data on brucellosis, as well as indicators including temperature, vapor pressure deficit, vegetation cover, precipitation, and grazing intensity. This study seeks to further reveal the seasonal and regional transmission patterns of brucellosis, assess the lagged accumulation effects of environmental exposures, and quantify the potential impact of these factors on disease incidence using ridge regression models. This study hopes to provide some value to the field of brucellosis research.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Environmental data

The map data of the Xinjiang region (province/municipality) are sourced from the standard map service system of China Map Publishing House (http://bzdt.ch.mnr.gov.cn/).

This study integrates multi-source meteorological and grazing intensity data, with all datasets undergoing standardized preprocessing to ensure spatiotemporal consistency. Meteorological data were obtained from authoritative datasets published by the National Qinghai-Tibet Plateau Science Data Center. Key meteorological elements such as temperature and precipitation utilize the 1-kilometer resolution meteorological dataset for China (1980—2024) developed by scholar Peng [21] (https://data.tpdc.ac.cn/zh-hans/data/71ab4677-b66c-4fd1-a004-b2a541c4d5bf). This dataset combines ground observations with remote sensing inversion techniques, maintaining spatial error within 15%. The meteorological data underwent cross-validation using ERA5 reanalysis products, demonstrating root mean square temperature errors below 1.2℃ and precipitation errors under 18 mm/month.

The atmospheric humidity index uses Zhang Hui [22] 1-kilometer resolution product, generated through fusion of MODIS surface temperature and ERA5 reanalysis data, which has demonstrated stable accuracy across 73 monitoring stations (R²>0.85).

Grazing intensity data are derived from the “China Grassland Long-term High-resolution Grazing Intensity Data Set” (LHGI) available on Figshare [23], covering 58 pastoral counties across seven major pastoral regions in China from 1980 to 2024.The grazing data was validated by 73 ground stations (pixel scaleR2 = 0.78) and cross-verified with provincial statistical yearbooks (1980—2022, average R²=0.73). The study acquired data only from 2015 to 2023.

The month-to-month NDVI is calculated as the monthly average NDVI. According to the monthly data specifications provided in https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/, the MOD13A3 (monthly 1-kilometer) VI product utilizes the MOD13A2 (16-day 1-kilometer) VI product through a time synthesis algorithm employing a weighted averaging method [24]. Specifically, the monthly NDVI data represents the weighted average of NDVI measurements collected over 16-day periods covering the calendar month.

All data were uniformly resampled to a 1-kilometer grid (Albers projection) and spatially processed using geographic algebra to accurately match environmental factors and grazing intensity data with the regional characteristics of Xinjiang. This process excluded outliers exceeding three standard deviations and interpolated to repair missing data.The data sources and information are shown in Table 1.

Brucellosis incidence data

From January 2015 to December 2023, monthly reported cases and incidence data of human brucellosis from 17 prefectures (including municipalities) in the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region were obtained from the Center for Disease Control and Prevention of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region. The clinical trial number is not applicable. Ethics declaration: This study has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and requirements. It does not involve the use of personal privacy or sensitive data (Approval number: 2024-23).

Analysis methods

The monthly averages of the all variables from 2015 to 2023 were extracted in Xinjiang’s area. The maps are all in GCS_WGS_1984 coordinate system, 13 cm long and 11 cm wide, with a spatial resolution of 1 km. An inverse distance weighted (IDW) spatial derivation model was built based on the monthly averages extracted from January and December to compare the spatial and temporal distribution [25, 26]. The model was applied using ArcGIS 10.8.2 software depending on the number of points entered, using only the geographical area covered by Xinjiang’s area as a specific range for the derivation.(see Supplementary file 1).

The Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test were first used to assess the normality and homogeneity of variance of the data. If the assumptions of normality (P < 0.05) and homogeneity of variance (P < 0.05) were not met, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied to analyze regional and seasonal differences. If the data met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test the incidence differences across regions and seasons, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

The time trend is assessed using the linear slope: a positive value indicates an upward trend, a negative value indicates a downward trend, and the absolute value reflects the rate of change. To verify the statistical significance of the trend, we applied the non-parametric Mann-Kendall (MK) test and conducted further analysis at the 90%, 95%, and 99% confidence levels.(see Supplementary file 2).

This study analyzes the effects of lagged and accumulated environmental variables on brucellosis, with lag and accumulation periods ranging from 0 to 3 months, and their sum not exceeding 4 months. Spatial alignment is performed through projection transformation, and linear interpolation addresses inconsistencies in time scales across multiple data sources to ensure data consistency and accuracy [27]. Lagged data generation is achieved by setting the current month and lag period, generating the corresponding lagged data for each month to represent the relationship between the current month and environmental variables from previous months [28]. Data for different lag periods are calculated based on these relationships for further analysis. The cumulative effect is quantified using the moving window averaging method, reflecting the sustained impact of historical environmental variables on current disease transmission. The core principle is that current environmental conditions are influenced not only by immediate factors but also by the cumulative effects of historical environmental variables. (see Supplementary file 3)

This study uses ridge regression to quantitatively analyze the relationship between brucellosis and climatic factors. First, the datasets are standardized to allow variables with different units to be computed together. Then, the standardized climatic factors (such as precipitation period, vegetation index, grazing intensity, etc.) and biological factors are input into the ridge regression model, with the variance inflation factor (VIF) calculated to address multicollinearity. To optimize the ridge regression parameters, grid search is used to minimize the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). Finally, the relative and absolute contributions of each factor to the incidence are quantified through regression coefficients and standardized trends. Model performance is evaluated using the coefficient of determination, R², to measure the model’s fit [29].(see Supplementary file 4).

Study area

Overview of brucellosis incidence

A total of 54,325 cases of human brucellosis were reported in Xinjiang between 2015 and 2023. Figure 2 shows that brucellosis cases in Xinjiang are primarily concentrated in five regions―Yili, Changji, Tacheng, Aksu, and Kashgar―which account for 57.61% of the reported cases.

After the division of North, South, and East Xinjiang, the regional comparison showed an H value of 58.51 (P < 0.001), indicating significant differences in incidence rates across regions. Seasonal comparisons yielded the following results: spring H = 14.16 (P = 0.0027), summer H = 24.44 (P = 2.02 × 10⁻⁵), autumn H = 34.44 (P = 2.48 × 10⁻⁶), and winter H = 39.86 (P = 6.52 × 10⁻⁷). Both regional and seasonal variations in brucellosis incidence were statistically significant(P < 0.01).

Spatiotemporal distribution of latent factors

As shown in Fig. 6a, precipitation in northern Xinjiang is significantly higher than in the south, with annual rainfall concentrated in specific areas such as the Ili River Valley and the northern Tianshan foothills. In contrast, the south experiences lower annual rainfall, mainly concentrated in the northern foothills of the Kunlun Mountains and the southwestern edge of the Tarim Basin (see Supplementary File 5). Figure 6b shows that the highest vegetation coverage is in northern Xinjiang, followed by the east, while the south has relatively sparse vegetation, mainly found in oases and riverbanks (see Supplementary File 6). These patterns are primarily influenced by terrain, latitude, and climate. As shown in Fig. 6d and e, due to the gorges in the central Tianshan Mountains and climatic factors, eastern Xinjiang, including the Turpan Basin, is the driest (~ 35 hPa) and hottest (~ 25℃). The southern region, influenced by the Taklamakan Desert and topography, has higher temperatures (~ 20℃) and high VDP values (7–20 hPa). In contrast, northern Xinjiang, influenced by higher latitudes, the westerlies, and the Tianshan Mountains, has lower temperatures (3–10℃) and moderate VDP values (~ 15 hPa). Average annual grazing intensity (see Supplementary File 7), temperature (see Supplementary File 8), and potential evapotranspiration (VDP) (see Supplementary File 9) further characterize the region. Northern Xinjiang, with abundant rainfall and favorable ecological conditions, is particularly suited for pastoralism. As shown in Fig. 6c, the region maintains an intensive grazing system, exhibiting a clear integration of agriculture and animal husbandry.

Discussion

The long-term incidence of brucellosis showed an upward trend from 2015 to 2020, with a distinct seasonal peak, where cases from May to August accounted for over half of the total, consistent with previous studies [30]. Moreover, the region experiences extreme diurnal temperature variations with hot days and cool nights in summer and autumn, while spring and winter are characterized by smaller diurnal temperature differences and cooler conditions. Harsh weather conditions in summer and autumn may affect the immune response or stress levels in both humans and animals [31]. The trend in brucellosis incidence under extreme climatic conditions aligns with previous studies. Our research highlights that Altai and Ili in northern Xinjiang are affected by harsh environmental factors, particularly the significant diurnal temperature variation in grazing areas [32]. Spatial analysis shows that precipitation in Xinjiang is more abundant in the north and scarcer in the south [33]. Vegetation coverage follows the trend of precipitation, with a smaller extent in southern Xinjiang, consistent with previous studies [34]. In eastern regions like Hami and Turpan, as well as the southern Xinjiang desert, severe drought and high temperatures make livestock farming reliant on irrigation, potentially increasing human-animal contact and facilitating the spread of brucellosis, especially during peak agricultural and pastoral periods [35, 36].

In this study, the temperature and humidity indicators exhibit a high degree of consistency with previous findings [37]. Previous studies have established a significant association between high temperatures (lagged 2.5 months) and brucellosis, while our results indicate a lag effect of 2.47 ± 0.95 months and a cumulative effect of 1.51 ± 0.92 months. These findings are largely consistent with prior research, further corroborating the role of temperature as a key environmental factor. The drying degree of VDP aligns directionally with the humidity results from earlier studies; however, our research reveals higher sensitivity, which may be attributed to the differing emphases of VDP and humidity as environmental indicators.Compared to previous studies modeling the relationship between NDVI and brucellosis [38], our work improves upon earlier limitations by addressing collinearity and precipitation factors while exploring their lag and cumulative periods. Although no relevant literature has yet validated the effectiveness of NDVI (lagged 1.04 ± 1.35 months and cumulative 1.15 ± 0.59 months) and precipitation (lagged 0.42 ± 0.91 months and cumulative 1.95 ± 1.02 months) in other regions, their role as significant influencing factors has been confirmed [39, 40].

The main transmission sources are sheep, cattle, and pigs, but due to Xinjiang’s diverse ethnic composition and dietary habits limiting pork consumption, pigs are less prevalent, with cattle and sheep farming being dominant [41]. Notably, the preventive measures and public awareness of brucellosis in some areas of Xinjiang remain low [42], particularly in pastoral and rural areas [43]. In our study, the number of cases in the Hotan and Kashgar regions increased approximately tenfold from 2015 to 2023.With the rapid growth of animal husbandry and increased human-animal interactions, areas with low current incidence but large cattle and sheep populations, such as Hotan, may become new high-incidence zones in the future [44].

Conclusion

This study quantifies the lag and cumulative effects of environmental factors on brucellosis using geospatial modeling and verifies the effectiveness of these factors through ridge regression. Recommendations for herders are provided from environmental and immunological perspectives. Environmental factors(precipitation, temperature, vapor pressure deficit, and vegetation cover) exhibit short-term lag effects, cumulative effects, and periodicity. During the spring and autumn seasons, mild climates help enhance immunity, while extreme weather conditions in the summer and autumn exacerbate livestock physiological stress, thereby increasing the risk of disease transmission. Therefore, it is recommended to control grazing intensity appropriately during the summer and autumn months to reduce livestock stress, while providing sufficient water and shade to optimize animal management. Furthermore, herders should closely monitor environmental changes, especially during seasons with significant climatic fluctuations, and take proactive preventive measures, adjusting grazing and livestock management strategies as needed to reduce the risk of brucellosis transmission.

Strengths and limitations

Although exploring the potential lag and cumulative relationship between the environment and brucellosis through spatial and temporal scales, validated by previous studies, offers a more comprehensive approach than unidimensional analysis, it still has certain limitations. First, the disease data were interpolated using the inverse distance weighting (IDW) method, which may introduce bias in areas with sparse data, potentially leading to inaccurate estimates of brucellosis incidence. Second, the large-scale analysis may not fully reflect individual-level data, which could lead to ecological fallacy. Additionally, our study did not account for dynamic human behaviors, socioeconomic factors, or animal migration, all of which can influence disease transmission. The research data on brucellosis can only be accurate to the month at most, which is the highest level of detail we have in our investigation. If there were more precise daily data, the prediction accuracy of the model would be higher, and the lag accumulation effect would also be more precise. This requires further research in the future.The grazing intensity data is only available at the annual scale. If monthly data were available, a more detailed analysis of cumulative lag effects could be conducted.
