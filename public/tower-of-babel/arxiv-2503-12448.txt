Introduction to inverse problems for non-linear partial differential equations


Abstract
Résumé We consider inverse problems for non-linear hyperbolic and elliptic equations and give an introduction to the method based on the multiple linearization, or on the construction of artificial sources, to solve these problems. The method is based on self-interaction of linearized waves or other solutions in the presence of non-linearities. Multiple linearization has successfully been used to solve inverse problems for non-linear equation which are still unsolved for the corresponding linear equations.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we consider inverse problems for non-linear elliptic and hyperbolic partial differential equation to demonstrate the
multiple linearization
method. In this method the nonlinearity helps one to solve inverse problems for non-linear equation
which are still unsolved for the corresponding linear equations. Our aim is give a review on recent progress on these inverse problems
and discuss the main ideas of the proofs in simple cases.

In the case where the underlying equation is linear, a standard example is the inverse problem of Calderon
[
14
]
. This problem arises in Electrical Impedance Tomography (EIT), a method proposed for medical and industrial imaging, where the objective is to determine the electrical conductivity or impedance in a medium by making voltage to current measurements on its boundary.
Another example of Calderon’s inverse problem is the determination of an unknown potential
q(x)
in a Schrödinger operator
\Delta+q(x)
from boundary measurements, first solved in
[
62
]
in dimensions
n\geq 3
.

Let us next consider analogous inverse problems under nonlinear settings. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with
C^{\infty}
-smooth boundary, and consider the equation

\Delta u+a(x,u)=0\text{ in $\Omega$}.

The Dirichlet problem for this equation is related to maintaining a temperature (or concentration or population)
f
on the boundary. The boundary measurements for such an equation, provided that it is well-posed for some class of boundary values, may be encoded by a Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{a}
, which maps the boundary value
f
to the flux (i.e., the normal derivative)
\Lambda_{a}(f)=\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega}
of the corresponding equilibrium state across the boundary.

Since 90’s, inverse problems for nonlinear elliptic equations have been widely studied. A standard method, introduced in
[
28
]
in the parabolic case, is to show that the first linearization of the nonlinear Dirichlet-to-Neumann map is actually the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map of a linear equation, and to use the theory of inverse problems for linear equations. For the semilinear Schrödinger equation
\Delta u+a(x,u)=0
, the problem of recovering the potential
a(x,u)
was studied in
[
60
]
in dimensions
n\geq 3
, and in
[
29
,
27
]
in dimension
n=2
. In addition, inverse problems have been studied for quasilinear elliptic equations
[
61
,
30
,
57
]
, the degenerate elliptic
p
-Laplace equation
[
58
,
13
]
, and the fractional semilinear Schrödinger equation
[
44
]
. Certain Calderón type inverse problems for quasilinear equations on Riemannian manifolds are considered in
[
50
]
, see also referenceis in
[
1
,
64
]
.

Let us introduce the mathematical setting to study canonical examples of non-linear elliptic equations. We will denote by
(M,g)
a compact Riemannian manifold with
C^{\infty}
boundary
\partial M
, where
\hbox{dim}\,(M)=n
,
n\geq 2
. Let
q\in C^{\infty}(M)
. We will consider semilinear elliptic equations of the form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta_{g}u+qu^{m}=0&\text{ in }M,\\
u=f&\text{ on }\partial M,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where

m\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ and }m\geq 2.

Here
\Delta_{g}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator, given in local coordinates by

\Delta_{g}u=\frac{1}{\hbox{det}(g)^{1/2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\left(\hbox{det}(g)^{1/2}g^{jk}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x^{k}}\right),

where
g=(g_{jk}(x))
and

. As usual, we denote the Euclidean Laplace operator,
corresponding to the Euclidean metric

in

The Dirichlet problem for (
1.1
) with a boundary condition

u|_{\partial\Omega}=f
has a unique small solution

for sufficiently small boundary data

, where
s>2
with

. More precisely this means that there is
\delta>0
such that whenever
\lVert f\rVert_{C^{s}(\partial M)}\leq\delta
, there is a unique solution

to (
1.1
) satisfying
\|u\|_{C^{s}(M)}\leq c\delta
. We will call

the unique small solution and denote it by
u_{f}
to indicate the boundary value. Here

is the standard Hölder space for
s>2
with
s\notin{\mathbb{N}}
(often written as
C^{k,\alpha}
if
s=k+\alpha
where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
0<\alpha<1
). Hence, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map is defined by using the unique small solution in a following way :

\displaystyle\Lambda_{M,g,q}:\{f\in C^{s}(\partial M)\mid\lVert f\rVert_{C^{s}(\partial M)}\leq\delta\}\to C^{s-1}(\partial M),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{M,g,q}:f\mapsto\partial_{\nu}u_{f}|_{\partial M},

where
\partial_{\nu}
denotes the normal derivative on the boundary
\partial M
. In what follows, we denote the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
by
\Lambda_{M,g}
to
when
q=0
. When
M=\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and

is the identity matrix, we denote the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map by
\Lambda_{q}
.

Next, we consider the case when

for

Theorem 1.1

(Reconstruction of non-linear term

Let

be a bounded domain with

for the equation

satisfy

\lVert f\rVert_{C^{s}(\partial M)}<\delta
, where

in

.

In the pioneering studies of the problem, the first and the second order linearizations of the nonlinear Dirichlet-to-Neumann map has been used in the works

,

]

Next, we present the outline of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
For the equation (
1.1
) with quadratic nonlinearity, that is,
m=2
, the first linearization of the nonlinear Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{q}
, linearized at the zero boundary value, is just the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for the standard Laplace equation :

D|_{f=0}\Lambda_{q}(f):C^{s}(\partial\Omega)\to C^{s-1}(\partial\Omega),\ \ f\mapsto\partial_{\nu}v_{f}|_{\partial\Omega},

where
s>1
is not an integer,
v_{f}
is the unique solution of
\Delta v_{f}=0
in
\Omega
with
v_{f}|_{\partial\Omega}=f
. Thus the first linearization does not carry any information about the unknown potential
q
. For a quadratic nonlinearity the
second linearization
of the map
f\to\Lambda_{q}(f)
, denoted by

D^{2}|_{f=0}\Lambda_{q}(f)[\cdot,\cdot]
, is a symmetric bilinear form from
C^{s}(\partial\Omega)\times C^{s}(\partial\Omega)
to
C^{s-1}(\partial\Omega)
.
The second order linearization is given by

\displaystyle D^{2}|_{f=0}\Lambda_{q}(f)[f_{1},f_{2}]=\partial_{{\varepsilon}_{1}}\partial_{{\varepsilon}_{2}}u_{{\varepsilon}_{1}f_{1}+{\varepsilon}_{2}f_{2}}|_{{\varepsilon}_{1}={\varepsilon}_{2}=0}\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega.

(1.2)

That is, one considers boundary data

f={\varepsilon}_{1}f_{1}+{\varepsilon}_{2}f_{2}\in C^{s}(\partial\Omega),

where
{\varepsilon}_{1},{\varepsilon}_{2}
are sufficiently small parameters, and takes the mixed derivative

w(x)=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varepsilon}_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial{\varepsilon}_{2}}\right|_{{\varepsilon}_{1}={\varepsilon}_{2}=0}u_{{\varepsilon}_{1}f_{1}+{\varepsilon}_{2}f_{2}}(x)

of the solutions of
u_{{\varepsilon}_{1}f_{1}+{\varepsilon}_{2}f_{2}}(x)
of the equation (
1.1
) with the coefficient
q
,
m=2
,
and the boundary value
f={\varepsilon}_{1}f_{1}+{\varepsilon}_{2}f_{2}
.
This yields the equation

\Delta w=-2qv_{f_{1}}v_{f_{2}},

(1.3)

where

v_{f_{k}}
are harmonic functions, i.e. the solutions to the linearized equation
\Delta v=0

with the Dirichlet boundary value
v_{f_{2}}|_{\partial\Omega}=f_{k}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle D^{2}|_{f=0}\Lambda_{q}(f)[f_{1},f_{2}]=\partial_{\nu}w|_{\partial\Omega}.

(1.4)

Assume next that there are two potential functions
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
such that

\Lambda_{q_{1}}(f)=\Lambda_{q_{2}}(f),\quad\hbox{for all }f\in C^{s}(\partial M),\ \ \lVert f\rVert_{C^{s}(\partial M)}\leq\delta.

Taking the mixed 2nd order derivative of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps yields

\partial_{\nu}w_{1}=\partial_{\nu}w_{2}
on
\partial\Omega
,
where
\Delta w_{j}=-2q_{j}v_{f_{1}}v_{f_{2}},
and
w_{j}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

Subtracting the equations (
1.3
) for
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, and integrating the resulting equation against the harmonic function
v_{f_{3}}
and using integration by parts yields the formula

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(q_{1}-q_{2})v_{f_{1}}v_{f_{2}}v_{f_{3}}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\Delta w_{1}-\Delta w_{2})v_{f_{3}}\,dx

(1.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\partial_{\nu}w_{1}-\partial_{\nu}w_{2})v_{f_{3}}\,dS(x)=0.

To apply this formula, let us denote
Q=q_{1}-q_{2}
. When
k\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, there are

\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
satisfying

\displaystyle\xi_{1}\,\cdotp\xi_{1}=0,\quad\xi_{2}\,\cdotp\xi_{2}=0,\quad\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}=ik,

where
\eta\,\cdotp\zeta=\eta_{1}\zeta_{1}+\eta_{2}\zeta_{2}+\eta_{3}\zeta_{3}

for
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3}),\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\zeta_{3})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}.

We define

\displaystyle v_{1}(x)=e^{\xi_{1}\cdotp\,x},\quad v_{2}(x)=e^{\xi_{2}\cdotp\,x},\quad v_{3}(x)=1.

These functions satisfy
\Delta v_{j}=0
and we can use formula (
1.5
)
with
{f_{j}}=v_{j}|_{\partial\Omega}
, that is,
v_{f_{j}}=f_{j}
.
The Fourier transform
\widehat{Q}(k)
of
Q(x)
(that is extended as a zero function in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\Omega)

satisfies

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\widehat{Q}(k)=\int_{\Omega}Q(x)e^{ik\cdotp\,x}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}Q(x)e^{\xi_{1}\cdotp\,x}\cdot e^{\xi_{2}\cdotp\,x}\cdot 1\,dx=\int_{\Omega}Qv_{1}v_{2}v_{3}\,dx=0.

Hence
Q=0
, that is,
q_{1}=q_{2}
. These arguments (with some details) prove Thm.
1.1
.

2
Inverse problems for waves

Let us consider a linear wave equation

\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)-c(x)^{2}\Delta u(t,x)=0\quad\hbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}\times\Omega

where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
c(x)
is the wave speed. This equation models e.g. acoustic waves in isotropic medium.
In inverse problems one has access to measurements of waves (the solutions
u(t,x)
) on the boundary, or in a subset of the domain
\Omega
, and one aims to determine unknown coefficients (e.g.,
c(x)
) in the interior of the domain.

We will consider
anisotropic
materials, where the wave speed depends on the direction of propagation. This means that the scalar wave speed
c(x)
, where
x=(x^{1},x^{2},\dots,x^{n})\in\Omega
, is replaced by a positive definite symmetric matrix
(g^{jk}(x))_{j,k=1}^{n}
. Anisotropic materials appear frequently in applications such as in seismic imaging, where one wishes to determine the interior structure of
the Earth by making various measurements of waves on its surface.

To model waves in anisotropic medium mathematically,
let
(N,g)
be an
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold with boundary and consider the wave equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)-\Delta_{g}u(t,x)=0\quad\text{in }(0,\infty)\times N,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}u|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\partial N}=f,\quad u|_{t=0}=0,\quad\partial_{t}u|_{t=0}=0,

where
\Delta_{g}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator corresponding
to a smooth time-independent Riemannian metric
g
on
N
.
We denote the unit speed geodesic of
(N,g)
emanating from a point
(y,\eta)\in SN
by
\alpha_{y,\eta}(t)=\exp_{p}(t\eta)
.

The solution of (
2.1
), corresponding to the boundary value
f
(which is interpreted as a boundary source), is denoted by

u^{f}=u^{f}(t,x)
.

Let us assume that the boundary
\partial N
is known.
The inverse problem is to reconstruct the manifold
N
and the metric
g
when we are given
the boundary
\partial N
, as a manifold, and
the
response operator

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N,g}:f\mapsto u^{f}|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\partial N},

(2.2)

which is also called the
Neumann-to-Dirichlet map
. Physically,

\Lambda_{N,g}f
describes the measurement of the medium
response to any applied boundary source
f
.

It is convenient to interpret the anisotropic wave speed
(g^{jk}(x))
also in a domain of the Euclidean space as the inverse of a Riemannian metric, thus modelling the medium as a
Riemannian manifold
.
This is due to fact that if
\Psi:N\to N
is a diffeomorphism such that
\Psi|_{\partial N}=Id
then all boundary measurements for the metric
g
and the pull-back metric
\Psi^{*}g

coincide. Thus to prove uniqueness results for inverse problems, one has to consider properties that are invariant in diffeomorphisms and try to reconstruct those uniquely, for example, to show that an underlying manifold structure can be uniquely determined. In practice, the inverse problem in a subset of the Euclidean space is solved in two steps. The first is to reconstruct the underlying manifold structure. The second step is to find an embedding of the constructed manifold in the Euclidean space using additional a priori information, see
[
31
]
. Below, we will concentrate on the first step.

In 1990s, the combination of the boundary control method developed by Belishev
[
6
]
and generalized for Riemannian manifolds in
[
7
]
and Tataru’s unique continuation theorem
[
63
]
gave a solution to the inverse problem of determining the isometry type of a Riemannian manifold

(N,g)
with given
boundary
\partial N
and the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{N,g}
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
7
,
63

)

Let
(N_{1},g_{1})
and
(N_{2},g_{2})
be compact Riemannian manifolds with boundary.
Assume that there is a diffeomorphism
\Phi:\partial N_{1}\to\partial N_{2}

such that

\Phi^{\ast}(\Lambda_{N_{1},g_{1}}f)=\Lambda_{N_{2},g_{2}}(\Phi^{\ast}f),\quad\hbox{for all }f\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times\partial N_{1}).

(2.3)

Then
(N_{1},g_{1})
and
(N_{2},g_{2})
are isometric Riemannian manifolds.

Above,
\Phi^{*}f(t,x)=f(t,\Phi(x))
is the pull-back of

in

, or more precisely, that of

.

2.1
Inverse problems with passive measurements

2.1.1

Let us consider a body in which there spontaneously appear point sources that create propagating waves.
In this subsection we assume that the velocity of the waves is independent of time and depends only the the spatial

We consider the inverse problem where we detect such waves either outside or at the boundary of the body and aim to determine the unknown wave speed inside the body.

3
Ideas for proofs and reconstruction methods

3.0.1
Light-observation sets and the inverse problem for passive observations

In this subsection, we outline the proof of Theorem
2.7
. Below, let
\overline{W}=\hbox{cl}\,(W)
be the closure of
W
in
M
,
such that
\overline{W}\subset J^{-}(p^{+})\setminus I^{-}(p^{-})
.
The set
\overline{A}\times\overline{W}
is compact and
the map
{\mathcal{F}}:\overline{W}\to C(\overline{A})
,
{\mathcal{F}}(q)=F_{q}
, is continuous.

Next we will show that the map

{\mathcal{F}}:\overline{W}\to{\mathcal{F}}(\overline{W})
,
{\mathcal{F}}(q)=F_{q}
is injective.
To prove this, we assume the opposite : Assume that there are
q_{1},q_{2}\in W
that satisfy
F_{q_{1}}=F_{q_{2}}
.
Then,

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{{U}}(q_{1})={\cal E}_{{U}}(q_{2}).

(3.1)

This implies that all light-like geodesics from
q_{1}
to
{{U}}
go through
q_{2}
and hence,
q_{1}
is in the past of
q_{2}
, or
vice versa. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
q_{1}
is in the past of
q_{2}
. Let
a_{1}\in{A}

and
p_{1}=\mu_{a_{1}}(f^{+}_{q_{1}}(a_{1}))\in\mu_{a_{1}}([-1,1])
.
Let now
\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{1}}([0,\ell_{1}])
be a light-like geodesics from
q_{1}
to
p_{1}
, that is,
p_{1}=\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{1}}(\ell_{1})\in\mu_{a_{1}}([-1,1])
.
Next, let
a_{2}\in{A}
be such that

\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{1}}\cap\mu_{a_{2}}([-1,1])=\emptyset
. Let

p_{2}=\mu_{a_{2}}(f^{+}_{q_{1}}(a_{2}))\in\mu_{a_{2}}([-1,1])

and
\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{2}}([0,\ell_{2}])
be a light-like geodesics from
q_{1}
to
p_{2}
, that is,
p_{2}=\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{2}}(\ell_{2})\in\mu_{a_{2}}([-1,1])
. Then, as
\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{1}}\cap\mu_{a_{2}}([-1,1])=\emptyset
,
\xi_{2}
and
\xi_{1}
are not parallel.
As all light-like geodesics from
q_{1}
to
{{U}}
go through
q_{2}
, we see that there are
t_{1}\in(0,\ell_{1})
and

t_{2}\in(0,\ell_{2})
such that

q_{2}=\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{1}}(t_{1}),\quad q_{2}=\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{2}}(t_{2}).

As the vectors
\xi_{2}
and
\xi_{1}
are not parallel, the geodesics
\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{1}}
and
\gamma_{q_{2},\xi_{2}}
are
not the same paths, and we see that the path
\beta=\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{1}}([0,t_{1}])\cup\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{2}}([t_{2},\ell_{2}])

is a union of two causal paths from the point
q_{1}
to
q_{2}
and then from
q_{2}
to
p_{2}
.
Since the paths the geodesics
\gamma_{q_{1},\xi_{1}}
and
\gamma_{q_{2},\xi_{2}}
are not the same paths with
different parametrizations, the path
\beta
is not a geodesic. As the manifold
(M,g)
is globally
hyperbolic, this implies
that there is a time-like path from
q_{1}
to
p_{2}
and hence it is not possible

p_{2}=\mu_{a_{2}}(f^{+}_{q_{1}}(a_{2})\in\mu_{a_{2}}([-1,1])
, as we assumed above.
This contradiction implies
F_{q_{1}}\not=F_{q_{2}}
.
Thus, the map

{\mathcal{F}}:\overline{W}\to{\mathcal{F}}(\overline{W})
,
{\mathcal{F}}(q)=F_{q}
is injective.

The map
\mathcal{F}:\overline{W}\mapsto C(\overline{{A}})
is continuous and injective.
As
\overline{W}
is compact, these imply that the map
\mathcal{F}:\overline{W}\mapsto C(\overline{{A}})
is a homeomorphism.
Thus,
\mathcal{F}(\overline{W})\subset C(\overline{{A}})
and
\overline{W}
are homeomorphic.
Furthermore, as
W
is an open subset of the manifold
M
, we can see that
\mathcal{F}(W)\subset C(\overline{{A}})
and
W
are homeomorphic,
so we can consider
{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)
as a topological manifold which is a
homeomorphic copy of the original manifold
W
. Thus, we have reconstructed
W
as
a topological manifold.

Left : Picture on proving that
q_{1}=q_{2}
by contradiction. Right : The function
X_{\vec{a}}:q\to(F_{q}({a_{j}})_{j=1}^{n+1}=(f^{+}_{a_{j}}(q))_{j=1}^{n+1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
defines coordinates near a point
q
on
M
.

Next, we consider the reconstruction of
W
as
a differentiable manifold. Below, we will only consider how the
coordinate functions on
{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)
are constructed and omit the proof
that this constructions gives a differentiable structure on
{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)

which makes it diffeomorphic to
W
. For details on this, we cite to
[
42
]
.

For
a\in{A}
, let us define the evaluation function
E_{a}[F]=F(a)
.
For
q_{0}\in W
, let us choose
n+1
parameters
a_{j}\in{A}
,
j=1,2,\dots,n+1
.
When the parameters
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n+1})
are chosen in a generic way
that is, this
({n+1})
-tuple is in an open and dense subset of
{A}^{n+1}
, the function

X_{\vec{a}}(q)=(F_{q}(a_{j}))_{j=1}^{n+1}=(f^{+}_{a_{j}}(q))_{j=1}^{n+1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
defines smooth coordinates on
M
near
q_{0}
(Here, we omit this part of the proof, see
[
42
]
on details).
This implies that for any
{{\mathcal{F}}}(q_{0})\in{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)
and generic parameters
a_{j}\in{A}
,
j=1,2,\dots,{n+1}

there is a neighborhood

\mathcal{V}\subset{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)
such the function
{\bf X}_{\vec{a}}:F\to(E_{a_{j}}[F])_{j=1}^{n+1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
defines coordinates in
\mathcal{W}

such that the map
{\mathcal{F}}
is a local diffeomorphism near the point
q_{0}
from the manifold
W
to

{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)
.

Next, we will consider the construction of the Lorentzian metric on
{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)
which
makes it conformal to
(W,g|_{W})
. To this end, let us consider a point
f_{0}={{\mathcal{F}}}(q_{0})\in{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)

and its open neighborhood
\mathcal{W}\subset{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)
where

\vec{a}=(a_{j})_{j=1}^{n+1}
define coordinates
{\bf X}_{\vec{a}}:\mathcal{W}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
near
f_{0}
.

When
B\subset{A}
is a sufficiently small neighborhood of the point
a_{1}
, the function
a\to f^{+}_{a}({q_{0}})
is smooth for
a\in B
.
Let
df^{+}_{a}
denote the co-derivative of the map
f^{+}_{a}:W\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
We see that the co-vectors

df^{+}_{a}|_{q_{0}}\in T^{*}_{q_{0}}M,\quad a\in B

are light-like co-vectors at
q_{0}
. Let
G={{\mathcal{F}}}_{*}g|_{W}
be the metric
g|_{W}
pushed
forward in the map
{{\mathcal{F}}}
to the manifold
{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)
and
E_{a}:{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)\to{\mathbb{R}}

be the evaluation maps
E_{a}:f\to f(a).

Then, we see that the co-derivatives

dE_{a}|_{{{\mathcal{F}}}(q_{0})}\in T^{*}_{{{\mathcal{F}}}(q_{0})}{{\mathcal{F}}}(W),\quad a\in B

are light-like co-vectors with respect to the metric
G
.

As
a\in B
is above arbitrary, we see that the set
\{s\,dE_{a}|_{{{\mathcal{F}}}(q_{0})}\in T^{*}_{{{\mathcal{F}}}(q_{0})}{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)\mid a\in B,\ s>0\}
contains an open subset of the light cone in the space
(T^{*}_{{{\mathcal{F}}}(q_{0})}{{\mathcal{F}}}(W),G)
.
As the light cone is a quadratic variety,
this set determines the whole light cone in
(T^{*}_{{{\mathcal{F}}}(q_{0})}{{\mathcal{F}}}(W),G)
.
These sets determine the conformal class of the metric
G
on
{{\mathcal{F}}}(W)
at the point

{{\mathcal{F}}}(q_{0})
.

3.0.2
Nonlinear interaction of waves and inverse problems for active measurements

In this subsection, we outline the proof of Theorem
2.8
.
To do this, we consider the non-linear equation
\square u+u^{4}=f
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{1+3}
, that is, we introduce the techniques by
considering the Minkowski space
{\mathbb{R}}^{1+3}
and the wave operator

\square=\partial_{x^{0}}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\partial_{x^{j}}^{2}
.

Let us consider the solutions
u_{\vec{\varepsilon}}(x)
of the non-linear wave equation

\square u_{\vec{\varepsilon}}+(u_{\vec{\varepsilon}})^{4}=0,\quad

that depend on parameters
\vec{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{4})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
.
Let us assume that
u_{\vec{\varepsilon}}|_{\vec{\varepsilon}=0}=0
. Then, the linearized waves

\displaystyle u_{j}(x)=\partial_{\varepsilon_{j}}u_{\vec{\varepsilon}}\big|_{\vec{\varepsilon}=0},\quad j=1,2,3,4

satisfy
\square u_{j}=0
. Moreover, the 4th order derivative

\displaystyle w=\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{3}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{4}}u_{\vec{\varepsilon}}(x)\big|_{\vec{\varepsilon}=0}

(3.2)

satisfies

\square w=S,\quad S=-24u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}u_{4}.

We say that
S
is an artificial source produced by the 4-interaction of the waves.

We use coordinates
x=(t,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1+3}
and consider,
as an example, the
linearized waves
u_{j}(t,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})
are

\displaystyle u_{1}(t,y)=\delta(t-y_{1}),\quad u_{2}(t,y)=\delta(t-y_{2}),

\displaystyle u_{3}(t,y)=\delta(t-y_{3}),\quad u_{4}(t,y)=\delta(t-(y_{1}+y_{2})/{\sqrt{2}})

Then,

\displaystyle u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}u_{4}=\delta_{p}(t,y),\quad\hbox{where }p=(0,0,0,0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1+3}

and the 4-interaction of waves,
w(x)
, is the solution of the wave equation

\displaystyle\square w=S,\quad S=-24u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}u_{4}=-24\delta_{p}.

(3.3)

In physical terms,
S
corresponds to an (artificial) point source in the space time.

Let us next consider a general globally hyperbolic,
(1+3)
dimensional Lorentzian manifold when

m=4
.
The proof of Theorem
2.8
is based on the analysis of the interaction of four waves.
Analogously to (
3.2
), the derivative
w=\partial_{\epsilon_{1}}\partial_{\epsilon_{2}}\partial_{\epsilon_{3}}\partial_{\epsilon_{4}}u_{\vec{\epsilon}}\big|_{\vec{\epsilon}=0}
of the solution
u_{\vec{\epsilon}}

of (
2.14
)–(
2.15
) with the source

f_{\vec{\epsilon}}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\epsilon_{j}f_{j}(x),\quad\vec{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3},\epsilon_{4}),

is given by formula (
3.3
),
where

u_{j}(x)

are solutions to the wave equation
\square_{g}u=f_{j}
.

When
f_{j}
is a
C^{\infty}
-smooth function outside the point
p_{j}\in M
, the linearized wave
u_{j}=\square_{g}^{-1}f_{j}
produced
by the source
f_{j}
is microlocally similar to Green’s functions (i.e. the fundamental solution of
the wave equation) corresponding to a point source at the point
p_{j}
.
Let us denote the light cones in
M
emanating from the point
p_{j}
to future by
K_{j}
,
and, for simplicity, assume that the light-like geodesics of
M
have no cut or conjugate points.
Then, in
M\setminus\{p_{j}\}
, the linearized solutions
u_{j}
are conormal distributions that are non-smooth on surfaces
K_{j}
(see
[
22
,
56
]
).
Then, two waves
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are singular on hyper-surfaces

K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, respectively, and these singularities interact on
K_{1}\cap K_{2}
.
This interaction creates only singularities that propagate along
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
on which the original singularities

u_{1}
and
u_{2}
propagate on.
The interaction of the three waves
u_{1}
,
u_{2}
, and
u_{3}
happens on the intersection
K_{123}=K_{1}\cap K_{2}\cap K_{3}

which is a curve in the space-time. As
N^{*}K_{123}

contains light-like directions that are not in union,
N^{*}K_{1}\cup N^{*}K_{2}\cup N^{*}K_{3}
, this interaction produces interesting singularities that start to propagate. These singularities correspond to the black conic wave in Fig. 3 (We note that these singularities produced by 3-interactions can also be used to determine the light-observation sets, see
[
21
]
).
Finally, singularities of all four waves
u_{j}
,
j=1,2,3,4
interact at the point
\{q\}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{4}K_{j}
.
The singularities from the point
q
propagate along the light cone emanating from this point and with suitably chosen sources
f_{j}
the wave

w
is singular at any given point of the the light cone
{\mathcal{L}}(q)
. Thus
S_{1234}
can be considered as a microlocal point source that sends singularities in all directions
as a point source located at the point
q
, and these singularities are observed in the set
{{U}}
.
In this way, the non-linear interaction
of waves determine the intersection of the light cone
{\mathcal{L}}(q)
and the observation domain
{{U}}
. Hence, we can determine the earliest light observation sets

\mathcal{E}_{{U}}(q)
for an arbitrary point
q\in I(p^{-},p^{+})
.
By letting
q
vary through the set
I(p^{-},p^{+})
, we can apply Theorem
2.6
to recover the topology, differentiable structure, and the conformal class of
g
in
I(p^{-},p^{+})
.
