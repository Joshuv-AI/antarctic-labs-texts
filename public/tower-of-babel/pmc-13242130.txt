Role of chest wall perforator flaps in reconstruction of upper inner breast defects
Authors: Ahmed Orabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast-conserving surgery (BCS) is the standard treatment for early-stage breast cancer; however, defects in the upper inner quadrant (UIQ) remain a significant reconstructive challenge due to paucity of local tissue and heightened cosmetic sensitivity of this quadrant. Chest wall perforator flaps (CWPFs) have emerged as a reliable volume replacement option with minimal donor-site morbidity, yet data focusing specifically on their use in UIQ defects are limited.MethodsThis retrospective study included 20 female patients with UIQ breast tumors who underwent partial breast reconstruction using CWPFs between January 2022 and December 2023. Data regarding patient and tumor characteristics, flap type, operative details, and postoperative complications were collected. Aesthetic outcomes were objectively assessed using BCCT.core software (version 3.1), while patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were evaluated at 12 months postoperatively using the BREAST-Q questionnaire.ResultsThe mean patient age was 40.7 ± 5.0 years. The most commonly used flap was the anterior intercostal artery perforator (AICAP) flap (40%), followed by combined LICAP/LTAP flaps (25%). The mean operative time was 79.0 ± 7.5 min. Postoperative complications occurred in 20% of patients, including seroma (10%), wound infection (5%), and fat necrosis (5%), with no cases of major flap loss or positive surgical margins. According to BCCT assessment, excellent and good cosmetic outcomes were achieved in 95% of patients. PROMs demonstrated favorable outcomes, with mean scores of 73.4 ± 4.7 for physical well-being, 73.8 ± 4.2 for psychological well-being, and 70.1 ± 6.3 for satisfaction with the breast.ConclusionCWPFs were used for reconstruction of UIQ defects following breast-conserving surgery with acceptable postoperative complication rates and favorable cosmetic assessment in this retrospective cohort. These findings describe the surgical and aesthetic outcomes observed in this challenging breast quadrant. Further prospective studies with larger patient populations are needed to better define the role of CWPFs in UIQ reconstruction.Trial registrationRetrospectively registered after the approval of Baheya Ethical Committee, IRB no. 202,506,300,026.

Introduction

Breast-conserving surgery (BCS) has become the standard strategy for treating early breast cancer, providing oncologic safety comparable to mastectomy while preserving the natural breast shape. However, partial mastectomy frequently causes contour abnormalities and asymmetry, especially when the resection volume surpasses 20% of the breast tissue. The upper inner quadrant (UIQ) remains one of the most challenging areas for oncoplastic reconstruction due to limited local tissue supply, proximity to the cleavage line, and high aesthetic exposure [1].

Traditional volume displacement techniques, such as glandular reshaping or reduction mammoplasty, are often unsuitable for patients with small- to medium-sized breasts or in cases requiring wide local excision. Consequently, volume replacement with autologous tissue flaps has gained increasing attention [2]. Perforator flaps, which preserve underlying muscle while utilizing skin and subcutaneous tissue supplied by isolated perforating vessels, represent an important advance in reconstructive breast surgery. Nevertheless, reconstruction of upper inner quadrant (UIQ) defects remains particularly challenging, and the available literature is limited [3]. Existing reports are largely confined to small case series or technical descriptions, including the fish-hook rotational flap described by Lee et al. (2015), the pedicled pectoralis major myofascial flap reported by Hu et al. (2024), and the omental flap described by Hisamitsu (2015). These techniques, while innovative, lack robust comparative data and standardized outcome assessment, underscoring the need for further systematic evaluation of reconstructive options for UIQ defects.

The anterior intercostal artery perforator (AICAP), lateral thoracic artery perforator (LTAP), and lateral intercostal artery perforator (LICAP) flaps are often used chest wall perforator flaps. They preserve muscle function, improve cosmetic results, and provide reliable soft-tissue replacement with reduced donor-site morbidity [4]. Perforator flaps are a useful choice for oncoplastic reconstruction following BCS since recent studies have shown positive outcomes in terms of patient satisfaction, local control, and cosmetic outcome [5–8].

Over the past decade, the paradigm of replacement oncoplastic surgery has undergone significant evolution, aiming to overcome the inherent limitations of traditional volume-displacement techniques. The growing adoption of local perforator flaps has markedly decreased reliance on distant autologous flaps such as the latissimus dorsi (LD) and abdominal-based flaps, thereby minimizing donor-site morbidity, shortening operative time, and preserving muscle integrity. Moreover, the integration of advanced preoperative imaging modalities, including handheld Doppler ultrasound and computed tomographic angiography, has improved the accuracy of perforator mapping. This advancement has enhanced flap reliability, reduced the risk of vascular compromise, and expanded the indications for perforator flap reconstruction in challenging breast quadrants [9–12].

Despite the growing body of evidence supporting chest wall perforator flaps, data specifically addressing their application in upper inner quadrant defects remain limited. The anatomical constraints of the UIQ, including the short arc of rotation of most perforator flaps and the limited subcutaneous laxity medially, make reconstruction particularly challenging. As a result, surgeons often hesitate to use perforator flaps for this quadrant, favoring instead more invasive or less aesthetic techniques [1–3].

Therefore, this study aims to assess the feasibility, aesthetic outcomes, and complication rates associated with chest wall perforator flap reconstruction for defects located in the upper inner quadrant of the breast following breast-conserving surgery. By directing attention to this under-reported and technically demanding region, the study seeks to clarify the indications, technical modifications, and inherent limitations of these flaps.

Methods

This retrospective study was conducted at Baheya Center for Early Detection and Treatment of Breast Cancer and included 20 patients with breast tumors located in the upper inner quadrant (UIQ) between January 2022 and December 2023. Eligible participants were women aged 25–60 years who required wide local excision with an anticipated volume loss of more than 20% of the breast, or those with smaller resections who wished to maintain their breast size. Patients with T1–T2 tumors in small to medium-sized breasts with adequate donor-site tissue were included. Patients with clinically operable nodal disease considered suitable for breast-conserving surgery according to multidisciplinary team evaluation and institutional guidelines were also eligible, while those with T3-4 tumors, multicentric disease, or very large breast size were excluded. Ethical approval for retrospective analysis and publication of anonymized patient data was obtained in 2025 after completion of data collection (IRB No. 202506300026).

Data collected from the electronic medical records included patient age, comorbidities, tumor characteristics, neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatment, flap type, number and location of perforators, complications, and mean operative time. Cosmetic outcomes were evaluated using the BCCT.core (version 3.1) software, and patient satisfaction was assessed using the BREAST-Q questionnaire after 12 months.

Surgical technique

The selection between lateral chest wall perforator flaps (LCWPFs) and anterior intercostal artery perforator (AICAP) flaps was based on donor-site suitability and the flap’s proximity to the tumor.

With the patient in the upright position, the tumor was marked with the planned safety margins, followed by flap design.

LCWPF technique

Without use of Doppler guidance, the triangle of plenty was identified between the anterior axillary line, the lateral border of the breast, and the inframammary fold. A lazy S–shaped design was planned toward the axilla, and flap dimensions were determined using the pinch test.

The procedure was initiated through a single incision along the anterior border of the flap, proceeding in the mastectomy plane to access the tumor, which was then excised with adequate margins. Axillary surgery was subsequently performed through the same incision. Flap harvesting was then done by incising the posterior border and dissecting from distal to proximal toward the perforator site within the triangle of plenty. When the lateral thoracic artery was present, dissection was continued to its perforators, typically located in the third or fourth intercostal spaces. The flap was ultimately based solely on the perforators to maximize reach toward the medial defect. If the flap could not reach the defect without tension, mobilization of the upper outer quadrant lateral breast tissue was performed to cover the defect, with the flap replacing the mobilized tissue (Fig. 1).

AICAP technique

For the AICAP flap, the upper border was defined by the inframammary fold. Perforators were identified using a handheld 8-Hz Doppler, and the lower border was determined according to skin laxity and the pinch test. The operative steps were similar, with the incision placed along the inframammary fold. Axillary surgery, however, was performed through a separate axillary incision (Fig. 2).

Postoperative care

All patients were discharged on the first postoperative day. The surgical drain was typically removed after one week, or after two weeks in cases where sentinel lymph node biopsy or axillary lymph node dissection had been performed, respectively. Patients were instructed to wear a soft supportive bra continuously for three months. Follow-up evaluations were conducted in the outpatient clinic at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively.

Aesthetic outcomes were evaluated using a computerized assessment with BCCT.core software version 3.1. To minimize subjectivity, an artificial intelligence–based system was employed to objectively assess cosmetic results through automated analysis of patients’ images, classifying outcomes into excellent, good, fair, or poor categories.

Standardized clinical photographs were obtained at the 12-month follow-up visit under consistent conditions for BCCT.core analysis. All photographs were taken in the upright standing position with the patient facing forward and their hands placed on the waist. Images were captured using a fixed camera distance of approximately 1.5 m under uniform room lighting conditions. Frontal views were obtained using the same clinical photography setting for all patients to minimize variability and improve reproducibility of the objective cosmetic assessment.

The BCCT.core software analyzes breast images based on asymmetry, color differences, and scar visibility, which are subsequently processed by a pattern recognition classifier to generate the final aesthetic category.

Breast asymmetry was quantified using multiple indices, including: breast retraction assessment (BRA), which measures differences in nipple position and reflects breast retraction; lower breast contour (LBC), indicating disparities in the inferior breast contour; upward nipple retraction (UNR), representing differences in nipple height; breast compliance evaluation (BCE), defined as the difference between the nipple and inframammary fold on both sides; breast contour difference (BCD), reflecting variation in breast contour length; breast area difference (BAD), denoting differences in breast surface area; and breast overlap difference (BOD), calculated as the non-overlapping area after mirroring one breast along the vertical axis and aligning both breasts at their thoracic junction points.

Patient satisfaction was assessed using the BREAST-Q Breast-Conserving Therapy (BCT) module at one-year follow-up. The BREAST-Q is a previously validated and published patient-reported outcome measure (PROMs) for breast surgery developed by Pusic et al. [13]. PROMs included psychosocial well-being, physical well-being, and satisfaction with the breast. Each domain was scored on a scale from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better outcomes. Scores were analyzed descriptively and are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24. Given the descriptive nature of the study and the limited sample size, only descriptive statistics were performed. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage.

Results

A total of 20 female patients were included in this retrospective study. The mean age was 40.7 ± 5.0 years (range 33–51). The mean body mass index (BMI) was 29.4 ± 3.9 kg/m² (range 21.8–40). Two patients (10%) were diabetic and two patients were hypertensive.

Most patients had cup size B, accounting for 65% of the cohort, while 7 patients (35%) had cup size C. T2 tumors were observed in 12 patients (60%), while T1 tumors in 7 patients (35%). Node-negative disease (N0) was present in 11 patients (55%), whereas 8 patients (40%) had N1 disease. The tumor was unifocal in 13 patients (65%). Sentinel lymph node biopsy was done in 12 patients (60%). Invasive ductal carcinoma was the most common histological type (70%). 12 patients (60%) had grade 2 tumor. Discussion

Few studies have addressed oncoplastic techniques for reconstruction of defects in the UIQ following tumor resection, despite the cosmetic challenges associated with this region. Lee et al. (2015) described the use of a fish-hook incision rotational flap in 13 patients; however, this approach was associated with a relatively prolonged operative time (118.0 ± 22.7 min), longer hospital stay (4.8 ± 0.9 days), and a large incision extending from the axilla to the whole inframammary fold. Similarly, Hu et al. (2024) reported the use of a pedicled pectoralis major myofascial flap in 15 patients with UIQ tumors. Although effective for defect coverage, this technique results in a visible breast scar and raises concerns regarding postoperative muscle atrophy, particularly following adjuvant radiotherapy. In addition, Hisamitsu Zaha (2015) described omental flap reconstruction for UIQ defects in 30 cases using a dual-incision approach, one breast incision and a submammary incision for laparoscopic omental harvest. Nevertheless, this technique has notable limitations, including difficulty in accurately estimating flap volume and challenges in delivering large omental flaps through a narrow subcutaneous tunnel, which may predispose to vascular trauma and partial flap necrosis.

In the era of chest wall perforator flaps (CWPFs), several studies have incorporated these flaps in only a limited number of cases within heterogeneous cohorts, without providing detailed technical descriptions specific to the UIQ [6, 8, 11, 12]. This underscores the relevance of the present study, which focuses exclusively on reconstruction of this particularly challenging quadrant and explores the role of CWPFs in addressing its unique anatomical and aesthetic demands.

CWPFs are ideally suited for small and medium sized breasts; however, several studies have reported their use in larger cup sizes [8–11]. In the present study, most patients had cup size B breasts (65%). This may be attributed to the technical challenges of using these flaps in the UIQ, where increased distance from the donor site and breast ptosis can limit flap reach and inset.

The mean operative time for CWPFs has been reported as relatively long in some studies. One study including 40 patients reported a mean operative time of 148 ± 13.6 min [4], while another study involving 150 patients reported a mean operative time of 164 ± 37 min [8]. Conversely, shorter operative times have been described, such as 100.5 ± 10.2 min in a study of 22 patients [5]. In our study, the mean operative time was 79.0 ± 7.5 min. Acceptable operative time was observed despite reconstruction being performed in the technically challenging UIQ.

The reported length of hospital stay following CWPF reconstruction varies considerably in the literature, ranging from a mean of 3 days to as long as 11 days [5, 12]. More recently, Ferrucci et al. reported overnight hospitalization in 71.2% of patients [14]. In our study, all patients were discharged after a single postoperative day, with no cases of major flap loss or reoperation recorded during the early postoperative period.

A wide spectrum of complications following CWPF reconstruction has been reported in the literature. In a study of 36 patients, seroma occurred in 6 cases (16.7%), wound infection in 2 cases (5.6%), and hematoma in 1 case (2.8%) [10]. Another study involved 42 patients reported seroma in 1 case (2.4%), hematoma in 3 cases (7.1%), wound infection in 2 cases (4.8%), and fat necrosis in 1 case (2.4%) [9]. More severe complications, including flap loss and abscess formation, have also been described [11], while delayed venous congestion was reported in 5 cases (4%) [12]. A recent series including 73 patients demonstrated an overall complication rate of 16.4%, with seroma, fat necrosis, and surgical site infection occurring in 6.8%, 8.2%, and 1.4% of patients, respectively, without complete flap loss [14]. Conversely, some studies demonstrated very low complication rates, reporting only a single case of wound infection in cohorts of 22 and 37 patients [5, 7].

Reconstruction of defects in the UIQ is technically demanding because of the increased distance from the perforator origin, limited skin laxity, and the frequent presence of breast ptosis, all of which may theoretically increase the risk of flap-related complications. Despite these challenges, the present study demonstrated an overall complication rate of 20%, with seroma, wound infection, and fat necrosis occurring in 2 (10%), 1 (5%), and 1 (5%) patients, respectively. Importantly, no cases of major flap loss were recorded. These findings suggest that CWPFs may be safely applied to UIQ defects, provided that careful patient selection, meticulous flap design, and appropriate intraoperative handling are ensured.

Intraoperative frozen section analysis was utilized in some studies to assess surgical margins [4, 5, 10]. In our study, frozen section analysis was not employed; nevertheless, all cases achieved negative surgical margins, and no patients required re-excision. Achieving adequate oncological clearance in the UIQ tumors can be particularly challenging in oncoplastic breast surgery because of limited exposure with reaching the tumor from remote incision, and the need to preserve cosmetic results. Despite these challenges, the absence of positive margins in our cohort suggests that careful preoperative imaging, accurate tumor localization, and meticulous oncoplastic planning can allow adequate margin clearance in the UIQ lesions without routine use of frozen section analysis. Also, Ferrucci et al. reported a low re-excision rate of 5.5% and completion mastectomy in 4.1% of patients following CWPF reconstruction, suggesting the oncological feasibility of perforator flap-based breast conservation approaches [14].

CWPFs may represent an effective option for preserving breast volume in patients with large cup sizes, particularly when breast size reduction is not desired. Achieving symmetry in UIQ defects is especially challenging due to the limited available tissue and the visibility of deformities in this quadrant. Some studies have reported the need for additional symmetrization procedures, such as delayed contralateral reduction mammoplasty or ipsilateral lipofilling [11]. In contrast, none of the patients in the present study required further oncoplastic procedures for symmetrization, suggesting that CWPFs can adequately restore volume and contour in UIQ defects without the need for secondary corrective interventions when careful flap selection and design are employed.

Overall, the cosmetic outcomes following CWPF reconstruction have been reported as excellent or good in the majority of cases across several studies. Korayem et al. (2024) assessed cosmesis using patient-reported surveys in conjunction with independent surgeon evaluation, reporting satisfactory cosmetic outcomes (excellent or good) in 88.9% of patients (n = 32). Similarly, Mohsen et al. (2023) reported excellent or good cosmetic results in 95% of their cohort. More recently, Anacleto et al. reported good or excellent BCCT.core aesthetic outcomes in approximately 70% of patients undergoing modified chest wall perforator flap reconstruction [15]. In accordance with these findings, 95% of patients in the present study demonstrated excellent or good cosmetic outcomes based on objective assessment using BCCT.core software. Notably, these favorable results were achieved despite reconstruction of defects in the UIQ, a region traditionally considered cosmetically challenging.

Several studies have utilized patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) to evaluate patient satisfaction following CWPF reconstruction. A study including 200 patients reported high satisfaction levels, with psychological well-being exceeding 87%, physical well-being reported at 78%, and satisfaction with breasts at 85% [6]. Another study reported mean scores of 68.1 ± 21.2 for physical well-being (chest), 72.4 ± 19.5 for psychological well-being, and 67.6 ± 18.8 for overall satisfaction with the breast [7]. Additionally, one study demonstrated psychological well-being scores of 87/100, postoperative satisfaction with breasts of 82/100, and postoperative physical well-being scores of 71/100 [9].

In our study, PROMs demonstrated favorable outcomes, with mean scores of 73.4 ± 4.7 for physical well-being, 73.8 ± 4.2 for psychological well-being, and 70.1 ± 6.3 for satisfaction with the breast. Importantly, these postoperative patient-reported outcomes were observed despite reconstruction being performed in the UIQ, a region traditionally associated with inferior cosmetic and psychological outcomes. Although baseline preoperative PROM data were unavailable for comparison, the reported postoperative scores suggest acceptable levels of patient satisfaction and well-being following CWPF reconstruction in this challenging quadrant.

This study has limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size, the single-center design, and the absence of a comparator arm may limit the generalizability and comparative interpretation of the findings. In addition, all procedures were performed within the same specialized breast surgery unit by the corresponding author, who was also responsible for retrospective data collection and manuscript preparation, without formal independent data verification. Second, the short follow-up period may not capture the late effects of radiation therapy on the flaps. Third, preoperative BREAST-Q assessment was not routinely performed; therefore, postoperative PROM scores were analyzed as descriptive cross-sectional outcomes without baseline comparison. Furthermore, only descriptive statistical analysis was performed because of the limited sample size and retrospective observational design, restricting the ability to explore associations between clinical variables and outcomes. Larger, multicenter studies with longer follow-up and comparative designs are warranted to validate these findings and further define the role of CWPFs in UIQ reconstruction.

Conclusion

CWPFs appear to represent a feasible and cosmetically acceptable option for reconstruction of UIQ defects following BCS. In the present cohort, reconstruction was associated with favorable operative times, manageable complication rates, and reliable oncological outcomes without major flap loss. Favorable cosmetic outcomes and acceptable postoperative PROM scores were observed following reconstruction in this technically challenging quadrant. With appropriate patient selection and meticulous surgical planning, CWPFs may be considered a valuable volume replacement technique for UIQ reconstruction, although larger prospective studies with longer follow-up and comparative designs are needed to further validate these findings.
