Association of Adverse Childhood Experiences and Cognitive Health in Older Adults: Rush Memory and Aging Project
Authors: Jocelyn Jaen, Melissa Lamar, Brittney S Lange-Maia, Daisy T Noriega-Makarskyy, Liliana Gómez-Flores-Ramos, Martin Lajous, David A Bennett, Francine Grodstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:To examine the association between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and cognitive function and decline in older adults.Design:Analysis of baseline cognitive and annual rates of cognitive decline in participants from the Rush Memory and Aging Project (MAP).Setting:MAP is an ongoing cohort of older adults initiated in 1997.Participants:1,869 participants without dementia at baseline.Measurements:ACEs were assessed using a 16-item questionnaire covering emotional neglect, financial need, parental intimidation, parental violence, and family turmoil. Cognitive function was evaluated annually using 19 neuropsychologic tests across five domains. Linear mixed-effects models estimated associations of ACEs with baseline global cognition and cognitive decline.Results:At baseline, participants had a mean age of 80 years and 15 years of education; 26% were male, and 92% were non-Latino White. The average total ACE score was 8.3 points (SD 7.6) of a possible 58 points; the mean follow-up was 7.6 years (SD 4.9). Total ACE score was not associated with baseline global cognition or with the annual rate of global cognitive decline. However, each increment of parental violence (mean difference = −0.032 standard units, p = 0.004), family turmoil (mean difference = −0.027, p = 0.007), and financial need (mean difference = −0.024, p = 0.01) was associated with lower baseline global cognition. Emotional neglect was associated with a steeper, annual rate of global cognitive decline (mean difference = −0.0014, p = 0.027).Conclusion:The total ACE score was not associated with cognitive health in later life. However, specific ACE dimensions, including parental violence, family turmoil, and financial need, may be related to baseline cognitive function in older persons.Keywords: Adverse childhood experiences, aging, childhood adversity, cognition

INTRODUCTION

In the United States, the population of older persons is expected to almost double in the next decade.1 Among the many factors influencing health in later life, early-life exposures have been linked to many chronic diseases of aging.2 Better understanding the relationship between early-life factors and late-life disease can contribute to shaping possible prevention strategies to reduce the risk of poor health outcomes associated with aging. Moreover, understanding these relationships could also help identify at-risk populations3,4 in whom health interventions may be most impactful.

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are severely negative events occurring before the age of 18, including a wide range of events from hunger to physical and psychological abuse to a dysfunctional household and financial challenges.5 Extensive evidence links ACEs to a variety of negative health outcomes in adulthood, both mental health as well as physical health.6,7 Moreover, research suggests that ACEs can disrupt brain architecture8,9; consequently, there is a growing interest in examining the relationship between childhood adversity and cognitive health in older adults. Studies to date, however,10-21 typically include a wide range of middle-aged and older adults, although cognitive decline is more marked at older ages. 22 Thus, longitudinal studies specifically considering older adults are needed to better evaluate whether ACEs may be related to later-life cognition.

In our own previous research investigating ACEs, we leveraged the Rush Memory and Aging Project (MAP), an ongoing cohort study of aging and cognitive health,23 and reported that higher levels of ACEs were associated with many risk factors for poor cognition, such as negative emotions (e.g., neuroticism, anxiety, hostility) and less social engagement in late life.24 Here, we extend this research to directly evaluate the association of ACEs with global and domain-specific cognition, considering both cognitive function at cohort baseline as well as cognitive decline over time.

METHODS

Participants

MAP started in 1997, recruiting older adults without known dementia at baseline, from retirement communities in the Chicago metropolitan area; recruitment is ongoing. The study aims to evaluate factors associated with aging, and conducts yearly clinical and cognitive assessments and postmortem organ donation.25 Follow-up in the cohort is high, exceeding 90%.26

Exposure Measures: ACEs

The baseline cohort assessment included a 16-item questionnaire designed to evaluate a wide spectrum of childhood adversity. The items were adapted from existing questionnaires and interviews, including the ACE Study questionnaire,24 and addressed issues such as abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction. For each of the six items related to emotional neglect, the score increased with more reported neglect, ranging from 0 to 3. To evaluate experiencing separation from, or problems within the family unit (i.e., alcohol disorder, hospitalization, incarceration), participants were given a list of four types of family problems and four types of family separation and replied yes or no, depending on whether they experienced each item; scores were then based on the count of the items experienced (0 to 8). For the remaining items, scores increased with the frequency of the reported adversity, ranging from never (0) to always (4). The sum of raw scores for all 16 items was used to create a summary measure of total childhood adversity, with a maximum score of 58 reflecting the presence of all adversities at the highest level.

In our previous examination of ACEs in MAP, we used a principal component analysis with varimax rotation to identify five factors that explained the most variance, and labeled the rotated factors as emotional neglect, financial need, parental intimidation, parental violence, and family turmoil.24 To create scores for each dimension, we grouped the items belonging to each and added their raw scores. Emotional neglect is the cumulative result of six items and has a maximum score of 18. Financial need had a maximum score of 8, after combining two items. Parental intimidation had a maximum score of 16, derived from four items; parental violence had a maximum score of 8, derived from two items; and family turmoil had a maximum score of 8, based on the count of eight adversities queried. Details on the questions and classification into dimensions can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Cognitive Measures

At baseline, and yearly after that, participants underwent a comprehensive cognitive assessment. This protocol encompassed 19 cognitive tasks designed to evaluate five cognitive domains. Briefly, the protocol included seven tests of episodic memory (Word list, Word list recall, Word list recognition, East Boston Story immediate recall, East Boston story delayed recall, Logical memory immediate, Logical memory delayed); two tests of perceptual orientation (Line orientation, Progressive matrices); four tests of perceptual speed (Symbol digits modalities, Number comparison, Stroop color naming, Stroop word reading); three tests of semantic memory (Boston Naming Test, CERAD verbal fluency, Reading test); and three tests of working memory (Digit Span subtest-forward, Digit Span subtest-backward, Digit ordering).27 Scores for each test were converted to z-scores using the baseline mean and standard deviation. Z-scores for each domain were averaged to obtain the score for that domain, and a global cognitive score was created by averaging all z-scores.28

Covariates

All covariates were derived from the baseline assessment, including age, sex, years of education, self-identified race, and ethnicity. We also considered childhood socioeconomic status as a potential confounding factor; to create this variable, we combined the report of parents’ years of education and the number of children in the family. These responses were transformed into z-scores and averaged to create a composite measure of childhood socioeconomic status.

Analytic Sample

We included participants free of dementia at baseline as determined by our baseline cohort evaluation, those with information on ACEs, and those with baseline and at least one follow-up cognitive assessment. Of the 2,425 participants enrolled in MAP as of the time of our analysis, we excluded 123 (7%) diagnosed with dementia at their baseline cognitive assessment, 108 (6%) who did not have a complete baseline cognitive assessment, 52 (3%) who did not have complete ACEs information, and 202 (11%) who did not have at least one follow-up cognitive assessment. Thus, a total of 1869 participants were included in this analysis.

Data Analysis

To evaluate the association between ACEs and cognitive function at baseline, as well as cognitive decline over time, we used linear mixed effects models. Our primary independent variables of interest were the total score of childhood adversity and, separately, the score for each of the adversity dimensions. We considered as dependent variables the global cognitive score as our primary outcome, and in additional analyses, we also considered each cognitive domain. Models included age, sex, childhood socioeconomic status, and years of education as covariates. In models examining financial need, we did not include childhood socioeconomic status as a covariate given the strong correlation between these variables. We examined total ACEs plus five ACEs dimensions, in relation to global cognition plus five cognitive domains; such multiple statistical comparisons can lead to false positive results. In analyzing the data, we weighed the risk of over-interpreting specific findings due to multiple comparisons with the risk of missing specific findings when correcting for multiple comparisons. We chose here to present results without correction for multiple comparisons; we emphasize that the analyses of the five specific cognitive domains are secondary analyses, and cautious interpretation is warranted of all findings. Analyses were conducted with SAS version 9.0.

RESULTS

Sample Characteristics

In our analytic cohort (Table 1), 26% of the participants were male, and 92% were Non-Latino Whites. Age at baseline ranged from 65 to 100 years, with a mean of 80.4 years (SD 6.6). Participants also reported a mean of 15 years (SD 3.2) of education. Eighty-eight percent of participants reported having experienced at least some degree of ACEs. Overall, the score for total childhood adversity in our sample averaged 8.3 points (SD 7.6) out of a maximum of 58. Among the dimensions of childhood adversity, neglect had the highest mean score (4.0 points, SD = 4.1, range: 0–18 points), whereas parental violence had the lowest (0.7 points, SD = 1.1, range 0–8 points). The mean follow-up time was 7.6 years (SD 4.9), with a maximum of 24.9 years.

Childhood Adversity and Cognitive Function at Baseline

Since childhood adversity occurred many years in the past, we were interested in potentially accumulated cognitive decrements as of participants’ entry into the cohort; thus, we assessed the association of ACEs to baseline cognitive function (Figure 1). We found that each increment in the total score of ACEs was not associated with global cognition (mean difference: 0.02 standard units, p = 0.17). We also found no relation of total adversity to any of the cognitive domains at baseline. To further consider whether various dimensions of childhood adversity could be specifically associated with cognitive function in older age, we analyzed each of the five ACE dimensions in separate models (Table 2). Overall, we found that three of the five ACE dimensions were significantly associated with lower levels of global cognition: family turmoil, financial need, and parental violence. Specifically, each one-point increment in family turmoil was related to lower baseline global cognition (mean difference = −0.027 standard units, p = 0.01); each one-point increment in financial need (mean difference = −0.024, p = 0.01) and parental violence (mean difference = −0.032, p = 0.004) were also associated with lower global cognition. Since these mean differences can be difficult to interpret, we compared the mean difference for each ACE to the mean difference we found in these models for each year of age. This can provide a point of reference for understanding the mean differences we find here. Specifically, the mean difference for each year of age was −0.024 standard units in our models. Thus, each one-point increment in family turmoil or financial need corresponds to one year of cognitive aging, while each increment in financial need corresponds to approximately 1.33 years of cognitive aging.

In secondary analyses, these findings remained largely consistent across cognitive domains. There was no association of total ACEs with any of the cognitive domains at baseline. There were significant associations specifically of family turmoil with most cognitive domains: perceptual orientation (mean difference = −0.051, p <0.001), perceptual speed (mean difference = −0.031, p = 0.03), and semantic memory (mean difference = −0.031, p <0.001). Additionally, there were significant associations of financial need with perceptual orientation (mean difference = −0.029, p = 0.02), semantic memory (mean difference = −0.033, p <0.01), and working memory (mean difference = −0.037, p <0.001); we also found associations of parental violence with perceptual orientation (mean difference = −0.04, p = 0.001), semantic memory (mean difference = −0.041, p <0.01), and working memory (mean difference = −0.055, p <0.001).

Childhood Adversity and Cognitive Decline Over Time

When we examined the relation of total ACEs to global cognitive decline (Table 3), we found no association (mean difference = 0.00, p = 0.25). Similarly, in secondary analyses, there was no association of total ACEs with decline in any of the cognitive domains. When we considered specific dimensions of childhood adversity, greater emotional neglect was consistently related to faster rates of decline in global cognition (mean difference −0.001, p = 0.027), as well as secondary analyses of decline in perceptual orientation (mean difference = −0.001, p = 0.02), perceptual speed (mean difference = −0.001, p = 0.02), semantic memory (mean difference=−0.002, p <0.01), and working memory (mean difference = −0.001, p = 0.04). However, there were generally few consistent findings of associations across decline in global cognition or the cognitive domains for the other ACEs dimensions.

CONCLUSION

We did not find an association of total reported ACEs to either global cognitive function at baseline or decline in global cognition over time, among nearly 2,000 older adults followed for a mean of 8 years. Thus, our work indicates that there may not be an aggregated impact of multiple types of ACEs on later-life cognitive functioning. Instead, the pattern of results suggests that specific dimensions of childhood adversity may be relevant to cognitive health in older adults. Specifically, family turmoil, financial need, and parental violence were consistently associated with lower global cognition at baseline. In helping interpret those findings, each increment in score for those ACEs dimensions appeared equivalent to approximately one additional year of cognitive aging. Our findings that these ACEs were primarily associated with baseline cognition and not with continued cognitive decline over time suggest that the potential detrimental association of specific ACEs to cognitive health may largely be already in place by the time individuals reach these older ages.

Although multiple studies have examined ACEs and cognitive function in late life,16,17,20,29-31 there are some challenges to interpreting the scientific literature. Besides differences in the age groups targeted, each study typically uses different approaches to defining and measuring ACEs, making it hard to compare results across studies, including our own. Nonetheless, despite defining total ACEs differently across studies, our finding of a lack of association between total ACEs with both cognitive function,17,20,29-33 and cognitive decline,33,34 has been reported consistently. Taken together, then, it appears that aggregated ACEs may not be related to cognitive health in late life.

Regarding the association of specific types of childhood adversity with late-life cognition, it has been suggested that ACEs representing deprivation (e.g., financial need) may be more strongly related to brain aging than those representing physical threat (e.g., abuse).35 This has been hypothesized based on the reduced exposure to social and cognitive stimuli that characterizes deprivation, and the direct implications it has for cognitive development.36 Our work here partially supports this. Our study and several others report that deprivation-related ACEs, such as financial need, are associated with lower cognitive function.12,29,37-39 Nonetheless, other studies and our work here show that threat-related ACEs are also associated with poorer cognitive outcomes.19,40,41 Moreover, our study and others have found that adversities affecting the family environment—a mix of deprivation- and threat-related experiences—are associated with lower cognitive function.12-14,19,20 Thus, it remains unclear exactly how specific dimensions of ACEs are related to cognition, and it will be important to continue refining our understanding of how distinct ACEs may function in relation to worse levels of cognition. Such research can contribute to both identifying at-risk groups and to understanding pathways involved in the relation of ACEs to cognitive health.

By contrast, epidemiologic studies that longitudinally assess the relation of ACEs to cognitive decline in older adult populations, while sparse, generally report no association between specific domains of ACEs and cognitive decline over time.17,33,42 Indeed, we found few relations of ACEs to cognitive decline; only emotional neglect was consistently associated with steeper rates of cognitive decline in our study. This singular finding is somewhat difficult to interpret, especially because we found no relation of emotional neglect to cognitive function cross-sectionally. Overall, our largely null findings for ACEs and cognitive decline might suggest that most of ACEs’ relationship with cognitive health is already established at these older ages (i.e., prior to participant’s entry into the cohort) and has likely persisted for many years.

Indeed, in terms of possible mechanisms, the substantial gap between ACE exposure and our measurement of cognition highlights how enduring associations between dimensions of childhood adversity and cognitive function may be. This longevity may point to both direct and indirect potential pathways involved. Direct alterations in brain morphology in relation to ACEs have been observed in studies in children8,43 and young adults,44 and more recently found in older adults who experienced childhood adversity.45 Thus, it is possible that damage to brain morphology earlier in life may be a lasting vulnerability that cannot be compensated. Additionally, it is possible that ACEs may impact mid-life health and behavior, such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes7,46 or alcohol use and smoking,7 and these factors in turn may indirectly lead to cognitive impairment years later.

Our study has many important strengths, including comprehensive measures of ACEs, enabling consideration of five different ACE dimensions. Further, our assessment of ACEs included the frequency of exposure, yielding continuous adversity scores rather than dichotomizing ACE exposure; this likely captures more variability in exposure and enhances our ability to detect associations. The large sample size and high follow-up also improve our ability to detect associations and reduce bias.

However, our study has limitations. We examined several different ACEs, and global cognition as well as five cognitive domains. Such multiple comparisons can cause statistically significant associations that are due to chance. Thus, our results should be interpreted cautiously. We note, however, that our findings for family turmoil, financial need, and parental violence were consistent across both global cognition (our primary outcome) as well as the majority of cognitive domains, which may indicate a coherence supporting true associations. In addition, we were not able to establish the exact time of exposure to childhood adversity; since there may be sensitive windows of brain development, by aggregating ACEs across childhood, we may overlook time-specific impacts to cognitive function. Like all studies assessing ACEs retrospectively in adults, the measurement of childhood adversity relies on recalling experiences that occurred decades before. Yet, individuals’ later perception of adversity and the level of lingering stress into adulthood may be at least, or even more relevant than any “objective” measure of ACEs. However, retrospective ACEs research is susceptible to survival bias, which may systematically reduce participation in the most severely affected individuals, particularly in our cohort of older adults. Indeed, in our cohort, the average level of ACEs was relatively low. This may limit our ability to detect relations of ACEs to cognitive health if higher levels of exposure to ACEs could be particularly damaging. Nonetheless, despite the likelihood that we are underestimating associations of ACEs to cognition, we found several relations of specific ACEs to cognitive function in these older adults, emphasizing that even modest exposure may be related to worse cognition many years later.
