A potential energy conserving finite element method for turbulent variable density flow: application to glacier-fjord circulation
Lukas Lundgren, Christian Helanow, Jonathan Wiskandt, Inga Monika Koszalka, Josefin Ahlkrona

Abstract
We introduce a continuous Galerkin finite element discretization of the non-hydrostatic Boussinesq approximation of the Navier-Stokes equations, suitable for various applications such as coastal ocean dynamics and ice-ocean interactions, among others. In particular, we introduce a consistent modification of the gravity force term which enhances conservation properties for Galerkin methods without strictly enforcing the divergence-free condition. We show that this modification results in a sharp energy estimate, including both kinetic and potential energy. Additionally, we propose a new, symmetric, tensor-based viscosity operator that is especially suitable for modeling turbulence in stratified flow. The viscosity coefficients are constructed using a residual-based shock-capturing method and the method conserves angular momentum and dissipates kinetic energy. We validate our proposed method through numerical tests and use it to model the ocean circulation and basal melting beneath the ice tongue of the Ryder Glacier and the adjacent Sherard Osborn fjord in two dimensions on a fully unstructured mesh. Our results compare favorably with a standard numerical ocean model, showing better resolved turbulent flow features and reduced artificial diffusion.

Introduction

Variable density flow is common in industry, appearing in natural convection flows within heat exchangers, cooling systems and thermal insulators
[
46
]
. It is also prevalent in nature; in fact, numerical simulations of variable density flow are central to climate models that can help us predict and prepare for the effects of global warming
[
55
]
. Improved models are specifically needed for turbulent gravity-driven flows in the climate system, such as the melt-water driven ocean circulation near the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets
[
57
]
. The rate at which the ice sheet melts is influenced by ocean velocity, temperature and salinity at the ice-ocean interface. In turn, these properties are significantly affected by the melt rate itself. Such non-linear feedback between glacial ice dynamics and fjord circulation has the potential to be an important contributing factor to changes in ice sheet mass loss and thus rising sea levels
[
57
,
50
,
55
,
51
,
25
,
4
,
19
,
20
,
60
]
.

Many simulation software for variable density flow and fluid dynamics in general - including atmospheric and ocean models - rely on the finite volume (FV) or finite difference (FD) method rather than the finite element method (FEM). This is largely due to the lack of conservation properties of traditional FEMs. However, FEM offers several attractive features such as a sound mathematical framework and the ability to accurately represent complex geometries. Properly representing flows in the presence of complex geometry is one of the major challenges in FD and FV climate models, where the use of terrain-following or partial cell techniques can introduce errors
[
17
,
56
,
34
]
.

Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods are often suggested to address the issues with conservation of standard FEMs, both in fluid dynamics in general and for the coastal ocean and meltwater-driven circulation in particular. In
[
33
]
a DG hydrostatic model is developed for the coastal ocean and in
[
34
]
and
[
59
]
DG discretizations of a non-hydrostatic Boussinesq approximation of the Navier-Stokes equations are presented and used to model idealized configurations of the interaction between melting ice sheets and ocean circulation. A drawback of DG methods is that they tend to be associated with a higher computational cost and can, in some software, be more cumbersome to implement compared to the more traditional
H^{1}
-conforming continuous Galerkin (CG) FEM methods.

However, recent progress in improving conservation properties for incompressible flow problems using
H^{1}
-conforming FEM opens up the possibility of CG models of density-driven flow. For
H^{1}
-conforming FEM, it is common for the divergence-free condition to be only satisfied weakly, which has consequences for stability and conservation properties. Recently,
Charnyi et al. [12]
derived a formulation of the convective term that enables Galerkin methods to be kinetic energy, momentum and angular momentum conserving (EMAC). This formulation has been extended to be more efficient
[
13
]
, further analyzed by
Olshanskii and Rebholz [52]
,
Olshanskii and Rebholz [53]
, and extended to projection methods by
Ingimarson et al. [27]
. In particular,
[
53
]
showed that
H^{1}
-conforming FEM also satisfies local conservation properties. The EMAC formulation consistently enhances the accuracy and reliability of simulations compared to other approaches, especially over long time intervals. Motivated by the success of these methods,
Lundgren and Nazarov [42]
extended the EMAC formulation to the variable density setting and derived a formulation that is shift-invariant, mass, squared density, kinetic energy, momentum and angular momentum conserving (SI-MEDMAC). Again, this was demonstrated to be more accurate and robust compared to other formulations in the literature.

These new types of CG methods are relevant to variable density flow in general. For variable density, gravity-driven, flow specifically there is a balance between kinetic and potential energy, and it is beneficial for a numerical method to capture this balance as it improves accuracy and robustness
[
38
]
. In this paper, we extend the SI-MEDMAC formulation so that potential energy is also conserved and we demonstrate its capabilities on a simulation of ocean circulation in the Sherard Osborn fjord adjacent to the Ryder Glacier, Greenland. The circulation near the ice is driven by buoyant meltwater rising along the ice surface, creating a turbulent plume (Fig.
3
).

Since the plume is highly turbulent, small-scale processes cannot be adequately resolved on a computational mesh. In the ocean modeling community, it is common to use tensor-based eddy viscosity
[
34
,
59
,
66
,
21
,
32
]
as a sub-grid scale model. This approach employs different viscosity coefficients in each coordinate direction, accommodating the significant variation in flow scales and mesh sizes between the horizontal and vertical directions. This enables a more accurate representation of these types of flow compared to scalar eddy viscosity. However, the tensors used in the references are not symmetric, resulting in the loss of angular momentum conservation
[
42
,
16
]
.

We introduce a new symmetric viscosity tensor that conserves angular momentum and dissipates kinetic energy. To automatically construct the viscosity coefficients and maintain stability and accurate representation of small-scale structures, we scale the viscosity coefficients based on the residual of the PDE, a method commonly known as the residual viscosity (RV) method
[
47
,
48
,
61
,
40
,
44
,
15
,
43
,
63
]
. Since the residual is small in smooth, resolved regions of the domain, high-order accuracy is preserved. For a comparison between the RV method and the well-known Lilly-Smagorinsky model, we refer to
Marras et al. [44]
. For additional suppression of small-scale oscillations and improved accuracy in smooth regions, we also propose adding the high-order dissipation operator proposed by
Kuzmin and Vedral [36]
which is a variation of local-projection stabilization
[
8
]
and the variational multiscale (VMS) method
[
31
]
. We note that the RV method is closely related to the entropy viscosity method
[
23
]
: for example, for scalar conservation laws, the residual is a special case of the entropy residual, since the solution to the equation can be chosen as an entropy functional. However, a similar proof of the convergence of the RV method for general non-linear scalar conservation laws, as in
[
47
]
, is not known for the entropy viscosity method. In addition, it can be difficult to choose the entropy functional so that the corresponding entropy viscosity method is robust.

Finally, to account for the boundary between the fjord and the surrounding open ocean, we also derive a modified directional do-nothing condition
[
9
]
which is consistent with the new conservative formulation. The boundary condition is to our knowledge the first condition of its kind for FEM models of ice-ocean interaction.

The FEM is validated in two ways: 1) via benchmark problems to demonstrate convergence rates and energy conservation and 2) by simulating the circulation in an idealized 2D version of the Sherard Osborn Fjord, comparing our results with the high-resolution simulations of
[
66
]
which were produced with the FV state-of-the-art ocean-atmosphere model MITgcm (Massachusetts Institute of Technology general circulation model)
[
45
,
1
]
.

2.1
Governing equations

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}{\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\nabla p}{\rho_{0}}+\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})^{\top}\right)\right)+{\boldsymbol{f}},\quad

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{x}},t)\in\Omega{\times}\left(0,\widehat{t}\ \right],

2.3
A novel conservative formulation

The momentum equations (
2.1a
) can be equivalently written as

{\partial}_{t}{\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}=-\nabla\tilde{p}+\nabla{\cdot}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})^{\top}\right)\right)-\frac{g\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}},

(2.9)

leading to a modified pressure
\tilde{p}=\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}p+gy
. This modification, aimed at eliminating cancellation errors resulting from
\rho_{0}+\delta\rho
, is frequently used in the Boussinesq and ocean modeling literature. Next, we consider the energy, momentum and angular momentum conserving (EMAC) formulation
[
12
]

{\partial}_{t}{\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{u}}=-\nabla\tilde{\tilde{p}}+\nabla{\cdot}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})^{\top}\right)\right)-\frac{g\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}},

(2.10)

where
\tilde{\tilde{p}}=\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}p-\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{u}}+gy
is a further modified pressure. The EMAC formulation has favorable conservation properties when discretized by a Galerkin method leading to increased stability and accuracy
[
52
,
13
,
27
]
. Similarly, the tracer equations can be written as

\partial_{t}\phi+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla\phi+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}})\left(\phi-\overline{\phi}\right)=\nabla{\cdot}(\kappa_{\phi}\nabla\phi)+\zeta(x)(\phi_{res}(y)-\phi),

(2.11)

where
\overline{\phi}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\phi\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}
is the mean of the tracer field. The formulation in was introduced by
Lundgren and Nazarov [42]
and can be shown to be shift-invariant in the sense that

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\phi+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla\phi+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}})\left(\phi-\overline{\phi}\right)&=\nabla{\cdot}(\kappa_{\phi}\nabla\phi),\\
&\Leftrightarrow\\
\partial_{t}(\phi+c)+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla(\phi+c)+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}})\left((\phi+c)-\overline{(\phi+c)}\right)&=\nabla{\cdot}(\kappa_{\phi}\nabla(\phi+c)),\quad\forall c\in{\mathbb R}.\end{split}

(2.12)

The formulation (
2.11
) also conserves tracer mass
\int_{\Omega}\phi\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}
and squared tracer density (tracer energy/variance)
\int_{\Omega}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}
(for
\kappa_{\phi}=0
) when discretized by a Galerkin method leading to increased stability and accuracy. Lastly, we introduce a formulation that, in combination with the reformulated tracer equations (
2.11
), is shift-invariant and tracer mass, potential energy, kinetic energy, squared tracer density, momentum and angular momentum conserving (SI-MEEDMAC):

{\partial}_{t}{\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{u}}=-\nabla P+\nabla{\cdot}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})^{\top}\right)\right)-\frac{g\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\left(\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}gy\right),

(2.13)

leading to the modified pressure
P=\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}p-\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{u}}+gy+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}gy
. As will be shown in Section
4
, the main novelty of the new formulation (
2.13
) compared to the EMAC formulation (
2.10
) is that the new formulation also conserves potential energy. Such energy conservation is an attractive feature for ocean models and gravity-driven flows in general. The idea of deriving a formulation that conserves potential energy was inspired by
Li et al. [38]
, who achieved this using a different approach.

Due to the use of pressure shifts, the modified directional-do-nothing boundary condition (
2.5b
) must be equivalently rewritten as

\begin{split}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)^{\top}\right)-\left(P+\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{u}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}gy\right){\mathbb I}\right){\boldsymbol{n}}-\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{n}})_{-}{\boldsymbol{u}}=-P_{hyd}{\boldsymbol{n}},\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{oc},\end{split}

(2.14)

where
P_{hyd}
is the modified hydrostatic pressure given by

P_{hyd}=C+\int_{y_{min}}^{y}-\frac{g\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}{\cdot}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}dy,

(2.15)

where
C
is chosen so that
p
is zero on the top of the domain. This leads to the exact same energy estimate as in
[
9
]

Discretization and implicit LES model

3.1

.

We note that the viscosity operators

\begin{split}&\left(-\frac{g\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}g(y-\overline{y}),\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right)\\
&=\left(\frac{g(y-\overline{y})}{\rho_{0}},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}{\cdot}\nabla\delta\rho_{h}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})(\delta\rho_{h}-\overline{\delta\rho_{h}})\right)\\
&=\left(g(y-\overline{y}),-\alpha_{T}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}{\cdot}\nabla T_{h}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})(T_{h}-\overline{T_{h}}))+\beta_{S}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}{\cdot}\nabla S_{h}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})(S_{h}-\overline{S_{h}}))\right),\end{split}

(4.11)

because
\overline{\delta\rho_{h}}
is a constant.
Next, by setting
\mathbf{w}=g(y-\overline{y})
in the tracer update (
3.12a
) for salinity and temperature, we further rewrite (
4.11
) as

\begin{split}\left(-\frac{g\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}g(y-\overline{y}),\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right)\\
=-(g(y-\overline{y}),-\alpha_{T}{\partial}_{t}T_{h}+\beta_{S}{\partial}_{t}S_{h})-(\nabla y,-g\alpha_{T}(\kappa_{T}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{T,h})\nabla T_{h}+g\beta_{S}(\kappa_{S}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{S,h})\nabla S_{h})\\
-(\nabla y-\Pi_{h}\nabla y,-g\alpha_{T}\bbkappa_{T,h,vms}(\nabla T_{h}-\Pi_{h}\nabla T_{h})+g\beta_{S}\bbkappa_{S,h,vms}(\nabla S_{h}-\Pi_{h}\nabla S_{h}))\\
=-(g(y-\overline{y}),-\alpha_{T}{\partial}_{t}T_{h}+\beta_{S}{\partial}_{t}S_{h})-(\nabla y,-g\alpha_{T}(\kappa_{T}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{T,h})\nabla T_{h}+g\beta_{S}(\kappa_{S}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{S,h})\nabla S_{h}),\end{split}

(4.12)

where
\Pi_{h}\nabla y=\nabla y
was used in the last step. This is true because the projection of a constant is the same as the constant. Finally, by applying integration by parts repeatedly, we have that

\begin{split}&\left(-\frac{g\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}g(y-\overline{y}),\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right)\\
&=-(g(y-\overline{y}),-\alpha_{T}{\partial}_{t}T_{h}+\beta_{S}{\partial}_{t}S_{h})-(\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}},-g\alpha_{T}(\kappa_{T}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{T,h})\nabla T_{h}+g\beta_{S}(\kappa_{S}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{S,h})\nabla S_{h})\\
&=-(g(y-\overline{y}),-\alpha_{T}{\partial}_{t}T_{h}+\beta_{S}{\partial}_{t}S_{h})+(g\alpha_{T}(\kappa_{T}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{T,h})\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}},\nabla T_{h})-(g\beta_{S}(\kappa_{S}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{S,h})\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}},\nabla S_{h})\\
&=-(g(y-\overline{y}),-\alpha_{T}{\partial}_{t}T_{h}+\beta_{S}{\partial}_{t}S_{h})-(\nabla{\cdot\,}((\kappa_{T}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{T,h})\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}),g\alpha_{T}T_{h})+(\nabla{\cdot\,}((\kappa_{S}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{S,h})\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}),g\beta_{S}S_{h})\\
&+({\boldsymbol{n}}{\cdot}((\kappa_{T}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{T,h})\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}),g\alpha_{T}T_{h})_{{\partial}\Omega}-({\boldsymbol{n}}{\cdot}((\kappa_{S}{\mathbb I}+\bbkappa_{S,h})\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}),g\beta_{S}S_{h})_{{\partial}\Omega}.\\
\end{split}

(4.13)

Inserting this into (
4.9
) concludes the proof.

∎

Theorem 4.2
.

The stabilized FEM conserves angular momentum in the sense that

{\partial}_{t}\int_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}{\times}{\boldsymbol{x}})_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=-\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{g\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(4.14)

Proof.

We define
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{x}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}
and note that
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}
has the properties
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}=0
and
\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})^{\top}=0
. For a correct definition of the cross product in 2D, the last component of all vectors is extended by 0. Following
[
12
,
27
]
, let
\Omega=\hat{\Omega}\cup\Omega_{s}
where
\hat{\Omega}
is a strictly interior subdomain of
\Omega
. We define the restriction
\chi({\boldsymbol{g}})\in{\mathbfcal V}_{h}
of an arbitrary function
{\boldsymbol{g}}
by setting
\chi({\boldsymbol{g}})={\boldsymbol{g}}
in
\hat{\Omega}
and
\chi({\boldsymbol{g}})=0
in
\Omega_{s}
. We assume that
{\boldsymbol{v}}
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}
are zero in
\Omega_{s}
. We set
{\boldsymbol{v}}=\chi({\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})
and obtain

\begin{split}{\partial}_{t}\int_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}{\times}{\boldsymbol{x}})_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=&({\partial}_{t}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})=-b({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})-b({\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})-((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}){\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})+(P_{h},\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})\\
&-(\gamma_{h}\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})-\frac{1}{2}\Biggl(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})^{\top}\right)\Biggr.\\
&\Biggl.+\sqrt{\bbnu_{h}}\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})^{\top}\right)\sqrt{\bbnu_{h}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}+\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{\top}\Biggr)\\
&-\frac{1}{2}\Biggl(\sqrt{\bbnu_{h,vms}}\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})^{\top}-\Pi_{h}\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}-(\Pi_{h}\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})^{\top}\right)\sqrt{\bbnu_{h,vms}},\Biggr.\\
&\Biggl.\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}+\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{\top}-\Pi_{h}\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}-\Pi_{h}\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{\top}\Biggr)\\
&-\left(\frac{g\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}gy,\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\right)\\
=&b({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})-\left(\frac{g\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}\right)=b({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})-\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{g\delta\rho_{h}}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},\end{split}

(4.15)

where
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}=0
,
\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}+\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}^{\top}=0
,
\Pi_{h}\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}=\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}
, and (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) was used.

Next, by using the definition of the trilinear form (
1.1
) it can be shown that

\begin{split}b({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})&=\frac{1}{2}b({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})+\frac{1}{2}b({\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})=\frac{1}{2}\left((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\right)+\frac{1}{2}((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}){\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})=0,\end{split}

(4.16)

since
\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{i})^{\top}=0
. Inserting this into (
4.15
) concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.1
.

The stabilized tracer update (
3.12a
) is shift-invariant (

), conserves tracer mass

5
Numerical experiments

We run two sets of experiments to validate our numerical method. The first set of experiments are aimed at validating the new theoretical results and the accuracy of the tensor-based stabilization. The purpose of the second experiment set is to test the method in a more realistic setting relevant to ocean circulation and glacial melting. We do this by reproducing one of the idealized Sherard Osborn fjord high-resolution MITgcm simulations from
[
66
]
. MITgcm has a numerical FV kernel for atmospheric and oceanic hydro-thermodynamic flow and has been used in modeling studies relevant to ice-sheet-ocean interactions in Greenland and Antarctica

11

Our FEM is implemented in the open-source finite element library FEniCS

with PETSc
[

The tracer fluxes

\displaystyle F_{S}=

\displaystyle(\gamma_{S}+m_{b})(S_{m}-S_{b}),

\displaystyle F_{T}=

\displaystyle(\gamma_{T}+m_{b})(T_{m}-T_{b}),

\displaystyle T_{b}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}S_{b}+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}p_{b}

\displaystyle m_{b}L+m_{b}c_{i}(T_{b}-T_{i})

\displaystyle=c_{m}\gamma_{T}(T_{m}-T_{b})

\displaystyle m_{b}S_{b}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{S}(S_{m}-S_{b}).

(5.5c)

Here the subscripts

5.2.4
Results and comparison to MITgcm

In Fig.
3
the time-average density distribution, streamlines and melt rates are shown. A buoyant layer of water forms close to the ice (typically referred to as a buoyant plume) and leaves the ice shelf when it reaches neutral buoyancy with respect to the surrounding water. The plume creates an estuarine circulation in the fjord, with an average overturning time
\tau_{0}=11.9
days, which is estimated as

\tau_{0}(t)=2|\Omega|/\int_{y_{min}}^{y_{max}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{u}}(x=21$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{m}$,t)|\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(5.8)

Due to circulation, warm Atlantic water (modeled by the restoring function at the open ocean boundary) flows along the ocean floor towards the glacier, causing it to melt further. The new open ocean boundary conditions, i.e. the modified directional do-nothing condition with a hydrostatic forcing (
2.5b
) let water flow in and out of the fjord.

The melt rate as computed by the three-equation formulation is on average
+17.95

\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{}}^{-1}

)

[

[
66

The higher melt rate can probably be attributed to the residual viscosity sub-grid model, which adds less artificial viscosity than the more diffusive eddy viscosity model of MITgcm. This results in more fine structures and higher variation in the solution, see Fig.

6
Conclusion and further work

This paper presents a continuous Galerkin finite element approximation of the Boussinesq equations suitable for variable density flow in general and ocean modeling and ice-ocean interaction in particular. The new methods are:

•

A new formulation that allows for total energy conservation. The new consistent formulation of the Boussinesq system in combination with the special formulation of the tracer (
2.11
) is shift-invariant and tracer mass, kinetic energy, potential energy, squared tracer density, momentum and angular momentum conserving (SI-MEEDMAC)

{\partial}_{t}{\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{u}}=-\nabla P+\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})^{\top}\right)\right)-\frac{\delta\rho g}{\rho_{0}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\left(\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}gy\right),

(6.1)

with modified pressure
P=\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}p+gy-\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{u}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}gy
. The formulation combines the EMAC formulation with a new force term via
\frac{1}{2}\nabla\left(\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}gy\right)
that enables the FEM to conserve potential energy. This marks an improvement over the EMAC formulation for constant density flow
[
12
]
and the SI-MEDMAC formulation for variable density flow
[
42
]
since now potential energy is also conserved.

•

A new sub-grid scale model that introduces less artificial diffusion compared to the eddy-viscosity models typically used in ocean models. The sub-grid scale model is based on a new symmetric viscosity tensor

\sqrt{\bbnu_{h}}\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h})^{\top}\right)\sqrt{\bbnu_{h}},

(6.2)

which we show conserves angular momentum and dissipates kinetic energy. The method uses dynamic tensor-based viscosity as a sub-grid scale model with coefficients constructed based on the PDE residual.

•

An open ocean boundary condition (
2.14
) that allows for inflow and outflow while conserving energy.

Together, the methods increase accuracy compared to standard methods and allow for the usage of unstructured meshes in glacier-fjord modeling. It compares well to the results of high-resolution MITgcm simulations
[
66
]
of the Sherard Osborn fjord, northern Greenland while allowing for coarser meshes in areas away from the ice and plume and avoiding overly diffusive velocity, temperature and salinity solutions. Future work involves the inclusion of the Coriolis force, investigating more realistic melt models and considering more realistic ice-sheet geometries. The last point involves developing stabilization techniques especially suitable for meshes with bad aspect ratios.
