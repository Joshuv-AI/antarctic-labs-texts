Trust‐building in pediatric hospital medicine discharge
Authors: Victor Do
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHospital discharge is a trust‐sensitive phase of care. This article reframes discharge as a longitudinal process in which families assess not only individual clinicians but also the reliability, coherence, and accountability of the healthcare system across hospitalization. Misalignment between verbal and written communication, unclear responsibility transfer, and unaddressed administrative workload can erode institutional trust, particularly for structurally marginalized families when signals of support are not sustained. Pediatric hospitalists can strengthen trust by aligning communication, responsibility, and support across care phases, anticipating postdischarge complexity, and signaling shared accountability. We propose a longitudinal, trust‐oriented approach to discharge and outline strategies to operationalize this.

INTRODUCTION

A family whose primary language is Tigrinya brings their infant to the hospital with bronchiolitis. Throughout the admission, communication is supported by professional interpretation, and the family feels reassured as their child improves. They trust the team. At discharge, however, they express concern that their child is not yet drinking at baseline. They are told this is expected and will improve. They are sent home with written instructions in English only. The next day, worried and uncertain that improvement is occurring, they return to the emergency department. When asked what happened, the parents respond, “We are just worried and not sure, do we just trust you she will be better?”

In another case, an infant with medical complexity has been hospitalized since birth and is now 9 months old, medically stable, and approaching discharge. The family receives extensive education, new equipment, medication adjustments, and multiple follow‐up appointments. Clinicians emphasize readiness for discharge. The family feels overwhelmed by the administrative and coordination demands awaiting them at home. In subsequent outpatient visits, tensions arise around missed appointments and delayed vaccinations. The family describes feeling unsupported after discharge and uncertain where responsibility lies: “We don't know who to trust since discharge.”

These are not stories of poor intentions or isolated missteps. They illustrate how trust that is shaped through cumulative interactions can quickly change to mistrust at discharge. They also highlight how the absence of explicit, ongoing approaches to sustaining trust across phases of care becomes most visible at the point of transition. For families of hospitalized children, trust develops or erodes through repeated encounters with clinicians, teams, and institutional processes. In pediatric hospital medicine, where children depend on caregivers to interpret information and enact care plans, families' confidence in the health system directly influences clinical outcomes.

Hospitalization often occurs when families' trust is already fragile, particularly for those who have experienced marginalization within healthcare or other institutions. 1 Trust is frequently framed as interpersonal and being rooted in clinician–family relationships. Yet families also experience trust as a property of the system itself. They assess whether the hospital is coherent, reliable, and responsive based on how care is organized, communicated, and transitioned.

Discharge is an important test of that assessment. It concentrates communication, coordination, and the transfer of responsibility from clinicians to families. 2 Because these elements unfold over time, discharge is better understood as a longitudinal process that begins early in admission and extends through the transition home. When understood as a longitudinal process rather than a transactional endpoint, discharge becomes a powerful opportunity either to reinforce or to destabilize institutional trust. This article invites consideration not only of what information is delivered at discharge, but also of how trust is actively built, maintained, or eroded across the trajectory of hospitalization.

TRUST AS A SYSTEM EXPERIENCE

Trust in pediatric hospital medicine is partly interpersonal: a caregiver's confidence in a clinician's competence and intentions. But families encounter more than individuals. They experience workflows, handoffs, documentation practices, multidisciplinary rounds, and evolving care plans. Over time, they infer whether the system itself can be relied upon through patterns that unfold across interactions rather than any single encounter.

Institutional trust reflects beliefs that a healthcare system acts competently, transparently, and in the best interests of patients and families. 3 These beliefs are formed through repeated, often subtle signals embedded in care delivery. Families notice whether messaging is consistent across team members, whether questions receive coherent answers, whether support persists across phases of care, and whether responsibility is shared or quietly shifted. 4 Through these signals, trust becomes cumulative and systemic.

Discharge is where these signals converge. If institutional trust has been building throughout admission, discharge is an opportunity for a consequential appraisal. Families assess whether the coordination attempts experienced in the hospital extend beyond its walls. When language‐concordant bedside conversations are followed by inaccessible written instructions, or when multidisciplinary cooperation gives way to fragmented outpatient coordination, the system may appear less dependable, not because any single interaction failed, but because coherence has diminished across the care trajectory.

This coherence matters acutely in an era of widespread health misinformation and declining public confidence in institutions. 5 Misinformation often exploits ambiguity. When discharge messaging is rushed, inconsistent, or misaligned between verbal and written communication, families may seek clarity elsewhere. 4 Discharge, therefore, does more than transmit clinical instructions; it also signals institutional reliability. Where discharge processes appear organized, transparent, and accountable, recommendations are more likely to be trusted. Where discharge processes appear fragmented or opaque, uncertainty grows even in the presence of accurate guidance.

Understanding trust as a system experience clarifies why discharge demands careful attention. It is not merely the end of hospitalization.

DISCHARGE AS A TRUST SENSITIVE PHASE OF CARE

Discharge is not a discrete event but a process unfolding throughout hospitalization. 6 It encompasses early expectation‐setting, anticipatory guidance, care coordination, education, and relationship‐building, culminating in the transition home. Because it compresses information exchange and responsibility transfer, discharge renders institutional trust highly visible 7 as families interpret whether prior support and communication will persist beyond the hospital setting.

Several features heighten its sensitivity. Complex care plans may be condensed into brief summaries, increasing the risk that essential information is misunderstood or deprioritized. 8 Communication frequently shifts from dialogic bedside discussions to static written instructions that may differ in tone, clarity, or language. Responsibility transitions from multidisciplinary teams to families expected to manage medications, monitor symptoms, coordinate follow‐up, and troubleshoot complications. 9 Simultaneously, families may be navigating equipment procurement and funding approvals. These are tasks that require system literacy that are beyond what many families have and are rarely made explicit during care. All of this is on top of the routine daily tasks families already need to tackle.

When discharge planning is fragmented or task‐focused without attention to trust, families may experience the transition as abrupt or isolating. 9 In contrast, when discharge is approached as a longitudinal trust‐building process, with explicit attention to comprehension, feasibility, and shared responsibility, it reinforces perceptions of reliability and partnership. Operationalizing this approach requires anticipating discharge‐related needs early, revisiting them iteratively, and aligning communication and responsibilities over time rather than concentrating them at the point of transition. Such an approach does not eliminate complexity. It does signal that institutional accountability persists beyond the hospital stay. 9

HOW DISCHARGE PROCESSES CAN UNDERMINE TRUST

Trust erosion during discharge often reflects cumulative system misalignment rather than isolated errors. 10 It emerges when signals that appeared aligned during hospitalization become inconsistent at the point of transition. Families may experience growing strain when communication that is clear and language‐concordant during hospitalization shifts at discharge, such as when interpreted bedside conversations give way to English‐only written instructions dense with medical terminology. 11 In these situations, families are left to reconcile inaccessible or conflicting information on their own, undermining confidence in the system even when individual clinicians have communicated with care. 11

For families of children with medical complexity, discharge frequently involves managing new equipment, medication changes, and multiple follow‐up appointments. 12 When these responsibilities are transferred without clear ownership, contingency planning, or a reliable point of contact, discharge may feel less like a supported transition and more like a sudden withdrawal of system involvement. Families may leave the hospital labeled as “ready,” yet uncertain about who will help when problems arise, 12 or whether prior support will persist beyond discharge.

These experiences disproportionately affect structurally marginalized families, including those who face language barriers, limited health literacy, or constrained access to resources. Over time, repeated exposure to these misalignments can reinforce perceptions that healthcare systems are not designed to function for them. Importantly, this form of trust erosion is rarely captured by standard discharge metrics such as length of stay or readmission rates, yet it shapes how families engage with care in the future, including whether they seek help, adhere to recommendations, and how they approach future healthcare encounters. 13

THE ROLE OF PEDIATRIC HOSPITALISTS IN BUILDING TRUST

Pediatric hospitalists influence how discharge processes are framed and enacted. While hospitalists may not control all structural elements of discharge, they can shape how readiness, responsibility, and uncertainty are communicated to families. 14 , 15 , 16 In doing so, they influence the signals families use to determine whether the system remains reliable beyond discharge. How clinicians name ongoing complexity, administrative workload, and limits of support can either reinforce confidence or deepen families' sense of being left to manage alone at the point when responsibility shifts.

Hospitalists can support trust by treating discharge planning as iterative and ongoing, eliciting families' concerns about managing care at home, and anticipating administrative workload related to equipment, funding, and follow‐up. 14 , 15 , 16 This includes introducing key discharge expectations early in admission, revisiting them over time, and aligning communication at discharge with prior discussions. Aligning verbal discussions with written materials, particularly when language or literacy barriers are present, and reinforcing clear pathways for postdischarge support signal that responsibility is shared 14 , 15 , 16 and that system accountability persists beyond the hospital stay. Explicitly clarifying who to contact, what to expect, and how uncertainty will be managed helps make this ongoing support visible. Together, these practices communicate institutional reliability without requiring idealized systems or additional resources by ensuring that signals of support remain consistent across the trajectory of care. Importantly, this approach extends beyond established discharge best practices by focusing not only on the completion of discrete tasks but on how these interactions are experienced by families as coherent, reliable, and sustained across the trajectory of care.

IMPLICATIONS FOR PEDIATRIC HOSPITAL MEDICINE

Routine discharge processes convey powerful signals about institutional trustworthiness. Whether families feel heard when raising last‐minute concerns or supported in navigating postdischarge complexity reflects not only individual clinician behavior but how systems define readiness, continuity, and shared accountability. These signals are shaped not only at the point of discharge, but across the trajectory of hospitalization. Discharge processes and trust building.

Reframing discharge as a trust‐sensitive process positions trustworthiness as a core outcome of pediatric hospital care alongside safety and efficiency. Conceptualizing discharge as a longitudinal, trust‐oriented practice offers a structured approach to operationalizing this goal in routine care. This perspective is particularly urgent as hospitals serve increasingly diverse populations and public confidence in healthcare institutions declines. A trust lens invites more rigorous examination of how clarity, coordination, and accountability are experienced by families, particularly how these elements remain consistent or fragment across phases of care, which are dimensions often invisible to traditional outcomes metrics.

Discharge is one of the moments when institutional trust becomes tangible. By recognizing it as such, pediatric hospital medicine can strengthen equity, reinforce partnership, and contribute meaningfully to broader efforts to understand how trust in healthcare systems is built, strained, and restored through everyday clinical processes.
