Exploring Efficiency Frontiers of Thinking Budget in Medical Reasoning: Scaling Laws between Computational Resources and Reasoning Quality


Abstract
This study presents the first comprehensive evaluation of thinking budget mechanisms in medical reasoning tasks, revealing fundamental scaling laws between computational resources and reasoning quality. We systematically evaluated two major model families, Qwen3 (1.7B to 235B parameters) and DeepSeek-R1 (1.5B to 70B parameters), across 15 medical datasets spanning diverse specialties and difficulty levels. Through controlled experiments with thinking budgets ranging from zero to unlimited tokens, we establish logarithmic scaling relationships where accuracy improvements follow a predictable pattern with both thinking budget and model size. Our findings identify three distinct efficiency regimes: high-efficiency (0 to 256 tokens) suitable for real-time applications, balanced (256 to 512 tokens) offering optimal cost-performance tradeoffs for routine clinical support, and high-accuracy (above 512 tokens) justified only for critical diagnostic tasks. Notably, smaller models demonstrate disproportionately larger benefits from extended thinking, with 15 to 20 % improvements compared to 5 to 10 % for larger models, suggesting a complementary relationship where thinking budget provides greater relative benefits for capacity-constrained models. Domain-specific patterns emerge clearly, with neurology and gastroenterology requiring significantly deeper reasoning processes than cardiovascular or respiratory medicine. The consistency between Qwen3 native thinking budget API and our proposed truncation method for DeepSeek-R1 validates the generalizability of thinking budget concepts across architectures. These results establish thinking budget control as a critical mechanism for optimizing medical AI systems, enabling dynamic resource allocation aligned with clinical needs while maintaining the transparency essential for healthcare deployment.

Experimental Design

We conducted comprehensive experiments using two prominent model families that support thinking mechanisms. The Qwen3 series
[
4
]
encompasses models ranging from 1.7B to 235B parameters, featuring native thinking budget support that allows direct control over reasoning depth through the
thinking_budget
parameter. The DeepSeek-R1 series
[
68
]
comprises models from 1.5B to 70B parameters. Unlike Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1 models lack native thinking budget control, necessitating a novel methodological approach to ensure fair comparison.

To address this challenge, we developed a truncation-based approach for DeepSeek-R1 models that maintains experimental rigor while enabling controlled comparison. Our method begins by generating complete thinking processes using each model’s native capabilities, then systematically truncates the thinking content to match desired budget constraints. The process involves extracting thinking content between
<think>
and
</think>
tags and truncating to specified token limits (64, 128, 256, 512, 1024). For the “None” budget condition, all thinking content is removed entirely, while the “inf” budget retains the complete original thinking process. This methodology ensures that all thinking content originates from the same model being evaluated, maintaining consistency across experimental conditions.

The thinking budget parameters we employed span a wide range to capture the full spectrum of reasoning behaviors. The “None” condition provides baseline performance without any thinking process, while token budgets of 64, 128, 256, 512, and 1024 allow us to observe how performance scales with increasing computational investment. The “inf” (unlimited) budget reveals each model’s maximum reasoning capability when unconstrained by computational limits.

Our evaluation encompasses 15 carefully curated medical datasets spanning diverse specialties and difficulty levels, building upon established medical AI benchmarks
[
46
,
69
,
70
,
71

. The datasets are stratified into two primary difficulty tiers reflecting real-world medical practice hierarchies. Chief physician level datasets represent the top 200 most challenging questions in each specialty, encompassing advanced cardiovascular disease diagnosis and treatment, complex gastrointestinal disorders, blood disorders and malignancies, complicated infectious disease cases, advanced kidney disease management, complex neurological conditions, and advanced pulmonary diseases. These datasets test the limits of medical reasoning with cases that would challenge experienced specialists.

Attending physician-level datasets comprise the top 200 difficulty questions, focusing on standard clinical scenarios including routine cardiovascular cases, common gastrointestinal disorders, basic hematological conditions, standard infectious diseases, common kidney disorders, basic neurological conditions, and common respiratory diseases. This tier represents the breadth of cases encountered in typical clinical practice. Additionally, we include the American Journal of Kidney Diseases (AJKD) dataset, which features research-oriented nephrology questions derived from cutting-edge medical literature, providing a unique perspective on how models handle emerging medical knowledge.

Each dataset contains 200 carefully validated multiple-choice questions designed to test different aspects of medical reasoning, from pattern recognition and differential diagnosis to treatment selection and prognostic assessment. This comprehensive coverage ensures our findings generalize across the full spectrum of medical decision-making scenarios.

Our primary evaluation metric is accuracy, defined as the percentage of correctly answered questions:

\text{Accuracy}=\frac{\text{Number of Correct Answers}}{\text{Total Number of Questions}}\times 100\%.

To quantify performance improvements, we define:

\Delta P(T_{b})=P(T_{b})-P(T_{0}),

where
P(T_{b})
represents performance at thinking budget
T_{b}
and
P(T_{0})
is baseline performance without thinking.

We also analyze the thinking token ratio:

R_{\text{thinking}}=\frac{T_{\text{output}}}{T_{\text{input}}},

which indicates how models scale their reasoning depth relative to question complexity.

This straightforward metric provides clear insights into model performance while enabling direct comparison across different thinking budget configurations. Beyond raw accuracy, we conduct extensive analyses of thinking token distributions to understand how models allocate computational resources, performance scaling patterns to identify optimal budget ranges through the optimization problem:

\max_{T_{b}}\frac{\text{Accuracy}(T_{b})}{\text{Cost}(T_{b})}\text{ subject to }T_{b}\leq T_{\text{budget}},

and saturation points where additional thinking yields diminishing returns, characterized by
\frac{\partial\text{Accuracy}}{\partial T_{b}}<\epsilon
for small
\epsilon>0
.

The experimental protocol ensures rigorous and reproducible evaluation across all conditions. For each model-dataset-budget combination, we sequentially process all 200 questions, applying the appropriate thinking budget control mechanism (native API for Qwen3 or truncation for DeepSeek-R1). Model responses are generated with controlled thinking depth, and answers are extracted and validated against ground truth. Throughout this process, we meticulously record both accuracy metrics and thinking token statistics for subsequent analysis.

Our statistical analyses encompass multiple dimensions of model behavior. We examine token length distributions for both questions and thinking processes to understand the relationship between input complexity and reasoning depth. Performance comparisons across model sizes within each family reveal scaling behaviors, while cross-family analyses at fixed thinking budgets illuminate architectural differences. We also identify efficiency frontiers for each dataset, determining the optimal trade-offs between computational investment and performance gains.

Our comprehensive evaluation encompassed 630 distinct experimental conditions, systematically varying across model families, model sizes, datasets, and thinking budget levels. This extensive experimental design enables robust conclusions about thinking budget mechanisms in medical reasoning contexts.

Both Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1 families demonstrate consistent performance improvements with increased thinking budgets. Larger models outperform smaller ones (e.g., Qwen3:235B achieves 62.5% vs 45% for 1.7B on chief cardiovascular), with most models showing rapid improvement up to 256 tokens and diminishing returns beyond 512 tokens.

Without thinking, Qwen3 models generally outperform DeepSeek-R1 counterparts, but both families show 15-20% improvements for smaller models and 5-10% for larger ones when thinking is enabled.

Token distribution analysis shows the chief physician questions average 400-600 tokens versus 250-350 for the attending level. Models adapt their thinking depth with ratios from 2.3x to 6.2x of question length, generating reasoning traces from 168 to over 15,000 tokens when unconstrained.

The empirical data reveal consistent scaling relationships across all datasets, which we model as:

\text{Accuracy}(T_{b},M_{s})=\alpha\log(T_{b}+1)+\beta\log(M_{s})+\gamma+\epsilon,

where
T_{b}
is the thinking budget,
M_{s}
is the model size in billions of parameters,
\alpha\approx 0.08
,
\beta\approx 0.12
,
\gamma
varies by dataset difficulty, and
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
represents model-specific variance with
\sigma\approx 0.02
.

The marginal utility of thinking tokens follows:

\frac{\partial\text{Accuracy}}{\partial T_{b}}=\frac{\alpha}{T_{b}+1}.

This derivative reveals why performance improvements diminish with larger budgets. At
T_{b}=255
, the marginal improvement is approximately
\frac{0.08}{256}\approx 0.0003
per token, while at
T_{b}=31
it is
\frac{0.08}{32}\approx 0.0025
per token—an 8× difference in efficiency.

Three distinct efficiency regimes emerge from our analysis: a high-efficiency regime (0-256 tokens) characterized by rapid accuracy improvements with efficiency
E>0.0003
suitable for real-time applications, a balanced regime (256-512 tokens) offering optimal performance-cost tradeoffs with
0.0001<E<0.0003
for routine clinical decision support, and a high-accuracy regime (512+ tokens) where marginal improvements with
E<0.0001
may justify additional computational costs only for critical diagnostic tasks.

The efficiency frontier can be formally characterized by the Pareto-optimal set:

\mathcal{F}^{*}=\{(T_{b},A(T_{b})):\nexists T^{\prime}_{b}<T_{b}\text{ such that }A(T^{\prime}_{b})\geq A(T_{b})\},

where
A(T_{b})
represents accuracy at thinking budget
T_{b}
. The optimal thinking budget for a given cost constraint
C_{\max}
is:

T^{*}_{b}=\arg\max_{T_{b}}\left\{A(T_{b}):C(T_{b})\leq C_{\max}\right\},

where the cost function
C(T_{b})=c_{0}+c_{1}T_{b}
includes both fixed overhead
c_{0}
and per-token cost
c_{1}
.

5
Limitations and Discussion

While our study provides comprehensive insights into thinking budget mechanisms in medical reasoning, several important limitations merit discussion. Our truncation-based approach for DeepSeek-R1 models, though ensuring consistency by using each model’s own generated content, may not perfectly replicate native thinking budget control. The truncation process could potentially interrupt reasoning chains at suboptimal points, possibly underestimating model capabilities under constrained budgets. We can quantify this truncation effect as:

\text{Loss}_{\text{truncation}}=\int_{T_{b}}^{T_{\text{full}}}p(t)\cdot v(t)\,dt,

where
p(t)
is the probability of critical reasoning at token position
t
and
v(t)
is the value of that reasoning. This methodological compromise was necessary given the absence of native thinking budget APIs in DeepSeek-R1, but future work should investigate more sophisticated truncation strategies that preserve reasoning coherence by minimizing
\text{Loss}_{\text{truncation}}
.

The scope of our evaluation, while extensive across 15 medical specialties, remains limited to multiple-choice questions. Real clinical reasoning encompasses far more complex tasks including open-ended problem solving, differential diagnosis generation, and treatment planning that cannot be fully captured by MCQ formats. Additionally, our exclusive focus on medical reasoning, though providing deep domain-specific insights, constrains the generalizability of our findings. Thinking budget requirements and scaling patterns may differ substantially in other domains such as mathematics, coding, or general reasoning tasks, warranting cross-domain validation studies.

From a technical perspective, we acknowledge the absence of a detailed computational cost analysis in our study. While we demonstrate clear performance improvements with increased thinking budgets, the relationship between thinking tokens and actual inference time or computational cost is non-linear and highly dependent on hardware configurations, batch processing strategies, and implementation details. Furthermore, our results suggest domain-specific optimal thinking budgets, but we currently lack a principled method for automatically determining these thresholds. The empirical approach we employed, while effective for research purposes, may not scale to all deployment scenarios.

Clinical deployment presents unique challenges that extend beyond our experimental setup. While thinking processes enhance interpretability—a critical requirement for medical AI—very long thinking traces exceeding 1000 tokens can overwhelm clinicians, creating a tension between comprehensive reasoning and practical usability. Our evaluation focuses primarily on final accuracy metrics but does not systematically analyze error types or reasoning failure modes. Understanding why models make specific errors under different thinking budget constraints could inform more targeted improvements and help build clinician trust.

Looking forward, several promising research directions emerge from our work. Adaptive thinking mechanisms that dynamically adjust reasoning depth based on question complexity indicators could significantly enhance efficiency. Our analysis of thinking token distributions reveals that models already exhibit some adaptive behavior, but making this explicit and controllable could yield substantial benefits. Additionally, current thinking processes are monolithic, but structured multi-stage reasoning with intermediate validation points could improve both efficiency and accuracy while providing natural breakpoints for clinical review.

Despite these limitations, our work establishes thinking budget mechanisms as a viable approach for controlling reasoning depth in medical AI systems. The consistent scaling laws we observe across diverse medical specialties suggest fundamental properties of how language models leverage computational resources for complex reasoning. As models continue to grow in capability, mechanisms for intelligently allocating computational resources will become increasingly critical for responsible deployment in high-stakes domains. The thinking budget paradigm represents an important step toward clinical AI systems that can dynamically balance performance, efficiency, and interpretability based on situational requirements.

6
Conclusion

This study presents the first comprehensive analysis of thinking budget mechanisms in medical reasoning tasks, establishing clear scaling laws between computational resources and reasoning quality. Through extensive experiments across 15 medical datasets with two major model families, we demonstrate that controllable reasoning depth represents a powerful paradigm for optimizing AI performance in clinical applications.

Our work establishes several fundamental insights that advance both theoretical understanding and practical deployment of reasoning-enabled AI systems. We empirically demonstrate logarithmic scaling relationships between thinking budget allocation and model accuracy, identifying clear efficiency frontiers at 256-512 tokens for most medical tasks. This finding provides crucial guidance for healthcare institutions seeking to balance computational costs with diagnostic accuracy. The consistency of scaling patterns between Qwen3’s native thinking budget API and our proposed truncation method for DeepSeek-R1 validates the generalizability of thinking budget concepts across different model architectures, opening new avenues for research on models without native thinking support.

The domain-specific patterns we uncover reveal that medical specialties differ significantly in their computational requirements. Neurology and gastroenterology consistently demand deeper reasoning processes, while cardiovascular and respiratory medicine often achieve strong performance with moderate thinking budgets. The aforementioned phenomena may be attributed to several factors: 1) the absence of a one-to-one correspondence between clinical symptoms and pathological lesions; 2) the delayed therapeutic response of certain interventions, which limits the reliability of diagnostic trials in assessing diagnostic accuracy; and 3) the predominant training of current models on datasets comprising common and typical disease presentations, resulting in insufficient processing protocols or standardized guidelines for rare or complex conditions. These factors align with the findings of this study, which indicate a notable performance disparity between LLMs in handling simple versus complex clinical diagnostic tasks. This discrepancy inevitably increases the number of reasoning steps required, as well as the volume of clinical information that healthcare professionals must gather and evaluate. It is important to note that this study did not assess the capacity of the model in differential diagnosis or subsequent diagnostic planning. Previous research has reported suboptimal performance of LLM in differential diagnosis in specific datasets, possibly due to incomplete information from the data set, particularly regarding the interrelationships of the disease and the absence of negative symptoms and physical signs. Furthermore, LLMs generally exhibit a conservative approach to treatment recommendations, which may not always align with real-world clinical practices. Therefore, final decisions regarding the adoption of more aggressive therapeutic strategies should remain within the discretion of qualified clinicians. These insights can guide the development of specialized medical AI systems tailored to specific clinical domains.

Furthermore, the complementary relationship between model size and thinking depth—where smaller models benefit disproportionately from extended thinking while larger models achieve high performance even with constrained budgets—suggests that optimal deployment strategies must consider both model selection and thinking budget configuration.The practical implications of our findings extend directly to clinical deployment scenarios. Independent studies have explored the application of artificial intelligence in healthcare, particularly in clinical practice and medical research, to generate reliable predictive probabilities. Several studies have demonstrated the diagnostic accuracy of LLMs in clinical settings, particularly in managing extreme or time-sensitive scenarios that involve the rapid integration of complex clinical information. However, significant limitations persist in their clinical applicability, especially regarding the classification and severity stratification of diseases, functions that are critical in real-world medical practice. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that the inclusion of nondeterministic sociodemographic variables, such as race, gender, income level, and educational background, within large language models may result in biased or harmful output. To address this concern, the EquityGuard framework has been proposed as a mitigation strategy to reduce the influence of sensitive attributes on model decision-making. Before the present study, existing literature has emphasized the value of active reasoning strategies, particularly those employing a secondary evaluation layer, to enhance the error correction capabilities of LLMs and improve their reliability as clinical decision support tools. , enabling physicians to verify AI reasoning and build trust through understanding. Clear efficiency frontiers allow organizations to quantitatively balance performance requirements against computational costs, moving beyond ad hoc deployment strategies to evidence-based resource management. Transparent reasoning processes are closer to clinical practice, especially in fields such as imaging and pathology, focusing on lesions and outputting natural language to facilitate the alignment of LLM’s focus with clinical thinking and avoid the black box effect. However, when judgment logic becomes strong evidence, it will help improve the efficiency of diagnosis and treatment, including diagnosis and treatment classification and risk stratification. From a clinical responsibility perspective, when disputes occur, it is possible to trace whether the judgment is due to model bias or data errors in artificial intelligence. However, as mentioned above, information overload in explanations remains a current challenge, but the current push for a shift in medical decision-making towards an evidence-driven paradigm is a hot topic for LLM.

Looking forward, this work opens several promising research directions. Adaptive thinking mechanisms that automatically adjust reasoning depth based on question complexity could further optimize resource utilization while maintaining diagnostic accuracy. Moving beyond monolithic thinking processes to structured, multi-stage reasoning with intermediate validation points could improve both efficiency and interpretability, providing natural checkpoints for clinical review. Extending our methodology to other high-stakes domains such as legal reasoning, financial analysis, or scientific research could reveal universal principles of thinking budget optimization that transcend specific applications.

The thinking budget paradigm represents a fundamental advance in making AI reasoning both controllable and interpretable—two properties essential for deployment in high-stakes medical settings. As language models continue to grow in capability, the ability to dynamically adjust reasoning depth becomes not just an optimization technique but a necessity for responsible deployment. By establishing clear relationships between computational investment and reasoning quality, we enable a new generation of medical AI systems that can adapt to diverse clinical needs while maintaining the transparency and efficiency demanded by real-world healthcare environments.

The future of medical AI lies not merely in developing larger models or accumulating more training data, but in creating intelligent systems that can allocate computational resources in alignment with clinical needs. This study demonstrates that with appropriate control mechanisms, AI can provide both powerful analytical capabilities and practical utility for medical decision-making. As the field progresses toward increasingly sophisticated reasoning capabilities, thinking budget mechanisms will play a crucial role in bridging the gap between laboratory benchmarks and clinical impact, ensuring that advances in AI translate into tangible improvements in patient care.

The patient’s eyes stare at the tip of the nose because:

The patient is an 84-year-old male with hypertension who developed sudden left hemiplegia, aphasia, and vomiting. Key finding: both eyes staring at the tip of the nose, indicating fixed downward gaze (vertical gaze palsy). CT shows right thalamic hemorrhage breaking into ventricles.

The question asks for the ABCD score, which is used to predict stroke risk after TIA. Let me calculate each component:

A - Age: The patient is 68 years old. In the ABCD score, age
\geq
60 years gets 1 point. So A = 1 point.

B - Blood pressure: The patient’s BP is 135/85 mmHg. For the ABCD score, BP
\geq
140/90 mmHg gets 1 point. Since 135/85 is less than 140/90, B = 0 points.

- Unilateral weakness = 2 points
- Speech disturbance without weakness = 1 point
- Other symptoms = 0 points

\geq

-

Total ABCD score = A + B + C + D + D = 1 + 0 + 2 + 0 + 1 = 4 points.

Appendix A.4
Implications for Medical AI Systems

These three cases collectively demonstrate several critical insights for medical AI deployment. First, the complexity of medical reasoning extends far beyond simple pattern matching—successful diagnosis and treatment planning require temporal analysis, comprehensive differential consideration, and nuanced understanding of therapeutic options. Second, thinking budget provides a controllable mechanism for ensuring thorough analysis in complex cases while maintaining efficiency for routine diagnoses. Third, the transparent reasoning processes enable clinicians to verify not just the final answer but the logic path taken, building trust through interpretability. Understanding these patterns of improvement—from incomplete differentials to comprehensive assessments, from missed treatments to full therapeutic planning—can guide both system design and clinical integration strategies. Most importantly, these examples show that with appropriate thinking budget allocation, AI systems can achieve expert-level performance on challenging medical cases while maintaining the transparency required for clinical acceptance.
