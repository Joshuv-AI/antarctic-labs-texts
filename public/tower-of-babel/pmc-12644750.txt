Effect on three-dimensional collective cell organization of minimal curvature surfaces
Authors: Mariana S. Flores-Jimenez, Alejandro Garcia-Gonzalez, Marifer Vargas-Camacho, Rita Q. Fuentes-Aguilar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Collective cell organization can be influenced by surface curvature, as studied in cylindrical, spherical, and sinusoidal surfaces; however, hyperbolic geometries have been overlooked due to their biofabrication complexity, despite their presence in biological structures, and their potential role in minimizing cell bending energy. Therefore, understanding cell behavior in complex-hyperbolic geometries is important to design in vitro biomimetic platforms and scaffolds. This work studies the role of curvature on collective cell spatiotemporal growth and adhesion, using the gyroid, a minimal periodic hyperbolic surface. The gyroid biofabrication has been limited by its interconnected curved structure, which is solved here by using its fundamental unit of symmetry, parametrized in terms of mean and Gaussian curvatures, achieving its biofabrication with>90% of resolution, allowing the exploration of its effect in guiding cell behavior, isolating the curvature variable from substrate stiffness and extracellular ligands. Three cell lines were studied: two epithelial-like (HEPG2 and HEK293), and one fibroblastic (NIH3T3), using superficial cell seeding and cell hydrogel encapsulation. It was observed by confocal microscopy that the gyroid was able to orient and guide the cells cytoskeleton in the direction to minimize their energy, showing less nuclei compression and eliciting more confluency than in semi-spheres and flat surfaces.

Introduction

Surface curvature can influence several physiological processes, and in combination with physical extracellular matrix (ECM) properties as stiffness and viscoelasticity, it enables formation of tissues and biological functions1, at different scales, ranging from forming membranes at sub-cellular scales (10 nm to 100 nm), affecting spatio-temporal orientation and adhesion at cellular scales (10 µm to 20 µm), and functionalizing tissues at 100 µmm to 1mm2,3. Curvature can be described as a local property of a surface in terms of the principal curvatures at a point, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{1}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{2}$$\end{document}, corresponding to the maximum and minimum values of the inverse of the radius of the osculating circle through all possible normal planes. This yields the definition of mean curvature H = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/2 (k_{1}+k_{2})$$\end{document}, and Gaussian curvature K = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{1}k_{2}$$\end{document}4. The sign of K identifies the elliptical shape when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K > 0$$\end{document}, cylindrical when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K = 0$$\end{document}, and hyperbolic for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K < 0$$\end{document}. While H is an extrinsic measure that represents convex (H \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>0$$\end{document}) and concave (H \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<0$$\end{document}) regions. In tissues, their structure is highly dependent on Gaussian and mean curvature distributions, contributing to geometry, surface tension, and surface-to-volume ratios.

Attachment orientation and collective organization of cells are two main responses to curvature, with a general preference for concavity due to energy minimization5. These responses have been studied mostly in elliptical and cylindrical geometries due to their simplicity in biofabrication, however, several anatomical structures are more complex and rely in hyperbolic geometries, such as mammary lobes, intestinal villi, alveoli, branching arteries and colon diverticula4,6. It has been reported that hyperboloid structures can induce cytoskeleton reorganization of human Mesenchymal Stem Cells (hMSCs), presenting an elongated morphology along the convex direction7, and the spatiotemporal organization of preosteoblast (MC3T3-E1) can be affected by both Euclidean and non-Euclidean surfaces8; a more uniform coverage of F-actin is presented in concave regions, but in general, more coverage is found in hyperbolic surfaces than in elliptic ones. However, it is reported that at extended time points (8 days), cells may be able to attach in regions with H \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$> 0$$\end{document}. The influence of concave surfaces in cells can be mechanically correlated with contact area9, since in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H < 0$$\end{document}, the cell body tends to be pulled upward in the z-direction. For cylinders, the F-actin in fibroblasts and epithelial cells aligns with the axial direction to avoid its bending, while it is randomly distributed on flat surfaces10. The effect of in-plane and out-of-plane curvature in epithelial cells was studied in hydrogel bowl-shaped 3D microwells. In-plane curvature exerted contractile forces that drove the centripetal orientation of nuclei. The out-of-plane convex edge led to vertical nuclei orientation, while the increasing out-of-plane curvature related to flat and elongated nuclei6. The role of out-of-plane curvature was studied in terms of proliferation using Madin-Darby Kidney cells (MDCK) and toroidal hydrogel structures. The toroid presents K\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$> 0$$\end{document} on the outside and K\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 0$$\end{document} on the inside; however, they found that proliferation depended more on cell density rather than on K11.

Minimal surfaces have zero mean curvature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H = 0$$\end{document}) and form energy and area minimizing structures, reducing surface tension4. An example of these surfaces is the catenoid, which can guide collective cell organization and directs the alignment of the cells cytoskeleton following a helicoid pattern5. However, a catenoid cannot form porous and interconnected scaffolds. For its part, the gyroid, also a minimal surface with negative Gaussian curvature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K < 0$$\end{document}), can divide the enclosed volume into two equal parts, forming interconnected porous structures and complying with curvature conditions desired in anatomy (hyperbolic and energy-minimization)12. In addition, the gyroid unit cubic cell can be formed by a fundamental unit of symmetry13, which facilitates its study and biofabrication.

It has been shown that gyroid-based scaffolds can replicate key features of the extracellular matrix, favoring cell attachment, adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell proliferation14 and osteogenic differentiation15. Due to its interconnection and facility to vary the pore size, it can promote bone ingrowth and mimic bone mechanical strength16,17. Furthermore, compared to other structures, such as square grids, gyroid scaffolds elicited more cell infiltration and bone formation thanks to the high surface area-to-volume ratio18, and even neovascularization19, making them a promising geometry choice for in vitro platforms.

In this work, the effect of the gyroid’s curvature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H = 0$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K < 0$$\end{document}) on collective cell organization is compared with flat and concave semi-sphere surfaces, by proposing a methodology for fabrication of the gyroid fundamental unit. For this, three cell lines were used: two with epithelial-like morphology (HEPG2 and HEK293) and one fibroblast (NIH3T3). Two types of substrates, with different stiffness, were used: one based on mold-casting PDMS and one based on a 3D extrusion bioprinting alginate-gelatin hydrogel, a blend commonly used in hydrogel scaffolds applications20,21. For the PDMS substrates, two ECM ligands were chosen: collagen type I22,23, and gelatin24. Finally, two cell seeding methods were tested: superficial (by sessile drop) and hydrogel-embedded, to study the influence of gyroid curvature when cells are encapsulated and, therefore, surrounded by the extracellular matrix (ECM). These study factors were selected to isolate the curvature effect from substrate stiffness, ECM ligand, and seeding method, and the effect was observed by three outcomes: the cell collective growth and confluency, cell attachment (F-actin filaments orientation), and nuclei orientation and polarization. The observations were made through confocal microscopy imaging.

Cell growth increases in gyroid minimal surface and follows changes in both mean and Gaussian curvatures

Cell confluency and cell growth rate (GR cell number/days), were calculated for each gyroid configuration (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${g_{1}-g_{4}}$$\end{document}), using DAPI-confocal images projected at the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x-y$$\end{document} plane. Each image was analyzed by 100 regions of interest (ROIs) based on the original NURBS mesh divisions as shown in Fig. 2a. To correlate cell growth rate with gyroid curvature, the growth rate (GR) was calculated at four time points (days 1, 3, 5, and 8) by determining the slope between the time points and the nuclei count, for all seeding conditions (Fig. S5). Examples of achieved cell density are shown in Supplementary Fig. S6a (for day 3), and Fig. S6b,c (for day 8). All nuclei analysis were performed in images with 63x magnification, as presented in Supplementary Fig. S7a–c. To determine the correlation between the maximum direction of change of GR and the maximum direction of change of H and K, the gradient vector field for these scalar functions was numerically calculated, this is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla GR(x.y)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla H(x,y)$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla K(x.y)$$\end{document}. Every gradient vector at each point (x.y) was normalized by its magnitude to obtain a unitary vector in the maximum direction of change. Then, the inner product between these unitary vectors was calculated, given the correlation from -1 to 1 between the maximum directions of change. Then, the GR was correlated with the parameters H and K by computing their vector fields and the inner products between them, following a standardization from -1 to 1. Cell confluency (surface coverage by cells) was also analyzed with the DAPI stained images, estimating the total area covered at each ROI. In Fig. 2g, the plots for average confluency percentage across the surface, at day 8, are reported. For all cell lines and geometry configurations, a general trend with higher confluency was observed in the PDMS-collagen substrate. For epithelial cell lines (HEPG2 and HEK293), more confluency was observed for the gyroid without any deformation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta = 0$$\end{document}, (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{2}$$\end{document}), with a preference for the softest substrate (hydrogel) by the HEK293, and for the stiffest substrate (PDMS) by the HEPG2. For the fibroblastic cell line (NIH3T3), the highest confluency percentage was observed in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{4}$$\end{document} gyroid configuration, which had the most variation in H. In the HEPG2 cell line it was also identified a more uniform surface coverage in the gyroid geometries, particularly in the non-deformed one (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{2}$$\end{document}), than in the semi-sphere, as can be observed in Fig. 2d–f, where cells covered almost the entire surface of the FP, but were primarily grouped at the bottom of the semi-sphere. In Fig. 2e, it can also be observed that more confluency is present in the stiffest substrate (PDMS-collagen), than in the softest one (hydrogel), as observed in Fig. 2d; however, in both cases, the coverage is more uniform than in the semi-sphere.

A live/dead assay was also performed to check the cell viability through the eight days of the study, a summary of the viability percentage is reported in Supplementary Table S4. In addition to count for live and dead cells, this assay allowed to observe attachment patterns of the complete cell (through membrane and not by single organelle level) at the collective scale. As can be seen in Supplementary Fig. S14c–e, epithelial cells preferred the bottom of the wells after one day of seeding, while the fibroblastic line is able to attach to the hyperbolic bridge (Fig. S14a). After eight days, the HEPG2 line in particular is able to distribute through the complete well following the in-plane pattern (Fig. 3f), while the fibroblastic line elongates across the entire surface (Fig. 3e), with a less uniform distribution than the HEPG2 line. In general, a viability of more than 90\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%$$\end{document} was achieved for all superficially seeded experiments, as observed in Fig. 3d–f, Supplementary Figs. S14a–f, S15a–i, and in Supplementary Table S4, while 85\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%$$\end{document} viability was achieved in embedded cells (Fig. S15j–l). In addition, the live/dead staining samples allowed us to visualize the cell sheet formation in the HEPG2 line, mostly in the ones seeded in PDMS-Collagen, where they detached at day 8, at regions with more negative H values, as observed in Supplementary Fig. S16a–d.

Since the cells behavior can vary depending the degree of ECM surrounding, we also revised the confluency and viability in hydrogel-embedded cells. Gyroid in-plane curvature guides collective nuclei orientation in epithelial-like cells

The orientation and polarization of the nuclei were calculated at each region of interest (ROI), using the 63x magnified images, as the example in Fig. 4a. The nuclei’ polarization was calculated as the ratio between the major and minor axes of the ellipsoid used to approximate the shape of each nucleus by image analysis. This gave us the aspect ratio; larger values indicate flat/ellipsoid nuclei shape, while rounder nuclei are represented by aspect ratios close to 1. In general, a lower aspect ratio was found in the softest substrate (hydrogel), as observed in the plots of Fig. 4d–f, for all cell lines and curvature configurations, except for the NIH3T3 line seeded in the flat configuration (where the aspect ratio was higher in the hydrogel. Discussion

In this work, the effect of gyroid curvature on collective cell behavior was evaluated, varying the mean H and K curvatures as the primary parameters, which were controlled by the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document} parameter to deform the gyroid surface. For this, a representative geometry was developed based on the fundamental unit of symmetry of the gyroid (FP). The FP was fabricated by 3D hydrogel-extrusion bioprinting and PDMS mold casting, both showing a resolution with less than 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%$$\end{document} error from the designs. For the bioprinted FPs, the resolution was achieved by the implementation of a new methodology, consisting on using a porous substrate loaded with calcium ions, as printer bed, to crosslink a 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%$$\end{document} alginate-5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%$$\end{document} gelatin hydrogel, allowing the non-planar deposition of the ink. The Young’s modulus of the crosslinked hydrogel was 10 kPa, a value that lies within the range of common hydrogels used for cell culture (0.14 to 50 kPa)26,34. The estimated mesh size was of 2.63 nm, a value below common reported sizes (5-50 nm)27,35. This small value can indicate a strong crosslinked and stable mesh but might hinder the free movement of embedded cells. For its part, Young’s modulus of the PDMS substrates was of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} 1.47 MPa, allowing the comparison of the H and K effects, between different stiffness. Two cell seeding strategies were tested: superficial and hydrogel encapsulation. For the first case, three different substrates were analyzed: alg4-gel5 hydrogel, and PDMS with two different coatings: collagen type I, and gelatin. From the F-actin and nuclei polarization analysis, it can be said that the substrate stiffness is more relevant to cell shape and attachment than the ECM ligand, since both PDMS substrates presented similar morphology in terms of F-actin filaments orientation, but they were different from the cell morphology in the hydrogel substrate. In general, cells were rounder in softer substrates independently of H and K variation.

From the cell growth rate (GR) and cell confluency analysis, it was identified that cells initially preferred regions near the bottom of the wells, either for FPs or semi-spheres, however, for the FPs, after eight days of culture, it was noticed that they were able to place themselves across the entire surface, contrary to what happened to cells in the semi-spheres, which remain mostly at the bottom. For the case of cells embedded, they were mostly grouped at the edges of the wells, for both FPs and semi-spheres. The GR direction of change, followed a perpendicular direction to the parameter H, particularly for the NIH3T3 and HEPG2, while HEK293 tend to be aggregated in cluster, which might be related to its less attachment energy36, compared to the attachment capability of the other two lines37,38. For all three cell lines, more homogeneous cell coverage was observed for the minimal non-scaled gyroid FP configuration, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta = 0, H = 0, K < 0$$\end{document}. In this research we didn’t consider a change in cell density due to migration, since we performed a brief migration assay (Supplementary Fig. S21a–c), and we got that cells mostly moved in their original seeding quadrant (or ROI), which agrees with the reports in22. In addition, when analyzing confluency and cell growth, we always obtained higher values across time points, in each ROI, indicating that despite any migration that could have occurred, each region presented an increase in cell density. However, further real-time migration monitoring is necessary for future work. A collective cell detachment was observed in the PDMS-collagen substrate, mostly for the HEPG2 cell line, which appeared on day 8, at the bottom corners of the FPs wells, corresponding to regions of K more positive and H more negative, agreeing with8,9, which, in addition to curvature influence, might be due to the overcrowding characteristic of this cell line39,40 and the little surface area available. This is correlated with the F-actin intensity values obtained for this substrate and cell line, which were higher in the FP wells than in semi-spheres, indicating that for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K < 0$$\end{document}, cells present more cytoskeletal tension, which at one point led them to detach themselves from the surface. The F-actin filaments of the HEPG2 followed the FP surface, arranging in centripetal patterns along the z-levels of the well, illustrating the influence of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K < 0$$\end{document} for the orientation of this cell line. The HEK293 remained rounded and formed clusters along the FP surface, while the NIH3T3 were randomly oriented, with a slight tendency to follow the axial direction of the FP well, that is, a mix between their behavior in cylindrical and flat surfaces, as reported in10. A clear tendency for the nuclei orientation for all cell lines is observed in the gyroid FPs wells. A symmetric alignment from the middle axis is identified, following an acute angle at the right side, and an obtuse angle at the left side, while being right as they get close to the middle axis. This tendency is more noticeable for the physically smallest gyroid \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {g}_{{1}}$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta = 0, H = 0, K<< 0$$\end{document}, indicating that the nuclei orientation is affected by more negative values of K. The nuclei orientation is also affected by the contractile actomyosin forces and the forces transmitted to the nuclear envelope through LINC complexes6, therefore, nuclear orientation at the curved wells entrance could drive centripetal organization in an effort to minimize contractile tension. Since the nuclear shape changes can implicate modulation of chromatin organization, different fluorescent intensities could be observed depending on its position in the well. With basic observation in our DAPI staining samples, we can observed higher levels of fluorescence intensity in flat nuclei than in rounder ones, Fig. 4a,b, and in zones with higher mean curvature Fig. 5a, agreeing with the discussion in5,6. This could open a study field to guide and modulate the patterning of complex environments and assembling of epithelial structures, by studying different cell lines in these curvatures. The nuclei polarization ratio, is generally lower in FPs wells, indicating that the nucleus is less compressed and more rounded than in semi-spheres and flat surfaces, which might be due to the surface minimization of the gyroid geometry. However, as stated earlier, this is largely affected by the substrate stiffness, since the polarization ratio is higher in the PDMS than in hydrogel, except for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {g}_{{1}}$$\end{document} configuration, confirming that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K<< 0$$\end{document} impacts the nuclei shape. In addition, for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {g}_{{1}}$$\end{document}, the polarization ratio increases as they get far from the middle axis, which agrees with the reports in33 for cells seeded in concave cylinders. These results can be applied for future flow shear stress studies, since, as reported in33, the alignment of cells can be directed by maximum shear stress direction, therefore, it could be interesting to see if the alignment already imposed by the gyroid curvature, is preserved under different flows.

Methods

We considered three different cell lines NIH3T3 (fibroblastic), and two epithelial-like morphology, one cancerous HEPG2, and one embryonic HEK-293, which were seeded over the surface of the scaffolds and embedded in hydrogel formulations (Bioinks). Three main experimental variables were studied:gyroid geometry and curvature, substrate stiffness and, ECM coating ligand. We were interested in three different outcomes, the cell collective growth and confluency, the cell attachment and, the nuclei orientation and polarization. All outcomes were studied by confocal microscopy imaging analysis.

3D extrusion bioprinting of hydrogel substrates

The designed FPs (see Table 4) and semi-spheres were 3D bioprinted using a hydrogel blend of 4% sodium alginate (Sigma Aldrich 120 kg/mol), and 5% gelatin (Sigma, gel strength 90-110 g, type A, porcine skin). The powders were UV-sterilized overnight and mixed under continuous stirring at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$60\,^\circ$$\end{document}C in cell culture media DMEM F12 (ThermoFisher). Once mixed, the ink was filtered using a 0.20 µm filter.

A non-planar approach was followed to extrude the hydrogel. For this, the gyroid FPs NURBS-based mesh (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{1}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{4}$$\end{document}), and the semi-spheres mesh, were taken as the g-code path. An increment of 100 µm at the mesh edges was added, considering the vertical movement of the printer nozzle, to avoid collisions with the previously deposited material.

A 3D bioprinter based on extrusion by piston movement was used. It was previously designed in our laboratory44 (Wanhao Duplicator i3 printer adapted with cartridge support and an SKR V1.3 motherboard), and it has a resolution of 100 µm in x, y and 25 µm in z.

The hydrogel was extruded over a polyurethane sponge (density 17 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$kg/m^{3}$$\end{document}), loaded with an aqueous crosslinking solution of 100 mM \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CaCl_{2}$$\end{document}. The sponge was a porous substrate that allowed the calcium diffusion to the alginate, forming a ”gel reaction front” as in45. For this, before the non-planar hydrogel deposition, a curved template was cut in the sponge using a hot wire of 100\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu m$$\end{document} diameter, and a heating device was placed in the same printer instead of the cartridge support. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S3a–c. Then, the alginate-gelatin hydrogel was extruded using a 3ml syringe, a plastic tapered nozzle with an internal diameter of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$100 \mu m$$\end{document}, a printer velocity of 6mm/s, and an extrusion pressure of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 5 kPa$$\end{document}.

Mold casting of PDMS substrates

The designed FPs and semi-spheres meshes were exported as a solid file (.stl), and were fabricated using stereolithography (SLA) with the Formlabs printer and black resin. Then, PDMS (Silicones Mexico, Part A), and crosslinker (Silicones Mexico, Part B), were mixed at a 10:1 concentration and poured on top of the resin templates. They were left to cure at room temperature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 22^\circ$$\end{document}C) for 3 hours. Concave FPs and semi-spheres were obtained by peeling the PDMS off from the resin templates. The PDMS substrates were sterilized by placing them in ethanol \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$70\%$$\end{document} and leaving them under UV light overnight.

Cell culture

Three cell lines were used, one fibroblastic (NIH3T3, ATCC®CRL-1658) and two with an epithelial-like morphology, one cancerous (HEPG2, ATCC®HB-8065) and one embryonic (HEK-293, ATCC®CRL-1573). For the case of the hydrogel substrates, two cell seeding methods were performed: superficial and embedded, while for the PDMS ones, just superficial seeding was employed.

The cell lines HEK293, HEPG2, and NIH3T3 were cultured in DMEM F12 (ThermoFisher), supplemented with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10\%$$\end{document} of fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma-Aldrich), as indicated in ATCC. Cell seeding

Cells were seeded on the substrates superficially by sessile drop and as a second approach, cells were embedded (cell-laden) in a hydrogel, as depicted in n Fig. S5a–c. The superficial seeding occurred for the PDMS and alg-gel hydrogel substrates. For each substrate, geometry, and cell line, nine samples were imaged at each time point, for every fluorescent assay.

Image analysis

The acquired images for each sample were saved as .czi z-stacks. Each stack was then opened in ImageJ software, and saved in .tif format, both as a stack and as a 2D projected image in z. All subsequent analyses were performed in Matlab®.

Level set slicing method for resolution assessment

To evaluate the resolution of the fabricated constructs, the z-stacks corresponding to the FPs and semi-spheres, stained with fluorescent paint, were accessed in Matlab®. Each image of the stack was opened as a blockedImage to access its dimensions and resize it to the dimensions of the NURBS mesh previously obtained in the physical space (x, y, z). Since this same mesh was used to create the g-code path and the solid files for PDMS molds, it was also used to develop control binary masks, and to compare them with the ones obtained from thin the stack was opened as a blockedImage to access its dimensions and resize it to match the dimensions of the NURBS mesh previously obtained in e fluorescent slices, by overlapping them and calculating the percentage of shared area.

Nuclei and F-actin intensity analysis

For this analysis, the z-projected images of DAPI-stained samples (blue channel), were used. Each image was opened as a blockedImage and resized to match the dimensions of the original NURBS mesh, which was also projected in 2D (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x-y$$\end{document}). The image was then binarized and divided into 100 regions of interest (ROIs), based on the imposed regions by the mesh elements. For each ROI, a ”nuclei mask” was created based on a color threshold (manually set). Then, the watershed transform was applied to get rid of overlapping nuclei. The subsequent properties were obtained using the regionprops function. For each ROI, the nuclei count was recorded in an array with the same dimensions as the original NURBS mesh (10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}10). This was repeated for each image at every time point. The growth rate at each ROI was then calculated as the slope of the time versus nuclei count plot. The average orientation and polarization of nuclei at each ROI were computed, using the ellipse fitted for each nucleus (region in the regionprops detected objects). The ratio between the major and minor axes of this ellipse represented the polarization. At the same time, the orientation was calculated based on the angle between the major axis and the horizontal axis of the FP and the semi-sphere well.

The F-actin intensity was quantified for every ROI of each 2D projected image. The intensity value in the RGB image at each ROI was escalated with respect to the mean intensity detected in the entire image and then normalized to the nuclei count at each ROI. The average normalized intensity at each ROI was also recorded in an array of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10 \times 10$$\end{document} for subsequent curvature correlation.

Analysis of collective cell detachment

To evaluate the collective cell sheet detachment from the substrate, an intensity analysis in z was carried out, using the DAPI-stained z-stacks, resizing each slice according to the NURBS mesh. For each image, the maximum intensity was recorded at each ROI. The z-value of the slice that presented the maximum intensity was recorded and used as the new z-value for the center of that ROI and its corresponding mesh element.

Data analysis

A Kruskal-Wallis test, between groups divided by substrates and FPs deformations, was performed, comparing inner product values, followed by a post-hoc test to determine the p-value between experimental groups. This was repeated for relationships between H and K, as well as growth rate, F-actin intensity, and nuclear polarization and orientation.
