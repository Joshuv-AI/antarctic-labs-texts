Morquio syndrome masquerading as juvenile idiopathic Arthritis: A case report
Authors: Zeineb Alaoui Kadiri, Imane El Binoune, Bouchra Amine, Anbar Kabbaj, Salma Zemrani, Samira Rostom, Bahiri Rachid

Background

Morquio syndrome, or Mucopolysaccharidosis type IV (MPS IV), is a rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder caused by a deficiency of enzymes involved in the degradation of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), specifically keratan sulfate and chondroitin-6-sulfate [1,2]. The resulting accumulation of GAGs in connective tissues leads to progressive skeletal dysplasia, joint deformities, short stature, and, in some cases, multi-systemic complications affecting the cardiovascular, respiratory, and ocular systems [3,4].

The disease is classified into two subtypes: MPS IVA, caused by mutations in the GALNS gene, and MPS IVB, caused by mutations in the GLB1 gene. Both forms share overlapping skeletal features, but the severity of extra-skeletal manifestations can vary widely [5].

Early clinical features often include joint pain, stiffness, and abnormal gait, which can mimic more common pediatric rheumatologic conditions, such as polyarticular Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA). This overlap frequently delays the diagnosis of Morquio syndrome, particularly in patients initially managed for inflammatory arthritis [1,3].

Recognition of characteristic skeletal abnormalities, radiologic findings, and family history, combined with enzymatic and genetic testing, is critical for timely diagnosis. Early identification enables targeted interventions, including enzyme replacement therapy, orthopedic management, and supportive care, which can significantly improve functional outcomes and quality of life [2,5].

In this report, we describe a 16-year-old adolescent initially followed for polyarticular JIA, whose progressive skeletal deformities prompted further evaluation and ultimately led to the diagnosis of Morquio syndrome. The case illustrates the diagnostic challenges of this rare disorder and underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in the management of complex pediatric musculoskeletal conditions.

Case presentation

A 16-year-old adolescent male, previously diagnosed with polyarticular JIA at age 7, had been receiving biological therapy (Infliximab) since diagnosis. He presented with generalized polyarthralgia, marked skeletal deformities, and a significant leg length discrepancy of 6 cm. He was born to a second-degree consanguineous union and had a similarly affected sibling, while both parents were asymptomatic.

On clinical examination, the patient exhibited notable hand deformities (Fig. 1), thoracic cage enlargement, and spinal involvement, with a Shober index of +1 cm compared to the normal value of 5 cm. A leg length discrepancy of 6 cm was also observed. Radiological evaluation revealed dysplasia of the bilateral femoral heads (Fig. 2) and deformities of the shoulders (Fig. 3). Radiographs of the thoracolumbar spine reveal classical features of dysostosis multiplex. There is generalized platyspondyly with reduced vertebral body height involving the thoracic and lumbar spine. Several vertebral bodies demonstrate mild central/anterior beaking. Mild thoracolumbar kyphosis with a subtle scoliotic curvature is also evident. No vertebral collapse or destructive osseous lesion is identified. (Fig. 4). Laboratory investigations showed negative results for anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies, rheumatoid factor, and antinuclear antibodies. Hemoglobin electrophoresis was normal, and osteonecrosis was excluded as a differential diagnosis.

Given the constellation of skeletal abnormalities and family history, MPS IV was suspected. Urinary glycosaminoglycan analysis over 12 hours confirmed mucopolysaccharidosis, and targeted genetic testing identified pathogenic variants in the GALNS gene, establishing the diagnosis of Morquio syndrome.

The patient was referred to a geneticist for ongoing management and to an orthopedic surgeon for potential corrective interventions.

Discussion

Morquio syndrome (MPS IV) is a rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder caused by a deficiency of enzymes responsible for degrading glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), primarily galactosamine-6-sulfatase in type A and β-galactosidase in type B [1,2]. Progressive accumulation of GAGs disrupts the architecture of connective tissues, explaining the severe skeletal manifestations observed in our patient.

In MPS IV, lysosomal accumulation of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) within chondrocytes and osteocytes disrupts normal skeletal development, leading to systemic dysplasia. This primarily affects long bones, resulting in short stature and limb length discrepancies; the pelvis and proximal joints, contributing to femoral head dysplasia and functional limitations; and the spine, increasing the risk of lordosis, kyphosis, and potential spinal cord compression [3,2]. In our patient, the observed 6 cm leg length discrepancy, combined with bilateral femoral head dysplasia and an enlarged thoracic cage, exemplifies these pathological mechanisms. The absence of significant extra-skeletal involvement is consistent with milder or atypical forms of Morquio syndrome, highlighting the phenotypic variability of the disease [4].

Initial presentations of Morquio syndrome often include polyarthralgia and joint deformities, which can closely resemble polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA), particularly in children with early-onset musculoskeletal symptoms. Distinguishing between these conditions is clinically challenging but essential, as management and prognosis differ significantly. In our patient, several features pointed toward a metabolic rather than inflammatory origin. The skeletal deformities were symmetrical and structurally related to underlying bone dysplasia, including pronounced femoral head abnormalities and shoulder malformations, which are uncommon in typical JIA. Laboratory evaluation further supported this distinction, with negative results for rheumatoid factor, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies, and antinuclear antibodies, effectively ruling out most autoimmune causes of polyarthritis. Additionally, the patient’s family history, including a similarly affected sibling and a background of second-degree consanguinity, increased the likelihood of an inherited genetic disorder [1].

These clinical and laboratory findings underscore the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for rare metabolic or lysosomal storage disorders in adolescents presenting with JIA-like symptoms that are atypical in their distribution, severity, or response to standard therapies. Failure to recognize such disorders may lead to prolonged misdiagnosis, inappropriate immunosuppressive treatment, and delayed initiation of disease-specific interventions such as enzyme replacement therapy or orthopedic correction. Therefore, careful integration of radiologic findings, family history, and laboratory evaluation is critical for timely diagnosis, appropriate counseling, and the implementation of a multidisciplinary management plan tailored to the unique needs of patients with metabolic skeletal disorders.

Radiologic evaluation plays a critical role in confirming the diagnosis and assessing disease severity. In this patient, imaging revealed dysplastic femoral heads, evolving coxopathy, thoracic cage widening, spinal deformities, and hand anomalies such as shortened phalanges and mild joint deformities. These structural changes help distinguish Morquio syndrome from inflammatory arthropathies and provide essential information for planning both medical and surgical management [3,4].

Genetic testing confirmed pathogenic variants in the GALNS gene, establishing the diagnosis of MPS IVA. The presence of a similarly affected sibling, combined with unaffected parents, supports a classic autosomal recessive inheritance pattern, with consanguinity likely increasing the probability of phenotype expression. Genetic confirmation not only guides diagnosis but also allows for family counseling, early detection of at-risk siblings, and tailored monitoring for potential complications [5].

Management of Morquio syndrome requires a multidisciplinary approach addressing its complex manifestations. Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with galsulfase can slow skeletal progression and improve joint mobility, although its efficacy depends on the patient’s age and baseline disease severity [5]. Orthopedic interventions, such as correction of limb length discrepancies, joint reconstruction, or spinal stabilization, may be necessary to maintain function and prevent further deformity. Functional rehabilitation through physiotherapy and occupational therapy helps preserve mobility and independence, while regular systemic monitoring, including cardiac, respiratory, and ophthalmologic evaluations, is essential even in the absence of initial symptoms [2,4]. Coordinated care among specialists optimizes functional outcomes, prevents irreversible complications, and ensures timely surgical planning.

Morquio syndrome remains progressive and incurable, but early diagnosis and proactive management can significantly improve physical function and quality of life. Emerging therapies, including gene therapy and novel enzymatic approaches, offer hope for more effective and targeted interventions in the future [2,5].

This case highlights the importance of considering rare metabolic disorders in adolescents with atypical joint deformities or inadequate response to immunomodulatory treatments, particularly in families with a history of consanguinity or affected siblings.

Conclusion

Morquio syndrome can mimic polyarticular JIA in adolescents and may remain undiagnosed until significant skeletal deformities arise. A high index of suspicion, combined with enzymatic and genetic testing, is essential for accurate diagnosis. Multidisciplinary care is crucial to optimize outcomes and improve quality of life in affected individuals.
