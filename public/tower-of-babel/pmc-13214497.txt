Polystyrene microplastics and hepatic fibrosis-related indices in type 2 diabetes: a cross-sectional analysis with experimental validation
Authors: Ying Sun, Xiaoqin Xu, Guoting Sun, Jiang Li, Bowei Yu, Yuying Wang, Kun Zhang, Fangzhen Xia, Yingli Lu, Ningjian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicroplastics (MPs) are emerging environmental contaminants with potential hepatotoxicity, yet direct epidemiological evidence linking internal MP exposure to liver injury is scarce, particularly in individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D). In this cross-sectional study nested within the METAL2 cohort, we aimed to characterize blood microplastic profiles in patients with T2D and to determine whether specific MP polymers are associated with hepatic steatosis and fibrosis risk, with experimental validation of the identified polymer.ResultsIn patients with T2D, multiple MP polymers were detectable in blood, with polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyamide 66 (PA66), and polystyrene (PS) being the most prevalent. Although PVC constituted the largest proportion of total MP burden, PS showed the most consistent nominal positive liver-related signal in the human analyses, including higher levels in participants with elevated fibrosis risk and higher FIB-4 and ALT in the highest exposure quartile. Experimental validation in diabetic mice demonstrated that PS microplastics markedly exacerbated hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and collagen deposition, leading to overt liver fibrosis. Mechanistically, PS-MPs exposure disrupted hepatic lipid homeostasis and concurrently activated the NLRP3 inflammasome and the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway, promoting inflammatory amplification and hepatic stellate cell activation.ConclusionThis integrated human and experimental study provides preliminary evidence that circulating PS-associated signals are linked to liver-related indices in T2D, while experimental findings support the biological plausibility that PS exposure may aggravate hepatic injury under diabetic conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12989-026-00681-w.Keywords: Microplastics, Polystyrene, Liver fibrosis, Type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Microplastics (MPs), plastic particles < 5 mm that can arise either from intentional manufacture at microscopic size or from the fragmentation and degradation of larger plastic materials, have become pervasive environmental contaminants and a growing public health concern [1]. Humans are exposed to MPs through ingestion, inhalation, and dermal contact, leading to their systemic distribution and accumulation in various organs, as evidenced by their detection in human blood [2], liver [3], placenta [4], and even cerebrospinal fluid [5]. While the ecological impacts of MPs are well-documented [6], their potential adverse effects on human health, particularly within vulnerable populations, remain unclear.

The liver, as the central organ for the xenobiotic metabolism and clearance, is a biologically plausible target organ for MP-related toxicity [3, 7–9]. Upon entering the systemic circulation, MPs can be sequestered by hepatic Kupffer cells and hepatocytes, potentially triggering a cascade of pathological events [8, 10]. Emerging experimental studies have demonstrated that MPs exposure can induce oxidative stress, inflammatory responses [11], lipid metabolic disorders [7], and even hepatic fibrosis [12]. These findings position the liver as a critical organ for understanding the systemic health risks of MPs. Recent human studies have begun to examine internally measured microplastics and nanoplastics in human blood, tissues, and biological fluids in relation to clinical outcomes [2, 13, 14]. Despite these emerging data, human studies directly linking internal polymer-specific MP exposure to liver-related phenotypes remain limited, particularly in metabolically vulnerable populations. Individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D), characterized by chronic inflammation and altered metabolic homeostasis, may represent a population with heightened susceptibility to environmental stressors like MPs [15, 16]. However, whether internal levels of specific MPs are associated with liver injury parameters in T2D patients is entirely unknown. Conversely, while animal models confirm hepatotoxicity [7, 9], they fail to recapitulate the complex metabolic milieu of diabetes, thus creating a translational gap between experimental findings and human relevance.

In this study, we characterized blood microplastic profiles in a diabetic cohort, examined their associations with liver health and metabolic markers, and performed a targeted mouse experiment to verify the hepatic impact of the prioritized MPs. We hypothesized that circulating MPs may be linked to hepatic injury in T2D, especially under diabetic conditions that exacerbate susceptibility to environmental stressors. By testing this hypothesis through both human observation and experimental validation, this work aims to bridge the translational gap between MPs exposure and liver disease risk in a vulnerable diabetic population.

Methods

Study population

The study population was derived from the METAL2 cohort (Shanghai Metabolic Disease and Environmental Exposure Study - Second Phase; registration no. ChiCTR1800017573), which was conducted from May to June 2023. Detailed design and methodology of the METAL1 and METAL2 study have been published previously [17, 18]. Briefly, the cohort randomly recruited patients with T2D, from 11 communities in Shanghai, China. All participants completed a comprehensive survey, including questionnaires, physical examinations, and blood sample collection. Patients with T2D defined by standard ADA criteria [19].

A total of 967 individuals with T2D were assessed in 2023. For the present study, we designed an exploratory cross-sectional substudy nested within the METAL2 cohort. Because reliable prior effect-size estimates for associations between polymer-specific blood microplastic levels and liver-related indices in T2D were unavailable, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. To improve representation across liver ultrasound phenotypes and reduce imbalance in key characteristics, participants were selected using a prespecified stratified matching scheme. Specifically, participants were categorized based on liver ultrasound findings into three strata: fatty liver, hepatic steatosis, and normal appearance. Then, participants were matched for sex, age, and diabetes duration in a 2:1:1 ratio across groups, yielding a target sample of 300 participants for blood microplastic analysis. During laboratory processing, six blood samples were discarded due to handling errors, and one participant was excluded because of missing body weight data. Consequently, a total of 293 participants were finally included in the current study (Supplemental Figure S1).

Measurement of blood microplastics

After at least 8 h of fasting, venous whole-blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein using a disposable sterile stainless-steel needle and an evacuated blood-collection system into 2-mL EDTA anticoagulant tubes. Whole-blood aliquots were then processed for polymer-specific microplastic measurement.

The levels of 9 specific microplastics in blood, including polystyrene (PS), polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyamide 66 (PA66), polylactic acid (PLA), and poly (butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT), were quantified using pyrolysis gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS) with a Frontier Lab PY-3030D pyrolyzer coupled to a Shimadzu GCMS-QP2020NX system. Briefly, approximately 1–2 g of whole blood were accurately weighed and subjected to acid digestion with nitric acid. This aliquot size was selected to match the gravimetric Py-GC/MS workflow and allow standardized processing across samples.

The digested and concentrated solution was loaded onto a sample cup and analyzed at a pyrolysis temperature of 550 °C. Specific pyrolysis products were identified and quantified based on their characteristic ions against external standard calibration curves with correlation coefficients (R²) > 0.99. To reduce procedural contamination, reagents were prefiltered before use, consumables were precleaned, blood digests were processed in glass vials, and procedural blanks were analyzed in parallel with study samples. This Py-GC/MS workflow provides polymer-level mass estimates based on characteristic pyrolysis products, but because it is a destructive single-platform method, it does not provide direct confirmation of particle number, size, shape, or morphology. Therefore, the resulting measurements should be interpreted as polymer-associated mass signals rather than highest-confidence particle identification. Additional analytical quality-control details, including calibration performance, procedural blank documentation, and blank-control information, are provided in the Supplementary Materials, Tables S1 and S2, Figure S2.

Definition of hepatic injury and metabolic indices

Hepatic injury and related metabolic profiles were assessed using clinical, laboratory, and ultrasonographic data. Hepatic steatosis was diagnosed via abdominal ultrasonography and categorized as normal, steatosis, or fatty liver. Venous blood samples were collected after at least 8 h of fasting. Key non-invasive fibrosis and steatosis indices were calculated from fasting venous blood samples: the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index as [age×AST (U/L)] / [platelet count (×10⁹/L)×√ALT (U/L)], and the Fatty Liver Index (FLI) based on BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides, and GGT [20]. A comprehensive panel of metabolic indices in blood was also evaluated. This included fasting plasma glucose (FPG), fasting insulin, triglyceride (TG), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), which were quantified using standard enzymatic methods. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured in triplicate by trained nurses using a validated electronic sphygmomanometer after participants had rested for at least 5 min. All biochemical analyses were performed following stringent quality control protocols.

Animal modeling

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were housed under a 12-hour light/dark cycle. After one week of acclimatization, age-matched male mice were assigned to a chow-fed/non-STZ control group or to diabetic induction by HFD/STZ. Mice in the diabetic group were fed a 60% high-fat diet (HFD, #PHF60, SLACOM, China) for four weeks to induce initial obesity and insulin resistance. This was followed by daily intraperitoneal injections of freshly prepared streptozotocin (STZ, 30 mg/kg/day) (S8050, Solarbio, China) for four consecutive days to establish hyperglycemia [21]. One week after the final STZ injection, diabetes induction was confirmed by FPG > 11.1 mmol/L. Diabetic mice were then randomized into two groups: HFD/STZ and HFD/STZ + PS. The HFD/STZ + PS group received drinking water containing 5 μm polystyrene microplastics (PS-MPs, #M122077, Aladdin, China) at 1000 µg/L for 9 weeks [22, 23], whereas the HFD/STZ group received vehicle water. Thus, the final animal experiment included three groups: Control, HFD/STZ, and HFD/STZ + PS.

Biochemical evaluation and histological analyses of the animals

Glucose homeostasis was evaluated via intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) one week before the endpoint, following our established protocols [24]. At the end of intervention, all mice were fasted for 12 h and then euthanized under anesthesia. Blood samples were collected from the orbit for subsequent serum biochemical analysis. Serum PS-associated polymer mass was quantified using the same polymer-specific Py-GC/MS workflow as that used for human blood samples. Serum levels of TG, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), ALT, and AST were quantified using a Beckman Coulter AU 680 automated biochemical analyser (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA).

Upon euthanasia, the liver was immediately excised, weighed, and photographed. A portion of the left lateral lobe was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24–48 h for histopathological evaluation, while the remaining liver tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for molecular analyses.The fixed liver tissues were processed routinely, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4–5 μm thickness. Serial sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) for general histological assessment of steatosis, inflammation, and ballooning. Lipid accumulation was specifically evaluated using Oil Red O staining on frozen liver sections. Collagen deposition, indicative of fibrosis, was assessed using Masson’s trichrome and Picro-Sirius Red staining.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded liver sections were subjected to immunoassays. After the sections were incubated in 3% H2O2 for 30 min at 37 °C to quench endogenous peroxidase activity, they were subjected to antigen retrieval in a microwave and then blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were labelled with the primary antibody for 3 h, incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, mounted with DAPI-containing mounting medium and then viewed under a fluorescence microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded liver sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in a microwave and then blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. Cultured cells were collected and then fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min, and blocked with 10% goat serum in PBS for 30 min. Paraffin-embedded sections and cultured cells were incubated with primary antibodies and secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using a Nikon microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from approximately 30 mg of snap-frozen liver tissue using TRIzol reagent. RNA concentration and purity were verified by spectrophotometry (A260/A280 ratio > 1.8). Total RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA in a 20 µL reaction using a reverse transcription kit (#0036A, TAKARA, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed in triplicate on a QuantStudio 5 system using SYBR Green Master Mix (#11203ES60, Yeasen, China). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method following prior study [20]. Primer sequences are provided in Supplemental Table S3.

Western blot

Protein was extracted from liver tissues using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay and separated by 8–12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Membranes were then incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature (Table S4). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and quantified by ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

For patients with T2D, the distribution of total and individual microplastic concentrations (µg/g) was described, along with the detection rate for each type. Baseline characteristics of individuals were compared across the quartiles of overall MPs concentration. Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). To evaluate the relationship between microplastic load and hepatic injury, the levels of total microplastics and the three most frequently detected types (PVC, PA66, PS) were compared across participant groups stratified by clinically relevant criteria: (1) presence of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, defined as ultrasound-diagnosed fatty liver or hepatic steatosis), (2) elevated fibrosis risk (defined as FIB-4 ≥ 2.0, applying an age-adapted cutoff for this elderly diabetic cohort) [25], and (3) severity of hepatic steatosis based on the FLI (low: <30; intermediate: 30–59; high: ≥60) [26]. Although PE was more abundant by mass than PS in descriptive analyses, its detection frequency was lower than that of PS and it was therefore not included in the prespecified primary polymer-specific regression analyses. Linear regression models were employed to evaluate associations between total MPs, PVC, PA66, PS concentrations and key liver/metabolic indices, including FIB-4, FLI, ALT, AST, BMI, TG, and FPG. All microplastic concentrations and metabolic outcome variables were log10-transformed to approximate normal distributions. Two models were constructed: Model 1 was minimally adjusted (for microplastics only; for individual PVC, PA66, and PS analyses, mutual adjustment was applied); Model 2 was further adjusted for age, sex, drinking status, smoking status, BMI, waist circumference, SBP, DBP, TG, FPG, and fasting insulin. As a sensitivity analysis for multiple testing, P values from the fully adjusted primary human association analyses were additionally adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). The prespecified primary family comprised the associations of total MPs, PVC, PA66, and PS with the two principal liver-related indices, FIB-4 and FLI. Associations involving ALT, AST, BMI, TG, and FPG were considered secondary exploratory analyses.

Data from mice are presented as mean ± SEM. Differences between three groups or two experimental groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA or an independent two-tailed Student’s t-test, respectively. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, USA). For the prespecified primary human analyses, FDR-adjusted q < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Secondary exploratory analyses are presented with nominal two-sided P values.

Results

Profiling of blood microplastics in patients with T2D

Blood levels of common MPs were quantified in 293 patients with T2D (Fig. 1). The composition of MPs varied substantially among individuals (Fig. 1A and B). The mean total concentration of MPs per participant was 16.59 µg/g, and PVC was the dominant polymer species, comprising over half (50.32%) of the total microplastic mass, followed by PA66 (24.81%), PE (15.44%), PBAT (5.39%), and PS (2.30%) (Fig. 1C). In terms of detection frequency, PVC was also the most prevalent, detected in 283 of 293 participants (96.6%), followed by PA66 (77.8%), PS (77.1%), PE (65.2%), PBAT (28.0%), and PLA (25.9%) (Fig. 1D). Among the targeted 9 analytes, PMMA, PP, and PET had detection rates below 6%, and these polymers were therefore aggregated into an “Others” category.

Baseline characteristics of patients with T2D by total MPs levels

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the current study are presented in Table 1, stratified by quartiles (Q1-Q4) of the total blood MPs concentration. The 293 participants had a mean age of 71.0 ± 6.8 years, with a nearly equal distribution of men and women (50.5% vs. 49.5%). Across the quartiles of increasing total MPs levels, there were no statistically significant differences in age, sex, smoking or drinking status, BMI, waist circumference, SBP, DBP, or key metabolic and liver function parameters, including FPG, insulin, TG, ALT, AST, gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), platelet count (PLT), FIB-4 index, and FLI (all P > 0.05). As expected, serum concentrations of all individual microplastics (PVC, PA66, PS, PE, PBAT, PLA) increased significantly across the quartiles of MPs (all P < 0.001).

Associations of microplastic concentrations with liver injury and metabolic indices

When comparing microplastic concentrations across clinically relevant stratifications of liver disease, we observed no significant differences in the levels of total MPs or individual polymers (including PVC, PA66, and PS) across groups defined by MASLD status, FIB-4 index, or FLI categories (all P > 0.05). However, PS was significantly higher in patients with T2D and an elevated fibrosis risk (FIB-4 ≥ 2.0) compared to those with FIB-4 < 2.0 (P = 0.0269) (Fig. 2).

To further quantify these relationships, we performed linear regression analyses to assess the associations of total MPs and the three most prevalent polymers with key liver and metabolic indices (Fig. 3, Table S5 and Table S6). In Model 1, no significant associations were observed for total MPs, PVC, PA66, or PS with any of the outcome variables, including FIB-4, FLI, ALT, AST, BMI, TG, and FPG (all P > 0.05). Moreover, total MPs levels showed a significant negative association with TG (β = -0.088, 95% CI -0.165 to -0.011, P = 0.025). After adjustment for demographic and metabolic confounders in Model 2, PVC showed a nominal inverse association with FIB-4 (β= -0.095, 95% CI -0.176 to -0.015, P = 0.021, q = 0.084), whereas PS showed a nominal positive association with FLI (β = 0.088, 95% CI 0.014 to 0.162, P = 0.020, q = 0.084). These primary human associations did not remain statistically significant after FDR correction and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

Microplastic levels in quartiles in relation to hepatic indices

When blood concentrations of total MPs and the major individual polymers were categorized into quartiles, we observed polymer-specific associations with the fibrosis index FIB-4 in the fully adjusted model (Table 2). Among the three most prevalent polymers, higher level of PVC (Q3: β = -0.38, 95% CI -0.71 to -0.05, P = 0.026) and PA66 (Q4: β = -0.36, 95% CI -0.71 to -0.01, P = 0.046) was associated with significantly lower FIB-4 scores. In contrast, participants in the highest PS quartile had significantly higher FIB-4 scores than those in the lowest quartile (β = 0.51, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.89, P = 0.008). Moreover, both the second quartile of total MPs and the highest quartile of PS were significantly associated with elevated ALT levels, with β = 5.33 (95% CI 0.76 to 9.89, P = 0.022) and β = 6.57 (95% CI 0.46 to 12.67, P = 0.035), respectively, when compared to their reference lowest quartiles (Table 3). No significant associations were observed for any microplastic type with AST, the AST/ALT ratio, or the FLI.

PS-MPs exacerbate hepatic steatosis and fibrosis in a diabetic mouse model

To validate the key association between PS-MPs exposure and liver injury observed in the human cohort, we employed a HFD combined with STZ mouse model of diabetes, with or without exposure to PS-MPs (Fig. 4A). Circulating PS levels were significantly higher in the HFD/STZ + PS group than in the HFD/STZ group (Fig. 4B). Relative to the Controls, HFD/STZ mice showed higher FPG, impaired IPGTT, changes in body weight, food intake and water intake, confirming successful establishment of the diabetic/metabolic phenotype (Figure S3). PS-MPs exposure did not significantly affect body weight, food intake, or water consumption in diabetic mice compared to the HFD/STZ controls (Figure S3A-S3E). Diabetic induction was confirmed by elevated FPG and impaired glucose tolerance (Figure S3F-S3H). Mice exposed to PS-MPs exhibited significant metabolic disturbances, as evidenced by elevated serum levels of ALT, AST, and TG, compared to the untreated HFD/STZ mice (Fig. 4C, E). Gross examination revealed that, beyond the prominent steatosis induced by the HFD in both groups, livers from the PS-MPs exposed group exhibited a more granular and uneven surface texture, a macroscopic feature often indicative of underlying fibrosis (Fig. 4F). The liver weight/body weight (%) was significantly higher in the HFD/STZ + PS group versus HFD/STZ group (Fig. 4G). H&E and Oil Red O staining demonstrated significantly exacerbated hepatic steatosis in the PS-MPs exposed group compared to the untreated HFD/STZ mice (both P < 0.05) (Fig. 4H). Masson staining showed extensive collagen deposition (blue), primarily localized in the perisinusoidal and periportal areas of the PS-MPs exposed livers, forming a dense and interconnected network that was markedly more severe than the sparse, isolated collagen fibers observed in the diabetic control. Sirius Red staining further showed that the PS-MPs exposed livers exhibited a significant increase in both the intensity and area of red-orange collagen signals, suggesting the advanced fibrogenesis (both P < 0.005).

PS-MPs activate fibrotic and inflammatory pathways in the liver of diabetic mice

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying PS-MPs-induced fibrosis, we first assessed inflammatory cell infiltration and hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a marked increase in F4/80-positive macrophages that were spatially associated with α-SMA-positive areas in the livers of PS-MPs-exposed mice (Fig. 5A, B), alongside upregulated mRNA expression of TNF-α, Il-6, Il-1β, and MCP-1, suggesting a link between inflammation and hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation (Fig. 5C). Immunohistochemistry for α-SMA and Collagen I revealed a pronounced increase in activated HSCs and collagen deposition in perisinusoidal and portal regions of PS-MPs exposed livers (Fig. 5D, E). At the protein level, the TGF-β1/Smad pathway was activated in the PS-MPs group, with elevated levels of TGF-β1, p-SMAD2/3, and downstream effectors α-SMA and Collagen I (Fig. 5F). Concomitantly, PS-MPs robustly activated the NLRP3 inflammasome, evidenced by increased NLRP3, cleaved Caspase-1, and mature IL-1β (Fig. 5G). Immunofluorescence staining also showed the expression levels of NLRP3 and IL-1β were significantly increased in the liver of HFD/STZ + PS group (Fig. 5H). A distinct yellow fluorescent signal was observed, indicating clear co-localization of NLRP3 and IL-1β in hepatic cells. These data indicate that PS-MPs trigger NLRP3-mediated inflammation, and activate the core TGF-β1/Smad fibrogenic pathway, collectively driving hepatic injury and fibrosis in diabetes.

Discussion

This study integrated a detailed assessment of blood MP profiles in patients with T2D with targeted experimental validation, revealing a polymer-specific association between circulating PS-MPs and liver-related indices. In our cohort of 293 individuals, PVC was the dominant polymer by mass, whereas PS accounted for only a small fraction of the total measured burden. Nevertheless, PS showed the most consistent positive signal across liver-related analyses: circulating PS was higher in participants with elevated fibrosis risk, continuous-model analysis suggested a positive association with FLI, and quartile-based analyses showed higher FIB-4 and ALT in the highest PS exposure group. Together with the diabetic mouse data, these findings support the conclusion that PS-MPs exposure exacerbates liver steatosis, inflammation, and collagen deposition. Mechanistic analyses indicated that PS-MPs disrupted hepatic lipid homeostasis and activated both the NLRP3 inflammasome and the TGF-β1/Smad fibrogenic pathway. These findings underscored the importance of polymer-specific risk assessment in hepatic health for patients with T2D.

The burgeoning literature on microplastics has predominantly focused on their ecological impacts and general toxicity in preclinical models [27]. While advanced analytical techniques have enabled the detection of MPs in specific human tissues, such as placenta [4], bone marrow [28], cerebrospinal fluid [5], these approaches are largely confined to post-mortem or invasive clinical samples. This inherently limits the scale and representativeness of such studies, constraining their utility for population-level exposure assessment. Furthermore, methodological challenges in quantifying internal, biologically relevant exposure remain a widely recognized bottleneck in the field [29]. Critically, epidemiological studies that systematically assess internal MP exposure and its health consequences in high-risk subpopulations, like T2D populations, are notably lacking. Accordingly, we quantified polymer-associated mass signals in blood using Py-GC/MS, a high-throughput approach that enables polymer-level screening in biological samples. However, this method is destructive and does not provide direct information on particle number, size, shape, or morphology. In addition, recent methodological studies have highlighted that blood is a particularly challenging matrix for Py-GC/MS-based microplastic analysis because matrix-related interferences, nonspecific pyrolysis products, and realistic detection limits may affect the reliability of polymer identification and quantification. Our data should therefore be interpreted with appropriate analytical caution [30, 31].

To our knowledge, this study provides one of the first detailed characterizations of internal microplastic exposure in individuals with T2D. Notably, when summing the mass of all detected polymers across the cohort, PVC constituted approximately half of the total burden and emerged as the dominant polymer by mass. This differs from some blood biomonitoring studies in generally healthy populations, in which PE was the most abundant polymer [32], whereas other cohorts have reported different leading polymers, including polystyrene and polypropylene [33]. Rather than being contradictory, these differences likely reflect substantial heterogeneity across studies in analytical platform, sample preparation, matrix effects, geographic exposure sources, and population characteristics. The predominance of PVC in our older T2D cohort may therefore reflect population-specific exposure histories. This interpretation remains speculative in the absence of detailed source-specific or lifetime exposure information [34].

In our analyses, total microplastic burden was not associated with the major liver indices after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that aggregate measurements may mask polymer-specific patterns. We also observed heterogeneous associations across polymers: PVC and PA66 showed inverse associations with FIB-4 in some models, whereas PS showed the most consistent positive liver-related pattern, including a positive association with FLI in the continuous model and higher FIB-4 and ALT in the highest exposure quartile. In descriptive analyses, PVC, PA66, and PE were more abundant than PS by mass. However, abundance alone does not determine which polymer will show the clearest liver-related signal in an exploratory dataset. In the present study, PA66 showed largely null or inverse associations in the adjusted models, whereas PE, despite being more abundant by mass than PS, was not included in the prespecified primary polymer-specific regression analyses because its detection frequency was lower than that of PS. Therefore, the current data do not support strong inference regarding the liver-related relevance of PE, and should not be interpreted as evidence that PE or PA66 are biologically inert. At the same time, humans are exposed to complex mixtures rather than isolated polymers [32], and the current study was not designed to resolve mixture effects or polymer–polymer interactions. Because the human component is cross-sectional, the observed associations should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence that PS is uniquely causal in diabetic liver injury. Taken together, our data support PS as the prioritized polymer for experimental follow-up in this dataset, while still leaving open important roles for more abundant polymers and mixed-microplastic exposure.

Although PS was not the dominant polymer in our cohort, experimental evidence supports its biological plausibility as a liver-relevant polymer. PS exposure has been shown to induce size-dependent oxidative stress in the liver [35, 36], accumulate in hepatic tissue, and aggravate inflammation and fibrosis-related changes in metabolically stressed mice [37]. Accordingly, our data should not be interpreted as indicating that PS is the predominant polymer in human blood, but rather that it showed the most consistent liver-related signal in this dataset.

To experimentally validate this association, we exposed mice to PS-MPs and assessed key liver outcomes. Consistent with the human data, our mouse model demonstrated that PS-MPs exposure exacerbated liver pathology in a diabetic context. The most salient finding was the marked induction of hepatic fibrosis, as evidenced by a significant increase in collagen deposition and α-SMA expression compared to the untreated HFD/STZ mice. PS-MPs exposure also led to elevated serum levels of ALT, AST, and increased hepatic expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, providing a mechanistic bridge between exposure and the pro-inflammatory milieu that precedes and promotes fibrosis.

Mechanistically, our data indicate that PS-MPs exacerbate liver fibrosis in diabetes through the co-activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and the TGF-β1/Smad signaling axis. Although these pathways have been separately implicated in PS-MPs-induced hepatotoxicity in non-diabetic settings [38, 39], we demonstrate that in the diabetic liver, PS-MPs concurrently activate the inflammatory and fibrogenic pathways. Specifically, PS-MPs trigger NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, leading to Caspase-1 activation and the maturation of potent cytokines such as IL-1β [40]. This inflammatory signal acts as a critical molecular link, directly enhancing the sensitivity and output of the canonical TGF-β1/Smad fibrogenic pathway, thereby driving hepatic stellate cell activation and excessive collagen deposition [41]. The resulting fibrosis and tissue remodeling may further perpetuate inflammation, suggesting the establishment of a self-amplifying feed-forward loop. Notably, this cascade closely mirrors the mechanism of PS-MPs aggravated diabetic nephropathy [42]. These parallels suggest that diabetic metabolic stress may amplify PS-related inflammatory and fibrogenic responses across organs. This maladaptive reprogramming funnels disparate pathological stimuli into core fibrogenic pathways, ultimately driving accelerated end-organ damage.

Several limitations of our study warrant consideration. First, the human component was cross-sectional, and therefore causality or directionality cannot be inferred. Reverse causality is possible, because impaired liver function or altered hepatobiliary handling may influence circulating microplastic levels and thereby contribute to the observed associations. Second, recent methodological work has highlighted that blood is a challenging biological matrix for this approach. Because we did not apply orthogonal confirmatory techniques and did not perform repeat measurements for individual blood samples, the measured values may not fully capture the true circulating microplastic burden and should be interpreted cautiously. Third, although contamination-mitigation procedures and procedural blanks were implemented, low-level contamination during blood collection, handling, or analysis cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the advanced age of our cohort. Given that aging itself is associated with altered metabolic function, hepatic susceptibility, and potential cumulative environmental exposures [43], the observed associations between PS-MPs and liver fibrosis might differ in younger individuals with T2D or in non-diabetic populations. Fifth, while the dosage of PS-MPs used in our mouse model was based on prior studies, the current study did not establish a comprehensive dose-response relationship or delineate the temporal dynamics of exposure. This design was primarily intended to provide proof-of-concept that PS-MPs can aggravate liver injury and alter key signaling pathways in a diabetic context. Finally, we did not further elucidate the upstream molecular initiating events. Specifically, how hepatic cells initially sense PS-MPs to trigger lipid dysregulation and NLRP3 activation remains unclear, a question that may delve into the realm of molecular structural toxicology.

Despite these limitations, our study advances the field by linking internal PS-MPs exposure to liver injury risk within the high-risk T2D population, a finding derived from direct blood measurement and robust confounding control. The human data prioritized PS for mechanistic follow-up, and the complementary animal experiment supports the biological plausibility that PS exposure can aggravate liver injury in a diabetic context. This epidemiological signal gains biological plausibility from our complementary animal experiment, which confirmed that PS-MPs exposure can activate key NLRP3 and TGF-β1/Smad pathways, thereby exacerbating liver injury in a diabetic context.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary evidence that higher circulating PS-associated polymer mass signals are linked to liver-related indices in individuals with T2D. Experimental findings in diabetic mice support the biological plausibility that PS exposure may aggravate inflammatory and fibrogenic signaling in the liver. These results underscore the importance of polymer-specific risk assessment and highlight the need to consider environmental MPs exposures in the multifactorial pathogenesis of liver disease progression in vulnerable populations.
