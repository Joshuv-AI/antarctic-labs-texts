Efficacy of arterial compression hemostasis devices in liver cancer treatment: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Handan Liu, Li Yang, Qin Qin, Lijun Cui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the efficacy of arterial compression hemostasis devices after femoral artery puncture interventions for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).MethodsWe systematically searched 10 electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE, The Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and CBM) up to October 20, 2024, to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of arterial compression hemostasis devices used after HCC interventions through femoral artery puncture. We used the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool to evaluate the trial quality, and we analyzed the data with Review Manager 5.4 software.Results17 RCTs involving 2,338 participants met the inclusion criteria. The meta-analysis demonstrated that, compared with conventional manual compression combined with sandbags, the use of arterial compression hemostasis devices significantly shortened the compression hemostasis time [MD = -13.9 min, 95% CI, -14.19 to -12.19; P < 0.00001] and limb immobilization time [MD = -8.79 min, 95% CI, -12.65 to -4.94; P < 0.00001]. Additionally, it significantly reduced the incidence of local bleeding [RR = 0.28, 95% CI, 0.20 to 0.40; P < 0.00001], hematoma formation [RR = 0.29, 95% CI, 0.18 to 0.46; P < 0.00001], skin ecchymosis [RR = 0.25, 95% CI, 0.18 to 0.35; P < 0.00001], dysuria [RR = 0.22, 95% CI, 0.14 to 0.34; P = 0.0002], skin damage [RR = 0.16, 95% CI, 0.05 to 0.54; P = 0.003], backache [RR = 0.28, 95% CI, 0.13 to 0.64; P = 0.002], and pseudoaneurysm [RR = 0.22, 95% CI, 0.10 to 0.51; P = 0.0004].ConclusionsThe analysis revealed that arterial compression hemostasis devices significantly reduce hemostasis and immobilization time, as well as vascular complications in liver cancer patients undergoing interventional therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-024-03599-9.

Introduction

Liver cancer has become the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1, 2]. According to statistics, every year, approximately 1 million new cases of liver cancer are diagnosed worldwide, and the death rate exceeds 700,000, with the proportion in China accounting for more than two-fifths of this total [2, 3]. In general, liver cancer is treated with common methods, such as surgical resection, liver transplantation, trans arterial chemoembolization, ablation, systemic chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiation therapy, and molecularly targeted therapies [4, 5]. Although various methods have been implemented to improve the survival of patients with liver cancer, most liver cancer patients reach middle or late stages before they are diagnosed, and the best time for surgical treatment is lost [6]. In accordance with current practice guidelines for the management of liver cancer patients and with the increasing maturity of minimally invasive treatment technology and its obvious curative effect, interventional therapy, represented by selective hepatic artery embolization, has gradually become the first choice for nonsurgical treatment of primary liver cancer [7–9].

Nonetheless, since interventional therapies are accompanied by repeated ischemic injury of the liver parenchyma and adverse events, postembolization survival outcomes remain poor [10]. Studies have shown that approximately 5–10% of patients develop vascular complications after femoral artery puncture [11–13]. Among the major vascular complications, pseudoaneurysm, hematoma, and local bleeding are caused mainly by technical problems and inadequate bleeding control [14]. After the puncture, the patient cannot move their lower limbs for a long time and is prone to symptoms such as backache and dysuria [15].

Arterial compression hemostasis devices have been developed to address these issues, providing a more efficient and patient-friendly alternative. These devices are designed to apply consistent and controlled pressure to the puncture site, potentially reducing the time required for hemostasis and subsequent immobilization [16]. Despite their growing use, there remains a need for systematic evaluation of their efficacy and safety in the context of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) interventional therapies. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to provide a comprehensive evaluation of arterial compression hemostasis devices used after femoral artery puncture in HCC patients. By synthesizing data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), this study seeks to determine whether these devices offer significant benefits over traditional methods in terms of reducing the hemostasis time, immobilization time, and the incidence of vascular complications. Additionally, the analysis addresses the potential adverse effects associated with these devices, providing a balanced perspective on their clinical utility.

Materials and methods

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17] and the recommendations outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [18]. This review was registered on the PROSPERO website (CRD42024535169).

Search strategy

We conducted an extensive search on October 20, 2024, of the PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and CBM databases to identify relevant articles. We used Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, Emtree terms, subject headings, and free text associated with the concepts, and no language or publication date restrictions were applied. The search used the terms “hemostases” OR “hemostasis” OR “hemostasis compression device” OR “hemostasis compression” OR “arterial pressure hemostat” and “liver neoplasms” OR “liver cancer” OR “liver tumor” OR “hepatocellular cancer” OR “hepatic cancer” OR “hepatocellular carcinoma” and “chemoembolization, therapeutic” OR “therapeutic chemoembolization” OR “embolization, therapeutic” OR “embolotherapeutic embolization” OR “interventional surgery” OR “interventional treatment”. At the same time, the references cited in the selected literature were traced back to ensure that all eligible articles were included. A comprehensive account of the literature search strategy used for each database and the dates of the last search are provided in Appendix 1.

Eligibility criteria

Participants

For patients who were diagnosed pathologically with liver cancer, the two groups of patients were consistent in terms of age, sex, disease condition, and compression site (P > 0.05).

Intervention types

In the experimental group, after hepatic artery embolization, patients with liver cancer were subjected to arterial compression hemostasis at the puncture point of the femoral artery.

Comparison

The patients in the control group were treated with conventional compression hemostasis combined with a compression bandage, adhesive compression bandage, sandbag compression, or salt bag compression.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this review was vascular complications as follows: local bleeding, hematoma formation, thrombosis, and pseudoaneurysm. The secondary outcomes included the following: the limb immobilization time, pressure hemostasis time, skin ecchymosis, skin damage, dysuria, and backache.

Study design

Only randomized controlled trials will be included in this study.

Exclusion criteria

Newspaper articles, conference papers, case reports, government reports, and textbook chapters were not included, and studies that did not provide sufficient data were excluded.

Quality appraisal of the included studies

Two reviewers (HD Liu and QQ) screened the literature, extracted the data, and cross-checked the data independently to ensure that consistent data were extracted from the literature. The data extracted included general data (first author, publication year, country, and sample size), participant characteristics (age), intervention characteristics (interventions, frequency), and outcomes. If crucial information was missing, attempts were made to contact the corresponding author via email to obtain the unpublished data. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer.

Risk of bias was assessed by following the latest recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook risk of bias assessment tool 2.0 (ROB 2.0) [19], which consists of five main sections: bias arising from randomization, bias from deviations from established interventions, bias from missing outcome data, bias from outcome measures and outcome selective reporting bias. Studies were rated as “low risk of bias”, “some concerns”, or “high risk of bias”. Two investigators independently assessed the risk of bias, and if there was disagreement, a consensus was reached via discussion or consultation with a third party.

Data analysis

A quantitative synthesis of the data was conducted with Review Manager (RevMan) Version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020). Heterogeneity among the studies was evaluated via the I2 test before data synthesis. Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to represent the results following the data assessment (local bleeding, hematoma formation, thrombosis, pseudoaneurysm, skin ecchymosis, skin damage, dysuria, and backache). The mean difference (MD) and 95% CI were calculated for changes in the limb immobilization time and pressure hemostasis time. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed via the I2, a random effects model was used for the analysis if the I2 was ≥ 50% (P < 0.10), and a sensitivity analysis was further performed. For analyses with an I2 < 50% (P > 0.10), a fixed-effects model was used. The statistical significance of the summary estimates was determined on the basis of a p value of 0.05. To verify the stability of the results of this study, we used forest plots to verify the existence of publication bias.

Results

Search process

The detailed flowchart of the screening and selection process in the PRISMA flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. In the initial search, a total of 489 studies were identified from the electronic database. All the papers were subsequently entered into Endnote 21 software to remove duplicate papers. Of the 489 records, 139 were identified as duplicates and were excluded; thus, 350 records were left for further review. Of these 350 records, 317 were excluded by screening the title and abstract, with the remaining 33 records being subjected to full-text screening. After careful checking and review, a total of 17 studies involving 2,338 liver cancer patients (1,213 patients in the experimental group and 1,125 patients in the control group) were ultimately included in our meta-analysis [20–36].

Quality of the included studies

The Cochrane risk of bias of the selected studies is shown in Figs. 2 and 3. Among the 17 studies, four were grouped via a random number table, one was grouped according to the order in which the patients were admitted to the hospital, and 11 referred to “randomization”, but the exact randomization method was unclear. None of the studies were clear about distribution concealment and could not implement blinding. The data from all 17 studies were complete, there was no measurement bias, and the existence of other form of biases was unclear. All studies included were Grade B [19–35].

Meta-analysis and data synthesis

Local bleeding

Fourteen RCTs that included 1,922 liver cancer patients reported local bleeding incidents [19–24, 26, 28–34]. Compared with the control groups, a meta-analysis of the fixed-effects models indicated that arterial compression hemostasis devices significantly reduced local bleeding incidents (RR = 0.28; 95% CI, 0.20 to 0.40; P < 0.00001) (Table 2; Fig. 4).

Hematoma formation

Ten RCTs that included 1,324 liver cancer patients reported incidence of hematoma formation [19, 20, 23, 24, 26, 28, 29, 33–35]. A meta-analysis of the fixed-effects model (I2 = 0%) verified that, compared with control devices, arterial compression hemostasis devices significantly reduced the incidence of hematoma formation (RR = 0.29; 95% CI, 0.18 to 0.46; P < 0.00001) (Table 2; Fig. 5).

Thrombosis

Three RCTs that included 602 liver cancer patients reported the incidence of thrombosis [19, 21, 28]. Compared with the control groups, a meta-analysis of the fixed-effects models (I2 = 0%) indicated that arterial compression hemostasis devices significantly decreased the thrombosis incidence (RR = 0.19; 95% CI, 0.05 to 0.71; P = 0.01) (Table 2).

Pseudoaneurysm

Nine RCTs that included 952 liver cancer patients reported the incidence of pseudoaneurysm [23, 24, 26, 29–34]. Compared with the results of the control group, a meta-analysis of the fixed-effects models (I2 = 0%) indicated that the use of arterial compression hemostasis devices significantly reduced the incidence of pseudoaneurysm (RR = 0.22; 95% CI, 0.10 to 0.51; P = 0.0004) (Table 2; Fig. 6).

Skin ecchymosis

Thirteen RCTs that included 1460 liver cancer patients reported the incidence of skin ecchymosis [19, 20, 22–26, 29–33, 35]. A meta-analysis of the fixed-effects model (I2 = 0%) revealed that arterial compression hemostasis devices significantly reduced the incidence of skin ecchymosis compared with that reported in the control group (RR = 0.25; 95% CI, 0.18 to 0.35; P < 0.00001) (Table 2; Fig. 7).

Dysuria

Four RCTs that included 516 liver cancer patients reported the incidence of dysuria incidents [19, 27, 28, 35]. Compared with the control group, a meta-analysis of the random-effects models revealed that arterial compression hemostasis devices significantly reduced the incidence of dysuria (RR = 0.22; 95% CI, 0.14 to 0.34; P = 0.0002) (Table 2; Fig. 8). A random-effects model was applied because of the high heterogeneity of the data (I2 = 53%, P = 0.10). We conducted a sensitivity analysis via the leave-one-out method (i.e., removing one study at a time and then repeating the analysis). When one of the studies was omitted [28], the heterogeneity decreased to 0 (I2 = 0%, P = 0.65), and the results were still robust, verifying that this study was the source of the heterogeneity (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

Skin damage

Three RCTs that included 354 liver cancer patients reported the incidence of skin damage [19, 24, 25]. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review that comprehensively evaluated the effects of arterial compression hemostasis devices compared with those of conventional manual compression devices after interventional therapy in patients with liver cancer. Overall, our study emphasized that arterial compression hemostasis devices used in liver cancer patients after interventional therapy can decrease the duration of compression hemostasis and immobilization and reduce the incidences of local bleeding, hematoma formation, skin ecchymosis, dysuria, skin damage, backache, pseudoaneurysm, and thrombosis.

The results of our study confirmed that hemostasis with arterial hemostasis instruments can reduce the incidence of vascular complications in patients undergoing interventional therapy for liver cancer. Arterial compression hemostasis devices mainly consist of a transparent base, a spiral handle, a dial, a pressure plate, and two or three self-adhesive fixed bandages. Its principle is to use the structure of mechanical compression and adhesive tape bonding and fixation to achieve external closure and hemostasis via the dial and a spiral handle, which regulates the pressure plate to compress the puncture [37]. Human factors influence manual compression hemostasis, such as the ease of displacement of sandbags or salt bags, which can easily lead to bleeding at the puncture point, subcutaneous ecchymosis and even hematoma. The advantage of using an arterial compression hemostasis device is that it covers a small area of the puncture point and enables medical staff, including family members, to observe the hemostasis status at any time through the transparent base. The compression position is clear, and the pressure can be adjusted at any time according to the patient’s situation, thus reducing the occurrence of vascular complications. Our study revealed that applying arterial compression hemostasis devices to patients with liver cancer after interventional surgery led to a shorter compression hemostasis time (MD = -13.19 min; 95% CI, -14.19 to -12.19; P < 0.00001) and a shorter limb immobilization time (MD = -8.79 min; 95% CI, -12.65 to -4.94; P < 0.00001) than conventional manual compression combined with sandbag/salt bag hemostasis, and could also reduce the incidences of local bleeding (P < 0.00001), hematoma formation (P < 0.00001), skin ecchymosis (P < 0.00001), dysuria (P = 0.0002), skin damage (P = 0.0003), backache (P = 0.002), and pseudoaneurysm (P = 0.0004). Li Xiaomei et al. [38]. noted that after 1 h of compression following the intervention, the handle should be rotated. After 6 h, the handle should be rotated counterclockwise in stages for 1–2 turns. The arterial compression hemostasis device should be removed after 24 h. Although no significant bleeding or hematoma was observed at the puncture site, 50% of the patients experienced local skin ulceration due to the prolonged compression bandaging. Wu Xueying, Peng Xia et al. [39, 40] reported that releasing the compression hemostasis device after 4–6 h not only did not increase bleeding or hematoma at the puncture site but also reduced the incidences of local skin damage, difficulty urinating, and lower back discomfort. Thus, the earlier the arterial compression hemostasis device is removed, the lower the incidence of skin blisters and skin damage, the higher the patient’s comfort level, and the lower the possibility of complications. Although sensitivity analysis was carried out in this study, and the results also revealed the advantages of arterial compression hemostasis devices in shortening the compression hemostasis and limb immobilization times, the quality of the included original studies was not high, which suggests that arterial compression hemostasis devices should be used appropriately and in a timely manner according to the specific situation of the patient in clinical practice.

The three main causes of thrombosis are as follows: intimal injury or inflammation of the vein, slow blood flow, and blood hypercoagulability [41]. Previous studies have confirmed that one of the complications following hepatic artery chemoembolization is thrombosis [41–44]. In this study, we were surprised to find that compression hemostasis via hemostasis instruments could reduce the incidence of thrombosis in patients with liver cancer after hepatic artery intervention. The possible reason is that the use of arterial compression hemostasis instruments can reduce the limb immobilization time and the time to get out of bed, thus potentially improving patients’ blood flow and ultimately reducing the occurrence of thrombi. Considering the small sample sizes of the included studies, this result should be treated with caution.

Owing to the risk of bias in the included studies, we should treat the results of this study with caution. Most of the included studies did not report blinding, allocation concealment, loss to follow-up, selective reporting, or other potential sources of bias in detail, which seriously reduced the reliability of the evidence. Considering the numerous benefits of arterial compression devices for patients with liver cancer, researchers need to conduct more high-quality and larger sample size studies in the future to further compare the effects of arterial compression hemostasis devices and traditional manual compression hemostasis in patients with liver cancer after interventional therapy.

Limitations of the study

However, there were several potential limitations in this meta-analysis. First, although we adopted a comprehensive retrieval strategy to minimize publication bias, ultimately, we included only Chinese language studies, and there may be a language bias. Second, the intervention times of the included studies were different, and there was a large gap, which reduced the credibility of the evidence. Third, the quality of the included studies was low, and the allocation concealment and blinding of most of the included trials were unclear, which reduced the credibility of the evidence. Future researchers should ensure the methodological quality of RCTs based on high-quality studies.

Conclusions

The analysis revealed that arterial compression hemostasis devices significantly reduce hemostasis and immobilization time, as well as vascular complications in liver cancer patients undergoing interventional therapy. However, the overall quality of the included data in this study is low and lacks data support from other countries. In the future, well-designed RCTs with large sample sizes should be conducted to further verify the results of this study.
