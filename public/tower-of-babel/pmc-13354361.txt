Short versus long myotomy during peroral endoscopic myotomy for achalasia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zain Ul Abideen, Muhammad Hassan Waseem, Rimmel Ali, Avan Chu, Muhammad Sheraz Hameed, Abdullah, Noor-Ul-Huda Ramzan, Pawan Kumar Thada, Prasun Kumar Jalal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) is an established treatment modality for achalasia. This meta-analysis aimed to compare the clinically relevant outcomes of short and long myotomy during POEM for achalasia.Methods:PubMed, Cochrane Central, and ScienceDirect were systematically searched from inception to February 2025. The risk ratios (RRs) and mean differences (MDs) were pooled using the random-effects model in Review Manager. Quality assessment was conducted using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2.0) tool and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.Results:Seven studies, pooling a total of 737 patients, were included in the analysis. The short myotomy showed no significant difference in clinical success [RR: 1.02; 95% CI: (0.99–1.05); P = 0.19], Eckardt score (P = 0.45), or lower esophageal sphincter pressure (P = 0.72) compared to the long myotomy. Similarly, the integrated relaxation pressures (P = 0.73), duration of hospitalization (P = 0.67), and the risk of erosive esophagitis (P = 0.13) were comparable between the two arms. The short myotomy arm significantly reduced overall procedural duration [MD: −15.84; 95% CI: (−21.05 to −10.63); P < 0.00001]. Short myotomy was also associated with a significantly lower risk of abnormal esophageal acid exposure [RR: 0.64; 95% CI: (0.43–0.95); P = 0.03].Conclusion:Short myotomy was associated with a significant reduction in procedural time and risk of abnormal esophageal acid exposure, whereas the remaining efficacy and safety endpoints were comparable between the two techniques.

Introduction

Achalasia is a rare esophageal motility disorder that has an annual incidence of approximately 1 in 100 000 cases and may be higher in certain populations[1]. The disease is characterized by impaired esophageal peristalsis and incomplete relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES).

Although the specific etiology of achalasia is not yet fully understood, several theories exist regarding its pathophysiology. Achalasia exhibits a loss of inhibitory neurons in the myenteric plexus, which results in an imbalance with unaffected excitatory neurons that impairs the relaxation of the LES. It is believed that the loss of inhibitory myenteric neurons is caused by inflammatory damage resulting from an abnormal autoimmune response, with possible viral and genetic involvement[2].

Patients often present with progressive dysphagia, regurgitation, and weight loss. Current achalasia treatment options aim to decrease the hypertonicity of the LES, with the goal of reducing symptoms and halting further dilation of the esophagus. These treatment options include endoscopic botulinum toxin therapy, pneumatic dilation, laparoscopic Heller myotomy, and peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM)[3].

The POEM technique has already been employed to address a range of esophageal conditions, including various types of esophageal diverticula, such as Zenker’s diverticulum[4–6]. POEM is a minimally invasive technique first introduced to clinical practice by Inoue et al, who reported that 17 patients with achalasia experienced a substantial reduction in symptoms and LES pressure after POEM treatment[7]. In addition, variations in the procedure have emerged, with shorter total myotomy lengths than the mean of 8.1 cm reported by Inoue et al[7].

The overall length of the standard long myotomy involves a minimum length of 6 cm into the esophagus and 2 cm below the gastroesophageal junction onto the cardia of the stomach[3]. POEM is generally considered to have a low rate of serious adverse events, with incomplete myotomy and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) serving as late adverse events reported in the literature[8]. However, it is currently unclear how the short myotomy compares with the standard long myotomy in terms of efficacy and safety during POEM, and how myotomy orientation interacts with myotomy length. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the clinical outcomes of short versus long myotomy during POEM in patients with achalasia.

Materials and methods

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions[9] and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines[10]. The protocol for this review was registered on PROSPERO. The AMSTAR and TITAN guidelines (2025) were adhered to during this review[11].

Data sources and search strategy

We performed a comprehensive search across PubMed, Cochrane Central, and ScienceDirect from their inception until February 2025. Additionally, we screened the reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews to identify further studies. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords used were: “Esophageal Achalasia,” “achalasia,” “Myotomy,” “Peroral Endoscopic Myotomy,” “POEM,” “Long myotomy,” and “short myotomy.” Details of the individual search strategies used across different databases are provided in Supplemental Digital Content Table S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies comparing short myotomy with long myotomy in patients with achalasia aged >18 years. Studies were excluded if they had a design other than RCTs or cohort studies or were only available as abstracts. No language restrictions were applied.

All the articles identified in our search were imported into EndNote, and any duplicates were removed. Two independent reviewers (A. and Z.U.A.) screened the titles and abstracts, excluding irrelevant studies. Full-text screening was then conducted according to the eligibility criteria. A third reviewer (M.H.W.) resolved disagreements. The detailed study selection process is depicted in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1). Figure 1.PRISMA flowchart of the study selection process.

Data extraction

Data were extracted into an Excel spreadsheet. The baseline variables extracted include author name, publication year, location, sample size, study design, mean age, gender distribution, achalasia subtype, and duration of symptoms. The primary outcome assessed was clinical success, while secondary outcomes included the Eckardt score, LES pressure, integrated relaxation pressure (IRP), procedural duration, hospitalization duration, incidence of erosive esophagitis, and abnormal esophageal acid exposure. These outcomes were evaluated using standard clinical assessments and esophageal manometry to determine the comparative effectiveness and safety of short versus long myotomy in the management of achalasia.

Definitions of myotomies and clinical success

The definitions of short and long myotomies varied across the included studies. Nabi et al[12] categorized them as ≤3 cm for short and ≥6 cm for long, while Gu et al[13] defined them as 3–4 cm for short and 7–8 cm for long. Huang et al[14] used ≤7 cm for short and >7 cm for long, and Familiari et al[15] used 8 cm for short and 13 cm for long. Li et al[16] classified them as 6–8 cm for short and 10–14 cm for long. Furthermore, Rai et al[17] characterized short as ≤3 cm and long as ≥6 cm, whereas Swei et al[18] defined short as ≤4 cm and long as >4 cm.

The endpoint of clinical success is defined as an Eckardt score of ≤3 or <4.

Data synthesis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Review Manager software (RevMan, Version 5.4.1). The risk ratios (RRs) and mean differences (MDs), along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were pooled for dichotomous and continuous outcomes under a random-effects model. Continuous data available as range and interquartile range were converted into standard deviation using the methods of Hozo et al[19] and Wan et al[20], respectively. Subgroup analysis was conducted according to the study design (RCT or cohort) and the myotomy orientation (anterior, posterior, or mixed). Heterogeneity assessment involved Higgins’ I2 statistics[21] and the Cochrane Q test, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. For outcomes with heterogeneity exceeding 50%, we investigated potential causes through leave-one-out sensitivity analysis.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias in RCTs was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool (RoB 2.0)[22]. This tool assesses bias in five domains, which comprise: (1) bias caused by the randomization process; (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (3) bias arising from missing outcome data; (4) bias in the measurement of the outcome; and (5) bias in the selection of the reported result. For observational cohort studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS)[23] was used to assess the selection of study groups, their comparability, and the ascertainment of exposure or outcome. Two independent reviewers (A.C. and R.A.) performed the assessments, categorizing studies as having high, low, or some concern of bias. A third investigator (M.H.W.) resolved any disagreements. Publication bias was assessed visually through funnel plots.

Result

Search results and study selection

A total of 3453 studies were identified through PubMed (1262), Cochrane Central (229), and ScienceDirect (1962). After removing 1035 duplicates, 2229 studies were excluded during the title and abstract screening. The remaining 189 studies underwent full-text screening, of which 182 were excluded as irrelevant, leaving seven studies[12–18] for final inclusion. The entire study selection process is shown in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

Seven studies (four RCTs and three cohort studies) with a total of 737 patients were included in the final analysis. The patients’ mean age ranged from 31 to 63 years across the studies, while the sample size ranged from 27 to 101. Three studies[13,14,16] were conducted in China, two[12,17] in India, one[15] in Italy, and the remaining one[18] was multicenter. Risk of bias assessment

The quality of the studies included was assessed using the RoB-2 tool[22] and NOS[23]. While assessing the RCTs, the study by Familiari et al[15] had a high risk of bias due to issues in the randomization process, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported results, while there were some concerns in the study by Gu et al[13] regarding the randomization process. The remaining RCTs were assessed as having a low risk of bias (Fig. 2). A low risk of bias was also assessed in non-randomized studies, as shown in the Supplemental Digital Content Table S2, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198. Outcomes

Clinical success

Seven studies, including 698 patients (324 short myotomy vs. 374 long myotomy), evaluated clinical success. The pooled analysis showed comparable clinical success in both myotomies (RR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.99–1.05; P = 0.19; I2 = 0%; Fig. 3). Figure 3.(A) Clinical success subgroup analysis based on study design. (B) Clinical success subgroup analysis based on myotomy orientation.

(A) Clinical success subgroup analysis based on study design. (B) Clinical success subgroup analysis based on myotomy orientation.

Eckardt scores

Seven studies, including 725 patients (334 short myotomy vs. 391 long myotomy), evaluated Eckardt scores. There was no significant difference in post-procedure Eckardt scores between short and long myotomies (MD: 0.05; 95% CI: −0.08 to 0.18; P = 0.45; I2 = 0%; Fig. 4). (A) Eckardt scores: subgroup analysis based on study design. (B) Eckardt scores: subgroup analysis based on myotomy orientation.

Lower esophageal sphincter pressures

Four studies, including 305 patients (134 short myotomy vs. 171 long myotomy), evaluated LES pressures. There was no significant difference in LES pressures between short and long myotomies in POEM (MD: −0.50; 95% CI: −3.19 to 2.19; P = 0.72). The overall heterogeneity observed was high (I2 = 71%; Fig. 5). Figure 5.(A) Lower esophageal sphincter pressures: subgroup analysis based on study design. (B) Lower esophageal sphincter pressures: subgroup analysis based on myotomy orientation.

(A) Lower esophageal sphincter pressures: subgroup analysis based on study design. (B) Lower esophageal sphincter pressures: subgroup analysis based on myotomy orientation.

Integrated relaxation pressures

Four studies, including 407 patients (201 short myotomy vs. 206 long myotomy), evaluated post-POEM IRP. There was no significant difference in IRP between short and long myotomies (MD: 0.24; 95% CI: −1.11 to 1.60; P = 0.73). The overall heterogeneity was high (I2 = 82%; Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198).

Procedure duration

Short myotomy group (337 patients) significantly reduced the procedure duration compared to the long myotomy group (399 patients) in POEM (MD: −15.84; 95% CI: −21.05 to −10.63; P < 0.00001), but with a considerable amount of heterogeneity across seven studies (I2 = 69%; Supplemental Digital Content Figure S2, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198).

Duration of hospital stay

Four studies, including 475 patients (215 short myotomy vs. 260 long myotomy), evaluated the duration of hospital stay. There was no significant difference in the duration of hospital stay between short and long myotomies in POEM, and considerable heterogeneity was observed (MD: 0.13; 95% CI: −0.45 to 0.70; P = 0.67; I2 = 63%; Supplemental Digital Content Figure S3, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198).

Erosive esophagitis

Seven studies, including 643 patients (304 with short myotomy vs. 339 with long myotomy), evaluated erosive esophagitis. There was no significant difference in post-POEM erosive esophagitis between short and long myotomies (MD: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.56–1.08; P = 0.13; I2 = 0%; Supplemental Digital Content Figure S4, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198).

Abnormal esophageal acid exposure

Short myotomy group (195 patients) significantly reduces post-POEM abnormal esophageal acid exposure compared to the long myotomy group (181 patients) in POEM (RR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.43–0.95; P = 0.03; I2 = 26%; Supplemental Digital Content Figure S5, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198).

Subgroup analysis

On subgrouping by study design, the results remained the same across all outcomes (Figure 3-5A and Supplemental Digital Content Figures S1–S4A, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198), except for the abnormal esophageal acid exposure endpoint, which became insignificant (P = 0.06) in the RCT subgroup (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S5A, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198).

On subgrouping analysis based on the orientation of the myotomy approach, the results remained the same across all outcomes (Fig. 3B,6B, Supplemental Digital Content Figure S1B, S3–S4B, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198) with the following exceptions: (1) the Eckardt score showed a slight increase in the short myotomy arm in the mixed myotomy approach subgroup (Fig. 4B); (2) the LES pressure showed a statistically significant decrease in the short myotomy arm in the anterior myotomy approach subgroup (Fig. 5B); (3) procedural duration and abnormal esophageal acid exposure pooled effect sizes became insignificant in the anterior myotomy approach subgroup (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S2B and S5B, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198).

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate heterogeneity (I2) sources, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed for outcomes with I2 values greater than 50%. By removing the study by Huang et al[14], the heterogeneity decreased from 71% to 38% for the outcome of LES pressure (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S6, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198). Similarly, heterogeneity decreased to 0% after removing the Familiari et al study[15] in the analysis of the IRP endpoint (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S7, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198), whereas the removal of the same study[15] from the outcome of the duration of hospital stay decreased the heterogeneity from 63% to 39% (Supplemental Digital Content Figure S8, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198).

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed visually through funnel plots. No significant publication bias was found upon inspecting the symmetry of the funnel plots (Supplemental Digital Content Figures S9–S16, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B198).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis compared the clinical outcomes of short versus long myotomies during POEM for the treatment of achalasia. Our pooled analysis, which included both RCTs and cohort studies, demonstrated that short myotomy is comparable to long myotomy in terms of clinical efficacy, as assessed by clinical success rates and post-POEM Eckardt scores. These findings have important implications for optimizing the POEM technique, guiding patient selection, and informing postoperative management strategies.

Clinical success was uniformly high across all included studies, with success rates consistently exceeding 90% in both the short- and long-myotomy groups. No statistically significant differences in clinical success were observed between the groups, consistent with previous large-scale analyses[24,25]. These results suggest that reducing the length of esophageal myotomy does not compromise procedural efficacy. Post-POEM Eckardt scores were comparable between groups, indicating substantial symptom relief regardless of myotomy length. This supports the hypothesis that short myotomy is sufficient to relieve LES dysfunction, the key pathophysiological abnormality in achalasia. A study by Nabi et al (2022)[26] reported that short myotomy was as effective as long myotomy in patients with type II achalasia.

Short myotomy confers several practical advantages. A consistent finding across multiple studies[13,15] was reduced operative time, which may result in lower anesthesia exposure, decreased procedural costs, and reduced risk associated with prolonged interventions, particularly important in patients with comorbidities. From a technical standpoint, long myotomies require extended submucosal tunneling, potentially increasing the risk of complications such as mucosal injury or bleeding. By limiting tunnel length, a short myotomy may theoretically reduce such risks.

GERD remains a major postoperative concern in POEM, as the procedure disrupts the LES without incorporating an anti-reflux mechanism such as fundoplication, which is commonly performed after Heller myotomy. Longer myotomies, particularly those extending more than 3 cm into the gastric cardia, may compromise the natural anti-reflux barrier and exacerbate reflux symptoms[7,27]. Several studies indicate that shorter myotomies may mitigate this risk. For instance, Nabi et al (2021)[12] reported lower rates of objectively measured GERD (via pH monitoring) in the short myotomy group, despite comparable clinical success. Similar findings were noted by Zhang et al (2022)[28] and Kahaleh et al (2019)[29], who documented reduced incidence of reflux esophagitis and abnormal acid exposure with shorter myotomy lengths. Nonetheless, GERD and esophagitis rates remain high in POEM patients overall, underscoring the importance of vigilant postoperative surveillance and, when indicated, acid-suppressive therapy.

Achalasia is a heterogeneous disorder, classified into types I, II, and III based on high-resolution manometry (HRM) findings[30]. While most patients in this meta-analysis had type I or II achalasia, type III – characterized by spastic distal esophageal contractions – typically necessitates longer myotomies extending beyond the LES to address more extensive disease. Current guidelines[31] support longer myotomy in type III achalasia, as short myotomy may not adequately relieve the spastic segment. Thus, while short myotomy appears sufficient for types I and II, a tailored approach remains essential, especially in type III or morphologically complex cases.

As the surgical endoscopy literature expands, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, is expected to become central to clinical practice and evidence synthesis. A recent global bibliometric analysis using machine learning by Guo et al[32] showed the rapidly increasing presence of LLMs in the medical field, highlighting their role in clinical decision-making and personalized patient care. However, the study also warns that, despite the growing global focus on this area, ethical constraints and challenges to clinical accuracy still need to be addressed. As the management approach for achalasia continues to evolve, future research should not only aim to refine surgical endoscopic techniques, such as POEM, but also explore how AI can safely improve treatment algorithms for achalasia management.

Despite its strengths, this analysis has several limitations. There was heterogeneity in the definitions of “short” and “long” myotomy, as well as in follow-up durations across studies. Inconsistencies in GERD assessment methods, ranging from symptom scores to pH monitoring and endoscopy, further complicate the interpretation of reflux outcomes. Moreover, the long-term durability of short myotomy remains unclear due to a paucity of data beyond 5 years. Potential selection bias in retrospective studies also warrants consideration. Additionally, most subgroup analysis results are based on a small number of pooled studies; caution should be exercised when interpreting these findings.

To validate and refine these findings, future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter RCTs with standardized definitions of myotomy length, rigorous GERD evaluation (including pH impedance testing), and stratified analysis by achalasia subtype. Investigating adjunctive strategies, such as selective fundoplication after POEM (POEM + F) or novel anti-reflux interventions, may further optimize long-term outcomes. Furthermore, it is important to consider patient-specific factors, such as HRM subtype, symptom severity, and esophageal morphology (e.g., sigmoid esophagus) when determining myotomy length.

In conclusion, short myotomy provides comparable clinical efficacy to long myotomy during POEM for patients with achalasia and offers potential advantages, including a significant reduction in procedural time and the risk of abnormal esophageal acid exposure. These benefits make short myotomy an attractive option, particularly for those at higher risk for reflux. However, individualized procedural planning based on achalasia subtype and patient characteristics remains essential. Further high-quality research is needed to establish long-term outcomes and refine subtype-specific recommendations.
