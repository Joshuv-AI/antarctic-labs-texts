Effect of mass transfers of enrollees from liquidated health insurance companies on the financial sustainability of health insurance: an ecological study in the Colombian health system
Authors: Carlos Eduardo Pinzon Florez, Ciro Gómez Ardila, Myriam Ruiz-Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe liquidation of health promoting entities (Entidades Promotoras de Salud (EPS)) in Colombia and the forced transfer of millions of enrollees to solvent insurers have raised concerns about the financial sustainability of the health insurance system. This study examines the impact of these uncompensated mass transfers on key financial indicators of receiving EPS.MethodsA multitemporal ecological study and a difference-in-differences (DiD) model were conducted using national administrative data. Nine continuously operating EPS that received enrollees from liquidated entities were included. Key financial indicators analysed included operating margin, capitation payment unit (Unidad de Pago por Capitación (UPC)) sufficiency, medical loss ratio, per capita medical cost and enrolment volume. Linear mixed-effects and fixed-effects DiD models were applied.FindingsAverage operating margins decreased from 3.2% (2017) to –0.59% (2024). In mixed-effects models, UPC sufficiency was the strongest positive correlate of margin (β=0.28, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.41), while per capita medical cost (β=−0.43, 95% CI −0.66 to −0.21) and enrollee transfers (β=−0.014, 95% CI −0.027 to −0.002) were negatively associated. DiD estimates show that mass transfers reduced operating margins by 1.2 percentage points (95% CI −1.5 to −0.9), with the strongest effects in the first 2 years and consistent results across sensitivity analyses.InterpretationUncompensated mass enrollee transfers from liquidated EPS have a significant negative impact on the financial sustainability of receiving insurers. Regulatory reforms, including risk-adjusted premium adjustments and interinsurer compensation mechanisms, are urgently needed.Keywords: Health insurance, Health systems, Universal Health Care, Health systems evaluation

Introduction

The financial sustainability of health insurance systems remains one of the most persistent structural challenges in contemporary healthcare. In Colombia, this challenge has been exacerbated by a progressive and cumulative phenomenon: the liquidation of health promoting entities (Entidades Promotoras de Salud (EPS)) and the mass transfer of their enrollees to other insurers. Between 2010 and 2024, over 20 EPS were liquidated, affecting millions of affiliates and continuously reshuffling financial burdens and risk across the system.1 2 While these decisions often stem from crises related to management, solvency or quality, the consequences for receiving entities and the overall stability of the health insurance system have been rarely quantified.

Brief overview of health financing and insurance in Colombia

Colombia’s health system underwent a profound transformation in the early 1990s with the enactment of Law 100 of 1993, which created the General System of Social Security in Health (Sistema General de Seguridad Social en Salud (SGSSS)). This reform established a regulated competitive insurance model based on universal enrolment, separation of financing and provision and a standardised benefits package financed through a capitation payment mechanism.

Under the SGSSS, health promoting entities (EPS) operate as regulated insurers responsible for enrolling the population, pooling health risks, purchasing services from public and private providers and managing population health. Financing is primarily public and is channelled through a risk-adjusted capitation payment known as the Unidad de Pago por Capitación (UPC), which is intended to cover the expected medical costs of the insured population. The UPC is differentiated by insurance regime (contributory and subsidised) and adjusted for basic demographic factors such as age and sex.

Risk pooling in Colombia is centralised, as UPC resources are collected and disbursed by a national fund administrator (currently ADRES), while EPS compete on management efficiency and service organisation rather than on premium setting. In principle, this design aims to combine solidarity with regulated competition. However, risk adjustment mechanisms remain limited, and the UPC has shown persistent insufficiency relative to observed medical costs, particularly in the presence of epidemiological transitions, population ageing and rising healthcare inflation.

The National Health Superintendence acts as the regulatory and supervisory authority, with powers to intervene, impose sanctions and administratively liquidate EPS that fail to meet financial, technical or quality standards. Since the late 2000s, Colombia has experienced a growing number of EPS liquidations due to solvency problems, governance failures and accumulated deficits. These liquidation processes have resulted in large-scale, mandatory transfers of enrollees from liquidated EPS to solvent insurers.

Unlike insurance systems with explicit interinsurer risk compensation mechanisms, enrollee transfers in Colombia are typically implemented abruptly and without ex ante or ex post financial adjustments to reflect the health risk profile of the transferred population. Receiving EPS must absorb new enrollees—often with unmet health needs, disrupted care trajectories and higher-than-average medical costs—using the same UPC resources originally calculated for their pretransfer population. Over time, the cumulative effect of repeated liquidations and uncompensated transfers has increased financial pressure on receiving EPS and raised concerns about the structural sustainability of the insurance model.

This institutional and financing context is essential to understanding the potential mechanisms through which mass enrollee transfers may affect the financial performance and operating margins of health insurers in Colombia, and it provides the background for the empirical analysis presented in this study.

Mass enrollee transfers as a structural stressor in regulated insurance systems

This phenomenon is not unique to the Colombian context. In Germany, which operates a mandatory social health insurance system with multiple insurers (Krankenkassen), transfers between funds are voluntary and regulated under principles of actuarial balance. Authorities ensure interfund compensation mechanisms to avoid financial imbalances, especially when an insurer attracts or receives members with higher health risks.3 Meanwhile, the Japanese system, based on multiple employment- and municipality-based insurance schemes, incorporates intergovernmental transfers to compensate for variations in risk profile and age structure, thus preventing territorial insurers from collapsing due to demographic or epidemiological shocks.4

Unlike these models, mass transfers in Colombia are often forced, abrupt and implemented with minimal financial risk planning, resulting in an asymmetric redistribution of healthcare liabilities. Receiving EPS must absorb enrollees with unmanaged chronic conditions, fragmented clinical histories, service backlogs and accumulated debts to providers without any additional resources or compensation mechanisms adjusted to the new risk.5 This scenario directly affects their operating margins, technical reserves and capacity for health risk management, thereby threatening not only their sustainability but also the viability of the insurance model itself.

The effects of mass transfers are not unfamiliar in other sectors. In finance, the absorption of clients from liquidated banks is typically accompanied by liquidity injections, state guarantees or temporary regulatory support to preserve solvency. In education, when closed institutions transfer students to others, the State generally provides additional resources per student to ensure continuity without quality loss.6 In healthcare, however, this transfer of responsibility often occurs without equivalent financial support, transferring institutional problems to other system actors.

Despite the magnitude of the issue, Colombia lacks a systematic assessment of the cumulative impact these transfers have on the financial sustainability of the health insurance system and on the operating margins and financial conditions of receiving EPS. This lack of analysis hinders evidence-based policymaking and limits the ability to anticipate structural risks to the model.

In this context, evaluating the effect of mass transfers from liquidated EPS on the operation of health insurance in Colombia is highly relevant to inform public policies, regulatory mechanisms and operational considerations for structural reforms in health systems worldwide. This study evaluates the financial impact of mass enrollee transfers from liquidated EPS on the operation and sustainability of Colombia’s health insurance system between 2017 and 2024.

Methods

Study design

A multitemporal ecological study was conducted using administrative data to analyse the association between uncompensated transfers of enrollees from liquidated EPS and the operating margin outcomes of receiving EPS during the 2017–2024 period within the Colombian health system.

Population and setting

At the beginning of the study period, 12 health promoting entities (EPS) were identified as having received enrollees from other insurers following liquidation processes. Three EPS were subsequently excluded because they did not operate continuously throughout the 2017–2024 period due to liquidation, merger or loss of regulatory authorisation. The final analytic sample therefore consists of nine EPS with uninterrupted operation over the study period, allowing construction of a balanced panel for longitudinal analyses. These EPS were selected based on the volume of transfers received, their national or regional coverage and the availability of consistent financial and operational data. They represent a diverse and representative sample of Colombia’s health insurance landscape.

The study was conducted within Colombia’s national health insurance system, which features a regulated market of competing insurers operating under a mandatory benefits package (PBS - Plan de Beneficios en Salud) and receiving public capitation payments (UPC). Under this system, the National Health Superintendence administratively liquidates underperforming EPS, whose enrollees are then reassigned en masse to solvent EPS without compensation mechanisms adjusted to risk.

The transfers involved populations with heterogeneous health risk profiles, often burdened by uncontrolled chronic conditions, care continuity gaps and service delays. It was hypothesised that these characteristics would negatively affect the financial and operational performance of receiving EPS. National databases from the Ministry of Health and Social Protection and the National Health Superintendence were used to obtain annual indicators such as operating margin, per capita medical cost, medical loss ratio (MLR), UPC sufficiency and number of enrollees.

Covariate selection and prespecification

Covariates were prespecified based on institutional knowledge of the Colombian health insurance system and prior empirical literature. We distinguished between baseline structural variables, treated as potential confounders, and time-varying operational indicators that may be influenced by enrollee transfers during the study period. Baseline covariates were defined using data from 2017, the year preceding the first major transfer events included in the analysis and capture structural characteristics of insurers that may influence financial performance independently of transfer shocks.

Baseline structural variables included technical reserves measured prior to major transfer events and macroeconomic indicators, such as healthcare inflation. Additional baseline financial indicators included operating margin, debt levels and medical expenditure intensity. These baseline characteristics are presented descriptively in table 1 and were used to inform model specification and interpretation of the regression analyses. Although insurers operate under different regimes (contributory and subsidised), regime type was not included in the regression models to avoid overparameterisation, given the limited number of clusters.

All monetary values were originally recorded in COP and adjusted for inflation using health-sector price indices. For descriptive presentation and ease of international comparison, selected financial aggregates are additionally reported in US$ using a constant average exchange rate (1 US$=4050 COP, 2024). Importantly, currency conversion was not required for estimation and did not affect any regression results, as all econometric analyses were conducted using percentage-based indicators or inflation-adjusted local-currency values.

Internal linkage and data structure

All datasets were linked annually and institutionally using each EPS’s unique code. Data were temporally harmonised for 2017–2024 and adjusted for inflation using health-specific sectoral indices. Cross-validation procedures ensured consistency across financial indicators and affiliate flows. No individual-level or personally identifiable data were used.

The integrated dataset allowed a robust longitudinal assessment of the relationship between user transfers, premium sufficiency, medical costs and EPS operating margins.7

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for each study variable, including means, medians, SD and minimum and maximum values per year. Trend graphs were developed to visualise changes in operating margin, UPC sufficiency, per capita medical cost and number of enrollees over time.

Correlation analysis

Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients (for non-normal distributions) were estimated to examine bivariate relationships among key variables, with particular attention to the association between enrollee transfer volume and operating margin.

Mixed-effects linear regression model

To identify the determinants of operating margin among EPS receiving large numbers of transferred enrollees, we estimated a mixed-effects linear regression model with EPS-level random intercepts to account for unobserved heterogeneity between insurers over time. The dependent variable was the operating margin of each EPS in year t.

The model included covariates capturing financial structure, population health risk and operational conditions of insurers. Specifically, the following explanatory variables were included: UPC sufficiency, UPC revenue per capita, number of transferred enrollees, per capita medical cost, accident rate, proportion of population with chronic illness, total insurer debt, technical reserves relative to claims, healthcare inflation and the cumulative deficit carried by each insurer.

Difference-in-differences model

The difference-in-differences (DiD) approach was employed to estimate the causal effect of mass enrollee transfers resulting from insurer liquidations on the financial performance of receiving health promoting entities (EPS) over the period 2017–2024.

Model design and treatment definition

Two comparison groups were defined. The treatment group comprised EPS that received enrollees from at least one liquidated EPS, operationalised as enrollee inflows exceeding 5% of an insurer’s total enrolled population within a single year. The control group consisted of EPS that did not experience significant enrollee inflows and did not participate in redistribution processes during the study period.

Treatment status varied over time, reflecting the staggered timing of insurer liquidations in the Colombian health system. The 5% threshold was selected to capture enrolment shocks that represent operationally meaningful disruptions to insurers’ risk pools and administrative capacity, rather than routine enrolment fluctuations. In practice, enrolment changes of this magnitude occur almost exclusively in the context of insurer liquidations in Colombia (online supplemental table S1).

The post-treatment period was defined as the year of the first mass transfer for each treated EPS (2018 in most cases). Annual data from 2017 (pretreatment) through 2024 (post-treatment) were used for both treated and control insurers.

Model specification

The primary DiD specification follows a parsimonious design, including only the treatment indicator, post-treatment period and their interaction, with insurer and year fixed effects. This approach avoids conditioning on variables that may lie on the causal pathway between enrollee transfers and financial outcomes. A linear regression model with fixed effects for entity and year was used to control for unobserved heterogeneity and time trends:

The coefficient β₃ represents the average treatment effect, that is, the differential change in operating margin attributable to transfers.

To reduce the risk of mechanical correlation, we carefully assessed whether covariates shared accounting components with the outcome variable. Although the operating margin reflects overall financial performance, it is not mechanically determined by any single regressor. Nevertheless, we estimated alternative specifications excluding cost- and revenue-based covariates and relying on higher-level indicators of financial pressure. These models serve as sensitivity checks to assess whether results are driven by accounting identities rather than underlying financial dynamics.

Given the staggered adoption of treatment across insurers, the DiD coefficient should be interpreted as a weighted average of cohort-specific effects rather than as a single homogeneous causal effect.

Model assumptions

The parallel trends assumption was assessed using graphical inspection of pre-treatment operating margin trajectories and formal pre-trend diagnostics. Onlinesupplemental figures S1 S2 display mean operating margins for treated and control EPS prior to the major transfer cohorts in 2018 and 2021, respectively, showing no systematic divergence before treatment.

Statistical inference and uncertainty

Because the number of insurer clusters in the panel is limited (nine EPS), conventional cluster-robust SEs may underestimate sampling variability. To address this, for the mixed-effects models we estimated cluster-robust sandwich SEs with CR2 small-sample correction (Bell-McCaffrey adjustment) at the EPS level.

For the DiD models, statistical inference was based on wild cluster bootstrap procedures with Rademacher weights and 999 replications, also clustered at the EPS level. These approaches are recommended in settings with a small number of clusters, as they provide more reliable inference by improving the estimation of SEs and associated CIs.

Importantly, these methods adjust the variance–covariance matrix of the estimators and therefore do not affect coefficient estimates. In our case, the application of these corrections resulted in only minor changes in SEs and p values, reflecting the relative stability of the panel structure.

Avoidance of post-treatment conditioning

Several operational indicators—such as medical costs, MLRs and the prevalence of chronic conditions—may be affected by mass enrollee transfers and therefore lie on the causal pathway between treatment and financial outcomes. To minimise post-treatment bias, these variables were excluded from the primary DiD specifications and included only in secondary robustness analyses.

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

A prespecified set of sensitivity analyses was conducted to assess the stability of the results under alternative assumptions and specifications. These included alternative functional forms and scaling of the transfer variable, reduced covariate sets, exclusion of influential EPS-year observations and leave-one-EPS-out estimations. For the DiD framework, we additionally tested alternative definitions of the post-transfer period and implemented placebo tests using preintervention periods. All robustness analyses employed cluster-robust SEs at the EPS level, consistent with the primary models.

Software and tools

Data processing was performed using Stata V.17.0 and R V.4.3.0. Graphs were produced with ggplot2 and mixed models were fitted using the lme4 package. A significance level of p=0.05 was used for all tests.

Results

General characterisation of receiving EPS (2017–2024)

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the analytic sample in 2017, prior to the major waves of enrollee transfers resulting from EPS liquidations. The nine receiving insurers exhibited substantial heterogeneity in size, financial structure and risk profile. Average operating margins were positive but modest (mean 3.2%), while MLRs were already close to levels associated with financial pressure (mean 0.91). The number of enrollees ranged widely across insurers, reflecting the coexistence of large national insurers and smaller regional entities. These baseline characteristics provide important context for interpreting the subsequent evolution of financial indicators and the regression analyses.

At the beginning of the study period, 12 health promoting entities (EPS) were identified as having received enrollees from other insurers following liquidation processes. However, three EPS were excluded from the final analytic sample, resulting in nine EPS included in the longitudinal analyses. These exclusions occurred because the affected insurers did not operate continuously throughout the 2017–2024 period, due to subsequent liquidation, merger or loss of regulatory authorisation. Restricting the sample to EPS with uninterrupted operation ensured the construction of a balanced panel and allowed consistent estimation of within-insurer financial dynamics over time. The final sample therefore consists of nine EPS that operated continuously between 2017 and 2024 and absorbed substantial volumes of transferred beneficiaries following liquidation processes (online supplemental table S2).

These baseline highlight the structural heterogeneity among insurers and underscore the importance of controlling for both financial and operational characteristics in the regression analyses that follow. From a financial perspective, operating margins deteriorated steadily over time.8 In the contributory regime, the mean operating margin declined from 4% (SD±9%) in 2017 to –1% (SD±5%) in 2024, indicating a progressive erosion of financial performance. In the subsidised regime, operating margins were higher throughout the period but also declined, from 31% (SD±19%) in 2017 to 13% (SD±11%) in 2024. The downward trend in both regimes is illustrated in figure 1, with a more pronounced deterioration observed from 2021 onwards.

Medical costs increased substantially during the study period. In the contributory regime, mean medical costs rose from 2.37 (SD±3.72) in 2017 to 5.79 (SD±7.91) in 2024, while in the subsidised regime they increased from 0.14 (SD±0.25) to 2.42 (SD±3.15) over the same period (table 1 and figure 2). In both regimes, growth in medical costs outpaced improvements in operating revenues, contributing to sustained pressure on operating margins.

Indicators of premium sufficiency and medical expenditure intensity further highlight structural financial strain. MLRs increased steadily between 2017 and 2024. In the contributory regime, mean MLR rose from 0.64 (SD±0.28) in 2017 to 0.87 (SD±0.30) in 2024, while in the subsidised regime MLR increased from 0.56 (SD±0.27) to 0.87 (SD±0.30) over the same period (table 1 and online supplemental figure 1). By the end of the study period, MLR levels in both regimes approached thresholds commonly associated with financial unsustainability.

Overall, the descriptive evidence documents a context of rising medical costs, declining operating margins and substantial heterogeneity across insurers, particularly in the contributory regime. These patterns provide a critical empirical backdrop for the mixed-effects and DiD analyses that follow.

Correlation among key variables

The correlation analysis showed a statistically significant positive association between UPC sufficiency and operating margin (r=0.56; p<0.001), as well as a negative correlation between the volume of transferred enrollees and operating margin (r=–0.41; p=0.003). MLR was positively correlated with per capita medical cost (r=0.62; p<0.001). (online supplemental table S3).

Causal evaluation using the DiD model

The DiD analysis indicates a statistically significant negative effect of mass enrollee transfers on the financial performance of receiving insurers. On average, operating margins declined by 1.2 percentage points (95% CI −1.5 to −0.9) among treated EPS compared with insurers that did not receive transferred enrollees during the same period.

Table 3 presents the results of the primary parsimonious DiD specification, which includes treatment status, the post-transfer period, their interaction term and insurer and year fixed effects. This specification avoids conditioning on operational indicators that may themselves be affected by enrollee transfers and therefore lie on the causal pathway between treatment and financial outcomes (table 3).

Dynamic DiD estimates further suggest that the financial impact is most pronounced during the first 2 years following mass transfers, with partial attenuation thereafter (online supplemental table S4). Visual inspection of preintervention trends shows no systematic differences between treated and control EPS prior to the major transfer cohorts in 2018 and 2021 (online supplemental figures S3 and S4), providing support for the parallel trends assumption.

Because transfers occurred at different points in time across insurers, the estimated DiD coefficient should be interpreted as an average treatment effect across heterogeneous transfer episodes, rather than as a precise cohort-specific causal estimate. Given the relatively small number of insurers included in the panel, the results should therefore be interpreted primarily as evidence of systematic post-transfer financial pressure affecting receiving EPS.

To further assess the robustness of the findings, we estimated alternative specifications incorporating operational indicators that may be influenced by enrollee transfers, including medical costs, MLRs, healthcare utilisation and chronic disease prevalence. These models are presented as robustness analyses in online supplemental table S5. As expected, inclusion of these variables attenuated some coefficients, consistent with their potential role as mediators rather than confounders in the relationship between enrollee transfers and insurer financial performance.

Across specifications, the estimated association between mass enrollee transfers and operating margins remained directionally consistent, reinforcing the conclusion that abrupt, uncompensated redistributions of insured populations impose measurable financial pressure on receiving insurers.

Robustness and sensitivity analyses

The robustness of the main findings was evaluated through a comprehensive set of sensitivity analyses designed to assess the stability of the estimated associations under alternative model specifications and assumptions.

Consistent with the modelling strategy described above, the primary DiD specification excludes operational indicators that may be affected by enrollee transfers, in order to avoid conditioning on potential post-treatment variables. As an additional robustness exercise, we estimated alternative specifications incorporating operational indicators that may lie on the causal pathway—such as medical costs, MLRs, healthcare utilisation and chronic disease prevalence. These models are presented in online supplemental table S5. Across specifications, the estimated association between mass enrollee transfers and operating margins remained directionally consistent, although some coefficients were attenuated, consistent with the potential role of these variables as mediators rather than confounders.

Within the mixed-effects framework, the negative association between enrollee transfers and operating margins remained stable across a range of alternative specifications. Effect sizes were comparable when using logarithmic transformations and alternative scaling of the transfer variable, when estimating more parsimonious covariate sets (including models with only year fixed effects) and when excluding EPS–year observations with exceptionally large transfer volumes. Winsorising the outcome variable yielded similar estimates, suggesting that the results were not driven by extreme values.

To address potential concerns regarding accounting identities, we estimated models excluding cost- and revenue-based regressors that may be mechanically related to the operating margin. In these specifications, the estimated association between mass enrollee transfers and operating margins remained directionally consistent and of similar magnitude, indicating that the main findings are not driven by mechanical relationships between accounting variables (online supplemental table S6).

We also conducted leave-one-EPS-out estimations, sequentially excluding each insurer from the panel. Across these iterations, coefficient estimates remained within a narrow range, demonstrating that the results were not driven by any single insurer and instead reflect a systematic pattern across receiving EPS.

Within the DiD framework, the estimated effect of mass enrollee transfers on operating margins was robust to alternative definitions of the post-transfer period, exclusion of insurers experiencing multiple major transfer events and omission of transition years. Importantly, placebo tests using preintervention periods yielded null effects, providing additional support for the identifying assumptions underlying the DiD design.

Finally, we implemented sensitivity analyses using an alternative observable proxy for mass enrollee shocks based on abrupt increases in total enrolment within insurers. Specifically, a mass-transfer event was defined as a year-on-year increase exceeding 5% of total enrolled members for a given EPS, and staggered-adoption DiD models were estimated accordingly. Although this operational definition does not distinguish the institutional origin of transferred enrollees (eg, from liquidated insurers), estimated effects remained directionally consistent, and placebo tests yielded null results. These results suggest that the primary conclusions are not driven by modelling choices, influential observations or timing assumptions (online supplemental table S7).

Discussion

The findings of this study provide robust evidence of the negative impact that mass transfers of enrollees from liquidated EPS have had on the financial sustainability of health insurance in Colombia between 2017 and 2024. Receiving EPS were forced to absorb populations with complex clinical profiles, service delays and without health or financial risk compensation mechanisms for proper management. This has created mounting pressure on their operating margins, reserves and response capacity. The situation has been particularly acute following the liquidation of large EPS such as Cafesalud, Coomeva, Medimás and Emssanar.

The DiD model estimated that receiving EPS experienced an average reduction of 1.2 percentage points in their operating margin compared with EPS that did not receive enrollees during the same periods—a statistically significant result (p<0.01). This loss is compounded by the increase in per capita medical costs, which exceeded the assigned UPC in the subsidised regime by up to COP 200 000 (US$49) annually, revealing a structural insufficiency in the capitation payment scheme when not adjusted for risk in scenarios involving abrupt redistribution of insured populations.

This phenomenon is not unique to the Colombian system. In Germany’s social insurance model, transfers between insurers (Krankenkassen) are regulated by a risk adjustment fund (Risikostrukturausgleich), which redistributes resources according to age, sex, morbidity and expected costs, thereby preventing receiving insurers from being penalised for enrolling populations with higher disease burdens.1 Similarly, Japan implements intergovernmental transfers among its multiple insurance schemes to balance the financial impact of affiliating high-risk or ageing populations, especially in rural or small municipalities.9 These mechanisms have proven effective in maintaining regulated competition among insurers without compromising system sustainability.

In contrast, Colombia lacks a risk compensation fund or automatic UPC adjustments in response to population shocks, shifting the burden of systemic imbalance to the receiving EPS. Studies in Latin America have reported similar challenges in subsidised schemes without risk adjustment,10 where more expensive and underserved enrollees tend to cluster in public entities or weaker insurers, perpetuating a cycle of financial collapse.11

Results from the mixed-effects models should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects, as key coefficients—such as UPC sufficiency and MLRs—reflect strong accounting and institutional relationships with operating margins. Accordingly, causal interpretation in this study relies primarily on the DiD analysis, which exploits temporal variation in mass enrollee transfers under explicit identifying assumptions.

Given the limited number of insurers included in the panel, we implemented small-sample corrections to cluster-robust variance estimators and wild cluster bootstrap procedures to improve the reliability of statistical inference. We therefore prioritise effect sizes, CIs and robustness across specifications over p values alone. To address concerns related to accounting identities, we emphasise the stability of results in specifications that exclude cost- and revenue-based regressors.

Because mass enrollee transfers occur at staggered points in time, conventional two-way fixed effects DiD estimates may reflect a weighted average of cohort-specific effects, rather than a single homogeneous causal effect, particularly in small-N panels.12 13 Consistent with recent methodological guidance, we therefore interpret the DiD estimates as capturing average post-transfer financial pressure, rather than precise cohort-specific causal impacts.12,14 We mitigate risks of overfitting by relying on parsimonious, theory-driven specifications and by demonstrating consistency across sensitivity analyses. While graphical diagnostics support the parallel trends assumption, unobserved time-varying shocks and potential spillovers cannot be fully excluded.

Finally, some operational indicators included in robustness analyses—such as medical costs and chronic disease prevalence—may be post-treatment variables. Conditioning on such variables can introduce bias and obscure causal pathways.14 15 For this reason, our primary conclusions rely on parsimonious specifications adjusting only for baseline confounders and fixed effects, while models including operational indicators are interpreted descriptively rather than causally.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Colombia’s current model of forced affiliate transfers, absent a risk compensation system, has contributed to the progressive financial deterioration of health insurance. Implementing regulatory mechanisms and both ex ante and ex post risk adjustments in premium allocation will enhance the resilience of the health system and improve equity in risk distribution.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has limitations, such as restricted access to individual-level clinical microdata and heterogeneity in the transfer processes. Nevertheless, the strength of the multitemporal ecological design and the analytical approach allows for the identification of robust causal patterns and supports evidence-based recommendations.

The relatively small number of insurers included in the panel (n=9) limits statistical power and may affect the precision of variance estimates, although we addressed this limitation using small-sample corrected cluster-robust estimators and bootstrap procedures.
