Three-dimensional bioprinting in cholangiocarcinoma: from organoid-based drug screening to spatially engineered precision oncology
Authors: Kai Zhang, Haifeng Xu
Published: 2026

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) remains one of the most challenging hepatobiliary malignancies because of its anatomical diversity, molecular heterogeneity, stromal richness and late clinical presentation (1). Although systemic treatment for advanced biliary tract cancer has entered the era of chemoimmunotherapy, and molecularly targeted therapies have expanded treatment opportunities for selected patients, durable responses are still limited to a minority of cases (2,3). For patients undergoing resection, recurrence is frequent; for those with unresectable or metastatic disease, treatment selection after first-line therapy is often empirical. These clinical realities highlight an unmet need for patient-derived models that can functionally interrogate therapeutic vulnerability beyond static genomic annotation. Organoids have provided an important step forward in CCA research by enabling long-term propagation of patient-derived tumor epithelium, preservation of key molecular features and individualized drug screening (4). However, conventional organoid systems remain limited by their dependence on poorly defined and expensive Matrigel, which are prone to batch-to-batch variability. These limitations affect reproducibility, mechanical control, and clinical-scale standardization. In this context, three-dimensional bioprinting offers a complementary strategy: it can engineer tumor cells, bioinks and microenvironmental components into controllable architectures, potentially transforming CCA models from tumor-cell avatars into spatially organized, clinically informative platforms.

The conceptual value of three-dimensional bioprinting lies in its ability to engineer the spatial, mechanical, and cellular context of CCA in a controlled manner. Unlike conventional organoids, which largely depend on spontaneous self-organization within Matrigel, bioprinting enables the defined placement of tumor cells, extracellular matrix components, and non-malignant stromal elements (5,6). Tumor cells can therefore be organized into reproducible structures with controlled size, geometry, and cell density, reducing experimental variability and facilitating standardized drug-response assessment. At the same time, multicellular printing allows cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), macrophages, endothelial cells, or immune cells to be incorporated into the same construct, thereby reconstructing key components of the CCA niche. Such models may better capture the mechanisms by which stromal cells promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition, extracellular matrix remodeling, immune exclusion, and therapeutic resistance. This engineering capacity also extends to the physical properties of the tumor microenvironment. CCA is characterized by dense desmoplasia and altered tissue stiffness, yet these features are difficult to reproduce in traditional organoid systems. By adjusting bioink composition, hydrogel concentration, and crosslinking conditions, three-dimensional bioprinting can more precisely modulate matrix stiffness and biochemical cues. This is important because matrix mechanics may influence cell polarity, invasion, stemness, drug penetration, and treatment response. Thus, the promise of bioprinting in CCA is not simply to generate more complex in vitro cultures, but to reconstruct disease-relevant tumor ecosystems with greater spatial and mechanical control.

Although the application of three-dimensional bioprinting in CCA remains at an early stage, several studies have already established its feasibility and translational potential. The first important step was the development of patient-derived intrahepatic CCA bioprinted models. In this setting, primary tumor cells were incorporated into hydrogel-based scaffolds to generate three-dimensional constructs that retained key tumor features and allowed anti-cancer drug testing (7). This work demonstrated that CCA tissue could be converted into a printable and functionally testable patient-derived model, providing proof of principle for individualized therapeutic evaluation. Subsequent studies extended the concept from tumor-cell modeling to tumor-microenvironment reconstruction. A three-dimensional bioprinted immune microenvironment model of CCA incorporated stromal and immune components to examine how non-malignant cells influence tumor behavior (8). This approach showed that stromal elements are not merely structural accessories, but active regulators of tumor proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, Wnt/β-catenin signaling and chemoresistance. These findings are particularly relevant because clinical CCA is characterized by abundant desmoplasia and immunosuppressive stromal niches. Another direction is the integration of bioprinting with organ-on-chip technology. A 3D-bioprinted CCA-on-a-chip model attempted to reproduce the liver-vascular-biliary context by combining CCA cells with hepatobiliary and endothelial components (9). Compared with static culture systems, such platforms may better model perfusion, nutrient gradients, drug exposure and the anatomical constraints of the biliary tract. This is important for CCA, whose biology is shaped by both malignant epithelium and the surrounding hepatic-biliary microenvironment.

More recently, patient-derived 3D-bioprinted intrahepatic CCA models have moved closer to clinical application by combining histological, genomic and transcriptomic validation with personalized drug-response testing (10). This represents a conceptual transition from technical feasibility to clinically anchored tumor avatars. Together, these studies suggest that 3D bioprinting can serve three complementary roles in CCA: a patient-specific drug-testing platform, a mechanistic model for stromal and immune interactions, and a spatially engineered system for reproducing the anatomical complexity of hepatobiliary tumors.

Beyond drug screening, three-dimensional bioprinting provides an experimental system for addressing fundamental biological questions in CCA. This disease is characterized by dense desmoplastic stroma, abundant extracellular matrix deposition, and extensive crosstalk between malignant epithelial cells and non-malignant components (11). A major value of bioprinting is that it allows these interactions to be reconstructed in a modular and controllable manner.

CAFs represent a particularly relevant component of this engineered niche. As central regulators of matrix remodeling, tumor invasion, and therapeutic resistance in CCA, CAFs can be co-printed with tumor cells as patient-matched populations or defined fibroblast subtypes (12). Such models allow investigators to examine how stromal composition, spatial proximity, and matrix stiffness regulate epithelial-mesenchymal transition, stem-like programs, and drug tolerance. They may also help distinguish whether resistance is primarily tumor-intrinsic or induced by the surrounding fibrotic niche. The same logic can be extended to immune regulation. CCA often develops an immune-suppressive microenvironment enriched with macrophages, myeloid cells, and excluded or dysfunctional T cells. Multicellular bioprinting could reconstruct tumor-macrophage-T cell interactions and provide a platform to test whether stromal or myeloid targeting can restore immune sensitivity. This is particularly relevant in the era of chemoimmunotherapy, where only a subset of patients achieves durable clinical benefit (13). Bioprinted systems may also help investigate spatial heterogeneity within the tumor ecosystem. Defined gradients of oxygen, nutrients, matrix density, and drug penetration can be engineered to mimic hypoxic cores, invasive fronts, and vascular-adjacent regions. These spatially organized models may reveal why different tumor regions show divergent proliferative, invasive, or resistant states.

For clinicians, the ultimate value of three-dimensional bioprinting in CCA will depend on whether it can provide information that changes therapeutic reasoning. At present, treatment decisions are mainly guided by tumor stage, anatomical resectability, performance status, liver function, molecular alterations and prior therapy (2,4). However, these parameters do not always predict whether an individual tumor will respond to chemotherapy, targeted therapy or combination regimens. A patient-derived bioprinted model could therefore serve as a functional layer of precision oncology, complementing next-generation sequencing rather than replacing it.

Several clinical scenarios may be particularly suitable for this approach. First, after surgical resection, sufficient tumor tissue is often available for model establishment (14). In this setting, bioprinted drug testing could help refine adjuvant treatment selection, especially for patients at high risk of recurrence. Second, for locally advanced or borderline resectable intrahepatic CCA, a rapid functional assay might help prioritize neoadjuvant or conversion strategies before patients undergo prolonged ineffective therapy (15). Third, in advanced disease after first-line treatment failure, bioprinted models could support rational selection among second-line chemotherapy, targeted agents, local-systemic combinations or clinical trial options (16).

Nevertheless, important barriers remain before clinical implementation. Establishment success rate, turnaround time, tissue requirement, tumor purity, preservation of stromal and immune components, bioink standardization and drug-response cutoff definition all require systematic validation. Biopsy-derived samples may be particularly challenging because tissue is limited and often spatially biased. Moreover, in vitro sensitivity does not automatically translate into clinical benefit, as pharmacokinetics, host immunity, liver function and treatment tolerance also influence outcomes. Therefore, the realistic near-term role of 3D bioprinting is not to dictate therapy independently, but to support decision-making in clinically ambiguous situations. Its output should be interpreted together with molecular profiling, radiological assessment, pathology and patient condition. If prospectively validated, bioprinted CCA models may become a clinically meaningful bridge between tumor biology and individualized therapeutic strategy.

Three-dimensional bioprinting offers a complementary and potentially transformative approach by enabling controlled reconstruction of tumor cells, extracellular matrix and microenvironmental components within defined architectures. Its significance lies not merely in creating more complex in vitro models, but in engineering the fibrotic and immune-suppressive niche that drives CCA progression and therapeutic resistance. For basic researchers, bioprinting provides a controllable platform to dissect tumor-stroma interactions, spatial heterogeneity and drug tolerance. For clinicians, it may eventually serve as a functional decision-support tool.
