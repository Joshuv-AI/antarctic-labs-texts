The global and domestic in policy: Impacts on medical education and migration
Authors: Zakia Arfeen, Dawit Wondimagegn, David Rojas, Katherine Woolf, Cynthia Whitehead
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealthcare systems around the world face major challenges related to globalisation, technological advancements, and inequities in resource and workforce shortages. As medical educators turn to social accountability to address the needs of their communities and regions, global and domestic issues are often incongruent. Globally, the migration of medical doctors affects almost all countries of all income levels. Imperial legacies continue to shape the migratory patterns of international medical graduates and the challenges that these doctors face during and after migration. This paper examines the impact of accreditation and regulatory practices on the landscape of migration policies and governance, discusses the experiences of doctors working overseas, detrimental effects on exporting countries, and examines some potential mitigating strategies that support exporting countries. Across all these areas, medical education can play a key role to reconcile tensions between competing domestic and global concerns.Keywords: Medical education, Medical migration, International medical graduates, Accreditation, Collaborations

Introduction

Healthcare systems across the world face significant challenges, including workforce shortages, rapid technological change and uneven resource distribution. These issues have economical, educational, ethical and political dimensions.1 While societal need and social accountability are often highlighted in medical institutions’ mission, vision and values statements, in practice, medical education often functions in academic silos.2 By concentrating on curriculum and assessment practices that meet regulatory and accreditation requirements, individual schools sometimes appear to care more about attracting top applicants, exam pass rates and the success of their graduates than addressing broader health systems challenges.3 For medical education to contribute more consciously to domestic and global issues, educators must recognise the effects of educational policies and practices on broader health systems issues.

In this paper, we discuss the many pressures that these policies and practices bring to global and domestic issues and their impact on medical migration. We first draw attention to how historical legacies continue to shape current migration tensions, using a post-colonial lens to frame our discussion. We provide brief historical examples of medical knowledge exchange and migration in the pre-colonial, colonial and post-colonial eras. We draw on migration in the present – centred around international medical graduates (IMGs),4 who form part of a broader, complex architecture of global medical migration – and look at accreditation and regulation, and their intersection with policies and practices. We then focus on IMGs in the UK, a country with deep colonial legacies, which is one of the largest importers of healthcare workers and has significant current workforce issues.5 Finally we discuss the impact of migration on exporting countries and the role that academic medical institutions could play in contributing to global mitigation strategies.

Historical context

Medical knowledge exchange and travel of medical personnel is a millennia-old phenomenon, as individuals and societies seek to learn from others and to share healing practices.6, 7 In medieval times, Japanese medicine built upon traditional Chinese medical teachings. Similarly, in the Islamicate world of the ninth to 11th centuries, scholars including Ibn Sina (known in Europe as Avicenna) studied Greco-Roman traditions as Baghdad became a leading medical education centre.6 In both cases, the sophisticated medical education structures and systems that developed drew upon knowledge from other cultures in developing their own medical traditions.

During the centuries of European colonisation and settlement, European governments exported their knowledge systems, institutional structures and practices. In India, for example, Thomas Macaulay’s infamous 1835 ʻMinute on Indian education’8 was scathing in its disdain for Indian knowledge, culture and healing practices, and helped cement the delivery of an English medical curriculum across the British Raj and other British colonies.9, 10, 11, 12 Whether led by colonial governments or missionary societies, Western medical doctors set up healthcare systems, structures and education that mirrored those in their own countries. Traditional Indigenous knowledges were largely ignored, suppressed or appropriated.13 As former colonies gained independence in the 1940s–60s, healthcare and education systems often remained intact, with continued support from western academic or philanthropic institutions.

The exchange of knowledge has come through short-term travel of medical personnel, but doctors have also moved for many reasons,14 including social, cultural or economic upheaval, or a sense that emigration will provide desirable opportunities not available at home. Graduates of Scottish medical schools travelled in large numbers to Australasia, South Africa, the British West Indies and Upper Canada in the 19th Century.15, 16 Historians estimate that thousands of Jewish and other doctors left Nazi-occupied nations in the 1930s and 1940s. With the advent of the NHS in England in 1948, NHS-wary English doctors moved to Canada, the USA, Australia and New Zealand.17, 18, 19, 20, 21 To help fill these gaps, NHS recruitment included medical graduates from Scotland and Wales, healthcare professionals from the Caribbean (as part of the Windrush generation),22 and doctors from India and Pakistan.23 These clinicians solidified and upheld the NHS, and continue to form a significant portion of the UK healthcare workforce.

Concerns about the losses led to some of the first studies tracking medical migration, documenting the movement of UK medical graduates to other countries. The term ‘brain drain,’ now used primarily to describe migration from lower- to higher-income countries, was coined in the 1950s by the Royal Society to describe the outflux of UK medical graduates at this time.24, 25, 26 Since the 1970s, there has been increased global monitoring of health workforce migration, with the World Health Organization (WHO) contributing landmark studies and reports.27, 28, 29 During these decades, the overwhelming flow of doctors has been from the Global South to the Global North and many found this convenient for workforce management:

ʻforeign medical graduates were perceived by receiving countries to be dispensable commodities, a supply to be turned on and off at will by the wealthier countries who can afford to employ them for their own needs.’26p.4

Recognising the serious effects of this migration, the WHO adopted a Code of Practice in 2010 on the recruitment of health workers.28 Although voluntary, the Code has established itself as the main international framework governing fair and ethical health workforce recruitment. It encourages international collaboration and countries to prioritise meeting their health workforce needs domestically. It aims to render migration mutually beneficial to the health systems of both exporting and receiving countries, preferably through bilateral government-to-government agreements.29 Fifteen years later, however, global health workforce mobility has increased with the receiving countries remaining largely the same, while exporting countries expanded.30 This has created large communities of doctors within host countries who are IMGs, making up 42%, 27% and 25% of doctors in the UK, Canada and the USA respectively.31, 32, 33 Globally, these communities do not migrate unchecked but are subject to gatekeeping and compliance checks, which are in turn affected by migration.

Accreditation, regulation and their influence on medical mobility

Accreditation and professional regulation are commonly framed as mechanisms for quality assurance and public accountability.34 Across multiple settings, however, they also enact structural conditions that impact how medical mobility occurs.35 They affect how credentials are interpreted and valued,36 influence institutional and curricular orientation,37 and shape the pathways through which migration becomes viable and desirable.38 It becomes essential for educators and policymakers to understand how accreditation and regulation are part of the underlying architecture of migration.

The global expansion of the World Federation for Medical Education (WFME) recognition programme, which sets internationally accepted expectations, carries practical and reputational weight for institutions and graduates alike.39 It supports standardisation, and can aid institutional quality improvement as well as strengthening assurance domestically.39 By making credentials more readily interpretable and acceptable to receiving-country regulators and employers, it can give graduates clearer and more predictable licensure pathways across borders,40 particularly for those from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)40 where outward migration is common.39 In medical training programmes that orient curricula, assessment, language of instruction and/or institutional positioning toward external licensure environments, accreditation becomes a way of demonstrating alignment with international training and practice norms.41 Migration, whether intended or not, becomes built into how programmes present themselves, how students imagine professional futures, and how qualification pathways are designed.42 In this way, regulatory systems integrate migration into the institutional logic of training rather than treating it as an unintended outcome,43 and contribute to making migration a credible and often attractive option within a wider set of workforce needs, immigration policies, and career incentives.42

These dynamics intersect sharply with workforce realities in many LMIC settings, where there is limited specialist coverage, uneven geographic distribution of healthcare workers and constrained training capacity.44 Losses in such contexts are a significant strain on supervision, postgraduate pipelines and academic continuity.45 Medical migration thus affects workforce service and training in ways that go far beyond individual career decisions.46, 47 Accreditation therefore can be an enabler of undermining domestic training while allowing graduates to evidence equivalence, engage with external labour markets, and to treat migration as a rational extension of this recognised training.43, 48

In receiving countries, these same arrangements support structured reliance on IMGs. Processes of performance verification and regulation allow the integration of IMGs into domestic workforces. These workforces depend on IMGs for service continuity, particularly in underserved regions and specialties.49, 50 However, the performance verification processes and licensing assessments can also produce integration challenges for IMGs, limit what doctors are permitted to do in practice, and lead to regression in their careers.51 Such misalignments also shape the experiences of those who later return to their home countries, where certification regimes and institutional expectations can differ substantially from those in which they trained or practised while abroad.52, 53

What emerges is a pattern that highlights how the wider systems of labour demand, educational strategy and workforce policy are dependent on processes of accreditation and regulation within medical education and how this interdependence plays out on global platforms and across national borders. The exact effects that accreditation and regulation of IMGs have on health policy vary in different contexts, depending on the priorities that regulators and governments try to pursue through IMGs (ie higher mobility within systems of labour and demand, expansion of services in underserved regions and specialties, etc). Local accreditation and regulation practices can influence medical migration pathways, but can also be impacted by them, as demonstrated by the migration patterns and experiences of IMGs in the UK.

Medical migration to the UK and the experiences of IMGs

The UK continues to struggle to train enough domestic medical students to meet demand, and current workforce shortages are key drivers in domestic policy development and implementation. With very low numbers of medical doctors per 10,000 population compared to the regional average,54 the UK continues to rely on IMGs to fill gaps in service delivery. IMGs often experience a stark difference in culture, society and healthcare systems between their home and host countries,55 and face many challenges in adapting.56, 57 Many IMGs must initially accept careers or locations that are less favourable in order to secure a job and financial stability.58 IMGs have long been less likely than domestic medical graduates to secure training posts.33, 59 In 2026, the UK government introduced structural barriers for IMGs by introducing the prioritisation of domestic medical graduates for training posts.60 IMGs experience differential attainment in postgraduate examinations and those in training are less likely to progress.61 IMGs are at greater risk of referral to governing bodies, and while progress has been made to address this, systemic action is still needed to address underlying issues.62, 63, 64, 65 IMGs’ struggles around identity, belonging and a sense of job satisfaction are linked to poorer morale and career progression.58 Due to social isolation, IMGs may have to contend with these challenges alone.66

Efforts to support and integrate IMGs can fall short.67 A recent study of NHS hospital trust retention policies for ethnic minority and international staff groups found evidence of anti-discrimination and anti-bullying policies, but relatively few interventions such as induction, onboarding and networking to engender a sense of belonging and trust among international staff.68 Interventions to support IMG acculturation should address organisational, training and personal factors,69 but patchy roll-out and a lack of robust evaluation may negatively affect outcomes and retention of this key workforce group.70 National NHS retention resources, like the NHS Employers Retention Guide,71 can lack specific information related to key issues for IMGs such as discrimination and inequalities in pay and career progression.72

The lack of support for IMGs impacts their wellbeing and progression, and can have significant consequences for patients and the health service. For example, if doctors feel at greater risk of referral, they may practise medicine defensively, which can risk harm to patients.73 A recent study found that IMG GPs struggle with the issues outlined and are more likely to leave general practice within 3 years of qualifying.56 Experiencing discrimination is also a strong predictor of healthcare worker attrition.74 A 2025 report from the UK medical regulator, the General Medical Council, showed a dramatic increase in IMGs giving up their medical licences.33 Poor experiences and fewer opportunities, compounded by stricter immigration rules and changes to recruitment that prioritise domestic medical graduates for training posts, may result in more IMGs leaving the UK and fewer joining, having significant impact on the workforce. Addressing issues for IMGs in the UK requires domestic policy and practice; however, migration patterns also negatively impact exporting countries and tackling these issues may require a more global approach.34

Negative effects on exporting countries and potential mitigating strategies

As previously mentioned, net migration of healthcare workers is from LMICs to high-income countries (HICs), creating a globally skewed health workforce distribution.5 Ethical and practical measures to ensure a more balanced exchange should be a matter of urgency and require a thorough understanding of both the persistent push factors in exporting countries and the pull factors in receiving countries, issues that have been constant for the past five decades.75 Inadequate remuneration and incentives, poor working conditions, political instability, corruption, inadequate attention to supportive management practices, and limited opportunities for career progression and professional development help drive migration out of exporting countries.76 Ageing populations and the consequent demand for health professionals in HICs significantly attract migrant health workers. High training costs to be a doctor in HICs make recruiting health professionals from LMICs cost-effective, thereby raising demand.

In the African region, for example, active recruitment of IMGs by HICs such as the UK, Germany, Canada and the USA has worsened the African region’s health workforce shortages.77 As of June 2025, 7.7% of hospital doctors in the UK were from Africa, representing an increase from 2.7% in June 2016.78 While medical migration can generate remittances for exporting countries,79, 80 the resulting health workforce shortages in LMICs, partly driven by outward migration, hinder their ability to deliver adequate healthcare and achieve health-related development goals such as universal health coverage.81 Developing effective strategies to address the push factors, which are under the jurisdiction of African countries, is an important step towards improving health workforce retention and mitigating the impacts of excessive brain drain.

The growing recognition that brain drain exacerbates shortages in LMICs, as well as concerns about the exploitation of migrant health workers, culminated in the adoption of the WHO Code by the World Health Assembly in 2010.28 The Code also established a Health Workforce Support and Safeguard List (SSL), which was last updated in 2023.82 Of the 55 countries on the WHO SSL facing critical health worker shortages, 37 are located in the African region. Active international recruitment from these countries is generally discouraged and only admissible under government-to-government agreements.

Unfortunately, experience with bilateral agreements has not yet been successful. As a potential alternative, educational partnerships between high- and low-income institutions focused on building capacity in LMICs might partially mitigate imbalances. These partnerships must be based on trust and active recognition of the historical determinants of inequality, such that HICs reciprocate by funding the training of health professionals in LMICs and providing their institutional capacity and experience to support low-income institutions. The Toronto Addis Ababa Academic Collaboration, a long-standing academic collaboration between Addis Ababa University in Ethiopia and the University of Toronto in Canada, in which two of our authors are closely involved, is one example of this model.83, 84 Fostering a partnership that is based on domestic needs and targeted domestic priorities in capacity building has helped to address the problem of brain drain and subsequent inequities.84 However, single and isolated cases can only indicate what is possible; support by robust policy frameworks will be required for the development of larger-scale global models.43 Recognising that universities and medical schools have historically been instruments used in empire building, could educational partnerships pave a path towards reparation and form part of a social and global responsibility of both governments and high-income higher education institutions? If medical education aims to contribute to resolving domestic and global concerns, HIC medical institutions must reflect on their own policies and how they contribute to global workforce issues.

Discussion

Global expansion of healthcare systems and education has had profound domestic and global effects on policy and practice, and in turn global workforce distribution. The widespread acceptance and enactment of WFME-led accreditation of undergraduate medical education lacked an evidence base, and many are now arguing for more systematic enquiries into this process. It is an example of policy development where introspection is essential to foster transparency and debate to avoid burdening certain countries and increasing inequity.85, 86 While medical migration involves a complex interplay between autonomy and socio-economic conditions globally, the effects on countries with a significant net loss of doctors raises ethical and material concerns that have been recognised yet unresolved for many decades. Mitigation strategies introduced over the past half century have failed to reduce inequities between countries, and these inequities are then mirrored among domestic communities of IMGs in host countries, where challenges persist, and pledges to improve support and retention remain variable.

To move beyond hopeful resolutions, there needs to be careful evaluation and evidence of the effectiveness and consequences – intended and unintended – of current promises to reduce reliance on international staff.87 In the UK, for example, the government has pledged to reduce overseas recruitment of NHS staff to less than 10% over the next decade. It aims to achieve this through actions including increasing recruitment among domestic communities by expanding medical school places and nursing apprenticeships.88 By explicitly framing these persisting inequitable global and domestic issues as linked and requiring simultaneous attention, health policymakers and medical educators have the potential to move beyond siloed approaches. Driven by evaluation evidence from current initiatives, successful elements can be amplified and unsuccessful ones modified.

Conclusion

Reconciling the tension between global influences and policies with domestic needs and priorities remains critical to promote global workforce sustainability to limit the damage caused by medical migration to exporting countries, and to protect the rights of health workers globally. Medical education has contributed to the historical development of these problems. While medical education alone cannot solve these challenges, it has a pivotal role to play in moving forward in concert with policymakers so that domestic graduates benefit and wider global implications are mitigated.
