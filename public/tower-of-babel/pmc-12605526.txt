Evaluation of the relation of CD133 expression measured by immunohistochemistry assay with the survival of the colorectal cancer patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Maliheh Naghavi, Keyvan Nikkhah Bahrami, Yekta Ghane, Ali Ezzatollahi Tanha, Farzane Ahmadi, Niloofar Nikkhahbahrami, Ahmad Jalilvand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent and deadly malignancy, often characterized by cellular heterogeneity and resistance to therapy. CD133, a proposed cancer stem cell marker, has been implicated in poor clinical outcomes, but its prognostic value remains controversial, particularly in diverse populations.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the relationship between CD133 expression and overall survival (OS) in patients with colorectal adenocarcinoma treated at Mousavi Hospital in Zanjan, Iran.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 55 patients who underwent surgical resection for histologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma between 2012 and 2017. CD133 expression was assessed using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and categorized as positive or negative. Associations with clinicopathological features and 5-year survival were examined using logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards (PH) models.ResultsCD133 expression was detected in 47.3% of tumors. It was significantly more prevalent in high-grade tumors (83.1% vs 18.7%, p = 0.001) and in female patients (66.7% vs 32.2%, p = 0.015). Five-year survival was significantly lower in CD133-positive patients (19.2%) compared to CD133-negative individuals (75.9%, p < 0.001). In multivariable Cox regression adjusted for tumor grade and age, CD133 positivity was associated with increased hazard of death (adjusted hazard ratio(HR) = 5.03; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.66–15.25; p = 0.004). Because of the modest sample size and wide confidence intervals, this association should be considered preliminary. In crude analyses, tumor grade impacted survival; in the adjusted model, only CD133 remained remarkable, while age, sex, and tumor location showed no significant associations.ConclusionsCD133 expression is significantly associated with high tumor grade and poor survival in Iranian CRC patients, but findings should be interpreted with caution, given the small cohort. Its evaluation could aid in identifying high-risk individuals and guiding personalized treatment strategies in larger confirmatory studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00384-025-05018-1.Keywords: CD133, Colorectal cancer, Immunohistochemistry, Cancer stem cell, Survival

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a significant public health challenge and is one of the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide. Globally, CRC accounted for approximately 1.93 million new cases and 0.90 million deaths in 2022, nearly 9.6% of all cancers and 9.3% of cancer deaths [1].

CRC exhibits marked heterogeneity at the histologic and molecular levels, which contributes to variable treatment responses and outcomes. Contemporary management is anchored by TNM staging and molecular profiling, notably mismatch repair/microsatellite instability (MMR/MSI) status and RAS (KRAS/NRAS) and BRAF mutations; additional frameworks such as consensus molecular subtypes, HER2, and circulating tumor DNA are increasingly used to refine risk and therapeutic selection [2–4]. Within this clinicomolecular context, any putative biomarker should be viewed as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, established determinants [4–6].

A key dimension of CRC heterogeneity is the presence of cancer stem cells (CSCs), a subpopulation with self-renewal, tumor-initiating capacity, and relative resistance to cytotoxic therapy, thereby contributing to recurrence and metastatic spread [4, 7, 8]. Among proposed CSC markers, CD133 (prominin-1) has been studied extensively in CRC and other solid tumors. CD133-positive colorectal cancer cells demonstrate enhanced clonogenicity, survival under treatment stress, migratory/invasive potential, and links to pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin and phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B pathway (PI3K/Akt), supporting a role in stemness, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and therapy resistance [7, 9–11]. Under hypoxia, CD133-positive CRC cells upregulate hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1α) and epithelial-mesenchymal transition features (e.g., N-cadherin, vimentin), changes that facilitate dissemination [11]. Co-expression with other markers, including C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), further augments metastatic behavior; CD133-positive/CXCR4-positive subpopulations are enriched in liver metastases, and CXCR4 blockade can curb liver-colonizing ability in preclinical models [10].

Clinically, multiple cohorts have associated higher CD133 expression with adverse features, including poor differentiation, nodal involvement, metastasis, and poorer survival. In stage II CRC, CD133-overexpressing tumors showed significantly worse 5-year overall and disease-free survival versus low-CD133 tumors (61.9% vs 80.2% and 64.8% vs 75.8%, respectively) [12]. Elevated CD133 has likewise correlated with advanced T category, positive nodes, higher grade, and visceral metastases, consistent with a clinically aggressive subset [13]. Meta-analytic syntheses, while limited by heterogeneity in antibody clones, IHC scoring thresholds, and incomplete molecular adjustment, have reported that CD133 positivity is associated with reduced overall and disease-free survival in CRC [14]. Emerging data also suggest that CD133 expression on circulating tumor cells (CTCs) may predict worse outcomes, hinting at a complementary liquid-biopsy avenue that warrants validation across platforms and stages [2].

Representative large and well-characterized cohorts support this overall pattern. In a German series, Horst et al. reported poorer 5-year survival for CD133-high tumors (multivariable HR ≈ 2.45) [15]. In a Chinese cohort, Li et al. likewise found that ≥ 5% CD133-positive cells predicted worse 5-year survival (multivariable HR ≈ 2.68) [16]. In Singapore, Ong et al. observed that CD133 positivity not only predicted inferior overall survival but also reduced the benefit from 5-fluorouracil-based chemotherapy [17]. A Japanese study by Kojima et al. found that CD133 overexpression was associated with poorer overall survival, with evidence of independence in stratified analyses [18]. More recently, in a Korean stage II cohort, Park et al. showed that CD133-high tumors had significantly worse overall and disease-free survival [12]. Together with meta-analyses, these data underscore a consistent, clinically adverse signal for CD133 across diverse settings (see Table S13).

We hypothesized that membranous CD133 positivity by immunohistochemistry is associated with worse overall survival after curative-intent resection of colorectal adenocarcinoma. Pre-specified objectives were to (I) describe the distribution of CD133 expression and its clinicopathologic correlates, (II) estimate crude and adjusted associations between CD133 status and overall survival, and (III) explore effect modification by tumor grade.

While several international studies have examined CD133 in CRC, there is a relative paucity of data from Middle Eastern populations, including Iran. Understanding the expression patterns of CD133 in local cohorts is crucial for validating its prognostic utility across diverse ethnic and geographic backgrounds. In this context, the present study aims to evaluate the relationship between CD133 expression, as determined by immunohistochemistry, and overall survival in patients with colorectal cancer treated at Mousavi Hospital in Zanjan, Iran. By correlating CD133 expression levels with clinical outcomes, we hope to contribute region-specific insights into its prognostic significance and further elucidate its potential role in CRC management.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort included patients who underwent curative-intent resection for colorectal adenocarcinoma at Mousavi Hospital, Zanjan, Iran (2012–2017). Follow-up data were accrued through December 2022. The protocol was approved by the Zanjan University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (IR.ZUMS.REC.1402.164); data were de-identified before analysis. All patient information was de-identified to ensure confidentiality. Data were used solely for research purposes. All investigators adhered to ethical guidelines for human research and maintained strict data privacy throughout the study.

This study adheres to STROBE; a completed checklist is provided as Supplementary Material (see supplementary table S14) [19].

Patient selection and eligibility criteria

The study population included all patients who underwent resectional surgery for histologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma during the study period. Inclusion criteria were (I) availability of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue blocks and accessible clinicopathological and follow-up data, including survival time and vital status. Exclusion criteria included (I) incomplete clinical or pathological records; (II) inability to contact the patient or family for follow-up on life status and survival; (III) death within 1 month postoperatively (perioperative mortality); (IV) death due to causes unrelated to cancer, such as trauma, suicide, or accidents; and (V) samples labeled with volvulus as the clinical diagnosis. Outcomes and variables

The primary outcome was overall survival, defined as the time from curative-intent surgery to death from any cause; patients without an event were right-censored at their last confirmed contact or at the administrative close in December 2022. The main exposure was membranous CD133 expression, categorized as high (≥ 50% positive tumor glands) vs low (< 50%). Pre-specified covariates for adjusted models were age and histologic grade. Sex and tumor site were explored in sensitivity analyses.

Immunohistochemical assessment of CD133

FFPE blocks from eligible cases were retrieved, and 3–4 µm sections were cut and mounted on poly-l-lysine-coated slides. Slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated through graded ethanol, subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0), and treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase.

FFPE slides were then incubated with a monoclonal anti-CD133 antibody (clone W6B3C1, BD Biosciences), followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen, and hematoxylin was used for counterstaining. Positive and negative controls were included in each staining batch to ensure validity. Control tissues were processed in parallel with study slides for each batch, and acceptance criteria were predefined for positive signal and background.

Only membranous staining was considered. Two pathologists independently scored membranous CD133 on tumor glands as the percentage of positive glands across at least five medium-power fields. Discrepancies were resolved by joint review. Pathologists were blinded to clinical outcomes and other covariates.

Data collection

We extracted the following data from medical records and structured telephone follow-up: anonymized patient code, age at surgery, sex, tumor location, histologic grade (well, moderate, poor), CD133 measures (intensity, percentage, immunoreactivity score (IRS)), vital status, and overall survival time in months. Vital status and dates were ascertained from hospital records and telephone confirmation with patients or designated contacts. Data abstraction was cross-verified by an independent reviewer. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R (version 4.5). Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as counts. Time-to-event analyses used Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests and Cox proportional hazards (PH) models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Because 28 deaths occurred, the primary multivariable Cox model included CD133 status, histologic grade, and age (age as a linear term), targeting approximately nine events per variable to limit overfitting. Sex and tumor site were evaluated but not retained in the primary model. Fuller models and sensitivity analyses are reported in the Supplement. PH assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. A logistic model of 5-year survival (alive at 60 months vs death before 60 months) was conducted as a secondary sensitivity analysis.

An interaction between CD133 and tumor grade was explored by adding a product term to the multivariable Cox model and comparing nested models via the likelihood ratio test (LRT). Model stability and influence were examined using differences in beta estimate (regression diagnostic) (DFBETAs) and residual diagnostics, and observations with |DFBETA|> 0.5 were flagged. Discrimination was summarized with Harrell’s c-index. Interobserver agreement for CD133 scoring was quantified using the κ statistic [20]. Robustness of the CD133 effect was probed via nonparametric bootstrap resampling (1000 replications). Given skewness, we report the geometric mean HR with 95% percentile CIs. Analyses were performed on available data without imputation; the number of observations with missing values for each variable is reported in the “Results” section. All machine-readable outputs and figures are provided in the Supplement (Tables S4-S11; Figures S1-S3).

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 208 histopathology records, including biopsies, polypectomies, and resection specimens (total/partial colectomy, appendectomy, terminal ileum with cecum), from 2012 to 2017, were retrieved from the pathology archive at Mousavi Hospital in Zanjan. Among these, 77 cases were histologically confirmed to be malignant. Tissue blocks were unavailable for five cases. Of the remaining 72, 15 patients were excluded due to a lack of contact information or non-response during follow-up. Two additional cases were excluded due to death within 1 month of surgery. Finally, 55 patients were included in the study. During follow-up to December 2022, 28 patients died, and 27 were alive or censored at administrative close.

The mean age of the participants was 64.98 years (standard deviation (SD) = 13.20), with a range of 37 to 92 years. Among them, 56.4% (n = 31) were male and 43.6% (n = 24) were female. The tumor was located in the colon in 47.3% of cases, in the rectosigmoid region in 34.5%, in the cecum in 12.7%, and metastases were present in 5.5% of patients. Regarding tumor grade, 58.2% (n = 32) had low-grade tumors, while 41.8% (n = 23) had high-grade tumors. Figure 2 represents different levels of CD133 expression in CRC. CD133 expression and its relationship with clinicopathological characteristics

IHC staining revealed that 47.3% (n = 26) of tumors were CD133-positive, while 52.7% (n = 29) were CD133-negative. Among CD133-positive tumors (n = 26), 61.5% (16/26) showed high expression (≥ 50% staining), and 38.5% (10/26) showed low expression (< 50% staining).

Table 1 presents the association between CD133 expression and selected demographic and clinicopathological variables. According to simple logistic regression, male sex was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of CD133 expression (OR = 0.24; 95% CI 0.07–0.74, 0.013). Patients with low-grade tumors exhibited significantly lower CD133 positivity compared to those with high-grade tumors (odds ratio (OR) = 0.10; 95% CI 0.03–0.35; p = 0.001). However, there was no significant association between age and CD133 expression (p = 0.554). In the multiple logistic regression (Firth-penalized model), tumor grade remained associated with CD133 positivity (adjusted OR = 15.89; 95% CI 4.19–83.97; p < 0.001), while sex showed a borderline association (adjusted OR = 0.29; 95% CI 0.06–1.14; p = 0.077).

Survival outcome and its relationship with clinicopathological characteristics

Out of the 55 patients, 49.1% (n = 27) survived more than 5 years post-surgery, while 50.9% (n = 28) died within the 5-year follow-up period. The mean SD survival time among patients was 38.11 (3.09) months. Additionally, Fig. 3 depicts the overall Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curve.

Kaplan–Meier curves showed lower survival in the CD133-positive group (log-rank p < 0.001); numbers at risk (CD133-negative vs CD133-positive) were 29/26, 26/17, 23/9, 22/8, 22/5, and 22/5 at 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months, respectively.

Table 2 displays the impact of CD133 expression and other clinical variables on 5-year survival among patients. In univariable (crude) Cox analysis, CD133 positivity was associated with increased hazard of death (HR = 5.28; 95% CI 2.23–12.53; p = 0.00016). In the pre-specified adjusted model, including CD133, tumor grade, and age, CD133 positivity remained associated with mortality (adjusted HR = 5.03; 95% CI 1.66–15.25; p = 0.004). Given the modest sample size and wide confidence interval, this estimate should be interpreted with caution as preliminary evidence. By contrast, although high tumor grade was associated with poorer survival in univariable analyses (Table 2), grade was not statistically significant in the primary adjusted model (adjusted HR for gradeHigh = 1.02; 95% CI 0.39–2.67; p = 0.964). Sensitivity analyses that additionally adjusted for sex and tumor location produced a similar but slightly attenuated estimate for CD133 (adjusted HR = 4.55; 95% CI 1.41–14.67). Because some strata were small, the CD133 × grade interaction analysis was considered exploratory. There was no evidence of effect modification of the CD133-survival association by tumor grade (LRT χ2 = 0.215, df = 1, p = 0.643). In the main adjusted Cox model (CD133, grade, age), CD133 positivity remained associated with increased mortality (adjusted HR = 5.03; 95% CI 1.66–15.25; p = 0.004). Because some grade × CD133 strata were small (low/CD133−: n = 26, events = 6; low/CD133+: n = 6, events = 5; high/CD133−: n = 3, events = 1; high/CD133+: n = 20, events = 16), stratified estimates would be unstable; therefore, no stratum-specific Cox estimates are reported here.

No individual observations had a large influence on model coefficients (no observations with |DFBETA|> 0.5) (Table S9). Residual ranges were consistent with an adequately fitting Cox model (deviance residuals range −1.78 to 2.05; martingale residuals range −1.59 to 0.95). Model discrimination was good (concordance ≈ 0.707). PH’s assumptions were evaluated using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. The global test did not reject proportionality (χ2 = 3.021, df = 3, p = 0.388). By covariate, the Schoenfeld tests were CD133 (χ2 = 0.607, p = 0.436), grade (χ2 = 0.005, p = 0.941), and age (χ2 = 2.441, p = 0.118) (Table S8 and Figure S1). Bootstrap resampling (1000 replications) produced a CD133 geometric mean HR = 5.52 (95% percentile CI 1.26–22.31) (Figure S2). Although the bootstrap estimate supported the primary finding, the very wide percentile CI reflects imprecision consistent with the limited number of events. Overall, these diagnostics support the robustness of the primary CD133 association, although the wide bootstrap CI indicates imprecision consistent with the modest event count.

Sensitivity analysis using Firth’s penalized Cox regression produced consistent results. In the penalized model (n = 55), CD133 positivity was associated with increased mortality (HR = 4.73; 95% CI 1.59–14.29; p = 0.0053). Tumor grade was not significant in the penalized model (HR = 1.005; 95% CI 0.410–2.752; p = 0.992). Age was also not significant (HR = 1.006; 95% CI 0.973–1.041; p = 0.722). The model likelihood ratio test was significant (LR χ2 = 16.38; p ≈ 0.00095). These penalized-likelihood estimates are consistent with the conventional adjusted Cox model and support the robustness of the association between CD133 expression and poorer survival. KM analysis confirmed significantly poorer survival among CD133-positive patients (log-rank p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of resected CRC, membranous CD133 positivity was associated with poorer overall survival after adjustment for age and histologic grade. The direction and magnitude of effect were consistent across sensitivity analyses (Firth penalization and bootstrap), supporting robustness, although imprecision remained evident in the wide confidence intervals due to the modest number of events.

The observed association aligns with reports that CD133 marks tumor cell subpopulations with stem-like features and adverse prognosis in CRC. While cut-off definitions vary across studies, our categorical threshold (≥ 50% vs < 50% positive glands) produced a consistent hazard signal; harmonization of scoring schemes and analytic cut-offs would aid comparability across cohorts.

Across Iranian CRC cohorts, KRAS mutations occur in roughly one-third of tumors, with variability across studies that is consistent with differences in case mix and testing approaches; for example, a large Tehran metastatic cohort reported 33.6% KRAS positivity [21]. Two syntheses encompassing ~ 1800–2600 Iranian cases estimate pooled KRAS ~ 33% (≈32.8–33.9%) and NRAS ~ 3%, with a codon-12 predominance [22, 23]. These patterns align with global benchmarks and support routine TNM and MSI/MMR, RAS, and BRAF profiling in our setting. Because our dataset lacked these variables, we cannot assess whether CD133 adds prognostic value beyond established clinicomolecular factors [22, 23].

Interpretation of CD133 studies remains complicated by methodological heterogeneity, differences in IHC antibody clones, staining cut-offs, tissue processing, and incomplete molecular adjustment in many cohorts, which may contribute to variability in reported effects.

Given the primacy of TNM stage and molecular features (MSI/MMR, KRAS/NRAS, BRAF) in CRC prognostication and treatment selection, our CD133-survival association should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating in this cohort [3, 24].

In our study, CD133 positivity was observed in 47.3% of colorectal tumor samples, a finding that aligns with earlier investigations reporting expression rates typically between 30 and 70% [15, 17, 25]. This variation in prevalence can be partly attributed to methodological heterogeneity, including differences in IHC protocols, antibody clones, and positivity thresholds [25, 26]. Despite these inconsistencies, meta-analyses support an adverse association between CD133 expression and survival in CRC [25, 27].

A remarkable finding in our cohort was the association between CD133 expression and higher tumor grade. CD133 was expressed in 87% of high-grade tumors but only in 18.7% of low-grade tumors. These findings mirror those of Kojima et al., who demonstrated a strong correlation between CD133 positivity and poor histologic differentiation, as well as deeper invasion and lymphatic involvement [18]. Similarly, Kostovski et al. found that high CD133 expression was significantly more frequent in aggressive tumor phenotypes, suggesting its role in defining a clinically unfavorable CRC subset [13].

Notably, CD133 expression was associated with reduced 5-year survival: patients with CD133-positive tumors had lower survival than those with CD133-negative tumors (19.2% vs 75.9%). These observations are consistent with prior work, including Park et al. and a comprehensive meta-analysis by Zhao et al., which reported worse overall and disease-free survival in CD133-positive CRC [12, 27].

We observed a sex-related difference in CD133 positivity (higher prevalence among females), but this association did not persist in multivariable models. Choi et al. and Horst et al. did observe certain demographic correlations, albeit inconsistently [15, 28]. The crude sex difference in our cohort is plausibly explained, at least in part, by confounding (notably by tumor grade, which was associated with CD133) and limited sample size; therefore, mechanistic inferences, for example, hormonal or genetic modulation, are premature and require validation in larger, independent cohorts and targeted biological studies.

Emerging data suggest that CD133 may be more informative when considered alongside other indicators. For example, CD133-positive/CXCR4-positive subpopulations have been linked to heightened metastatic potential, and CD133 expression on CTCs has been associated with worse disease-specific outcomes in some cohorts. These complementary approaches were not assessed in our study but may help refine risk stratification in future work [2, 10].

In contrast, no significant association was found between CD133 expression and patient age or tumor location in our cohort. Although metastatic sites showed higher expression trends, statistical significance was not reached (p-value = 0.920). These findings align with earlier reports by Ren et al. and Chao et al., who also failed to find consistent correlations between CD133 expression and tumor localization or patient age [29, 30].

Survival analysis by age similarly revealed no statistically significant relationship (p-value = 0.231), although there was a trend toward reduced survival with increasing age. This finding is consistent with Siegel et al., who acknowledged age as a known risk factor for CRC incidence and mortality but emphasized that prognostic variability often depends more on molecular and tumor-specific characteristics than chronological age alone [31]. Furthermore, Ren et al. noted that the prognostic relevance of CSC markers like CD133 might be driven more by tumor biology than by patient demographics, including age [29]. This may explain why, despite the known epidemiological association between aging and CRC, age did not emerge as an independent prognostic factor in our model.

In terms of survival based on tumor grade, the current study demonstrated that patients with low-grade tumors had significantly better outcomes compared to those with high-grade lesions. The 5-year survival was 65.6% for low-grade tumors versus 26.1% for high-grade tumors (p-value = 0.006), a finding in agreement with previous studies that demonstrated grade as a robust histopathological prognostic indicator [13, 18]. While sex and tumor location were not significantly associated with survival in our study, patients with metastases uniformly showed poor outcomes, underscoring the well-established importance of staging and metastatic spread in CRC prognosis [31].

Furthermore, its role in defining the CSC population makes it a promising target for future therapies aimed at eradicating tumor-initiating cells and overcoming resistance to conventional treatments [26, 32]. The association between CD133 and mortality remained significant in a Firth-penalized Cox model (HR 4.73; 95% CI 1.59–14.29), which helps mitigate small-sample bias; however, the wide CIs underscore the need for larger validation cohorts.

This retrospective, single-center study with a modest sample size limits generalizability and the complexity of multivariable modeling. The absence of TNM stage and key molecular covariates, including MSI/MMR, KRAS/NRAS, and BRAF, constrains the assessment of CD133’s independence and leaves room for residual confounding. CD133 was dichotomized by an IRS-based threshold without a universally accepted cut-off, which may affect reproducibility; IHC scoring, despite standardized dual review, retains subjective elements and potential misclassification near the boundary. Some strata were small (e.g., high/CD133), limiting precision for stratum-specific estimates. We evaluated model assumptions and stability (PH tests, influence diagnostics, bootstrap). Although no observation exceeded the pre-specified DFBETA threshold and bootstrap supported the direction of effect, occasional convergence warnings prompted confirmation with Firth-penalized Cox models, which yielded directionally consistent results.

Findings are most applicable to patients undergoing curative-intent resection in similar surgical–pathology settings to ours; external validation in multicenter cohorts with standardized IHC platforms is warranted.

Future work should prospectively validate CD133 in a multicenter cohort with full clinicomolecular profiling (AJCC TNM, MSI/MMR, KRAS/NRAS, BRAF, and, where feasible, HER2, CMS, ctDNA), using a pre-registered protocol powered by conservative events-per-parameter targets. The CD133 IHC assay should be standardized (clone, retrieval, scoring cut-off, membranous criteria), with inter-observer reliability and/or digital image analysis reported. To capture stemness-linked risk more completely, studies should test composite biomarkers, including CD133-positive/CXCR4-positive on tissue (CD133 on CTCs) and include mechanistic corollaries (hypoxia/EMT programs such as HIF-1α, E/N-cadherin, vimentin) in translational sub-cohorts. Beyond statistical significance, investigators should quantify incremental prognostic value: c-index change, calibration, reclassification (NRI/IDI), and decision-curve analysis, and assess treatment endpoints (DFS, recurrence, regimen-specific response) to probe prediction of benefit or resistance. Findings should undergo external validation across centers and populations, supported by biobanking/SOPs for longitudinal assays, and paired with feasibility and cost-effectiveness evaluations to gauge real-world implementability.

Conclusion

This study found that CD133 expression was associated with higher tumor grade and poorer survival in an Iranian CRC cohort. In multivariable analysis adjusted for grade and age, CD133 remained significantly associated with mortality, although the relatively wide confidence intervals and limited sample size mean these results should be interpreted as preliminary. A sex-related difference in CD133 expression was observed but did not remain significant after adjustment, warranting exploration in larger studies. These findings support the hypothesis that CD133 marks a biologically aggressive CRC subset, consistent with international evidence. Future research should validate these results in larger, multiethnic cohorts with comprehensive staging and molecular profiling and explore whether combining CD133 with other biomarkers improves prognostic accuracy and guides personalized therapy.
