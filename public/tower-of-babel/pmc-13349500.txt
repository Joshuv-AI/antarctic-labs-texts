Mitigating Iatrogenic Air Entrainment During Pulsed Field Ablation
Authors: Ting-Yung Chang, Chin-Yu Lin, Pei-Yi Wang, Hsin-Lei Huang, Kuo-Hsiang Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystemic air embolism remains a critical risk during left atrial procedures, particularly during the frequent exchanges required in pulsed field ablation (PFA). In this study we evaluated the air intrusion patterns using the VARIPULSETM PFA system with a relatively small sheath lumen, and assessed a custom-made “Adapter Sink” designed to eliminate air entry.Methods and ResultsUsing an ex vivo water-tank model, we monitored the insertion of the VARIPULSETM catheter through an 8.5Fr CARTO VIZIGO® Bi-Directional Guiding Sheath. Three protocols were compared: (1) standard insertion without suction, (2) insertion with active manual suction, and (3) insertion using the custom Adapter Sink (submerged entry). Air volume was quantified across 10 trials per group. Baseline insertion (no suction) consistently introduced visible small air bubbles, although manual suction by an assistant reduced the bubbles’ volume and frequency significantly. In contrast, the Adapter Sink group had zero air intrusion by operator alone without assistance, providing a complete physical barrier against atmospheric air.ConclusionsEven with smaller profile sheaths such as the 8.5Fr VIZIGO®, a continuous saline drip may be insufficient. The Adapter Sink effectively minimized air intrusion and optimized manpower by requiring only a single operator, unlike manual suction, which relies on an assistant. However, further in vivo or clinical studies are warranted to validate the feasibility of our setup under physiological conditions.

Central Figure

The rapid adoption of pulsed field ablation (PFA) for treating atrial fibrillation (AF) has highlighted the need for stringent safety protocols, because although PFA offers superior tissue selectivity, the procedural nature, often involving large-bore access and multiple catheter exchanges, increases the risk of iatrogenic air embolism.1–4

PFA is a cutting-edge, nonthermal modality designed for selective myocardial tissue destruction. By mitigating the significant complication risks associated with traditional radiofrequency and cryoballoon ablation, PFA represents a promising therapeutic alternative characterized by a superior safety profile and a lower incidence of adverse events.5–9

Air embolism is a rare but potentially devastating complication of catheter ablation, resulting from inadvertent introduction of air into the vasculature.10 In particular, the use of long sheaths into the left atrium during AF ablation significantly increases the risk of severe embolic events.11,12 Recent advancements have included the VARIPULSETM PFA platform (Johnson & Johnson), which is used together with the 8.5Fr CARTO VIZIGO® steerable sheath. However, despite the smaller footprint compared to first-generation PFA systems, the risk of air ingress through the hemostatic valve during rapid catheter advancement remains a concern.

Standard clinical practice relies on manual suction (negative pressure) to clear the sheath side-arm, but this is operator-dependent and prone to inconsistency. We hypothesized that a submerged-entry environment, facilitated by a custom-made Adapter Sink, would offer a superior, automated defense against air intrusion.

Methods

Ex Vivo Simulation Setup

An experimental model was used to simulate the left atrium (Figure 1). The model comprised a water tank with 1 exit used for catheter and sheath insertion from the imaginary inferior vena cava. The tail of the sheath was left above the water surface and over the bench top. A new saline bag was used for each air bubble experiment after being cut open and the air in the bag evacuated under water (Figure 1A). The tip of the 8.5Fr CARTO VIZIGO® steerable sheath was inserted into the saline bag (Figure 1A) and any air bubbles forming while inserting the VIZIGO® could be quantified by withdrawing the air by syringe after the procedure. Study Protocol

Three experimental protocols were established (Figure 1B), each performing 10 catheter insertion cycles.

Group 1 (Control): The VARIPULSETM catheter was advanced into the VIZIGO® sheath without any adjunctive suction or barrier.

Group 2 (Manual Suction): Standard negative pressure was applied via the sheath side-arm during catheter entry, mimicking current “best practice”.

Group 3 (Adapter Sink): The proximal end of the VIZIGO® sheath and the VARIPULSETM catheter tip were completely submerged in a custom-made stainless steel Adapter Sink filled with saline prior to and during insertion.

Quantification

Following each procedure, any trapped air in the collection bag was aspirated using a 1-mL syringe to determine the total volume in milliliters.

Custom-Made Adapter Sink

The Adapter Sink made by Gimer Medical Co. (New Taipei City, Taiwan) was a stainless steel water sink specifically designed to facilitate the operation of PFA catheters (Figure 2). The design and application of the Adapter Sink are protected under a pending patent filed by Taipei Veterans General Hospital (App. No. 115105626). Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on the normality of the distribution. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data. To compare the volume and frequency of iatrogenic air entrainment across multiple experimental groups (e.g., conventional technique vs. submerged-entry technique under various simulated maneuvers), a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. If significant differences were detected, Tukey’s HSD (honestly significant difference) post-hoc test was applied for pairwise comparisons to identify specific differences between techniques. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Quantitative Assessment of Air Entrainment

The volume of entrained air significantly varied among the 3 experimental groups (Figure 3). In Group 1, which used the conventional connection setup, the mean air volume entrained was 0.45±0.13 mL (range: 0.20–0.65 mL). In contrast, Group 2 demonstrated a substantial reduction in air entrainment, with a mean volume of 0.020±0.042 mL. Remarkably, Group 3, utilizing the custom-made Adapter Sink, achieved complete elimination of air entrainment across all 10 trials. One-way ANOVA confirmed a statistically significant difference in air entrainment volumes across the 3 groups (P<0.001). Post-hoc analysis using Tukey’s HSD test further indicated that the air volume in Group 1 was significantly higher than that in both groups 2 and 3 (both P<0.001). Although Group 3 showed a lower mean air volume than Group 2, the difference between them did not reach statistical significance (P=0.17). These findings suggested that both of the modified setups (groups 2 and 3) effectively minimized air entrainment compared to the conventional method, with the custom-made Adapter Sink in Group 3 providing the most consistent results. Discussion

Main Finding

The current study demonstrated a significant disparity in air entrainment values across 3 study groups. Specifically, Group 1 exhibited a markedly higher value (0.450±0.133) that Group 2 (0.020±0.042) and Group 3 (0.000±0.000), with both comparisons reaching high statistical significance (P<0.001). The custom-made Adapter Sink completely prevent the risk of air intrusion during catheter insertion.

Mechanistic Distinction of Microbubble Formation

A critical distinction must be made regarding the etiology of microbubbles observed during ablation procedures. In the study by Zhou et al.,13 the formation of microbubbles was primarily attributed to the biophysical effects of pulsed field energy delivery, such as electrolysis or localized thermal dynamics at the electrode–tissue interface, which could be mitigated by saline irrigation. However, the current study focused on a different but equally significant source of gaseous emboli: procedural air intake during the exchange and manipulation of catheters.

Our experimental design specifically targeted the prevention of air entry during the “in-and-out” movement of the delivery system. The significantly higher air entrainment values observed in Group 1 likely reflect the vulnerability of standard procedural protocols to accidental air entrainment, which our intervention in the other 2 groups effectively neutralized.

Strategic Implications for Procedural Safety

Cerebral and systemic embolisms during catheter ablation procedures are rare, but serious complications during catheter ablation procedures.10 One of the main causes of embolisms related to catheter ablation is the generation of clots and thrombi due to overheating in tissues or in the blood. PFA for cardiac procedures has a low stroke risk, especially compared to older thermal ablation methods, as preclinical data suggest no cerebral emboli formation with PFA. Clinical studies show a stroke rate of approximately 0.12–0.3% in large patient groups, though a 3% rate of silent cerebral lesions has been observed.14,15 A recent clinical trial identified the potential risk of air emboli as a stroke cause after PFA ablation.16

Clinical Recommendations and the Role of the Adapter Sink

To prevent procedural air embolism, it is recommended to routinely maintain negative pressure during catheter insertion and manipulation of the catheter and sheath. Our study confirmed that this maneuver significantly reduces the formation of air bubbles, but several practical challenges remain in real-world clinical settings.

First, there is currently no standardized protocol defining the “optimal” level of negative pressure required to effectively seal the system against air intrusion. Second, manual aspiration is inherently prone to human error; for instance, the plastic suction tubing can become inadvertently kinked during complex manipulations, which may compromise suction efficacy without being noticed by the operator or the assistant.

To address these limitations, we developed our custom-made Adapter Sink system for use during catheter exchange. Our findings demonstrated that this device completely prevented air intrusion by providing a constant liquid seal. Notably, the Sink functioned as a passive safety mechanism, ensuring systemic integrity without requiring additional personnel to manually maintain negative pressure, which not only simplifies the procedural workflow but also eliminates the risks associated with manual suction failure or tubing occlusion, thereby enhancing the overall safety profile of complex cardiac interventions.

Study Limitations

A static water-tank model cannot fully replicate the dynamic hemodynamics, continuous blood flow, and fluctuating intracardiac pressures of a real left atrium, all of which could influence air entrainment and microbubble transport. Consequently, the protective efficacy of our maneuvers under high-flow or high-pressure conditions remains to be further validated in in vivo studies.

Conclusions

Although conventional negative pressure aspiration significantly reduces the volume of air entry during PFA catheter insertion, its efficacy relies heavily on the constant presence of an assistant. Our ex vivo study demonstrated that our novel, custom-designed Adapter Sink system was equally effective at minimizing procedural air intrusion, but with a distinct operational advantage. By providing a passive liquid seal that can be managed by the operator alone, this system optimizes clinical workflow and mitigates the risks of human error associated with manual suction. However, as this was a benchtop proof-of-concept, further in vivo and clinical studies are warranted to validate its safety and practical feasibility under physiological conditions.
