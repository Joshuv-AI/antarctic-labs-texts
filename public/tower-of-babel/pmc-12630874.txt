A novel inherently hydrophobic and physicomechanically robust coconut oil-based polyurethane-modified mortar
Authors: Jason Jon Joshua B Paraguya, Roger G Dingcong, Hernando P Bacosa, Gerard G Dumancas, Arnold C Alguno, Maria Sheila K Ramos, Roberto M Malaluan, Arnold A Lubguban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWater penetration in mortar structures has long been a significant challenge in the construction industry, resulting in metal bar corrosion, structural deterioration, reduced durability, and increased maintenance costs. Hence, enhancing water resistance is crucial. While surface coatings are common solutions, they are often costly and require frequent maintenance. To address this concern, this study presents a novel coconut oil (CO)-based polyurethane (PU)-modified mortar, leveraging the intrinsic hydrophobic properties of CO owing to its saturated fatty acid structure. Different concentrations of COPU (0–7 wt.%) were investigated to determine the optimal formulation. The characterization results indicated enhanced hydrophobicity, with a water contact angle of 125° achieved at the 2 wt.% COPU addition to the PMM mixture, compared to 110° of the control sample. Additionally, the water absorption test demonstrated a significant 54 wt.% reduction compared with that of pristine mortar, attributed to the gap-filling and binding actions of the COPU matrix with ettringites, as evidenced by SEM analysis. This modification also resulted in a 40 wt.%, being the highest improvement in compressive strength (from 13.1 to 18.4 MPa at 2 wt.% COPU) and a slight reduction in density. Additionally, the prepared samples were evaluated using thermogravimetric analysis, X-ray diffraction, and X-ray fluorescence analyses. The findings in this work advance the field by presenting eco-friendly COPU-modified materials that improve water resistance and durability in mortar, promoting sustainable practices.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Mortar is a predominant construction material worldwide because of its high compressive strength, versatility, and economic viability.1 However, many mortar structures fail to reach their designed life because of their low durability in various working environments.2 One significant factor contributing to the premature failure of mortar structures is water absorption, which can lead to internal damage, thereby compromising structural integrity.3

Water penetration in cementitious materials has been extensively studied, with researchers exploring various modification strategies. Traditional approaches include the use of silane-based water repellents, which typically achieve water absorption reductions of 20–30 wt.%.4 Polymer latex modifications have shown promise, with styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR) modifications reducing water absorption by approximately 35–45 wt.%.5 However, these conventional approaches often suffer from durability issues, with hydrophobic treatments losing effectiveness over time due to UV degradation and weathering.6

Reinforced mortar, which incorporates steel bars, has revolutionized modern construction by increasing the tensile strength and load-bearing capacity of mortar7, enabling the erection of taller and more complex structures. Nonetheless, these reinforced mortar structures exhibit shorter service lives than traditional unreinforced mortar does, primarily due to water ingress and the associated corrosion of steel reinforcement.8–10.

Recent studies have shown that introducing hydrophobic materials can significantly alter the water contact angle of mortar, with contact angles typically ranging from 90° to 110° for conventional polymer modifications.11 This finding suggests that chemical and physical modifications are necessary for optimal hydrophobicity.

Pristine mortar typically exhibits high water absorption, ranging from 10 to 15 wt.% under 24-h water immersion conditions.12 This high water absorption rate or permeability raises durability concerns for mortar structures.13 To mitigate this issue, various surface modifications and overlays have been employed to reduce water absorption in mortar structures. Among these solutions, epoxy-based materials have proven to be highly effective, demonstrating excellent water resistance with absorption rates as low as 0.5 wt.%.14 Modifying cementitious materials with polymers has emerged as an alternative approach to increase durability.15 Incorporating polymers can alter the microstructure and improve the overall performance of mortar, including enhancing the adhesion between mortars and aggregates and the mechanical properties.16 For example, latexes, emulsions, or dispersions based on styrene-butadiene, acrylics, and vinyl acetate derived from petrochemicals have shown promise as cement modifiers.17 However, concerns about the high carbon footprint and nonbiodegradable nature of many conventional petroleum-based polymers18 have shifted attention to developing sustainable biobased polymers specifically for integration in cementitious mixes.

One emerging class of polymers is polyurethane (PU)19,20, which can be derived from natural oils21. Plant oils such as soybean22, palm23, canola24, and castor oils25 have been used as precursors to PU.26 The unique chemical structure and hydrophobic nature of natural oil-based PUs make them promising candidates for enhancing durability and water resistance. PUs derived from natural oils have shown promising properties, including good mechanical, thermal, and chemical properties.21,27,28 These developments offer green alternatives to fossil-based polymers and contribute to environmental protection. In particular, coconut oil (CO) has recently gained attention as a biobased feedstock for producing polyol, a critical component in PU synthesis. Notably, CO is naturally hydrophobic because of its highly saturated fatty acid structure29, potentially imparting water-resistant properties to mortar without the need for additional modifications. Previous studies have reported promising physicomechanical properties of rigid PU foam with its polyol derived entirely from CO.30 These polyols are mainly composed of coconut diethanolamides and free glycerol, collectively known as p-CDEA.31 The high concentration of primary hydroxyl groups in p-CDEA influences PU matrix formation32, making it a promising candidate for microstructure modification and reinforcement of mortar prepared from ordinary Portland cement.

Despite extensive research on polymer-modified mortars, critical gaps persist—most bio-based polymer modifications exhibit limited hydrophobic performance, typically achieving water contact angles below 110%33; natural oil-based polyurethanes, particularly those derived from coconut oil with a high saturation level have not been systematically explored in cementitious systems; furthermore, the specific influence of saturated fatty acid content on the hydrophobicity of such bio-based polymers remains poorly understood.

This study evaluated the physicomechanical properties of a novel CO-based PU-modified mortar (PMM) using several characterization techniques. Hydrophobicity was assessed through contact angle measurements, demonstrating the water-resistant nature of COPU, while water absorption analysis highlighted the material’s enhanced durability. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was utilized to observe microstructural changes, and mechanical testing provided insights into improved compressive strength. Furthermore, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was employed to assess the thermal stability, while both X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analyses were conducted to investigate the crystalline structure and chemical composition, thereby confirming the homogeneity and purity of the PMM components. The findings significantly contribute to the development of high-performance, eco-friendly cement-based materials that utilize renewable resources, thereby advancing sustainable construction practices.

Materials and methods

Materials

Commercially available Portland cement, purchased from Republic Cement Philippines, was used as the cementitious binder, with fine sand serving as the natural aggregate. The coconut oil used in this study is Refined, Bleached, and Deodorized (RBD) CO, commercially sourced from Granex Philippines. Polymeric methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (MDI), calcium oxide (CaO), zinc oxide (ZnO), and diethanolamine (DEA) were obtained from QR Scientific. Refined glycerol, purchased from Merteflor Laboratory, was also used. These materials constituted the primary components for synthesizing COPU.

Synthesis of coconut oil-based polyol

The coconut oil-based polyol (p-CDEA) was synthesized via a two-step process involving glycerolysis followed by amidation, as described in previous work by Dingcong et al.30 CO and glycerol were reacted in a closed Parr reactor equipped with automated temperature control and mechanical agitation for the glycerolysis step. A 6:1 weight ratio of CO to glycerol was used, with 0.5 wt.% CaO (based on the total weight of CO and glycerol) added as a catalyst. The reactor was heated to 220 °C and maintained at that temperature for 2 h under continuous agitation. Following glycerolysis, the resulting product underwent amidation in the same reactor setup by adding DEA at a concentration of 0.015 wt.% ZnO catalyst. The amidation reaction was conducted at 140 °C for 4 h to yield the p-CDEA. The real-time transformation—from CO feedstock, to coconut monoglyceride (CMG), and finally to p-CDEA polyol—is illustrated in Fig. 1. Polymeric MDI served as the isocyanate component, providing reactive –NCO groups for urethane bond formation; Refined glycerol acted as a transesterification agent for glycerolysis reaction, contributing to polyol production; CaO catalyzed the glycerolysis reaction; ZnO catalyzed the amidation reaction, and DEA functioned as the amidation agent, yielding the p-CDEA polyol for subsequent polyurethane synthesis.30.

Characterization

All samples were prepared from 28-day cured specimens. Hydration was stopped using the solvent exchange method36 with isopropanol for 24 h, followed by vacuum drying at 60 °C for 48 h to remove residual moisture and prevent further hydration reactions during analysis.37,38 This procedure ensures that the microstructural and chemical analyses represent the accurate state of the material at 28 days of curing.

Contact angle

The hydrophobicity of the mortar surface was evaluated via water contact angle measurements. A Theta Lite Optical Tensiometer (Biolin Scientific) was used to measure the contact angle of the water droplets on the mortar surface in an ambient environment. Higher contact angles indicate improved water repellency, which is desirable for enhancing the durability of mortar in moist environments.

Water absorption test

Water absorption (WA) in cement-based materials is a crucial property that impacts durability and performance. The water absorption test was conducted by ASTM C642-13 guidelines, with modifications for PMM specimens. The samples were dried to a constant mass at 105 ± 5 °C for 24 h and then immersed in distilled water for 24 h at 23 ± 2 °C.29 After immersion, the samples were removed, and the surface water was wiped off with a damp cloth within 10 s. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was conducted to identify the functional groups in both the control and the optimal sample PMM2. The FTIR spectra were recorded via an IRTracer-100 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan) with a scanning rate of 0.5 cm−1 per second, collecting 40 scans at wavelengths ranging from 4000 to 1000 cm−1 to ensure a high signal-to-noise ratio.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

The microstructural features of all the fractured sample surfaces were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). SEM analysis was performed using a JEOL JSM-IT200 SEM at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. The sample surfaces were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to increase the surface conductivity and obtain clear images. Micrographs were captured at a magnification of 10,000 × with a scale bar of 1 micron to observe the morphology and dispersion of the COPUs within the mortar matrix.

X-ray diffraction analysis

The BTX InXitu Portable XRD was utilized to determine the crystallinity of the samples. The samples were mounted on the XRD sample holder to optimize signal intensity from all crystalline planes. A full scan was performed over a 2θ angle range of 5° to 55° using Cu-Kα radiation at 30.1 kV and 0.012 mA, with a wavelength of 1.54 Å. This configuration enabled accurate calculation of inter-atomic distances based on Bragg’s equation. The analysis had a resolution of 0.020°, with a detection limit of 5%, which is largely influenced by the degree of crystallinity in the samples (Fig. 2).

Actual 50 × 50 × 50 mm cube mold used for PMM casting in this study.

Compressive strength test

The compressive strength of the PMM samples was tested via the compression testing machine model TBTCTM-300 C, following ASTM C109/C109M34 Standards using a loading rate of 0.25 MPa/s. The PMM samples, with dimensions of 50 × 50 × 50 mm, were cast in molds, de-molded after 24 h, and cured under standard conditions for 28 days before testing. The compressive strength was measured by applying a uniaxial load until failure, directly assessing the material’s mechanical performance.

XRF analysis

The XRF characterization was performed using the Thermo Scientific XRF 9900 series, a high-performance system designed for precise elemental analysis. This equipment offers advanced capabilities for identifying both major and trace elements, ensuring reliable quantification of the chemical composition. Its non-destructive nature allows for rapid analysis with minimal sample preparation, making it ideal for evaluating complex matrices such as PMM. The instrument’s sensitivity and wide elemental range provide detailed insights, supporting further assessments of material properties and potential performance indicators.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to investigate the thermal stability and decomposition behavior of mortar and PMM samples. The analysis was conducted via a PerkinElmer TGA 4000 instrument under a nitrogen atmosphere, with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1, from room temperature (25 °C) to 200 °C. Dried powdered samples from the 28-day cured samples were placed in the ceramic vessel of the thermal gravimetric analyzer. TGA provides valuable information on the thermal degradation profile and the effect of COPU modification on the thermal stability of PMM.

Results and discussion

Water contact angle

Water contact angle measurements are critical indicators of PMM surface properties, including wettability, hydrophobicity, and overall durability—factors that significantly influence PMM’s long-term performance. The relationship between contact angle and moisture ingress is well-documented, with water typically penetrating PMM through capillary action41,42. High water absorption, for example, can increase permeability, making the PMM more vulnerable to freeze–thaw damage, chemical attacks, and corrosion of embedded reinforcement.43–45 As illustrated in Fig. 3, adding COPU to the PMM matrix significantly improved the hydrophobicity. The control sample demonstrated moderate hydrophobicity, with a water contact angle of 109.02° (Fig. 3a). In contrast, PMM2 exhibited a significantly higher water contact angle of 125.49° (Fig. 3b). This increase is attributed to the presence of highly saturated fatty acid chains in COPU, which enhance the non-polar characteristics of the PMM. Consequently, the reduced intermolecular interactions between the PMM and water result in improved hydrophobicity. This substantial increase in water contact angle demonstrates that PMM2 offers superior hydrophobicity, which has several important implications: increased water repellency, enhanced resistance to water ingress, the potential for lower water absorption rates, and improved overall durability. These improvements make PMM2 a promising candidate for applications in wet environments or areas prone to water exposure.

Water absorption

Water absorption in mortar is typically measured as a percentage of the weight of the mortar sample before and after immersion in water for a specified period.46 This property is influenced by several factors, including the type of aggregates used, the water-to-cement ratio, the presence of additives, and the curing conditions. Recent studies have highlighted the role of polymer modifications in reducing water absorption. For example, mortar has demonstrated significantly lower water absorption rates compared to traditional mortar mixes. This is attributed to the hydrophobic nature of COPU, which creates a barrier that limits water penetration.47 The incorporation of COPUs has been shown to enhance the impermeability of PMM, making it more suitable for applications in wet environments or where exposure to aggressive chemicals is expected.48

Figure 4 presents the water absorption percentages of PMM samples containing various concentrations of COPU after 24 hours of immersion in water. The results indicate a significant decrease in water absorption as the COPU content increases. Specifically, with just 2 wt.% COPU, the water absorption is reduced by 54%, from 10.8 wt.% of the control sample to 4.9 wt.% of PMM2. This downward trend continues with increasing COPU content, as PMM3.5 exhibits 3.9 wt.% absorption, PMM5 absorbs 3.65 wt.%, and PMM7 achieves the lowest absorption at 2.81 wt.%. This finding aligns with research by Islam et al. (2022), which demonstrated that incorporating supplementary cementitious materials and carefully selected aggregates can reduce PMM’s permeability and enhance its durability.49

Water absorption of polymer modified mortar with varying p-CDEA content.

The influence of aggregate type is crucial in determining water absorption. Natural aggregates, used in this study, generally exhibit lower porosity and fewer surface defects compared to recycled aggregates, which tend to absorb more water due to their higher porosity and surface irregularities.50–52 This characteristic of natural aggregates complements the hydrophobic effect of COPU, enhancing the overall water resistance of the material.

Table 2 shows the comparative performances of related studies in the literature in terms of water absorption and water contact angle. The results revealed that for their corresponding optimum formulation, the present study exhibits the lowest water absorption of approximately 4.9 wt.% and the highest water contact angle of up to 125.49° compared to previous reports in the literature.53,54 This advantage owes to the intrinsic chemical structure of coconut oil having highest saturation degrees of fatty acid chains. This makes the PMM2 highly non-polar and hydrophobic in its natural form. Consequently, PMM2 position itself as a promising candidate in applications where water absorptions are paramount.

The reduced water absorption in PMM is attributed to two key mechanisms. First, COPU creates a more hydrophobic surface, as indicated by the high contact angle, reducing surface wetting and moisture ingress. Second, the increasing concentration of hydrophobic COPU, as shown in Figure 5, contributes to reduced porosity and minimizes pathways for water ingress. This trend is supported by the micrographs, which show a progressively more prominent polymer network with higher COPU content, consistent with the observed decrease in water absorption in the PMM samples. These mechanisms align with findings from recent studies, which have demonstrated that incorporating supplementary cementitious materials and optimizing aggregates can enhance microstructural properties and reduce PMM’s permeability. 55–57

In conclusion, the use of natural aggregates and COPU offers practical benefits, such as improved long-term durability, resistance to moisture-related damage, and lower maintenance requirements. These properties are especially valuable for applications in harsh environments where exposure to water and moisture can compromise structural integrity. Moreover, reducing water absorption is crucial for extending the service life of PMM structures, particularly in adverse environmental conditions.58 The findings from this study provide a strong foundation for further exploration of COPU-modified PMM as a sustainable alternative for enhanced moisture resistance in construction materials.

Microstructure analysis

The SEM microphotographs in Figure 5 provide valuable insights into the microstructural changes and the impact of incorporating COPU as its content increases from 0 to 7 wt.% in PMM samples cured at 28 days. Figure 5a shows the microstructure of the control sample, revealing a dense arrangement with a dominant presence of distinct needle-like crystal formations typical of ettringite crystals formed during cement hydration.59 Figure 5b, which represents the PMM2 sample, shows similarities with the control, yet reveals significant differences. While ettringite crystals are still present, they appear partially enclosed or embedded within a continuous polymer phase. The red arrows in the SEM image highlight this feature, and the yellow arrows indicate the presence of unblown elastomeric COPU.60 Additionally, the successful incorporation of COPU effectively fills voids and spaces around the ettringite crystals, thus creating an interlocking mechanism that reinforces the microstructure.

As the concentration of COPU increased in the PMM samples, notable microstructural changes were observed. In PMM3.5 (Fig. 5c), the ettringite crystals appeared less defined, while the COPU phase became increasingly prominent. This shift indicates a growing disruption in the crystalline structure, primarily due to the interference of the COPU phase during the early stages of cement hydration. The disruption is attributed to two primary mechanisms: chemical competition for water and spatial hindrance.

COPU is synthesized from polymeric MDI and a polyol, both of which react with water.61 During the curing process, this reaction competes with the cement hydration reaction, specifically the formation of ettringite, which requires adequate water availability. As the amount of COPU increases, more water is consumed for urethane and urea bond formation, limiting the water accessible for ettringite crystallization. Additionally, COPU polymerizes rapidly, forming a three-dimensional network within the matrix that physically obstructs the growth and organization of ettringite crystals. This spatial hindrance disrupts the orderly development of hydration products, leading to a more amorphous microstructure.

These effects become more pronounced in PMM5 (Fig. 5d) and PMM7 (Fig. 5e), where the microstructure exhibits a predominantly amorphous appearance, and the presence of crystalline phases is significantly diminished. The yellow arrows in the micrographs highlight the increasing distribution of the COPU phase, which correlates with the loss of structural order as COPU content rises.

FTIR

Figure 6 shows the FTIR spectra of the control PMM sample and the PMM2. Each peak in the spectrum corresponds to a specific functional group present in the sample. Notably, several peaks in the modified sample exhibited greater intensity or changes in shape than those in the control sample did, indicating alterations in the chemical composition or increased concentrations of specific components. The vibrations in the range of 900–1100 cm−1 and 850–1000 cm−1 are attributed to Si–O and Al–O bonds, characteristic of the silicate and aluminate phases, respectively, present in cement and PMM.62 The peak at 1730 cm−1, which can be assigned to Al‒O vibrations in the aluminate phases of cement, shows a slight decrease in intensity for PMM2 compared with the control. This reduction might indicate some interaction between the COPU and the aluminate phases. Of particular interest is the peak at 1417 cm−1, which appears stretched or broadened in the PMM2 sample compared with the control. This peak is attributed to the presence of carbonates (CO₃2⁻) in mortar.59 In PMM2, this peak also reflects the stretching C–H bonds associated with the alkyl groups present in COPU, as their transmittance occurs at a similar wavelength. The presence of both components in PMM2 ultimately increases the peak intensity at around 1417 cm−1, as observed in Figure 6.

FTIR spectra of control and PMM2 sample showing characteristic peaks.

Additionally, the peak range 3300–3500 cm-1, corresponds to both O–H and N–H stretching bonds. Notably, the IR spectra of PMM2 within this range exhibit a sharper peak compared to the control sample. This sharper peak of PMM2 indicates the presence of the N–H group of its urethane component.63 This suggests the successful formation of COPU in the PMM2 sample. Additionally, the slight difference of both peak sharpness indicates the presence of O–H groups associated with the CaOH and H2O, which are prominently present for both samples.

These observations, particularly the stretched peak at 1417 cm−1, the increased intensities of the C–H related peaks, and the slight changes in the cementitious phase peaks (S–O and Al–O), collectively provide strong evidence for the successful incorporation of the COPU phase into the PMM matrix in PMM2. The alterations in peak characteristics suggest not only the presence of COPU but also potential chemical interactions or physical entanglements between the polymer and the cementitious components, which could influence the overall properties of the PMM.

XRD analysis

Figure 7 illustrates the XRD patterns of the control and PMM2, evaluating the influence of COPU incorporation on the crystallinity of the PMM matrix. Within the 2θ range up to 40°, characteristic peaks associated with cement hydration products are observed, allowing for a comparative assessment of phase development and structural changes.64 It can be observed that the control sample (Figure 7a) exhibits distinct diffraction peaks at 5.70°, 20.96°, 22.04°, 26.64°, 27.88°, 29.76°, and 39.34° 2θ, which correspond to hydration products of Portland cement such as calcium silicates (C₃S, C₂S), calcium aluminates (C₃A), portlandite (Ca(OH)2), and calcium aluminoferrites (C₄AF).65 The peak near 5.70° 2θ is particularly attributed to ettringite, a key early hydration product formed by the reaction of C₃A with sulfate ions.66 In PMM2 (Figure 7b), a significant reduction in peak intensity is observed across the diffractogram. Notably, the ettringite-associated peak is substantially weakened despite the morphological evidence in SEM (Figure 5). This attenuation is interpreted as a result of the interaction between isocyanate (–NCO) groups from MDI and hydroxyl-containing hydration products, which disrupts the growth of crystalline phases. Isocyanates are known to react rapidly with –OH groups67 and such reactions likely interfere with normal hydration pathways and crystal nucleation.

XRD patterns of (a) control and (b) PMM2 samples.

These interactions inhibit the development of well-ordered ettringite crystals and other crystalline hydration phases, resulting in lower overall crystallinity. The reduced peak intensity and lack of distinct signals for some expected hydration products in the PMM2 sample indicate that a portion of the matrix has transitioned into a more amorphous or disordered phase, likely attributed to the integration of an amorphous COPU network.

Density

Figure 6 illustrates the density values of the different PMM samples, demonstrating a clear decreasing trend in density from the control sample (22.36 g/cm3) down to PMM7 (18.275 g/cm3). The control sample, which lacks COPU, maintains the highest density, while samples with increasing concentrations of COPU exhibit progressively lower densities. For instance, PMM2, with a moderate amount of COPU, has a density of 21.36 g/cm3, while PMM3.5, PMM5, and PMM7 show further reductions to 20.08 g/cm3, 19.275 g/cm3, and 18.275 g/cm3, respectively. The decrease in density can be attributed primarily to the incorporation of COPU, which forms a polymer network within the cementitious matrix. COPUs are typically formed through a gelling reaction between polyol and MDI components.30 When water is present, it acts as a blowing agent by reacting with MDI (blowing reaction), generating CO₂ gases that cause expansion. In the preparation of PMM samples, both the gelling and blowing reactions occur, leading to partial expansion of the COPU matrix and a reduction in its apparent density. As a result, the inclusion of COPU lowers the overall density of the PMM samples while maintaining compressive strength, effectively utilizing COPU’s natural hydrophobic and lightweight properties at an optimal concentration. Additionally, PMM2 can be utilized in construction applications, particularly in tall buildings, where its lightweight nature minimizes the load on foundations and support structures, enabling taller designs and improved seismic performance in earthquake-prone areas.

Compressive strength

The compressive strength of the PMM samples (Figure 8) reflects the influence of COPU incorporation on the microstructure, particularly in terms of interfacial bonding, porosity, and phase distribution. At lower COPU concentrations (1–2 wt.%), a notable enhancement in mechanical performance is observed. Specifically, PMM2 (2 wt.% COPU) achieves the highest compressive strength of 18.4 MPa, representing a significant improvement over the control sample (13.1 MPa). This increase is attributed to the formation of a well-dispersed COPU network resulting from the reaction between p-CDEA and polymeric MDI. As seen in Figure 5b, this network effectively fills microvoids and reinforces the ettringite structures within the matrix, resulting in enhanced interfacial adhesion and more efficient stress distribution. Beyond the optimal 2 wt.% COPU loading, compressive strength declines progressively—14.1 MPa for PMM3.5, 10.8 MPa for PMM5, and 9.1 MPa for PMM7. This trend corresponds with microstructural observations (Figures 5c–e), where higher COPU content disrupts the crystalline matrix, as shown in Figure 5, leading to a less distinct ettringite morphology. In particular, PMM7 exhibits a COPU-dominated structure with minimal visible ettringite features, suggesting reduced cohesion within the PMM matrix. The reduction in strength at elevated COPU levels may also be attributed to limited cement hydration, as excess MDI competes with water, reducing ettringite formation—an essential component for structural integrity. These findings emphasize the importance of optimizing the COPU content: while moderate additions enhance mechanical performance through microstructural reinforcement, excessive loading can compromise matrix integrity and hydration efficiency.

XRF analysis

The XRF analysis of the PMM2 sample in Table 3 shows similar oxide compositions for both the control and PMM2 samples. Their composition is dominated by SiO₂ (~47 wt.%), indicating a silica-rich material, likely from aggregates or cementitious components. CaO (~23 wt.%) indicates a significant presence of Portland cement, which contributes to strength and hydration reactions. Al₂O₃ (~10 wt.%) and Fe₂O₃ (~8 wt.%) are present in typical amounts for alumino-silicate-based systems, likely aiding in early strength development and enhancing durability. MgO (~5 wt.%) might originate from dolomitic limestone, while Na₂O (~3.5 wt.%) indicates alkali content, which should be monitored for potential alkali-silica reactions (ASR). Finally, SO₃ (1.2 wt.%) suggests the addition of gypsum, which is essential for controlling the setting time of the cement. Overall, the XRF analysis reflects the typical cementitious matrix, with elements that influence the mechanical and durability properties of both control and PMM2 samples.

Chemical resistance test

PMM2’s chemical resistance was assessed through weight change measurements, compressive strength measurements, and SEM micrograph analysis after 24 hours of exposure to acidic, saline, basic, and neutral environments. This comprehensive evaluation provides crucial insights into the material’s performance and potential applications in various settings. The chemical resistance of the samples was assessed through weight retention and compressive strength tests after 24 hours of exposure to acidic, saline, basic, and neutral environments (Figure 10). The results reveal varying degrees of resistance, with the material retaining 88.78 wt.%, 98.18 wt.%, 99.655 wt.%, and 99.9 wt.% under acidic, saline, basic, and neutral conditions, respectively. Correspondingly, the compressive strengths after exposure were 7.8 MPa, 14.8 MPa, 16.1 MPa, and 16.9 MPa for the same environments. PMM2 demonstrated the highest vulnerability to acidic conditions, a common challenge for cementitious materials, with a notable 11.22 wt.% loss and a 53.85% decrease in compressive strength compared with the neutral environment. In contrast, the material exhibited excellent stability in saline and basic environments. The material’s notable resistance to saline exposure, as evidenced by minimal weight loss and only a 12.43% reduction in compressive strength relative to the control, suggests potential applications in marine or coastal structures. The performance under basic conditions was particularly impressive, with a mere 0.345 wt.% loss and a 4.73% reduction in compressive strength compared to the neutral environment, indicating the suitability of PMM2 for alkaline settings. These findings highlight the promising chemical resistance of these materials, especially in saline and basic environments.

The (a) corresponding weight loss and (b) compressive strength of PMM2 after soaking in acidic, saline, basic, and neutral environments for 24 h.

The SEM microphotographs in Fig. 11 provide visual evidence of the surface morphology of PMM2 after exposure to the various chemical environments, corroborating the weight retention and compressive strength data. In the acidic environment (Fig. 11a), the micrograph reveals noticeable surface etching and potential degradation of the cement paste, aligning with the significant weight loss and strength reduction observed. This etching is primarily due to the reaction between hydrochloric acid (HCl) and calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) in the cement paste, forming calcium chloride (CaCl2) and water, which leads to the dissolution of the cement matrix.71 The roughened surface texture and possible exposure of aggregate particles indicate the aggressive nature of the acid attack on the material. The saline environment micrograph (Fig. 11b) shows some salt crystal formations on the surface, but the overall structure appears largely intact, which is consistent with the weight retention and moderate strength loss observed with PMM2. This suggests that while salt may penetrate the surface, it does not significantly compromise the integrity of the material. The basic environment micrograph (Fig. 11c) displays minimal visible changes compared with the neutral condition, supporting the excellent performance in alkaline settings indicated by the quantitative data. The surface appears smooth and cohesive, with the COPU modification likely playing a role in protecting the PMM matrix. Finally, the neutral environment micrograph (Fig. 11d) serves as a control, showing the typical surface characteristics of the unexposed PMM2.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and characterized a novel coconut oil-based COPU-modified mortar (PMM) that addresses critical water penetration challenges in construction materials. The optimal 2 wt.% COPU loading achieved superior hydrophobic performance with a 125° contact angle and 54% reduction in water absorption, while simultaneously demonstrating a 40% improvement in compressive strength from 13.1 to 18.4 MPa. Comprehensive characterization confirmed successful polymer integration and microstructural modification. These technical achievements translate to enhanced durability and reduced maintenance costs for structures in wet environments, with potential applications in marine construction, waterproofing, and infrastructure exposed to harsh weather. The material represents a sustainable alternative to petroleum-based polymer modifications using renewable coconut oil feedstock, while its lightweight properties make it suitable for high-rise construction and seismic-resistant design. Future research should focus on long-term durability studies under accelerated weathering conditions, scale-up investigations for field applications, economic analysis comparing COPU-PMM with conventional methods, investigation of other natural oil-based systems, and integration with other sustainable materials. The findings advance sustainable construction practices by demonstrating that bio-based polymer modifications can achieve superior performance while reducing environmental impact, establishing a foundation for developing eco-friendly, high-performance construction materials that meet increasingly stringent durability and sustainability requirements.
