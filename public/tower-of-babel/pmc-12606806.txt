Thrombomodulin is essential for recombinant Fc-fusion protein production in Chinese hamster ovary cells via multiple signaling pathways
Authors: Xiaonan Ma, Nawei Wang, Qihang Yan, Shuang Li, Guorong Hu, Lunxiao Zhao, Ruoyin Xiao, Mingqi Wang, Ningning Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells are the predominant cell line used for biotherapeutic production. To reduce the cost of therapeutic recombinant proteins produced in CHO cells, efforts have been made over decades to improve overall yield through media and process optimization, as well as genetic engineering aimed at enhancing cell proliferation or productivity. Within an intricate cellular framework, such as the CHO cell system, it is indisputable that numerous genes with seemingly disparate functions may be involved in the process of expressing recombinant proteins.ResultsThrombomodulin (TM), encoded by the Thbd gene, is primarily known for its roles in coagulation, innate immunity, inflammation, and tumor cell proliferation. Our research was the first to reveal that the presence of thrombomodulin is highly correlated with recombinant protein production in CHO cells producing an Fc-fusion protein. Knocking out Thbd resulted in approximately an 82% reduction in recombinant protein yield by the end of fed-batch culture, indicating that TM is essential for efficient production. Further investigation revealed that this loss was due to a dramatic reduction in mRNA levels of the recombinant protein. Re-expression of TM in the Thbd-knockout cell line restored mRNA levels, confirming TM’s role in maintaining transcription. Phosphorylation levels of PKC, MEK, and ERK were elevated in the knockout cells compared to untreated wild-type cells, whereas phosphorylation of mTOR and AKT was decreased. Additionally, overexpression of Thbd led to moderate increases in c-Myc and Bcl2 expression, which appeared to slow the decline in cell viability during cultivation. Background

Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells are esteemed as the quintessential expression system for the production of over 70% of recombinant biopharmaceutical proteins, including monoclonal antibodies and intricate human proteins. This preference arises from their proficiency in executing human-like post-translational modifications and their low susceptibility to human viral infections [1]. Despite advances in biopharmaceutical production, the cost of these products remains substantial, necessitating significant enhancements in overall yield. To address the escalating global demand at a more affordable cost, various strategies have been employed, including cell engineering, the development of enriched culture media, and process optimization techniques [2–5]. However, further improvements in yield and quality are essential to achieve cost reductions. Such advancements are contingent upon a deeper understanding of CHO cell biology and their behavior within bioprocesses, thereby necessitating intensified efforts in this field [6, 7]. Besides media and bioprocess optimization, major genetic modifications include enhancements in cell proliferation, protein secretion, anti-apoptosis activity, and metabolic engineering strategies. Most of the identified genes participate in these bioprocesses, which is logical given their expected correlation with recombinant protein expression [8–11]. Within an intricate cellular framework, such as the CHO cell system, it is indisputable that numerous genes with seemingly disparate functions may be involved in the process of expressing recombinant proteins.

Thrombomodulin (TM), a glycosylated type Ⅰ transmembrane protein, is a critical component of a complex system that plays a vital role in various biological processes. It is primarily localized in the vascular endothelium and is involved in the regulation of coagulation, innate immunity, inflammation, and tumor cell proliferation [12, 13]. The structure of TM includes five regions: a C-type lectin-like domain, six epidermal growth factor-like (EGF-like) repeat regions, a serine-threonine-rich region, a single-pass transmembrane stretch, and a cytoplasmic tail, from the N-terminal to the C-terminal [14]. TM is also found in various cell types in both mice and humans, including dendritic cells, urothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and certain tumor cells [15–18]. Given the known biological functions of it, there appears to be no relationship between TM and recombinant protein expression. Moreover, no published studies have reported any such correlation.

In this study, the role of thrombomodulin in regulating the yield of an Fc-fusion protein was examined by generating CHO-K1 cell lines with Thbd knockout and overexpression. Subsequently, the involvement of relevant signaling pathways in TM-mediated regulation of recombinant protein production was investigated. Finally, individual TM domains were analyzed to determine which regions contribute most significantly to these effects.

Methods

Cell culture and reagents

An Fc-fusion protein-producing CHO-K1 cell line was established in our laboratory. This cell line was used as the parental cell line and designated CHO-Fc-WT (WT), as it expresses the wild-type thrombomodulin molecule without any genetic modification or treatment. All cell lines were routinely passaged every three days at a seeding density of 0.5 × 10⁶ cells/mL in a 125-mL shake flask, with a working volume of 20 mL, incubated at 37 °C with 8% CO₂ and 120 rpm. The CD CHOK1 medium (Yishengke, Zhejiang, China) was supplemented with 20 µM MSX (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for the CHO-Fc-WT and CHO-Fc-KO cell lines. Additionally, 6 µg/mL puromycin was added to the above medium for CHO-Fc-OE (OE), CHO-Fc-Match (Mat), and CHO-Fc-KO-OE (KO-OE) cell lines. CSC03-CL medium (Yishengke, Zhejiang, China), containing 10% FBS, 20 µM MSX, and 6 µg/mL puromycin, was used during the construction of OE, Mat, and KO-OE cell lines in 96-well and 24-well plates. Cell viability and viable cell counts were determined at each sampling time using a Counter Star (Shanghai RuiYu Biotech, China). All abbreviations and engineered cell line names used in this study are summarized in Table S3.

Plasmid construction

The coding sequence (CDS) of the full-length, non-intron-containing Thbd gene, which encodes thrombomodulin (TM, wild-type), was synthesized into the pcDNA3.1-CMV-PuroR plasmid based on the NCBI reference (Gene ID: 100765076) by Jinweizhi (China), and validated by sequencing. This plasmid was used to establish TM-overexpressing cell lines.

An additional set of ten plasmids was constructed, comprising nine Thbd mutants with various domain deletions (as illustrated in Fig. 6a) and the full-length Thbd gene. All constructs were cloned into the pcDNA3.1-CMV-PuroR-EGFP vector and collectively designated as pcDNA3.1-CMV-Thbd-Mut1–9/WT-PuroR-EGFP. These plasmids were validated by agarose gel electrophoresis (Figure S3) and subsequently used to transfect the CHO-Fc-KO cell line.

Fed-batch culture, Qp determination, RNA extraction, RT-qPCR and osmolality

Fed-batch culture, specific productivity (Qp) determination, RNA extraction, and RT-qPCR were performed as previously described. The reference gene used in RT-qPCR analysis was Akr1a1 which was identified in our previous work [19]. All primers were designed according to the MIQE guidelines [20] using NCBI Primer-BLAST to ensure target specificity. Medium osmolality was measured daily using Fiske osmometer (Norwood, USA).

Thbd knockdown via RNA interference

A total of 1 × 10⁵ cells were seeded in each well of a 24-well plate with 0.5 mL of medium one day before transfection. A 0.8 µM siRNA solution (sequence: GTGCAGAGCTAGAGTACAA), along with the transfection reagent (Lipo8000™, C0533, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), was added dropwise to each well. Relative mRNA expression was assessed by RT-qPCR at 48 h post-transfection, and recombinant protein yield was measured by ELISA at 72 h.

Thbd overexpression via liposomal transfection

1 × 10⁵ cells were seeded into 24-well plates with 0.5 mL of medium per well one day prior to transfection. The medium was refreshed, and 0.8 µg of pcDNA3.1-Thbd-PuroR plasmid was added along with 0.8 µL LipoFiter™ reagent (HB-LF-1000, HanBio, Shanghai, China). After 6 h of incubation, the medium was replaced, and cells were further incubated. mRNA expression levels were measured by RT-qPCR after 48 h, and r-protein yield was determined by ELISA after 72 h.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min on glass slides. Permeabilization was performed using 0.25% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. Cells were incubated with a primary antibody against thrombomodulin (Rabbit pAb, 1:500), followed by a FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L secondary antibody (1:500). For Fc-fusion protein visualization, a rhodamine (TRITC)-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (H + L) antibody diluted 1:100 was applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Samples were mounted with DAPI-containing mounting medium (Abcam, MA, USA). Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Leica fluorescence microscope (Germany) at 20× magnification.

Thbd knockout via CRISPR-Cas9

Six sgRNAs (Table S1) were designed using the E-CRISP tool (http://www.e-crisp.org/E-CRISP/index.html) and cloned into the pUC19-U6 vector. CHO-Fc-WT cells were co-transfected with the sgRNA and pCas9-EGFP plasmids via electro-transfection. Thbd-knockout cells were sorted using flow cytometry. Cells were expanded stepwise from 96-well plates to shake flasks. TM protein expression was assessed by Western blot. The validated knockout cells were designated as CHO-Fc-KO (KO).

Thbd overexpression and re-expression via electro-transfection

CHO-Fc-WT and CHO-Fc-KO cells were transfected with the pcDNA3.1-CMV-Thbd-PuroR plasmid containing the Thbd CDS via electro-transfection. Cells were selected with 6 µg/mL puromycin to isolate monoclonal cell lines. After expansion, TM expression was confirmed by Western blot. TM-overexpressing cell lines were designated as CHO-Fc-OE (OE). Re-expression in the KO background targeting wild-type TM levels was achieved by transfecting CHO-Fc-KO cells with the same plasmid, generating CHO-Fc-Mat (Mat) cell lines. Cell lines derived from CHO-Fc-KO with TM expression levels exceeding those of CHO-Fc-WT were designated as CHO-Fc-KO-OE (KO-OE).

SDS-PAGE and Western blot

A total of 2 × 10⁶ cells were harvested, and total lysates were extracted with RIPA buffer containing phenylmethan esulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Beyotime ST505, China) on ice for 10 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min, protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Proteins were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE and visualized using the Taron Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Shanghai, China).

For Western blot analysis, proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes following SDS-PAGE, then incubated with primary antibodies, followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signal detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and visualized with the Tanon Chemiluminescent Imaging System. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

To assess the phosphorylation levels of PKC, mTOR, and ERK by Western blot, total cell lysates were extracted using RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF and phosphatase inhibitor (CWBIO 23835, China), 1 µg of the appropriate primary antibody was added to the supernatant and incubated for 1 h at 4° C. Subsequently, 20 µL of Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose was added, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. Immune complexes were collected by centrifugation at 2,500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, washed with RIPA buffer, and resuspended in 50 µL of RIPA buffer prior to SDS-PAGE analysis.

ELISA

A 200 µL supernatant sample was collected from well plates or shake flasks and centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 3 min. The concentration of Fc-fusion protein was measured using a sandwich ELISA kit (E80-104, Bethyl, Texas, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry

CHO-Fc-KO cells were transfected with ten pcDNA3.1-CMV-Thbd-Mut1-9/WT-PuroR-EGFP plasmids and a pcDNA3.1-CMV-PuroR-EGFP control plasmid. Transfection efficiency was evaluated by detecting EGFP fluorescence using a BD FACSAria III cell sorter (USA). Data analysis was conducted with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from three independent experiments. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests, and multiple groups were analyzed by ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test in GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). p-values are indicated.

Results

Recombinant protein production correlated with Thbd mRNA levels in CHO cells during RNA interference and transient overexpression

To investigate the impact of the Thbd gene on recombinant protein (r-protein) yield in CHO cells, we first explored the effects of knocking down Thbd via siRNA and overexpressing it through liposomal transfection in an Fc-fusion protein-producing CHO-K1 cell line (CHO-Fc-WT cell). The transient effects on the mRNA level of the gene Thbd were evaluated on the 2nd day through RT-qPCR analysis and changes in r-protein production was examined on the 3rd day with ELISA tests, following treatment. As shown in Fig. 1A, the transcriptional level of the Thbd gene decreased by approximately 32.1% under RNA interference, while it increased by 20.5% under overexpression in CHO-Fc-WT cell. Fc fusion protein production was reduced by 18.7% upon knocking down Thbd and increased by about 13.5% upon overexpression. These findings suggest a positive correlation between r-protein yield and the expression abundance of the Thbd gene at the mRNA level, with a correlation coefficient of 0.969 (Fig. 1B, p = 0.0014).

Deficiency of TM resulted in a dramatic decrease in r-protein production in CHO cells

For further investigation, stable monoclonal CHO-Fc-WT cell lines with either overexpression or knockout of the Thbd gene were successfully established. The expression of thrombomodulin (TM), encoded by Thbd, was confirmed via immunofluorescence (Fig. 2A) and Western blot (Fig. 2B). In the TM knockout (TM⁻/⁻ labelled as CHO-Fc-KO(KO)) cell line, neither TM (green fluorescence) nor Fc-fusion proteins (red fluorescence) exhibited obvious fluorescence signals. In contrast, the TM overexpression (CHO-Fc-OE(OE)) cell line displayed significantly stronger green fluorescence compared to the wild-type (CHO-Fc-WT(WT)) cell line, indicating elevated TM levels. However, the red fluorescence intensity corresponding to recombinant protein expression in the OE strain was comparable to that of the WT. Western blot analysis (Fig. 2B) revealed that TM expression in OE cells was approximately 13.3-fold higher than in WT cells, while TM expression in the KO cell line was reduced to less than 10% of that in WT cells (Fig. 2C), consistent with the immunofluorescence results.

During fed-batch culture (Fig. 2D), the viable cell density of the OE cell line was lower than that of the WT and KO cell lines before day 9, suggesting that overexpression of TM may reduce CHO cell growth in the early stage of cultivation. The KO cell line exhibited the highest growth rate throughout the culture period, indicating that the absence of TM enhances CHO cell proliferation. Meanwhile, the OE cell line maintained higher cell viability compared to the WT cell line (Fig. 2D and E), which led to its viable cell density surpassing that of WT during the later stages of culture (days 9 and 11, Fig. 2F), thereby supporting sustained recombinant protein secretion.

The recombinant protein titers on days 9 and 11 for each cell line are shown in Fig. 2G. Compared with the WT cells, the r-protein yield of the KO cell line decreased by 84.1% and 82.04% on days 9 and 11, respectively—significantly more than the reduction observed in previous RNA interference experiments. This result strongly suggests that the presence of TM is essential for efficient recombinant protein production in CHO cells, even though the KO cell line exhibited higher viable cell counts than the WT and OE lines throughout the process (Fig. 2D and F), which would typically be expected to result in greater protein output. However, stable overexpression of TM in the OE cell line did not lead to a higher final r-protein yield (Fig. 2G, day 11) compared to previous transient treatments; on the contrary, the change in yield was even lower, at 10.4%. This suggests that a markedly increased abundance of TM molecules on the cell membrane does not consistently enhance r-protein production.

To further evaluate the recombinant protein production capacity, the specific productivity (Qp) of each cell line was calculated during the rapid growth phase, from day 3 to day 5 and from day 5 to day 7 (Fig. 2H). Consistent with previous observations, the loss of TM led to a dramatic reduction in Qp, decreasing from 9.94 pg/cell/day in the WT strain to 1.35 pg/cell/day in the KO strain—an 86.4% decline. This indicates that the absence of TM severely impairs the ability of individual cells to synthesize recombinant protein. In contrast, the Qp of the OE cell line was not significantly higher than that of the WT strain, suggesting that the increased recombinant protein yield in OE cultures is primarily attributable to higher cell density. However, in the KO cell line, despite exhibiting the highest cell density, the final protein yield remained substantially lower than that of the other two cell lines, due to a pronounced loss in single-cell productivity.

Because the nutrient-rich feed used in fed-batch culture can markedly increase medium osmolality, which in turn influences cell behavior and productivity, the osmolality of the three cell lines was measured (Fig. 2I). The KO cell line exhibited the lowest osmolality despite its highest viable cell density, whereas the WT and OE lines showed higher osmolality, likely due to their greater recombinant protein production. Production stability was assessed over 20 consecutive 3-day passages (Fig. 2J and K). The coefficients of variation for recombinant protein titers in WT, KO, and OE cells were 11.2%, 16.3%, and 8.55%, respectively, indicating stable production across all three cell lines.

The loss of TM led to a significant reduction in r-protein mRNA levels and affected the PKC/MAPK signaling pathways

The fold changes in Thbd and r-protein gene expression between KO and WT, as well as OE and WT, were measured at the mRNA level using RT-qPCR (Fig. 3A). The knockout and overexpression of TM in the CHO-Fc-WT cell lines were validated through mRNA expression analysis. In KO cells, Thbd expression was reduced to only 3.1% of the WT level, indicating successful deletion of the gene. Conversely, the Thbd mRNA level in CHO-Fc-OE cells was approximately fourfold higher than in WT cells, confirming TM overexpression.

Regarding the r-protein gene, its mRNA level in KO cells was approximately 14.8% of that in the WT strain, whereas OE cells showed no significant increase in r-protein mRNA compared to WT. These findings suggest that the loss of TM results in a substantial reduction in r-protein transcription in CHO cells.

Furthermore, the PKC and MAPK signaling pathways were implicated as a downstream effect of Thbd gene knockout and overexpression (Fig. 3B). The phosphorylation-to-total protein (P/T) ratios of PKC delta, MEK (Ser 222 and 226), and ERK were significantly higher in KO cells compared to WT. In contrast, OE cells exhibited lower P/T ratios for all three enzymes relative to the WT strain (Fig. 3C and E).

AKT/mTOR signaling pathways are modulated by TM expression levels

The mTOR signaling pathway was also affected by the loss of TM (Fig. 4A). Unlike the trend observed in the MAPK pathway, the phosphorylation-to-total (P/T) ratios of mTOR and AKT (S473) were significantly decreased in KO cells compared to WT, indicating reduced pathway activation in the absence of TM (Fig. 4B and C). However, overexpression of TM in OE cells did not result in a notable increase in the phosphorylation levels of mTOR or AKT compared to WT cells.

As the viability of the CHO-Fc-OE cell line exceeded that of the WT in fed-batch culture, two cell viability–related genes—c-Myc and Bcl2—were found to be differentially expressed based on RT-qPCR analysis. Only the OE cell line exhibited increased expression of both genes, with mRNA levels elevated by 27.1% for c-Myc and 15.7% for Bcl2. In contrast, the CHO-Fc-KO cell line showed no significant changes in the expression of either gene (Fig. 4D and E).

Re-expression of TM in CHO-Fc-KO cell lines compensated for the decrease in r-protein production

TM was re-expressed in the CHO-Fc-KO cell line through transfection, and monoclonal cell lines with TM expression levels comparable to those of the WT strain were selected and named CHO-Fc-Mat ((Mat), “Mat” representing “match”) based on Western blot analysis. Additionally, a cell line with higher TM expression than the WT was identified and named CHO-Fc-KO-OE ((KO-OE), Fig. 5A and B). Fed-batch cultures of CHO-Fc-WT, KO, Mat, and KO-OE were performed, and broth samples collected on the final day of culture were analyzed by SDS-PAGE to compare overall r-protein production (Fig. 5C and D). Re-expression of TM in the Mat cell line restored r-protein yield to levels similar to those observed in WT cells. However, the KO-OE cell line, which exhibited a 4.09-fold increase in TM expression compared to the WT, only showed a slight increase (19.1%) in r-protein production compared to the WT cell, mirroring the results observed in the OE cell line. Re-expression of TM in the Mat cell line also restored r-protein mRNA expression to 91.4% of the WT level (Fig. 5E). In contrast, although the KO-OE cell line exhibited a 3.47-fold increase in Thbd mRNA levels compared to the WT, this did not result in a significant increase in r-protein mRNA expression (approximately 98% of the WT), similar to the results observed in the OE cell line. Fed-batch cultures of CHO-Fc-WT, Mat, and KO-OE cell lines were conducted and comparatively analyzed (Figure S2). During the latter half of the culture process, the KO-OE cell line demonstrated higher viability compared to the other two cell lines, resulting in an increased final cell density, comparable to that observed in OE cells.

Investigation of the effects of different TM domains on r-protein production

The TM molecule comprises a lectin-like domain, six epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like domains, a Ser/Thr-rich region, a transmembrane domain, and a short cytoplasmic tail. To determine which domain(s) of the TM molecule are most critical for r-protein production, the leave-one-out domain-deletion strategy was intentionally chosen as a conceptually simple and widely used first step for mapping structure–function relationships in modular proteins: by removing each domain in turn the experiment identifies regions whose absence alters a defined phenotype and therefore merit follow-up mechanistic study [21]. Nine plasmids encoding different combinations of TM domains (Fig. 6A and Figure S4) and EGFP were constructed and transfected into CHO-Fc-KO cells. These were tested alongside a plasmid containing the full-length TM sequence. A plasmid lacking any TM sequence was used as a negative control.

Transfection efficiency was assessed via flow cytometry by measuring EGFP fluorescence encoded in the plasmid backbone in 10,000 cells per sample. As shown in Fig. 6B, transfection efficiencies ranged from 43.3% to 54.6%, except for Mut5 (62.13%) and the negative control (39.53%). At the end of the fed-batch culture, CHO-Fc-KO-WT cells produced the highest yield of r-protein, highlighting the importance of the full-length TM molecule in enhancing r-protein production. The CHO-Fc-KO-Ctr cells produced the lowest yield, suggesting that even partial TM expression can contribute to r-protein expression to some extent. Among the TM domain mutants, both Mut1 (lacking the lectin-like domain) and Mut9 (lacking the cytoplasmic tail) resulted in notably lower r-protein production, underscoring the essential roles of the N-terminal lectin domain and cytoplasmic tail. In contrast, deletion of the EGF6 domain in CHO-Fc-KO-Mut7 had the least impact on r-protein yield compared to other domain deletions, indicating that EGF6 may be less critical in this context (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

CHO cells have been of great interest in biopharmaceutical field for decades due to its high protein production capacity and human-like post-translational modification. Even significant progress in production has been made, the absolute yield is still low compared with prokaryotic E. coli and eukaryotic S. cerevisiae expression systems. Accordingly, biological mechanisms related to productivity are of great interest.

Thrombomodulin is a transmembrane glycoprotein that plays a crucial role in blood coagulation and endothelial function. It was first discovered in the 1980s and has since been extensively studied for its biological functions and therapeutic potential [13]. However, no evidence has been found to be connected with the production of therapeutic recombinant protein in any cell lines in vitro.

In this study, the Thbd-knock out cell line (CHO-Fc-KO) showed dramatic decrease in r protein production to less than one fifth of the wild type CHO-Fc-WT cell. It was further found due to a 97% reduction in the mRNA level of r-protein. PKC delta, MAPK signal pathway was found to be affected by the loss of TM and might be one cause of the inhibition in transcription of r-protein. PKC delta, MEK and ERK were activated in CHO-Fc-KO cell lines (Fig. 7). Activation of PKC can regulate gene expression changes associated with cell growth and differentiation, stimulating other kinases [22]. And PKC activation is often found to be correlated with MAPK pathway [23, 24]. ERK1/2 signaling pathway plays an important role in changes in gene expression due to activated ERK1/2 entering the nucleus and phosphorylating transcription factors. Although ERK activation typically promotes gene expression, it can also exert repressive effects by phosphorylating inhibitory transcription factors or co-repressors, thereby regulating their activity [25, 26]. This mechanism is part of the broader regulatory role of phosphorylation in controlling transcription factor function. Therefore, ERK might suppress the transcription of the r-protein gene when phosphorylated in CHO-Fc-KO cells in order to ease the burden to produce the exogenous protein to grow faster. But the opposite trend was not observed in CHO-Fc-OE cell line where TM was stably highly overexpressed (13.3-fold of TM to WT). The r-protein yield just exhibited slight increase (10.4%) which was even lower than the transient overexpression (13.5% increase in r-protein yield). Similar situation was observed in CHO-Fc-KO-OE cell (4.09-fold of TM to WT and 19.1% higher than WT in r-protein yield). This made us hypothesize that there might be a threshold value for TM abundance in CHO cell which can improve the r-protein production maximally.

Schematic diagram of signaling pathways modulated by TM knockout

The Mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)/AKT signaling pathway is a central regulator of numerous biological functions related to cell growth, survival, metabolism, and homeostasis. Herein, phosphorylation level of AKT and m-TOR in CHO-Fc-KO cell was found to be lower than the CHO-Fc-WT cell (Fig. 7), which might be another factor responsible for the big drop of the r-protein yield. Inhibition of mTOR and AKT phosphorylation has been shown to suppress protein expression at the transcriptional level through various mechanisms, as well [27–30].

The mRNA levels of the c-Myc and Bcl-2 genes were found to be upregulated in the CHO-Fc-OE cell line, which may contribute to the enhanced cell viability observed during fed-batch culture. c-Myc, a transcription factor, plays a critical role in regulating cell growth and viability [31]. Elevated expression of c-Myc has been shown to promote cell proliferation in certain cancer cell types [32, 33]. Moreover, c-Myc can directly bind to the promoter region of the Bcl-2 gene, leading to its transcriptional activation [34]. Bcl-2 exerts its anti-apoptotic effect by prolonging the time-to-death of cells [35]. Collectively, these findings support our observations of increased final cell viability and density in CHO-Fc-OE and CHO-Fc-KO-OE cell lines during fed-batch culture.

In a study by Zhang et al., overexpression of TM in tumor cells resulted in reduced cell proliferation and tumor growth, indicating that TM possesses antiproliferative effects independent of its anticoagulant function [36]. This finding aligns with our observation of a reduced growth rate prior to day 9 of fed-batch culture in the CHO-Fc-OE cell line. Conversely, loss of TM expression in the CHO-Fc-KO cell line was associated with a higher growth rate compared to both KO and OE cells.

In fed-batch cultures, nutrient-rich feeds can elevate medium osmolality, affecting CHO cell growth and metabolism. In our study, the KO cell line exhibited the lowest osmolality and the highest viable cell density, consistent with Zhu et al. [37], yet its recombinant protein mRNA was markedly reduced, resulting in substantially lower protein production. WT and OE lines showed comparable mRNA levels, Qp, and osmolality, however, the OE line achieved higher viable cell density and recombinant protein production during the later stages of culture. This increase is unlikely to be due to differences in osmolality or transcriptional activity and may instead reflect enhanced cell proliferation, improved nutrient utilization, or reduced apoptosis [1, 38, 39] consistent with the observed upregulation of c-Myc and Bcl-2 in the OE cell line.

All three lines maintained stable protein production across 20 consecutive 3-day passages, demonstrating that both productivity and stability can be preserved under fed-batch conditions. These findings highlight that transcriptional activity and cell-line-specific growth traits, rather than osmolality alone, are key determinants of recombinant protein yield and robustness.

Thrombomodulin (TM) is a highly modular protein consisting of a lectin-like domain, six EGF-like repeats, a serine/threonine-rich spacer, a transmembrane region, and a short cytoplasmic tail—each with distinct and experimentally confirmed biological roles. Previous studies have shown that the N-terminal lectin-like domain of TM exhibits distinct biological functions that are largely independent of its anticoagulant activity. This domain plays a protective and regulatory role in maintaining cell viability and modulating inflammation and may influence cell proliferation and survival indirectly through its anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and anti-angiogenic properties [36, 40]. The cytoplasmic tail, a relatively short intracellular domain, is implicated in maintaining endothelial cell quiescence and regulating the actin cytoskeleton, thereby influencing cell shape and motility—particularly within the context of endothelial cell signaling and inflammation modulation [41]. Given these diverse and independent functions, we hypothesized that different TM domains may differentially influence CHO cell physiology and recombinant protein productivity. In the present study, the leave-one-out deletion analysis revealed domain-specific contributions to recombinant protein production. The lectin-like domain and cytoplasmic tail of TM appear to be more closely associated with r-protein production. Based on our findings, this association may involve the PKC/MAPK and mTOR/AKT signaling pathways. However, the underlying mechanisms require further investigation (e.g., point mutagenesis, and interaction assay).

Conclusion

This study identifies TM as a previously unrecognized regulator of recombinant protein production in CHO cells. Beyond its established roles in coagulation and immunity, TM is shown to influence transcriptional activity and key signaling pathways associated with cell productivity and viability. These findings reveal a novel biological function for TM and offer new insights into the complex molecular mechanisms governing recombinant protein expression in CHO cells, with important implications for improving biotherapeutic manufacturing.
