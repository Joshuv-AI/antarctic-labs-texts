All Your Knowledge Belongs to Us: Stealing Knowledge Graphs via Reasoning APIs
Zhaohan Xi

Abstract
Knowledge graph reasoning (KGR), which answers complex, logical queries over large knowledge graphs (KGs), represents an important artificial intelligence task with a range of applications. Many KGs require extensive domain expertise and engineering effort to build and are hence considered proprietary within organizations and enterprises. Yet, spurred by their commercial and research potential, there is a growing trend to make KGR systems, (partially) built upon private KGs, publicly available through reasoning APIs. The inherent tension between maintaining the confidentiality of KGs while ensuring the accessibility to KGR systems motivates our study of KG extraction attacks: the adversary aims to “steal” the private segments of the backend KG, leveraging solely black-box access to the KGR API. Specifically, we present Kgx , an attack that extracts confidential sub-KGs with high fidelity under limited query budgets. At a high level, Kgx progressively and adaptively queries the KGR API and integrates the query responses to reconstruct the private sub-KG. This extraction remains viable even if any query responses related to the private sub-KG are filtered. We validate the efficacy of Kgx against both experimental and real-world KGR APIs. Interestingly, we find that typical countermeasures ( e.g. , injecting noise into query responses) are often ineffective against Kgx . Our findings suggest the need for a more principled approach to developing and deploying KGR systems, as well as devising new defenses against KG extraction attacks.

Nevertheless, the delicate balance between upholding the KG confidentiality and facilitating access control implies the potential information “leakage” through APIs. Given well-crafted queries, adversaries could unveil the confidential knowledge from APIs, consequently undermining the integrity of knowledge management. This, in turn, motivates us to explore strategies to extract confidential knowledge through restricted API accessibility.

Our work –
This work represents the design, implementation, and evaluation of
Kgx
, the first KG extraction attack against KGR APIs.
Kgx
focuses on uncovering the relational structures of the private sub-KG. At a high level, it progressively and adaptively generates reasoning queries that are likely to intersect with the private sub-KG; it then infers the intersections based on the query answers, even if any answers relevant to the confidential sub-KG are excluded; moreover, it consolidates the intersections of different queries to reconstruct the private sub-KG.

We validate the practicality of
Kgx
using a range of benchmark KGs, models, and reasoning methods, leading to
the following interesting findings.

RA
1
– We demonstrate that KGR APIs are highly vulnerable to KG extraction attacks even if any query responses related to private sub-KGs are filtered. For instance, compared with the ground truth, the sub-KG reconstructed by
Kgx
attains over 94% precision and 90% recall.

RA
2
– We further evaluate
Kgx
against KGR APIs “in the wild” (
e.g.
, Google KG
[
11
]
). We show that, despite the enormous size (1.37 million entities and 9.65 million edges) and the open-world nature of Google KG,
Kgx
attains performance comparable with the closed-world setting.

RA
3
– Lastly, we discuss potential countermeasures and
their challenges. Although it is straightforward to
conceive high-level mitigation strategies, it is non-trivial to concretely implement them. For instance, injecting random noises into query answers amounts to balancing the delicate trade-off between KGR performance and attack robustness.
Even worse,
Kgx
may neutralize the noises by aggregating related queries, thereby diminishing the effectiveness of such defenses.

Contributions –
To the best of our knowledge, this work represents the first systematic study on the privacy vulnerabilities of KGR APIs to KG extraction attacks. Our contributions are summarized as follows.

We present
Kgx
, the first KG extraction attack, which highlights the following features: (
i
) it assumes only black-box query access to the KGR API, (
ii
) it extracts private sub-KGs that highly align with the ground truth, and (
iii
) it succeeds under limited query budget.

We empirically demonstrate the efficacy of
Kgx
against both experimental and real-world KGR APIs across different types of KGs, models, and reasoning methods. The evaluation characterizes the inherent vulnerabilities of KGR APIs to extraction attacks.

We further discuss potential mitigation and challenges, which sheds light on improving the current practice of operating KGR, pointing to several research directions.

Queries.
A variety of reasoning tasks can be performed over KGs

v,v^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{q}\cup\{v_{?}\},r\in{\mathcal{R}}\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(1)

and entity of interest

{\mathcal{A}}_{q}

{\mathcal{V}}_{q}

KGR
is essentially a graph-matching problem that matches the entities and relations in queries with that in KGs. Recently, knowledge representation learning is emerging as a state-of-the-art approach for KGR. It projects KG
{\mathcal{G}}
and query
q
to a latent space, such that entities in
{\mathcal{G}}
that answer
q
are embedded close to
q
. Answering an arbitrary query

is thus reduced to finding entities with embeddings most similar to
q
, thereby implicitly imputing missing relations
[
28
]
and scaling up to large KGs
[
22
]
.

Typically, representation-based KGR comprises two key components: embedding function

projects each entity in
{\mathcal{G}}
to its latent embedding; transformation function
\phi
computes query
q

\psi_{q}
based on the embeddings of individual entities in
q
and their relations. Both functions are typically implemented as trainable neural networks
[
35
]
.

To process query
q

{\mathcal{A}}_{q}
and iteratively applies the transformation function until reaching the entity of interest
v_{?}
with the results as
q

\psi_{q}

{\mathcal{G}}
with the most similar embeddings to
\psi_{q}
are then identified as the query answer
\llbracket q\rrbracket

[
34
]
.

The training of KGR samples a collection of query-answer pairs from the backend KG
{\mathcal{G}}
as the training set
{\mathcal{D}}
and optimizes
\phi
and
\psi
with respect to
{\mathcal{D}}
in a supervised manner. We defer the details of training and reasoning to §
A. Details of KGR
.

II-C

Private/Public sub-KGs

To model the confidentiality of KG
{\mathcal{G}}
, without loss of generality, we assume that
{\mathcal{G}}
comprises public sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
and private sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}

{\mathcal{G}}={\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}\cup{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
.

Public sub-KG

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
refers to the segment of
{\mathcal{G}}
containing non-sensitive information that is typically accessible without any limitations. For instance, in the context of cyber KGs,
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
could include entities from the
CVE

[
19
]

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
may include relations between
Drug
and

, but exclude the relations between
Drug
and

due to privacy concerns
[
25
]
.

Private sub-KG

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
refers to the fraction of
{\mathcal{G}}
which contains sensitive information and can only be accessed with specific permissions. For example, providers of KGR services may demand licenses to query entities/relations from certain categories
[
18
]
; they may also implement service differentiation
[
3
]
, setting a higher price for access to a more sensitive sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. Additionally, as representation learning-based KGR models often lag behind their underlying KGs in terms of updates
[
19
]
, a sub-KG that has been removed during an update can also be deemed as
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
.

An apparent approach to safeguarding the confidentiality of

First, incorporating

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
contribute to the effective training of the embedding and transformation functions. Following the setting of §

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, respectively, and assess their performance in answering queries related to entities in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
. Figure

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, measured by HIT@
5

on three distinct KGs. It is worth noting that the inclusion of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
in KGR training significantly enhances its performance in answering queries solely related to

(

Hence, a more pragmatic approach is to filter out any query responses related to
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. Specifically, in the case of first-order conjunctive queries, entities in the query response
\llbracket q\rrbracket

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}

.

III
Threat Model

We describe the threat model of KG extraction attacks according to the
adversary’s objectives, knowledge, and capability.

III-A

Adversary’s objectives

The adversary’s objective is to reconstruct (“steal”) the private sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
through querying the KGR API. Formally, let
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{prv}}
denote the private sub-KG reconstructed by the adversary. The attack is successful if
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{prv}}
is highly similar to the ground-truth
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
:
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{prv}}

\approx

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
.

However, as the API excludes any entities in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
from the query answers, it is infeasible to reconstruct the concrete entities in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
(
e.g.
, entity names). Rather, we focus on recovering the relational structures of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, including the relations between different nodes as well as their categories.

III-B

Subsequent impacts

Note that the relational structures of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
capture the essential semantics of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, which support knowledge representation learning to generate the semantic embeddings of entities
[
49
,
26
,
50
,
35
]
. Thus, uncovering the relational structures of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
raises several subsequent impacts as adversary’s next-step objectives, specifically:

1. Commercial crisis –
With equivalent KG structures, an adversarial is capable of developing an alternative KGR service that can match the performance of the authentic service. This scenario introduces a challenge to the pay-to-access privilege and poses a risk to IP protection.

2. Subsequent attacks –
Exposing the structural information within the KG is sufficient to facilitate follow-up attacks, including the creation of deceptive or “poisoning” knowledge
[
66
]
, even in cases where the specific terminology of entities remains undisclosed.

3. Entity resolution –
With access to supplementary domain knowledge, an adversarial can potentially establish connections between nodes within
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{prv}}
and specific, tangible entities. For example, it becomes feasible to identify a concrete malware entity within
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{prv}}
by analyzing its relationships in auxiliary threat reports. This discovery may subsequently incentivize the adversary to exploit potentially vulnerable software by leveraging the extracted
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{prv}}
structures that connect to the resolved “malware” entity.

Despite the above implications, this work mainly focuses on the step of uncovering the relational structures of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
and considers its subsequent impacts as ongoing research.

III-C

Adversary’s knowledge

We now consider the adversary’s prior knowledge regarding the target KG
{\mathcal{G}}
and the KGR model.

Public knowledge –
Recall that
{\mathcal{G}}
comprises public sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
and private sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, wherein obtaining an adequate part of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
is feasible in two ways. First, real-world KGs are often crowdsourced from public, free knowledge bases, such as MITRE
[
16
]
for building ThreatKG and UMLS
[
24
]
for building clinical KG. It is thus easy to construct a large-scale KG with adequate entities. Second, the adversary is able to query the unconstrained and free parts through the KGR API, thereby disclosing a part of the public sub-KG. By combining those two strategies, the adversary is able to collect an adequate part of a public sub-KG to start the attack with.

For the convenience of discussion, we temporarily assume the adversary has partial access to
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
. Note that this assumption is not necessary. We discuss how to bootstrap the attack without access to
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
in §
V
. Further, we assume the adversary has zero knowledge about
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
.

Private entity category –
Real-world KGs typically organize their entities as a set of entity groups (e.g., threats, mitigations, or products), while the relation types are often formed between different groups (e.g.,
mitigate-by
relations between threats and mitigations). Thus, the adversary can sample queries to reveal the entity categories in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. For instance, a query using evidence of cyber threats to ask for mitigations can extract concrete answers, but directly asking for “what cyber threats are related to given evidence” may lead to low-confident answers. In this way, we reveal that the “threat” category contains partial
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
.

KGR–
To make the attack more practical, we assume the adversary has no knowledge regarding the KGR system including its underlying models (
e.g.
, GNN
[
68
]
), training and reasoning methods (
e.g.
, Query2Box
[
49
]
versus GQE
[
35
]
), and configurations (
e.g.
, embedding dimensionality).

III-D

Adversary’s capability

API access –
we assume the adversary solely relies on black-box query access to the KGR API to perform the attack. Any entities in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
are purged from the query answers. Further, the adversary is constrained by a query budget
n_{\mathrm{query}}

We now present

IV-A

Attack overview

As illustrated in Figure
5
,
Kgx
reconstructs the private sub-KG through progressively and iteratively querying the KGR API and integrating the query responses. In a nutshell,
Kgx
iterates between two steps: query generation and knowledge consolidation, as described below.

Query generation
adaptively generates queries that potentially intersects with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. Using such queries to “probe” the KGR API may reveal the relational structures of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. Specifically, one may adopt two strategies to generate query
q
: (
i
)
exploitation
generates
q
in the vicinity of the already-uncovered segment of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, which has a high chance of intersecting with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
but may only reveal limited new information; (ii)
exploration
generates
q
away from the already-uncovered segment of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, which incurs the risk of missing
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
but, if intersected, may effectively reveal unknown structures of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. In
Kgx
, we seek to balance these two strategies to maximize the query efficiency.

Knowledge consolidation
integrates the responses of multiple queries to infer the unknown structures of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. Given a query
q
that intersects with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, even if any entities in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
are excluded from
q
’s answer
\llbracket q\rrbracket
, it remains feasible to infer (i) whether
q
intersects with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
and (ii) what is the intersected structure. By merging such intersected structures from multiple queries,
Kgx
progressively uncovers the relational structures of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. Note that the adversary may re-query the KGR API to validate this merging operation.

IV-B

Query generation

Recall that first-order conjunctive queries ask for entities that satisfy existential and conjunctive constraints. While many types of queries are possible, we focus on generating path queries that specify a sequence of conjunctive constraints from an anchor entity
v_{a}
to an answer entity
v_{?}
:

\hskip-3.0pt\llbracket q\rrbracket=v_{?}\,.\,\exists\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{h}:v_{a}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{0}}$}}v_{1}\wedge v_{1}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{1}}$}}v_{2}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{h}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{h}}$}}v_{?}

(3)

For instance,
\textsf{\small BusyBox}\scriptsize\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 12.52715pt\hskip-12.52715pt\hbox{$\xrightarrow{\makebox[18.5161pt]{$\scriptstyle\text{target-by}$}}$}\hskip-12.52715pt\hskip-0.58333pt\raisebox{-0.66693pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\,$}}\hskip-0.58333pt\hskip 12.52715pt}}v_{\text{malware}}\scriptsize\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 15.1025pt\hskip-15.1025pt\hbox{$\xrightarrow{\makebox[23.6668pt]{$\scriptstyle\text{mitigate-by}$}}$}\hskip-15.1025pt\hskip-0.58333pt\raisebox{-0.66693pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\,$}}\hskip-0.58333pt\hskip 15.1025pt}}v_{?}
is a path query asking for the mitigation for malware targeting
BusyBox
. We choose path queries due to their simplicity and the flexibility of merging multiple queries (details in §
IV-C
). Note that we expect path queries to go through
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, i.e., the anchor and answer are in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, while intermediate variables are in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. Therefore, we generate path queries with at least two-hop relations to guarantee such nature.

To generate a path query, one needs to specify (i) the anchor entity
v_{a}
and the (expected) answer entity
v_{?}
and (ii) the relational path from
v_{a}
to
v_{?}
(i.e., the sequence of relations). We first answer the question of how to generate the relational path from
v_{a}
to
v_{?}
assuming
v_{a}
and
v_{?}
are given.

Generating relational paths.
Given entity pair
(v_{a},v_{?})
, we employ a model to suggest a likely relational path from
v_{a}
to
v_{?}
. Specifically, we extend
DeepPath

[
67
]
, a reinforcement learning framework for learning multi-hop relational paths in KGs. Intuitively,
DeepPath
trains a policy network that, given an entity pair, iteratively extends the most likely path between them. However, it is infeasible to directly apply
DeepPath
, as it assumes access to KG
{\mathcal{G}}
and iteratively updates the path by traversing
{\mathcal{G}}
, which is inaccessible in
Kgx
. To address this limitation, we train a set of relation-specific projection operators
\{\phi_{r}\}_{r}
, in which
\phi_{r}(\vec{v})
computes the embeddings of entities with relation
r
to
v
(
\vec{v}
denotes the embedding of entity
v
). Similar to
[
49
]
, the projection operators are implemented as trainable neural networks.

Under this setting, the state vector is defined as:

s=(\vec{v},\vec{v}_{?}-\vec{v})

(4)

where
\vec{v}
is the embedding of the current entity
v
(which is initialized as the anchor entity
v_{a}
). The action is defined as extending a relation
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
from
v
, with the embedding of the current entity updated as
\phi_{r}(\vec{v})
and the state updated as:

s^{\prime}=(\phi_{r}(\vec{v}),\vec{v}_{?}-\phi_{r}(\vec{v}))

(5)

The process iterates until
v_{?}
is reached (i.e.,
\|\vec{v}_{?}-\phi_{r}(\vec{v})\|\leq\epsilon
) or the search fails (i.e., the maximum number of hops
n_{\mathrm{hop}}
is reached). If successful, a relational path
q(v_{a},v_{?})=v_{a}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{0}}$}}v_{1}\ldots v_{h}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{h}}$}}v_{?}
is suggested.
2
2

2

Below we abuse the notation of
q
for both a query and its relational path.
Given the probabilistic nature of
DeepPath
, we may generate multiple distinctive paths to ensure the diversity of the generated queries.

Also, note that
q(v_{a},v_{?})
may partially overlap with public sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
. To optimize the distinguishing power of the generated query, we trim the relations trailing
v_{a}
(or preceding
v_{?}
) that intersect with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
and update the anchor (or answer) entity accordingly. For instance, if
q(v_{a},v_{?})=v_{a}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{0}}$}}v_{1}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{1}}$}}v_{2}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{2}}$}}v_{?}
and the first hop
v_{a}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{0}}$}}v_{1}
overlaps with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
, we update the anchor entity
v_{a}
as
v_{1}
and trim the relational path as
q(v_{a},v_{?})=v_{a}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{1}}$}}v_{2}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{2}}$}}v_{?}
.

Generating entity pairs.
Next, we answer the question of how to generate the entity pair
(v_{a},v_{?})
. We consider two potential strategies.

Exploration
generates
(v_{a},v_{?})
by randomly sampling a pair of entities from
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
. Due to the randomness, it is likely that (i) there may not exist a relational path
q(v_{a},v_{?})
and (ii) even if
q(v_{a},v_{?})
exists, it may not overlap with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. However, given that
q(v_{a},v_{?})
exists and intersects with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, it tends to reveal previously unknown structures of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
.

Exploitation
generates
(v_{a},v_{?})
by modifying a “hit” query
q
(i.e.,
q
’s relational path exists and overlaps with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
). Specifically, let
v_{a}^{q}
(
v_{?}^{q}
) be
q
’s anchor (answer) entity. We search
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
for the nearest neighbor of
v_{a}^{q}
(
v_{?}^{q}
) in the embedding space as
v_{a}
(
v_{?}
). Further, we ensure that
v_{a}
and
v_{a}^{q}
(
v_{?}
and
v_{?}^{q}
) share the same category. As
q
is a hit query, the path from
v_{a}
to
v_{?}
tends to also exist and intersect with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. However, due to its proximity to
q
, this path may only reveal limited new information about
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
.

To leverage both strategies,
Kgx
interleaves between exploration and exploitation queries.

IV-C

Knowledge consolidation

We use the queries generated in the previous step to probe the KGR API. Below we first describe how to utilize a single query to infer one path in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
and then show how to integrate the paths uncovered by multiple queries to form more complex structures.

Utilizing individual queries.
Recall that a path query specifies a sequence of relations
q(v_{a},v_{?})=v_{a}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{0}}$}}v_{1}\ldots v_{h}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{h}}$}}v_{?}
, while
q
’s answer
\llbracket q\rrbracket
returned by the API is a ranked list of entities with confidence scores. We determine the existence of
q(v_{a},v_{?})
by checking (i) whether
v_{?}
appears in
\llbracket q\rrbracket
and (ii) whether its confidence score exceeds a threshold
\lambda
.

We set
\lambda
empirically to differentiate between true and false answers. Specifically, we sample from
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
a set of path queries and their answers as the positive cases
{\mathcal{D}}^{+}
, and then perturb the relations or replace the answers (with irrelevant ones) in
{\mathcal{D}}^{+}
to form the negative cases
{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
. We set
\lambda
to maximize the difference between the answers in
{\mathcal{D}}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
. More details are deferred to §
-A
.

Another way to validate the path between
v_{a}
and
v_{?}
(without confidence scores) is to query the path reversely. Specifically, we switch the roles of
v_{a}
and
v_{?}
(
v_{a}
as the answer and
v_{?}
as the anchor) and query the path
q(v_{?},v_{a})
. If
v_{a}
also appears in
\llbracket q\rrbracket
, we admit the existence of the path from
v_{a}
to
v_{?}
.

Once we determine that
v_{?}
is a true answer for
q
, we consider that the path
v_{1}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{1}}$}}v_{2}\ldots v_{h-1}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{h-1}}$}}v_{h}
exists in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
.
3
3

3

In general,
v_{1}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{1}}$}}v_{2}\ldots v_{h-1}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{h-1}}$}}v_{h}
may intersect with
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
at disjoint segments, which however is unlikely for a small
h
(
e.g.
,
h\leq 3
in our setting).

Integrating multiple queries.
We may further integrate the hidden paths uncovered from multiple queries into conjunctive forms, revealing more complex structures of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
.

Specifically, we examine queries that share the same anchor
v_{a}
(or answer
v_{?}
) and greedily merge the common relations trailing
v_{a}
(or preceding
v_{?}
) in their paths. For instance, consider queries
q
and
q^{\prime}
with paths
v_{a}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{0}r_{1}r_{2}}$}}v_{?}
and

v_{a}^{\prime}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r_{0}^{\prime}r_{1}^{\prime}r_{2}^{\prime}}$}}v_{?}^{\prime}
(intermediate variables are omitted), respectively. As shown in Eq.
6
, if
v_{?}=v_{?}^{\prime}
and
r_{2}=r_{2}^{\prime}
,
q
and
q^{\prime}
are merged as (i); if
v_{a}=v_{a}^{\prime}
and
r_{0}=r_{0}^{\prime}
,
q
and
q^{\prime}
are merged as (ii).

(i)\;\;\begin{array}[]{c|}v_{a}\xrightarrow{r_{0}r_{1}}\\
v_{a}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{r_{0}^{\prime}r_{1}^{\prime}}\end{array}\xrightarrow{r_{2}}v_{?}\qquad(ii)\;\;v_{a}\xrightarrow{r_{0}}\begin{array}[]{|c}\xrightarrow{r_{1}r_{2}}v_{?}\\
\xrightarrow{r_{1}^{\prime}r_{2}^{\prime}}v_{?}^{\prime}\end{array}

(6)

To validate this merge operation, we may query the API using the merged structure. In the case of (i) in Eq.
6
, the new structure represents a valid first-order conjunctive query (with anchors
\{v_{a},v_{a}^{\prime}\}
); we can thus directly query the API to check whether
v_{?}
appears in the query answer. In the case of (ii) in Eq.
6
, the new structure does not correspond to a first-order conjunctive query. However, we may switch the roles of anchor and answer entities by considering
v_{a}
as the answer entity and
{
v_{?}
,
v_{?}^{\prime}
}
as the anchor entities. This makes the new structure a valid first-order conjunctive query, which can be readily checked.

IV-D

Bootstrapping

Thus far we assume the adversary knows the public sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
to launch
Kgx
. Here, we lift this assumption and show how to bootstrap the attack.

Instead, we assume the adversary knows a set of public entities in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
. We argue that this assumption is minimal and practical, as large KGs are often built upon multiple sources, including public sources. For instance, Google KG partially inherits Freebase
[
33
]
, a public KG,

Starting with this set of entities as a seed KG
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{pub}}
, we may query the API to reconstruct
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
. Specifically, for each seed
v
, we use it as the anchor, sample a relation
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
, and generate a one-hop query
q(v,v_{?})=v\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r}$}}v_{?}
.
By querying the API with
q
and filtering out entities in
\llbracket q\rrbracket
with confidence scores below a threshold
\lambda
, we add the remaining entities to
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{pub}}
(with relation
r
to
v
). By iteratively executing the above steps, we expand
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{pub}}
to approach
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
. Note that it often suffices for
Kgx
to work that
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{pub}}
covers a fraction (e.g., 50%) of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
(details in §
V
).

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
share a high overall similarity. Note that the absolute numbers of unmatched entities and facts are large-volume. In §
-B
, we provide a detailed analysis of properties on those unmatched entities and facts.

KG properties matter.
Observe that the measures of the same metric vary greatly across different KGs. For instance, the recall of facts ranges from around 0.65 for YAGO to around 0.9 for CyberKG. This phenomenon may be explained by the complexity of the relational structures of different KGs. As shown in Table
II
, CyberKG has 23 relation types, while YAGO has 78. It is relatively easier for
Kgx
to infer the relational structures of CyberKG, compared with the more complex structures of YAGO.

Interpretation on limited performance –

Although the precision or recall are not astonishingly high, given the large size of backend KGs, the attack successfully extracts a large number of entities and relations under a limited query budget. For instance, 70% recall on UMLS corresponds to 613K facts within total 870K facts in private sub-KGs. Moreover, due to the intricate relational structures, it is difficult to extract a perfect copy (e.g., around 100% precision and recall) of KGs, especially with only black-box accessibility. This work provides a proof-of-concept of how such attacks may be feasible in practical settings.

Next, we examine
Kgx
’s design spectrum and explore its best practices under various settings.

Query budget allocation –

By default, we allocate half of the query budget
n_{\mathrm{query}}
to expand surrogate KG

Budget allocation balances precision and recall.
Observe that both
Kgx
’s precision and recall steadily increase as more budget is allocated to expand
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{pub}}

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
improves the chance of generating high-quality queries to probe
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}

{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}

Kgx

Kgx
’s precision and recall (e.g., allocating 30-50% on expanding
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{pub}}

VI

Discussion

Declaring
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
is straightforward to block adversaries from acquiring confidential knowledge while easy to control by KGR developers (also defenders). However, as we have empirically shown in §
V
,
Kgx
with constrained API accessibility and budgets effectively extracts the
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, which implies the limitation of simply using
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
to protect privacy. Next, we study potential countermeasures on top of solely using
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. Finally, we discuss the limitations of this work and outline several directions for future research.

VI-A

Potential countermeasures

Due to the uniqueness of KGR tasks and KG extraction attacks, existing defenses against other privacy attacks
[
45
,
38
]
or in predictive tasks
[
44
,
60
,
57
]
are often inapplicable. Instead, we investigate two potential countermeasures tailored to KGR APIs. Specifically, (i) we may conceal the confidence scores of query answers and further shuffle their rankings to make it challenging for
Kgx
to distinguish true and false answers; (ii) motivated by defenses against model extraction attacks
[
56
]
, we may also inject random noise into the confidence scores of query answers to mislead the adversary. We detail the countermeasures and findings as follows.

Concealing confidence scores –
Recall that the answer
\llbracket q\rrbracket
of a given query
q
is often a ranked list of entities with associated confidence scores. As
Kgx
relies on the ranking and/or confidence information to distinguish true and false answers in probing the private sub-KG, a simple countermeasure is thus to conceal the confidence and shuffle the ranking. Further, we may only return the top-
k
entities in
\llbracket q\rrbracket
. Under this setting, we assume
Kgx
validates path existence using bi-directional queries (details in §
IV-B
).

Figure
14
shows
Kgx
’s performance on CyberKG and Google KG under this setting (with results on other KGs deferred to Figure
18
). Observe that concealing confidence scores has a limited impact on
Kgx
. With a sufficiently large
k
(e.g.,
k\geq 7
),
Kgx
attains fairly high recall (e.g., close to 1.0 in CyberKG) while maintaining acceptable precision (e.g., around 0.74 in CyberKG). This may be explained by that, even without confidence scores, bi-directional querying effectively validates path existence. Further, note that setting a small
k
(e.g.,
k=1
) is not always feasible. Despite its effectiveness against
Kgx
, a small
k
implies a high chance that the true answers are excluded from the query responses, which negatively impacts the utility of KGR APIs.

Distorting confidence scores –
Alternatively, we consider distorting the confidence scores to mislead the adversary. We apply the Laplace mechanism from differential privacy to inject random noise into the confidence scores. Specifically, we sample noise from a Laplace distribution parameterized by
\epsilon
, with a smaller
\epsilon
implying more significant noise.

Figure 15
:
Kgx
and KGR performance on APIs with confidence scores distorted by a Laplace distribution parameterized by
\epsilon
.

Figure
15
illustrates
Kgx
’s performance on CyberKG and Google KG under varying
\epsilon
(results on other KGs deferred to Figure
19
). We also assess the impact of this defense on the performance of KGR APIs, measured by the metrics of mean reciprocal rank (MRR) and HIT@
K
(
K=1,5,10
). Notably, a small
\epsilon
(e.g.,
\epsilon=0.01
) effectively prevents
Kgx
from extracting private sub-KGs. However, it also significantly impacts the KGR performance (e.g., MRR
\leq
0.2 on CyberKG). Apparently, there exists a delicate trade-off between attack robustness and KGR performance.

VI-B

Limitations and Future work

We now discuss the limitations of this work and outline several directions for future research.

Complete KG extraction –
This work focuses on the critical and primary step of KG extraction, uncovering the relational structures of private sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
. To completely re-construct
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{prv}}
, we also need to map the nodes in the extracted sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{prv}}
to concrete entities. However, entity resolution typically requires auxiliary domain knowledge and resources. For instance, the adversary with threat-hunting expertise may explore threat reports to find specific relations between
vulnerability
,
software
, and
mitigation
. Then, by examining the relations between a
vulnerability
node in
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{prv}}
and
software
and
mitigation
entities in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{pub}}
, the adversary may resolve this
vulnerability
node. We consider developing principled methods for entity resolution as a promising research direction.

Alternative reasoning tasks –
This work mainly focuses on KGR with individual entities as answers. There exist other reasoning tasks (e.g., path reasoning
[
62
]
aims to find a logical path with a given pair of starting and ending entities). It is worth exploring the vulnerabilities of such alternative reasoning tasks to KG extraction attacks, and particularly, whether it is possible to extend
Kgx
to such settings.

Defenses by design –
This work considers add-on defenses readily applicable to any KGR APIs without modification. Despite their simplicity, such defenses have apparent limitations. A more effective approach is to treat the robustness against KG extraction attacks as a first-class citizen and to build the attack robustness into the design of KGR systems.

VIII

Conclusion

To sum up, this work represents the first study on the privacy risks of knowledge graph reasoning (KGR). We present
Kgx
, a new class of KG extraction attacks that exploit black-box access to KGR APIs to reconstruct (“steal”) confidential KGs. We demonstrate the practicality of
Kgx
under both experimental and real-world settings, raising concerns about the current practices of training and operating KGR. We also discuss potential countermeasures, which sheds light on applying KGR in a more principled and privacy-aware manner.

A. Details of KGR

Here, we detail the training and reasoning of KGR.

Reasoning –
Representation-based KGR typically consists of two main components:

Embedding function
\phi
– This function maps each entity in the knowledge graph
{\mathcal{G}}
as a latent embedding based on the relational structures within
{\mathcal{G}}
. The entity embedding for entity
v
is denoted as
\phi_{v}
, and the set of entity embeddings for all entities in
{\mathcal{G}}
is denoted as
\phi_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Transformation function
\psi
– This function computes the embedding
\phi_{q}
for a given query
q
. KGR defines a set of transformations, including the projection and intersection operators. The projection operator
\psi_{r}(\phi_{v})
computes the embeddings of entities connected to entity
v
by relation
r
. The intersection operator
\psi_{\wedge}(\phi_{{\mathcal{N}}_{1}},\ldots,\phi_{{\mathcal{N}}_{n}})
computes the embeddings of entities that belong to the intersection of sets of entities
{\mathcal{N}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{N}}_{n}
. Other transformations, such as disjunction and negation, can be converted to the intersection operator
[
49
,
50
]
. These transformation operators are often implemented as trainable neural networks
[
35
]
.

To process query
q
, KGR starts with the query’s anchors
{\mathcal{A}}_{q}
and iteratively applies the transformation function until reaching the entity of interest
v_{?}
, resulting in the query’s embedding
\psi_{q}
. The entities in
{\mathcal{G}}
with embeddings most similar to
\psi_{q}
are then identified as the query answer
\llbracket q\rrbracket
[
34
]
.

Training –
KGR is typically trained in a supervised manner. We sample a set of query-answer pairs
(q,\llbracket q\rrbracket)
from
{\mathcal{G}}
as training data. The objective is to optimize the embedding and transformation functions by minimizing the prediction loss of each query
q
with respect to the ground-truth answer
\llbracket q\rrbracket
.

Threshold of confidence scores

Figure 17

Distribution of confidence scores among positive and negative cases.

Here, we discuss how to empirically set the threshold
\lambda
to distinguish true and false answers. Intuitively, we refer to correct query answers as “positive” cases and denote their score distribution as
{\mathcal{D}}^{+}
. Conversely, incorrect query answers are “negative” cases, and the distribution of their scores is
{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
.

Are positive and negative cases separable?
We launch experiments to evaluate the disparity of
{\mathcal{D}}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
. We sample 10K queries with various structures from different knowledge graphs such as CyberKG, UMLS, and YAGO. We collect
the scores of answers and non-answers as
{\mathcal{D}}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
, respectively. In addition, we perturb the queries by introducing non-existent relations and use these perturbed queries to retrieve answer scores. We augment
{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
with the scores obtained from these perturbed queries. Figure
17
illustrates the distribution of
{\mathcal{D}}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
. The areas under the curves are normalized to 1. Notably,
{\mathcal{D}}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
are mostly separate from each other, indicating the validity of using confidence scores to distinguish true and false answers.

How to set the threshold in
Kgx
?
We first sample a small set of queries from the surrogate KG
{\mathcal{G}}^{*}_{\mathrm{pub}}
. Specifically, we sample 1,000 queries for CyberKG and YAGO, and 2,000 queries for UMLS and Google KG. We then retrieve the positive and negative scores (
{\mathcal{D}}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
) using the same procedure as described earlier.
After obtaining the scores, we establish a threshold
\lambda
that is higher than all the scores in
{\mathcal{D}}^{-}
. This threshold filters all negative cases, even though at the cost of a few positive cases.
