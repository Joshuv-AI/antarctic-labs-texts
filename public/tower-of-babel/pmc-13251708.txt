Clinical perspectives on pediatric weight-management programs: A nationwide look at models of care
Authors: Niharika Alla, Jacqueline Chan, Rushika Conroy
Published: 2026

Background

Obesity is a complex, and often persistent chronic disease associated with serious health and social consequences [1]. Childhood obesity is typically defined as having a BMI ≥95th percentile for age and sex, while severe obesity is defined as a BMI ≥120% of the 95th percentile [1]. In the USA, the CDC reports that about 1 in 5 children and adolescents (ages 2–19) have obesity. More recently, data from 1999 to 2000 to August 2021–August 2023 show a rise in obesity prevalence among youth (ages 2–19) from 13.9% to 21.1%, and an increase in severe obesity from 3.6% to 7.0% [2]. Early referral is emphasized to maximize treatment success, as delays are associated with poorer outcomes [3].

Management involves multidisciplinary teams including pediatricians, dietitians, behavioral specialists, and exercise professionals who deliver intensive behavior and lifestyle treatment. Data looking at the impact of lifestyle modification on pediatric obesity suggest that the most effective programs provide ≥26 hours of face-to-face, family-based, multicomponent treatment over 3–12 months [1]. Data looking at the most effective visit structure for pediatric patients receiving both lifestyle modification and medication has not been reported. Attrition remains a common challenge; a tertiary care pediatric hospital reported a 37% dropout rate over 5 years in their lifestyle intervention program [2,4].

A report on pediatric obesity management by pediatric endocrinologists who are members of the Pediatric Endocrine Society was conducted in 2020. Data indicate variability in practice patterns: most providers saw 5–10 patients with obesity per week, had limited use of pharmacotherapy, and over half referred patients for bariatric surgery when indicated. Access to multidisciplinary programs and bariatric surgery varied by region and institution [5]. Among practitioners using pharmacological interventions, therapeutic strategies varied widely [5]. As the prevalence of obesity has increased considerably and the treatment options have expanded, a fresh look at current practices among providers who treat pediatric patients with obesity is warranted. With this information, we can work toward establishing guidelines across institutions with respect to optimal pediatric obesity treatment.

Purpose and objectives of the study

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the structure, staffing, services, and clinical practices of weight management programs across healthcare settings among pediatric providers who treat patients with obesity. The objective is to identify variations in care models, treatment approaches, and patient engagement strategies to inform best practices and improve obesity care delivery. Specifically, we sought information on a) staffing and interdisciplinary team composition, b)clinical services and interventions offered, c) screening and intake processes, d) program eligibility and enrollment criteria, e) frequency and mode of follow-up, f) laboratory and diagnostic monitoring, g) screening practices for mental health and eating disorders, h) genetic and sleep disorder assessments, i) patient education and accessibility measures, j) operational logistics and referral management, k) financial and billing models and l) transition of care strategies.

Methods

Survey questions were developed among a small group of pediatric endocrinologists who are active members of the Pediatric Endocrine Society (PES) Obesity Special Interest Group (SIG). Final questions were put into REDCap as a structured REDCap survey consisting of 51 primary questions with conditional branching logic and follow-up sub questions based on participant responses. The survey was distributed to members of the PES Obesity SIG, as well as the pediatric committee of the Obesity Medicine Association and members of the pediatric obesity interest group list serve. The survey data was collected between September 2025 to January 2026. Among these members, we included those who treat pediatric patients with obesity and excluded those who do not. The estimated total number of providers is 168. Multiple responses from the same clinic were not allowed. This was a cross-sectional review of 38 clinics. We report purely descriptive statistics (mean/median/IQR).

Results

Clinical Overview

A total of 38 physicians responded to the survey. 61% of weight management programs operate within the pediatric endocrine division, while the remaining 39% operate elsewhere (e.g. within general pediatrics, pediatric gastroenterology, surgery, or as stand-alone multidisciplinary weight centers). The programs were categorized as large (34%) that see >300 new patients per year; medium (42%) that see 100–300 new patients per year; and small (24%) that see fewer than 100 new patients per year. Large programs tend to report having a dedicated web page for their weight management clinic and offering bariatric surgery as a treatment option.

61% of programs are engaged in clinical research related to obesity. Larger academic centers were more likely to be research-active than small community programs. Research was mostly grant funded (62%), with 14% of research being industry sponsored and 14% being either internally funded or not funded at all. Studies include pharmaceutical sponsored medication trials as well as registry collections.

68% of programs bill the visit under the physician's services. 24% use hospital facility charges or a program service fee in addition to or instead of physician billing (more common in hospital-based multidisciplinary programs). 5% operate as unbilled or free programs, supported by hospital funds or philanthropy (mostly pilot programs or those with grant funding). 5% of respondents were unsure of the exact billing structure.

Among programs sending forms, most expect families to return them before or at the visit; 27% require the forms to be completed prior to scheduling the visit. In 12 programs, the provider reviews it during or just prior to the visit itself. Only 5 programs have the provider review forms ahead of the visit. In those instances, if an intake form or referral note flags an urgent concern, 1 program indicates that their staff will reach out to the primary care provider or family prior to the visit.

The survey investigated whether programs have formal referral criteria. 79% of providers report that their program requires patients to have a minimum BMI percentile to enroll. This was set at ≥95th percentile in 62% programs, and ≥85% percentile in 22% programs. 13% of programs require HbA1c levels to be in the prediabetes or diabetes ranges. 87% have no lab-based entry requirements. 24% of programs require the presence of obesity-related comorbidities for entry (or will give priority to those cases).76% of programs do not require patients to have a comorbidity for entry into their program. The remainder have a combination of BMI and comorbidity criteria.

Half of the programs have defined age criteria for participation. The minimum age for participation varies widely, from 2 years to 12 years, with median minimum age reported to be 5 years old. Of the 50% respondents, 33.4% programs treat patients through age 18 years, while 22% continue follow-up until age 21 years if the patient began the program as a teenager, essentially bridging adult care. One program indicated an upper limit of 25 years for special cases (e.g., young adults in an “adolescent” program). 22% did not report a maximum age.

Obesity care and management practices

The most common follow up frequency interval reported is every 3 months. A subset of programs (particularly those with intensive lifestyle interventions) see patients every 4–8 weeks. 18% reported monthly follow-ups initially, especially for those starting medication or needing close monitoring. Another 15% use every 2 months as a standard. Some less intensive programs or those with capacity issues schedule every 6 months by default, and 10% indicated a semi-annual follow up for stable patients. When asked how often providers prefer to see patients, 47% answered that monthly follow-ups would be ideal, while 15% said every 2 months might be ideal. 28% thought that every 3 months is ideal.

Providers reported that 60–75% of their visits are in-person, with 25–40% via telehealth. 89% of programs offer telehealth visits in some form. Among them, 95% offer provider visits, 90% offer dietitian visits, 80% offer behavioral health counseling, and 50–60% offer fitness trainer visits as telehealth options.

24% of providers note that their programs offer group visits as part of their care model. Most support groups are led by behavioral health professionals. The programs that offer group visits tend to be larger academic centers and/or those with behavioral health staff. Telehealth group sessions were common in the group-visit model. Content often focuses on education and skill-building. Types of group sessions offered include cooking classes, grocery store tours, exercise classes, parent support groups, group therapy sessions for teens, and peer support/education workshops. Group visits are generally optional; attendance can be low, and some clinics have struggled to get consistent participation. Those with successful group visits often have dedicated funding or personnel to run them.

50% of clinics offer other ancillary services beyond the core team. All programs surveyed ensure that patients see a dietitian as part of the program. The dietitian is integrated into clinic visits or scheduled separately. Visits with the dietitian are more frequent at the start (e.g., every 2–4 weeks for a few sessions) then taper. For programs with a psychologist or social worker as part of care, visits are often on an as-needed basis. 70% of these offer individualized scheduling for follow ups, while 3 programs provided monthly visits; 1 program had a set 3-month interval between visits and 1 a 6-month interval; 58% have the behavioral health provider do an initial evaluation and then schedule follow-up if issues are identified or if the family is interested. Follow up frequency varies for visits with a physical therapist or exercise specialist. Only 1 program reported 3-month follow up intervals, while others are non-standardized based on family engagement. Seven programs said patients see another type of specialist as part of the program, including pediatric endocrinology, sleep medicine, gastroenterology, orthopedics or sports medicine.

95% of providers report obtaining laboratory tests prior to starting medication for weight loss. Only 2 providers said they do not routinely do labs before starting medication, but instead order labs as needed, and 4 said they do not routinely repeat labs for medication monitoring (Table 4). 89% of programs perform routine lab monitoring, with normal labs being repeated annually and abnormal labs being repeated every 3–6 months by 71% of respondents.

84% of providers report that eating disorder screening is done in their weight management program. Typically, screening starts for patients age 12 years and tools such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q), Binge Eating Disorder Screener-7 (BED7) and Sick Control One stone Fat Food (SCOFF) are used to screen for depression, binge eating and disordered eating behaviors.

Monogenic obesity testing is offered by 40% of respondents. Testing is done for patients with early-onset obesity and hyperphagia. Testing is done through sponsored panels or based on insurance preferences.

Sleep apnea screening is done by all programs with a Snoring Tiredness Observed Pressure BMI Age Neck Gender (STOP BANG) or modified STOP BANG depending on the age of the patient. Positive screens result in a referral to sleep medicine or a sleep study.

89% of programs give educational handouts or materials to patients and families. All use materials from sources such as government or professional organization websites, including MyPlate.gov, American Academy of Pediatrics, CDC, or specialist groups (e.g., pediatric obesity guidelines). Half of the programs create custom handouts within their practice. 31.6% adapt handouts from colleagues or other institutions. Two programs indicated other sources like commercial patient education vendors or apps. 15% of programs give a packet of core handouts at the initial visit, akin to a starter kit with information on general nutrition, physical activity and behavior modification tactics. Additional materials are given later if needed. 10% of programs have a structured schedule for handouts to ensure coverage of topics systematically. 47% noted translated handouts in the predominant non-English language of their patient population; 39% of programs indicated they provide culturally tailored nutrition guidance for families.

Clinic scheduling and operations

In 21% of programs, a clinic physician or provider reviews referrals before the patient is scheduled. In 52% of programs, a clinic nurse or administrative coordinator screens referrals initially. 26% reported that referrals are not specifically reviewed. In 18% of programs, after referral is placed, families are instructed to call and schedule the appointment themselves, while in 60%, clinic staff call the family to schedule an initial visit once the referral is received. Many programs have adopted this to reduce no-shows/new appointments with no uptake. A smaller number (8%) indicated that primary care providers’ offices schedule the visit at the time of referral. Two programs have group orientation sessions scheduled before an initial visit is booked.

61% of programs have a defined no-show policy, while the rest do not have a formal policy beyond the standard affiliated medical center policy. A common approach is giving families a warning letter after the first and second no-show visits and potentially not rescheduling after a third. A few (particularly bariatric surgery programs) impose a no-show fee for missed appointments.

Fourteen providers reported a no-show rate in their program of 11–20%. No provider reported >50% no-show rate. 26% of clinics double-book or overbook slots in anticipation of no-shows. The remaining 74% do not routinely double-book, either due to hospital policy, variable no-show rates, or lack of capacity to handle two patients if they both show. Many pediatric providers prefer not to overbook to avoid long waiting times for those who do arrive. Some providers noted they will overbook specific situations, for example, if historically many new patients cancel or no-show, they may book a follow up and initial visit in the same slot.

48% of programs have support for patients who frequently miss appointments. The majority do not have a formal support plan beyond standard reminder calls. Seventeen providers rely on social workers to reach out to families with repeated no-shows. The social worker will attempt to identify barriers (transportation issues, scheduling conflicts, family stresses) and help solve them or reschedule the patient at a better time. Clinics have front desk representative clinic coordinators who make follow-up calls after a missed appointment to address barriers and re-motivate the family. However, many clinics lack resources for intensive outreach and thus simply continue standard care for those who return. Only 15 programs reported having a program coordinator.

With respect to visit durations, 56% of programs schedule 60 minutes for a new patient visit with the physician or APP, 33% schedule 45 minutes and 8% have 30-minute new patient visits, relying on other team members to gather pertinent history. Two percent of programs book 75 minutes for complex new visits (especially if multiple team members see the patient in that period). 64% of programs have 30-minute follow-ups. 18% have shorter 15-minute follow-up slots; these tend to be high-volume clinics or those focusing on quick check-ins. 18% use 45-minute follow-ups, particularly if the physician and dietitian see the patient in the same visit. 10% use 60-minute follow-ups for very complex cases or if combining multidisciplinary visits in one slot.

Only 13% of programs reported having a formal process to transition patients back to primary care (or to adult care) once they finish the pediatric weight management program. The rest may handle it ad hoc or not at all (some patients simply stop coming or age out without a special protocol). Those with a process typically include a structured handoff where a summary letter or call is provided to the primary care provider detailing the patient's progress, treatments, and recommendations for ongoing care. 16.7% ensure that the PCP is updated when they discharge the patient from the program. If the patient is aging out of pediatric weight management but still needs intensive management, programs will refer to an adult weight management clinic (if available in their system) or to an adult bariatric center if surgery is indicated. If there is no adult obesity program in the area, they notify the PCP to monitor weight and comorbidities. Only 9 providers transition outpatients when they age out of the program. Reasons include BMI out of obesity range (32%), patient stable on obesity treatment regimen (26%), patient not willing to continue treatment (16%), and when goals are achieved (25%).

Discussion

This national survey of 38 pediatric weight management programs reveals substantial variability in program structure, clinical practices, and resource availability, yet several consistent themes emerge. The findings highlight both the strengths of current models and opportunities to standardize and optimize care delivery. The most notable differences were in staffing across clinics, gaps between preferred to currently scheduled follow up frequency and lack of formal transition process from weight management clinics.

Program structure and capacity

Programs vary widely in size, with one-third seeing more than 300 new patients annually. Larger programs are more likely to offer comprehensive services, including bariatric surgery and dedicated clinic webpages, suggesting that breadth of services may drive higher patient volume.

Staffing

Nearly all programs include physicians and dietitians, but behavioral health, social work, and exercise specialists are inconsistently available. Although AAP guidelines from 2023 mention “Comprehensive treatment can include nutrition support, physical activity treatment, behavioral therapy, pharmacotherapy, and metabolic and bariatric surgery”, it does not clearly specify the ancillary services required [6]. This may reflect cost or a gap between staff availability and demand, as larger volume practices have more ancillary services than smaller volume ones.

Age range and entry criteria

Minimum age varies from toddlerhood to adolescence, with a median of 5 years. Most programs require BMI ≥95th percentile for entry, with some requiring comorbidities or abnormal labs to be referred. This may reflect provider capacity and a preference to see patients with more severe disease, as the need for treatment may be more urgent.

Pharmacotherapy practices

Pharmacotherapy is universal. Our survey excluded those who do not treat pediatric patients with obesity, so this finding is not unexpected. Programs demonstrate many safety practices. Most programs had formal consent for medications. This underscores the careful approach to pediatric pharmacotherapy in obesity and the increase in FDA-approved medication options for pediatric patients over the last few years. Off-label use of medication continues, which is not uncommon for those who treat pediatric patients. The 10% of providers who used off-label compounded semaglutide for their pediatric patients was surprising. Compounded semaglutide is a formulation of semaglutide that is neither approved nor regulated by the FDA. Numerous societies have discouraged its use for reasons including lack of consistency in medication ingredients and doses, leading to user hospitalization or severe illness [7].

Research engagement

61% of programs conduct research, mostly relying on grant funding, revealing promising trends of growing interest in research participation.

Operational practices and intake processes

Only 39% of programs use pre-visit intake forms, despite their value in streamlining visits. Such forms could benefit all programs, with modifications to content and method of delivery based on the patient population.

Frequency and modality

Most programs schedule quarterly visits, though half of providers believe monthly visits are ideal. Telehealth is widely used, with many programs offering virtual dietitian and behavioral health visits. The gap between provider preferences on actual and ideal follow up intervals highlights the divide between clinical best practice and real-world capacity.

Group visits and support services

Group visits and support groups remain underutilized. Programs that offer them report benefits in education and peer support. There was a relative lack of support groups in most programs that may reflect resource limitations. Implementing group visits in a way that is revenue generating can not only improve access but also enhance visits, as patients receive individualized attention, education and support from peers.

Laboratory and comorbidity screening

Baseline and ongoing lab monitoring is nearly universal, with 95% obtaining labs before medication initiation and 89% monitoring during treatment. As we gather more data on weight outcomes with newer medications, consideration of monitoring vitamins, protein and mineral levels, as is done in post bariatric surgery patients, may be needed. Pregnancy tests were not done as routine monitoring and were done by 19% prior to starting medication. As we do not have data on reproductive outcomes on most of the pharmacotherapy medications, pregnancy testing could be considered in post menarchal adolescent cis females or trans males [8].

Educational materials and cultural tailoring

Most programs (90%) provide educational handouts, and only 39% offer culturally tailored nutrition guidance. The gap in culturally tailored guidance highlights a potential limitation in addressing health behaviors and outcomes across different populations. Making such documents shareable across programs would increase efficiency and address an unmet need.

Scheduling, No show management, and outreach

No show rates average 11–20%. Only half of programs have a formal no show policy, and 48% have outreach systems for frequent no shows. Social work involvement is common in these efforts. Missed appointments remain a notable challenge, yet structured policies and consistent follow-up approaches are not universally in place. Although social work involvement is common, the absence of standardized strategies may hinder sustained patient engagement and continuity of care. In addition, the ethics of refusing to see a patient for treatment in a location where there are no other places for them to seek treatment comes into play. There is a difficult balance to achieve between what is best for the patient and what is best for the program to thrive.

Visit duration and workflow

Most programs allocate 60 min for new medical visits and 45–60 min for initial dietitian visits. Follow up durations vary widely. Longer visits allow for more in-depth discussion, education and treatment planning, but productivity and access may lead to shorter visits.

Transition of care

Only 13% of programs have a formal transition process, despite the chronic nature of obesity. Programs with processes typically provide a summary to the PCP and prepare families for adult care. Transitioning patients back to the PCP in a manner that allows the patient to continue medication to maintain weight loss is critical, as this allows weight management providers to see more patients for obesity treatment. Without a transition out of the specialty clinic, appointment slots fill, and new patients may experience significant treatment delays. However, to safely transition patients back to the PCP, the PCP needs to be comfortable with continuing treatment. This may involve education regarding how the medication is prescribed, insurance hurdles, how often to monitor, when/if labs are needed, and when to re-refer. Access to nutrition support, whether through dietitians or online classes or information, will be important to support patients who are working on weight loss maintenance.

Transitioning patients to adult obesity medicine physicians can be difficult if geographic access to such a provider is limited. A number of online weight loss programs have been developed, and patients may find such a mode of treatment a useful option. Adult primary care providers may be comfortable with managing weight loss medications for their patients, in which case the transition may be less difficult. The AAP guidelines also encourage having a transition of care plan given the complexity and chronicity of the disease but do not provide specifics on what this looks like or how it can be implemented [9]. These findings suggest a gap in continuity of care, as relatively few programs report having structured transition processes despite the chronic nature of obesity.

Limitations

The inferences made from the survey results must be interpreted within the context of its scale. With 38 responding programs, the dataset provides valuable insights but cannot fully capture the diversity of pediatric weight management services across the country. Other limitations exist. Findings are based on self-reported survey data and may be subject to reporting bias. The cross-sectional design captures practices at a single time point without assessing changes over time. Clinical outcomes were not evaluated, limiting the ability to link practices with effectiveness. Future studies may want to expand on our findings and address questions on demographics of the clinic or focus further into pieces of our survey such as intake form content, patient payor mix, appointment access and wait times, Electronic Health Record Systems and social determinants of health.

Conclusion

This survey provides a detailed snapshot of current practices within pediatric weight management programs, highlighting both the strengths and the substantial variability that characterize the field. The findings demonstrate that while many clinics share core elements—such as multidisciplinary care, routine comorbidity screening, and near universal use of pharmacotherapy, there remains divergence in program structure, staffing, visit frequency, intake processes, and operational workflows. These differences reflect the various environments in which programs operate but also underscore the absence of standardized models for delivering pediatric obesity care.

Larger, more representative surveys are needed to validate these findings, identify regional or institutional differences, and establish benchmarks for best practices. Expanding the scope of future surveys would also allow for deeper exploration of emerging issues such as equity, cultural tailoring, long-term outcomes, and integration with community resources. Ultimately, this survey is an important foundation, but broader national efforts are essential to guide standardization, inform policy, and support the development of high-quality, accessible pediatric obesity care.
