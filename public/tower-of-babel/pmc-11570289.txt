Balancing academics and athletics: Examining academic and athletic identity profiles in a large sample of NCAA Division I college athletes
Authors: Mariya A Yukhymenko-Lescroart
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCollege athletes balance academic and athletic roles and, as a result, can hold different combinations of academic and athletic identities. The purpose of this study was to identify common identity profiles in a large sample of Division I (elite) college athletes in the U.S. and to examine these profiles on a number of measures in academics and athletics: performance, motivation (e.g., achievement goals, autonomous motivation, competence, interest, effort, value, persistence, passion), satisfaction, and ethical conduct (e.g., cheating, plagiarism, sportspersonship, gamesmanship, aggression). Profiles were derived in Study 1 (N = 1124) and cross-validated with an independent sample in Study 2 (N = 184). Results showed four common identity profiles: (1) fully engaged college athletes with the highest academic and athletic identities, (2) committed athletes with a low academic identity and a high athletic identity, (3) academics-focused college athletes with an average academic identity and a low athletic identity, and (4) academically unengaged athletes with an extremely low academic identity and a low athletic identity. The identity profiles differed significantly across the measures of academic and athletic performance, achievement motivation (mastery, performance, task, and ego goals), satisfaction, and ethical conduct (academic misconduct, homework cheating, plagiarism, test cheating, sportspersonship, gamesmanship, instrumental aggression) in Study 1, as well as autonomous motivation (competence, relatedness, value/usefulness, effort/importance), academic persistence, and athletic passion (harmonious, obsessive) in Study 2. Taken together, findings provided evidence of both the multidimensional nature of college athletes’ identities and the heterogeneity of the college athlete population. Overall, these findings point to the importance of concurrent examination of academic and athletic identities as separate yet interrelated dimensions in research studies. This approach allows for a comprehensive understanding of college athletes' experiences and provides a more nuanced perspective on their identity development.Keywords: Elite athletes, College athletes, Student-athletes, Cluster analysis, Athlete identity, Athletic identity, Student identity, Academic identity, Academic performance, Profile analysis

Identity is a multifaceted construct that is conceptualized in term of social engagements and connections among individuals [1]. Because individuals can belong to multiple social groups, they may hold multiple identities. College athletes face the challenges of balancing requirements and responsibilities simultaneously in two social domains: academic and athletic, which constitute the two primary and dominant social contexts, within which their identities develop and are maintained. As college athletes spend time pursuing their goals and aspirations in these two domains, they develop and maintain identities of being college students and college athletes. Yukhymenko-Lescroart et al. (p. 4) [2] defined these identities as the extents to which being a student and being an athlete are central to college athletes’ sense of self. The relationship between these two identities have been described as competitive, especially for college athletes who experience pressures for sport performance [[3], [4], [5]]. The pressures for sport performance are especially high at universities that compete at the highest competitive level. In the United States, the highest and most competitive level of intercollegiate college athletics is Division I sanctioned by the National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA). It is characterized by the most scholarships and grants for athletes, large budgets, high revenues, elaborate athletic facilities, and national media coverage, especially of high-profile sports. Because many college athletes join Division I universities to play their sport, they start college with a strong athletic identity; whereas their academic identities often lag behind [6]. The strong athletic identities often inhibit academic performance, progress towards degree, and career readiness among college athletes.

There is a growing body of literature that focuses on the relationships between the athletic passions and academic obligations of college athletes, particularly at the Division I level. A general consensus in qualitative studies is that college athletes tend to report a strong athletic identity that competes with and often dominates over their academic identity [[3], [4], [5]] and, as a result, are likely to underperform academically. Quantitative studies also support the notion of the inhibiting role of athletic identity on academic success. For example, studies have shown that the more central the role of athletic participation takes for college athletes, the lower their academic performance [[7], [8], [9]], effort [10], and cognitive abilities [11]. Yet, most of the studies that examined cross-domain inferences of athletic identity on academic success and vice versa have utilized a variable-centered approach, which does not take into account heterogeneity in college athletes’ identifications with academic and athletic roles. Further, many of these studies only focused on one identity without considering the other or only examined the relevant importance of athletic identity over academic identity, thus, investigating the role of academic and athletic identities individually on performance and well-being college athletes.

Limited attention has been given to utilizing person-centered analytical techniques, such as cluster analysis, to identify distinct groups of college athletes by considering unique combinations of academic and athletic identities in college athletes. Because of the multidimensional nature of identity in college athletes, cluster analysis may provide useful insights for exploring the relationship between college athletes’ identity, performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct in academics and athletics. College athletes who perform successfully and are satisfied with their college experiences are more likely to persist with their educational aspirations at their university and, thus, are more likely to be successful during the post-college transition out of their college athlete roles. Therefore, of particular interest was examining the heterogeneity of how college athletes perform in academics and athletics, and the extent to which they are motivated in academics and athletics, satisfied with their academic and athletic experiences, and conduct themselves ethically in classrooms and sport fields by identifying and investigating identity profiles of Division I college athletes based on their academic and athletic identities.

The context of NCAA Division I college athletics in the United States

Division I college athletes are subject to specific regulations outlined by the NCAA [12], governing their eligibility, academic progress, amateurism, and overall collegiate experience. These regulations reflect the unique approach and traditions within NCAA college athletics in the U.S., setting the distinct context for Division I college sports. Over 350 institutions are currently classified as full Division I members, participating in NCAA-run championships across 26 different sports, 13 men's and 13 women's sports.

The NCAA places clear priorities on Division I, with a primary emphasis on education for college athletes. Division I institutions maintain a commitment to amateurism, providing athletics programs as part of the students’ educational journey. This differentiation from professional athletes is established through NCAA bylaws, creating a clear line of demarcation between NCAA college athletes, who are termed by the NCAA “student-athletes,” and professional athletes. Consequently, Division I college athletes are not considered “dual career” athletes under NCAA regulations [12].

Division I college athletes under NCAA regulations are typically not considered "dual career" athletes because NCAA rules emphasize the amateur status of student-athletes [12,13]. According to NCAA regulations, athletes must maintain their amateur status to be eligible to compete in college sports [13]. This contrasts with other contexts, such as in Europe, where "dual career" typically refers to athletes balancing their sports careers with professional or academic careers outside of sports, as outlined in the EU Guidelines on Dual Careers of Athletes [14]. Therefore, within the NCAA framework, the term "dual career" does not typically apply to college athletes due to their amateur status requirements.

Within the NCAA Division I context, strict rules govern athletics eligibility, academic eligibility, playing and practice seasons, and the number of competitions per sport. Fulfilling eligibility requirements is crucial for Division I college athletes to participate in regular-season competitions, NCAA championships, and post-season games. The five-year rule limits participation to a maximum of five consecutive years in their respective sports. Additionally, college athletes must be full-time students, maintain good academic standing, and make annual progress towards their degree to represent their Division I institution in intercollegiate competitions. Athletically related activities are limited to a maximum of 4 hours per day and 20 hours per week. Some Division I college athletes may receive athletic scholarships but be ineligible to compete for one academic year, commonly known as redshirting, due to voluntary, medical, or academic reasons [12].

Despite the educational priorities set by the NCAA [12], many Division I college athletes are deeply committed to their athletic pursuits. This dedication stems from the fact that only a small percentage of highly invested high school students (approximately 2.5 %) in these sports progress to compete at the Division I level [15]. However, after college, the majority of Division I college athletes face retirement from competitive sports in their early 20s, as very few continue their athletic careers at the professional level. For example, the NCAA [15] estimates that only about 1.2 % of Division I men's basketball participants and 0.8 % of women's basketball participants pursue professional sports following graduation. This constant tension between academic and athletic commitments poses a significant challenge for Division I college athletes as they navigate the delicate balance between their academic and athletic identities.

The relationship between academic and athletic identity

In qualitative studies (e.g., Refs. [[3], [4], [5]]), the relationships between the academic and athletic identities of college athletes have been primarily described as competitive. These studies have suggested that college athletes are unable to fully commit to either academic or athletic roles and, therefore, are forced to constantly negotiate and compromise between the two. It was also suggested that athletic identity tends to dominate academic identity in college athletes (e.g., Refs. [3,5]). Lally and Kerr [6], for example, interviewed eight full-time students in their fourth and fifth year at a large institution in Canada and reported that college athletes recognized that their athletic experiences had an impact on their career plans, especially during the early college years as these years tend to be characterized by high athletic involvement. College athletes reported that an almost exclusive investment in their athletic roles during early college years prevented them from pursuing non-athletic careers. However, as they engaged with their studies, the exclusivity of athletic identity diminished and their academic identity strengthened, leading them to explore non-sport career options.

Almost four decades ago, Snyder [16] proposed a typology of college athletes based on their commitments to academic and athletic roles, which he posited to be either strong or weak. Thus, he speculated that there are four main types of college athletes based on the different combinations in weak or strong academic and athletic roles: scholar-athletes, pure scholars, pure athletes, and neither. The first type of college athletes, scholar-athletes, were described as highly committed to both academic and athletic roles. Pure scholars are college athletes who are predominantly committed to their academic roles but non-committed to their athletic roles. In contrast, pure athletes are college athletes who are predominantly committed to their athletic roles but not to their academic roles. Finally, the fourth type are college athletes who do not commit to any of these roles. While this theoretically proposed typology was not empirically derived, it was applied empirically in a study by Marx et al. [17]. Specifically, they used a single bipolar ordinal-scale item that read, “How do you want to be remembered for your time in college?” with five response options: “1 = almost completely as an athlete,” “2 = mostly as an athlete,” “3 = equally as an athlete and as a student,” “4 = mostly as a student,” and “5 = almost completely as a student.” Notably, this item did not allow for deriving a group that did not identified with either of these two roles, as well as for assessing the extent to which college athletes, who indicated that they wanted to be remembered equally as an athlete and as a student, identified with each of the roles. Results showed that none of the 128 Division I college athletes indicated that they wanted to be remembered almost completely as a student, and only five wanted to be remembered mostly as a student. Therefore, only three common categories were formed: “student-athletes” which were conceptualized as college athletes with a strong commitment to both roles, “mostly athletes,” and “pure athletes.”

Finally, Killeya-Jones [18] examined the discrepancy between the roles of student and athlete in a sample of 40 Division I male college athletes, forming four identity structures: convergent, overlapping, disjunctive, and residual. Greater convergence between the two roles was associated with a more positive elaboration of the student role. In other words, college athletes who valued their academic identity were more likely to show greater convergence between the roles of student and athlete. However, valuing athletic identity was not associated with the degree of role discrepancy. These results suggested that some college athletes are able to harmoniously synthesize or compartmentalize academic and athletic identities without experiencing any conflict between them. In contrast, college athletes who viewed the two identities as dissimilar tended to neglect or de-emphasize one of these roles.

Identity, performance, and satisfaction of college athletes

In the studies reviewed above, the relationship between academic and athletic identities was shown to be associated with college athletes’ performance and satisfaction. For example, Marx et al. [17] reported that the more that Division I college athletes wanted to be remembered for their time in college as a student, the higher they ranked in their graduating class in high school and the higher they scored on their college grade point average. Results of Killeya-Jones [18] revealed that the greater the discrepancy was between the roles of student and athlete, the lower level of academic satisfaction was indicated by Division I college athletes. However, athletic satisfaction was unrelated to the discrepancy between the roles of student and athlete.

Similar results were observed in more recent studies that contrasted athletic identity to academic identity in studies that focused on performance and satisfaction of college athletes. For example, Beron and Piquero [7] utilized a sample of about 13,800 Division I college athletes and found that college athletes who saw themselves more as athletes than students reported lower grade point averages. Antshel et al. [11], who used a sample of almost 20,000 college athletes from all three NCAA divisions, found that college athletes who identified themselves as athletes experienced greater difficulties thinking or concentrating when compared to college athletes who identified as students. Notably, in Antshel et al.’s study [11], the strength of athletic identity was measured relative to the strength of academic identity with an item that reads, “How much do you agree or disagree with the following statement: I view myself as more of an athlete than as a student?” with a 6-point Likert-type response options anchored by 1 (strongly disagree) and 6 (strongly agree). Overall, these studies have expanded our knowledge on the relationships between identity, performance, and satisfaction of college athletes. However, these studies contrasted athletic identity to academic identity instead of assessing both of these identities simultaneously. Because the academic and athletic identities were measured in relation to each other, such as if one identity was reported to be strong, the other identity was assumed to be weak, these studies assumed that one identity precedes over the other and did not allow for examining the extent to which each of these identities is central to the sense of self.

More recently, Yukhymenko-Lescroart developed and validated the Academic and Athletic Identity Scale (AAIS) to measure the centrality of academics and athletics to the sense of self in college athletes [19] and examined the relationship between college athletes’ identities and various outcomes in several subsequent studies with Division I student-athletes, utilizing variable-centered approaches [[8], [9], [10]]. The great advantage of using the AAIS is that it consists of two discreet subscales, academic identity and athletic identity, and a high score on one identity does not preclude a high score on the other identity. Collectively, studies that utilized the AAIS have shown cross-domain spillover effects of identity on achievement and performance of college athletes. Specifically, results indicated that Division I college athletes reported high grade point average (GPA) when their academic identity was strong and their athletic identity was weak [8,9]. In contrast, Division I college athletes with a strong athletic identity reported lower effort towards their studies [10], but also lower intentions to withdraw from college [2].

Notably, Steele et al. [20] conducted a systematic literature review focusing exclusively on the academic and athletic identity development of student-athletes, summarizing the results from 42 studies. They reported that academic identities tend to be less important to college athletes than athletic identities, and that the more congruent the two roles are, the more adjusted college athletes are. Similarly, a meta-analytic investigation of academic performance of U.S. college athletes, which was based on 73 studies (the majority of the analyzed studies (72.6 %) were centered on NCAA Division I college students, although the overall sample also encompassed other U.S. college athletes) and 79 unique samples with a combined sample size of 43,617, revealed academic identity as a positive correlate and athletic identity as a negative correlate of GPA [21]. More importantly, role negotiation also emerged as a positive correlate of GPA, suggesting that the ability to successfully balance the two identities, academic and athletic, is beneficial for academic achievement of college athletes in the U.S. Aligned with these findings of variable-centered analyses, O'Neil et al. [22] utilized person-centered analytical technique to derive four profiles of Division I college athletes based on the measures of enthusiastic and constrained commitment in academics and athletics. Results showed that college athletes with enthusiastic-dominant commitments to both academics and athletics reported the highest levels of academic and athletic engagement, as well as the lowest levels of academic and athletic burnout. Thus, the growing body of literature is pointing to the plausibility that Division I college athletes who strongly and equally identify with both roles of student and athlete are likely to be most adjusted to college and post-college life.

The present study

College athletes appear to be a heterogeneous group with several distinct homogenous subgroups that differ based on their academic and athletic identities and, as a result, on academic and athletic performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct. Existing studies have highlighted that different identity profiles exist depending on college athletes' identification with and commitments to their sports and studies, providing preliminary insights into possible identity profiles. Yet, common identity profiles have not yet been explored in a large sample of Division I college athletes while simultaneously assessing both academic and athletic identities as two separate constructs. Doing so would allow for deriving profiles that differ not only in terms of the centrality of one identity relative to the other but also in terms of the extent to which each identity is central to the sense of self. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to examine common identity profiles of Division I college athletes based on their academic and athletic identities, and to compare the groups on performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct in academics and athletics. These particular outcomes are relevant because they have important practical implications for college athletes. This is because college athletes’ performance in academics and athletics, motivation in academics and athletics, satisfaction with their academic and athletic experiences, as well as ethical conduct in classrooms and on sport fields are likely to affect their commitment to the university and, as a result, can be linked with their retention and graduation. The specific research questions were.

1.What are common identity profiles of Division I college athletes based on their academic and athletic identity?

What are common identity profiles of Division I college athletes based on their academic and athletic identity?

2.What is the composition of these identity profiles in terms of background characteristics of Division I college athletes, such as gender, race/ethnicity, reasons for attending college, first-generation, athletic recruitment, athletic scholarship, institution type, and residency?

What is the composition of these identity profiles in terms of background characteristics of Division I college athletes, such as gender, race/ethnicity, reasons for attending college, first-generation, athletic recruitment, athletic scholarship, institution type, and residency?

3.To what extent do Division I college athletes in the identified identity profiles differ on their performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct in academics and athletics?

To what extent do Division I college athletes in the identified identity profiles differ on their performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct in academics and athletics?

With these questions in mind, it was hypothesized that common identity profiles would emerge based on the different extents to which academic and athletic identities are central to the sense of self of college athletes. Based on existing literature [7,17,18,20], it was expected that groups would be composed of college athletes for whom both student and athlete identities are central and for whom athletic identity is more central than academic identity. It was also hypothesized that profiles would also differ in the extent to which these identities are central to college athletes’ sense of self. Additionally, aligned with the literature that focused on the relationships between identity, performance, motivation, and satisfaction [7,18,[20], [21], [22]], it was hypothesized that college athletes belonging to the identity profile with a strong and harmonious identification with both academic and athletic roles would be the most adjusted to their college lives and report high performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct in academics and athletics. Conversely, college athletes with weak identifications with academic and athletic roles would be least adjusted and report low academic performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct in academics and athletics. Finally, college athletes with a dominant and competing relationship of athletic identity over academic identity would be least adjusted to academics and report relatively low academic performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct but relatively high athletic performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct. Profiles were derived in Study 1 and validated in Study 2 to assess the correspondence of the results with an independent sample.

Study 1 method

Participants

The data for this study came from an existing dataset of NCAA Division I college athletes in the United States [9,23,40]. The original survey used in Yukhymenko-Lescroart [9,23] included a number of variables of identity, motivation, and ethical conduct. For the purposes of this study, only the measures assessing identity, performance, and satisfaction were included. Because the purpose was to identify common identity profiles, the data were screened for outliers using a criterion of |z| > 3.0 for academic and athletic identity. Of the 1151 participants in the original study, six indicated extremely low academic identity (min = 1.83, max = 2.17, M = 2.03, SD = 0.16) and 22 indicated extremely low athletic identity (min = 1.17, max = 3.33, M = 2.84, SD = 0.57). These 27 outliers were excluded from further analyses (one case had low scores on both variables). Thus, the sample in the current study included 1124 (55.6 % male) college athletes from public (66.9 %) and private (33.1 %) Division I institutions. Participants in this sample were Division I college athletes in various men's and women's team sports, such as men's baseball, basketball, football, ice hockey, lacrosse, soccer, as well as women's basketball, field hockey, ice hockey, lacrosse, soccer, softball, and volleyball. The majority of college athletes self-identified as White (70.8 %), followed by Black (19.8 %), Latinx (2.6 %), Asian (1.1 %), and other (5.7 %). About a quarter of the sample were first-generation college students (23.4 %). In terms of academic year, there were 32.4 % of freshmen, 26.3 % of sophomores, 26.0 % of juniors, and 15.3 % of seniors, which in the traditional college system refers to the first, second, third, and fourth years of undergraduate study. Most athletes reported being actively recruited for their sport in their high school (89.8 %). They attended their institution in state (40.2 %), out-of-state (54.4 %) and international (5.4 %).

Measures

Academic and athletic identity

Academic and athletic identity was measured with the Academic and Athletic Identity Scale (AAIS) [19]. The AAIS is an 11-item scale which has been shown to be valid and reliable measure for the use with college athletes. Participants were directed to rate the centrality of each item in relation to their sense of self using a 6-point Likert-type scale spanning from “not central to my sense of self” (1) to “very central to my sense of self” (6). Academic identity is measured with five items, one example item reading, “Doing well in school.” Athletic identity is measured with six items, an example item being, “Doing well during sport competitions.” The AAIS was developed and validated for the use with college athletes, including Division I participants (Study 2) [19]. It has also been translated to other languages and shown to be a valid and reliable measure in other cultural contexts, including in Brazil [24], France [25], Japan [26,27], and Ukraine [28], as well as acknowledged to have a potential to “become a gold standard in dual career research” (p. 83) [20]. Given it is brief nature and ability to measure both academic and athletic identity dimensions, it is a practical tool with good psychometric properties. Cronbach's alphas in the current study were .93 for academic identity and .91 for athletic identity.

Academic and athletic performance

Academic performance was measured with self-reported cumulative grade point average (GPA), which ranges from 0 to 4.00 and the values correspond to average grades of A (4.00), B (3.00), C (2.00), D (1.00), and F (0). The GPA system is commonly used in several countries, such as the U.S., Canada, Australia, India, Philippines, South Korea, Malaysia, Taiwan, to measure academic performance. In the U.S. specifically, academic performance of undergraduate students is usually expressed in terms of GPA, which has been proposed as an objective measure with good internal reliability and temporal stability [29,30]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 years of research into psychological correlates of university students’ academic performance, Richardson et al. [31] (p. 354) posited that “no other measure of tertiary academic performance rivals the measurement utility of GPA.”

To measure athletic performance, participants were asked to indicate the percentage of the time they participated during competitive events with the following six response options: less than 10 % (1), 10–24 % (2), 25–49 % (3), 50–74 % (4), 75–89 % (5), and 90–100 % (6). This method captured athletes' engagement within their sport teams as well as standardized the measurement of athletic performance across different sports and sport types. Because this measure reflected the level of an athlete's involvement within their respective teams (e.g., competing in all events, competing in some events, practicing but competing infrequently, practicing without competing), it was applicable to both individual and team sports. Additionally, team-level athletic performance for each team was assessed by recording the team's win percentages using the NCAA national ranking statistics [32].

Academic and athletic motivation

We have also included additional measures that were available in the existing dataset used in this study for a more comprehensive analysis of the identity profiles. Specifically, as described in Yukhymenko-Lescroart [23], measures of academic and athletic achievement motivation included academic mastery goal (three items, e.g., “I want to learn as much as possible from my classes" [33], current study α = 0.89), academic performance goal (three items, “It is important for me to do better than other students” [33], current study α = 0.93), athletic task goal (e.g., “I learn a new skill by trying hard” [34], current study α = 0.87), and athletic ego goal (e.g., “I score the most points/goals/hits” [34], current study α = 0.87), which used a 7-point response scale ranging from “not at all true of me” (1) to “very true of me” (7).

Academic and athletic satisfaction

Participants were asked two questions about whether they were satisfied with their academic and athletic experiences (i.e., “Are you satisfied with your academic experiences at your institution?” and “Are you satisfied with your athletic experiences at your institution?”) using a 7-point Likert-type scale with all response options labeled: very dissatisfied (1), dissatisfied (2), slightly dissatisfied (3), neutral (4), slightly satisfied (5), satisfied (6), and very satisfied (7). A number of studies have supported the use of single-item measures when assessing doubly concrete constructs, such as satisfaction [[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]].

Academic and athletic ethical conduct

Also as described in Yukhymenko-Lescroart [9,23,40], the datasets included the measures of academic and athletic ethical conduct, which were operationalized as measures of academic misconduct and conduct in sport. Specifically, as reported in Yukhymenko-Lescroart [23,40], academic misconduct was a 6-item measure from Stephens et al. [41] and consisted of items assessing self-reported engagement in most typical types of academic misconduct, such as homework cheating (e.g., “Copied all or part of another student's homework and submitted it as your own”), plagiarism (e.g., “Obtained or purchased a complete paper and submitted it as your own work”), and test cheating (e.g., “Used unpermitted notes during tests or exams”). In the current study, Cronbach's alpha was .85.

Likewise, as reported in Yukhymenko-Lescroart [9,23,40], athletic misconduct was an 18-item scale that assessed self-reported frequency of engaging in sportspersonship (six items, e.g., “I congratulated an opponent even when I lost” [40,42,43], current study α = 0.78), gamesmanship, which included both technical aspects of gamesmanship (three items, e.g., “I distracted an opponent on purpose to gain advantage over them” [40,42,43], current study α = 0.78) and mental aspects of gamesmanship (four items, e.g., “I used physical taunting (e.g., making faces or showing obscene gestures) to make an opponent commit a mistake” [40,42,43], current study α = 0.86), and instrumental aggression (five items, e.g., “I intentionally harmed an opponent to take out the opposition” [40,42,43], current study α = 0.97). Participants indicated frequency of engagement over the last year on a 5-point frequency response scale with the following response options: never (1), once or twice this year (2), about once a month (3), about once a week (4), and almost daily (5).

Procedure

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committees and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Ethical approvals were obtained from each participating institution. Participants were recruited for this study through electronic communications sent to Athletic Directors at several Division I institutions. The surveys were mailed to contact persons at each institution to be administered on paper by team captains after team practices. All surveys were conducted anonymously and on a voluntary basis. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. After completing the surveys, participants placed them in an envelope, which was sealed by the team captain and delivered to the contact person. The contact person them mailed the surveys back to the researcher.

Data analysis

Cluster analysis was employed to address the first study purpose and to enumerate distinct groups. Cluster analysis is a multivariate technique that aims to identify groups of survey respondents with similar profiles on a given set of characteristics [44]. Prior to clustering, outliers were removed for all observations using standardized z-scores of above 3.0 as a criterion for removal so that the clustering solution would more accurately represent the typical groups of college athletes (p. 204) [44]. A combination approach with two steps was employed with hierarchical and non-hierarchical clustering methods, which results in more accurate cluster memberships [44].

In the first step, hierarchical clustering with Ward's cluster method and a squared Euclidean distance as a dissimilarity measure was performed on academic and athletic identities to examine agglomeration schedule and dendrogram, enumerate the groups, and obtain cluster centers. In the second step, the cluster centers served as initial cluster seeds in the nonhierarchical k-mean procedure to obtain the final solution. The clusters were then examined and interpreted on their academic and athletic identities using profile analysis. Profile analysis is a special application of repeated measures MANOVA aimed at examining whether groups have the same patterns on more than one dependent variable [45]. The profiles were then validated across reasons for attending college and examined on demographic and other characteristics (i.e., gender, race/ethnicity, first-generation, athletic recruitment, athletic scholarship, institution type, and residency) using chi-square analysis. Finally, the groups were compared on academic and athletic performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct using a series of ANOVAs, followed by pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment. All analyses were performed in SPSS version 28.

Results

Table 1 shows descriptive statistics and correlations among the study variables. In the overall sample, academic and athletic identity were positively correlated. Academic identity was positively correlated with academic performance, academic goals (mastery, performance), athletic task goal, academic satisfaction, and athletic sportspersonship, as well as negatively correlated with athletic performance (individual and team), academic misconduct (overall, homework cheating, plagiarism, test cheating), athletic gamesmanship (overall, technical aspects, mental aspects), and athletic instrumental aggression. Athletic identity was positively correlated with athletic performance (individual), academic goals (mastery, performance), athletic goals (task, ego), satisfaction (academic, athletic), as well as negatively correlated with athletic performance (team), academic misconduct (overall, plagiarism, test cheating), and athletic instrumental aggression. Deriving identity profiles: Cluster analysis

The cluster analysis suggested that a 4-cluster solution for academic and athletic identities was optimal based on empirical considerations and conceptual expectations. Table 2 displays descriptive statistics and mean comparisons across the four identity profiles. Fig. 1 displays the four profile diagrams. As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 1, identity profiles differed with regards to profile prevalence, as well as the levels of academic identity, athletic identity, and the disparities between the levels of these two identities. The differences in identity profiles were further examined by utilizing profile analysis, which subsequently allowed for a more meaningful comparison and description of each identity profile. Specifically, profile analysis was used to explore between-subjects (cluster group) and within-subjects (identity, interaction between identity and cluster group) differences.

Profile analysis indicated a significant main effect for the identity, F(1, 1120) = 1172.06, p < .001, ηpartial2 = 0.51, indicated that, on average, college athletes reported a significantly stronger athletic identity than academic identity. A significant main effect for cluster group, F(3, 1120) = 1945.26, p < .001, ηpartial2 = 0.84, indicated that the four groups different significantly on their identity. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment showed significant differences between all four cluster groups (all p < .001). Specifically, Cluster 1 was the most strongly identified profile, followed by Cluster 2, and Cluster 3. Cluster 4 was the least identified profile. Most importantly, a significant interaction between identity and cluster group, F(3, 1120) = 513.07, p < .001, ηpartial2 = 0.58, indicated that identity profiles differed across the groups. Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment indicated that all four groups were significantly different from each other on academic identity (p < .001 for all pairwise comparisons). For athletic identity, on the other hand, Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 (p = 1.000) and Cluster 3 and Cluster 4 (p = .281) were not significantly different; but other pairs were significant (p < .001).

Cluster 1, labeled as “fully engaged college athletes” (n = 469), represented 41.7 % of participants and was the largest group. It was characterized by the highest academic identity and the highest athletic identity, with a slightly stronger athletic identity than academic identity.

Cluster 2, labeled as “committed athletes” (n = 274), represented 24.4 % of participants, who were characterized by a low academic identity and a high athletic identity. The athletic identification was much stronger than academic identification.

Cluster 3, labeled “academics-focused college athletes” (n = 253), represented 22.5 % of participants, who were characterized by an average academic identity and a low athletic identity. This was the only group of college athletes with a significantly stronger academic identity than athletic identity.

Finally, Cluster 4, which was labeled “academically unengaged athletes” (n = 128), represented 11.4 % of participants and was the smallest group. This group was characterized by an extremely low academic identity and a low athletic identity. Yet, athletic identity was significantly stronger than academic identity.

Composition of identity profiles

To validate the four identity profiles, they were compared on reasons for attending college, showing significant differences, χ2 (6, N = 1096) = 89.80, p < .001. Adjusted residuals showed that fully engaged college athletes were more likely to report both academic and athletic reasons (adjusted residual = 3.8) and less likely to report mostly athletic reasons (−5.1). Committed athletes were more likely to report mostly athletic reasons (5.5) and less likely to report mostly academic (−3.8) and both academic and athletic reasons (−2.8). Academics-focused college athletes were more likely to report mostly academic reasons (3.7) and less likely to report mostly athletic reasons (−3.7). Finally, academically unengaged athletes were more likely to report mostly athletic reasons (5.4) and less likely to report mostly academic (−2.1) and both academic and athletic reasons (−3.8). These results are aligned with expectations.

Identity profiles were also compared on gender, race/ethnicity, first-generation, athletic recruitment, athletic scholarship, institution type, and residency. Differences were found on first-generation student status, χ2 (3, N = 1124) = 36.09, p < .001, showing that academics-focused college athletes were more likely to be returning-generation students (2.8) and that academically unengaged athletes were more likely to be first generation students (5.5). Differences were also found on institution type, χ2 (3, N = 1124) = 20.87, p < .001, with fully engaged college athletes being more likely to be in private institutions (4.3) and with committed athletes being more likely to be in public institutions (2.9). Table 3 shows the distribution across the four clusters for these variables. No other differences were found.

Differences between identity profiles on motivation

As also shown in Table 4, the four profiles differed significantly on academic and athletic achievement motivation. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment showed that fully engaged college athletes reported the highest levels of academic mastery and academic performance goals, followed by academics-focused college athletes, committed athletes, and academically unengaged athletes who reported the lowest levels of academic motivation. For athletic motivation, both fully engaged college athletes and committed athletes reported the highest levels of athletic task goals, followed by academics-focused college athletes, while academically unengaged athletes reported the lowest levels of athletic task goal. Finally, athletic ego goals were the highest in both fully engaged college athletes and committed athletes and the lowest in both academics-focused college athletes and academically unengaged athletes.

Differences between identity profiles on satisfaction

Descriptive statistics comparing the four identity profiles on satisfaction are shown in Table 4. MANOVA results showed significant multivariate main effects for academic and athletic satisfaction, F(6, 2240) = 17.46, p < .001, ηpartial2 = 0.05. The four groups were different on both, academic satisfaction, F(3, 1120) = 29.53, p < .001, ηpartial2 = 0.07, and athletic satisfaction, F(3, 1120) = 16.63, p < .001, ηpartial2 = 0.02. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correlation showed that fully engaged college athletes, committed athletes, and academics-focused college athletes reported significantly higher academic satisfaction than academically unengaged athletes. Additionally, fully engaged college athletes reported higher academic satisfaction than committed athletes. For athletic satisfaction, fully engaged college athletes and committed athletes reported being more satisfied than academics-focused college athletes and academically unengaged athletes.

Differences between identity profiles on ethical conduct

As shown in Table 4, fully engaged college athletes reported the lowest frequency of engagement in academic misconduct, while academically unengaged athletes reported the highest frequency. Similar patterns emerged for athletic ethical conduct, with fully engaged college athletes reporting the highest ethical conduct and academically unengaged athletes reporting the lowest ethical conduct in athletics. Additionally, there were also notable nuances among other identity profiles. For example, for the most extreme forms of unethical conduct in academics and athletics, such as test cheating and instrumental aggression, committed athletes reported comparable levels of ethical conduct to fully engaged college athletes. In contrast, academics-focused college athletes reported similar levels of ethical conduct to fully engaged college athletes for academic test cheating, but were on par with academically unengaged athletes in terms of athletic instrumental aggression.

Study 2

Given the nature of cluster analysis, it is recommended to validate results with a separate sample to compare the cluster solutions and assess the correspondence of the results [44]. Therefore, the purpose of Study 2 was to validate identity profiles derived in Study 1 with an independent sample of Division I college athletes and to compare groups on their performance.

Method

Participants

Study 2 data also came from an existing dataset of NCAA Division I college athletes in the United States [8,10]. For the purposes of this study, only the measures assessing identity and performance were included. Satisfaction was not examined in Study 2 because it was not measured in this dataset. Similar to Study 1, the variables of academic identity and athletic identity were converted into z-scores to identify outliers using a criterion of |z| > 3.0. Of the 187 participants in the original study, three cases were removed: two with extremely low scores on academic identity (M = 1.0, SD = 0) and one on athletic identity (M = 2.67). Thus, the sample in the current study included 184 (30.4 % male) college athletes from one public Division I institution. Participants in this sample were Division I college athletes in various men's and women's individual (33.7 %) and team sports (66.3 %), such as men's baseball, basketball, football, golf, tennis, track and field, as well as women's basketball, equestrian, golf, lacrosse, softball, soccer, swimming, tennis, track and field, and volleyball. The majority of college athletes self-identified as White (62.0 %), followed by Black (13.6 %), Latinx (13.6 %), Asian (6.5 %), and other (11.6 %). The sample included 33.0 % of first-generation college students. In terms of academic year, 26.9 % were freshmen, 25.3 % sophomores, 26.9 % of juniors, 18.1 % of seniors, and 2.7 % of graduate students.

Measures

Academic and athletic identity

Participants completed the AAIS [19] used in Study 1, rating 11 items with a modified 7-point scale with all response options labeled: 1 = not central to my sense of self, 2 = slightly central, 3 = somewhat central, 4 = central, 5 = very central, 6 = extremely central, 7 = the central core. Cronbach's alphas in the current study were .94 for academic identity and .92 for athletic identity.

Academic and athletic performance

Academic and athletic performance were measured using GPA and percentage of participation in competitive events, consistent with Study 1. Additionally, athletes classified their status in their sport with four options: 1 = “first team (competing in your preferred events/a starter),” 2 = “second team (competing in some events/a regular substitute),” 3 = “third team (practicing but competing infrequently),” and 4 = “practicing but not competing.” This classification item, used by the NCAA in multiple GOALS studies [46], preceded the athletes’ athletic performance question on participation percentage. This classification was used for construct validation purposes in the preliminary analysis. However, it was not used for examining clusters in the study results because the chi-square test assumptions were violated, with over 25 % of the cells having an expected count less than 5.

Preliminary analyses indicated significant differences in participation percentages across the four team classification levels, F(3, 182) = 118.32, p < .001. Post-hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction showed all pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (p ≤ .029). On average, first-team student-athletes reported 5.40 (5 = 75–89 %, 6 = 90–100 %) participation time, second-team student-athletes 3.25 (3 = 25–49 %; 4 = 50–74 %), third-team student-athletes 2.09 (2 = 10–24 %), and those practicing but not competing 1.06 (1 = less than 10 %). Item usage across individual and team sports was also examined, showing consistent use of all response options across the two types of sports, χ2 (5, N = 184) = 7.47, p = .188, with no missing data, indicating no confusion about the item. Overall, the results of preliminary analyses provided evidence of construct validity for the athletic performance item for both individual and team sports. Nevertheless, Study 2 results for the athletic performance item were supplemented with analyses focused on a subsample of team sports student-athletes (n = 122). Additionally, for team sports, team-level athletic performance was assessed by recording the team's win percentages using the NCAA national ranking statistics [32], similar to Study 1.

Academic and athletic motivation

As reported in Yukhymenko-Lescroart [10], the dataset also included a number of measures of autonomous motivation in academics and athletics, including academic and athletic psychological needs (competence, choice, relatedness [47]), academic and athletic autonomous motivation (intrinsic interest/enjoyment, value/usefulness, effort/importance [47]). Specifically, these scales were: academic and athletic competence (six items, e.g., “I think I am pretty good in my classes,” current study α = 0.84; “I think I am pretty good at my sport,” current study α = 0.80), choice (seven items, e.g., “I selected my major because I had no choice (reversed),” current study α = 0.83; “I participate in sports because I have no choice (reversed),” current study α = 0.87), relatedness (eight items, e.g., “I feel close to my classmates,” current study α = 0.83; “I feel close to my teammates,” current study α = 0.82), intrinsic interest/enjoyment (seven items, e.g., “I enjoy studying for classes in my major very much,” current study α = 0.92; “I enjoy participating in my sport very much,” current study α = 0.89), value/usefulness (seven items, e.g., “I think that studying for courses in my major is useful for my future career,” current study α = 0.91; “I think that participation in my sport is useful for my future career,” current study α = 0.92), and effort/importance (five items, e.g., “I put a lot of effort into studying for my classes,” current study α = 0.89; “I put a lot of effort into practicing my sport,” current study α = 0.79). Response options for the SDT (n.d.) scales ranged from not at all true (1) to very true (7).

Additionally, as reported in Yukhymenko-Lescroart [8], the dataset also included academic persistence (six items, e.g., “When I become confused about something I'm studying, I go back and try to figure it out,” adapted from Refs. [48,49], current study α = 0.85), which used a 7-point response scale ranging from not at all true (1) to very true (7).

Finally, as reported in Yukhymenko-Lescroart [8], athletic passion for sport [50] consisted of the two subscales: athletic harmonious passion (seven items, e.g., “Participating in my sport is in harmony with the other activities in my life,” current study α = 0.85) and athletic obsessive passion (seven items, e.g., “I have almost an obsessive feeling for participating in my sport,” current study α = 0.92). These items used a 7-point agreement scale ranging from completely disagree (1) to completely agree (7).

Procedure

After an ethical approval was obtained for research with human subjects, participants were recruited at one Division I public university. This was a different university from those sampled in Study 1; thus, this was an independent sample of student-athletes. The procedure for data collection was identical to that in Study 1.

Data analysis

Clusters were derived following the same data analysis steps as in Study 1.

Results

Descriptive statistics and correlations for Study 2 studied variables are shown in Table 5. Similar to Study 1, academic identity and athletic identity were positively correlated. Academic identity was positively correlated with academic performance, academic competence, academic relatedness to classmates, academic interest/enjoyment in major, academic value/usefulness, academic effort/importance, and academic persistence, as well as negatively correlated with athletic performance (team). Athletic identity was positively correlated with athletic performance (individual), academic competence, athletic competence, athletic interest/enjoyment, athletic value/usefulness, athletic effort/importance, athletic harmonious passion, and athletic obsessive passion. Significant gender differences were found for academic performance F(1, 165) = 5.98, p = .016; athletic performance (team sports only, n = 122), F(1, 120) = 5.52, p = .020; academic competence, F(1, 178) = 5.22, p = .024; academic choice of major, F(1, 178) = 14.33, p < .001; academic relatedness to classmates, F(1, 178) = 5.59, p = .019; academic interest/enjoyment in major, F(1, 178) = 7.44, p = .007; academic value/usefulness, F(1, 178) = 30.31, p < .001; academic effort/importance, F(1, 178) = 13.33, p < .001; academic persistence, F(1, 179) = 4.50, p = .035, with women reporting higher scores than men. In contrast, men reported significantly higher scores than women on athletic value/usefulness, F(1, 178) = 7.18, p = .008, and athletic obsessive passion, F(1, 182) = 4.78, p = .030.

Deriving identity profiles: Cluster analysis

The cluster analysis suggested a 4-cluster solution. Table 2 displays descriptive statistics and mean comparisons across the four identity profiles. Fig. 2 displays the four profile diagrams.

Cluster 1 = “fully engaged college athletes”; Cluster 2 = “committed athletes”; Cluster 3 = “academics-focused college athletes”; Cluster 4 = “academically unengaged athletes.”

Profile analysis indicated a significant main effect for the identity, F(1, 180) = 85.18, p < .001, ηpartial2 = 0.32, indicated that, on average, college athletes reported a significantly stronger athletic identity than academic identity. A significant main effect for cluster group, F(3, 180) = 238.22, p < .001, ηpartial2 = 0.80, indicated that the four groups different significantly on their identity. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment showed significant differences between all four cluster groups (all p < .001). Specifically, Cluster 1 was the most strongly identified profile, followed by Cluster 2, and Cluster 3. Cluster 4 was the least identified profile. Most importantly, a significant interaction between identity and cluster group, F(3, 180) = 84.46, p < .001, ηpartial2 = 0.59, indicated that identity profiles differed across the groups. Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment indicated that all four groups were significantly different from each other on academic identity (p < .001 for all pairwise comparisons). For athletic identity, on the other hand, Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 (p = 1.000) and Cluster 3 and Cluster 4 (p = .223) were not significantly different; but other pairs were significant (p < .001).

Cluster 1 “fully engaged college athletes” (n = 76) represented 41.3 % of participants and was the largest group. It was characterized by the highest academic identity and the highest athletic identity, with a slightly stronger athletic identity than academic identity. Cluster 2 “committed athletes” (n = 58) represented 31.5 % of participants, who were characterized by a low academic identity and a high athletic identity. The athletic identification was much stronger than the academic identification. Cluster 3 “academics-focused college athletes” (n = 30) represented 16.3 % of participants, who were characterized by an average academic identity and a low athletic identity. This was the only group of college athletes with a significantly stronger academic identity than athletic identity. Finally, Cluster 4 “academically unengaged athletes” (n = 20) represented 10.9 % of participants and was the smallest group. This group was characterized by an extremely low academic identity and a low athletic identity. Yet, athletic identity was significantly stronger than academic identity. Thus, the cluster solution from Study 1 was validated.

Composition of identity profiles

Identity profiles differed on reasons for attending college, χ2 (6, N = 182) = 23.59, p < .001. Adjusted residuals showed that fully engaged college athletes were more likely to report both academic and athletic reasons (adjusted residual = 3.2) and less likely to report mostly athletic reasons (−3.0). Committed athletes were more likely to report mostly athletic reasons (3.8) and less likely to report both academic and athletic reasons (−2.4). Academics-focused college athletes were more likely to report mostly academic reasons (2.4). These results were generally aligned with expectations. Table 3 shows the cross-tabulation of identity profiles across reasons for attending college. No differences in composition of identity profiles were found on gender, race/ethnicity, academic year, first-generation, and athletic scholarship. Additionally, exploratory analysis showed no differences in composition of profiles on type of sport (individual, team).

Differences between identity profiles on motivation (autonomous motivation, persistence, passion)

As shown in Table 6, differences emerged across identity profiles for all academic motivation measures, except for academic choice of major and academic interest in major. Specifically, fully engaged college athletes had the highest levels of academic competence, academic relatedness to classmates, academic value/usefulness, academic effort/importance, and academic persistence. Additionally, academics-focused college athletes reported as high academic competence as fully engaged college athletes. In contrast, committed athletes reported the lowest levels of academic competence, academic relatedness to classmates, and academic value/usefulness. As well, academically unengaged athletes reported just as low academic competence as committed athletes.

With regards to athletic motivation, differences emerged on athletic competence. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment showed that committed athletes reported the highest athletic competence, whereas academics-focused college athletes reported the lowest athletic competence. Additionally, differences emerged on athletic harmonious and obsessive passions. For harmonious passion, fully engaged college athletes reported the highest level, while academics-focused college athletes reported the lowest level. For obsessive passion, the highest levels were reported by fully engaged college athletes and committed athletes; whereas the lowest levels were in academics-focused college athletes and academically unengaged athletes.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to identify and examine the common profiles of college athletes based on their academic and athletic identity and to further explore these profiles on performance and satisfaction in academics and athletics. Rather than considering the role of academic and athletic identities individually on the performance and wellbeing of college athletes, this study utilized cluster analysis to examine unique combinations of academic and athletic identities of college athletes. Examining college athletes' identity profiles, while considering the academic and athletic contexts in conjunction with each other, expands on our understanding of the multidimensional nature of identity in college athletes and may provide valuable information to support and enhance college athletes’ commitments and academic success. Findings in Study 1 revealed four distinct profiles of academic and athletic identities representing fully engaged college athletes, committed athletes, academics-focused college athletes, and academically unengaged athletes. Findings in Study 2 further validated these four identity profiles of Division I college athletes with a sample of college athletes in both individual and team sports.

Findings showed that two of the four identity profiles, committed athletes and academically unengaged athletes, reported a much stronger athletic identity than academic identity. Compared to other profiles, college athletes in these two profiles also were more likely to indicate that they were attending college for mostly athletic reasons. Some of the previous studies assessed academic and athletic identities of college athletes in relation to each other [7,11,17], asking college athletes to rate whether they viewed themselves mostly as athletes or as students. As a result, they lumped these two identity profiles into one because of a stronger athletic identity relative to their academic identity. However, committed athletes and academically unengaged athletes differed on the extent to which athletic and academic identities were central to their sense of self. Specifically, committed athletes were more identified than academically unengaged athletes. Notably, for committed athletes, the relative importance of athletic identity over academic identity did not preclude them from reporting that academic identity was also central to their sense of self, just that it was to a lesser degree.

Findings also showed that fully engaged college athletes who indicated the highest levels of academic and athletic identities also reported a stronger athletic identity than academic identity. Yet, unlike committed athletes and academically unengaged athletes, fully engaged college athletes were less likely to report attending university for mostly athletic reasons and more likely to report attending university for both academic and athletic reasons. Therefore, this identity profile represented college athletes, for whom pursuing academic studies were as important as playing sports. Overall, this finding highlighted that for college athletes, athletic identity is inherent to how they identify themselves. This was also evident in the academics-focused college athletes profile, which was the only group who reported a significantly stronger academic identity than athletic identity. Findings showed that college athletes in this profile were more likely to attend college for mostly academic reasons than for the other reasons. Nevertheless, these athletes unlikely view themselves as mostly students because they indicated that athletic identity still played a prominent role in their sense of self as evident by their ratings above the mid-point of the response scale. This finding can also explain why in previous studies (e.g., Ref. [17]) a profile of “pure scholars” or “mostly students” did not emerge. This is because the athlete role is prominent to the sense of self of Division I college athletes, including those who hold strong academic identities.

Taken together, findings indicated that the four profiles were generally aligned with previous literature, but also highlighted a few differences. A few similarities emerged between the identity profiles empirically derived in this study and the typology that was theoretically proposed by Snyder [16]; yet, there are also important differences. Specifically, fully engaged college athletes and committed college athletes correspond to “scholar-athletes” and “pure athletes” of Snyder [16]. Yet, academically-focused college athletes are not “pure scholars” and academically unengaged athletes do not represent the “neither” group proposed by Snyder [16] because athletic identity is similar and somewhat strong in these two groups. There are also some similarities with the three categories formed by Marx et al. [17], which were “mostly athletes,” “pure athletes,” and “student-athletes.” Specifically, committed college athletes and academically unengaged college athletes correspond to “pure athletes” and “mostly athletes.” In contrast, fully engaged college athletes and academically-focused college athletes are likely to be the ones who were conceptualized by Marx et al. [17] as “student-athletes” who reported that they wanted to be remembered for their time in college equally as an athlete and as a student. Overall, these findings highlighted the multidimensional nature of their academic and athletic identities and indicated that it is important to measure academic and athletic identities as two independent dimensions.

In terms of the relationships between identity profiles with academic and athletic performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct, findings showed that fully engaged college athletes were the most adjusted: they reported the highest performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct in both academics and athletics. Similar to fully engaged college athletes, academics-focused college athletes reported as high academic performance, academic competence, and academic satisfaction, and as low academic test cheating; but they were not satisfied with their athletic experiences and scored the lowest on athletic ego goal orientation, athletic competence, athletic harmonious and obsessive passions, and athletic instrumental aggression. Thus, they were generally academically adjusted. In contrast, committed athletes appeared to be most adjusted athletically, but not academically. Specifically, they scored the highest on athletic task and ego goal orientations, athletic competence, athletic obsessive passion, and athletic satisfaction while also scoring the lowest on academic competence, academic relatedness to classmates, academic value/usefulness, and academic persistence. Notably, committed athletes reported the lowest academic test cheating and athletic instrumental aggression; yet, they also reported the highest academic homework cheating and both types of athletic gamesmanship (technical, mental). Finally, the least adjusted group was academically unengaged athletes who reported the lowest grades and were least satisfied with their academic and athletic experiences. They also reported the lowest levels of academic and athletic achievement motivation, academic competence, academic persistence, and obsessive passion for sport, and the highest levels of academic and athletic ethical misconduct. Notably, these college athletes were on athletic teams that had the highest percentages of wins. Therefore, this group of college athletes is at most risk in terms of their college commitments and well-being.

Overall, these findings are consistent with the growing body of studies with Division I student-athletes [2,[8], [9], [10], [11],17,20,23]. Previous research proposed that a strong athletic identity is a roadblock to academic success of college athletes. Yet, surprisingly, academic performance and satisfaction were just as high in fully committed college athletes, who reported the highest levels of academic and athletic identities, as in academics-focused college athletes. This highlights two important suppositions. First, the positive effect of a strong academic identity on academic outcomes is likely to outweigh the negative effect of athletic identity. Indeed, while the strengths of the relationships were not tested for significant differences in previous studies, the relationship between academic identity and academic outcomes appears to be stronger than the relationship between athletic identity and academic outcomes [2,[8], [9], [10]]. In this study, fully engaged college athletes had the highest academic performance, academic motivation, academic satisfaction, and academic ethical conduct despite the strongest identification with athletic roles because of the strongest academic identity. Study findings indicated that a strong and relatively equal identification with student and athlete roles is the most optimal combination for college athletes because the positive (and relatively stronger) effect of a strong academic identity is likely to compensate the negative (and relatively weaker) effect of the athletic identity. Indeed, this is consistent with several more recent empirical, review, and meta-analytical studies [[20], [21], [22]], which suggested that Division I college athletes are most adjusted, engaged, and successful when they have equally strong identities in both academics and athletics.

Second, findings suggested that there is a domain alignment between the primary identity and outcomes in that domain. In contrast to Killeya-Jones [18] who found that a greater discrepancy between academic and athletic identities of Division I college athletes is associated with lesser academic satisfaction, this study findings indicate that the level of satisfaction is higher when identity in that domain is higher even when the discrepancy between the two identities is high. For example, the discrepancy in academic and athletic identity was the highest for committed athletes; yet, they were more academically satisfied than academically unengaged college athletes, which is likely due to a stronger academic identity. Notably, Killeya-Jones's study [18] was based on a modest sample size of 40 Division I college athletes, all male, because the Hierarchical Classification (HICLAS) procedure utilized to derive identity structures was time- and effort-intensive. This study utilized a larger sample which was also representative of both male and female college athletes. Taken together, current findings highlight the importance of considering both identities simultaneously and independently in studies with college athletes when examining cross-domain relationships between academic and athletic variables.

Implications, limitations, and future directions

The findings of this study suggest that applied sport practitioners (e.g., university coaches and staff working with college athletes) should help college athletes to develop and maintain holistic identities that are strongly aligned with both roles of being a student and being an athlete. When college athletes identify strongly with both academics and athletics, they report the highest academic and athletic performance as well as satisfaction with both academic and athletic experiences. College athletes should also be encouraged to get integrated in both their academic and athletic lives. The more college athletes feel like they belong to their university and to their athletic team, the stronger they identify with both of these social contexts. Findings of this study also suggest that it is important to measure both academic and athletic identities of college athletes in studies that are aimed at examining the relationships between identities of college athletes and various academic and athletic outcomes.

This study has limitations that are important to acknowledge. First, this study was conducted with Division I college athletes in the U.S. Given the unique context of Division I college athletics, the generalizability of the study findings to college athlete populations in other contexts should be approached with caution. It is possible that other identity profiles exist in college athletes that are less common for Division I college athletes. Future studies can be conducted with college athletes in other sport settings and levels (e.g., Division II and III, club sports) as well as college athletes in other countries. For example, efforts have already been made to examine profiles among dual career athletes in the U.K. incorporating exploration of their academic and athletic identities [51]. More research is needed to further support the findings and examine cross-cultural and cross-contextual differences. Second, this study used a cross-sectional design. While identity profiles were identified and validated using two independent samples of college athletes, the findings of this study can be extended in future studies by utilizing longitudinal designs to examine changes in identity profiles of college athletes. Third, performance and satisfaction were measured with single-item questions. While it is common to assess these variables with single items, the use of multiple-item measures is encouraged in the future studies because such measures allow for lower measurement error. Notwithstanding the limitations, the major strengths of this study are a large sample size and cross-validation of findings with an independent sample. This was also the first person-centered study that considered academic and athletic identities as two independent dimensions. Future research should continue exploring the role of identity of college athletes on academic and athletic outcomes from a multidimensional conceptualization.

In this study, a distinction was drawn between Division I college athletes and "dual career" athletes under NCAA regulations [12]. The intention was not to dismiss or ignore European perspectives on dual career athletes but to recognize the unique context of NCAA regulations, which emphasize the amateur status of student-athletes. This distinction is crucial as it shapes how the challenges and experiences of student-athletes within the U.S. collegiate system are conceptualized and studied. The NCAA's emphasis on amateurism [13] contrasts with the European framework, where dual career athletes often balance sporting careers with education or work, as outlined in the EU Guidelines on Dual Careers of Athletes [14]. This distinction influences the applicability of terms like "dual career" within this study's context, which focused on understanding academic and athletic identities among NCAA Division I student-athletes. Future research that links Division I student-athletes with European dual career studies could provide valuable insights.

Conclusion

In sum, the purpose of this study was to examine identity profiles in a large sample of Division I college athletes in the U.S. based on their academic identity and athletic identity to gain a better understanding of their experiencing balancing academic and athletic commitments and responsibilities. Results of Study 1 led to the identification of four identity profiles, which were also cross-validated in Study 2: (1) fully engaged college athletes, characterized by the highest academic identity and the highest athletic identity, (2) committed athletes, characterized by a low academic identity and a high athletic identity, (3) academics-focused college athletes, characterized by an average academic identity and a low athletic identity, and (4) academically unengaged athletes, characterized by an extremely low academic identity and a low athletic identity. Notably, athletic identity was found to be inherent to the sense of self of Division I college athletes regardless of the identity profile. Findings showed that fully engaged college athletes were the most adjusted to college life, successfully balancing academics and athletics. Specifically, they reported the highest levels of performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct compared to other identity profiles. For committed athletes, the levels of performance, motivation, and satisfaction were high in athletics but low in academics. Additionally, committed athletes were found to be at a high risk of engaging in academic misconduct, such as homework cheating, as well as engaging in dubiously unethical conduct in athletics, such as gamesmanship. In contrast, for academics-focused college athletes, the levels of performance, motivation, and satisfaction were high in academics but low in athletics. Moreover, they reported high levels of more common ethical conduct in both academics and athletics, comparable to those of fully engaged college athletes. However, they were found to be at a greater risk of engaging in more extreme forms of misconduct, such as academic plagiarism and athletic instrumental aggression. Finally, academically unengaged athletes, on the other hand, reported the lowest levels of individual performance, motivation, satisfaction, and ethical conduct in academics and athletics, but they were on athletic teams with highest team's win percentages based on the NCAA national ranking statistics. Taken together, findings provided evidence of the heterogeneity in academic and athletic identities among the college athlete population. Furthermore, findings highlighted the multidimensional nature of Division I college athletes' identities, emphasizing the need to simultaneously consider academic and athletic identities as separate yet interconnected dimensions in research studies. This approach allows for a comprehensive understanding of college athletes' experiences and provides a more nuanced perspective on their identity development.
