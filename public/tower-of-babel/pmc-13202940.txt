New contributions to the taxonomy and phylogeny of Cyrtophoria ciliates (Alveolata, Ciliophora, Phyllopharyngea) from China, with description of Dysteria bella sp. nov. and two rare species
Authors: Congcong Wang, Qian Zhang, Jianan Sun, Xianzhi Yao, Rui Wang, Xiaozhong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCiliates constitute a vital component of eukaryotic diversity, playing an essential trophic role in aquatic ecosystems by mediating carbon and nutrient cycling. As specialized primary consumers in biofilms, cyrtophorian ciliates (Class Phyllopharyngea de Puytorac et al., 1974) are ubiquitous in periphytic habitats; yet, their biodiversity remains significantly underestimated compared to well-studied model groups. Here we document three species from the urban-adjacent coastal waters in East China. Integrative studies suggest that one of them represents a novel species and the other two are rare species.ResultsMorphological and molecular investigations were conducted on three species belonging to the order Dysteriida Deroux, 1976, Dysteria armata Huxley, 1857, Dysteria bella sp. nov., and Orthotrochilia agamalievi Deroux, 1976. For Dysteria armata (the type species of the genus), detailed morphometric data and an improved diagnosis are provided based on a Chinese population. The new species is characterized by size in vivo 75–110 × 40–60 μm, body long obovate, and seven right kineties including five frontoventral kineties. The SSU rRNA gene sequences were first obtained for all three species. Phylogenetic analyses reveal that Dysteria bella sp. nov. and D. armata form a fully supported sister clade. Consistent with morphological diversity, the molecular topology shows the non-monophyly of the genus Dysteria. Furthermore, the first molecular data for Orthotrochilia confirms its evolutionary distinctiveness from the genus Trochilioides.ConclusionsBy providing detailed morphological and molecular characterizations of one new and two poorly known marine cyrtophorian species, this work not only advances our knowledge of the biodiversity within this specialized ciliate group but also resolves the systematic placement of the type species D. armata. Background

Cyrtophorian ciliates (subclass Cyrtophoria Fauré-Fremiet in Corliss, 1956), belonging to the class Phyllopharyngea, represent a highly specialized and morphologically diverse group of protozoans [1–5]. They inhabit virtually all aquatic ecosystems, predominantly adopting a free-living benthic lifestyle [6–10]. The most distinctive morphological characteristics of this lineage include a complex oral apparatus reinforced by a pharyngeal basket (cyrtos) and a body configuration that is typically dorsoventrally or laterally flattened [4, 8, 11–13]. Although classic descriptions of cyrtophorians date back to the early 20th century, their species diversity has long been underestimated due to their small body size and a lack of sufficient attention. Driven by the integration of modern taxonomic approaches and intensive faunal surveys along the Chinese coastline over the past two decades, the description of numerous novel cyrtophorian taxa has substantially expanded our understanding of their biodiversity [5, 8, 12, 14–16]. Despite being considered one of the best-resolved subclasses, a recent review highlighted that the paucity of molecular data remains a predominant issue for Cyrtophoria, necessitating further sequencing efforts to underpin their phylogeny [17].

Within the subclass Cyrtophoria, the genus Dysteria Huxley, 1857 (Dysteriidae Claparède & Lachmann, 1859) stands out as one of the most species-rich and morphologically distinct lineages [2–4]. Unlike most cyrtophorians (e.g., Chlamydonella, Cyrtophoron), which are dorsoventrally flattened, members of Dysteria possess a strongly laterally compressed body [5, 10, 18, 19]. This body plan allows them to move flexibly within the interstitial spaces of microbenthic environments. The primary diagnostic features of the genus include a specialized ventral groove, degenerated left kineties, robust cytopharyngeal basket, a podite located at the posterior end [4, 20].

In contrast, the genus Orthotrochilia Deroux in Song, 2003, assigned to the family Hartmannulidae Poche, 1913, exhibits a dorsoventrally flattened body configuration typical of many cyrtophorians. Species of this genus are distinguished by two specialized separated circumoral kineties, gradually shortened somatic kineties as well as the presence of a podite in subterminal portion [21, 22]. Notably, Orthotrochilia was historically treated as a nomen nudum due to the lack of a type species designation until it was validly re-established and revised by Song in 2003 [22].

However, the taxonomy of genera Dysteria and Orthotrochilia presents significant challenges. First, morphological differences between species in vivo are often subtle. The presence of intraspecific morphological variations and interspecific morphometric overlap has led to numerous synonyms and misidentifications in historical literature [1, 7, 20, 21, 23, 24]. Second, the infraciliature patterns are crucial for species circumscription, and can only be clearly revealed through complex cytological techniques such as protargol impregnation [14, 25]. Furthermore, recent studies have indicated that some cyrtophorians, particularly Dysteria species, frequently host ectosymbiotic bacteria [5, 15]. While often overlooked in past taxonomic descriptions, these symbiotic associations may hold ecological significance and offer potential auxiliary characters for taxonomic identification, as demonstrated in other ciliate lineages where symbiont-host co-evolutionary patterns have been revealed [26].

To address these taxonomic impediments, modern ciliate taxonomy emphasizes an integrative strategy that combines in vivo observation, silver staining to reveal infraciliature, and molecular phylogenetic analyses inferred from SSU rRNA gene sequences [6, 10, 27]. The application of molecular markers has proven instrumental not only in uncovering species identification but also in correcting phylogenetic relationships previously established solely on morphological grounds [27–29].

In the present study, two Dysteria species and Orthotrochilia agamalievi isolated from the urban-adjacent coastal waters in Qingdao, China, were documented. Based on detailed morphological and morphometric comparisons of live and silver-stained specimens, one Dysteria form was identified as a new species, while the other was identified as the type species, D. armata. Additionally, phylogenetic analyses based on SSU rRNA gene sequences were conducted to clarify their evolutionary positions, providing the first molecular data for the genus Orthotrochilia.

Morphological description of Qingdao population

Cell size 80–105 × 40–60 μm in vivo, about 80 × 45 μm after protargol staining (Table 1). Length to width ratio approx. 1.8:1, thickness-to-width ratio about 1.7:1; body bilaterally flattened, kidney-shaped in left lateral view; both ends bluntly rounded, dorsal margin convex, ventral margin straight or slightly concave, posterior margin thinner, right plate slightly wider than left one, left plate anterior margin irregular (Figs. 1A, C and 2A and B). Fusiform in ventral view, anterior and posterior ends notched; right plate arched, left plate relatively straight (Figs. 1A and 2A and C); left plate with numerous fine transverse stripes in mid-body; right with single thicker transverse stripe (about 25 μm in length) medially (Figs. 1A, C and 2E and G). Podite pen nib-shaped, positioned subterminal ventral groove; 21 μm in length (Figs. 1A, C and 2A).

Cytostome about 8 μm in width, ventrally and subapically located. Cytopharynx strengthened by two robust pharyngeal rods (about 50 μm long) with irregular apical teeth; pharyngeal fibers extending to posterior body end (Figs. 1A, B and 2A, D and I). Cytoplasm grayish to colorless, food vacuoles (about 25 × 6 μm) contain yellow or green cyanobacteria, purple pigment vacuoles (4–9 μm in diameter), abundant some granules (about 2 μm in diameter) (Figs. 1A and 2A and F). Rod-shaped bacteria clustered in the middle portion of cell, forming an ellipsoid (Fig. 2F). Single ventrally located contractile vacuole in mid-to-posterior portion of cell; 9 μm in diameter, about 15 s expulsion interval (Figs. 1A, C and 2B). Ellipsoidal heteromeric macronucleus centrally located, about 25 × 11 μm in vivo and 30 × 10 μm post-protargol staining (Figs. 1A, B and 2F, H and K). Micronucleus undetected in either live or stained specimens. Locomotion usually by slow crawling on substrate, or rotation around ventral axis while attached via podite.

Cilia 15–25 μm long, restricted to ventral groove (Figs. 1A and 2A). Constantly six right somatic kineties, each composed of dense groups (3–6 basal bodies each group), posterior ends curved dorsally in hook-like pattern, terminating at same level (Fig. 1D, and 2H; Table 1). Outermost four right kineties equal in length and with anterior ends bending dorsally to form four frontoventral kineties, each containing 137–205 basal bodies, usually three basal bodies positioned near anterior end of frontoventral kinety, two innermost right kineties commencing at different level near cytostome (Fig. 1B, D, and 2H). Seven to nine left kineties situated in the middle region, with densely arranged basal bodies (Fig. 1B, H, K). Equatorial fragment comprised of 6–44 basal bodies, terminal fragment containing 12–18 basal bodies (Fig. 1B, and 2H, J and K).

Oral ciliature located in anterior ventral groove, composed of two circumoral kineties, one preoral kinety, and three left frontal kineties. Preoral kinety positioned above circumoral kineties, both dikinetidal; left frontal kineties adjacent to circumoral kineties, monokinetids (Fig. 1D and 2J).

Etymology

The species-group name bella (Latin adjective; bell beautiful) alludes that this species appears beautiful due to the presence of purple pigment vacuoles.

Morphological description

Size 85–110 × 45–60 μm in vivo; approx. 93 μm × 50 μm after protargol staining; the ratio of length to width about 1.8:1, laterally flattened about 2.6:1 (Table 1). Cell elongate obovate to elliptical in lateral view, with both ends bluntly rounded; anterior part broader than posterior part, dorsal margin straight (Figs. 3A and 4A, B); right plate arched, left one anteriorly arched, medially concave, posteriorly straight (Fig. 3B); right plate 20% wider than left, bearing a marginal rim about 2 μm wide; anterior margin of left plate irregular, posterior hyaline (Figs. 3A and 4A, E). Conspicuous thicker transverse stripes medially on both plates, accompanied by fine transverse stripes in-between (Fig. 4C). Ventral groove C-shaped (Figs. 3A, B and 4A, E). Podite pen nib-shaped, 22 × 8 μm in vivo, subterminal located in ventral groove (Figs. 3A and 4A, F).

Cytostome ovoid, about 7 μm in width, located at anterior 1/4–1/3 of body. Cytopharynx strengthened by two robust pharyngeal rods (each about 40 μm in length); apical teeth irregular; pharyngeal fibers extending to posterior body end (Figs. 3A, D and 4F, H, I and K). Cytoplasm colorless, containing many purple pigment vacuoles (2–6 μm in diameter), food vacuoles (about 7 μm in diameter), and some granules (about 1 μm in diameter); pigment vacuoles fade rapidly when ruptured (Figs. 4 A, B and F–H). Rod-shaped bacteria (about 2 μm in length) attached to mid-posterior ventral surface, or aggregating into an ellipsoidal body (Fig. 4D). Contractile vacuole not observed in vivo (n = 3), but single contractile vacuole pore recognized at posterior third (Figs. 3D and 4I and K). Heteromeric macronucleus ellipsoidal, positioned in middle and lower part of the body, about 45 × 18 μm in vivo, and 37 × 16 μm after protargol staining (Figs. 3A, D and 4H, I, K). Micronucleus undetected both in vivo and post-staining. Locomotion typically by slow substrate crawling or ventral-axis rotational swimming.

Somatic cilia 11–17 μm long in vivo, located within ventral groove (Fig. 4E). Seven right kineties, each mostly composed of groups with 3–6 basal bodies, all extending posteriorly to subcaudal level, terminating at same level (Figs. 3D and 4I and K; Table 1). Outmost five right kineties extending anteriorly to form frontoventral kineties; innermost frontoventral kinety comprising 157–283 basal bodies, accompanied anteriorly by two fragments (each consisting of 2–6 basal bodies); anterior ends of two innermost right kineties commencing near cytostome level (Figs. 3 C, D and 4I, L). Six to nine left kineties positioned mid-body with densely arranged basal bodies (Figs. 3D and 4I and K). Equatorial fragment consisting of 8–25 basal bodies; terminal fragment containing 15–19 basal bodies (Figs. 3C, D and 4I–L).

Oral structure comprised three components, two parallel circumoral kineties of equal length, one preoral kinety positioned above circumoral kineties, both dikinetidal; three left frontal kineties adjacent to circumoral kineties, monokinetids (Figs. 3C, D and 4J).

Morphological description of Qingdao population

Cell size 30–50 × 15–30 μm in vivo, about 42 × 22 μm after protargol staining (Table 2). Ratio of length to width about 1.8:1. Body elliptical in ventral view, dorsoventrally flattened. Ventrally elliptical, anterior end broadly rounded protrudes to left, a conspicuous rostriform protrusion on anterior left (Figs. 5A and 6A, B and E). A rib-like protrusion present at the posterior end (Figs. 5A and 6A, B and G), pellicular conspicuous in ventral side, reticulated (Fig. 6D). Podite leaf-shaped, positioned subterminal ventral side; 7 μm in length (Figs. 5A and 6A, C and G).

Cytostome subapically located, about 7 μm in width, encircled by 34–46 slender pharyngeal rods (about 5 μm long) with small apical teeth, pharyngeal fibers extending to middle body (Figs. 5A, B and 6A, E, J and K). Cytoplasm colorless to gray, containing spherical granules of various sizes (2–5 μm in diameter) and numerous small granules (Figs. 6E, F and H). Two contractile vacuoles (3 μm in diameter), located along the right margin, one in the anterior quarter and the other in the posterior quarter; about 6 s expulsion interval (Figs. 5A and 6E, F). Macronucleus ellipsoidal, centrally located, about 16 × 11 μm in vivo, and 14 × 9 μm after staining (Figs. 5A, C and 6H, J). Micronucleus undetected. Moderate swimming speed, locomotion commonly irregular crawling on substrate, or rotational swimming around ventral-axis.

Somatic cilia 6–9 μm long, 14–18 somatic kineties totally, three or four rightmost kineties extending to posterior end with hook-like curvature; remaining kineties progressively shortened leftward (Figs. 5B–D and 6I and K). Rightmost two kineties extending anteriorly onto left dorsal margin to form frontoventral kineties, outer frontoventral kinety typically interrupted near the level of the cytostome, forming anterior part (21–24 basal bodies) and posterior part (54–83 basal bodies) (Fig. 5B, and 6J, K; Table 2), other somatic kineties commencing near cytostome, leftmost kinety comprised 4–15 basal bodies (Fig. 5B–D, and 6K). Terminal fragment located at left dorsal margin, containing seven or eight basal bodies (Fig. 5B, D and 6J).

Oral ciliature positioned obliquely above cytostome, composed of one preoral kinety and one circumoral kinety, both dikinetidal; preoral kinety with about 7 basal bodies, circumoral kinety with about 9 basal bodies (Fig. 5B, D and 6J).

Gene sequences and phylogenetic analyses

The small subunit ribosomal RNA (SSU rRNA) gene sequences for the three investigated species were determined and deposited in the NCBI database. The sequence of Dysteria armata (accession number: PX919691) is 1,899 bp long with a G + C content of 45.34%. The sequence of the new species, D. bella sp. nov. (accession number: PX919692) possesses 1,514 bp with a G + C content of 45.64%. A pairwise comparison of the aligned SSU rRNA sequences between these two congeneric species revealed 16 nucleotide differences, corresponding to a sequence identity of 98.9%. The sequence of Orthotrochilia agamalievi (accession number: PX919693) is 1,520 bp long with a G + C content of 45.00%, its closest relative within the family Hartmannulidae is Hartmannula sinica (EF623827), sharing 91.28% sequence similarity.

Phylogenetic analyses inferred from SSU rRNA gene sequences, using both Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) algorithms exhibited nearly identical topologies. Therefore, only the ML tree is presented, with nodal support values from both methods (ML/BI) indicated at the branches (Fig. 7). In the resulting phylogeny, the genus Dysteria is paraphyletic, with Mirodysteria decora and Spirodysteria kahli nested among Dysteria species. D. armata and D. bella sp. nov. formed a sister clade with 100% support, which in turn grouped with D. nabia and D. proraefrons. Orthotrochilia agamalievi, the first sequenced representative of the genus Orthotrochilia, clustered within a monophyletic group comprised Hartmannula, Heterohartmannula, Trichopodiella, and Aegyria. Trochilioides recta was positioned outside this group with full support values.

The genus Dysteria was established by Huxley (1857) [30], with Dysteria armata designated as the type species. The original description characterized the species as having an oval body, slightly wider anteriorly than posteriorly, containing one to several purple pigment vacuoles, a single contractile vacuole, a pen nib-shaped podite, and an ellipsoidal structure resembling a thick-walled sac in the mid‑body.

Subsequently, Entz (1884) [31] described an Italian population that closely resembled the type population in terms of body size and general living morphology. However, a notable difference was the number of contractile vacuoles, whereas the original population possessed only a single contractile vacuole situated near the mid-ventral region, the Italian isolate exhibited four contractile vacuoles, one dorsal and three arranged ventrally from the mid-body to the posterior end [30, 31]. Kahl (1931) [7] later summarized the characteristics of both populations. Fauré-Fremiet (1965) [32] provided the first description of the infraciliature for this species, reporting six right kineties, four of which are frontoventral kineties, oral ciliature composed of two circumoral kineties, one preoral kinety, and three left frontal kineties. In addition, this population differed from the two earlier ones in possessing two contractile vacuoles, one located dorsally and the other located in the mid-ventral posterior region. The Chinese population investigated in the present study aligns well with the original description in terms of body size, outline, and key living characteristics, particularly the presence of a single contractile vacuole, purple pigment vacuoles, and the shape of the podite. Furthermore, its ciliary pattern is identical to that described by Fauré-Fremiet (1965) [32]. Therefore, the identification is confirmed, and this work provides more detailed living morphology and the ciliary pattern.

A particular feature noted in the original description was a “beyond the contractile space there is a curious oval body, it appears like a thick-walled sac.” [30]. In the Chinese populations, a rigid, ellipsoidal aggregate of bacterial appearance was observed adjacent to the macronucleus. The comparable structure is also present in the new species Dysteria bella sp. nov. To date, bacterial associations in the subclass Cyrtophoria have primarily been reported as ectosymbionts in species such as D. brasiliensis Faria et al., 1922, D. ozakii Wang et al., 2021, and D. compressa (Gourret and Roeser, 1886) Kahl, 1931 [15]. No previous reports exist of endosymbiotic bacteria, especially one aggregated into an ellipsoid. In Paramecium bursaria, infection of the micronucleus by Holospora acuminata and of the macronucleus by H. curviuscula has been documented [37]. Considering undiscernible micronucleus in the two Dysteria species investigated in the present study, we hypothesize that the “thick-walled sac” described by Huxley and the ellipsoidal bacterial aggregate observed in our study may represent a specific bacterial infection, possibly of the micronucleus. Although the ellipsoidal aggregate observed in association with the micronucleus is hypothesized to represent a possible bacterial endosymbiont based on morphological evidence, ultrastructural (TEM) or molecular (e.g., FISH or 16S rRNA gene sequencing) confirmation is required to verify its nature and taxonomic identity. Unfortunately, due to the depletion of the original sample material and the failure to recollect these two species from the original collection site later, such validation could not be performed in the present study. Future investigations with newly collected material will be essential to resolve this issue.

Comparison of Dysteria bella sp. nov. with closely related congeners (Table 3)

Based on body size and general morphology, Dysteria bella sp. nov. should be compared with 11 congeneric species. The new species can be distinguished from Dysteria armata by its elongate obovate body shape (vs. kidney-shaped) and the presence of a higher number of right kineties (7 vs. 6) [32].

Several species can be separated from D. bella sp. nov. based on distinct differences in the body shape and ciliature pattern. Dysteria brasiliensis Faria et al., 1922 differs by having an elongate-triangular or rectangular body shape (vs. elongate-obovate), a posterior spine (vs. absent), and fewer right kineties (5 vs. 7) [23, 38, 39]. Dysteria crassipes Claparède and Lachmann, 1859 is slightly smaller (50–80 μm in length vs. 85–110 μm) and possesses fewer right kineties (4 vs. 7) than the new species [23]. Dysteria derouxi Gong and Song, 2004 is clearly distinguishable from the new species by its rectangular body outline (vs. elongate obovate), more right kineties (8 or 9 vs. 7), and lower number of frontoventral kineties (3 or 4 vs. 5) [14, 35, 40]. D. lanceolata Claparède and Lachmann, 1859 is smaller (60–80 μm vs. 85–110 μm in length) and has an ovate body shape (vs. elongate obovate) [36]. D. monostyla (Ehrenberg, 1838) Kahl, 1931 differs primarily in having fewer right kineties (5 vs. 7) [20].

Compared with the new species, both D. procera Kahl, 1931 and Dysteria paraprocera Qu et al., 2015 are slender in outline, appear flatter in ventral view, and possess far fewer right kineties (3 vs. 7 in the new species) and frontoventral kineties (2 vs. 5) [18, 34]. Although D. pectinata (Nowlin, 1911) Kahl, 1931 shares the same number of right kineties with the new species, it has only two frontoventral kineties (vs. 5 in the new species) [23]. D. parovalis Wilbert and Song, 2005 and Dysteria proraefrons Clark, 1865 both exhibit a broadly ovate outline (vs. elongate obovate) and differ in the number of both right and frontoventral kineties, distinguishing them from the new species [14, 41].

The taxonomic history of this species dates to 1974, when Agamaliev reported a population from the Caspian Sea but misidentified it as Trochilioides recta, inadvertently conflating the genera Orthotrochilia and Trochilioides. The species was subsequently rediscovered and thoroughly redescribed from the port of Roscoff, France, by Deroux (1976) [3]. Deroux corrected the earlier Caspian record, established the new genus Orthotrochilia, and formally named the species O. agamalievi in honor of Professor Agamaliev [3, 42, 43]. More recently, a Chinese population from the Yellow and Bohai Seas was recorded by Song et al. (2009) [8].

The newly collected Qingdao population closely resembles the three previously reported populations (Caspian Sea, French, and Chinese) in both living morphology and ciliary pattern. Common characters include body length (30–50 μm vs. 50–60 μm, 40–50 μm, and about 40 μm, respectively), elliptical body outline, two contractile vacuoles, comparable number of somatic kineties (14–18 vs. 20–22, 16–18, and 13–15, respectively), and outermost frontoventral kinety fragmented near cytostome [3, 8, 42].

Nevertheless, a few minor, population-dependent differences are as follows: (1) In the French population, the outermost frontoventral kinety remained unfragmented in some individuals, whereas it was consistently fragmented in other populations. (2) A few individuals of the French population exhibited 5–7 short fragments in the anterior of the preoral kinety, a feature lacking in the other populations; Deroux 1976 [3] considered that these were these variant individuals. (3) Equatorial fragment shows unstable presence across populations. It was present in the French population [3], absent in the Caspian and Qingdao populations [42], and occurred only in some individuals of the Chinese population reported by Song et al. 2009 [8].

In addition, the present work provides photomicrographs from live and newly notes a distinct, reticulated pellicular layer on the ventral surface.

SSU rRNA gene phylogeny reveals the positions of three species

Based on the SSU rRNA gene sequence data, the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 7) positions the family Dysteriidae within the subclass Cyrtophoria, representing one of the most derived lineages. The genus Dysteria, characterized by an extremely laterally compressed body, a distinct ventral groove, and the complete reduction of left kineties, consists predominantly of free-living marine species.

The close evolutionary relationship between Dysteria bella sp. nov. and D. armata, corroborated by low sequence divergence, mirrors their morphological resemblance, particularly regarding body size and the number of somatic kineties as well an ellipsoidal aggregate, a possible apomorphy for this subclade (Table 3).

The family Hartmannulidae, characterized by a highly arched dorsal side that encroaches onto the ventral side, represents an early-diverging lineage within the order Dysteriida. This family is closely related to the Dysteriidae, sharing features such as the presence of a podite and the gradually shortening left kineties. Apart from Brooklynella and Trochilioides, the remaining hartmannulids form a well-supported monophyletic core clade. Significantly, this study provides the first molecular data for the genus Orthotrochilia, with Orthotrochilia agamalievi is positioned basal to this core clade with moderate support (80/75/0.99). This placement clearly separates Orthotrochilia from Trochilioides, justifying the identity of Orthotrochilia Deroux [3].

Phylogenetic analysis reveals non-monophyly of the genus Dysteria

In the present analysis, the 19 nominal Dysteria species do not form a monophyletic group but are split into two major clades. Clade I comprises D. brasiliensis, D. compressa, D. crassipes, and D. derouxi, and others; Clade II includes D. procera, D. ozakii, D. nabia, and others, as well as the newly sequenced D. bella sp. nov. and D. armata. At the genus level, the sister relationship between Clade I and Clade II is supported by fully supported values. Notably, the genera Mirodysteria and Spirodysteria are nested within Clade I and Clade II, respectively, rendering the genus Dysteria non-monophyletic.

This raises several possible explanations for this phylogenetic relationship. First, the extreme lateral flattening of the body into two lateral plates and the reduction of the left front kineties to three rows, likely represents a symplesiomorphy inherited from a common ancestor of these lineages. In contrast, the widely spaced kinetosomes in the right kineties of Mirodysteria and the conspicuously twisted body shape of Spirodysteria likely represent adaptive apomorphies that evolved during the diversification of their respective clades, possibly as specialized modifications for interstitial life or crawling on filamentous substrates. Although these two genera share the fundamental laterally compressed body with Dysteria, their derived features distinguish them as separate lineages nested within the larger Dysteria radiation. Second, while the current taxa sampling of Dysteria is relatively broad, information on the genera Mirodysteria and Spirodysteria remains extremely incomplete. The inclusion of additional species or populations, particularly those from undersampled geographical regions, could further clarify the phylogenetic positions of these nested genera and help determine whether their current placement reflects true evolutionary relationships or sampling bias.

Finally, although the SSU rRNA gene is a powerful marker for genus-level phylogenetics, it is relatively conserved and may lack the resolution to fully resolve rapid radiations or deep divergences within the Dysteriidae [29]. Future phylogenomic analyses incorporating multiple genetic markers (e.g., ITS regions, mitochondrial genes, or transcriptomic data) will be essential to test the stability of these clades and to definitively determine whether the non-monophyly of Dysteria reflects true evolutionary history or is an artifact of insufficient phylogenetic signal. This underscores the importance of integrating molecular, morphological, and ecological evidence to refine the taxonomy of the Dysteriidae.

The global distribution of Dysteria

A survey of global distribution (Fig. 8) shows that members of Dysteria (38 species and 98 populations) have been recorded from multiple continents, with the highest species richness (23 species) and most nuclear SSU rRNA sequences (36 out of 40) from China, indicating a strong sampling bias. Other regions, such as France, Brazil, Japan, and the North Sea, harbor fewer species and populations, and most lack molecular data.

The global distribution of Dysteria aligns with the moderate endemicity model proposed for ciliates [5, 18, 44], where some species are cosmopolitan while others may be regionally restricted. However, the current data are heavily biased toward China, which accounts for most species’ records and molecular sequences, limiting our understanding of true biogeographic patterns. As noted for other cyrtophorian lineages [13, 16, 45], undersampling likely obscures distributional ranges. The absence of molecular data from most regions (e.g., France, Brazil, Japan) further hampers phylogenetic assessments of geographic structuring. Future extensive sampling, especially in underrepresented areas, coupled with multi-gene analyses, is necessary to resolve the evolutionary history and biogeography of the Dysteriidae and to test whether the observed non-monophyly reflects true relationships or not.

Conclusions

This study presents an integrative characterization of three cyrtophorian ciliates from urban-adjacent coastal waters in Qingdao, China. The detailed redescription of the type species, Dysteria armata, clarifies its diagnostic boundaries and resolves long-standing ambiguities regarding its morphological variability. The identity of Dysteria bella sp. nov. is robustly supported by both distinct morphological features and its phylogenetic placement as a sister taxon to D. armata. Furthermore, the first acquisition of the SSU rRNA gene sequence for Orthotrochilia agamalievi fills a critical gap in the molecular archives, confirming the validity of the genus Orthotrochilia and its evolutionary distinction from Trochilioides. Collectively, these findings significantly refine the systematics of the families Dysteriidae and Hartmannulidae, underscoring the necessity of combining classical morphological methods with molecular phylogenetics to elucidate the diversity and evolutionary history of neglected microeukaryotes.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, observation and identification

Samples were collected from tide pools in the intertidal zone of Taiping Bay (36°03’00” N; 120°21’34” E) (Fig. 9C–E), Qingdao, China, during the period from 2020 to 2021. Dysteria armata was isolated from the collected samples on 7 October and 2 December 2021, when the water temperatures were 21 °C and 7 °C, the salinities were approximately 25‰ and 30‰, respectively. Dysteria bella sp. nov. was isolated on 21 September 2020, when the water temperature at sampling site was 25 °C and the salinity was approximately 30‰. Orthotrochilia agamalievi was sampled on 2 December 2021 (7 °C, salinity ~ 30). Biofilms and associated organic debris were scraped from the substrate surfaces (rocks or macroalgae) within the tide pools, and approximately 500 mL of in situ seawater was collected. Samples were transported to the laboratory and transferred into Petri dishes (10 cm diameter) for maintenance as raw cultures at room temperature (25 °C). To promote ciliate growth, larger metazoan predators were removed using tweezer under a dissecting microscope. One to two days later, when cells have become abundant in the petri dish, morphological experiments were performed.

Map and photographs of sampling sites. A, B showing the location of Taiping Bay on the map. C, D, E Different tidal pools along the coast of Taiping Bay

Live ciliates were observed with an optical microscope (Zeiss AXIO Imager D2) and their size and characteristics in vivo were recorded. Ciliary pattern and macronucleus were revealed by the protargol staining method [25]. Line drawings of stained specimens were conducted with the aid of a drawing microscope. Terminology is according to Adl et al. 2019 [46] and Lynn 2008 [4].

DNA extraction, PCR amplification and sequencing

For each species, one to three cells were isolated from the raw cultures and washed five times with sterile habitat water. Genome DNA was extracted using DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the SSU rRNA gene was performed with primer sets according to Medlin et al. (1988) [47], 18SF: 5ʹ-AACCTGGTTGATCCTGCCAGT-3ʹ; 18SR: 5ʹ-TGATCCTTCTGCAGGTTCACCTAC-3ʹ. The PCR products were purified using the Easy Pure Quick Gel Extraction Kit (TransGen, China). The sequencing protocol follows exactly that described in Wang et al. 2023 [16].

Phylogenetic analyses

The newly obtained SSU rRNA gene sequences were aligned with 91 sequences of related cyrtophorians and suctorians (selected as the outgroup) retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database using the MAFFT online server (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/server/) [48]. The resulting alignment was manually trimmed at both ends using BioEdit v.7.0.5.2 [49].

Maximum Likelihood (ML) analyses were conducted using RAxML-HPC2 on XSEDE v.8.2.12 [50] via the CIPRES Science Gateway (http://www.phylo.org/portal2) and IQ-TREE v.3.0.1 [51]. Bayesian Inference (BI) was performed using MrBayes v.3.2.7 [52]. Settings of ML analyses and Bayesian inference followed Wang et al. 2025 [10]. All phylogenetic trees were visualized in SeaView v5.0 [53].
