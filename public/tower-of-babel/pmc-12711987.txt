Healthcare waste management practice and associated factors among health workers of public health centers in Adama City, Southern central Ethiopia, 2024: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Derara Workineh Tadesse, Fufa Balcha Tolossa, Worku Dugasa Girsha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealthcare wastes are among the most hazardous wastes worldwide. If not properly managed, healthcare waste can have severe effects on human health and the environment. Safe healthcare waste management practices by health workers are crucial and cost-effective measures to reduce healthcare-associated infections. There are, however, few studies on the extent of healthcare waste management practices among healthcare personnel in developing countries like Ethiopia. This study aims to assess the extent of healthcare waste management practices and associated factors among health workers in public health facilities in Adama City, 2024. A facility-based cross-sectional study design was conducted in the public health Centers of Adama city. A total of 221 health care workers were included in the study from November 4 to December 9, 2024. Data were collected with a self-administered questionnaire. The collected data were checked for completeness, edited, coded, and entered into Epi Info version 7.2.5 & exported to SPSS version 25.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used in the form of text and a table. Binary logistic regression was used to identify associated factors of outcome variables at p-value < 0.05 and 95% confidence interval. Of the total, 102 (46.2%) respondents were practicing proper practice of waste management. Health workers who were reading healthcare waste management manuals (AOR = 3.661,95%CI (1.670, 8.024) and facilities with adequate personnel protective equipment practicing proper healthcare (AOR = 2.236, 95% CI (1.051, 4.56), Having good knowledge of healthcare waste management (AOR = 3.293, 95% CI (1.226, 8.845) and proper attitude toward health care waste managment (AOR = 3.678, 95% CI (1.328, 10.189) were factors associated to healthcare waste management practice. The overall proper practice of healthcare waste management was low compared to different findings. Reading of healthcare waste management manual, availability of adequate personal protective equipment, knowledge level and attitude of health workers were significantly associated with healthcare waste management practice. Thus, stakeholders must work on awareness creation and provide manuals and personal protective equipment. regular monitoring and supervision are needed. Background

It has been widely promoted that every citizen has the right to a clean environment and the right to basic health-care services1. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines healthcare waste as wastes including all the wastes generated by health-care institution establishments, health-care research facilities and medical laboratories, as well as waste from small or dispersed sources, such as waste from at-home medical procedures like insulin injections and dialysis2.

Healthcare waste includes all the waste generated by medical activities, encompassing the activities of diagnosis, prevention, curative, and palliative treatments in the field of human medicine2. Health professionals, patients, and the communities surrounding health institutions are all at risk due to a lack of proper waste management techniques1. Health institutions provide diagnostic, therapeutic, research, and rehabilitative services in an attempt to manage health problems and protect the public from different health risks, generate hazardous waste that can endanger the environment, the general public, and healthcare personnel3.

The WHO estimates that between 75 and 90% of the garbage generated by healthcare providers is “nonhazardous” or “general health-care waste,” which is similar to household waste. The remaining 10–25% of medical waste is classified as “hazardous” or “infectious,” which could lead to several health dangers for people and animals as well as environmental issues4.

Patients, healthcare professionals, the public, and the environment may all be at risk for health problems if healthcare waste (HCW) is not properly managed. At least 50% of the world population is threatened by environmental, occupational, and public health risks due to poor healthcare waste management. The UN Basel Convention considers health care waste as the second most dangerous waste after nuclear waste1. Health care waste can cause serious harm if not managed properly4,5. Improper disposal of HCW, including open dumping and uncontrolled burning, increases the risk of spreading infections and of exposure to toxic emissions from incomplete combustion6.

One sector that has made significant strides globally is the health sector. In developing countries like Ethiopia, where the international or local policy that waste generators are responsible for the proper management, treatment, and disposal of waste has remained on paper and has not yet been implemented, it appears that the percentage of waste generated by healthcare institutions has received less attention than other categories of pollution7,8.

WHO recommends that HCW storage be located away from service units and public areas, in appropriately sized, separate spaces. If space is lacking, daily collection and disposal should be enforced. It is recommended that specific wheeled trolleys, bins, or carts be used for the transportation of waste within facilities9. WHO and Ethiopia’s FMOH recommend color-coded waste segregation: black for non-infectious, yellow with biohazard for infectious, red with biohazard for pathological/anatomical, brown for chemical, yellow with radioactive label for radioactive, and yellow “SHARPS” box with biohazard for sharps1,10.

Occupational needle stick and sharp injury (NSSI) affects around 35 million healthcare workers (HCWs) worldwide each year. As many as 20 blood-borne pathogens can be transmitted by accidental injury; the potentially life-threatening ones are HIV, HBV, and HCV2,11. In 2014, the WHO estimated that about 1.67 million HBV infections, 315,120 cases of HCV, and 33,877 HIV infections were associated with unsafe injections4,5.

Worldwide, the improper management of healthcare worker from its generation, to collection and disposal, is a serious public health concern because of its negative impacts on health workers, waste collectors, the public, and the environment at large. Healthcare workers who are involved in the care of patients and working in managing healthcare wastes are facing occupational hazards because of exposure to sharp injuries and human blood and body fluids1,11. Infectious and sharps waste that is not separated, handled, or disposed of appropriately can lead to bodily harm, disease transmission, environmental contamination (of the air, water, and soil), climate change, and social unrest1.

The fact that HCW is being illegally disposed of and burned in unknown sites, harming people’s and the environment’s health, and that HCW disposal is an issue in other parts of the country cannot be accounted for. In countries with lower incomes, proper HCW management lags well below the suggested standards. According to a WHO assessment conducted in 22 developing countries, the proportion of health facilities ranges from 18 to 64% do not use proper waste disposal methods3,5,12.

Ethiopia is also among where numerous HCW have been dumped in residential areas, where children have been found playing with HCW materials such as syringes. This problem is faced by the black lion specialized teaching hospital, which treated some children with AZT after some were picked with needles & others ate potentially lethal pills they found around the hospital13.

Operations remain highly fragmented since many nations lack rules to direct HCWM operations, even in the face of WHO and locally adopted standards. HCWM is frequently not integrated into the health care delivery system, and many countries lack a strategic direction to guide the development of integrated plans of action. Weak coordination between and among sectors and partners is partly to blame for this. There is also a low rate of adoption of appropriate technologies due to a lack of evidence-based decision-making, locally available technologies, and a general lack of resources for such activities4.

This is likewise the case in Ethiopia, where a few HCWM investigations revealed that the majority of the HCFs under study lacked waste segregation. At every HCF surveyed, medical waste was improperly kept, transported, handled, and disposed of11. Despite three HCWM guidelines prepared by the FMoH, FMHACA, and FEPA independently, there was a gap for the HCFs to enforce them for the proper management of the hazardous waste and a lack of proper compliance with their implementation14.

In contrast to Initiatives like clean and safe health facility (CaSH) to make health facilities clean and healthy areas, credible evidences show that healthcare waste management practices of healthcare expriances across Ethiopian health institutions are inadequate. Poor awareness, lack of protective devices, lack of basic training, and low attention given by the authorities aggravate the seriousness of the problem11. This study will reveal the gap in healthcare waste management practices at healthcare facilities that will be used as baseline information to develop strategies and intervention guidelines for healthcare waste management. Health Centers (HCs) in the country are becoming larger in number, including the study area. Thus, HCs’ management needs specific and detailed status and the gap of these facilities related to HCWM. Therefore, this study aimed to conduct on the practice of HCW management and associated factors of public Health Centers in Adama City.

Methods and materials

Study setting and period

The study was conducted in Adama City Public Health Centers from November 4, 2024, to December 9, 2024. Adama city is one of the cities in the Oromia National State region and is located 99 km southeast of Addis Ababa, the Southern Central Part of Ethiopia. It is situated at an elevation of 1712 m at 8.54°N 39.27°E. The city is situated between the Great Rift Valley to the east and the foot of an escarpment to the west. Adama experiences an average yearly temperature of 20.7 °C (69.3 °F). About 371 mm | 14.6 inches of precipitation falls annually. Administratively, Adama City has 6-subcities and 18 kebeles. The total 2024 estimation of the population under the Adama city catchment was 1,025,148.

Adama city health catchment included has variety public and private health facilities: One public teaching hospital, one public primary hospital, 10 private hospitals(5 general and 5 primary), 15 health centers(including the 2 private HCs), 35 private dental clinics (primary, medium & Special), 139 private medium clinics, 81 private pharmacies, one public pharmacy, 33 primary clinics and 22 specialty clinics &/or centers.

Study design

Institution-based cross-sectional study design was used to assess the magnitude and associated factors of healthcare waste management practice in Adama City.

Population

All healthcare workers working in Public Health Centers included under the catchment of Adama city health office (13 health centers) were the source population, and the study population was all healthcare workers from the selected eight public health centers during the study period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All health workers working in Public Health Centers of Adama City who directly engaged in activities generating both general and hazardous wastes and working for six months & above were included in the study. Health workers who had started working there recently (< 6months) in Public Health Centers during data collection, who were working for free service, or who were part-time workers and administrative and supportive workers were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

Sample size was determined by using the single population proportion formula with the following assumptions: P = 0.526 ~ P = 0.5315 with a margin of error of 5%, and 95% confidence level.

After adding a 10% Non-response rate, the final sample size was 421 study participants. As our total source population was 492, which means less than 10,000, a finite correction formula was implied as follow;

n = n1/(1+n1⁄N) Where: n1 = 421, N = 492.

n= 402/(1+402/492) = 226, Then the final sample 226, was used for the study.

Sampling procedure

The Adama City Office has thirteen public health centers. Out of the 13 public health centers in the Adama City Catchment, eight8 were chosen at random using a lottery method to provide study samples. To improve representativeness, a computer-generated simple random sampling was conducted from a subset of Health Centers following proportion allocation. The sampling frame was obtained from each Health Center’s payroll registration (Fig. 1).

Dependent variable

Healthcare waste management practice.

Independent variable

Socio-demographic factors: Age, Sex, Stream of education, profession category, marital status, education level, work experience, and HBV vaccination.

Data collection tools and techniques

Structured and pretested questionnaires were used to collect data through self-administered questionnaires. The questionnaire that was adapted and modified from different literature was used to collect data. The English version questionnaire was translated & administered in Afan Oromo and Amharic to ease the interview processes. Four selected Clinical II medical students collected the data. The Principal investigator and data collectors were checking the completeness of collected data daily.

Data quality assurance

Before the actual data collection at Awash Melkessa Health Center, 5% (n = 11) of the sample underwent pretesting of the instruments. Based on the result of the pretest, the needed modification was made to the tool. The questionnaire was developed in English and translated to Afan oromo (local language) and then back to English to keep the consistency. Orientation on the objective and overall procedure of the study was given by the principal investigator for supervisors and data collectors. Training was given for data collectors for a day before the pretest on the objectives of the study, the method of data collection, and checking the completeness of the questionnaire.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were checked manually for completeness, coded, and entered into Epi Info version 7.2.5 and exported to SPSS version 25.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics like mean, standard deviation, and proportions were computed to summarize data. The outcome variable was dichotomized, and a binary logistic regression analysis was run between the outcome variable and each of the explanatory variables. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate logistic regression analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression for further analysis to identify variables associated with the dependent variable. P-value < 0.05 was used to declare statistical significance. Adjusted odds ratio with 95% confidence interval was used to state the strength of association. Final results were presented in tables, graphs, or charts.

All methods used in this study adhere to the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Practice of healthcare workers on health centers’ waste management

Only 103 (46.6%) of the 221 individuals donned gloves most of the time and consistently when working with healthcare workers. Approximately 68 (30.8%) and 20% of healthcare professionals, respectively, fill safety boxes and infectious containers more than three-fourths of the way. About 190(86%) were wearing gowns while handling HCW and patients. Only 62(28%) and 45(20.4%) participants were wearing an Apron and safety equipment, respectively.

In a similar vein, roughly 30.3% of research participants lacked any criteria that would support the HCWM practice. Of the responders, twenty (9%) had used improper methods for sanitizing reusable items. Most Working Units of 217(98.2%) respondents had waste disposal, among which plastic buckets accounted for 75.6%. Only 24(10.9%) participants reported color-coded dustbins and waste segregation at the point of generation. Only about 37 (16.7%) of the study participants’ healthcare personnel covered the waste container when transporting HCW to ultimate disposal locations. Almost all, 215(97.3%) respondents answered that Healthcare waste was transported within a 24-hour duration. According to reports, the most popular technique for handling contagious, sharp debris is incineration. The most common method used for the treatment of waste in study facilities was incineration in 87.5% Health Centers (One Health Center had no functional Incinerator).

Of the total 221 respondents, those who scored mean and above the mean value, 119 (53.8%) of them had improper practice of Health Center waste management, and 102(46.2%) of participants had proper practice of waste management (Table 5).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the independent predictors of HCWM practice among health workers in Public Health Centers. It indicated that reading the HCWM manual or guidelines related to waste handling, receiving training on HCW management and handling, having adequate PPE, possessing a positive attitude, and having good knowledge about HCWM were significantly associated with HCWM practice at a p-value less than 0.05.

Accordingly, the Odds of good practicing healthcare waste handling among health workers who received training on HCWM were higher by more than two times higher (AOR = 2.302(1.090, 4.859), with p-value of 0.029) than those who did not.

This study also found that the odds of healthcare waste management practice of health care workers who had ever read the HCWM manual/guideline were higher by 3.66 times higher (AOR = 3.661(1.670, 8.024), p < 0.001), and more likely to have good HCWM practice than their counterparts. The Odds of practicing healthcare waste management where adequate personal protective equipment was about two times (AOR = 2.236(1.051, 4.56) at p-0.037) compared to those who had not.

The odds of healthcare workers who had good knowledge and a favorable attitude of good HCWM practice were about three times (AOR = 3.293(1.226, 8.845) and three and a half times (AOR = 3.678 (1.328, 10.189) respectively, with significant P-values (Table 7).

Discussion

The level of HCWM practice and potential contributing factors among health workers in public health facilities based on the suggested HCWM process (segregation, collection, storage, transportation, treatment, and disposal) have been usefully demonstrated by this study, supporting current policies and future investigations.

This study revealed that 46.2% of Health workers of Adama City Public Health centers were practicing proper healthcare waste management, whereas 53.8% of health workers performed improper healthcare waste management practice. This is consistent with the study done in Public Hospitals in South West shewa, Oromia regional state, which showed that 47.6% of healthcare workers were doing proper healthcare waste management practice and 52.4% performed improper practice11.

This finding is less than study conducted in Kenya, Kenyatta Hospital (59%), South African Hospital (79%), Bahir Dar City Hospital(65.3%), Ilu Abba Bor Zone on health Workers of Private health facilities(58.7%) and compared meta-analysis done Among Healthcare Workers in Health Facilities in Ethiopia revealed the pooled HCWM practices in Ethiopia was 52.86%3,15,16,18,19. This discrepancy could be explained by variations in healthcare system policies, how standard guidelines are applied, and the degree of regulatory body supervision and control over the use of HCWM practices assessment tools by private healthcare facilities in the various studies. The discrepancy in the healthcare planners’ attitudes toward the practice of HCWM may also be the cause. The inclusivity of medical facilities, the study time, and the study environment may also support this.

In contrast, this study is higher than the study done on health workers in Eastern Ethiopia, Jigjiga showed that 42.3% of respondents had good practice on healthcare waste management17. The current study was conducted recently, when healthcare management practices are improving due to increased awareness and attitude of healthcare workers, due to information, education, and communication. This could be one of the possible reasons for this variation, as the study period differed. A further explanation for this discrepancy can be the sample size, participant knowledge, and the use of various HCWM practice assessment instruments in various research.

Out of 221 participants, only 24(10.9%) reported for color color-coded dust bins and waste segregation at the point of generation. This finding is lower than the study conducted in Kenya(67.3%), Bale Zone hospitals(53.8%), Private Facilities in Ilu Abba Bor Zone(78.4%), Hospitals in South West Shewa (40.63%), and Governmental Health centers in Addis Ababa(50%)3,8,10,11. In Contrast, the current finding of waste segregation is higher compared to the South African Hospital20. This discrepancy could be explained by variations in healthcare system policies, how standard guidelines are applied, and the degree of regulatory body supervision and control over the use of HCWM practices assessment tools by private healthcare facilities in the various studies. Additionally, this may also be justified by the fact that the study setting, study time, and inadequate compliance of stakeholders toward HCWM recommendations.

Additionally, the current study found a number of characteristics related to healthcare waste management techniques. Accordingly, this study has revealed that Health Centers with healthcare workers who were reading HCWM manuals practiced good healthcare waste management more than three times than those that did not. This study is in line with one that was carried out among health care workers at private healthcare facilities in the Ilu Abba Bor Zone. It also revealed that Health Workers who were trained on healthcare waste management were practicing twice as good healthcare waste management compared to their counterparts. This finding was in line with a study done in South West Shewa (4 times), Gondar(2.29 times), and a systematic meta-analysis done in Ethiopia(3.49 times)3,11,15,21. This could be because Health Centers’ staff gained more awareness by reading and being trained on the manuals/guidelines of the healthcare waste management protocol. In addition, reading guidelines improves the subject understanding of health workers, which promotes effective HCWM practices.

According to this study, about 137(62%) health workers had good knowledge of Healthcare waste management practice, and these workers practiced proper Healthcare waste management 3-folds compared with those with low knowledge. This finding is consistent with the findings of studies done in Ilu Abba Bor Zone private Health facilities, Kenya, South West Shewa Zone Public Hospitals, and Gondar3,11,19,22. The similarity may arise from the fact that skilled healthcare professionals would more effectively separate waste, which in turn dictates the waste disposal system, therefore enhancing excellent practice.

This study showed that 123(55.7%) health workers reported that there was adequate personal protective equipment and revealed that practicing good Healthcare waste handling two times compared to those who did not have adequate PPE. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Kenya and in contrast with a study done in Ilu Abba Bor Zone among health workers in Private Health Facilities3,16,19. The disparity of this might be attributed to the difference in study time, data collection tools, and the level of control & enforcement of law/regulation of HCW of the facility. Furthermore, the difference could be due to the study setting (Private health facilities, as they are more business-oriented, they may not provide adequate PPE).

In this study, healthcare workers who had a favorable attitude toward healthcare waste management (HCWM) were about three and a half times more likely to practice good HCWM compared to those with an unfavorable attitude. This finding indicates that attitude plays a crucial role in shaping proper waste management behavior among healthcare professionals. Similar associations have been reported in studies conducted in South Africa23 and Ethiopia, Addis Ababa24, where healthcare workers with favorable attitudes were significantly more likely to practice safe HCWM than their counterparts. The consistency across different settings may be due to the fact that favorable attitudes enhance workers’ sense of responsibility and awareness regarding the health and environmental risks of poor HCWM. However, slight variations in the magnitude of association across studies could be attributed to differences in training coverage, institutional support, or enforcement of HCWM policies.

Despite offering insightful information about HCWM practices and related factors, this study has a limitation: the cross-sectional methodology precludes drawing inferences about causality. It was restricted to solid HCWM among employees of public health centers; hospitals and private facilities were not included. Estimates might have been off due to the lack of standardized instruments and the use of self-reported data.

Conclusion

According to this study, the overall Proper practice of healthcare waste management by healthcare workers of Public Health Centers in Adama City was low compared to different findings in Ethiopia as well as in Africa. This study found that reading manuals, the presence of adequate waste management protective equipment, being trained on HCWM practice of workers, favorable workers’ attitude, and good knowledge about HCWM were the identified factors associated with good HCWM practice, with improvements ranging from two- to three-fold. It recommends that the Ministry of health, Oromia Health Bureau, and Adama City Health Office ensure the availability of manuals, conduct continuous training and supervision, provide adequate personnel protective equipment (PPE), and address gaps in implementation.
