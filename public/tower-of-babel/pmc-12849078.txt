Prevalence and multimodal factors associated with impaired kidney function among persons with and without HIV in a routine clinic setting: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Cornelius Simutanda, Nelson Wandira, Luyando Mutelo, Joreen P Povia, Benson M Hamooya, Evelyn Funjika, Annet Kirabo, Sepiso K Masenga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis cross-sectional study aimed to determine the prevalence and factors associated with impaired kidney function (IKF), defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m², in a mixed HIV serostatus cohort attending a routine care clinic at Livingstone University Teaching Hospital in Zambia.Results descriptionThe overall prevalence of IKF was 12.0% (29/241) and was significantly higher in people living with HIV (PLWH) than those without (17.0% vs. 4.3%, p = 0.003). Sequential multivariable analyses revealed consistent associations. Multivariable analysis using a data-driven Firth’s penalized-likelihood logistic regression model identified HIV infection (Adjusted Odds Ratio [AOR] = 7.04; 95% CI 2.15–22.94; p = 0.001), increased interventricular septum diameter in diastole (AOR = 39.56; 95% CI 5.20-300.80; p = 0.0003), increased right ventricular dimension in diastole (AOR = 6.01; 95% CI 1.64–22.03; p = 0.007), and higher serum uric acid levels (AOR = 1.01 per mmol/L; 95% CI 1.00-1.01; p = 0.009) to be independently associated with IKF. A clinical model confirmed associations with older age, HIV, and higher fasting blood sugar. When adjusting for these clinical factors, higher levels of soluble ST2, uric acid, and spot microalbumin remained significantly associated with IKF. This study reveals a high burden of undetected kidney impairment in a routine clinic setting, disproportionately affecting PLWH. These findings warrant validation in larger prospective studies.Keywords: Impaired kidney function, eGFR, HIV, Kidney dysfunction, Uric acid, Echocardiography

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a major and growing public health challenge worldwide, affecting over 800 million individuals and contributing significantly to global mortality and morbidity [1]. Its insidious nature often leads to late diagnosis, at which point progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) is frequently inevitable, necessitating dialysis or transplantation, treatments that remain largely inaccessible in many low-resource settings like Zambia, leading to high mortality [2]. Early detection through routine screening is therefore paramount to implement timely interventions that can slow disease progression [3].

The epidemiology of CKD is particularly complex in the context of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. The advent of effective antiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV into a chronic manageable condition, consequently unmasking and amplifying the risk of non-communicable diseases, including CKD [4, 5]. PLWH face a heightened risk of renal impairment due to a confluence of factors, including direct viral effects, ART-related nephrotoxicity, and a higher prevalence of traditional risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes [6, 7]. Despite this recognized vulnerability, routine screening for kidney disease in HIV care settings is not universally standardized, and many cases may go undetected until advanced stages.

Furthermore, the understanding of risk factors for CKD is evolving beyond traditional markers. Biomarkers of inflammation (e.g., Interleukin-6 [IL-6], soluble Suppression of Tumorigenicity 2 [sST2]), neurohormonal activation (e.g., aldosterone, angiotensin), and metabolic intermediates like uric acid are increasingly implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of renal dysfunction [8–10]. Additionally, the intricate bidirectional cardiorenal axis suggests that structural cardiac changes may be closely linked to renal impairment, offering potential early clues for diagnosis [11, 12]. However, the interplay of these multimodal factors: demographic, serological, and echocardiographic, in a cohort including both PLWH and HIV-negative individuals in a routine clinic setting remains inadequately characterized.

The primary objective of this study was to determine the prevalence and the comprehensive set of factors associated with IKF among persons with and without HIV attending a routine clinic visit. By elucidating these associations, this study aims to provide robust evidence on the yield of spot screening and inform the development of targeted screening strategies in similar patient populations.

Main text

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional study conducted at the medical outpatient clinic of Livingstone University Teaching Hospital in Livingstone, Zambia. This clinic provides general medical services and dedicated HIV care, serving a diverse patient population of both PLWH and HIV-negative individuals, making it an ideal setting for this comparative investigation.

Definition of variables

The primary outcome, impaired kidney function (IKF), was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m², calculated from serum creatinine using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation (race-free) [13]. Hypertension was defined as a self-reported diagnosis, current use of antihypertensive medication, or a blood pressure reading ≥ 140/90 mmHg.

Sample size calculation and sampling strategy

A power calculation was performed to justify the sample size. Assuming a prevalence of IKF of 10% in the general clinic population and a doubling of odds associated with key exposures (e.g., HIV), a sample size of 241 participants provided over 80% power at an alpha of 0.05 to detect significant associations in a logistic regression model. Sampling was purposeful; consecutive eligible patients attending the clinic for routine visits during the study period were invited to participate until the target sample size was achieved.

Study participants and inclusion criteria

The study enrolled adult patients aged 18 years and above, both living with and without HIV, who presented for a routine clinic visit between 6th April 2024 and 10th August 2024. All persons living with HIV were on antiretroviral therapy (Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF, 300 mg), Lamivudine (3TC, 300 mg) and Dolutegravir (DTG, 50 mg)) and were virally suppressed. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, known end-stage renal disease on renal replacement therapy, and acute febrile illness at the time of recruitment, as these could transiently affect kidney function measurements.

Data collection and measurements

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire and through abstraction from medical records. Collected data included demographic and clinical data (Age, sex, HIV status, duration on ART for PLWH, smoking, peripheral artery disease and history of hypertension), anthropometric measurements (Weight, height, body mass index (BMI), and waist circumference (WC)) measured using standard protocols.

Blood pressure was measured twice in a seated position after a 5-minute rest, and the average of the two readings was used for analysis.

Biochemical analyses

Following an overnight fast, venous blood samples were drawn for the measurement of serum creatinine (for eGFR calculation), fasting blood sugar, lipid profile (total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol), uric acid, aldosterone, angiotensin, IL-6, and sST2. All assays were performed in the hospital’s accredited central laboratory. A spot urine sample was collected for the measurement of microalbuminuria.

Echocardiography

A comprehensive transthoracic echocardiogram was performed by a certified sonographer using a DW-P30 D/5D color doppler portable ultrasound diagnosis system according to the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines. Measurements were performed offline by a single, experienced cardiologist who was blinded to the participants’ clinical and laboratory data. Measurements included right ventricular dimension in diastole (RVDd), left ventricular dimension in diastole (LVDd), ejection fraction (EF), interventricular septum diameter in end-diastole (IVSd), left ventricular posterior wall diameter in end-diastole (LVPWd), and derived left ventricular mass index (LVMI).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and StatCrunch, Pearson’s web-based statistical software. Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test, as their distribution was non-normal. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate logistic regression was performed to assess the crude association between each variable and IKF. To enhance clinical interpretability, odds ratios for IL-6 and sST2 are reported per 10-unit increase, and for uric acid, aldosterone, and angiotensin per 100-unit increase.

For the multivariable analysis, we addressed the issue of potential model overfitting and perfect separation caused by several strong predictors and the limited number of outcome events. We employed a sequential modeling approach to provide different perspectives on the factors associated with IKF. The outcome variable for all regression models was IKF.

First, we constructed a data-driven model (Model 1) using variables from Table 1, primarily those with a univariate p-value < 0.05. This pool included demographic factors (age, sex), clinical status (HIV, hypertension), metabolic biomarkers (fasting blood sugar, lipids, uric acid), neurohormonal and inflammatory biomarkers (aldosterone, angiotensin, IL-6, sST2), and echocardiographic parameters. Variables were pre-screened for missingness and low variance. Highly correlated predictors were pruned. We then used a stepwise selection procedure constrained to a maximum of 6 variables to ensure a parsimonious model, with the cross-validated Area Under the Curve (AUC) as the selection criterion. The final model from this selection process was refit using Firth’s penalized-likelihood logistic regression to reduce small-sample bias and provide stable estimates. Firth’s bias-reduced logistic regression (penalized likelihood), which handles separation and yields finite, stable estimates is an established approach for small-sample or separation-prone data.

Subsequently, we built a second model (Model 2) based on a priori physiological and clinical knowledge, including age, HIV status, fasting blood sugar, and hypertension, using standard logistic regression.

Finally, we created a third set of models (Model 3) to evaluate specific biomarkers of interest (sST2, peripheral artery disease, smoking, uric acid, and spot microalbumin). Each biomarker was evaluated in a separate logistic regression model adjusted for the core clinical covariates from Model 2 (age, HIV, fasting blood sugar, and hypertension). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 241 participants were included in the final analysis. The overall median age was 48 years (IQR: 39–59 years), and 160 (66.4%) were female. Of the participants, 147 (61.0%) were living with HIV. Among PLWH, the median duration on ART was 10 months (IQR: 7, 16). The prevalence of impaired kidney function (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) in the entire cohort was 12.0% (n = 29). The prevalence of IKF was significantly higher in PLWH compared to HIV-negative individuals (17.0% [25/147] vs. 4.3% [4/94]; p = 0.003), Table 1.

Notably, several biochemical and inflammatory markers were significantly elevated in the IKF group, including fasting blood sugar, aldosterone, angiotensin, IL-6, sST2, and uric acid, Table 1. Spot microalbumin was also significantly higher in those with IKF.

The results of the sequential multivariable logistic regression analyses are presented in Table 2. To ensure model stability given the limited number of outcome events, we initially employed Firth’s penalized-likelihood method in an agnostic, data-driven model (Model 1). This analysis identified four factors independently associated with IKF: increased interventricular septum diameter in diastole (Adjusted Odds Ratio [AOR] = 39.56; 95% CI 5.20-300.80; p = 0.0003), HIV infection (AOR = 7.04; 95% CI 2.15–22.94; p = 0.0012), increased right ventricular dimension in diastole (AOR = 6.01; 95% CI 1.64–22.03; p = 0.0067), and higher serum uric acid levels (AOR = 1.01 per mmol/L increase; 95% CI 1.00-1.01; p = 0.0089).

Given the strong, independent association of echocardiographic parameters in Model 1, we constructed a second model based on established physiological and clinical risk factors to provide a more traditional clinical context (Model 2). We found that older age (AOR = 1.05 per year; 95% CI 1.00-1.09; p = 0.0225), HIV infection (AOR = 4.61; 95% CI 1.47–14.45; p = 0.0087), and higher fasting blood sugar (AOR = 1.41 per mmol/L; 95% CI 1.00-1.99; p = 0.0468) were significantly associated with IKF. Hypertension was not significantly associated with IKF in this model (AOR = 1.07; 95% CI 0.43–2.65; p = 0.8727).

We further explored the role of specific biomarkers by evaluating their association with IKF after adjusting for the core clinical factors in Model 2 (age, HIV, fasting blood sugar, and hypertension). In this analysis (Model 3), each biomarker was entered into a separate model adjusted for these core factors. We found that higher levels of soluble ST2 (sST2) (AOR = 1.02 per 10 pg/mL increase; 95% CI 1.00-1.05; p = 0.0385), spot microalbumin (AOR = 1.06 per 10 mg/L increase; 95% CI 1.01–1.12; p = 0.0218), and uric acid (AOR = 1.48 per 100 µmol/L increase; 95% CI 1.08–1.89; p = 0.0178) remained significantly associated with IKF. In these adjusted models, peripheral artery disease and smoking history were not significantly associated with the outcome.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study conducted in a routine hospital clinic setting found a high prevalence (12%) of impaired kidney function, with HIV infection emerging as one of the strongest independent predictors. The prevalence was significantly higher in PLWH (17%) than in their HIV-negative counterparts (4%). The findings underscore the critical importance of integrated renal screening within primary care and HIV clinics, as spot testing identified a significant burden of undiagnosed renal impairment.

The strong independent association between HIV seropositivity and IKF (AOR = 7.04) aligns with a substantial body of literature confirming the elevated nephropathy risk among PLWH [5, 7]. This persists in the ART era and may be driven by both traditional risk factors and HIV-specific pathways, including residual viral replication, chronic immune activation, and ART toxicity [14]. The fact that ART duration itself was not significant in the multivariable model suggests that the risk is inherent to HIV infection itself or related to factors not captured by duration alone, such as specific drug regimens. In our study, PLWH were all on Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) based regimen, a potentially nephrotoxic drug. This could explain the disproportional burden of IKF in PLWH. Furthermore, several biomarkers significant in univariate analysis, including IL-6, sST2, aldosterone, and angiotensin, were not retained in the final multivariable model. This is likely due to multicollinearity, as these markers share overlapping biological pathways involving inflammation, fibrosis, and neurohormonal activation (the RAAS pathway), and their effects may be captured by the stronger, independent predictors in the model.

A novel and striking finding of our study is the profound independent association between echocardiographic parameters and IKF. A thickened IVSd was the strongest predictor of IKF. This finding illuminates the core of the cardiorenal axis. While hypertension is a common cause of both left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and CKD, the persistence of this association in a stable model suggests that subclinical cardiac structural remodeling may be a potent marker or perhaps even a precursor to renal dysfunction, reflecting shared underlying pathophysiological processes such as chronic pressure overload, volume expansion, and neurohormonal activation. Similarly, an enlarged right ventricular dimension was also an independent predictor, a finding less commonly reported that may reflect volume overload or shared pathways in cardiorenal pathophysiology.

The independent role of uric acid is also noteworthy. While often dismissed as an epiphenomenon of reduced renal excretion, emerging evidence suggests hyperuricemia may be a direct contributor to renal injury through inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and activation of the renin-angiotensin system [8]. The association of elevated uric acid and altered cardiac structure with IKF is consistent with prior literature, which lends external validity to our findings [15–19].

The findings from our sequential modeling approach provide complementary insights. The data-driven model (Model 1) highlighted the profound association of subclinical cardiac structural changes with IKF, which may serve as potent markers of shared pathophysiology along the cardiorenal axis. However, to contextualize these findings within established clinical knowledge, we constructed a physiological model (Model 2) using a priori risk factors. This model confirmed the expected independent associations of older age and higher fasting blood sugar with IKF. Critically, it also reinforced HIV infection as a strong predictor (AOR = 4.61) even after adjusting for these traditional factors, underscoring the specific vulnerability of PLWH that extends beyond conventional risk profiles. The absence of a significant independent association for hypertension in this model may reflect the specific characteristics of our cohort or suggest that its effects are mediated through other pathways captured in the data-driven model.

We further investigated specific biomarkers by evaluating their association with IKF after adjustment for the core clinical factors in Model 2. In this analysis (Model 3), higher levels of soluble ST2, a biomarker of myocardial stress and fibrosis, remained significantly associated with IKF. This reinforces the involvement of inflammatory and fibrotic pathways in renal impairment. The persistent association of uric acid after clinical adjustment supports its potential role as an active contributor to renal injury rather than merely a consequence of reduced excretion. Similarly, the independent association of spot microalbuminuria underscores its value as a marker of glomerular damage even in a single measurement.

The consistency of HIV as a key factor across all models, alongside the identified roles of cardiac structure, uric acid, and specific biomarkers, suggests a complex interplay of factors driving IKF in this setting. The strong associations in the data-driven model (Model 1) must be interpreted with caution given the small sample size and potential for overfitting, despite our use of penalized regression. The more conservative effects observed in Models 2 and 3 likely provide a more realistic estimate of the strength of these associations.

Limitations

The interpretations of this study are constrained by several limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design precludes any determination of causality; we can only identify associations. Secondly, the single-center design and purposeful sampling may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations and settings. Thirdly, the definition of IKF was based on a single eGFR measurement and spot microalbuminuria, confirming persistence over three months would have strengthened the CKD diagnosis per guidelines [3]. Furthermore, we lacked detailed history of specific ART regimens which could provide deeper insights into the HIV-related risk. Finally, and most importantly, the relatively small number of IKF cases (n = 29), particularly the small number of HIV-negative individuals with IKF (n = 4), is a major limitation. This affects the precision of our estimates, particularly the magnitude of the odds ratios in Model 1, and limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions, especially regarding the contribution of HIV.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a high prevalence of impaired kidney function among patients attending a routine medical clinic, with a consistently observed disproportionate burden among persons living with HIV across multiple analytical models. The findings suggest that alongside traditional risk factors like age and dysglycemia, factors such as serum uric acid, cardiac structure, and specific biomarkers like sST2 and microalbuminuria may be relevant in this population. While causal inferences cannot be drawn and the estimates require validation in larger studies, these results highlight the potential value of routine spot screening for kidney disease in similar clinical settings, particularly within HIV care programs. An integrated approach that considers both traditional and novel risk markers may improve risk stratification. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these associations and elucidate the underlying causal pathways.
