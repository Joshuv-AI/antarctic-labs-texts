Emerging gene therapies in the treatment of recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa: a systematic review
Authors: Abolfazl Nikkhoy, Amirhossein Larijani, Erfan Rajabi, Hojat Eftekhari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (RDEB) is a life-limiting collagen type VII alpha-1 (COL7A1)-related disorder characterized by skin fragility, chronic wounds, and early mortality. Gene therapies aim to restore collagen type VII (C7) expression and improve wound healing, but clinical evidence remains limited.Objective:To synthesize current evidence on the safety and effectiveness of gene therapy approaches for RDEB.Methods:Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, we searched major databases through 7 March 2025 for interventional studies evaluating gene therapies in RDEB. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment. Given the substantial heterogeneity, findings were synthesized narratively without meta-analysis.Results:Five clinical trials met the inclusion criteria and evaluated ex vivo autologous cell-based therapies and in vivo topical vector delivery. Topical herpes simplex virus type 1-based COL7A1 therapy achieved a significantly higher rate of complete wound closure than controls (71% versus 20% at 3 months; P < 0.001). Ex vivo epithelial grafting demonstrated durable clinical benefit during long-term follow-up. Molecular analyses generally showed restoration or increased C7 expression. Patient-reported outcomes improved alongside sustained wound healing. Treatments were generally well tolerated, with predominantly mild adverse events, no treatment-related serious safety signals, and evidence of immunologic tolerance to recombinant C7.Conclusion:Gene therapy for RDEB demonstrates a favorable early safety profile and promising efficacy. However, the small number of heterogeneous early-phase trials and limited follow-up preclude definitive conclusions. Larger, rigorously designed studies with extended follow-up are required to confirm long-term safety and clinical effectiveness.

Introduction

Recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (RDEB) is a severe, often life-limiting genodermatosis caused by biallelic loss-of-function mutations in the collagen type VII alpha-1 (COL7A1) gene, which encodes collagen type VII (C7), the essential component of anchoring fibrils at the dermal–epidermal junction (DEJ)[1,2]. This structural deficit results in profound skin fragility, leading to widespread blistering, chronic non-healing wounds, pseudosyndactyly, and joint contractures from early childhood[2,3]. Systemic complications, including esophageal strictures, severe anemia, and highly aggressive cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs), contribute to significant morbidity and a reduced lifespan, often under 30 years for severe variants[3,4]. Current management is exclusively palliative, focusing on wound care, infection prevention, and pain control, but fails to address the underlying genetic cause[5]. Consequently, gene therapy has emerged as a promising strategy to directly correct the genetic defect. This encompasses two main strategies: gene augmentation (transferring a functional COL7A1 transgene) and genome editing (correcting the endogenous mutant alleles). Ex vivo approaches involve harvesting patient keratinocytes, genetically correcting them with viral vectors (e.g., retroviruses), and transplanting the engineered epidermal sheets onto wounds. Early-phase trials have demonstrated sustained wound healing, with one study reporting ≥50% healing in 70% of treated areas, persisting for over 5 years[1,8]. In parallel, in vivo topical approaches, such as the herpes simplex virus-based gel Beremagene geperpavec (B-VEC), have shown efficacy in randomized trials, successfully restoring C7 and accelerating wound re-epithelialization with a favorable safety profile[3,8]. Furthermore, precise genome-editing tools like clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9), base editors, and prime editors have shown preclinical success in correcting COL7A1 mutations in patient-derived cells, leading to functional C7 expression and anchoring fibril formation in animal models[6,8].

However, significant translational hurdles remain, including challenges with efficient in vivo delivery, potential off-target effects, immune responses, and manufacturing scalability. Existing studies also vary greatly in vector design, endpoints, and follow-up duration, lacking head-to-head comparisons and standardized long-term data. Therefore, a systematic review is needed to synthesize the available evidence on the efficacy and safety profiles of these diverse gene therapy modalities, identify the most promising approaches, and guide the future development of curative treatments for RDEB.

Methods

Study design

All steps of this systematic review were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines[9]. This systematic review was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251121097) before its design and conduct.

Eligibility criteria

The review question was developed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, Study design (PICOS) structure. The population comprised individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of RDEB. Interventions of interest encompassed any form of gene therapy, including gene augmentation, genome-editing platforms (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9), ribonucleic acid (RNA)-directed treatments, topical gene delivery systems (e.g., B-VEC), gene reactivation approaches, induced pluripotent stem cell-derived strategies, advanced protein or combination regimens, and ex vivo cell therapies with intentional genetic modification. Acceptable comparators were placebo, vehicle control, standard wound care, or no comparator; single-arm trials were therefore eligible. The primary endpoint was wound closure/epithelialization rate; secondary endpoints included C7 expression, reconstitution of anchoring fibrils, patient-reported measures (pain, itch, health-related quality of life), and adverse events covering safety, immunogenicity, and malignant transformation.

Articles were eligible if they (1) enrolled human participants with confirmed RDEB, (2) evaluated one or more of the specified gene-based interventions, and (3) used an interventional study design, comprising randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or non-randomized interventional studies (including single-arm and controlled phase I–III clinical trials). Studies were excluded if they (1) focused on epidermolysis bullosa subtypes other than RDEB, (2) were preclinical or conducted in animals, (3) used non-interventional designs (e.g., reviews, meta-analyses, observational studies, case reports or case series, cross-sectional or case–control studies, editorials), or (4) tested interventions that could not be classified as gene or cell therapies involving deliberate genetic modification. Studies published in English were eligible; non-English publications were excluded if no English translation or abstract was available.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted to obtain articles in databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and the top 500 articles in Google Scholar up to 7 March 2025, without publication date restrictions. The search strategy included terms and keywords related to concepts such as “Recessive Dystrophic Epidermolysis Bullosa,” “Gene Therapy,” and “Intervention,” as well as Boolean operators (AND, OR). The search syntax used in PubMed is as follows, and it has been modified for the other databases while maintaining the same assumptions: (“Epidermolysis Bullosa” OR (EB[tiab])) AND (“Genetic Therapy” OR “Genetic Techniques” OR (“Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeat*”[tiab]) OR (CRISPR[tiab]) OR (“Antisense Oligonucleotid*”[tiab]) OR (AON[tiab]) OR (mRNA[tiab]) OR (protein[tiab]) OR (gene[tiab]) OR (“stem cell*”[tiab])) AND ((intervention*[tiab]) OR (experiment*[tiab]) OR (trial[tiab]) OR (quasi-experimental[tiab]) OR (“Randomized clinical trial”[tiab]) OR (RCT[tiab])). The search strategy used in other databases is presented in Supplemental Digital Content Appendix 1, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B376

Study selection

All records retrieved from the database searches were imported into EndNote 21.0, and duplicates were identified and removed using automated deduplication followed by manual verification. Screening proceeded in two sequential stages. In Stage 1, two reviewers (A and B) independently screened all titles and abstracts against the predefined eligibility criteria; records were excluded only when both reviewers agreed they were clearly ineligible, and any record considered potentially eligible by either reviewer was retained for full-text review. In Stage 2, the same two reviewers (A and B) independently assessed the full text of all retained records for final eligibility. Disagreements at both stages were resolved through structured discussion; unresolved disagreements were adjudicated by a third reviewer (D.).

Data extraction and management

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (A and C) using a standard data extraction form. The data extraction form captured study identification (first author, year, country), study design (phase, randomization, blinding), sample size, target gene, carrier type, delivery method, method of evaluation, intervention description, and key outcomes. Any discrepancies between extractors were identified through cross-comparison of completed forms and resolved by consensus discussion; if consensus was not reached, a third reviewer (D) made the final decision. Authors of included studies were not contacted for missing data in this review, as all required information was available in published reports.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was assessed and reviewed by two independent reviewers (A and B) for included articles, using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB II) for randomized trials[10] the ROBINS-I tool (for non-randomized studies)[11] and a narrative quality assessment approach (for preclinical studies). Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis

In this study, quantitative synthesis was considered inappropriate because of substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included studies. Clinical heterogeneity was evident in patient characteristics, disease severity, gene therapy platforms (including ex vivo, in vivo, and genome-editing approaches), intervention protocols, and follow-up duration. Methodological heterogeneity arose from differences in study design (randomized and non-randomized trials), trial phase, sample size, comparator groups, and outcome assessment methods. We therefore conducted a systematic review, following the synthesis without meta-analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline, to ensure transparent and reproducible methods[12]. Studies were grouped by intervention strategy and outcome domain, with predefined standard criteria for each domain where feasible; grouping and selection criteria were reported and justified to show how study-level data guided the synthesis. Three complementary SWiM-recommended approaches were combined: (1) descriptive tabulation of study characteristics and raw results to preserve study-level detail; (2) vote counting by direction of effect (improvement, no change, worsening) across studies within each domain, considering sample size and precision to identify the predominant direction of evidence; and (3) pooled P-value synthesis for studies testing comparable clinical endpoints, enabling evidence aggregation across small heterogeneous trials while avoiding inappropriate pooling of point estimates.

Results

Study selection

Initially, 6351 articles were retrieved through a systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and the first 500 Google Scholar articles. After removing 1741 duplicate articles, 4610 articles were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 4593 articles were excluded due to an irrelevant study design, an animal study population, a non-interventional approach, a different disease subgroup, or unavailability of full text. The full texts of the remaining 17 articles were assessed for eligibility for data extraction, and 12 articles were excluded because of insufficient data (n = 1) or a preclinical study design (n = 11). Finally, five articles met all inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review. Characteristics of included studies

All included studies were clinical trials: phase I[13], phase II[14], phase III[15], or phase I/II[1,3], with sample sizes ranging from 3[14] to 31 participants[15]. Most of these studies (n = 3) were conducted in the United States[1,3,15]. Some of them had randomized methodological designs[3,15], while the remainder were non-randomized[1,13,14]. The interventions implemented in these studies were a mix of ex vivo and in vivo approaches[1,13,14], except for two studies that used only a single approach[3,15]. Quality assessment

Of the two randomized trials, the study by Guide et al showed an overall low risk of bias. The study used a concealed within-patient randomization method, a double-blind design, protocol-defined interventions, and predefined statistical handling of missing data. The primary outcome was objectively assessed using standard imaging and blinded review, and all prespecified endpoints were reported[15]. The study by Gurevich et al included a within-patient randomized design and blinded review, but its open-label design at the site level, minor protocol deviations, and incomplete reporting of some secondary outcomes resulted in moderate uncertainty in the domain[3].

Among the nonrandomized studies, Lwin et al[13], Matsumura et al[14], and So et al[1] were all assessed as having an overall serious risk of bias. The main source of bias in these early-phase trials was confounding due to their open-label, single-arm design and very small sample sizes, which limited the ability to control for patient- or lesion-level variability. Other concerns included unblinded outcome assessment and incomplete or variable follow-up data. Bias was further amplified by the selection of target wounds based on the investigators’ judgment of suitability. This practice can systematically favor lesions with better vascularization or less chronic inflammation, thereby weakening the validity and generalizability of the reported effects. Nevertheless, all three studies employed standardized procedures with clearly defined interventions and provided transparent reporting of safety and feasibility outcomes.

The evidence review found one randomized trial[15] to be at low risk of bias, judged one randomized trial[3] as having some concerns, and found three early-phase non-randomized trials[1,13,14] to be at serious risk of bias. These qualitative assessments were explicitly taken into account in the interpretation of the results, with greater weight given to findings from the low-risk randomized trial[15] and a cautious interpretation adopted for outcomes from early-phase non-randomized trials[1,13,14].

Collectively, these risk-of-bias assessments indicate that the current evidence base is dominated by early-phase studies with important methodological limitations, particularly small sample sizes, non-randomized designs, and potential confounding. Consequently, although the overall direction of the evidence consistently favors gene therapy, confidence in the magnitude and durability of treatment effects remains limited. Therefore, findings from the RCTs were considered more reliable and were given greater interpretive weight than results from non-randomized studies when drawing overall conclusions. Cell-based therapies with ex vivo gene modification involve harvesting autologous patient cells, genetically modifying them in a laboratory setting, and subsequently reintroducing them into the body. In the Lwin et al study, a self-inactivating lentiviral vector was employed for the ex vivo transduction of autologous dermal fibroblasts. The in vivo delivery modality involved intradermal injections of the modified cells into intact, non-blistered skin. Each participant received three injections at a concentration of approximately 1 × 106 cells/cm2, with the injection sites tattooed to facilitate long-term longitudinal sampling. Molecular efficacy was assessed using immunofluorescence mean fluorescence intensity for C7, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay[13].

So et al focused on the genetic modification of epidermal keratinocytes. This group used a retroviral vector for ex vivo gene transfer. In vivo delivery was achieved through the surgical transplantation of cultured epithelial sheets (5 × 7 cm) of the modified keratinocytes directly onto chronic RDEB wounds under general anesthesia; each participant received six grafts during the treatment session. Efficacy assessments included the Investigator Global Assessment of wound healing and patient-reported outcomes (PROs), while long-term safety was monitored through replication-competent retrovirus (RCR) testing and PCR of SCC samples[1]. A different ex vivo approach was reported by Matsumura et al, which involved ex vivo cultivation of autologous keratinocytes using Green’s method to generate epithelial sheets without the use of an artificial viral vector. The delivery consisted of surgical transplantation of these sheets onto target ulcers. Re-transplantation was permitted up to three times to counteract recurrence. Efficacy was primarily assessed by the ulcer epithelialization rate, visual analog scale for symptoms, and electron microscopy for anchoring fibrils[14].

The direct gene transfer strategy uses vectors applied directly to the open wound, circumventing the need for cell harvesting, ex vivo modification, and surgical grafting. Two separate studies, Guide et al and Gurevich et al, utilized an engineered, replication-defective herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) vector for topical COL7A1 gene delivery. The phase III trial by Guide et al involved the weekly topical application of the B-VEC gel to open wound surfaces. The dose was adjusted based on the baseline wound area, and the regimen was explicitly repeat-compatible for up to 26 weeks or until the wound healed[15]. This approach was mirrored in the phase I/II study by Gurevich et al, where the gel was applied dropwise over the wound surface during routine dressing changes and subsequently covered with occlusive dressings[3]. Both B-VEC studies focused on complete wound closure as a primary endpoint[15], which Gurevich et al supplemented with structural (immunoelectron microscopy) and molecular (quantitative PCR) evaluations[3].

Outcomes and effectiveness

Primary clinical endpoints: epithelialization rate and wound-closure rate

Wound-closure results showed statistically significant short-term superiority of topical B-VEC over placebo.

In the placebo-controlled phase III trial by Guide et al, the topical B-VEC vector achieved markedly higher rates of complete wound closure. At 6 months, 67% of B-VEC wounds achieved complete healing versus 22% of placebo wounds, resulting in a highly significant difference (P = 0.002). This difference was even more pronounced at 3 months (71% versus 20%; P<0.001)[15]. Similarly, the phase I/II study by Gurevich et al reinforced these findings, confirming significant differences in complete wound closure: 79% of B-VEC wounds met the responder definition versus 0% of placebo wounds (P = 0.0026). Moreover, B-VEC significantly reduced the median time to complete closure (13.5 versus 22.5 days for placebo), indicating more rapid wound healing[3]. These controlled data provide the strongest evidence of superior short-term efficacy for the topical B-VEC approach.

Ex vivo cell-grafting approaches have demonstrated notable long-term durability. In the long-term follow-up (mean of 5.9 years) reported by So et al, 70.0% of retrovirus-modified graft sites maintained ≥50% wound healing[1]. This represents the highest sustained efficacy reported among all interventions. In comparison, Matsumura et al reported a high mean epithelialization rate of 81.6% at the 4-week endpoint; however, Patient 3 required re-transplantation up to three times due to re-ulceration, suggesting potential limitations in achieving permanent repair in all cases[14].

Molecular and structural efficacy: C7 expression and anchoring fibril formation

Comparison of the molecular results highlights a challenge in directly linking C7 protein expression to structural restoration across different platforms.

In the Lwin et al study, intradermal injection of lentivirus-modified fibroblasts resulted in a significant increase (1.26-fold to 26.10-fold) in C7 mean fluorescence intensity in the injected skin (P < 0.05). While this confirmed successful molecular activity, TEM failed to detect any new mature anchoring fibrils in the treated skin within the 12-month study timeframe. This finding suggests that while the fibroblast injection produced C7 protein, it did not necessarily translate into structural repair and the formation of functional anchoring structures at the DEJ[13]. Conversely, studies focusing on epidermal cell grafting or direct keratinocyte transduction achieved significant clinical success in wound closure[1,3]. This clinical success, confirmed in the B-VEC trial with immunoelectron microscopy, strongly implies that these latter approaches enable basal keratinocytes to produce C7 that integrates structurally into the DEJ, which is essential for mechanical stability[3].

PROs: pain and itch

PROs, particularly measures of relief from debilitating symptoms such as pain and itch, correlate strongly with successful long-term clinical stability.

The long-term study by So et al provided the strongest evidence of improvement in PROs, reporting a highly significant association between durable wound healing and symptom elimination. At year 5, 0% of graft sites with ≥50% healing were painful or pruritic, compared to 66.7% of sites with insufficient healing (P < 0.001 for both outcomes)[1]. The topical B-VEC approach also showed a directional trend favoring B-VEC in pain reduction[15]. While not as statistically robust as the long-term grafting data, it indicates a favorable short-term trend.

Consequences of gene therapy

Treatment-related adverse events and tolerability

A unifying finding across all platforms is the generally excellent short- and long-term tolerability of the interventions. Crucially, no serious adverse events (SAEs) related to the gene therapy treatment itself were reported in any of the reviewed studies[1,3,13,14].

Gurevich et al reported 21 total adverse events for B-VEC exposure, but these were predominantly mild local or systemic symptoms, with no grade ≥2 B-VEC-related SAEs[3]. Lwin et al also confirmed that injections were tolerated without serious reactions, with adverse events limited to mild injection-site effects[13]. The main clinical consequence related to efficacy was noted by Matsumura et al, where Patient 3 required re-transplantation up to three times due to re-ulceration[14], highlighting a limitation in achieving permanent repair in some cases, though not a severe adverse event.

Immunogenicity monitoring

Assessing potential autoimmune reactions against recombinant C7 is vital, as an immune response could negate therapeutic efficacy. Both integrating and non-integrating platforms demonstrated successful immunologic tolerance. Lwin et al reported no autoimmune reactions against recombinant C7 during 12 months of follow-up[13]. This tolerance was sustained in the long-term retroviral cell grafting platform[1]. This consistency across vector types confirms a positive safety profile regarding C7 immunogenicity.

Safety of non-integrating vectors (HSV-1)

Non-integrating, replication-defective vectors like B-VEC rely on rapid clearance. In the phase I/II B-VEC study by Gurevich et al, safety was supported by the absence of vector shedding in tested biological samples[3].

Long-term safety of integrating vectors (Retrovirus)

Because retroviral vectors can permanently integrate into the host-cell genome, long-term monitoring for oncogenesis (insertional mutagenesis) and RCR is crucial. The So et al study, with the longest follow-up, confirmed no replication-competent retroviral infections. Furthermore, while two participants developed cutaneous SCCs during the follow-up period, these were assessed as unrelated to grafting, and critically, no SCC was identified at the graft sites[1]. These findings provide the highest level of long-term oncological safety assurance for the integrating cell grafting platform.

Discussion

RDEB is a severe genetic disorder characterized by skin fragility, blistering, and chronic wounds; current management remains purely palliative[16,17]. Gene therapy, which aims to correct the underlying COL7A1 mutations, presents a promising disease-modifying strategy[18]. This systematic review was conducted to evaluate the evidence for the efficacy and safety of these emerging therapies for patients with RDEB.

The analysis of five clinical studies demonstrates that both ex vivo (cell-based) and in vivo (topical) gene therapy approaches can effectively restore functional C7 and drive significant clinical improvement in wound healing. Long-term data from an ex vivo study using gene-corrected autologous epidermal sheets showed sustained efficacy, with ≥50% wound healing achieved in 70% of treated areas after 5 years[1]. Complementing this, a phase III trial of the topical gel B-VEC reported a markedly superior wound closure rate of 71% at 3 months compared to 20% in the placebo group[15]. Although findings underscore the substantial therapeutic potential of gene therapy across different delivery modalities to address the core deficit in RDEB, these results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Most studies were early-phase clinical trials involving relatively small patient populations and limited follow-up durations, thereby reducing statistical precision and restricting assessment of the long-term durability of treatment effects. Furthermore, early-phase studies are primarily designed to evaluate safety and feasibility rather than definitive clinical efficacy. Consequently, while the available evidence suggests promising therapeutic potential, the current findings should be regarded as preliminary until confirmed by larger, adequately powered randomized clinical trials with extended follow-up.

One of the most important parameters for measuring the success of these therapies is the long-term stability of their clinical outcomes. While early results have been encouraging, there is a concern that the level of expression of the therapeutic gene may diminish over time. This problem was also observed in our review of the study by Lwin et al[13], which reported a decline in C7 expression over the long term. This observation is consistent with the review by Danescu et al[19], which reports that in certain ex vivo strategies, there is evidence that COL7A1 gene expression diminishes gradually over time. A study by Hofbauer et al[20] attributes this difficulty to a basic biological principle: to achieve a permanent cure, one must successfully target epidermal stem cells, which are responsible for the ongoing regeneration of the skin.

The promise of attaining genuine, long-term correction has been amply illustrated by the successful management of Junctional Epidermolysis Bullosa, wherein targeting these stem cells has resulted in the generation of a stable and functional epidermis for over 5 years. Alternatively, new strategies, such as the topical gel B-VEC, overcome the durability issue with an alternative model: frequent re-administration of a non-integrating vector to sustain the therapeutic effect. While this strategy constitutes a significant improvement, long-term follow-up data are still being gathered to establish its final durability[21].

This systematic review’s primary findings indicate that the five included RDEB gene therapy trials demonstrate a good safety profile, with no reported SAEs, tumorigenesis, or systemic toxicity. However, these promising results must be contextualized within the broader safety considerations of the gene therapy field. As noted in the literature, immune responses pose a dual challenge: reacting against the viral vector capsid or the newly introduced transgene protein, potentially causing inflammation and elimination of treated cells. Furthermore, integrating vectors, such as the lentiviral and retroviral vectors used in the reviewed studies[1,13], carry a theoretical risk of insertional mutagenesis, where vector integration near an oncogene can activate it. This risk is not merely theoretical, as evidenced by leukemia cases in early X-linked severe combined immunodeficiency trials[22] and recent reports of myelodysplastic syndrome/acute myeloid leukemia with lentiviral-based therapies, such as Skysona [23]. Therefore, although the included studies generally reported favorable safety profiles and encouraging therapeutic responses, the available evidence remains limited by the relatively short duration of follow-up in most trials. Consequently, the long-term safety of these gene therapy approaches, including the potential for delayed adverse events and the durability of clinical benefit, cannot yet be established with confidence. Continued long-term monitoring of treated patients and adequately powered studies with extended follow-up are therefore essential to determine whether the observed therapeutic effects are maintained over time and to better characterize the long-term risk–benefit profile of these interventions.

This study primarily analyzes clinical trials of gene augmentation, which introduces a functional COL7A1 gene. Our focus on trials, however, excludes another significant research area: genome editing using tools such as CRISPR/Cas9. Unlike augmentation, editing aims for a permanent correction by directly fixing the causal mutation in a patient’s own COL7A1 gene. Although excluded from this review, there is extensive preclinical work demonstrating substantial progress in using these techniques to edit cells from patients with RDEB and restore functional C7 expression in vitro and in animal models[24].

Although our review focuses on deliberate gene augmentation and excludes genome editing approaches, a definitional caveat applies to Matsumura et al[14]: the study used cultured epidermal autografts derived from spontaneously revertant skin–keratinocytes that carry endogenous COL7A1 reversion mutations–rather than exogenous genetic modification or viral vector delivery. This somatic reversion achieves the same biological endpoint (restored COL7A1 expression) by a mechanistically distinct route, and inclusion is clinically and biologically justifiable because therapeutically effective, genetically distinct autologous cells were used, but it should be declared as a limitation. We explicitly state that the study by Matsumura et al falls outside conventional definitions of gene therapy or cell therapy that rely on intentional genetic modification, and we recommend that future reviews adopt clear operational definitions distinguishing intentional gene augmentation, genome editing, and therapies exploiting spontaneous somatic reversion.

Despite preclinical promise, clinical application of CRISPR/Cas9 for RDEB faces challenges. A primary hurdle is the efficient and safe in vivo delivery of editing reagents to epidermal stem cells[25]. This creates an ongoing tension between viral vectors, typically offering higher delivery efficiency but raising additional safety concerns, and non-viral delivery systems, such as nanoparticle carriers, which may be more favorable from a safety perspective yet often deliver editing reagents less effectively. In parallel, mitigating off-target editing risk is essential before broad clinical adoption, since unintended genomic alterations could undermine both efficacy and safety. In this context, the search strategy of the present study deliberately incorporated terms related to CRISPR/Cas9 and RNA-based therapies to maximize retrieval; however, no interventional clinical trials using these approaches have yet been conducted in patients with RDEB. Consequently, no studies implementing genome editing or RNA-based therapies satisfied the inclusion criteria, and the resulting evidence base therefore reflects only those modalities for which clinical trial data are currently available. Moreover, the interpretation of findings should take into account the methodological quality of the included studies: although observed efficacy and safety outcomes were generally consistent, the predominance of early-phase, single-arm trials with serious risk of bias limits the certainty of these conclusions. Specifically, potential confounding factors, lack of randomization, small sample sizes, and limited blinding may have contributed to an overestimation of treatment effects. Accordingly, greater confidence should be placed in evidence derived from RCTs, while results from non-randomized designs should be viewed primarily as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future research should therefore prioritize addressing these methodological gaps by conducting larger multicenter clinical trials to improve generalizability and statistical robustness. Equally important, more rigorous standardization of efficacy, safety, and molecular outcome measures would enable more meaningful cross-study comparisons and strengthen future evidence synthesis. Finally, extended follow-up is required to establish the durability of treatment benefits over time and to better characterize the long-term safety profile of gene therapy for RDEB, thereby providing a stronger and more reliable foundation for future clinical implementation.

Overall, rapid advances in genome editing, including precise base and prime editors that avoid double-strand breaks, offer safer, more accurate tools. These innovations hold strong potential for developing effective therapies for genetic disorders such as RDEB.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that gene therapy for RDEB has a favorable early safety profile and promising efficacy, with both ex vivo and in vivo approaches supporting further development and larger confirmatory studies. Topical non-integrating B-VEC produced the most consistent short-term wound closures, whereas ex vivo retroviral grafts tended to give longer-lasting improvement. Molecular studies point to restored keratinocyte-derived C7 at the DEJ as the main mediator of clinical response. No clear treatment-related SAEs, autoimmune phenomena, or malignant changes have been reported to date. These results support further development, but larger, controlled studies are required to define optimal delivery methods and long-term effectiveness.
