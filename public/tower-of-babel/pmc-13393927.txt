Comparative anatomical and clinical evaluation of three surgical spaces for contralateral internal carotid artery C7-segment aneurysm clipping: the role of the subchiasmatic-preinfundibular space
Authors: Xiaoliang Wang, Shiliang Wang, Shang Gao, Xuebin Wang, Yang Wu, Hailong Du, Chao Zhang, Lei Zhao, Xiaosong Liu, Xiaomeng Liu, Gengshen Zhang, Jianliang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aims to anatomically define and clinically evaluate the subchiasmatic-preinfundibular space (SCPIS) as an alternative surgical space for clipping contralateral ICA C7-segment aneurysms, by comparing its outcomes with those of the standard prechiasmatic space (PCS) and contralateral opticocarotid space (OCS).MethodsBasic Research: Five cadaveric heads were dissected via the pterional approach. Contralateral ICA exposure was measured through three spaces— PCS, contralateral OCS), and SCPIS—before and after anterior clinoid process (ACP) drilling. Clinical Research: A retrospective analysis of 53 patients with multiple intracranial aneurysms (MIAs) treated via unilateral craniotomy compared outcomes across three groups: SCPIS (Group 1), PCS (Group 2), and contralateral OCS (Group 3). Postoperative aneurysm occlusion rates, complications, discharge mRS scores, and 6-month neurological deficits were assessed.ResultsThe lengths of the contralateral ICA exposure increased significantly post-ACP drilling: PCS (5.62 ± 0.91 mm vs. 7.11 ± 0.84 mm, p < 0.001), contralateral OCS (4.44 ± 2.33 mm vs. 6.03 ± 2.30 mm, p = 0.001), and SCPIS (4.98 ± 1.91 mm vs. 6.67 ± 2.01 mm, p < 0.001). Significant increases were also observed in the lengths of the inferior border of the optic chiasm and/or optic nerves, the deep superior border of the diaphragma sellae, and the area of this space after ACP drilling (p < 0.05). SCPIS clipping showed no significant differences in hospitalization duration, complications (pneumonia, electrolyte disturbances, optic nerve injury, cerebral infarction), or discharge mRS scores (p > 0.05) compared to other approaches. The rate of complete angiographic occlusion of the contralateral aneurysms was comparable among the three groups (PCS: 88.6%, OCS: 90.9%, SCPIS: 85.7%; p = 0.92). Six-month neurological outcomes were satisfactory.ConclusionSCPIS is a viable alternative for contralateral ICA C7-segment aneurysm clipping. ACP drilling improves surgical access, and the approach is safe and effective.

Introduction

Intracranial aneurysms, a prevalent neurosurgical condition, pose a critical threat to patient survival owing to spontaneous subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) secondary to aneurysm rupture, which is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates [1–4]. Epidemiological studies indicate an annual incidence of 6–10 per 100,000 people [5–7], with post-rupture mortality rates reaching 40%–60% [8–10]. Multiple intracranial aneurysms (MIAs), accounting for 18%–30% of all intracranial aneurysms [11], demonstrate bilateral distribution in 20%–40% of cases. Notably, MIA patients exhibit higher SAH incidence and poorer prognoses compared to those with solitary aneurysms [12]. Current therapeutic strategies for MIAs primarily include microsurgical clipping and endovascular intervention [13]. While endovascular techniques have gained prominence, microsurgical clipping remains indispensable due to economic constraints and anatomical limitations of interventional approaches [9]. During craniotomy clipping procedures, conventional anatomical spaces often prove challenging to achieve adequate exposure for contralateral optic chiasm-obscured internal carotid artery (ICA) C7-segment aneurysms. Therefore, this limitation underscores the necessity of exploring novel surgical pathways to optimize exposure.

This study employs meticulous anatomical exploration and a comparative clinical analysis against conventional spaces (PCS and OCS) to investigate the feasibility, safety, and surgical utility of the subchiasmatic-preinfundibular space SCPIS) for microsurgical clipping of contralateral ICA C7-segment aneurysms, aiming to define its role as a novel alternative when conventional exposure is inadequate.

Materials and methods

Basic research

Specimen preparation

Five formalin-fixed cadaveric heads with latex dye-perfused intracranial arteries were utilized for anatomical dissection.

Instrumentation

Standard neurosurgical instruments were provided by the Department of Human Anatomy, Hebei Medical University. Key equipment utilized included: ① A ZEISS surgical microscope with a magnification range of 4–25 ×, equipped with video recording capability to facilitate the documentation of anatomical procedures. ② The Medtronic high-speed pneumatic drill system, which was employed for bone flap sculpting and bone removal. ③ The microsurgical neurosurgical instruments, cranial fixation frame, Yuwell electric suction system, and film ruler with 0.1 mm precision were employed for precise dimensional assessment of anatomical structures.

Dissection protocol

Head fixation

The specimen was positioned according to Yasargil's pterional approach protocol, with slight head flexion and 45° contralateral rotation, then securely immobilized using a cranial fixation frame with three-point fixation. This ensures stable cranial positioning for subsequent microsurgical procedures.

Incision design

Following Yasargil's pterional approach protocol, the skin incision originates 1 cm anterior to the tragus, vertically ascending to the superior temporal line, and then curves anteromedially along the hairline's posterior margin until reaching 1.5 cm paramedian to the midline.

Flap elevation

following free flap preparation, perform atraumatic traction and secure fixation to achieve optimal surgical field exposure.

Bone flap formation

Drill two burr holes in the skull. Burr Hole 1 (keyhole) is located at the posterior aspect of the zygomatic process of the frontal bone, 1 cm superior to the frontozygomatic suture. Burr Hole 2 is drilled superior to the root of the zygomatic arch and inferior to the squamous temporal bone. Subsequently, the burr holes are interconnected using a high-speed craniotome to fashion the bone flap.

Expansion of the cranial base

A high-speed drill is used to drill the sphenoid wing and the irregular bone at the lateral frontal base, thereby maximally exposing the inferior aspects of the anterior and middle cranial fossae to enhance surgical access.

Dural incision

After incising the dura mater, suture it to the wound margin with sutures to further expose the intracranial structures.

Space exploration and measurement

The frontal lobe was gently elevated, and the arachnoid membrane was dissected along the sylvian fissure to expose the basal cranial nerves and vessels, providing access to the prechiasmatic space (PCS) and contralateral opticocarotid space (OCS). Under microscopic visualization, the length of the contralateral internal carotid artery (ICA) and the dimensions of the SCPIS were measured via PCS, contralateral OCS, and SCPIS. Statistical analysis

Paired t-tests were performed using SPSS 21.0 software to compare the data before and after anterior clinoid process resection, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Clinical study

Subjects

A retrospective analysis was conducted on 53 consecutive patients with multiple intracranial aneurysms treated at the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee and adhered to the ethical standards outline in the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments. All patients underwent unilateral approach microsurgical clipping for bilateral aneurysms, with the contralateral aneurysm located at the ICA C7-segment. Patients were stratified into three groups based on surgical space:① SCPIS group;② PCS group;③ Contralateral OCS group. The side for the unilateral pterional craniotomy was selected based on the location of the ruptured aneurysm (in SAH cases) or the aneurysm with the highest rupture risk/most complex morphology (in unruptured cases), as determined by preoperative imaging (CT pattern of hemorrhage, aneurysm size, morphology on DSA/CTA) and clinical assessment.

Surgical procedure

Under general anesthesia, a standard pterional approach or extended pterional approach was performed [14]. The sylvian fissure was microsurgically dissected to fully expose the ipsilateral ICA and optic nerve. In cases of severe subarachnoid hemorrhage with significant cerebral edema, External Ventricular Drain (EVD) or fenestration of the lamina terminalis may be performed to release cerebrospinal fluid, thereby reducing intracranial pressure and facilitating brain relaxation for improved working corridor exposure. Initially, the feasibility of clipping the contralateral ICA C7-segment aneurysm was assessed through PCS. If exposure was inadequate, the contralateral carotid cistern was partially opened. The optic nerve was dissected posteriorly to the chiasm, and the ipsilateral ICA was dissected to its bifurcation. The aneurysm was then re-evaluated through OCS. If visualization remained insufficient, microsurgical exploration via the SCPIS was employed to access the contralateral ICA. The SCPIS was specifically utilized when intraoperative assessment via the PCS and contralateral OCS confirmed inadequate visualization of the aneurysm neck, typically due to: (a) a posteriorly or inferiorly projecting aneurysm dome that was obscured by the body of the optic chiasm; (b) the presence of a pre-fixed optic chiasm; or (c) dense peri-chiasmatic arachnoid adhesions secondary to SAH that prevented sufficient mobilization for safe clipping through the conventional spaces.

Clinical documentation and follow-up

Preoperative records included detailed documentation of patient demographics, modified Fisher score, Hunt-Hess grade, cerebral edema status, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease), aneurysm laterality, and hospitalization duration. Preoperative aneurysm morphology, including the maximum dome diameter and neck width of the contralateral ICA C7-segment aneurysm, was measured on three-dimensional digital subtraction angiography (3D-DSA) or computed tomography angiography (CTA) reconstructions. The use and duration of temporary parent artery occlusion during contralateral aneurysm clipping were recorded from operative notes. Aneurysm occlusion status was evaluated on postoperative imaging using CTA or DSA. Routine assessment was performed via CTA within the first postoperative week. DSA was utilized in select cases with complex aneurysm morphology or as part of a formal follow-up protocol. The degree of occlusion was classified according to the Raymond-Roy grading scale: Class I (complete obliteration), Class II (residual neck), and Class III (residual aneurysm). Postoperative complications (pneumonia, electrolyte imbalance, optic nerve injury, cerebral infarction) were documented. For prognostic evaluation, functional outcomes were assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at discharge and neurological deficit status at the 6-month follow-up. The mRS is a standardized, published ordinal scale ranging from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death), with higher scores indicating greater disability [15].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 software. Based on whether the data were normally distributed, Student’s t-test was used to determine significant differences in continuous data. Categorical variables were compared among the three groups using the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test, which is particularly suitable for our data given the small sample size in one of the comparison groups (SCPIS, n = 7). The significance level set at α = 0.05, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic research

Definition and measurement of anatomical spaces

The PCS (also termed Space 1) is formed by the bilateral optic nerves and the planum sphenoidale, with three sides of varying lengths. In 10 sides of exploration, the origin of the contralateral posterior communicating artery (PComA) was visualized through this space in 4 sides. The contralateral OCS (also termed Space 2) is anatomically defined by the optic nerve, supraclinoid segment of the ICA, and A1 segment of the anterior cerebral artery (ACA). In 10 sides of exploration, the origin of the contralateral PComA was visualized through this space in only 1 side. The SCPIS is anatomically bounded by the inferior border of the optic chiasm and/or optic nerve, anterior border of the pituitary stalk, and deep superior border of the diaphragm sellae. Visualization of the contralateral PComA origin through this space was similarly challenging, also achieved in only 1 of the 10 explored sides. Postoperative aneurysm imaging outcomes

All 53 patients underwent postoperative vascular imaging to evaluate the occlusion status of the clipped contralateral ICA C7-segment aneurysms. High rates of complete angiographic occlusion (Raymond-Roy Class I) were achieved across all three surgical spaces. In the PCS group, 31 of 35 patients (88.6%) demonstrated complete aneurysm obliteration. Similarly, in the OCS group, complete occlusion was confirmed in 10 of 11 patients (90.9%). Within the SCPIS group, 6 of 7 patients (85.7%) achieved Raymond-Roy Class I occlusion. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference in the complete occlusion rates among the PCS, OCS, and SCPIS groups (p = 0.92). Postoperative assessment was primarily performed using CTA within one week of surgery. A subset of eight patients (5 in the PCS group, 2 in the OCS group, and 1 in the SCPIS group) with complex aneurysm morphology or as part of scheduled surveillance underwent additional DSA, which corroborated the CTA findings. Postoperative complications and follow-up outcomes

No intraoperative rupture of the contralateral internal carotid artery aneurysm occurred during microsurgical clipping in all enrolled cases. (PCS: 0/35; OCS: 0/11; SCPIS: 0/7). Imaging examinations revealed no new hemorrhagic foci in any patients postoperatively. No reoperations were required across the cohort. The mean hospital stay was 13.0 ± 5.1 days for PCS, 13.5 ± 2.6 days for OCS, and 12.6 ± 5.9 days for SCPIS groups (p = 0.91). This results indicate that, after adjustment for the major confounding factor of aneurysm rupture status, the choice of surgical space per se did not independently prolong hospital stay. Optic nerve function was assessed clinically via pupillary light reflex examination. Injury, manifesting as an abnormal light reflex, was documented in 4 cases (PCS:2(5.7%), OCS:1(9.1%), SCPIS:1(14.3%); p = 0.58), all of which demonstrated significant clinical recovery at the 6-month follow-up. While this study’s retrospective design precluded systematic formal perimetry, limiting assessment of subtler visual field deficits, the low incidence of clinically apparent injury (4/53, 7.5%) supports the premise that, with meticulous microsurgical technique, the risk of major visual pathway damage remains low even when working through the deep SCPIS corridor. Pneumonia of varying severity was observed in 11 patients (PCS:7(20%), OCS:1(9.1%), SCPIS:3(42.9%); p = 0.24), with all infections effectively controlled before discharge. Electrolyte imbalance developed in 7 cases (PCS:4(11.4%), OCS:2(18.2%), SCPIS:1(14.3%); p = 0.84), all normalized prior to discharge. Cerebral infarction was identified in 9 patients (PCS:5(14.3%), OCS:3(27.3%), SCPIS:1(14.3%); p = 0.66). The infarcts were not confined to a specific vascular territory that would suggest a direct surgical injury from a particular space. Instead, they were distributed across both anterior (e.g., ACA, MCA) and posterior circulations, a pattern more indicative of sequelae from aneurysmal SAH, such as vasospasm, rather than a procedure-specific complication. These patients recovered well at the 6-month follow-up. However, the retrospective design of the present study inevitably limited our ability to fully exclude procedure-related confounding factors, including potential perforator vessel injury. Therefore, this finding further emphasizes that meticulous intraoperative surgical manipulation should be strictly implemented to minimize unnecessary vascular traction and mechanical disturbance. Discussion

With the advancement of neurointerventional techniques, the treatment of intracranial aneurysms has shifted toward endovascular therapy [16–18], leading to a progressive decline in the use of microsurgical clipping. Although studies have demonstrated comparable efficacy between endovascular therapy and surgical treatment [19, 20], endovascular approaches remain associated with higher rates of hemorrhagic or ischemic complications in specific aneurysm subtypes. These include aneurysms with tortuous proximal vasculature or complex anatomy, lesions harboring daughter sacs, and recurrent aneurysms after prior endovascular intervention [21, 22]. Consequently, microsurgical clipping retains irreplaceable value as a definitive therapeutic strategy for these challenging cases. Microsurgical clipping carries inherent risks of parent artery manipulation-induced vasospasm and iatrogenic cerebral injury due to intraoperative brain retraction and vascular dissection [23, 24]. Single-stage clipping of MIAs often requires prolonged operative time and extensive manipulation, potentially amplifying these risks. However, Wachter D et al. [2] demonstrated that single-stage clipping within 72 h post-SAH does not increase cerebral vasospasm risk compared to clipping a single aneurysm. The unilateral approach for bilateral aneurysm clipping is technically feasible and safe [25], though not all bilateral aneurysms are suitable for this strategy due to anatomical constraints [26, 27]. Thus, comprehensive preoperative evaluation is critical to optimize therapeutic outcomes.

The critical aspect of unilateral-approach protocols for bilateral intracranial aneurysm clipping lies in selecting an optimal surgical space that ensures complete aneurysm occlusion while preserving postoperative neurological function [28]. Oshiro EM et al. [29] pioneered a systematic anatomical study on contralateral aneurysm clipping, four contralateral surgical space and reporting a 50% success rate for contralateral posterior communicating artery (PComA) aneurysm clipping, significantly higher than VanderArk GD's reported 16% success rate [30]. The PCS provides adequate visualization of the supraclinoid and ophthalmic segments of the ICA, with partial exposure of the PComA origin, though distal segments may be obscured by optic chiasm. The OCS offers limited ICA exposure due to anatomical interference from the ICA itself and optic chiasm. In this study, four patients occurred postoperative optic nerve injury (ipsilateral loss of direct light reflex and contralateral loss of consensual light reflex), likely due to compression from aneurysm clips positioned between the ICA and optic chiasm/nerve. Optic chiasm injury could also manifest as bitemporal hemianopsia [31].

Therefore, Based on anatomical principles and clinical outcomes, we propose a pragmatic framework for selecting surgical spaces during contralateral clipping of ICA C7-segment aneurysms via a unilateral pterional approach: PCS is recommended as the initial space for most contralateral C7 aneurysms, providing a wide and direct view especially for superior or anterosuperior projecting lesions unobscured by the chiasm; OCS is explored if PCS exposure is insufficient, particularly for laterally or posterolaterally projecting aneurysms with mobile optic structures; and SCPIS serves as the salvage space when PCS and OCS fail to visualize the aneurysm neck—typically for posteriorly or inferiorly projecting lesions obscured by a pre-fixed chiasm (especially PComA and inferiorly projecting AChA aneurysms)—where temporary clipping is frequently required. No absolute size contraindication for the contralateral approach was identified, with successfully clipped aneurysms having a mean size of 5.8 ± 2.0 mm; the main limiting factor is not absolute size but the complex interaction of aneurysm size, dome/neck morphology, projection, chiasmal obstruction, and vascular tortuosity that restricts visualization and instrument maneuverability, with final decisions based on intraoperative assessment.

Our study builds upon the important foundational work of Oshiro et al., who systematically defined contralateral surgical spaces and reported successful clipping in 50% of attempted contralateral PComA aneurysms. Several key distinctions and advancements emerge from our comparative analysis. First, while Oshiro et al.'s anatomical study defined four spaces, our work provides the first quantitative, pre- and post-drilling measurements for the SCPIS, a space not explicitly detailed in their microsurgical anatomical taxonomy. Clinically, our series demonstrates a higher rate of complete contralateral occlusion (85.7% for SCPIS, 88.6% for PCS, 90.9% for OCS) compared to the historical benchmark (50%), which may reflect advances in microinstrumentation, the routine use of ACP drilling, and the availability of the SCPIS as a salvage option when conventional spaces fail. Furthermore, unlike studies that primarily discuss the PCS and OCS, our manuscript provides explicit, intraoperatively validated selection criteria for transitioning to the SCPIS, linking its use to specific anatomical obstructions. This represents a progression from anatomical description to a practical, decision-based surgical algorithm. Finally, our comparative safety data, showing no increase in optic nerve injury or cerebral infarction with the SCPIS despite its deeper trajectory, adds a new layer of evidence regarding the risk profile of this alternative pathway.

The primary finding of this study is that the SCPIS constitutes a feasible and effective surgical space for microsurgical clipping of contralateral ICA C7-segment aneurysms. The definitive measure of efficacy is the ability to achieve complete angiographic occlusion. As presented in Table 5, the rate of complete aneurysm occlusion (Raymond-Roy Class I) attained via the SCPIS (85.7%) was comparable to the rates achieved through the conventional PCS (88.6%) and contralateral OCS (90.9%) approaches, with no statistically significant difference among the three groups (p = 0.92). This demonstrates that utilizing the deeper and more confined SCPIS does not compromise the fundamental therapeutic goal. The efficacy of SCPIS is achieved despite its inherent technical challenges. The space was specifically employed in seven cases where the predominant obstacle was the anatomical interposition of the optic chiasm, often compounded by a pre-fixed chiasm and SAH-induced adhesions, which rendered conventional spaces (PCS and OCS) inadequate. Operating within this deep, narrow space presents difficulties, as underscored by the significantly higher frequency of temporary parent artery occlusion in the SCPIS group compared to others. However, this technical challenge is manageable. The absence of intraoperative aneurysm rupture attests to the safety afforded by meticulous arachnoid dissection and clip application. Furthermore, while temporary clipping is a recognized risk factor, the comparably low incidence of postoperative cerebral infarction across all groups—despite differing rates of temporary clip use—suggests that the SCPIS approach does not inherently confer a higher ischemic risk when performed with meticulous technique and by minimizing occlusion duration. Postoperative infarction in this SAH-predominant cohort is multifactorial, with cerebral vasospasm being a major contributor. Consequently, the SCPIS should be considered not merely an anatomical curiosity but a viable and effective alternative surgical strategy.

In basic research, despite only 1/10 specimens achieving exposure of the contralateral PComA origin via the SCPIS—likely due to reduced vascular mobility post-latex dye perfusion and limited maneuverability from the challenge in proximal vessel occlusion—clinical practice suggests higher exposure feasibility owing to in vivo vascular elasticity and maneuverability. This clinical study demonstrates that the narrow anatomical confines of this space complicate operations for complex or large aneurysms, often necessitating temporary parent artery occlusion or gentle retraction to achieve adequate visualization. However, no statistically significant difference in postoperative neurological recovery was observed among the three surgical space groups, indicating the practical utility of this approach despite technical challenges.

Following ACP drilling, the SCPIS and exposure length of the ICA were increased. However, the expanded exposure primarily overlapped with PCS, failing to significantly improve visualization of chiasm-obscured ICA segments. This limitation arises from the restricted length of the pituitary stalk (preventing excessive superior retraction of the optic chiasm) and the fixed exit point of the contralateral optic nerve, which remains unaffected by ipsilateral ACP drilling. Nevertheless, the expanded space facilitates microinstrument maneuverability. Given the deep and narrow space for contralateral aneurysm clipping, sharp arachnoid dissection is essential to create sufficient space for aneurysm clip placement and clip applicator manipulation, thereby enhancing procedural safety. An additional technical consideration, not formally assessed in this study, is the potential influence of surgeon handedness and craniotomy laterality on ergonomics. For a right-handed surgeon, a right-sided craniotomy for a left contralateral aneurysm may offer a more favorable instrument trajectory than the converse scenario. Surgeons should be aware of this potential ergonomic variable when planning the side of approach, particularly when anticipating the use of a demanding space like the SCPIS.

SAH secondary to aneurysm rupture can induce cerebral vasospasm, vascular stenosis, or even occlusion [4, 32]. In this study, the multifocal territorial distribution of postoperative cerebral infarctions across multiple vascular territories, coupled with a comparable incidence rate among all surgical groups, indicates that delayed cerebral ischemia secondary to SAH-induced vasospasm served as a important etiological factor. The majority of patients required therapeutic intervention for symptomatic vasospasm. SAH-induced cerebral edema increases the anatomical complexity of contralateral approaches, and excessive intraoperative retraction may lead to iatrogenic cerebral injury [8, 33]. For patients with modified Fisher scores of 0–2, meticulous dissection of basal cisterns or ventriculostomy should be performed to facilitate CSF drainage prior to contralateral aneurysm clipping, thereby minimizing brain retraction and reducing frontal lobe injury. In cases with modified Fisher scores of 3–4, surgical complexity increases due to severe hemorrhagic edema and arachnoid adhesions. Severe SAH is frequently associated with high Hunt-Hess grades (Grade Ⅲ–Ⅴ), which correlates with poorer prognosis. Studies have demonstrated that elevated Hunt-Hess grade serves as an independent risk factor for neurological deterioration in patients with ruptured aneurysms [9, 34]. Electrolyte imbalances occurred in 7 patients (13.2%), mostly presenting as hyponatremia. These were likely related to common neurohormonal disorders after SAH, with no evidence of diabetes insipidus. Although dissection near the pituitary stalk and hypothalamus poses a theoretical risk, the similar low incidence among groups indicates that the SCPIS approach does not increase hypothalamic-pituitary injury.

In clinical practice, the decision to utilize a specific surgical space, including the SCPIS, should be guided by a synthesis of preoperative imaging (DSA/CTA) findings—such as aneurysm location, projection, and its 3D relationship to the optic chiasm—and intraoperative assessment of anatomical constraints (e.g., chiasm position, arachnoid adhesions). This decision-making is integral to meticulous preoperative planning. Furthermore, successful outcomes depend on advanced microsurgical technique and specific postoperative vigilance, particularly in managing SAH-related complications such as cerebral vasospasm, hydrocephalus, and electrolyte imbalances.

This study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective study, potential selection bias cannot be excluded. Secondly, the single-center design employed in this study may limit the generalizability of our findings. Third, given the relatively small sample size in the SCPIS group, we were unable to conduct formal stratified statistical analyses of outcome indicators including length of hospital stay stratified by aneurysm rupture status. Nor could we further explore the subtle effects of management measures such as cerebrospinal fluid drainage on outcomes across different subgroups. Nevertheless, all enrolled cases were managed following clinically optimal strategies at the time, and rigorous statistical analyses supports our conclusions. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts are warranted to develop predictive criteria for the SCPIS approach. This would require the systematic collection and analysis of detailed preoperative imaging features, such as aneurysm geometry (e.g., dome-to-neck ratio, aspect ratio), its 3D spatial relationship to the optic apparatus (e.g., projection angle, degree of chiasmal obscuration), chiasm position, and the severity of local subarachnoid hemorrhage. Correlating these imaging characteristics with the intraoperative necessity for utilizing the SCPIS could help establish preoperative assessment guidelines. Naturally, the assessment of visual outcomes was limited to clinical evaluation of pupillary reflexes and gross function. The absence of systematic preoperative and postoperative formal visual field testing represents a significant limitation in fully evaluating the risk of optic apparatus injury, a critical endpoint for any approach manipulating the parasellar region. This is also an important focus for future prospective studies. Ultimately, this would facilitate the transition of the SCPIS from a salvage intraoperative option to a strategically planned surgical space, thereby optimizing preoperative workflow and decision-making.

Conclusion

The SCPIS enables exposure of contralateral ICA segments obscured by the optic chiasm, permitting clipping of aneurysms involving these regions. ACP drilling expands the operative space, enhancing microinstrument maneuverability. Compared to the PCS and contralateral OCS, the SCPIS represents a safe and effective alternative surgical space.
