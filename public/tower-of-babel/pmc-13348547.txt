Knowledge, attitude, and practices on gestational weight gain among pregnant women, partners, female household members, and healthcare providers: a mixed-method study in Tanzania
Authors: Emmy Metta, Alfa Muhihi, Mohamed Bakari, Andrea B Pembe, Dongqing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational weight gain (GWG) is a significant indicator of the mother’s health and the quality of life of her growing fetus. However, little is documented on knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding GWG management in low— and middle-income countries, particularly in Tanzania. This study aimed to examine knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding gestational weight gain among pregnant women, their partners and female household members, and healthcare providers in Tanzania.MethodsA cross-sectional mixed-method study was conducted between January and March 2024 at Mnazi Mmoja Hospital and Buguruni Health Center in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. Quantitative data were collected on knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding GWG through structured researcher administered questions to pregnant women (n = 100). Qualitative data were collected through 37 in-depth interviews with pregnant women and focus group discussions with partners (n = 24) and female household members (n = 18). Descriptive analysis was used to analyze quantitative data from structured questions, and thematic analysis to analyze qualitative data.ResultsGWG during pregnancy was confirmed as an essential indicator of maternal and fetal health. Most pregnant women (74%), partners (79.2%), female members (77.8%), and health care providers (100%) perceived GWG as very important for fetal health. However, excessive or inadequate weight gain was reported to be unfavorable, although few knew the recommended weight gain during pregnancy. Family members encouraged women to gain weight and eat as if they were “eating for two”. Weight measurements were routinely conducted; however, unless alarming signs were observed, results were not communicated to the women due to personnel shortages and heavy workloads. Healthcare providers lacked competence in GWG counseling. Only 58% of healthcare providers reported being aware of at least one GWG guideline. Inaccurate and unreliable weighing machines challenged the consistency of gestational weight management during antenatal care visits.ConclusionsLimited knowledge of recommended gestational weight gain, limited counseling skills, and unreliable weight-management amenities challenged GWG management practices. Efforts to improve understanding and practices regarding GWG among stakeholders are critical for strengthening weight-gain measurement and management during pregnancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09248-1.Keywords: Gestational weight gain, Weight management, Pregnancy, Excessive weight gain, Inadequate weight gain, Knowledge, Attitudes, Practices

Introduction

Gestational weight gain (GWG) is a significant predictor of maternal and fetal health [1]. Inadequate or excessive GWG increases the risk of poor maternal and fetal health outcomes [2]. Inadequate GWG raises the risk of low birth weight, small-for-gestational-age babies, and preterm delivery, whereas excessive GWG increases the risk of maternal and perinatal complications, including gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, and intrauterine fetal death [3, 4]. Excess weight gain during pregnancy is also linked to women’s postpartum health consequences, including postpartum weight retention, depression, and obesity [1]. Managing GWG is critical to counteract short- and long-term adverse pregnancy and childbirth outcomes. outcomes.

The adequacy of GWG is generally measured against the body mass index (BMI)-specific recommendations set by the Institute of Medicine (IOM) of the United States. The recommended range of weight gain depends on the woman’s pre-pregnancy BMI. According to the 2009 IOM guidelines, women with a pre-pregnancy BMI less than 18.5 kg/m2 should gain 12.5–18 kg; those with a pre-pregnancy BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m2 should gain 11.5–16 kg; those with a pre-pregnancy BMI of 25.0 to 29.9 kg/m2 should gain 7–11.5 kg; and those with a pre-pregnancy BMI of ≥ 30.0 kg/m2 should gain 5–9 kg [5]. The guidelines require healthcare providers to counsel women, educate them on the recommended GWG, and monitor their weight gain throughout the pregnancy [6]. However, implementation of the guidelines depends, among other factors, on whether healthcare workers are aware of their existence and knowledgeable about the recommendations.

Discordant weight from the clinically recommended weight gain exposes the woman to a multitude of risks in pregnancy and birth outcomes [7]. The woman should gain the appropriate weight during her pregnancy as a precursor to a positive pregnancy and birthing experience [6]. Inadequate weight gain is reportedly a public health challenge in Sub-Saharan African countries [8, 9]. As such, there is a need for a detailed understanding of the knowledge, attitudes, and practices characterizing GWG management among pregnant women to inform tailored interventions that strengthen GWG management and improve maternal and infant health.

Existing literature indicates varying attitudes and perceptions toward GWG among pregnant women, with some viewing weight gain and larger body sizes as indicators of a healthy pregnancy [10]. In contrast, others associate excessive weight gain with complications during pregnancy and childbirth [1]. It also shows that women’s knowledge of the appropriate GWG during pregnancy and the related health consequences of excessive weight gain on fetal health outcomes is limited [11, 12]. Misconceptions about weight gain and a lack of knowledge about the required GWG are widely reported among women, regardless of age and educational level [13, 14]. This could be a result of inadequate GWG education provided by healthcare providers to pregnant women during their antenatal care visits [6, 7]. Understanding providers’ feelings about discussing GWG management with pregnant women would yield insights to improve GWG management practices.

Studies documenting gestational weight-management practices in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in Tanzania, remain limited. The current study aimed to document the existing knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding GWG management in an urban setting among healthcare providers, pregnant women and their partners, and female household members. This information could be useful for shaping context-specific interventions to improve weight-gain management practices during pregnancy and to improve maternal health and childbirth outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a mixed-methods design, integrating quantitative and qualitative data collection approaches. The quantitative component assessed participants’ knowledge of GWG and gestational weight management practices, while the qualitative component explored participants’ experiences, perceptions, and contextual factors influencing gestational weight gain and management during pregnancy. Data were integrated during interpretation to triangulate findings from both components, situating numerical trends within participants’ experiences and perspectives. The integration of quantitative and qualitative data enabled a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of gestational weight gain and management than either approach alone, thereby strengthening the study’s validity and the depth of its conclusions.

Data collection took place from January to March 2024 at two public antenatal clinics in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. Mnazi Mmoja Hospital and Buguruni Health Center in Ilala Municipal were purposively selected because they served a diverse population and had higher annual antenatal attendance.

Participants and recruitment

Pregnant women

Participants for this study were 100 pregnant women attending antenatal care (ANC) clinics at Mnazi Mmoja Hospital and Buguruni Health Center in Dar es Salaam were approached during routine clinic visits and invited to participate in the study’s quantitative component. Eligible participants were women aged 18–49 years, at varying gestational ages and parity levels (primigravida and multigravida), who provided written informed consent. Participation was voluntary, and the study sample does not represent all women attending ANC at the two facilities during the study period. Participants were approached consecutively and consented to participate until the sample size of 100 was reached. From this group, a purposive subset of 18 pregnant women was selected to participate in in-depth interviews (IDIs) to obtain detailed insights into their attitudes, perceptions, and practices regarding gestational weight gain (GWG).

Spouse/partner

Spouses or partners of enrolled pregnant women were invited to participate in the study to capture household-level perspectives on GWG. Partners were approached for recruitment either when they accompanied the pregnant women to the ANC visit or when not present, were contacted after the visit. Participating spouses and partners took part in Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) to gain a broader understanding of their perceptions and attitudes toward GWG and its management.

Female household members

Female relatives living in the same household as the enrolled pregnant women were also recruited to the study to examine family influences on GWG. Recruitment occurred either during the ANC visit, when female household members accompanied the pregnant woman or through follow-up contact when they were not present at the clinic.

Healthcare providers

Healthcare providers who delivered ANC services at the study clinics were recruited to participate in the study. Eligible participants included nurses and clinicians with experience providing ANC. The healthcare providers were purposively recruited from a list of ANC providers stratified by study clinic and cadre.

Data collection

Quantitative data collection

Trained research nurses collected demographic and socioeconomic information, reproductive history, pre-existing health conditions, and self-reported pre-pregnancy weight from pregnant women using interviewer-administered questionnaires (Supplementary file 1b). The questionnaire also collected information on pregnant women, their partners, and their female household members’ knowledge of GWG and gestational weight-management practices. It covered questions about awareness of optimal GWG, the short- and long-term effects of inadequate and excessive GWG on maternal and child health, and the perceived importance of appropriate gestational weight gain for both mother and baby. Structured questions were also used to collect data on knowledge and practices related to gestational weight gain from health care providers (Supplementary file 2b), partners of the pregnant women (Supplementary file 3b), and female household members (Supplementary file 4b). The questionnaire was pretested with a small sample of pregnant women and healthcare providers with characteristics similar to those of the study population to assess face validity, clarity, and comprehension. Feedback from the pre-test was used to refine wording and improve question clarity before the main data collection.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data were collected by a team of experienced research assistants with bachelor’s degrees in medical sociology, public health, and nutrition. Before the main data collection, the team pretested the interview guides with a separate group of individuals not included in the study to ensure clarity, comprehensibility, and appropriateness of the questions. The pre-testing enabled the team members to familiarize themselves with the topic guides, estimate the required time per interview and FGD, assess the suitability of the questions and themes for addressing the study objectives, evaluate the reliability of the data collection procedures, and practice using the audio-recording equipment. The piloted IDI and FGD guides (Supplementary files 1a, 2a, 3a, and 4a) were used to guide the study interviews and focus group discussions, respectively. The IDIs were one-to-one interviews that engaged pregnant women and healthcare providers. The FGDs engaged the partners of the pregnant women and female household members and were moderated and managed by a facilitator and a note-taker. The IDIs and FGDs captured participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices [15] and provided detailed information on their prevailing perspectives and experiences regarding GWG management. The use of IDIs facilitated a thorough understanding of the phenomena in question [16], and the FGDs provided breadth of insight into participants’ views and ideas [17] that were significant for addressing the study objective.

All data collection activities were conducted in Swahili, the local language spoken by the participants and the data collection team. The interviews and FGDs were conducted in private rooms within the participating health facilities, accessible only to the study participants, to maintain their privacy and confidentiality. The interviews lasted 45 to 55 min, while the FGDs lasted 60 to 90 min. All IDIs and FGDs were audio recorded with the participants’ informed consent. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved within each participant group, defined as the point at which no new themes or insights emerged. Saturation was assessed separately for pregnant women, spouses/partners, female household members, and healthcare providers to ensure adequate representation of perspectives relevant to gestational weight gain and its management.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Basic characteristics of the study participants were examined descriptively, with percentages for categorical variables and means and standard deviations for continuous variables. Participants’ knowledge of GWG was described using percentages.

The audio recordings of IDIs and FGDs were transcribed verbatim into Swahili Word documents within 72 h of their completion to facilitate follow-up on emerging issues and points of clarification during subsequent discussions and interviews. The transcribed data were reviewed and cross-checked for quality and comprehensiveness before being imported into NVivo 14 software to facilitate management and analysis. All transcripts were analyzed in their original language, “Swahili,” to preserve participants’ meanings.

Open coding was used to label, define, and develop categories from participants’ descriptions. Both inductive and deductive approaches were used to group codes into themes. Data coding and analysis followed the thematic analysis framework prescribed by Broun & Clarke [18], including familiarizing with the data, generating initial codes, creating themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming the themes, and writing the manuscript. The analysis focused on emerging themes, patterns, similarities, and differences. This approach enabled the search for latent, deeper meanings that shaped the knowledge, attitudes, and practices observed in the study. Verbatim quotations were translated into English and incorporated into the results to present participants’ sentiments directly. Translations were conducted by the first and second authors and cross-checked through discussion to ensure accuracy and fidelity to participants’ intended meanings.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

A total of 100 pregnant women were enrolled in the quantitative component of the study, of whom 18 participated in the qualitative study component. Their ages ranged from 18 to 34, with a mean of 25.5 (5.1) years. Most (77.8%) were married or lived with their partners. More than half (55.6%) had completed secondary education, and 61.1% had been pregnant previously and were in their third trimester. More than three-quarters (77.8%) perceived their pre-pregnancy weight as normal, and 94.4% had their weight measured at each ANC visit (Table 1).

Awareness and knowledge of GWG

One-third (33%) of pregnant women, 20.8% of spouses/partners, and 61.1% of female household members reported hearing about GWG. All healthcare providers (100%) were aware of GWG. Despite most participants acknowledging the importance of appropriate GWG during pregnancy for the health of the mother and baby, only 11% of pregnant women, 16.7% of spouses/partners, and 11.1% of female household members reported knowing the best amount of weight a pregnant woman should gain throughout pregnancy (Table 3).

During IDIs and FGDs, participants expressed knowledge of the importance of GWG for the mother’s health, as well as for the health of the fetus and the future baby. Participants generally agreed that as a pregnant woman gains weight, the fetus also grows. When emphasizing the importance of gaining weight during pregnancy, a pregnant woman said:

“…It is important to gain weight because how I gain weight is how the fetus in my womb grows, so I need to gain weight.” Preg208.

Discussions between pregnant women and partners also cited weight gain during pregnancy as an indicator that the mother and the unborn baby are healthy. To have a healthy baby, “a baby with good weight” was spontaneously reported by pregnant women as the main reason for gaining weight during pregnancy. This was explicitly stated in the women’s interviews, with some questioning whether gaining weight during pregnancy is an option for a mother who wants to have a healthy baby. Gaining weight was considered essential for a pregnant woman, as explained:

“… Isn’t it necessary for a pregnant woman to gain weight to have a healthy baby? Is there an option? Now, what do you think? Who doesn’t want a healthy baby? Honestly, I would like my baby to be born healthy; at the moment, I am setting aside the fear of becoming obese. Now, I am focusing on the health of the one I am carrying …” Preg213.

The pregnant women further explained that, during their routine antenatal care visits, nurses insist that they eat well to ensure the health of both them and their babies. Similarly, healthcare providers encourage pregnant women to gain weight, reasoning that doing so will ensure the mother has a healthy baby. The pregnant women elaborated that a healthy baby is born weighing many kilograms, and everyone who sees it knows the baby is healthy, which makes mothers feel good. One of the pregnant women was explicitly detailed as follows:

“A healthy baby is one born weighing many kilograms. She looks healthy, and anyone who sees her knows she was born healthy… You hear people say “mashallah,” she is healthy… that makes me feel good…” Preg350.

Generally, all study participants spontaneously indicated that weight gain is expected during pregnancy, and none of the pregnant mothers was expected not to gain weight.

Knowledge of the recommended GWG

Despite participants reporting that gaining weight during pregnancy is essential, demonstrating knowledge of the recommended weight gain was challenging. None of the participants were comfortable or confident enough to express awareness of the recommended weight gain for pregnant women, even among the pregnant women themselves. “I do not know” and “I am not aware that there is a limit” were common responses on this topic, as one of these women detailed:

“…I do not know the limit… what I know is that a woman experiences bodily changes because of the pregnancy. I must add weight because the baby in me is growing and gaining weight as well… but I am not aware that there is a limit I should gain.” Preg216.

Discussions among female household members were mixed regarding gestational weight-gain recommendations. Some participants opined that weight gain during pregnancy should vary by woman. It was noted that women who are expected to gain more weight during pregnancy are taller, as height helps balance the weight. Other participants agreed that each pregnant woman should gain weight, regardless of height. A few participants argued that the weight limit during pregnancy should not exceed 100 kg, regardless of height. Reaching over 100 kg of weight was considered improper during pregnancy, regardless of whether the pregnancy was for twins, because the woman would be too heavy to support herself and the baby. In one of the female household members’ focus group discussions, a discussant said:

“…in any case, her weight should not exceed 100 kilograms, regardless of height, because that would make her too heavy to manage herself and the baby, even if she had twins….” FGD05.

Healthcare providers also reported limited knowledge of recommended weight gain during pregnancy: only 58.3% were aware of GWG guidelines, and 54.2% applied them in clinical practice. The proportion of HCPs who knew the correct GWG recommended by the IOM for underweight, normal weight, overweight, and pregnant women with obesity was 25.0%, 20.8%, 16.7%, and 4.2%, respectively (Table 3).

During IDIs, no HCP could explicitly state the recommended GWG for each category. A common response was that health care providers had not been informed or oriented to gestational weight-management guidelines, despite routinely weighing women during antenatal clinic visits. HCPs also reported that a pregnant woman should gain weight, but were unsure of the amount to be gained.

“What I know is that a pregnant woman should gain weight. How much she should gain, I don’t know, to be honest, but she should be gaining weight each time she comes for the routine antenatal care services…” HCP405.

Similar to discussions with female household members, a few interviews with healthcare providers also raised the idea of varying gestational weight gain. However, these providers were confident that the magnitude of weight gain should vary among women depending on their pre-pregnancy weight. They further explained that a woman who is underweight before pregnancy is expected to gain more weight during pregnancy to ensure sufficient energy to support herself and the baby. Similarly, healthcare providers expressed the view that women with pre-pregnancy obesity might not need to gain much weight during pregnancy because they will already have enough strength for themselves and the baby.

“Although I do not know exactly how much weight they should gain, I believe it will not be the same for all women. I imagine the underweight pre-pregnant woman should gain more weight during her pregnancy to support herself and the baby, while for the obese woman, even a small gain will be enough because she already has that extra energy…” HCP406.

When probed about the specific recommended weight based on women’s pre-pregnancy weight status (i.e., underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obese), healthcare providers hesitated to express their understanding. Responses such as “I am unsure” or “I never followed this closely” were common. Except for one, all healthcare providers reported a lack of awareness of GWG management guidelines. This persisted even after being explicitly probed about understanding the IOM guidelines for pregnancy weight gain and the INTERGROWTH-21st standards for GWG. The individual who reported awareness noted that she had previously heard of the INTERGROWTH-21st but could not recall where she had heard of it or what it recommends. The only guideline reported as familiar to healthcare providers was the Providers’ Guide, Antenatal Care in Tanzania Mainland (URT 2018), which included information on diet and weight gain. However, no one was able to cite the guide’s specific statements. These findings indicate substantial gaps in healthcare providers’ knowledge of gestational weight gain recommendations and limited familiarity with both international and national guidelines.

Perceived consequences of inadequate GWG

More than a quarter (27%) of pregnant women, more than one-third (37.5%) of spouses/partners, and 77.8% of female household members were aware of at least one consequence of inadequate GWG. During IDIs with pregnant women and FGDs, opinions were mixed regarding the consequences of inadequate GWG. Some noted that inadequate GWG is an indication of unfavorable pregnancy experiences and outcomes. It was perceived to be related to frequent headaches, fatigue, feeling unwell, loss of appetite, and frequent illnesses. Delayed fetal growth —defined as slower-than-expected fetal growth observed during antenatal visits—and unhappy pregnancies, referring to pregnant women’s experiences of emotional distress or dissatisfaction during pregnancy, were also perceived as consequences of inadequate GWG. Other participants perceived that losing weight during pregnancy is uncommon, questioning how a pregnant woman can lose weight while carrying a living, growing fetus. When elaborating on this, one of the participants in the partners’ focus group discussions said:

“… I am not sure how it is possible that one is pregnant and is not gaining weight. I think something might be wrong because it is not usual. One would expect that, because this woman is carrying a living thing that is also growing, the weight will accumulate; short of that,… I think … is a big problem…” FGD02.

Healthcare providers added that inadequate gestational weight gain increased the risks of frequent illnesses and opportunistic infections in mothers during pregnancy due to low immunity. It was further cited that insufficient GWG increased the risk of anemia and miscarriage.

“When a pregnant woman is not adding adequate weight, it may lead her to frequent illnesses and opportunistic infections because we know when the mother is severely or moderately malnourished, she becomes anemic, and her immunity also weakens, making it easy to get diseases and even miscarriage.” HCP511.

Inadequate GWG was perceived to carry severe consequences for maternal and fetal outcomes during delivery. There was consensus among participants that a woman with inadequate weight during pregnancy would not have enough strength to push her baby during delivery, leading to a cesarean section. Similar to healthcare providers, the female household member added that a woman with insufficient weight gain during her pregnancy is more likely to experience premature labor. Some of the pregnant women noted that, depending on individual bodily makeup and strength, a woman with insufficient GWG could have a healthy baby. However, she will experience challenges during delivery because she will not have enough strength to push the baby out, and, if not well supported, may end up losing her life or that of the baby, or both.

“…I do not know how to say it because it is a problem, but it also depends on the individual’s bodily makeup; you may find that one had insufficient gestational weight gain yet had a healthy baby… the main challenge I see is during labor because she will not be able to push the baby out on her own; she will need support. If she does not get enough support, the chances are high that she will either lose her life or that of her baby, or both of them…” Preg306.

The reason given for the possibility of an underweight woman having a healthy baby was that the fetus takes everything it needs without considering what remains for the mother. As a consequence, you find the mother is weak, but the baby is healthy.

“…what I know is that anything a pregnant woman eats goes directly to the baby in the womb, without regard for what remains for the mother, until the baby has taken enough… that is why you can find an underweight woman giving birth to a healthy baby, until you envy her… I mean, you find the woman is weak to the extent that she cannot get out of the labor ward, but her baby is laughing and in good health. That is where you fail to understand God, and in most cases, women of this kind die, leaving their babies behind” Preg311.

It was further reported that underweight pregnant women are prone to delivering low-birth-weight infants. Delivery of unhealthy babies and a shortage of breast milk were also reported as post-delivery consequences for pregnant women with inadequate GWG. The pregnant women thought that babies born to women who were underweight were susceptible to illnesses because their bodies would not have accumulated enough immunity.

“…the baby will be weak and can easily get diseases because she did not grow well and did not accumulate enough immunity before birth…” Preg210.

Some pregnant women reported that their family members encouraged them to make efforts and ensure they gain adequate weight to avoid the risks of delivering underweight babies and being held for extended observation at the facility after delivery. It was explained that low-birth-weight infants are typically retained at the health facility for several days after delivery, allowing physicians to continue monitoring before discharge. Furthermore, infants born with low birth weight were described as not good-looking and weak, which raised family members’ doubts and questions about whether the mother had other chronic health conditions. When explaining this, one of the women referred to her neighbor’s daughter, who had an underweight baby.

‘…she gained very little as a result, had a tiny baby, I mean, not even enough to carry in my hands (showing her two hands combined). This made her aunt ask if she was okay or if there might be a chronic disease unknown to them because people felt bad seeing the baby, and by that time, she had already stayed in the hospital for more than two weeks.” Preg309.

A few pregnant women reported being informed by healthcare providers that they needed to gain weight, although the reason for this advice was never explained:

“I mean, when they check your weight and find it is not rising, they tell you to minimize stress and try your best to get a balanced diet, so you gain weight… with your pregnancy, not gaining weight is not good.” Preg319.

Generally, participants recognized that both inadequate and excessive weight gain are undesirable, though their understanding of specific risks varied.

Perceived consequences of excessive GWG

Excessive weight gain was perceived to carry several risks, not only during pregnancy but also during and after delivery. More than a quarter (28%) of pregnant women, one-third (33%) of spouses/partners, and 72.2% of female household members were aware of at least one consequence of excessive GWG. During IDIs and FGDs, it was noted that pregnant women who gain too much weight are at increased risk of developing hypertension and gestational diabetes. Female household participants also mentioned swollen legs, frequent fatigue, and difficulty breathing as potential consequences of excessive weight gain during pregnancy. Similar concerns were also raised during in-depth interviews with the pregnant women:

“When the weight is too much, the legs swell, high blood pressure develops, you become tired and strengthless, you have difficulty breathing, and you may also develop gestational diabetes… I mean, problems become many, and whenever you try to do something, you feel very tired, the weight overpowers you, and you become lazy and helpless because you have too much weight…” Preg001.

Excessive weight gain was also associated with the fetus’s poor breathing and increased risk of perinatal mortality:

“If the weight is too much, the mother’s breathing becomes less, and the baby in the womb may not be able to breathe properly, leading to death. If the mother undergoes surgery, it is possible that the mother recovers and the baby dies.” Preg223.

Carrying excess weight was considered a potential trigger for severe consequences for the pregnant woman and her baby during delivery. It was noted that in most cases, women with obesity experience cesarean sections because they usually have larger babies, making vaginal delivery difficult. Women reiterated that they were advised not to gain excessive weight during pregnancy due to the related consequences, as this pregnant woman expressed:

“The nurse said we are not supposed to gain too much weight because there are consequences, such as failing to have a vaginal delivery due to large babies, which can cause severe vaginal tears or the death of the baby during delivery, so we have to do our best to avoid gaining too much weight……” Preg314.

Stillbirth, excessive bleeding, eclampsia during delivery, and maternal death were also reported as potential consequences of delivery among women with excessive GWG. Most of these perceived risks were attributed to what participants heard from pregnant women attending the antenatal clinic, as elaborated during the focus group discussions with female household members.

“Too much weight causes problems. Once, there was a very fat woman at the clinic; she came for delivery, but they could not handle her there. She was sent to the district hospital for an operation… she was too fat, had swollen legs, and hypertension… the nurse told us that fatness is not good for a pregnant woman because the baby also becomes too big… the mother won’t be able to push. It can cause problems during delivery, and the mother or the baby may be lost…” FGD05.

Gaining excessive weight during pregnancy was further documented as likely to adversely affect the newborn. Discussions among female household members and partners indicated that babies born to women with obesity are prone to birth defects, but none mentioned the types of defects envisioned. It was also noted that those babies are likely to have high blood pressure, diabetes, and heart disease, similar to their mothers.

“A child may be born with heart illness… in most cases, they are born with many health problems. Some are hypertensive and have difficulty breathing. I mean, similar problems that an overweight woman experiences during pregnancy also affect her baby” FGD03.

Ideas were raised that babies born to women with excessive GWG are at risk of having a convulsion at birth. Surprisingly, many pregnant women reported not knowing the consequences of excessive weight gain during pregnancy for their babies. Instead, they believed that pregnant women with excessive weight gain would have babies who weigh many kilograms, “healthy babies” that are perceived to grow well because a healthy baby rarely becomes ill.

“… honestly, I don’t know if there is any health problem related to gaining too much weight during pregnancy; I thought that if a pregnant woman gains excessive weight, the baby will weigh many kilograms, she will be healthy, and babies born weighing many kilograms grow very well because diseases rarely attack those babies…” Preg342.

Babies born to women who are obese were also reported to be lazy and inactive, to have difficulty or delayed walking because they are too heavy, and to be likely to become slow learners.

Gestational weight management practices

Pregnant women reported that their body weight is measured most often during routine pregnancy monitoring at the antenatal care clinic. However, they held mixed views on the value of these routine weight measurements. Some of these women believed that weight measurements were beneficial for understanding how they and their future babies were progressing, whether they were gaining too much or too little weight, because either could cause problems. Other pregnant women viewed weight measurement as a standard clinic procedure, and a few wondered why healthcare providers measured their weight during each ANC visit.

“…honestly, I do not know yet why they [healthcare providers] measure my weight every month I come to the clinic.” Preg210.

The healthcare providers noted that routine weight measurement is standard practice for all pregnant women at the ANC. It helps providers monitor the health of both the mother and the baby and identify potential issues early. When asked how it was done, the women reported that they usually attended the ANC clinic once or twice per month, depending on the pregnancy trimester, and the first service before seeing the doctor was weighing and measuring blood pressure. Despite regular measurements, pregnant women expressed concern that they were rarely informed of their weight results. It was noted that the healthcare provider would commonly record results on the clinic card and hand it over to the woman to continue with other clinic processes.

“I mean, after measuring, she fills out the card and hands it over to you; that is all; she says nothing,” Preg309.

The pregnant woman expressed the need to be informed about their weight and the consequences it might entail. The desire to know how much one should gain during pregnancy and what to do to reach the target was also expressed. Most of the pregnant women reported that healthcare providers never provided them with counseling or education on how to manage their gestational weight or the importance of doing so. They further explained that the healthcare providers did not have time to discuss weight gain issues with the women unless they found one with a problem, either underweight or obese. A few pregnant women mentioned that they sometimes heard providers question one of the women about why she was not gaining weight and insist that she eat well to gain weight.

“You are not gaining weight; why? Why are you not gaining? Ensure you eat well and take enough food to gain weight. This weight is not good for you with your pregnancy condition.” Preg342.

Some women mentioned that they never intended to manage and control their gestational weight because they were not informed about the required weight limits and the associated consequences. Consequently, even when one gains excessive weight, one still thinks it will be okay, as described by this woman:

“… first of all… I never controlled my weight or even thought about losing weight because I am less informed about the limits and the associated consequences of fatness… that is why you see, when pregnant, we all wish to gain much weight, believing we will give birth to healthy babies …we wish we knew because when you lose weight, you become worried, thinking, I will have a small child, what will happen consequently, even if the weight increases tremendously, you think it is okay, as you don’t know about the consequences, since we are less informed …” Preg213.

During interviews with healthcare providers, the shortage of healthcare personnel was cited as a reason for the lack of communication with pregnant women about their weight measurement results, as affirmed by one of the healthcare providers:

“We understand the importance of providing feedback to the women about their weight measurements, but sometimes, when you are alone at the clinic and see the queue of women waiting, you find yourself prioritizing other aspects of care. Honestly, we are trying our best to do what we can without having to delay the mothers too much.” HCP409.

Healthcare providers maintained that women must gain weight within specific limits during pregnancy: “It should not be too much or too little,” a principle monitored through routine weighing. They further explained that when a woman experiences inadequate or excessive weight loss or gain at each visit, this is considered a cause for concern. In such circumstances, regardless of clinic workload, they would provide counseling and education to women on the consequences and advise them on achieving or maintaining an appropriate weight. When such women were identified based on their gestational age, referrals were arranged for further support to higher-level health facilities:

“In case we find a woman we consistently observe, instead of gaining weight, her weight decreases or remains stagnant, or increases by too many kilograms each visit, say 4 kilos each visit, then we know there might be a problem. We do not stay with that woman. We provide a referral so she gets more support at a higher level to prevent serious problems later.” HCP507.

When healthcare providers were asked about their feelings regarding discussing GWG matters with pregnant women, “I feel good; it is a good thing” was a common response. Some reported feeling good about guiding pregnant women to ensure healthy babies and their own health.

“I feel good because it is educational; it gives women guidance on what to do so they can give birth to babies in good health, and the mother remains in good health, too.” HCP401.

The women further reported that family members provided them with advice on managing gestational weight gain. The most commonly cited family members included husbands/partners, sisters, parents, in-laws, aunts, neighbors, and friends, whereas the specific information provided varied. The main issue was that, because of the pregnancy, the women should eat frequently to avoid losing weight; however, none indicated the minimum or maximum weight to maintain. Some women mentioned that their husbands expressed concerns about their weight loss:

“… why are you losing weight? What is the problem? You are not eating well; tell me what the problem is. Are you not getting enough food, or are you getting too tired? I don’t want to see you like that; you are carrying my baby, and you are supposed to be healthy. I want to see you gaining weight, not losing it anymore.” Preg215.

The pregnant women indicated that their family members encourage them to gain weight during pregnancy because the extra weight is good for the baby and considered healthy. Discussions with female household members and partners also revealed that pregnant women are pushed to eat more to gain weight for themselves and for their pregnancy health. This was also noted during the pregnant women’s interviews, when they reported being told to eat and eat because they are eating for two. Ideas that family members thought pregnant women needed to gain more weight for the baby’s health were common in most of the pregnant women’s interviews. When detailing this, one of the women commented:

“My mother pushes me to eat often because she believes that the fatter I become, the healthier the baby will be.” Preg203.

Two women reported being advised by family members, including a sister, not to gain or lose weight during pregnancy. This advice was sometimes experienced as pressure and motivated by the desire to maintain their weight after delivery. They also reported being advised not to overeat and to avoid oily foods, as explained by this woman:

“My sister advises me not to gain too much or lose weight; she insists I watch my eating, avoid overeating, and reduce intake of oily foods so that my weight remains moderate and I do not gain or lose much, so I can retain my weight even after delivery.” Preg328.

Overall, gestational weight management practices were inconsistently implemented, with limited communication and guidance provided during routine antenatal visits.

Challenges to managing gestational weight gain

Healthcare providers reported limited knowledge of the recommended weight gain and found it challenging to incorporate into gestational weight-management practices. They noted that health facilities have insufficient staffing, including personnel with expertise in gestational weight-management counseling. When asked about their confidence in gestational weight management, most were hesitant to express a position, citing the need for training. Many of the providers reported that they are aware that a pregnant woman should gain weight, but are not clear on how much weight should be gained until delivery. They noted that the practice has focused more on weighing pregnant women to determine whether they have gained or lost weight, rather than on the weight-gain limit during pregnancy. When detailing this, one of these providers reported:

“Managing weight gain in these women is challenging because we don’t have the knowledge of the required gestational weight gain. As we speak, I don’t know, for example, how many kilograms should be gained within a month or during the pregnancy. I know that at each visit, a woman should demonstrate weight gain. We focus on the change in weight relative to her previous visit. But to say this woman has gained enough weight and should not add any more, I don’t know that, to be honest.” HCP405.

Limited awareness and familiarity with existing gestational weight gain guidelines among healthcare providers were cited as contributing to these challenges:

“…We do not have adequate awareness and knowledge on guidelines for gestational weight management that could help us refer when weighing these mothers and guide us in counseling when we see deviations….” HCP505.

The providers further noted that gestational weight management is complicated because some pregnant women book their first antenatal clinic visit late, limiting continuous monitoring of their weight gain. They said that sometimes a woman may report to the clinic during her first trimester but disappear until the third trimester, returning to the clinic near her due date and finding that she had either gained excessive or inadequate weight. Dealing with the weight of such a woman was described as difficult.

“The problem is that some women start their clinic and then disappear, showing up late, often near their due date. For no reason, she just doesn’t come, and unfortunately, when she does, you find she either has inadequate weight gain or has gained a lot of weight. Do you think it will be easy to deal with such a woman? It is not an easy task because she will already have other complications.” HCP508.

It was also noted that cultural beliefs and community attitudes toward fatness challenge gestational weight gain management. Healthcare providers reported that in the communities, people view fatness as a sign of health, whether or not one is pregnant. Such beliefs were reported to encourage gaining weight during pregnancy to signal health. Gaining weight as a sign of health during pregnancy was expressed across the focus group discussions with partners, female household members, and the women’s interviews. However, as indicated above, a lack of awareness of the recommended weight gain and its related consequences was also expressed.

Another reported challenge is the lack of accurate and reliable weighing machines. Weighing machines were frequently out of order, provided unreliable results, or measured only weight, not height. The machines are sometimes available but are shared with other departments, including outpatient departments, which generally have long queues. Consequently, at times, some women are not weighed. The findings indicate that structural, sociocultural, and health-system barriers impede effective monitoring and management of gestational weight gain.

Discussion

This study examined knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding gestational weight gain (GWG) management among pregnant women, healthcare providers, partners, and female household members in Tanzania. Five key themes emerged: [1] awareness and knowledge of gestational weight gain [2], knowledge of the recommended gestational weight gain [3], perceived consequences of inadequate and excessive gestational weight gain [4], gestational weight management practices, and [5] challenges to managing GWG. These themes highlight gaps in awareness, variations in counseling and self-management practices, and structural and contextual barriers that influence GWG management among pregnant women, their families, and healthcare providers. To enhance interpretive clarity, the discussion is organized within a Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) framework, in which Themes 1 and 2 reflect knowledge, Theme 3 captures attitudes, and Themes 4 and 5 address practices and contextual constraints shaping GWG management across the study populations.

Awareness and knowledge of GWG

Participants’ awareness of GWG was limited. Most pregnant women reported lack of awaness about how much weight they should gain during pregnancy, often responding, “I do not know” or “I am not aware that there is a limit.” Healthcare providers also showed limited knowledge of recommended GWG, with few aware that weight gain should be tailored to pre-pregnancy weight. Partners and female household members likewise lacked accurate knowledge of GWG recommendations. These findings align with other studies showing limited awareness of GWG guidelines among both healthcare providers and the general population [10, 19–21]. This widespread lack of awareness and knowledge on the recommended GWG suggests that gestational weight gain remains poorly understood within routine antenatal care, limiting informed decision-making and consistent support for appropriate weight-gain management during pregnancy. Consequently, current antenatal care systems may inadequately support evidence-informed GWG counseling, potentially contributing to inappropriate weight gain and related maternal and child health risks.

The Provider Guide for Antenatal Care in Tanzania Mainland [22] adopts the IOM recommendations for weight gain based on pre-pregnancy weight; however, only a few providers mentioned that GWG should vary accordingly. Most providers admitted, “I am unsure” or “I never followed this closely.” The lack of familiarity with GWG guidelines reflects gaps in pre- or in-service training, limited access to updated clinical guidelines, or the absence of effective dissemination strategies. Similar observations were also reported in a recent systematic review of midwives’ and obstetricians’ knowledge of GWG guidelines [23]. Furthermore, resource limitations and competing priorities in busy antenatal clinics may hinder HCPs from staying current with evidence-based guidelines [24]. This situation is of particular concern and may affect the quality of counseling provided to pregnant women, underscoring the need for targeted dissemination of evidence-based guidelines and training.

Attitudes towards GWG

Despite limited knowledge of the recommended weight-gain range, participants perceived both inadequate and excessive GWG as unfavorable. Pregnant women expressed concerns that excessive GWG could lead to gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, difficulty during labor, preeclampsia, and perinatal mortality, consistent with findings reported elsewhere [2, 25]. They also noted long-term consequences, such as postpartum weight retention and increased risk of maternal and childhood obesity, as reported in other studies [26]. Inadequate GWG was associated with risks including stillbirth, low birth weight, small-for-gestational-age babies, and preterm delivery, aligning with evidence from Ethiopia, Tanzania, and other contexts [3, 27–29]. Evidence also exists on the association between excessive GWG and rising adiposity and morbidity in children [3, 26]. The observed perceptions of the risks of inadequate or excessive gestational weight gain underscore the importance of fostering informed attitudes toward optimal gestational weight gain.

Household members often reinforced the belief that women should gain weight, emphasizing that extra weight would benefit the baby and promote health. Common recommendations included frequent eating and avoiding weight loss, reflecting cultural norms such as “eating for two.” Partners and female household members were reported to actively encourage women to gain weight. While well-intentioned, these attitudes may inadvertently contribute to excessive GWG or postpartum weight retention [30]. Similar attitudes and cultural norms were also reported elsewhere [10, 31]. Efforts to strengthen gestational weight management should consider how women’s household members’ attitudes toward GWG influence their own perceptions and behaviors, highlighting the importance of accurate information and a supportive social environment. This underscores the need to further explore the roles of families, social support, and cultural norms and beliefs in GWG management practices.

Healthcare providers reported mixed attitudes toward GWG management. Some expressed a positive outlook—for example, ‘I feel good; it is a good thing’—but routine discussions with pregnant women were infrequent unless there was a concern. This inconsistent communication may lead women to perceive GWG as inevitable and low-risk, aligning with the literature indicating that women often consider weight gain significant only when providers explicitly discuss it [32, 33]. These findings highlight how healthcare providers’ attitudes and practices can directly shape women’s perceptions of the importance of weight management during pregnancy. They underscore the need for targeted interventions that equip providers to engage confidently in GWG discussions, fostering positive attitudes and informed decision-making among pregnant women.

Community beliefs strongly shape attitudes toward body weight and health. In many settings, larger body size (“fatness”) is perceived as a sign of health and prosperity [34, 35] a perception that often extends to pregnant women. Such sociocultural norms can reduce motivation to monitor or manage weight gain during pregnancy, potentially contributing to excessive gestational weight gain. These findings underscore the need for culturally sensitive counseling and educational interventions that promote healthy gestational weight gain while respecting local body ideals and avoiding stigmatization. Engaging community members in these interventions may help address misconceptions about body size and enhance understanding of the health risks associated with both gestational underweight and overweight [36].

Practices related to GWG

Gestational weight management practices

Weight management is recommended as a routine component of ANC in Tanzania. However, pregnant women reported that healthcare providers rarely communicated weight measurement results or discussed GWG management strategies. ANC practices often focus on tracking weight changes without reference to recommended weight-gain limits. Healthcare providers cited lack of training, limited skills, time constraints, and heavy workloads as barriers to consistent communication—a pattern also reported in Ethiopia [36], Canada [37], the United States [38], and elsewhere [39, 40]. Although Tanzanian ANC guidelines mandate managing women’s weight and promoting healthy dietary intake and physical activity [22] providers in this study primarily focused on weighing women rather than counseling them on recommended weight-gain limits or lifestyle practices. Personnel shortages and heavy workloads are common in Sub-Saharan African healthcare facilities [32, 41–44], and inappropriate GWG is frequently reported in the region [9, 32]. These findings highlight the need to balance workload demands with comprehensive support for pregnant women, ensuring that providers engage women in discussions about GWG management to promote optimal weight gain and improve maternal and birth outcomes.

Household and social practices

Family members frequently guided women’s weight-related practices, encouraging eating and discouraging weight loss. Cultural norms and partners’ expectations shaped women’s attitudes toward weight gain, which could contribute to excessive GWG [30]. These findings underscore the importance of engaging social networks in interventions aimed at supporting healthy weight gain during pregnancy.

Structural challenges to GWG management

Challenges in GWG monitoring and management included a lack of accurate and reliable weighing equipment. Machines were sometimes out of order, uncalibrated, limited to measuring weight without height, or shared between departments, compromising the consistency and accuracy of measurements [45]. Height measurement is essential for accurately assessing gestational weight status; omitting it increases the risk of error. Addressing these infrastructural barriers is critical for effective GWG monitoring and management.

Integrated practices across populations

The interplay among healthcare practices, family guidance, and structural challenges highlights the complex context of GWG management. While providers may weigh women regularly, a lack of counseling, limited skills, and equipment constraints reduces the effectiveness of these practices. At the same time, family members’ advice can both support and complicate optimal weight gain. Efforts to improve GWG management should therefore target providers, pregnant women, and their broader social networks.

Methodological considerations

This study employed a mixed-methods design, combining qualitative interviews, focus groups, and structured questions. This approach enabled data triangulation, capturing the nuances of knowledge, attitudes, and practices across diverse populations. The design provided a comprehensive explanation of the themes that emerged, enhancing rigor and trustworthiness [18]. Although conducted in only two health facilities, the study included diverse population groups, capturing a wide range of perspectives likely representative of similar Tanzanian and regional contexts. Future research in rural and varied sociodemographic settings would provide complementary insights to inform interventions promoting optimal GWG.

Conclusion

Weight gain during pregnancy was widely perceived as inevitable, yet knowledge and awareness of recommended GWG remain limited. Inadequate counseling skills, inconsistent communication, and unreliable weighing equipment hindered effective GWG monitoring and management. Integrated interventions targeting pregnant women, household members, and healthcare providers—aimed at improving knowledge, strengthening counseling skills, and addressing structural challenges—are essential to promoting appropriate GWG and safeguarding maternal and child health.
