Tomato DOR encodes a signal peptide peptidase-like protein required for adventitious organogenesis and rooting
Authors: Marybel Jáquez-Gutiérrez, Sandra Bretones, Constanza Martin-Vásquez, Rocío Fonseca, Alberto Aguiar, Benito Pineda, Rafael Lozano, Vicente Moreno, Fernando J Yuste-Lisbona, Alejandro Atarés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKey MessageThe identification of the tomato DOR gene that is essential for adventitious organogenesis provides a genetic target for improving regeneration efficiency and root system performance in tissue culture and plant breeding.AbstractPlant rooting and adventitious organogenesis represent major bottlenecks in the application of plant tissue culture techniques. In this study, we characterize the dor mutant (defective in organogenesis and rooting) identified in our collection of tomato T-DNA lines. This mutant exhibits normal callus proliferation but fails to differentiate adventitious buds. Additionally, it displays underdeveloped embryonic roots, and its adventitious roots derived from various explants are also altered. Grafting experiments revealed compromised in vivo development primarily due to its abnormal root system. Upon identifying an allelic mutant in another tomato line, we observed no co-segregation between a T-DNA insert and the phenotype in either of the identified allelic mutants. Through mapping-by-sequencing, we identified Solyc12g098670 as the gene responsible for this mutation, which is homologous to SIGNAL PEPTIDE PEPTIDASE-LIKE (SPPL) genes from Arabidopsis thaliana, particularly SPPL3 and SPPL5. The expression pattern of DOR in tomato is nearly ubiquitous, similar to SPPL3 in Arabidopsis, yet the regeneration and rooting of sppl3 Arabidopsis mutants resemble the wild-type. In both tomato and Solanum pennellii (Correll) D’Arcy, RNAi lines and plants edited by CRISPR/Cas exhibit a dor mutant phenotype. However, by increasing the expression level of DOR, the plants become indistinguishable from the wild-type in terms of in vitro and in vivo development. Overexpression of this gene in the mutant has enabled the regeneration of plants with a wild-type phenotype. These results demonstrate that DOR is the first member of the tomato SPPL gene family known to be associated with root development and adventitious organogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00299-026-03979-3.Keywords: Adventitious organogenesis, Root development, Solanum lycopersicum, SPPL family, Insertional mutagenesis

Introduction

Vascular plants have a structure with two well-defined parts: above-ground organs usually responsible for photosynthesis and sexual reproduction and below-ground organs responsible for fixing the ground and absorbing water and nutrients. In the very early stages of embryonic development, the shoot apical meristem (SAM) and the root apical meristem (RAM) are already distinguished. The cells that originate in these meristems are differentiated to give many tissues with different functions. The activity of meristems is highly controlled to balance the stem cell division and differentiation. Gene signaling mechanisms operating in the SAM and RAM domains maintain stem cell niches through complex regulatory pathways which involve WUSCHEL (WUS) and WUSCHEL-RELATED HOMEOBOX 5 (WOX5), respectively (Sarkar et al. 2007). In addition, the differentiation is partially reversible since the appearance of new meristems, both SAM (Shin et al. 2020) and RAM (Jing et al. 2020), from differentiated tissues is possible. These processes are known as de novo shoot organogenesis (DNSO) or de novo root regeneration (DNRR).

Plant tissue culture has long been used for micropropagation and genetic improvement. These techniques take advantage of the totipotency that some plant cells maintain throughout their development. Adventitious organ formation is essential for applying these biotechnological techniques and has three different phases: cell dedifferentiation, determination of competent cells to develop a specific organ and organ morphogenesis (Christianson and Warnick 1983). Plant tissue culture methods have been mainly developed with an empirical approach since Skoog and Miller (1957) demonstrated that the balance of auxin and cytokinin led to adventitious organogenesis. The hormonal combination and the selection of the explant type were usually the most investigated factors. However, the effect of the genotype has been rarely analyzed despite evidence of its influence on regeneration capacity. In the case of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), there is significant variability between lines or varieties for morphogenetic potential (Koornneef et al. 1986), a few genes with major effects in this trait have already been identified (Koornneef et al. 1993; Tadeu De Faria et al. 2002; Trujillo-Moya et al. 2011) and some mutants altered in different steps of the adventitious regeneration process have been reported (Sánchez-López et al. 2021). The identification of the genes that determine the morphogenetic response would open new perspectives in basic and applied research. In addition, they could be efficiently transferred and expressed to improve the morphogenetic response.

Roots are essential organs for plant productivity, especially in low water and nutrient availability environments (Lynch 1995). The cellular origins of different roots (e.g., embryonic, lateral, adventitious) can vary greatly, but the adult organs are remarkably similar. Although different stimuli can initiate roots, late patterning mechanisms are frequently shared among them (Birnbaum 2016). Either in the formation of lateral roots or in the regeneration of adventitious roots, auxins play a central role (Adem et al. 2024). Indole butyric acid (IBA) and indole 3-acetic acid (IAA) are the most used hormones because of their higher root-inducing capacity compared with others. While the physiology of adventitious root formation is reasonably well known (Steffens and Rasmussen 2016), the genetic and molecular mechanisms involved are still poorly understood. The identification of the genes that determine the rooting response would open new perspectives in basic and applied research. Knowledge of the sequence of these genes would shed light on the nature of the molecular mechanisms responsible for this morphogenetic response. Their own or flanking sequences could be used as markers in breeding programs. In addition, by new gene editing techniques, such as CRISPR/Cas9, it would be possible to obtain significant information on the effects of specific changes in the sequence of these genes.

Signal peptide peptidase (SPP) is a multitransmembrane aspartic protease with two active sites and plays a crucial role in the human immunological response (Weihofen et al. 2002). In humans, this protein and the four related SPP-like (SPPL) proteases are localized in the endoplasmic reticulum and other subcellular localizations such as Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, and plasma membrane (Mentrup et al. 2017). Orthologs to the human SPP gene have been identified in other animals, microorganisms, and plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana (Tamura et al. 2008) and Oryza sativa (Tamura et al. 2009). In Arabidopsis, there is an ortholog of human SPP and five SPP-like proteases. The mRNAs of AtSPP, AtSPPL1, AtSPPL2, and AtSPPL3 were detected by RT-PCR in roots, leaves, stems, flowers, siliques, and seeds but AtSPPL4 and AtSPPL5 mRNAs were undetectable. In situ hybridization showed that AtSPP was expressed in the endoplasmic reticulum as human SPP while AtSPPL1 and AtSPPL2 were associated with endosomes (Tamura et al. 2008). Moreover, AtSPP has a critical role in the development and function of reproductive tissues in Arabidopsis, especially in male gametophyte development and pollen maturation. T-DNA mutants in AtSPP could not be recovered and the transmission of the mutant allele through pollen was reduced to less than 2% in reciprocal crosses (Han et al. 2009). After the screening of candidate substrates of AtSPP from Arabidopsis pollen or roots expressed genes, fifteen out of twenty-two signal peptides were cleaved (Kusunoki et al. 2020). In rice, two SPP proteases localized in the endoplasmic reticulum have been identified: OsSPP1 and OsSPP2. Both were strongly expressed in vegetative shoot apex, panicle, and florets (Tamura et al. 2009). More recently, rice SPP/SPPL proteases localized to the endoplasmic reticulum were shown to participate in protein degradation associated with this organelle and in tolerance to endoplasmic reticulum stress. Interestingly, single mutants affecting either protease showed no evident stress-related phenotype, whereas the corresponding double mutant exhibited increased sensitivity, indicating functional redundancy (Lu et al. 2026).

The identification of genes that control organogenic capacity is relevant for understanding fundamental mechanisms involved in plant development and manipulating plant traits such as rooting and in vitro regeneration. Consequently, a deeper understanding of these processes would allow faster progress in plant tissue culture methodologies. Insertional mutagenesis has become one of the most useful tools for assessing gene functions. In our case, we have obtained more than 4000 tomato T0 lines and we have already identified 35 genes related to traits such as chlorosis (Quevedo-Colmena et al. 2024) adventitious organogenesis (Sánchez-López et al. 2021), albinism (Micol-Ponce et al. 2020) or leaf development (Jáquez-Gutiérrez et al. 2019). Although T-DNA tagging is the most efficient method for identifying the gene responsible for a mutant phenotype, the mapping-by-sequencing forward genetic approach (Yuste-Lisbona et al. 2021) enables gene isolation even in non-tagged T-DNA mutants. Moreover, the use of in vitro culture as a screening method in T1 lines allowed us to identify many recessive mutants affected in early developmental traits, such as rooting or adventitious organogenesis (Sánchez-López et al. 2021).

In this work, we report the phenotypic, physiological, and genetic characterization of the tomato defective in organogenesis and rooting (dor) mutant that was isolated from the screening of a collection of T-DNA lines generated with an enhancer trap construct. This mutant showed an altered root system, and its explants could not develop adventitious buds. We identified the altered gene underlying the dor mutant by mapping-by-sequencing and found that it was caused by the loss of function of a SPPL gene. We confirmed that both DOR downregulation by RNAi gene silencing and DOR disruption with CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing were responsible for the same observed alterations in the mutant. Our work reveals the crucial role of this tomato SPPL gene in a non-previously described function of this gene family in plants.

Material and methods

Identification and phenotypic characterization of dor mutant

We obtained two tomato collections (cv. P73 and cv. Money Maker) of T-DNA lines by using the enhancer trap vector pD991 that contains the NEOMYCIN PHOSPHOTRANSFERASE II (NPTII) marker gene by the transformation methods previously described (Ellul et al. 2003; Atarés et al. 2011; Pérez-Martín et al. 2017). The T0 lines were self-pollinated to obtain their T1 progenies. We performed an in vitro screening to detect mutants affected in early developmental traits (Jáquez-Gutiérrez et al. 2019; Sánchez-López et al. 2021). T1 seeds were surface-sterilized for in vitro experiments with 50% commercial bleach (a sodium hypochlorite solution equivalent to 50 gL−1 of active chlorine) and some drops of Triton X-100 for 30 min, followed by three washes with sterile water for 5, 10, and 15 min. After sterilizing, T1 seeds were cultivated in seed germination medium (SGM) MS salts + Sucrose 1% + Agar 0.8% (Murashige and Skoog 1962). Shoot apex explants were cut off and transferred to MB3 (Moreno et al. 1984) to produce axenic plants. Cotyledon and hypocotyl explants from 7-day-old T1 seedlings were cultured in tomato shoot (TSIM) and root (TRIM) inducing medium (Sánchez-López et al. 2021). These explants were also cultured in (TCIM) tomato callus inducing medium (MB3 + 2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) 0.1 mg·L−1 + NAA 2.5 mg·L−1 + BAP 1.0 mg·L−1). The pH was adjusted to 5.7 in all media before autoclaving at 121 °C for 25 min. The growth conditions were 25 ᵒC and 16-h light/8-h darkness photoperiod. The light had a photosynthetically active radiation intensity of 100 μmol m−2 s−1 provided by fluorescent tubes (GRO-LUX F36W/GRO-T8; Sylvania, Erlangen, Germany). The embryonic roots of 20-day-old seedling of tomato cv. P73 and dor mutant were photographed and analyzed with the specific software for roots WinRhizo 2019 (Regent Instruments Inc. Canada). The evaluated traits were the total root length (the sum of the lengths of all the roots), the average root diameter and the number of tips.

Reciprocal grafting

Wild-type and dor mutant seedlings were grafted by cutting obliquely at the middle of the hypocotyl as soon as they germinated and both parts were joined with a silicon grafting tube clip. At least three replicates were made for each combination of rootstock and scion. During the first days, plantlets were covered with translucent plastic lids, thus the relative humidity was increased to improve the grafting success. Both self-grafted plants of wild-type and mutant were used as controls. The grafted plants were grown in the greenhouse. All evaluations in planta were performed in a controlled greenhouse environment with long-day photoperiod (16 h of natural light supplemented with Osram lamps 400 W—Powerstar HQI-BT), 24 °C during the day and 18 °C at night. Plants were grown in pots with a mixture of peat:vermiculite (1:1 v/v) and daily automatically irrigated with Hoagland’s nutrient solution.

Hormone quantification

The seeds from P73 and dor mutant plants were surface-sterilized and cultivated in SGM for fifteen days. Each biological replicate contained 100–200 mg of cotyledons (fresh weight) from 6 to 8 plantlets. The tissue was flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground. The samples were suspended in 80% methanol and 1% acetic acid containing internal standards and mixed by shaking for one hour at 4ᵒC. The extract was kept at − 20 ᵒC overnight and then centrifuged and the supernatant dried in a vacuum evaporator. The dry residue was dissolved in 1% acetic acid and passed through an Oasis HLB (reverse phase) column (Seo et al. 2011). For gibberellins, IAA, ABA, SA, and JA quantification, the dried eluate was dissolved in 5% acetonitrile−1% acetic acid, and the hormones were separated using an autosampler and reverse phase UHPLC chromatography (2.6 µm Accucore RP-MS column, 100 mm length × 2.1 mm i.d.; ThermoFisher Scientific) with a 5%–50% acetonitrile gradient containing 0.05% acetic acid, at 400 µL/min over 21 min. For cytokinins, the extracts were additionally passed through an Oasis MCX (cationic exchange) and eluted with 60% methanol- 5% NH4OH to obtain the basic fraction containing CKs. The final eluate was dried and dissolved in 5% acetonitrile with 1% acetic acid and CKs were separated with a 5%–50% acetonitrile gradient over 10 min. The hormones were analyzed with a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Orbitrap detector; ThermoFisher Scientific) by targeted selected ion monitoring. The concentrations of hormones in the extracts were determined using embedded calibration curves and the Xcalibur 4.0 and TraceFinder 4.1 SP1 programs. Deuterated internal standards (except in the case of JA, which used dihydrojasmone) served as control. A significant difference was analyzed through the t-test between the mean concentration of the wild-type and the mutant for each analyzed hormone.

Genetic characterization

A segregation analysis was performed in T1 progenies to check if the mutation was produced by a single gene and to estimate the number of inserts carrying a functional NPTII marker gene (Jáquez-Gutiérrez et al. 2019). A co-segregation analysis was performed in vitro by studying the association between the mutant phenotype and the NPTII gene expression in the T2 progenies by means of a germination test with seeds cultivated in SGM supplemented with kanamycin 100 mgL−1. As the mutant phenotype was not correlated with tolerance to kanamycin, one homozygous line for the mutant phenotype but azygous for tolerance to kanamycin was selected and used for the cloning by sequencing experiments.

Gene cloning

As the T-DNA insertion did not cosegregate with the dor phenotype, a mapping-by-sequencing approach was employed to identify the causal mutation (Yuste-Lisbona et al. 2021). An F₂ mapping population was generated by self-fertilizing F₁ plants derived from a cross between a mutant plant lacking T-DNA insertion and the wild-type tomato S. pimpinellifolium accession LA1589. Genomic DNA was extracted from 100 mg of young leaves using the Plant DNAzol Reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies, San Diego, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of DNA from 40 randomly selected phenotypically wild-type plants and 40 phenotypically mutant plants were pooled to construct wild-type and mutant DNA pools, respectively. These pools were sequenced using paired-end reads on the Illumina HiSeq2000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The sequencing data were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive database at the National Center for Biotechnology Information under BioProject accession number PRJNA1222187. Paired-end reads were aligned to the tomato reference genome (ITAG4.0) using Bowtie2 with default parameters (Langmead and Salzberg 2012). The allele frequency ratio for biallelic variants was calculated as the non-reference allele count divided by the total allele count. A sliding window approach with a window size of 1000 variants and a step size of 100 variants was used to compute the average allele frequency per chromosome using a custom R script. The resulting allele frequency distributions were plotted to pinpoint the chromosomal region harboring the dor mutation. Variants within the candidate genomic region were filtered based on two criteria: (1) the alternative allele must be heterozygous (0/1) in the wild-type pool and homozygous (1/1) in the mutant pool, and (2) the alternative allele must be a unique variant not previously reported in sequenced tomato genomes (Aflitos et al. 2014; Lin et al. 2014).

RNA preparations and gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from leaves using TRIZOL (Invitrogen Life Technologies, San Diego, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. To eliminate residual DNA contamination, the RNA samples were treated with TURBO DNase (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA). cDNA synthesis and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analyses were conducted according to the methodology described by Pérez-Martín et al. (2018). qRT-PCR reactions were performed on a 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using three biological replicates and two technical replicates per genotype. The housekeeping gene UBIQUITIN3 (Solyc01g056940) was used as an internal control. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method (Winer et al. 1999). The specific primer pairs used for qRT-PCR analyses are provided in Table S2.

Gene constructions and transgenic lines

To analyze the function of the DOR (Solyc12g098670) gene, RNA interference (RNAi), CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutagenesis, and overexpression approaches were employed. For gene silencing, an RNAi construct was generated by amplifying a 192-bp fragment spanning the fifth and sixth exons of DOR using the DOR-Ri-F and DOR-Ri-R primers. This fragment was cloned in both sense and antisense orientations into the pKannibal vector (Wesley et al. 2001). The modified pKannibal vector was then digested with NotI, and the resulting restriction fragment was subsequently inserted into the pART27 vector (Gleave 1992). CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutagenesis of DOR was performed following the protocol described by Vazquez-Vilar et al. (2016). The single-guide RNA (sgRNA) target sequence (GCATAGCTGTATAGTATCCC) was designed using the Breaking-Cas software (Oliveros et al. 2016) to target the seventh exon of the DOR gene. For gene overexpression, the full open reading frame of DOR was amplified from cDNA using the DOR-35S-F and DOR-35S-R primers. The amplified cDNA was then cloned into the binary vector pROKII (Baulcombe 1996). Primer sequences used for generating RNAi, CRISPR/Cas9, and overexpression constructs are listed in Table S2. Overexpression and silencing binary vectors, as well as CRISPR/Cas9 constructs, were electroporated into Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404. Genetic transformation of P73 and Money Maker tomato cultivar cotyledons and Solanum pennellii (Correll) D’Arcy leaves was performed as previously described (Ellul et al. 2003). The ploidy level in transgenic plants was evaluated by flow cytometry according to the protocol formerly described (Atarés et al. 2011) and diploid transgenic lines were selected for further phenotypic and expression analyses. Transformation frequency was estimated as the number of diploid independent transgenic events divided by the total number of inoculated cotyledon explants. Primers spanning the sgRNA recognition site (Table S2) were designed to genotype T0 CRISPR/Cas9 lines. PCR products were then purified and cloned into the pGEM-T vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). For allele characterization, at least 10 clones per PCR product were sequenced. Genome editing efficiency was quantitatively assessed using the TIDE software (Brinkman et al. 2014).

Phylogenetic analysis

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic reconstruction of the tomato and Arabidopsis SPP proteases were performed with MEGAX (Kumar et al. 2018). Full-length proteins were aligned using MUSCLE (Edgar 2004). The phylogenetic tree was generated by the Neighbor-Joining method, using Jones-Taylor-Thornton substitution model. A bootstrap analysis with 1000 replications was performed to confirm the reliability of the constructed phylogeny.

Characterization of Arabidopsis lines

Arabidopsis SAIL_841_C07 (ID: N837575) and SAIL_596_D03 (ID: N825395) lines, each harboring a T-DNA insertion that disrupts the coding sequences of AtSPPL3 (AT2G43070) and AtSPPL5 (AT1G05820), respectively, were obtained from the Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre (NASC), University of Nottingham, UK. Arabidopsis Columbia-0 (Col-0) was used as the wild-type control. Genotyping of both wild-type and mutant alleles at the AtSPPL3 and AtSPPL5 loci was performed by PCR, using primers listed in Table S3.

Results

Phenotypic characterization of adventitious bud formation and rooting of dor mutant plants

We screened a tomato T-DNA collection previously generated by our laboratory under axenic conditions to detect affected mutants in early development characters such as rooting or adventitious organogenesis. Interestingly, the dor mutant cotyledon and hypocotyl explants produced a normal dedifferentiated callus in TCIM (Fig. 1a-b). However, no bud regeneration was observed from any of these explants cultured in TSIM (Fig. 1c-d). Moreover, there was no adventitious root development from cotyledon or hypocotyl explants cultured in TRIM (Fig. 1e-f). The dor mutant T1 seedlings also have a markedly affected root development (Fig. 2a). Its embryonic root system in a 20-day-old seedling had half the weight and total length of the wild-type (WT). The average diameter of both roots was similar, but the mutant had a smaller number of tips indicating that lateral rooting was altered in the embryonic root (Fig. 2b). Roots formed adventitiously from stem tissue had the same phenotype as embryonic ones, i.e., slower growing and less branching (Fig. 2c). In 60-day-old seedling, the dor mutant exhibited reduced development in both the roots and aerial parts (Fig. 2d). The mutant is capable of completing its life cycle and producing normal flowers and fruits, although it required more time to do so.

Grafting of a wild-type root restores the shoot altered phenotype of dor mutant plants

We made grafts between rootstock and scion between WT and dor seedlings in every possible way. We analyzed grafted plants for the shoot and root development at 60 days post-grafting. As expected, root development was minimal in self-grafted dor seedlings compared with self-grafted WT plants. The grafting of a WT scion onto a dor rootstock did not affect the anomalous mutant root development. However, when a dor scion was grafted onto a WT rootstock, the development of dor shoot was significantly improved (Fig. 2e). These findings reveal that the adult plant mutant phenotype is mainly caused by root genotype because a plant with WT root and mutant shoot is not distinguished from a WT plant.

Hormone influence on the dor mutant phenotype

Hormone endogenous content was assessed from cotyledon explants of 7-day-old seedlings. There was no significant difference (p < 0.05) in the content of hormones related to rooting or adventitious regeneration hormones such as auxins and cytokinins (Table 1). The only hormone that presented statistically significant differences was ABA. However, the endogenous hormonal analysis carried out from young leaves showed no significant effect for ABA content (t(3) = 0.028, p=0.489), despite dor mutant (M = 440.6 SE = 20.7) attaining slightly higher scores than WT (M = 429.7 SE = 66.1). Moreover, cultivation of shoot apexes in media supplemented with different auxins failed to reverse the mutant phenotype, with mutant plants consistently displaying an underdeveloped root system regardless of the medium used (Figure S1). We also cultivated cotyledon and hypocotyl explants in different modified TSIM media that doubled their hormonal concentration, but the result did not change. No adventitious bud formation was obtained (data not shown). Considering all these results, neither the synthesis nor the perception of hormones seems to be responsible for the mutant phenotype.

Genetic analysis of a dor allelic mutant

The observed T1 co-segregation (37 WT: 12 dor) for mutant phenotype clearly evidenced a single gene recessive inheritance pattern (chi-square = 0.007; P = 0.933). Moreover, segregation analysis in a kanamycin-containing medium (38 KanR: 11 KanS) showed that dor mutant plants bore one T-DNA insert with a functional NPTII marker gene (segregation 3 KanR: 1 KanS; chi-square = 0.170; P = 0.680). We identified three kanamycin-sensitive mutant plants, indicating that the T-DNA insertion did not cosegregate with the mutant phenotype and suggesting that the dor mutation arose through somaclonal variation rather than from the T-DNA insertion.

Another mutant dorMM with a similar dor phenotype and without co-segregation between the mutant phenotype and the expression of the NPTII marker gene was identified in a Money Maker T-DNA line. Root development, organogenic response and phenotype of greenhouse-grown plants were very similar to dor. A genetic complementation test was carried out by crossing homozygous mutant plants to check whether dorP73 and dorMM were allelic mutations. The evaluation of the F1 (dorP73 x dorMM) plants showed that all of them were mutants proving that dorP73 and dorMM were altered in the same gene (Fig. 3a).

The dor phenotype is due to a mutation in a signal peptide peptidase gene with ubiquitous expression in tomato

To identify the mutation responsible for the dor phenotype, we performed mapping-by-sequencing on an F₂ population derived from a cross between dorP73 and the wild tomato S. pimpinellifolium (accession LA1589). The dor mutant phenotype segregates as a monogenic recessive trait (158 wild-type plants, 40 mutants, χ2 = 2.43, P = 0.12). Two DNA pools containing 40 WT and 40 dor F₂ plants were subjected to genome sequencing. A genome-wide analysis of allele frequencies identified a region at the distal end of chromosome 12 as a candidate for harboring the causal mutation (Fig. 3b). Variant analysis of a 5-Mb interval within this candidate region revealed a thymine (T) insertion in exon 14 of the Solyc12g098670 gene (Figure S2). The identified T insertion caused a frameshift, resulting in a premature stop codon at amino acid position 511 out of 532 in dor (Fig. 3c). Co-segregation analysis in the F₂ mapping population confirmed that all 40 plants exhibiting the dor phenotype were homozygous for the T insertion. In contrast, among 158 WT F₂ plants, 51 lacked the T insertion, while 107 were heterozygous for it, suggesting that this insertion mutation in Solyc12g098670 is responsible for the dor phenotype. To further validate this hypothesis, we sequenced the Solyc12g098670 locus in the dorMM mutant, revealing a T deletion in exon 10 (Figure S2). This deletion caused a frameshift, leading to a premature stop codon at amino acid position 395 out of 532 in dorMM (Fig. 3c). Together, these findings provide compelling genetic evidence that Solyc12g098670 is the causal gene underlying the phenotypic alterations observed in the dorP73 and dorMM mutants.

The Solyc12g098670 gene encodes an SPP-like (SPPL) protease homologous to the Arabidopsis AtSPPL3 and AtSPPL5 (Fig. 3d). This gene exhibits ubiquitous expression in both vegetative (root, stem, and leaf) and reproductive organs across all developmental stages analyzed, from floral buds to red ripe fruits. Transcript levels were highest in breaker-stage fruit, while the lowest expression was detected in roots, shoot apices, and floral buds smaller than 2.9 mm (Fig. 3e).

Silencing or loss-of-function mutations in Solyc12g098670 gene phenocopies the dor mutant

To further confirm whether mutations in Solyc12g098670 are responsible for the dor phenotype, we generated RNAi-mediated knockdown lines in two distinct tomato accessions. Notably, the transformation efficiency for obtaining RNAi tomato plants was significantly lower (0.42%) than our typical laboratory rate (~ 15%). In fact, we could not obtain any RNAi-DOR plants in the P73 genetic background and obtained only two RNAi-DOR plants in the Money Maker accession (Table S1). Additionally, we performed RNAi-mediated knockdown in S. pennellii leaf explants, as this related wild species typically has a high transformation efficiency. However, the S. pennellii transformation efficiency was also markedly lower than usual, 6.2% vs 135% (Atarés et al. 2011), resulting in the regeneration of only five transgenic plants. Phenotypic characterization of the RNAi-DOR lines revealed developmental alterations resembling those of the dor mutant in two T0 lines: one in the Money Maker background (RNAi-DORMM-2) and the other in the S. pennellii background (RNAi-DORSp-1). Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of Solyc12g098670 expression confirmed that these two plants were the only ones exhibiting gene silencing (Fig. 4b). Both plants displayed delayed rooting, with shorter and less branched roots compared to WT and the other T0 RNAi lines. Additionally, leaf explants from these silenced lines failed to produce adventitious roots when cultured on rooting medium. Moreover, the regeneration capacity of leaf explants from these lines was impaired, similar to the dor mutant phenotype (Fig. 4c). The correlation between Solyc12g098670 silencing and loss of regeneration capacity was further validated in T1 progeny from RNAi-DORMM-2. Specifically, cotyledon explants from kanamycin-resistant T1 plantlets failed to regenerate shoots on TSIM medium, while kanamycin-sensitive plantlets successfully regenerated (Fig. 4d).

Furthermore, we generated knockout mutations using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Fig. 5a) with an sgRNA targeting the seventh exon of Solyc12g098670 (Fig. 5b). Once again, the transformation efficiency was lower than expected, at 5.82% (Table S1). We obtained three kanamycin-resistant diploid lines from P73 (CR-DORP73) and fourteen from Money Maker (CR-DORMM). No edits were detected in the CR-DORP73 lines, and all plants exhibited a WT phenotype. However, molecular characterization of CR-DORMM lines revealed that two of them bore edited alleles. The CR-DORMM-1 line was biallelic for edited alleles, harboring TC (dorCR−1 allele) and AT (dorCR−2 allele) deletions, whereas the CR-DORMM-9 plant carried an ATAGTAT (dorCR−3 allele) deletion in the homozygous state (Fig. 5c). In total, three new different alleles were identified (Figure S2), whose transcripts contained open-reading frames predicted to be translated into truncated, non-functional proteins (Figure S3). Consistent with the predicted loss of function, CR-DORMM-1 and CR-DORMM-9 plants displayed phenotypic alterations, rooting and developing more slowly than the other lines when cloned (Fig. 5d). Although the CR-DORMM-1 plant failed to produce T1 progeny, we successfully obtained T1 offspring from the CR-DORMM-9 line through grafting onto WT rootstocks. The assessment of CR-DORMM-9 progeny revealed that ten out of ten T1 plants displayed phenotypes closely resembling those of dor mutant plants, characterized by reduced shoot development and adventitious roots with slower growth and less branching (Fig. 5e). Collectively, these results confirm that mutations in Solyc12g098670 (hereafter referred to as DOR) are responsible for the phenotypic alterations observed in the dor mutant.

Overexpression of DOR gene and mutant complementation

The DOR gene, under the control of a constitutive promoter (Fig. 6a), was introduced into P73 (35S-DORP73) and Money Maker (35S-DORMM) genetic backgrounds, achieving a mean transformation efficiency of 15.23% (Table S1), similar to the rates typically obtained in our laboratory (~ 15%). Eleven 35S-DORP73 and nineteen 35S-DORMM independent T0 overexpression lines were obtained. Expression analysis revealed that most plants displayed DOR gene expression levels comparable to WT, with only a few lines showing expression levels twice as high as WT (Fig. 6b–c). Despite these differences in expression, no phenotypic differences were observed between the overexpression lines and their respective controls.

Additionally, a molecular complementation assay was performed by overexpressing the DOR gene in dor mutant plants. Although the mutant is unable to regenerate adventitious plants, a transgenic line was successfully obtained. This plant exhibited the ability to form roots and displayed vegetative development similar to WT, with DOR gene expression levels nearly four times higher than those observed in the control (Fig. 6d), indicating that DOR overexpression in the mutant was sufficient to rescue the dor mutant phenotype.

Functional homology between DOR and Arabidopsis homologs AtSPPL3 and AtSPPL5

The protein encoded by the tomato DOR gene shares 54.8% and 57.5% sequence identity with its Arabidopsis homologs AtSPPL3 and AtSPPL5, respectively. The expression pattern of DOR closely resembles that of AtSPPL3 in Arabidopsis, with transcript accumulation throughout most of the plant organs, including roots, leaves, stems, and reproductive organs. However, AtSPPL5 shows no detectable expression in any of the organs analyzed in Arabidopsis (Tamura et al. 2008). With the aim to elucidate functional homology between DOR and Arabidopsis homolog genes, we characterized phenotypic alterations promoted by the loss of function of these latter genes, which have not been previously described in Arabidopsis. We evaluated the SAIL_841_C07 and SAIL_596_D03 lines, which carry a T-DNA insertion disrupting the coding sequences of AtSPPL3 and AtSPPL5, respectively (Fig. 7a). In the SAIL_841_C07 line, some individuals exhibited reduced growth compared to WT plants (Fig. 7b), with a segregation ratio consistent with monogenic recessive inheritance (71 WT: 26 mutant; χ2 = 0.17, P = 0.68). Notably, co-segregation analysis between the T-DNA insertion and the dwarf phenotype revealed that all plants with reduced growth were homozygous for the T-DNA insertion, whereas WT plants were either heterozygous (51 plants) or azygous (20 plants) for the insertion. In contrast, in the SAIL_596_D03 line, although the T-DNA insertion segregated among the progeny, all plants displayed a WT phenotype (Fig. 7c), suggesting that the disruption of AtSPPL5 does not result in phenotypic alterations.

To assess whether the loss-of-function of AtSPPL3 shares any other features with the tomato dor mutant, we evaluated root development and regeneration capacity in the Atsppl3 mutant. Although Atsppl3 mutant plants exhibited reduced vegetative growth, their roots grew similarly to WT, with no significant differences in root weight (Fig. 7d). Moreover, the regeneration capacity of these plants was unaffected, as they were able to produce organogenic structures (Fig. 7e). These findings suggest that DOR and AtSPPL3 may have divergent functional roles in tomato and Arabidopsis.

Discussion

Plant tissue culture harnesses the innate totipotency of plant cells, enabling de novo organogenesis when provided appropriate hormonal cues. While media composition and explant type have been extensively studied since the seminal work of Skoog and Miller (1957), genetic factors underlying regenerative capacity have only recently gained attention. Notably, regeneration competence varies widely among species and even among cultivars of the same species. At least a few loci with major influence on shoot organogenesis have been described in tomato (Trujillo-Moya et al. 2011). Likewise, introgression of alleles from wild relatives can enhance regeneration in cultivated tomato (Tadeu De Faria et al. 2002). These findings established that the ability to regenerate organs is, in part, genetically determined. The discovery of the tomato DOR gene adds a crucial new piece to this puzzle. Our results demonstrate that DOR is essential for both adventitious shoot organogenesis and root development, two processes that are cornerstones of plant developmental plasticity. These processes are typically governed by major hormonal cues; for instance, endogenous auxin accumulation and transport are crucial triggers for adventitious root formation under natural conditions (Ahkami et al. 2013; Xu and Huang 2014). Despite the relationship between these characters and certain plant hormones, mainly auxins and cytokinins, no response has been observed in the roots of the dor mutant when adding different auxins to the culture medium (Figure S1). Moreover, there were no significant differences in the endogenous content of most analyzed hormones, including one auxin and three cytokinins. The only one that showed a significant difference with respect to WT was the ABA content in the cotyledons (Table 1). However, in leaves, an organ with limited regenerative capacity, ABA levels showed no significant variation. In this regard, neither other tomato mutants with a higher ABA endogenous content, such as 7B-1 (Fellner et al. 2001) and its allelic mutant sl-2 (Singh and Sawhney 1998), nor transgenic plants with a higher endogenous content of this hormone (Thompson et al. 2000) showed phenotypic characteristics similar to those of the dor mutant. Therefore, these findings suggest that the phenotypic alterations in the dor mutant are not directly associated with endogenous hormone levels. Instead, DOR likely influences the cellular reprogramming process itself, possibly by enabling cells to perceive or execute organogenic signals beyond the classical hormone-driven route.

Through map-based cloning, we identified DOR as a gene encoding a putative intramembrane aspartyl protease of the SPPL family. Phylogenetic analysis identified eight SPP/SPPL proteins in tomato, with DOR (Solyc12g098670) clustering most closely with Solyc09g098190 in a subgroup related to the Arabidopsis AtSPPL3 and AtSPPL5 proteins (Fig. 3d). Arabidopsis contains one AtSPP and five AtSPPL genes (AtSPPL1–AtSPPL5) (Tamura et al. 2008), raising the possibility that functional redundancy among family members could contribute to the absence of DOR-like phenotypes in Arabidopsis single mutants. Indeed, functional redundancy between plant SPPL proteins has recently been demonstrated in rice, where single mutants at the OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2 loci showed no evident phenotype under endoplasmic reticulum stress, whereas the corresponding double mutant displayed increased sensitivity (Lu et al. 2026). Nevertheless, redundancy may not fully explain the differences observed between tomato and Arabidopsis. Whereas loss-of-function of DOR in tomato alters the development of the embryonic root, mainly due to less formation of lateral roots (Fig. 2b), Arabidopsis Atsppl3 mutant showed only mild stunted growth and no impairment in root development or in vitro regeneration (Fig. 7). Arabidopsis Atsppl3 roots were similar to WT in biomass and could generate organogenic callus and shoots normally. Likewise, disrupting AtSPPL5 had no obvious phenotype, consistent with earlier reports that AtSPPL5 is barely expressed in Arabidopsis tissues (Tamura et al. 2008). Furthermore, the severe rooting and regeneration defects caused by independent DOR loss-of-function alleles in tomato, together with the similar requirement for DOR in the wild relative S. pennellii, suggest that the closest tomato paralog, Solyc09g098190, cannot fully compensate for DOR function in these developmental processes. These data suggest that tomato DOR and its Arabidopsis homologs have divergent functional roles, despite their conserved protein sequence. In tomato (and even in its wild relative S. pennellii), DOR is clearly indispensable for regenerative organogenesis, whereas Arabidopsis SPPL3/5 seem largely dispensable for similar processes. In Brassica napus (rapeseed), a particular SPPL (BnaSPPL4) is crucial for pollen development and fertility (Li et al. 2024), reflecting a role that neither tomato DOR nor Arabidopsis SPPL3/5 play. Similarly, Arabidopsis SPP is essential for male gametophyte development (Han et al. 2009), whereas tomato DOR is critical for root and shoot regeneration. Thus, although partial or context-dependent functional redundancy among Arabidopsis SPPL proteins cannot be excluded without analysis of double or higher-order mutants, the available evidence suggests that SPP/SPPL proteases have functionally diversified across plant lineages.

Our findings with DOR shed light on the genetic and molecular regulation of adventitious organogenesis and rooting. The pleiotropic phenotype of the dor mutant, characterized by severely reduced lateral root formation, absence of adventitious roots and failure to regenerate shoots from callus, indicates that DOR operates at a fundamental step of organogenic reprogramming. It has been proven that the reduced aerial growth phenotype observed in dor is partially due to root alterations, since grafting the mutant onto a WT rootstock restored practically normal vegetative and reproductive development (Fig. 2e). Hence, the defective embryonic root system of dor seedlings, mainly due to reduced lateral root formation, has clear consequences on aerial development. Despite their distinct cellular origins, the adventitious root emerging from the stem has similar characteristics to those of the embryonic one, less development and branching (Birnbaum 2016; Steffens and Rasmussen 2016). In dor, adventitious root did not reverse their mutant phenotype under any of the tested media containing different types and concentrations of auxins (Figure S1). On the other hand, adventitious root formation was not observed in mutant hypocotyl and cotyledon explants in tomato (Fig. 1e–f), nor in leaf explants from gene-silenced tomato and S. pennellii (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, in dor explants, callus formation proceeds normally, but tissues remain trapped in an undifferentiated state, unable to initiate organized meristematic structures. This suggests a failure in the determination phase of organogenesis, when cells become committed to organ formation. The parallel involvement of DOR in rooting and shoot organogenesis suggests that it controls a core cellular process, such as cell fate reprogramming, meristem organization or cell–cell signaling, that is required for de novo organ initiation in both root and shoot contexts. Given the predicted intramembrane aspartyl protease activity of DOR, its role in morphogenetic competence may involve the proteolytic processing or turnover of membrane-associated substrates. In Arabidopsis, AtSPP was shown to cleave 15 out of 22 candidate signal peptides in a heterologous yeast assay (Kusunoki et al. 2020), demonstrating that plant SPP proteins can catalyze intramembrane cleavage of signal-peptide substrates. More recently, the rice proteins OsSPPL1 and OsSPPL2 have been implicated in endoplasmic reticulum associated protein degradation and shown to facilitate the degradation of an endoplasmic reticulum localized substrate defective in signal-peptide cleavage (Lu et al. 2026). However, to our knowledge, there is currently no direct evidence that plant SPP/SPPL proteins activate signaling peptides involved in developmental reprogramming. Therefore, the endogenous substrates of DOR and whether its activity modulates signaling, protein turnover, or other cellular processes required for regeneration remain to be determined.

Beyond its biological significance, DOR offers promising practical applications for improving plant tissue culture outcomes. A major bottleneck in plant transformation and micropropagation is the low efficiency of regenerating whole plants from cultured cells, particularly in recalcitrant genotypes or species. In this context, our results indicate that DOR plays a central role in determining morphogenetic competence during in vitro regeneration. The adventitious shoot regeneration from mutant explants was impossible because the callus was dedifferentiated. The importance of this gene in the regeneration process is also reflected in the regeneration efficiencies obtained after transformation with the different constructs used. Thus, when transformed with the overexpression construct, the transformation efficiency does not differ from the values normally achieved with our protocol (15.23%). However, transformation efficiencies decrease when using constructs that either reduce DOR gene expression (RNAi) or knock out its function (CRISPR), with efficiencies of 0.42% and 5.82%, respectively (Table S1). In addition, the gene has also been shown to be key in the adventitious regeneration of S. pennellii (Fig. 4c), a tomato-related species, which has an even higher regeneration capacity and can reach transformation efficiencies of 135% (Atarés et al. 2011). Moreover, the complementation test, in which mutant explants were transformed with the overexpression construct, led to the regeneration of a plant exhibiting a vegetative development similar to WT. These results encourage us to further investigate the involvement of this gene in the regeneration process and to use it to improve the regeneration and transformation efficiencies in different species. It would be of interest to test whether introducing DOR into recalcitrant tomato cultivars or into other difficult-to-regenerate species (such as woody perennials or legumes) can enhance their tissue culture responsiveness. However, a note of caution arises from our observation that among the 30 plants transformed with the overexpression construct, none exhibited DOR expression levels exceeding twofold those of WT (Fig. 6b–c). This could indicate that very high levels of DOR expression may be deleterious or not tolerated by the plant, potentially imposing an upper limit on the extent to which this gene can be exploited to improve regeneration.

From all the above results, we can affirm that this is the first time that an SPPL family member has been described as a key regulator of both adventitious regeneration processes and root development. The pronounced regeneration defects in dor mutants and the successful restoration of regeneration through the DOR transgene highlight the importance of this protease in plant development and suggest that mechanisms beyond classical hormone pathways are at play in controlling plant totipotency. Our results provide a strong foundation for further investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying DOR-mediated regeneration and its potential application in enhancing regeneration and transformation efficiencies across different plant species. Future studies could explore its function in various genetic backgrounds, assess its interaction between key hormonal and developmental pathways, and evaluate its feasibility as a biotechnological tool for improving transformation protocols in recalcitrant species.
