Resilience of recombinant antibiotic resistance gene-containing plasmids against common cell culture disposal methods
Authors: Austin Gluth, Christian Zmasek, Stephen Chiu, Tae Seok Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAntibiotics have saved an untold number of people and animals since penicillin’s miraculous discovery in 1928. In the following half-century, progressive discoveries involving antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), the microorganisms responsible, and their transferrable genetic material have yielded the tools necessary for genetic engineering, birthing the biotechnologies that continue to revolutionize healthcare. After half a century of antibiotic use in the biological sciences, we are, however, faced with an inconvenient question: what happens to residual antibiotics and ARG-containing recombinant DNA after experiments? According to sequencing, we demonstrate that neither severe bleach treatments nor autoclaving completely destroys plasmid-encoded ARGs in bacterial cultures. Furthermore, we show that various bacteria can be transformed using the isolated DNA, confirming that intact plasmids survived these common cell culture disposal methods. This work will catalyze future policy discussions, the development of antibiotic-free selection systems, and continued support for research into the underexplored anthropogenic sources of engineered DNA.

Introduction

The biological sciences have been pivotal in discovering antibiotics and characterizing mechanisms by which bacteria resist them. Advances in phage therapy,1 synthetic biology-driven enzyme and pathway engineering,2,3,4 precision antimicrobial therapies5 (as opposed to broad-spectrum antibiotics), engineered probiotics,6 genome mining for antimicrobial peptides and biosynthetic gene clusters,7,8,9,10 and AI-driven bioactive compound screening11,12 exemplify the field’s continued contributions to addressing one of the most pressing contemporary problems—the rapid rise in antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Yet, biological research is profoundly reliant on antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) as markers for initially selecting cells that contain desired gene edits and for continued propagation of ARG-containing DNA in the genetically engineered cells. To complicate matters, most antibiotics commonly used in laboratory research are designated as critically important antimicrobials and/or essential medicines by the World Health Organization (WHO)13,14 (Table 1), even if some are seldom prescribed in the clinic. Furthermore, many ARGs confer resistance to multiple antibiotics (Table 1). Wastewater from pharmaceutical manufacturing, hospitals, and agriculture is the major source of antibiotics pollution in the environment.49 These anthropogenic sources of antibiotics provide unprecedented selection pressures that drive mechanisms of resistance, primarily through spontaneous mutations or horizontal gene transfer (HGT) among diverse microorganisms.50 Compared to antibiotic use in healthcare and agriculture, the biological sciences present a unique regulatory challenge because antibiotics are used in parallel with recombinant DNA (rDNA) containing ARGs. Unlike the gradual breakdown of environmentally released antibiotics, extracellular DNA (especially plasmids) can persist in the environment,51,52 and foreign DNA acquired via HGT can be replicated indefinitely. Additionally, the recombinant vectors employed in biological research are, by design, composed of highly mobilizable gene elements, efficient origins of replication (more than one in the case of shuttle vectors) as well as conjugation cassettes, and robust selectable markers from various organisms—ultimately making them compatible with several organisms. For these reasons, cell culture disposal methods should precautionarily destroy or otherwise significantly inactivate rDNA containing ARGs.53,54

According to the NIH guidelines for research involving rDNA,55 “all contaminated liquid or solid wastes [must be] decontaminated before disposal” via chemical and/or heat treatments. Currently accepted, widely used disposal methods include bleaching, autoclaving, and incineration.56 If decontamination includes neutralization of rDNA, shockingly few studies have evaluated the effectiveness of chemical and heat treatments at destroying rDNA, despite some surveys demonstrating environmental release of genetically modified bacteria and synthetic DNA.57,58,59 Earlier work in forensic science has shown that extracellular DNA can be destroyed or at least damaged by disinfectants like bleach60,61; however, the degradative effects can vary by treatment severity and the DNA context (e.g., in a bodily fluid vs. on a physical surface).62,63,64,65 In a recent study, Loret et al. compared chemical and thermophysical methods to determine which sufficiently destroys plasmid DNA in E. coli cultures.59 They found that sulfuric acid, a commercial quaternary ammonium disinfectant, and steam sterilization were more effective than sodium hypochlorite (bleach); however, these chemical treatments present additional hurdles for sewer disposal, and quaternary ammonium compounds may be a less effective biocide than bleach for certain bacteria.66 Moreover, the autoclave sterilization times/temperatures that they determined for >90% degradation of plasmid DNA in cell cultures are unreasonable. Based on their work and that of Lan et al.,67 it is still unclear if autoclaving plate cultures or large, dense cell cultures (OD600 > 0.65) would be effective. Lastly, these studies did not test if residual DNA isolated from treated cell cultures could be used to retransform bacteria. Gao et al. also studied the DNA degradation efficiency of thermal treatments.68 They reported that autoclaving almost completely degraded free, naked plasmid DNA, severely limiting its transforming capacity. Interestingly, their work demonstrates that salts and other chemicals can stabilize DNA during boiling. This result suggests that some chemicals in growth media may stabilize plasmid DNA during autoclaving; however, they did not evaluate the stability and transformability of DNA isolated from autoclaved cell cultures.69

In this work, we re-evaluate the resilience of different recombinant plasmids against common cell culture disposal methods, focusing primarily on bleaching and autoclaving as established laboratory methods for decontaminating E. coli cultures. Unlike previous work, we expand our analyses to examine a wider range of bleach treatment times (including overnight) and severities, while additionally investigating the effect of autoclaving plate cultures. Using a suite of established analytical methods, we demonstrate that neither autoclaving (121°C for 20 min) nor bleaching (>0.5% sodium hypochlorite for >20 min) cell cultures destroys plasmid-derived ARGs. Furthermore, we employ whole-plasmid nanopore sequencing and retransformations to show that DNA isolated from autoclaving or bleaching still contains intact plasmids. Using DNA isolated from these treatments to model HGT, we were able to retransform biosafety level 1 (BSL-1) bacteria including naturally competent Acinetobacter baylyi as well as E. coli, Salmonella enterica, Klebsiella aerogenes, and Pseudomonas putida. This is concerning because these bacteria are related to the pathogens Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp.50,70 We adamantly believe that this and the aforementioned studies provide the impetus for future research into the potential health and environmental consequences of antibiotic use in the biological sciences, and that regulatory frameworks should at least encourage the use of antibiotic-free selection systems when feasible.71,72

Results

Bleaching E. coli cultures does not completely destroy plasmid-encoded ARGs

Bleaching is a quick and effective method for surface cleaning and biocidal treatments of low-volume cell cultures. Bleach solutions are generally alkaline due to the presence of the stabilizer sodium hydroxide. Under these basic conditions, the active ingredient hypochlorite preferentially oxidizes purine nucleotides.73 Hypochlorite reacts with nucleobases to form chloramine intermediates, which can decay into reactive radicals that contribute to nucleobase saturation, additional DNA modifications, and strand lesions.73,74,75,76 The oxidative mechanisms are highly complex in vivo because hypochlorite reacts competitively with proteins, lipids, and other cellular constituents—affecting both the extent and exact mechanisms of plasmid DNA damage.74,77

To ensure effective cell culture decontamination, working solutions of bleach typically contain 0.5%–2.0% hypochlorite (referred to as HOCl hereafter) and are applied for 20 min at minimum. In previous work, wash steps were omitted prior to DNA purification,59 raising the possibility that residual HOCl and other radical species (e.g., chlorinated proteins77) influenced DNA recovery and downstream analyses such as PCR.78 Thus, we first tested a 0.5% sodium thiosulfate wash step to neutralize residual HOCl after bleaching. Cell cultures of E. coli carrying a plasmid that confers ampicillin resistance were treated with or without 0.5% bleach for 20 min and then centrifuged and washed twice with water or once with 0.5% sodium thiosulfate and water (Figures S1A and S1B). Then, miniprepped DNA was quantified and analyzed via gel electrophoresis. Incorporating a sodium thiosulfate wash step did not affect DNA yield (t test p values in Data S1), and plasmid was observed in every condition (Figures S1C and S1D).

With a sample processing pipeline in hand, we proceeded to re-evaluate whether bleaching cell cultures destroys ARG-containing rDNA59 while also expanding our analyses to include extended treatment times (>20 min) and severer bleach treatments. Bleaching had a clear effect on the DNA isolated from plasmid preparation cultures, although negligible differences were observed for different treatment times (10, 20, and 60 min) within each of the % HOCl conditions (ANOVA p value = 0.464; Data S1). 0.5% and 1.0% bleach treatments significantly decreased the amount of purified DNA (∼10–20 ng/μL) relative to the positive control (∼40 ng/μL) (Figure 1B), according to t tests for the 20-min time point (p values = 0.008 and 0.005, respectively; Data S1). Interestingly, 0.1% bleach treatments improved the total DNA yield (t test p value = 0.001 for 20-min comparison; Data S1). Bleach contains sodium hydroxide, which may have contributed to cell lysis given that sodium hydroxide is also used in the miniprep kit lysis buffer. Untreated negative controls of E. coli DH10B without plasmid indicated that genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated during miniprepping. Lane smearing was observed in gel electrophoresis results for bleach-treated samples, including the negative controls without plasmid (Figure S2C). This indicates that gDNA was likely fragmented and purified. According to gel densitometry analysis (Figure 1C), bleaching degraded DNA as the treatment severity increased, which has been observed previously for gDNA.83 To evaluate if KanR was still present among the fragmented DNA, PCR amplifications were tested using two DNA polymerases. The KanR amplicon was not present in 0.5% and 1.0% bleach conditions according to results using Phusion DNA polymerase (Figure S2D). Nonetheless, GoTaq PCR amplifications and the corresponding sequencing results confirmed the presence of KanR after all the tested bleach treatments (Figure S2E; Table 2). In the experiments detailed above, cell cultures containing residual growth medium components were bleached, rather than treating cells or naked DNA directly. Because hypochlorite is a non-discriminate oxidizer, unspent LB medium (rich in salts, proteins, and other organics) may effectively interfere with the DNA degradation potential of bleach.59 Thus, cell pellets (∼4 mg dry weight) from 1 mL of culture were used for severer 60 min treatments with bleach (0.5%, 1.0%, and 5.0%) or concentrated sodium hypochlorite (∼11% and 15%) (Figures S4A and S4B). A negative correlation between % HOCl and miniprepped DNA concentrations was observed (Figure S4C), demonstrating that HOCl degrades DNA and/or affects DNA purification. However, despite a reduction in amplifiability for conditions with ≥1% HOCl, KanR was still present in all conditions except ∼11% HOCl (Figure S4D). In addition to the fact that ≥5% HOCl is corrosive and cannot be disposed into sewers without dilution, these results show that bleaching is ineffective at destroying ARG-containing DNA in plasmid preparation cultures, consistent with previous work.59,61

Autoclaving E. coli cultures does not completely destroy plasmid-encoded ARGs

Autoclaving at 121°C is the preferred decontamination method for disposing large culture volumes, cultures that may contain spores, and those that contain thick, organic-rich materials. Autoclaving subjects DNA to high temperatures in the presence of saturated steam; these conditions promote destabilization of hydrogen bonding between base pairs, leading to strand separation/denaturation and hydrolytic cleavage of covalent bonds in the backbone.84,85 The extent of strand breakage and fragmentation ultimately depends on the solution composition and context.86 Indeed, several reports demonstrate the high thermal stability of naked and in vivo DNA,85,86,87,88,89,90,91 although many focus on gDNA. Autoclave treatments were conducted to reassess findings from previous work59,67 with a focus on plasmid-bearing, liquid cell cultures containing rich, undefined growth medium. Moreover, we expanded our analyses to include plate cultures, which are incompatible with bleaching. 5 and 50 mL cultures underwent a 20-min liquid cycle, and then samples were centrifuged to collect supernatants and pellets for miniprepping (Figures 1A and S5A). For plate cultures, ∼100 mg fragments were excised from a Petri dish containing a lawn of E. coli. In parallel, an entire Petri dish containing streaked cells was autoclaved using a gravity autoclave cycle, which is commonly employed for decontaminating solid biological wastes. After autoclaving, DNA was gel-extracted. Isolated DNA was quantified and analyzed via gel electrophoresis, and the presence of KanR was tested using PCR amplifications and sequencing. Finally, DNA from the liquid autoclave condition was used for initial E. coli transformation tests.

As with bleach treatments, autoclaving clearly affected the quantity and quality of isolated DNA. Interestingly, substantially more DNA was purified from the cell pellet after autoclaving (∼1 μg/μL) compared to the untreated positive control (∼44 ng/μL), with a t test p value = 0.0003 for the 5 mL condition (Figure 1D; Data S1). A large amount of fragmented gDNA or other cellular components were purified from autoclaved plasmid-free E. coli (negative control), which likely explains the higher DNA concentrations observed in the autoclaved, plasmid-bearing cultures. Indeed, intact plasmids were not apparent in gel electrophoresis analyses (Figures 1E and S5B), suggesting also that column purification selected for shorter gDNA fragments or other contaminants. PCR amplifications and sequencing results demonstrated that ARG-containing DNA was, nevertheless, still present in the autoclaved samples and was even released into culture supernatants as a result (Figure S5C; Table 3), which has been observed previously.92 Streak plates were also autoclaved, with the resulting analyses recapitulating those for autoclaved liquid cultures (Figure S6). Only autoclaving a small amount of scraped cells was effective at inhibiting ARG amplifiability. Initial sequencing and transformation results show that plasmid survives bleaching and autoclaving

Compared to Sanger sequencing, nanopore sequencing yields substantially longer reads that can even span the full-length plasmid (hence, “whole-plasmid sequencing”). Together with transformations, whole-plasmid sequencing provides strong evidence for the presence of intact plasmids (refer to “data and code availability” for read length histograms). DNA isolated from untreated, bleached (0.5% HOCl, 20 min), and autoclaved (5 mL, 20 min) E. coli cell cultures was analyzed using nanopore sequencing. According to the results (Figure 1F), intact plasmid was likely present after bleaching. No assembly was generated for the autoclave condition; however, this may be due to an excessive amount of contaminating E. coli gDNA (Figure 1G), though gDNA was also present in the bleach condition. Furthermore, these results explain the lane smearing observed in the gel electrophoresis results (Figures S2, S3, S5, and S6). 500 ng of isolated DNA was also used to confirm the presence of transformable plasmid DNA. Notably, E. coli could be retransformed with DNA recovered from the bleach and autoclave conditions, though the transformation frequencies and efficiencies were reduced ∼1.5–3 log10-fold, respectively (Figures 1H and 1I; t test p values included in Data S1). These lower transformation efficiencies may be explained by the presence of gDNA, other contaminants from DNA isolation kits/approaches, and/or partially degraded plasmid DNA. Regardless, these initial tests as a whole show that KanR+ plasmid DNA survives bleach and autoclave treatments—decontamination methods that are commonplace in biology labs.

Modeling HGT under ideal lab conditions using DNA extracted from bleached and autoclaved cell cultures

Acinetobacter baylyi is a naturally competent BSL-1 bacterium that readily uptakes DNA from the environment. Competence is common among clinical isolates of drug-resistant Acinetobacter spp.,70 making A. baylyi ideal for exploring natural transformations with laboratory-released rDNA. Using A. baylyi, we modeled HGT of ARGs from decontaminated cultures containing a broad-host range plasmid (Figure S7A). Briefly, an E. coli plasmid preparation culture was split for bleaching (0.5% HOCl, 20 min) and autoclaving (5 mL, 20 min) (Figure 2A). DNA was isolated and analyzed (Figures S7B–S7D; Table S2), showing that ARG resilience is not plasmid-specific, and that the aforementioned results are repeatable. The isolated DNA was then added to fresh cultures of A. baylyi and incubated overnight (Figure 2A). Cultures were serially diluted and spotted the next day. All replicates of the bleach condition yielded transformants of A. baylyi, though with lower transformation efficiencies (t test p value = 0.002; Figure 2B; Data S1). Plasmid DNA was isolated from two colonies for the bleach condition, both of which perfectly matched the original plasmid according to whole-plasmid sequencing (Figure 2C). No transformants were observed for the autoclave condition, even when all cells were plated and when additional transformations were evaluated using 1.5, 3, and 10 μg of DNA. Because A. baylyi transformations using DNA from the autoclave condition did not yield colonies, we first closely evaluated our workflow for DNA isolation prior to additional transformations. Like the other plasmids tested herein, neither bleaching nor autoclaving destroyed GmR in E. coli plasmid preparation cultures (Figures S7F–S7G; Table S2). However, according to whole-plasmid sequencing of the resulting miniprepped DNA, no assembly was generated for the autoclave condition yet again (Figure S7H). We surmised that column purification was selecting for shorter DNA fragments from degraded plasmids and gDNA. Given that Lan et al. used a total cellular DNA extraction kit to isolate DNA from autoclaved cell cultures,67 we decided to use isopropanol precipitation and gel extractions to test this hypothesis. Gel electrophoresis results comparing isopropanol precipitation to miniprepping clearly showed that shorter DNA fragments were competitively binding during column purification (Figure S8A). This may explain the negative results acquired for A. baylyi transformations using DNA from the autoclave condition. Thus, we performed inverse PCR to verify the presence of the nearly full-length plasmid (Figure S8B). The plasmid used for A. baylyi transformations was not amplifiable from both the isopropanol precipitated and miniprepped DNA templates. Inhibitory compounds present in those samples, low natural transformation efficiencies, and/or sample loss during gel extractions may explain these results; however, inverse PCR did work for the GmR+ broad-host range plasmid, supporting the conclusion that intact plasmid can survive autoclaving (Figure S8B).

To reaffirm that intact, transformable plasmid survives cell culture disposal and to highlight the potential risk posed by incomplete destruction of ARG-containing rDNA, DNA isolated from bleached and autoclaved cell cultures was used for electroporation transformations of E. coli, S. enterica, K. aerogenes, and P. putida (Figure 3B). As shown in Figures 3C–3J, these bacteria could be transformed using DNA isolated from the bleach condition (0.5% HOCl, 20 min), and the presence of the original plasmid was confirmed for two colonies using whole-plasmid sequencing. When 700 μL of recovering cells was plated, E. coli and S. enterica transformants were also observed for the autoclave condition (5 mL, 20 min), confirming the presence of intact plasmid (Figures 3C–3F and S8C). The lower overall transformation efficiencies observed for the positive controls of K. aerogenes and P. putida may explain why colonies were not observed for the autoclave condition. Although a relatively large amount of DNA was used for these idealistic electroporation transformations (500 ng), these results cement the potential risk of rDNA release: HGT of ARGs and highly-mobilizable gene elements to pathogenic bacteria.

Preliminary search for engineered DNA sequences in wastewater metagenomes

Wastewater treatment plants are hot spots for HGT.16 As a preliminary search for engineered DNA present in these environments, we used a tool called “Synsor” developed by Tay et al.99 Synsor uses 7-mer signature differences and a neural network to predictively classify sequences as natural or engineered.99 As a test dataset, we used the metagenome-assembled genomes of 226 activated sludge samples from 142 wastewater treatment plants.100 Out of the 15,637 sequences searched, 256 (1.6%) were deemed synthetic according to Synsor (Data S2). We then performed BLASTn101 alignment searches against the non-redundant nucleotide database using the predicted sequences. These results were evaluated using keyword searches: “vector,” “plasmid,” “artificial,” “synthetic,” “clone,” “cloning,” “construct,” and “32630” (the NCBI taxonomy ID corresponding to “synthetic construct”). After inspecting the results, it seemed unlikely that the Synsor-predicted sequences were associated with engineered constructs; nevertheless, 88 (34%) of the sequences returned no BLASTn hits. To investigate those sequences further, we used another tool developed by McGuffie and Barrick for engineered plasmid annotation.102 Interestingly, six of those sequences contained fragments of synthetic genetic elements: three tandem HA epitope tags, an ARS1 autonomous replicating sequence from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (sometimes used in low copy plasmids), a synthetic polyadenylation signal, the CLIP-tag optimized for mammalian expression, a chicken β-actin promoter (CAG) with a 21-bp deletion (synthetic constitutive promoter in mammalian gene editing), and a transcription terminator tL3 from phage λ (Data S2). Because of the low coverage for these sequences, it is likely that they are false positives, though. These results demonstrate the hurdles remaining for largescale environmental searches of engineered/synthetic DNA signatures.

Discussion

ARGs are powerful and convenient selectable markers for genetic engineering. Yet, without proper biocontainment and effective biological waste disposal, their widespread use introduces the potential of environmental release and transfer to pathogenic bacteria.103 Incomplete degradation of antibiotics and the corresponding ARGs may even have a dual effect, wherein selective pressure hotspots are created in waste streams that promote the uptake/transfer of genetic elements (including ARGs) and the development of resistance mechanisms.104 This is concerning for bleached or autoclaved cell cultures that are dumped down the drain because not all wastewater is properly treated,105 and current treatment processes may be ineffective at destroying rDNA-derived ARGs, even with expensive, advanced oxidation processes.106,107,108,109,110,111,112 Further complicating matters, residual chlorine in the disposed bleach-containing cultures may cause cell membrane and DNA damage in environmental bacteria, thereby inducing an oxidative stress response that stimulates the uptake of plasmid-encoded ARGs.113 As a ballpark estimate that accounts for partial DNA degradation from bleach and autoclave treatments, we calculated that 50 years’ worth of laboratory-derived ARG-containing rDNA is roughly equivalent to 1% of ARG DNA naturally found in global urban wastewater (assumptions, references, and calculations provided in Data S3).114,115,116,117,118,119,120,121,122 Albeit miniscule, this metric suggests that current disposal methods for cell cultures containing antibiotics and recombinant, plasmid-encoded ARGs could contribute to emergence of drug resistance and downstream routes of pathogen exposure (e.g., through biosolid applications in agriculture123,124). If the NIH,55 CDC, and USDA125 definitions of decontamination extend beyond viable biohazardous agents to include rDNA, it behooves us to thoroughly evaluate disposal methods.

In this work, we assessed the resilience of recombinant ARG-containing plasmids against two widely accepted methods for disinfecting cell cultures prior to disposal: bleaching or autoclaving. Hypochlorite, the active disinfecting ingredient in bleach, is known to chlorinate DNA, leading to strand breakage.73,74,75,76,126,127,128 In cell cultures, various other components, such as proteins, can compete for the corresponding oxidation reactions.77,126,129,130,131 The presence of salts and organics in rich, undefined media may partially explain why we observed recombinant ARG cassettes after treating E. coli plasmid preparation cultures with bleach (1% sodium hypochlorite) for 24 h. In agreement with previous work,59 even severe sodium hypochlorite (≥1%) treatments of E. coli cell pellets (∼4 mg CDW) for 1 h did not completely destroy plasmid-encoded ARGs. According to established biosafety protocols, cell cultures should be disinfected using a 1:10 dilution of bleach (0.5% sodium hypochlorite) for a minimum of 20 min.56,132 Following these guidelines, we used whole-plasmid sequencing to show that DNA isolated from bleached cell cultures still contain intact plasmids, and that this DNA can be used to transform all five of the bacteria that we tested. To model HGT, we show that DNA from bleached cell cultures can confer antibiotic resistance to A. baylyi, a naturally competent bacterium that readily uptakes environmental DNA133 (Figure 2). We could also transform other BSL-1 bacteria related to multi-drug-resistant ESKAPE pathogens (Figure 3). This is alarming because Pseudomonadota are major carriers of ARGs in wastewater treatment systems.100 These results highlight the risks involved in using shuttle vectors or broad-host range plasmids134,135 that confer resistance to antibiotics like gentamicin, which is still frequently used to treat infections.38,136

Autoclaving can damage DNA by destabilizing hydrogen bonding and causing strand clipping.68,91 However, the effectiveness of this method at degrading DNA is contextual (plasmid-encoded vs. genome-integrated ARGs) and depends on whether free DNA or more complex cell cultures containing growth media are autoclaved.92 Of course, for free rDNA or cell pellets, biohazardous waste collection followed by incineration is preferable. Though we did not test genome-integrated ARGs in this work, we demonstrated that autoclaved plasmid preparation cultures and streak plates still contain ARGs, agreeing with previous work.67 We noted that autoclaving facilitates cell lysis, releasing fragmented gDNA and ARGs. If autoclaved liquid cultures are discharged to the sewer before cooling, the resulting heat shock may even promote the uptake of extracellular DNA by drain-dwelling biofilms.137 This is partially substantiated by our results using DNA isolated from autoclaved 5 mL cultures (121°C for 20 min) to transform E. coli and S. enterica (Figures 1 and 3). However, the transformation efficiencies were much lower than the bleach condition, given the appreciable amount of co-purified gDNA (or other contaminants). Why we did not observe transformants of A. baylyi, K. aerogenes, or P. putida for the autoclave condition is unclear, but may be due to residual inhibitory compounds from DNA isolation (E. coli lysis, isopropanol precipitation, and gel extraction), or the fact that transformation protocols are optimized for E. coli (closely related to S. enterica). As with bleaching, plasmid attributes such as composition (e.g., conjugative elements), GC-content, copy number, and size may affect not only stability under the thermal or oxidative conditions but also the downstream transferability of the “surviving” recombinant plasmids. Regardless, autoclaving liquid cell cultures may be preferred over bleaching because some antibiotics are heat-sensitive.138 Outside of information gleaned from water treatment studies,139,140,141,142 a comprehensive study of how bleach affects common laboratory antibiotics has not been conducted.

From a biosafety perspective, it is clear that neither bleaching nor autoclaving is a “one size fits all” solution for disposing cell cultures containing rDNA. Unfortunately, approaches using other biocides like glutaraldehyde or mixtures of quaternary ammonium compounds do not completely destroy ARGs and cannot be dispensed into sewer systems at the minimum effective concentrations.59,92 Even treatments such as autoclaving followed by ozonation,67 advanced oxidation processes,110,143 and theoretical applications of DNases144,145 are costly, inefficient, and limited to treating liquid waste streams. Because airborne ARGs have been detected in laboratories and other settings,146,147,148,149 all cell culture disposal methods discussed herein do not address the problem at its root—biological research’s reliance on antibiotics. In the absence of evidence-based risk assessments for the emergence of resistance mechanisms attributed to laboratory-derived antibiotics and ARGs, it is prudent to seek and apply alternative selection markers for genetic modifications when feasible. Ultimately, the use of ARGs in genetically engineered microorganisms is already prohibited for human therapeutics, environmental applications, and industrial (>10 L) fermentations.71,103 Several versions of antibiotic-free selection systems have been developed during the past four decades and rely on the expression of an essential gene, antitoxin gene, and/or an auxotrophic marker.72,150,151,152 Of course, deploying these systems for routine laboratory use presents a significant hurdle but is incentivized by eliminating steps to remove resistance markers (“curing”) and by acquiring performance data for ARG-free strains prior to high-value applications.153,154,155

From an ecological perspective, if recombinant vectors with ARGs are released at significant frequencies and persist in the environment, a range of outcomes are possible depending on the context and niche. For instance, ARGs are notably redundant in soil and wastewater microbiomes, yet the direct association of these genes with the overall community composition and stability varies.156,157 This is because, in certain niches like wastewater, HGT can decouple the function and stability of ARGs from certain taxa: as Wang et al. keenly demonstrate, HGT is programmable method for stabilizing gene functions in the face of fluctuating subpopulations (e.g., as a result of antibiotics treatments).158 Introducing highly mobile rDNA into certain niches with high HGT may in effect alter the stability of ARGs, thereby causing lasting impacts on microbial community dynamics and resistance profiles. In relation to this point, biologists have been using molecular cloning techniques tailored to and arising from Gammaproteobacteria and bacteriophages for decades. Are current practices releasing genetic tools that are advantageous to certain bacteria engaging in HGT? Could the uncontained recombinant plasmids provide a fitness advantage to those bacteria that affects interactions with co-occurring fungi, algae, and other microorganisms?

Before calling for a revolution in genetic engineering, it is crucial to quantify the risks associated with releasing antibiotics and recombinant ARG cassettes from laboratories. This could be addressed via a coordinated survey of laboratory antibiotic use and the corresponding vector-encoded ARGs, paired with extensive metagenomics analyses100,104,159 for samples acquired at potential escape routes: lab coats and shoes, municipal solid waste and recyclables, transportation routes and the offsite centers for treating biohazardous wastes, laboratory-connected drains/sewer systems, and HVAC/air filtration systems. Confidently determining which of the sampled DNA sequences are derived from research involving rDNA is, nevertheless, still a challenge.

Recent approaches in biosecurity and biosafety research have primarily addressed the “lab attribution” problem to predict the origin of rDNA by training models on plasmid repositories like Addgene160,161 or using classifiers.162,163,164,165,166 Similarly, Kunjapur et al.167 leveraged Addgene data to distinguish synthetic from natural genes based on, for instance, codon usage. Other biosurveillance tools have made strides toward annotating engineered sequences168,169 yet have not been employed to systematically search (meta)genomes and may not even be appropriate to do so. These tools rely on internet-era databases, many of which are not contemporaneous with foundational plasmids developed prior to the 2000s and, thus, disproportionately reflect the surge in new recombinant plasmid sequences. This disconnect makes it difficult to detect older signatures as well as newer synthetic sequences that were deposited after available (meta)genomics datasets.

Despite these challenges, Tay et al.99 attempted to search wastewater metagenomes for synthetic DNA signatures using their Synsor tool. However, their work did not focus on biocontainment issues regarding recombinant vectors. Using their tool in combination with BLASTn101 and pLannotate,102 we searched 226 activated sludge samples from 142 wastewater treatment plants100 and observed fragments of engineered DNA (Data S2). It is unclear, though, if these are derived from released rDNA, and none of the sequences we observed were associated with ARGs. Accurate rule sets and analytical pipelines to confidently detect engineered motifs within complex environmental data do not exist. In future work, we will define synthetic DNA motifs based on manually curated, popular commercial and academic plasmids (many of which are not listed in contemporary databases like Addgene). We will use frameworks from existing tools164,168,169 in addition to our rule sets to search (meta)genomes and establish an evidence-based risk assessment for rDNA containment. In parallel, we are actively pursuing the development of convenient, ARG-free cloning systems71,72 and are closely evaluating the efficacy of nuclease kill switches for biocontainment of genetically engineered microorganisms.145,152 As bioengineering research continues to grow globally, we, as stewards of responsible rDNA use, must continue exploring the risks involved to prevent deleterious effects on human health and the environment.

Limitations of the study

Contrary to popular belief, we demonstrate that common bleaching and autoclaving practices for bacterial cell cultures do not completely destroy recombinant plasmids according to PCR amplifications, sequencing results, and transformations. In light of this, we provide a detailed discussion about the consequences of unintentionally releasing recombinant plasmids, yet our work does not track the fate of rDNA, nor do we provide any direct evidence of bacteria in the environment being transformed by laboratory-derived rDNA. Rather, transformation experiments were conducted under ideal laboratory conditions using high concentrations of isolated DNA from the treated cell cultures. Even then, we observed mixed transformation results for the autoclave condition, which may be explained by a combination of factors: plasmid attributes (composition, size, GC-content, and copy number), co-isolation of fragmented gDNA, inhibitory components from heated cells, and/or chemicals from agarose gels as well as the extraction kit. Though we did not complete compositional analyses nor evaluate the extent of plasmid DNA damage (vs. strand breakage or destruction), it is possible that these complex samples may have affected the quality of inverse PCR amplifications and whole plasmid sequencing assemblies, too. To put it bluntly, these considerations do not relate to the actual fate of residual rDNA in biowaste streams (e.g., down the drain) because we isolated/purified DNA for our simplified HGT models. Nevertheless, performing transformations directly with the bleached or autoclaved cultures raises additional complications due to residual antibiotics and/or other inhibitory compounds arising from bleaching/autoclaving. We are actively exploring approaches to improve our models for evaluating these biocontainment issues.

Experimental model and study participant details

All bacterial plasmid sequences used in this study have been deposited to Mendeley (see Data and code availability). All the primers used in this study were synthesized by IDT (Coralville, IA). Escherichia coli DH10B (Invitrogen EC0113) was used for all plasmid preparations (“plasmid prep.”). In addition to E. coli, all bacteria detailed hereafter were used for modeling horizontal gene transfer using DNA isolated from bleached/autoclaved cell cultures. Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium (ATCC 53647), Klebsiella aerogenes (ATCC 15038), and Acinetobacter baylyi ADP1 (ATCC 33305) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA). Pseudomonas putida KT2440 (ATCC 47054) was kindly provided by Prof. Laura Jarboe170 from Iowa State University. Note: all experiments detailed herein were performed under ideal laboratory conditions to model the fate of rDNA derived from common cell culture decontamination practices.

Strains, plasmids, and growth conditions

The following strains were cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium at 37°C with 250 rpm shaking: E. coli DH10B, Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium (ATCC 53647; referred to as S. enterica), and Klebsiella aerogenes (ATCC 15038). Cultivations of Acinetobacter baylyi ADP1 (ATCC 33305) and Pseudomonas putida KT2440 were performed in LB medium at 30°C with 180 rpm shaking. For E. coli experiments performed with pSC308 (colE1-GFP-LacI-AmpR), pSC200 (p15A-RFP-KanR), or pBWB162 (pBAV1k-repB-mCherry-LacI-KanR),171 LB medium was supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin or 50 μg/mL kanamycin (Gold Biotechnology) as necessary. pSC308 and pSC200 were designed using Benchling and assembled in E. coli DH10B via Gibson Assembly. For A. baylyi experiments using pBWB162, LB medium was supplemented with 25 μg/mL kanamycin. Experiments using gentamicin were performed with pMP2463 (pBBR1-EGFP-GmR).172 Refer to the Data and code availability section for the plasmid sequences used in this study. The following concentrations of gentamicin (MP Biomedicals) were used according to a minimum inhibitory concentration assay described in the respective subsection below: 50 μg/mL for E. coli, 75 μg/mL for S. enterica, 10 μg/mL for K. aerogenes, and 20 μg/mL for P. putida. For minipreps and sequencing transformants, all 5 mL LB cultures were inoculated with a single colony and incubated for 18 h as described above. Refer to the following methods subsections for specifics regarding the cultivations used for bleach and autoclave treatments as well as those used for retransformations.

Method details

Bleach treatments of E. coli plasmid prep. cultures

For bleach treatments, 50 mL E. coli plasmid prep. cultures with a final OD600 of ∼3 were combined and then split into 4 mL aliquots. Bleach (ULINE; 6% sodium hypochlorite) was added to achieve final w/v % sodium hypochlorite concentrations varying from 0.1–1.0% (Figure 1) in a 5 mL total volume that was reached using sterile MilliQ H2O. Following the treatment times, culture tubes were centrifuged at 4°C and 3,000 × g for 5 min. Supernatants were then discarded, and 2 mL of 0.5 w/v% sodium thiosulfate was immediately added to resuspend the cell pellets by vortexing for 20 s. The resuspension was centrifuged as before, and the wash was repeated with 2 mL sterile MilliQ H2O. Finally, pelleted cells/debris were miniprepped according to manufacturer guidelines (Invitrogen) and eluted in cloning water (Corning). All raw miniprep data are included in Data S2. 4 mL E. coli cultures carrying pBWB162 or pMP2463 were cultivated and bleached (0.5% sodium hypochlorite, 20 min) as aforementioned. All conditions were analyzed in duplicate unless otherwise specified. Untreated positive controls (+Ctrl) as well as treated and untreated negative controls (E. coli DH10B without plasmid; − Ctrl) were included in all experiments. Note that any mention of “% bleach” herein actually designates final w/v % sodium hypochlorite concentrations.

For the experiment shown in Figure S4, a 50 mL plasmid prep. culture was split into 4 mL aliquots to analyze cell and supernatant dry weights, as well as 1 mL aliquots in microcentrifuge tubes for severe sodium hypochlorite treatments. The aliquots were centrifuged as above to separate supernatants and cell pellets. The supernatant and cell pellet fractions from the 4 mL aliquots were lyophilized (Millrock Technology) in duplicate for 48 h. Cell dry weights (CDWs) and supernatant dry weights were then acquired by gravimetric analysis, and cell dry weights were calculated for 1 mL aliquots to evaluate treatment severities. For the 1 mL aliquots, supernatants were discarded after centrifugation, and cell pellets were treated in the dark with germicidal bleach or a concentrated solution of sodium hypochlorite (∼11–15% w/v; Thermo Scientific). Afterward, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatants were discarded except for the ∼11 and ∼15% sodium hypochlorite conditions, for which 800 μL was removed because pellets were not observed. No wash steps were conducted; thus, all miniprepping was conducted in a fume hood.

Autoclaving E. coli plasmid prep. And plate cultures

For liquid autoclave loads, 50 mL plasmid prep. cultures with a final OD600 of ∼3 were combined and then split into 5 mL and 50 mL aliquots. These culture aliquots were then autoclaved using a 20 min sterilization step at 121°C. Autoclaved cultures were removed at ∼50°C and cooled to room temperature. E. coli DH10B without any plasmid was autoclaved as a negative control. Aliquots of plasmid-bearing E. coli (positive control) were stored at 4°C while the other culture aliquots were autoclaved. E. coli DH10B without plasmid was autoclaved in parallel as a negative control. After autoclaving, cultures were centrifuged at 4°C and 3,000 × g for 5 min. Supernatants were collected for the autoclave condition to evaluate ARG-containing DNA released from thermally lysed cells. Pellets were washed once with 2 mL MilliQ H2O then miniprepped for the analyses shown in Figures 1 and S5, S7, and S8 with raw data included in Data S1. 50 mL E. coli cultures carrying pBWB162 or pMP2463 were cultivated and autoclaved as aforementioned. For the electroporation transformations shown in Figure 3, modified approach was used to isolate DNA from autoclaved cultures. Briefly, miniprepping was performed up to the centrifugation step that follows neutralization. Then, the supernatant was transferred to tubes containing 0.7 volumes of room temperature isopropanol. After incubating the tubes at −20°C for 15 min, they were centrifuged for 10 min at 16,000 × g, and the supernatant was removed. The pellet was washed once with 500 μL of ice-cold 70% ethanol and then centrifuged again. The resulting pellets were resuspended in 300 μL TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 0.1 mM EDTA) at room temperature. Following complete resuspension (∼8 h), the solution was loaded into multiple cut wells of a 0.9% agarose gel. After TAE gel electrophoresis, extractions were performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines (ZymoResearch).

For solid autoclave loads, a gravity cycle with a 15 min sterilization step at 121°C was used. ∼100 mg fragments of a Petri dish containing a lawn of E. coli + pSC200 were aseptically excised and then transferred to 15 mL capped glass tubes and placed in a 1 L Pyrex beaker containing 200 mL H2O to prevent boiling over. These are referred to as “Agar Slants.” An entire Petri dish containing streaked cells was placed in a capped glass jar and autoclaved in parallel. Lastly, 10 μL inoculating loops were loaded with cells and spread along the bottom of 15 mL glass tubes, which were also autoclaved. As a negative control, ∼100 mg fragments from a Petri dish containing E. coli DH10B without plasmid were also excised, and the corresponding tubes were placed in a beaker with H2O. Samples in the glass tubes were removed at ∼50°C and immediately transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes. The autoclaved, damaged Petri dish was allowed to cool to room temperature. ∼100–200 mg of agar + cell debris was aseptically transferred to microcentrifuge tubes in duplicate. For these conditions, DNA was isolated using a gel extraction kit (ZymoResearch) and eluted in cloning water (Corning).

DNA analysis, detection, and sequencing

Following isolation, DNA was quantified using a plate-reader spectrophotomer (260/230 ratio; Tecan). 200 ng of isolated DNA was loaded onto 1% agarose gels for TAE (40 mM Tris, 20 mM acetic acid, 1 mM EDTA; Thermo Scientific) gel electrophoresis. All gel images were captured using a DNA imager station (Analytik Jena UVP GelSolo). VisionWorks software was used for densitometry analyses with areas designated according to the positive controls. Two-sample, two-tailed Student’s t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed to analyze DNA yields and gel densitometry results for select comparisons, and p-values are reported in the results section text. PCR amplifications using GoTaq (Promega) or Phusion (NEB) polymerases were conducted according to manufacturer guidelines and included the appropriate positive and negative controls. For each gel, the same volume of PCR products was loaded. For Sanger sequencing (Azenta Life Sciences), 2 μL of each PCR product or 8 μL of each miniprep product was submitted. Refer to the Data and code availability section for information regarding the primers and Sanger sequencing results. Whole-plasmid sequencing (low range option: 20–200 ng/μL) was performed by Plasmidsaurus using Oxford nanopore technology, and the results, including read length histograms, were also deposited to a data repository (see Data and code availability).

Gentamicin minimum inhibitory concentration assay for various Gammaproteobacteria

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of gentamicin was approximated for E. coli, S. enterica, K. aerogenes, and P. putida according to Kadeřábková et al.174 with slight modifications. Briefly, 5 mL LB seed cultures were incubated at the respective temperatures for 18 h. The following day, the cultures were diluted in sterile 0.85% w/v NaCl and then adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 in a 96 deep well plate. 400 μL of LB containing 10–200 μg/mL gentamicin was prepared in 96 deep well plates via serial dilution. An optical density to cell count conversion estimate of 1 OD600 ≈ 5 × 108 to 1 × 109 CFU/mL175,176,177 was used to inoculate ∼5 × 105 cells/mL per well (n = 2). In addition to positive controls without antibiotic, uninoculated controls were included in both plates as blanks and to check for contamination. The low-volume MIC assay was conducted with an outgrowth of 20 h, after which OD600 values were recorded (Data S1). Finally, gentamicin concentrations were chosen based on visual inspections of the MIC assay plate, the corresponding ODs, and subsequent trials using cells streaked onto LB + gentamicin plates.

Retransformations using DNA isolated from bleached or autoclaved plasmid prep. cultures

Transformations of naturally competent A. baylyi were performed as described by Renda et al.178 using 500 ng of DNA isolated from untreated, bleached, and autoclaved E. coli cell cultures bearing pBWB162. Electrocompetent stocks of E. coli, S. enterica, K. aerogenes, and P. putida were prepared in-house: 5 mL cultures were incubated overnight, and then 500 μL of culture was used to inoculate two 50 mL flasks containing LB. These were incubated shaking until reaching an OD600 of 0.4–0.6, after which the cultures were centrifuged for 5 min at 5,000 × g. The cells were washed with ice-cold 10% glycerol and combined for two additional washes. Finally, the cells were resuspended in 1 mL of 10% glycerol, and 50 μL aliquots were stored at −80°C. 500 ng of DNA was used for each electroporation transformation in triplicate using an Eppendorf Eporator with a 2.5 kV pulse and acceptable time constants of 3–6 ms. Immediately following each electroporation, cells were transferred to 1 mL SOC and incubated for 1 h. Recovering cells were removed for 1:10 serial dilutions in sterile 0.85% (w/v) NaCl. A multichannel pipette was used for spotting 8 μL from each condition. For the autoclave condition, the remaining 700 μL of recovering cells was centrifuged for 5 min at 3,000 × g then plated. Pictures of the transformation plates were taken the following day. Transformant cell counts were estimated for each biological replicate by averaging colony forming units from two dilutions then averaging among the replicates. Transformation frequencies (Figure 1H) were calculated by dividing the resistant cell counts by those plated on non-selective medium. Transformation efficiencies (Figure 1I) were calculated by dividing the resistant cell counts by the total amount of DNA used in each transformation (0.5 μg) then correcting for the volume spotted. For select comparisons, two-sample, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were performed, and p-values are reported in the results section text as well as the Data S1.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experimental conditions were evaluated in duplicate, unless otherwise specified. Error bars indicate one standard deviation from the mean of those replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio and Microsoft Excel. To determine statistical significance for select comparisons, p-values were calculated from two-sample, two-tailed Student’s t-tests and/or analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p-value <0.05 was considered significant. No sample size calculations were performed during the design of experiments. No samples were excluded.
