Non-linear simulations of the onset and non-linear dynamics of inertial waves in solar and stellar interiors
Souza-Gomes, M. D, Finotti, C.S, Guerrero, G, Triana, S. A, Dikpati, M, Smolarkiewicz, P.K, Botelho, E.S

Abstract
Inertial modes have been recently detected in the Sun via helioseismology, yet their origin, evolution, and role in the dynamics of the solar plasma and magnetic field remain poorly understood. In this study, we employ global numerical simulations to investigate the excitation mechanisms and dynamical consequences of inertial modes in the Sun and stellar interiors. We validate first our numerical setup by analyzing the evolution of sectoral and tesseral perturbations imposed on a rigidly rotating sphere. The results confirm that a perturbation of a given mode can excite neighboring modes with both smaller and larger wavenumbers along the dispersion relation of Rossby waves. Subsequently, we use a physically motivated forcing to impose differential rotation with varying shear amplitudes, and examine the spontaneous onset and nonlinear evolution of inertial modes. The simulations reveal that the growth of velocity perturbations is primarily driven by baroclinic instability. It gives rise to high-latitude inertial modes in the form of retrograde polar vortices whose properties depend on the imposed shear. Equatorial Rossby modes are also excited, albeit with lower intensity than their high-latitude counterpart. Perturbations with arbitrary azimuthal wavenumbers lead to the excitation of Rossby modes for all available wave numbers, sustained by both direct and inverse energy cascades. In simulations with stronger shear, the high latitude modes produce Reynolds stresses able to modify the imposed differential rotation and accelerate the rotation of the poles.

I
Introduction

Perturbations in a stably or unstably stratified differentially rotating gas subject to gravity and rotation restoring forces give rise to a plethora of waves and instabilities. Examples of the later are the convective, shear, baroclinic and barotropic instabilities, examples waves are the internal gravity waves and various inertial modes including the famous Rossby waves.
In the Earth context, both, instabilities and waves
(
37
;
26
, e.g.,)
have been vastly documented and studied. For the case of astrophysical bodies we have mainly theoretical and numerical predictions, yet not sufficient observational evidence
(see
43
, for a review)
. Starting from
32
, solar observations identified various inertial patterns in the Sun’s surface through helioseismic
(
19
;
29
;
35
;
20
;
16
)
and motion tracking techniques
(
25
)
. Nowadays, it is widely accepted the existence of the equatorial Rossby waves, the high latitude modes, the critical latitude inertial modes, and the high frequency modes reported by
22
which are about three times faster than the equatorial Rossby modes.

Rossby modes, also known as planetary waves or r-modes , are a subset of inertial oscillations whose theoretical dispersion relation is given by
\omega=-2m\Omega/l(l+1)
, where
l
and
m
are the angular degree and azimuthal wavenumber, respectively. Their flow velocity is purely horizontal, i.e., toroidal, and they propagate in the retrograde direction with respect to the rotating frame. When
l=m
, the mode is called sectoral, whereas for
l>m
, it is classified as tesseral. The former is antisymmetric in velocity and symmetric in vorticity, while the latter can exhibit either symmetry depending on the value of
l-m
. If the difference
l-m
is an odd (even) number, the modes are symmetric (antisymmetric) in velocity and antisymmetric (symmetric) in vorticity.

The high-latitude modes manifest in the Sun’s velocity field as spiral structures near the poles. These modes were first reported by
24
and described as giant convective cells. Subsequent analyses by
23
and
16
reclassified them as inertial waves. The predominant mode corresponds to azimuthal order
m=1
, exhibiting the largest amplitude among observed inertial modes. For a fixed azimuthal order
m
, the least-damped high-latitude mode has a frequency approximately equal to
\omega\approx m\Omega_{2}

(
15
)
. These modes occur in differentially rotating systems and are highly sensitive to latitudinal entropy gradients
(
15
;
2
)
. At low azimuthal orders,
3
demonstrated that they may become baroclinically unstable in the presence of latitudinal entropy gradients, promoting angular momentum transport toward the poles and contributing to the regulation of solar differential rotation (DR).

The critical-latitude modes are inertial modes whose maximum amplitude occurs near the latitude where the wave’s angular velocity matches the local rotation rate of the fluid, they have been detectecd by
16
. And, finally, the high-frequency modes exhibit an antisymmetric vorticity about the equator and possess a frequency approximately three times higher than that of sectoral Rossby modes
21
.

In the stellar context, the eigenvalue problem of non-radial oscillations, without and with rotation, where first studied by
34
, followed by
14
and
13
, who extended the study considering DR and applied the analysis to the solar case. These studies found that the Rossby modes may develop in cavities either below the convection zone or near the photosphere. In these locations the ratio
N^{2}/\Omega_{0}^{2}
is above unity; here
N
marks the Brunt-Väisälä frequency and
\Omega_{0}
is the angular velocity of the rotating frame. They also explored the development of oscillations driven by the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability. Depending on the shear profile, these oscillations may destabilize the DR. Further developments on these shear instabilities were performed by
10
.

More recent theoretical studies have focused on reproducing the solar observations, most of them solving the linear eigenvalue problem. For instance, the solar critical latitude modes
(
17
)
and in the solar equatorial Rossby modes
(
2
;
5
)
. Furthermore
42
demonstrated that the eigenvalue solution for a solid rotating cavity is able to capture both, the equatorial Rossby modes and the high frequency modes. These modes were also reproduced by
5
, and by
4
who explored the dependence of the high frequency modes on the DR and the adiabaticity of the layer. In other words, these works have focused on the excitation mechanisms of inertial modes.

Other efforts have focused on identifying inertial modes in global convective dynamo simulations
(
7
)
. That study demonstrated that inertial modes can coexist with convective and dynamo instabilities. However, because of the complexity of the simulation, the study did not clarify the origin of the modes or their role in the overall dynamics of the simulations. More recently,
3
addressed the origin of the high-latitude modes using a 3D model of differential rotation based on the theory of the
\lambda
-effect
(see
36
, and references therein)
that permits non-axisymmetric perturbations and employs a modified continuity derived from the reduced speed of sound approximation. Their results suggest that the high-latitude modes are driven by baroclinic instability, and that the properties of these modes are sensitive to the thermal stratification at the base of the convection zone.

Despite the remarkable progress made in recent years, theoretical investigations still lack an integrated framework that simultaneously addresses the conditions in the solar interior conducive to the excitation of inertial modes and their subsequent nonlinear evolution. Furthermore, the contribution of these modes to the overall dynamics of solar and stellar interiors still requires a better understanding.

The objective of the present work is to fill this gap by developing a numerical model that reproduces the conditions of solar and stellar interiors and enables the investigation of the onset and nonlinear evolution of inertial modes under these conditions. The model is implemented using the hydrodynamic branch of the EULAG-MHD code
(
39
)
. We first validate our model by studying the evolution of Rossby waves in rigidly rotating layers. We then introduce DR by adding a body-force term to the momentum equation in such a way that the longitudinal velocity relaxes to the target mean flow over an arbitrary time scale. Yet, we allow for the development of non-axysymmetric perturbations along the mean flow, evolving in fluid layers with thermodynamic stratification representative of the solar interior while exploring DR profiles resembling the solar case yet with different amount of shear. We examine the growth of these perturbations as well as their spectral structure and their spatial and temporal propagation.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we describe the numerical model. In Section
III
, we first present the results for solid-body rotation, which serve to validate the model. We then examine the case with differential rotation, interpret the results in the framework of the Taylor–Proudman theorem, propose the instability most relevant for the development of inertial modes, and analyze the evolution of these modes and their contribution to angular momentum transport. Finally, in Section
IV
, we summarize our conclusions.

III.2
Inertial modes in simulations with differential rotation

The simulations presented in this section start with all variables set to zero and the forcing term of Eq.
7
turned on. After a few relaxation times,
\tau_{s}
, a mean profile of differential rotation establishes in the domain. Since perturbations are allowed on both, the potential temperature and the longitudinal velocity, the resulting DR is not exactly similar to the imposed forcing. Below we explore the excitation of hydrodynamic instabilities for cases with different relative shear defined as
\Omega_{2}/\Omega_{0}
. These instabilities are the source of inertial waves.

III.2.1
Taylor-Proudman theorem and the adiabaticity of the convection zone

The turbulent motions resulting in convection may be the source of inertial modes. Although tracking these modes is not impossible
(
7
)
, it is hard to disentangle their properties from those of convection. Additionally,
convection evinces turbulent viscosity which is much larger than the molecular viscosity estimated for the convection zone. This may affect the dumping of inertial waves, particularly those with small spatial scales. Thus, to better capture the evolution of inertial modes, in the simulations presented below we avoid turbulent convection in the upper layer of the simulation’s domain. To do so, we consider for this layer a polytropic index
m\geq 1.5
.

In Fig.
8
we present the forced DR profile for a 20% of relative shear (panel a), and the resulting profiles for simulations where
m_{1}=1.5
(b) and
m_{1}=1.50001
(c). In this way, as mentioned above, our forcing profile, Eq. (
7
), together with the thermodynamic stratification defined by Eqs. (
5
) and (
4
), generate three different radial layers. The bottom layer of the domain has solid body rotation and highly sub-adiabatic ambient temperature gradient (
m_{0}=2.5
). The middle layer is also highly sub-adiabatic, yet its rotation is differential. Finally, the upper layer has an ambient temperature gradient either adiabatic,
m_{1}=1.5
(panel b) or slightly sub-adiabatic,
m_{1}=1.5001
(panel c), and has differential rotation. The tachocline is located in the bottom layer. We noticed that the mean velocity reaches a steady state with the profiles presented in Fig.
9
in less than 5 years. In this stage, the four forces present in the model, namely Coriolis, gravity, pressure gradient and the body force, must balance. In the vertical direction, gravity balances the radial gradient of pressure. In the horizontal direction, the pressure gradient force and the forcing term balance the Coriolis force. Thus, in order to conserve angular momentum, a meridional circulation (MC) develops together with the DR. In addition, there are perturbations of potential temperature with a meridional profile satisfying some sort of thermal wind balance,

For the polytropic index
m_{1}=1.5
, corresponding to a strictly adiabatic uppermost layer, the resulting DR profile presents contours that are aligned with the rotation axis. This means that the upper layer of the domain obeys the Taylor-Proudman theorem. In this case both sides of Eq.

Conversely, if

is slightly larger than
1.5

At lower (higher) latitudes the contours are tilted away (towards) the rotation axis. In the Sun, all contours are tilted away from the axis as shown in Fig.
8

\tau
reverse the tilt of the
\overline{\Omega}
contours at higher latitudes, causing them to point away from the rotation axis. This modification, however, does not affect the evolution or the spectral properties of the inertial modes discussed below, apart from their amplitude. We also verified that the results are only weakly sensitive to variations in
\tau_{s}
. For this reason, we defer a detailed parametric analysis of these variables to a companion paper, where the forcing is prescribed by the observed differential rotation (Finotti et al., in prep.).

Recent numerical findings suggest that the lower portion of the solar convection zone may be convectively stable according to the Schwarzschild criterion, yet capable of transporting heat efficiently due to the downward enthalpy flux driven by overshooting plumes

Motivated by these considerations, and by the fact that a slightly subadiabatic profile can induce tilted rotational contours, the ambient stratification adopted in the simulations presented below includes a mildly subadiabatic thermal stratification in the upper layer of the domain. The preferential development of baroclinicity in subadiabatic layers might be due to the fact that gradients of potential temperature developed by thermal wind balance may persist in the layer even in the presence of waves or perturbations. Conversely, in strictly adiabatic layers, marginally stable to convection, even small perturbations may quickly homogenize the gradients of potential temperature. The same is true for super-adiabatic convective layers.
The sustainment of potential temperature gradients at higher latitudes may of course allow for potential energy availability when parcels of fluid displace in the proper direction. When this energy is transferred to the fluid motions, it develops the so-called baroclinic instability.

III.2.3
Morphology of the polar vortex

Polar vortices appear to be ubiquitous in rotating fluid spheres. They have been observed in Earth’s atmosphere, in the gaseous planets, and in the Sun. If their generation arises from baroclinic instability, their presence is expected: in Earth’s atmosphere and in the gas giants due to differential solar heating. In the Sun there is no a comprehensive explanation for the existence of a latitudinal gradient of potential temperature (viz. entropy) in the convection zone. Nevertheless, the tilted contours of differential rotation suggests its existence. Underneath the convection zone, at tachocline levels, if the Sun is in thermal wind balance, the latitudinal gradient of

Angular momentum transport

IV
Conclusions

We developed a numerical model to study the onset and nonlinear dynamics of inertial waves in the solar interior. Our results can be extended to other stars with different DR. The model solves the anelastic hydrodynamic equations in a rotating spherical shell with thermodynamical properties resembling those of the solar interior. The potential temperature equation allows perturbations around an ambient state, which is assumed to be a known equilibrium solution of the same system of equations in steady state. Therefore, the simulations naturally develop instabilities and oscillatory modes while approaching (and during) a statistically steady state, considered the most likely solution of the model for the given initial and boundary conditions.

We validate our model by considering the evolution of perturbations in the form of spherical harmonics as initial conditions in a spherical shell with two thin layers: one sub-adiabatic, mimicking the solar radiative zone, and one strictly adiabatic. The perturbations propagate in the retrograde direction with frequencies consistent with the Rossby wave dispersion relation. Remarkably, both sectoral and tesseral modes transfer energy toward smaller and larger scales, while retaining frequencies that always agree with the same dispersion relation.

Subsequently, we impose DR in the model through a body-force term in the momentum equation. The differential rotation establishes itself after a few multiples of the relaxation time,
\tau_{s}
, of this body force. We find that the relaxed longitudinal velocity does not exactly match the target profile. This discrepancy arises because the system must satisfy thermal wind balance, which links velocity and potential temperature in statistical equilibrium. Consequently, the resulting profile depends strongly on the thermodynamic stratification of the uppermost layer. If this layer is adiabatic, the system approaches Taylor–Proudman balance, whereas in slightly sub-adiabatic layers, thermal wind balance is maintained while preserving the baroclinicity required to tilt the contours of angular velocity. The underlying reason for this behavior lies in the evolution of thermal perturbations in layers of differing adiabaticity, an issue to be explored further in a companion paper. To investigate the development of inertial waves, we therefore adopt an uppermost layer that is slightly sub-adiabatic and apply forcing terms that generate different amounts of shear, ranging from a
5\%
equator–pole difference up to
40\%
.

The results from simulations with varying shear amplitudes are summarized as follows:

•

Strong radial shear at tachocline depths requires a large latitudinal gradient of potential temperature for the simulations to achieve thermal wind balance.

•

When the radial shear is sufficiently strong, as in simulation DR10, baroclinic instability develops. The animations associated with Fig.
11
show unstable modes growing at the poles and tachocline levels, subsequently propagating in both directions.

•

Except for the simulation with
5\%
shear (DR05), which remains stable, the growth rate of instabilities increases with shear. This trend agrees with the magnitude of radial shear at the poles and with the high-latitude gradient of
\Theta^{\prime}
. Saturation occurs for shears larger than
35\%
.

•

Baroclinic unstable modes form polar vortices that extend across the entire radial and latitudinal domain. However, these vortices show no radial variation, i.e., no radial modes are present. In the Northern Hemisphere the vortices rotate clockwise, contrary to solar observations. However, when filtering the results to include only spherical harmonics with
m=1
and
l_{\rm max}\lesssim 28
, the vortices appear counterclockwise, in agreement with observations. Nevertheless, the velocity amplitude of these modes remains weaker than in the Sun, where most of the energy resides in the lowest-order modes.

•

At the equator, where the rotation rate of the uppermost layer is
\Omega_{0}
, the frequencies of the
m=1
,
2
, and
3
modes are
\omega=-78
,
-125
, and
-164
, respectively, in fair agreement with the frequencies observed for polar vortices in the Carrington frame.

•

The spectra of the modes vary with radius and latitude and depend on the imposed shear. As shown in Fig.
14
, the most intense high-latitude modes are those driven by baroclinic instability, with dispersions consistent with their theoretical prediction. Other, weaker modes may correspond to critical-latitude modes.

•

Equatorial Rossby modes are absent from the spectra. Although some modes appear at lower latitudes, only one or two satisfy the Rossby dispersion relation.

•

If a sectoral or tesseral perturbation is imposed on top of a statistically steady-state simulation (Fig.
15
shows the spectra for DR20), equatorial modes consistent with Rossby waves appear across all available wavenumbers (up to
m=15
shown). The imposed mode dominates the energy, yet it is remarkable that all other Rossby modes are rapidly excited through both direct and inverse cascades.

•

Finally, we explore the contribution of high-latitude modes to angular momentum transport. Our results clearly show that these modes transport angular momentum poleward: the flux is negative in the Northern Hemisphere and positive in the Southern Hemisphere, thereby accelerating the poles. Both the magnitude and latitudinal extent of this transport increase with shear, peaking near the poles for weak shear and shifting to
\sim\pm 50^{\circ}
latitude for
35\%
–
40\%
shear. In the latter cases, the influence of the unstable modes becomes so strong that their growth rate saturates because the radial shear diminishes relative to the target DR profile.

In summary, from our hydrodynamic model we conclude that the solar polar vortices and the associated high latitude modes have origin at the tachocline and are driven by the baroclinic instability. During the non-linear evolution, the inertial modes captured in the simulations accelerate the poles while heat is pumped towards the equator in the attempt to homogenize the temperature gradient. This confirms, in a conceptually different model, the results obtained by
3
. However, in our case DR20, most similar to the Sun, the contribution of the high-latitude modes to the DR is still weak. Therefore, we can not argue that the Sun’s differential rotation is controlled by these modes. We believe that the reason for the discrepancy between the two models might be in the thermodynamical structure of the star’s interior.

Intense Rossby modes do not seem to be driven by the physics included in our simulations. External perturbations are needed to onset these modes and, remarkably, even a single mode perturbation quickly propagates along the dispersion relation for Rossby modes. Identifying this mode in solar observations may bring information about the motions present in the solar convection zone. Finally, the so-called high-frequency modes are also not observed in our simulation’s results.

In forthcoming work, we will investigate the role of magnetic fields in the evolution of baroclinically excited modes. To enhance the accuracy of the results in comparison with the solar case, the model will be forced using the observationally inferred differential rotation profile rather than an analytical prescription.

Appendix A: Thermal wind balance equation

The thermal wind equation is derived through the longitudinal component of the vorticity, which can be obtained from the curl of the momentum equation. Assuming a steady-state and an azimuthal average, the longitudinal component of the vorticity is given by:

\begin{split}\underbrace{2\Omega_{0}\frac{\partial\left\langle u_{\phi}\right\rangle}{\partial z}}_{\textrm{Inertia}}=&\underbrace{-\left\langle\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\triangledown u_{\phi}+\frac{\omega_{\phi}u_{r}}{r}+\frac{\omega_{\phi}u_{\theta}\cot(\theta)}{r}\right\rangle}_{\textrm{Stretching}}\\
&+\underbrace{\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\triangledown\omega_{\phi}+\frac{u_{\phi}\omega_{r}}{r}+\frac{u_{\phi}\omega_{\theta}\cot(\theta)}{r}\right\rangle}_{\textrm{Advection}}\\
&+\underbrace{\left\langle\omega_{\phi}\left[\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial(r^{2}u_{r})}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r\sin(\theta)}\frac{\partial(\sin\theta\ u_{\theta})}{\partial\theta}+\frac{1}{r\sin(\theta)}\frac{\partial u_{\phi}}{\partial\phi}\right]\right\rangle}_{\textrm{Compressibility}}\\
&+\underbrace{\left\langle\frac{g(r)}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\frac{\Theta^{\prime}}{\Theta_{0}}\right)\right\rangle}_{\textrm{Baroclinicity}}\;.\end{split}
