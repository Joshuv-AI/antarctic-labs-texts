21st-century skills among generation Z physical therapy students: a cross-sectional study on associated factors
Authors: Erhan Secer, Melda Baser Secer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe emergence of evidence-based practice in healthcare has positioned physical therapy (PT) to play a unique role in the 21st-century. This study aimed to examine the 21st-century skills of PT students and compare them across certain individual and academic characteristics that might be relevant.MethodsA total of 407 PT students [median age, 21.0 (20.0/22.0) years] were included in the study. The structured survey form created using Google Forms was sent to the students through messaging apps. The survey comprised questions addressing students’ individual and academic characteristics, along with items from the Multi-Dimensional 21st-Century Skills Scale.ResultsPT students demonstrated a good level of 21st-century skills. Male students showed higher scores in information and technology literacy and entrepreneurship and innovation skills, while female students exhibited greater career awareness (p˂0.05). Students who actively participated in social and academic activities displayed higher scores in information and technology literacy, entrepreneurship and innovation, social responsibility and leadership, and career awareness (p˂0.05). Fourth-year students scored higher in information and technology literacy, critical thinking and problem-solving, entrepreneurship and innovation, and social responsibility and leadership skills (p˂0.05).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that fostering active engagement in diverse social and academic activities is crucial for holistic skill development and that curriculum design should consider gender-specific strengths to ensure all students are comprehensively prepared for their future careers.Keywords: Critical thinking, Entrepreneurship, Information technology, Leadership, Problem-Solving, Social responsibility, Technological innovations

Background

Generation Z (late 1990s–early 2010 s) are digital natives, inherently shaped by their complete immersion in a world of ubiquitous internet and smartphones [1, 2]. This constant connectivity profoundly influences their communication, learning styles, and worldview [3]. Characterized by pragmatism and a value for authenticity, Gen Z often uses their digital fluency to advocate globally for social and environmental change [4].

These traits place Generation Z at the forefront of 21st-century skills—a dynamic set of abilities critical for navigating today’s rapidly evolving world [5]. These essential competencies are defined in the literature as a multifaceted cluster of knowledge, character traits, and skills that extend beyond traditional academic content [6]. These skills are vital for success in the workplace and effective citizenship. The primary framework, the Partnership for 21 st Century Learning (P21) Framework [7], organizes them into three core areas: Learning and Innovation Skills (e.g., critical thinking, collaboration) [8], Information, Media, and Technology Skills (literacy in these domains) [9], and Life and Career Skills (e.g., flexibility, productivity) [10]. For Gen Z, mastering these abilities is a necessity for succeeding in a complex global landscape and a rapidly changing job market [11].

The emergence of evidence-based practice has positioned physical therapy (PT) as crucial in 21st-century healthcare [12]. The PT philosophy, aligning with Hippocratic principles, emphasizes prevention, reversal, and management, especially for lifestyle-related conditions [13]. Prioritizing non-invasive interventions can yield superior patient outcomes—including reduced morbidity, enhanced health, and decreased reliance on biomedical care (e.g., fewer hospital stays and reduced medication use)—compared to solely biomedical approaches [14]. Achieving these comprehensive global outcomes necessitates closer collaboration between physical therapists and medical colleagues [12].

The 21 st century’s rapid technological advancements necessitate a fundamental change in physical therapy (PT) education [15]. Traditional models are insufficient to prepare Generation Z students for a healthcare landscape defined by innovation, collaboration, and holistic care [16]. Thus, equipping future PTs with 21st-century competencies is a global imperative, enabling them to become innovators in the sector [17]. The need for these skills is inherent to the profession, which is fundamentally based on complex problem-solving and adaptive reasoning [18]. Critical thinking is essential for evidence-based practice and applying research to individualized patient care [19]. Furthermore, communication and collaboration are foundational, requiring PTs to build rapport, explain complex plans, and coordinate with interdisciplinary teams [20]. Finally, the growing use of tools like telehealth and wearable devices makes digital literacy a professional necessity, not an option [15]. Cultivating this robust set of 21st-century skills is, therefore, a critical prerequisite for future physical therapists to thrive in their continuously evolving profession.

A review of the literature shows a strong focus on 21st-century skills among students in various healthcare professions, particularly nursing and midwifery [21–27]. However, there is a conspicuous absence of research specifically dedicated to the development and assessment of these skills among Physical Therapy (PT) students. This gap is concerning because the evolving healthcare landscape and the focus on evidence-based practice and preventive care demand that PTs move beyond traditional roles [2]. The core 21st-century skills—including critical thinking, collaboration, digital literacy, and problem-solving—are not supplementary; they are fundamental prerequisites for professional success in the contemporary PT field.

Without specific data on how PT students acquire these skills, there is a risk of a disconnect between their education and real-world professional demands. While findings from other health science fields may suggest a similar skill profile, this study provides the first empirical validation of these trends specifically within the physical therapy context. Having concrete, discipline-specific data is critical, as it is necessary to directly inform curriculum development and assessment strategies. Ultimately, this research is vital for ensuring that future PT graduates are adequately prepared to lead, innovate, and provide high-quality care in an evolving healthcare environment.

Based on this background, literature analysis, and current situation, the aims of this study were as follows; (i) to examine whether these skills vary according to the individual and academic characteristics of the PT students, (ii) to investigate how curriculum design shapes skill acquisition within PT education, (iii) to explore whether the professional identity of PT students interacts with 21st-century skill development in ways that are distinct from other health sciences.

Methods

Design and ethical aspect of the study

This cross-sectional study was conducted using an online survey in Turkey. The study protocol received approval from the Manisa Celal Bayar University Health Sciences Ethics Committee (Decision Date: 04.06.2025; Decision Number: 20.478.486/3196) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants through the online survey after they were fully informed about the study’s aims, procedure, and benefits.

Participants

The sample size was calculated using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7, Heinrich-Heine-Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany). This determination was based on a power analysis that utilized the mean and standard deviation of total scores from the “Multidimensional 21st-Century Skills Scale” (in terms of gender) from a previously conducted study [28]. With an effect size of 0.327, a Type 1 error rate of 0.05, and a power of 0.90, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 322 participants were required for this study. Within the scope of the study, the entire population of 672 students enrolled in a PT program at a state university in Turkey was approached electronically. The online survey was distributed via an official channel (such as a departmental email list or institutional learning management system). Participation was strictly voluntary, and the inclusion criteria explicitly required students to be volunteering for the study; consequently, they were not obligated to complete the survey and were entirely free to refuse. Of the 672 students approached, 407 [median age: 21.00 (20.00/22.00) years] fully completed the questionnaire, resulting in a strong recruitment rate (response rate) of approximately 60.6%. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: volunteering to participate in the study, being enrolled in a PT program, and being able to read and understand Turkish. The exclusion criterion was: incompletely filling out the online survey sent as part of the study. The term “incompletely filling out” refers to a failure to respond to all items on both the Multidimensional 21st-Century Skills Scale and the mandatory demographic questions.

Procedure and online survey

Data for this study were collected using Google Forms, an online survey tool developed by Google (Mountain View, California, USA). An online survey created using this application was sent to the PT students. The online survey included questions about individual [e.g., age, gender, perceived income status, maternal/parental education status] and academic characteristics [e.g., participation in social and academic activities, academic level, cumulative grade point average (CGPA)] and the Multidimensional 21st-Century Skills Scale. Completing all questions was mandatory to submit the questionnaire. Duplicate entries from the same participant were prevented, ensuring that users with the same login credentials could not access the survey more than once.

Multidimensional 21st-Century skills scale

Students’ 21st-century skills were assessed with the Multidimensional 21st-century skills scale. This scale, which was not developed for the current study, is a 5-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1-Strongly Disagree to 5-Strongly Agree) comprising 41 items and five sub-dimensions, developed by Cevik and Senturk in 2019 [29]. Additionally, this scale has been used in many studies aiming to assess the 21st-century skills of students studying in various fields [30–33]. Scores on the scale can range from 41 to 205, with higher average scores indicating a greater level of students’ 21st-century skills. The Information and Technology Literacy Skills sub-dimension consists of 15 items, with a minimum score of 15 and a maximum score of 75. Higher average scores in this sub-dimension reflect improved information and technology literacy skills among students. The Critical Thinking and Problem-Solving Skills sub-dimension includes 6 items, with scores ranging from a minimum of 6 to a maximum of 30. An increase in the average score signifies enhanced critical thinking and problem-solving abilities. The Entrepreneurship and Innovation Skills sub-dimension, comprising 10 items, has a score range of 10–50. Higher average scores in this area indicate a greater level of entrepreneurial and innovative skills. The Social Responsibility and Leadership Skills sub-dimension is made up of 4 items, with scores ranging from 4 to 20. As the average score rises, students’ social responsibility and leadership skills improve. Lastly, the Career Awareness sub-dimension of the scale consists of 6 items, with a minimum score of 6 and a maximum score of 30. Higher average scores here correspond to an increased level of students’ career awareness skills. The overall Cronbach’s Alpha reliability coefficient for the scale is 0.86, and the coefficient for each sub-dimension is above 0.70 [29]. Scores on the Multidimensional 21st-Century Skills Scale are categorized into four levels, indicating proficiency in 21st-century skills. A total score ranging from 41 to 82 suggests a low level of 21st-century skills. Scores between 83 and 123 indicate a moderate level of these skills. A score from 124 to 164 reflects a good level of 21st-century skills, while a score between 165 and 205 signifies an advanced level of 21st-century skills [29].

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the data was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Standard Concurrent User Version 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA). The normality assumption for numerical variables was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test and graphical analysis using histograms. Given the violation of the normal distribution assumption, numerical variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables are reported with frequency (n) and percentage (%). For group comparisons, nonparametric tests were utilized due to the nature of the data, as detailed previously. Specifically, the Mann-Whitney U test, which is the non-parametric counterpart to the independent samples t-test, was used for all comparisons involving two independent groups. This decision was made because the assumption of normal distribution was not met for the data, thus necessitating the use of a non-parametric test. When analysis involved comparing more than two independent groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed, which was further adjusted using a Bonferroni correction to control for Type I errors across multiple comparisons. Also, effect size (ES) was calculated (d = M1 - M2/σpooled) for all statistically significant findings. ES values in the range of 0.00–0.19.00.19 were considered negligible, and values in the ranges of 0.20–0.49, 0.50–0.79, and ≥ 0.80 were considered small, moderate, and large, respectively [34]. Statistical significance was determined at an alpha level of 0.05.

Results

The final sample consisted of 407 PT students with a median age of 21.0 years (IQR: 20.0 to 22.0). The sample was predominantly female (73.0%) and reported a moderate (87.0%) perceived income status. Maternal and paternal education levels varied, with high school or primary/secondary school being the most common categories for both. Academically, the participants were well-distributed across all four years, with second-year students representing the largest group (31.2%). A majority reported participation in academic activities (61.9%), and 42.8% demonstrated strong academic performance with a high CGPA of 3.00 to 4.00. Discussion

The main findings of this study indicated that PT students generally possess a good level of 21st-century skills. Furthermore, these skills were found to vary significantly based on certain individual and academic characteristics among the PT students.

While the focus remains largely on nursing and midwifery students [21–27], existing research generally indicates a strong presence of 21st-century skills within these cohorts [22, 24, 25]. Studies consistently show that students possess a sufficient to good level of these competencies, with skills like computer and ICT literacy scoring particularly high [23]. Nuances, however, suggest areas for targeted intervention: male nursing students scored significantly higher in entrepreneurship and innovation [25], and active participation in social and scientific activities correlated positively with higher skill levels overall [24]. Interestingly, one study noted that Generation Y students showed higher competencies in knowledge, character, and meta-learning compared to Generation Z [27]. In summary, while the literature confirms successful skill acquisition, future educational strategies should focus on boosting innovation among female students, encouraging extracurricular engagement, and exploring the reasons behind the observed generational differences.

This concentration on nursing and midwifery students, while valuable for those disciplines, creates a significant and undeniable gap in the current literature concerning the 21st-century skills specifically possessed by PT students and the various factors that influence these competencies. This gap underscores a critical need for more targeted research within the PT domain to provide a comprehensive and nuanced insight into the preparedness of these vital healthcare professionals for the demands of a rapidly changing global landscape. Addressing this void would not only enrich academic understanding but also offer valuable data to inform curriculum development, pedagogical strategies, and professional development initiatives tailored to the unique needs and challenges faced by PT students.

Our study found that PT students demonstrated a good level of 21st-century skills, a finding which broadly reinforces positive trends observed in previous nursing and midwifery literature [22–24]. Extending beyond the existing literature, our data highlighted specific gender differences: while male PT students scored higher in information and technology literacy and entrepreneurship and innovation (aligning in part with Çoban et al. [25]), female PT students demonstrated greater career awareness. Furthermore, we found that active participation in social and academic activities positively correlated with higher scores across multiple key domains (information and technology literacy, entrepreneurship, social responsibility, and career awareness), supporting the findings of Erbaş et al. [24]. Finally, a clear progression was noted, as fourth-year students scored significantly higher than earlier cohorts in information and technology literacy, entrepreneurship and innovation, and social responsibility and leadership.

Due to the current scarcity of studies specifically examining 21st-century skills among PT students, drawing discipline-exclusive conclusions and making direct comparisons remains challenging. Nevertheless, the highly encouraging finding that PT students generally possess a good level of 21st-century skills suggests that current PT education is successfully equipping future professionals. Beyond core clinical knowledge, the cultivation of critical skills—such as critical thinking, information literacy, collaboration, and adaptability—underscores the strength of existing PT curricula in preparing well-rounded practitioners for effective patient care and the demands of a dynamic healthcare environment [35].

While our gender-based findings (males higher in technology/entrepreneurship, females higher in career awareness) may seem predictable, their underlying dynamics warrant in-depth examination. We propose these differences stem from societal gender roles and socialization, where males are traditionally steered toward technology and risk-taking, while females often prioritize future-oriented planning in human-centric fields like PT [36]. These results offer crucial practical implications for PT education, serving as a call to action for gender-balanced curricula. Programs should be designed to support male students’ career awareness and strengthen female students’ entrepreneurial potential through targeted interventions, such as specialized mentorships and projects. Thus, our observations provide an actionable guide for creating educational strategies that ensure equitable professional skill development [36].

The highly significant correlation between regular participation in social and academic activities and higher 21st-century skills (e.g., information literacy, entrepreneurship, leadership, career awareness) suggests that learning extends beyond the classroom [37]. Extracurriculars, clubs, and collaborative research provide crucial experiential learning opportunities that foster practical knowledge application, teamwork, and real-world initiative. This underscores the vital role of holistic engagement in developing well-rounded professionals. Therefore, institutions should actively promote and facilitate diverse student involvement to complement formal education and better prepare students for dynamic careers [37].

The improvement of 21st-century skills among PT students over academic years, while predictable, is valuable because it empirically maps this progression within the specific context of PT education. This progression is primarily driven by the structural and developmental nature of the curriculum [38]. Early years focus on foundational skills (e.g., critical thinking), while later years, through clinical applications, actively utilize more complex, integrated skills (e.g., collaboration, entrepreneurship). By documenting this development with concrete data, our study acts as a diagnostic tool, revealing which skills are acquired at which stage. Therefore, these results move beyond simple observation, establishing an actionable foundation for identifying curricular strengths and weaknesses and optimizing professional skill development [38].

While this research may appear to confirm existing findings from other healthcare fields, its significance lies in the empirical validation of these trends within the unique context of PT education. Demonstrating that PT students share a similar skills profile with peers in nursing and midwifery provides a crucial, previously missing concrete foundation for the literature. This consistency suggests the need for a deeper theoretical understanding, leading us to propose the Core Skills Model. This model posits that the shared generational characteristics of Gen Z form a foundational “core” of competencies common to all health science students, upon which unique professional curricula build. This study offers the first direct evidence supporting this model in PT, which has crucial practical implications. By confirming alignment across disciplines, PT educators can confidently adapt successful curriculum strategies from nursing or midwifery. Furthermore, the shared skill set strongly justifies promoting interdisciplinary education and collaboration to ensure PT graduates are prepared to work seamlessly with their colleagues.

Strengths and limitations

We believe this study offers several significant contributions. Foremost, it provides the first empirical validation of 21st-century skills specifically among PT students, thereby filling a notable research gap in health science education. Secondly, a robust methodology—including a high response rate (60.6%) from a near-census approach—ensures a high degree of internal validity and population representativeness. Finally, by successfully mapping skill progression and identifying key correlations with academic involvement, this research delivers actionable, discipline-specific insights crucial for optimizing future PT curriculum design.

It is important to acknowledge several limitations when interpreting these findings. First, the reliance on an online, structured questionnaire meant students could not seek clarification on ambiguous items, potentially affecting the consistency of responses. Second, the use of a self-reported instrument introduces inherent limitations, as results reflect students’ self-perceptions and are subject to recall bias. Future research should integrate alternative methods, such as observational assessments, to gain a more objective understanding of skills. Third, while sociodemographic and basic academic data were collected, other uninvestigated factors (e.g., prior educational experiences, learning styles) may also influence skill development. Finally, the sample was confined to PT students from a single public university, severely limiting the generalizability of the findings. Comparative studies across diverse universities, regions, and countries are needed to understand how varying educational contexts and cultural backgrounds influence 21st-century skill development within the PT profession.

Conclusions

The results indicate that PT students generally possess a good level of 21st-century competencies. Furthermore, the study revealed significant variations in these skills based on students’ individual and academic characteristics. The research demonstrated a direct impact of curriculum design on skill acquisition within PT education. However, the findings suggest that the professional identity of PT students does not significantly influence the development of these skills, which distinguishes it from other health science disciplines. Based on these observations, we offer specific recommendations to enhance the PT curriculum.

First, the program must proactively address gender disparities by integrating mandatory career exploration modules to boost male students’ career awareness, while making technology and entrepreneurship projects (like developing VR apps) a core component for all students to foster mutual learning. Second, given the link between involvement and skill development, the curriculum should actively promote and reward extracurricular participation through academic credit or bonus grades. Adopting Project-Based Learning (PBL), such as assigning students to design community-based training programs, is crucial for applying knowledge and building soft skills like leadership. Finally, to accelerate the skill progression seen in fourth-year students, a more intentional spiral curriculum structure should be implemented to progressively build complex skills. Assessment should shift from traditional exams to performance-based methods (e.g., portfolio presentations) to holistically measure 21st-century proficiency, ensuring PT education aligns with professional demands.
