Multiple myeloma presenting as a giant osteolytic skull mass: Surgical challenges and pitfalls – A case report
Authors: Shahd H I Abbastanira, Modar Mouhsen Monther, Muhammad Mukhtar Khan, Esraa Mohamed Abdullah Elameen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Osteolytic skull lesions can result from a broad spectrum of benign and malignant conditions, with multiple myeloma (MM) being a significant malignant cause in adults. Although skull involvement is relatively common in MM, giant exophytic lesions with extensive calvarial destruction and intracranial extension are exceedingly rare and pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Case Description: A 48-year-old male presented with progressive right-sided weakness and a visibly enlarging skull mass. Computed tomography (CT) brain scan revealed a large, well-defined, enhancing mass measuring 9.02 × 7.29 × 6.5 cm, located extra-axially, causing significant compression of the left cerebral hemisphere and ventricle. Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a large, destructive left frontoparietal lesion with marked bone erosion, and mass effect on the underlying cerebrum. The patient underwent preoperative embolization, followed by surgical resection of the tumor with wide margins and decompression, including removal of the involved skull. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses confirmed a high-grade malignant plasma cell neoplasm consistent with MM. Subsequent positron emission tomography-CT and bone marrow biopsy demonstrated systemic disease. The hematology team took over care of the patient for definitive systemic therapy.Conclusion: Giant exophytic skull lesions due to MM are rare and often mimic other neoplastic conditions. This case underscores the importance of early recognition, thorough diagnostic workup, and a multidisciplinary treatment approach. Radical neurosurgical resection combined with systemic oncological therapy is essential to optimize patient outcomes in such uncommon but aggressive presentations.

INTRODUCTION

Osteolytic skull lesions are uncommon in neurosurgical practice, accounting for approximately 1% of all skull tumors.[12] They are broadly categorized into congenital, traumatic, inflammatory, and neoplastic types,[12] encompassing both benign and malignant conditions.[16] Clinically, these lesions may present as enlarging palpable masses – either painful or painless – with or without associated tenderness.

In adults, metastatic disease and multiple myeloma (MM) represent the most frequent malignant etiologies. Given the high likelihood of systemic involvement, identification of a primary tumor is critical. Other, less common lytic lesions include fibrous dysplasia, lymphoma, intraosseous meningioma, sarcoma, and intracranial tumors with secondary skull erosion. Initial imaging typically includes skull X-rays and computed tomography, with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and bone scans providing further detail for surgical planning and systemic evaluation.[3] MM is a malignant plasma cell disorder characterized by clonal proliferation within the bone marrow.[14] Diagnosis is based on the international myeloma working group (IMWG) criteria, which require ≥10% clonal bone marrow plasma cells or biopsy-proven plasmacytoma, in addition to one or more myeloma-defining events such as hypercalcemia, renal impairment, anemia, or lytic bone lesions – or biomarkers of malignancy, including ≥60% plasma cells, serum-free light chain ratio ≥100 mg/L, or more than one focal MRI lesion larger than 5 mm.[17]

While skull involvement in MM is not uncommon, massive exophytic lesions with extensive calvarial destruction and intracranial extension are exceedingly rare. In this report, we present a case of a giant osteolytic skull lesion due to MM and highlight the associated surgical challenges, radiologic features, and multidisciplinary management required.

CASE REPORT

A 48-year-old male previously healthy presented to the emergency department with a 1-week history of progressive right-sided weakness, which had worsened over the preceding 2–3 days. He also reported headaches, dizziness, and speech difficulties. Notably, he had observed a progressively enlarging skull swelling over the past 3 months. He denied seizure activity or visual disturbances. On examination, the patient was vitally stable, with a Glasgow coma scale score of 15/15. Motor strength in the right leg was reduced (4+/5), while strength in the remaining extremities was preserved (5/5). He demonstrated an ataxic gait. A CT scan of the brain revealed a large, well-defined, enhancing extra-axial mass measuring 9.02 × 7.29 × 6.5 cm, causing significant compression of the left cerebral hemisphere and lateral ventricle, with a midline shift of approximately 6.8 mm. The lesion demonstrated complete erosion of the left frontoparietal calvarial bones with both intracranial and extracranial extension [Figure 1]. MRI with contrast showed a nodular soft-tissue lesion with intraosseous destruction, heterogeneous enhancement, and spread through the dura. A thick dural membrane appeared to separate the mass from the brain parenchyma, although there were signs of dural involvement and a dural tail. The lesion was fed by enlarged meningeal vessels, and MR venography confirmed that the superior sagittal sinus was not involved [Figure 2].

To reduce intraoperative bleeding, preoperative superselective embolization was performed through a right femoral artery approach. The patient then underwent left frontoparietal craniectomy with complete tumor excision [Figure 3]. The overlying scalp was intact but markedly stretched by the underlying mass. A standard trauma flap was created, intentionally avoiding the inferior limb anterior to the ear to preserve the superficial temporal artery for potential reconstruction. The tumor was well-encapsulated and clearly demarcated from the overlying skin, allowing for complete resection. The plastic surgery team outlined a reconstructive strategy using a pedicled rotational flap, marked intraoperatively, with a plan for potential thigh-based rotational flap coverage of the defect. Postoperatively, the patient remained stable, and recovery was uneventful. A protective helmet was recommended. A follow-up clinic appointment was scheduled, and fine-cut CT imaging was arranged for custom cranioplasty planning.

Histopathological evaluation confirmed a diagnosis of MM. The tumor showed a high-grade undifferentiated malignant neoplasm involving the skull, dura, and brain tissue, with areas of necrosis and hemorrhage. Immunohistochemical staining revealed strong, diffuse expression of cluster of differentiation (CD)38, CD138, leukocyte common antigen (LCA), and kappa light chains, with negative expression for CD20, CD3, CD99, CD10, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), Cytokeratin AE1/AE3, CD30, and lambda light chain. Ki-67 revealed a proliferative index of 40%.

Bone marrow aspirate showed increased plasma cell content. The myelogram demonstrated erythroid hyperplasia with normal myeloid and megakaryocytic maturation. Trephine biopsy revealed a hypocellular marrow (~30%) with interstitial plasma cell infiltration and atypical aggregates. Fluorescence in situ hybridization and karyotyping identified poor prognostic features, including 1p deletion in 34% of cells, though 65% demonstrated trisomy 11q22, a favorable marker. Positron emission tomography-CT revealed multiple lytic skeletal lesions typical of MM; however, these were not metabolically active. The combined histopathological, immunohistochemical, and imaging findings confirmed the diagnosis of MM. The hematology team assumed responsibility for further systemic treatment and oncologic management.

DISCUSSION

MM accounts for about 1% of all cancers and 10% of hematologic malignancies.[10] According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer, there were approximately 160,000 new cases worldwide in 2018, with an age-standardized incidence of 2.1/100,000.[8] Solitary plasmacytoma (SP) makes up only 5% of all plasma cell neoplasms, with solitary bone plasmacytomas (SBPs) representing 2–5% of plasma cell malignancies.[6,9] In contrast, the incidence of giant exophytic skull lesions caused by MM is not reported in large epidemiologic datasets, with available data limited to isolated case reports. Most skull involvement in MM appears as multiple osteolytic “punched-out” calvarial lesions rather than a single destructive exophytic mass, making our case an infrequent presentation. Anda et al.,[1] reported a case involving a 51-year-old male who presented with a frontal subcutaneous mass, where imaging revealed a large bony defect with multiple punched-out lesions of the frontal bone. The diagnosis was immunoglobulin G lambda-type plasmacytoma.[15] In another report, a 51-year-old woman with a 9-year history of MM developed a large plasmacytoma originating from the temporal bone, which manifested as marked swelling in the left temporal region with associated osteolytic destruction.[13] These cases highlight the exceptional rarity of exophytic skull-based MM lesions and emphasize the importance of early recognition, comprehensive imaging, and multidisciplinary management.[1]

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of skull-based exophytic MM lesions involves aggressive osteolytic activity driven by malignant plasma cells. These cells disrupt the bone microenvironment by enhancing osteoclast activation and suppressing osteoblast function – primarily through elevated levels of receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa B ligand, interleukin-6, and sclerostin. Furthermore, tumor necrosis factor-alpha and Notch signaling have been implicated in osteocyte apoptosis, contributing to impaired bone remodeling and structural collapse. These mechanisms facilitate widespread calvarial destruction and, in advanced or delayed cases, may enable tumor extension beyond the skull.[5,7]

Diagnostic and treatment methods

Advanced imaging – particularly CT and MRI – is essential to evaluate the full extent of both intracranial and extracranial tumor involvement. Histological confirmation remains the diagnostic gold standard, with immunohistochemical staining typically positive for CD138 (syndecan-1) and restricted expression of light chains.[4]

In our case, the patient underwent radical surgical excision aimed at decompressing the brain and debulking the mass. Given the severe calvarial destruction, a wide local resection was required – an unusual approach for MM in neurosurgical practice. This included the removal of nearly half of the skull. A custom-made cranioplasty was planned to restore cranial protection and cosmesis. Postoperative radiotherapy was deferred, and the patient was referred to hematology for initiation of systemic therapy with chemotherapy or novel agents in accordance with current MM treatment protocols.[2,11]

Technical considerations

Extensive calvarial removal in the setting of MM presents uncommon technical challenges. Large cranial defects risk brain herniation, infection, and cosmetic disfigurement, necessitating meticulous planning and execution. Preservation of overlying scalp vascularity was critical, and collaboration with plastic surgery was essential to assess and prepare for soft-tissue reconstruction. The craniectomy included wide margins with en bloc tumor removal to minimize local recurrence.

Survival and prognosis

The prognosis of SP is generally better than that of systemic MM. However, progression to overt MM is common, especially for SBP, with a progression rate from SBP to MM ranging from 20% to 60% within 3 years.[6] Once systemic disease is established, the outlook worsens significantly, and chemotherapy or systemic therapy becomes necessary. Giant exophytic skull lesions caused by MM, although very rare, tend to indicate advanced disease and are associated with guarded outcomes. Larger lesions may signify higher tumor load, greater mass effect, and increased surgical complexity. Although early radical surgical intervention followed by systemic therapy offers the best chance at functional recovery and disease control, available data on long-term outcomes are limited due to the rarity of such cases.[2,5]

CONCLUSION

Giant exophytic MM of the skull is an exceptionally rare clinical entity that poses unique neurosurgical and hematological challenges. These include the need for urgent decompression, extensive calvarial resection, and customized cranioplasty reconstruction. Early diagnosis and a coordinated multidisciplinary approach are critical to optimizing patient outcomes. In this case, following radical surgical excision and skull removal, the oncology team assumed responsibility for adjuvant management, including systemic therapy and radiotherapy, to address the risk of local recurrence due to exposed dura.
