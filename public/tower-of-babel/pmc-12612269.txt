Crystal lattice site occupation-based optical properties in Co2+-doped ZGGO persistent luminescence nanoparticles
Authors: Yaqi Zhu, Jian Yang, Hancheng Zhu, Nuernisha Alifu, Yingni Duan, Hairegu Tuxun, Wenyao Zhang, Zechen Bai, Zhen Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this work, near-infrared (NIR) persistent luminescence nanoparticles Co2+ ions doped Zn2(1−x)Co2xGa3Ge0.75O8 (ZGGO:xCo2+) were successfully synthesized via hydrothermal synthesis. The average particle size decreased from 65.9 to 52.3 nm with increasing Co2+ concentration (x = 0.001–0.05). Co2+ ions can substitute for Zn2+ at tetrahedral and antisite octahedral sites in the ZGGO host lattice, with corresponding formation energies (ΔE) values are − 4.60 and − 3.03 eV, respectively. The crystal field intensities for Co2+ in the tetrahedral and octahedral sites were relatively weak, with values of 0.45 and 1.08, respectively. All samples exhibit strong emission peaked at 685 nm and weak emission peaked at 825 nm. The strong peak was deconvoluted into two distinct peaks at 672 nm and 697 nm, which were attributed to the4A2(4F) → 4T1(4F) transition of Co2+ ions occupying octahedral sites, and the4T1(4P) → 4A2(4F) transition of Co2+ ions occupying tetrahedral sites, respectively. The NIR persistent luminescence of samples was longer than 2 min and primarily originates from octahedral Co2+ sites. The relevant defects, specifically GaoZn and Zn’Ga-GaoZn, act as the charge traps. The corresponding ΔE values are − 0.55 eV and 1.84 eV, respectively. These results substantially advance the fundamental understanding of the doping and luminescence mechanisms of Co2+ in spinel gallate-based persistent luminescence materials.

Introduction

Persistent luminescence nanoparticles (PLNPs) are special optical materials that emit light lasting from a few seconds to days after the cease of light irradiation, and are widely used in sensing1,2, information anti-counterfeiting and storage3–5, biological diagnosis and treatment6,7. In particular, the PLNPs emitting in the near-infrared (NIR) region play a crucial role in the field of biomedicine due to their exceptional tissue penetration capability and their ability to circumvent tissue autofluorescence interference and tissue photodamage8,9.

At present, gallate nanomaterials doped with transition metal ions exhibit superior NIR persistent luminescence characteristics. This is attributed to their emission spectra predominantly falling within the NIR range, which can be precisely controlled and regulated. Additionally, the gallate host has remarkable stability and possesses a crystal structure characterized by unique antisite defects10,11. Zinc gallogermanate (ZnxGayGezOm, ZGGO) is an excellent PLNPs host material, which belongs to typical gallate spinel structure. Normally, Zn2+ occupies the tetrahedral site surrounded by four oxygen atoms, while Ga3+ occupies the octahedral site surrounded by six oxygen atoms. However, a series of antisite defects (Zn’Ga, GaoZn, Zn’Ga-GaoZn) formed by the exchange of approximately 3% of Zn2+ and Ga3+ positions are used to store charge carriers in persistent luminescence12. It is noteworthy that the Ge4+ ion introduced here can produce more antisite defects, leading to a shallower trap depth and an enhanced luminescence13,14. Therefore, ZGGO is an ideal host lattice for luminescence central ions.

The luminescence center plays a decisive role in the emission wavelength range of PLNPs. For the transition metal ions, the large Stokes shift characteristic between the d-electron transition energy levels result in the luminescence predominantly occurring in the NIR region. Additionally, due to the d-electron energy level splitting and the influence of crystal field environment, their NIR emission wavelength are broad and controllable, making these one of the key luminescence centers for NIR-PLNPs in the biomedicine15. In recent years, the optical properties of Cr3+, Mn2+, Fe3+ and Ni2+ ions within different hosts, such as gallate, have been extensively studied and explored as PLNPs materials16. Until now, Cr3+-doped gallate NIR-PLNPs have been the most potential optical probe for medical diagnostics12,17. Notably, Ni2+ ion as the luminescence center preferentially occupies the octahedral crystal site to form the NIR-II afterglow nanomaterials can achieve high quality imaging18,19. Specifically, our previous study demonstrated that ZGGO: Ni2+ nanoparticles possess the capability for deep tissue imaging within a depth range of 10 to 30 mm20. Co2+ ions exhibit a tetrahedral site preference in gallate-based and aluminate-based spinel structures, demonstrating photoluminescence attributable to tetrahedral coordination sites21–23. Nevertheless, the inherent anti-site disorder in spinel structures inevitably induces partial occupancy of octahedral sites by Co2+ ion24,25. Particularly in gallogermanates hosts, the enhanced antisite defects concentration introduces critical uncertainties regarding site occupation preference (tetrahedral vs. octahedral) and corresponding optical properties. Consequently, it is imperative to explore further their doping mechanisms and luminescence characteristics.

In this paper, NIR-PLNPs with Co2+ ions as the luminescence center (Zn2(1-x)Co2 + 2xGa3Ge0.75O8 (x = 0.001–0.05)) were successfully synthesized via hydrothermal synthesis. The microstructure, doping mechanism and luminescence properties of ZGGO: xCo2+(x = 0.001–0.05) nanoparticles were studied systematically. With the increase of Co2+ ions concentration, the average particle size of the spherical nanoparticles gradually decreased from 65.9 nm to 52.3 nm. The first principles were used to calculate the formation energy (ΔE) of Co2+ ions substitution tetrahedral and octahedral sites and the related traps. Based on the absorption and emission spectra, along with crystal field theory, the energy level transitions of Co2+ ions were systematically assigned to those occupying tetrahedral and octahedral sites in ZGGO. Furthermore, the relationship between the substitution sites of Co2+, their corresponding emissions and the crystal field strength (Dq/B) were elaborated in detail. The persistent luminescence property and the corresponding afterglow mechanism were investigated through afterglow decay curves and phosphorescence emission spectra. The relevant charge traps, which play a dominant role in persistent luminescence, were identified as GaoZn and Zn’Ga-GaoZn via the ΔE calculation.

Results and discussion

The XRD patterns of Zn2(1−x)Co2 + 2xGa3Ge0.75O8 (ZGGO:xCo2+) powders with different Co2+ concentrations (x = 0.001, 0.0025, 0.005, 0.01, 0.025, 0.05) exhibit excellent agreement with the standard XRD card for ZnGa2O4 (PDF#38-1240), as shown in Fig. 1a. This indicates that all Co2+-doped samples are have a spinel structure. According to the report, the ionic radii of Co2+ and Zn2+ are nearly identical and larger than that of Ga3+, regardless of whether they occupy tetrahedral or octahedral lattice sites, as listed in Table 126,27. Generally, the doped ions are prefer to substitute the host ions that have the same valence state, similar ionic radii or a comparable coordination environment28. The local magnification diagram XRD pattern of diffraction peak corresponding to the (311) plane shows a tendency to small shift towards a higher angles with increasing Co2+ concentration (Fig. 1b). This phenomenon is quantitatively explained by Bragg’s law: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:2\text{d}\text{s}\text{i}\text{n}{\uptheta\:}=\text{n}{\uplambda\:}$$\end{document}, where the ionic radius of Co2+ is slightly smaller than Zn2+, increasing Co²⁺ doping concentration leads to reduced lattice spacing (d) and the diffraction peak position moves towards a higher angle in the crystal plane29, indicating that Co2+ replace Zn2+ lattice site.

As the aforementioned, in the ZGGO host with spinel structure, majority of Zn2+ occupy the tetrahedral site, whereas a minor fraction (≤ 3%) of Zn2+ can occupy octahedral site through exchanging with Ga3+, as illustrated in Fig. 1c30,31. Meanwhile, in CoGa2O4 spinel system, Co2+ and Ga3+ ions occupy the tetrahedral and octahedral sites, forming a partially inverse structure expressed as: (CoηGa1−η)[CoηGa2−η]O4 (η: inversion parameter)32. Therefore, Co2+ ions may substitute for the normal sites (tetrahedral sites) and antisites (octahedral sites) of Zn2+ ions in ZGGO. To further investigate the replacement of Zn2+ ions in tetrahedral and antisite octahedral sites with Co2+ ions, the corresponding ΔE were calculated to be − 4.60 eV and − 3.03 eV, respectively, using the DFT calculation method. Both ΔE values were negative, indicating that Co2+ ions can readily substitute for Zn2+ at any site in the ZGGO host lattice.

To investigate the effects of Co2+ doping on the morphology of the particles, the SEM images of ZGGO: xCo2+ (x = 0-0.05) samples are presented in Fig. 2a-f, with inserted high-magnification TEM images to show finer surface features and grain boundaries. It is observed that as the Co2+ ion concentration increases, particle agglomeration is mitigated, and the size gradually decreases. The high-magnification TEM images reveal progressive homogenization of particles, with particle edges becoming blunted and some particles appearing spherical. Meanwhile, the particle diameters of the samples doped with different concentrations were measured from the SEM images, respectively. Each sample analyzed had more than 180 particles, and the particle size distribution was directly displayed using a particle size distribution histogram, as shown in Fig. 3a-f. The average particle size and corresponding standard deviation were analyzed using error-bar histograms, as shown in Fig. 3g and corresponding values are listed in Table 2. It is evident that the particle sizes of these samples are all within the nanoscale range, exhibiting a consistent decrease from 110.6 nm to 52.3 nm as the Co2+ ion doping concentration increases (x = 0-0.05), which may be attributed to the incorporation of Co2+ ions accelerate the nucleation rate and diminishes the crystal growth33. Furthermore, the standard deviation of the average particle size of ZGGO: Co2+ nanoparticles were also reduced after Co2+ doping, suggesting an improving uniformity of the nanoparticles. Particularly, when x = 0.01, the uniformity of ZGGO: xCo2+ nanoparticles are optimal. As previous studies, the smaller and uniform particle size can facilitate surface functionalization of nanoparticles and enhance their applicability on biomedicine34.

To investigate the optical properties of Co2+ in ZGGO nanoparticles and the relevant crystal field environment, the absorption spectra of ZGGO: xCo2+ (x = 0.001–0.05) nanoparticles are presented in Fig. 5a. For all samples, broad absorption peaks are observed in the ranges of 500–800 nm and 800–2000 nm, which result from the superposition of multiple peaks. Additionally, two weak absorption peaks are identified at 360 nm and 500 nm. Since the electronic transition of the d-orbital, such as that of Co2+, is significantly influenced by the crystal field environment, it can be inferred that the multi-peak superposition may result from the diversity of crystal field environments in which Co2+ ions are situated within ZGGO. As aforementioned discussions, Co2+ can occupy octahedral and tetrahedral sites in ZGGO host. The Tanabe-Sugano (T-S) energy level diagrams of d7 electron configuration associated with Co2+ occupying octahedral (d7-Oh) and tetrahedral sites (d7-Td) are shown in Fig. 5b and c36. As highlighted in the report by Rack et al., the crystal field strength of different sites within a single host can be evaluated by examining the bond lengths between anions and cations37. The detailed assignments of the absorption peaks and the calculated crystal field parameters are summarized in Table 3. Referencing the bond lengths of Zn-O (0.5) and Ga-O (0.2), and integrating insights from prior research, the absorption peaks centered at 360 nm (27778 cm− 1), 640 nm (15625 cm− 1) and 1452 nm (6887 cm− 1), can be ascribed to the spin-allowed electronic transition4T1(4F) → 2A1(2G), 4A2(4F), 4T2 (4F) of the Co2+ ions occupying the octahedral sites, respectively38–40. These peaks were designated as v1, v2 and v3. To clarify the photoluminescent energy level attribution of the Co2+ in tetrahedral and octahedral site, the excitation (λem = 685 nm) and emission spectra (λex = 555 nm) of ZGGO:xCo2+ (x = 0.001–0.05) nanoparticles are shown in Fig. 6(a and b). For all samples, a series of characteristic excitation peaks of Co2+ ions were detected, including weak peaks at 415 and 495 nm, as well as a strong broad band with multiple peaks (centered at 555, 595, and 625 nm). According to the T-S diagram(Fig. 5b, c), the peaks at 415 and 625 nm 4T1(4F) →2A1(2G) and 4T1(4P) of Co2+ occupying octahedral sites, and the peaks at 495, 555 and 595 nm are attributed to 4A2(4F)→2A1(2G), 4T1(4P) and 2E(2G) of Co2+ occupying tetrahedral sites, respectively22,45. Among them, the peak in the ultraviolet (UV) region has been extensively discussed in previous studies. For undoped Co2+ ions (ZGGO) nanoparticles, the excitation peak is located in the UV region, with emission peaks ranging from 442 to 480 nm, belongs to the self-activation center of the Ga-O group in the spinel lattices20,46. Therefore, the excitation peak at 280 nm in the doped system (ZGGO: xCo2+ (x = 0.001–0.05)) is related to the Ga-O charge transfer states in the ZGGO host. Moreover, the absorption peak of ZGGO: Co2+ overlaps with the emission peak of ZGGO nanoparticles, indicating persistent energy transfer from the host to the Co2+ ions35.

For the emission spectra (Fig. 6b), it can be seen that there is a strong peak in the range of 600–800 nm and an extremely weak peak in the 800–900 nm, both of which belong to the Co2+ ions characteristic peaks47,48. Both peaks exhibit asymmetric broadening that can be accurately deconvoluted into two Gaussian components. For the ZGGO:0.001Co2+, the strong emission peak centered at 685 nm (14598 cm− 1) was fitted with two sub-bands at 672 nm (14875 cm− 1, Peak 1) and 697 nm (14342 cm− 1, Peak 2), as shown in Fig. 7a. The weaker peak in the 800–900 nm range is not discussed in detail due to its negligible intensity. Based on the analysis of the crystal field intensity (Dq/B), it can be inferred the Peak 1 (14875 cm− 1) is assigned to the Co2+ ions in octahedral sites, corresponding to 4A2(4F)→4T1(4F) transitions. The Peak 2 (14342 cm− 1) can be attributed to spin-allowed 4T1(4P)→4A2(4F) transition of Co2+ in tetrahedral site within the ZGGO49,50. The integrated luminescence intensity ratio I1/I2 of the fitting Peak 1 and Peak 2 change with the Co2+ concentration, as shown in Fig. 7b. All of I1/I2 values were greater than 1, suggesting that Co2+ ions preferentially occupy the octahedral sites rather than the tetrahedral sites in ZGGO. This result is consistent with the findings by Akihiko and Naka et al., which are based on the occupancy refinement obtained from single-crystal XRD analysis of the CoGa2O4 spinel system51,52. Notably, I1/I2 decreases gradually as the Co2+ concentration increases from 0.001 to 0.05, indicating that the occupying ratio of Co2+ in tetrahedral sites also increased with the rising Co2+ concentration.

To investigate the relationship between persistent luminescence intensity and Co2+ concentrations, the afterglow decay curves of ZGGO:xCo2+ (x = 0.001–0.05) nanoparticles are presented in Fig. 8a. These curves were monitored at 685 nm following 5 min UV (254 nm) irradiation. It can be observed that the afterglow intensity decreases as the Co2+ doping concentration increases from 0.001 to 0.05. The afterglow duration exceeds 2 min. However, when x > 0.01, the afterglow signal becomes invisible, which may be attributed to luminescence quenching caused by the high concentration of Co2+ doping25. This concentration-dependent luminescence behavior follows the same excitation/emission spectral variation trends observed in the nanoparticles, as shown in Fig. 8b. For ZGGO:xCo2+ (x = 0.001–0.05) nanoparticles, the emission intensity initially peaks at a doping concentration of 0.001 and subsequently declines for x > 0.001 due to concentration quenching effects dominated by non-radiative decay processes22,53. In general, the quench type can be further classified based on the critical distance (Rc), where exchange interactions correspond to Rc < 5 Å, while multipolar interactions occur when Rc > 5 Å54. For ZGGO: Co2+, the RC between Co2+ ion can be roughly estimated using the equation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{C}\approx\:{2[3V/4\pi\:{X}_{C}N]}^{1/3}$$\end{document}, where V and N are volume and total number of replaceable cationic sites present per unit cell, respectively54,55. In the zinc gallate spinel structure (space group: Fd-3m), the unit cell volume is calculated as 579.01 Å3 through crystallographic refinement (Rietveld method). Since Co2+ ions substitute for Zn2+ cations, the total number of available substitution sites equals the Zn2+ site count (N = 8). And XC is the critical concentration of Co2+ ions in ZGGO, at which the luminescence intensity of ZGGO: Co2+ begins to decrease. Here, XC is 0.001. Consequently, the calculated Rc value of approximately 51.7 Å indicates that the corresponding luminescence quenching type is dominated by multipolar interactions. According to Van Uiter’s theory, the multipolar interaction can be determined by the change of emission intensity (I) with doping concentration(x). The slope of the lg(I/x) function was determined to be -1.68, which corresponds to (θ/3). Therefore, the value of θ is approximately 6, suggesting that the concentration quenching mechanism of ZGGO: Co2+ nanoparticles is attributed to dipole-dipole interaction.

To investigate the persistent luminescence mechanism of ZGGO: Co2+ nanoparticles, the defect energy levels of the ZGGO: Co2+ system were studied using first-principles calculations. By calculation, the ΔE of Zn’Ga-GaoZn, and GaoZn defects were − 0.55 and 1.88 eV, which are 2–10 times smaller than that of Zn’Ga, VZn’’, VGa’’’ and VO’’defects (4.90, 9.93, 14.78 and 10.30 eV), indicating that GaoZn and Zn’Ga-GaoZn defects are more easily formed in the ZGGO system58,59. Figure 10 shows the schematic diagram of the ZGGO: Co2+ persistent luminescence mechanism with thermal activation processes. Under UV irradiation, associated with the Ga-O charge transfer state, electrons in the valence band were excited to the conduction band, generating corresponding holes in the valence band. Subsequently, Co2+ ions at both tetrahedral and octahedral sites were excited via energy transfers resulting from the recombination of those electrons and holes. The excited electrons transitioned from the higher excited state levels of Co2+ ions, specifically 2A1(2G), into CB from via the thermal activation processes. These electrons were subsequently transported through the CB and eventually captured by traps, including GaoZn and/or Zn’Ga-GaoZn. After the UV light irradiation is stopped, the trap level gradually releases the captured electrons, enabling them to return to the conduction band (CB) and reach the excited state of the corresponding Co2+. Subsequently, these electrons return to the ground state via a radiative transition process, thereby generating near-infrared (NIR) persistent luminescence.

Conclusion

In summary, Zn2(1−x)Co2 + 2xGa3Ge0.75O8(x = 0.001–0.05) NIR persistent luminescence nanoparticles was successfully synthesized via hydrothermal synthesis. With the increase of Co2+ ions concentration, the average particle size of the nanoparticles gradually decreased from 65.9 nm to 52.3 nm, and the size gradually homogenized. According to the first principles, the ΔE values of Co2+ ion replaces the tetrahedral and antisite octahedral sites of Zn2+ ions were calculated to be − 4.60 and − 3.03 eV, respectively, indicating that Co2+ ions can readily substitute for Zn2+ at any site in the ZGGO host lattice. The crystal field intensities for Co2+ ions occupying the tetrahedral and octahedral sites are 0.45 and 1.08, respectively, which indicates Co2+ located in a relatively weak crystal field environment in ZGGO. The emission peaks of all samples are composed of strong peak near 685 nm and weak peak around 825 nm. The strong peak is fitted by two peaks located at 672 and 697 nm corresponding to the emission of Co2+ ions occupying octahedral and tetrahedral sites, respectively. And the occupying ratio of Co2+ in tetrahedral sites also increased with the rising Co2+ concentration. The NIR persistent luminescence time exceeds 2 min, which contribution is mainly from Co2+ ions located at the octahedral sites and related defects, specifically GaoZn and Zn’Ga-GaoZn defects. These findings are significant in facilitating a deeper understanding of the doping and luminescence mechanisms of spinel gallate-based persistent luminescence materials.

Methods

A series of ZGGO:xCo2+ (x = 0.001, 0.0025, 0.005, 0.01, 0.025, 0.05) () persistent luminescence nanoparticles (PLNPs) were prepared via the hydrothermal method. Firstly, the raw materials of Zn(CH3COO)2 (99.99%, Macklin), Ga(NO3)3·xH2O (99.99%, Aladdin), GeCl4 (99.99%, Alfa Aesar) and Co(NO3)2·6H2O (99.99%, Aladdin) were dissolved into 15 mL deionized water according to the proportion of the chemical formula. The pH of mixed solution was tuned to 8.0 by adding NaOH. Then the final solution was transferred into 50 ml Teflon-lined steel autoclave and heated at 180 ℃ for 5 h. After the cooling down to room-temperature, the precipitates were taken out and washed three times by using deionized water and dried at 60 ℃ for 12 h. The dried powder was sintered for 60 min at 800 ℃ in vacuum (pressure: 5.5 × 10− 4 Pa) with 5 ℃/min heating. The final ZGGO:xCo2+ PLNPs were obtained.

The crystal structure the samples were analysed by using an X-ray diffractometer (D/MAX-2500, Rigaku) with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). Their morphology, size, energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectrum and elemental composition were determined and analyzed by Scanning electron microscope (SEM, The Quanta FEG) and transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-F200). Their absorption spectra were acquired using a UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Jasco V770) equipped with an integrating sphere. Emission, excitation and afterglow decay curves of the samples were acquired using a RF-5301pc equipped with R928 photomultipliers. Luminescence decay curves were detected by a spectrophotometer (Zolix, Omin l-300).

The first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed via the CASTEP module of Materials Studio package. The exchange-correlation potential for the electrons was the Perdew-Wang 91 (PW91) functionals using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). In the 2 × 2 × 2 supercell (Zn16Ga32O64), one Co atom replaced for Zn atom, which locates in tetrahedral and octahedral site.
