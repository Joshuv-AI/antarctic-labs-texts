Development of a humanized anti-fibrin monoclonal antibody for the treatment of neuroinflammatory and retinal diseases
Authors: Aaron B. Kantor, Mathias Rickert, Hank Cheng, Kenneth Flanagan, Vasudha Salgotra, Anjana Suppahia, Jae Kyu Ryu, Paul F. Widboom, Joseph R. Warfield, Katerina Akassoglou, Jeffrey B. Stavenhagen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vascular dysfunction and subsequent innate immune activation are key players of neurodegenerative, retinal, and inflammatory diseases, including Alzheimer's disease (AD), multiple sclerosis (MS), diabetic retinopathy (DR), and age-related macular degeneration (AMD). At sites of vascular damage, conversion of the blood coagulation protein fibrinogen to fibrin exposes a cryptic inflammatory epitope, γ377–395, which can bind CD11b/CD18 and CD11c/CD18 complement receptors on microglia, macrophages, and dendritic cells. Genetic targeting of the fibrin γ377–395 epitope or its pharmacologic inhibition with the mouse monoclonal antibody 5B8 protects from inflammation and neurodegeneration in AD and MS mouse models. Here, we present the development of THN391, a first-in-class humanized antibody, to neutralize fibrin toxicity without adverse anticoagulant effects for the treatment of neurodegenerative, retinal, and inflammatory diseases. THN391 was affinity matured with 100-fold greater affinity than 5B8, engineered to lack Fc effector function, and have improved developability properties for clinical use. THN391 blocks the interaction of fibrin with CD11b/c and does not bind fibrinogen nor interfere with coagulation, consistent with the crystal structure of its binding interface to the γ377–395 epitope. THN391 and its Fc wild-type counterpart THN313 showed preclinical efficacy in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) mouse models of MS and in a rodent model of retinal disease. Both THN391 and THN313 reduced demyelination, inflammatory foci, and clinical scores in EAE, demonstrating that anti-fibrin γ377-395 antibodies function as pure antagonists, blocking fibrin from activating CD11b/c complement receptors. THN391 was as effective as the standard of care vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) antagonists in reducing laser-induced neovascular lesions in a rat model of neovascular macular degeneration. Taken together, these results support the clinical development of THN391 for neurological diseases and ophthalmic indications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03650-w.

Introduction

Fibrin deposition and impaired fibrinolysis have long been implicated as drivers of chronic inflammation following the loss of vascular integrity in many central nervous system (CNS), retinal, and peripheral inflammatory diseases. Dysregulation of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and blood-retinal-barrier (BRB) are early events that allow fibrinogen to enter the CNS, where it is converted to insoluble fibrin by activation of the coagulation cascade [1, 2]. Fibrin deposits and levels of fibrinogen in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) increase with disease severity in patents with dementia and neurological diseases [3–5]. In multiple sclerosis (MS), fibrin deposits are co-localized with microglia and can be detected early in the brains of both relapsing–remitting and progressive patients and persist throughout the course of disease [6–9]. In diabetic retinopathy (DR), diabetic macular edema (DME), and age-related macular degeneration (AMD), vascular leakage leads to elevated fibrinogen in the vitreous humor [10–12] and fibrin accumulation in hard exudates and the retina [13, 14]. Loss of vascular integrity, as well as transcriptional responses of microglia and astrocytes, are shared among AMD, AD, and MS, suggesting co-pathologies of these inflammatory neurodegenerative diseases that may be amenable to common interventions [15].

Genetic and pharmacology studies have established fibrin as a global activator of pathogenic immune responses in neurologic and peripheral diseases with vascular damage [2, 3, 16]. Fibrinogen conversion to fibrin exposes a cryptic amino acid sequence in the fibrinogen γ chain, γ377–395, which binds to the αI-domain of CD11b/CD18 (CR3, Mac-1, αMβ2) and CD11c/CD18 (CR4, αXβ2) on microglia, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells [2, 3, 17]. Fibrin-CD11b/c signaling triggers an inflammatory and pro-oxidant response that promotes leukocyte recruitment and neurodegeneration [3, 18–20]. Transcriptome and phosphoproteome profiling revealed a fibrin-specific gene signature of neurotoxic innate immune genes and signaling cascades involved in neurodegeneration, leukocyte recruitment, and oxidative stress [21]. Injection of plasma or fibrinogen into the corpus callosum of healthy mice induces neuroinflammation, as shown by robust microglial transcriptional changes, macrophage infiltration, and demyelination [21, 22]. In contrast, injection of plasma derived from Fga−/− or Fggγ390–396A mice, which express a fully coagulable fibrinogen that specifically lacks the CD11b/c receptor binding site, largely reverses blood-induced microglia gene signatures and protects from demyelination and neuroinflammation [21, 22], suggesting that fibrin-CD11b/c signaling is a key culprit in the blood inducing neuroinflammation.

Genetic elimination of the fibrin inflammatory domain in Fggγ390–396A mice is protective in preclinical models of MS, AD, traumatic brain injury, and COVID-19 [19–25]. These results are consistent with protection or resolution of neurological pathology in animal models of MS [24–26], AD [27] and DR [28] by use of ancrod, the snake venom enzyme that depletes fibrinogen. In the 5XFAD mouse model of AD, crossing with Fggγ390–396A mice reduces expression of microglial lipid metabolism and neurodegenerative gene signatures and protects from neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment [20, 29, 30]. Fggγ390–396A mice subjected to the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model of MS have reduced clinical severity, microglia activation, and axonal damage [21, 24, 25]. Furthermore, Fggγ390–396A mice are protected in peripheral models of inflammation, including rheumatoid arthritis, Duchene muscular dystrophy, colitis, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), periodontitis, and COVID-19 [17–19, 31, 32]. Together, these results suggest that targeting fibrin's inflammatory domain could selectively neutralize toxic immune responses in a wide range of diseases with vascular damage. While anticoagulants can inhibit fibrin formation, they can also increase the risk of bleeding. A safe therapeutic to neutralize fibrin toxicity in humans without adverse anticoagulant effects could be highly impactful for treatment of a range of neurodegenerative, retinal, and inflammatory diseases.

To this end, Ryu et al. generated mouse MAb 5B8, which selectively targets the cryptic fibrin γ377–395 inflammatory epitope without adverse anticoagulant effects [33]. The antibody can enter and engage the target in the CNS and is effective in treating rodent models of AD, MS, and COVID-19 neuropathology [19, 33]. MAb 5B8 selectively inhibits fibrin-induced microglia activation and expression of neurodegenerative and oxidative stress genes in the cortex of AD mice without global inactivation of innate immune responses [2, 33]. These properties support the development of a humanized anti-fibrin MAb to treat neurodegenerative diseases.

Here, we report the successful development and preclinical efficacy of THN391, a first-in-class humanized monoclonal antibody that selectively targets the fibrin γ377–395 inflammatory epitope. Engineered to lack Fc effector function, THN391 blocked fibrin-induced activation of CD11b/c complement receptors similar to its wild-type (WT) Fc counterpart. THN391 binds with high affinity and specificity, and demonstrates preclinical efficacy, offering the potential to treat neurodegenerative, retinal, and inflammatory diseases without affecting coagulation. Notably, we also demonstrate for the first time that an anti-fibrin antibody provides protection against neuroinflammation in the eye.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6 mice, SJL/J mice, and 5XFAD (MMRRC 034840-JAX-WT) mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. 6- to 8-week-old Brown Norway rats were obtained from Charles River Laboratories. Mice were housed in groups of five under standard vivarium conditions and a 12 h light–12 h dark cycle. EAE and fibrinogen-induced encephalomyelitis (FIE) animal protocols were approved by the Committee of Animal Research at the University of California, San Francisco, and were in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines. All ophthalmology experiments involving animals were approved by the Vanderbilt University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and performed in accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

In silico design and functional characterization of humanized 5B8

Humanization of 5B8 was conducted by characterizing a panel of humanization designs produced in yeast. Designs were generated by grafting CDR mouse sequences into human framework sequences that had been in silico predicted to be the most compatible with the original mouse frameworks. Based on 5B8, 27 antibodies representing combinations of 9 humanized VH and 3 humanized Vκ were produced, expressed in yeast, and characterized for expression and fibrin γ377–395 peptide binding.

Combinatorial diversification and selection of antibody variants

Antibody optimization was performed by introducing diversities into the HC and LC variable regions. Oligonucleotides comprised either the CDRH1, CDRH2, or CDRH3 and a flanking region on either side of the CDR. Amino acid positions in the CDRs were variegated via NNK diversity, introduced into the CDR oligos. The DNA of the HC variable region was then DNase treated to create fragments of 50–200 bps in size. The CDRH1, CDRH2, and CDRH3 oligos were then recombined via overlap extension PCR to incorporate the CDR diversity oligos into the HC variable region sequence. The library was then created by transforming this diversified HC variable sequence and the HC expression vector into yeast already containing the LC plasmid of the parent. A similar process was performed to introduce diversity in CDRL1, CDRL2, and CDRL3. These diversified LC variable regions and the LC expression vector were transformed into yeast, already containing the HC plasmid of the parent. Additional sets of libraries were built focusing on diversity exclusively within the CDRH3. Walking singlet diversity was introduced into the CDRH3 by overlap extension PCR between VH FRI through FR3 and an oligonucleotide with diversity in the CDRH3. Selections based on expression and fibrin γ377–395 peptide antigen binding were performed by FACS sorting (FACS Aria, BD Biosciences) for three rounds and described previously [34]. After the final round, yeast were plated and individual colonies picked for characterization.

Binding, biophysical profiling and crystallography of antibody clones

Clone characterization included ELISA using fibrin γ377–395 peptide, fibrinogen, and fibrin as capture reagents; clot lysis assays to assess anticoagulant activity; biolayer interferometry (BLI) to measure binding kinetics; hydrophobic interaction chromatography for biophysical profiling; and polyspecificity assays to evaluate off-target reactivity. Fab THN313/fibrinogen gamma chain peptide γ377–395 co-crystals were produced at Charles River Laboratories by standard hanging drop method of vapor diffusion, dataset were collected at the Swiss Light Source (SLS) beam line X06DA PXIII and processed by MOSFLM and AIMLESS (CCP4).

Binding assay for fibrin, fibrinogen, and γ377–395 peptide

Ligand binding ELISA was used to assess the binding of 5B8, THN313, and THN391 to fibrin, fibrinogen, and γ377–395 peptide in a standard ELISA format. Streptavidin 96-well microplates were coated with biotinylated human fibrin, fibrinogen (Molecular Innovations, Novi Mi, # FIB-S and #FIB-BIO) and γ377–395 (Biotin-YSMKKTTMKIIPFNRLTIG Elim Biopharmaceuticals, Hayward, CA). Samples containing varying amounts of THN313 and THN391 were added and bound THN313 and THN391 were detected with a commercially available HRP enzyme conjugated polyclonal anti human IgG antibody.

Biolayer interferometry (BLI)

Biolayer interferometry (BLI) was conducted on the ForteBio Octet RED384.4 for clone screening and the Gator Prime (Palo Alto, CA) for further characterization. Streptavidin sensors and captured biotinylated fibrinogen and fibrin γ377–395 were used to evaluate antibody binding.

CD11 αI-domain blocking assay

CD11 αI-domain blocking was evaluated by ELISA. Streptavidin-coated High-Capacity Plates (Pierce, #15,503) were prepared with biotinylated N-terminal fibrin γ377–395 peptide (Elim Bio) and blocked (Superblock, Sigma Aldrich, #37,515). Recombinant CD11c αI-domain protein (Genescript, Piscataway, NJ) was added to the plate with varying amounts of THN313 and THN391. CD11c capture was detected with anti-CD11c (ThermoFisher, H00003687-M03) labeled with HRP (Ab102890- Abcam) and detected by fluorescence (ThermoFisher, #15,169).

Activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)

THN313 and THN391 were evaluated with commercial aPTT reagents. THN313 and THN391, were incubated with normal pooled human plasma (Innovative Research) at 37 °C for 2 h. This was followed by further incubation with clot activation agents, 20 mM CaCl2 and APTT-XL (Ellagic Acid Activator, ThermoFisher 100402TS). Normal and abnormal coagulation controls (ThermoFisher, 100595TS, 100596TS, 100597TS) were included in the assay evaluation. Clotting time was monitored spectrophotometrically at 405 nm in real time.

Thromboelastography (TEG)

TEG assessments were conducted by Prolytix (Essex Junction, VT) on behalf of Therini Bio using the TEG 5000 Analyzer (Haemoscope Corporation, Niles, IL). THN391 and an isotype IgG control were titrated (100, 50, 25, and 0 μg/mL) into samples of fresh citrated whole blood from 8 healthy donors prior to initiation with Kaolin and analysis in the TEG instrument in the presence of tissue-type PLG activator. Assays were done in duplicate. Four standard TEG measurements were reported: 1) R—time to initial fibrin deposition, which is analogous to clot time; 2) K—time until clot strength reaches a fixed value of 20 mm, which is a kinetic rate measurement; 3) MA—Maximum amplitude, which depends highly on platelet content; and, 4) CLT -clot lysis time, which is similar in concept to the R time, but indicative of fibrin breakdown until no resistance is observed.

Biodistribution of [89Zr]DFO*-labeled THN391

Biodistribution of [89Zr]DFO*-labeled THN391 was measured by post-mortem tissue gamma counting in the MOG EAE mouse model (Invicro Needham, MA). Animals were enrolled on Day 15 post-EAE-induction, with a clinical score of 2–2.5. Mice were given a single 10 mg/kg IV dose of [89Zr]THN391. The animals were humanely euthanized via exsanguination and transcardially perfused with saline. The radiolabeled drug was measured in the CNS, plasma, and other tissues.

THN392 target engagement in 5XFAD mice

5XFAD mice and age-matched non-transgenic controls were given IP injection of 30 mg/kg of biotinylated THN392 every 2 days. Mice were anesthetized with avertin, perfused with saline, and their brains rapidly removed. Brains were bisected through the midline and processed for fresh frozen sections and immunohistochemical analysis as described [33].

Fibrinogen-induced encephalomyelitis (FIE)

C57BL/6 mice were anaesthetized with avertin and placed in a stereotactic apparatus. Plasminogen-free fibrinogen (Calbiochem) was dissolved in endotoxin-free distilled water (HyClone), diluted to 5 mg ml − 1 with artificial cerebral spinal fluid (aCSF). Fibrinogen (1 μL of 5 mg ml − 1) or aCSF, was slowly injected (0.3 μL min − 1) with a 10-μL Hamilton syringe attached to a 33 gauge needle into the brain corpus callosum at coordinates: anteroposterior, − 1.0 mm; mediolateral, − 0.7 mm; dorsoventral, − 1.325 mm from the bregma, as described [22]. THN313 at 10 and 30 mg/kg or isotype control at 30 mg/kg was administered by retro-orbital IV injection 6 h after fibrinogen introduction. On day 3, mice were transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS under avertin anesthesia. Brains were removed, post-fixed, and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in PBS for cryostat sectioning and immunohistochemistry.

EAE

EAE was induced in 9–10-week-old female SJL/J mice (Jackson Laboratory, #:000686) by subcutaneous immunization with 60 µg myelin proteolipid protein (PLP)139–151 (HSLGKWLGHPDKF; Auspep) in complete Freund's adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, F5506) supplemented with 400 µg of heat-inactivated Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra (Difco Laboratories). Two days after immunization, mice were given IP injection of 5 ng pertussis toxin (Sigma-Aldrich, P7208). In some experiments, EAE was induced with 60 µg myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG)35–55 (MEVGWYRSPFSRVVHLYRDGK; Auspep) in complete Freund's adjuvant in 9–15-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Mice were observed daily and EAE disability was scored by observers blinded to treatment: 0) no symptoms; 1) loss of tail tone; 2) ataxia; 3) hindlimb paralysis; 4) hindlimb and forelimb paralysis; 5) moribund. Mice were administered intraperitoneally with THN313, THN391, or isotype-matched control human IgG1 every twice-a-week starting on day 0 as prophylactic treatment. Dexamethasone was administered IP as a positive control at 1 mg/kg daily. Experiments were completed in-house and at Hooke Laboratories (Lawrence, MA).

Laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (LCNV) model and intravitreal treatment

Laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (LCNV) studies were conducted at VO-CRO (Nashville, TN). LCNV was generated in 6- to 8-week-old male Brown Norway rats on experiment day 0 (D0). Rats were anesthetized with an IP injection of ketamine/xylazine, and 1% tropicamide was applied for pupil dilation. Using a hand-held coverslip as a contact lens and GenTeal lubricating eye gel as a medium contacting the coverslip to the surface of the cornea, a Nidek GYC-500 green laser photocoagulator coupled to a Nidek SL-1800 slit-lamp was used to create six lesions equidistant from the optic nerve head in the retinal midperiphery. Laser parameters include: 532 nm wavelength, 100 μm spot size, 0.1 s duration, and 120 mW. On day 1, treatments were administered via intravitreal injection. Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane and topical analgesia was provided with proparacaine. 2.0μL injections were given using a short barrel, 5μL syringe with a custom-made, detachable 33-gauge needle under an operating microscope. After injection, neomycin/polymyxin B/gramicidin ophthalmic drops were applied to prevent infection.

Quantitative fluorescein angiography (qFA)

Lesion permeability was assessed via qFA [35]. Rats were anesthetized as above, and 10% sodium fluorescein administered via IP injection at a dose of 1μL/g bodyweight. Fluorescent fundus images were captured for one eye of each rat with a Micron IV imaging system at four minutes post-fluorescein injection and again two minutes later. ImageJ software was used by a blinded observer to quantify the fluorescence intensity for one lesion per eye using the "integrated density" function. The difference in integrated density between 4- and 6-min post-injection was recorded as a readout of vascular leakage.

CNV lesion area and histology

Rats were euthanized and their eyes enucleated. The extent of CNV at the Bruch's membrane rupture sites was measured by masked observers using computer-assisted image analysis of FITC-conjugated isolectin-B4-stained choroidal flat-mounts [36]. As the treatments were administered bilaterally, the areas for all lesions in both eyes of each rat were averaged and reported as a single data point. Statistical analyses of qFA, and CNV area measurements were performed by ANOVA and a parametric post hoc test.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism (Version 10.0). Data are represented as dots for individual samples (e.g., mouse, eye) and mean ± 95% CI. Sample sizes were based on previous experiments and were not predetermined by statistical methods. Across experiments, observers were blinded for physical, bioanalytical, and image assessments. Animals were randomized with respect to treatment across all experiments. Data normality was assessed by consensus results of the D'Agostino-Pearson, Shapiro–Wilk, Anderson-Darlin, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests in GraphPad Prism. Multiple comparisons use one-way, two-way ANOVA or mixed effect analysis with Tukey or Dunnett's corrections. Fisher's exact test was used for categorical data, and details are given in the individual figures. EAE and biochemical studies performed in the Akassoglou lab were independently validated by Therini Bio and Hooke Laboratories.

Results

Development and characterization of a high-affinity humanized anti-fibrin therapeutic antibody

To generate a lead clinical candidate with low probability of eliciting an immune response in patients, we produced affinity-matured humanized versions of mouse MAb 5B8 by CDR grafting humanized VH and Vκ frameworks [37, 38] predicted to be the most compatible to the original mouse frameworks and minimize its immunogenic potential (Fig. 1A). MAb 5B8 was chosen based on its selective recognition of the fibrin anti-inflammatory domain and pre-clinical efficacy [33]. Humanized recombinant variants were expressed in yeast and selected for affinity maturation and optimization using the Adimab yeast platform to increase affinity to the fibrin γ377–395 inflammatory epitope and improve biophysical properties to support robust manufacturability [39, 40]. Based on developability and binding profile, we conducted pharmacological studies with both THN313 and its Fc null effector counterpart THN391, which contains two point mutations in the CH2 portion of the Fc domain, L234A/L235A (aka LALA), to minimize FcγR and C1q binding [41]. The anti-fibrin γ377–395 monoclonal antibodies discussed in this paper are listed in Table 1. The preclinical experiments presented include evaluation of four anti-fibrin γ377–395 monoclonal antibody variants with the identical CDR variable regions: THN313 and THN391, with humanized WT-Fc and LALA-Fc, respectively and THN392 and THN393, with murine WT- and LALA-Fc, respectively (Table 1). Anti-Fibrin γ377–395 Monoclonal Antibodies

THN313 and THN391 showed sub-nanomolar avidity (bivalent) KD for fibrin γ377–395, which is increased 100-fold from the parent murine antibody 5B8 (Fig. 1B, Fig. S1, Table 2). THN313/THN391 Fab had a monovalent affinity of 24 nM to fibrin γ377–395 as measured by biolayer interferometry (BLI) and ELISA (in Fig. 1B and Table 2). THN313 bound equally well to mouse and rat γ377–395, which differs by 2 amino acids from the human sequence, supporting its use in rodent models (Table S1). Like the parent antibody, THN313 and THN391 binding to fibrinogen was undetectable by BLI and ELISA assays (KD > 1 μM, Table 2, Fig. 2A). Developability was evaluated using polyspecific reactivity (PSR) and hydrophobicity interacting column (HIC) binding profiles [39, 42]. THN313 had the best hydrophobicity retention time (10.2 min) among all humanized antibody candidates and was much improved over 5B8 (12.7 min). THN313 also had an excellent polyspecific reactivity score (Fig. S2A). These features predict good developability and support a final clinical product that is stable at 100 mg/mL. The affinity matured antibodies did not interfere with fibrin polymerization or fibrinolysis in the screening assay (Fig. S2B). We next evaluated the ability of THN313 and THN391 to compete and block the binding of CD11c to plates coated with fibrin γ377–395 peptide. Binding was quantified using labeled antibodies specific to CD11c or CD11b. Both THN313 and THN391 blocked the binding of the CD11c αI-domain in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1D). Next, we co-crystalized Fab THN313 and fibrin γ377–395 peptide, and solved a model of the complex crystal structure with an overall resolution of 1.5 Å (Fig. 1C and Fig. S3). The fibrin γ377–395 peptide binds at an approximately 45-degree angle relative to the binding groove of the antibody Fab molecule with the C-terminus of the peptide reaching deep into the Fab binding pocket (Fig. S2). Only the 12 residue C-terminal portion of γ377–395 (residues M384-G395) were resolved in the crystal. Twenty-one Fab residues (14 VH and 7 Vκ) interacted with ten residues 384-MKIIPFNRLTIG-395 within the γ377–395 peptide, which include the NRLTIG binding site of fibrin to the CD11b I-domain [43]. The N-terminal portion of the peptide (Y377 to T383) could not be refined and is missing in the THN313 Fab/peptide co-structure. Multiple hydrogen bonds were identified with a distance between 2.7–3.3 Å. Six peptide amino acids took part in 7 interfacial hydrogen bonds with the Fab: 4 peptide amino acids form 5 hydrogen bonds with 5 VH amino acids (1 CDR1, 2 CDR2, 2 CDR3) and 2 peptide amino acids form 2 hydrogen bonds with 2 Vκ amino acids (1 CDR1, 1 FR3). Two of the hydrogen bonds were part of an electrostatic interaction between the side chains of peptide K411 and THN313 D52/D54. Besides interfacial hydrogen bonds, there were many hydrophobic interactions between γ377–395 and THN313, especially involving the side-chains of γ377–395 L418, T419, and I420, all of which were buried at the interface. THN313 side-chains that interact with the hydrophobic side-chains of the peptide include VH W33, V37, L50, Y56, A93, K96, W103, and VL L94, L96, F98. The presence of buried hydrophobic side chains agreed with the analysis of known protein-peptide complexes in which binding affinity depends in part on maximizing the hydrophobic surface area in the buried interface [44], with the noted importance of hydrophobic and aromatic residues in antigenicity [45] and with the prevalence of complementary hydrophobic and aromatic residues in the binding sites of antibodies [46]. Together, these results identify a high affinity binding interface between antibody and the fibrin inflammatory epitope.

Immunoglobulin pharmacologic properties depend highly on the Fc region. For example, Fc interactions with C1q initiate complement activation and binding to various Fc receptors, which induce antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent cell-mediated phagocytosis [41]. Fc engineering can thus clinically affect the therapeutic windows of safety and the efficacy of therapeutic antibodies [41, 47, 48]. Using the LALA mutation in the THN313 CH2 constant region, THN391 was engineered to be an antagonist, lacking antibody-dependent effector function. Humanized fibrin-targeting antibody does not affect fibrin polymerization and blood coagulation

We evaluated multiple parameters of blood clotting to determine if either THN313 or THN391 interfere with the ability of fibrinogen to mediate coagulation. THN313 and THN391 did not affect activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), which began at the same time (12 s) for plasma samples containing THN313, THN391, or no antibody (Fig. 2B). Thromboelastography (TEG) is a well-accepted method for evaluating the viscoelastic properties of a blood sample during clotting under low shear stress. Using this method, we assessed platelet function, clot strength, and fibrinolysis following titration of THN391 and an isotype IgG control up to 100 µg/mL into samples of whole blood from 8 healthy donors. THN391 had no impact on the kinetics of fibrin deposition, clot strength, maximum clot amplitude, which depends highly on platelet content; or clot lysis time (Fig. 2C). Taken together, these results demonstrate that THN391 does not interfere with normal blood clotting function, a critical goal in its development.

Humanized fibrin-targeting antibodies enter the brain in two different mouse models of neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration

We evaluated the whole-body uptake and biodistribution of THN391 labeled with [89Zr]DFO* by post-mortem tissue gamma counting in the MOG-EAE mouse model. Mice were given a single 10 mg/kg IV dose of [89Zr]THN391 on day 15, when the clinical score was 2–2.5. Multiple perfused tissues were evaluated for administered drug from 0.25–72 h. We observed minimal uptake of drug across all tissues, with whole blood maintaining the highest concentration of THN391 (Fig. 3B). The perfused brain-to-plasma ratio at 72 h post injection was 1.2% and the lumbar spinal cord-to-plasma ratio was 5.3%. These are higher than the levels for IgG of ~ 0.1–0.4% for intact WT brain [49, 50] and consistent with increased BBB permeability in EAE models [25, 51]. Similar levels of antibody and lumbar spinal cord to brain ratio is observed with a [89Zr] anti-CD20 in MOG-EAE [52].

Next, we characterized THN392 (mouse chimeric THN313) uptake in the brain of 5XFAD mice, which model features of AD including neuroinflammation and fibrin deposition [20, 33]. Biotinylated THN392 was administered intraperitoneally in 15-month-old 5XFAD or WT mice. In brain tissue sections, THN392 spatially correlated with fibrin(ogen)-rich areas in both the cortex and hippocampus of 5XFAD mice, suggesting engagement of the target (Fig. 3C). THN392 was not detected in the parenchymal area of the cortex and hippocampus in aged-matched littermate controls (Fig. 3C).

In dose–response studies of 0.2, 1, and 5 mg/kg given twice-a-week in PLP-EAE, THN313 was maximally effective at 5 mg/kg, with complete prevention of EAE-induced hindlimb paralysis (peak EAE score ≥ 3) (Fig. 4C). Treatment with 1 or 5 mg/kg THN313 significantly reduced demyelination in the spinal cord as determined by staining for myelin basic protein (Fig. 4D, Fig, S6B). THN313 at 5 mg/kg significantly reduced infiltrating Mac-2+ macrophages and Iba-1+ microglia in the spinal cord (Fig. 4E,F). Postmortem analysis of MOG-EAE spinal cord tissue at peak disease showed target engagement, with IHC staining positive for THN313 in EAE mice, but not healthy controls. Neither isotype control nor secondary antibody alone stained any tissue (Fig. S5). These results suggests that THN313 at low therapeutic dose protects from inflammatory demyelination in EAE.

In previous studies, MAb 5B8, a mouse IgG2b with antibody-dependent effector functions, was therapeutic in MS models [33]. To evaluate whether antibody effector function was required for efficacy, we compared two versions of our lead candidate, namely THN313 WT-Fc with effector function and THN391 null-effector function. THN391 significantly reduced the clinical score in EAE at 10 mg/kg similar to THN313, suggesting that effector function is not required for antibody efficacy (Fig. 4G). Treatment showed significant and consistent improvements in inflammatory foci (Fig. 4H), and reduction in infiltrating CD11b+ macrophages (Fig. 4I) and CD4+ T cells in the spinal cord lesions (Fig. 4J). Similar levels of efficacy were seen following treatment with either THN313 or THN391, further supporting that anti-fibrin γ377–395 antibodies function as pure antagonists, blocking fibrin from activating CD11b/CD18 and CD11c/CD18 receptors on the surface of innate immune cells.

Discussion

Blood leakage into the CNS and retina creates a toxic inflammatory environment. Fibrin deposits at sites of vascular damage induce microglia to express a set of harmful genes associated with oxidative stress and neurodegeneration, destroying neurons in both AD and MS [2, 21]. Binding of the fibrin inflammatory epitope, γ377–395, to CD11b/CD18 and CD11c/CD18 on microglia and other innate immune cells is necessary and sufficient to trigger neurotoxic inflammation [16, 17]. Here, we describe THN391, the first therapeutic candidate to block these detrimental effects of fibrin. THN391 is a fully humanized monoclonal IgG1 antibody derived from mouse MAb 5B8 [33]. CDR regions were transplanted into human framework regions to maintain target specificity and minimize immunogenicity potential. THN391 is affinity matured and selected for sub-nanomolar binding affinity and superior developability properties. The THN391 Fc region was engineered with the L234A/L235A (LALA) mutation to remove FcγR and C1q binding [55], rendering it a pure antagonist incapable of mediating antibody effector functions. In the EAE model of MS, THN391 and wild-type THN313 were equally effective in improving clinical score, reducing inflammatory foci, and reducing infiltrating CD11b + macrophages and CD4 + T cells in the spinal cord, demonstrating that the fibrin antibody functions as a pure antagonists and does not require effector function for its therapeutic efficacy. A pure antagonist may offer a safety advantage relative to existing treatments that require full effector function for therapeutic efficacy, such as anti-Aβ MAbs. Thus, we selected THN391 for further clinical development due to its optimal safety and efficacy profile. Unlike broadly immunosuppressive agents, this novel approach selectively restricts harmful local immune responses without suppressing the body's immune system and its ability to fight infection and other disease. Thus, fibrin-targeting immunotherapy with THN391 offers a promising and selective treatment for multiple indications by suppressing chronic inflammation at sites of increased vascular permeability in neurological and retinal diseases.

Anti-Aβ MAbs are currently the leading disease-modifying therapies for AD. Passive immunotherapy with aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab all lower Aβ burden, but these agents are only partially effective in slowing cognitive decline [56]. In animal models of AD, fibrin-induced microglial activation leads to dendritic spine elimination, neuronal loss, and subsequent cognitive decline independent of Aβ plaques. Fibrin induces a gene expression signature in microglia characteristic of oxidative stress, neurodegeneration, and generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [21, 33]. Genetic removal of the fibrin inflammatory epitope and treatment with fibrin γ377–395 targeting antibodies restores a favorable homeostatic gene signature and protects from cognitive impairment [20, 21, 30, 33]. Thus, blocking fibrin-mediated microglia activation and neuronal damage with antibodies to the fibrin inflammatory epitope may be an effective orthogonal approach to anti-Aβ therapy for AD.

AD patients treated with anti-Aβ MAbs have a significant risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) which reflect brain edema, microbleeds and occasionally large brain bleeds, and which have been associated with some deaths and morbidity [56]. It is hypothesized that anti-Aβ ARIA risk may be caused or exacerbated by either FcγR microglia activation and macrophage recruitment and/or C1q activation of the classical complement pathway [57, 58]. In THN391, the LALA modification reduces both FcγR and C1q binding, which should confer a reduced relative risk of ARIA. Thus, THN391 may offer an essential treatment approach to slow the decline of cognitive impairment in AD with potentially reduced ARIA risk, particularly when given in combination with anti-Aβ therapy.

There are over 20 approved disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) for the treatment of relapsing–remitting MS, but progressive MS remain an unmet clinical need [59]. Established DMTs have a broad range of mechanisms-of-action, primarily targeting adaptive immunity. The progressive phases of MS are dominated by neurodegeneration and compartmentalized innate immune responses in intrathecal inflammatory niches [60]. However, although microglia and macrophages are central to MS pathogenesis and progression, they have not been targeted with current DMTs. Fibrin is extensively deposited in the progressive MS brain and is associated with intrathecal inflammation and reduced neuronal density [7, 9]. Results presented here with THN391 together with previous EAE studies in Fggγ390–396A mice or 5B8-treated mice [21, 24, 25, 33] suggest that fibrin immunotherapy may be effective in stopping MS progression.

Evidence presented here shows for the first time that anti-fibrin therapy is effective in animal models of retinal disease. For the last two decades, VEGF antagonists have been the mainstay of treatment for retinal vascular diseases, most notably DR, DME, and AMD. Patients failing to respond immediately, over time, or requiring frequent treatment with intravitreal injections represent a significant unmet need. A subset (15–40%) of eyes do not respond or only partially respond to VEGF therapies [61]. Even if a patient responds initially, they may still progress to atrophy and/or fibrosis [62–64], underscoring the need for additional therapeutic agents. In addition, anti-VEGF treatments address the fluid accumulating in leaky vessels, but not inflammation, and while steroids can control inflammation, they have undesirable consequences in the eye, potentially leading to cataract formation, increased intraocular pressure, and even glaucoma after repeated use [65]. Therefore, there is a growing effort to develop better anti-inflammatory drugs for retinal disease. As DR progresses, chronic retinal inflammation worsens and persists, and macrophages are more likely to transition to a pro-inflammatory phenotype [66]. This shift exacerbates the release of ROS and destruction of the BRB and causes damage to neurovascular units [67]. Anti-fibrin γ377–395 antibodies block the release of ROS [19, 21, 33], and thus might be considered for front-line therapy to protect both retinal and vascular cells.

Retinal myeloid cells, including microglia, perivascular macrophages, and macrophage-like hyalocytes within the retinal and vitreoretinal space—and macrophages derived from infiltrating monocytes [68–70] contribute to the acquired resistance to anti-VEGF therapy and the failure of the therapy [71, 72]. Further, fibrinogen levels are increased in vitreous humor of proliferative DR patients treated with anti-VEGF by intravitreal injection [73], which may contribute to resistance mechanisms. Given the role of fibrin in macrophage recruitment [22, 29, 33], we propose that THN391, by blocking the vascular-inflammatory axis, will be beneficial in both preventing acquired resistance to VEGF therapy and improving outcomes in patients who are already refractory to VEGF treatment. A previous study in a mouse model that combines streptozotocin-mediated (STZ) hyperglycemia and systemic endotoxemia to mimic the inflammatory microenvironment and vascular pathology observed during DR progression with recurrent acute infections showed microglial activation, fibrin(ogen) deposition, and axonal loss in the retina. In that model, depletion of fibrinogen by ancrod treatment reduced not only fibrin(ogen) deposition, but also microgliosis and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in diseased retinal tissues [28]. Thus, we speculate that neutralizing fibrin toxicity by THN391 has the potential to treat a wide range of retinal diseases.

Anti-fibrin therapy is expected to be safe and well-tolerated. Both IV and IVT administration of THN391 have proven safe in nonclinical toxicology studies. Fggγ390–396A mice deficient in fibrin inflammatory epitope and mice treated with 5B8 are not immunocompromised, and do not develop opportunistic infections when housed in conventional animal facilities [33, 74]. In contrast, Fibrinogen mutant mice are protected from staph and covid-19 infection [19, 75]. Similarly, in humans, congenital afibrinogenemia, a genetic disorder characterized by a complete absence of fibrinogen, is associated with excessive bleeding but no increase in opportunistic infections [76]. The fibrin inflammatory epitope, γ377–395, is distinct from the coagulation epitope, γ400–411 [77], and not required for normal coagulation. Mutation of the epitope in Fggγ390–396A mice does not interfere with fibrin polymerization, platelet aggregation or normal clotting time [24, 74]. THN391 is highly specific for its target and does not interfere with fibrin polymerization, blood coagulation or fibrinolysis as measured by both aPTT and TEG. Consistently, extensive analysis of hemostasis in the Phase1a safety study using ROTEM rotational thromboelastometry demonstrated that THN391 impact on clot formation or fibrinolysis (Kantor et al., in preparation). Fibrinogen in the CNS inhibits remyelination and neurogenesis, and depletion of fibrin promotes repair in models of MS and stroke [2, 78–80]. In contrast to anti-inflammatory treatments blocking phagocytotic pathways, inhibition of the γ390–396 epitope selectively blocks neurotoxic responses and does not interfere with clearance of amyloid in AD mice [20, 21, 33]. Thus, THN391 has the potential to selectively inhibit neuroinflammation with high efficacy and a well-tolerated safety profile without adverse effects on blood coagulation or general immunosuppression.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Our goal was to develop a therapeutic fibrin antibody suitable for clinical use, focusing on humanization, affinity maturation and improved safety by eliminating Fc effector function of the parent mouse monoclonal antibody. Extensive genetic loss-of-function and pharmacological studies using MAb 5B8 have already established proof-of-concept efficacy and mechanism of action in multiple preclinical models [3, 21, 33]. Thus, we did not replicate the full spectrum of all prior studies with our humanized lead candidate. For instance, therapeutic dosing experiments at 30 mg/kg dose in models of MS and AD or chronic administration in MS models were not repeated. In addition, future studies could include more detailed characterization of microglial and macrophage subsets, their recruitment, and additional comparisons between Fc-null and wild-type isotypes. Our study shows for the first time therapeutic effect of fibrin-targeting antibody in retinal diseases showing efficacy in models of wet AMD. Future studies will evaluate THN313 in additional models of retinal disease including diabetic retinopathy. Our study demonstrating therapeutic efficacy at low dose in vivo of a significantly improved fibrin antibody with increased affinity and abolished Fc effector function supports its clinical development and testing in additional models of neurologic and retinal diseases.

Conclusion

THN391 represents a novel therapeutic approach for targeting fibrin as a driver of neuroinflammation in neurodegenerative and inflammatory diseases. A first-in-human Phase 1a study to assess THN391 safety and tolerability following intravenous administration has been completed (EU CT # 2024–517955-10–00). THN391 was safe and well-tolerated and had no impact on hemostasis [81]. Based on its mechanism of action and preclinical validation reported in this study, we anticipate that THN391 will be effective in inhibiting neuroinflammation, reducing neurotoxicity and potentially slowing the decline of cognitive ability in dementia patients and improving vision in patients with retinal diseases. Phase 1b clinical trials are underway in early AD (NCT06814730) and DME (NCT06701721). THN391 may therefore provide a new opportunity to enhance the therapeutic toolbox for physicians treating patients with neurodegenerative disorders that involve fibrin-driven neuroinflammation.
