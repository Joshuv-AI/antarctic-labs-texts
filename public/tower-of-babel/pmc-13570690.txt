Relationship between Myers–Briggs Type Indicator, Achievement Motivation, and Clinical Performance of Pharmacy Students in Iran: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Sara Salarian, Saeed Mohammad Soleymani, Zahra Khoramdel, Hadi Esmaily
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:The link between Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI), achievement motivation, and clinical performance in Iran’s pharmacy education, using Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE), is understudied. This study examines associations between MBTI, achievement motivation, and OSCE performance in Iranian pharmacy students.Methods:This was a cross-sectional study, conducted from February 2020 to June 2021 at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences. The MBTI and Hermans achievement motivation questionnaire were provided to the participants. The OSCE was used to assess the students’ clinical performance. All scores were calculated, and data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0.Findings:From a total of 253 students, 196 of them agreed to participate in the study, and 180 students completed the survey (response rate 91.8%). 117 participants were female, and 63 participants were male. The mean age of the students was 24 ± 2.06 years. There were significant correlations between achievement motivation and the percentage of extraversion, thinking, and judging. Analysis of the relationship between students’ personality type and scores in different aspects of clinical performance showed that there is a significant and positive correlation between the percentage of extraversion and communication skills, and also a significant and positive correlation between the percentage of judgment and discipline.Conclusions:This study shows that the evaluation of different personality types of pharmacy students can help to improve pharmacist education systems, guide them more effectively in choosing their career in the pharmacy system, and this self-awareness and self-improvement can increase the probability of their future career success.

INTRODUCTION

It is important to consider the psychological characteristics of pharmacy students, especially in a clinical setting.[1] Understanding the factors that increase the probability of students’ academic success can lead to the early separation of successful students and those in need of academic interventions.[2] Identifying specific personality styles related to academic performance can help educators to identify the success factors of students.[2] It can also be said that personality styles are very influential in curriculum design, teaching, and recruitment and admission processes.[3]

Identifying a person’s personality type can help determine tasks that are better suited to their personality, and thus lead to greater productivity.[1] There are several methods for determining personality types, of which the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is one of the most valid, advanced, and complete methods.[4]

In addition to psychological characteristics, environmental and social conditions can affect the motivation of each person to achieve success.[5] If medical students have enough motivation to progress in this field before entering the clinic, it can be effective in the quality of services they provide to patients and their professional behavior.[6] The MBTI personality types of pharmacy students have an effect on their self-esteem, so that students with introverted, intuitive, and perceptive personality types show higher scores on the Clance Imposter Phenomenon scale, and those with introverted or perceptive personality types are at severe impostor risk. Therefore, knowing the personality type of pharmacy students can lead to the use of an active combination of strategies and resources in a curriculum to support students in normalizing and reducing anxiety.[7] Furthermore, the MBTI index can indicate how people manage their stress and maintain their health.[8]

Achievement motivation is the basic motivator for all actions and the realization of dreams, and can influence how a person performs. It refers to the dynamics of our behavior, which includes needs, desires, and ambitions in life.[9]

Previously published articles have examined personality types in various careers, including pharmacists. Rosenthal et al. examined the relationship between personality traits and pharmacists’ performance.[10] Hardigan related job satisfaction and career choice to personality types.[11] Jessee et al. related personality types and learning preferences of dentistry students to teaching methods.[12] Similarly, Ware examined the relationship between MBTI personality types and The North American Pharmacy Licensure Examination performance.[13] But what has been neglected so far is the relationship between the personality type of pharmacy students based on the MBTI test and the motivation of pharmacy students to progress, as well as their performance in the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE).

This study evaluated the association between the MBTI and Hermans’ achievement motivation, alongside various dimensions of clinical competency in pharmacy students.

METHODS

The Ethics Committee of the School of Pharmacy, Nursing, and Midwifery granted approval for this study (IR.SBMU.PHARMACY.REC. 1399.305), and informed consent was obtained from all participating students before their involvement in the activity. This was a cross-sectional study conducted from February 2020 to June 2021 at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (SBMU). The participants in this study were pharmacy students enrolled in an advanced pharmacy practice course who were eligible for an OSCE at the end of their course. To assess various aspects of students’ clinical performance, an OSCE was used, which was validated in 2020 at SBMU.

Study design and participants

This was a cross-sectional study conducted from February 2020 to June 2021 at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran. The participants were pharmacy students enrolled in an advanced pharmacy practice course who were eligible for an OSCE at the end of their course.

With an alpha coefficient of 0.05, a power of 80%, and a correlation coefficient of 0.216, the calculated sample size was 165.[14] Due to the fact that some of the students might not complete the questionnaire, a target sample size of 250 students was selected for this purpose. Therefore, the 5th and 6th year Pharm D students of the school of pharmacy of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences in Tehran were invited to voluntarily participate in this study after studying the conditions of the study, and 250 of the first registrants entered the study. The native language of the students who participated in the study was Persian. Therefore, the Persian version of the tests revalidated in the Iranian context was used.[15] Students completed the MBTI and the Hermans questionnaire during their semester, and the OSCE was held at the end of the semester.

Myers–Briggs Type Indicator

MBTI, initially developed in 1940, is based on Carl Jung’s Psychological Type theory and provides insight into an individual’s personality type/characteristics.[16] In the MBTI test, the subjects’ personality is categorized based on four dimensions with two preferences. By combining these options, sixteen personality types are described, and each person is assigned a type.[16] The MBTI Form M (a self-rated 93-item instrument) was used to classify the participants’ personality type. Form M has higher reliability than Form G.[1617] The result of this test can distinguish the personality preferences of the test participants in four dichotomies: Extraversion (E) and introversion (I); (E/I), sensing (S) and intuition (N); (S/N), thinking (T) and feeling (F); (T/F), and judging (J) and perceiving (P); (J/P). Extroverts attend to and draw energy from the external world of people and things, while introverts spend their time in the inner world of ideas and images. Sensing personality types take in information through the five senses, while intuitional personalities see the patterns and possibilities in the information they receive. Thinking personalities make decisions impersonally, while feeling personalities make decisions based on personal values. Judging personalities prefer a structured lifestyle, while perceiving personalities prefer flexible routines.

Herman’s Achievement Motivation Questionnaire

The Herman’s Achievement Motivation Questionnaire is used to assess the need to get ahead. It was developed by Hermans (1970) based on existing theoretical and empirical findings and related studies.[14] The Hermans achievement motivation test was used to determine the students’ achievement motivation.[18] Hermans chose 10 distinguishing characteristics between highly motivated and lowly motivated individuals from previous studies as a basis for selecting the questions. The questionnaire consists of 29 four-choice questions that can be scored from 1 to 4. The total score indicates the motivation of individuals to progress, ranging from 29 to 116.[1419] Hermans questionnaire was chosen due to its simplicity and the emphasis of several valid studies on motivation to progress.[20]

Objective structured clinical examination

The OSCE used in this study was designed, implemented, and validated at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, as described in detail by Aram et al.[21] Briefly, the examination consisted of 9 main stations and one rest station, each lasting 9 min. The stations included: disease state monitoring (2 stations), prescription reading (2 stations), pertinent laboratory values (1 station), interprofessional teamwork (1 station), polypharmacy (1 station), drug information for special populations (pregnancy/breastfeeding) (1 station), and self-care recommendation (1 station).

Scoring procedure

The scoring method followed the validated protocol established by Aram et al.[21] At each station, two trained clinical pharmacist examiners were present: one portrayed the patient or healthcare provider, while the other completed a structured evaluation form in real-time.

For each station, a predefined checklist was developed by a panel of pharmacy education experts using the Delphi method. Each checklist consisted of 10–15 key items representing essential steps or correct responses for that clinical scenario. Each item was assigned a point value based on clinical importance, with a total possible score of 100 points per station, divided equally into two components:

Knowledge Assessment (50 points): Evaluated pharmacotherapy, evidence-based medicine, differential diagnosis, and regulatory knowledge

Final Score Calculation: Scores from all 9 stations were aggregated. Total OSCE score (range: 0–200) was calculated by summing knowledge scores (0–100) and clinical performance scores (0–100) across all stations. Clinical performance subdomains were converted to a 0–100 scale for reporting.

Quality Assurance: Internal consistency across the 9 stations was confirmed with Cronbach’s alpha exceeding 0.7 in most stations, consistent with the validation study. Inter-rater reliability, assessed by double-scoring 10% of sessions, showed excellent agreement.

Statical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to determine the normality of quantitative variables for statistical analysis.[1922] The t-test for independent samples was used to compare mean ± standard deviation (SD) to detect discrepancies in two grouped parametric data, such as males/females, extraversion/introversion, and other dimensions. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used to compare medians and percentiles of nonparametric variables. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to assess the internal consistency of different aspects of clinical performance. Pearson’s Chi-square test was used to detect discrepancies in the frequencies of the categorical variables and the sex of the subjects. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A one-way analysis of variance test was used to compare means ± SD of quantitative variables between personality types. Results were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 26.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Students from two different entry levels of the SBMU school of pharmacy were invited to participate in this study. Out of a total of 253 students, 196 (77.5%) agreed to participate in the study, and all of them completed the Hermans test. In fact, people expressed their consent by completing the Hermans questionnaire. However, 180 students eventually completed both the Hermans and MBTI questionnaires, resulting in a 91.8% response rate.

From a demographic perspective, 117 participants were female (65%), and 63 participants were male (35%). The mean age of the students was 24 ± 2.06 years. According to the MBTI test, the predominant preferences of the students were extraversion (E), thinking (T), and judging (J), respectively, while sensing (S) and intuition (N) were tied (I > E, S = N, T > F, and J > P). DISCUSSION

Previous studies have examined personality types in various professions, including among pharmacists, but the relationship between pharmacists’ personality type and the various aspects of clinical performance has not been previously examined.[2324]

Since knowledge is an independent component of individuals’ personality type, examining the relationship between personality type and overall exam scores is misleading. However, differences in the various parameters that shape personality can play a role in human performance.[25] An instrument that can independently assess knowledge and performance is needed to investigate this hypothesis. In this study, using a validated OSCE helped us assess knowledge and different aspects of clinical performance separately. The results of this study showed that some aspects of clinical performance differ significantly among some personality types.

Ware examined the relationship between MBTI personalities and the North American Pharmacy Licensure Examination (NAPLEX) in pharmacy students. His results showed that extroverted and feeling personalities scored higher on the NAPLEX. But this study did not demonstrate a significant relationship between personality types and overall OSCE scores; however, the results of this study suggest that students with a higher percentage of extraversion possessed better communication skills, which may help extroverted pharmacists communicate more effectively with patients at the pharmacy, and that students with a higher percentage of judgment also scored higher on discipline.[13] Previous studies have also shown that extraversion is correlated with communication ability.[26] There was no significant relationship between the percentage of feeling and the percentage of thinking in the clinical performance assessments.[13]

The significant positive correlation between judging preference and discipline scores (r = 0.172, P = 0.02) can be explained by the core characteristics of the judging personality type. Individuals with a judging preference tend to be organized, plan-oriented, and rule-abiding, which directly aligns with the concept of discipline in clinical settings, defined as adherence to protocols, punctuality, and systematic task completion.[17] This finding is consistent with Wietholter et al., who reported that pharmacy students with judging preferences demonstrate higher levels of academic professionalism.[2] Interestingly, no such correlation was found with other performance domains like communication or clinical judgment, suggesting that while judging enhances structured behaviors, it may not influence interpersonal or analytical skills.

The MBTI index can be somewhat predictive of how students manage stress and maintain health.[18] Behaviors caused by people’s personality type may affect the clinical performance of pharmacy students, so people’s personality type is effective on work avoidance behaviors based on their tendencies and calculation of progress goal orientations. This study analyzed the correlation between the personality types of pharmacy students with their progress and clinical performance by using the MBTI evaluation index. The findings of this study suggest that students with introverted personality types may benefit from additional practice and targeted educational interventions to enhance their communication skills and overall clinical performance.[27]

This is the first study to examine the relationship between MBTI indicators and achievement motivation in pharmacists and may raise awareness about the differences between personality types in motivation to progress. Statistical analysis showed that students with higher percentages in extraversion, thinking, and judgment had higher achievement motivation scores. Therefore, E, T, and J personalities, including ESTJ and ENTJ, are more motivated to progress.

Strengths

This study, unlike previous studies, simultaneously examined the relationship between personality traits and motivations for progress, as well as the clinical performance of pharmacy students.

Limitation

This study had some limitations. For one thing, all the data collected were from only one pharmacy school, which is not necessarily generalizable. For another, the MBTI test has been primarily designed to determine the preferences and personality types of individuals, but their representation in percentages could prove to be flawed. This is because individuals’ answers to the questions should reflect their current mood and are meant to represent them as they are. However, as is often the case, their answers reflect what they think is right and acceptable in society. Therefore, expressing them in percentages may influence their preferences. Nevertheless, we explained to the students how they should answer the questions in order to avoid errors in calculating percentages as much as possible.

Personality traits and motivation were subjectively assessed with student questionnaires, which may have inherent differences with reality. Another limitation could be that one OSCE was chosen to assess clinical knowledge and skills, but this could extend to several OSCEs over several years.

Future research directions

Future longitudinal and multicenter studies are recommended to confirm these findings and enhance generalizability. Interventional research should explore whether targeted training (e.g., communication skills for introverted students) can improve clinical performance. Additionally, investigating other psychological variables, such as emotional intelligence, and conducting qualitative studies, would provide deeper insights into how personality influences pharmacy education and practice.

CONCLUSIONS

According to the MBTI, this study showed that pharmacy students with higher percentages of extraversion, thinking, and judgment are more motivated to progress. In addition, students with higher percentages of extraversion scored higher on communication skills, and students with higher percentages of judgment scored higher on discipline. Assessing the performance of different personalities in pharmacist positions can help improve pharmacist education systems, guide students more effectively in their career choices in the pharmacy system, and raise pharmacists’ self-awareness and self-improvement in their careers. Findings of the present study represent weak correlations and suggest that the findings may show certain personality traits to have a slightly higher tendency on motivation, but that there are likely multiple other factors that need to be elucidated.
