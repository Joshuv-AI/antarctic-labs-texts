Current status and future prospects of biodegradable magnesium-based implants developed for orthopaedic applications
Authors: Dewei Zhao, Weidan Wang, Ye Li, Frank Witte, Qing Lu, Weirong Li, Tianwei Zhang, Xiuzhi Zhang, Ling Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiodegradable magnesium-based implants are the most clinically translated biodegradable metallic biomaterials in orthopaedics, owing to their favorable biocompatibility, bone-matched mechanics, and bioactive degradation behaviour. Through Mg2+ release, these implants may support osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and immunomodulation while avoiding permanent implant retention. However, their broader adoption remains constrained by degradation/healing mismatch, gas-associated issues, site-specific mechanical limitations, and insufficient long-term safety evidence. This review summarises the current status of biodegradable magnesium-based orthopaedic implants from a translational perspective by integrating biological mechanisms, preclinical animal models, clinical progress, long-term and regulation-related challenges. We particularly highlight the dual biological effects of Mg-based degradation, the translational relevance of disease-oriented small- and large-animal models, and emerging clinical evidence. By linking mechanistic insight with preclinical and clinical evidence, this review highlights the major opportunities and unresolved barriers that will shape the next stage of clinical translation in this field.The translational potential of this article.This review summarises biodegradable magnesium-based orthopaedic implants from a translational perspective, linking biological mechanisms, preclinical animal models, clinical evidence, long-term safety, and regulation-related considerations. Introduction

As a representative material of biodegradable metallic materials, biodegradable Mg-based materials (BMM, pure Mg or Mg alloys) are the most extensively studied and widely used materials for clinical applications [1,2]. At present, three major biodegradable metal systems dominate orthopaedic biomaterials research: magnesium (Mg) [3], iron (Fe) [4], and zinc (Zn) [5] alloys. Each exhibits distinct biomechanical, degradation, and biological responses, which define its application scenarios and translational barriers. In terms of mechanical compatibility with human cortical bone (10–30 GPa), the elastic modulus of pure Mg and Mg alloys is approximately 41–45 GPa, substantially lower than that of Fe (∼211 GPa) and Zn (∼90 GPa), thereby effectively reducing the stress-shielding effect commonly associated with permanent metallic implants. Regarding degradation behaviour, Fe alloys undergo sluggish corrosion and are therefore more suitable for applications requiring prolonged structural support, such as cardiovascular stents; while in the context of bone healing, excessively slow degradation may compromise the clinical value of a biodegradable implant. Zn alloys exhibit an intermediate degradation rate between Mg and Fe, which are currently being explored for load-bearing orthopaedic applications, but still face practical limitations related to mechanical strength and long-term load-bearing capacity. To date, among biodegradable orthopaedic metals, BMMs have achieved the most substantial clinical translation.

From a biochemical perspective, healthy adults weighing 70 kg store approximately 21–28 g of Mg element, representing over 0.03% of the total body content, with approximately 60% stored in the bones [6]. Notably, Mg2+ plays a multitude of essential roles in various physiological processes and is able to catalyze and activate more than 300 known enzymatic reactions [7]. From the perspective of the electrochemical mechanism of metal corrosion [8], the electrode potential of the Mg electrode is far more negative than the equilibrium electrode potential of the hydrogen reaction. Thus, under standard conditions, Mg metal degrades and releases Mg2+ and hydrogen in aqueous solution. This unique degradation behaviour underlies both the advantages of BMM implants (BMMI) and the challenges associated with their clinical application [9,10].

The clinical appeal of BMM extends far beyond inherent biodegradability, as they address multiple unmet orthopaedic clinical needs. Conventional permanent metallic implants, such as titanium alloys and stainless steel devices, provide reliable fixation but may be associated with stress shielding, long-term implant retention, soft-tissue irritation, imaging interference, in selected patients and indications, frequent need for secondary removal surgery [11]. By contrast, absorbable polymeric implants avoid permanent retention but often suffer from limited mechanical strength, acidic degradation products, inflammatory reactions, and unpredictable loss of fixation during degradation. Against this backdrop, the ideal next-generation orthopaedic implant should provide sufficient early-stage mechanical support, undergo controllable degradation synchronised with tissue remodeling, facilitate bone or tendon-bone regeneration, and eliminate both long-term implant retention and degradation-related complications. BMMI are currently among the most promising candidates for this role; however, whether they can consistently achieve this balance remains the central translational challenge in the field.

Conversely, unregulated, spatially heterogeneous degradation of BMMI would trigger premature loss of structural integrity, localized alkalization, subcutaneous/intramedullary hydrogen gas accumulation, radiolucent lesions, and adverse host tissue reactions [12]. These problems are particularly relevant at anatomically complex or load-bearing sites and may contribute to substantial variation in outcomes across indications. Moreover, as biodegradable metallic implants move from laboratory investigation toward real-world clinical practice, the field is no longer challenged solely by short-term proof-of-concept questions. Greater emphasis must now be placed on long-term stability, tolerance, clinical effectiveness, and the definition of indication-specific failure boundaries. In particular, systematic evaluation of delayed complications—such as insufficient osseointegration, persistent radiolucent or cystic lesions, delayed inflammation, and the potential local burden of degradation products—has become indispensable to assessing the long-term clinical viability of BMMI.

Accordingly, this review not only summarises the biological performance and preclinical advances of BMMI, but also highlights their translational development in orthopaedics. The present work is organised around the orthopaedic application pathway of BMM. We elaborate in vitro biofunctional and biosafety mechanisms, the design and translational value of disease-oriented small and large animal models, representative preclinical and clinical outcomes across diverse orthopaedic indications, and the key opportunities and challenges related to long-term evaluation, clinical translation and regulation. By integrating mechanistic evidence, animal validation, clinical observations, and unresolved long-term safety issues, this review aims to provide a more balanced and clinically relevant perspective on the current status and future prospects of BMMI.

In vitro biocompatibility and biofunction of Mg

BMM demonstrate extensive biofunctional potential in orthopaedic applications, while their in vitro biological effects are not universally beneficial. Growing evidence indicates that cellular responses to BMM are highly dependent on the degradation kinetics, local Mg2+ concentration, OH− release, hydrogen evolution, and the composition and quantity of degradation products. Therefore, it should be emphasized that the favorable bioactivity of Mg should be interpreted within a dose- and microenvironment-dependent framework, rather than being regarded as a universally positive effect.

Mg-enhanced osteogenesis

Numerous studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of BMMI [13,14]. The release of Mg2+ facilitates the adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation of osteoblasts. Studies confirm that Mg2+, whether in salt solutions or from degrading biomaterials, upregulates both early (e.g., ALP, RUNX2) and late (e.g., OCN, BMP2) osteogenic markers in a concentration-dependent manner [2,15]. Qin et al. reported that Mg2+ influx via MagT1 and TRPM7 channels in a rat model stimulates the release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) from sensory neurons. CGRP then binds to receptors on periosteal-derived stem cells, upregulating camp response element-binding protein 1 (CREB1) and Osterix to drive bone formation [16,17] (Fig. 2B). Concurrently, Zhao et al. demonstrated that Mg2+ enhances osteoblast migration and upregulates RUNX2 and ALP via the TRPM7/PI3K signaling pathway.

However, the degradation kinetics of BMM could significantly influence osteogenic outcomes. Excessively high local Mg2+ concentrations (>16 mM) can induce cytotoxicity in osteoblasts, characterized by reduced cell viability, increased apoptosis, and impaired mineralization capacity [20]. Beyond ionic concentration, local alkalization resulting from rapid BMM degradation represents another safety concern. Although mildly alkaline conditions may transiently facilitate mineralization-related processes, sustained or pronounced pH elevation can induce multiple detrimental effects, including inhibition of adhesion and proliferation of MSCs; disruption of cell membrane stability, and altered protein adsorption behaviour. Notably, the alkalization effect may be particularly pronounced in static in vitro culture systems. Importantly, the PI3K activation also protects cells from the cytotoxic alkaline stress associated with Mg degradation, thereby elucidating the dual role of Mg2+ in promoting bone repair while mitigating implant-related cytotoxicity [18] (Fig. 2C).

Stem cells are utilized for bone repair due to their self-renewal and osteogenic differentiation capacities [21,22]. However, stem cell senescence compromises bone healing [23]. Therefore, mitigating the aging of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) is a key strategy for enhancing regeneration in aging individuals.

Research by Qi et al. demonstrated that Mg2+ released from akermanite bioceramics (Ca2MgSi2O7) enhance osteogenesis and alleviate cellular senescence [19]. The mechanism involves Mg2+ stimulating BMSCs to release exosomes enriched with miR-196a-5p. This microRNA targets and downregulates Hoxa7, subsequently activating the MAPK signaling pathway to counteract senescence and promote bone formation. In aged rats with cranial defects, 3D-printed Mg-containing scaffolds significantly improved bone regeneration (Fig. 2D). Thus, exosome-mediated therapy triggered by Mg2+ release presents a promising strategy for bone repair in the elderly [24].

Mg-enhanced angiogenesis

Adequate vascularization is essential for bone formation, as it ensures nutrient supply for osteogenic cells [25]. In recent years, Mg2+ have been shown to play a significant role in promoting revascularization and vascular-osteogenic coupling at bone defect sites [26]. Moreover, Mg2+ have been confirmed to trigger trigeminal neurons via Trpv1 to produce the neuropeptide CGRP, which promotes angiogenesis and osteogenesis [27] (Fig. 3A).

Mg2+ upregulates the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in BMSCs [17]. In vivo, a rat skull defect model showed significant vascularized bone regeneration associated with Mg2+. In a steroid-induced osteonecrosis (SAON) rat model [28], oral Mg2+ supplementation combined with vitamin C reduced TNF-α expression and oxidative damage in bone marrow, improved the proximal tibial blood perfusion, and reduced the leakage particles in the distal tibia (Fig. 3B).

In distraction osteogenesis (DO) models, Mg2+ from degrading implants potently enhanced angiogenesis [29]. The released Mg2+ stimulate CGRP synthesis in sensory nerves. CGRP then activates focal adhesion kinase (FAK), increasing VEGFA expression. This CGRP-FAK-VEGF axis promotes endothelial cell migration and tubulogenesis, linking sensory nerve activation to vascular regeneration (Fig. 3C). This mechanism clarifies how Mg2+ promotes coupled angiogenesis and osteogenesis, providing a scientific basis for the future design of BMMI [30].

Mg-inhibited osteoclastogenesis

Bone homeostasis depends on the balance between osteoblast-mediated formation and osteoclast-mediated resorption [31]. Mg2+ supplementation alleviates bone loss, while Mg deficiency enhances osteoclastogenesis. A recent work summarised how BMMI degradation products inhibit osteoclast activity through multiple pathways [32]. The localized alkaline environment inhibits the expression of osteoclast resorption factors. Furthermore, Mg2+ competitively inhibit the RANK/RANKL binding, suppress calcium influx, and downregulate inflammatory factor activation.

Mg also mitigates implant-related osteolysis. In vitro, pure Mg leaching solution (MLL) suppresses osteoclast formation, polarization, and bone resorption by inhibiting nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) activation [33]. In vivo experiments further confirmed that BMMI attenuate wear particle-induced osteolysis. These studies highlighted the potential of BMMI for the treatment of osteoporosis-related diseases caused by excessive osteoclast formation and activity [34]. Compared to the well-studied osteogenic effects of Mg, research on its anti-osteoclastic mechanisms remains less extensive. BMMI inhibit osteoclast adhesion and activity via Mg2+ release, hydrogen evolution, and alkaline microenvironments.

As illustrated, Mg2+ released from BMMI activates diverse signaling cascades to coordinately regulate bone remodeling processes. Fig. 4 presents an integrated schematic framework illustrating the multifaceted roles of Mg2+ in osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and osteoclast activity modulation. Regarding osteogenesis, Mg2+ enters BMSCs/osteoblasts via the TRPM7 channel, triggering the PI3K/Akt/mTOR, MAPK/ERK, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, thereby upregulating the expression of osteogenic transcription factors (Runx2) and markers (ALP, OCN, OPN, Collagen I). Meanwhile, Mg2+ stimulates osteoblasts and endothelial cells to secrete CGRP, which further facilitates osteogenic differentiation and bone matrix synthesis via paracrine and autocrine signaling. For angiogenesis, Mg2+ induces VEGF secretion in osteoblasts and endothelial cells directly or through the HIF-1α/PI3K/Akt axis to promote endothelial tube formation. CGRP additionally stabilizes nascent vascular structures through sensory neuron-mediated signaling, enhancing local blood supply synergistically. In terms of anti-osteoclast effects, Mg2+ elevates the OPG/RANKL ratio to suppress osteoclast differentiation. It also blocks Ca2+ influx in osteoclast precursors and inhibits NFATc1 activation, ultimately reducing bone resorption. Given the multifaceted biological activities of Mg2+ and its great potential for clinical translation, further investigations into the therapeutic efficacy of BMMI against pathological bone injuries are highly warranted.

Mg-modulated osteoimmune properties

The design concepts of orthopaedic implant materials have transitioned from ‘immune-friendly’ to ‘immunoregulatory’ [35]. Macrophages are the central regulators of the immune response and play crucial roles in bone tissue repair and regeneration. Their polarization into different states leads to varying inflammatory states, which are key to the differences in bone repair ability. Quantitative evidence demonstrated that an extracellular Mg2+ concentration of approximately 8 mM (10× physiological level) significantly enhances intracellular Mg2+ levels in macrophages and up-regulates TRPM7 expression by over two-fold [36]. This creates an immune microenvironment by facilitating the recruitment and polarization of monocytes and macrophages, which ultimately promotes bone tissue regeneration.

Nevertheless, BMMI exerts a concentration-dependent dual effect on macrophage polarization and subsequent osteogenic differentiation by regulating the immune microenvironment via Mg2+ release. On one hand, Mg2+ intrinsically possesses anti-inflammatory properties and promotes M2 macrophage polarization. On the other hand, the rapid degradation of BMM triggers abrupt increased local pH value and hydrogen accumulation. Existing evidence [37,38] confirmed that the alkaline environment of pH ≥ 8.2 is pro-inflammatory and can induce macrophage polarization towards M1 phenotype. Consequently, the fate of macrophages is governed by the dynamic balance between the anti-inflammatory effect of Mg2+ and the pro-inflammatory effects arising from localized alkalization and other degradation products. Notably, excessive Mg2+ concentrations (>50 mM) or high contents of degradation particles (>1600 μg/mL) may also reverse the aforementioned anti-inflammatory biological outcomes.

Additionally, as one of the target cells for tissue repair and immune regulation, BMSCs exhibit a significant correlation between their activity and the Mg2+ concentration [39]. A study found that although the high-concentration degradation particles derived from high-purity magnesium can regulate macrophage polarization [40], they unexpectedly suppress osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, revealing the boundary between 'moderate immune regulation' and 'excessive intervention'.

Furthermore, we summarised the regulation of macrophage behaviours by Mg2+ concentrations (Table 1). At the physiological concentration of 0.8 mM, Mg2+ exhibited neither immunomodulatory effects nor significant osteogenic promotion. Concentrations below 5 mM mildly suppressed M1 macrophage polarization while moderately enhancing BMSC adhesion and osteogenesis. Within the optimal therapeutic window of 5–10 mM, Mg2+ potently induced M2 macrophage polarization, suppressed NF-κB signaling and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and maximized BMSC adhesion, migration, and osteogenic differentiation. When the concentration was further elevated to 25 mM and above, prominent cytotoxicity emerged, with M1 macrophage polarization predominating and tissue repair being inhibited.

Notably, BMMI continuously release degradation products (DPs) after in vivo implantation. Sun et al. found that treatment with DPs significantly upregulated pro-inflammatory genes, triggering chronic local inflammation and M1 macrophage polarization, whereas isolated Mg2+ treatment yielded the opposite effect by suppressing pro-inflammatory gene expression [40]. This indicates that DPs themselves, rather than free Mg2+, serve as the core factor driving macrophage inflammatory activation, while Mg2+ at appropriate concentrations exerts anti-inflammatory effects.

In conclusion, the influence of Mg on cellular behaviour is not a simple linear relationship, but is governed by a complex interplay among ionic concentration, local pH, degradation kinetics, hydrogen evolution, degradation particles, and alloying-element-related factors. While moderate Mg release can promote osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and favorable immunoregulation, uncontrolled degradation may instead induce cytotoxicity, pro-inflammatory macrophage activation, suppression of stem-cell osteogenesis, and persistent local inflammatory responses. Therefore, precise regulation of BMMI degradation dynamics to maintain Mg2+ levels within a therapeutic window, while minimizing excessive alkalization, gas accumulation, and adverse degradation-product burden, is critical for achieving both efficacy and biosafety in orthopaedic applications.

Preclinical evaluation of BMMI

Preclinical animal studies play a pivotal role in fulfilling registration requirements for medical devices. They deliver quantitative and qualitative data regarding tissue responses, osteogenic performance, and bone biocompatibility, serving as an indispensable prerequisite and fundamental evaluation basis for subsequent clinical trials [69]. This necessitates the establishment of animal models that can closely simulate clinical scenarios, forming a fundamental basis for evaluating the biosafety and efficacy of Class II or III medical devices. This section summarises the construction of several animal models and evaluation methods commonly used in orthopaedic clinics [70]（Table 2). These models include osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH), fracture fixation, anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, critical bone defects, and cartilage defect models. The models used included mice, Sprague Dawley (SD) rats, New Zealand white rabbits, pigs, sheep/goats, cattle, emus and beagle dogs. Criteria for animal species selection cover the following core dimensions: comparability of bone remodeling models with human bone tissue; analogous biological responses to therapeutic interventions, hormonal modulation, and other confounding factors relative to humans; accessibility and cost efficiency including animal housing expenses; ease of husbandry and surgical manipulation; and ethical sensitivities across diverse cultural contexts. Preclinical animal models for BMMI should not be selected solely on the basis of convenience or historical precedent, but according to the specific translational question being addressed.

Osteonecrosis model

ONFH is a common bone disease often caused by trauma, alcoholism, or corticosteroid use. The widespread clinical application of glucocorticoids (GCs) for conditions like severe infections (e.g., COVID-19, SARS) and autoimmune diseases, while essential, has been systematically linked to an increased risk of ONFH, particularly with long-term or high-dose regimens [71,72].

The Association Research Circulation Osseous (ARCO) classifies ONFH from stages 0 to IV. Young patients presenting with hip pain are frequently diagnosed at stage IV, where total hip arthroplasty (THA) is often indicated but may be suboptimal due to cost and longevity concerns [73]. Consequently, hip-preserving surgeries (e.g., core decompression, bone grafting) are recommended for younger, active patients [74].

BMM showed promise for hip preservation. In a rabbit ONFH model induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and methylprednisolone (MPS), a PLGA/TCP/Mg scaffold promoted osteogenesis and angiogenesis via Mg2+ release [47]. Similarly, Mg/BMP-2/PLLA microspheres implanted in a comparable rabbit model demonstrated osteogenic, angiogenic, and immunomodulatory properties [48]. Research has advanced to bipedal emu models, which better mimic human biomechanics, confirming the efficacy of combined LPS/MPS in inducing ONFH [75,76]. An innovative Mg-based screw designed for bone cement injection has also been utilized for ONFH repair [77].

As for the medication-related osteonecrosis, Su et al. indicated that the biodegradable Mg implant could alleviate the development of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, possibly via upregulating VEGF- and CGRP-mediated angiogenesis [46]. Compared with implantation of Ti implant to bisphosphonate (BP)-treated rat, Mg implant significantly reduced occurrence of lesion and histological osteonecrosis. Above all, BMMI have the potential to be developed as a novel treatment for patients at risk of ONFH.

Fracture fixation model

For the fracture fixation model, the researchers established fracture models at both the low-bearing bone site and the weight-bearing area. The low-load-bearing bone site includes the ulnar fracture, medial femoral condyle fracture, and mandibular fracture fixation models. Femoral neck and long bone fracture models (such as tibial and femoral fractures) are recommended as weight-bearing evaluation models. Compared to conventional bioinert materials, BMMI exert its advantages considering the less stress-shielding mechanical support and favorable osteogenesis promotion ability. As for the materials system, both pure Mg and Mg alloys have been studied in fracture fixation model.

Sfeir et al. demonstrated effective healing and new bone formation around Mg plates/screws in a rabbit ulna fracture model, with mechanical properties matching healthy bone [55]. Their results confirmed observations of new bone formation around Mg devices, especially above the devices where the periosteum and muscle tissue were present. For weight-bearing applications, hybrid fixation systems combining Mg with titanium or stainless steel have been proposed [57]. These leverage the mechanical strength of inert metals alongside the osteogenic degradation products of Mg. In fact, the first test of hybrid fixation system was conducted by Lambotte in 1906 [78,79], with the combination of one Mg plate and six steel screws to fix tibia fracture. Unfortunately, by the accumulation of extensive subcutaneous gas, the clinical trial failed at that time. Qin et al. developed a thin compact layer of polymer coating film on the surface of Mg screw, which can work as a barrier to prevent direct contact between Mg and Ti implants, thereby greatly reducing the galvanic corrosion [57]. Further, a finite element analysis was performed to evaluate the feasibility of this hybrid fixation system. The results indicated a similar results as in vivo animal experiment, that the hybrid system is suitable to fix the fracture of cortical bones. In conclusion, the expansion of clinical applications of BMMI can be achieved by novel hybrid systems, especially for biological fixation of challenging fractures.

In a goat femoral condyle model, Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr alloy screws showed superior osteogenic factor expression compared to PLA controls, though degradation rates require further optimisation [51]. Addressing high-stress sites, Huang et al. developed a high-purity Mg screw for femoral neck fractures in goats. Over 48 weeks, it provided sufficient mechanical support, a matching degradation rate, and promoted bone formation with improved local blood supply [52], supporting its translational potential for load-bearing applications.

Ligament reconstruction model

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is a common sports-related condition [80]. For severe partial or complete ruptures, ligament reconstruction (ACLR) is crucial to prevent tissue deterioration and mitigate osteoarthritis risk [81]. Conventional absorbable polymer interface screws risk inflammation from acidic degradation by-products, while their sudden disintegration can lead to unpredictable failure. Non-degradable metallic interface screws face challenges of high Young's modulus and biomechanical mismatch with bone, potentially leading to bone marrow enlargement and tendon-bone fusion damage.

Mg screws have shown promise in the fixation and repair of tendon grafts as they promote new bone formation. Wang et al. evaluated Mg-6Zn-0.5Sr alloy screws in a rabbit ACLR model versus polylactic acid (PLA) screws [59]. Mg group demonstrated superior torsional properties in vitro. In vivo, the Mg screws degraded completely by 12 weeks, with significantly less peri-tunnel bone loss than the PLA group. By 16 weeks, the cavity left by the degradation of the Mg alloy was rapidly filled with new bone. The biomechanical testing results showed a significant improvement in the maximum failure load of the Mg alloy group [59].

Another study using high-purity Mg screws in rabbits found comparable biomechanical properties to titanium screws at 12 weeks. Histological analysis revealed that a fibrocartilage transition zone had developed at the tendon-bone interface in the high-purity Mg group, while disordered fibrocartilage layer was observed in the Ti group. The Mg2+ release of high-purity Mg group enhanced the expression of BMP-2 and VEGF during early-stage tendon-bone healing, demonstrating potential for promoting fibrocartilaginous entheses regeneration in ACL reconstruction [82]. Further, high-purity Mg screws were shown to modulate macrophage polarization via the AKT1 and PI3K/AKT pathways, promoting an M2 pro-healing phenotype and enhancing fibrocartilage interface regeneration compared to Ti screws [42].

Bone defect regeneration model

Bone defect regeneration models represent a unique, highly relevant preclinical category for evaluating BMM, particularly for critical-sized or challenging bone defects with limited self-healing capacity [83]. The implanted material is expected to provide both structural support and sustained biological stimulation. Bone defect models place greater emphasis on whether the implant can restore a regenerative microenvironment, support tissue ingrowth, promote vascularized bone regeneration, and gradually transfer mechanical and biological functions to newly formed bone. Therefore, these models are especially suitable for assessing porous Mg-containing porous composite scaffolds [84,85].

Recent advances in additive manufacturing (AM) have further strengthened the translational value of bone defect models for Mg-containing scaffolds. As summarised by Li et al., AM biodegradable porous metals exhibit prominent strengths for bone defect repair, as their pore architecture, porosity, interconnectivity and mechanical properties can be customized to match regenerative demands of specific bony defects [86]. Nevertheless, Wang et al. highlighted persistent technical bottlenecks for AM Mg-based metallic implants, including oxidation control, internal defect suppression and tunable corrosion regulation [87].

In terms of preclinical animal research, bone defect models have evolved beyond simple void-filling assessments to investigate how Mg-containing systems modulate local microenvironments. Lai et al. fabricated low-temperature 3D-printed PLGA/TCP/Mg porous scaffolds, verifying that magnesium incorporation simultaneously enhances mechanical integrity of regenerated bone and boosts angiogenic/osteogenic bioactivities for complex defect reconstruction [47]. Recent research further expands the application scope of Mg scaffolds from conventional defect filling toward multifunctional, microenvironment-responsive bone regenerative systems. Li et al. engineered time-sequential 3D-printed MgO2/PLGA scaffolds for post-osteosarcoma bone defects; the scaffold initially releases reactive oxygen species to exert antitumor and antibacterial effects, followed by sustained Mg2+ secretion that stimulates osteogenesis and remodels pro-healing immune niches [88].

Collectively, existing evidence demonstrates that Mg-containing scaffold systems facilitate bone repair via multiple synergistic pathways: provision of interconnected porous networks for tissue ingrowth, sustained Mg2+ delivery, accelerated early angiogenesis, and coordinated coupling of osteogenic activity with local biological responses. Accordingly, animal bone defect models serve as pivotal platforms to validate magnesium biomaterials under clinically relevant conditions, covering not only scaffold degradability and mechanical performance but also their inherent osteogenic and other biological functions.

From an evidence-based medicine perspective, Zhang et al. reported substantial heterogeneity across animal studies of BMM for bone repair, encompassing animal species, anatomical sites, defect design, implant geometry and outcome metrics [89]. They concluded that standardized, unified evidence frameworks are urgently required to advance clinical translation. In summary, bone defect regeneration models constitute an indispensable translational platform for developing BMM porous scaffolds, bridging materials engineering, additive manufacturing and regenerative orthopaedic practice.

Other orthopaedic injury models

The biological and biomechanical environment is critical for meniscus healing. Zhang et al. repaired rabbit meniscal injuries using high-purity Mg sutures [66]. Compared with polymer absorbable sutures and blank controls, Mg sutures enhanced tissue regeneration at 12 weeks post-operation, maintained mechanical strength, and reduced cartilage degeneration. Mg promotes endogenous stem cell recruitment and fibrocartilage matrix synthesis, showing promise for in situ meniscus repair.

Rotator cuff injury is the leading cause of shoulder pain and dysfunction [90]. It has a prevalence reaching 65% in individuals over 80 [91]. In clinical practice, achieving fibrocartilage regeneration at the attachment site remains challenging with traditional anchors (e.g., titanium alloy, PLA), often leading to complications like bone defects and aseptic inflammation. To address this, Chen et al. repaired sheep rotator cuff injuries using high-purity Mg suture anchors [92]. In vivo results demonstrated that high-purity Mg suture anchor provided reliable anchoring function within 12 weeks, without causing any toxic effects on animal organs. Although gas cavities were generated during the degradation of the high-purity Mg suture anchor, no negative effects on implant fixation and tendon-bone healing were observed within 12 weeks. It is worth noting that gas cavities or radiolucent zones around BMMI have been reported in numerous studies involving both small [[93], [94], [95]], large animals [96,97] as well as clinical results [[98], [99], [100]]; however no harmful effect on bone healing was observed. Although hydrogen is a byproduct of Mg corrosion in aqueous media, Kuhlmann et al. have shown that hydrogen is only a minor component of these gas formations, as hydrogen can rapidly diffuse through surrounding tissues, including the skin and the vascular system [101]. Zhang et al. utilized Mg-2Zn-0.5Nd alloy wire to suture the supraspinatus tendon of a rat model with acute rotator cuff tears [67]. The Mg suture exhibited higher ultimate tensile strength in terms of tendon-bone healing. After 12 weeks, the Mg alloy suture showed significant fibrocartilage regeneration and mineralization transition structure, indicating a milder inflammatory response.

These findings demonstrate the potential of using high-purity Mg and Mg alloys to develop wires, screws, plates etc., in the repair and regeneration of rotator cuff injuries. They offer promising prospects for improving the clinical outcome of rotator cuff tear treatments by facilitating tendon-bone healing, reducing inflammation, and promoting fibrocartilage regeneration.

Clinical translation relevance

The development of orthopaedic implants generally follows a paradigm of “material exploration and mechanism clarification—preclinical animal validation—clinical translation and application”. Within this pathway, animal models serve not merely as isolated efficacy tests, but as a hierarchical bridge between fundamental biomaterials research and real-world orthopaedic practice. This bridging function is especially important for BMMI because their in vivo performance depends not only on biological compatibility, but also on the dynamic interaction among degradation behaviour, local tissue response, mechanical environment, and indication-specific healing requirements.

From a regulatory perspective, animal studies for BMMI should not be regarded merely as exploratory efficacy experiments, but as a critical component of the preclinical evidence package supporting clinical translation and future registration. Recent evidence-based analyses have highlighted that the translational value of animal studies of BMMI is always limited by heterogeneity in model selection, inadequate study design, and insufficient reporting quality. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, Zhang et al. demonstrated there is significant heterogeneity in animal studies on bone defect repair in terms of species, anatomical location, defect models, and outcome assessment. More high-quality and standardized evidence is urgently needed to promote clinical translation [89]. Similarly, Ding et al. assessed degradable metal studies for fracture and bone defect repair using the ARRIVE 2.0 framework and found generally low reporting quality, particularly for key design elements related to bias control and reproducibility [102]. These findings suggest that publicly available regulatory and evidence-based guidance currently supports a risk-based, indication-oriented, and evidence-quality-driven approach, rather than mandating one universal animal species or one fixed observation period for all orthopaedic applications. Accordingly, animal model selection should be based primarily on anatomical and biomechanical relevance to the target indication, while the observation period should be sufficient to capture both early healing performance and the ongoing degradation process of BMMI.

Significant interspecies differences in skeletal structure and biological characteristics establish a hierarchical preclinical validation system for BMMI, supporting a progressive verification system in the preclinical evaluation of BMMI. Pearce et al. reported that there are significant differences between rat and human bone tissues [103]. New Zealand white rabbits are the most widely adopted model for bone implant research due to their simple operation, low cost and high repeatability [104], despite their limited consistency with human skeletal biological characteristics [105]. Regardless of these species discrepancies, small animals such as rats and rabbits are applicable to the early-stage screening of BMMI. They enable rapid assessment of in vivo biosafety, degradation behaviour and biological effects, facilitate preliminary optimisation of material composition and structure design, which lays a foundation for subsequent large-animal efficacy verification and clinical research.

In advanced preclinical validation, canines exhibit a higher similarity to humans in bone composition, whereas their application is largely restricted by ethical concerns over companion animal research. In comparison, large animals including sheep, pigs and emus can avoid ethical and emotional controversies. Their body weight are highly consistent with human clinical conditions, which can faithfully simulate the in vivo environment of BMMI and accurately evaluate their osteogenic capacity, mechanical adaptability and long-term degradation performance. Data obtained from these large-animal models can be directly correlated with clinical scenarios, providing key references for implant selection and postoperative rehabilitation assessment. Nevertheless, their application is limited by difficult housing, complex operation and insufficient sample availability, restricting high-throughput repetitive experiments.

In conclusion, no animal model can perfectly replicate human physiological characteristics. Differentiated model selection based on research stage and clinical requirements is essential for ensuring preclinical data reliability and improving translational accuracy. A consensus hierarchical evaluation system has been established for BMMI preclinical research. Small animals (mice, rats, rabbits et al.) are used for early biosafety screening to clarify the basic in vivo performance of BMMI, while large animals (goats, sheep, beagle dogs, pigs, emus et al.) are applied for refined advanced validation to evaluate the clinical adaptability of BMMI. This hierarchical strategy compensates for the deficiencies of single-model evaluation, progressively verifies the in vivo safety and efficacy of BMMI, and provides systematic experimental support for clinical protocol optimisation, indication screening and risk control.

Current clinical progress of Mg-based metallic orthopaedic implants

MAGNEZIX® CS is a fracture-compression screw (Herbert screw) developed in Germany, and it represents the pioneering use of Mg screws commercially available for orthopaedic applications. Characterized as an MgYReZr alloy system, MAGNEZIX® CS is manufactured using a powder metallurgy process. Windhagen et al. conducted a prospective randomised controlled clinical study at the Orthopaedic Clinic of Hanover Medical College [106], which demonstrated that MAGNEZIX® screws exhibited comparable or superior performance in hallux valgus surgery when compared to traditional titanium implants. As orthopaedic surgeons gradually accepted biodegradable Mg screws, MAGNEZIX® screws have been used in cases such as radial styloid fractures, medial malleolar fractures, scaphoid fractures, elbow fractures, and intra-articular fracture fixation. Follow-up results (Fig. 5) showed that the degradation of the Mg alloy did not interfere with fracture healing.

In 2015, the K-MET bioresorbable bone screw with a chemical composition of Mg-5wt%Ca-1wt%Zn alloy, received approval from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety in Korea (MFDS, previously known as KFDA). The safety and efficacy of the Resomet screw was evaluated in patients with hand fractures who required internal fixation [100]. A one-year follow-up of 53 bone fracture cases revealed accelerated early bone healing, followed by complete replacement with new bone.

The eutectic reaction occurs during the solidification of the Mg alloy; therefore, secondary phases are distributed along the grain boundaries. Due to the chemical potential difference, micro-galvanic corrosion occurs between the second phase and the Mg matrix, which accelerates the corrosion rate [110]. In China, the first approved clinical trial involved the use of 99.99% high-purity Mg screws. The use of high-purity Mg screws mitigates concerns about potential toxicity linked to alloy elements, and high corrosion resistance is attainable [111], considering that no secondary phases emerge during manufacturing. Consequently, issues related to the degradation inhomogeneity of the second-phase materials and the influence of the degradation products are mitigated. Lastly, biodegradable high-purity Mg screws offer the benefits of a straightforward composition and enhanced biocompatibility [112,113].

In a hip-preserving treatment for stage II/III ONFH, according to the ARCO classification, biodegradable high-purity Mg screws were utilized to fix vascularized bone grafts [114]. In a 12-month long-term follow-up study (Fig. 6A), the treatment group fixed with high-purity Mg screws exhibited more favorable clinical outcomes and a higher Harris Hip Score (HHS) than that of the unfixed control group (n = 23 in the Mg group and n = 25 in the control group) [114]. No abnormal concentrations of calcium, Mg, or phosphorus were observed in the serum of the patients after surgery. Additionally, there were no significant differences in the liver or renal function between the high-purity Mg screw treatment and control groups. In another study, we used high-purity Mg screws to fix iliac bone grafts with vascular pedicles to treat displaced femoral neck fractures in young adults [115] (Fig. 6B). An average follow-up period of 16 months involving 18 patients revealed favorable therapeutic efficacy and a lower incidence of complications (such as ischaemic necrosis and non-union) in the high-purity Mg screw group than those in the control group fixed with traditional implants.

The aforementioned studies revealed the promising application potential of biodegradable high-purity Mg screws in orthopaedic clinic and encouraged Zhao et al. to explore bone flap fixation in a broader range of orthopaedic clinical indications. The latest unpublished data of the preoperative X-ray image of a 13-year-old female patient diagnosed with femoral head epiphyseal dislocation (SCFE) was shown in Fig. 6C, where the patient underwent treatment with two high-purity Mg screws. Over the 6-month follow-up period, the Mg screws gradually degraded, leading to a significant reduction in the shadow volume around the screws. The follow-up results revealed stable SCFE fixation without further slippage. In a patient with an avulsion fracture of the greater tubercle of the humerus, Zhao et al. successfully achieved fracture fixation using two high-purity Mg screws. Anteroposterior radiographs presented in Fig. 6D demonstrate excellent fracture healing effect and gradual degradation of the Mg screws after 18-month follow-up.

The clinical application of BMMI in ankle fractures has attracted worldwide attention. As the prominence of the metallic implant is a frequent cause of pain after fracture fixation, several BMMI have reported to show promising application in ankle fracture [98,[116], [117], [118]]. A biodegradable Mg-3Nd-0.2Zn-0.5Zr alloy (JDBM) exhibited favorable comprehensive mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and an almost uniform corrosion behaviour [119]. Furthermore, a calcium phosphate coating, predominantly composed of CaHPO4·2H2O (DCPD), was prepared using chemical conversion processes [120]. Following the application of the coating, the corrosion rate of the JDBM alloy in Hank's solution decreased to 0.39 mm/a, resulting in an approximately 30% enhancement in corrosion resistance. Furthermore, the haemolysis rate of the JDBM alloy decreased significantly from 48% to 0.68%, meeting the requirements of the medical device standards. In 2020, the clinical outcomes of nine patients with medial malleolus fractures treated using DCPD-coated JDBM screws were reported [98]. The efficacy and safety of these screws were assessed postoperatively using radiography, visual analogue scale (VAS) scores, and AOFAS scores. There were no instances of screw breakage before fracture healing at the 12-month follow-up. Fig. 7A showed preoperative (A1) and postoperative radiographs (A2-A6 represented 2, 3, 4, 13, 17 months preoperative) of a young female patient utilized two JDBM alloy screws to fix the trimalleolar fracture. The DCPD coating demonstrated the ability to decrease the degradation rate of the Mg alloy matrix while reducing the radiation transmittance in the vicinity of the implant, showing promising clinical applications [98].

In late March 2023, the RemeOs™ trauma screw (Finland) received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), marking the introduction of the biodegradable metallic implant to the US market. With the composition of rare-earth element-free Mg alloys, the mid-term results of a clinical study using Mg-0.45 wt%Ca-0.45 wt%Zn (ZX00) were first disclosed in 2021. Between July 2018 and October 2019, 20 patients with isolated biankle or triankle fractures who underwent fixation with ZX00 screws were enrolled [[116], [117], [118]]. Follow-up assessments conducted 6, 12 months and updated to a mean of 2.5 ± 0.6 years postoperatively revealed positive patient-reported outcome measures. Fig. 7B showed radiographic evaluation of a 20-year-old male patient with a trimalleolar fracture, received two ZX00 screws for the medial malleolus fracture and locking compression plates for the lateral and posterior malleolus fractures. Postoperative radiographs were obtained at 6 weeks (B1), 12 months (B2) and 34 months (B3) after surgery. They rated the visibility of the ZX00 screws as (+++) at 6 weeks, (+/++) at 1 year, and (−) at the final follow-up. During the whole observation period, one female patient discontinued participation 17 months due to a second ankle surgery by an old osteochondral lesion, and another female patient was unreachable 12-week post operatively, the other 18 patients with complete clinical radiographic data showed good ankle function, without complications or the necessity for screw removal.

High-purity Mg screw also indicated its potential in ankle fractures. In the recent long-term follow-up study [121], twelve patients were treated with high-purity Mg screws with conventional titanium alloy plates and screws as control. None of the patients reported infection, malunion, failure of internal fixation or other complications postoperative. Fig. 7C showed representative radiograph results of a 29-year-old female patient with a trimalleolar fracture (C1, preoperative; C2, the fracture was fixed with four high-purity Mg screws; C3, 91 weeks follow-up). The 91 weeks follow-up results showed a satisfactory fracture arrangement, with hardly noticeable high-purity Mg screws and the radiolucent zones. Biodegradable Mg screws offer greater advantages than bioinert implants by avoiding the risk of skin irritation associated with material conflict.

Despite promising therapeutic outcomes reported for BMMI in several studies, their clinical translation faces significant challenges. A recent retrospective study on the MAGNEZIX® screws for scaphoid fractures revealed concerning results, sounding a cautionary note for its comprehensive clinical evaluation [122]. In this long-term study of 20 patients with scaphoid fractures or nonunions, with a mean follow-up of 78.3 months, the overall nonunion rate was 40%. Postoperative imaging identified screw breakage in 25% of cases and periscrew cystic lesions in the majority. Although functional scores indicated moderate recovery (mean Q-DASH: 11.5; mean Mayo Wrist Score: 75.7), grip strength and wrist range of motion on the injured side were significantly inferior to the contralateral side. The investigators concluded that Mg screws may fail to provide sufficient stability for small bones like the scaphoid, which has a unique vascular supply and complex biomechanical environment, with the risk of failure markedly increased in complex scenarios such as delayed fixation or established nonunion. However, another study on the same MAGNEZIX® screws reported markedly different clinical outcomes [109]. In a cohort of 8 acute fractures and 4 nonunions with a mean follow-up of approximately 32 months, all acute fractures achieved union, and 3 of the 4 nonunions healed. Functional outcomes were favorable, with the acute fracture group attaining a mean modified Mayo Wrist Score of 95. This finding stands in stark contrast to reports of favorable outcomes with Mg screws in other anatomical sites, highlighting the highly site-specific efficacy of magnesium-based implants.

In 2023, the utilization of Resomet® K-wire (Mg-Ca alloy, with diameter of 1.6 mm, length of 100 mm) was reported in South Korea for intramedullary fixation in 19 patients with metacarpal or phalangeal fractures [99]. This study included 20 cases of 19 patients who sought treatment at the clinic between May 2019 and July 2021. All patients achieved bone healing after the K-wire implantation with an average bone healing duration of 10.5 weeks. Compared with conventional absorbable materials, K-wires exhibit higher rigidity. While highlighting the K-wire efficacy in promoting bone healing, researchers have underscored the importance of remaining vigilant regarding the potential complications associated with stiffness and deformity. These studies depict a complex picture regarding the evaluation of this emerging material and technology of BMMI. Discrepancies in outcomes may be attributable to critical factors such as differences in fracture complexity and follow-up duration between the studies. Furthermore, variations in patient demographics (e.g., age, gender distribution) could influence bone healing capacity. Future efforts should focus on delineating the safe and effective boundaries of its application through more rigorous research, precise patient selection criteria, standardized surgical protocols, and optimised implant design. Importantly, establishing and disseminating a standardized surgical technical guideline for BMMI application is essential. This should encompass precise intraoperative imaging guidance, drilling and fixation principles, and compatible instrumentation to minimize technique-related variability and its impact on outcomes.

Beyond screws, wires, and other fixation devices, Mg-containing scaffold systems have also entered clinical translation. A landmark recent advance is the prospective multicenter randomized controlled trial reported by Gong et al., wherein a low-temperature-derived 3D-printed biodegradable Mg-containing composite porous scaffold (Bongolle®) was assessed for treating critical-size bone defects in trauma-related extremity injuries [123]. The composite scaffold was fabricated with a composition of 10% ± 3% Mg, 80% ± 3% PLGA, and 10% ± 3% β-TCP via a 3D low-temperature rapid prototyping (LT-RP) printing machine. The Mg-containing scaffold achieved significantly faster residual bone fusion at 12 weeks than the control scaffold, while both groups showed similarly high fusion rates at 24 weeks. The study also reported a favorable biosafety profile based on blood chemistry monitoring and preclinical safety evaluation. From a translational perspective, this study is important because it extends Mg-based orthopaedic biomaterials from fixation devices to regenerative bone substitutes and provides higher-level clinical evidence for their use in critical-size regenerative bone defect repair.

Collectively, despite the encouraging available data of BMMI, the long-term in vivo safety across diverse indications still limit their broader and more reliable adoption. The primary clinical translation challenge lies in the mismatch between implant degradation and the biological healing procedure. Excessively rapid degradation may compromise the mechanical integrity of implants prior to adequate fracture union or graft fusion. Recognized as a fundamental drawback of BMMIs, this limitation is highly indication-dependent. Recent investigations have demonstrated that this issue can be addressed via combinatorial strategies integrating material composition modulation, surface modification, and structural design. For example, high-purity Mg reduces the influence of secondary phases and thereby mitigates inhomogeneous corrosion and micro-galvanic degradation [[111], [112], [113]]. Surface modification has been validated as an effective strategy to improve osseointegration and corrosion control [84,124]. DCPD brushite-coated JDBM screws exhibit suppressed corrosion rates and favorable safety profiles for medial malleolar fracture treatment [98,120,125]. In mechanically demanding settings, Mg/Ti hybrid fixation systems offer another targeted solution by combining the early strength of inert metals with the biological advantages of Mg degradation [57].

The second translational barrier arises from clinical management associated with hydrogen gas evolution and radiolucent zone formation. Kuhlmann et al. demonstrated that hydrogen is only a minor component of these cavities and can escape rapidly through surrounding tissues and the vascular system [101]. Multiple preclinical animal studies and clinical reports corroborate that transient gas cavities or radiolucent regions do not inherently impede bone healing [[92], [93], [94], [95], [96],101]. Even so, gas accumulation remains clinically relevant, as its volume, location and persistence may signal insufficient corrosion regulation, interfere with radiographic interpretation, or indicate localized pathological risks. Accordingly, targeted mitigation strategies should prioritize early corrosion rate suppression rather than mere gas management. Clinically, standardized postoperative radiographic follow-up protocols are required to differentiate benign transient radiolucency from progressive cystic lesions or fixation failure.

The third translational obstacle is the insufficient datasets of systematic long-term in vivo safety evidence, especially covering various indications, anatomical sites and patient cohorts. While accumulating preliminary data yield encouraging outcomes, inconsistencies persist in follow-up durations and endpoint definitions. The adverse long-term outcomes observed in scaphoid fixation procedures demonstrate that the safety and efficacy of BMMIs cannot be generalized to all implantation sites [122]. Future clinical translation efforts should therefore adopt indication-specific long-term evaluation frameworks encompassing the following metrics: implant visibility and resorption, radiopacity deficiency or cystic degeneration, retention of fixation stability, osseointegration quality, serum biochemical markers, visceral organ function parameters, and revision or implant failure rates.

Future prospects of BMMI: opportunities and challenges

Biodegradable Mg-based orthopaedic implants have progressed from conceptual exploration to early-stage clinical translation. Nevertheless, the next phase of development should be guided by a clinical-oriented and regulation-aware roadmap. First, degradation behaviour is supposed to be synchronised with indication-specific healing biology and mechanical demand, as degradation remains the most fundamental translational issue in the field of BMMI. The core goal is to define an appropriate degradation window for each orthopaedic scenario.Second, long-term safety and efficacy shall be established as primary translational endpoints. Although short- and mid-term outcomes are encouraging in several indications [[116], [117], [118],121], the long-term safety boundaries of BMMI remain insufficiently defined. Future studies shall establish standardized long-term follow-up frameworks encompassing not only fracture union and patient-reported outcomes, but also implant degradation kinetics, radiolucent or cystic change, gas evolution, fixation durability, osseointegration quality, delayed local inflammatory reactions, and serum biochemical indices where appropriate. In addition to prospective clinical trials, post-market surveillance and registry-based real-world data are indispensable for identifying delayed or less frequent complications across broader patient populations. This is especially important given the indication-specific heterogeneity already observed in the field [109,118,121,122]. Third, implant design shall shift toward personalized, indication-specific customization. Current evidence indicates that the satisfied performance of BMMI depends on local biomechanics, vascularity and required support duration. Therefore, future innovation should move away from a “one device for multiple indications” strategy. Instead, implant geometry, such as thread design, diameter, and surface modification, corresponding surgical instruments compatibility should be tailored to the target application. From a clinical trial perspective, this necessitates refined patient stratification criteria in future trials. Fourth, successful translation hinges on tighter integration of regulatory science, standardized evaluation protocols, and cross-disciplinary collaboration. The development of innovative biomaterials is frequently hampered by a lack of dedicated evaluation methodologies, technical specifications, and regulatory guidance at early R&D stages [126,127]. Regulatory authorities including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), European Medicines Agency (EMA), and National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) have already introduced broader frameworks for innovative materials and medical devices [[128], [129], [130], [131], [132]]. However, the next practical step for BMMI is not merely stricter regulation, but better alignment between research design and regulatory requirements. Preclinical and clinical programs moving forward shall prioritize long-term safety and efficacy endpoints. Published regulatory guidelines generally emphasize risk-based evidence adequacy rather than mandating uniform animal species or identical testing workflows for all orthopaedic indications. Accordingly, a regulation-driven translational strategy shall integrate scientifically validated animal models, clinically relevant outcome metrics, and registration-ready evidentiary datasets. Within this regulatory framework, commonly used large animals such as beagle dogs may still retain value in regulatory-oriented studies despite ethical concerns, whereas goats and sheep remain highly important for orthopaedic preclinical validation because of their biomechanical relevance. By contrast, less conventional species such as emus may serve niche mechanistic or disease-modeling research purposes, but they are less commonly incorporated into a regulatory-oriented evidence framework than conventional large-animal species.

It is anticipated that with the further improvement of relevant regulations and standards, BMMI will better meet the demands of personalized medicine care, promote medical technology innovation, and ultimately achieve safer and more effective clinical outcomes. Standing at a promising new starting point, the development and application of BMMI herald revolutionary changes in the field of orthopaedic implants. This transformation will not only alter traditional treatment methods, but also offer patients with more reliable and durable solutions, while simultaneously reducing the risk of long-term complications, thereby improving the quality of patient life.
