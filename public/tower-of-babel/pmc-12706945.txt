From exceptionalism to universal testing: an historical review of HIV testing in Portugal
Authors: Raul Manarte, Margarida R. Henriques
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPortugal has made significant progress over the last 40 years in the response against the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection and the Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS). However, as of 2021, it remained one of the three Western European countries with the highest rates of new HIV infections and AIDS cases. Initially shaped by HIV exceptionalism, Portugal began transitioning to a universal testing strategy in 2011. This study provides a historical and policy review of the evolution of HIV testing in Portugal, focusing on alignment with international guidelines and national implementation outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a narrative policy review covering the period from 1998 to 2024. Our analysis drew on scientific literature, national health plans, surveillance reports, and international guidelines. Documents were selected through structured searches in multiple academic databases and government repositories using relevant Portuguese and English search terms. Thematically coded findings were mapped chronologically and assessed against evolving WHO and CDC recommendations.ResultsPortugal gradually moved from a targeted testing approach to a more comprehensive, universal strategy. Key policy shifts occurred in 2011 and 2017, accompanied by an expansion of testing modalities, including self-testing and community-based testing. Despite these developments, implementation has been uneven. The lack of standardized protocols, limited integration into primary healthcare, and regional disparities tied to a contract-based health system have contributed to inconsistent service delivery. Additionally, testing uptake among older adults, migrants, and other key populations remains suboptimal. The COVID-19 pandemic temporarily disrupted testing services but also accelerated the use of self-testing strategies.DiscussionPortugal’s experience illustrates the challenges of operationalizing universal HIV testing within a hybrid public–private healthcare system. Although policies increasingly reflect international best practices, structural barriers continue to hinder equitable implementation.ConclusionTo close existing testing gaps, Portugal must strengthen implementation by standardizing procedures, improve disaggregated data collection in monitoring systems, and ensure greater integration of HIV testing in primary healthcare. Enhanced outreach to underserved populations will be critical to achieving national and international HIV prevention targets. This paper offers a historical and policy perspective to inform more equitable and effective national testing strategies.

Introduction

Portugal is widely recognized as a public health success story in the fight against HIV/AIDS, particularly for having achieved the UNAIDS 90–90-90 targets—three years ahead of the 2020 deadline [85, 104, 133, 153, 154]. These targets, focused on diagnosing, treating, and achieving viral suppression in people living with HIV (PLWH), have become global benchmarks for effective HIV responses. However, despite this progress, Portugal remained among the countries with the highest rates of new HIV infections and AIDS diagnoses in Western Europe as of 2021 [48].

This apparent paradox—high performance on international benchmarks coexisting with ongoing domestic challenges—raises important questions about how HIV testing policies in Portugal have evolved and how effectively they have been implemented. This review examines the development of HIV testing policies between 1998 and 2024, and the extent to which they have ensured equitable access for key and vulnerable populations. In particular, it explores the gap between policy and practice, and how structural, regional, and institutional factors have shaped access to testing over time.

At the national level, HIV testing in Portugal is governed by the Directorate-General of Health (DGS), while epidemiological surveillance is overseen by the National Institute of Health Dr. Ricardo Jorge (INSA). Implementation occurs across a mixed health system that includes public primary care centers, hospitals, and a network of community-based and civil society organizations. These actors have distinct but overlapping responsibilities, often shaped by regional health administrations and shifting programmatic priorities. Over the past two decades, Portugal has gradually transitioned from an exceptionalist approach—characterized by voluntary testing and extensive counseling—to a more universal, routine testing model aligned with international guidelines [17, 56, 151, 156]. This article provides a critical historical and policy review of HIV counseling and testing in Portugal from 1998 to 2024, analyzing how the country transitioned from exceptionalism to universal testing, and identifying policy gaps, implementation barriers, and population-level disparities in access to testing services. It situates the Portuguese experience within the broader context of global HIV policy, particularly by comparing alignment with WHO and CDC recommendations.

This review is original in its integration of epidemiological data, national policies, grey literature, and international guidelines to assess not only what policies exist, but how they have been translated (or not) into practice. In doing so, it highlights underexplored issues—such as disparities in testing among migrants and people over 50, the decentralization of testing services, and the impact of austerity and the COVID-19 pandemic on program delivery.

By offering this combined historical and comparative perspective, the review aims to inform improvements in Portugal’s national HIV testing strategy and provide policy-relevant insights for countries with similar hybrid health systems and regional disparities.

Methods

This study employed a narrative policy review methodology to analyze the evolution of HIV testing strategies in Portugal between 1998 and 2024. The goal was to identify major policy milestones, examine implementation outcomes, and assess alignment with international recommendations from organizations such as the WHO, CDC, UNAIDS, and ECDC.

Rationale and timeframe

The selected timeframe begins in 1998, a turning point in Portugal’s HIV testing policy. That year marked the formal integration of HIV screening into prenatal care, as well as initial efforts to structure voluntary counseling and testing services [3, 20, 38, 39]. This period also aligns with a global shift in HIV testing paradigms—from exceptionalism toward more routine, provider-initiated strategies which reinforced the need for structured national approaches to testing [17, 147, 151, 156]. The review includes documents up to May 2024 to ensure coverage of the most recent national and international developments.

Data sources and search strategy

The review drew on a range of materials, including: Peer-reviewed scientific literature; National policy documents, health norms, and strategic plans from the Directorate-General of Health (DGS); Surveillance reports from the National Institute of Health (INSA); International guidelines from WHO, UNAIDS, ECDC, and CDC; National legislation, decrees, and official communiqués; Grey literature, such as implementation manuals, program evaluations, and NGO reports.

Structured searches were performed using databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and SciELO. Search terms included combinations of the following in both Portuguese and English: HIV testing in Portugal, HIV policy, AIDS Portugal, testing guidelines, timeline, PrEP, key populations, and community-based testing. Government and institutional repositories were manually searched to retrieve official documents and reports. Inclusion criteria were: publication between January 1998 and May 2024; full-text availability; and relevance to HIV testing policy, strategy, or implementation in Portugal.

Analytical approach

Documents were first screened for relevance through titles and abstracts. Full texts were then manually reviewed using a structured reading guide. The reading guide was developed by the lead author and applied independently by two researchers to ensure consistency in thematic extraction. This guide organized information into the following categories: Types of testing strategies (e.g., voluntary counseling and testing, provider-initiated testing, community-based testing, self-testing); Target populations (e.g., men who have sex with men [MSM], migrants, older adults); Policy changes and timelines; Implementation barriers and system-level limitations; Alignment with international recommendations. Although no formal coding software or qualitative data analysis tools were used, reliability was supported through triangulation of sources (e.g., matching policy documents with surveillance data and literature). This approach supported internal consistency and helped ensure that interpretations were grounded in multiple forms of evidence. No formal quality appraisal of individual documents was conducted, consistent with the descriptive and interpretive goals of narrative policy reviews.

Themes and patterns were identified through repeated reading and comparative analysis. These were then organized chronologically to construct a detailed narrative of how national testing policy evolved, and how that evolution corresponded to—or diverged from—global frameworks (e.g., [17, 56, 146, 147, 151, 156].

Results

Early steps: exceptionalism

By 1985 the antibody HIV testing had become widespread in industrialized nations [128]. While some defended wide-scale testing, others saw the test as a risk of adding a social burden at a time when little could be done from a healthcare perspective to treat the infection [8]. Thus, early testing policies and guidelines stressed voluntarism, counseling and confidentiality [15, 144]. There was still much confusion about its reliability and fear of discrimination in case of a positive test result in areas such as life insurance and job application [115]. According to this view, the HIV test could not be considered as just another test. Early HIV testing policies were shaped by what Bayer [7] termed “HIV exceptionalism”—the idea that HIV, unlike other infectious diseases, required a unique ethical and policy framework due to its social, political, and medical implications. This perspective emphasized voluntarism, informed consent, confidentiality, and pre- and post-test counseling, setting HIV apart from other sexually transmitted or life-threatening infections [15, 103, 144].

In contrast, the “traditional public health perspective,” as described by Bayer [7], treated HIV testing like any other communicable disease control measure. It favored routine, sometimes even mandatory, screening—often applied regardless of symptoms or individual risk—and was typically conducted under assumptions of presumed consent. Within this view, testing was considered a neutral tool of medical diagnosis and surveillance, not one requiring exceptional ethical safeguards. Advocates of this approach argued that broad testing was essential to identify cases early and integrate HIV into standard public health frameworks [7, 8, 31, 144].

Defenders of the exceptionalist stance strongly opposed this model, arguing that generalized testing—meaning widespread screening beyond clearly defined risk groups—could deter individuals from seeking healthcare due to fears of stigma, marginalization, or legal repercussions. They viewed mandatory or routine testing as an infringement on fundamental rights, particularly those related to autonomy, confidentiality, and informed consent [7, 8].

Counseling and testing on the international stage

Between 1993 and 1994, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) published updated HIV counseling and testing guidelines, replacing the previous ones from 1987 [16], and the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNAIDS proposed Voluntary Counseling and Testing (VCT) as a strategy for the prevention and control of HIV [129, 144, 146].

Internationally, counseling was recommended for all individuals at risk for HIV infection regardless of the test result, as it was considered effective in reducing risk behavior and considered cost-effective with no negative impact [17, 66, 124]). VCT goals became broader as rapid testing and mobile testing centers allowed for better access in community and rural settings and as VCT spread to many countries [6]. These goals ranged from preventing HIV transmission and acquisition, to promoting early uptake of services—including medical, emotional, social, and legal support. Additional aims included normalizing testing, reducing stigma, increasing awareness, and supporting adherence to treatment [6, 61, 74, 112, 123]. VCT became widely practiced around the world with great heterogeneity in national guidelines and practices [6, 32, 105]. It was practiced mainly in a client-initiated approach and depended on a person’s deliberate and voluntary decision to seek it [151].

Challenging the exceptionalism view

From the mid-1990s onward, the previously widespread consensus on the indispensability of counseling and the exceptional status of HIV testing began to be challenged, driven by changing clinical and political contexts [8]. Some argued that exceptionalist standards, such as the requirement for extensive pre-test counseling and strict confidentiality protocols, may have contributed to under-testing and delayed diagnosis in high-prevalence settings. De Cock et al. [31] raised early and influential concerns about whether counseling requirements—while well-intentioned—were sustainable or even appropriate in contexts like sub-Saharan Africa, where the scale of the epidemic demanded more pragmatic approaches. They suggested that these procedures might have hindered rapid testing scale-up and thus delayed life-saving interventions. They argued that prioritizing anonymity—beyond basic confidentiality—may have fostered secrecy and discouraged openness and normalization. Ironically, these protective measures may have increased the shame and social isolation experienced by people living with HIV. Bayer and Edington [8] later contended that such procedures, including individualized counseling, might reinforce stigma by portraying HIV as fundamentally different from other infections, thereby further discouraging testing [8, 31].

Another important development in the 1990 s was that antiretroviral therapy began to have a significant impact on the clinical course of the infection in developed nations, transforming the knowledge of one’s serostatus into an important factor for clinical decision-making and prevention [23, 100, 126]. This shift laid the groundwork for global discussions on how testing could be expanded beyond voluntary models without compromising ethical standards.

In 2001 the WHO convened a consultative meeting to discuss ways to increase knowledge of serostatus. Although the meeting reaffirmed the counseling and testing service delivery model, participants in the meeting acknowledged that the demanding procedures of this standard may in fact help perpetuate the stigma associated with HIV and that one-to-one individual pre-test counseling is not an absolute prerequisite. Instead, it should be replaced by pretest information [8, 31, 145]. While pre-test counseling refers to a personalized, often face-to-face session addressing risk, consent, and emotional preparedness, pre-test information involves the standardized delivery of essential facts—typically through brief oral explanations, written materials, or videos—without the need for individualized counseling [8, 156], (ECDC, 2010). The goal of this shift was to maintain ethical standards while improving testing uptake and program efficiency.

A year later, the change in paradigm was clear: the WHO called for the routine offer of HIV testing in antenatal clinics, in clinical settings where patients showed symptoms of AIDS and in any context where it could improve patient care [147]. In contexts where antiretroviral therapy was available, HIV testing gained a fundamental role in linking individuals to care and enough evidence had been compiled to support the impact of knowing one’s serological status in behavioral change, including reductions in unprotected sex and partner numbers, increased condom use, earlier care-seeking, and improved disclosure to partners [8, 89, 147]. These behavioral changes are key to reducing onward transmission and improving linkage to care. In the following years, support grew for scaling up HIV testing and adopting less resource-intensive approaches to counseling [31, 130, 131].

Universal testing and community-based approaches

Universal testing, along with antiretroviral therapy, were becoming important tools to tackle the disease at a global level [65]. In 2005, only 10% of people living with HIV knew their status (WHO & UNAIDS, 2007). By 2007, WHO’s efforts to scale up testing were consolidated into a new guidance document, emphasizing that provider initiated testing in health facilities should be offered to all patients in an “opt-out” approach, with simplified pre-test information as a viable alternative of pre-test counseling (WHO & UNAIDS, 2007). Widespread provider initiated testing proved useful in identifying undiagnosed individuals [72, 109].

Addressing the rapidly growing levels of the epidemic in eastern Europe, in 2011 the WHO consolidated an action plan for the European region that, among other issues, advocated for provider initiated testing in health care settings and promotion of rapid testing in appropriate settings [149]. The WHO started advocating for a mixture of different models of HIV testing and counseling according to the nature of the epidemic, cost-effectiveness, equity of access and resources available, advocating for both facility and community based interventions [151].

Currently, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) recommends universal HIV testing in high-prevalence settings and regular testing—every 3–12 months—for key populations, depending on local epidemiology and individual risk [56, 60, 142]. In the United States, the CDC advises at least one lifetime test for individuals aged 13 to 64, with periodic testing for those at increased risk [17].

Vulnerable populations—often referred to in international guidelines as key populations—are groups that are disproportionately affected by HIV infection, regardless of whether the epidemic is generalized or concentrated [134, 139]. According to WHO and UNAIDS, these include men who have sex with men (MSM), sex workers, people who inject drugs, transgender individuals, prisoners, and, in some settings, migrants. These groups experience both higher infection rates and lower access to testing and prevention services. Their heightened vulnerability stems not only from behavioral factors but also from broader legal, social, and structural conditions—such as stigma, criminalization, and social exclusion—that increase exposure to HIV and hinder access to care [145].

Periodic testing refers to repeated HIV tests at defined intervals based on risk exposure and local epidemiology. The recommended frequency varies by population and should be tailored to context [56, 60]. Routine testing, by contrast, involves systematically offering HIV testing in specific settings—such as sexual health clinics or prisons—regardless of symptoms or patient request. For individuals at high risk, testing is generally recommended at least annually [56, 60].

Alongside facility-based strategies, community-based testing approaches became an essential complement, particularly for key populations [131, 150]. These models often rely on peer-led initiatives delivered in trusted community settings—such as NGOs, drop-in centers, mobile clinics, and outreach services. A key strength of these models is peer-to-peer engagement, where service providers share similar backgrounds, experiences, or identities with those being tested [68]. This peer-based dynamic fosters trust, reduces stigma, and helps overcome barriers related to discrimination, criminalization, or prior negative experiences in formal healthcare systems [83, 95]. It also promotes shared language, mutual understanding, and culturally congruent support—especially important for men who have sex with men (MSM), sex workers, people who inject drugs, and transgender individuals [19, 94]. Evidence shows that such approaches not only increase uptake of testing and early diagnosis but can also support HIV self-testing and treatment adherence among vulnerable groups [19, 127]. These interventions help ensure that counseling and testing are perceived as supportive and empowering, rather than clinical or judgmental. Community-based strategies proved effective in increasing early HIV diagnoses, reducing HIV-related stigma, and enabling timely prevention interventions. They also showed strong uptake rates and potential cost-effectiveness compared to facility-based testing. [111, 123]. These approaches are essential for achieving equity in HIV prevention and care, particularly in contexts where formal systems remain inaccessible or untrusted. Even familial models of counseling and testing—such as couples-based or family-supported approaches—have been explored as culturally appropriate ways to increase uptake in certain contexts [141].

Exceptionalism in Portugal

In 1983, the first AIDS case was diagnosed in Portugal. By 1985 the country had established a case notification system and the first national work group was established (Table 1; [22, 28]. Its objectives were to detect AIDS cases (according to CDC guidelines), keep track of incidence levels, give information back to the health services and the WHO [27]. In 1986 this group determined mandatory testing for all dialysis patients. In 1993 the Ministry of Health and the National Association of Pharmacies started a nationwide needle-exchange program for people who inject drugs (PWID) (Table 1; [25]). In 1998, the Portuguese parliament recognized prenatal diagnosis as an essential component of prenatal health care and the importance of optimizing resources, which were still insufficient in Portugal to fully meet existing needs [20, 38]. Specifically, a set of guiding principles was published for primary health care, prenatal diagnostic centers in perinatal support hospitals and prenatal diagnostic and therapeutic centers in differentiated perinatal support hospitals. Until 1998, there was no official universal policy on HIV screening during pregnancy. From then on, it was routinely proposed to pregnant women, patients with tuberculosis and sexually transmitted infections, and prisoners (Table 1) [20, 33, 39, 41]. In 1999 the country launched a national strategy on drug addiction that encompassed harm reduction strategies, proposals for prevention, treatment and re-integration, and in 2000 consumption of drugs was decriminalized (Table 1, [10]). Between 2004 and 2012, Portugal consistently had the highest HIV incidence and AIDS rates in Western Europe [22, 44, 58–60]. In 2004, Portugal's HIV incidence was 9.5 per 100,000 population, more than double the Western European average of 4.2. Although incidence gradually declined over the years, by 2012 it remained high at 8.5 in Portugal compared to 3.8 in Western Europe. This persistent disparity highlights the unique epidemiological burden faced by the country during this period. In response to this sustained epidemiological burden, the National Program for HIV/AIDS 2012–2016 outlined key priorities related to prevention, early diagnosis, and improved access to care, including for vulnerable populations such as migrants [42]. However, while the program reaffirmed the importance of timely testing, it lacked clear operational guidance on how to implement universal or opt-out HIV testing models in either primary care or community-based settings.

In 2017 Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) became freely available for key populations (KP) including PWID (Table 1; [45]). As a result of these measures, the rate of yearly diagnosis linked to PWID has dropped from 50% to 2.5% between 2000 and 2022 [28, 48, 118].

The foundation for provision of counseling and testing in Portugal started in 1998 with the creation of 2 VCTs in Lisbon, one created by the local health authority (Sub-Região de saúde de Lisboa) and another by a private foundation (Fundação da Nossa Senhora do Bom Sucesso, Northern Regional Health Administration [ARSN], 2017; [106]). These centers already operated in a free of charge, confidential and client-initiated provision of counseling and testing that characterizes VCT. In 2000 another testing center started activities in Faro and in 2001 the Portuguese National Commission to Fight AIDS (CNLCS) recognized the importance of expanding national coverage for counseling and testing nationwide, determining that every district should have a VCT center called CAD (Centro de Aconselhamento e Deteção) [3, 26, 106]. In 2006, rapid tests were integrated into the CAD network, and the number of HIV tests performed grew sustainably until 2008 when the number hit 25,894 tests (Table 1; [48]). The introduction of rapid tests brought a new dynamic to counseling and testing. With results available in minutes, pre- and post-test counseling could occur on the same day—even within a single session—without compromising counseling effectiveness [91]. Between 2007 and 2014 the CAD network was responsible for detecting 11.3% of new HIV cases in the country [44]. Since 2010 the number of tests performed in the CADs has been gradually reducing (Fig. 1) (from 15,989 in 2014 to 7,270 in 2022) while the percentage of positive results has been increasing (from 0.95% in 2014 to 3.5% in 2022) [49]. These concurrent trends likely reflect broader shifts in the national testing landscape—such as the expansion of testing in PHC, NGO, and pharmacy settings—rather than a direct causal relationship. According to some of the CAD professionals, the reduction in the number of people using these services may be related to a progressive lack of need for this specialized response or may be related to other factors like access, public awareness about the centers, reduced proactivity of the centers, poor location and stigma [3]. Through the years, the CADs have suffered changes regarding structure, functioning procedures, and human resources [3]. The last CAD opened in 2005 and in that year 22 centers where operational (Aveiro, Beja, Bragança, Castelo Branco, Coimbra, Évora, Faro, Guarda, Leiria, 3 in Lisbon, Portalegre, Porto, Santarém, 3 in Setúbal, Viseu, Viana do Castelo e Vila Real; the centers in Portalegre, Guarda and Vila Real have since been closed and Porto opened a mobile center in 2006) [3]. In 2023, according to DGS, there are 14 CADs in operation and internal documents suggest that the model of anonymity, pre and post-counseling is still used [3, 12, 48]. Another factor that might help explain the diminishing number of users in the CAD network is the change in national testing strategies towards universal testing [4, 41, 156].

From exceptionalism to universal testing in Portugal

Before 2011 HIV testing was performed mainly in the CAD network. In Primary Health Care (PHC) settings HIV screening was mainly non routine, provider or patient initiated or when there were symptoms of opportunistic infection [25]. In 2011, the Portuguese parliament adopted a resolution to expand routine, population testing in primary and secondary health care settings (Table 1; [4, 41]). This decision follows a strategy in line with the recommendations from the WHO, the CDC and the French Haute Autorité de Sante [11, 70, 147–149, 156];. As a result, the Portuguese National Health Service started offering HIV testing as a routine option for all individuals aged 18–64 who interact with the public health system. Portugal’s guidelines recommended targeted testing for key populations with higher risk of exposure, such as MSM, people who inject drugs, and sex workers, but do not establish specific intervals or routine testing protocols [43].

This strategy followed the opt-out model, in which patients are informed of the test and must actively decline it if they do not wish to proceed [156]. This strategy was implemented in order to enhance the number of people who knew their serological status, to increase early diagnosis, to diminish stigma and discrimination associated with testing and for people who were diagnosed to start preventive measures as soon as possible [41]. Studies suggest that an opt-out strategy offered universally to health care users would be favored by physicians in the country and that they consider it important to increase testing with strategies such as self-testing, testing outside health care and a more proactive attitude by health care providers [71]. Evidence from Portugal supports the cost-effectiveness of routine and expanded HIV screening, especially in high-risk populations [157]. Furthermore, Portuguese health professionals expressed openness to treating HIV testing similarly to other diagnostic tests, reducing barriers such as pre-test counseling requirements [71]. There is some evidence suggesting that the austerity measures implemented in 2011 in the aftermath of the 2008 economic crises may have caused a decline in access to health care in Portugal particularly in population groups who do not benefit from exemption of user charges [80, 82, 114]. The crisis led to budget cuts in HIV prevention and treatment in many countries [140].

In 2012, the National Programme for HIV/AIDS reaffirmed the importance of early diagnosis and prevention but provided only general objectives regarding HIV testing, without specific guidance on how routine or universal testing should be operationalized within PHC settings [42]. This lack of implementation detail may have contributed to delays in fully integrating routine HIV testing into general medical practice. It was only in 2014 that Portugal began rolling out rapid tests in PHC centers, reversing the prior downward trend in testing performed within these structures (Fig. 2) [44].

In 2017, Cascais, Lisbon, and Porto joined the Fast-Track Cities initiative, signaling a political and programmatic commitment to accelerate HIV diagnosis and linkage to care, especially among key populations. This global partnership—led by UNAIDS, the United Nations Human Settlements Program (UN-Habitat), and the International Association of Providers of AIDS Care (IAPAC)—aims to strengthen and align urban HIV responses, particularly in high-incidence cities, to meet the UNAIDS 90–90-90 targets [75]. In Portugal, as of April 2024, Lisbon, Porto, Sintra, Odivelas, Amadora, Oeiras, Almada, Cascais, Loures, Região da Madeira, Portimão and Vila Nova de Gaia were part of this initiative [76].

In 2019, in an attempt to reach KP, the Portuguese government initiated the sale of self-testing kits with 3,292 tests sold to the public between October of 2019 and August of 2020, which was considered a good uptake during that initial 10-month period (Table 1; [5, 35]). However, no data is available on the post-test linkage to care, something that a recent systematic review found to be a difficult challenge [159].

Data from the National Institute for Health (INSA) and the Directorate-General of Health (DGS) states that in 2022, 373,031 tests were prescribed in PHC centers (Fig. 2) and 13,508 rapid tests were performed (Fig. 1) [46]. This implies an increase of 63% between 2020 and 2022 [48]. The number of tests performed in Voluntary HIV Counseling and Testing Centers (CAD) dropped 71%, between 2014 and 2022 (Fig. 1), and only 15 of these centers remain active [47]. Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) and community based organizations (CBO) have had an increasing role in counseling and testing since 2014, targeting KP [116]. In 2022, 46,724 rapid tests were performed by NGOs and CBOs, an increase of 1146% when compared with 2014 (Fig. 1) (a year with 3,751 tests, [48]). Regarding HIV prevalence, both PHC and NGO settings have decreased values since 2016 whereas VCT centers have reported an increase in prevalence outcomes over the last six years [48]. Since 2018, the usage of rapid tests in community pharmacies has been steadily rising with 4,254 tests used in 2022 (Fig. 1) [37, 48, 88]. National surveillance reports provide the total number of HIV tests conducted, but do not disaggregate data by the number of unique individuals tested [48]. This limitation constrains interpretation of national testing indicators in several ways. First, it obscures testing coverage, making it difficult to assess how many individuals from key populations are actually being reached. Second, it hinders assessment of equity and access, as aggregated data cannot reveal whether certain subgroups remain under-tested despite high overall testing volumes. Moreover, aggregated figures may be inflated by frequent testers, leading to overestimation of coverage and program success (ECDC, 2010, p. 15). Consequently, current reports cannot determine essential metrics such as the proportion of people testing for the first time, a key indicator for evaluating the reach and effectiveness of HIV testing programs. Addressing these gaps through disaggregated monitoring systems would improve accuracy and allow more targeted resource allocation, as emphasized by international guidance on HIV data reporting [138].

Linked with the intent of increasing testing, some studies addressed psychosocial factors associated with the decision of taking the HIV test, in the Portuguese general population and in CAD users specifically. A general favorable attitude towards testing was reported in the Portuguese population, especially between the ages of 25 and 44, and in people who know someone living with HIV or that has died of AIDS [63]. People who have higher Perception of Risk for HIV (PRHIV) and higher knowledge about HIV have higher chances of having been tested for HIV [90]. Being male, highly educated, young, having a higher level of knowledge about HIV and living in an urban setting has also been reported as predictors of HIV testing in Portuguese VCT centers [110].

By 2022, the landscape of HIV testing in Portugal had shifted significantly: CAD testing declined sharply, while NGO- and pharmacy-based rapid testing rose substantially, with over 46,000 tests conducted by NGOs and 4,254 in pharmacies [48]. These developments reflect a more diversified and community-centered testing strategy—though, as the next section discusses, the COVID-19 pandemic would pose significant challenges to its continuity.

Decline in HIV testing during the COVID-19 pandemic

The COVID-19 pandemic had a huge effect in HIV testing, not only in Portugal but in the entire WHO European Region and in KP around the world [117, 122]. Preliminary results of a study conducted in 34 European countries, show that 95% of respondents reported testing less than half the expected number of times during the first months of the COVID-19 pandemic between March and May 2020 [117].

In Portugal, the number of new cases across different age groups has generally shown a decreasing trend over the past decade. However, the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic has made it difficult to interpret trends for the most recent years [48]. There was an increasing testing trend in PHC between 2015 and 2019, which was interrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic (a 20.23% decrease in 2020) and has not yet regained the previous momentum [48]. The same trend was found in the CAD network with an approximate 50% decrease in testing between 2019 and 2021 [48]. Regarding self-tests however, there was a positive trend (1,225 tests in 2019; 3,107 tests in 2020; 3,801 tests in 2021 and 4,254 in 2022 [48]. During the pandemic, a collaboration between pharmacies and a non-governmental development organization (NGDO), developed a pilot project/application—App to Test—to help distribute self-tests to the Portuguese population, with the aim of reinforcing the national strategy to combat HIV. Currently, this application makes it possible to collect self-tests free of charge in the Porto area.

Globally, there is an increasing trend in the number of tests conducted in the post-pandemic period and, consequently, in the number of people who know their serological status [117].

Key populations in Portugal

Key populations (KP) are groups disproportionately affected by HIV infection, regardless of epidemic type (generalized or concentrated) or setting [134, 139]. As discussed above, HIV testing strategies must be adapted to effectively reach key populations and other vulnerable groups with a disproportionate HIV burden—many of which are particularly relevant in the Portuguese context [137]. In 2021, KP and their partners accounted for 70% of new infections worldwide [136]. Another significant trend in the global epidemic is the increasing prevalence of HIV in people aged 50 years or over [87, 108, 120, 125, 132, 138, 158]. In Portugal, this age group has accounted for, in 2022, 23% of all new diagnoses and 35% of new infections through heterosexual contact [48]. The majority of people of this age group (72.4%) acquired the virus via heterosexual contact [48]. There is also a high rate of late diagnosis in this age group (69.9%), a trend that was consistently between 2018 and 2023 [46–49].

Both the 2012 and 2017 National Programmes for HIV/AIDS identified key populations—including MSM, sex workers, people who inject drugs, and migrants—as strategic priorities for prevention and testing [42, 45]. Migrants, in particular, were highlighted for targeted outreach, due to their overrepresentation in national HIV statistics, as well as barriers to healthcare access stemming from legal insecurity, language differences, and social exclusion [67]. However, while these populations were acknowledged in broad terms, the Programmes did not outline population-specific testing protocols, monitoring frameworks, or implementation strategies tailored to their needs.

The rate of HIV transmission among men who have sex with men (MSM) in Portugal doubled between 2001 and 2014 and appears to have declined slightly in recent years [48, 62, 101]. Although Portugal has followed broader European testing trends, its epidemiological pattern remains distinct. In most EU/EEA countries, MSM represent the majority of new HIV diagnoses (46.7% in 2023), whereas in Portugal, heterosexual transmission accounted for a larger share (49%) compared to MSM (41.7%) [49, 57]. Still, the burden among MSM remains significant: HIV diagnoses in this group increased from 32.2% in 2014 to 41.7% in 2023, while diagnoses attributed to heterosexual contact declined from 59 to 49% [49]. These trends underscore the continued need for focused prevention and testing strategies targeting MSM, even if they do not currently account for the majority of new cases [24, 84, 101].

Beyond epidemiological trends, HIV continues to function as a socially disabling condition for the MSM population in Portugal, marked by stigmatizing and discriminatory behaviors in psychosocial and interpersonal contexts. These experiences can lead to reluctance in disclosing HIV status to sexual partners [102]. MSM in Portugal have expressed the need for greater normalization of HIV, enhanced public education, and sustained dialogue [102]. These findings are supported by data from 2016 [97], reporting that people living with HIV in Portugal had significantly lower quality-of-life scores than their Finnish counterparts across all domains of the WHOQOL-HIV-Bref instrument. Additionally, a psychometric validation study of the Portuguese version of this instrument [13] suggests that broader social and environmental factors may contribute to reduced well-being among PLWH in Portugal, reinforcing the need to examine contextual determinants of quality of life. Nevertheless, available evidence does not indicate major difficulties in accessing health care or treatment [90, 102].

Some KP (such as migrants and sex workers) experience both higher HIV prevalence and lower testing uptake in the National Health Service [1, 51, 52]. In Europe 26.7% of all new HIV cases occur in migrant populations [60]. This is attributable not only to high prevalence rates in countries of origin but also to the post migration context that increases vulnerability to sexually transmitted infections [50, 54]. In Portugal, 44.5% of new HIV cases were people born outside of the country and of those cases 45.4% acquired the virus inside the country [48]. The majority of HIV diagnoses in sex workers occurred inside the country and about 40% were born outside Portugal [48]. Studies also indicate that women, particularly those who are migrants or engaged in sex work, may experience intersecting stigmas that hinder access to HIV testing and care [48], 50, 54].

Discussion

Portugal continues to report a high rate of new cases of HIV infection and AIDS among Western European countries [48, 60]. Reviewing the evolution of Portugal’s public HIV testing policies [4, 41, 43] and practices, it becomes clear that they were based on international guidelines albeit with some delays or caveats. For example, in a 2016 survey, Portugal was one of six countries (in ten) who reported having used the ECDC 2010 guidance to revise existing HIV testing policy but not in regards to the public health sector, only regarding NGOs. The same is true concerning supporting, monitoring or evaluating HIV testing practices [55].

Additionally, there is no procedure manual for PHC centers, only a procedure manual developed by the regional department of the ministry of health at a regional level [2], and an operational manual for carrying out rapid screening tests for HIV, Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) and Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) infections in community pharmacies and clinical pathology/clinical analysis laboratories [77]. It should also be noted that in terms of policies, there are differences between the decree law and the DGS standard of the same year [41]. While the decree referred to universal testing [4], the DGS standard would only include it three years later in the 2014 update [43].

The lack of a standardized procedure manual regarding testing in PHC settings may have a negative impact on the quality of service delivery, normalization of practices and may be a barrier to HIV related interventions including testing [9, 14, 18, 29, 30, 78, 79, 81, 86, 98, 99, 113, 121, 143].

Moreover, PHC centers in the country function according to a contracted health care model, where each center can choose a set of different indicators that determines performance assessment and budgeting. There is a specific indicator for HIV testing coverage and each PHC center may or may not use it as part of their model of contract [93]. This contractual health care model may result in asymmetric coverage of HIV testing between PHC centers depending on whether or not the specific indicator—indicator 306—was included in the contract.

Although increased HIV testing among key populations has been recommended globally [153], in Europe [92], and in Portugal [157], some of these populations continue to experience high HIV incidence rates that are not matched by adequate testing coverage. The National Program for HIV/AIDS has historically included references to expanding testing and reaching vulnerable populations [42]. However, the 2012–2016 program, while emphasizing universal access to HIV testing as a strategic goal, did not include detailed guidance or periodic recommendations tailored to specific populations, as found in more recent international protocols from WHO or ECDC. Subsequent national strategies have continued to promote broad access and collaboration with civil society organizations but still lack the granularity and implementation clarity regarding universal and routine testing [45]. This suggests a persistent gap between national strategic aims and operational testing guidelines.

While Portugal has been recognized for its high-quality HIV surveillance and consistent data reporting within Europe (ECDC, 2021), some gaps remain that could improve policy precision. For example, national reports tend to present the total number of tests performed, but not the number of unique individuals tested. According to WHO, the aggregation of national programmatic data should consider that “the number of tests performed in a given period is more than the number of individuals tested; both should be reported” ([152], p. 3 annex H). This distinction is relevant for evaluating repeated testing practices and identifying testing saturation among key populations. Such refinements could enhance the ability to monitor true coverage, evaluate equity in service provision and inform targeted testing strategies. As recent research has emphasized, improving HIV prevention requires systematic and strategic use of monitoring data to inform programming and evaluate outcomes effectively [69].

Recent data show that people over 50 years old account for 23% of new diagnoses and 35% of cases attributed to heterosexual transmission, yet this age group also shows disproportionately high rates of late diagnosis [48]. This suggests missed testing opportunities. Several barriers may limit HIV testing in people over 50. These include difficulties distinguishing age-related symptoms from HIV-related ones, low perceived risk by individuals or healthcare professionals, and limited inclusion of older adults in awareness campaigns [119, 120, 158]. Therefore, it is important to consider a multifactorial approach, which can integrate the training of health professionals who work with people in this age group on the risk factors and symptoms of HIV infection [120], the construction and use of educational materials that incorporate the specific needs for HIV prevention in this age group, the dissemination of information to the general population regarding epidemiological trends in this age group, educating PLWH on how to prevent subsequent transmissions, and testing for all STIs in routine care [108]. It is also important to integrate the needs of people aged 50 or over into HIV health promotion campaigns (with the aim of reducing transmission trends in this group) and to continue research to identify the specific behaviors that put people in this age group at risk of infection [120].

Migrants represent a key population in HIV testing strategies across Europe, yet they remain insufficiently addressed in Portugal’s national implementation frameworks. International guidelines—including those from the ECDC and WHO—recommend tailored, population-specific testing approaches for migrants, such as opt-out testing in high-prevalence settings, culturally competent outreach through community organizations, and removal of legal and administrative barriers to access [57, 155]. While Portuguese National Programmes have acknowledged migrants as a vulnerable group, they fall short of establishing concrete protocols, dedicated monitoring systems, or systematic outreach strategies aimed at this population.

In 2022, 36.2% of all new HIV diagnoses in Portugal were among people born outside the country, with particularly high rates among individuals from sub-Saharan Africa and Brazil [48]. Nearly half of these cases were classified as late presenters, indicating substantial delays in testing and linkage to care. Studies show that many migrants are unaware of their HIV status, face language and knowledge barriers, and experience social vulnerability and reduced access to health services—particularly when undocumented or engaged in sex work [50, 52–54]. These findings echo those reported by GAT [67], which showed that more than half of immigrant participants faced difficulties accessing HIV-related care due to irregular status, administrative burdens, or stigma—despite high awareness of where to seek help. Structural determinants such as legal insecurity, lack of culturally appropriate services, language barriers, and fear of discrimination remain key barriers to testing and care [67]. While general legal provisions ensure access to healthcare for undocumented migrants (e.g., [34, 36]), these entitlements have not been explicitly integrated into national HIV testing protocols or strategies. Without explicit integration of migrant needs into Portugal's national HIV testing strategy, these disparities are unlikely to improve. Aligning with international best practices, a strategic shift toward migrant-specific interventions—such as systematic outreach, peer-led education, legal support, and multilingual services—should be considered a public health priority. To strengthen these efforts, the national response must address structural and procedural barriers by implementing intercultural training for healthcare professionals, simplifying administrative procedures, and expanding interpretation and language support. Clear communication of healthcare rights and the deployment of community-based health mediators or peer navigators are also essential to foster trust, improve service navigation, and reduce delays in diagnosis and care [67].

Men who have sex with men (MSM) remain one of the most disproportionately affected populations in the HIV epidemic globally and in Portugal [49, 155]. Over the past two decades, Portugal has made substantial efforts to strengthen testing and prevention services for this group, including the development of community-based testing services, the expansion of condom distribution programs, and the implementation of Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) with MSM as a priority population [49]. These actions align with WHO and UNAIDS recommendations, which call for MSM to be offered comprehensive and combination prevention strategies, including regular HIV testing, PrEP access, condom and lubricant provision, and tailored sexual health services [135, 155].

Nonetheless, recent data highlight persistent gaps in prevention uptake and service accessibility, particularly among migrants, men who have sex with men (MSM), and non-Portuguese citizens—especially those from African countries—and among individuals living outside urban centers [50, 54, 60]. While the high number of new HIV diagnoses among young MSM justifies targeted interventions [48], focusing solely on youth may overlook the needs of other MSM subgroups who face structural barriers to care. Although awareness of PrEP among MSM is rising, uptake remains uneven, with many eligible individuals not using it consistently — particularly outside urban areas [67]. Between 2014 and 2022, the CAD network reported rising HIV positivity rates among MSM, including non-nationals, which may reflect a more effective outreach, ongoing transmission and the use of these services as a re-entry point into care [48, 64].

To close these gaps, Portugal could invest further in differentiated testing and prevention strategies, including mobile and self-testing initiatives, outreach to underserved MSM populations, and training for providers to offer stigma-free services in both specialized and primary care settings [48, 57, 67]. Sustained political commitment and monitoring frameworks are essential to ensure equitable access and progress toward elimination goals. In parallel, ensuring consistent access to prevention tools such as condoms, lubricants, and harm reduction services for those who use psychoactive substances—remains essential. While some improvements in stigma and discrimination indicators have been reported since 2013, significant barriers persist for MSM and other KP in healthcare settings [48].

Overall, there seems to be room for improvement regarding national data guiding national policy and practices. The Netherlands is considered a good example of a country that practices effective HIV testing procedures and uses the resulting data to inform national plans and prevention programs [57, 60, 107]. Monitoring and evaluation are recognized as fundamental to informing the development and implementation of policy and programmatic frameworks related to HIV/AIDS [135]. Every year in the Netherlands, the annual comprehensive surveillance report and a brief annual policy letter are published to inform national STI/HIV policy [21]. For example, in Amsterdam, prevalence, incidence, and transmission data were the motto for creating the H-Team Initiative, a consortium involved in HIV prevention and care [73]. The H-Team Initiative aimed to design and implement a multidisciplinary and integrated approach to reducing the incidence of HIV infection [73]. This initiative consisted of a combination of interventions, including PrEP, innovative test-and-treat strategies for general practitioners used in centers for sexual health and hospitals, and research studies on motives and barriers to testing. There is a high probability that this initiative has contributed significantly to the 79% decline in new HIV diagnoses observed in Amsterdam (from 300 in 2010 to 62 in 2022) and to reaching and exceeding the UNAIDS 95–95-95 targets in 2022 [73].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, while we aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of HIV testing policies and practices in Portugal, our analysis relied primarily on publicly available policy documents, surveillance reports, and published literature. Some relevant programmatic data—such as disaggregated testing uptake by key population or region—are not consistently available, limiting our ability to assess the full equity and effectiveness of testing implementation. Second, although we included epidemiological trends and testing statistics, this article does not present primary data or conduct statistical modeling; rather, it offers a critical narrative review. Finally, interpretations of policy may reflect the authors’ analytical lens and are subject to contextual updates as new guidelines emerge.

Conclusion

Although Portugal’s HIV testing policies are based on international recommendations, gaps remain due to delays, policy adjustments, and instances of non-compliance with certain guidelines. The lack of a standardized manual of procedures for testing in PHC settings may hinder the quality of service provision and the widespread implementation and standardization of practices. Portugal’s contractual healthcare model may result in unequal testing coverage across regions, depending on whether performance indicators—such as Indicator 306—are adopted at the local level. This study provides insights for other nations facing similar challenges in operationalizing universal testing frameworks.

Beyond expanding access, Portugal may benefit from adopting more integrated models of care that align testing with treatment, follow-up, and prevention—similar to successful approaches in other European contexts. Persistent disparities remain in access and prevention coverage, particularly among migrants, MSM, and non-Portuguese citizens. Strengthening data systems to include disaggregated indicators—such as testing by population group and first-time testers—will be essential to accurately monitor equity, guide interventions, and ensure that testing services effectively reach those most at risk.

Systematic reviews have shown that integrated healthcare approaches improve outcomes for people living with HIV and multimorbidity in high-income contexts [96]. Future studies could assess how HIV testing is being implemented in PHC centers, including health professionals’ perceptions of the test and the barriers to its implementation. User perspectives—particularly among key populations—also warrant investigation to evaluate acceptability and accessibility of services. Voluntary counseling and testing centers have been identified as key structures in increasing access to key populations [62, 64, 135]. Given the continued decline in the number of tests performed within the CAD network and the closing of CAD sites, it will be important to assess whether key populations are making the transition to NGO/CBO settings or to PHC centers for testing. Evaluating the effectiveness of NGO/CBO initiatives in reaching these groups and reducing HIV incidence remains a critical research priority.

Implications for practice

This study highlights critical gaps in HIV testing policy and practices in the country. Portugal lacks a national procedures manual for health centers and there is an urgent need to fill this gap. It is essential that Portugal properly uses the information gathered from testing to implement changes in national policies to fight HIV and AIDS. Furthermore, given the high number of late diagnoses in people aged 50 and over, it would be important to adopt and implement preventive measures [48] and integrate routine testing for all STIs in this population [108]. In addition, It would also be important to provide specific training for health professionals in PHC centers, with the aim of increasing HIV awareness and testing in this population as well as stigma reduction training focused on key populations and women.
