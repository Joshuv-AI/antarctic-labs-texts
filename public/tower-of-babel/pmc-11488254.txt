A higher peak heart rate during head-up tilt test predicts the therapeutic efficacy of metoprolol in vasovagal syncope
Authors: Kemei Liu, Pengyu Liu, Zhuoshan Huang, Lin Wu, Xujing Xie, Jinlai Liu, Jieming Zhu, Yuen Liang, Ruimin Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough beta blockers, such as metoprolol, have been widely used in the management of vasovagal syncope (VVS), their efficacy remains debated, with larger studies showing limited benefit. Identifying patient-specific characteristics that predict a positive response to metoprolol could optimize its use. This study aims to investigate the key factors that may determine which VVS patients are suitable candidates for metoprolol treatment.MethodsThis retrospective study was conducted at a single center. Patients diagnosed with VVS and treated with metoprolol for a minimum of three months were included. A 50% reduction in syncope symptom score (SS) after three months of metoprolol treatment was used to define metoprolol responders, while those who did not achieve this reduction were classified as non-responders. After a three-month follow-up, patients were divided into two groups: responders and non-responders. Baseline data—including age, gender, comorbidities, laboratory tests, and the type of VVS—were analyzed for each patient. Changes in heart rate (HR) and blood pressure during the head-up tilt test (HUTT) were also evaluated between the two groups. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify potential predictors of metoprolol response, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the value of these predictors in selecting potential responders.ResultsA total of 168 VVS patients were enrolled between January 2016 and December 2020. Of these, 100 patients responded positively to metoprolol, while 68 did not. No significant differences in baseline characteristics or HUTT results were observed between the two groups (p > 0.05). However, responders exhibited a significantly higher peak HR compared to non-responders during the HUTT (120.13 ± 19.33 vs. 106.60 ± 22.32, p < 0.001). The increase in HR was also more pronounced in responders (0.60 ± 0.23 vs. 0.41 ± 0.25, p < 0.001). Additionally, HR variability was significantly greater in responders than in non-responders (16.18 ± 5.51 vs. 12.61 ± 5.50, p < 0.001).ConclusionHigher peak HR, larger increases in HR, and greater HR variability during the HUTT may serve as reliable predictors of a positive response to metoprolol in patients with VVS. Keywords: Vasovagal syncope, Metoprolol, Heart rate, Predictors

Introduction

Syncope is defined as transient loss of consciousness (TLOC) due to cerebral hypoperfusion, characterized by a rapid onset, short duration, and spontaneous complete recovery [1]. It is a common condition, affecting nearly 33% of individuals at least once during their lifetime [2]. Among those who experience an initial episode, up to 20% may suffer recurrent syncope within the first four years. The prognosis of syncope patients largely depends on their cardiovascular health and the underlying cause of the event. According to the Framingham study, patients with syncope had twice the risk of death compared to those without syncope. However, in cases of vasovagal syncope (VVS), there was no significant increase in the risk of major outcomes [3].

VVS accounts for approximately 21.2% of all syncopal episodes, making it a prevalent subtype of syncope [4]. Historically, VVS was considered a protective mechanism, safeguarding the brain and heart from permanent damage caused by ischemia. When severe ischemia occurs, the accompanying loss of consciousness and reduced muscle tone may counteract the effects of gravity, promoting blood flow to the brain and heart [5]. While VVS is generally regarded as a benign condition, recurrent episodes can significantly impact the quality of life and increase the risk of severe injury [6]. Some patients suffer physical trauma due to VVS-related falls, leading to emergency department visits and higher rates of hospital admissions.

Management strategies for VVS patients often include recommendations such as adequate hydration, discontinuation of diuretics and hypotension-inducing medications, and increased salt intake. Physical counterpressure maneuvers and orthostatic training have also been employed to prevent fainting episodes [7]. Despite educational efforts, lifestyle modifications, and the use of counterpressure techniques, up to 30% of VVS patients continue to experience recurrent symptoms [8]. Pharmacological treatments, including metoprolol, midodrine, and fludrocortisone, have shown promise in managing VVS in more severe cases. Among these, β-receptor blockers were among the earliest treatments considered for VVS, but the role of beta-blockers, particularly metoprolol, remains contentious.

In a non-randomized trial [9], 296 patients with syncope were followed for an average of 28 ± 11 months. The recurrence rate of syncope was significantly lower in patients who consistently used beta-blockers compared to those who were untreated or partially treated. However, a network meta-analysis on the pharmacologic treatment of VVS, which examined a range of medications, including metoprolol, found that while midodrine significantly reduced syncope recurrence, metoprolol was not statistically effective for VVS patients [10]. This discrepancy underscores the importance of identifying patient-specific characteristics that may predict a favorable response to metoprolol therapy. Given the conflicting evidence regarding the efficacy of beta blockers in the general VVS population, exploring whether certain subgroups of patients could benefit more from metoprolol therapy is crucial. Recent studies have pointed to cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction as a key factor in VVS, suggesting that patients with specific hemodynamic profiles, such as higher HR variability or greater peak HR during HUTT, may be more responsive to beta blocker treatment. Our study seeks to fill this knowledge gap by identifying physiological markers that may aid in selecting VVS patients who will respond positively to metoprolol. In this study, we aim to investigate the potential factors that may predict the effectiveness of metoprolol in patients with vasovagal syncope, to improve patient selection and optimize treatment outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study was part of the Exploring the Treatment Strategies and Prognostic Influence Factors Analysis of Vasovagal Syncope: a single-center real-world study (ESPIAL-VVS Study, chiCTR2300078474), conducted in the Department of Cardiology at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University from January 2016 to December 2020. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University and adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients or their guardians.

Patients were included based on the following criteria: (1) individuals diagnosed with VVS according to established guidelines [11] and aged 18 years or older; (2) patients who had received metoprolol treatment for at least three months; (3) those with complete data from the head-up tilt test (HUTT); and (4) individuals who completed the follow-up process without participating in other clinical trials during the study period.

Patients were excluded if they had severe cardiovascular or non-cardiovascular diseases such as coronary artery disease, aortic valve stenosis, atrial fibrillation, cancer, or other significant conditions. Patients with syncope due to other causes (e.g., pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, epilepsy, anemia) or contraindications to beta-blockers were also excluded. Additionally, patients unable to provide informed consent or those who were pregnant were not included in the study.

All participants received metoprolol sustained-release tablets, starting at an initial dose of 23.75 mg per day. Dose adjustments were made every two weeks based on the patient's heart rate (HR) and blood pressure (BP), with a maximum daily dose not exceeding 90 mg. HR was maintained between 60–80 beats per minute (bpm), and BP was kept above 90/60 mmHg throughout the treatment period.

The syncope symptom score (SS) was determined based on the frequency of syncope episodes experienced by the patient. The scoring was structured as follows: 0 points: no syncope spells, 1 point: one syncope spell per month, 2 points: 2–4 syncope spells per month, 3 points: 2–7 syncope spells per week, 4 points: more than one syncope spell per day.

Patients who demonstrated a 50% reduction in SS after three months of metoprolol therapy were classified as metoprolol responders, while those who did not meet this criterion were designated as non-responders [12–14]. The study evaluated a variety of demographic and clinical parameters. Demographic data included age at admission, sex, body mass index (BMI), the prevalence of hypertension or type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hemoglobin levels, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), total triglycerides (TG), creatinine levels, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Additionally, the type of vasovagal syncope, syncope symptom score before and after metoprolol treatment, and syncope duration were collected.

Hemodynamic data were also analyzed, including supine HR, supine systolic pressure, supine diastolic pressure, peak HR during HUTT, changes in HR during HUTT, and the overall hemodynamic response during the HUTT. These variables were compared between the responder and non-responder groups to identify potential predictors of metoprolol efficacy.

HUTT (Head-Up Tilt Test)

Patients were instructed to abstain from vasoactive medications for at least five half-life periods and to fast for a minimum of four hours before the test. A quiet, warm examination environment equipped with necessary first aid medications and equipment was provided. Upon arrival, patients rested on the examination bed for 10 min, during which their heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), and electrocardiogram (ECG) readings were continuously monitored. Once these parameters stabilized, patients were positioned to stand on a 70-degree inclined bed and were observed until they exhibited a positive response or until the 30-min test duration was completed.

For patients who did not exhibit a positive response after the initial 30 min, 500 µg of sublingual nitroglycerin was administered to provoke a response. The criteria for a positive response to the HUTT included the onset of syncope or presyncope, accompanied by at least one of the following physiological changes: A drop in systolic pressure to ≤ 80 mmHg, and/or diastolic pressure to ≤ 50 mmHg, and/or a mean BP decrease of ≥ 25%; A reduction in heart rate to 50 bpm; Sinus arrest followed by compensatory junctional escape rhythm; Transient second- or third-degree atrioventricular block, or asystole lasting ≥ 3 s.

The Vasovagal Syncope International Study (VASIS) classification was employed to categorize the hemodynamic responses observed during HUTT. The classifications include vasodepressor type, cardioinhibitory type, and mixed type [15]: Characterized by a significant drop in BP without a marked reduction in HR. Cardioinhibitory type: Defined by a substantial reduction in HR without a significant drop in BP. Mixed type: Exhibited marked reductions in both HR and BP. All enrolled patients underwent HUTT prior to the initiation of metoprolol treatment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, New York). For continuous data that followed a normal distribution, values were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between the two groups were made using the independent sample t-test. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution, the rank sum test was applied. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (numbers of cases) and were analyzed using the Chi-square test.

To identify variables associated with the efficacy of metoprolol treatment, stepwise logistic regression analysis was performed. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed to assess the utility of potential indices in identifying responders to metoprolol therapy. Incomplete or unavailable data were excluded from the analysis. A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between January 2016 and December 2020, a total of 395 patients were diagnosed with vasovagal syncope (VVS) in our department (Fig. 1). Of these, 178 patients discontinued beta-blocker therapy within three months, 2 patients had permanent pacemakers, 12 patients had significant cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases, and 35 patients did not complete the follow-up process. Ultimately, 168 patients met the inclusion criteria and had no exclusion factors. Following a three-month follow-up period, the effectiveness of metoprolol was evaluated in these 168 cases.

Age was a significant differentiator between the two groups, with responders being notably younger than non-responders (39.39 ± 17.06 vs. 45.15 ± 17.44, p < 0.05). No significant differences were found between the groups in terms of body mass index (BMI), the prevalence of hypertension, or type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Similarly, there were no significant differences in laboratory parameters such as hemoglobin levels, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), creatinine levels, or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).

The most common type of VVS observed in both groups was the mixed type, followed by the vasodepressor type and the cardioinhibitory type. Responders had an initial SS of 1.74 ± 0.77, which dropped significantly to 0.03 ± 0.17 after treatment. Most responders had a syncope symptom score of 0, with a small number of patients scoring 1 or 2. In contrast, non-responders had an SS of 1.84 ± 0.94 before treatment, which decreased only marginally to 1.44 ± 1.26 after treatment (Table 2).

Hemodynamic changes during HUTT

Hemodynamic changes during the HUTT were analyzed between the two groups (Table 3). The average duration of syncope in responders was 8.11 ± 4.23 min, while in non-responders, it was 7.87 ± 4.47 min, showing no significant difference. Responders had a baseline heart rate (HR) of 75.76 ± 12.50 bpm, and their peak HR reached 120.13 ± 19.33 bpm, with an HR ascensional range of 0.60 ± 0.23. In contrast, non-responders had a baseline HR of 76.31 ± 13.30 bpm, a peak HR of 106.60 ± 22.32 bpm, and an HR ascensional range of 0.41 ± 0.25. The differences in peak HR and HR ascensional range between the two groups were statistically significant. Additionally, HR variability was larger in responders compared to non-responders.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the potential indicators of metoprolol effectiveness in patients with vasovagal syncope (VVS). Our findings demonstrate that the maximum heart rate (HR), HR ascensional range, and HR variability during the head-up tilt test (HUTT) were significantly different between responders and non-responders. Additionally, we identified that peak HR, HR ascensional range, HR variability, and the highest diastolic pressure during HUTT can serve as predictive factors for determining metoprolol efficacy in VVS patients. This approach provides an accessible and practical method for clinicians to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from metoprolol therapy, thereby improving treatment outcomes.

Although beta blockers have traditionally been used as a first-line treatment for VVS, previous clinical studies have produced inconsistent results regarding their effectiveness. For instance, Cox et al. [9] observed a significant reduction in syncope recurrence in patients treated with beta blockers compared to untreated or partially treated patients over 28 ± 11 months. Mahanonda and colleagues [16], in a randomized trial, found that 62% of VVS patients treated with atenolol experienced symptom improvement, compared to only 5% in the placebo group. Ventura et al. [17] also reported that 71.5% of patients receiving beta blockers showed no recurrence of syncope, compared to 28.6% of those who received no pharmacological therapy. These studies suggest a protective effect of beta blockers in VVS management. However, conflicting results have emerged from other trials. A randomized, placebo-controlled, multicenter study involving 208 patients found no significant difference in syncope recurrence between the metoprolol and placebo groups over 11 years of follow-up [18]. Alegria et al. [19] reported a trend toward higher syncope recurrence in patients treated with beta blockers compared to controls, while Madrid [20] found no significant difference in recurrence between atenolol-treated and placebo-treated groups in a 12-month follow-up. These discrepancies highlight the need for a more personalized approach to beta blocker therapy in VVS patients. In contrast, midodrine has shown more consistent efficacy in reducing syncope recurrence in VVS patients. A network meta-analysis on pharmacological treatments for VVS found that midodrine significantly reduced syncope recurrence, while beta blockers, including metoprolol, showed no significant benefit [10]. This suggests that while midodrine is effective in reducing syncope events broadly, metoprolol may be more suitable for a specific subset of patients with unique hemodynamic characteristics.

Our study contributes to this evolving understanding by identifying key predictive factors—higher peak HR, a larger ascensional range of HR, increased HR variability, and higher diastolic pressure during HUTT—that may indicate which VVS patients will respond favorably to metoprolol. These findings align with previous research by Leor and colleagues [21], who noted that the development of tachycardia during HUTT predicted beta blocker efficacy. Similarly, Zhang et al. [22] demonstrated that a heart rate increase of 30 bpm before a positive response during HUTT had high sensitivity and specificity for predicting metoprolol responsiveness in VVS children. Our results reinforce these findings, suggesting that monitoring HR changes during HUTT can provide valuable insights into which patients will benefit from beta blocker therapy.

The autonomic nervous system plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of VVS, regulating the hypotension and/or bradycardia that often accompany syncope episodes [23]. As previous studies have shown, HR variability analysis is commonly used to evaluate autonomic function [24]. High HR variability is typically associated with greater adaptability of the cardiovascular autonomic system, whereas low variability suggests impaired autonomic control. In our study, VVS patients with higher HR variability showed a better response to metoprolol, which may indicate that the degree of cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction could influence the effectiveness of beta blocker therapy. This hypothesis warrants further investigation in larger, multicenter studies to confirm its validity. One of the strengths of our study lies in its focus on identifying predictive markers, such as HR variability and peak HR during HUTT, which could guide clinicians in selecting patients more likely to benefit from metoprolol therapy. By highlighting these physiological indicators, the study contributes to a more personalized treatment approach for VVS patients, potentially improving outcomes for those with recurrent syncope who have limited therapeutic options.

There are several limitations to our study. First, the sample size of 168 patients was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the follow-up period of three months was short, particularly for patients with a low annual incidence of syncope. Given that many VVS patients experience only 1–2 syncope episodes per year, a three-month follow-up period may not fully capture the long-term efficacy of metoprolol. A longer follow-up would be necessary to evaluate whether the benefits of metoprolol are sustained over time, particularly in patients with low-frequency syncopal episodes. Furthermore, as a retrospective study, our analysis relied on archived medical records, which may introduce bias. To validate the relationship between HR variability and beta blocker efficacy, future research should include randomized, double-blind, multicenter studies conducted over longer follow-up periods.

Conclusion

Our study found that metoprolol responders demonstrated significantly higher peak heart rate (HR), a wider HR ascensional range, and greater HR variability during the head-up tilt test (HUTT) compared to non-responders. These parameters—peak HR, ascensional HR range, HR variability, and the highest diastolic pressure during HUTT—may serve as valuable predictors of metoprolol efficacy in patients with vasovagal syncope (VVS). The underlying mechanism may be related to cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction, suggesting that patients with better autonomic adaptability are more likely to benefit from beta blocker therapy. However, to solidify these findings, a randomized, double-blind, multicenter study with extended follow-up is necessary to further explore the relationship between HR variability and the long-term efficacy of beta blocker therapy in VVS patients.
