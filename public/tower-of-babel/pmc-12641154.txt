Molecular Characterisation of Fungi Populations in the Chronic Wounds of Filarial Lymphedema Patients in Southern Ghana: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Dzidzor Yawa Ayeke, Priscilla Osei‐Poku, Drake Byamukama, Emmanuel Kobla Atsu Amewu, Arnold Abakah, Abdul Latif Koney Shardow, Susana Makafui Abla Yeboah, Cindy Antwiwaa, Mary Boampomaa Wilson, Solomon Wireko, Amma Larbi, Alexander Kwarteng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsLymphatic filariasis (LF) and associated chronic wounds are of public health concern worldwide. Despite the significant burden of lymphatic filariasis (LF) and its associated chronic wounds, the fungal component of the wound microbiome remains poorly understood. Previous research has primarily focused on bacterial pathogens, neglecting the potential role of fungi in wound pathogenesis and progression. A comprehensive understanding of the microbial landscape, especially the overlooked fungal component, is crucial for developing effective prevention and treatment strategies. The study aimed to molecularly characterize the fungal populations present in these wounds via Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) sequencing.MethodsThis was a cross‐sectional study, with a total of 63 individuals sampled from eight (8) LF‐hyperendemic communities within the Ahanta West District in Ghana. Skin and wound swabs were collected from each participant between December 2021 and February 2022 and subjected to standard culture‐based techniques. Fungal isolates were identified, and their DNA was extracted for subsequent PCR amplification of the ITS region. The sequencing was performed using the Oxford Nanopore platform.ResultsOf the 63 patients sampled, fungal colonies were observed in 16 individuals (25.4% prevalence) through culture‐based methods. Molecular characterisation identified 24 Twenty‐four (24) distinct fungal species at different frequencies from nine different genera: Cladosporium, Aspergillus, Periconia, Penicillium, Candida, Lasiodiplodia, Acrophialophora, Zygosporium, and Trichoderma. Cladosporium spp. was the most prevalent, accounting for 30% of isolates, followed by Aspergillus spp. (28.6%), Periconia spp. (14.3%), Penicillium spp. (12.9%), and Candida spp. (8.6%). A smaller proportion of isolates represented Lasiodiplodia spp., Acrophialophora spp., Zygosporium spp., and Trichoderma spp., each comprising 1.4% of the total isolates. Notably, only 2 out of the 16 patients did not present with mixed fungal populations in their wounds.ConclusionsThe presence of fungal pathogens and the potential for polymicrobial infections within the LF‐wound environment highlight the need for comprehensive and tailored treatment protocols. Effective wound management is essential to address these complex wounds and mitigate the development of antibiotic resistance, a growing concern in chronic wound care.Keywords: chronic wounds, fungi, lymphatic filariasis, lymphedema

Introduction

Lymphatic filariasis (LF) is a debilitating, neglected tropical disease caused by parasitic worms transmitted by mosquitoes. Primarily affecting the lymphatic system, LF leads to long‐term disability and severe disfigurement. The most prevalent causative agent is Wuchereria bancrofti, although Brugia malayi and Brugia timori are also responsible for a portion of cases. Endemic in regions of sub‐Saharan Africa, Asia, the Pacific, the Caribbean, and South America, LF afflicts over 120 million people globally [1]. Patients with LF often experience recurrent episodes of acute inflammation (ADLA) and develop chronic lymphedema, characterized by swelling and thickening of affected limbs. The resulting skin lesions and sores create entry points for opportunistic microorganisms, exacerbating the disease burden. Chronic wounds, a common complication of LF, harbor diverse microbial communities, including bacteria and fungi, contributing to persistent inflammation, pain, and impaired quality of life [2, 3]. These secondary infections are recognized as key contributors to the pathogenesis and progression of lymphedema [4]. Recent investigations into the skin microbiome and resistome of filarial lymphedema patients in rural Ghana have revealed significant alterations in bacterial communities compared to unaffected individuals [5]. These studies, representing the first comprehensive analysis of the cutaneous microbiota in this population, have also demonstrated a marked reduction in bacterial load as lymphedema advances [5, 6]. Concurrently, other research has identified a diverse array of pathogenic bacteria, including Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Streptococcus pyogenes, Acinetobacter junii, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, within the wound environment of lymphedema patients [7]. Moreover, these studies have highlighted the emergence of phenotypic resistance among Staphylococcus saprophyticus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Proteus mirabilis [7, 8]. While the bacterial microbiome associated with filarial lymphedema and its related chronic wounds has been the subject of growing research interest in recent years, the fungal component remains largely unexplored.

Fungi, commonly found on the skin and in the environment, are frequent inhabitants of chronic wounds, which, unlike bacterial infections, can sometimes cause persistent infections that are difficult to treat with standard therapies [9]. They can directly impede wound healing and create a conducive environment for the proliferation of antibiotic‐resistant bacteria. This complex interplay between fungi and bacteria has been reported to contribute to the persistent and challenging nature and management of chronic wounds. A study of 915 chronic wounds, including venous, pressure, diabetic foot, and non‐healing surgical wounds, found that 23% were positive for fungal colonization [10]. Another study investigating fungal infections in diabetic wound patients reported a prevalence of approximately 30%. Candida species were identified as the predominant fungal pathogen, followed by Aspergillus and Trichosporon genera [11]. The prevalence of fungal infections, as well as other fungal species involved in opportunistic infections in southern Ghana is unknown. However, studies have shown that approximately 4% of Ghanaians suffer from serious fungal infections [12, 13, 14].

Additionally, previous studies have reported fungal colonization rates of approximately 23% and 27% in chronic and diabetic lower leg wounds, respectively [11, 15]. Therefore, the authors hypothesized that chronic wounds associated with LF harbor a diverse fungal microbiome that significantly contributes to wound pathogenesis and may complicate the management of these wounds by promoting antibiotic resistance and impairing healing. Whereas significant strides have been made in understanding the bacterial communities associated with filarial lymphedema in Ghana, effective wound management among these patient cohorts requires accurate information about the microbial composition of the wound, including fungi, to inform targeted treatment strategies to eliminate the fungi and promote timely wound healing [10]. To this end, there is a clear need to characterize the fungal component and its effect on the chronicity of these wounds. The growing prevalence of fungal co‐infections underscores the importance of such research, as the fungal microbiome is increasingly recognized as a critical factor in various physiological and pathological processes of infectious conditions and wounds.

Considering the second goal of the Global Program for Elimination of Filariasis (GPELF), optimizing patient outcomes through Morbidity Management and Disability Prevention, this study seeks to answer the question: “What is the composition of the fungal microbiome in chronic wounds associated with lymphatic filariasis, and how might these fungi influence the progression and management of these wounds?” To this end, the study aims to molecularly characterize the fungal populations present in these wounds via Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) sequencing. By establishing a baseline understanding of the fungal microbiome, we seek to inform definitive clinical decisions and interventions in managing filarial lymphedema pathology and related wound‐infected complications. Furthermore, such mycobiome profiles and foundational molecular insights will provide the premise for future mechanistic investigations into the complex interplay between fungi, the immune system, and the progression of filarial lymphedema.

Methodology

Study Design

A cross‐sectional study design was employed to phenotypically and molecularly characterize the fungal microbiome associated with lymphatic filariasis (LF) wounds in the Ahanta West District of Ghana [6, 7].

Study Population and Recruitment

A total of (n = 63) filarial lymphedema patients were purposively sampled from eight (8) LF‐hyperendemic communities, as shown in Figure 1. including Dixcove, Achowa, Busua, Butre, Asemkow, Ampatano, Princess Town, and Akatakyi. The patients with chronic lymphedema in Stages I–VII, as classified according to Dreyer et al. [16], were recruited. Participants recruited were both male and female, aged 18 years and older, who had lived in filarial‐endemic communities for over 10 years and presented with at least one ulcer/wound. Individuals under 18 years old and those with edema not caused by lymphatic filariasis were excluded. Each patient was carefully examined by an experienced lymphatic filarial expert.

Map of Ghana highlighting the study area in Southern Ghana. This map indicates the geographical region where study participants with lymphatic filariasis‐associated chronic wounds were recruited, providing the geographical context for the research.

Ethical Considerations

The study received approval from the Committee for Human Research, Publication and Ethics (CHRPE/AP/036/21) at the School of Medical Sciences and Dentistry, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, Kumasi, Ghana. Informed consent of participants was taken with strict confidentiality maintained. Major issues such as culture, religion, and human rights of the respondents were well‐respected.

Data and Sample Collection

Before sample collection, socio‐demographic data of participants were collected via developed questionnaires (S1) to capture demographic, clinical, and wound‐specific information. Clinical data encompassed patient biodata, the presence and absence of lymphedema, and the staging of lymphedema, providing an overview of the study population and their presenting conditions as shown in Figure 2. To obtain microbial specimens, wound swabs were collected aseptically from each participant between December 2021 and February 2022. Sterile swab sticks were rotated across a 1‐cm wound area after cleansing with physiological saline. Samples were meticulously labelled, preserved in liquid nitrogen to maintain sample integrity, and transported under strict cold chain conditions to the West African Centre for Cell Biology of Infectious Pathogens (WACCBIP), Legon, Accra, for subsequent microbiological analysis.

Representative image of chronic lymphedema wounds typical of those from which fungal cultures were obtained. These photographs clearly show the swelling and skin changes in study participants' limbs, highlighting the chronic wound environment examined in this study.

Culture and Isolation

Swab specimens were initially suspended in nuclease‐free water and subjected to mechanical disruption using a thermo‐shaker for 30 min. Subsequent serial dilutions were prepared and inoculated onto Dichloramphenicol Rose Bengal agar plates. Plates were incubated at 35°C–37°C for 72–120 h. Colonies exhibiting distinct morphological appearances, such as grayish and fluffy, pinkish colonies, green colonies, dark with spores, and green with a white patch, were sub‐cultured onto Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) for isolation and purification. This process was repeated to obtain pure fungal cultures.

Molecular Identification and Characterization

DNA Extraction

Fungal genomic DNA was extracted using the Quick‐DNA Fungal/Bacterial Miniprep Kit following the manufacturer's instructions with modifications. Fungal cells were subjected to bead beating in a ZR Bashing Bead Lysis Tube to disrupt cell walls. Following centrifugation, the lysate was transferred to a Zymo‐Spin III‐F filter for clarification. Genomic lysis buffer was added, and the mixture was further purified using a Zymo‐Spin IICR column. Wash steps with DNA Prewash and g‐DNA Wash buffers were performed to remove contaminants. Finally, DNA was eluted in a DNA Elution Buffer.

Polymerase Chain Reaction

PCR amplification was performed using a Q5 Hot Start Fidelity Master Mix, with ITS primers targeting the internal transcribed spacer region [17]. Reaction mixtures containing DNA template, primers, and master mix were subjected to PCR cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 50°C for 45 s, and extension at 72°C for 50 s. A final extension step at 72°C for 5 min was included. Amplicon size and integrity were confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. The size of the bands was around 600 bp for the target amplicon size.

Amplicon Library Preparation and Minion Sequencing

DNA Amplicon Sequencing was used to sequence the amplicons using the Oxford nanopore platform. Amplicon purification was carried out using AMPure XP beads. Following bead‐binding and washing steps, purified amplicons were eluted and quantified using a Qubit fluorometer. To normalize amplicon concentrations for library preparation, samples were diluted to a final concentration of 50 ng/µL. Unique DNA barcodes were added to individual DNA samples to enable sample multiplexing. Barcoding was performed by ligating specific barcode sequences to the end‐repaired DNA fragments. A pooling step was then conducted, combining all barcoded samples. The pooled library was purified using AMPure XP beads to remove unwanted fragments. Adapter ligation involves attaching short DNA sequences (adapters) to the ends of DNA fragments, a crucial step for sequencing. The ligation reaction was performed using a mixture of adapter oligos, ligation buffer, and DNA ligase. Following ligation, the reaction mixture was purified using AMPure XP beads to remove unincorporated adapters and other impurities. The purified library was quantified to determine DNA concentration using a Qubit fluorometer. The prepared DNA library was loaded onto a MinION flow cell for sequencing. The flow cell was primed with a buffer solution to prepare the surface for DNA binding. The library was mixed with a sequencing buffer and loading beads, which were then loaded onto the flow cell. The MinION device performed real‐time sequencing, generating raw sequence data that was subsequently analyzed using bioinformatics pipelines [18].

Sociodemographic Data Analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Chi‐square tests were used to compare categorical variables. The study employed CDC's Epi Info 7 for questionnaire design, data entry. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.3.

Sequence Data Analysis

Raw sequence data generated by the MinION sequencer was basecalled into FASTQ format using Guppy v6.0. Quality control was implemented to filter reads based on length (450–550 bp) and quality (Phred score > 9). Non‐fungal reads were removed by aligning sequences against the UNITE fungal ITS reference database using minimap2 and samtools. Barcode sequences were removed using porechop, and the resulting reads were demultiplexed based on their respective barcodes. To improve sequence accuracy, error correction and de novo assembly were performed using the RATTLE software, generating draft genome assemblies for each sample. These assemblies were further polished using Medaka to obtain consensus sequences. Taxonomic identification was conducted using BLASTn, with a stringent cutoff of 97% identity, low E‐value (1e‐5), and high bit score to assign genus and species levels. Phylogenetic analysis was performed to investigate the evolutionary relationships among identified fungal taxa. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using Figtree with 1000 bootstrap replicates [19, 20].

Statistical tests were performed primarily for the sociodemographic data using R version 4.3.3. χ 2 tests, with priori level of significance (α = 0.05) and the use of two‐sided tests was performed.

Fungal Colonies Identified From Culture‐Based Investigation

Out of the 63 study participants, cultures of the wound swabs from 16 individuals (25.4%) showed fungal colonies from the culture‐based approach. This represents the prevalence of fungal populations among chronic lymphoedema patients in Southern Ghana. These colonies showed various morphological characteristics: pinkish colonies, some looked whitish and fluffy, some looked sporous, some looked green, and others looked dark with spores, as indicated in Figure 3.

Molecular Identification of Fungal Populations Within the LF Wound Environment

Following culture, the different fungi colonies were identified by sequencing their respective amplified ITS region. These investigations showed fungal populations from nine (9) different genera: Aspergillus, Penicillium, Cladosporium, Candida, Periconia, Lasiodiplodia, Acrophialophora, Zygosporium, and Trichoderma (Figure 4). Further analysis revealed 24 distinct fungal species from these genera occurring at different frequencies (Figure 5). Cladosporium spp. emerged as the predominant species, accounting for 30.0% of isolates. Aspergillus spp. followed closely at 28.6%, followed by Penicillium spp. (12.9%), Periconia spp. (14.3%), and Candida spp. (8.6%). A smaller proportion of isolates represented Lasiodiplodia spp., Acrophialophora spp., Zygosporium spp., and Trichoderma spp., each comprising 1.4% of the total. Although not the predominant genus, Aspergillus showed the highest diversity in species. It was also discovered that only 2 out of the 16 patients (F005 and F006) did not present with mixed fungal populations in their lymphedema wounds, as indicated in Table 2 below.

Discussion

Despite extensive research on fungal infections in chronic wounds, the role of fungi in filarial lymphedema wounds still needs to be studied, hindering effective management [21]. Our study revealed a significant gender disparity in lymphedema prevalence, with women disproportionately affected and often lacking formal education. This aligns with findings that societal roles and cultural beliefs influence healthcare access in endemic regions [22, 23]. Additionally, the agricultural livelihoods common in these areas increase exposure to mosquito vectors, contributing to the burden of lymphedema and disabilities that impede workforce participation, exacerbating poverty [24, 25]. The majority of participants in this study were involved in agriculture, further supporting the link between agricultural livelihoods and lymphatic filariasis prevalence.

In the present study, culturing samples from the LF wound swabs showed diverse morphological characteristics, highlighting the complex and heterogeneous nature of fungal infections in this patient population. Here, fluffy colonies observed from the culture are often associated with filamentous fungi such as Aspergillus and Penicillium. These genera are characterized by their rapid, cotton‐like growth due to the extensive production of hyphae [26]. Culture‐based methods have traditionally identified Malassezia, and Candida species as common skin commensals, and Aspergillus as opportunistic skin infection in recent molecular studies, with next‐generation sequencing (NGS), confirming their prevalence [27, 28]. The isolation of these yeast‐like and filamentous fungal colonies in LF wounds indicates a complex fungal ecology that can directly impair wound healing by interfering with re‐epithelialization, collagen synthesis, and angiogenesis. While the study identified a significant diversity of fungal species using molecular methods, it is important to acknowledge the relatively low culture positivity rate, with only 16 out of 63 (25.4%) patient samples yielding culturable fungi. This discrepancy can be attributed to several factors, including the inherent limitations of traditional culture methods, which may not support the growth of all fastidious or viable but non‐culturable fungal taxa present in complex wound environments. Additionally, the nature of superficial wound swabs may not adequately capture fungi embedded deeper within tissues or biofilms, while the presence of competing bacterial communities or antimicrobial treatments in the wound microenvironment could further suppress fungal growth in vitro [29, 30].

Notably, this study revealed that most patients (14 out of 16) from whom fungi were isolated presented with mixed fungal populations in their lymphedema wounds. While this observation suggests the highly complex and often polymicrobial and diverse nature of chronic wounds associated with lymphatic filariasis, it is important to acknowledge that the identification of these mixed fungal populations from superficial swabs does not definitively distinguish between active infection and colonization by environmental or commensal fungi [10]. Nevertheless, the prevalence of such mixed fungal populations highlights the potential for complex host‐pathogen interactions and may contribute to the persistent and challenging nature of these wounds, potentially influencing treatment outcomes and creating an environment conducive to antibiotic resistance, as discussed previously.

With the identities of these fungal colonies investigated based on ITS region sequencing, Cladosporium spp. emerged as the predominant species, accounting for 30.0% of isolates. Aspergillus spp. followed closely at 28.6%. The mixed colonisation of Cladosporium and Aspergillus species in the wound environment is concerning, as these opportunistic pathogens can cause severe infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals such as filarial patients presenting with damaged lymphatics [31, 32, 33]. The ubiquitous nature of Cladosporium species, particularly Cladosporium cladosporioides, commonly isolated from diverse environmental niches, suggests a potential exogenous source for their presence in wound infections [34]. Therefore, the predominance of Cladosporium spp. is unsurprising due to increased exposure of the lower extremities to the environment, due to the patient's inability to wear appropriate footwear due to limb enlargement [35]. Cladosporium spp. are known to produce melanins and other extracellular enzymes, which can contribute to chronic inflammation and tissue damage. Their ability to survive harsh conditions and form biofilms can impede wound healing, making them persistent colonizers that might delay re‐epithelialization and increase the chronicity of the wound. Clinically, their presence may necessitate prolonged wound care and, in certain cases, antifungal interventions if they are implicated in persistent inflammation or poor healing [36, 37].

While Aspergillus species were not the most prevalent fungal genus identified, they exhibited the greatest species diversity within the study population with eight distinct Aspergillus spp. identified from the molecular investigation. The phylogenetic diversity observed among Aspergillus species, characterized by multiple closely related strains, suggests a recent evolutionary divergence. This diversification may be attributed to adaptation to different ecological niches or selective pressures, potentially explaining the coexistence of generally both pathogenic (Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus niger) and non‐pathogenic (Aspergillus aculeatus, Aspergillus corrugatus, Aspergillus spp., Aspergillus subversicolor, Aspergillus sydowii, and Aspergillus versicolor) Aspergillus species identified within this study cohort. In the context of chronic wounds, Aspergillus can cause invasive infections, leading to tissue necrosis and systemic spread, although this is less common in superficial colonization. Even as colonizers, Aspergillus can produce mycotoxins that may contribute to local tissue damage and inflammation, thereby exacerbating the wound environment. The filamentous nature of Aspergillus allows for deeper tissue penetration compared to yeasts, potentially complicating debridement and creating a more challenging environment for healing [38, 39].

The fungal microbiome of the foot and other moist skin areas often includes Candida, Aspergillus, and Penicillium [11, 40]. The study also identified Periconia spp., Penicillium spp., and Candida spp., which are ubiquitous dematiaceous (dark‐pigmented) fungi commonly found in the environment, particularly in soil, decaying vegetation, and as airborne spores, and in opportunistic infections. Their prevalence in chronic wounds suggests they act as opportunistic colonizers or secondary invaders, rather than being primary pathogens in every case. Although not typically considered primary pathogens, their persistent presence may contribute to the chronic nature of wounds [10]. This occurs either directly through the production of mycotoxins and allergens, which can exacerbate tissue damage and inflammation, or indirectly by creating a polymicrobial environment that hinders healing [41, 42, 43]. This fungal diversity suggests a complex microbial ecosystem that can interact synergistically with bacteria, potentially enhancing virulence or contributing to antibiotic resistance, as reported for some mixed fungal‐bacterial biofilms [44]. Candida spp., a dimorphic fungus, can evade host defence and establish persistent infections. A close genetic relationship observed in the phylogenetic tree between Aspergillus and Penicillium suggests similar ecological roles or habitats.

A previous study by [45] reported a significant prevalence of fungal infections (9.1%–40.1%) in diabetic wounds, predominantly involving Candida species. In contrast, our study identified a lower prevalence of Candida species in filarial lymphedema patients, with C. orthopsilosis, C. parapsilosis, and C. tropicalis being the primary isolates. Although less prevalent, these Candida species, especially C. orthopsilosis, are recognized as significant pathogens in wound infections. This low prevalence might be attributed to several factors, including the specific characteristics of the LF wound environment, the presence of competing microbial flora, or the sensitivity of the isolation methods used. It is also possible that while Candida might cause symptomatic infections, the chronic nature of LF wounds might favor the persistence of more environmentally hardy fungi. Further studies are needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which these prevalent environmental fungi interact with the host and other microbes in the LF wound microenvironment.

Additionally, the study identified fungal species that are not commonly human pathogens but can act as opportunistic pathogens under certain conditions. Lasiodiplodia spp. and Zygosporium spp. identified are known plant pathogens that are increasingly implicated in active infections, including bacteremia, meningitis, and endocarditis in immunocompromised patients [46]. Acrophialophora spp., another group of ubiquitous environmental fungi, was isolated. They are primarily associated with subcutaneous infections and are known for their ability to cause disseminated disease in vulnerable patient populations [47]. Trichoderma spp. is well‐known for its biocontrol properties and has also emerged as an opportunistic pathogen in certain clinical settings [48, 49]. The identification of these plant and environmental fungal species, which are uncommon human pathogens, suggests potential environmental contamination. It is possible that this contamination may have been introduced through inadequate hygiene practices or the use of soil‐based or plant‐derived substances in wound care rather than true colonizers actively proliferating within the wound or contributing to its pathogenesis [50].

The phylogenetic analysis significantly strengthened our understanding of the strain diversity, potential sources of colonization, and host‐pathogen dynamics of the fungal populations in these wounds, as shown in Figure 6. Specifically, the clustering of Periconia spp. and Cladosporium spp. into distinct monophyletic clades, even from different patients, suggests a relatively homogenous genetic makeup or a predominant widespread environmental strain for these genera within the study area, indicative of their ubiquitous presence and consistent interaction with these wounds. Conversely, the dispersed clustering of Candida, Penicillium, and Aspergillus spp. isolates indicate higher genetic diversity within these genera, implying multiple independent colonization events from varied sources, consistent with their established roles as opportunistic pathogens with diverse environmental or host‐associated strains, as widely reported in complex microbial environments like chronic wounds [51]. This genetic heterogeneity among clinically relevant fungi underscores the varied pathogenic potential and presents challenges for generalized therapeutic strategies, highlighting the need for precise identification to guide effective patient management.

Future studies should incorporate methods such as quantitative PCR to differentiate between transient environmental contamination, colonization, and active infection. Assessing fungal load, viability assays, or in‐depth histopathological examination of wound biopsies to observe tissue invasion by fungal elements will also be relevant. Furthermore, incorporating detailed microscopic examinations, including the use of light microscopy and potentially electron microscopy, could enhance the phenotypic characterization and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the fungal isolates. Such approaches would provide a clearer understanding of the pathogenic role of identified fungal species in chronic LF wounds.

Additionally, Oxford Nanopore Sequencing (ONT) provides valuable insights into fungal genetics, but ITS sequencing alone has limitations for precise species‐level identification. Combining ITS sequencing with other targeted sequencing techniques, such as beta‐tubulin and 28S rRNA sequencing, and advanced bioinformatics tools can address these limitations, enabling a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of fungal diversity and resistance profiles within LF wounds. This information can inform clinical decisions and optimize patient outcomes. Furthermore, while the findings reveal a significant fungal presence, further research is warranted to ascertain the precise pathogenic roles of these fungi (e.g., biofilm formation, enzyme production, toxin release), and their interaction with the host immune response is crucial for tailoring LF‐associated wound care protocols.

Conclusion

This study presents, for the first time, a diverse range of fungal populations in individuals with chronic LF wounds in southern Ghana. The presence of these fungi may contribute to an environment that fosters the emergence of antibiotic resistance, underlining the complexity of host‐pathogen interactions in this vulnerable population. The findings emphasize the need for a comprehensive approach to managing chronic wounds that includes addressing both bacterial and fungal components.
