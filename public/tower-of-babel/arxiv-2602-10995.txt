Abstract
. In the current Large Language Model (LLM) ecosystem, creators have little agency over how their data is used, and LLM users may find themselves unknowingly plagiarizing existing sources. Attribution of LLM-generated text to LLM input data could help with these challenges, but so far we have more questions than answers: what elements of LLM outputs require attribution, what goals should it serve, how should it be implemented? We contribute a human-centric data attribution framework, which situates the attribution problem within the broader data economy. Specific use cases for attribution, such as creative writing assistance or fact-checking, can be specified via a set of parameters (including stakeholder objectives and implementation criteria). These criteria are up for negotiation by the relevant stakeholder groups: creators, LLM users, and their intermediaries (publishers, platforms, AI companies). The outcome of domain-specific negotiations can be implemented and tested for whether the stakeholder goals are achieved. The proposed approach provides a bridge between methodological NLP work on data attribution, governance work on policy interventions, and economic analysis of creator incentives for a sustainable equilibrium in the data economy.



1.
Introduction

The current Large Language Models (LLM) ecosystem is socially unsustainable. LLM performance is predicated on high-quality training data
(
Sankaran 2024
)
, but its creators currently have little agency over the use of their data, or economic benefits from it. Instead, they witness their data being scraped or watch their publishers license their content without their consent
(
AI watchdog 2025
)
. At the same time, LLM users can not tell either whether the outputs are trustworthy
(
Jaźwińska and Chandrasekar [n. d.]
)
, and when they infringe on someone’s rights
(
Gupta and Pruthi 2025a
)
. The LLM industry currently benefits from the status quo, but long-term it also depends on a healthy data economy: if creators do not have meaningful incentives to participate with new high-quality data, their contributions will decline
(
Longpre et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
)
.

In theory, data attribution presents a solution.
We consider the data attribution task broadly as providing acknowledgment that a certain work was in some way used by the model to produce an output. In Natural Language Processing (NLP) research, attribution is typically

Data attribution could help to sustain a healthy data economy by incentivizing the creators to contribute to the data available to LLMs, and supporting the LLM users by references to trustworthy sources.

Data attribution for LLMs is an active research area, but it is still largely unsolved.
It requires transparency about the training data, which commercial services currently do not even disclose.
More fundamentally, it is not clear what requires attribution. Intuitively, it varies: nobody needs a reference for “grass is green”, whereas news-worthy quotes or claims clearly need a source. But there is a lot of gray area, and many use cases require different solutions (e.g. some facts may be attributable via string matching, but this would not work for stylistic features in creative writing). Which technical solutions are needed can only be defined by practical goals, but they are external to NLP research.

This work situates the data attribution problem in the LLM data ecosystem (
section

3
). We characterize the variety and conflicts of goals of different stakeholders, which make a single data attribution solution impossible. To address that, we contribute a human-centric data attribution framework (
section

4
), which grounds the implementation of data attribution in case-by-case negotiations between stakeholders.
We then present a moonshot for what LLM data attribution could look like in practice (
section

5
). This work builds a bridge between practical NLP research and the broader socioeconomic context of data attribution task, and we hope it would also be useful for economic and governance work, providing a blueprint for how the data attribution task could be specified for a practical implementation.

NLP Work on Data Attribution and Valuation.

In modern LLM systems, data can shape model behavior in two ways: as
training data
which determines model parameters, as as
input context
which provided to the model at inference time.
With regards to training data attribution, most existing methods are post hoc, aiming to associate a given LLM generation with training examples using techniques such as influence functions
(
Koh and Liang 2017
)
, Shapley-value-based data attribution
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, text similarity functions
(
Akyurek et al. 2022
)
, among others.
There are also approaches relevant to attribution that focus on different roles of subsets of training data at model development phase:
this includes methods like explicit training data weighting
(
Yoon et al. 2019
)
and modular model architectures with respect to data
(
Shi et al. 2025
)
, among others.
A complementary line of work examines attribution-aware system design, among which the notable example are retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
that restrict model conditioning to an explicit set of input data, also enabling direct attribution of generations to underlying sources
(
Menick et al. 2022
)
.
Livni et al. 2024
proposes a notion of attribution based on differential privacy, where the system would give credit for works that influence a given generation.
Finally, data valuation research
(
Sim et al. 2022
;
Choe et al. 2024
)
aims to quantify the value of a given data point or data subsets for model training. See
(
Worledge et al. 2024
;
Deng et al. 2025
)
for methodological surveys of attribution methods.

Work on Socioeconomic Aspects of Data Attribution.

There have long been calls to recognize data as a kind of labor
(
Lanier 2014
;
Arrieta Ibarra et al. 2017
)
. There is recent discussion of AI systems as data markets (rather than search engines or chatbots)
(
Jordan 2025
)
, and game-theoretic analysis of consequences of unfair data pricing
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
. Warning of the dangers for the information economy,
Vincent et al. 2025
argue for collective bargaining by a large coalition of data creators to counter the power of companies providing LLM services.

Our Contribution.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work attempting to provide a practical bridge between the above areas, i.e. NLP work on data governance and attribution, and socioeconomic work on LLMs in the data economy. To the latter, it may prove useful by outlining the main aspects of NLP research on data attribution and assessing the capabilities of current technology. For the former, this work explicitly brings in the stakeholders and their varying/conflicting goals into the definition of data attribution problem.
Wang et al. 2024
propose a specific solution for the compensating of creators (based on Shapley-values for collections of training data), and call for cooperation between creators and LLM-developers. We contribute a broader framework for similar contributions across many attribution use cases.

3.
The Current LLM Ecosystem: Creators, Distributors and Recipients of Data

We consider the
LLM data ecosystem
as the set of main stakeholders who directly or indirectly participate in LLM service development and use.
The diversity of their goals and options to achieve them is discussed in
section

3.1
.
Historically, the relations between data economy stakeholders have been mediated by legal and professional norms such as copyright, plagiarism and authorship, which we assume the reader to be broadly familiar with. For reference, we outline them in
appendix

A
. We then summarize how this data ecosystem and its norms were disrupted when LLMs became a new information distribution channel (
3.2
). Finally, we outline what role data attribution research could play in the re-negotiation of stakeholder roles for a sustainable data economy.

3.1.
Stakeholders

In scope of this work, we consider five groups of stakeholders in the LLM data ecosystem, defined as follows:

•

Creators:
the individuals who satisfy the authorship criteria (
section

A.4
) relevant for their field of activity.

•

Publishers:
the organizations disseminating the creators’ work, typically with at least a partial transfer of rights (see
section

A.1
) to the publisher. Publishers may add value through e.g. editorial services (though this is not always the case
(
Phelps 2022
)
) and prestige
(
Palmer 2024
)
.

•

Platforms:
the third-party services providing the creators with means of dissemination and/or monetization of their work (e.g. Kindle self-publishing, Reddit, blog platforms like Medium). Unlike publishers, platforms typically do not add editorial value. They also historically differ from publishers in terms of liability for content they provide
(
Skorup and Huddleston 2019
)
.

•

AI industry:
organizations that commercially provide LLM-based services, typically branded as ‘AI’, and derived predominantly from the creators’ data.

•

Readers/Users:
the individuals acting as consumers of the creators’ work for professional or recreational purposes.

We consider the stakeholder groups representing the individuals as
primary stakeholders
: the creators and their intended audience. This is motivated by the fact that they are the main sources of value in the ecosystem (producers and consumers). Three other groups represent organizations that currently act as
intermediaries
between the two: publishers, platforms and AI industry. For example, a blogger is a creator, who may distribute their work to readers via a platform like Medium in order to monetize it via ads.

We group the incentives into extrinsic and intrinsic, per classic high-level distinction of motivation types in psychology
(

In contrast, social incentives heavily depend on attribution: a content creator e.g. can only establish themselves as an expert if their knowledge is attributed to them. Our literature review suggests the following observations relevant to the attribution problem.

•

Motivations within stakeholder groups are highly heterogeneous.
Even within a single sub-domain such as travel blogs, writers may work under different mixes of economic, social and intrinsic incentives. Likewise, intermediary organizations vary in how much precedence their non-financial goals may have.

•

The stakeholder roles may be mixed.
For example, creators may also act as their own publishers (providing content for free, for subscriptions or donations, or add-supported via their own websites or platforms). They may also themselves be users of LLM services, which results in an interesting conflict: creators may prefer to indirectly use other creators’ work for free, while getting paid for their own work (e.g. some writers use generated art
(
Robertson 2025
)
). Public-interest research, at least in NLP, is increasingly mixed with commercial conflicts-of-interest

(
Abdalla et al. 2023
)
. For-profit AI industry may contribute to open-source, e.g. to undercut their competition
(
Widder et al. 2024
)
. The mixed roles may be reflected in different attitudes of other stakeholders: e.g. a creator may be more willing for their texts to be processed by a public-interest researcher than a commercial entity developing a product (though even research use is ethically complicated
(
Klassen and Fiesler 2022
;
Kairam et al. 2024
)
and has its own attribution issues
(
Bruckman 2002
)
).

•

Conflicting interests.
The clearest example is that the working authors may prefer to maximize their earnings, while the intermediaries who distribute their work or its derivatives may prefer to maximize their share of that transaction (see
section

3.2
). Intermediaries may have conflicting interests between themselves, e.g. in news organizations trying to get platforms to pay for their content
(
Nicholls 2024a
)
. Stakeholders may differ in their position to other stakeholders: e.g. publishers largely oppose the existence of the Internet Archive as a non-profit digital library for humans
(
202 2022
)
, while many authors support it
(
100 [n. d.]
)
.

•

Structural power asymmetries.
Even before LLMs, major publishers and platforms, as large organizations, had a significant advantage in legal resources over individuals (both creators and users). They may also enjoy a monopsony position
(
Manning 2005
;
Brooks 2020
)
, i.e. become relatively free from competition to which the individuals could easily move. For-profit academic publishers are a prominent example
(
Phelps 2022
;
Palmer 2024
)
. Big tech platforms show a similar trend: once a given market is captured and it is costly or impossible to switch, they can unilaterally change the terms in their favor
(
Doctorow 2025
)
, and refuse to negotiate even in response to legislation
(
Zandbergen 2023
;
Nicholls 2024b
)
. Among recent examples, consider that after Stackoverflow announced a deal with Microsoft
(
202 2024
)
, some of its outraged users started deleting their past contributions – and got banned for it
(
Edwards 2024
)
.

To conclude, the data economy is characterized by a highly heterogeneous landscape of incentives for various stakeholders, which may have mixed roles, different levels of market power, and conflicting interests. This motivates our proposal for a granular rather than broad approaches to the data attribution problem, in which the intrinsic, financial and social incentives of specific stakeholders would be considered on a case-by-case basis.

LLMs as a New Information Distribution Channel

As discussed in
section

3.1
, the data economy involves various intermediaries between the primary stakeholders (creators and readers/users). In such a situation, one could expect conflicts of interests based on how exactly the intermediaries mediate the transactions
and what would be a fair share for them. From that point of view, the generative chatbots could be described as a new distribution channel through which information moves from creators to readers. This disrupts the original flow of value, and does not provide equivalent incentives for the creators.

4.
Human-centric Data Attribution

In this section, we present our human-centric data attribution framework as a bridge between stakeholder negotiations and technical implementation. In §
4.1
, we show how attribution goals are shaped through negotiations among creators, users, and intermediaries for particular LLM use cases.
In §
4.2
, we analyze the technical attribution criteria that can be used to realize these goals in practice, and discuss their feasibility and consequences.

4.1.
From Stakeholder Goals to Attribution Requirements

To reiterate, we consider data attribution as the broad problem of establishing if the model used a certain work to produce a certain output. This definition covers multiple technical approaches: attribution at instance level versus broad model functionality (e.g. a specific claim that came from a specific document, generating text in a certain style), attribution to data provided at training versus inference time (e.g. with RAG), attribution based on internal model processes or human-interpretable similarity. In all of these cases, insofar as attribution provides a trace of certain data being used, it can support the economic incentives, social signals, and epistemic traces relevant for the stakeholders.

As discussed in
section

3.1
, stakeholder motivations vary a lot. For example, a fiction writer may be willing for LLMs to be trained on their work, but only if they are compensated. At the same time, the authors of academic papers may be willing for their texts to be used for RAG for free, since their main ‘currency’ is citations and social credit. This variety entails that it is neither possible nor desirable to have a single solution that would cover all the cases (especially given the imbalance in the power of stakeholders to negotiate it).

To address this, we propose the human-centric attribution framework, shown in
Figure 2
. The main goal is to accommodate the possible needs of various stakeholders without prescribing a specific set of preferences. Each attribution scenario is specified in the following steps.

Step 2: Negotiate the Intended Outcomes of Attribution.

Since there may be conflicting objectives for data attribution, the relevant stakeholders must negotiate to find a sustainable balance between their needs.
We note that this process follows the overall spirit of copyright law, which aims to “strike the right balance between the interests of content creators, developers and investors and the public interest in being able to access and use creative content.”
(
202 2020

As one possible example of conflicting objectives in such a negotiation, let us consider information seeking queries to LLMs. They have fact-checking as a natural use case for data attribution. Figure

Economically, the LLM service may prefer to maximize its own revenue, the users may prefer to get the information for free, whereas the creators may prefer that LLM services would compensate the expert sources when their information is viewed via the service. In terms of the epistemic signal, the creators may prefer that the service prominently displays relevant snippets of cited sources, given that the RAG summary may misrepresent the cited sources
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Jaźwińska and Chandrasekar [n. d.]

. As for the users, many may prefer to get simple generated answers, and the LLM service might generally prefer to do whatever is more popular with users. However, there may also be policy considerations of public informedness, especially on high-risk topics like health advice, which might encourage fact-checking. If enough institutional customers such as schools have such policies, this may even create a financial incentive for LLM services to better integrate fact-checking.

4.2.
Technical Criteria for Data Attribution

At present, there are three broad groups of attribution criteria: similarity to existing content (
section

4.2.1
), causal influence on the model (
section

4.2.2
),
and whether the data was used for model development or deployment
(
section

4.2.3
).
They rely on different technical principles, and would likely result in fundamentally different outcomes for the stakeholders. It is possible that one negotiated case involves more than one type of attribution (e.g. both dataset-level for training, and for specific instances when they are used in generation).

4.2.1.
Is the Output Similar to Existing Content?

A generated text could be conceptually and/or structurally similar to a text that was provided to the model at some point, and on this basis the attribution link would be established. This attribution may be the easiest to grasp for humans, but such similarity does
not
indicate that the model relied on a given instance in the generation process
(
Huang et al. 2024
)
.
Worledge et al. 2024
describe such attribution as ‘corroborative’.

The actual criteria for similarity may vary widely across use cases, and should be agreed on by the relevant stakeholders.
Programmers may prioritize structural matches with their proprietary algorithm – the way a problem is solved as opposed the exact way the code is written.
In academia, where writing style is relatively rigid and uniform to begin with, attributing scientific claims may be more important than attributing n-gram overlaps or argument structure. For fiction writers, attributing claims may be negligible, while structures like style are essential.

Once it is clear what kind of similarity should be measured, we need to ensure that the attribution is manageable for the user. A given generation may be supported by hundreds of highly similar instances. Hence, we need a fair strategy for selecting the most relevant ones. We propose that the following implementation principles be considered:

(1)

Uniqueness
: attribution units should be considered when they are relatively rare (e.g. there is no need for a reference for “sky is blue” or “time is money”).

(2)

Chronological precedence
: assuming there are multiple potential references, i.e., high similarity matches, the most attribution-worthy is the one that is the oldest. Without such mechanism, there would be an incentive to create multiple copies of content for the attribution credit.

(3)

Extent of shared context
with the training document(s) the attributable unit appeared in. This criterion characterizes how competitive the output is with the attribution candidate document. For example, if a given trope is reused in the context of a fiction story with the similar story arc, it increases its competitiveness and attribution-worthiness.

Current Technical Feasibility

Measuring similarity between two text units is a core NLP task that has been studied for a long time and has many efficient solutions, including string-based
(
Kusner et al. 2015
)
, statistical
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
, based on linguistic structure
(
Oya 2020
)
, and neural
(
Mikolov et al. 2013
)
. There are also newer, more computationally expensive methods including contextual embeddings
(
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
)
, LLM-based methods
(
Vera et al. 2025
;
BehnamGhader et al. 2024
;
Su et al. 2023
)
and hybrid methods
(
Formal et al. 2021
)
. However, most of this research has focused on generic distributional similarity, rather than the aspect-specific similarity that would e.g. enable a writer to compare their story arc to a possible source. Instruction-following methods could specify fine-grained similarity criteria
(
Weller et al. 2025
)
, but this would require running an extra LLM just for attribution. To the best of our knowledge, there is only one proposed solution that supports the model generation process with similar snippets from the model training data, and it only supports verbatim string matches
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
.

Suggested directions for future NLP research.

To support various attribution use cases, the NLP community would need efficient methods for identifying specific types of textual similarity,
as well as efficient semantic search solutions for LLM-scale corpora.
Any such solutions would also need to be evaluated for precision, recall and also potential bias for any specific attribution scenario.

4.2.2.
Did the Data Causally Influence the Model?

Causality-based attribution (‘contributive attribution’ in
Worledge et al. 2024
) aims to quantify the influence that training data points have on model behavior by estimating the change in model outputs or parameters under counterfactual training scenarios, such as removing or reweighting data points.
In contrast to the above similarity-based approach, text identified by causality-based methods may seem unconnected to the generated text. For attribution to training data, causality-based methods are functions that estimates the importance of a training instance given a specific model, its training data, a given input and the output generated in response to that input. Another relevant line of research is
data curation
where data ablations or data quality assessments is used to establish how much training on a given dataset contributed to model improvement on a given metric
(
Wettig et al. 2025
;
Olmo et al. 2025
)
.

Shi et al. 2025
proposes an architectural paradigm of federated training different experts of a Mixture-of-Experts model
(
Jacobs et al. 1991
;
Shazeer et al. 2017
)
on isolated datasets from known owners. The trained router of the model can then be used for attributing the contribution of different datasets to a models’ output.

Current technical feasibility

Causality-based methods are challenging to implement on an LLM scale, as their costs scale with dataset size and parameter counts, making them prohibitively expensive.

Wang et al. 2024
propose using Shapley values, which estimate the utility of training data subsets to value data and compensate contributors. The main drawback is that a model has to be retrained multiple times to establish the utility of different data subsets. Model-centric approaches such as influence functions measure a data point’s counterfactual effects on the model parameters
(
Worledge et al. 2024
)
. Given a generated output, the influence of each individual training sample can be calculated.
At present, while several teams proposed influence functions-based data attribution solutions at the smaller LLM scale
(
Park et al. 2023
;
Chang et al. 2024
;
Grosse et al. 2023
)
, but there are also concerns about their performance
(
Li et al. 2024
)
and what they actually reflect
(
Bae et al. 2022
)
.

Suggested directions for future NLP research.

Further work is needed on computationally efficient and model-faithful data attribution methods.
Furthermore, since these methods reflect the model process, but the result may not be human-interpretable, it remains to be seen whether they could be effectively used by non-experts in practical data attribution scenarios.
To use causal attribution methods for data valuation, future research also needs to consider how training goals and the interaction between different training data impact valuation. We need to understand for example to which extent coding data may be more valuable than fiction data for a code assistant model or if the value of an additional fiction dataset may be decreased if one fiction dataset has already been included in training.
Finally, the RAG setting poses faithfulness challenges that require research: should the credit of the citation change based on whether the retrieved information was ‘new’ to the model, or was already available in parametric knowledge?

4.2.3.
Was the Data Used in Any Capacity During Model Development or Deployment?

For this criterion, any usage of a creator’s data
during model development (including any intermediary models) or deployment
elicits attribution. This holds even if the data does not appear in a particular output, does not causally influence it, and even if training on a given dataset did not improve the model performance on a metric of interest.
This criterion is relevant in cases where attribution serves as acknowledgment rather than as a measure of causal influence, and any remuneration does not depend on a disclosure about how or to what extent that data shaped specific outputs or functionality.
For example, if a dataset is overall free to use with attribution (e.g. Wiki data), and the model is used for language assistance rather than information, a data partner may be satisfied if the use of their data is simply publicly acknowledged.

Current technical feasibility

With cooperation of all parties, this would be the easiest and computationally cheapest method to implement.
It would require open training and development corpora, source use transparency in RAG setups and the creators’ informed consent if their data is to be included. This could follow the recommendations described by
(
Kyi et al. 2025
)
, standardized in a license for AI training that content owners can grant to model developers to establish “collective or representative consent mechanisms”
(
Kyi et al. 2025
)
.

In the absence of such a cooperation, relatively little can be done as a comprehensive solution. Inference attack methods may be used by specific stakeholders to inspect models that they suspect of violating their rights. For example, a model could be simply prompted to complete a snippet of proprietary text, and its success would indicate that the content was seen during training and memorized
(
Carlini et al. 2022
)
.
Methods designed to detect data contamination, meaning overlaps between training and evaluation data of a language model, could also be extended to attribution.
Deng et al. 2024
provide an overview, including detection methods that investigate if models can correctly reproduce key information (such as named entities), and sensitivity tests, which measure if a model has a preference for the canonical order of input data.

Suggested directions for future NLP research.
The biggest problem with inference attack methods is that the onus is on the creator to find out about the violation of their rights, and then take legal action. One could imagine solutions to support a signed-up list of authors, who would like to systematically monitor new models for rights violations, and coordinate for class action. Then a further challenge would be proving the exact source and extent of exposure (e.g. maybe some elements from copyrighted works were learned from their mentions in non-copyrighted materials).
Beyond inference attacks, NLP research could further advance model transparency, documentation and reporting practices (e.g. datasheets
(
Gebru et al. 2020
)
, model cards
(
Mitchell et al. 2019
)
, see
section

2
), that could serve as conventions for such acknowledgment-based attribution.

6.
Conclusion

So far, LLM services have been in an adversarial relation with the creators whose work makes them possible. But it does not have to be this way long-term. We are only in the beginning of a long process of legislative and normative adaptation to the new technology. Data attribution research can support the collective action processes, in which the terms would be iteratively re-negotiated with intermediary parties. The proposed human-centric data attribution framework (
Section

4
) bridges socioeconomic research and policy work with research on NLP methods, which could facilitate meaningful incentives for high-quality contributions to the data economy. To the former we provide a blueprint for how case-specific attribution requirements could be obtained and specified for a technical implementation, and what is currently feasible. NLP researchers benefit from a clear grounding of their work in the real-world demands, and an external source of construct validity and evaluation criteria.

For the creators, the current state of data economy leaves much to be desired, but it is also ‘a crisis we shouldn’t let go to waste’
(
Germain 2025
)
. Pre-LLMs, much of the web had to rely on ad-based monetization, which incentivized its own questionable practices such as clickbait. LLM services backed by data attribution could provide new incentives to create content in direct response to demand for its substance. And they could do so in a clear, legitimate dialogue with other creators, crediting their influences and receiving their own acknowledgments.

Limitations and Future Work

This work is an interdisciplinary endeavor, with origins and primary audience in data attribution NLP research. We aim to position the task of data attribution in the current socioeconomic reality, and explicate what role it could play in shaping a more socially sustainable future. We expect that economists, governance experts, political scientists or legal scholars would describe the processes in
section

3
differently, using the rich theoretical apparatus of their disciplines. We hope that they would not judge this work too harshly (as long as the analysis is generally correct and actionable), and that they would engage with the community developing attribution methods to explain any missing pieces of this puzzle.

As described in
section

3.1
, the LLM ecosystem contains a multitide of stakeholders that perform similar roles with widely varying motivations and hence would respond to different incentives. Our overview of these incentives focuses on three main types of motivation (social, financial and intrinsic factors). A more fine-grained classification of possible objectives could be developed to assist stakeholder negotiations. It is possible that the process of such negotiations would identify necessary revisions to the framework (thought the overall multi-stakeholder perspective is essential). A more detailed blueprint for stakeholder negotiations could be developed by experts in mediation, and more implementation options for possible use cases could be proposed (e.g. based on the analysis of arguments of both parties in ongoing litigation). While there is much discussion of economic and epistemic aspects of attribution, its potential to support the creators’ moral rights (including the right to object to misrepresentation) should also be considered.

In this paper we do not describe any experiments with human subjects, users and/or deployed systems. Further, we do not rely on any sensitive user data.
This work focuses on mitigating negative impacts of LLM-based technology that is currently deployed.

Appendix A
Background: Relevant Legal and Professional-Conduct-Related Concepts

A.1.
Copyright

Copyright is a subtype of intellectual property that applies to “creative expression of ideas in many different forms”
(
202 2020
, p.21-22)
, and includes both economic and moral rights (see
section

A.3
). It may be implemented differently in various national legal systems.
In the US copyright law, legal action requires establishing substantial similarity, i.e. that two works are “so alike in protected elements that one work infringes upon the other”
(
Hickey 2015
)
. However, determining substantial similarity in legal practice is highly non-trivial (ibid).
A relevant part of copyright law is subsidiary rights: they enable e.g. publishing houses to further license content translation or TV adaption.

A.2.
‘Fair use’ Doctrine

In the current LLM ecosystem, companies that build commercial large language models have mostly been non-compliant with copyright and licensing agreements. In the US, where major LLM developers are located, they argue that their application falls under the ‘fair use’ doctrine
(
Belanger 2023
)
. However, even in the US, whether the ‘fair use’ applies depends in each case on transformativeness, nature of copyrighted work, amount and substantiality, and effect on market for the original work
(
Henderson et al. 2023
, p.5)
. Importantly, the market effect must be from infringement, not merely from competition from a noninfringing work
(
Henderson et al. 2023
, p.7)
. The interaction between these factors is complex and case-specific, and the US legal battle for commercial LLMs is not yet settled.

A.3.
Moral Rights of the Authors

While most countries have some version of copyright laws, which can result in criminal charges, the moral rights may be covered by civil law or even only be part of social conventions. Many countries are signatories to the Berne Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works, but it is not by itself legally binding. Article 6 asserts the moral right “to claim authorship; to object to certain modifications and other derogatory actions”
(
197 1979
)
, while leaving the means of redress to the legislation of individual countries. For example, the US is a signatory to this convention, but has no federal law to comprehensively define such means of redress, relying instead on “a patchwork of federal and state laws, as well as industry customs and other forms of private ordering”
(
Temple 2019
)
. In France, on the other hand, both the moral and pecuniary rights are separate categories recognized by law
(
Sutherland-Smith 2016
)
, and the moral rights include not only the right of integrity of the work, but also the right to recall it if the author changes their point of view.

A.4.
Authorship

There are multiple traditions and views on authorship, in legal, literary, and academic spheres among others. According to
(
Sutherland-Smith 2016
)
, the legal origins of intellectual property in English common law are intertwined with the Romantic notion of an author as “the sole creator or originator of the text”. Important aspects of authorship in this sense are the
individual labor of the author
and the
originality
of the resulting text. In literary theory, that view has been challenged for ignoring the external influences on the author, the intertextual relations, and the role of the reader in constructing the meaning out of written words
(
Barthes 1988
)
. However, that discussion is about the role of original author’s intent in interpretation of literary texts, rather than the economic rights of writers.

The world of non-fiction has at least one more important criterion for the notion of authorship:
responsibility
. The first principle listed in the code of ethics of the Society of Professional Journalists
(
201 2014
)
is to “take responsibility for the accuracy of their work”. In the scientific world, the definition of authorship of the Association for Computation Machinery emphasizes not only intellectual contributions to content and drafting of the academic work, but also that all authors are aware that the work is being published, and take equal responsibility for it
(
Board 2023a
)
.

A.5.
Plagiarism

We consider plagiarism as “using someone else’s intellectual product (such as texts, ideas, or results), thereby implying that it is their own”
(
Helgesson and Eriksson 2015
)
. The notion of plagiarism relies more on the social norms and the moral rights of authors than the copyright legislation: e.g. if a student copies a part of a copyrighted textbook in their essay, this would typically be viewed in the lens of academic misconduct rather than copyright infringement. The legal profession has its own norms over copying the text of other lawyers, also viewed through the lens of professional conduct
(
Rendleman 2020
)
.
The Vancouver Convention lists plagiarism as a form of scientific misconduct, together with fabrication or falsification of data and failure to disclose relationships and activities
(
van 2025
, p.8)
.

As other concepts explored in this section, plagiarism is complicated due to the variety of specific norms and expectations for attribution in various spheres of human activity, which also change over time. For example, the perception of what does not need citations because everybody in a given academic field already knows this changes over time, as that field develops conceptually.
Haviland and Mullin 2009
argue that academics engage in the practice of their disciplines to “participate in knowledge-building communities, to know and to be known in the professional arenas that matter to them”
Haviland and Mullin 2009
. Over time they learn how the knowledge-building works in their scientific community, and they are typically careful to observe those norms, even though they are not universal or easy to formalize.

Likewise, in journalism, it is not always clear when attribution is required
(
Lewis and Zhong 2013
)
, and it is not uncommon to “pick up on ideas that have previously been published elsewhere in a similar form”. Still, the blurriness and fluidity of human attribution norms does not mean that plagiarism is not a real phenomenon. According to
(
Meier 2024
)
, journalistic plagiarism happens “when inventive ideas or creative wording are copied from others systematically and deliberately and, in particular, the reader is led to believe in a creative uniqueness that simply does not exist”.

Different spheres of activity may have different attribution expectations for copying the form or content of a certain work. In the academic realm the focus is on the content. For example, the Association for Computational Machinery specifically addresses as forms of plagiarism “intentionally paraphrasing portions of another’s work”, and “using automated tools that rephrase existing work as one’s text without proper attribution”
(
Board 2023b
)
. On this definition, if LLM developers were to deal with copyright problem by detecting verbatim matches to known sources, and rephrasing to avoid detection, this would still constitute plagiarism.
