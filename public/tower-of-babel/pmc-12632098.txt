Clinical outcomes of a primary care mental health implementation program scale-up in the Eastern Province of Rwanda: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Hildegarde Mukasakindi, Alphonse Nshimyiryo, Stephanie L Smith, Vestine Mukandayisenga, Aphrodis Ndayisaba, Augustin Mulindabigwi, Fredrick Kateera, Nadine Karema, Joel M Mubiligi, Vincent K Cubaka, Dale A Barnhart
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Mentoring and Enhanced Supervision at Health Centers for Mental Health (MESH-MH) program supports mental health care delivery by non-specialist, primary care providers in Rwanda to improve access to quality mental health care. After an initial pilot in one rural district, the program was scaled to support service delivery across two additional districts. We aimed to assess changes in symptoms and functioning outcomes among patients who received treatment at selected health centers supported by MESH-MH. We compare them with patient outcomes from the original pilot district.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study among patients with mental health conditions and epilepsy who started treatment at ten health centers in the Rwinkwavu and Kirehe Hospital catchment areas between November 2020 and December 2021. We assessed patients’ symptoms using the 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) and daily functioning using the 12-item World Health Organization Disability Assessment Scale (WHO-DAS 2.0 Brief). We collected data at baseline, midline (on average, 71 days from treatment initiation), and endline (on average, 197 days from treatment initiation). We measured changes over time in outcomes and associations with the duration of treatment using Wilcoxon signed rank tests and linear mixed models. We compared findings with those of our previous study in the Burera district using linear mixed models and adjusting for possible confounding factors.ResultsOf 151 participants enrolled, primary diagnoses were epilepsy (n = 45, 29.8%), depression (n = 36, 23.8%), brief psychosis (n = 26, 17.2%), and schizophrenia (n = 20, 13.2%). The median GHQ-12 score improved from 24 (IQR: 18–30) at baseline to 11 (IQR: 5–17) at endline (median change: -12 [IQR: -19, -6], p < 0.001). The median WHO-DAS Brief score improved from 21.8 (IQR: 13–30) to 6 (IQR: 3–15) (median change: -13 [IQR: -22, -4], p < 0.001). The median number of days with having difficulties to carry out regular activities declined from 20 (IQR: 7–20) at baseline to 5 (IQR: 2–15) at endline (median change: -10 [IQR: -21, 0], p < 0.001) with comparable significant reductions in the number of days where patients were less able to engage in usual activities. We consistently observed significant improvements when using linear mixed models and across patient diagnoses. The scale-up sites revealed slightly greater reductions in symptoms relative to Burera, the pilot site study, while they showed comparable improvements in functioning.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that patients with mental health conditions and epilepsy who received treatment at health centers newly supported by MESH-MH experienced significant improvements in symptoms and functioning. These improvements were comparable to the outcomes seen in the initial pilot of MESH-MH. Scaling-up interventions like the MESH-MH model could help to decentralize and increase access to mental health services in Rwanda and other similar settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07610-9.Keywords: Primary health care, Mental health, Depression, Epilepsy, Severe mental health conditions, Task-sharing, Rural Rwanda

Background

Globally, approximately one in two individuals will experience at least one mental health condition over their lifetime [1], and mental health conditions are responsible for one-third of the global Years Lived with Disability (YLDs) [2]. In Africa alone, about 9% of the population lives with a depressive condition [3]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the proportion of mental health issues among adolescents in the general population stands at 26.9% [4]. However, the majority of individuals with mental health conditions in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) lack access to quality mental health care [5]. For example, in low-income countries, only 12% of people suffering from psychosis had access to treatment in 2020 [6]. Poor access to mental health care in LMICs is attributed to various factors, including a lack of adequate human resources, limited financial resources, health systems ill-suited for delivering mental health care, inadequate health information systems, and stigma surrounding mental health conditions [7–9]. To close the mental health treatment gap [10, 11], and ensure accessible care to people with mental health conditions no matter where they live [12, 13], the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended a task-sharing approach that involves using non-specialists in mental health services, who work under the supervision and mentorship of mental health specialists [6, 7].

Rwanda experiences high rates of mental health conditions, partially attributable to the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi [14]. In 2018, the prevalence of mental health conditions was estimated at 20.5% in the general population and 52.2% among genocide survivors [15]. The devastating demand for mental health care following the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi put an enormous burden on an already struggling healthcare system, which was facing significant limitations in both financial and skilled human resources [16]. Yet, in 2017, like other LMICs, Rwanda had insufficient mental health professionals per 100,000 population, including 1.38 mental health nurses, 0.39 psychologists, 0.17 social workers, 0.06 psychiatrists, 0.02 occupational therapists, and no child psychiatrists [17, 18]. To address human resource limitations in mental health care, Rwanda has adopted the WHO recommendations for mental health task sharing by planning to integrate mental health care into the existing public primary health care system [19]. However, in Rwanda like in other places, the practical implementation of task sharing has encountered challenges such as increased workload for primary health care providers, limited mental health knowledge and skills among existing health care providers, limitations in the psychotropic medication supply chain, and inadequate infrastructure and information systems supporting mental health care [12, 20–23]. To effectively implement and scale task-sharing of mental health services in primary health care, it is critical to employ system-strengthening approaches that can support both the skill development of non-specialists delivering mental health care services and enable the health system to support the management of patients with chronic diseases [24].

Partners In Health (PIH), an international non-profit organization dedicated to health system strengthening in low-resource contexts, has supported the public health delivery system in three rural districts of Rwanda since 2005 [25]. Beginning in 2012, PIH and the Ministry of Health (MoH) collaboratively introduced the Mentorship and Enhanced Supervision at Health Centers for Mental Health (MESH-MH) program [26, 27]. The aim was to strengthen the health system with community-based care for severe and common mental health conditions, as well as epilepsy, in line with Rwanda’s National Mental Health Policy [28]. MESH-MH encompasses a set of implementation strategies focused on the mentorship of primary care nurses and systems-based quality improvement measures, which draw from successful models of task-sharing for HIV care, pediatrics, and maternal health in Rwanda. The program is described elsewhere in the literature [29]. From 2014 to 2016, MESH-MH model was initially piloted and scaled across 19 government-led health centers in Burera district, which is in Rwanda’s mountainous Northern province bordering Uganda. A subsequent outcomes evaluation demonstrated significant improvement in psychological distress, functioning, and individual and household economic status for patients with severe mental health conditions and epilepsy cared for by nurses supported by the MESH-MH program [27]. Building on lessons learned in the Burera district, beginning in 2018, the MESH-MH program was sequentially implemented at government-run health centers throughout two additional PIH-supported districts, Southern Kayonza and Kirehe, which are contiguous and located in the relatively flat area close to Tanzania. Although the COVID-19 pandemic necessitated some adjustments to the MESH-MH implementation strategies based on Rwanda’s infection prevention and control protocols, such as the incorporation of remote training and supervision, the program was scaled to all 25 health centers in the two districts by 2021. This scale-up represents one of the first documented efforts to scale a successful implementation program enabling task-sharing in mental health care delivery at primary care centers in resource-limited settings.

There are very few evaluations of the clinical effectiveness of task-shared mental health services within LMIC government settings and even fewer that document the clinical outcomes of a program scaled beyond its initial pilot area [30]. To this end, this study aims to evaluate the change in symptoms and functioning outcomes among patients with severe mental health conditions and epilepsy receiving treatment at health centers in the Southern Kayonza and Kirehe Districts, as the MESH-MH program was scaled throughout the two districts. Additionally, we sought to assess whether the changes in clinical outcomes and patient functioning observed in Southern Kayonza and Kirehe were at least as good as those previously observed in Burera district during the initial implementation of MESH-MH. Insights from this study can provide valuable information to decision-makers regarding the potential expansion of the community-based MESH-MH program in Rwanda and similar settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a prospective cohort study including patients with mental health conditions and epilepsy who received care at selected MESH-MH-supported health centers in the catchment areas of Kirehe District Hospital ([KDH] population 460,860) and Rwinkwavu District Hospital ([RDH] population 475,156) [31]. These district hospitals and their affiliated health centers are supported by Partners In Health/Inshuti Mu Buzima (PIH/IMB), an international organization that has been operating in Rwanda since 2005. This organization supports the Ministry of Health through health system strengthening and providing health care to vulnerable populations and has historically provided support to a wide range of clinical programs, including child development, non-communicable diseases, and infectious disease in these districts. Both KDH and RDH supervised a total of 25 health centers. Health centers provide a range of primary health care services including preventive services such as vaccinations, as well as the diagnosis and treatment of infectious diseases such as malaria, HIV and Tuberculosis [32]. Each health center is supported by associated community health workers, volunteer people who have been trained to serve as a vital link between health centers and the community [33].

Mental health services in Rwanda

Rwanda currently has two central national referral centers managing mental health conditions in the capital city of Kigali: The Neuropsychiatric hospital of Ndera and the ambulatory mental health department of Kigali Teaching Hospital [33]. In addition, all district-level hospitals in the country do also have at least one psychiatric nurse and a psychologist who manage outpatient mental health clinics at these hospitals [19]. To enhance access to mental health services, the MoH has also taken steps to assign at least one specialized mental health clinician to provide mental health care at each of the more than 500 health centers in the country, although progress to achieve that goal is ongoing [28].

Mentorship and enhanced supervision for mental health (MESH-MH) program

MESH-MH is a community-based implementation program that aims to increase access to equitable and quality mental health services in health centers in Rwanda through the capacitation of primary care nurses to deliver basic mental health care, and to improve general health system functioning for mental health care delivery. MESH-MH encompasses a set of implementation strategies, including decentralized training and mentorship of primary care nurses by mental health nurses and psychologists, utilizing a structured supervisory checklist to maintain clinical quality “audit and feedback”, and systems-based quality improvement projects at the health center level. The packages of mental health care delivery that MESH-MH supports includes care for schizophrenia and psychosis, depression and anxiety-related conditions, bipolar disorder, and epilepsy. The packages include basic assessment, treatments including psychoeducation, medication management, low-intensity individual psychotherapy (the WHO-developed Problem Management Plus) [34, 35], and psychosocial rehabilitation programs including vocational support and enhancement of social reintegration [26].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, MESH-MH program implementation strategies were rapidly adapted to align with COVID-19 prevention measures [36]. For example, mental health care trainings for primary care nurses were transitioned to a hybrid-training model alternating between virtual and in-person sessions, and an e-learning course around psychotherapy was developed and made available (Table 1). Group-based psychosocial rehabilitation interventions such as psycho-education, self-help groups were temporally suspended to prevent COVID-19 infection among the project participants. Mentorship and supervision transitioned to remote support when required, and virtual meeting spaces were created for primary care nurses to ask questions and receive support. Additionally, Psychological First Aid (PAF) – an intervention to support people with stress symptoms and resilience after emergencies [37] was incorporated into training for providers at all levels, to support people with acute distress within the health care system and associated communities during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Study site selection and participant recruitment

The final 10 health centers in Southern Kayonza and Kirehe Districts(out of twenty-five total), which were newly providing mental health and epilepsy services supported by MESH-MH at the beginning of the study period, were chosen to participate in the outcomes evaluation. Study participants’ enrollment started in mid-November 2020, immediately after primary health care providers at these ten facilities had completed didactic training and had begun receiving mentorship from psychiatric mentors, and enrollment continued until December 2021. Inclusion criteria for individual participants were (a) being newly patient in the mental health clinic at one of the 10 selected health centers newly implementing the MESH-MH model, and (b) being 18 years old or older. Patients who needed to be immediately referred to the district hospital (for example, those experiencing suicidal ideation or acute psychiatric crisis), those who had severe cognitive impairment or intellectual disability, and those who were homeless and needed additional immediate support were excluded from this study. Excluded patients were expected not to be able to respond to the survey and/or available for the constant follow-up that was needed for the cohort of selected participants. The target sample size for this study was 145 patients, which would have allowed us to conduct a non-inferiority test comparing the MESH-MH outcomes in Kirehe and South Kayonza to what was previously observed in Burera, (n = 127, change in GHQ-12:11.6, 95% CI: 10.2, 13.9) assuming a type-one error of 0.05, a type-two error of 0.20, a non-inferiority margin of no more than 30%, and 20% lost to follow-up.

Data collection & outcome measures

Outcome measures included the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12), a screening tool established in the 1970s to help recognize general psychological distress among populations. The tool measures patients’ distress with a 12-item Likert scale ranging from 1 to 4, with higher scores indicating higher levels of distress [38]. The World Health Organization Disability Assessment Scale (WHO-DAS 2.0 Brief), is a 12-item tool that was used to measure patients’ functional impairments [39]. The 12 items in the WHO-DAS 2.0 Brief tool are related to difficulties engaging in daily activities due to mental health problems and scored on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating higher levels of impairment. These scales were previously translated into the local language (Kinyarwanda) and used in the initial MESH-MH outcomes evaluation in Burera district [27]. Three additional questions (WHO-DAS Brief questions H1-H3) were also used to assess patients’ abilities to engage in regular activities, including “How many days in the past 30 days have you had difficulties?”, “How many days in the past 30 days have you been unable to carry out usual activities?”, and “How many days in the past 30 days have you experienced a reduction in usual activities?”. For all outcome measures, a reduction in the total scores on the GHQ-12 and WHO-DAS Brief or the number of days when the patient was unable to carry out regular activities, reflected improvement in terms of symptoms and functioning.

Trained psychologists traveled to health centers to collect outcome measurements at three interview time points throughout the study. Originally, data collection was planned to occur at baseline (at the patients’ initial visits), at two months, and at four months. However, due to COVID-19-related barriers to data collection, data collection took longer than the initial plan and the timing of the second and third data points varied substantially from patient to patient (Supplementary Fig. 1), necessitating adaptations to the analysis plan. Additional demographic and clinical data were collected at baseline and included district, sex, age, marital status, insurance status, primary and secondary mental health diagnoses, and primary and secondary psychotropic medications prescribed. Data were collected and stored on password-protected tablets programmed with Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) software [40].

Data analysis

We described the characteristics of study participants at baseline using frequencies and percentages for categorical data, and medians and interquartile ranges for continuous data. To assess for potential selection bias due to differential loss to follow-up, we used Fisher’s exact and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to compare baseline characteristics among patients who completed the study and those who did not. To assess changes in patients’ outcomes throughout treatment, we conducted two sets of analyses. Each set of analyses makes different statistical assumptions and therefore provides complementary information regarding the sensitivity of our findings to statistical assumptions. Our first set of analyses used non-parametric methods and categorized interviews as occurring during baseline, midline (between baseline and 119 days), and endline (≥ 120 days after the baseline) periods. Due to the COVID-19-related disruptions in data collection, some individuals’ second interviews occurred after the end of the initial midline period, resulting in patients who had two interviews occurring during the endline period. For these patients, we retained only the endline interview occurring closest to 180 days from baseline for our non-parametric analysis. We then calculated within-person changes between baseline and midline as well as between baseline and endline and used Wilcoxon signed rank tests to assess whether there were significant within-person changes from baseline.

Our second set of analyses used parametric methods and treated time since the start of treatment as a continuous variable. We included data from all first, second, and third interviews collected throughout the study. We assessed the association between changes in outcomes and duration of treatment using a linear mixed model with a random intercept for each patient to account for clustering, as a result from repeated measurements on the same patient over time. Each model included a linear term for the number of days since baseline. Because we hypothesized that the benefits of treatment could level off over time as patients became stable, we also considered a quadratic term for days since baseline for each outcome. If the quadratic term was significant at a p < 0.05 level using the Wald test, we retained it in the final model. We reported the coefficients and 95% confidence intervals from these linear mixed models as well as model-based estimates of the change in outcomes associated with 90 and 180 days of treatment.

In addition to the initial study objectives, we conducted a post-hoc analysis to assess whether patient outcomes differed by patients’ primary diagnosis. We collapsed patients’ primary diagnosis categories into epilepsy, mood disorders (depression or bipolar disorder), brief psychosis, schizophrenia, and anxiety-related disorders (somatoform disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder [PTSD], or anxiety disorder). We then constructed linear mixed models that included an indicator for each disorder type, an interaction term between each disorder type and days since treatment, and random intercepts for each patient, while adjusting for possible confounding factors (i.e. patient’s sex, age and marital status). We assessed for differential treatment effects by testing for significant differences in the treatment duration-by-disorder interaction terms. However, due to limited sample size and power for this post-hoc sub-group analysis, we did not assess quadratic trends for this analysis.

Finally, we also used linear mixed models to compare changes in GHQ-12 and WHO-DAS Brief outcomes between patients from the current study, who received treatment in Kirehe and South Kayonza, and patients from the previous study in Burera. This analysis included individual-level data from an additional 146 patients from the previous study. For each outcome, the linear mixed model included fixed effects of study site and duration of intervention, the interaction between site and that intervention duration, controlling for sex, age and marital status, and accounting for the random intercept for each patient. Then, we used the linear combination of model coefficients to estimate the adjusted mean differences in outcomes between the study sites at 3 and 6 months of intervention duration. All data analyses were conducted using Stata version 15.1.

Results

A total of 151 eligible patients were included in this study (Fig. 1). Out of the 434 completed interviews, 151, 111, and 141 interviews were identified as valid baseline, midline (interviews conducted at between 55 and 119 days after baseline), and endline (interviews conducted at between 120 and 274 days after baseline) interviews, respectively. Eight patients dropped out after the baseline interview, while three patients were lost to follow-up between the second and third interviews. From information provided by healthcare providers and patients themselves on the telephone, reasons for drop-out included relocation outside the study area, refusal to continue treatment, or protracted time to return for the midline and endline interviews due to COVID-19 restrictions.

Enrollment and retention of study participants

Among study participants at baseline, the majority (92.0%) of patients were from the Kirehe district, had health insurance (87%), and were aged between 30 and 59 years old (60.9%) (Table 2). Less than half (45.0%) were married or living with a partner. The most common primary mental health diagnoses were epilepsy (29.8%), depression (23.8%), and brief psychosis (17.2%), while schizophrenia (13.2%) and somatoform disorder (9.9%) were less common. A minority of these patients (13.9%) also had a secondary mental health diagnosis. Amitriptyline and Haldol were the most commonly prescribed primary medications, with 28.5% of patients being prescribed multiple medications. For patients who dropped out in the middle of the study, they were significantly more likely to be from the Kayonza district (25.0% vs. 5.8% in Kirehe) and to be male (14.9% vs. 3.9% among females), but did not differ on any other baseline characteristics, including psychological distress (GHQ-12), functioning (WHO-DAS), or days with difficulties or restrictions in activities at baseline (Supplementary Table 1).

In our non-parametric analysis, we observed statistically significant improvements across outcome measures when comparing baseline and midline as well as when comparing baseline and endline (p < 0.001 for all) (Table 3). Patients’ median GHQ-12 score declined from 24 (IQR: 18, 30) at baseline to 15 (IQR: 9, 20) at midline and 11 (IQR: 5, 17) at endline. The median WHO-DAS Brief score declined from 33.8 (IQR: 25, 42) at baseline to 25 (IQR: 19, 34) at midline and 18 (IQR: 15, 27) at endline, respectively. The median number of days with difficulties declined from 20 (IQR: 7, 30) at baseline to 10 (IQR: 5, 20) at midline and 5 (IQR: 2, 15) at endline with comparable reductions in the number of days when the patient was unable to carry out usual activities and the number of days with a reduction in usual activities. When comparing the results of the MESH-MH program in Kirehe and Southern Kayonza to results previously reported in Burera, we included data from an additional 146 patients from the previous study into our models. While there were no significant demographic differences between the two groups of patients, those from Kirehe and Kayonza were slightly older and more likely to have been separated (Supplementary Table 2). After adjusting for the patient’s sex, age and marital status, we observed significantly greater improvements in GHQ-12 scores after 6 months of treatment among those treated in Kirehe and Southern Kayonza compared to those previously treated in Burera (Table 6). Patients in Kirehe and Southern Kayonza had an average GHQ-12 score 2.32 points lower (β = −2.32; 95% CI = − 4.07, − 0.58) than those in the Burera study at 6 months of intervention duration. Additionally, the gap in the rate of symptom reduction on the GHQ-12 score between study sites was widened in favor of Kirehe and Southern Kayonza site, with an average of extra 1.35 points lower (β = −1.35; 95% CI = − 2.43, − 0.27) that developed between the 3-month and 6-month time-points. However, there was no significant difference in the rates of change in outcomes between study sites for the GHQ-12 score outcome at 3 months of intervention duration, or for the WHO-DAS score outcome at any time-point.

Discussion

The results from our prospective cohort study demonstrate that treatment delivered by primary care nurses for mental health conditions and epilepsy, supported by the MESH-MH program, is associated with significant improvement in patient’s symptoms and functioning. After 3 months of intervention, the median GHQ-12 score; was reduced by 38%, and after 6 months of the intervention, the median GHQ-12 score had reduced by 54%. Similarly, the median WHO-DAS Brief score; was reduced by 26% and 47% at 3 and 6 months of intervention, respectively. Mental health interventions delivered at the health centers were also associated with a significant reduction in the median number of patient-reported days of having difficulties (75% reduction), days unable to carry out usual activities (73% reduction), and days with a reduction in usual activities (73% reduction). Our findings are consistent with results from previous studies conducted in similar resource-limited settings, including Ethiopia [41] and Nepal [42, 43], which reported that decentralized and non-specialist-provided mental health interventions at primary care centers are associated with significant improvement in patients’ symptoms and functioning. Although we observed significant improvements in functioning across all patient diagnoses, the reductions in the GHQ-12 significantly varied by diagnosis, with relatively higher improvements among patients diagnosed with mood disorders and lower improvements among patients with schizophrenia. The heterogeneous effect of these interventions by type of mental health or neurological problem (diagnosis) was also reported elsewhere [43].

Our study is the second to evaluate mental health outcomes among patients receiving treatment at primary care health centers supported by the MESH-MH program in Rwanda. Compared to the previous study in the Burera district, enrolled patients in the Southern Kayonza and Kirehe districts experienced greater reduction in psychological distress measured by the GHQ-12, but similar improvements in functioning measured by the WHO-DAS Brief. Interestingly, there is significantly greater effect of the intervention on symptom reduction, despite the fact that the scale-up of the MESH-MH program into the Eastern province coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic – which has been broadly associated with worsened mental health and increases in psychological distress globally [44–46]. For the similar improvements in functioning observed between Burera district in 2014-16 and Southern Kayonza and Kirehe districts in 2020–2022, we hypothesize that there would have also been significantly greater improvement in functioning in favor of patients in Kirehe and Southern Kayonza if we could exclude the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. The implemented COVID-19-related measures of infection prevention and control negatively affected the functioning of rural populations.

It is notable however that patients receiving care provided by primary care nurses at the selected MESH-MH-supported health centers in the Southern Kayonza and Kirehe districts, still saw significant improvements in distress and functioning overall. These health centers represented the last round of scale-up of MESH-MH in three districts in Rwanda (the final ten of 45 health centers total in the three districts to be supported by MESH-MH) over an 8 year period, and demonstrates that the process of scale-up, with all its attendant challenges such as additional infrastructure and human resource limitations, and operational and management needs on a much larger scale, alongside COVID-19 related MESH-MH adaptations, could still result in overall improvements for patients.

The MESH-MH program supported the delivery of pharmacologic and psychotherapeutic care alongside psychosocial support, including psycho-education, home visits, and CHWs accompaniment, direct social support in terms of food and clothes, and psycho-rehabilitation activities. One or more of these components, or the synergism interaction between them, may explain the program’s success. There is substantial literature to suggest that both facility-based and community-based components of mental health care may play key roles in improving mental health among the population. For example, psychosocial interventions with medical-based treatment for mental health have been associated with better outcomes in patients with schizophrenia, depression, and anxiety disorders [47–52]. Future studies could analyze the level of contribution of specific care components supported by the MESH-MH care implementation program on patient clinical outcomes as well as their cost analysis.

Our study does have some important limitations. First, there was no concurrent control group, so we do not know whether patients would have experienced comparable improvements if they had been treated in clinics that provided “care as usual” rather than services supported by MESH-MH.

In our setting, implementing a control group was not feasible or ethical for two reasons. First, MESH MH provides support on patient diagnosis, so it would have been difficult to identify a pool of control patients from facilities before the intervention. Second, in the absence of MESH-MH, “care as usual” can be very minimal, and it would have not been ethical to diagnose and collect data on these patients but not provide them with clinical services. However, the magnitude of patient improvement was large and relatively rapid, despite the challenges brought by expanding the program during the initial phases of the COVID-19 pandemic, suggesting that the changes observed during this study would not have occurred in the absence of the MESH-MH program. Second, although our original study design included midline interviews at two months and endline interviews at four months, COVID-19 disrupted the data collection schedule, resulting in wide variability in the timing of data collection across patients. We addressed this concern by developing a new definition of midline and endline periods and by providing a complementary set of analyses that treated time since the start of the intervention as a continuous variable. The consistency of our results across multiple statistical models suggests that our findings are not sensitive to these analytic decisions regarding the modeling of time. A third constraint is that we do not have information on any changes in specific psychiatric symptoms based on patient diagnosis (e.g. psychotic symptoms in people with schizophrenia) or seizure reduction among patients with epilepsy, as we needed to use a general tool applicable across diagnoses such as the GHQ-12, to ensure the feasibility of the study. In the future, focusing on clinical changes (such as improvements in the numbers of seizures) in specific subsets of patients will provide the program with better information to use for program improvements. Finally, our results may not be fully generalizable to other districts in Rwanda, as both Southern Kayonza and Kirehe District receive substantial health system support beyond the MESH-MH program from Partners In Health. However, we have provided a detailed description of the health system strengthening strategies employed by MESH-MH, and the Rwanda MOH has expressed its intent to utilize MESH-MH implementation strategies to support the integration of mental health care into the primary health system of rural districts nationwide, beyond the PIH-supported districts. This intention is consistent with the overall MoH strategy and national policy prescription to decentralize mental health care to the district and community levels. Additional implementation research within MESH-MH-supported health centers and communities as the country scales the program will be helpful in understanding the feasibility of MESH-MH as a Rwanda national implementation strategy for mental health care. A cost-analysis of the MESH-MH program across Southern Kayonza and Kirehe districts has recently been completed and is in submission, which will provide additional important information to understand the feasibility of MESH-MH as a national scale-up strategy for decentralized mental health care across Rwanda [53, 54].

Conclusions

Patients with mental health conditions and epilepsy who received care at MESH-MH-supported health centers experienced significant improvements in symptoms and functioning outcomes, as the program was scaled up within two PIH/IMB-supported districts. Our findings are consistent across multiple patient diagnoses and multiple statistical models, and the magnitude of this improvement is large, even though expansions of the MESH-MH model coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic. This analysis joins previous research to suggest that the MESH-MH implementation program is a viable strategy for integrating mental health care into primary care settings, even in rural Rwanda where mental health resources are very limited, and our study demonstrates that the MESH-MH program can lead to similar patients outcomes when scaled across districts beyond its initial pilot. Therefore, expansion of the MESH-MH model could be considered as a strategy to improve decentralized access to quality mental health services at primary health centers in other districts in Rwanda and other resource-limited settings.
