Generation of implant-type tissue-engineered cartilage from human embryonic stem cell-derived chondrocytes
Authors: Yen-Chih Huang, Hiroko Komura, Arhans Chairul Ismael, Yukiyo Asawa, Junlong Chen, Tomoyuki Kawasaki, Shin Enosawa, Hidenori Akutsu, Yasushi Fuchimoto, Kazuto Hoshi, Akihiro Umezawa, Makoto Komura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAutologous cartilage transplantation has been applied clinically but its efficacy is limited by donor site morbidity, inter-donor variability, and high manufacturing costs. Primary chondrocytes also tend to dedifferentiate during expansion, which further limits their redifferentiation potential. Cartilage is weakly immunogenic and is thus an ideal tissue source for allogeneic transplantation. Allogeneic transplantation has drawn much interest due to the immune-privileged nature of cartilage and the recent success of allogeneic chondrocyte therapies. Meanwhile, human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) offer a scalable source of chondrocytes with high redifferentiation capacity. Building on the xeno-free automated chondrogenic differentiation of SEES2 hESCs in previous research, this study aims to establish an implant-type regenerative cartilage platform using hESCs for potential allogeneic transplantation.MethodshESCs (SEES2) were differentiated into cartilage tissue using an embryoid body-based protocol and cultured on collagen-coated dishes for 60 days. Cartilage-derived chondrocytes were isolated and expanded in a monolayer. Passage 3 (P3) cells were used for pellet culture, maintained for 21 days, and analyzed using histology and biochemical assays. Gene expression was assessed by RNA sequencing. Additionally, P3 chondrocytes were seeded onto bioresorbable PGA nonwoven fabric scaffolds, implanted subcutaneously into athymic nude mice for 4 weeks, and subjected to histological and biochemical analyses.ResultsHuman embryonic stem cell-derived chondrocytes (hESC-Chs) were successfully isolated and expanded over 1014-fold cumulatively. They expressed chondrogenic markers that were comparable to those of human auricular chondrocytes (hACs) and lacked pluripotency-associated genes. In pellet culture, hESC-Chs produced cartilage matrix, which was less robust than that produced by hACs. RNA sequencing showed overall similarity between groups, and extracellular matrix-related genes such as CHI3L1 and HAS3 were upregulated in hACs. In scaffold-based constructs, hESC-Chs exhibited inferior matrix production at low density (5.0 × 107 cells/cm3) but showed improved glycosaminoglycan and type II collagen deposition at high density (2.0 × 108 cells/cm3), reaching levels equivalent to those of hACs under high-density conditions. Notably, PD-L1 expression was elevated in hESC-Chs, indicating their potential immunomodulatory properties.ConclusionWe successfully established a reproducible method for isolating and expanding chondrocytes from hESC-derived cartilage tissue. Under optimized culture conditions, these cells demonstrated the capacity for redifferentiation and in vivo cartilage regeneration, highlighting their potential as a stable and scalable allogeneic cell source for cartilage repair.

Introduction

Cartilage is a specialized connective tissue that provides structural support and enables smooth joint movement. It primarily consists of extracellular matrix (ECM) containing type II collagen (COL2) and aggrecan, and is embedded with chondrocytes [1]. However, due to its avascular nature and low cellularity, cartilage has very limited self-repair capacity [2,3]. Consequently, the development of effective and clinically applicable cartilage regeneration strategies remains a critical challenge in regenerative medicine.

Currently, autologous chondrocyte implantation represents one of the earliest clinically validated methods for cartilage regeneration. With this technique, a patient's own chondrocytes are harvested, expanded in vitro, and subsequently reimplanted into the articular defect [4,5]. This method has provided the basis for advancements in tissue engineering by integrating three fundamental components—cells, scaffolds, and signaling factors [6]. Following this principle, implant type tissue-engineered cartilage can be generated in an autologous manner [7].

In our previous clinical trial, autologous chondrocytes were isolated from auricular cartilage, expanded in vitro, and seeded onto scaffolds to fabricate tissue-engineered nasal cartilage for reconstruction in patients with cleft lip and palate. This approach demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes [8]. Such scaffold-based autologous approaches offer the advantage of excellent biocompatibility. However, they still face several limitations, including donor site morbidity, inter-individual variability in cell quality, and a time-consuming and costly manufacturing process [9,10]. Autologous strategies frequently depend on donor-derived chondrocytes, which exhibit variability in cell quality, proliferative capacity, and potential for redifferentiation induction. Furthermore, each batch requires individual isolation, expansion, and quality control processes, which contribute to elevated production costs and hinder scalability.

Allogeneic transplantation has gained significant interest as a way to address these limitations. Additionally, cartilage tissue is considered to be immune-privileged, which allows allogeneic transplantation to be performed without the need for immunosuppression [11,12]. Recent clinical and preclinical studies have further demonstrated that allogeneic chondrocyte sheets can repair articular cartilage defects safely and effectively without immunosuppression therapy [13]. The establishment of a reliable source of high-quality chondrocytes that are suitable for allogeneic transplantation could greatly improve the clinical feasibility and scalability of cartilage regenerative therapies.

However, primary chondrocytes, including those derived from allogeneic donors, face inherent limitations due to their tendency to dedifferentiate during in vitro expansion, leading to reduced redifferentiation potential [[14], [15], [16]]. This procedure requires the continual procurement of new cell sources to ensure effective cartilage formation, similar to autologous methods, while maintaining consistent cell quality.

In contrast, pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) can be expanded indefinitely and consistently provide chondrocytes with high redifferentiation capacity [17,18]. This fundamental advantage supports the development of stable and scalable cell sources for regenerative cartilage therapies.

At the National Center for Child Health and Development (NCCHD) in Japan, eight human embryonic stem cell (hESC) lines (SEES-1 to SEES-7 and SEES-X) have been established. Among them, various cell lines have successfully differentiated into several somatic cell types, such as epithelium, intestinal tissue, cartilage, and liver, demonstrating the general establishment of differentiation methods using hESCs and their clinical utility [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. As a notable example of clinical success, SEES-2-derived hepatocyte-like cells (HLCs) were used as bridge therapy in a physician-led clinical trial involving five infants with urea cycle disorder. HLCs were transplanted into the liver via the portal vein, improving hyperammonemia and allowing growth until successful living donor liver transplantation. Thus, hESC-derived cells were successfully used as bridge therapy, highlighting their potential in future clinical applications [23].

In particular, the NCCHD has developed a protocol for cartilage differentiation using embryoid body (EB) formation, enabling the scalable generation of tissue with hyaline cartilage characteristics [22]. An automated culture system using xeno-free medium was developed in the same study, allowing for scalable production of cartilage tissue from hESCs. Cartilage tissue can be generated in vitro using this protocol. However, its direct clinical application is currently limited due to the inability to control tissue size. Consequently, ongoing research aims to develop methods for precise size regulation to enable future clinical use.

For clinical application, it is critical to generate cartilage with specific dimensions and morphology. This study plans to establish an implant type tissue-engineered cartilage platform using hESCs for potential allogeneic transplantation. To achieve this objective, we aim to develop a technique to isolate chondrocytes and obtain the required cell count from hESC-derived cartilage tissue. We will assess the chondrogenic and redifferentiation capabilities of the isolated cells and analyze their characteristics. Based on these cellular attributes, we will establish methods for the production of transplantable cartilage.

Methods

Ethics statement

Human cells in this study were utilized in full compliance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects [Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare (MHLW, Japan); Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology (MEXT, Japan)] and performed in full compliance with the Ethical Guidelines for Clinical Studies (MHLW, Japan). The cells were banked after approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board at the National Institute of Biomedical Innovation (May 9, 2006). The derivation and cultivation of hESC lines were performed in full compliance with “the Guidelines for Derivation and Distribution of Human Embryonic Stem Cells (Notification of MEXT, No. 156 of August 21, 2009; Notification of MEXT, No. 86 of May 20, 2010) and “the Guidelines for Utilization of Human Embryonic Stem Cells (Notification of MEXT, No. 157 of August 21, 2009; Notification of MEXT, No. 87 of May 20, 2010)”. All animal procedures during the experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee (Ethics permission No. A2023M135).

Differentiation of cartilage tissue from hESCs

The differentiation of cartilage tissue from hESCs was carried out according to a previous protocol [22], as summarized below. hESCs (SEES2) were cultured on iMatrix-511 silk-coated dishes (1.7 μg/ml, 37 °C, 1 h) in StemFit medium, with daily medium changes. Cells were passaged every 7 days at 550 cells/cm2 after detachment using a 1:1 mixture of 0.5 mM EDTA and TrypLE™ Select for 5 min. For the formation of EBs, dissociated hESCs were seeded onto ultra-low attachment 96-well plates using XF32 medium. This medium is composed of 81 % Knockout Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 10829018, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), 15 % Knockout Serum Replacement XF CTS (12618013, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mM GlutaMAX (35050061, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.1 mM Non-Essential Amino Acids (11140050, Thermo Fisher Scientific), penicillin/streptomycin (15070063, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 50 μg/ml l-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate (A8960, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), 10 ng/ml heregulin-1β (080–09001, FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals, Richmond, VA), 200 ng/ml recombinant human IGF-1 (85580C, Sigma-Aldrich), and 20 ng/ml human bFGF (PHG0021, Thermo Fisher Scientific). EBs were then collected at 4 days post-passage. A total of 672 EBs were plated on 100 mm dishes precoated with NMP collagen PS (0.3 mg/ml, 37 °C, 1 h) and cultured in XF32 medium under 5 % CO2 at 37 °C, with medium changes every 3 days using an automated exchange system. EBs fused into sheets over several days and became easily detachable after approximately 60 days, at which point cartilage constructs were harvested.

Isolation of human embryonic stem cell-derived chondrocytes (hESC-Chs)

hESC-derived cartilage tissue was dissected to remove the perichondrium and cut into small fragments. The cartilage fragments were digested in a 0.3 % type II collagenase solution (Worthington, Freehold, NJ) for 1 h. The released cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals) and cultured in dishes. Isolated cells were grown in Ham's F12 medium (DMEM/F12, Sigma-Aldrich) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator. The chondrocytes were expanded until sufficient cell numbers were obtained for subsequent experiments.

Culture of hESC-Chs

For the monolayer culture, the isolated cells were seeded on type I collagen-coated dishes (Asahi Glass Co., Funabashi, Japan) and cultured in DMEM/F12 (Sigma-Aldrich) containing 5 % human serum (Sigma-Aldrich), supplemented with 100 ng/ml FGF-2 (Kakenseiyaku, Tokyo, Japan) and 5 μg/ml insulin. The medium was changed out twice a week.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (74104, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Complementary DNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Perfect Real Time) (RR036A, Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) and the 96-Well Thermal Cycler, 0.2 mL (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied BioSystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Platinum™ SYBR™ Green qPCR SuperMix-UDG (11733046, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Gene expression levels were normalized to the reference gene UBC, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Pellet culture

Passage 3 (P3) cartilage cells were used in a pellet culture system for chondrogenic differentiation. Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL in 15 mL conical tubes, using DMEM (Low Glucose) with l-Glutamine and Phenol Red (041–29775, FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals) supplemented with 10 % FBS (10437-028, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min to form a pellet and then incubated for 48 h until spheroid formation was observed.

After spheroid formation was confirmed, the culture medium was replaced with Mesenchymal Stem Cell Chondrogenic Differentiation Medium (C-28012, PromoCell, Heidelberg, Germany). Cultures were maintained for 21 days, with medium changes every 3 days.

Gene expression analysis (RNA sequencing)

RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were outsourced to Macrogen Japan Corp. (Tokyo, Japan). RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

All downstream analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0). Read count matrices were obtained and processed using the edgeR package. Genes with low expression were filtered out using the filterByExpr function. Normalization factors were calculated using the calcNormFactors, estimateCommonDisp, and estimateTagwiseDisp functions. Normalized expression values were reported as counts per million using the cpm function.

Differential gene expression analysis between hESC-Chs and human auricular chondrocytes (hACs) was performed using the exactTest function in edgeR. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered to be significantly and differentially expressed. Volcano plots displaying log2 fold changes and FDR values were generated to visualize differences between the two groups.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed on the significantly and differentially expressed genes using the enrichGO function from the clusterProfiler package, with gene annotations provided by the org.Hs.eg.db database. Redundant GO terms were simplified using the simplify function, and results were visualized using the barplot function from the enrichplot package.

A heatmap was generated using the ComplexHeatmap package to visualize the expression profiles of genes with an FDR <0.05 and a fold change >2.0. All figures were generated using ggplot2 and ComplexHeatmap unless otherwise stated.

Scaffold preparation and cell seeding

To improve chondrocyte adhesion to the PGA nonwoven fabric sheets provided by Gunze Medical, Kyoto, Japan, scaffolds were pre-treated with ethanol prior to cell seeding. The scaffolds were sequentially soaked in ethanol solutions of decreasing concentrations (100 %, 70 %, and 50 %, each for 15 min), followed by rinses in sterile distilled water and PBS for 15 min each. The scaffolds were then immersed in DMEM/F12 medium for 60 min to complete the preparation process.

P3 hESC-Chs were seeded onto bioresorbable composite scaffolds composed of 10 × 10 × 0.15 mm PGA nonwoven fabric sheets placed on medical-grade silicone sheets in a 10 cm culture dish. Two seeding densities were used: 5.0 × 107 cells/cm3 and 2.0 × 108 cells/cm3.

A 30 μl cell suspension was seeded onto the scaffold and incubated in a 5 % CO2, 37 °C incubator for 1 h to allow cell attachment to the scaffold.

Subcutaneous implantation

The scaffolds that were seeded with chondrocytes and remained attached to the silicone sheets were implanted into the subcutaneous dorsal space of athymic nude mice. Anesthesia was performed using 2 % isoflurane inhalation administered via an anesthesia machine. The dorsal surface was sterilized with 70 % ethanol, and a small incision was made to create subcutaneous pockets. The scaffolds were inserted into the pockets together with the silicone sheets to facilitate placement. The medical-grade silicone was then carefully removed from the pocket, and the incision was closed using 4-0 Vicryl sutures (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Post-implantation analysis

The constructs were retrieved after 4 weeks to evaluate their cartilage formation potential in this in vivo system. Histological analysis and biochemical measurements were performed to assess the quality and extent of cartilage formation.

Histology of implanted constructs

The cartilage constructs were retrieved and embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT compound 4583 (Sakura Finetechnical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and then frozen. The specimens were subsequently sectioned into 7 μm slices and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), Toluidine Blue, and Safranin O.

Biochemical analyses

The minced samples were incubated at 4 °C in a solution containing 10 mg/mL pepsin and 0.05 M acetic acid for 48 h. Subsequently, 1 mg/mL pancreatic elastase and 10 × Tris-buffered saline were added to the solution and samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min to remove debris. The resulting supernatants were used for quantification of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and COL2, using the Blyscan GAG Assay Kit (B1000, Biocolor Ltd., Belfast, UK) and the Type II Collagen Detection ELISA Kit (6018, Chondrex, Inc., Woodinville, WA), respectively, according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Results

Isolation and in vitro cultivation of cells isolated from hESC-derived cartilage

We successfully isolated cells from hESC-derived cartilage tissue through type II collagenase digestion (Fig. 1a–c). The cells were passaged stably in vitro and achieved >1014-fold cumulative expansion after 20 passages (Fig. 1d). During expansion they gradually acquired a spindle-shaped, fibroblast-like morphology (Fig. 1e) and showed a higher proliferative capacity than hACs. qRT-PCR analysis of P3 cells revealed similar expression levels of representative chondrogenic markers (ACAN, COL2A1, SOX9, Vimentin) and pluripotency markers (OCT3/4, NANOG) in both groups (Fig. 1f). No significant differences in gene expression were observed between isolated cells from hESC-derived cartilage and hACs. Matrix formation in pellet culture

Pellet culture was performed using P3 cells from both hACs and hESC-Chs. After 3 weeks of pellet culture, macroscopic inspection showed that hAC-derived pellets (diameter approximately 3–4 mm) were larger in size than those formed by hESC-Chs (diameter approximately 1 mm) (Fig. 2a and b). Low-magnification (4 × ) HE staining revealed a well-organized structure in hAC pellets, whereas hESC-Ch pellets appeared to be smaller and less organized (Fig. 2c and d). High-magnification (20 × ) images of the boxed areas with HE, Safranin O, and Toluidine Blue staining indicated that both cell types produced cartilage matrix, although the staining intensity was lower in hESC-Ch pellets (Fig. 2e and f). Principal component analysis and correlation analysis showed that although the two groups clustered separately, their overall gene expression profiles were highly similar (r2 = 0.83) (Fig. 3a and b). Heatmap and volcano plot analyses identified several differentially expressed genes, although only a few were directly related to matrix formation, including CHI3L1 and HAS3, which were significantly upregulated in hACs (Fig. 3c and d). Discussion

This study describes the isolation of chondrocytes from hESC-derived cartilage, their expansion, and the fabrication of implant type tissue-engineered cartilage using scaffold-based tissue engineering. This approach enabled the controlled production of engineered constructs with a defined size and shape, offering a potential strategy for clinically applicable cartilage repair. hESC-Chs had lower GAG levels and less COL2 in vitro than P3 hACs. However, in vivo experiments demonstrated that increasing the number of seeded cells reduced these differences in cartilage matrix production. RNA-seq analysis further revealed significant downregulation of CHI3L1 and HAS3, which are thought to be involved in ECM formation. Notably, by increasing the seeding density on PGA nonwoven fabric scaffolds, the cartilage-forming ability of the hESC-Chs was effectively restored, enabling the formation of morphologically precise cartilage constructs that were suitable for implantation. Building upon our previously established xeno-free, fully automated differentiation protocol [22], our approach leveraged these hESC-derived cartilage tissues as a source to isolate and expand chondrocytes for the subsequent engineering of implant-type constructs.

Chondrogenic differentiation from hESCs has been achieved using four main strategies: co-culture with mature chondrocytes, EB-based induction with growth factors, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-like intermediates, and targeted differentiation [17,[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]]. Chondrocyte differentiation was demonstrated using gene expression or surface marker profiles for all these approaches, but only co-culture and EB-based induction have shown actual cartilage matrix formation through histological staining [[24], [25], [26],30]. MSC-like and targeted differentiation strategies have not resulted in matrix production [17,[27], [28], [29], [30]]. However, these studies have primarily focused on inducing chondrocyte differentiation from ESCs and have not proposed the fabrication of implantable cartilage constructs with controlled morphology. Our method combined an EB-based differentiation strategy with a xeno-free, automated culture platform, enabling the direct formation of cartilage matrix in adherent 2D culture and eliminating the need for additional 3D induction steps. In contrast, recent advances in induced PSC (iPSC)-derived cartilage have led to the successful repair of articular cartilage defects in a primate model by press-fitting preformed cartilage into defect sites [31]. Although this study highlights the clinical potential of iPSC-derived cartilage, the approach did not include the design or control of complex 3D morphology that is required for clinical reconstruction. Thus, for PSC-derived cartilage, including hESCs and iPSCs, no previous strategy has addressed the fabrication of implantable constructs with precise morphological control. Our scaffold-based engineering system enables the production of transplantable cartilage with a customizable size and shape to meet clinical demands, offering a promising solution for future personalized reconstructive therapy. Furthermore, in this study, we developed a novel technique that combined PGA nonwoven fabric scaffolds with high-density cell seeding to achieve these goals.

Tissue engineering cell sources for cartilage regeneration may include elastic cartilage (e.g., auricular cartilage), hyaline cartilage (e.g., nasal septal cartilage), fibrocartilage (e.g., costal cartilage), and stem cells with multipotent differentiation potential. In previous autologous transplantation strategies, chondrocytes from auricular cartilage were widely utilized. Auricular chondrocytes exhibited significantly higher expression of COL2A1 and GAG, indicating superior ECM synthesis capacity [32]. Comparative analyses of auricular chondrocytes and nasal septal chondrocytes revealed marked differences in matrix production under 3D culture conditions [32]. Our previous studies utilized auricular cartilage as the cell source [8], but the limited supply of autologous cartilage cells remains a major barrier in its clinical application. To address this issue, we employed hESC-Chs as an alternative, scalable cell source. Compared with iPSCs, hESCs are established without reprogramming by exogenous factor expression, thereby avoiding process-related genomic alterations or transgene insertions. While iPSCs overcome ethical concerns associated with embryonic derivation, their induction and subsequent genetic stability remain critical considerations for clinical use. Conversely, despite ethical limitations, hESCs provide a genetically unmodified and stable platform, which may facilitate future studies involving targeted gene manipulation in cartilage tissue engineering. Although hESC-derived cartilage had lower ECM formation, protocol optimization such as high-density seeding on scaffolds enhanced COL2 and GAG production to levels equivalent to those of hACs. Thus, this system may provide a stable, reproducible platform for cartilage transplantation.

Scaffold-based systems are essential in cartilage tissue engineering, since they promote cell engraftment, enhance matrix deposition, and enable morphological precision and size control [33]. The novel PGA scaffolds have been acknowledged for their biocompatibility, facilitation of cell adhesion, and capacity to promote matrix accumulation [34,35]. In this study, the use of PGA nonwoven fabric scaffolds in combination with high-density hESC-Ch seeding enhanced ECM production, leading to the formation of well-defined cartilage constructs. Based on these findings, this study is the first to successfully fabricate implant type tissue-engineered cartilage using hESC-Chs.

hESC-Chs exhibit higher expression of PD-L1 compared to elastic cartilage cells, making them promising candidates for allogeneic transplantation [[36], [37], [38]]. However, the optimization of cartilage regeneration techniques remains a significant challenge in the use of these chondrocytes for regenerative medicine. Complementary strategies have been effective in overcoming this hurdle. In this study, visual harvesting methods were used to collect regenerated cartilage tissue in sufficient quantities. To enhance efficiency, selective separation may be facilitated by employing previously identified chondrocyte-specific surface markers with PSC-derived chondrocytes and articular chondrocytes [39,40]. Gene analysis has also revealed reduced expression of the CHI3L1 gene located upstream of IGF-1 and IGF-2 [41,42], suggesting that supplementation with these growth factors may represent a potential strategy to enhance matrix formation. For clinical application, our in vivo evaluation using subcutaneous pockets in immunodeficient mice with a 4-week observation period serves only as an initial verification of chondrogenic potential. Longer-term implantation (e.g., 8–12 weeks) and orthotopic transplantation into joint environments will be necessary to assess durability and clinical relevance.

This study presents a clinically relevant strategy for generating hESC-derived cartilage constructs by integrating xeno-free automated differentiation and scaffold engineering. These findings establish a novel method for producing scalable cartilage tissue of consistent quality, thereby directly addressing any clinical challenges. These advances support the development of standardized allogeneic cartilage grafts. We propose that hESC-derived constructs could serve as next-generation, off-the-shelf therapeutics for cartilage repair, with future studies poised to investigate their first-in-human application.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that hESC-Chs possess a stable chondrogenic phenotype and matrix-forming capacity under optimized conditions, exhibit matrix-forming capacity equivalent to that of hACs. Moreover, transcriptomic analysis reveals a high degree of similarity in cartilage-related gene expression for the two cell types. This underscores the potential of hESC-Chs as a renewable and standardized cell source for cartilage tissue engineering and regenerative therapy.
