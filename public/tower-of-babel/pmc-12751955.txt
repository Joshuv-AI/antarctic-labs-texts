Identification, characterization, and expression profiling of the bHLH gene family in Beta vulgaris
Authors: Haizhu Yang, Zhonghua Peng, Guoxing Xue, Weijiao Wu, Hui Zhou, Xin Yao, Chao Ma, Ailing He, Jingjun Ruan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBasic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) is one of the largest families of transcription factors in plants, animals, and microorganisms and plays important complex physiological role in plant growth and development, metabolic regulation, and environmental stress. With the development of bioinformatics, bHLH transcription factor families have been continuously identified and analyzed, but the bHLH family in Beta vulgaris has not been previously analyzed.ResultsUsing whole-genome sequencing data from B. vulgaris, we identified 96 BvbHLH genes with relatively large differences in physicochemical properties that were unevenly distributed across nine chromosomes. A phylogenetic developmental tree was constructed using the BvbHLH and AtbHLH proteins, which were ultimately categorized into 21 subgroups and one unclassified group. The gene structure, conserved motifs, gene duplication events, and evolutionary relationships of the 96 BvbHLH genes were analyzed using various bioinformatics approaches. The results showed that motifs 1 and 2 were widely distributed in BvbHLHs, had highly conserved gene structures within the same subfamily, presence of four tandem duplication events, and six pairs of segmental duplication events, and were more closely homologous to dicotyledonous plants. We also analyzed the tissue-specific expression of 12 BvbHLH genes during B. vulgaris maturity and their responses to eight abiotic stresses using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction.ConclusionsBioinformatics analysis of the gene structure, evolutionary relationship, and expression pattern of the B. vulgaris bHLH gene family could help understanding better the evolution of the bHLH gene family as well as screening candidate genes in B. vulgaris.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07759-1.

Background

Transcription factors (TFs) are an essential class of regulatory factors in plants that facilitate biological functions including the regulation of cell differentiation, tissue development, organ construction, signal transduction, metabolic physiology, and adaptation to adverse environments. Based on protein structure analysis, transcription factors generally include DNA-binding regions, transcriptional regulatory regions, oligomerization sites, and nuclear localization signaling functional regions [1]; that is, they act as central regulators and molecular switches that activate or repress the transcription of multiple target genes [2].

The basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) family is the second largest TF family in plants and is widespread in animals, plants, and microorganisms. The bHLH gene family was first identified in animals, where hydrophobic amino acid residues in the helical structure are highly conserved and the region is critical for dimerization and DNA binding [3]. In plants, the first bHLH gene was found in the maize R gene, and it was determined to be a gene that plays a role in the process of anthocyanin biosynthesis [4]. The bHLH gene family is named after its basic helix-loop-helix structure. This structural domain comprises 50–60 amino acid residues that can be divided into two conserved regions: the basic region at the N-terminal end of the polypeptide chain and the HLH region at the C-terminal end [3, 5]. The N-terminus comprises 10–15 basic amino acids associated with DNA binding that can bind to the cis-acting element E-box; therefore, the number of basic amino acids and their residues at the N-terminus determines whether the bHLH transcription factor is active in binding to DNA. The C-terminus consists mainly of approximately 40 hydrophobic amino acid residues, in which a loop region of variable length is formed separating the two α-helices, which is favorable for the formation of dimers in the HLH region and for the stabilization of the secondary structure of proteins [6–9]. Based on evolutionary origin relationships, sequence similarity, gene function, and DNA-binding properties, bHLH transcription factors in animals are classified into six groups (A-F). Group B proteins bind to G-boxes (CACGTG), and most plant bHLH proteins belong to group B of the animal family [5, 10–13].

Since the discovery of the bHLH family of transcription factors, its members have been identified in many plant species. Based on previous phylogenetic relationships and conserved region results, it has been suggested that bHLH proteins in plants belong to 15–25 subfamilies [6, 9, 14]. The number of bHLH family members and subfamilies varies considerably among species. For example, 147 AtbHLH proteins have been identified in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana [12] and classified into 21 subfamilies; 167 OsbHLH proteins were identified in Oryza sativa [15] and classified into 22 subfamilies; 100 NtbHLH proteins were identified in Nicotiana tabacum [16] and classified into 15 subfamilies; 159 TabHLH proteins were identified in Triticum aestivum [17] and classified into 19 subfamilies; 208 ZmbHLH proteins were identified in Zea mays [18] and classified into 18 subfamilies; 602 BnbHLH proteins were identified in Brassica napus [19] and classified into 35 subfamilies; and 10 AbbHLH proteins were identified in Agaricus bisporus [20] and classified into 7 subfamilies.

Plant bHLH transcription factors can function as activators or repressors in a wide variety of ways and have been extensively validated in many plants. DYSFUNCTIONAL TAPETUM1 (DYT1) can regulate anther development and transcriptional expression in A. thaliana through interactions with bHLH10, bHLH89, and bHLH90 [21]. PIF7, PIF3, and PIF4 play combined roles in A. thaliana photopigment signaling to regulate the level of the red-light receptor phyB, thus prolonging the response to red light [22]. PIF4 also regulates the process of hypocotyl elongation in A. thaliana [23, 24] and binds to FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) to thereby control thermosensory flowering behavior [25], and interacts with BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 1 (BZR1) to regulate plant growth and developmental processes [26]. AtbHLH112 inhibits root development, although it positively regulates anti-salt, drought, and osmotic stresses [27]. AhbHLH112, which is homologous to AtbHLH112, enhances reactive oxygen species scavenging by regulating peroxidase-mediated H2O2 homeostasis and participates in abscisic acid (ABA)-dependent stress response pathways, which is a positive factor in drought stress tolerance [28]. ZmbHLH85 (BA1) is involved in the regulation of stem architecture, ZmbHLH164 (MS23) and ZmbHLH62 (MS32) are essential for Z. mays reproduction, and ZmbHLH8 (Zmks) is involved in ABA reactions [18]. TabHLH110 is specifically expressed in roots, and the gene is homologous to AtABS5 (AT1G68810), which is expressed in the roots of A. thaliana and affects vascular cell division in the apical meristematic tissue. OsbHLH13 and OsbHLH16 regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis [15]. Overexpression of OsbHLH148 can induce the upregulation of related genes involved in the stress response such as OsDREB and OsJAZ and the upregulation of related genes of the jasmonic acid signaling pathway, and relevant experiments have confirmed that it plays a regulatory role [29]. Another study showed that OsbHLH6 is also involved in the jasmonate signaling pathway and, thus, in drought stress and trauma response [30]. These findings suggest that bHLH transcription factors exhibit biological diversity in the network of plant growth and development and in the regulation of responses to adversity.

Beta vulgaris is a biennial herbaceous plant of the beet genus in the subfamily Chenopodiaceae [31]. It is a key cash crop and a key raw material for the sugar industry, with advantages in terms of salinity, cold, drought, wide adaptability, and strong resistance, especially in semi-arid, lightly saline areas with low yield and low quality of food crops. It also has better economic benefits [32].

The bHLH gene family in B. vulgaris has not yet been identified and studies related to this gene family under adverse stress treatments have rarely been reported, adding a certain degree of difficulty to the study of its gene function. In this study, we analyzed the gene structure, chromosomal distribution, and evolutionary relationships of bHLH transcription factors in B. vulgaris using genome-wide identification and subfamily classification methods. Furthermore, we used quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) to study the expression levels of some BvbHLH family members during ripening and under abiotic stress, laying the foundation for understanding the function and evolutionary mechanism of the bHLH gene family in B. vulgaris.

Results

Characterization of the BvbHLH genes

Two BLAST algorithms were used to identify 96 bHLH genes in the B. vulgaris genome. The 96 identified bHLH genes were named BvbHLH1 to BvbHLH96 based on their position on the chromosome (Additional file 1: Table S1). We then analyzed the genes for coding sequence (CDS) length, molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), structural domains, instability index (II), aliphatic index (AI), and subcellular localization prediction [33]. The average number of amino acids contained in members of the B. vulgaris bHLH gene family was 367, and the average molecular weight was 40.60 kDa. The longest protein was BvbHLH77 (911 amino acids, 98.97 kDa), and the shortest protein was BvbHLH62 (94 amino acids, 10.5 kDa). The isoelectric points ranged from 4.32 (BvbHLH37) to 11.57 (BvbHLH20), with a mean value of 6.45, and 80.21% of the BvbHLH proteins had an isoelectric point of less than 7 and were rich in acidic amino acids. Among all BvbHLH family members, 13 genes contained both bHLH-MYC_N and HLH structural domains, and 83 genes contained HLH structural domains. Subcellular localization predictions showed that 87 BvbHLH genes were located in the nucleus, 8 BvbHLH genes were located in the chloroplasts, and BvbHLH16 was located only in the cytoskeleton.

Evidently, members of the bHLH gene family are also very different in number from those of other crops (Additional file 2: Table S2), and the proportion of BvbHLH genes to total genes in the B. vulgaris genome was approximately 0.47%, which was close to that of Solanum lycopersicum (0.46%) [34]; lower than that of A. thaliana (0.59%) [12], Brachypodium distachyon (0.55%) [15], Fagopyrum tataricum (0.49%) [35], and Setaria italica (0.48%) [36]; and higher than that of O. sativa (0.44%) [15], Populus tomentosa (0.40%) [37], and Hordeum vulgare (0.35%) [38].

Phylogenetic analysis, classification and multiple sequence comparison of BvbHLH proteins

A phylogenetic tree of 96 BvbHLH proteins and 61 AtbHLH proteins was constructed. (Fig. 1A, Additional files 1 and 3: Tables S1 and S3). Based on the tree topology and classification methods proposed by previous authors [9, 12], the 96 BvbHLH proteins were categorized into 20 subgroups and one unclassified (UC) group in the phylogenetic tree. The UC group contained three BvbHLH proteins (BvbHLH16, BvbHLH72, and BvbHLH76). The 93 BvbHLH proteins were unevenly distributed across 21 subgroups, which is consistent with the classification of bHLH proteins in (A) thaliana [12, 15]. However, the absence of BvbHLH family members in subgroup 6 suggests that they may have been lost or have remained undifferentiated during the long-term evolution of (B) vulgaris. Of the 21 subfamilies, subfamily 18 had the most members (13), whereas subfamilies 7 (BvbHLH73) and 21 (BvbHLH92) had the fewest (1). Phylogenetic tree results showed that most of the BvbHLHs were tightly clustered with AtbHLHs (bootstrap support ≥ 70), indicating that these proteins have high homology and similar functions. Amino acid sequences of AtbHLH and BvbHLH proteins from subgroups 1–21 (excluding subgroup 6) were selected for multiple sequence comparisons (Fig. 1B); 93 BvbHLH proteins were found to have typical bHLH structural domains, and three proteins from ungrouped clusters (BvbHLH18, BvbHLH72, and BvbHLH76) contained predicted bHLH structural domains with low confidence. According to previous studies, the basic region contains 17 amino acids, the helical region contains 15 amino acids, and the ring region contains 6 amino acids [12]. The bHLH structural domain of B. vulgaris spans 53–58 amino acids. The structural domains of (A) thaliana and (B) vulgaris bHLH are relatively well conserved, but the regions outside the bHLH structural domains are mostly in a state of differentiation and diversification [8, 15]. Nevertheless, the loop region has the greatest variation in amino acid structural domains, with some proteins showing marked increases in the number of amino acids in the loop region, especially in subfamilies 1, 2, and 11. This has been observed in other plant bHLH proteins, including A. thaliana [12], O. sativa [15], Solanum tuberosum [39], S. lycopersicum [34], and F. tataricum [35].

Analysis of bHLH gene structure and protein conserved motifs in Beta vulgaris

To understand the structural components of the B. vulgaris bHLH genes and to better explore the BvbHLH genes, the intron-exon structures of 96 BvbHLH genes were obtained by comparing the DNA sequences of the B. vulgaris bHLH genome. A comparison of the number and position of introns-exons showed that the 96 BvbHLHs genes had different numbers of exons, ranging from 1 to 11 (Fig. 2A and B; Additional file 1: Table S1). In addition, 14 genes (14.58%) contained only 1 exon, the rest had 2 or more exons, including only 1 gene with 11 exons (BvbHLH42, 1.64%), and more genes contained 4, 5, 6, or 7 exons (n = 11). These 14 intron genes belonged to six subfamilies (8, 13, 14, 18, 19, and 20) but were predominantly distributed in subfamilies 8 and 13. Meanwhile, the largest number of BvbHLH genes contained no introns, and none of the subfamily 8 members had introns; subfamily 4 members had the greatest variation in the number of introns, with the smallest having only two introns (BvbHLH21) and the largest containing nine introns (BvbHLH84). Comparative analyses revealed a high degree of homology between genes in subfamilies 1, 2, 5, 8, 9, 10, 14, 17, 18, and 19. To further explore the structural diversity of B. vulgaris bHLH proteins, the conserved motifs of 96 BvbHLH proteins were analyzed using the online software MEME (Fig. 2C; Additional file 1: Table S1), and 10 different motifs were identified (Additional file 4: Table S4). Except for BvbHLH46, BvbHLH72, BvbHLH79, and BvbHLH80, motifs 1 and 2 were widely distributed among the 96 BvbHLH proteins and were positioned adjacent to each other. In contrast, motif 7 had the least distribution, being present in only nine BvbHLH proteins, and motif 6 was distributed in only 10 BvbHLH proteins. BvbHLH members of the same group typically contain similar motifs. For example, groups 2, 3, 4, 10, 11, 13, 19, and 20 contained motifs 1 and 2; groups 5 and 9 contained motifs 1, 2, 4, and 5; group 8 contained motifs 8, 1, and 2; group 12 contained motifs 1, 2, and 6; group 16 contained motifs 3, 1, and 2; group 17 contained motifs 3, 1, 2, and 6; and group 18 contained motifs 3, 1, 2, and 9. Of course, some motifs can only be distributed in specific subfamilies; for example, motif 6 is specific to groups 12 and 17, motif 9 is specific to group 18, and motifs 3, 8, 9, and 10 are specific to groups 8 and 10. Further analysis revealed that some motifs could only be distributed at specific locations in the pattern; for instance, motif 1 was always distributed at the beginning of groups 1, 2, 3, 11, and 13; motif 3 was always distributed at the beginning of groups 16, 17, and 18; motif 10 was always distributed at the beginning of group 8; motif 5 was always distributed at the end of groups 5, 8, and 9; motif 6 was always distributed at the end of groups 12 and 17; and motif 9 was always distributed at the end of group 18. The structural domains of bHLH are composed of multiple conserved motifs, indicating genetic diversification. The functions of most of these conserved proteins have not been confirmed; however, similar gene structures and motif compositions are consistent with the results of their phylogenetic analyses and support the reliability of grouping the B. vulgaris bHLH gene family.

Cis-acting element analysis of BvbHLH genes

Cis-acting elements are sequences that are upstream of the promoter region of a gene and act as binding sites for transcription factors with essential regulatory roles in plant physiological processes [40]. In this study, 2000 bp upstream of the start codon of 96 BvbHLHs genes were selected as promoter sequences, and their cis-acting elements were predicted using the PLANTCARE online website (Additional file 5: Table S5). In total, 198 cis-acting elements were found in the promoter regions of 96 BvbHLHs genes distributed across various promoter sequences, including light- responsive, development-related, environmental stress-related, hormone-responsive, promoter-associated, and binding site-associated elements. Light-responsive elements (66; 33.33%) and other elements (42; 21.21%) were predominant. Light-response- related elements (Box 4; TCT-motif and GT1-motif) are present in most BvbHLH genes; other elements are also widely distributed in the BvbHLHs gene family; for example, Unnamed_4 (CTCC) is present in all BvbHLHs genes; however, the function of the element is unknown. Additionally, development-related elements related to meristem expression (CAT-box) and elements involved in zein metabolism regulation (O2-site) were distributed among some of the genes. Most of the BvbHLHs genes encoded methyl jasmonate (CGTCA-motif, TGACG-motif) and abscisic acid (ABRE), and a few genes had genes encoding growth factors (TGA-element, AuxRR-core, AuxRE), salicylic acid (TCA-element), and gibberellins (P-box, GARE-motif, and TATC-box).

We found environmental stress-related promoters involving anaerobic induction and MYB-binding sites involved in drought-induced environmental stress that are widely present in BvbHLHs genes, a few of which also have cis-acting elements involved in drought (LTR), defense and stress (TC-rich repeats), trauma-responsive WUN-motif, dehydration, low temperature, and salt stress (DRE). This also proved that the promoter analysis was reliable. Finally, to further analyze the significance of these elements and predict the function of BvbHLHs, the cis-acting elements contained in 96 BvbHLHs genes were characterized using a Venn diagram (Fig. 3). For example, none of the genes in the development-related elements contain circadian (CAAAGATATC), GCN4-motif (TGAGTCA), CAT-box (GCCACT), or O2-site (GTTGACGTGA) cis-acting elements. Among the environmental stress-related elements, four genes (BvbHLH12, BvbHLH55, BvbHLH57, BvbHLH12, and BvbHLH84) contained LTR, MNS, Tc-rich repeats, and ARE motif elements. Among the hormone response elements, 36 BvbHLH genes (Additional file 5: Table S5−1) contained the ABRE, CGTCA-motif, and TGACG-motif elements. Among the light-responsive elements, 31 BvbHLH genes (Additional file 5: Table S5−1) contained Box 4, G-box, GT1-motif, and TCT-motif elements. Among the promoter-related elements, 18 BvbHLHs contained TATA-box, CCGTCC, and CAAT-box motif elements and, of these, all BvbHLHs contained both TATA-box and CAAT-box motif elements.

Chromosomal distribution and gene duplication events of BvbHLH genes

Studies have shown that genome duplication events occur in both seed plants and angiosperms, resulting in an increase in the number of genes in plants and the presence of a large number of repetitive sequences in the genome [41, 42]. Expansion of gene family members is an important driver of gene family expansion, which drives species evolution primarily through gene duplication and segmental duplication events. Mapping of the physical locations of BvbHLHs genes based on the latest genome database of B. vulgaris (Fig. 4A) revealed that the BvbHLHs genes were unevenly distributed on nine chromosomes. Each BvbHLH gene is named according to its physical position from top to bottom on Chr1 to Chr9. The highest number of genes for BvbHLHs was found on Chr6 (20/96, 20.83%), followed by Chr5 (18/96, 18.75%), and Chr2 contained the lowest number of genes (3/96, 3.12%). In addition, Chr3 contained 14 genes (14.58%), Chr1 contained 12 genes (12.5%), Chr9 contained 11 genes (11.46%), Chr8 contained 8 genes (8.33%), Chr7 contained 6 genes (6.25%), and Chr4 contained 4 genes (4.17%). BvbHLHs genes of the same subfamily were also randomly distributed on these nine chromosomes, with the majority of BvbHLHs genes located at the head or end of the chromosome. Tandem duplication events refer to two or more chromosomal regions within 200 kb of the same genomic region [43]. On Chr1, BvbHLH9 and BvbHLH10 were found to be tandem duplication genes, and both genes belonged to subfamily 19; on Chr5, BvbHLH48 and BvbHLH49 were found to be tandem duplication genes, and both genes belonged to subfamily 8; on Chr6, BvbHLH69 and BvbHLH70 were found to be tandem duplication genes, and both genes belonged to subfamily 5; on Chr8, BvbHLH81 and BvbHLH82 were found to be tandem duplication genes, and both genes belonged to subfamily 19 (Additional file 6: Table S6). In addition, segmental duplication genes analysis showed (Fig. 4B) that 12 (12.5%) genes with evolutionary relationships and 6 pairs of segmental duplication events were identified in the BvbHLH genes and consist of the following: BvbHLH44/BvbHLH71, BvbHLH40/BvbHLH64, BvbHLH14/BvbHLH56, BvbHLH5/BvbHLH52, BvbHLH3/BvbHLH36, and BvbHLH23/BvbHLH30. Apart from BvbHLH14 and BvbHLH56, two pairs of BvbHLHs genes belonged to subfamily 18, and the other three pairs belonged to subfamilies 8, 12, and 16, respectively, and were all chained within their subfamilies. BvbHLHs genes were unevenly distributed among the nine linkage groups, with LG6 having the most BvbHLH members (n = 6, 50%), LG2, 3, and 4 having the fewest (n = 1; 8.33%), and LG7, 8, and 9 having no family members (Additional file 6: Table S6−1).

This suggests that some of the BvbHLH genes may have arisen from gene duplication events that played a driving role in the evolution of B. vulgaris, which in turn increased the number of BvbHLHs gene family members.

Synteny analysis of BvbHLHs genes

To further explore the evolutionary relationship between bHLH genes in B. vulgaris, an interspecific collinear map was constructed in this study between B. vulgaris and six representative plants, including three monocotyledons (B. distachyon, O. sativa, and Z. mays) and three dicotyledons (A. thaliana, S. tuberosum, and S. lycopersicum) (Fig. 5, Additional file 7: Table S7). The results showed that a total of 96 BvbHLH genes displayed syntenic relationships with those in S. lycopersicum (62), S. tuberosum (60), (A) thaliana (49), (B) distachyon (21), Z. mays (14), and O. sativa (21). The collinearity of the B. vulgaris bHLHs family with dicotyledonous bHLH genes were relatively high, with B. vulgaris having the highest number (85 homologous pairs) with both S. tuberosum and S. lycopersicum, followed by (A) thaliana (73 pairs). In contrast, the (B) vulgaris bHLH family has low collinearity with monocotyledonous plants, which had only 34, 31, and 22 homologous pairs with B. distachyon, O. sativa, and Z. mays, respectively. At the same time, it was found that BvbHLH4, BvbHLH7, BvbHLH9, BvbHLH47, and BvbHLH71 had co-linear orthologous genes with three dicots. BvbHLH9 and BvbHLH30 had co-linear orthologous genes with all three monocotyledons, suggesting that these genes may have developed gradually after the independent differentiation of dicotyledons and monocotyledons. BvbHLH9 had co-linear orthologous genes with all six plants, whereas 51 genes, including BvbHLH6, BvbHLH10, BvbHLH15, and BvbHLH16, did not have co-linear orthologous genes with any of the six plants, suggesting that BvbHLH9 may be a primitive gene that had not been lost or strongly diverged over the course of the long period of evolution of B. vulgaris.

Expression patterns of BvbHLH genes in different tissues

Discussion

Identification and structural analysis of the BvbHLH genes

The bHLH gene family, a large superfamily of eukaryotic transcription factors plays a series of important regulatory roles in metabolism, growth, development, and physiological processes in higher organisms [37, 40, 44]. Thorough identification of bHLH proteins in the whole genome is difficult, but studying the bHLH transcription factor family of any species at the genome-wide level and characterizing the functions of some members of the family is conducive to the systematic discovery of genes or functions among genes, and it can also provide some theoretical support for the development of research on the bHLH gene family of the species.

In this study, we used bioinformatic methods to characterize the B. vulgaris genome, systematically studied the B. vulgaris bHLH transcription factor family, identified 96 BvbHLH genes, and analyzed their physicochemical properties, including CDSs, theoretical pI, MW, and II. All encoded proteins showed significant differences; for example, the amino acid length ranged from 94 (BvbHLH62) to 911 (BvbHLH77) amino acids, and the theoretical pI ranged from 4.32 (BvbHLH37) to 11.57 (BvbHLH20) kDa, indicating that the bHLH transcription factor family has diverged inconsistently over the long-term evolution of B. vulgaris, leading to its high complexity [39]. Among them, 80.21% of the BvbHLH proteins had pIs of less than 7, indicating that the B. vulgaris bHLH gene family is rich in acidic amino acids, which is in agreement with findings related to the bHLH gene family in monocotyledons [36, 45, 46]. Subcellular localization predictions showed that the vast majority of BvbHLH proteins (90.6%) were localized in the nucleus, suggesting that this family of transcription factors plays a dominant regulatory role mainly in the nucleus.

The bHLH transcription factor family contains two functionally distinct components: a basic region and helix-loop-helix. The basic region mainly determines whether the bHLH gene is active and whether it can polymerize with other transcription factors to form homo- or heterodimeric structures. Atchley et al. [42] and Toledo Ortiz et al. [12] analyzed the DNA-binding capacity of the alkaline regions of BvbHLHs. The bHLH gene sequences are divided into two categories: E-box binding genes and non-E-box binding genes. Because the conserved amino acids in this region mainly recognize the shared core site, while other residues determine the specificity of the E-boxes, causing the E-boxes to show various forms, the E-boxes can be subdivided into G-boxes and non-G-boxes [42, 43]. Among them, G-box-binding proteins contain the three essential amino acids, His-9/Arg-9, Glu-13, and Arg-17, and are categorized as non-G-box binding proteins if they contain only Glu-13 and Arg-17. The bHLH structural domain contains two essential amino acids, Glu-13 and Arg-16. If it contains only one of these amino acids, it is a non-E-box binding protein. In addition, if the basic region lacks essential amino acids or only contains Glu-13 or Arg-16, and the number of amino acids in this region is less than four, then it is a non-DNA-binding protein. Specifically, they possess no capacity to bind to DNA. Among the 96 BvbHLH proteins, 74 (77.08%) were categorized as E-box binding proteins, of which 72 BvbHLHs were G-box binding proteins and two BvbHLHs (2.08%) were non-G-box binding proteins. The remaining four BvbHLHs (BvbHLH22, BvbHLH79, BvbHLH80, and BvbHLH89) were regarded as non-DNA-binding proteins because of the lack of Glu-13 or Arg-17 required for binding to the E-box. Non-DNA-bound HLHs may participate in the physiological and biochemical processes of a plant by forming a heterodimer (with no ability to bind DNA) that may act as a negative regulator [47]. The two α-helices that are both hydrophilic and lipophilic in the HLH region are separated by loops of different lengths. If only the primary structure and length variability are considered, the loop is present only to provide the gap required for dimerization, and at least five amino acid residues are required to maintain a parallel four-helix bundle structure; furthermore, the amino acid content of the loop is not a determining factor if the function or evolutionary relationship of the bHLH gene is investigated [12]. Analysis of the loop of B. vulgaris bHLH showed that all 96 BvbHLH proteins contained five or more amino acids in the loop structure and 86% of the BvbHLH proteins showed a pattern of amino acid conservation with the presence of basic residues (K/R) at site 47 [12]. In terms of the size and amino acid composition of BvbHLH proteins, the loop is the most variable region of the structural domain, with variable lengths that are difficult to align, which is consistent with the bHLH proteins observed in other organisms [7]. We observed that the vast majority of BvbHLH subfamilies had conserved helix-loop-helix structural domains, with subgroups 11, 16, 17, 18, and 19 being the most conserved, suggesting that proteins in these subgroups are a key class of regulatory components in the transcriptional network of plant developmental pathways [48]. Subgroup 3 was the least conserved, suggesting that subgroup 3 of BvbHLH may have evolved different functions based on its DNA-binding ability and recognition sequence specificity. The conservation profiles of the above subgroups have similarities and differences with those of Sorghum bicolor [46], Chenopodium quinoa [49], S. italica [36], and B. distachyon [50], indicating that the conserved structural domains are not specific to monocotyledonous or dicotyledonous leaves.

Among the identified plant bHLH subfamilies, bHLH proteins may play essential roles in plant growth, development, differentiation, and adaptation in chili Capsicum annuum [45], Vitis davidii [51], T. aestivum [17], and Glycine max [52], among others. Based on a phylogenetic tree constructed from 96 BvbHLHs and AtbHLHs, we found that at least one BvbHLH gene was identified in every subfamily of A. thaliana except for subgroup 6, suggesting that the bHLH family diverged earlier than the period of species formation in (A) thaliana and (B) vulgaris. By analyzing the differentiation of the BvbHLH family and the number of members in the subfamilies, we classified the 96 BvbHLH proteins into 21 subfamilies and one UC family, which is consistent with the number of subfamilies compared to A. thaliana [14] and one subfamily less than O. sativa [15]. Among them, subfamily 18 had the highest number of BvbHLH genes (n = 13.54%), which was consistent with the results for A. thaliana, indicating that subfamily 18 showed more intense differentiation during long-term evolution. Meanwhile, we found that subgroup 6 was present in (A) thaliana [12], O. sativa [15], S. tuberosum [39], and Z. mays [18], suggesting that other subgroups replaced group 6 to play the corresponding roles, were not differentiated, or were lost because of adaptation to the environment during the evolution of (B) vulgaris.

The conserved structural domains and motif combinations of genes within the same subfamily have some similarities, which do not exclude obvious deletions or amplifications between the same subfamily, while the differences between different subfamilies are more obvious. Approximately 85.41% of BvbHLH genes have 1–10 different numbers of introns distributed within the structural domain, none of the members of subfamily 8 have introns, BvbHLH6 had fewer introns than the other members of the same subfamily, and the genes with a low number of introns or missing introns have a lower level of expression in plants [53]. It has been shown that introns are subject to less selective pressure, their degree of variation is higher than that of coding sequences, and the frequency of recombination between genes is also higher [54, 55]. In other words, there is an obvious intron deletion or amplification that may result in the expansion of the gene family or loss of function. We found that motif 2 was present in all 96 BvbHLHs, indicating that motif 2 is a conserved motif in the bHLH family. Overall, the number and location of motifs contained in BvbHLH genes within the same subfamily were similar, suggesting high conservation and functional similarity between subfamilies; however, there are large differences in structure and motifs between subfamilies, which, to a certain extent, leads to the differentiation of the gene functions of the bHLH family, thus regulating complex physiological processes in plants [56].

Cis-acting elements are specific DNA sequences in tandem with structural genes that regulate the precise initiation of gene transcription and transcriptional efficiency by binding to transcription factors [57]. In this study, by predicting cis-acting elements from the upstream 2000 bp promoter sequences of 96 BvbHLH genes, we found that the promoter regions of BvbHLHs contained cis-acting elements for growth and development, adversity stress, phytohormones, and light response. Among them, light-responsive elements (Box 4, TCT-motif, and GT1-motif) were present in most of the BvbHLHs and accounted for the largest proportion of genes, suggesting that light signaling may play a key role in the transcriptional regulation of BvbHLH genes [58]. Notably, the promoter sequence of BvbHLH57 (subfamily 18) was predicted to contain four classes of abiotic response elements and four classes of light-responsive elements, suggesting that this gene may have multiple regulatory mechanisms.

Evolutionary relationships of BvbHLH genes

Gene duplications are prevalent in organisms and provide the most primitive base of genetic material for the evolution of organisms. They can generate new genes through mutation and selection and are an essential driving force in the diversity of genomes and genetic systems [59, 60]. During the evolution of different species, the number of bHLH genes vary owing to genome size or duplication and the biological environment, such as in (A) thaliana (147) [12], O. sativa (167) [15], F. tataricum (164) [35], and T. aestivum (159) [17]. Gene duplication events are driven mainly by two modes, tandem duplication and segmental duplication, which play essential roles and lead to the expansion of gene family members [35, 61, 62]. For example, large-scale gene duplication events occur in Brassica pekinensis [63], Gossypium spp [52]., and (B) napus [19]. In this study, we found that four tandem repeat events were unevenly distributed across the chromosomes in the BvbHLH gene family, encompassing eight genes, particularly in subfamily 19. Furthermore, six pairs of segmental duplication events involving 12 genes were also identified, especially in subfamily 18, and the highest numbers of members were in subfamily 18, suggesting that tandem duplications and segmental duplication events occurred mainly in genes in subfamilies 18 and 19, which contributed to the expansion of BvbHLH family members.

In addition, we analyzed the collinearity of B. vulgaris with three monocotyledonous plants (B. distachyon, O. sativa, and Z. mays) and three dicotyledonous plants (A. thaliana, S. lycopersicum, and S. tuberosum) and found that the collinearity of B. vulgaris with dicotyledonous plants was relatively high. It was also found in the interspecies evolutionary tree that clustered closer to dicotyledonous plants, whereas there were fewer orthologous genes in monocotyledonous plants, suggesting that the BvbHLH family was more closely related to the dicotyledonous bHLH family. Specifically, BvbHLH9 has orthologous genes in six plants; BvbHLH4, BvbHLH7, BvbHLH9, BvbHLH47, and BvbHLH71 have orthologous genes in three dicotyledonous plants, suggesting that these six genes may be primitive genes that exist before the differentiation of dicotyledonous plants and have significant regulatory mechanisms in plants involved in physiological and biochemical processes, such as growth and development, biosynthesis, stress resistance, and signal transduction.

Gene expression pattern and function prediction of bHLHs in Beta vulgaris

There are many members of the bHLH transcription factor family, second only to the MYB transcription factor family. To date, only the bHLH transcription factor family of the model crops (A) thaliana and O. sativa has been studied in a more mature way, functioning biologically as both transcriptional activators and transcriptional repressors and acting in a variety of ways. In the presence of blue light, AtMYC2 acts as a transcriptional activator that regulates the formation of lateral roots [64]. The SPATULA gene acts as a transcriptional suppressor regulating seed germination [65]. Overexpression of AtMYB52 enhances sensitivity to ABA and regulates drought [66]. In contrast, there are no relevant reports on the bHLH transcription factor family in (B) vulgaris.

In this study, we selected 12 BvbHLH genes from 21 subgroups (subgroup 6 contained no BvbHLH genes) for spatiotemporal expression analysis under tissue-specific and abiotic stress conditions. The results revealed that the expression levels of the 12 BvbHLHs were somewhat different in the roots, stems, and leaves at maturity and that the expression levels of all BvbHLH genes were higher in the roots. The relative expression of the same gene in different tissues varies widely. For example, BvbHLH68 of subfamily 5 has the highest and significantly higher expression in roots and stems, respectively, than in other genes, whereas the opposite is true in leaves. An analysis based on the gene correlation heatmap revealed a bias toward negative regulation among the genes of BvbHLHs and a highly significant negative correlation between BvbHLH68 and BvbHLH93 (subfamily 18), whose relative expressions were significantly altered in different tissues. This also suggests that BvbHLHs act together (antagonistically or synergistically) to exert their functions during the growth and developmental stages of B. vulgaris. It was further hypothesized that there may be antagonism between BvbHLH68 and BvbHLH93 to regulate the development of B. vulgaris roots, stems, and leaves; however, the specific mechanism of action still needs to be verified by in-depth experiments.

To further investigate the adaptive mechanism of BvbHLHs under environmental stress, qRT-PCR was used to analyze the responses of the 12 BvbHLH genes to abiotic stress. The results revealed that the relative expression levels of these 12 genes were similar under different time and abiotic stress treatments and that different BvbHLHs exhibited similar expression patterns. Specifically, under heat, PEG and HCl treatments, the relative expression levels of BvbHLH53 were basically unchanged, whereas the relative expression levels of the other 11 genes in roots decreased significantly at 2 h, and were upregulated to the normal range at 24 h. Simultaneously, the relative expression in leaves decreased significantly at 24 h. Under dark, NaCl, NaOH and cold treatments, except for BvbHLH53, the expression trends of the other 11 genes were similar. In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that PIF3 (AtbHLH8) not only plays a regulatory role in the positive and negative reactions of seedling desiccation and signal transduction but also acts as a negative regulatory factor in response to cold stress [67, 68]. BvbHLH20 is homologous to AtbHLH8, and its negative regulatory mechanisms are similar. CsbHLH18 (homologous to AtbHLH27) directly or indirectly regulates the expression of antioxidant enzyme genes that can enhance cold tolerance [69]. BvbHLH25 is homologous to AtbHLH27 and can be used as a negative regulator of cold stress response. Similar regulatory mechanisms exist in the corn bHLH family [18]. The above results indicate that the expression patterns of the BvbHLH gene family in different tissues and abiotic stress treatments were tissue-specific and diverse, suggesting that members of the BvbHLH gene family play key biological roles as transcriptional activators and repressors that mediate different biological functions of tissue development and defense.

Conclusion

In this study, we performed a systematic analysis of the BvbHLH transcription factor family at the genome-wide level. A total of 96 BvbHLH genes with HLH structural domains were identified, which were categorized into 21 subgroups and one UC group, with the same subgroup having higher conservation of gene structure and motif. Motifs 1 and 2 were widely distributed among the 96 BvbHLHs. The 96 BvbHLH genes were unevenly distributed across nine chromosomes, and four tandem repeat events and six pairs of segmental repeat events were identified. Synteny analysis revealed that the BvbHLH family was closely related to the dicotyledonous bHLH family, with B. vulgaris having the highest number (85) of homologous pairs with S. lycopersicum and S. tuberosum. The qRT-PCR results showed that the 12 selected BvbHLH genes were differentially expressed in the tissues of B. vulgaris at the mature stage and were subjected to eight abiotic stresses. BvbHLH93, BvbHLH85, BvbHLH11, BvbHLH68, BvbHLH13 and BvbHLH25 have exerted important effects on the growth and development of beetroots, stems and leaves. The analysis coefficients of positive or negative correlations between genes were high, and each gene responded to stress to different degrees. These results suggest that these genes may improve the adaptability of plants to stress by regulating growth and developmental processes such as root development and leaf morphology. Among these, BvbHLH20 and BvbHLH25 adapt to cold stress as negative regulatory factors; however, the specific mechanism remains to be studied. Cold tolerance is a very important stress resistance factor in the breeding program of sugar beet.

Taken together, these results suggest that certain functions of BvbHLHs are related to intergene network mechanisms. These data provide a foundation for future bioinformatics and functional studies investigating B. vulgaris and enhance our understanding of BvbHLH genes at the structural, functional, and evolutionary levels.

Materials and methods

Identification of the bHLH genes of Beta vulgaris

The whole genome data of B. vulgaris in this study were downloaded from https://plants.ensembl.org/info/data/ftp/index.html. Based on two BLASTp methods [70, 71], bHLH family members were identified. Firstly, BLASTp (score ≥ 100, e ≤ 1e10) was used to retrieve potentially identifiable bHLH proteins in all sequences of B. vulgaris. Secondly, the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) file, corresponding to the bHLH structural domain (PF00011), was downloaded from the Pfam Protein Family Database (http://pfam.sanger.ac.uk/) [72]. Thirdly, the bHLH protein sequences were compared using the HMM model in HMMER 3.0, with a cutoff value of 0.01 (http://plants.ensembl.org/hmmer/index.html) [73]. Next, the PFAM and SMART programs (http://smart.emblheidelberg.de/) were used to validate the bHLH core sequences (HLH and bHLH-MYC_N) [74, 75]. A total of 96 BvbHLH genes were identified in the B. vulgaris genome. Next, 96 BvbHLH proteins were validated via the NCBI protein database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi?PROGRAM=blastp&PAGE_TYPE=BlastSearch&LINK_LOC=blastome) using BLASTp for bHLH proteins. Finally, the basic characteristics (coding sequence length, pI, molecular weight, and subcellular localization) of the three helical proteins of the B. vulgaris bHLH gene family were identified using ExPasy (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/, http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2/).

Analysis of the bHLH gene structure

ClustalW predicted conserved amino acid sequences between 96 BvbHLH and AtbHLH proteins using default parameters to establish multiple protein sequence comparisons and to further analyze the characteristic structural domains of BvbHLH proteins [76]. The amino acid sequence of the bHLH structural domain was manually adjusted using GeneDoc software. The exon-intron structure of each BvbHLH gene was analyzed using the Gene Structure Display Server (http://GSDS.cbi.pku.edu.cn) [77] to explore structural differences. The MEME server (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme) was used to identify BvbHLH protein motifs [78, 79] using the following optimized parameters: the maximum number of motifs was 10 and the optimal width was 6–200 residues [79, 80]. The promoter sequences for each BvbHLH gene (2000 bp upstream of the start codon ATG) from the B. vulgaris genome file were used to predict the presence of cis-acting elements in the promoter sequences of the BvbHLH gene family using the PlantCare website (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [81]. After removing the members with no specific functions, a Venn diagram of the cis-acting elements was plotted using Tbtools.

Chromosome distribution and gene duplication events of the BvbHLH genes

All BvbHLH genes were localized on nine chromosomes of B. vulgaris using physical location information from Circos and the sugar beet genome database [82]. Gene duplication events were analyzed using the Multiple Collinearity Scan toolkit (MCScanX) with default parameters [83]. bHLH genes homology analysis between B. vulgaris and O. sativa, and B. distachyon, Z. mays, S. lycopersicum, and S. tuberosum were studied using the TBtools dual syntony plotter (https://github.com/CJChen/TBtools). Interspecific collinearity maps were plotted using a Dual Synteny plotter (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools). Non-synonymous (ka) and synonymous (ks) substitutions were calculated for the duplicated bHLH gene pairs using the ka/ks-calculator 2.0 [84].

Classification and phylogenetic analysis of the BvbHLH gene family

The 96 identified BvbHLH genes were grouped according to the classification of the model plant A. thaliana bHLH gene family. Phylogenetic trees with bootstrap values of 1000 were constructed using the Jukes-Cantor model in MEGA 7.0, and assigned to Genetic R11 using the BLOSUM62 cost matrix. Based on the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) and published journals, the protein sequences of three monocotyledons (B. distachyon, O. sativa, and Z. mays) and three dicotyledons (A. thaliana, S. lycopersicum, and S. tuberosum) were obtained and combined with 96 BvbHLH sequences for phylogenetic analysis.

Plant material, growth conditions, and abiotic stresses

The B. vulgaris variety (2n = 18) MA097 (Denmark Mairuibo International Seed Industry Co., Ltd. Harbin Representative Office, Harbin, China) was selected for this experiment and planted on April 9, 2022, at the Guizhou University Experimental Farm. Fully seeded, uniformly sized, pest- and disease-free B. vulgaris seeds were selected, rinsed, sterilized, rinsed again, and then sown on washed and sterilized Petri dishes (90 mm diameter) covered with two layers of sterile, washed filter paper (90 mm diameter). They were placed in a constant-temperature incubator at 16 h/25 ± 1 °C during the day and 8 h/20 ± 1 °C at night with 75% relative humidity, and incubated until germination (7 d). After 7 d, B. vulgaris seedlings with uniform growth potential were transplanted into pots and pans with a diameter of 22.5 cm and a height of 17.5 cm. The substrate composition was autoclaved mixed nutrient soil (soil: vermiculite = 1:1), and the cultivation and management practices of the material were consistent with those practiced in the field. Three seedlings were planted per pot and placed under specific incubation conditions of 16 h/25 ± 1 °C during the day and 8 h/20 ± 1 °C at night, with 75% relative humidity. Two-leafed, one-centered B. vulgaris seedlings (21 d) were subjected to eight abiotic stresses (heat: 40 °C; cold: 4 °C; PEG6000: 35% w/v; NaCl: 150 mmol·L− 1; flooding: whole plant; darkness: complete shade; acid: HCl; 0.2 mol·L− 1; base: NaOH; 0.2 mol·L− 1) treatments. There were three biological replicates for each treatment, and roots, stems, and leaves were collected at 0, 12, and 24 h, of course, tissues were then rapidly placed in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Alternatively, when B. vulgaris grows to maturity, its roots, stems, and leaves are extracted and quickly stored at −80 °C for later use.

Total RNA extraction, cDNA reverse transcription, and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

B. vulgaris samples (0.1 g) were ground completely in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted from each sample using a plant RNA extraction kit (Takara Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and treated with RNase-free DNase I to remove trace DNA. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA in 20 µL reaction system using Hiscript II Q RT Superfix for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) kit (Vazyme Biotech Co, Ltd., Nanjing, China) and stored at −20 °C. The cDNA was used for qPCR reaction analysis. Primer Premier 5.0 software (Premier Inc., Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada) was used to design the qPCR-specific primers (Additional file 10: Table S10), and BvACTIN was used as an internal reference gene [85]. qRT-PCR was performed using the CFX96 real-time detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Reaction system: template cDNA 1.0 µL, 2×SYBR mixture 10.0 µL, corresponding primer 0.4 µL, and the system was supplemented to 20 uL with ultrapure H2O (Takara Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd.). Reaction program: 30 s pre-denaturation (95 °C), 5 s denaturation (95 °C), 20 s annealing (60 °C), 20 s extension (72 °C), cycling 40 times, and performing three technical replicates. Fluorescence signal accumulation was used to monitor the entire PCR process in real-time, and a real-time amplification curve was established. The 2−ΔΔCt formula was applied to calculate the relative expression of the target gene and the internal reference gene [86].

Data analysis

All experiments were performed with three replications. The experimental data were entered and statistically analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2010. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) (P < 0.05) and multiple comparisons (Duncan) were performed using IBM SPSS 26.0 software, and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). We used Origin 8.0 software to plot the bar graphs.
