miR-629-3p affects postmenopausal osteoporosis through targeted regulation of BACH1
Authors: Dong Yun, Jinxiang Zhang, Siyuan Ha, Jing Xiao, Caifeng Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoporosis (OP) represents a major global health challenge, particularly among postmenopausal women. Currently, there remains a shortage of reliable diagnostic biomarkers and targeted therapies for OP.MethodsA cohort of 254 postmenopausal women (127 OP patients and 127 healthy controls) was analyzed. Serum miR-629-3p levels were measured via qRT-PCR, and diagnostic potential was assessed using ROC analysis. In vitro, MC3T3-E1 osteoblasts were transfected with miR-629-3p mimic/inhibitor and BACH1 overexpression vector (oe-BACH1). Proliferation and apoptosis were evaluated by functional assays (CCK-8, Annexin V-FITC/PI staining), while osteogenic markers (FN1, ITGB1 and RUNX2) were assessed by qPCR. The binding of miR-629-3p to the BACH1 3’UTR was confirmed by luciferase reporter assays.ResultsOur findings revealed significantly decreased miR-629-3p levels in OP patients (P < 0.001), negatively correlating with BACH1 (r = -0.696, P < 0.001) and showing diagnostic potential (AUC = 0.929). Mechanistically, miR-629-3p mimic suppressed BACH1 (P < 0.001), enhancing proliferation, reducing apoptosis, and upregulating RUNX2 (all P < 0.001), while BACH1 overexpression reversed these effects. Luciferase assays confirmed miR-629-3p directly targets BACH1 3’UTR (P < 0.001).ConclusionmiR-629-3p attenuates OP progression by inhibiting BACH1, thereby promoting osteoblast survival, proliferation, and differentiation. Its diagnostic potential and therapeutic role in restoring osteogenic function highlight miR-629-3p/BACH1 as a promising axis for OP management. However, the findings of this study require further validation in animal models to fully establish causality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06413-8.

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a common metabolic bone disease [1] characterized by reduced bone mass and an increased risk of fragility fractures [2], and has become a major global public health concern. Postmenopausal women are at the highest risk of developing OP [3, 4], and biomarkers hold potential value for treatment monitoring [5–7]. Although drugs such as alendronate [8] and denosumab [9] can effectively increase bone mineral density (BMD) and reduce fracture risk, the lack of reliable early diagnostic biomarkers remains a major challenge. Recent studies have shown that non-coding RNAs (including microRNAs and small interfering RNAs) play key roles in bone metabolism and related disorders [10, 11]. For example, microRNAs are significant in the pathophysiology, diagnosis, and treatment of osteoarthritis [12]; siRNAs, on the other hand, are used to explore molecular targets for rheumatoid arthritis and OP, as well as to evaluate the efficacy of specific drugs [13, 14]. In the regulation of osteogenesis, miR-140 is involved in bone formation [15], miR-1303 promotes osteogenesis by targeting COCH expression [16], and miR-106a-5p facilitates osteogenic differentiation and bone defect repair [17].The function of miR-629-3p is more complex: in calcific aortic valve disease, it inhibits osteogenic differentiation by targeting TAGLN, exerting an anti-calcification effect [18]; whereas in severe asthma, its upregulation is associated with neutrophil infiltration, demonstrating a pro-inflammatory effect [19]. However, the specific mechanism of this molecule in OP remains unclear.

BACH1, a transcriptional repressor of the bZIP family, is widely involved in cellular metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress regulation [20, 21]. In bone metabolism, it suppresses the expression of antioxidant enzymes (e.g., HO-1, SOD) by inhibiting Nrf2, leading to ROS accumulation: in osteoblasts, this impedes bone formation, while in osteoclasts, it promotes bone resorption [22, 23]. Inhibiting BACH1 restores Nrf2 activity, which bidirectionally regulates bone homeostasis and presents a novel therapeutic strategy for oxidative stress-related osteoporosis, such as postmenopausal or senile osteoporosis.

We prioritized miR-629-3p for investigation based on a combination of prior clinical evidence and in silico predictions. Specifically, a study by Dong et al. [24], reported that miR-629-3p was significantly downregulated in OP patients, while its predicted target, BACH1, was upregulated. Bioinformatics analyses further suggested that miR-629-3p could directly target the 3’-UTR of BACH1. However, the functional relevance of this interaction and its role in OP pathogenesis remained experimentally unvalidated. This strong preliminary evidence positioned miR-629-3p as a highly compelling candidate for further mechanistic exploration in our study. This study investigates whether miR-629-3p affects OP progression by targeting BACH1 to regulate oxidative stress and osteoblast-osteoclast balance, with the goal of identifying new treatment strategies. Using clinical data from 254 postmenopausal women (127 with OP, 127 controls) and experiments in MC3T3-E1 osteoblasts, we examine the expression and role of miR-629-3p, its influence on osteoblast function, and its targeting of BACH1, to clarify the miR-629-3p/BACH1 pathway in OP and propose a novel therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This investigation was performed in compliance with Helsinki Declaration ethical requirements, with prior approval obtained from Wuhan No.9 Hospital Ethics Review Board (IRB approval number: 20210112). From 2022 to 2024, we enrolled 254 postmenopausal women, including 127 OP patients and 127 healthy controls, all of whom provided written informed consent. Inclusion criteria for the control group: [1] Postmenopausal for ≥ 2 years; [2] Bone mineral density (BMD) T-score ≥ − 1.0; [3] Absence of fractures or medications affecting bone metabolism. Additional criteria for OP group: [1] Diagnosis according to the “Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary OP” (2022) (T-score ≤ -2.5 or presence of fragility fractures); [2] Exclusion of secondary OP (hyperthyroidism, glucocorticoid use, etc.); [3] No malignancies or metabolic bone diseases.

Patient information and serum sample collection

During clinical consultations, we prospectively collected comprehensive demographic and clinical data, including age, BMI (calculated and classified per WHO criteria), smoking history (pack-years), alcohol consumption frequency, diabetes history, and hypertension history (defined as BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg or antihypertensive use). Fasting blood draws (5 mL) were performed aseptically into serum separator tubes. After 30-minute room-temperature clotting, samples were processed by refrigerated centrifugation (3000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The resulting serum was aliquoted into 500 µL cryovials and immediately stored at -80 °C in monitored ultra-low temperature freezers, with sample integrity verified through periodic quality control checks (hemolysis index < 5).

Cell culture

The study employed MC3T3-E1 murine pre-osteoblastic cells (ATCC, USA) as an in vitro model system for examining osteoblast differentiation. Cell cultures were grown in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (56 °C, 30 min) and antibiotic-antimycotic solution (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin; Sigma-Aldrich). Maintenance conditions included a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ humidified incubator, with biweekly medium changes.

For subculturing, cells at 80–90% confluence were detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco, USA) for 2–3 min at 37 °C, followed by neutralization with complete medium. Cell counts and viability assessments were performed using trypan blue exclusion and a hemocytometer. All experiments were conducted using cells between passages 3–8 in the logarithmic growth phase to ensure phenotypic stability. Prior to osteogenic induction, cells were seeded at optimized densities (determined by preliminary proliferation assays) and allowed to attach for 24 h in complete medium.

Cell transfection

For mimic/inhibitor transfection: Cells were divided into 5 groups: [1] Control group; [2] mimic-NC group; [3] miR-629-3p mimic group; [4] inhibitor NC group; [5] miR-629-3p inhibitor group. Cell transfection was carried out with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfection success was assessed via quantitative PCR analysis 48 h post-transfection. For co-transfection experiments: In addition to miR-629-3p mimic group, cells were co-transfected with BACH1 overexpression vector (oe-BACH1), with empty vector (oe-NC) as control.

qRT-PCR

RNA was isolated with TRIzol™ (Thermo Fisher, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After verifying RNA quality by electrophoresis and spectrophotometry (A260/A280 = 1.8-2.0), reverse transcription was performed using PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, Japan). qPCR analysis using SYBR Green chemistry (Takara, Japan) was normalized to U6 (miRNA) and GAPDH (mRNA), with data analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

CCK-8 and Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining

MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates. For the proliferation assay, CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) was added at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection, and the OD450 was measured after 2 h of incubation. For the apoptosis assay, at 48 h post-transfection, cells were harvested by trypsinization using trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (Solarbio, China). Cell pellets were resuspended in binding buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and adjusted to an appropriate concentration. The cell suspension was stained with Annexin V-FITC (BD Biosciences, USA) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by the addition of propidium iodide (BD Biosciences, USA). Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a BD FACSCanto II system (BD Biosciences, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, USA).

Bioinformatics prediction

To identify miRNAs that potentially target BACH1, we performed a bioinformatic prediction analysis. The 3’UTR sequence of the BACH1 gene was retrieved from the NCBI database. Subsequently, potential miRNA binding sites were predicted using the Target Scan Human algorithm (release 8.0; http://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/) under default parameter settings.

Luciferase reporter assay

Based on the potential binding sites between miR-629-3p and the BACH1 3’UTR predicted by Target Scan Human, we further performed experimental validation. pGL3 vectors (Promega, USA) containing either wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) BACH1 3’UTR were constructed and co-transfected with mimic NC, miR-629-3p mimic, inhibitor NC or miR-629-3p inhibitor. Luciferase activity was measured after 48 h using Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (TSINGKE, China).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD (Student’s t-test), while categorical variables were analyzed by χ² test. Multivariable logistic regression yielded adjusted ORs (95% CI). For experimental data, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test was used for multiple comparisons, and ROC analysis assessed diagnostic performance. All tests were two-tailed (P < 0.05).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of OP patients and controls

This study included 254 participants, comprising 127 OP patients and 127 healthy controls (Table 1). Significant age differences were observed between groups (OP group: 65.25 ± 4.99 years vs. control group: 64.20 ± 5.60 years, P = 0.124). No significant BMI differences were detected (23.70 ± 3.15 vs. 24.33 ± 3.14 kg/m², P = 0.110). Lifestyle factors including smoking (35/92 vs. 29/98, P = 0.386) and alcohol consumption (38/89 vs. 30/97, P = 0.114) showed comparable distributions. Diabetes prevalence was numerically higher in OP group (38/89 vs. 30/97) without statistical significance (P = 0.257). Hypertension prevalence was higher in controls (37/90 vs. 33/94, P = 0.574). Laboratory tests revealed significantly lower 25(OH)D levels (15.17 ± 3.42 vs. 16.63 ± 4.99 ng/mL, P = 0.007) and higher ALP levels (68.91 ± 6.82 vs. 65.57 ± 7.90 U/L, P < 0.001) in OP group. Serum calcium (2.37 ± 0.82 vs. 2.52 ± 0.70 mmol/L, P = 0.105) and phosphorus (1.02 ± 0.19 vs. 1.04 ± 0.23 mmol/L, P = 0.259) showed no significant differences. The lumbar spine BMD value in the OP group was 0.65 ± 0.02 g/cm², significantly lower than that in the control group (0.97 ± 0.03 g/cm², P < 0.001). Correspondingly, the lumbar spine T-score in the OP group was − 3.29 ± 0.15, also significantly lower than that in the control group (-0.7 ± 0.24, P < 0.001).

Diagnostic potential of miR-629-3p for OP

Comparative analysis revealed significantly decreased miR-629-3p expression in OP patients versus controls (Fig. 1A). ROC curve analysis demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.929) with 93.7% sensitivity and 81.1% specificity (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between miR-629-3p expression levels and bone mineral density (r = 0.7427, P < 0.001; Fig. 1C).

Multivariate logistic regression identified miR-629-3p (OR = 0.013, 95%CI: 0.005–0.033, P < 0.001) and 25(OH)D levels (OR = 0.440, 95%CI: 0.197–0.984, P = 0.046) as significant protective factors against OP, while ALP was identified as a risk factor for OP (OR = 2.347, 95%CI: 1.053–5.232, P = 0.037). Age, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, serum calcium and phosphorus showed no significant associations (P > 0.05, Table 2).

miR-629-3p regulates osteoblast function through Proliferation, apoptosis and osteogenic gene expression

Transfection with miR-629-3p mimic or inhibitor effectively modulated expression levels in MC3T3-E1 cells (Fig. 2A, P < 0.01). CCK-8 assays showed miR-629-3p mimic enhanced proliferation (P < 0.001), while inhibitor suppressed it (P < 0.001, Fig. 2B). Flow cytometry revealed that mimic reduced apoptosis (P < 0.001) and inhibitor increased it (P < 0.001, Fig. 2C).

Analysis of osteogenic markers showed that the miR-629-3p mimic significantly up-regulated the expression of FN1 (P < 0.01; Fig. 2D), ITGB1 (P < 0.001; Fig. 2E), and RUNX2 (P < 0.001; Fig. 2F). Conversely, the opposite effects were observed in the inhibitor-treated group. These results indicate that miR-629-3p plays a functional role in modulating osteoblast activity.

miR-629-3p directly targets BACH1

Bioinformatics analysis predicted the complementary binding sites between miR-629-3p and the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of BACH1 (Fig. 3A). Dual-luciferase reporter gene assays were used to verify the direct interaction between miR-629-3p and BACH1 3’UTR. Compared with the negative control, miR-629-3p mimic significantly reduced the luciferase activity of the WT BACH1 3’UTR construct by 62.4% (P < 0.001), while having no significant effect on the MUT constructs with disrupted seed sequences at the predicted binding sites (Fig. 3B). qRT-PCR was used to analyze the serum BACH1 levels of OP patients and healthy controls, and it was found that the expression of BACH1 mRNA in OP samples was significantly up - regulated. The relative expression level of BACH1 in OP patients was significantly higher than that in the healthy control group (P < 0.001, Fig. 3C). Correlation analysis of clinical samples showed a strong negative correlation between the expression levels of miR-629-3p and BACH1 (r = -0.696, P < 0.001, Fig. 3D). These findings collectively indicate that miR-629-3p directly targets BACH1 and negatively regulates its expression in OP.

miR-629-3p modulates osteoblast function through BACH1 targeting

Co-transfection experiments with miR-629-3p mimic and oe-BACH1 demonstrated: [1] Successful BACH1 expression rescue (P < 0.001, Fig. 4A); [2] Partial reversal of proliferative promotion by mimic at 48 h/72h (P < 0.001, Fig. 4B); [3] Attenuation of anti-apoptotic effects (P < 0.001, Fig. 4C). Regarding osteogenic markers, oe-BACH1 counteracted mimic-induced FN1, ITGB1 and RUNX2 upregulation (P < 0.001), confirming BACH1’s mediatory role in miR-629-3p’s regulatory network (Fig. 4D and F).

Discussion

OP is a common metabolic bone disease among postmenopausal women, which not only severely affects patients’ quality of life but also significantly increases the risk of fractures [25]. Epidemiological studies indicate that approximately 30–50% of postmenopausal women develop osteopenia, and 15–20% of them progress to OP [26]. Current clinical diagnosis primarily relies on bone mineral density testing, while emerging indicators such as waist-to-height ratio (WTHR), conicity index (CI), lipid accumulation product (LAP), and triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index are gaining attention. However, the precise correlation between these indicators and the pathogenesis of OP still requires further validation [27]. In addition, simple clinical predictors such as body weight have been shown to be effective for preliminary OP screening in women, with a cut-off value of < 57.4 kg identifying high-risk individuals who may require further diagnostic confirmation [28]. Beyond clinical markers, genetic factors also contribute to OP risk; for instance, polymorphisms in the GLP-1R gene (e.g., rs3765468-A, rs3765467-A) are significantly associated with susceptibility to postmenopausal OP in the Chinese Han population [29].

In terms of treatment, although menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) effectively increases bone mass and reduces fracture risk, its long-term use has been associated with an elevated risk of breast cancer and cardiovascular events, thereby limiting its broader clinical applicability [30, 31]. For patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs), surgical interventions such as percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) are commonly used. However, risk factors for short-term residual back pain (SRBP) after PKP include lower bone density, greater preoperative kyphosis, thoracolumbar fascia injury, and suboptimal cement distribution [32]. Moreover, the efficacy of vertebroplasty for painful OVCFs with edema is currently being evaluated in a double-blind randomized sham-controlled trial, which may provide high-level evidence to guide future clinical practice [33].

In this study, miR-629-3p showed significant downregulation in OP patients, consistent with previous reports of its reduced expression in OP [24]. Notably, miR-629-3p demonstrated an AUC of 0.903 for OP diagnosis in our research, outperforming conventional biomarkers β-CTX (AUC = 0.876) [34] and SVM (AUC = 0.827) [35]. Multivariate analysis revealed that miR-629-3p is an independent predictor of OP, and its expression level showed a positive correlation with BMD, suggesting its potential as a novel diagnostic biomarker for postmenopausal OP.

BACH1, a CNC-bZIP family transcription factor, contains both BTB and CNC homology domains [20]. Unlike Nrf2 (a transcriptional activator of HO-1/NQO1), BACH1 competitively inhibits their expression, functioning as a negative regulator of the antioxidant response [36]. As a key regulator of ROS generation, BACH1 exhibits dual regulatory roles in OP: it promotes osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption by suppressing the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant pathway [37, 38], while concurrently inhibiting osteoblast activity and differentiation capacity to impair bone formation [23]. Studies demonstrate that BACH1 silencing alleviates dexamethasone-induced suppression of osteogenic differentiation through ATG7-mediated autophagy activation [39], and BACH1 inhibitors can induce Nrf2-regulated antioxidant enzymes to reduce intracellular ROS levels, thereby suppressing RANKL-mediated osteoclastogenesis [22]. Our study revealed significantly elevated BACH1 expression in OP patients, consistent with Dong’s report. Mechanistic investigations confirmed BACH1 as a direct target of miR-629-3p through dual-luciferase reporter assays. Cellular experiments further demonstrated that miR-629-3p mimic significantly downregulated BACH1 expression, whereas its inhibitor upregulated BACH1 levels. The observed negative correlation between reduced miR-629-3p and elevated BACH1 in OP patients confirms miR-629-3p-mediated regulation of BACH1 expression through targeting its mRNA 3’UTR.

From a pathogenic perspective, impaired osteogenic activity contributes to OP development, primarily through dysregulation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs): diminished proliferation capacity, reduced osteogenic differentiation potential, and disrupted osteogenic-adipogenic balance [40–43]. In fundamental research, investigations into osteogenic differentiation predominantly focus on cellular behaviors (e.g., proliferation and apoptosis) and the regulatory effects of key genes such as FN1, ITGB1, and RUNX2 on osteoblast function. Recent studies reveal that miRNAs play pivotal roles in osteogenic metabolism regulation. For instance, miR-142a-5p promotes osteoblast differentiation by targeting nuclear factor IA [44]; miR-217 not only enhances osteoblast differentiation but also stimulates BMSCs proliferation [45]. Concurrently, miR-196b-5p maintains bone homeostasis through bidirectional regulation of osteoblast and osteoclast differentiation [46].

In this study, we identified miR-629-3p as a key regulator in this process. Our findings demonstrate that it serves as a potential molecular biomarker for OP, a notion supported by its significant downregulation in clinical OP cohorts. Mechanistically, we elucidated that miR-629-3p modulates osteoblast function through direct targeting of BACH1. This regulatory axis was evidenced by the fact that BACH1 overexpression partially reversed the proliferative promotion and anti-apoptotic effects induced by miR-629-3p mimic, and markedly counteracted its regulation of key osteogenic markers (FN1, ITGB1 and RUNX2). In conclusion, our study reveals a novel epigenetic mechanism by which the miR-629-3p-BACH1 axis controls bone formation, providing not only direct evidence for the miRNA-BACH1 axis in regulating osteoblast biological behaviors but also advancing the theoretical framework for miRNA-based therapeutic development in OP.

Despite these findings, our study has limitations. Firstly, the results are from a single-center cohort. Although miR-629-3p shows high diagnostic potential (AUC = 0.929), the absence of external validation—using multi-center cohorts or established OP animal models—limits the generalizability and clinical applicability of our conclusions. Secondly, despite adjusting for key confounders in multivariate analysis, unmeasured or residual confounding may still exist, as is inherent in observational studies. Thirdly, while we demonstrated that miR-629-3p regulates osteoblast function, we did not investigate its effects on osteoclasts. Given that bone remodeling involves both formation and resorption, miR-629-3p may play a dual role, possibly via pathways such as RANKL/RANK/OPG, which should be explored in future research.

Therefore, future studies should focus on: [1] validating miR-629-3p’s diagnostic value in larger, multi-center, and diverse populations; [2] investigating the miR-629-3p/BACH1 axis in osteoporotic animal models to evaluate its therapeutic potential; and [3] clarifying the mechanism of miR-629-3p in both osteoblast and osteoclast activity.

This study has several limitations. It was a single-center study with a limited sample size and lacked external validation, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Although key variables were adjusted for, residual confounding factors cannot be excluded. The downstream signaling of BACH1 in osteogenesis remains unclear, and the cellular findings require further validation in established animal models. The role of miR-629-3p in osteoclastogenesis also warrants deeper investigation.
