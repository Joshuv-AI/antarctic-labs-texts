The Most Dispersed Subset of Random Points in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}
Fabio Deelan Cunden, Noemi Cuppone, Giovanni Gramegna, Pierpaolo Vivo

Abstract
Consider a population of N N individuals, each having d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 different traits, and an additive measure, called dispersion , which rewards large pairwise separations between traits. The goal is to select M ≤ N M\leq N individuals such that their traits are as dispersed as possible. We compute analytically the full statistics (including large deviation tails) of the maximally achievable dispersion among sub-populations of size M M when the traits are independent and identically distributed. Two complementary approaches are developed, one based on a mean-field theory for order statistics, and the other on the replica method from the field of disordered systems. In all dimensions d d , and for rotationally symmetric distributions, the optimal subset for large populations consists of all points lying outside a d d -dimensional ball whose radius is determined self-consistently. For a single trait ( d = 1 d=1 ), the statistics of the maximal dispersion can be tackled for finite N , M N,M as well. The formulae we obtained are corroborated by numerical simulations on small instances and by heuristic algorithms that find near-optimal solutions.

Introduction

Selecting a subset of size
M\leq N
from a larger population of
N
individuals, which maximises a notion of “dispersion” among their traits, is a fundamental problem in numerous disciplines.

For instance, the accuracy of polls and surveys often relies on picking a sufficiently representative sample of individuals that cover a broad spectrum of political opinions, personal or health characteristics
[
31
,
30
]
. Policies are often drawn to ensure that committees, panels, juries, university cohorts, and several collective bodies are hired or nominated ensuring some diversity of viewpoints, backgrounds, and other attributes
[
20
,
18
,
2
,
9
]
. In facility location problems, one needs to distribute facilities to serve different geographical regions while minimising interference
[
7
,
10
]
.
In finance, portfolio diversification strategies aim to select assets that are minimally correlated to reduce overall risk
[
8
]
. Other contexts to which the maximum dispersion problem may be applicable include selection of diverse traits in plant and animals controlled breeding
[
32
,
15
]
and workforce management
[
29
]
. The abundance of different “traits” in a model of integer vectors was shown to correlate with the appearance of Zipf’s law
[
25
]
.

In the Operations Research field, these questions fall generally under the category of
Maximum Diversity/Dispersion Problems
(MDP) (see
[
24
]
for a recent review).
For the most common measures of dispersion and for generic number of traits, the MDP is known to be NP-hard, placing it among a prominent class of computationally challenging combinatorial optimisation problems
[
16
,
21
,
11
]
that are usually tackled using heuristic and metaheuristic algorithms that may find efficient and accurate solutions for large instances
[
19
,
17
,
23
]
.

Populations with randomly assigned traits constitute important validation benchmarks for the performance of these heuristic algorithms (see e.g. the standard benchmark library MDPLIB, which includes many instances with individuals’ traits sampled uniformly at random
[
21
]
). However, analytical results on the statistics of the maximally achievable dispersion and the “geometry” of the maximising subset for random traits are surprisingly scarce.

In this paper, we address a specific instance of MDP – closely related to the so-called
quadratic Knapsack Problem

[
13
]
– on populations having
random
traits in a
d
-dimensional space. This is based on the
M
-dispersion
measure (see Section
2
for details), which rewards large pairwise separations between traits. We tackle this problem in all dimensions
d\geq 1
for large
N,M
with their ratio fixed using two complementary approaches: one based on a mean-field theory for order statistics, and one based on the replica method of disordered systems. Moreover, for
d=1
and
N,M
finite, we show that – while exact formulae are very challenging to obtain – an approach based on finite-
N,M
approximants gives asymptotically exact results that match the numerics on small instances with striking precision. For one of the very few available theoretical studies on quadratic Knapsack Problem with random instances, see
[
33
]
.

For large
N,M
and in all dimensions
d\geq 1
, we compute the full distribution of the maximal dispersion in the sense of large deviations. From this distribution, all cumulants can be obtained, to leading order, by differentiation in the large-
N,M
limit. As a consequence, the probabilities of rare events – away from the typical Gaussian regime – in which the maximal dispersion is anomalously larger or smaller than its expected value are characterised exactly for large
N,M
. Moreover, we find that the subset that maximises the dispersion is composed of individuals in the
d
-dimensional trait space lying outside a ball, whose radius can be determined explicitly. Finally, the analytical results are in excellent agreement with numerical simulations for moderate instance sizes and with heuristic solutions for larger problems.

2
Problem setting

2.1
M
-Dispersion function

Given a set of
N
points
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a binary vector
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{N})^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, such that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}=M
, we define the
M
-dispersion as

\displaystyle D^{M}(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{N}|\sigma):

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}|^{2}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\ .

(1)

We indicate by
|\bm{x}|=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
the Euclidean norm, and the binary vector
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{N}
encodes which
M
points are selected:
\sigma_{i}=1
if
\bm{x}_{i}
is chosen, and
\sigma_{i}=0
otherwise. We define the maximal
M
-dispersion
D^{M}_{\mathrm{max}}
as

D_{\max}^{M}(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{N}):=\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N,M}}D^{M}(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{N}|\sigma)\ ,

(2)

with
\Sigma_{N,M}=\{\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{N}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}=M\}.

2.2
Statistics of maximal
M
-Dispersion and Scaled Cumulant Generating Function

Let
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N}
be i.i.d. in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with distribution
F
. Then
D^{M}_{\max}(\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{N})
is a random variable of order
N^{2}
. We consider its rescaled form

\frac{1}{N^{2}}D^{M}_{\max}\ ,

(3)

for
M=\alpha N
, and denote with
\kappa_{\ell}^{(N,M,d)}
the
\ell
-th cumulant of this rescaled variable. Generically
\kappa_{\ell}^{(N,M,d)}=O(N^{1-\ell})
, and we define the scaled limits

\kappa_{\ell}^{(d)}(\alpha):=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}M,N\to\infty\\
M/N=\alpha\end{subarray}}N^{\ell-1}\kappa_{\ell}^{(N,M,d)}\ .

(4)

The large-
N
Scaled Cumulant Generating Function (SCGF) is

\Phi_{\alpha}(p):=\lim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{E}\big[\rme^{-pD^{M}_{\max}/N}\big]\ ,

(5)

so that

(-1)^{\ell-1}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{\ell}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p^{\ell}}\Phi_{\alpha}(p)\Big|_{p=0}=\kappa_{\ell}^{(d)}(\alpha)\ .

(6)

Its Legendre-Fenchel transform gives the large-deviation rate function
\Psi_{\alpha}(x)
:

\Pr\Big(D_{\max}^{M}=N^{2}x\Big)\approx\rme^{-N\Psi_{\alpha}(x)}\ ,

(7)

where
\approx
stands for equality on a logarithmic scale in the
N\to\infty
limit. The precise relation between the rate function and the SCGF is
[
34
]

\Psi_{\alpha}(x)=\sup_{p}\left(\Phi_{\alpha}(p)-px\right),\hskip 20.00003pt\Phi_{\alpha}(p)=\inf_{x}\left(\Psi_{\alpha}(x)+px\right)\ .

(8)

We assume that the probability distribution
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has a rotationally invariant density,

f(\bm{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=r^{d-1}g(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hat{\bm{n}}}{|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|}\ ,

(9)

where
r=|\bm{x}|
, and
\hat{\bm{n}}=\bm{x}/|\bm{x}|
is a vector on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, whose surface measure is

|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hat{\bm{n}}=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)\ .

(10)

The mean of
f
is zero; this entails no loss of generality, since the dispersion is invariant under translations. In the next section, we tackle the case of a single trait
(d=1)
, with the trait values of
N
individuals being random real variables, and the goal is to compute the statistics of the maximal
M
-dispersion and the geometry of the optimising
M
-subset.

3
Single trait (
d=1
)

In this section, we first focus on the case of a single trait
d=1
, where the
N
individual values lie on the real line. The natural ordering of real numbers has an interesting consequence on the “geometry” of the subset of
M
individuals that maximises the
M
-dispersion, as detailed in the next subsection.

3.1
Geometry of the optimal subset

In
d=1
, we can characterise the geometry of the optimal
M
-subset that will give maximal dispersion. For each
M\geq 2
, there exists an optimal
\sigma^{N,M}
of the form

\sigma^{N,M}=(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k},\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{N-M},\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{M-k})\ ,

(11)

i.e. the
k
leftmost and
M-k
rightmost points, for some
1\leq k\leq M
. We call this structure
prefix-suffix
. To prove this, consider
N
points
x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{N}
on the line. Let
\{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{M}\}
be selected indices.
Write

D^{M}(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{M}})=2(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{M}})^{2}+2\!\sum_{j=2}^{M-1}\!\big[(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{j}})^{2}+(x_{i_{j}}-x_{i_{M}})^{2}\big]+D^{M}(x_{i_{2}},\dots,x_{i_{M-1}})\ .

The first term is maximised by
i_{1}=1
,
i_{M}=N
.
If there are gaps in the interior indices, shifting them outward increases the sum.
Thus optimal sets are prefix–suffix blocks.

Hence the maximiser grows recursively.
Denote by

\bar{x}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\sigma^{N,M}_{i}

(12)

the mean of the optimal
M
-set
\sigma^{N,M}
.
The
(M+1)
-optimal set is obtained by adjoining the extremal point farthest from the current mean
\bar{x}
:

\sigma^{N,M+1}=\sigma^{N,M}+e_{i^{*}},\hskip 20.00003pti^{*}=\operatorname{argmax}_{i\in\{k+1,M-k-1\}}|x_{i}-\bar{x}|\ .

(13)

Thus, in
d=1
, optimal subsets are nested: the
(M+1)
-set contains the
M
-set. For
d>1
, however, this nesting property fails: adding one point may require a global rearrangement of the selected set (see Fig.
1
).

In the following, we will first focus on the large-
N,M
limit of the problem (with
M/N=\alpha
), developing a mean-field theory for the distribution of maximal dispersion that relies on the geometry of the optimal subset. Later, we will tackle the finite
N,M
case, which requires more delicate considerations.

3.2
Mean-field approach for large-
N,M

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
be i.i.d. real random variables with common distribution function
F(x)
whose density is
f(x)
. We denote by
X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(N)}
their order statistics,
X_{(1)}\leq X_{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq X_{(N)}
. Fix the fraction of leftmost and rightmost points
p_{L},p_{R}>0
with
p_{L}+p_{R}=\alpha
, and define

\mathcal{I}_{N}(p_{L},p_{R})=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor p_{L}N\rfloor\ \}\sqcup\{N-\lceil p_{R}N\rceil+1,\ldots,N\}\ ,

(14)

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
and
\lceil\cdot\rceil
denote floor and ceiling functions, respectively.

For a symmetric kernel
h(x,y)
vanishing on the diagonal, set

S=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}_{N}(p_{L},p_{R})}h(X_{(i)},X_{(j)})\ .

(15)

In the limit
N\to\infty
, the empirical distribution of the i.i.d. random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
, converges to their common parent distribution
F
, and the rescaled sum
S/N^{2}
in (
15
) converges to its mean-field limit

\frac{1}{N^{2}}S\;\to\;\iint_{((-\infty,a)\sqcup(b,\infty))^{2}}h(x,y)f(x)f(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\ ,

(16)

provided that the integral on the r.h.s. converges. The edge points
a,b
are the quantiles of the distribution
F
corresponding to the fractions
p_{L}
and
p_{R}
; they are given by

F(a)=p_{L},\hskip 20.00003pt1-F(b)=p_{R}\ ,

(17)

or, equivalently
a=G(p_{L})
,
b=G(1-p_{R})
, with
G=F^{-1}
the functional inverse of
F
.

Specialising equation (
16
) to the maximal dispersion case corresponding to
h(x,y)=(x-y)^{2}
, we obtain the mean-field expression

\frac{1}{N^{2}}D_{\max}^{M}\;\to\;\sup_{a,b:F(b)-F(a)=1-\alpha}\;\iint\limits_{((-\infty,a)\sqcup(b,+\infty))^{2}}(x-y)^{2}f(x)f(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\ ,

(18)

which is finite for sufficiently light-tailed
f
.

The problem of maximising (
18
) over all possible choices of
a,b
, reduces to the optimisation in a single variable
p_{L}
, with
p_{R}=\alpha-p_{L}
, as

\kappa_{1}^{(1)}(\alpha)=\sup_{0\leq p_{L}\leq\alpha}\;\iint\limits_{\Omega(p_{L};\alpha)}(x-y)^{2}f(x)f(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\ ,

(19)

where

\Omega(p_{L};\alpha)=((-\infty,G(p_{L}))\sqcup(G(1-\alpha+p_{L}),+\infty))^{2}\ .

(20)

The limit in (
19
) is equal to the (scaled) average value
\kappa_{1}^{(1)}(\alpha)
defined in (
4
).

Differentiating the integral in (
19
), and using the identity
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p_{L}}G(p_{L})=\frac{1}{f(G(p_{L}))}
, yields

2\int_{-\infty}^{G(p_{L})}(x-G(p_{L}))^{2}f(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-2\int_{G(1-\alpha+p_{L})}^{+\infty}(x-G(1-\alpha+p_{L}))^{2}f(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
+2\int_{G(1-\alpha+p_{L})}^{+\infty}(x-G(p_{L}))^{2}f(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-2\int_{-\infty}^{G(p_{L})}(x-G(1-\alpha+p_{L}))^{2}f(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\ .

(21)

The symmetry
f(x)=f(-x)
implies we have
G(1-p)=-G(p)
. Therefore, the derivative vanishes at
p_{L}=\alpha/2
. In summary,

\kappa_{1}^{(1)}(\alpha)=\iint\limits_{\Omega^{\star}}(x-y)^{2}f(x)f(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\ ,

(22)

where

\Omega^{\star}=\Omega(\alpha/2;\alpha)\ .

(23)

This implies that in the mean-field limit, the optimal dispersion is realised by a
balanced
configuration where the leftmost and rightmost blocks have equal weight. By the symmetry of
f
, formula (
22
) can be further simplified, and we have

\kappa^{(1)}_{1}(\alpha)=4\alpha\int_{-G(\alpha/2)}^{+\infty}x^{2}f(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\ .

(24)

Example 1

(Uniform distribution in an interval)
.

Suppose that the points
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are uniformly distributed in the symmetric interval
[-1/2,1/2]
. In this case
F(x)=1/2+x
for
x\in[-1/2,1/2]
and
G(p)=p-\frac{1}{2}
. From (
24
) we get

\kappa_{1}^{(1)}(\alpha)=4\alpha\int_{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}^{1/2}x^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\alpha^{2}(\alpha^{2}-3\alpha+3)}{6}\ .

(25)

3.3
Back to finite
N,M

To compute the distribution of the maximal dispersion for finite
N,M
, one should evaluate

\mathrm{Pr}\left(D_{\mathrm{max}}^{M}<x\right)=\mathrm{Pr}\left(D^{M}(1)<x,\ldots,D^{M}(M-1)<x\right)\ ,

(26)

where with
(D^{M}(1),\ldots,D^{M}(M-1))
we denote the dispersions correspond to prefix-suffix configurations with
k=1,\ldots,M-1
points in the leftmost interval. This evaluation is however technically challenging in general. We therefore resort to an approximate but very accurate evaluation which is asymptotically exact for large
N,M
.

Recall that, as
N\to\infty
, the prefix-suffix optimal subset contains
balanced
fractions
p_{L}=p_{R}=\alpha/2
on the left and on the right. For the dispersion of the balanced configuration, using the notation (
14
),

D_{\mathrm{bal}}^{M}=\sum_{i,j\in{\mathcal{I}_{N}(\alpha/2,\alpha/2)}}\left(X_{(i)}-X_{(j)}\right)^{2}\ ,

(27)

the following holds

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N,M\to\infty\\
M/N=\alpha\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{1}{N^{2}}D_{\max}^{M}-\frac{1}{N^{2}}D_{\mathrm{bal}}^{M}\right|=0\ .

(28)

Hence, the rescaled dispersion of a balanced subset is
asympotically close
to the rescaled maximal dispersion. However, note that this property does not hold for the finite
N,M
case: the configuration that realises the optimal dispersion is always a prefix-suffix, but not necessarily a balanced one.

.

4.1
Typical maximal
M
-dispersion

By the very same considerations made for the
d=1
case, for general
d>1
, we expect the rescaled maximal
M
-dispersion to converge to its continuous (mean-field) analogue,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2}}D_{\mathrm{max}}^{M}=\sup_{\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\int_{\Omega}f(\bm{x})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\alpha}\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}f(\bm{x})f(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\ ,

(38)

provided that the integral on the r.h.s. converges.
Equation (
38
) is the analogue of (
19
) for
d>1
.

For
d=1
, the supremum is attained for

\Omega^{*}=(-\infty,G(\alpha/2)]\cup[G(1-\alpha/2),+\infty)\ ,

(39)

(see (
23
)), where
G=F^{-1}
is the quantile function of the parent distribution
F
. What plays the role of
\Omega^{*}
in higher dimensions?

We first rewrite the functional:

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}f(\bm{x})f(\bm{y})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle=2\alpha\int_{\Omega}|\bm{x}|^{2}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-2\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}x_{\ell}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\alpha\int_{\Omega}\Big|\bm{x}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\Omega}\bm{y}f(\bm{y})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\Big|^{2}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ .

(40)

Thus the problem reduces to:
given a density
f(\bm{x})
of unit mass, find the region
\Omega
of measure
\alpha
that maximises the moment of inertia of
f(\bm{x})\mathds{1}_{\Omega}
about its own center of mass.

Guided by the one-dimensional case, the maximiser
\Omega^{*}
is expected to be the complement of a ball centered at

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{X}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ ,

(41)

with radius
R(\alpha)>0
determined by

1-\alpha=\int_{|\bm{x}|\leq R(\alpha)}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ .

(42)

Hence,

\Omega^{*}=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\bm{x}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}]|\geq R(\alpha)\}\ .

(43)

If
f
is rotationally symmetric, then
\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}]=0
, and

\kappa_{1}^{(d)}(\alpha):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathrm{max}}^{M}]=2\alpha\int\limits_{|\bm{x}|\geq R(\alpha)}|\bm{x}|^{2}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ ,

(44)

where

\int\limits_{|\bm{x}|\geq R(\alpha)}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\alpha\ .

(45)

4.2
SCGF and Rate Function of the maximal
M
-dispersion

The analysis for large
N
, which revealed the typical value of
D_{\mathrm{max}}^{M}
, can be extended to investigate its fluctuations. One may think of the typical configuration as the most probable spatial arrangement of points: a fraction
\alpha
of them lie beyond a certain radius
R(\alpha)
, itself determined by the self-consistent relation

\int_{|\bm{x}|\geq R(\alpha)}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\alpha\ .

(51)

The corresponding limiting form of
D_{\mathrm{max}}^{M}
follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}D^{M}_{\max}\simeq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\colon|\bm{X}_{i}|,|\bm{X}_{j}|\geq R(\alpha)}|\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X}_{j}|^{2}

(52)

\displaystyle=2\frac{M}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\colon|\bm{X}_{i}|\geq R(\alpha)}\left|\bm{X}_{i}\right|^{2}-2\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\colon|\bm{X}_{i}|\geq R(\alpha)}\bm{X}_{i}\right|^{2}\to 2\alpha\int_{|\bm{x}|\geq R(\alpha)}|\bm{x}|^{2}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ ,

(53)

since by rotational symmetry, the second term vanishes.

To go beyond the typical value, consider the Laplace transform

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\rme^{-pD^{M}_{\max}/N}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\!\cdots\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\bm{x}_{1})\cdots f(\bm{x}_{N})\exp\!\left[-\frac{p}{N}D_{\mathrm{max}}^{M}\right]\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{1}\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{N}\ .

(54)

For large
N
, this integral is dominated by those configurations that realise the most likely dispersion.
At
p=0
, we recover the typical geometry described earlier, in which a fraction
\alpha
of the points lies outside the ball of radius
R(\alpha)
.

When
p\neq 0
, the picture tilts.
For
p>0
, the weight favors configurations with atypically small
D_{\mathrm{max}}^{M}
; for
p<0
, it favors those with atypically large dispersion.
We assume, by analogy with the typical case, that these atypical configurations are again radially organised, characterised by a fraction
\alpha
of points lying beyond some radius
R(\alpha,p)
, smaller or larger than
R(\alpha)
according to the sign of
p
.

In such a configuration, one expects

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j\colon|\bm{X}_{i}|,|\bm{X}_{j}|\geq R(\alpha,p)}|\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X}_{j}|^{2}\sim 2\alpha\sum_{i\colon|\bm{X}_{i}|\geq R(\alpha,p)}\left|\bm{X}_{i}\right|^{2}\ ,

(55)

so that to each
p
there corresponds an optimal radius
R(\alpha,p)
balancing the competing weights. Thus, asymptotically,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\rme^{-pD^{M}_{\max}/N}]

\displaystyle\approx{\sup_{R\geq 0}}\binom{N}{M}\left(\int_{|\bm{x}|\leq R}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\right)^{N-M}\left(\int_{|\bm{x}|\geq R}f(\bm{x})\rme^{-2\alpha p|\bm{x}|^{2}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\right)^{M}\ .

(56)

In the joint limit
N,M\to\infty
with
M/N\to\alpha
, the combinatorial term contributes the entropy

\lim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{N}\log\binom{N}{M}=\alpha\log\alpha+(1-\alpha)\log(1-\alpha)\ .

(57)

Consequently, the transform (
56
) assumes the large-deviation form

\mathbb{E}[\rme^{-pD^{M}_{\max}/N}]\approx\rme^{-N\inf_{R\geq 0}L_{\alpha}(p,R)}\ ,

(58)

with

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}(p,R)=-\alpha\log\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{|\bm{x}|\geq R}f(\bm{x})\rme^{-2\alpha p|\bm{x}|^{2}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\right)-(1-\alpha)\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{|\bm{x}|\leq R}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\right)\ .

(59)

In this way, the SCGF emerges as the minimal value:

\Phi_{\alpha}(p)=\inf_{R\geq 0}L_{\alpha}(p,R)=L_{\alpha}(p,R(\alpha,p))\ ,

(60)

where the optimal radius
R(\alpha,p)
satisfies the stationarity condition

\frac{\partial}{\partial R}L_{\alpha}(p,R)\Big|_{R=R(\alpha,p)}=0\ ,

(61)

which reads explicitly as

(1-\alpha)\frac{1}{\int_{|\bm{x}|\leq R(\alpha,p)}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}-\alpha\frac{\rme^{-2\alpha pR(\alpha,p)^{2}}}{\int_{|\bm{x}|\geq R(\alpha,p)}f(\bm{x})\rme^{-2\alpha p|\bm{x}|^{2}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}=0\ .

(62)

At
p=0
, one recognises the equilibrium condition

\int_{|\bm{x}|\geq R(\alpha,0)}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\alpha\ ,

(63)

so that
R(\alpha,0)=R(\alpha)
.
The structure is clear:
the Laplace principle selects a critical radius
R(\alpha,p)
balancing two complementary masses of
f
, the outer one being
weighted by the exponential tilt.
At
p=0
, this balance collapses to the simple geometric
\alpha
-quantile condition
R(\alpha,0)=R(\alpha)
defining the typical state.

With (
62
), the SCGF takes the compact form

\Phi_{\alpha}(p)=\log(1-\alpha)+2\alpha^{2}pR(\alpha,p)^{2}-\log\left(\int_{|\bm{x}|\leq R(\alpha,p)}f(\bm{x})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\right)\ .

(64)

Derivatives of
\Phi_{\alpha}(p)
at
p=0
give the leading cumulants (see Eq. (
6
)).
Since
\partial_{R}L_{\alpha}=0
along the optimal path, we have

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}^{(d)}(\alpha)=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p}\Phi_{\alpha}(0)=\frac{\partial L_{\alpha}}{\partial p}+\underbrace{\frac{\partial L_{\alpha}}{\partial R}}_{=0}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}R}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p}=\frac{\partial L_{\alpha}}{\partial p}\biggl|_{(0,R(\alpha,0))}\ ,

(65)

while, to leading order, the variance reads

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}^{(d)}(\alpha)=-\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p^{2}}\Phi_{\alpha}(0)=-\frac{\partial^{2}L_{\alpha}}{\partial p^{2}}\biggl|_{(0,R(\alpha,0))}+\frac{\left(\frac{\partial^{2}L_{\alpha}}{\partial R\partial p}\right)^{2}}{\frac{\partial^{2}L_{\alpha}}{\partial R^{2}}}\biggl|_{(0,R(\alpha,0))}\ .

(66)

-dispersion and its cumulants in the limit of large
N
, we adopt the replica approach from the theory of disordered systems
[
27
]
.

Let
\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N}
be independent points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with common density
f(\bm{x})
.
Define the auxiliary partition function

\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N}):=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{N,M}}\exp\!\left[\frac{\beta}{N}D^{M}(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N}|\sigma)\right]\ ,

(88)

where the factor
1/N
in the exponent ensures extensivity of the effective “internal energy”, and
D^{M}(\cdot|\sigma)
is defined in Eq. (
1
).

Eq. (
88
) is the canonical partition function of a disordered model of
N
“constituents”, comprising
M
spins (
\sigma_{i}=+1
) and
N-M
vacancies (
\sigma_{i}=0
), where the interaction matrix between spins
J_{ij}=|\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X}_{j}|^{2}
is of the Euclidean type
[
28
]
. This spin system bears some similarities with the Euclidean matching problems
[
3
,
4
]
, the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick spin glass
[
27
]
, and the random link travelling salesman problem
[
26
]
. However – to our knowledge – the partition function (
88
) has not been studied previously in this precise form.

At zero temperature
(\beta\to\infty)
, the sum is dominated by configurations that maximise the dispersion,

\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}\approx\exp\!\left[\frac{\beta}{N}D^{M}_{\max}(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N})\right]\ ,

(89)

so that

D^{M}_{\max}(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N})=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{N}{\beta}\log\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}\ .

(90)

Averaging over the disorder yields the first cumulant

\kappa_{1}^{(N,M,d)}=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{N}{\beta}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\log\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}\big]\ .

(91)

The replica identity

\mathbb{E}[\log\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}]=\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\big[\left(\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}\right)^{n}\big]\ ,

(92)

where
n
is first promoted to an integer, allows us to evaluate the average over the disorder first. The resulting expression for the scaled first cumulant in the large
N,M
limit with
M/N=\alpha
fixed reads

\kappa_{1}^{(d)}(\alpha)=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\lim_{n\to 0}\frac{1}{n}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}\big)^{n}\right]\ ,

(93)

where
the limit
N\to\infty
has to be taken first. Although the formula (
93
) for the first cumulant can be computed explicitly in all dimensions, it is convenient to follow a different route and determine directly the SCGF
\Phi_{\alpha}(p)
, which encodes all cumulants by differentiation (see Eq. (
6
)).

We follow here the recipe proposed in
[
12
]
.
Evaluating the average of the replicated partition function this time in the double-scaling limit
n\to 0,\beta\to\infty
with
n\beta=-p=O(1)
for large
\beta
, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}\big)^{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left[-p\,\frac{1}{\beta}\log\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}\right]\right]\approx\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left[-p\,\frac{D^{M}_{\max}(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N})}{N}\right]\right],

(94)

so that the scaled cumulant generating function can be written as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\alpha}(p)=\lim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{E}\!\left[\rme^{-p\,D^{M}_{\max}/N}\right]=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{E}\!\left[\big(\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}\big)^{-p/\beta}\right].

(95)

The task is then to evaluate the large-
N
asymptotics of the disorder-averaged replicated partition function, which we tackle in the next subsection.

5.1
Averaging the replicated partition function over the disorder

The expected value of replicated partition function reads explicitly for
n
integer

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N})\right)^{n}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{i}~f(\bm{x}_{i})\sum_{\{\sigma_{i}^{a}\}}\prod_{a=1}^{n}\delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{a},M\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\times\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{i,j}^{N}|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}|^{2}\sigma_{i}^{a}\sigma_{j}^{a}\right)\

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{\sigma_{i}^{a}\}}\dfrac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\prod_{a=1}^{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau^{a}\rme^{\mathrm{i}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\tau^{a}(N\alpha-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{a})}~\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}|\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X}_{j}|^{2}\sigma_{i}^{a}\sigma_{j}^{a}\right)\ \right]\ ,

(96)

where we used the Fourier representation for the Kronecker
\delta
-functions
\delta(x,y)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau/(2\pi))\exp(\mathrm{i}\tau(x-y))
, and we used the notation

\sum_{\{\sigma_{i}^{a}\}}:=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\sigma^{k}_{i}=0,1}=\sum_{\sigma_{1}^{1}=0,1}\cdots\sum_{\sigma_{N}^{1}=0,1}\sum_{\sigma_{1}^{2}=0,1}\cdots\sum_{\sigma_{N}^{2}=0,1}\cdots\sum_{\sigma_{N}^{n}=0,1}\ .

(97)

Write the
d
-dimensional vectors in components as
\bm{x}_{i}=(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,d})
,
i=1,\ldots,N
. We now define
n
mean-field densities, depending on vectors
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for all
a=1,\dots,n
, as

\rho^{a}(\bm{x}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i})\sigma_{i}^{a}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\delta(x_{\ell}-x_{i,\ell})\sigma_{i}^{a}\ ,

(98)

with normalisation

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho^{a}(\bm{x})=\alpha\ .

(99)

From the definition,

\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}|^{2}\sigma_{i}^{a}\sigma_{j}^{a}=N^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}~\rho^{a}(\bm{x})\rho^{a}(\bm{y})|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}\ .

(100)

By enforcing the definition (
98
) with functional deltas as

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}[\rho^{a}]\delta\left[N\rho^{a}(\bm{x})-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\delta(x_{\ell}-x_{i,\ell})\sigma_{i}^{a}\right]

\displaystyle=\iint\mathcal{D}[\rho^{a}]\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}^{a}]\exp\left[\mathrm{i}N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho^{a}(\bm{x})\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})\sum_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\delta(x_{\ell}-x_{i,\ell})\sigma_{i}^{a}\right]

\displaystyle=\iint\mathcal{D}[\rho^{a}]\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}^{a}]\exp\left[\mathrm{i}N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho^{a}(\bm{x})\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x}_{i})\sigma_{i}^{a}\right]\ ,

(101)

the expected value (
5.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N})\right)^{n}\right]\propto\int\{\mathcal{D}[\rho^{a}]\}\{\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}^{a}]\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\vec{\tau}~\rme^{\mathrm{i}\alpha N\sum_{a}\tau_{a}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\mathrm{i}N\sum_{a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho^{a}(\bm{x})\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})+\beta N\sum_{a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}~\rho^{a}(\bm{x})\rho^{a}(\bm{y})|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\{\sigma_{i}^{a}\}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{X}_{i})\sigma_{i}^{a}-\mathrm{i}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\tau^{a}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{a}\right)\right]\ ,

(102)

where we omitted proportionality constants, and denoted
\{\mathcal{D}[\rho^{a}]\}:=\prod_{a=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}[\rho^{a}]
,
\{\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}^{a}]\}:=\prod_{a=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}^{a}]
, and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\vec{\tau}:=\prod_{a=1}^{n}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau^{a}
.

Now

\displaystyle\sum_{\{\sigma_{i}^{a}\}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{X}_{i})\sigma_{i}^{a}-\mathrm{i}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\tau^{a}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{a}\right)\right]=

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{i}f(\bm{x}_{i})\prod_{a=1}^{n}\sum_{\sigma_{i}^{a}=0,1}(\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{i}^{a}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x}_{i})-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{i}^{a}\tau^{a}})\right]=\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})\prod_{a=1}^{n}(1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{a}})\right]^{N}\ .

(103)

Therefore, the average of the replicated partition function can be cast in the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N})\right)^{n}\right]

\displaystyle\propto\int\{\mathcal{D}[\rho^{a}]\}\{\mathcal{D}[\hat{\rho}^{a}]\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\vec{\tau}~\rme^{N\mathcal{S}^{(\beta,n)}[\{\rho^{a}\},\{\hat{\rho}^{a}\},\{\tau^{a}\},\{\lambda^{a}\}]}\ ,

(104)

where the action
\mathcal{S}^{(\beta,n)}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{(\beta,n)}[\{\rho^{a}\},\{\hat{\rho}^{a}\},\{\tau^{a}\},\{\lambda^{a}\}]:=\beta\sum_{a=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}~\rho^{a}(\bm{x})\rho^{a}(\bm{y})|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{i}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho^{a}(\bm{x})\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})+\mathrm{i}\alpha\sum_{a=1}^{n}\tau^{a}+\log{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~f(\bm{x})\rme^{\sum_{a=1}^{n}\log{(1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{a}}})}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{a=1}^{n}\lambda^{a}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\rho^{a}(\bm{x})-\alpha\right)\ .

(105)

The Lagrange multipliers
\{\lambda^{a}\}
enforce the normalisation of the mean-field densities (
99
). The form (
105
) lends itself to a saddle-point evaluation for large
N
, leading to

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{Z}^{(\beta)}_{N,M}(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N})\right)^{n}\right]\overset{N\to\infty}{\approx}\rme^{N\mathcal{S}^{(\beta,n)}[\{\rho*^{a}\},\{\hat{\rho}*^{a}\},\{t*^{a}\},\{\lambda*^{a}\}]}\ ,

(106)

where
[\{\rho*^{a}\},\{\hat{\rho}*^{a}\},\{t*^{a}\},\{\lambda*^{a}\}]
maximise
\mathcal{S}^{(\beta,n)}
.
The saddle-point equations read
\forall\ a=1,...,n
(omitting the
*
for simplicity),

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}^{(\beta,n)}}{\delta\rho^{a}}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})+2\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}~\rho^{a}(\bm{y})|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}-\lambda^{a}=0\ ,

(107)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}^{(\beta,n)}}{\delta\hat{\rho}^{a}}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{i}\rho^{a}(\bm{x})+\frac{f(\bm{x})\rme^{\sum_{b}\log(1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{b}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{b}})}\left[\frac{\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{a}}(-\mathrm{i})}{1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{a}}}\right]}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}~f(\bm{y})\rme^{\sum_{b}\log(1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{b}(\bm{y})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{b}})}}=0\ ,

(108)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}^{(\beta,n)}}{\partial\tau^{a}}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{i}\alpha+\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}~f(\bm{y})\rme^{\sum_{b}\log(1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{b}(\bm{y})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{b}})}\left[\frac{\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{y})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{a}}(-\mathrm{i})}{1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{y})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{a}}}\right]}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}~f(\bm{y})\rme^{\sum_{b}\log(1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{b}(\bm{y})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{b}})}}=0\ .

(109)

From (
107
) we get

-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{a}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=2\beta\left[\alpha\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}x_{\ell}^{2}+\overline{(r^{2})^{a}}-2\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}x_{\ell}\overline{(x_{\ell})^{a}}\right]-\lambda^{a}\ ,

(110)

where we defined

\displaystyle\overline{(r^{2})^{a}}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{d}\ \rho^{a}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}x_{\ell}^{2}\ ,

(111)

\displaystyle\overline{(x_{\ell})^{a}}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{d}~\rho^{a}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})~x_{\ell}\ ,\hskip 20.00003pt\forall~\ell=1,\dots,d,

(112)

and we used the normalisation condition (
99
).

From (
108
) we obtain

\displaystyle\rho^{a}(\bm{x})=\frac{f(\bm{x})\rme^{\sum_{b}\log(1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{b}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{b}})}\left[\frac{\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{a}}}{1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{a}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{a}}}\right]}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}f(\bm{y})\rme^{\sum_{b}\log(1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}^{b}(\bm{y})-\mathrm{i}\tau^{b}})}}\ .

(113)

We now assume a
replica-symmetric
structure, namely there should not be any dependence on the specific replica index
a
chosen (see
[
27
,
5
,
35
,
22
]
for detailed discussions about replica symmetry and its breakdown in disordered spin models)

\rho^{a}\equiv\rho\,;\hat{\rho}^{a}\equiv\hat{\rho};\tau^{a}\equiv\tau\,;\lambda^{a}\equiv\lambda\ .

(114)

Equation (
113
) therefore becomes

\rho(\bm{x})=\frac{f(\bm{x})\rme^{n\log(1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau})}\left[\frac{\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau}}{1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{i}\tau}}\right]}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}f(\bm{y})\rme^{n\log(1+\rme^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}(\bm{y})-\mathrm{i}\tau})}}\ .

(115)

Inserting now Eq. (
110
) for
\mathrm{i}\hat{\rho}
, we get

\rho(\bm{x})=\frac{f(\bm{x})\rme^{n\log(1+\rme^{\beta\xi(\bm{x})})}\left[\frac{\rme^{\beta\xi(\bm{x})}}{1+\rme^{\beta\xi(\bm{x})}}\right]}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}f(\bm{y})\rme^{n\log(1+\rme^{\beta\xi(\bm{y})})}}\ ,

(116)

where

\xi(\bm{x})=2\left[\alpha\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}x_{\ell}^{2}+\overline{r^{2}}-2\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}x_{\ell}\overline{x_{\ell}}\right]-\eta=\xi_{0}(\bm{x})-\eta

(117)

with
\eta=\lambda+\mathrm{i}\tau
(after a harmless rescaling
\lambda\to\beta\lambda
and similarly for
\mathrm{i}\tau
).

Multiplying (
107
) by
\rho(\bm{x})
and integrating we obtain

\displaystyle\beta\iint\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}~\rho(\bm{x})\rho(\bm{y})|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}=\frac{\lambda\alpha\beta}{2}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\rho(\bm{x})\hat{\rho}(\bm{x})=

(118)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\alpha\beta}{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho(\bm{x})[\xi_{0}(\bm{x})-\lambda]=\frac{\beta}{2}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho(\bm{x})\xi_{0}(\bm{x})\ ,

(119)

where we used again the normalisation condition (
99
) and (
110
).

Inserting this back into (
105
) within the replica-symmetry assumption, we get for the action at the saddle point

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{(\beta,n)}[\rho;\hat{\rho};\eta]

\displaystyle=\frac{n\beta}{2}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho(\bm{x})\xi_{0}(\bm{x})+n\beta\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho(\bm{x})[\lambda-\xi_{0}(\bm{x})]+\mathrm{i\alpha}\beta n\tau

\displaystyle+\log\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~f(\bm{x})\rme^{n\log(1+\rme^{\beta\xi(\bm{x})})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n\beta}{2}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho(\bm{x})\xi_{0}(\bm{x})+\alpha\beta n\eta+\log\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~f(\bm{x})\rme^{n\log(1+\rme^{\beta\xi(\bm{x})})}\ .

(120)

5.2
SCGF of the maximal
M
-dispersion

Now we can set
n=-p/\beta
in both the saddle-point density and the action, and extract the
\beta\to\infty
limit to compute the SCGF using Eq. (
95
).

Starting with the density in (
116
), and using the limits

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\log(1+\rme^{\beta\xi})

\displaystyle=\xi\theta(\xi)\ ,

(121)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{\rme^{\beta\xi}}{1+\rme^{\beta\xi}}

\displaystyle=\theta(\xi)\ ,

(122)

where
\theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function, we have

\rho(\bm{x})\sim\frac{f(\bm{x})\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})\theta(\xi(\bm{x}))}\theta(\xi(\bm{x}))}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}~f(\bm{y})\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{y})\theta(\xi(\bm{y}))}}

(123)

for large
\beta
. The term in the exponent can be simplified as

\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})\theta(\xi(\bm{x}))}=\theta(\xi(\bm{x}))\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}+1-\theta(\xi(\bm{x}))\ .

(124)

Inserting the density (
123
) into the action (
120
), we have to compute the following terms:

\displaystyle\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~\rho(\bm{x})\xi_{0}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})\theta(\xi(\bm{x}))\xi_{0}(\bm{x})[\theta(\xi(\bm{x}))\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}+1-\theta(\xi(\bm{x}))]}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~f(\bm{x})[\theta(\xi(\bm{x}))\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}+1-\theta(\xi(\bm{x}))]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}\xi_{0}(\bm{x})}{\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})[\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}-1]+1}

(125)

\displaystyle\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~f(\bm{x})\rme^{n\log(1+\rme^{\beta\xi(\bm{x})})}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~f(\bm{x})\rme^{-\frac{p}{\beta}\log(1+\rme^{\beta\xi(\bm{x})})}\sim\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}+1

\displaystyle-\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~f(\bm{x})\ ,

(126)

where the domain of integration
\Gamma
is such that
\xi(\bm{x})>0
, i.e.

\Gamma=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:2\left[\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}+\overline{r^{2}}-2\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\overline{x}\right]-\eta>0\right\}\ .

(127)

The constants
\overline{r^{2}},\overline{x_{i}},\eta
are determined by the
d+2
conditions

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}}{\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})[\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}-1]+1}

(128)

\displaystyle\overline{x_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}x_{i}}{\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})[\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}-1]+1}\ ,\text{for all $i=1,\dots,d$}

(129)

\displaystyle\overline{r^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}}{\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})[\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}-1]+1}\ ,

(130)

which follows from Eqs. (
99
), (
111
) and (
112
) with the replica index
a
suppressed, and
\rho(\bm{x})
given by (
123
).

Combining Eqs. (
95
), (
106
), (
120
), (
125
) and (
126
), and assuming replica symmetry, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left[-p\,\frac{D^{M}_{\max}(\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{N})}{N}\right]\right]\approx\mathrm{e}^{-N\Phi_{\alpha}(p)}\ ,

(131)

where the cumulant generating function
\Phi_{\alpha}(p)
is

\Phi_{\alpha}(p)=-\frac{p}{2}\frac{\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}\xi_{0}(\bm{x})}{\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})[\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}-1]+1}+\alpha p\eta-\log\left[\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}f(\bm{x})\rme^{-p\xi(\bm{x})}+1-\int_{\Gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{x}~f(\bm{x})\right]\ .

(132)

Note that in this replica derivation we did not assume rotational invariance of the probability density
f(\bm{x})
. If we restore this condition, we are now able to show that (
132
) – supplemented by the conditions (
128
) and (
130
) – precisely recovers the formula (
60
) obtained from order statistics considerations. This proof will therefore conclude our alternative derivation of the main formula for the SCGF of the maximal
M
-dispersion problem in
d
dimensions.

For a rotationally symmetric
f(\bm{x})
(see (
9
)), the integration domain
\Gamma
simplifies to

\Gamma=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\xi_{0}(\bm{x})-\eta>0\}

(133)

where

(143)

The greedy routine then essentially goes as follows:

•

\arg\max_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}D_{ij}

7
Conclusion and Outlook

In this paper, we considered the full statistics of the maximal
M
-dispersion of
N
random points in
d
dimensions. This problem originates in the field of operations research, where the objective is to select a “sub-committee” of
M
members out of a population of
N
individuals each having
d
different traits, in such a way that the traits of the sub-committee’s members are as “dispersed” as possible. This is in general a difficult problem that – for large but fixed instances – can only be solved approximately using heuristic algorithms whose performance is often tested on
randomly generated
instances
[
14
]
. However, the current lack of analytical control over the expected maximal dispersion and its fluctuations in these random instances makes it difficult to establish a reliable benchmark to assess the performance of such heuristic algorithms.

In our work, we use an additive measure of dispersion that receives larger contributions whenever the pairwise distance between the trait vectors increases (see Eq. (
1
)). First, for
d=1
we could characterise exactly the “geometry” of the optimising subset: on the line, one should always pick the
k
leftmost and
M-k
rightmost points, for an optimal
k
to be determined instance by instance (see Section
3.1
). Guided by this intuition, we set up a mean-field theory valid for
d\geq 1
and
M,N
large whereby the set of points that typically maximise dispersion would lie on the complementary of a ball centered at the origin, and whose radius could be precisely characterised (Sec.
4.1
). With a slight tilting of the argument, we could also extract the rate (or large deviation) function governing rare fluctuations of the maximal dispersion (Sec.
4.2
). Alternatively, the same final formulae for the SCGF and the rate function are obtained from a replica approach, where the maximiser of the dispersion is obtained from the zero-temperature free energy corresponding to an auxiliary thermodynamical system in the canonical ensemble (Sec.
5
).

For the special case of a single trait (
d=1
), we exploited exact tools from order statistics – the field of probability theory dealing with random variables that are sorted in increasing order – to compute average and variance of the dispersion of
balanced
configurations for finite
N,M
(Sec.
3.3
). Balanced configurations are prefix-suffix with the same number of points in the two extreme blocks: while these configurations are asymptotically optimal for large instances, they do not necessarily yield the maximal dispersion on every single, finite instance. However, they provide excellent approximants even for moderate values of
N
, while allowing an exact finite-
N,M
treatment.

Using exact enumerations for smaller instances in
d=1
and heuristic numerical algorithms for larger dimension, we could show excellent agreement between simulations and our theoretical predictions. As shown in Fig.
6
, the maximal
M
-diversity has Gaussian fluctuations around the mean on a scale
\sim O(1/\sqrt{N})
, whereas larger fluctuations
O(1)
are governed by the full rate functions computed here.

One of the future challenges will be to consider a dispersion measure where the objective function to maximise is actually more sensitive to the
local
gaps between traits: consider for instance the quadratic
M
-dispersion measure defined in Eq. (
1
) for the
d=1
case, with the single trait being the
wealth
of each individual. The subset of a large population whose wealth is as dispersed as possible would be formed by selecting the
\sim M/2
poorest and
\sim M/2
richest individuals in the population, which leads to a sub-committee not necessarily representative of all intermediate classes of wealth. Another interesting direction of research will be to consider a case where the
N
individuals in the initial population are not created equal, or where there are correlations or particular structures between traits across different individuals that may better mimic the complexity and “hardness” of real-life scenarios. Moreover, the full analysis of the finite
N,M
case in all dimensions is still a challenging open problem. Finally, the mean-field approach developed in Sec.
4
hinges on the pdf
f(\bm{x})
being sufficiently light-tailed: it would be very interesting to find a corresponding theory for heavy-tailed trait distributions as well.

Appendix A
Generalities on order statistics
(d=1)

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
be i.i.d. real random variables with common distribution function
F(x)
with density
f(x)
. We denote by
X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(N)}
their order statistics,
X_{(1)}\leq X_{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq X_{(N)}
.

From the general discussion in the main text, the maximal
M
-dispersion will be the sum of the quadratic difference between the
i
smallest and the
N-j+1
largest order statistics on the line, for some
1\leq i<j\leq N
.
For all
i<j
, the joint density of the first
i
and the last
N-j+1
order statistics
X_{(1)},\cdots,X_{(i)},X_{(j)},\cdots,X_{(N)}
is

\displaystyle f_{X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(i)},X_{(j)},\ldots,X_{(N)}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j},\ldots,x_{N})=

\displaystyle\frac{N!}{(j-i-1)!}f(x_{1})\cdots f(x_{i})\left[F(x_{j})-F(x_{i})\right]^{j-i-1}f(x_{j})\cdots f(x_{N})\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\theta(x_{i+1}-x_{i})\ ,

(144)

where the Heaviside function is
\theta(x)=1
for all
x\geq 0
and
\theta(x)=0
otherwise. The interpretation of (
144
) is textbook: there are random variables taking values at
x_{1},\cdots,x_{i},x_{j},\cdots,x_{N}
(which happens according to the respective probability densities), multiplied by the probability that all other
j-i-1
variables sit between
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
; the combinatorial factor counts the number of ways the variables can be distributed in the three groups (left-middle-right).

Similarly, the bivariate density of
X_{(i)},X_{(j)}
is

\displaystyle f_{X_{(i)},X_{(j)}}(x_{i},x_{j})=\frac{N!}{(i-1)!(j-i-1)!(N-j)!}

\displaystyle\times F(x_{i})^{i-1}f(x_{i})\left[F(x_{j})-F(x_{i})\right]^{j-i-1}f(x_{j})(1-F(x_{j}))^{N-j}\theta(x_{j}-x_{i})\ .

(145)

It follows that the conditional density of
X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(i)},X_{(j)},\ldots,X_{(N)}
conditioned on
X_{(i)},X_{(j)}
is

\displaystyle f_{X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(i)},X_{(j)},\ldots,X_{(n)}|X_{(i)},X_{(j)}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j},\ldots,x_{N})=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(i)},X_{(j)},\ldots,X_{(n)}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},x_{j},\ldots,x_{N})}{f_{X_{(i)},X_{(j)}}(x_{i},x_{j})}=

\displaystyle=(i-1)!(N-j)!\frac{f(x_{1})}{F(x_{i})}\cdots\frac{f(x_{i-1})}{F(x_{i})}\frac{f(x_{j+1})}{1-F(x_{j})}\cdots\frac{f(x_{N})}{1-F(x_{j})}\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\theta(x_{i+1}-x_{i})\ .

(146)

From (
146
) we see that
X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(i)},X_{(j)},\ldots,X_{(n)}
conditioned on
X_{(i)},X_{(j)}
have the same density of the union of two order statistics:

1.

the order statistics of a set of
(i-1)
i.i.d. random variables in the (random) interval
(-\infty,X_{(i)}]
with truncated and renormalised density
\frac{f(x)}{F(X_{(i)})}\theta(X_{(i)}-x)
, and

2.

the order statistics of a set of
(N-j)
i.i.d. random variables in the (random) interval
[X_{(j)},+\infty)
with truncated and renormalised density
\frac{f(x)}{1-F(X_{(j)})}\theta(x-X_{(j)})
.

The conditional density in (
146
) precisely features in the general expectation formula (
29
) of the main text. For more details, we refer the reader to the classical reference
[
1
]
.
