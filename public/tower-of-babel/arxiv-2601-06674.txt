Reduction and classification of higher-order Markov chains for categorical data
C. Gallesco, C. T. Genovese Huss Oliveira, D. Y. Takahashi

Abstract
Categorical time series models are powerful tools for understanding natural phenomena. Most available models can be formulated as special cases of m m -th order Markov chains, for m ≥ 1 m\geq 1 . Despite their broad applicability, theoretical research has largely focused on first-order Markov chains, mainly because many properties of higher-order chains can be analyzed by reducing them to first-order chains on an enlarged alphabet. However, the resulting first-order representation is sparse and possesses a highly structured transition kernel, a feature that has not been fully exploited. In this work, we study finite-alphabet Markov chains with arbitrary memory length and introduce a new reduction framework for their structural classification. We define the skeleton of a transition kernel, an object that captures the intrinsic pattern of transition probability constraints in a higher-order Markov chain. We show that the class structure of a binary matrix associated with the skeleton completely determines the recurrent classes and their periods in the original chain. We also provide an explicit algorithm for efficiently extracting the skeleton, which in many cases yields substantial computational savings. Applications include simple criteria for irreducibility and essential irreducibility of higher-order Markov chains and a concrete illustration based on a 10th-order Markov chain.



1
Introduction

Markov chains provide a fundamental framework for modeling dependence in categorical time series and play a central role in probability theory and its applications
[
1
,
5
]
. While the classical theory of Markov chains is largely developed for first-order chains,
m
-th order Markov chains naturally arise in contexts where longer memory effects cannot be neglected, including symbolic dynamics, information theory, and time-series models
[
2
,
6
,
4
]
. Several modern machine learning algorithms are instances of higher-order Markov chains
[
8
]
.

A standard approach to studying an
m
-th order Markov chain on a finite alphabet
A
consists of rewriting it as a first-order Markov chain on the product space
A^{m}
, see for example
[
5
]
. Although this reduction is often adequate, it does not explore the sparseness and the special structure of the reduced chain, which contains a large number of zero-probability transitions that do not correspond to genuine prohibitions in the original dynamics, but rather to artifacts of the embedding into
A^{m}
. Failing to explore the structural properties of the reduced chain can make the analysis of irreducibility, recurrence, and periodicity more obscure and computationally intensive, especially for high-order Markov chains, which are standard in modern applications
[
6
]
.

These difficulties motivate the development of tools that allow one to analyze higher-order Markov chains more intrinsically. In this work, we introduce a reduction framework based on a structural object associated with the transition kernel, which we call the
skeleton
. The skeleton captures precisely the minimal contexts responsible for prohibiting transitions and encodes the essential connectivity structure of the chain.

Our first main result shows that the classification of an
m
-th order Markov chain,
i.e.
, its decomposition into recurrent classes and their periods, is entirely determined by the class structure of a binary matrix naturally associated with the skeleton. In particular, closed classes and their periods for the skeleton matrix correspond exactly to recurrent classes and their periods in the original chain. This establishes the skeleton as a complete invariant for classification purposes.

Beyond its theoretical significance, the skeleton can lead to substantial computational gains. We introduce an explicit and efficient algorithm for extracting the skeleton from a given transition kernel, based on a pruning procedure on a tree representing the transition probabilities of the Markov chain. In several situations, this algorithm enables the effective analysis of chains with large memory requirements. We further derive simple criteria for the irreducibility and essential irreducibility of a Markov chain directly from its skeleton, which, in some instances, allow one to bypass the computation of powers of the transition matrix.

We illustrate the proposed framework on a 10-th order Markov chain example, where the skeleton has significantly smaller order. This example highlights both the conceptual clarity and the computational advantages of the skeleton-based approach.

Finally, in the same vein as this article, we recently introduced a criterion for the uniqueness of chains of infinite order when prohibited transitions exist, extending some of the concepts discussed here to non-Markovian chains
[
3
]
. However, the notion of the skeleton and the complete classification of recurrent classes were not addressed in
[
3
]
and do not appear to generalize easily to the non-Markovian setting.

2
Theoretical results

Let
A
be a finite set, called the
alphabet
, and
|A|
the cardinal of
A
. We consider a Markov chain of order
m\geq 1
,
X=(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, with state space
A
. We denote by
p
its transition kernel, that is,

p:A^{m}\times A\to[0,1]
is such that

p(x,a)=P(X_{n+1}=a\mid X_{n}=x_{m},X_{n-1}=x_{m-1},\dots,X_{n-m+1}=x_{1})

for all
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in A^{m}
and all
n\geq m-1
.
Equivalently,
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
can be seen as a Markov chain of order 1 on the state space
A^{m}
. In this case, we will denote by
\mathbb{P}
its transition matrix.

For
x\in A^{k}
we denote by
x_{i}
the
i
-th coordinate of
x
and for
1\leq i\leq j\leq k
we write
x^{j}_{i}:=(x_{i},\ldots,x_{j})
. For
x\in A^{k}
and
y\in A^{l}
, a
concatenation

xy
is a new sequence
z\in A^{k+l}
such that
z_{1}^{k}=x_{1}^{k}
and
z_{k+1}^{k+l}=y_{1}^{l}
.
We use also the convention that
A^{0}=\{e\}

where
e
is the neutral element of the concatenation operation, that is
ex=xe=x
. For
x\in A^{k}
we will also denote by
|x|
the length of
x
, that is,
k
.

Definition 1
.

For all
a\in A
and
x\in A^{m}
, we define

\tau(x,a)=\min\Big\{i\geq 0:p(yx_{m-i}^{m},a)=0,\forall y\in A^{m-i-1}\;\text{or}\;\;p(yx_{-i}^{0},a)>0,\forall y\in A^{m-i-1}\Big\}

and

\tau_{x}=\sup_{a\in A}\tau(x,a).

The set
\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{x\in A^{m}}\{x^{m}_{m-\tau_{x}}\}
is called the skeleton of the kernel
p
.
Let
K:=\sup_{x}\tau_{x}\in\{0,\dots,m\}
.
K
is called the order of the skeleton.

In this paper, we will consider binary square matrices with entries in a finite set
E
, that is, matrices whose elements belongs to
\{0,1\}
. In this case, the addition and product will always be the boolean ones. For example,
1+1=1
,
0\times 1=0
, etc. With these operations, the set of binary square matrices of a given dimension is a boolean algebra.
We can naturally transpose the notions of communicating class, closed (communicating) class and period of a closed (communicating) class from stochastic matrices to binary square matrices. For example, a communicating class
\mathcal{C}\subset E
is closed if, for all
i\in\mathcal{C}
,
B^{n}(i,j)=0
for all
n\geq 1
and
j\notin\mathcal{C}
. For a stochastic matrix, a closed class is also called
recurrent
. As for stochastic matrices, a binary square matrix induces the following partition of
E
:
there exit
N\geq 1
,
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\dots
,
\mathcal{C}_{N}
closed classes and
\mathcal{T}
which does not contain any closed class and is disjoint from
\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{C}_{N}
such that

E=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{C}_{N}\cup\mathcal{T}
(
\mathcal{T}
may be empty).

Definition 2
.

Consider a kernel
p
with skeleton of order
K
. The skeleton matrix
\mathbb{M}
is the binary matrix of dimension
A^{K}\times A^{K}
defined by

\mathbb{M}(u,v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mbox{if}\phantom{*}(u,v)=(x_{-K+1}^{0},x_{-K+2}^{0}a)\;\;\text{and}\;\;p(yx_{-K+1}^{0}a)>0,\;\text{for all}\;\;y,\\
0,&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Let also

\mathcal{A}=\Big\{a_{1}^{m}\in A^{m}\;\text{such that}\;\mathbb{M}(a_{1}^{K},a_{2}^{K+1})\mathbb{M}(a_{2}^{K+1},a_{3}^{K+2})\dots\mathbb{M}(a_{m-K}^{m-1},a_{m-K+1}^{m})=1\Big\}.

We now state our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Consider a Markov chain of order
m
,
X
, with skeleton of order
K
. If
\mathbb{M}
has
N\geq 1
closed classes
\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{N}
, then
\mathbb{P}
has
N
recurrent classes
\mathcal{R}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{R}_{N}
. The classes
\mathcal{R}_{i}
satisfy

\mathcal{R}_{i}=\{ab:a\in\mathcal{C}_{i}\;\text{and}\;ab\in\mathcal{A}\}

for
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
Moreover, the period of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
is equal to the period of
\mathcal{R}_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.

Let us note that Theorem
1
does not say anything about the transient states of
X
. In fact, it may happen that the set
\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
and
X
has some transient states. Also,
\mathcal{T}
may be a communicating class and even so
X
has several transient classes.

Proof.

We first observe that for
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
,
\mathcal{R}_{i}
is a communicating class. Indeed, consider
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
. By definition, there exist
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
and
b_{1},b_{2}\in A^{m-K}
such that
c_{1}=a_{1}b_{1}
and
c_{2}=a_{2}b_{2}
. Since
\mathcal{C}_{i}
is closed and
a_{2}b_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
, we deduce that
\mathbb{P}^{n}(a_{1}b_{1},a_{2}b_{2})>0
. Also observe that, by definition, the classes
\mathcal{R}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
are recurrent and disjoint.

To prove the first part of the theorem, it remains to show that
\mathbb{P}
cannot have more than
N
recurrent classes. If
(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{C}_{N})^{c}=\emptyset
, then either
(\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{R}_{N})^{c}=\emptyset
(and the first part of the theorem is proved) or
(\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{R}_{N})^{c}\neq\emptyset
. In this second case,
(\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{R}_{N})^{c}
contains elements which are not in
\mathcal{A}
and therefore cannot be recurrent. If
(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{C}_{N})^{c}\neq\emptyset
, then either an element of
(\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{R}_{N})^{c}
does not belong to
\mathcal{A}
(and thus cannot be recurrent), or is of the form
ab
where
a\in(\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{C}_{N})^{c}
and
ab\in\mathcal{A}
. In this second case, there exists
n\geq 1
,
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and
d\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
such that
\mathbb{P}^{n}(ab,d)>0
and therefore
ab
is not recurrent.

Let us denote by
d(\mathcal{C}_{i})
and
d(\mathcal{R}_{i})
the periods of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
and
\mathcal{R}_{i}
respectively. To prove the second part of the theorem, first observe that
d(\mathcal{C}_{i})\leq d(\mathcal{R}_{i})
by definition of
\mathcal{R}_{i}
. We will show by contradiction that we cannot have
d(\mathcal{C}_{i})<d(\mathcal{R}_{i})
. For the rest of the proof, let us denote
d=d(\mathcal{C}_{i})
and let
D_{0},\dots,D_{d-1}
be the corresponding cycling classes. Consider
c\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
. We have that
c_{1}^{K}\in D_{j}
for some
j
and
c_{m-K+1}^{m}\in D_{k}
for
k-j=m-K+1

\text{mod}(d)
. Then, for all
n\geq n_{0}
we have

\mathbb{M}^{nd+d-(k-j)}(c_{m-K+1}^{m},a)=1
.
Thus, we have,
\mathbb{M}^{(n+1)d}(c,c)>0
for all large enough
n
. Suppose that
d(\mathcal{R}_{i})>d
.
By the former affirmation
d(\mathcal{R}_{i})

divides
(n+1)d
and
(n+2)d
for large enough
n
and thus divides
(n+2)d-(n+1)d=d
. But since
d(\mathcal{R}_{i})>d
, we have a contradiction.
\;\;\square

In our context, the following definition will be useful

Definition 3
.

A Markov chain
X
with finite state space is essentially irreducible if
\mathbb{P}
has a unique recurrent class.

Observe that in the above definition we do not exclude the presence of transient states. Therefore the notion of essential irreducibility is different from the notion of irreducibility. It is well known that an essentially irreducible chain has a unique invariant probability measure.
As a direct consequence of Theorem
1
we have the following

Corollary 1
.

If
\mathbb{M}
has a unique closed class then
X
is essentially irreducible.

To check that
\mathbb{M}
has a unique closed class we can use the following simple result.

Proposition 1
.

\mathbb{M}
has a unique closed class if and only if the matrix
\sum_{n=1}^{|A|^{K}}\mathbb{M}^{n}
has a column with only 1’s.

Proof:
Suppose that
\mathbb{M}
has at least two closed classes. Since
\sum_{n=1}^{|A|^{K}}\mathbb{M}^{n}
has a column with only 1’s, there exists a state
j_{0}\in A^{K}
such that for all
i\in A^{K}
there exists
n_{i}\in\{1,\dots,|A|\}
such that
\mathbb{M}^{n_{i}}(i,j_{0})=1
. Clearly,
j_{0}
must belong to a closed class. But we obtain a contradiction because if there are at least two closed classes there must exist some element from which
j_{0}
is not accessible.

On the other hand, suppose that
\mathbb{M}
has a unique closed class and consider
j_{0}
in this closed class. For all
i\in A^{K}
, there exists some integer
n_{i}
such that
\mathbb{M}^{n_{i}}(i,j_{0})=1
. Since
A
is finite, we can always take
n_{i}\leq|A|^{K}
. This implies that
\sum_{n=1}^{|A|^{K}}\mathbb{M}^{n}
has a column with entries all equal to 1.
\;\;\square

Regarding the irreducibility of a Markov chain, we have the following

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
X
is irreducible. Then, the only two possible cases are
K=m
or
K=0
. The case
K=0
corresponds to
p(x,a)>0
for all
x\in A^{m}
and
a\in A
.

Proof:

Consider
\mathbb{P}
irreducible and suppose that
K<m
. Then,
\mathbb{M}
has the following structure

\mathbb{M}(u,v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mbox{whenever}\phantom{*}(u,v)=(x_{-K+1}^{0},x_{-K+2}^{0}a),\;x_{-K+1}^{0}\in A^{K}\mbox{ and }a\in A,\\
0,&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

But, if
\mathbb{M}
has the above structure then
p(x,a)>0
for all
x\in A^{m}
and
a\in A
. Therefore we deduce that
K=0
.
\;\;\square

The contraposition of Proposition
2
gives us a simple criterion to check that a given Markov chain is not irreducible: if
m>K>0
then the chain is not irreducible. When
K=0
, the chain is clearly irreducible. The only remaining case is when
K=m
, in which case we have the following simple criterion.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a skeleton of order
K=m
. If there exists
w\in\mathcal{S}
of length strictly smaller than
m
, such that
p(zw,a)=0
, for some
a\in A
and
z\in A^{m-\ell}
, then
X
is not irreducible.

Proof.

Since
w\in\mathcal{S}
, we obtain that
p(yw,a)=0
for all
y\in A^{m-\ell}
. Since
|w|<m
we have that
|wa|\leq m
, hence
\exists\;u\in A^{m}
such that
wa
is a suffix of
u
. We deduce that for all
x\in A^{m}
,
\mathbb{P}(x,u)=0
and therefore
X
is not irreducible.
∎

Skeleton determination algorithm

In this section we present a simple algorithm to obtain the skeleton of a Markov chain.

We consider a Markov chain
X
of order
m
with state space
A=\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{|A|}\}
where
|A|
is the cardinality of
A
. Then, we consider the following
tree

T
associated to
X

T:=\Big\{v_{m}\dots v_{2}v_{1}\varnothing,\;v_{i}\in A,\;i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\Big\}

where
\varnothing
is the root of the tree. For
1\leq k\leq m
, an element
v_{k}\dots v_{1}
is a
node
of depth
k
of the tree
T
. We define the
sister leaves
as leaves (nodes with no child-nodes) stemming from the same parent node.
We now equip
T
with the following probability kernel: for each node
v_{k}\dots v_{1}
,

\hat{p}(v_{k}\dots v_{1},a)=P(X_{n+1}=a\mid X_{n}=v_{1},X_{n-1}=v_{2},\dots,X_{n-k+1}=v_{k}).

For a parent node
\chi^{*}
, the set of associated sister leaves is
\chi=\{\chi_{j}:\chi_{j}=a_{j}\chi^{*},\;j=1,\cdots,|A|\}
. For each
\chi_{j}\in\chi
we define its
transition vector

t(\chi_{j})
as follows:
t(\chi_{j}):=(t_{1},\cdots,t_{|A|})
, where
t_{i}=\mathbf{1}\{\hat{p}(\chi_{j},a_{i})>0\}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,|A|\}
.

The algorithm is as follows:

Skeleton determination algorithm

Input:
Tree
T
of depth
m
with transition vectors
t(\cdot)
.

Output:
Reduced tree isomorphic to the skeleton
\mathcal{S}
.

Set
\ell\leftarrow m
and
T_{\ell}\leftarrow T
.

while

\ell\geq 1

do

For each set
\chi\subset T_{\ell}
of sister leaves at depth
\ell
:

if

t(\chi_{j})=t(\chi_{j^{\prime}})
for all
\chi_{j},\chi_{j^{\prime}}\in\chi

then

Remove all nodes in
\chi
from
T_{\ell}
.

end if

Set
T_{\ell-1}
equal to the resulting tree.

if

T_{\ell-1}=T_{\ell}

then stop
.

Set
\ell\leftarrow\ell-1
.

end while

The tree obtained at the end of the algorithm is isomorphic to the skeleton
\mathcal{S}
of the original Markov chain. Also, the transition vectors of its leaves provides the
skeleton matrix

\mathbb{M}
.

Computational cost

We now examine the computational cost to verify the essential irreducibility of higher-order Markov chains. For this, we state without proof the following classical criterion for essential irreducibility.

Proposition 4
.

The Markov chain
X
with transition matrix
\mathbb{P}
is essentially irreducible if and only if the matrix
\sum_{n=1}^{|A|^{m}}\mathbb{P}^{n}
has a column with only strictly positive entries.

Using a naive multiplication approach to test the essential irreducibility of a first-order Markov chain with alphabet
B
, one may require up to
|B|-1
matrix powers, each involving
|B|^{3}
operations. We then have to sum all the terms, leading to an overall computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(|B|^{4})
. Consequently, after reducing an
m
-th order Markov chain to a first-order chain on the enlarged alphabet
A^{m}
, testing essential irreducibility using Proposition
4
can require up to
\mathcal{O}(|A|^{4m})
operations.

Alternatively, computationally efficient graph-based methods such as Tarjan’s algorithm may be used to verify the uniqueness of the recurrent class,
i.e.
, essential irreducibility, directly from the transition graph
[
7
]
. Applied to the reduced first-order chain, this approach has complexity
\mathcal{O}(|A|^{m+1})
.

By contrast, if the associated skeleton has order
K<m
, essential irreducibility can be assessed by simply computing the skeleton and then applying Tarjan’s algorithm to the matrix
\mathbb{M}
. This procedure has computational cost
\mathcal{O}(|A|^{m})
. When
K\leq m/4
, even if we naively use Proposition
1
, we still obtain a computational cost
\mathcal{O}(|A|^{m})
. This shows that, in general, using the skeleton determination algorithm can reduce the computational complexity of checking the essential irreducibility of a high-order Markov chain.
