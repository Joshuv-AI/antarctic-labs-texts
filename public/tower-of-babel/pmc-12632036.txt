The role of sucrose in phosphate starvation response: transcriptomic and physiological insights in Arabidopsis Thaliana
Authors: Xiubin Chen, Meiyan Wang, Qingnian Tian, Li Liu, Haijing Wang, Xinhua Song, Wenlong Sun, Xiuli Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPi (inorganic phosphate) is an essential mineral nutrient and a key limiting factor for plant growth. Environmental factors of carbon and energy status provided by sucrose influence plant growth and stress adaption; however, the mechanisms by which plant seedlings cope with Pi-starvation stress in conjunction with these factors remain largely unknown.ResultsThis study investigates the interplay between sucrose status and Pi-starvation response using a combined Pi/sucrose treatment. Through comparative transcriptomic and physiological analyses, we demonstrated that: (1) the upregulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis under Pi-starvation is significantly attenuated under sucrose deficiency, coinciding with reduced anthocyanin and ROS (reactive oxygen species) accumulation; (2) photosynthesis is downregulated under Pi-starvation with sufficient sucrose supply but upregulated under sucrose limitation, with chlorophyll content decreasing under sufficient sucrose supply and increasing under deficiency; (3) ATP-consuming anabolic pathways are downregulated under Pi-starvation with sufficient sucrose supply but showed no significant changes under sucrose deficiency; and (4) plant-pathogen interaction pathways, typically upregulated during Pi-starvation, are downregulated under insufficient sucrose supply. Additionally, we identified PIF3 (phytochrome-interacting factor 3) as a potential regulator of Pi-starvation responses, with loss-of-function mutants exhibiting retarded growth and gain-of-function mutants showing enhanced growth under Pi-starvation.ConclusionThese results highlight the critical role of sucrose status in modulating plant Pi-starvation response and provide new insights into the adaptive mechanisms by which plants coordinate C/Pi balance to cope with nutrient stress, thus extending and complementing previous research on Pi-starvation response mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07677-2.Keywords: Phosphate starvation, Carbon status, Energy status, Sucrose, Transcriptomic and physiological insight, Arabidopsis thaliana

Background

Phosphorous (P) plays multiple crucial roles in plant growth and development, such as serving as a key element of nucleic acids, phospholipids, and ATP, and being involved in protein modification. These functions contribute to genetic activity, cell membrane composition, energy supply, and the regulation of cellular activities [45, 49]. Bioavailable P exists primarily as Pi in the forms H2PO4−, HPO42−, and PO43−; however, Pi in soil is often deficient and requires supplementation through fertilization. This deficiency arises from the incomplete circling of Pi in ecosystems, which is partly caused by its loss through water movement and its binding with heavy metals, such as Fe (iron), Al (aluminum), forming insoluble compounds that are difficult for plants to access [45, 49].

Although plants are immobile, they have evolved multiple strategies to adapt to Pi-starvation condition, such as reshaping root architecture, excreting substances to release P from soil through symbiotic interactions, and recycling Pi from internal organic P [12]. Under Pi-starvation, root system architecture undergoes extensive remodeling, involving changes in primary root length, the lateral root length and density, the root hair number and length [21, 59]. These morphological adaptations are regulated by sophisticated Pi signaling pathways involving phytohormones such as auxin, ethylene, cytokinin. Additionally, post-translational modifications, including ubiquitination and SUMOylation, the upregulation of cellulose synthesis, and the enhanced production and secretion of malate, play critical roles in these processes [21, 25, 43]. Microbes in the rhizosphere also contribute to plant adaptation by supplying nutrients, including Pi, in exchange for carbon sources provided by plants [54]. Interestingly, Pi-starvation can shape the root microbiome, aiding plants in coping with Pi deficiency, as exemplified in Arabidopsis, where the PHR1-RALF-FERONIA pathway suppresses immunity to alleviate Pi-starvation by upregulating the expression of PSR (phosphate starvation response) genes [8, 14, 66]. On the other hand, Pi excess can reduce plant resistance to diseases and increase susceptibility to pathogen infections in rice [7].

Sucrose serves as a carbon source and energy supplier for plant growth and development [16, 55, 79]. SHMT1 (Serine hydroxymethyltransferase 1), which has been reported to contribute to primary root growth, exhibits arrested root growth in its loss-of-function mutant under sucrose-starvation condition; however, root growth is restored when sucrose was supplied [79]. MOS1 (modifier of SNC1-1), a modulator of plant immunity, responds to high-sugar condition with its mutant showed arrested root growth and increased anthocyanin accumulation when grown under high-sugar condition [82].

The coordination of carbon and nitrogen in regulating plant growth has been extensively studied, with the proposed mechanism suggesting that N assimilation is energetically costly and relies on energy supplied by carbon skeletons [37, 46, 52, 68]. Similarly, Pi assimilation is also energetically demanding; however, studies focusing on the coordination of carbon and P in regulating plant growth are relatively limited, and the associated processes and mechanisms remain unclear.

Results

Sucrose influences the plant’s Pi-starvation response by regulating growth and various physiological activities

Sucrose can be sensed by plants, promoting growth and increasing biomass production by up to 60% in Arabidopsis [27, 58]. How sucrose supply influence plant Pi-starvation response remains unclear.

The sucrose starvation concentration was initially determined based on previous studies [47, 73] and our own phenotypic analysis. A concentration of 0.2% (w/v) sucrose was selected to represent sucrose starvation conditions, as it supported normal seed germination and seedling growth (Supplemental Fig. 1 A). Gene expression analysis revealed that the sugar sensor gene HXK1 (Hexokinase 1) and the ATL8 (RING-type ubiquitin ligase Tóxicos en Levadura 8) were upregulated under 0.2% (w/v) sucrose compared to sufficient sucrose conditions (2% w/v), whereas the expression of SUC4 (Sucrose Transporter 4) and MORF9 (Multiple Organellar RNA Editing Factor 9) was downregulated (Supplemental Fig. 1B). These results are consistent with previous studies [47, 73], further supporting the use of 0.2% (w/v) sucrose as a sucrose starvation condition.

To investigate the role of sucrose in the Pi-starvation response, we cultured seedlings under Pi-starvation conditions with either sufficient (2%, w/v) or insufficient (0.2%, w/v) sucrose, designated as LP20 and LP2, respectively. Additionally, seedlings without Pi-starvation were cultured under the same sucrose conditions, designated as MS20 and MS2. Analysis of the growth phenotype and quantitative indicators, including fresh weight and anthocyanin content, revealed that in response to Pi-starvation, seedlings with sufficient sucrose exhibited poor growth and more increased anthocyanin accumulation (Figs. 1A-C). In contrast, seedlings with insufficient sucrose showed much slower growth rate and less increased anthocyanin accumulation (Figs. 1A-C). Further Pi content analysis revealed that the shortage of sucrose supply decreased Pi accumulation under Pi-starvation condition (Fig. 1D). To assess the effect of exogenous sucrose supply on endogenous soluble sugar accumulation, we measured soluble sugar levels in seedlings. The results showed that Pi starvation led to a marked accumulation of soluble sugars, whereas this accumulation was attenuated under limited sucrose supply (Fig. 1E). These findings suggest that sucrose regulates plant biomass accumulation, anthocyanin accumulation, Pi accumulation, and endogenous soluble sugar accumulation under Pi deficiency stress.

Root hairs possess high plasticity in response to changing environment and their growths are promoted in Pi-starvation stress [75]. Our results showed that root hair length and density were all enhanced in seedlings under Pi-starvation condition with sufficient sucrose; however, this promotion was significantly decreased with the shortage of sucrose supply (Fig. 2F-H, Supplemental Fig. 2). These results suggest that the regulation of root hair growth during coping with Pi-starvation stress is a sucrose-dependent process.

RNA-seq data evaluation

To investigate the transcriptome profiles of the Pi-starvation response affected by sucrose, we prepared three biological replicates seedlings of MS20 (with sufficient-Pi and sufficient-sucrose), LP20 (with insufficient-Pi but sufficient-sucrose), MS2 (with sufficient-Pi but insufficient-sucrose), and LP2 (with insufficient Pi and insufficient-sucrose) for RNA-seq analysis. Quality control of the sequenced data showed that the Q30 value for all samples was greater than 94.04% (Supplemental Table 1). After filtering and trimming the raw reads, the number of clean reads ranged from 40.94 to 43.48 million. The percentage of mapped reads ranged from 97.52% to 97.90%, and the percentage of uniquely mapped reads ranged from 95.77% to 96.13% (Supplemental Table 2). PCA (Principal component analysis) revealed significant differences among the MS20, MS2, LP20, and LP2 groups, with PC1 accounting for 32.34% and PC2 for 15.77% of the variance (Supplemental Fig. 3). Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess sample consistency, with Pearson R² values ranging from 0.83 to 1.00 (Supplemental Fig. 4). These results ensured the high quality of the sequencing data for subsequent analysis.

Differential gene set analysis in plant Pi-starvation response regulated by sucrose

To analyze the role of sucrose in plant adaption to Pi-starvation environment, differential gene set analysis was performed, and DEGs in the four comparisons sets -LP20/MS20, LP2/MS2, LP2/LP20, MS2/MS20 -were analyzed individually.

Volcanic map analysis revealed 2385 upregulated genes and 1470 downregulated genes in Pi-starved plants with sufficient sucrose supply (Fig. 3A, Supplemental Table 3); while 1666 upregulated genes and 968 downregulated genes were found in Pi-starved with insufficient sucrose supply (Fig. 3B, Supplemental Table 3). Further analysis showed that more Pi-starvation-related genes were downregulated when sucrose supply was sufficient, with 286 upregulated genes and 403 downregulated genes in the comparison between LP2 and LP20 (Fig. 3C, Supplemental Table 3). Sucrose alone also regulates plants growth, as evidenced by 1080 upregulated genes and 446 downregulated genes in the comparison between MS2 and MS20 (Fig. 3D, Supplemental Table 3). These results suggest that plant response to Pi-starvation is jointly regulated by Pi and sucrose supply.

To further investigate the role of sucrose supply in the Pi-starvation response, KEGG (Kyoto encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway classification analysis was performed. The DEGs identified in the KEGG analysis were grouped into five major categories: cellular processes, environmental information processing, genetic information processing, metabolism, and organismal systems. The results showed that the LP20/MS20 and LP2/MS2 exhibiting similar patterns, with more genes being upregulated than downregulated (Figs. 3E-F). However, certain DEGs were enriched in the LP20/MS20 comparison but not in LP2/MS2, including genes related to the ribosome (genetic information processing), protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (genetic information processing), and circadian rhythm (organismal systems) (Figs. 3E-F). When plants were grown under Pi-starvation conditions with a sucrose shortage, the number of downregulated genes exceeded the number of upregulated genes in the LP2/LP20 comparison (Fig. 3G). However, exceptions were observed in certain metabolic categories, including valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, photosynthesis-antenna proteins, galactose metabolism, purine metabolism, and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, where the number of upregulated genes surpassed that of downregulated genes (Fig. 3G). Further analysis revealed that under Pi-sufficient conditions, sucrose shortage led to more upregulated genes than downregulated ones (Fig. 3H). These findings indicate that Pi-starvation induces more upregulated than downregulated genes under both sucrose-sufficient and sucrose-insufficient conditions, while sucrose deficiency under Pi-starvation results in a higher proportion of downregulated genes.

Since plants have evolved complex mechanisms to regulate intracellular Pi status, including Pi sensing via SPX (Syg1, Pho81, and Xpr1) domain-containing proteins, Pi transport through PHTs (phosphate transporters), and transcriptional regulation of Pi-responsive genes [17]. Heat maps analysis were further performed to investigate the role of sucrose in this process. PHT1 family proteins facilitate Pi uptake from soil into root cells across the plasma membrane, with PHF1 (phosphate transporter traffic facilitator 1) assisting their targeting to the plasma membrane. PHO2 (phosphate 2, an ubiquitin E2 conjugase) and NLA (nitrogen limitation adaptation, an ubiquitin E3 ligase) mediate the degradation of PHT1s in the endoplasmic reticulum and the plasma membrane, respectively [17, 69]. Analysis showed that PHT1s and PHF1 were induced in both LP20 and LP2 groups, but the induction was lower in LP2 group (Fig. 4A), suggesting that sucrose plays a regulatory role in this process. NLA1 was downregulated in both LP20 and LP2 groups but upregulated under MS2 group (Fig. 4A), indicating a dual regulation by Pi and sucrose, with Pi likely playing a dominant role. Conversely, the protein of PHO2, involved in PHT1 degradation, was upregulated in response to Pi starvation (Fig. 4A). Other PHTs, essential for Pi transport across endomembranes, were also differentially regulated by Pi starvation and sucrose availability. PHT5 family proteins, located at the vacuole, regulate Pi transport across the tonoplast to maintain cytosolic Pi homeostasis [17, 69]. PHT5;1 and PHT5;3 were upregulated in both LP20 and LP2 groups, though induction was weaker in LP2 (Fig. 4A). Notably, PHT5;3 was downregulated by insufficient sucrose alone in the MS2 condition (Fig. 4A). PHT3 family proteins mediate Pi transport across the mitochondrial membrane for ATP generation [17]. PHT3;2 was upregulated in both LP20 and LP2 groups (Fig. 4A), indicating its key role in Pi response, whereas PHT3;1 was downregulated in MS2 and LP2 (Fig. 4A), suggesting its involvement in sucrose response. PHT2;1 and several PHT4 family members (PHT4;1 to PHT4;5) facilitate Pi transport across the plasmid membrane, while PHT4;6 exports Pi from the Golgi. Among them, PHT2;1, PHT4;3, and PHT4;5 mediate Pi influx, while PHT4;2 facilitates Pi efflux [17, 69]. The results show that PHT2;1 was downregulated in both MS2 and LP2 groups (Fig. 4A), indicating its role in sucrose response. PHT4;1, PHT4;3, and PHT4;4 were similarly downregulated in both LP20 and MS2 groups, with an overlapping effect in LP2 (Fig. 4A), indicating coordinated regulation by Pi and sucrose. PHT4;5 appears particularly sensitive to sucrose, as it was downregulated in the MS2 group (Fig. 4A).

SPX domain-containing proteins sense intracellular P levels and can be classified into four groups: SPX single-domain proteins (SPX1-4), SPX-EXS (PHO1), SPX-MFS (PHT5), and SPX-RING (NLA). In response to Pi starvation, PHR1 (phosphate starvation response 1) and PHL (PHR1-like) proteins are released from their binding with SPX single-domain proteins, inducing the expression of PSR (Pi starvation-responsive) genes, such as IPS1 (induced by phosphate starvation 1), RNS1 (ribonuclease 1), PAP1 (production of anthocyanin pigment 1), and TT8 (transparent testa 8) [69]. PAP1 and TT8 further promote the expression of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes [20]. Heat map analysis showed that SPX1-3 were upregulated in response to Pi starvation, while SPX4 was downregulated (Fig. 4A), consistent with previous reports [69]. This regulation was weakened when sucrose supply was insufficient in LP2 groups (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the expression of SPX single-domain proteins was induced by insufficient sucrose alone in MS2 group (Fig. 4A). PHR1 was downregulated in response to Pi starvation in both LP20 and LP2 groups, with weaker regulation in LP2 group (Fig. 4A). PSR genes (IPS1, RNS1, PAP1, and TT8) were strongly induced by Pi starvation, but their induction was attenuated with insufficient sucrose supply (Fig. 4A). Collectively, sucrose affects intracellular Pi signaling regulation, but in distinct ways depending on the specific molecules involved.

During Pi-starvation condition, primary root growth is inhibited, and the STOP1 (sensitive to proton rhizotoxicity 1) - ALMT1 (aluminum activated malate transporter 1) and PDR2 (phosphate deficiency response 2) - LPR1 (low phosphate root 1) signaling pathways were involved in this regulation [19]. In response to Pi starvation, ALMT1 is induced by STOP1 at the transcriptional level. ALMT1 translocated to the plasma membrane facilitates malate release into the apoplast. Concurrently, LPR1 is released from its interaction with PDR2, further oxidizing Fe2+ to Fe3+ at the apoplast. Fe3+ forms a complex with malate, which further activates ROS production and callose deposition, ultimately inhibiting primary root growth. The Heat map analysis showed that, in response to Pi starvation, ALMT1, ALMT6, and ALMT9 were induced in both LP20 and LP2 groups, but with the induction was lower in LP2 group (Fig. 4B), indicating the regulatory role of sucrose in this process. Interestingly, STOP1 showed no obvious change in response to Pi starvation, but was induced upon insufficient sucrose supply in MS2 group. Although LPR1 and LPR2, both regulate root meristem activity and contribute to primary root growth inhibition, LPR2 was significantly induced in LP20 and LP2 groups, while LPR1 appeared to be downregulated in LP20 and LP2 groups (Fig. 4B). Besides, PHO1 (phosphate 1), which facilitates Pi loading into the xylem in the root, was induced in both LP20 and MS2 groups, with an overlapping effect in LP2 (Fig. 4B), indicating coordinated regulation by Pi and sucrose.

Functional analysis of sucrose-affected Pi-starvation-induced DEGs (differentially expressed genes) based on venn diagram analysis

To further investigate the function of DEGs induced in the Pi-starvation response regulated by sucrose, we performed venn diagram analysis between the LP20/MS20 and LP2/MS2 groups to examine the uniquely and co-expressed DEGs. The analysis revealed 1106 DEGs uniquely upregulated and 961 DEGs uniquely downregulated in the LP20/MS20 group, 387 DEGs uniquely upregulated and 459 DEGs uniquely downregulated in the LP2/MS2 group, and 1279 DEGs co-upregulated and 509 DEGs co-downregulated in both the LP20/MS20 and LP2/MS2 groups (Figs. 5A and 6A). Additionally, in the LP2 vs. LP20 comparison, 172 DEGs were downregulated (Fig. 5B), 59 DEGs were upregulated (Fig. 6B). Moreover, 140 DEGs were upregulated in the LP20/MS20 comparison but downregulated in the LP2/LP20 comparison (Fig. 5B), whereas 98 DEGs were downregulated in the LP20/MS20 comparison but upregulated in the LP2/LP20 comparison (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, KEGG pathway analysis was performed to explore the functional roles of these DEGs, revealing pathways influenced by sucrose supply during the plant’s response to Pi-starvation stress.

ROS regulation and anthocyanin biosynthesis in the Pi-starvation response are downregulated when sucrose supply is insufficient

ROS are known to play a role in the plant response to Pi-starvation [20, 35, 62]. In our analysis, the “flavone and flavonol biosynthesis”, “flavonoid biosynthesis” and “glutathione metabolism” pathways were uniquely enriched in the LP20/MS20 upregulated group (Fig. 5C), as further validated by heat map analysis (Supplemental Fig. 5 A). The “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” pathway was enriched in both the LP20/MS20 and LP2/MS2 upregulated groups (Fig. 5E), with this enrichment also supported by heat map analysis (Supplemental Fig. 5B). Additionally, the “anthocyanin biosynthesis” pathway was enriched in both upregulated groups, with a higher expression level in the LP20/MS20 group compared to the LP2/MS2 group (Figs. 5E-F, Supplemental Fig. 5A). This finding is consistent with the anthocyanin content analysis (Fig. 1C), emphasizing the critical role of sucrose in promoting anthocyanin biosynthesis during Pi-starvation stress. In addition, the “flavone and flavonol biosynthesis” and “flavonoid biosynthesis” pathways were also enriched among the upregulated genes in the LP20/MS20 comparison, but were downregulated in the LP2/MS2 comparison (Supplemental Fig. 6 A), with the enrichment further confirmed by heat map analysis (Supplemental Fig. 6B). These results suggest that ROS regulation and anthocyanin accumulation are less upregulated in response to Pi-starvation stress when sucrose supply is insufficient.

Previous studies have shown that H2O2 accumulates in response to Pi-deficient stress [70]. To confirm this, we performed a DAB staining experiment, which showed that under Pi-starvation conditions with sufficient sucrose, the brown staining intensity was significantly higher than those with insufficient sucrose in the maturation zone of primary root, with no obvious difference in the meristem zone (Supplemental Figs. 7 A-C). We further measured MDA (malondialdehyde) content, a product of membrane lipid peroxidation, under Pi-starvation stress with either sufficient or insufficient sucrose. The results showed that seedlings grown under Pi-starvation conditions with sufficient sucrose supply accumulated significantly higher MDA levels compared to those grown under with insufficient sucrose (Supplemental Fig. 7D). These results indicate that insufficient sucrose supply reduces the activities of plant seedlings to cope with Pi-starvation stress, partially in terms of ROS regulation and anthocyanin biosynthesis.

Photosynthesis is upregulated and ATP invest is decreased in the Pi-starvation response when sucrose supply is insufficient

Previous study showed that photosynthesis-related antenna proteins are downregulated in response to Pi-starvation stress in Cucumis melo L [34]. In our study, transcriptome data revealed that the “photosynthesis” and “porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism” pathways were downregulated in the LP20/MS20 group (Fig. 6C), while the related pathways of “photosynthesis-antenna proteins” and “photosynthesis” were upregulated in the LP2/MS2 group (Fig. 5D). These enrichments were further validated by heat map analysis (Supplemental Fig. 8 A). This finding is consistent with the chlorophyll a and b content analysis, which showed a decrease in chlorophyll a and b content in the LP20 group compared to the MS20 group, while chlorophyll content increased in the LP2 group compared to the MS2 group (Supplemental Figs. 9 A-B). These results indicate that photosynthesis in response to Pi-starvation stress is downregulated when sucrose supply is sufficient, but upregulated when the supply is insufficient.

It is interesting that ATP-consuming pathways, including “Ribosome”, “DNA replication”, “Aminoacyl-rRNA biosynthesis”, and “mismatch repair”, which are involved in DNA and protein synthesis, were uniquely downregulated in the LP20/MS20 group, as validated by heat map analysis (Fig. 6C, Supplemental Fig. 8B). In contrast, ATP-generating pathways, such as “Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis” pathway were uniquely upregulated in the LP20/MS20 group (Fig. 5C, Supplemental Fig. 8 C). Additionally, the “Starch and sucrose metabolism” pathway was upregulated in both the LP20/MS20 and LP2/MS2 groups, although the upregulation was weaker in the LP2/MS2 group (Figs. 5E, Supplemental Fig. 8D). These results indicate that when the supply of sucrose is sufficient, more ATP is allocated to help plant seedlings cope with Pi-starvation stress.

Lipid metabolism is decreased in the Pi-starvation response when sucrose supply is insufficient

Membrane lipid metabolism has been reported to play a role in the Pi-starvation response [2, 67]. In our study, the pathways involved in “Sphingolipid metabolism”, “Galactose metabolism” and “Biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids” was downregulated in both the LP20/MS20 and LP2/MS2 groups, as further confirmed by heat map analysis (Fig. 6E, Supplemental Fig. 10). Further analysis revealed that the “Sphingolipid metabolism” and “Galactose metabolism” pathways were less downregulated in the LP2/MS2 group compared to the LP20/MS20 group (Fig. 6F, Supplemental Fig. 10). Since lipid metabolism is known to be involved in Pi recycling in response to Pi starvation stress, this finding indicates that sucrose availability plays a significant role in the regulation of Pi recycling during the plant response to Pi starvation stress.

Plant-pathogen interaction is downregulated in the Pi-starvation response when sucrose supply is insufficient

The regulation of plant-pathogen interactions is a strategy for plants to cope with Pi starvation stress. Previous studies have shown that microbe-induced plant immunity is suppressed by Pi-starvation [8, 14, 66].

In our study, the “plant-pathogen interaction” pathway was uniquely enriched in the LP20/MS20 upregulated group, as further validated by heat map analysis (Fig. 5C, Supplemental Fig. 11 A), which is consistent with the previous finding that plant immunity is suppressed, and plant-pathogen interaction is enhanced under Pi-starvation stress [66]. However, the “plant-pathogen interaction” pathway was uniquely enriched in the LP2/MS2 downregulated group (Fig. 6D), with the enrichment also supported by heat map analysis (Supplemental Fig. 11 A). This result suggests that the regulation of plant-pathogen interactions in response to Pi-starvation depends on the supply of sucrose.

Additionally, the pathway of “alpha-linolenic metabolism” was enriched in both the LP20/MS20 upregulated group and the LP2/MS2 upregulated group, as further validated by heat map analysis (Fig. 5E, Supplemental Fig. 11B). In addition, the “alpha-linolenic metabolism” pathway was also enriched in the downregulated genes of the LP20/MS20 comparison, but was upregulated in the LP2/LP20 comparison (Supplemental Figs. 11 C-D). Since JA (jasmonic acid) biosynthesis begins with linoleic acid [33], the role of alpha-linolenic acid and its potential involvement in JA-mediated responses to Pi-starvation in conjunction with sucrose supply warrants further investigation.

Other cellular activities in the Pi-starvation response are not significantly affected by sucrose

ABC (ATP-binding cassette) transporters play crucial roles in various cellular processes, including the Pi-starvation response [15, 71]. Glucosinolates, a class of sulfur- and nitrogen-containing secondary metabolites, are involved in plant physiological regulation, including growth and stress responses [1, 3]. In this study, the pathways of “ABC transporters” and “glucosinolate biosynthesis” were both enriched in the co-upregulated group of LP20/MS20 and LP2/MS2, as further confirmed by heat map analysis (Fig. 5E, Supplemental Figs. 12 A-B). The influence of sucrose on the response of ABC transporters and glucosinolates to Pi-starvation stress requires further investigation.

Valine, leucine, and isoleucine belong to BCAAs (branched-chain amino acids), and the BCAAs pathway was uniquely enriched in the LP2/MS2 downregulated group, and also in the co-downregulated group of LP20/MS20 and LP2/MS2, as further supported by heat map analysis (Figs. 6D-E, Supplemental Fig. 12 C). While BCAAs have been shown to regulate salt stress response by promoting vitamin B5 biosynthesis in rice [64], the role of BCAAs in Pi-starvation response and how sucrose modulates this process warrants further exploration.

The pathway of “MAPK signaling pathway-plant” was enriched in both the LP2/MS2 upregulated and downregulated groups (Figs. 5D and 6D), with the enrichment also supported by heat map analysis (Supplemental Fig. 12D). Previous studies have reported the involvement of the MAPK signaling pathway in Pi-starvation responses [4, 77, 83]. More detailed studies are needed to clarify the role of MAPK signaling in Pi-starvation responses regulated by sucrose. Additionally, the “selenocompound metabolism” pathway was uniquely enriched in the LP2/MS2 upregulated group (Fig. 5D). Selenium and selenocompounds are known to play a role in ROS scavenging [26], and the regulation of this process by sucrose in plant Pi-starvation responses warrants further investigation.

Circadian rhythm in plant coordinates growth with various environmental signals, including photoperiod perception and stress responses [53, 74]. In the KEGG analysis, the “circadian rhythm” pathway was enriched in the LP20/MS20 upregulated group, as further validated by heat map analysis (Fig. 5C, Supplemental Fig. 12E). Further studies are needed to explore how circadian rhythms respond to Pi-starvation and how this process is influenced by sucrose.

qRT-PCR validation of RNA-Seq data

To verify the quality of the expression levels from the RNA-seq data, qRT-PCR was used to detect the expression levels of nine DEGs, including six Pi-starvation upregulated genes, three Pi-starvation downregulated genes. The results showed a similar expression tendency to the RNA-seq data (Supplemental Fig. 13), indicating the reliability of RNA-seq data.

Phenotypical ananlysis of PIF3 responding to Pi-starvation stress

Light plays a key role in both photosynthesis and signal transduction in plants [48]. PIF3, a bHLH transcription factor, is well known as a negative regulator that represses photomorphogenesis during light signal transduction [32]. Our RNA-seq data and subsequent qRT-PCR showed that PIF3 was upregulated in response to Pi starvation under both sucrose-sufficient and sucrose-insufficient condition (Supplemental Fig. 13 F). Phenotypic analysis revealed that the loss-of-function of PIF3 mutants pif3-1 and pif3-3 exhibited retarded growth under Pi starvation, with a decrease in fresh weight and primary root growth (Figs. 7A-C), while the gain-of-function mutant PIF3-OE displayed enhanced growth in response to Pi-starvation (Figs. 7D-F). Previous studies have shown that PIF3 represses chloroplast development and chlorophyll biosynthesis in Arabidopsis and buried seedlings [40, 41, 63]. Whether the change of chlorophyll content under Pi-starvation stress is regulated by PIF3 and how sucrose regulates this process requires further investigation.

Discussion

Endogenous sucrose is involved in Pi-starvation response

Sucrose sensing and signaling play crucial roles in the regulation of plant growth and development. Sucrose has been reported linked to Pi-starvation response from the aspects of increased degradation of phospholipids and increased intracellular Pi concentration in plant cells in response to sucrose-starvation [22, 23]. The expression of PSI (Pi-starvation-induced) genes is also regulated by sugar signaling, with PSI gene expression being influenced by sucrose concentration in Arabidopsis seedlings [24].

In response to Pi-starvation, SUC2 (sucrose transporter 2), a high affinity sucrose/proton symporter expressed in phloem companion cells and root vascular tissues, plays a key role in Pi-starvation response. Overexpression of SUC2 results in a Pi-starvation-sensitive phenotype, while defective of SUC2 lines exhibit resistance to Pi-starvation stress [28]. While sucrose and Pi interact with each other, the detailed regulatory mechanisms between them remain poorly understood. This study revealed genes that are uniquely or co-expressed in response to both sucrose and Pi, demonstrating that sucrose supply regulates anthocyanin accumulation, plant-pathogen interactions, and both plant growth and Pi content under Pi-starvation conditions. Sucrose in plant comes from photosynthesis, with energy provided by light and carbon supplied by CO2. Sucrose has been reported to influence the C-status in plants during nitrogen responses that the downregulated of nitrate transporter genes during the dark period can be restored by the supplementation of sucrose [30]. Similarly, the downregulated of PSI gene during the dark period can also be restored by the supplementation of sucrose [24, 38], suggesting that sucrose plays a role in regulating the C-status of plants in response to Pi-starvation stress. In our study, the exogenous supply of sucrose affected the endogenous soluble sugar levels during the Pi-starvation response (Fig. 1E). The impact of environmental factors such as light or increased CO2 concentrations on plant Pi-starvation responses requires further investigation.

It was observed that MS2 contained significantly lower Pi levels than MS20 under Pi-sufficient conditions (Fig. 1D), which suggests a potential crosstalk between sucrose availability and Pi homeostasis. Sucrose plays central roles in plant growth and development, serving not only as a metabolic substrate for glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and mitochondrial respiration to generate ATP, but also as a long-distance carbohydrate transported from source to sink tissues [5, 11, 78]. Pi uptake, in contrast, is primarily mediated by the PHT1 family of H+-coupled H2​PO4− transporters located at the plasma membrane, an ATP-dependent process driven by the proton gradient established through H+-ATPase activity [50, 60, 84]. Thus, differences in sucrose-derived energy supply could partially explain the lower Pi accumulation observed in MS2 compared to MS20. Beyond its role as an energy source, sucrose may also function as a signaling molecule; however, its contribution to Pi homeostasis in this context remains unclear. Pi may influence sucrose homeostasis through modulation of photosynthesis efficiency [56, 57]. Consistently, in our study, photosynthesis was downregulated in the LP20/MS20 group (Fig. 6C), whereas it was upregulated in the LP2/MS2 group (Fig. 5D), potentially reflecting a compensatory response to limited sucrose availability. Collectively, these observations highlight a complex homeostatic relationship between sucrose supply and Pi accumulation that warrants further investigation.

Anthocyanin accumulation in response to Pi-starvation stress is regulated by sucrose

In response to Pi-starvation, anthocyanin accumulation has been recognized as a marker of Pi-deficiency in plants [20]. In our study, Pi-starvation induced anthocyanin accumulation in the LP20 group, but this accumulation was absent in the LP2 group, suggesting that sucrose plays a critical role in anthocyanin biosynthesis under Pi-starvation stress (Figs. 1C and 5F). Anthocyanins are a class of secondary metabolites, collectively known as flavonoids, with more than 635 types identified in plants [6, 65]. These compounds accumulate in the vacuoles of cells in fruits, leaves, and flowers, giving plants a wide range of colors, from red to purple and blue, depending on the intracellular pH [6]. Two types of genes control anthocyanins biosynthesis: structural genes and regulatory genes. Structural genes encode enzymes that catalyze anthocyanin biosynthesis, such as CHS (chalcone synthase) and DFR (dihydroflavonol 4 reductase). Regulatory genes encode transcription factors, including MYB (ν-myb avian myeloblastosis viral oncogene homolog), bHLH (basic helix-loop-helix), and WD40 (WD-40 has a scaffolding function) [20, 76]. Pi-starvation induced anthocyanin accumulation has been previously reported. For instance, SPX4 negatively regulates this accumulation by interacting with the R2R3 MYB protein PAP1, which inhibits DFR expression [20]. PHR1 has been identified as a positive regulator of Pi-starvation responses. In Arabidopsis, PHR1 promotes anthocyanin accumulation by upregulating anthocyanin biosynthesis genes [36]; Similarly, in tomato, SlPHL1 (Solanum lycopersicum PHR1-like 1) also promoted Pi-starvation-induced anthocyanin accumulation by activating anthocyanin biosynthesis genes [72].

Based on the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway (Supplemental Fig. 14 A), the expression patterns of genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis were analyzed using heat maps (Supplemental Fig. 14B). The analysis revealed that the expression of structural genes, such as CHI, DFR, ANS, CHS, and F3H, was significantly induced under Pi starvation, with this induction attenuated when sucrose supply was limited (Supplemental Fig. 14B, Supplemental Table 4). A similar pattern was observed for the positive regulators PAP1 and TT8, whereas the expression of the negative regulator MYBL2 exhibited an opposite trend (Supplemental Fig. 14B). To further validate these observations, qRT-PCR experiments were conducted, and the results confirmed the trends observed in the heat maps (Supplemental Fig. 14 C). A previous study demonstrated that the Pi-responsive transcription factor PHR1 regulates the expression of PAP1 and TT8 during Pi starvation [20], suggesting a potential link between Pi availability, sucrose supply, and anthocyanin biosynthesis. Taken together, the molecular analysis of gene expression aligns with the anthocyanin content analysis, indicating that under Pi starvation, an insufficient sucrose supply impairs the regulation of genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis.

Energy and plant Pi-starvation response

Energy is essential for plant response to various stress responses. In response to Pi-starvation stress, high affinity Pi-transporter, such as PHT1;1 and PHT1;4, facilitate the uptake of Pi into cells, accompanied by H+, which requires plasma membrane H+-ATPase to provide the necessary motive force [81]. In our study, the upregulation of PHT1;1 and PHT1;4 became weaker when sucrose supply was insufficient (Supplemental Figs. 13 A-B). RNA-seq data revealed that energy-consuming physiological processes such as DNA replication and repair, as well as protein biosynthesis, were downregulated uniquely in response to Pi-starvation stress when sucrose supply was sufficient (Fig. 6C). In contrast, energy-generating physiological processes such as glycolysis and gluconeogenesis were upregulated under conditions of sufficient sucrose supply to cope with Pi-starvation stress (Fig. 5C). Our findings suggest complex energy-processing pathways, as evidenced by the upregulation of starch and sucrose metabolism pathways in response to Pi-starvation stress, regardless of sucrose supply. However, this regulation became weaker when sucrose supply was limited (Figs. 5E-F). Additionally, plant energy is derived from photosynthesis, and previous studies have shown that photosynthesis-related antenna proteins are downregulated in response to Pi-starvation stress [34]. In our study, photosynthesis-related genes were indeed downregulated when sucrose supply was sufficient under Pi-starvation condition (Fig. 6C). Interestingly, when sucrose supply was insufficient, photosynthesis-related genes were upregulated (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, BCAAs, such as leucine, isoleucine, and valine, can be catalyzed by BCKDH (branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase) to contribute to energy production during sugar-starvation [18]. However, in our study, BCAA degradation was downregulated under both sucrose-sufficient and sucrose-insufficient conditions in response to Pi-starvation stress (Figs. 6E-F). This may be due to the timing of seedling collection for transcriptome analysis, as protein degradation had already occurred, leaving insufficient raw materials of BCAAs to support the reaction. The interaction between Pi-starvation responses and energy processing in plant cells requires further investigation to fully understand these complex regulatory mechanisms.

PIF3 in Pi-starvation response

In this study, we demonstrated that PIF3 is upregulated at the transcriptional level in response to Pi-starvation (Supplemental Fig. 13 F). The PIF3 loss-of-function mutants, pif3-1 and pif3-3, exhibited a Pi-starvation-sensitive phenotype, while the PIF3 gain-of-function mutant (PIF3-OE) displayed enhanced tolerance to Pi-starvation (Figs. 7A-F). However, the precise relationship between PIF3 and sucrose’s role in the Pi-starvation response remains unclear.

To further explore the regulatory role of PIF3 in the Pi-starvation response, we measured the Pi content in PIF3 loss-of-function and gain-of-function mutant seedlings. The results revealed that, under Pi starvation conditions, Pi accumulation per seedling was significantly decreased in the pif3-1 and pif3-3 mutants, whereas no significant change in Pi content was observed in the PIF3-OE seedlings (Supplemental Fig. 15). Further transcript-level analysis revealed no additional upregulation of PIF3 expression in the PIF3-OE seedlings in response to Pi-starvation stress (Supplemental Fig. 19E). These findings are generally in accordance with the Pi-starvation phenotype analysis (Figs. 7A-F), supporting the notion that PIF3 plays a role in the plant Pi-starvation response.

To investigate whether the PIF3-mediated Pi-starvation response is linked to endogenous sugar reallocation or sugar signaling, we performed sugar content analysis in both shoots and roots of PIF3 loss-of-function and gain-of-function mutant seedlings. The results showed no significant difference in Pi-starvation-induced soluble sugar accumulation between the PIF3 loss-of-function mutants (pif3-1, pif3-3), the gain-of-function PIF3-OE seedlings, and the wild-type Col-0, in both shoots and roots (Supplemental Figs. 16 A-B). These results suggest that the PIF3-mediated Pi-starvation response may not be directly associated with endogenous sugar levels or sugar reallocation.

In response to Pi-starvation stress, ROS-related pathways and ROS levels increase in our study (Figs. 5C, E, Supplemental Figs. 7 A-D). Previous studies have shown that PIF3 and PIF1 physically interact with HY5 (elongated hypocotyl 5) and HYH (HY5 homology) to form a transcriptional module, which regulates light and ROS signaling under both dark and light conditions [9]. However, these factors exert antagonistic effects on ROS signaling in the light, where PIF3 and PIF1 repress ROS-responsive gene expression, while HY5 and HYH activate it [9]. To examine whether PIF3 plays a role in ROS signaling during the Pi-starvation response and its relationship with HY5, we analyzed the expression of HY5 and ROS-responsive genes in PIF3 loss-of-function and gain-of-function mutant seedlings. The results showed that HY5 was downregulated in response to Pi starvation in Col-0 and PIF3-OE, while its expression was upregulated in pif3-1 and pif3-3 mutants (Supplemental Fig. 17 A). ROS-responsive genes, such as ZAT10 and ERF4, were induced at the transcriptional level by Pi starvation, with a stronger induction observed in pif3-1 and pif3-3 seedlings, and a weaker induction in PIF3-OE seedlings, compared to the wild-type Col-0 seedlings (Supplemental Figs. 17B-C).

Additionally, PIF3 has been shown to be involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis in coordination with HY5 [61]. In our study, under Pi-starvation conditions, the expression of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes CHS and F3H (Flavanone 3-hydroxylase) was more strongly induced in pif3-1 and pif3-3 seedlings, and less induced in PIF3-OE seedlings, compared to wild-type Col-0 seedlings (Supplemental Figs. 17D-E). To further elucidate the role of PIF3 in the Pi-starvation response, we measured MDA levels, which are commonly used as indicators of oxidative stress, in PIF3 loss-of-function and gain-of-function mutant seedlings. The results indicated that seedlings of Col-0, pif3-1, pif3-3, and PIF3-OE accumulated significantly higher MDA levels under Pi-starvation conditions, but no significant differences were observed between Col-0, pif3-1, and pif3-3 in both shoots and roots (Supplemental Figs. 18 A-B). However, MDA content in roots under Pi-starvation was much more attenuated in roots when sucrose supply was insufficient (Supplemental Figs. 18 A-B). The role of sucrose, and the role of PIF3 in Pi-starvation response, as well as its interplay with ROS and sugar signaling, requires further investigation.

Although PIF3 has been reported to participate in sucrose-induced hypocotyl elongation in Arabidopsis [39], its role in other sugar-related biological processes appears limited. For instance, sucrose and blue light can both induce anthocyanin biosynthesis, but only blue light also triggers PIF3 upregulation in detached Lycium ruthenicum leaves [80]. Similarly, in Arabidopsis, cytokinin signaling promotes anthocyanin biosynthesis by modulating the redox state of the photosynthetic electron transport chain through sucrose signaling, but not through the HY5-PIF3-mediated light signaling pathway [13]. Collectively, these observations, along with our results (Supplemental Figs. 16 A-B), indicate that sugar and PIF3 both regulate Pi-starvation responses, but likely act through independent mechanisms.

Taken together, our study provides new insights into the relationship between Pi-starvation responses and the regulation of various plant physiological activities. These findings supplement and expand on previous studies, but further research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying these relationships.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that sucrose plays a pivotal role in regulating plant responses to Pi starvation. Integrative transcriptomic and physiological analyses revealed that sucrose availability affects key processes, including anthocyanin biosynthesis, photosynthesis, ATP-dependent metabolism, and plant–pathogen interactions. The upregulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis commonly induced by Pi starvation was markedly reduced under sucrose deficiency, resulting in lower anthocyanin and ROS accumulation. Photosynthetic regulation also depended on sucrose availability, with chlorophyll content and photosynthetic activity displaying opposite trends under sufficient versus limited sucrose conditions. Moreover, both the downregulation of ATP-consuming anabolic pathways and the upregulation of ATP-generating catabolic pathways under Pi starvation were attenuated when sucrose supply was limited. Plant–pathogen interactions were similarly weakened, and the Pi-starvation-induced gene PIF3 appeared to regulate Pi responses through a sucrose-independent mechanism.

Overall, this study provides new insights into how plants integrate sucrose status to coordinate responses to Pi starvation, revealing complex interactions among nutrient signaling, metabolic regulation, and stress adaptation. These findings not only deepen our understanding of Pi-starvation mechanisms but also highlight the dynamic relationship between sucrose and Pi availability in shaping plant stress responses.

Methods

Plant materials and growth condition

Arabidopsis thaliana lines used in this study are listed as follows: Columbia-0 (Col-0) was used for RNA-seq analysis; pif3-1 (Salk_030753), pif3-3, and PIF3-OE were kindly provided by Yanguo (China Agricultural University). Mutant identification was conducted as previously described [31, 51]. Briefly, pif3-3 is a deletion mutant with a deleted fragment spanning from position 3,074,453 to 3,076,965 upstream of PIF3 coding sequence, and pif3-1 is a T-DNA insertion mutant (Supplemental Fig. 19 A). Genotyping of the pif3-3 mutant was confirmed using primers within the deleted region; lack of PCR amplification indicated a homozygous deletion (Supplemental Fig. 19B). For pif3-1, genotyping was performed using the T-DNA border primer (LBa1) in combination with primers targeting the PIF3 genomic sequence (Supplemental Fig. 19 C). Additionally, transcript analysis revealed reduced PIF3 expression in both pif3-1 and pif3-3 mutants, and elevated expression in the PIF3-OE line (Supplemental Fig. 19D). These mutants were subsequently used for phenotype analysis in response to Pi-starvation. Pi-sufficient and Pi-deficient MS (Murashige and Skoog) medium were prepared as follows. Stock solutions were first prepared: (1) high-concentration substances: KNO3 (100×, 19%, w/v), NH4NO3 (200×, 33%, w/v), KH2PO4 (200×, 3.4%, w/v), MgSO4 (200×, 3.6%, w/v), CaCl2 (200×, 6.6%, w/v); (2) trace substances (100×): 100 mL ddH2O was dissolved with 223 mg of MnSO4.4H2O, 8.3 mg of KI, 62 mg of H3BO4, 86 mg of ZnSO4.7H2O, 20 µL stock of CuSO4.5H2O (200×, 1.25%, w/v), 20 µL stock of CoCl2.6H2O (200×, 1.25%, w/v), and 200 µL stock of Na2MoO4.2H2O (200×, 1.25%, w/v); (3) ferric salt (100×): 100 mL ddH2O was dissolved with 280 mg of FeSO4.7H2O and 375 mg of Na2EDTA. Pi-sufficient MS medium was prepared including all substances listed above. Pi-deficient MS medium was prepared by the extrusion of KH2PO4, but the supplementation of KCl (200×, 1.86%, w/v). Sucrose was added at a concentration of 2% (w/v) for sucrose-sufficient conditions or 0.2% (w/v) for sucrose-insufficient conditions, and the pH was adjusted to 5.8. Phytagel was added at 0.3% (w/v) for horizontal grown seedlings and 0.5% (w/v) for vertical grown seedlings.

Seeds were sterilized with a disinfectant solution containing 0.2% (v/v) triton X-100 and 8% (v/v) NaClO for 15 min, and then rinsed three times with sterile water. The sterilized seeds were sown on indicated MS medium and grown either horizontally or vertically in a controlled growth chamber at 22℃ under 16-h light/8-h dark cycle with light intensity of 100 µmol m− 2s− 1.

RNA-Seq and data preprocessing

Five-day-old seedlings were transferred individually to different mediums: MS20, LP20, MS2, and LP2. After 5 d of growth, seedlings were collected under liquid nitrogen condition for the subsequent RNA-seq experiment.

The RNA-seq experiment included sample detection, cDNA library construction, library quality control, and machine sequencing, all performed by BMK, Qingdao, China. Sequencing was conducted using the Illumina NovaSeq platform, and clean reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis thaliana reference genome (TAIR10.1) using HISAT2 software, followed by assembly with StringTie for further analysis. Gene expression levels were normalized using FPKM (Fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped). Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate reproducibility between samples.

Analysis of differential gene expression and gene annotation

RNA-seq data analysis was performed on the BMKCloud platform (www.biocloud.net). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software. FC (fold change) ≥ 1.5 and FDR (false discovery rate) < 0.01 were used as screening criteria to screen DEGs. The expressed genes were functionally annotated using the KEGG database. KEGG pathways were defined as significantly enriched pathways or terms with a q-value < 0.05.

Validation of DEGs by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Code No. DP432, TIANGEN Company, China) following the manufacturer’s instruction. RNA quality was assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ASP-2680, ACTGene, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Code No. RR047A, TaKaRa) following the manufacturer’s instruction. qRT-PCR was carried out using the TB Green Premix Ex kit (Code No. RR420A, TaKaRa) following the manufacturer’s instruction, with reactions performed on a LightCycler 480 II instrument (Roche). The primers used are listed in Supplemental Table 5.

Anthocyanin content determination

Anthocyanin content was analyzed as previously described with slight modifications [29]. Briefly, seedlings (up to 100 mg per aliquot) were collected into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes containing a steel ball (2.8 mm in diameter). The seedlings were homogenized under liquid nitrogen. Then, 500 µL of extraction buffer (1% HCl-EtOH, v/v) was added, and the mixture was extracted using ultrasonic treatment for 20 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 10 min, and the supernatant was measured at A530 nm using a microplate reader. Anthocyanin content was calculated based on a standard curve.

Chlorophyll a and b content determination

Chlorophyll a and b content was analyzed as previously described with slight modifications [42]. In brief, seedlings (up to 100 mg per aliquot) were collected into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes containing a steel ball (2.8 mm in diameter). The seedlings were homogenized under liquid nitrogen. To the homogenate, 800 µL of ethanol was added, and the mixture was extracted using ultrasonic treatment for 10 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 10 min, and the supernatant was measured at A665 nm and A649 nm using a microplate reader. Chlorophyll contents were calculated using the formula:

Chlorophyll a content (mg/L) = 13.95×A665-6.88×A649;

Chlorophyll b content (mg/L) = 24.96×A649-7.32×A665.

MDA content determination

MDA content was analyzed as previously described with slight modifications [42]. Briefly, seedlings (up to 100 mg per aliquot) were collected into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes containing a steel ball (2.8 mm in diameter). The seedlings were homogenized under liquid nitrogen. To the homogenate, 400 µL of extraction buffer (10% TCA, w/v) was added immediately, and the mixture was extracted using ultrasonic treatment for 10 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 10 min, and the supernatant was used for detection at a ratio of 300 µL of supernatant to 300 µL of reaction solution (0.6% TBA dissolved in 10% TCA). The reaction mixture was incubated at 90℃ for 15 min, then cooled to room temperature and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 10 min. The supernatant was measured at A532 nm and A450 nm using a microplate reader. MDA content was calculated using the formula:

MDA content (µmol/L) = 6.45×A532-0.56×A450.

DAB staining

DAB staining was performed as previously described with modifications [70]. Eight-day-old seedlings were incubated overnight in the staining solution (1 mg/mL DAB dissolved in 10 mM MES-NaOH, pH 5.5) in the dark. The seedlings were then washed twice with ethanol, with each wash lasting for 30 min, and subsequently stored in 50% ethanol for further microscopic analysis. Images of DAB-stained root tips were captured under bright-field illumination using an optical microscope (Nikon eclipse 55i).

Pi content determination

Pi content was analyzed as previously described [10]. Briefly, seedlings grown under Pi-deficient or Pi-sufficient and sucrose-deficient or sucrose-sufficient conditions were collected (no more than 100 mg per aliquot) into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes containing a steel ball (2.8 mm in diameter). The seedlings were homogenized under liquid nitrogen. To the homogenate, 100 µL extraction buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) and 900 µL glacial acetic acid were added, mixed and incubated at 42℃ for 30 min. The tubes were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 10 min, and the supernatant was used for detection. A 150 µL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 350 µL of reaction solution (2.8 mM (NH4)6Mo7O24, 0.44 M H2SO4, 1.4% ascorbic acid). After incubating at 42℃ for 30 min, Pi content was measured at A820 nm by a microplate reader. Pi content was calculated based on a standard curve of KH2PO4.

Soluble sugar content determination

The soluble sugar content was quantified as previously described [44]. Briefly, seedlings grown under Pi-deficient or Pi-sufficient and sucrose-deficient or sucrose-sufficient conditions were collected (no more than 100 mg per aliquot), and placed into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes containing a steel ball (2.8 mm in diameter). The seedlings were homogenized under liquid nitrogen. To the homogenate, 800 µL of ddH2O was added, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at 95℃ for 5 min, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 10 min. The sample was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was mixed with the reaction solution (1 mg/mL anthrone dissolved in 74% H2SO4) at a 1:9 (v/v) ratio. After reaction at 95℃ for 10 min, the soluble sugar content was measured at A630 nm using a microplate reader. The soluble sugar content was then calculated based on a glucose standard curve.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The Image J software was used to measure root length of the seedlings. The Graphpad Prism software was used to analyze the data using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, or a two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test.
