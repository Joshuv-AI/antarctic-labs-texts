Evaluation of the real-world safety of Canakinumab in the treatment of systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis in children aged 0–16: a comprehensive analysis based on FAERS data
Authors: Peng Xiao, Bingqi Wei, Guilong Zhang, Xinyue Zhang, Hejia Wan, Yifan Qi, Liu Chen, Yong Huang, Jianjia Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCanakinumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody that primarily targets interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and is primarily indicated for systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA). Since it is predominantly used in children aged 0–16 years, exploring its safety in real-world settings is of paramount importance.MethodsThis study evaluated the real-world safety of Canakinumab in clinical practice by analyzing adverse events (AEs) associated with the use of the drug in the 0–16 year age group with sJIA from 2014 to 2023, using data retrieved from the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS). Disproportionality analysis of relevant AEs was performed via four methods: the reporting odds ratio (ROR), the proportional reporting ratio (PRR), the multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS), and the Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN). Additionally, the Weibull shape parameter (WSP) test distribution was employed to model the risk of AEs over time.ResultsA total of 439 adverse events (AEs) were included in this study. Positive signals were identified for several adverse reactions listed on the drug label, including inappropriate schedule of product administration, incorrect dose administered, rash, illness, gastroenteritis, thrombocytopenia, lymphadenopathy, leukopenia, transaminases increased, aspartate aminotransferase increased and alanine aminotransferase increased. Furthermore, the study identified potential adverse reactions not mentioned on the label, such as hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, COVID-19, nasal congestion and increased serum ferritin. These findings underscore the importance of AE monitoring, particularly emphasizing the value of early detection.ConclusionThis FAERS-based pharmacovigilance study provides preliminary safety data on the use of Canakinumab in children aged 0–16 years with sJIA, confirming several known adverse reactions and revealing additional potential risks. These findings offer clinicians valuable safety information for the use of Canakinumab in the treatment of sJIA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12969-025-01176-2.Keywords: Systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Canakinumab, FAERS, Real-world safety, Comprehensive analysis

Introduction

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the chronic rheumatic disease in children, encompassing a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by persistent arthritis of unknown etiology [1]. Among its subtypes, systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) represents a distinct clinical and biological entity [2]. sJIA is characterized not only by arthritis but also by systemic manifestations such as quotidian fever, evanescent rash, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and serositis, which reflect excessive activation of interleukin 1β (IL-1β)–mediated inflammatory responses [3]. This cytokine-driven autoinflammatory process distinguishes sJIA from other JIA subtypes and provides a clear rationale for therapies targeting IL-1β. Therefore, in-depth research on sJIA is essential to improve disease prevention and management and to reduce the burden on affected children and their families.

Canakinumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody that primarily targets and neutralizes IL-1β, a key cytokine involved in immune and inflammatory responses [4]. Canakinumab was first approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2009 for the treatment of cryopyrin-associated periodic syndrome (CAPS) and was later approved in 2013 for the treatment of sJIA [5, 6]. According to one study, 84% of sJIA patients treated with Canakinumab achieved the ACR Pedi 30 criteria by day 15, demonstrating promising efficacy [7]. Another five-year study reported that after two years of treatment, 62% of patients met the ACR Pedi 50 response criteria, 54% met the ACR Pedi 90 criteria, and 32% achieved clinical remission [8]. In summary, Canakinumab has shown high efficacy in the treatment of sJIA, particularly in patients with systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis, significantly alleviating the condition and achieving a high clinical response rate. However, existing research indicates that the use of Canakinumab in the treatment of sJIA may lead to certain adverse events(AEs) [9]. Therefore, further exploration of the AEs associated with Canakinumab in the treatment of sJIA is essential to provide valuable insights for safe clinical use.

The FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database is a public resource used by the FDA to monitor the safety of drugs and biological products [10]. The database is primarily employed to detect potential safety signals, such as a significant increase in reports of adverse reactions following the use of a specific drug. FAERS has been widely used to identify AEs associated with various medications, including reports of adverse reactions to immune checkpoint inhibitors [11]. In this study, FAERS data were mined for safety signals related to Canakinumab, with a focus on the 0–16 age group diagnosed with sJIA. The goal of this study was to identify adverse reactions associated with Canakinumab and provide feasible safety references for its effective use in the prevention and treatment of sJIA.

Materials and methods

Data sources, cleaning, and study design

The data used in this study were sourced from the FAERS (https://fis.fda.gov/extensions/FPD-QDE-FAERS/FPD-QDE-FAERS.html), which is a publicly accessible and anonymized database [12]. As a result, no ethical approval was required for this research. The analysis included AE reports where Canakinumab was the primary suspected drug. Both the brand name and the generic names “Canakinumab” and “Ilaris” were searched, covering the period from the first quarter of 2014 to the fourth quarter of 2023. The patient population was limited to those with sJIA, with an age restriction of 0–16 years. The FAERS database does not provide detailed subclassifications of JIA; therefore, it was not possible to differentiate sJIA from other JIA subtypes based solely on indication codes. As Canakinumab is specifically approved by the FDA for the treatment of systemic JIA and its use in other JIA subtypes is uncommon, all FAERS reports coded as “juvenile idiopathic arthritis” associated with Canakinumab were treated as sJIA-related cases in this analysis. This case classification strategy is consistent with established practices in prior FAERS-based pharmacovigilance studies and ensures methodological comparability across studies. It also reflects real-world prescribing patterns of Canakinumab as documented in post-marketing surveillance data. To maintain methodological transparency and scientific rigor, this potential indication misclassification was explicitly acknowledged and addressed in both the data interpretation and limitations sections of the manuscript. Data cleaning was performed to remove duplicate reports. To reduce duplicate-report bias, deduplication followed FDA guidance using a two-step rule: for each Case ID, the record with the latest FDA received date was retained; if multiple entries shared the same Case ID and FDA received date, the one with the largest Primary ID was kept and the others removed. The specific deduplication rules and examples are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The AE terms reported were standardized via version 26.1 of the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) to ensure the reliability of the statistical analysis [13]. Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was used to characterize AEs associated with Canakinumab treatment for sJIA. Signal detection for AEs in the 0-16-year-old sJIA patient population treated with Canakinumab was conducted via the following four major algorithms: reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS), and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN). The criteria for these algorithms are listed in Supplementary Table 2 [14]. As per FAERS methodology, Canakinumab was compared against all other drugs in the database to identify disproportionality signals. To evaluate the temporal characteristics of AEs, we conducted a time-to-onset analysis using the Cumulative Risk Function (CRF), which estimates the cumulative probability of AE occurrence over time. This function was applied to visualize the dynamic risk pattern of Canakinumab-associated AEs throughout the observation period. Additionally, the Weibull shape parameter (WSP) test was employed to model and detect changes in the incidence of AEs over time [15]. All the analyses were performed via R software, version 4.2.2.

Results

The primary age group was 6–12 years, representing 40.3% of all reports. Most reports were submitted by consumers (32.1%), health professionals (29.6%), and physicians (27.3%). A majority of the reports (60.1%) originated from the United States. A detailed distribution of other reporting countries is provided in Supplementary Table 3. Time-to-onset analysis of AEs

An analysis of AEs associated with Canakinumab was conducted, summarizing the time-to-onset characteristics of the events. A total of 204 cases were reported, with the overall mean time-to-onset for AEs being 195 days (β = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.73–0.91), as shown in Table 4. Among these, 116 cases involved female patients, with a mean time-to-onset of 195 days (β = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.70–0.94), whereas 85 cases involved male patients, with a mean time-to-onset of 174 days (β = 0.84, 95% CI = 0.70–0.98). The distribution suggests that AEs exhibit an early-onset declining pattern.

Discussion

The FDA approved Canakinumab for the treatment of sJIA in 2013. The drug works by inhibiting IL-1β, reducing inflammatory responses, and helping to control disease activity [6]. This study evaluated the AEs associated with Canakinumab after its market approval, covering the period from 2014 to 2023. Overall, the number of reported AEs showed a gradual upward trend over time, accompanied by short-term fluctuations during the observation period. As shown in Fig. 2, the number of AE reports decreased in 2018–2019 but increased again after 2020. This pattern reflects normal fluctuations in reporting activity and Canakinumab utilization, together with strengthened pharmacovigilance awareness during the COVID-19 period, when global monitoring of immune-related and inflammatory conditions intensified. Such temporal variations are common in spontaneous reporting systems and do not indicate any change in the intrinsic safety profile of Canakinumab.

Given that sJIA affects the 0–16 age group, this study also limited its population to this age range [16]. In terms of sex, 58.1% of AEs occurred in female sJIA patients, whereas 40.3% occurred in males. This finding indicates a modest female predominance among the reported cases. However, the FAERS database does not provide sufficient clinical detail to determine the underlying cause of this difference. Previous literature has suggested that certain autoimmune and autoinflammatory conditions show sex-related variations, but evidence for such patterns in sJIA remains inconsistent [17]. Therefore, this observation should be interpreted with caution. Further analysis of the reporting sources revealed that 32.1% of AEs were reported by consumers, ranking first. This suggests that healthcare professionals have delays in reporting AEs. Additionally, 60.1% of the reports were from the United States, indicating that future efforts should focus on enhancing the reporting of AEs from other countries or regions to improve the reliability and applicability of the results.

This study identified 15 relevant AEs through four signal detection methods: ROR, PRR, MGPS, and BCPNN. Among these, 11 AEs had been previously reported on the label.

First, one of the clinical features of sJIA includes rash and lymphadenopathy, so the results suggest that AEs related to rash, lymphadenopathy, and illness align with the nature of sJIA [18]. This finding also validates the reliability of the screening algorithms used in this study.

Second, in patients with sJIA who develop MAS, clinical management sometimes requires dose escalation of Canakinumab under medical supervision. Such treatment adjustments are often accompanied by hematologic abnormalities, including reductions in white blood cell and platelet counts [19, 20].These hematologic findings are consistent with previously reported post-marketing safety data, thereby reinforcing the internal consistency and clinical credibility of the FAERS-based findings. This also aligns with the identified adverse event “Inappropriate schedule of product administration, incorrect dose administered” indicating that attention should be given to the timing of medication administration and time-to-onset patterns in clinical practice.

Third, gastroenteritis has been reported as a common adverse event in patients receiving Canakinumab [21]. The clinical trials have revealed that a small number of patients experience gastroenteritis after the use of Canakinumab [21]. Hügle B et al.conducted a retrospective analysis of data from the German Center for Pediatric and Adolescent Rheumatology from 2010 to 2015, and among 82 patients diagnosed with sJIA, one was diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease and had a history of Canakinumab use [9]. Therefore, it is important to remain vigilant about potential gastrointestinal reactions when Canakinumab is used as an IL-1 antagonist in disease treatment.As a monoclonal antibody targeting IL-1β, Canakinumab transiently influences intestinal mucosal immunity and gut microbiota balance, which explain the occurrence of mild and self-limited gastrointestinal symptoms observed during treatment [22, 23].

Additionally, Canakinumab is a monoclonal antibody with minimal impact on liver metabolism; thus, the mechanism by which it causes elevated ALT and AST levels during treatment remains unclear [24]. As Arostegui JI et al. reported, in a study involving nine patients treated with Canakinumab for hyperimmunoglobulin D with periodic fever syndrome, one patient presented significantly elevated ALT levels [25]. Although the precise mechanism remains unclear, the repeated observation of elevated liver enzymes in both clinical reports and AE data suggests that Canakinumab may be associated with mild hepatic effects. These findings underscore the importance of continued liver function monitoring and further investigation into the underlying mechanisms.

Furthermore, this study revealed that the use of Canakinumab in children aged 0–16 years with sJIA was associated with reported adverse events including HLH, COVID-19, increased serum ferritin, and nasal congestion. However, these signals do not necessarily indicate direct drug-induced reactions. Elevated ferritin and HLH are regarded as disease-related manifestations of sJIA rather than toxic effects of Canakinumab [26, 27]. In this context, HLH corresponds clinically to MAS, a severe hyperinflammatory complication that can occur during disease flares or infections [28]. Ferritin, as an acute-phase reactant, serves as a sensitive biomarker of systemic inflammation and MAS activity, and its marked elevation is a characteristic laboratory feature of this complication [29]. The COVID-19 signal reflects the reporting period coinciding with the global pandemic rather than an effect specific to Canakinumab exposure. Collectively, these observations highlight the importance of distinguishing disease or time-related findings from true drug-associated adverse reactions when interpreting FAERS data. Nasal congestion was consistently reported and represents a localized immune response secondary to IL-1β inhibition. Mechanistically, IL-1β blockade disrupts the nasal mucosal cytokine network, leading to Th2-skewed inflammation, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and compensatory activation of IL-18, IL-13, IL-25, and IL-33, which together contribute to mucosal edema and vascular leakage manifesting as nasal congestion [30–33].

This study also conducted a time-to-onset analysis of AEs and used the WSP to detect time-related characteristics, helping to characterize temporal trends. The results suggest that for 0–16-year-old sJIA patients treated with Canakinumab, the period within 195 days warrants particular pharmacovigilance attention. As shown in Table 4, AEs followed an early-onset decline pattern, indicating that the risk decreased over time. This highlights the need for early detection to ensure patient safety and treatment stability. As shown in Fig. 3, the majority of AEs occurred within the first 90 days, indicating that early-onset events predominate. Moreover, by day 90, the incidence of adverse reactions in females was greater than that in males, indicating that a higher proportion of reports involved female patients, emphasizing the importance of continued sex-specific safety evaluation. However, conclusions about sex differences cannot be drawn on the basis of these data alone, and further multicenter, large-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to validate these findings in the future.

This study has certain limitations. First, as the FAERS database is a self-reporting system primarily involving reports from consumers, doctors, and pharmacists, it may be prone to underreporting, duplicate entries, and inaccuracies. To reduce duplicate-report bias, deduplication followed FDA guidance using a two-step rule: for each Case ID, the record with the latest FDA received date was retained; if multiple entries shared the same Case ID and FDA received date, the one with the largest Primary ID was kept and the others removed. The specific deduplication rules and examples are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Second, the FAERS database lacks subclassifications of JIA, preventing distinction between systemic and non-systemic forms. As Canakinumab is specifically approved for sJIA and seldom used for other subtypes, all JIA reports linked to Canakinumab were analyzed as sJIA-related. This approach reflects real-world prescribing patterns and established FAERS methodologies. To reduce potential bias, results were interpreted with reference to Canakinumab’s approved indication and known clinical features. Third, the FAERS database lacks long-term follow-up information and provides only qualitative signal detection, which may limit causal inference. To address this, we employed multiple validated disproportionality algorithms and cross-checked detected signals against labeled safety information and published evidence. This triangulated approach reduces random bias and enhances the robustness and credibility of the findings.

Conclusion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of Canakinumab-related AEs via the FAERS database, focusing on reports of AEs in 0–16-year-old patients with sJIA between 2014 and 2023. The analysis confirmed known adverse reactions and identified potential new reactions not mentioned on the label. These findings provide valuable insights for clinicians when using Canakinumab for treating JIA and highlight the importance of early detection.
