Development of an Integrated 6-Year Preventive Medicine Curriculum for Medical Students in Korea: An Expert-informed Deliberative Curriculum Development Study
Authors: Junmoo Ahn, Yune Sik Kang, Kwan Lee, Jae-Hyun Park, So Yeon Ryu, Mina Ha, Yong-jun Choi, Juhwan Oh, Seung-Hee Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In Korea, preventive medicine education has remained fragmented across the undergraduate curriculum, with limited longitudinal continuity and increasing pressure to incorporate population health, healthcare systems, community practice, planetary health, and digital health. This study aimed to develop an integrated 6-year preventive medicine curriculum for medical students that would support health promotion, disease prevention, and social accountability at the individual and population levels. We conducted an expert-informed deliberative curriculum development study framed by Walker’s deliberative (naturalistic) model and supplemented by Kern’s 6-step approach. The development process comprised a literature review and planning activities, 2 curriculum development workshops, 2 structured group deliberation sessions with preventive medicine faculty leaders, small-group meetings, a confirmation workshop, and signature course development meetings. Development records and curriculum artifacts generated through this process were subjected to descriptive synthesis. The final curriculum specified 1 educational purpose, 4 educational objectives, 7 competencies, 5 curricular domains, 3 learning levels, 102 instructional hours, Foundational and Applied course labels, 6 signature courses, and instructional guidebooks. It arranges healthcare systems, determinants of health and disease, planetary health, community-based preventive medicine practice, and integrative analysis of complex health issues along a progression from foundational concepts to advanced integrative application. Expert deliberation refined course terminology, clarified learning progression and course placement, and identified issues relevant to anticipated implementation feasibility. The proposed curriculum positions preventive medicine as a longitudinal, spiral, competency-linked component of undergraduate medical education. As a national reference framework, it is intended to help future physicians connect evidence, systems, communities, and emerging public health challenges with proactive health promotion and disease prevention.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Preventive medicine now occupies a broader place in undergraduate medical education because physicians are expected to address population health needs, community contexts, environmental change, health-system complexity, and digital transformation in addition to individual illness. In Korea, preventive medicine education has contributed to public health improvement, but its curricular role requires continued renewal as health needs and models of medical education change [1,2].

The recent shift from a conventional 2+4 structure to a continuous 6-year medical curriculum has made longitudinal curriculum design more urgent. Korean medical education literature has emphasized that premedical and medical education should be connected through explicit educational aims, coherent competency development, and stakeholder engagement [3,4]. This need is especially pronounced in preventive medicine, where students often encounter topics as fragmented lectures rather than as a progressive curricular thread tied to professional roles, community responsibility, and public health practice.

Preventive medicine education in Korea also faces a substantive content challenge. Core topics such as epidemiology, health promotion, disease prevention, occupational health, environmental health, and healthcare systems remain essential; at the same time, climate change, planetary health, global health, social determinants of health, and digital health require explicit curricular attention [5–9]. International experience suggests that public health and epidemiology education in medical schools can be strengthened through competency-based approaches, longitudinal sequencing, evidence-based decision-making, and closer alignment with contemporary health-system needs [5,7,10].

A preventive medicine curriculum model should therefore do more than select topics. It should align educational purpose, objectives, competencies, learning levels, instructional strategies, and assessment. This study aimed to develop and present an integrated 6-year preventive medicine curriculum for medical students in Korea; clarify the alignment among its purpose, objectives, competencies, domains, learning levels, Foundational and Applied course labels, and signature courses; and examine how expert deliberation refined the curriculum into a proposed national reference framework for undergraduate preventive medicine education.

METHODS

Study Design

This study was designed as an expert-informed deliberative curriculum development study. Because curricular decisions were made through discussion and reconciliation of divergent views rather than through formal voting, the overall design was framed by Walker’s deliberative (naturalistic) model of curriculum development. Walker’s model conceptualizes curriculum making as a movement from a shared platform of beliefs and perceived problems, through participant deliberation, to concrete design decisions [11]. In the present study, the literature review and planning meeting established the platform; the workshops and structured group deliberation sessions formed the deliberation phase; and the confirmation workshop and signature course development produced the design outputs. Within this deliberative frame, Kern’s 6-step approach was used to organize the components of curriculum development: problem identification and general needs assessment, targeted needs assessment, goals and objectives, educational strategies, implementation, and evaluation and feedback [12]. Kern’s model was retained because it clarifies the elements of curriculum development, whereas Walker’s model more accurately describes how decisions were reached. The Kern approach was modified for this study: the first 4 steps were fully enacted, whereas implementation and formal outcome evaluation, corresponding to steps 5 and 6, were addressed only through implementation-resource development and were reserved for future work.

The Context, Input, Process, and Product (CIPP) model was considered a complementary framework for future evaluation because it is widely used for educational program evaluation and continuous improvement [13]. However, this study was concerned with curriculum development and refinement, not with measuring learner or institutional outcomes after implementation. CIPP was therefore positioned as a post-implementation evaluation framework rather than as the primary study design. The deliberative consensus process was reported with reference to ACcurate COnsensus Reporting Document (ACCORD), which emphasizes transparent reporting of the purpose, participants, process, and decision-making procedures in consensus studies [14], and to medical education literature on consensus group methods [15].

Development Process and Participants

The curriculum was developed through 4 linked stages from January 2023 to October 2024. Across all stages, preventive medicine faculty members were purposively recruited by the Korean Society for Preventive Medicine as expert informants. They represented medical schools nationwide and included departmental leaders and educators responsible for preventive medicine teaching. Because the panel was assembled through expert nomination rather than probabilistic sampling, participation reflected disciplinary expertise rather than statistical representativeness of all faculty members or institutions. Participant numbers reported for each workshop and deliberation session refer to attendance at that session. Participants in each session were independently recruited for that session, and no individual participated in more than one session; therefore, the counts represent distinct groups of faculty members. Stage 1 consisted of a literature review and a planning meeting. Domestic and international sources, including the Research Information Sharing Service, PubMed, and Scopus, were reviewed to identify curriculum development cases and educational needs in preventive medicine. A planning meeting held on January 14, 2023, clarified problems in current preventive medicine education and considered the academic society’s role in curriculum development.

Stage 2 consisted of 2 curriculum development workshops. The first workshop, held on March 6, 2023, included 25 participants and addressed the educational purpose, objectives, competencies, and major course topics. The second workshop, held on June 9, 2023, included 18 participants and addressed revision of the educational purpose and objectives as well as specification of course topics, learning objectives, teaching hours, teaching methods, and faculty roles.

Stage 3 consisted of 2 structured group deliberation sessions with preventive medicine faculty leaders from medical schools nationwide. The sessions included 20 participants and 14 participants, respectively, exceeding the 6–10 participants typically recommended for focus group discussions; accordingly, they are described here as structured group deliberations rather than focus group discussions. The research team facilitated each session using a predefined agenda and structured worksheets. Participant feedback was documented through written worksheets and meeting records rather than through verbatim audio transcription and formal coding. The first round was held on August 18, 2023, with 20 participants, and the second was held on August 26, 2023, with 14 participants. The sessions reviewed the draft educational purpose, objectives, competencies, curricular domains, educational levels, course allocation, course names, and anticipated implementation feasibility. Worksheet feedback was incorporated into subsequent revisions.

Stage 4 included small-group meetings, a confirmation workshop, and signature course development meetings. A small-group meeting on October 14, 2023, selected course topics and candidate signature courses, and a confirmation workshop on November 1, 2023, finalized course topics, year placement, teaching hours, and signature courses. Two signature course development meetings, held on July 29, 2024, and October 15, 2024, reviewed guidebook drafts, revised instructional plans, and confirmed lecture scenarios. The curriculum was therefore derived from the cumulative outputs of meetings, workshops, structured group deliberations, confirmation activities, and guidebook development.

Study Materials and Analytic Approach

Study materials consisted of records and artifacts generated during curriculum development: literature review summaries, planning meeting materials, workshop worksheets, structured group deliberation worksheets, course-mapping matrices, versioned curriculum drafts, confirmed bilingual course-name tables, signature course guidebook drafts, and lecture-scenario materials. The analytic unit was not an individual interview transcript but the documented development output produced through the multistage consensus process.

A descriptive-analytic synthesis was used to reconstruct the decision-making process and final curriculum framework [16]. Materials were grouped into 6 analytic categories: educational purpose and objectives, competencies, curricular domains, staged learning levels and teaching hours, Foundational and Applied course labels, and signature courses with instructional guidebooks. When feedback revealed ambiguity or overlap among curricular domains, the research team reviewed the wording and placement of each domain and course to determine whether its primary function was conceptual, analytic, practice-oriented, or integrative. Final decisions were accepted when the confirmation process established internal coherence and feasibility for adaptation by Korean medical schools. Consensus was operationalized qualitatively rather than by formal voting: draft decisions were retained when they were endorsed in group discussion, elicited no sustained objection, and were ratified at the confirmation workshop. Trustworthiness was supported by versioned curriculum drafts, which provided an audit trail of decision development; by the confirmation workshop, which allowed participating faculty to verify the synthesized outputs; and by iterative categorization and refinement by the research team.

Ethics Statement

This study analyzed non-identifiable, aggregate curriculum-development outputs generated through a curriculum development project organized by the Korean Society for Preventive Medicine. The analyzed materials included documented educational purposes, educational objectives, competency statements, course-mapping results, consensus-derived curriculum structures, and signature course instructional development outputs. No patients, students, or members of the public were recruited, and the study included no individually identifiable private information, sensitive personal data, biological specimens, or biomedical interventions. Participating faculty members took part as expert members of the academic society in a curriculum development project organized and funded by the Korean Society for Preventive Medicine, and they were informed that outputs from the curriculum development process could be used for academic reporting and publication. Therefore, this study did not constitute human subjects research, and institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required.

RESULTS

Overview of the Curriculum Development Process

The curriculum development process proceeded from needs identification to course design, field-based refinement, final confirmation, and development of implementation resources. The literature review and planning meeting identified the need to strengthen continuity in preventive medicine education, connect foundational and applied learning, and address community and global health needs. The 2 curriculum development workshops translated these needs into an educational purpose, educational objectives, competencies, course topics, learning objectives, instructional methods, and teaching hours. The 2 structured group deliberation sessions with faculty leaders provided field-based refinement. Small-group meetings, the confirmation workshop, and signature course development meetings then finalized the curriculum and generated guidebooks for selected signature courses.

Educational Purpose, Objectives, and Competencies

The final curriculum established 1 overarching educational purpose: medical students who learn preventive medicine should understand the determinants of health and disease and the healthcare system and should be able to fulfill the social responsibilities of health professionals through health promotion and disease prevention at global, national, and community levels (Figure 1). This purpose framed preventive medicine as a longitudinal discipline linking individual care, population health, public health practice, and social responsibility.

Four educational objectives were derived. Students should be able to analyze and evaluate the determinants of health and disease using epidemiologic research methods; understand and apply healthcare systems for health promotion, disease prevention, and management; identify and explain community and global environmental health issues and needs; and evaluate and improve health promotion and disease prevention activities. Seven competencies translated these objectives into learner outcomes: logical and critical thinking, integrative thinking, problem-solving ability, research ability, communication ability, action and performance ability, and leadership. Together, these competencies shift the curriculum from content delivery toward outcome-oriented design, consistent with principles of competency-based medical education [10,17].

Curricular Domains, Staged Learning Levels, and Instructional Hours

The curriculum was organized into 5 curricular domains, 3 learning levels, and 102 instructional hours (Table 1 and Figure 2). The 5 domains were understanding the healthcare system and its application; epidemiologic analysis and evaluation of determinants of health and disease; planetary health; community-based preventive medicine practice; and integrative analysis of complex health issues. This domain structure separates intended outcomes from curricular organization: the 4 educational objectives define what students should achieve, whereas the 5 domains define how the content is organized for teaching.

The curriculum followed a spiral progression across the full 6-year trajectory. The early years introduce basic concepts and problem awareness, the middle years emphasize analytic and applied learning, and the later years address advanced or integrative topics. In Table 1, representative years indicate where dedicated instructional hours for each domain are concentrated; they do not indicate that the domain is taught only in those years. Core themes are introduced early, revisited across domains, reinforced through the integrative analysis of complex health issues domain and year-specific signature courses, and deepened with increasing complexity in later years. The curriculum therefore operates as a single spiral structure rather than as separate sequential blocks. The staged learning levels were defined as novice, intermediate, and advanced. Novice-level learning focused on exploration and understanding of basic concepts; intermediate-level learning emphasized causal reasoning, interpretation, and application; and advanced-level learning emphasized contextualized knowledge organization, inductive reasoning, and complex problem-solving. This design follows the principle of a spiral curriculum, in which core themes are revisited with increasing depth and complexity across the 6-year sequence [18,19].

Confirmed Foundational and Applied Course Map

The confirmed course map specified the academic year, curricular domain, official course title, and course-level label for each course (Table 2). Foundational courses provide common foundational knowledge and essential application for all medical students. Applied courses extend foundational learning into contextualized application, critical review, community or system practice, and integrative analysis. The final course map therefore served as both a content inventory and a developmental map of learner progression. The 2-tier Foundational and Applied course labels and the 3-tier novice, intermediate, and advanced learning levels describe different constructs. Course labels indicate the nature of each course, distinguishing common required content essential for all students (Foundational) from courses that extend learning into contextualized, critical, and integrative application (Applied). By contrast, learning levels indicate the targeted cognitive depth within each domain across the 6-year sequence. To avoid confusion between these 2 axes, the course labels, originally expressed as Basic and Advanced in the confirmed course map, are presented in this article as Foundational and Applied so that the label terminology does not overlap with the advanced learning level. Thus, a course may carry a Foundational label while contributing to intermediate-level learning within its domain, because a common required course can still target causal reasoning and application. The map also specifies the recommended number of instructional hours for each course, shown in parentheses after the official course title; these hours total 102 across the 5 domains.

Review of course names led to terminology refinements that were applied consistently across the manuscript and tables. The healthcare systems domain was expressed as understanding the healthcare system and its application. The epidemiology domain was expressed as epidemiologic analysis and evaluation of determinants of health and disease. The environmental domain was finalized as planetary health. The community practice domain was finalized as community-based preventive medicine practice. The case-based integrative domain was retained as integrative analysis of complex health issues. At the course level, titles such as biostatistics, methods in epidemiology, causal inference, critical review of epidemiologic evidence, healthcare financing and coverage, and digital health were standardized.

Signature Courses and Instructional Guidebooks

Six year-specific signature courses were selected to support implementation (Table 2): climate change and health in year 1; measurement and evaluation of disease and health status in year 2; chronic disease management, including screening and health examinations, in year 3; social determinants of health in year 4; healthcare system in year 5; and digital health in year 6. These courses were selected because they represented the central learning focus of each educational phase and could provide visible anchor points for institutional adoption.

The signature course guidebooks used a common instructional structure that included a topic overview, topic explanation, competencies, course outcomes, educational materials, instructional methods and formative assessment, summative assessment, timetable, and lecture scenario. This structure was intended to make implementation more feasible while allowing each medical school to adapt the curriculum to local conditions.

Major Refinements Derived From Expert Deliberation

Expert deliberation produced several substantive refinements (Table 3). These refinements show how issues raised during deliberation were translated into final design decisions. First, the distinction between educational objectives and curricular domains was clarified. Second, the environmental health domain was reframed as planetary health to emphasize climate change, One Health, environmental risks, and planetary health perspectives rather than a narrower environmental hygiene focus. Third, the preventive services domain was refined as community-based preventive medicine practice to strengthen practical relevance, community linkage, and the connection among prevention, screening, health examinations, chronic disease management, and community health program planning. Fourth, complex health issues were positioned within an integrative domain, integrative analysis of complex health issues, so that topics such as digital health, climate change, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), and health examinations could be addressed through systems thinking and cross-domain synthesis. These refinements improved the internal coherence and anticipated feasibility of the final curriculum.

DISCUSSION

This study developed an integrated 6-year preventive medicine curriculum for medical students through a multistage process of expert deliberation and consensus. The final framework comprised 1 educational purpose, 4 educational objectives, 7 competencies, 5 curricular domains, 3 staged learning levels, 102 instructional hours, Foundational and Applied course labels, 6 signature courses, and instructional guidebooks. The curriculum itself is the principal study output: it offers a structure through which preventive medicine can function as a coherent 6-year educational thread rather than as a fragmented lecture block.

The curriculum is grounded in the premise that future physicians should learn prevention as a progressive professional responsibility. Early learning introduces the determinants of health and disease, climate and planetary health, and basic measurement. Middle-year learning develops epidemiologic reasoning, chronic disease management, community health problem-solving, and health communication. Later learning integrates healthcare systems, policy, quality, patient safety, digital health, and other complex public health cases. This spiral arrangement allows students to revisit core concepts with increasing depth, complexity, and practical relevance [18,19].

The curriculum was also designed to advance the social accountability of preventive medicine education. Its educational purpose states that students should understand the determinants of health and disease and the healthcare system and use health promotion and disease prevention to fulfill social responsibilities at global, national, and community levels. This orientation broadens the physician’s role beyond diagnosis and treatment to include proactive prevention, population health improvement, and system-level contribution. It is consistent with broader calls for medical schools in Korea to strengthen accountability to communities, regions, and the country [20]. Because the curriculum was developed primarily by preventive medicine faculty, however, this social accountability orientation should be interpreted as a design aspiration embedded in the curriculum rather than as an achieved outcome. Its realization will need to be verified through implementation and engagement with students, communities, and other stakeholders.

The framework supports competency-based medical education by aligning the educational purpose, objectives, competencies, domains, courses, teaching hours, and guidebooks. The 7 competencies—logical and critical thinking, integrative thinking, problem-solving ability, research ability, communication ability, action and performance ability, and leadership—indicate that preventive medicine learning should lead to applied judgment and professional action. In this framework, preventive medicine is not presented as a list of topics but as an outcome-oriented sequence of learning experiences [10,17].

The curriculum updates preventive medicine content for contemporary medical education. Climate change and health, planetary health, global health and One Health, social determinants of health, healthcare systems, community-based preventive medicine practice, healthcare quality and patient safety, and digital health were explicitly placed within the 6-year sequence. These domains reflect changing practice conditions and the need for medical students to connect evidence, systems, communities, and emerging public health threats [6,8,9]. This orientation also builds on earlier national learning objectives for preventive medicine, whose most recent revision emphasized health promotion and disease prevention but was organized primarily as a list of learning objectives rather than as a longitudinal, competency-linked sequence [1,2]. The present framework retains that content foundation while adding explicit purpose-objective-competency alignment, staged learning levels, and implementation resources.

For implementation, the curriculum’s content framework can be followed as presented, but delivery should be integrated with clinical and research education rather than confined to isolated lecture blocks. In particular, the community-based preventive medicine practice domain and community-based practice courses should be linked with hospital-based clinical clerkships so that students experience prevention as an integral component of patient care across community and hospital settings. When these courses are delivered, learner-centered methods such as small-group discussion, case-based learning, team-based learning, and flipped learning are preferable to lecture-dominant formats, consistent with the curriculum’s competency orientation [10,17].

Walker’s deliberative model provided an appropriate overall frame for the curriculum development process, which moved through platform building, deliberation, and design decisions [11]. Kern’s 6-step approach complemented this frame by organizing the components of development, from needs identification to goals, educational strategies, implementation resources, and future evaluation planning [12]. The CIPP model remains appropriate for the next phase, when medical schools implement the curriculum and evaluate CIPP outcomes [13].

This study has several limitations. First, the findings are based on curriculum development records and expert consensus materials rather than implementation data; therefore, the curriculum cannot yet be interpreted as evidence of educational effectiveness. Second, although preventive medicine faculty leaders participated in the process, student, resident, patient, community, and policy stakeholder perspectives were not systematically incorporated, limiting claims about social accountability at this stage. Third, the curriculum was developed as a national reference framework, and each medical school will need to adapt it to its institutional structure, available instructional time, and faculty capacity. Fourth, because implementation data are not yet available, statements about implementation feasibility and educational benefit should be regarded as hypotheses for future implementation and outcome studies rather than as demonstrated effects.

Future studies should examine implementation of this curriculum across diverse medical school contexts. Relevant outcomes include learner achievement of preventive medicine competencies, student perceptions of relevance and workload, faculty acceptability, alignment with institutional outcomes, and effects on community-based or systems-oriented learning. Assessment tools linked to each competency should also be developed, and the signature course guidebooks should be evaluated as a mechanism for maintaining curriculum consistency while allowing local adaptation.

CONCLUSION

The integrated 6-year preventive medicine curriculum is proposed as a national reference framework for embedding prevention, population health, community responsibility, healthcare systems, planetary health, and digital health across undergraduate medical education. By aligning the educational purpose, objectives, competencies, curricular domains, learning levels, course labels, and signature courses, the curriculum may help medical schools cultivate physicians who address health risks before disease develops, reduce preventable disease burden, fulfill their social responsibilities, and contribute to sustained improvements in public health.
