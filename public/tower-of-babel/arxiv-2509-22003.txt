Quantitative periodic homogenization of parabolic equations with large drift and potential
Kshitij Sinha

Abstract
This work aims to study the rates in the context of periodic homogenization of parabolic problems with large lower order terms (both drift and potential). We demonstrate that the solution is a product of three terms: (i) a function of time, (ii) the ground-state of an exponential cell eigenvalue problem and (iii) the solution to a parabolic equation with zero effective drift. For the latter, we derive L 2 \displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{2} rates in the homogenization limit.

Introduction

In this paper, for the unknown
\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}:\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
, we study the following parabolic initial boundary value problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial u_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-\dv\left(A\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}b\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}c\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)u_{\varepsilon}&=0\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)&=0\qquad\mbox{ on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega,\\
u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)&=u_{\varepsilon}^{0}(x)\qquad\mbox{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

Here the parameter
\displaystyle\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. The bounded domain
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is assumed to be open and with Lipschitz boundary
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
. The coefficients
\displaystyle A,b,c
are assumed to be bounded in
\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
and to be
\displaystyle Y
-periodic with the standard notation
\displaystyle Y:=[0,1)^{d}
for the unit cell. Furthermore,
\displaystyle A
is assumed to be uniformly elliptic, i.e.

A(y)\xi\cdot\xi\geq\mu\left|\xi\right|^{2}\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mbox{ and for a.e. }y\in Y,

for some
\displaystyle\mu>0
. We will be commenting more on the initial datum
\displaystyle u^{0}_{\varepsilon}
later.

u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=e^{\frac{-\lambda t}{\varepsilon^{2}}}v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\psi\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle(\lambda,\psi)

) are already available in
[

,

\displaystyle Y
-periodic eigenvalue problems in homogenization can be traced back to
[
6

. Allaire and Orive in
[

, Lemma 2.1]
(see also
[

, Lemma 3]
, where Capdeboscq treats operators with no lower order terms) show the existence of a unique
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the eigenvalue problems (with
\displaystyle A^{\ast}
denoting the adjoint of
\displaystyle A
):

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\dv_{y}({A}(y)\nabla\psi(y))+b(y)\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi(y)+c(y)\psi(y)=\lambda\psi(y)\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,1)^{d}\\
y\mapsto\psi(y)e^{-2\pi\theta\cdot y}\quad\mbox{ is }Y\mbox{-periodic},\end{aligned}\right.

(1.3)

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\dv_{y}({A}^{\ast}(y)\nabla\psi^{\ast}(y))-\dv_{y}(b(y)\psi^{\ast}(y))+c(y)\psi^{\ast}(y)=\lambda^{\ast}\psi^{\ast}(y)\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,1)^{d}\\
y\mapsto\psi^{\ast}(y)e^{2\pi\theta\cdot y}\quad\mbox{ is }Y\mbox{-periodic},\end{aligned}\right.

(1.4)

have the following properties:

•

Both the problems (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) have the same first eigenvalue which is real, positive and simple.

•

The first eigenfunctions of (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) can be chosen such that

\min_{Y}\{\psi,\psi^{\ast}\}\geq a>0\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\int_{Y}\psi(y)\psi^{\ast}(y)\,{\rm d}y=1.

(1.5)

•

The function
\displaystyle\beta:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by

\beta(y):=\psi(y)\psi^{\ast}(y)b(y)+\psi(y)A^{\ast}(y)\nabla_{y}\psi^{\ast}(y)-\psi^{\ast}(y)A(y)\nabla_{y}\psi(y)\quad\mbox{ for }y\in Y,

(1.6)

is divergence-free and is of zero average over
\displaystyle Y
, i.e.

\int_{Y}\beta(y)\,{\rm d}y=0.

The factorization (
1.2
) implies that
\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)
and
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}(0,x)
are equal to each other up to a factor of the first eigenfunction
\displaystyle\psi\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)
mentioned above. Observe further that, for the factorization (
1.2

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
should necessarily satisfy the following initial boundary value problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \sigma\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\frac{\partial v_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-\operatorname{div}\left(\alpha\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\beta\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}&=0\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
v_{\varepsilon}&=0\qquad\mbox{ on }(0,T]\times\partial\Omega,\\
v_{\varepsilon}(0,x)&=u_{0}(x)\qquad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.7)

where the coefficient vector field
\displaystyle\beta
is defined in (
1.6
) and the coefficients
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle\alpha
are
\displaystyle Y
-periodic and are defined as follows:

\sigma(y):=\psi(y)\psi^{\ast}(y)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\alpha(y):=\sigma(y)A(y)\quad\mbox{ for a.e. }y\in Y.

(1.8)

Remark 1.1
.

The coefficient
\displaystyle\sigma
is
\displaystyle Y
-periodic because

\sigma(y)=\psi(y)\psi^{\ast}(y)=\psi(y)e^{-2\pi\theta\cdot y}e^{2\pi\theta\cdot y}\psi^{\ast}(y)

Remark 1.2
.

We have made a choice of taking the initial datum for
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
in (
1.7
) to be
\displaystyle\varepsilon
-independent. The factorization (
1.2
), then, forces the initial datum for
\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}
in (
1.1
) to be of the form

u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=u^{0}_{\varepsilon}(x)=u_{0}(x)\psi\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)

This is sometimes referred to as the well prepared initial datum. Similar well preparedness assumptions on the data have been made in the literature
[
2
,
3
]
. This choice results in strong convergence of the factor
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
to its homogenized limit. If one were to keep the data
\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)
independent of the parameter
\displaystyle\varepsilon
, then the factorization forces the initial datum for
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
to be

v_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=\frac{u_{0}(x)}{\psi\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)}.

(1.9)

It should be noted that the equation (
1.7
) can still be homogenized with (
1.9
) as the initial datum. The homogenized equation remains the same, but with the initial datum for the limit problem being

\left(\int_{Y}\frac{1}{\psi(y)}\,{\rm d}y\right)u_{0}(x).

All of this can be argued using the notion of two-scale convergence
[
1
]
, for example. In this case, the convergence will be weak and hence cannot talk about rates.

Our main result is the following quantitative homogenization result for the factor
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
in (
1.2
) which solves the initial boundary value problem (
1.7
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to (
1.7
) and let
\displaystyle v_{0}
be the solution to the corresponding homogenized problem (
1.12
). Then, there exists a positive constant
\displaystyle C
, independent of
\displaystyle\varepsilon
, such that the following estimate holds:

\displaystyle\left\|v_{\varepsilon}-v_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{4}}\left\{\left\|\partial_{t}v_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,d}(\Omega))}+\left\|v_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega))}+\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right\}.

(1.10)

In the above result, there is an inherent assumption that the terms on the right hand side of the bound are finite. This essentially translates to the data being sufficient smooth to guarantee adequate regularity for the solution
\displaystyle v_{0}
of the homogenized equation. The proof of the above theorem is inspired by the ideas in the work of Jun Geng and Zhongwei Shen
[
11
]
that dealt with
\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{2}
convergence rates in the context of periodic homogenization of parabolic problems.

Remark 1.4
.

Our estimate (
1.10
) is not sharp as in
[
11
]
. In there, the authors have obtained an
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
rate. Upon deriving a
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
rate for
\displaystyle\left\|v_{\varepsilon}-v_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})}
, the authors employ a duality argument to improve the rate to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
. We emphasise here that the same strategy can be followed in our case as well. Thus, improving the rate in Theorem
1.3
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
.

Before we comment further on the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we return to the equation (
1.7
). As
\displaystyle\beta
(defined by (
1.6
)) is periodic, divergence-free and is of zero average, there exists a periodic skew-symmetric matrix-valued function
\displaystyle y\mapsto B(y)
such that (see
[
7
, Lemma 1, p. 809]
)

\beta(y)=-\dv_{y}B(y)\qquad\mbox{ in }Y.

Taking a
\displaystyle Y
-periodic matrix-valued function
\displaystyle y\mapsto M(y):=\alpha(y)+B(y)
, the evolution equation in (
1.7
) can be rewritten as

\sigma\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\frac{\partial v_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-\dv\left(M\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right)=0\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega.

(1.11)

Observe that
\displaystyle M
is also elliptic:

M(y)\xi\cdot\xi=\alpha(y)\xi\cdot\xi\geq b\left|\xi\right|^{2}\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mbox{ and for a.e. }y\in Y,

where the ellipticity constant
\displaystyle b
depends on the ellipticity constant
\displaystyle\mu
of
\displaystyle A
and the lower bound
\displaystyle a
in (
1.5
). Using classical homogenization arguments (see
[
9
, Theorem 11.4, p. 211]
), we can show that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left\|v_{\varepsilon}-v_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})}=0,

where
\displaystyle v_{0}
satisfies the following homogenized initial boundary value problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial v_{0}}{\partial t}-\dv\left(M_{h}\nabla v_{0}\right)&=0\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
v_{0}&=0\qquad\mbox{ on }(0,T]\times\partial\Omega,\\
v_{0}(0,x)&=u_{0}(x)\qquad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.12)

where
\displaystyle M_{h}
is the homogenized matrix with constant entries given by

M_{h}=\int_{Y}\left(M(y)+M(y)\nabla_{y}\chi(y)\right)\,{\rm d}y,

with
\displaystyle\chi(y)=\left(\chi_{1}(y),\dots,\chi_{d}(y)\right)
, where
\displaystyle\chi_{j}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\text{per}}(Y)
solve the cell problems:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dv_{y}\left(M(y)\nabla_{y}\left(\chi_{j}(y)+y_{j}\right)\right)&=0\qquad\mbox{ in }Y,\\
\int_{Y}\chi_{j}(y)\,{\rm d}y&=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.13)

for
\displaystyle j=1,\dots,d
. Theorem
1.3
quantifies the aforementioned strong convergence of
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
to
\displaystyle v_{0}
.

The results of this paper also yields quantitative rates for the periodic homogenization of parabolic equations with nondivergence form elliptic part and with large lower order terms. We draw inspiration from the work of Wenjia Jing and Yiping Zhang
[
12
]
which derived quantitative rates for the periodic homogenization of nondivergence form elliptic equations with large drift terms. The key idea in
[

is to use the invariant measure associated with the underlying diffusion process. This is a well-known procedure (see
[

]

\displaystyle\rho_{\varepsilon}:\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
, that we examine is the following:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\rho_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right):\nabla^{2}\rho_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}q\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\cdot\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}r\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\rho_{\varepsilon}&=0\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,x)&=0\qquad\mbox{ on }(0,T)\times\partial\Omega,\\
\rho_{\varepsilon}(0,x)&=\rho_{\varepsilon}^{0}(x)\qquad\mbox{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.14)

Here the scalar-valued function
\displaystyle r
, the vector-valued function
\displaystyle q
and the symmetric matrix-valued function
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}
are all assumed to be
\displaystyle Y
-periodic. The coefficients
\displaystyle q
and
\displaystyle r
are assumed to be bounded in
\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(Y)
. We further assume
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}
to be elliptic and that it belongs to
\displaystyle\mathrm{W}^{1,s}(Y)
for some
\displaystyle s>d
. Invoking Sobolev embedding, one gets
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\in\mathrm{C}^{0,\tau}(Y)
for some
\displaystyle\tau\in(0,1]
. Additionally, we shall assume that
\displaystyle\dv_{y}\mathcal{K}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(Y)
. Thanks to these assumptions, the evolution equation in (
1.14
) can be rewritten as

\frac{\partial\rho_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-\dv\left(\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\dv_{y}\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)+q\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\cdot\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}r\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\rho_{\varepsilon}=0

This is similar to the evolution equation in (
1.1
). Hence, analogous to the factorization (
1.2
), we shall have that the solution to (
1.14
) can be factored as follows:

\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=e^{\frac{-\widehat{\lambda}t}{\varepsilon^{2}}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\widehat{\psi}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right),

(1.15)

where
\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}
and
\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}
are an eigenpair associated with the exponential eigenvalue problems akin to (
1.3

1.4

A(y)=A^{\ast}(y)=\mathcal{K}(y);\qquad b(y)=\dv_{y}\mathcal{K}(y)+q(y);\qquad c(y)=r(y).

Thus, Theorem
1.3
gives a quantitative rate of convergence for
\displaystyle\varphi_{\varepsilon}
to the solution of the corresponding homogenized equation.

2
Quantitative rates in
\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})

In this section, we prove a general quantitative homogenized result for the following parabolic problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \zeta\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\frac{\partial f_{\varepsilon}}{\partial t}-\dv\left(\Theta\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\right)&=0\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
f_{\varepsilon}&=0\qquad\mbox{ on }(0,T]\times\partial\Omega,\\
f_{\varepsilon}(0,x)&=f_{\text{in}}(x)\qquad\mbox{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.1)

In the above problem, the coefficient
\displaystyle\zeta\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}_{\text{per}}(Y)
and is assumed to satisfy

\int_{Y}\zeta(y)\,{\rm d}y=1.

(2.2)

The matrix-valued coefficient
\displaystyle\Theta\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}_{\text{per}}(Y)
is assumed to be uniformly elliptic, i.e.

\Theta(y)\xi\cdot\xi\geq\kappa\left|\xi\right|^{2}\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mbox{ and for a.e. }y\in Y,

for some
\displaystyle\kappa>0
. Considering the solutions
\displaystyle\omega_{j}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\text{per}}(Y)
to the cell problems

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dv_{y}\left(\Theta(y)\nabla_{y}\left(\omega_{j}(y)+y_{j}\right)\right)&=0\qquad\mbox{ in }Y,\\
\int_{Y}\zeta(y)\omega_{j}(y)\,{\rm d}y&=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.3)

for
\displaystyle j=1,\dots,d
, we can define a homogenized matrix as follows:

\Theta_{h}=\int_{Y}\left(\Theta(y)+\Theta(y)\nabla_{y}\omega(y)\right)\,{\rm d}y,

with
\displaystyle\omega(y)=\left(\omega_{1}(y),\dots,\omega_{d}(y)\right)
. Next, we consider the homogenized problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial t}-\dv\left(\Theta_{h}\nabla f_{0}\right)&=0\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,\\
f_{0}&=0\qquad\mbox{ on }(0,T]\times\partial\Omega,\\
f_{0}(0,x)&=f_{\text{in}}(x)\qquad\mbox{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.4)

Our main result (Theorem
1.3
) is a special case of the following quantitative result corresponding to the periodic homogenization of (
2.1
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to (
2.1

\displaystyle f_{0}

\displaystyle C
, independent of
\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle\left\|f_{\varepsilon}-f_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{4}}\left\{\left\|\partial_{t}f_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,d}(\Omega))}+\left\|f_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega))}+\left\|f_{{\rm{in}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right\}.

(2.5)

Our line of approach is inspired by the work of Geng and Shen
[
11
]
. We introduce the following notation: for
\displaystyle s>0
,

\Omega_{s}:=\left\{x\in\Omega\,\mbox{ such that }\mbox{ dist}(x,\partial\Omega)<s\right\}.

We fix a space cut-off function
\displaystyle\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\displaystyle 0\leq\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}\leq 1
and it further satisfies:

\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}=1\quad\mbox{ in }\quad\Omega\backslash\Omega_{4\sqrt{\varepsilon}},\qquad\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}=0\quad\mbox{ in }\quad\Omega_{3\sqrt{\varepsilon}},\qquad\left|\nabla\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\quad\mbox{ in }\quad\Omega.

We also fix a time cut-off function
\displaystyle\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{C}_{c}^{\infty}(0,T)
such that
\displaystyle 0\leq\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}\leq 1
and it further satisfies:

\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}=1\quad\mbox{ in }\,\left(8\varepsilon,T-8\varepsilon\right),\qquad\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}=0\quad\mbox{ in }\,(0,4\varepsilon]\cup(T-4\varepsilon,T),\qquad\left|\partial_{t}\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\quad\mbox{ in }\,(0,T).

Next, we consider a parabolic smoothing operator borrowed from
[
11

:

S_{\varepsilon}(g)(t,x):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d+2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}g(t-s,x-y)\,\theta\left(\frac{s}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}s,

(2.6)

where
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{K})

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\theta(s,y)\,{\rm d}y\,{\rm d}s=1.

Here the set
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is defined below:

\mathcal{K}:=\left\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ such that }\left|t\right|+\left|x\right|^{2}\leq 1\right\}.

We shall also define the following subset of
\displaystyle\Omega\times(0,T)

\Omega_{T,\delta}:=\Big(\Omega_{\delta}\times(0,T)\Big)\cup\Big(\Omega\times(0,\delta^{2})\Big)\cup\Big(\Omega\times(T-\delta^{2},T)\Big)\qquad\mbox{ for }\delta>0.

Let

(2.8)

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\int_{\Omega_{T}}\zeta_{\varepsilon}\omega_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\left[S_{\varepsilon}(\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}\nabla f_{0})\right]\psi+\int_{\Omega_{T}}\left(\Theta_{h}-\Theta_{\varepsilon}\right)\left\{\nabla f_{0}-S_{\varepsilon}(\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}\nabla f_{0})\right\}\cdot\nabla\psi

Here
\displaystyle\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\phi\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle Y

\dv_{y}\phi(y)=\Theta(y)+\Theta(y)\nabla_{y}\omega(y)-\Theta_{h}\qquad\mbox{ in }Y.

Proof.

A direct computation leads to

\zeta_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}w_{\varepsilon}=\zeta_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}-\zeta_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}f_{0}-\varepsilon\zeta_{\varepsilon}\omega_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\left\{S_{\varepsilon}\left(\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}\nabla f_{0}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\left\|f_{\varepsilon}-f_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|w_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})}+\varepsilon\left\|\omega\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\cdot S_{\varepsilon}\left(\eta_{1}^{\varepsilon}\eta_{2}^{\varepsilon}\nabla f_{0}\right)\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|w_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})}+\varepsilon\left\|\nabla f_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega_{T})}

Hence the estimate follows.
