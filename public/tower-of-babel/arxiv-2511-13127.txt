VEIL: Jailbreaking Text-to-Video Models via Visual Exploitation from Implicit Language
Zonghao Ying, Moyang Chen, Nizhang Li, Zhiqiang Wang, Wenxin Zhang, Quanchen Zou, Zonglei Jing, Aishan Liu, Xianglong Liu, [2mm] State Key Laboratory of Complex & Critical Software Environment, Beihang University

Abstract
Jailbreak attacks can circumvent model safety guardrails and reveal critical blind spots. Prior attacks on text-to-video (T2V) models typically add adversarial perturbations to obviously unsafe prompts, which are often easy to detect and defend. In contrast, we show that benign-looking prompts containing rich, implicit cues can induce T2V models to generate semantically unsafe videos that both violate policy and preserve the original (blocked) intent. To realize this, we propose VEIL , a jailbreak framework that leverages T2V models’ cross-modal associative patterns via a modular prompt design. Specifically, our prompts combine three components: neutral scene semantic anchors, which provide the surface-level scene description extracted from the blocked intent to maintain plausibility; latent auditory triggers, textual descriptions of innocuous-sounding audio events ( e.g . , creaking, muffled noises) that exploit learned audio–visual co-occurrence priors to bias the model toward particular unsafe visual concepts; and stylistic modulators, cinematic directives ( e.g . , camera framing, atmosphere) that amplify and stabilize the latent trigger’s effect. We formalize attack generation as a constrained optimization over the above modular prompt space and solve it with a guided search procedure that balances stealth and effectiveness. Extensive experiments over 7 T2V models demonstrate the efficacy of our attack, with an average attack success rate improvement of +23% in commercial models. Related Work

2.1
Text-to-Video Generative Models

Recent advances in T2V generation have enabled high-fidelity and temporally coherent video synthesis from natural language prompts. Early frameworks such as CogVideo
16

28

34

20

35

42

18

14
;
5
;
2
;
3
. While these advances enable high-fidelity video synthesis, the growing accessibility and capability of such models also present unique safety challenges. The power of video generation introduces new modalities for creating harmful content, such as violence and pornography
7
;
25
;
39
.

2.2
Safety for Text-to-Video Generative Models

Recent work has begun to systematically evaluate safety risks unique to T2V models. T2VSafetyBench
23
was introduced as the first comprehensive benchmark for assessing T2V safety. It organizes safety concerns into a structured taxonomy (14 critical aspects) and assembles a malicious‑prompt dataset that mixes real-world examples, LLM-generated prompts, and jailbreak-style inputs for large-scale evaluation. The benchmark employs a combination of automated assessors (
e.g
.
, GPT-4
1
) and manual review to expose temporal and contextual vulnerabilities not present in image-only settings. Building on this foundation, T2V‑OptJail
22
frames T2V jailbreaks as a discrete prompt-optimization problem and proposes a joint-objective optimization approach that couples iterative LLM-guided search with prompt-variation strategies to actively explore model vulnerabilities. Concurrently, SAFEWATCH
8
employs an MLLM with policy mechanisms for effective video safety detection, but it focuses on content moderation without directly mitigating jailbreak attacks.

3
Methodology

3.1
Preliminaries and Threat Model

A T2V model can be abstracted as a function
M_{\theta}
parameterized by weights
\theta
, which maps a textual prompt
P\in\mathcal{P}
to a generated video
V\in\mathcal{V}
. This mapping is typically mediated by a sequence of latent codes
\bm{z}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{T}\}\in\mathcal{Z}^{T}
, and can be decomposed into an encoder–decoder structure:

P\xrightarrow{E_{\phi}}\bm{z}\xrightarrow{D_{\psi}}V,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptM_{\theta}=D_{\psi}\circ E_{\phi},\ \theta=(\phi,\psi).

(1)

In practical deployments, beyond the inherent safety-alignment mechanisms of T2V models, these models are often equipped with external safety guardrails. Such guardrails typically include a pre-input filter
f_{T}:\mathcal{P}\to[0,1]
, which evaluates and rejects unsafe prompts before generation, and a post-generation filter
f_{V}:\mathcal{V}\to[0,1]
, which assesses whether the generated video is harmful and only allows safe outputs to be be released.

Our study focuses on optimizing adversarial prompts
P^{\star}
that can bypass the pre-input filter (if such a filter exists) while still inducing the model
M_{\theta}
to produce harmful video content. Specifically, we assume a realistic black-box threat model, where the attacker interacts with the target T2V system solely through its publicly available API, without access to the model’s weights, architecture, or safety mechanisms. The attacker’s capabilities are limited to those of an ordinary user: they can compose and submit textual prompts and observe the API’s responses, including potential error messages or the generated videos, but possess no privileged or internal access to the system.

3.2
Exploiting Learned Cross-Modal Associations

The vulnerabilities we exploit are rooted in the implicit world knowledge that T2V models acquire from large-scale, multimodal training data. These models learn powerful statistical associations not just between objects and their names, but between complex textual concepts and their visual manifestations. Our attack specifically weaponizes two such channels of association.

Auditory-Visual Association.

The first channel is the strong correlation between sound descriptions and the visual events that produce them. Training corpora are replete with examples like captions saying “the screech of tires as two cars collide,” paired with corresponding video footage. Consequently, the model learns a robust mapping from an auditory concept
A
to a distribution over visual scenes
V
. Formally, the model learns to approximate the joint distribution
p(A,V)
, allowing it to infer plausible visual dynamics when prompted with a sound:

p_{\theta}(V\mid P=A)\propto p(A,V).

(2)

Stylistic-Thematic Association.

The second, equally potent channel is the association between stylistic descriptors and visual themes. Captions often include artistic or cinematic directives, such as “a cinematic shot in the style of Alfred Hitchcock.” From these, the model learns to connect a stylistic concept
S
to a whole constellation of visual attributes, including color grading, shot composition, pacing, and overall mood. A prompt containing “Hitchcock style” thus biases the generation towards suspenseful themes, specific camera angles, and a muted color palette, steering the content semantically without explicit instructions.

Our key insight is that these two associative channels can be composed to create a highly effective steering mechanism, where one channel re-contextualizes the other. A seemingly benign auditory cue can be transformed into a component of a harmful scene when placed within a specific stylistic frame. For instance, the prompt “the sound of a door closing” would typically generate a neutral video. However, embedding this auditory cue within a stylistic modulator, such as “a point-of-view shot, in the style of a found-footage horror film, we hear the sound of a door slowly creaking shut,” dramatically alters the expected outcome. The stylistic context imbues the neutral sound with a sinister meaning, compelling the model to generate a threatening or violent scene.

Our attack leverages this synergy by constructing a prompt
P
that combines an auditory trigger
A
and a stylistic modulator
S
. The model is thus guided to sample from a much more constrained and targeted posterior distribution, conditioned on both concepts:

V\sim p_{\theta}(V\mid P\supset\{A,S\})\propto p(V,A,S).

(3)

This compositional approach allows us to steer the model towards generating prohibited content with remarkable precision, while the individual components of the prompt may appear innocuous. This principle forms the theoretical bedrock for the adversarial grammar we introduce next.

3.3
Jailbreak as a Constrained Optimization Problem

To systematically discover prompts that weaponize the cross-modal associations discussed in Sec.
3.2
, we move beyond manual prompt engineering and formulate the task as a principled optimization problem. The goal is to find an optimal prompt
P^{*}
that maximizes video harmfulness and alignment with the attacker’s intent, while remaining stealthy and adhering to a specific structure that enables the attack. This is challenging due to the discrete nature of the prompt space and the black-box access to the target model.

Guidance Oracles.

To provide the necessary guidance signals for this optimization, we introduce two oracles. The
visual oracle

O_{V}
assesses the generated video
V=M_{\theta}(P)
. It first employs a video captioning model to produce a textual description (caption)
C_{V}
. This caption is then evaluated by an auxiliary LLM to derive a visual safety score
s_{V}\in[0,1]
, which quantifies the harmfulness of the video’s content. The full output is thus
O_{V}(V)=\{C_{V},s_{V}\}
.
The
textual oracle

O_{T}
directly uses an auxiliary LLM to evaluate the input prompt
P
itself, yielding a textual safety score
s_{T}=O_{T}(P)\in[0,1]
that indicates its likelihood of being flagged as malicious.
These oracles provide the essential zeroth-order feedback for our black-box optimization.

Problem Formulation.

Using the signals from our oracles, we formulate the jailbreak task as the following optimization problem:

\begin{split}\min_{P}\quad&\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{harm}}(P)}_{\text{Harmfulness}}+\lambda\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}(P;P_{\text{unsafe}})}_{\text{Alignment}}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&\underbrace{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{grammar}}}_{\text{Structural Constraint}}\quad\text{and}\quad\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\text{stealth}}(P)\leq\tau}_{\text{Stealth Constraint}}.\end{split}

(4)

Our problem formulation consists of an objective function and two primary constraints. The objective function is a weighted sum of two components: a harmfulness loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{harm}}
and an alignment loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}
. The harmfulness loss,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{harm}}(P)=-s_{V}
, directly encourages the generation of visually unsafe content by maximizing the visual safety score
s_{V}
. Concurrently, the alignment loss,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}(P;P_{\text{unsafe}})=1-\text{sim}(\text{E}(P_{\text{unsafe}}),\text{E}(C_{V}))
, ensures that the generated video’s content, as described by its caption
C_{V}
, remains semantically faithful to the attacker’s original unsafe intent
P_{\text{unsafe}}
. Here,
\text{E}(\cdot)
is a sentence embedding model and
\text{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes cosine similarity. The hyperparameter
\lambda
is crucial, as it not only balances these two differently-scaled losses but also conceptually controls the attack strategy: a smaller
\lambda
prioritizes maximizing harmfulness for a general attack, whereas a larger
\lambda
enforces strict semantic fidelity for a precision attack.

The first constraint,
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{grammar}}
, forms the structural backbone of our method. It is the direct embodiment of our synergistic steering strategy from Sec.
3.2
, mandating that the prompt
P
adheres to our
adversarial grammar
. This grammar is defined as the concatenation
P=P_{\text{anchor}}\oplus P_{\text{trigger}}\oplus P_{\text{modulator}}
, where the
trigger
component carries the auditory cue (
A
) and the
modulator
introduces the stylistic context (
S
). This compositional structure is fundamental to our attack, enabling the re-contextualization of benign concepts to produce harmful outcomes, rather than just being a tool for search tractability.

The second constraint,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{stealth}}(P)\leq\tau
, ensures the prompt’s textual inconspicuousness. Defined as
\mathcal{L}_{\text{stealth}}(P)=s_{T}=O_{T}(P)
, it requires the prompt’s textual safety score to remain below the threshold
\tau
, thereby ensuring that the adversarial prompt can bypass pre-emptive, text-based safety filters.

Solving Eq. (
4
) is non-trivial, as it requires navigating a discrete, non-differentiable space with only black-box access to the objective and constraint functions.

3.4
Synergistic Steering Zeroth-Order Search

To solve the constrained optimization problem defined in Eq. (
4
), we employs a LLM to navigate the discrete, grammar-constrained prompt space. The method is a form of zeroth-order optimization because it relies solely on scalar feedback from the oracles, without requiring gradients. The algorithm iteratively explores the neighborhood of a current candidate prompt by proposing modifications that respect the problem’s constraints.

Constraint-Aware Proposer.

Given a current prompt
P^{(t)}
, the proposer generates a set of new candidate prompts
\{P^{\prime}_{1},P^{\prime}_{2},P^{\prime}_{3}\}
. Crucially, the proposer is designed to inherently satisfy the structural constraint
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{grammar}}
. It achieves this by operating at the component level: it is instructed to modify only one of the components (
P_{\text{anchor}}
,
P_{\text{trigger}}
, or
P_{\text{modulator}}
) at a time. The LLM thus acts as an intelligent generator proposing semantically meaningful variations, effectively performing the search within the structured space defined by our adversarial grammar.

Guided Search with Early Termination.

The core of our algorithm is an iterative search process that is guided by the objective function but terminates as soon as a successful attack is found. For this purpose, we define a success threshold for our objective function,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{obj}}\leq\tau_{\text{success}}
, where a sufficiently low loss indicates a successful attack (
i.e
.
, a video that is both harmful and well-aligned).

In each iteration, the framework processes the batch of candidate prompts proposed by the LLM. For each candidate
P^{\prime}
, it first checks the textual stealth constraint,
O_{T}(P^{\prime})\leq\tau
. If the candidate is valid, it is evaluated by querying the T2V model and computing its objective loss,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{obj}}(P^{\prime})
. If this loss is below our success threshold (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{obj}}(P^{\prime})\leq\tau_{\text{success}}
), the search terminates immediately, and
P^{\prime}
is returned as the successful jailbreak prompt.

If no candidate in the entire batch triggers early termination, the algorithm selects the prompt with the lowest objective loss from the batch to act as the seed for the next iteration. This ensures the search continually progresses towards more promising regions of the prompt space, as defined by our optimization objective. The process ends either when a candidate meets the success threshold or when the maximum number of iterations, three, is reached. This strategy makes our approach both principled, since it is guided by a formal objective, and practical because it ceases exploration as soon as a successful jailbreak is achieved. Fig.
2
presents the framework of our method, and the algorithm is provided in App.

Dataset.

We construct our evaluation dataset from T2VSafetyBench

The original dataset provided by T2VSafetyBench is a mixture of pristine prompts and prompts already perturbed by attack methods, which is not suitable for direct head-to-head evaluation. From this filtered set, we then randomly selected 50 prompts per category, resulting in a final evaluation subset of 700 distinct unsafe prompts (
P_{\text{unsafe}}

These 14 categories encompass a wide range of safety concerns, including pornography, borderline pornography, violence, gore, disturbing content, public figures, discrimination, political sensitivity, copyright, illegal activities, misinformation, sequential actions, dynamic variations, and coherent contextual scenes.

Robustness Against Defense

To further evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method against potential defenses, we slightly modify the prompts provided in App.

.
VEIL
remains substantially more robust than all baselines: on Seedance, it sustains a 30.0% ASR, while the next best method (DACA) drops to 6.0%.

This stark contrast reflects a fundamental difference in attack mechanisms. The LLM defender, operating purely in the text domain, easily suppresses baselines: TSB contains explicit harmful cues; DACA merely substitutes sensitive words with transparent euphemisms; and RAB often produces semantically strained prompts that are readily flagged by a strong language model. In contrast,
VEIL
is inherently resistant to semantic inspection. Its prompts are composed of individually benign elements, including a safe anchor, an ambiguous auditory trigger, and a stylistic modulator. None of these components encode harmful intent when considered in isolation. The unsafe meaning emerges only within the T2V model’s cross-modal latent space, making it inaccessible to an LLM that analyzes text alone. As a result, the defense can validate the prompt’s surface-level safety but cannot anticipate the harmful visual outcome. These findings underscore that
VEIL
embodies a qualitatively more resilient, cross-modal attack paradigm capable of bypassing even state-of-the-art LLM-based safeguards.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce a novel class of vulnerabilities in T2V models, revealing that a largely overlooked attack surface lies in the models’ learned, latent cross-modal associations rather than explicit textual semantics. We reframed the jailbreaking problem from a task of obfuscating unsafe prompts to one of orchestrating individually benign concepts to induce harmful outcomes. To this end, we proposed
VEIL
, a framework that formalizes this new paradigm as a constrained optimization problem. By using a novel adversarial grammar and an LLM-guided zeroth-order search,
VEIL
effectively discovers compliant-by-design prompts that weaponize the synergistic interplay between auditory cues and stylistic directives. Our extensive experiments validated this approach, showing that
VEIL
achieves state-of-the-art attack success rates and exhibits remarkable resilience against advanced LLM-based defenses, confirming the potency of attacking a model’s implicit world knowledge.

Limitations.

❶ The LLM-guided search can be query-intensive, as each iteration calls both the LLM and target T2V model multiple times; future work could explore more efficient black-box strategies. ❷
VEIL
’s performance relies on the guidance oracles, and errors in video captioning or safety scoring may introduce noise. As more capable models emerge, the framework’s precision is expected to improve, aiding both attack evaluation and defense.
