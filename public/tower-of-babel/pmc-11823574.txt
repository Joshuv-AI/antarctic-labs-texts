Endocrown-retained fixed partial dentures: Revolutionizing tooth restoration or risky business? A finite element study
Authors: Nivedha Muthukumar, Parthasarathy Natarajan, Seenivasan Madhan Kumar, Shanmuganathan Natarajan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim:This study aimed to compare the biomechanical performance of endocrown-retained and post-and-core-retained fixed partial dentures (FPDs), focusing on stress distribution, tensile, and shear stress.Materials and Methods:Finite element analysis was used to simulate occlusal loading on two FPD designs: endocrown-retained zirconia and post-and-core-retained zirconia which were subjected to a simulated occlusal load of 150 N. Stress distribution was analyzed using von Mises stress, tensile stress, and shear stress. Material properties for zirconia, resin cement, enamel, dentin, bone, Periodontal ligament (PDL), and fiber post were incorporated.Results:The endocrown design showed lower von Mises stress (176.35 MPa) than the post-and-core design (298.29 MPa). It also had lower shear stress at the cement interface and reduced stress on abutment teeth.Conclusion:Endocrown-retained FPDs provide better stress distribution and structural integrity, offering a reliable, minimally invasive option for restoring endodontically treated molars.Clinical Significance:Endocrown retained 3-unit fixed partial denture can be a promising alternative to the fiber-post retained FPD for restoring molars with extensive tooth loss.

INTRODUCTION

The restoration of endodontically treated teeth is a significant challenge in restorative dentistry because of their compromised structural integrity. These teeth often suffer from reduced biomechanical properties such as diminished stiffness and fracture resistance, primarily due to the extensive loss of tooth structure caused by caries, trauma, or endodontic procedures.[1] Moreover, the absence of pulpal tissue and the subsequent loss of neurosensory feedback further complicate their restorative process. Conventionally, the restoration of these compromised teeth involves the use of postretained foundations and crowns. While these methods can improve the retention of prosthetic crowns, they also introduce a greater risk of root fractures due to the additional weakening of the tooth structure.[2] This has led to a search for alternative restorative techniques that can provide adequate retention without compromising the structural integrity of the tooth.

The advent of adhesive dentistry has revolutionized the restoration of endodontically treated teeth. The development of effective dentin adhesives has paved the way for the introduction of endocrowns, a prosthetic option designed to restore teeth with excessive tissue loss. Endocrowns eliminate the need for radicular posts, reducing the necessity for invasive procedures and preserving the remaining tooth structure. This approach aligns with the principles of minimally invasive dentistry, emphasizing tissue conservation and promoting long-term tooth preservation.[3] Endocrowns were first described by Pissis as “mono-block porcelain technique” and were later coined by Bindl and Mormann in 1999.[4] An endocrown is essentially an adhesive monolithic ceramic restoration anchored within the pulp chamber and utilizes the walls of the pulp chamber for micromechanical retention. This technique is particularly advantageous in cases where traditional post and core restorations are not feasible because of the limited interproximal space, short or curved root canals or extensive tissue loss.[5]

The biomechanical performance of endocrowns depends on the factors such as preparation design, material choice and adhesive techniques, with optimal preparation requiring a 2.5 mm flat pulpal floor and a 1 mm butt joint margin for retention and stability. The use of high-strength ceramic materials such as lithium disilicate or zirconia further enhances the fracture resistance of endocrowns, making them suitable for restoring molars subjected to high occlusal forces.[6] Endocrowns offer several advantages over traditional post and core restorations: they require less invasive preparation, preserving more of the natural tooth structure; they reduce the risk of root fractures by eliminating the need for radicular posts; they provide superior esthetics compared with metal-based restorations; and they simplify the restorative procedure, reducing chair time and patient discomfort.[7]

The clinical success of endocrowns has been well-documented, with studies reporting high survival rates and favor outcomes in terms of fracture resistance and marginal integrity. For example, Biacchi and Basting demonstrated that endocrowns fabricated from lithium disilicate had better fracture resistance than traditional post and core restorations.[8] Moreover, clinical trials have confirmed the long-term durability of endocrowns, making them a reliable option for restoring endodontically treated molars. This study evaluates the biomechanical performance of endocrown-retained fixed partial dentures (FPDs) versus traditional post-and-core FPDs, focusing on stress distribution under occlusal loading, tensile and shear stress at the cement interface and within restorative materials, to provide evidence-based recommendations for restoring endodontically treated molars.

The findings of this study are expected to contribute significantly to the field of restorative dentistry by providing insights into the biomechanical advantages of endocrowns. By highlighting their superior performance in terms of stress distribution and fracture resistance, this study aims to support the broader adoption of endocrowns in clinical practice. Ultimately, the goal is to enhance the outcomes of restorative procedures for endodontically treated teeth, ensuring better patient satisfaction and long-term success. In conclusion, the study of endocrown retained FPDs represents a critical advancement in restorative dentistry, offering a promising solution to the challenges associated with restoring endodontically treated teeth. The detailed evaluation of their biomechanical properties will help refine clinical practices and promote the use of minimally invasive, durable, and esthetically pleasing restorative options.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study began by creating a detailed posterior model using CAD software, incorporating two abutment teeth – the second molar and second premolar – commonly involved in posterior restorations. The aim was to compare two FPD designs: an endocrown-retained zirconia FPD and a post-and-core retained zirconia FPD. Abutment preparation followed clinical guidelines, including a 6º axial wall convergence, 1.5 mm thickness, 1.5 mm occlusal reduction, and a shoulder finish line to ensure retention and resistance form.[910] A 100 µm cement layer between the restoration and abutment was simulated to mimic clinical cementation.[11]

For the endocrown abutments, additional modifications included a 2.5-mm pulp chamber depth, a 6º taper, flat pulpal floor, and a 1-mm butt joint margin for secure retention.[1213] A 1.5 mm crown thickness and 7 mm occlusal surface thickness were also specified.[14] These designs were verified and transferred to Ansys CAE (Ansys Inc., Southpointe 2600, Ansys drive, Canonsburg, PA 15317, USA) for further analysis.

Mechanical properties were carefully chosen to simulate each material accurately: zirconia for its strength, resin cement for the adhesive layer, enamel and dentin for abutment teeth, cortical and cancellous bone for supporting structures, PDL for stress distribution, and fiber posts for the post-and-core design [Table 1].

RESULTS

This study conducted a finite element analysis (FEA) to compare the mechanical behavior of two designs for FPDs restoring a missing first molar: an endocrown-retained zirconia FPD and a post-and-core-retained zirconia FPD. The analysis focused on three key stress parameters – von Mises stress, tensile stress and shear stress – under simulated occlusal loading of 150 N to assess stress distribution and potential failure points in both designs [Table 2 and Figures 1, 2].

Von Mises stress

Von Mises stress is used to predict the yielding of materials under the complex loading conditions and is essential for understanding the overall stress distribution within FPDs. The endocrown-retained FPD exhibited a maximum von Mises stress of 176.35 MPa while the post-and-core-retained FPD showed a significantly higher stress of 298.29 MPa. This difference suggests that the endocrown design is more effective in distributing occlusal forces, minimizing stress concentrations that could lead to material failure. The uniform stress distribution in the endocrown-retained FPD is crucial for its longevity and durability, reducing the likelihood of catastrophic failures such as fractures [Table 2 and Figures 1, 2].

Tensile stress

Tensile stress, which stretches and pulls apart materials, was evaluated in both the ceramic material of the FPD and the cement interface. The endocrown-retained FPD demonstrated a maximum tensile stress of 6.287 MPa within the ceramic, compared to 3.893 MPa in the post-and-core FPD. This higher tensile stress in the ceramic of the endocrown indicates its ability to better withstand stretching forces. Moreover, tensile stress at the cement interface was lower in the endocrown design, reflecting improved structural integrity and a reduced risk of failure due to tensile forces. Lower tensile stress within both the ceramic and cement layer of the endocrown-retained FPD suggests a lower risk of fracture initiation and propagation, enhancing the restoration’s structural stability over time [Table 2 and Figures 1, 2].

Shear stress

Shear stress which is critical for evaluating the bond strength between materials was analyzed at the cement interface. The endocrown-retained FPD exhibited a maximum shear stress of 0.589 MPa, significantly lower than the shear stress observed in the post-and-core-retained FPD. This reduced shear stress in the endocrown design implies a more reliable adhesive bond between the restoration and the abutment teeth, decreasing the likelihood of debonding – a common cause of failure in dental restorations. The lower shear stress observed in the endocrown-retained FPD underscores its potential for better long-term performance and durability [Table 2 and Figures 1, 2].

Abutment teeth stress

The stress distribution within the abutment teeth was also evaluated to assess the impact of each FPD design on the supporting structures. Both FPD designs performed similarly in terms of stress distribution within the abutment teeth, although the endocrown-retained FPD showed a slight advantage, with lower maximum stress values in the abutment teeth compared to the post-and-core design. This reduction in stress concentration in the abutment teeth is beneficial, as it minimizes the risk of secondary complications, such as tooth fractures or recurrent caries, which could compromise the restoration’s long-term success [Table 2 and Figures 1, 2].

Crown stress

Stress within the crown portion of the FPDs was also analyzed. The endocrown-retained FPD exhibited a maximum crown stress of 176.35 MPa, much lower than the 298.29 MPa observed in the post-and-core-retained FPD. This lower stress concentration within the crown suggests that the endocrown design is more effective at distributing occlusal forces, reducing the likelihood of crown fractures – a common failure mode in dental restorations. The uniform stress distribution in the endocrown-retained FPD contributes to its ability to withstand functional loads without compromising the integrity of the crown material [Table 2 and Figures 1, 2].

Monoblock design advantage

The superior mechanical performance of the endocrown-retained FPD is largely due to its monoblock structure, which integrates the crown and core into a single unit. This design minimizes stress concentrations and distributes forces more evenly, reducing the risk of material failure. In contrast, the post-and-core-retained FPD exhibited higher tensile and shear stress concentrations, which can lead to decementation – one of the primary failure modes in these restorations. The fiber post in the post-and-core design introduces additional stress points that can weaken the overall structure, particularly under repeated functional loads.

Fracture strength

The endocrown-retained FPD demonstrated superior fracture strength compared to the post-and-core-retained FPD, indicating a lower risk of catastrophic failure such as root fracture or crown debonding. The more favorable stress distribution in the endocrown-retained FPD suggests that it can better resist occlusal forces, enhancing the restoration’s longevity and reducing the need for future replacements.

DISCUSSION

The FEA in this study revealed significant advantages of endocrown-retained zirconia FPDs over post-and-core-retained zirconia FPDs showing better stress distribution, lower tensile and shear stresses, and improved structural integrity. The monoblock design of the endocrown which integrates the crown and core, minimizes stress concentrations and promotes uniform occlusal force distribution.[15] This aligns with the findings by Bindl et al.[16] and Biacchi and Basting[8] who reported lower stress concentrations and reduced material failure risks in endocrowns.

Endocrowns also demonstrated lower von Mises stress (176.35 MPa) compared to post-and-core designs (298.29 MPa), indicating a reduced risk of material failure.[16] In addition, the lower tensile stress in the endocrown (6.287 MPa) compared to post-and-core FPDs (3.893 MPa) aligns with Pissis,[17] who emphasized minimizing tensile stress to prevent fractures. The lower shear stress of the endocrown at the cement interface (0.589 MPa) supports findings by Magne et al.,[18] indicating a more reliable adhesive bond.

Clinical studies further validate these findings. Rocca et al. and Bindl et al. reported superior survival rates for endocrowns with 2-year survival rates of 100% compared to 66.7% for post-and-core restorations.[1619] Failures in post-and-core restorations often stemmed from fractures or recurrent caries, while endocrowns showed fewer catastrophic failures. In addition, Belleflamme et al.[20] and Godil et al. (2021)[21] noted better mechanical performance and esthetics for endocrowns, reinforcing their clinical benefits.

Endocrowns not only simplify clinical procedures, reducing chair time and patient discomfort, but also offer superior mechanical performance, leading to greater long-term success and reduced replacement costs.[22] However, as FEA is a computational model, future clinical trials are needed to validate these results and explore the impact of different materials and cementation techniques on endocrown performance.

The study’s findings are based on FEA, which models idealized conditions. In the real-world scenarios, patient-specific factors such as variations in tooth anatomy, occlusal forces, and oral habits can significantly impact the effectiveness and longevity of endocrown-retained restorations. These factors might limit the generalizability of the study’s results. While FEA provides valuable insights into stress distribution and potential failure points, it is a computational tool that cannot fully replicate the complex biological environment of the oral cavity. Therefore, the outcomes should be validated through clinical trials to confirm their applicability in a clinical setting.

The study’s findings that endocrown-retained FPDs exhibit lower von Mises stress, tensile stress, and shear stress suggest that these restorations could have a longer survival time compared to traditional post-and-core designs. This reduction in stress could minimize the risk of material failure and fractures over time, leading to fewer complications and potentially reducing the need for replacement or repairs. The lower stress concentrations in endocrowns indicate a decreased likelihood of common restoration failures, such as debonding or structural fractures. This could result in fewer complications, contributing to better long-term clinical outcomes for patients.

Endocrowns are particularly advantageous in cases with significant tissue loss, short or curved root canals, or limited interproximal space where traditional post-and-core restorations may be less effective or pose higher risks of failure. The endocrown’s monoblock design, which integrates the crown and core, offers superior stress distribution and structural integrity, making it an ideal choice in these challenging scenarios.

Future studies should focus on validating these FEA results through long-term clinical trials and exploring the impact of different materials, cementation techniques, and patient-specific factors on the performance of endocrowns. This could help to confirm the clinical relevance of the biomechanical advantages observed in the study and further support the adoption of endocrowns in restorative dentistry.

CONCLUSION

A detailed analysis of the stress distribution, tensile and shear stresses, and clinical survival rates strongly supports the use of endocrown retained zirconia FPDs as superior alternatives to post and core retained zirconia FPDs for the restoration of extensively damaged molars.[23] The findings of this study highlight the mechanical advantages, clinical relevance, and potential for improved patient outcomes associated with endocrown restorations.
