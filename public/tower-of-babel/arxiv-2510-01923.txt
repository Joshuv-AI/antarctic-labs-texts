The Constant Speed Schedule for Adiabatic State Preparation: Towards Quadratic Speedup without Prior Spectral Knowledge
Mancheon Han, Hyowon Park, Sangkook Choi

Abstract
The efficiency of adiabatic quantum evolution is governed by the adiabatic evolution time, T T , which depends on the minimum energy gap, Δ \Delta . For a generic schedule, T T typically scales as Δ − 2 \Delta^{-2} , whereas the rigorous lower bound is 𝒪 ⁡ ( Δ − 1 ) \mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-1}) . This indicates the potential for a quadratic speedup through the adiabatic schedule construction. Here, we introduce the constant speed schedule, which traverses the adiabatic path of the eigenstate at a uniform rate. We first show that this approach reduces the scaling of the upper bound of the required evolution time by one order in 1 / Δ 1/\Delta . We then provide a segmented constant speed schedule protocol, in which path segment lengths are computed from eigenstate overlaps along the adiabatic evolution. By relying on the overlaps on the fly, our method eliminates the need for prior spectral knowledge. We test our algorithm numerically on the adiabatic unstructured search, the N 2 molecule, and the [2Fe-2S] cluster. In our numerical experiments, the method achieves the optimal 1 / Δ 1/\Delta scaling in a small gap region, thereby demonstrating a quadratic speedup over the standard linear schedule.



I
Introduction

The adiabatic theorem
Born and Fock 1928
;
Kato 1950
;
Jansen et al. 2007
states that
a quantum system remains in its instantaneous eigenstate as long as the state
of interest is separated by an energy gap from other eigenstates and the Hamiltonian changes
sufficiently slowly. This principle, developed about a century ago, plays
important roles in modern quantum computation
Farhi et al. 2000
;
Georgescu et al. 2014
;
Albash and Lidar 2018
;
Aspuru-Guzik et al. 2005
;
Veis and Pittner 2014
.
The adiabatic (analog) quantum computer
Farhi et al. 2000
;
Georgescu et al. 2014
;
Albash and Lidar 2018
exploits this principle to
obtain the ground state of a complex Hamiltonian by continuously manipulating the effective Hamiltonian
of a quantum device. Adiabatic evolution can also be implemented within the
circuit model
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
using discrete quantum gates, a method called adiabatic state preparation (ASP)
Aspuru-Guzik et al. 2005
;
Veis and Pittner 2014
.
ASP is regarded as one of the standard approaches for preparing quantum states
sufficiently close to a target eigenstate, enabling other algorithms such as quantum
phase estimation
Kitaev 1995
;
Abrams and Lloyd 1999
to obtain precise eigenstates and
their corresponding eigenvalues.

In both analog and digital implementations of adiabatic evolution,
the key cost factor is the total evolution time
T
. We consider
the adiabatically evolved state
\ket{\psi(T)}
acceptable if its
fidelity with the target eigenstate
\ket{\Phi_{f}}
,

\mathcal{F}=|\braket{\psi(T)|\Phi_{f}}|^{2}
, exceeds a certain threshold (e.g.,
\mathcal{F}\geq 0.5
).
Accordingly, we use the term
adiabatic evolution time

T

to denote the minimum evolution duration required to achieve a fidelity above the threshold.

Among various parameters that affect the required adiabatic evolution time
T
,
the minimal energy gap
\Delta
plays a major role. Both lower and upper bounds for
T
in terms of
\Delta
are established
Albash and Lidar 2018
. The adiabatic evolution
starts with the eigenstate
\ket{\Phi_{i}}
of the easily solvable
Hamiltonian
H_{i}
, and aims to obtain the eigenstate
\ket{\Phi_{f}}
of
H_{f}
,
the target Hamiltonian. The rigorous adiabatic theorem
Jansen et al. 2007

applies to any variation of the Hamiltonian between
H_{i}
and
H_{f}

as long as
H(t)
, the Hamiltonian as a function of time
t
,
is a function of the renormalized time
\tau=t/T

that starts with
H_{i}
and ends at
H_{f}
.
Here we narrow down our discussion to the following form ,

\displaystyle H(s(\tau))=H_{i}+(H_{f}-H_{i})s(\tau),

(1)

The parameter
s
is a monotonically increasing function of the normalized time
\tau
, referred to as the schedule. The schedule
s(\tau)
maps the normalized time
\tau\in[0,1]
to the interpolation parameter
s\in[0,1]
, satisfying
s(0)=0
and
s(1)=1
.

For the given adiabatic path
\{\ket{\Phi(s)}\}_{s\in[0,1]}
of instantaneous eigenstates
\ket{\Phi(s)}
of
H(s)
, the lower bound for
T
is given by
Boixo and Somma 2010

\displaystyle T>\mathcal{O}(L/\Delta)

(2)

where
L=\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\partial_{s}\ket{\Phi(s)}\right\|ds
is
the adiabatic path length. As
L
is often independent
of
\Delta

Boixo and Somma 2010
, the required adiabatic evolution time
T

scales at least as
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-1})
.
On the other hand, a sufficient condition for adiabaticity
Jansen et al. 2007
;
Albash and Lidar 2018

yields an upper bound,

\displaystyle T<\max\biggl\{

\displaystyle\max_{\tau\in[0,1]}\frac{\|\ddot{H}(s(\tau))\|}{\Delta^{2}(s(\tau))},\max_{\tau\in[0,1]}\frac{\|\dot{H}(s(\tau))\|^{2}}{\Delta^{3}(s(\tau))},

\displaystyle\max_{\tau\in[0,1]}\frac{\|\dot{H}(s(\tau))\|}{\Delta^{2}(s(\tau))}\biggr\},

(3)

where
\dot{\phantom{v}}
denotes a derivative with respect to
\tau
. This condition implies that
T
scales at most as

\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-2})
or even
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-3})
.

This different scaling behaviors between upper and lower bound highlights the potential role of scheduling, i.e., the choice of
s(\tau)
. The adiabatic Grover algorithm exemplifies this point: with a standard linear schedule one requires
T=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-2})
, whereas with an optimized schedule one attains the optimal
T=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-1})
scaling
Roland and Cerf 2002
. This insight into the scheduling
Roland and Cerf 2002

motivated numerous studies for optimal scheduling strategies
Rezakhani et al. 2009
;
Rezakhani et al. 2010
;
Isermann 2021
;
Matsuura et al. 2021
;
Shingu and Hatomura 2025
;
Jarret et al.
;
Braida et al. 2025
.
These approaches can be broadly categorized by their reliance on spectral information.
Spectra-informed methods exploit knowledge of eigenvalues or eigenstates to construct the schedule.
For example, a local schedule proportional to the inverse square of the instantaneous gap achieves optimal scaling in the adiabatic Grover search
Roland and Cerf 2002
,
while the adiabatic brachistochrone framework minimizes a geometric cost functional involving spectral projectors and resolvents
Rezakhani et al. 2009
.
In general, certain families of Hamiltonians (e.g. Hamiltonian that is diagonal in computational basis) admit quadratic speedups if
\Delta(s)
(or related quantities) is available a priori
Jarret et al.
;
Braida et al. 2025
.
These spectra-informed approaches can offer optimality guarantees in some cases. However, they are generally infeasible for large or interacting systems where spectral data is unknown in advance
Braida et al. 2025
. Spectra-uninformed strategies include variational frameworks that optimize parameterized schedules without spectral input
Matsuura et al. 2021
,
as well as Krylov-subspace-based approximations to the adiabatic gauge potential that yield trajectories close to geodesics
Shingu and Hatomura 2025
. Spectra-uninformed methods alleviate the limitation of the spectra-informed spetratey but often rely heavily on the choice of ansatz
Matsuura et al. 2021

or lack rigorous performance guarantees.

In this work, we introduce a new algorithm to construct schedules that
improve the gap scaling. Inspired by the geometric origin of
the optimal
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-1})
scaling, our approach
focuses on controlling the evolution speed along the adiabatic path.
We propose a
constant speed schedule
, which traverses
the adiabatic path at a uniform rate, and show that it
can improve the scaling of the upper bound of
T
in
1/\Delta
by one order
through theoretical analysis of the adiabatic error.
We then propose a practical classical–quantum hybrid algorithm to
implement this schedule using eigenstate overlaps computed along the evolution. Finally, we demonstrate
the effectiveness of our method on three distinct problems: the adiabatic
Grover algorithm, the ground-state preparation of the N
2
molecule, and a [2Fe-2S]
molecular cluster. In all cases, our algorithm successfully finds schedules that
exhibit the optimal
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-1})
scaling for a small gap region,
a quadratic speedup over
the
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-2})
scaling of the standard linear schedule.
Unlike conventional spectra-informed methods, our method computes the
necessary quantities for scheduling on the fly
without spectral knowledge
a priori
.

II
Methods

Before discussing the methods in detail, we first establish the notation used
throughout this section. As in the introduction, we denote the initial and final Hamiltonians as
H_{i}
and

H_{f}
, with corresponding eigenstates
\ket{\Phi_{i}}
and
\ket{\Phi_{f}}
along the adiabatic path.
The scaling analysis of the total evolution time
T
under the adiabatic theorem considers the Hamiltonian as a function of the normalized time,
\tau=t/T
.
The derivative with respect to
\tau
is denoted by
\dot{\phantom{x}}
.
The state of the system at time
t
,
\ket{\psi(t)}
, evolves from the initial state

\ket{\Phi_{i}}
according to the Schrödinger equation. Using the normalized time

\tau
, this is formally written as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(t)}=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-iT\int_{0}^{\tau}H(s(\tau^{\prime}))d\tau^{\prime}\right)\ket{\Phi_{i}},

(4)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the time-ordering operator.
From now on, we will adopt a simplified convention. Depending on the
context, the composite function
H(s(\tau))
will be denoted as
H(\tau)
or
H(s)
.
Similarly, the instantaneous eigenstate
\ket{\Phi(s(\tau))}
will be denoted as either

\ket{\Phi(\tau)}
or
\ket{\Phi(s)}
.

II.1
Adiabatic Evolution Error

We begin by analyzing the adiabatic evolution error, which quantifies the
deviation of the evolved state from the desired final eigenstate. This error is
measured as
1-\mathcal{F}
, where
\mathcal{F}=|\braket{\psi(T)|\Phi_{f}}|^{2}
is the fidelity between the final state

\ket{\psi(T)}
and the target eigenstate
\ket{\Phi_{f}}
. The adiabatic theorem
provides an upper bound on this error
Jansen et al. 2007
:

\displaystyle 1-\mathcal{F}\leq\left(\frac{\mathcal{C}[s]}{T}\right)^{2}.

(5)

Here, the functional
\mathcal{C}[s]
depends on the chosen schedule
s(\tau)

and is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}[s]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1\}}\frac{\|\dot{P}\|}{\Delta(\tau)}+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\|Q\ddot{P}P\|}{\Delta(\tau)}d\tau

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\|\dot{P}\|^{2}}{\Delta(\tau)}d\tau+\int_{0}^{1}2\frac{\|\dot{H}\|\|\dot{P}\|}{\Delta^{2}(\tau)}d\tau,

(6)

where
P(\tau)=\ket{\Phi(\tau)}\bra{\Phi(\tau)}
is the projection
operator onto the instantaneous eigenstate
\ket{\Phi(\tau)}
of interest,

Q(\tau)=\mathbb{I}-P(\tau)
, and
\Delta(\tau)
denotes the minimum
energy gap between the eigenvalue of
\ket{\Phi(\tau)}
and the rest of the
spectrum of
H(\tau)
. For notational simplicity, the explicit
\tau

dependence has been omitted in the numerators of the integrands above. The
evolution time
T
required to reach a chosen fidelity threshold is therefore
proportional to
\mathcal{C}[s]
.

If one does not exploit the geometric structure of the adiabatic path, the terms
in Eq. (
6
) can be bounded using derivatives of the
Hamiltonian and the inverse of the energy gap
Jansen et al. 2007
:

\displaystyle\|\dot{P}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\dot{H}(\tau)\|}{\Delta(\tau)},

\displaystyle\|Q\ddot{P}P\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\ddot{H}(\tau)\|}{\Delta(\tau)}+4\frac{\|\dot{H}(\tau)\|^{2}}{\Delta^{2}(\tau)}.

(7)

Substituting these estimates into Eq. (
6
) yields the
commonly cited scalings for the required evolution time:

\displaystyle T=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\|\dot{H}\|}{\Delta^{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\|\ddot{H}\|}{\Delta^{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\|\dot{H}\|^{2}}{\Delta^{3}}\right).

(8)

This explains the origin of the non-optimal
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-2})
and

\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-3})
scaling discussed in the introduction.

II.2
The Constant Speed Schedule

The lower bound of the adiabatic evolution
time is determined by the geometrical properties of the adiabatic path of
eigenstates
Boixo and Somma 2010
. This suggests that the scaling of the adiabatic evolution time
with respect to the minimum gap can be improved by relating
Eq. (
6
) to geometric properties of the path. In
geometrical terms, the natural parametrization of a curve is by its arc
length
O’Neill 2006
, defined as

\displaystyle l(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\|\partial_{s^{\prime}}\ket{\Phi(s^{\prime})}\|\,ds^{\prime}.

(9)

Accordingly, the instantaneous speed along the path is

\displaystyle v(\tau)=\frac{dl}{d\tau}=\|\ket{\dot{\Phi}(\tau)}\|.

(10)

The quantity
\|\dot{P}\|
in Eq. (
6
) is exactly this speed
v(\tau)
:

\displaystyle\|\dot{P}\|

\displaystyle=\|\ket{\dot{\Phi}(\tau)}\bra{\Phi(\tau)}+\ket{\Phi(\tau)}\bra{\dot{\Phi}(\tau)}\|

\displaystyle=\|\ket{\dot{\Phi}(\tau)}\|=v(\tau).

(11)

The second term, involving
\|Q\ddot{P}P\|
, is associated with the
curvature of the adiabatic path, which can be defined as
Alsing and Cafaro 2024

\displaystyle\kappa(\tau)=\Bigl\|Q(\tau)\frac{d^{2}}{dl^{2}}\ket{\Phi(\tau)}\Bigr\|.

(12)

Using
\|Q\ddot{P}P\|=\|Q\ket{\ddot{\Phi}}\|
, it can be rewritten in
terms of
v(\tau)
,
\dot{v}(\tau)
, and
\kappa(\tau)
as (see Sec. I A of the Supplementary Information for detailed derivation)

\displaystyle\|Q\ddot{P}P\|=\sqrt{\dot{v}^{2}(\tau)+\kappa^{2}(\tau)v^{4}(\tau)}.

(13)

Thus,
\mathcal{C}[s]
in Eq. (
6
) can be written with
geometric terms as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}[s]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1\}}\frac{v(\tau)}{\Delta(\tau)}+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sqrt{\dot{v}^{2}(\tau)+\kappa^{2}(\tau)v^{4}(\tau)}}{\Delta(\tau)}d\tau

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{v^{2}(\tau)}{\Delta(\tau)}d\tau+\int_{0}^{1}2\frac{\|\dot{H}(\tau)\|v(\tau)}{\Delta^{2}(\tau)}d\tau.

(14)

For the constant-speed schedule
s_{c}(\tau)
, the evolution speed becomes a
global property of the path,
v(\tau)=L
with
L=\int_{0}^{1}v(\tau)d\tau
, and
the acceleration term
\dot{v}
vanishes. Substituting these into
Eq. (
14
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}[s_{c}]=

\displaystyle L\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1\}}\frac{1}{\Delta(\tau)}+L^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\kappa(\tau)+1}{\Delta(\tau)}d\tau

\displaystyle+2L\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\|\dot{H}(\tau)\|}{\Delta^{2}(\tau)}d\tau.

(15)

This leads to the following scaling for the total time
T
:

\displaystyle T=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{L}{\Delta}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{L^{2}(\kappa+1)}{\Delta}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{L\|\dot{H}\|}{\Delta^{2}}\right).

(16)

Comparing with Eq. (
8
), the advantage of the constant-speed
schedule becomes evident: every contribution to the bound improves by one order
in
\Delta
. The reduced gap dependence is replaced by geometric quantities
(
L,\kappa
) that are typically independent of
\Delta

Boixo and Somma 2010
,
which makes this one-order improvement meaningful in practice.
Consequently, the constant-speed schedule would yield the optimal

T=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-1})
scaling in cases where arbitrary schedules
require
T=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{-2})
, for example Hamiltonians belonging to the Gevrey class
Elgart and Hagedorn 2012
.

II.3
The implementation of the Constant Speed Schedule

The preceding analysis shows that the constant speed schedule
improves the scaling of the adiabatic evolution time by one order in the energy gap. We now propose a method to
implement this schedule based on the length of adiabatic path segments,

\displaystyle\Delta l=\int_{s}^{s+\Delta s}\left\|\frac{d\ket{\Phi(s^{\prime})}}{ds^{\prime}}\right\|ds^{\prime}.

(17)

The equation suggests that we can traverse the adiabatic path in a uniform rate
by keeping the ratio
\Delta l/\Delta t
constant.
This quantity can be obtained from the overlap between nearby
eigenstates, as stated below.

Lemma 1

Let
\ket{\Phi(s)}
be a normalized and differentiable family of eigenstates of a parameter-dependent Hamiltonian
H(s)
, describing an adiabatic path for
s\in[0,1]
. Then, to the leading order in
\Delta s
, the overlap between nearby eigenstates satisfies

\displaystyle|\braket{\Phi(s)|\Phi(s+\Delta s)}|^{2}=1-(\Delta l)^{2},

(18)

where
\Delta l
is defined in Eq. (
17
).

Proof.

By differentiating
\braket{\Phi(s)|\Phi(s)}=1
with respect to
s
, we obtain

\partial_{s}\braket{\Phi(s)|\Phi(s)}=2\operatorname{Re}\braket{\Phi(s)|\partial_{s}\Phi(s)}=0.

The global phase of
\ket{\Phi(s)}
is arbitrary,
so we fix the gauge such that

\braket{\Phi(s)|\partial_{s}\Phi(s)}

is real. Then the above equation implies

\braket{\Phi(s)|\partial_{s}\Phi(s)}=0.

Differentiating this condition once more gives

\braket{\Phi(s)|\partial_{s}^{2}\Phi(s)}=-\left\|\partial_{s}\ket{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}.

Using a second-order Taylor expansion of
\ket{\Phi(s+\Delta s)}
, we obtain

|\braket{\Phi(s)|\Phi(s+\Delta s)}|^{2}=1-\left\|\partial_{s}\ket{\Phi(s)}\right\|^{2}(\Delta s)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((\Delta s)^{3}).

Meanwhile, the path segment length satisfies

\Delta l=\left\|\partial_{s}\ket{\Phi(s)}\right\|\Delta s+\mathcal{O}((\Delta s)^{2}).

Substituting this into the overlap expression yields

|\braket{\Phi(s)|\Phi(s+\Delta s)}|^{2}=1-(\Delta l)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((\Delta s)^{3}),

which proves the lemma.

\Box

The following theorem provides a discretized approximation
to the constant speed schedule.

Theorem 1

Suppose a monotonic sequence
s_{0}=0<s_{1}<\dots<s_{m}=1
is given, and that the eigenstate overlaps

|\braket{\Phi(s_{j})|\Phi(s_{j+1})}|^{2}=1-(\Delta l_{j+1})^{2}

are known for each adjacent pair
(s_{j},s_{j+1})
. Then, any schedule

\hat{s}(\tau)
satisfying
\hat{s}(\tau_{j})=s_{j}
and

\tau_{j+1}=\tau_{j}+\frac{\Delta l_{j+1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta l_{i}}

converges to the constant speed schedule
s_{c}
in the limit
\max_{j}(s_{j+1}-s_{j})\rightarrow 0
.

Proof.

By construction, the average speed on each interval
[s_{j},s_{j+1}]
is given by

\frac{\Delta l_{j+1}}{\tau_{j+1}-\tau_{j}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta l_{i}.

As
\Delta s_{j}\rightarrow 0
, we have
\tau_{j+1}-\tau_{j}\rightarrow 0
, and the left-hand side converges to the instantaneous speed in normalized time
\tau
:

\frac{\Delta l_{j+1}}{\tau_{j+1}-\tau_{j}}\rightarrow\left\|\frac{d}{d\tau}\ket{\Phi_{s}(\tau)}\right\|_{\tau=\tau_{j}}.

At the same time, the right-hand side satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta l_{i}\rightarrow L
.
Therefore, in the limit
\max_{j}(s_{j+1}-s_{j})\rightarrow 0
,

v_{s}(\tau)=\left\|\ket{\dot{\Phi}_{s}(\tau)}\right\|=L,

so we have the constant speed schedule.

\Box

Based on Theorem
1
, we can construct a segmented constant speed
schedule,
\hat{s}_{c}(\tau)
, by interpolating the discrete points
\{(\tau_{j},s_{j})\}
.
This schedule serves as a practical, discretized realization of a true constant
speed schedule. The core challenge in this approach lies in the computation
of the path segment lengths
\Delta l_{j}
, which, according to
Lemma
1
, requires calculating the eigenstate overlaps

|\braket{\Phi(s_{j})|\Phi(s_{j+1})}|^{2}
.

One approach to measure this overlap is through the use of projection operators,

P(s)=\ket{\Phi(s)}\bra{\Phi(s)}
. The squared overlap can be computed from the
ratio of two expectation values:

\displaystyle|\braket{\Phi(s_{j})|\Phi(s_{j+1})}|^{2}=\frac{p_{j}(s_{j+1})}{f_{j}},

(19)

where

\displaystyle f_{j}

\displaystyle=\|P(s_{j})\ket{\psi(t_{j})}\|^{2},

(20)

\displaystyle p_{j}(s_{j+1})

\displaystyle=\|P(s_{j+1})P(s_{j})\ket{\psi(t_{j})}\|^{2}.

(21)

The quantity
f_{j}
serves as a fidelity estimate between the evolved state

\ket{\psi(t_{j})}
and the instantaneous eigenstate
\ket{\Phi(s_{j})}
, and it remains
non-negligible as long as the evolution time is sufficiently long. Consequently, the
segment length is computed as

\displaystyle\Delta l_{j+1}=\sqrt{1-\frac{p_{j}(s_{j+1})}{f_{j}}}.

(22)

This procedure provides a general framework for constructing the schedule, assuming
one has a method to implement the required projection operators. The entire procedure
is summarized in Algorithm
II.3
, and a schematic illustration is
provided in Fig.
1
.

At first glance, it might appear simpler to compute segment lengths at pre-determined uniform grid points rather than using the root-finding procedure in Algorithm
II.3
. However, as seen in the adiabatic Grover problem (Fig. 2),
the energy gap
\Delta(s)
often becomes small only in a very narrow region of
s
,
so a uniform grid may fail to capture the point where long evolution time is required.
In contrast, our root-finding procedure adaptively places grid points in proportion to the required time, ensuring that such regions are properly resolved.

A concrete implementation of the projection operators required in
Algorithm
II.3
can be realized using the Quantum Zeno
Monte Carlo (QZMC) method
Han et al. 2025
. The QZMC method
approximates the projector with a Gaussian that can be represented as a integral of
time evolution,

\displaystyle P(s)

\displaystyle\approx e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(H(s)-E(s))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}}e^{-i(H(s)-E(s))t}dt

(23)

where
E(s)
is the energy eigenvalue of
H(s)
corresponding to
\ket{\Phi(s)}
. This approach
requires two additional parameters:
\beta
, which determines the
width of the projection operator, and the number of Monte Carlo
samples
N_{\nu}
to construct integral in Eq. (
23
). The accuracy of this approximation and the
associated resources are governed by these two parameters.
And the energy eigenvalue
E(s)
is determined using the method described in
Sec. II of the Supplementary Information.

We analyze the computational cost of the proposed algorithm in terms of circuit depth and iteration count.

The
circuit depth
is determined by the total time evolution
length, which consists of the adiabatic evolution time
T

and the additional time length
T_{p}
required to implement the
projection operator. The
latter scales as
\mathcal{O}(\beta)

Han et al. 2025
,
where
\beta
is the parameter in the Gaussian approximation of
the projection operator in Eq. (
23
).
Therefore, the total time evolution length is

\displaystyle T_{\text{tot}}=T+\mathcal{O}(\beta),

(24)

Here,
T
is a adiabatic evolution time with the constant speed schedule.
For our algorithm to be reliable, the adiabatic evolution time

should be large enough to ensure
f_{j}>1-\eta
for all

, with
\eta
taken
not too large (e.g.,
\eta<0.5
).

To construct the projector that resolves the target eigenstate from
nearby eigenstates,
\beta
should satisfy
\beta>\Delta_{\text{min}}^{-1}
, where
\Delta_{\text{min}}
denotes the minimum energy
gap in the adiabatic path, i.e.,
\Delta_{\text{min}}=\operatorname{min}_{\tau}\Delta(\tau)
. In our numerical experiments,
setting
\beta=2\Delta_{\text{min}}^{-1}
was sufficient. Therefore,

\displaystyle T_{\text{tot}}=T+\mathcal{O}(\Delta_{\text{min}}^{-1}).

(25)

This shows that the additional cost from the projection operator,

\mathcal{O}(\Delta_{\text{min}}^{-1})
, matches the optimal scaling (Eq. (
2
)) of
adiabatic algorithms with respect to the minimum energy gap.
Therefore, the projection does not worsen the overall scaling of the
evolution time. Further rigorous analysis shows that the required
\beta
also depends on
parameters such as the target segment length
\Delta l_{t}
and the precision

\epsilon
of the estimate
p_{j}(s_{j+1})/f_{j}
, which are independent of the Hamiltonian,
so is not discussed here. Such details are provided in Sec. II of the Supplementary Information.

The
iteration count
of the algorithm is determined by two
factors: (i) the number of schedule segments
m
, and (ii) the
number of Monte Carlo evaluations required for each segment.

The number of segments is determined by the overlap condition

\displaystyle|\braket{\Phi(s_{j})|\Phi(s_{j+1})}|^{2}=1-\Delta l_{t}^{2},\quad\Delta l_{j+1}=\Delta l_{t}.

(26)

Since the total path length
L
is approximately equal to
\sum_{j}\Delta l_{j}
, the number of segments is

\displaystyle m\approx\frac{L}{\Delta l_{t}}.

(27)

The primary computational task within each segment is the
root-finding search for the next point
s_{j+1}
that satisfies
Eq. (
26
). This search is iterative,
requiring multiple evaluations of the overlap. We define

\overline{N}_{\text{root}}
as the average number of these
evaluations needed per segment. In addition to these

\overline{N}_{\text{root}}
steps, a separate evaluation is
required to compute the fidelity term
f_{j}
. Therefore, the total
number of Monte Carlo evaluations per segment is approximately

\overline{N}_{\text{root}}+1
.
Each of these evaluations uses
N_{\nu}
Monte Carlo samples, and the
standard deviation for this statistical estimation is bounded by
\sqrt{2}N_{\nu}^{-1/2}(1-\eta)^{-1}
(See
Sec. II C in the Supplementary Information for derivation). To ensure the error in
the overlap estimation is below a threshold
\epsilon
, it suffices
to set
N_{\nu}\geq 2\epsilon^{-2}(1-\eta)^{-2}
.

Combining these factors, the total number of Monte Carlo samples
required,
N_{\text{total}}=m\cdot(\overline{N}_{\text{root}}+1)\cdot N_{\nu}
, is

\displaystyle N_{\text{total}}\approx(\overline{N}_{\text{root}}+1)\frac{L}{\Delta l_{t}}\frac{2}{\epsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{(1-\eta)^{2}}.

(28)

In our numerical experiments, we used
\Delta l_{t}=0.2

and
N_{\nu}=10^{4}
, which yielded accurate
schedules with moderate computational overhead. Under these
parameter settings, the average number of root-finding steps per
segment was observed to be
\overline{N}_{\text{root}}<20
.

Finally, we highlight several practical aspects of the algorithm.
First, in many applications, the minimum gap
\Delta
is not
known beforehand. For such cases, a suitable value of

\beta
can be found empirically by starting with a small value
and iteratively increasing it until convergence is reached. Second,
the initial segment time
t_{1}
sets the overall timescale for
the evolution via the relation
T\approx t_{1}\cdot(L/\Delta l_{t})
. A suitable value for
t_{1}
can be estimated from
the application of Eq. (
6
) through a perturbative expansion
\ket{\Phi(s)}
near
s=0

or can be adjusted iteratively until the
fidelity
f_{1}
exceeds a desired threshold. Furthermore, the
root-finding step in Algorithm
II.3
need not be highly
precise. Theorem
1
does not require the segment
lengths
\Delta l_{j}
to be uniform; it is sufficient to
identify a point
s_{j+1}
that yields an overlap close to the
target value, so only a few root-finding steps are necessary. Lastly, for
constructing a continuous schedule from the discrete
points, we employed monotonic cubic spline
interpolation
Fritsch and Butland 1984
, as implemented in
SciPy
Virtanen et al. 2020
. Our numerical results in
Figs.
2
-
4
show that while this interpolation
method can introduce small variations in the final fidelity, it does
not affect the scaling of the adiabatic evolution time
with respect to the minimum gap.

III
Applications

We apply our method to a variety of quantum systems, ranging from quantum search
problems to quantum chemistry. In the following analysis, we account for the projection
time overhead,
T_{p}
, required by Algorithm
II.3
. The projector is
implemented as an integral of time evolutions weighted by a Gaussian with standard
deviation
\beta
. To ensure this projector can resolve the minimum energy gap,
\Delta\equiv\min_{s}\Delta(s)
, we set
\beta=2\Delta^{-1}
. Since each step of the
algorithm requires two such projection operations to compute the necessary overlap, we
estimate the total overhead
T_{p}
as
T_{p}=2\beta=4\Delta^{-1}
, which is used
throughout this section. Moreover, as mentioned earlier,
we used
\Delta l_{t}=0.2
and
N_{\nu}=10^{4}
throughout our numerical calculations.

III.1
Adiabatic Grover Search

The Grover search algorithm
Grover 1997
is a well-known quantum algorithm that
identifies a marked item in an unstructured database. While a classical algorithm
requires
\mathcal{O}(N)
queries to find the target among
N
items, the Grover
algorithm achieves a quadratic speedup, requiring only
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})
steps.

The adiabatic version of Grover’s algorithm
Roland and Cerf 2002
encodes the solution in
the ground state of a final Hamiltonian. Starting from the uniform superposition state

\ket{\Phi_{i}}=\ket{+}^{\otimes n}
, the system evolves toward the marked state

\ket{m}
, which is the computational basis state corresponding to the target item. The
initial and final Hamiltonians are given by

\displaystyle H_{i}=\mathbb{I}-\ket{\Phi_{i}}\bra{\Phi_{i}},\quad H_{f}=\mathbb{I}-\ket{m}\bra{m},

(29)

and the interpolation Hamiltonian is defined as in Eq. (
1
).
As demonstrated in prior work
Roland and Cerf 2002
, using a linear schedule
s_{l}
,
(
s_{l}(\tau)=\tau
), an adiabatic evolution time
T
of
\mathcal{O}(N)
is required to
reach
\ket{m}
, which offers no advantage over classical search. This makes the Grover problem a
canonical testbed for exploring improved scheduling strategies.

The quadratic speedup for this problem is achieved by adopting the optimal schedule

s_{o}(\tau)

Roland and Cerf 2002
;
Albash and Lidar 2018
, given by

\displaystyle s_{o}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1}}\tan\left[\left(2\tau\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1\right)\tan^{-1}(\sqrt{N\scalebox{0.75}[1.0]{$-$}1})\right].

(30)

While this schedule is optimal, its derivation relies on the condition
\dot{s}\propto\Delta(s)^{-2}
, which requires a priori knowledge of the spectral gap
\Delta(s)
across
the entire path
Roland and Cerf 2002
. This requirement renders the approach impractical for
general problems where such information is inaccessible.

It turns out that the optimal schedule
s_{o}(\tau)
possesses a fundamental geometric
property: it is, in fact, a constant speed schedule. This equivalence is formally
stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 2

The optimal schedule
s_{o}(\tau)
for the adiabatic Grover problem is a
constant speed schedule,
s_{c}(\tau)
.

The proof is provided in the Sec. I B of the Supplementary Information.
This result is significant because it implies that our algorithm—which is designed
to find constant speed schedules geometrically—provides a practical method for
constructing the optimal schedule for the Grover problem, replacing the need for the
full spectral gap function,
\Delta(s)
, with locally computed eigenstate overlaps.

Figure
2
presents the numerical results for our algorithm applied
to the adiabatic Grover search problem with
N=2^{14}=16384
.
Panel (a) shows the energy spectrum of the Grover Hamiltonian, which has a
minimum gap at
s=0.5
. Correspondingly, our algorithm adaptively generates a
schedule,
\hat{s}_{c}(\tau)
, that slows down significantly in this region,
as shown in panel (b). The calculated schedule points (red) lie almost
perfectly on the theoretical optimal curve
s_{c}(\tau)
, indicating that the
deviations stem only from the interpolation between points. This
demonstrates that the algorithm successfully identifies the region requiring
slower evolution without prior knowledge of
\Delta(s)
.

The practical advantage of this adaptive scheduling is quantified in
Fig.
2
(c), which compares the fidelity
\mathcal{F}
as a function
of the evolution time
T
. Our constant speed schedule achieves the target
fidelity of 75% approximately 92 times faster than the linear schedule
(requiring
T=3.19\times 10^{2}
versus
T=2.94\times 10^{4}
).

Finally, Fig.
2
(d) confirms the asymptotic scaling of the evolution
time with the minimum gap on the adiabatic path
\Delta
.
For the adiabatic Grover search,
\Delta=1/\sqrt{N}

Roland and Cerf 2002
,
so the plot also directly connects
evolution time with the number of items in the database
N
.
We plot the adiabatic evolution time
T

and the total time
T_{\text{tot}}=T+T_{p}
, where
T_{p}
is the additional time
required for the projection operations. The numerical fit shows that the linear
schedule’s evolution time scales as
T\propto\Delta^{-2}=\mathcal{O}(N)
,
while our constant speed schedule achieves the optimal scaling of
T\propto\Delta^{-1}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})
. Since the projection cost
T_{p}
also
scales as
\Delta^{-1}
, the total time
T_{\text{tot}}
maintains this optimal
scaling, albeit with a larger prefactor.

III.2.2
[2Fe-2S] cluster

Finally, we demonstrate our method on a [2Fe-2S] cluster, a common bioinorganic motif that serves as a representative problem for strongly
correlated systems in quantum chemistry
Beinert et al. 1997
;
Johnson and Smith 2005
;
Noodleman et al. 2004
;
Reiher et al. 2017
;
Motta and Chan 2018
. Here, we show that
achieving the optimal gap scaling with the constant speed schedule not only provides a speedup but also significantly enhances the
reliability of adiabatic state preparation (ASP).

To explore the impact of various initial states, we follow the procedure of Lee et al.
Lee et al. 2023
, preparing different Slater
determinants as ground states of the initial Hamiltonian,
H_{i}
. Their work, which employed a standard linear interpolation for the
adiabatic path, revealed that the ASP performance can be highly unpredictable. They found the required evolution time to vary by over eight
orders of magnitude depending on the choice of
H_{i}
. This extreme sensitivity is a consequence of the underlying
T\propto\Delta^{-2}

scaling, which amplifies the effect of any variation in the minimum gap
\Delta
caused by different initial states.

Our constant speed schedule mitigates this issue by achieving the optimal
T\propto\Delta^{-1}
scaling. To quantitatively compare the
two approaches for excessive amount of different initial states, we use the heuristic measure
T^{\textrm{est}}_{\textrm{ASP}}
from Ref.
Lee et al. 2023
:

\displaystyle T^{\textrm{est}}_{\textrm{ASP}}=\max_{\tau\in[0,1]}\frac{|\braket{\Phi(\tau)|\dot{H}(\tau)|\mathcal{E}(\tau)}|}{\Delta^{2}(\tau)},

(31)

where
\ket{\mathcal{E}(\tau)}
is the first excited state at
\tau
.

The results in Fig.
4
(b) confirm that the optimal gap scaling leads to improved reliability.
While
T^{\textrm{est}}_{\textrm{ASP}}
for the linear schedule (black) shows the previously reported large variation, the constant speed schedule (red) exhibits much smaller variance.
Furthermore, it remains below the estimated cost of quantum phase estimation (
T_{\textrm{QPE}}
), defined in Ref.
Lee et al. 2023
, shown as the dotted horizontal line in Fig.
4
(b).
By achieving optimal scaling, our method thus makes ASP performance robust and less unpredictable, consistently satisfying the practical feasibility criterion
T^{\textrm{est}}_{\textrm{ASP}}<T_{\textrm{QPE}}
for all tested cases.

The crosses in (b) suggest a speedup of more than two orders of magnitude with the constant speed schedule, and Fig.
4
(d) confirms this expectation.
Using the constant speed schedule, the system achieves a fidelity of 75% with
T=9.48\times 10^{3}
, being 289 times faster than the linear schedule that requires
T=2.74\times 10^{6}
.

IV
Conclusion

We have introduced the constant speed schedule as a general strategy to improve the gap scaling of adiabatic state preparation. By enforcing uniform evolution along the eigenstate path, the method reduces the gap dependence of the runtime by one order compared with generic schedules, and an overlap-guided construction makes this approach practical without requiring prior spectral knowledge.

Our numerical results on the adiabatic Grover search, the nitrogen molecule, and the [2Fe-2S] cluster clearly demonstrate the quadratic speedup, reducing the scaling of the adiabatic runtime from
O(\Delta^{-2})
to
O(\Delta^{-1})
and significantly improving the reliability of state preparation. While a rigorous proof of optimality remains an open question, and extensions to other quantum platforms are still to be explored, our findings indicate that constant speed scheduling provides a promising and broadly applicable tool for quantum simulation.
