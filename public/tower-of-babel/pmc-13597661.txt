Acupuncture for Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis with Trial Sequential Analysis
Authors: Zhifeng Pan, Junshang Wu, Yici Xie, Mingzhu Xu, Haifu Huang, Wenhao Liu, Yuting Wang, Run Lin, Bing Deng, Zining Guo, Shaoyang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective options for managing chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) remain limited. Evidence supporting acupuncture for CIPN continues to accumulate, warranting a comprehensive synthesis to clarify its effects.MethodsWe searched eight databases and extracted study characteristics from the included studies. We assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool and graded the certainty of evidence using the GRADE framework. Meta-analyses evaluated CIPN symptoms, clinical effectiveness rate (ER), nerve conduction study (NCS) parameters, and quantitative sensory testing (QST) outcomes. Prespecified subgroup, sensitivity, publication-bias, and trial sequential analyses (TSA) were conducted.ResultsThe review included 40 RCTs involving 2496 participants. Compared with controls, acupuncture interventions, used alone or with cointerventions, improved several CIPN symptom outcomes, including FACT-Ntx scores (MD = −1.43, 95% CI [−1.91, −0.95]) and increased the ER (RR = 1.74, 95% CI [1.57, 1.93]). Acupuncture also improved nerve conduction velocity and amplitude; however, no significant effects were found for worst pain scores, onset latency, or QST outcomes. GRADE rated the certainty of evidence for FACT-Ntx, EORTC QLQ-CIPN20, and ER as very low. TSA crossed the monitoring boundaries for FACT-Ntx, EORTC QLQ-CIPN20, and ER. Subgroup findings varied by acupuncture type, intervention purpose, and chemotherapy regimen.ConclusionBoth manual acupuncture and electroacupuncture were associated with higher ERs, whereas their effects on specific outcomes varied by intervention type, including whether acupuncture was combined with other treatments. LI4 and ST36 were the most frequently used acupoints, and distal hand and foot acupoints were also commonly selected. Future studies should assess how acupuncture modality, intervention purpose, and chemotherapy regimen influence outcomes. Because the certainty of evidence was very low, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Introduction

Cancer imposes a major global health burden, with 18.5 million new cases reported in 2023 and incidence projected to increase over the next two decades.1 Chemotherapy is a mainstay of cancer treatment that improves prognosis but causes diverse toxicities, potentially limiting its clinical benefit.2 Among these toxicities, chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) is one of the most common and challenging, affecting approximately 68% of patients within 1 month of treatment.3,4 CIPN primarily manifests as hypoesthesia, such as numbness in the extremities, or hyperesthesia, such as tingling. CIPN symptoms can substantially impair patients’ daily activities and quality of life; severe CIPN may also necessitate dose reduction, treatment delays, or chemotherapy discontinuation, potentially compromising therapeutic efficacy and clinical outcomes.5,6 Pharmacological options for CIPN remain limited. Duloxetine is the only drug recommended by current clinical guidelines for established painful CIPN, although the recommendation remains limited in strength.7 Other agents, including gabapentinoids and tricyclic antidepressants, are frequently used in practice despite limited supporting evidence. Adverse effects such as nausea, dizziness, and somnolence, together with potential drug interactions, may further complicate care for patients receiving multiple anticancer and supportive medications, reinforcing the need for safer therapeutic alternatives.

Acupuncture, a nonpharmacological intervention, has attracted growing attention in supportive oncology care.8–10 It is widely used to alleviate chemotherapy-related adverse events and has a favorable overall safety profile.11,12 The American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) clinical practice guideline on CIPN13 indicates that acupuncture may benefit CIPN prevention and treatment, but limitations in the evidence preclude definitive recommendations. As higher-quality evidence on acupuncture for CIPN treatment has emerged,14–17 a comprehensive and rigorous synthesis is needed to inform clinical practice guidelines. Existing evidence syntheses of acupuncture for CIPN have included heterogeneous acupuncture modalities, complicating interpretation. In clinical practice and oncology guidelines, manual acupuncture (MA) and electroacupuncture (EA) receive the greatest attention;18 however, to our knowledge, these modalities have not been systematically evaluated specifically for CIPN. In addition, previous syntheses have not adequately addressed the risk of type I error in cumulative meta-analysis or determined whether the accumulated sample size is sufficient to support stable conclusions.

Accordingly, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis (TSA) to evaluate the clinical effects of MA and EA for preventing and treating CIPN and to identify factors that may influence these effects, thereby informing clinical practice and future trial design.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (see the PRISMA 2020 checklist). The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251272891).

Data Sources and Search Strategy

Two reviewers (ZFP and JSW) independently searched eight databases—PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science (WOS), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP), SinoMed, and WanFang—from database inception to January 2026. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) with free-text terms; for example, the PubMed search included the terms “Chemotherapy,” “Neoplasms,” “Peripheral Nervous System Disorders,” “Acupuncture,” and “randomized controlled trial.” No language restrictions were applied; the complete search strategies are provided in Supplementary Tables 1–8. We also manually screened the reference lists of included studies for additional eligible records.

Inclusion Criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined according to the Participants, Interventions, Comparators, Outcomes, and Study design (PICOS) framework as follows:

(1) Participants: Eligible participants had established CIPN or a pathologically confirmed malignant tumor and were scheduled to receive chemotherapy associated with peripheral neurotoxicity (eg, platinum agents, taxanes, or vinca alkaloids).

(2) Interventions: The intervention group received MA or EA, either alone or combined with other treatments. Eligible comparators included sham acupuncture, conventional drug treatment, and other control interventions. Conventional drug treatments were defined as prescription medications used for CIPN, including duloxetine and mecobalamin.

(3) Outcomes: Studies were required to report at least one validated measure of CIPN. Continuous outcomes included the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy/Gynecologic Oncology Group-Neurotoxicity (FACT-Ntx), Visual Analog Scale (VAS), nerve conduction study (NCS) parameters, and quantitative sensory testing (QST) outcomes; the binary outcome was the clinical effectiveness rate (ER), as defined in each original RCT.

(4) Study design: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Exclusion Criteria

(1) Studies reporting only unvalidated outcome measures.

(2) Reviews, case reports, animal studies, other non-RCT designs, studies with incomplete data, and duplicate publications.

Study Selection

Records were imported into EndNote 21 and automatically deduplicated. Two reviewers (ZFP and JSW) then independently screened titles and abstracts, manually checked for duplicate records, and assessed potentially eligible full texts for inclusion. Disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (ZNG).

Data Extraction

Two reviewers (ZFP and JSW) independently extracted data using a prespecified standardized form. Extracted study characteristics included author names, publication year, country, cancer-related variables, and acupuncture-related variables; outcome data included binary and continuous measures. For binary outcomes, we extracted event counts and total sample sizes; for continuous outcomes, we extracted postintervention means and standard deviations (SDs). For multi-arm studies, the shared control group was split equally across the treatment arms according to the Cochrane Handbook, with its sample size divided by the number of treatment arms to avoid inflated statistical power. For studies reporting only change scores, we reconstructed the variance using an assumed correlation coefficient, as described by Borenstein et al and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions,19,20 the correlation coefficient r = 0.5. For studies reporting medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), the Box-Cox method21 was used to estimate the corresponding means and SDs. For binary outcomes with zero events in either group, a continuity correction of 0.5 was added to each zero cell before calculating the effect estimate and confidence interval. Disagreements during data extraction were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (ZNG).

Risk of Bias Assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool (RoB 2) was used to assess risk of bias in the included RCTs. RoB 2 evaluates five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. Each domain was rated as “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias.” The overall risk-of-bias judgment was derived from the five domain-level assessments. Disagreements between the two reviewers (ZFP and JSW) were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (ZNG).

Certainty of Evidence

Using GRADEpro (https://www.gradepro.org/), the GRADE framework22 was used to assess the certainty of evidence for FACT-Ntx, the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire–Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy 20-item scale (EORTC QLQ-CIPN20), and ER. Certainty was evaluated across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Potential upgrading factors were also considered, and certainty was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Data Analysis

Meta-analyses were performed using the “meta” package in R version 4.5.2. Pooled estimates for binary outcomes were expressed as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were used when measurement units were consistent; otherwise, standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% CIs were calculated. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. A random-effects model was used when I2 > 50% or P < 0.10; otherwise, a fixed-effect model was applied. To explore heterogeneity, prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted by acupuncture intervention type, intervention purpose, and chemotherapy type; objective outcomes were also subgrouped by measurement site (see Supplementary Table 9). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates. For outcomes including at least 10 studies, Begg’s and Egger’s tests were used to assess small-study effects and potential publication bias.

Trial sequential analysis (TSA) was conducted for FACT-Ntx, EORTC QLQ-CIPN20, and ER to evaluate the stability of the cumulative evidence. TSA combines principles of interim analysis with cumulative meta-analysis and uses a required information size (RIS) and trial sequential monitoring boundaries to control random error and the risk of type I error. A cumulative sample size that reached or exceeded the RIS was considered to provide sufficient information for a stable conclusion. If this threshold was not reached, additional evidence was considered necessary to clarify the result. TSA was performed using TSA version 0.9.5.10 beta (https://www.ctu.dk/tsa/).

Results

Study Selection

The database searches identified 2,588 records, of which 1,095 were removed as duplicates. Of the remaining 1,493 records, 1,310 were excluded after title and abstract screening. The full texts of 183 reports were then assessed for eligibility. Reasons for full-text exclusion are provided in Supplementary Table 10. Study Characteristics

The 40 studies, published from 2009 to 2026, included 2,496 participants. Most studies were conducted in China; the remainder were conducted in the United States, Germany, the United Kingdom, or Brazil. Most studies included mixed cancer types (n = 22); among studies restricted by cancer type, breast cancer (n = 9) and gastrointestinal cancers (n = 5) were the most common. The most frequently evaluated neurotoxic chemotherapy classes were taxanes (n = 10) and oxaliplatin (n = 9). Interventions comprised EA alone (n = 12), MA alone (n = 11), EA combined with other treatments (n = 6), and MA combined with other treatments (n = 13). Comparators included no intervention, conventional treatment, wait-list control, rehabilitation exercise, and sham acupuncture. Frequently used acupoints included LI4, ST36, LI11, SP6, LR3, and EX-LE10, as detailed in Table 1. Risk of Bias

Among the 40 included studies, 10 were judged to have a low risk of bias, 15 to raise some concerns, and 15 to have a high risk of bias. The main concerns were unclear reporting of randomization and allocation concealment, unspecified analysis sets for the primary analyses, lack of assessor blinding, and absence of preregistered protocols; the risk-of-bias plot was generated using robvis (Figure 2).60 Study-level risk-of-bias assessments are presented in Supplementary Figure 1. CIPN Symptoms

FACT-Ntx

After one study was excluded based on the sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figure 2), 10 studies were included in the FACT-Ntx analysis. The results of the meta-analysis showed that acupuncture was more effective than the control group in improving FACT-Ntx scores, indicating an improvement in the overall symptoms of CIPN (MD = −1.43, 95% CI [−1.91, −0.95], P < 0.001, I2 = 0%; Figure 3A). In the subgroup analysis by acupuncture type (Supplementary Figure 3A), MA significantly improved FACT-Ntx scores compared with control (MD = −1.33, 95% CI [−1.87, −0.78], P < 0.001), whereas EA did not (MD = −1.42, 95% CI [−2.96, 0.12], P = 0.07). By intervention purpose (Supplementary Figure 3B), therapeutic acupuncture significantly improved FACT-Ntx scores (MD = −1.41, 95% CI [−1.91, −0.92], P < 0.001), whereas preventive acupuncture did not (MD = −1.80, 95% CI [−4.23, 0.63], P = 0.15). By chemotherapy type, acupuncture significantly improved FACT-Ntx scores in the mixed-chemotherapy subgroup (MD = −1.43, 95% CI [−1.93, −0.92], P < 0.001; Supplementary Figure 3C) but not in the taxane subgroup (MD = −1.34, 95% CI [−3.54, 0.86], P = 0.23). Begg’s (P = 0.282) and Egger’s (P = 0.929) tests showed no evidence of publication bias (Supplementary Figure 4). EORTC QLQ-CIPN20

After one study was excluded based on the sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figure 5), four studies were included in the EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 analysis. Acupuncture significantly reduced EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 scores compared with control (MD = −5.01, 95% CI [−6.9, −3.12], P < 0.001, I2 = 0%; Figure 3B). In the prespecified analysis by acupuncture type (Supplementary Figure 6A), both EA alone and MA combined with other treatments significantly reduced EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 scores (EA: MD = −3.99, 95% CI [−7.23, −0.75], P = 0.02; MA + other: MD = −5.54, 95% CI [−7.86, −3.21], P < 0.001). By chemotherapy type (Supplementary Figure 6B), acupuncture significantly improved EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 scores in the mixed-chemotherapy subgroup (MD = −5.54, 95% CI [−7.86, −3.21], P < 0.001). Each remaining chemotherapy-type subgroup contained only one study, with effect estimates in the same direction as that of the mixed-chemotherapy subgroup.

NRS

Nine studies were included in the NRS analysis. Acupuncture significantly reduced numeric rating scale (NRS) scores compared with control (MD = −0.97, 95% CI [−1.35, −0.60], P < 0.001, I2 = 0%; Figure 3C). By acupuncture type (Supplementary Figure 7A), both MA combined with other treatments and EA alone significantly reduced NRS scores (MA + other: MD = −0.80, 95% CI [−1.34, −0.25], P < 0.001; EA: MD = −0.87, 95% CI [−1.50, −0.24], P = 0.007). In the analysis by intervention purpose (Supplementary Figure 7B), eight therapeutic studies showed a significant reduction in NRS scores (MD = −0.99, 95% CI [−1.38, −0.60], P < 0.001). By chemotherapy type (Supplementary Figure 7C), acupuncture significantly reduced NRS scores in the mixed-chemotherapy subgroup (MD = −0.99, 95% CI [−1.38, −0.60], P < 0.001). The sensitivity analysis did not materially alter the pooled estimate (Supplementary Figure 8C).

VAS

Eleven studies were included in the VAS analysis. Acupuncture significantly reduced VAS scores compared with control (MD = −1.34, 95% CI [−1.96, −0.71], P < 0.001, I2 = 64%; Figure 3D). Acupuncture type did not explain the observed heterogeneity (P = 0.47; Supplementary Figure 9A). EA combined with other treatments significantly reduced VAS scores (MD = −1.87, 95% CI [−3.06, −0.67], P = 0.002), whereas EA alone and MA alone did not (EA: MD = −0.85, 95% CI [−1.72, 0.03], P = 0.06; MA: MD = −1.97, 95% CI [−4.30, 0.35], P = 0.10). All 11 studies evaluated acupuncture for treatment rather than prevention. Chemotherapy type also did not explain the heterogeneity (P = 0.53; Supplementary Figure 9B). Acupuncture significantly reduced VAS scores in the mixed-chemotherapy subgroup (MD = −1.41, 95% CI [−1.95, −0.88], P < 0.001) but not in the taxane subgroup (MD = −0.92, 95% CI [−2.39, 0.56], P = 0.22). The sensitivity analysis did not materially alter the pooled estimate (Supplementary Figure 10). Begg’s (P = 0.815) and Egger’s (P = 0.172) tests showed no evidence of publication bias (Supplementary Figure 11).

BPI-WPS

Two studies were included in the Brief Pain Inventory Worst Pain Score (BPI-WPS) analysis, which showed no statistically significant difference between acupuncture and control in worst pain severity (MD = −0.59, 95% CI [−1.45, 0.28], P = 0.18, I2 = 0%; Figure 3E).

Clinical Effectiveness Rate

Twenty studies were included in the ER analysis. The likelihood of meeting the study-defined effectiveness criterion was significantly higher with acupuncture than with control (RR = 1.74, 95% CI [1.57, 1.93], P < 0.001, I2 = 48%; Figure 4). Acupuncture type did not explain the observed heterogeneity in ER (P = 0.87; Supplementary Figure 12A). EA combined with other therapies had the largest effect estimate (RR = 1.95, 95% CI [1.38, 2.76], P < 0.001); the estimates for EA alone, MA alone, and MA combined with other therapies were also statistically significant (EA: RR = 1.77, 95% CI [1.42, 2.22], P < 0.001; MA: RR = 1.64, 95% CI [1.28, 2.09], P < 0.001; MA + other: RR = 1.73, 95% CI [1.50, 1.99], P < 0.001). Intervention purpose explained some of the between-study heterogeneity (P = 0.02; Supplementary Figure 12B). Therapeutic RCTs defined effectiveness as a decrease of ≥1 grade on a neuropathy assessment scale or an increase of ≥2 points on a patient-reported outcome scale; preventive RCTs defined effectiveness as the non-occurrence of CIPN or a grade of 0 on a neuropathy assessment scale. The ER was significantly higher with acupuncture in both therapeutic (RR = 1.63, 95% CI [1.46, 1.82], P < 0.001) and preventive trials (RR = 2.35, 95% CI [1.76, 3.14], P < 0.001), with a larger effect estimate in the preventive subgroup. Chemotherapy type also explained some of the heterogeneity in ER (P = 0.04; Supplementary Figure 12C). Acupuncture was associated with significantly higher ERs in the taxane, mixed-chemotherapy, and oxaliplatin subgroups (taxane: RR = 2.31, 95% CI [1.77, 3.01], P < 0.001; mixed: RR = 1.58, 95% CI [1.35, 1.86], P < 0.001; oxaliplatin: RR = 1.57, 95% CI [1.35, 1.83], P < 0.001). The sensitivity analysis did not materially alter the pooled estimate (Supplementary Figure 13). The funnel plot was asymmetric, and both Begg’s (P = 0.019) and Egger’s (P = 0.029) tests indicated potential publication bias. After five studies were imputed using the trim-and-fill method, the pooled RR decreased from 1.74 to 1.52 (Supplementary Figures 14 and 15). Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS)

Sensory Nerve Conduction Velocity

Across 33 studies, acupuncture significantly improved sensory nerve conduction velocity (SCV) compared with control (SMD = 0.66, 95% CI [0.45, 0.88], P < 0.001, I2 = 76%; Figure 5A). By acupuncture type (Supplementary Figure 16A), neither EA alone nor MA alone significantly improved SCV (EA: SMD = 0.46, 95% CI [−0.06, 0.99], P = 0.08, I2 = 80%; MA: SMD = 0.09, 95% CI [−0.19, 0.37], P = 0.54, I2 = 0%), whereas both EA and MA combined with other treatments did (EA + other: SMD = 1.55, 95% CI [1.18, 1.91], P < 0.001, I2 = 34%; MA + other: SMD = 0.76, 95% CI [0.57, 0.94], P < 0.001, I2 = 54%). Intervention purpose did not explain the heterogeneity in SCV (P = 0.13; Supplementary Figure 16B). Acupuncture significantly improved SCV in both therapeutic and preventive trials (therapeutic: SMD = 0.6, 95% CI [0.32, 0.88], P < 0.001, I2 = 79%; preventive: SMD = 0.87, 95% CI [0.66, 1.08], P < 0.001, I2 = 33%). Chemotherapy type explained some of the heterogeneity in SCV (P = 0.009; Supplementary Figure 16C), and acupuncture significantly improved SCV in the mixed-chemotherapy, capecitabine, oxaliplatin, and taxane subgroups (mixed: SMD = 0.51, 95% CI [0.10, 0.93], P < 0.001; capecitabine: SMD = 0.71, 95% CI [0.45, 0.97], P < 0.001; oxaliplatin: SMD = 1.36, 95% CI [0.99, 1.73], P < 0.001; taxane: SMD = 0.74, 95% CI [0.53, 0.94], P < 0.001). Nerve type did not explain the heterogeneity in SCV (P = 0.24; Supplementary Figure 16D). Acupuncture did not significantly improve superficial peroneal nerve (SPN) SCV (SMD = 0.46, 95% CI [−0.3, 1.23], P = 0.24, I2 = 85%) but significantly improved SCV in the common peroneal nerve (CPN), median nerve (MN), tibial nerve (TN), and sural nerve (SN) (CPN: SMD = 0.98, 95% CI [0.52, 1.45], P < 0.001, I2 = 75%; MN: SMD = 0.64, 95% CI [0.24, 1.04], P = 0.02, I2 = 83%; TN: SMD = 1.01, 95% CI [0.46, 1.57], P < 0.001, I2 = 58%; SN: SMD = 0.44, 95% CI [0.13, 0.76], P = 0.006, I2 = 31%). The sensitivity analysis did not materially alter the pooled estimate (Supplementary Figure 17). The funnel plot was symmetrical, and Begg’s (P = 0.114) and Egger’s (P = 0.101) tests showed no evidence of publication bias (Supplementary Figure 18). Motor Nerve Conduction Velocity

Across 22 studies, acupuncture significantly improved motor nerve conduction velocity (MCV) compared with control (SMD = 0.78, 95% CI [0.49, 1.08], P < 0.001, I2 = 84%; Figure 5B). Acupuncture type explained some of the heterogeneity (P < 0.001; Supplementary Figure 19A), but neither EA alone nor MA alone significantly improved MCV (EA: SMD = 0.75, 95% CI [−0.4, 1.9], P = 0.2, I2 = 93%; MA: SMD = 0.11, 95% CI [−0.18, 0.4], P = 0.47, I2 = 0%). Both EA and MA combined with other treatments significantly improved MCV compared with control (EA + other: SMD = 1.75, 95% CI [1.05, 2.45], P < 0.001, I2 = 80%; MA + other: SMD = 0.77, 95% CI [0.55, 0.98], P < 0.001, I2 = 60%). By intervention purpose (Supplementary Figure 19B), therapeutic acupuncture significantly improved MCV (SMD = 0.87, 95% CI [0.53, 1.21], P < 0.001, I2 = 86%), whereas preventive acupuncture did not (SMD = 0.5, 95% CI [−0.07, 1.08], P = 0.09, I2 = 84%); intervention purpose did not explain the heterogeneity (P = 0.29). Chemotherapy type explained some of the heterogeneity in MCV (P = 0.02; Supplementary Figure 19C), and acupuncture significantly improved MCV across the mixed-chemotherapy and single-agent subgroups (mixed: SMD = 0.78, 95% CI [0.28, 1.28], P < 0.001, I2 = 89%; taxane: SMD = 0.55, 95% CI [0.16, 0.94], P = 0.006, I2 = 72%; oxaliplatin: SMD = 1.4, 95% CI [1.03, 1.77], P < 0.001, I2 = 0%; capecitabine: SMD = 1.05, 95% CI [0.5, 1.6], P < 0.001, I2 = 76%). Nerve type did not explain the heterogeneity in MCV (P = 0.78; Supplementary Figure 19D), and acupuncture significantly improved MCV in the MN, CPN, and TN (MN: SMD = 0.88, 95% CI [0.37, 1.4], P < 0.001, I2 = 89%; CPN: SMD = 0.66, 95% CI [0.25, 1.07], P = 0.002, I2 = 80%; TN: SMD = 0.84, 95% CI [0.26, 1.41], P = 0.005, I2 = 69%). The sensitivity analysis did not materially alter the pooled estimate (Supplementary Figure 20). The funnel plot was symmetrical, and Begg’s (P = 0.844) and Egger’s (P = 0.855) tests showed no evidence of publication bias (Supplementary Figure 21).

Compound Motor Action Potential Amplitude

Across 14 studies, acupuncture significantly improved compound motor action potential amplitude compared with control (SMD = 0.43, 95% CI [0.23, 0.63], P < 0.001, I2 = 0%; Figure 5C). By acupuncture type (Supplementary Figure 22A), neither EA alone nor MA alone significantly improved amplitude (EA: SMD = 0.23, 95% CI [−0.2, 0.66], P = 0.29, I2 = 0%; MA: SMD = 0.28, 95% CI [−0.03, 0.58], P = 0.07, I2 = 5%), whereas MA combined with other treatments did (SMD = 0.76, 95% CI [0.41, 1.11], P < 0.001, I2 = 0%). Chemotherapy type explained some of the heterogeneity in amplitude (P = 0.04; Supplementary Figure 22B). Acupuncture did not significantly improve amplitude in the taxane subgroup (SMD = −0.09, 95% CI [−0.6, 0.43], P = 0.75, I2 = 0%) but significantly improved it in the mixed-chemotherapy subgroup (SMD = 0.52, 95% CI [0.3, 0.74], P < 0.001, I2 = 0%). By nerve type (Supplementary Figure 22C), acupuncture did not significantly improve SN or TN amplitude (SN: SMD = 0.39, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.78], P = 0.06, I2 = 9%; TN: SMD = 0.3, 95% CI [−0.23, 0.83], P = 0.27, I2 = 0%). In contrast, acupuncture significantly improved MN and CPN amplitude (MN: SMD = 0.39, 95% CI [0.04, 0.74], P = 0.03, I2 = 0%; CPN: SMD = 0.59, 95% CI [0.2, 0.98], P = 0.003, I2 = 0%). All included interventions were therapeutic rather than preventive. The sensitivity analysis did not materially alter the pooled estimate (Supplementary Figure 23). The funnel plot showed potential asymmetry: Egger’s test was statistically significant (P = 0.004), whereas Begg’s test was not (P = 0.250; Supplementary Figure 24).

Onset Latency

Across five studies, acupuncture did not significantly improve onset latency (OL) compared with control (SMD = −0.03, 95% CI [−0.37, 0.32], P = 0.88; Figure 5D). All five studies evaluated MA for treatment rather than prevention (Supplementary Figure 25A). Chemotherapy type did not explain the subgroup difference (P = 0.58), and acupuncture did not significantly improve OL in either the mixed-chemotherapy or taxane subgroup (mixed: SMD = −0.11, 95% CI [−0.56, 0.34], P = 0.64, I2 = 0%; taxane: SMD = 0.08, 95% CI [−0.44, 0.61], P = 0.75, I2 = 0%). Nerve type did not explain the subgroup difference (P = 0.41; Supplementary Figure 25B), and acupuncture did not significantly improve OL in either the TN or SN (TN: SMD = −0.19, 95% CI [−0.7, 0.33], P = 0.47, I2 = 0%; SN: SMD = 0.1, 95% CI [−0.35, 0.56], P = 0.66, I2 = 0%). The sensitivity analysis did not materially alter the pooled estimate (Supplementary Figure 26).

Quantitative Sensory Testing

The included trials reported four QST outcomes: tactile detection threshold (TDT), vibrational detection threshold (VDT), thermal detection threshold (THDT), and thermal threshold (THT). VDT, THDT, and THT were reported only by W. I. Zhi 2023, with no statistically significant between-group differences. Six studies reported TDT; the pooled analysis showed no statistically significant effect of acupuncture (SMD = −0.27, 95% CI [−0.63, 0.09], P = 0.14; Figure 6). By acupuncture type, neither EA nor MA significantly improved TDT compared with control (EA: SMD = −0.17, 95% CI [−0.6, 0.25], P = 0.43, I2 = 0%; MA: SMD = −0.51, 95% CI [−1.18, 0.16], P = 0.14, I2 = 0%; Supplementary Figure 27A). By chemotherapy type (Supplementary Figure 27B), acupuncture did not significantly improve TDT in either the mixed-chemotherapy or taxane subgroup (mixed: SMD = −0.17, 95% CI [−0.6, 0.25], P = 0.43, I2 = 0%; taxane: SMD = −0.51, 95% CI [−1.18, 0.16], P = 0.14, I2 = 0%). By measurement site (Supplementary Figure 27C), acupuncture did not significantly improve TDT in either the hands or feet (hands: SMD = −0.34, 95% CI [−0.85, 0.18], P = 0.2, I2 = 0%; feet: SMD = −0.21, 95% CI [−0.72, 0.3], P = 0.43, I2 = 0%). Both trials reporting the additional QST outcomes evaluated therapeutic acupuncture. The sensitivity analysis did not materially alter the TDT estimate (Supplementary Figure 28). Trial Sequential Analysis

TSA used the following parameters:61 a type I error (α) of 5%, a type II error (β) of 10%, the DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model, and a constant continuity correction of 0.5 per group for studies with zero events. Between-study heterogeneity was adjusted using the diversity estimate (D2). For FACT-Ntx and EORTC QLQ-CIPN20, the variance and minimal clinically important difference (MCID) were based on values observed and pooled in the current meta-analysis. For ER, the control-group event rate was estimated from the pooled incidence in the current meta-analysis. A 25% relative increase in ER was prespecified as the minimally important effect. For FACT-Ntx, the cumulative Z-curve crossed both the monitoring boundary and the RIS, supporting the stability of the pooled finding (Figure 8). For EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 and ER, the cumulative Z-curves crossed the monitoring boundaries but did not reach the RIS at the prespecified 25% relative-effect threshold. These findings suggest that the pooled effects were unlikely to be explained entirely by random error; however, the information size remained insufficient for EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 and ER. Discussion

Summary of Main Findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 40 studies involving 2,496 patients found that acupuncture may provide clinical benefits for CIPN; TSA supported the stability of the FACT-Ntx finding but yielded less conclusive evidence for EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 and ER because the required information sizes were not reached. However, GRADE rated the certainty of evidence for the three assessed outcomes—FACT-Ntx, EORTC QLQ-CIPN20, and ER—as very low; therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. For CIPN symptoms, acupuncture significantly improved several validated scale scores compared with control. Specifically, acupuncture significantly improved FACT-Ntx (MD = −1.43), EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 (MD = −5.01), NRS (MD = −0.97), and VAS (MD = −1.34) scores but did not significantly improve BPI-WPS. For ER, acupuncture increased the likelihood of a clinical response (RR = 1.74); after trim-and-fill adjustment, the effect remained statistically significant and in the same direction (RR = 1.52). For objective neurophysiological outcomes, acupuncture significantly improved SCV (SMD = 0.66), MCV (SMD = 0.78), and compound motor action potential amplitude (SMD = 0.43), whereas no significant effects were found for OL or TDT.

The subgroup analyses provided preliminary, hypothesis-generating evidence relevant to clinical decision-making. Acupuncture type may have influenced the effects on FACT-Ntx, SCV, and MCV. EA combined with other treatments yielded larger effect estimates for VAS, ER, SCV, and MCV, consistent with previous findings.62 Differences by intervention purpose were most apparent for ER, for which preventive trials yielded a larger effect estimate than therapeutic trials. This finding may reflect the smaller number of trials and participants in the preventive subgroup. Among outcomes analyzed by intervention purpose, therapeutic acupuncture significantly improved FACT-Ntx, NRS, VAS, ER, SCV, MCV, and compound motor action potential amplitude, whereas OL and TDT did not improve significantly. Chemotherapy type may have contributed to subgroup differences in ER, SCV, MCV, and compound motor action potential amplitude. For taxane-related CIPN, acupuncture significantly improved ER but not compound motor action potential amplitude. For oxaliplatin-induced neuropathy, acupuncture significantly improved both sensory and motor nerve conduction velocities. In the mixed-chemotherapy subgroup, acupuncture significantly improved ER, SCV, MCV, and compound motor action potential amplitude; however, these subgroup findings do not establish efficacy across all chemotherapy regimens. For SCV, MCV, OL, and TDT, subgroup differences by nerve or measurement site were not statistically significant. These findings may be compatible with a systemic rather than exclusively local effect, but the subgroup analyses cannot establish the underlying mechanism.

The generally high risk of bias across the included studies warrants caution. Beyond the inherent difficulty of blinding acupuncture practitioners, key concerns included unclear reporting of random sequence generation and allocation concealment, lack of outcome-assessor blinding, and absence of prospectively registered protocols in some studies. These limitations may have introduced selection and measurement bias. Several pooled analyses also showed moderate-to-high heterogeneity. Although prespecified multidimensional subgroup analyses were conducted, some heterogeneity remained unexplained. Potential sources include between-study differences in tumor type, treatment regimen, baseline symptom severity, acupuncture protocols (acupoints, treatment courses, and parameters), and NCS equipment and methods.

Implications for Practice

No clinical consensus has been established regarding the optimal acupuncture protocol for CIPN. Our findings showed that MA alone significantly improved FACT-Ntx, whereas MA combined with other treatments significantly improved EORTC QLQ-CIPN20; both scales assess neurosensory symptoms and motor dysfunction in patients with CIPN. EA alone did not significantly improve FACT-Ntx but significantly improved EORTC QLQ-CIPN20. Previous research has also reported limitations of EA for relieving CIPN-related pain.27,63 Previous research has suggested that traditional MA may be more effective for CIPN-related pain.64 EA combined with other therapies significantly improved VAS scores, and MA combined with other therapies significantly improved NRS scores, both with large effect estimates; however, the supporting studies24,40–42,46,52 were predominantly conducted in China and may have been limited by small samples, publication bias, and methodological constraints. One study suggested65 that high-frequency or continuous physical stimulation may increase neuronal excitability and synchronized firing in a pathological nervous system. Electrical-stimulation parameters may therefore influence individual CIPN symptoms differently.33,66

Heterogeneity in EA parameters may have contributed to variation in the efficacy estimates for CIPN. Combining EA with adjunctive therapies, including bloodletting, moxibustion, reduced glutathione, or mecobalamin, may reduce parameter-related variability and produce complementary effects. Li et al reported67 that, in mice with CIPN, EA may alleviate pain, numbness, and other sensory abnormalities by inhibiting neuronal CCL2 signaling, reducing proinflammatory macrophage infiltration into the dorsal root ganglion, and partially restoring peripheral sensory structures, particularly intraepidermal nerve fibers. Bloodletting and moxibustion have been proposed to regulate local qi and blood and modulate immune inflammation;68,69 reduced glutathione and mecobalamin may exert antioxidant and neuroprotective effects.70,71 These combined approaches may improve CIPN symptoms through complementary effects on inflammation, neural structure, and functional recovery. A clinical study reported59 that only a small proportion of patients with CIPN showed NCS abnormalities. In some patients, CIPN predominantly affects small nerve fibers (ie, A-δ or C fibers), whereas NCS primarily assesses large nerve fibers and may yield normal results in small-fiber neuropathy. In that study, combined EA significantly improved SCV and MCV compared with control among patients with established NCS abnormalities. The same study suggested that patients with predominantly subjective sensory symptoms, such as pain and numbness, but no marked NCS abnormalities may be more suitable candidates for MA.59 Conversely, patients with substantial sensory and motor conduction deficits may derive greater benefit from EA combined with other treatments.17 Although the preventive subgroup yielded larger effect estimates for outcomes with between-group differences, the therapeutic subgroup findings were more consistently statistically significant. Thus, acupuncture may have potential for both preventing and treating CIPN, although these subgroup findings remain exploratory. By chemotherapy type, acupuncture improved ER in taxane-related CIPN and neurophysiological outcomes in oxaliplatin-related CIPN; these subgroup findings may inform future trials but do not establish regimen-specific clinical recommendations.

Acupoint selection is central to traditional clinical acupuncture protocols. Rational selection and combination of acupoints may influence acupuncture efficacy, with individual points serving distinct therapeutic roles in CIPN management.57,72 We summarized and analyzed the most frequently used acupoints (Figure 9). The most frequently used acupoints were Hegu (LI4), Zusanli (ST36), Quchi (LI11), Sanyinjiao (SP6), Taichong (LR3), and local experiential points on the extremities (EX-UE9 and EX-LE10). This pattern suggests that an empirical consensus may be emerging around prescriptions intended to regulate qi and blood circulation and improve distal-limb sensory disturbances. Many frequently used acupoints were located along peripheral nerve pathways or near NCS sites. One RCT indicated that local toe and finger acupoints were particularly important to acupuncture efficacy in CIPN.17 This finding highlights the potential importance of distal hand and foot points such as EX-UE9 and EX-LE10, which earlier systematic reviews and meta-analyses did not emphasize.73 Figure 10 summarizes the other acupuncture parameters. Across the 40 included studies, treatment comprised 6–28 sessions (median, 12); one study did not report the number of sessions. Sessions most commonly lasted 30 min (range, 10–45 min). Treatment was most often administered 2–3 times per week (15/40 studies), with an overall range of 1–7 sessions per week; total treatment duration ranged from 1.4 to 12 weeks (median, 4 weeks). Future Research

Future research should prioritize rigorously designed, large, multicenter randomized controlled trials to strengthen the evidence base. Such trials could support the development of standardized CIPN acupuncture protocols, including acupoint selection and procedures for MA and EA. Combination therapies and differences among acupuncture modalities warrant further investigation. Our findings suggest that EA combined with other treatments—including pregabalin, mecobalamin, and moxibustion—may produce benefits across several CIPN outcomes despite variation in EA parameters. EA combined with analgesics, moxibustion, or other adjunctive treatments therefore warrants further evaluation. Future studies should also clarify the optimal timing of acupuncture in CIPN management. It remains unclear whether acupuncture is more suitable for prevention or treatment; one study27 suggested that EA may be more appropriate as a therapeutic intervention than as a preventive adjunct. Future trials could compare the same acupuncture protocol for preventing CIPN and treating established CIPN while evaluating how intervention timing affects long-term outcomes. Finally, we used the Standards for Reporting Interventions in Clinical Trials of Acupuncture (STRICTA) guidelines to evaluate the reporting completeness of the included acupuncture RCTs (Supplementary Table 11). Most trials reported basic acupuncture parameters, such as acupoint names and needle-retention time, but inadequately described the theoretical basis for acupuncture, needle-insertion depth, practitioner background, and treatment setting. Future acupuncture trials for CIPN should follow STRICTA to improve protocol reporting and research reproducibility.

Strengths

This review evaluated the effects of MA and EA on CIPN-specific outcomes, including subjective rating scales and objective neurophysiological measures. The review also incorporated TSA to assess the stability of the meta-analytic findings, providing a complementary perspective on the effects of MA and EA within the existing evidence base. Prespecified subgroup analyses examined intervention type and purpose, the type of chemotherapy associated with CIPN, and objective-outcome measurement sites. These analyses identified potential effect modifiers and sources of heterogeneity and generated hypotheses for tailoring acupuncture strategies to different patient groups.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, most included trials were conducted in China, potentially limiting generalizability to other populations and healthcare settings. Second, many trials lacked sham-acupuncture controls, making it difficult to distinguish the physiological effects of acupuncture from placebo effects. Third, some studies had a high risk of bias, particularly in randomization, allocation concealment, and outcome-assessor blinding, which may have inflated the treatment-effect estimates. Fourth, some included studies may have inconsistently reported the scoring direction of CIPN measures, particularly the positive and negative scoring conventions of FACT scales.74 Finally, although the inherent neurotoxicity of individual chemotherapeutic agents is a primary determinant of CIPN risk,75,76 moderate-to-high heterogeneity persisted for some outcomes after subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Unexplored clinical or methodological differences, including variation in control interventions and ER definitions, may have contributed to this heterogeneity.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides preliminary evidence that acupuncture may benefit patients with CIPN. TSA supported the stability of the FACT-Ntx finding; although the monitoring boundaries were crossed for EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 and ER, neither outcome reached the required information size. Acupuncture type, intervention purpose, and chemotherapy type may influence effect estimates. However, because GRADE rated the certainty of evidence for FACT-Ntx, EORTC QLQ-CIPN20, and ER as very low, higher-quality, geographically diverse randomized controlled trials are needed before MA or EA can be recommended for CIPN prevention or treatment. Further trials should determine the optimal acupuncture modalities, timing, and therapeutic targets and identify patient subgroups that may derive greater benefit. Such evidence could support the development of standardized, personalized acupuncture strategies for different populations with CIPN.
