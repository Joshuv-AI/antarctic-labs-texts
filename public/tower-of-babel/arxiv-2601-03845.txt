Formally Explaining Decision Tree Models with Answer Set Programming
Akihiro Takemura, Masayuki Otani, Katsumi Inoue

Abstract
Decision tree models, including random forests and gradient-boosted decision trees, are widely used in machine learning due to their high predictive performance. However, their complex structures often make them difficult to interpret, especially in safety-critical applications where model decisions require formal justification. Recent work has demonstrated that logical and abductive explanations can be derived through automated reasoning techniques. In this paper, we propose a method for generating various types of explanations, namely, sufficient, contrastive, majority, and tree-specific explanations, using Answer Set Programming (ASP). Compared to SAT-based approaches, our ASP-based method offers greater flexibility in encoding user preferences and supports enumeration of all possible explanations. We empirically evaluate the approach on a diverse set of datasets and demonstrate its effectiveness and limitations compared to existing methods.

Introduction

The widespread adoption of decision tree models in machine learning can be attributed to their high accuracy and computational efficiency.
However, more complex ensemble variants, such as random forests and gradient-boosted trees, tend to operate as black-box models, rendering their internal decision-making processes opaque to users
[
13
]
.
This lack of transparency is particularly problematic in high-stakes applications, such as healthcare, finance, and criminal justice, where trust in model decisions hinges on a clear understanding of how predictions are made.

Explainability, often referred to as eXplainable AI (XAI), seeks to bridge this gap by making model behavior understandable
[
13
]
.
In practice, however, producing faithful explanations is challenging due to the complexity of modern machine learning models.
Naive or heuristic-based techniques often fall short because they fail to capture the full logical structure underlying the model’s decisions
[
24
,
20
]
.

Well-known post-hoc explanation methods such as LIME
[
22
]
, SHAP
[
19
]
, and Anchors
[
23
]
rely on heuristic approximations, and can yield explanations that are not guaranteed to align with the model’s true behavior
[
21
]
.
In contrast, logic-based approaches offer rigorous guarantees and have been successfully applied to generate various types of explanations, including sufficient and contrastive ones
[
16
,
17
,
15
,
5
,
6
,
4
,
3
]
.
These logic-based approaches have been largely based on techniques of Boolean satisfiability (SAT).
However, SAT-based methods often lack flexibility, especially when it comes to incorporating user-defined preferences or supporting complex reasoning tasks like explanation enumeration.

In this work, we propose an alternative approach using Answer Set Programming (ASP), a declarative logic programming paradigm well-suited for combinatorial reasoning.
We show how ASP can be leveraged to encode and compute different types of explanations, including sufficient, contrastive, majority, and tree-specific explanations, for decision tree models.
The high expressiveness of ASP allows us to concisely capture various explanation types and flexibly incorporate constraints, making it a promising tool for formal explainability.

Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We develop ASP encodings for multiple explanation types, including sufficient, contrastive, majority, and tree-specific explanations.

2.

We implement and evaluate our approach across 20 datasets, comparing its performance against existing SAT-based methods.

3.

We analyze the ASP-based method’s strengths and limitations, highlighting where it excels (majority explanation enumeration) and challenges (scalability to large forests).

Background

This section introduces the necessary concepts for the rest of the paper.
We first review Boolean functions and their relationship to logical reasoning in the context of machine learning, and the structures of decision tree models. We then present a concise overview of ASP, the declarative framework used to encode and compute formal explanations in our approach.

2.1
Boolean Functions

Let
X_{n}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be a set of Boolean variables.
A
Boolean function
is a mapping
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, which assigns each Boolean assignment
\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
a Boolean value.
We say
\bm{x}
is a
positive instance
if
f(\bm{x})=1
, and a
negative instance
if
f(\bm{x})=0
.
A propositional formula over
X_{n}
is constructed using logical connectives: conjunction (
\land
), disjunction (
\lor
), and negation (
\neg
).
A
literal

L
is either a variable
x_{i}
(positive literal) or its negation
\neg x_{i}
(negative literal).
A
term
is a conjunction of literals.

For an assignment
\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
t_{\bm{z}}=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{z_{i}}
represents its corresponding term, where
x_{i}^{0}=\neg x_{i}
and
x_{i}^{1}=x_{i}
.
A term
t_{1}
is a
subterm
of a term
t_{2}
if the literals appearing in
t_{1}
appear in
t_{2}
.
For example,
x_{1}\land x_{3}
is a subterm of
x_{1}\land\neg x_{2}\land x_{3}
.
A term
t

covers

\bm{z}
if
t
is a subterm of
t_{\bm{z}}
, i.e.,
t
is satisfied under
\bm{z}

[
3
]
.
This captures the idea that
t
holds whenever the assignment
\bm{z}
satisfies all literals in
t
.
A term
t
is an
implicant
of a propositional formula
f
if
t\models f
, i.e., every assignment
\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
that satisfies
t

also satisfies
f
. A term
t
is a
prime implicant
of
f

if
t\models f
and no proper subterm of
t
entails
f
.

2.2
Trees and Forests

We consider a finite set of attributes (or features)
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\}
, where each attribute
A_{i}
takes values from a domain
D_{i}
, which may be Boolean, categorical, or numerical.
Decision tree learning algorithms create Boolean tests: threshold tests
(A_{i}\leq c)
for numerical attributes and membership tests
(A_{i}\in S)
for categorical attributes.
Let
L=\{L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\}
denote the Boolean literals from tree node tests, where each
L_{i}
corresponds to a Boolean variable from
X_{n}
.
An instance
\bm{z}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
with
v_{i}\in D_{i}
evaluates each Boolean variable
x_{i}
based on the tree node tests.
The corresponding term is
t_{\bm{z}}=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{z_{i}}
, where
z_{i}=1
if the Boolean test for
x_{i}
evaluates to true and
z_{i}=0
if it evaluates to false.

Decision Trees.

A
decision tree
classifier over
\mathcal{A}
is a binary tree
T
where internal nodes test Boolean conditions and leaves are labeled with classes in
\{0,1\}
.
Tree traversal starts at the root and follows left branches for true conditions and right branches for false conditions.

Random Forests.

A
random forest

RF=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{m}\}
is a set of decision trees
T_{i}
s, where its output is determined by majority vote
[
7
]
:

RF(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{i}(\bm{x})>\frac{m}{2},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Regression Trees and Forests.

A
regression tree
is similar to a decision tree, except leaves are labeled with real-valued outputs.
A
forest

F=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{m}\}
consists of
m
regression trees with aggregated weight
w(F,\bm{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}w(T_{k},\bm{x})
.

Boosted Trees.

A
boosted tree

BT=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{m}\}
for binary classification is a sequence of trees
[
10
]
(typically regression trees, as in gradient boosted trees such as XGBoost
[
8
]
) with prediction determined by the sign of cumulative weights.

BT(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }w(BT,\bm{x})>0,\\
0&\text{if }w(BT,\bm{x})<0,\end{cases}\;\;\mathrm{where}\;\;w(BT,\bm{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}w(T_{k},\bm{x}).

We define the size of a forest
F
, and a boosted tree
BT
, as
|F|=|BT|=\sum_{k=1}^{m}|T_{k}|
, where
|T_{k}|
is the number of nodes in the tree
T_{k}
.

2.3
Answer Set Programming

Answer Set Programming
(ASP) is a form of declarative programming oriented towards solving hard combinatorial problems.
It allows one to encode a problem as a set of logical rules, and uses a solver to compute its solutions, called
answer sets
, based on the stable model semantics
[
12
]
.

A typical ASP rule is written as:

\mathrm{a_{1}}\ \texttt{:-}\ \mathrm{a_{2}},\ \dots,\ \mathrm{a_{m}},\ \texttt{not}\ \mathrm{a_{m+1}},\ \dots,\ \texttt{not}\ \mathrm{a_{n}}.

Here, each
\mathrm{a_{i}}
is a first-order atom, and
not
denotes
default negation
.
A rule with only the head
\mathrm{a_{1}}
is called a
fact
, while one without a head is an
integrity constraint
, used to eliminate undesired models.

Modern ASP systems, such as
clingo

[
11
]
, support expressive constructs that simplify problem modeling, for example:

•

Conditional literals of the form
\{\mathrm{a}(\texttt{X}):\mathrm{b}(\texttt{X})\}
, which represent sets of atoms
\mathrm{a}(\texttt{X})
for all values of
X
satisfying
\mathrm{b}(\texttt{X})
.

•

Cardinality constraints of the form
s_{1}\ \{\mathrm{a}(\texttt{X}):\mathrm{b}(\texttt{X})\}\ s_{2}
, which restrict the number of true literals in the set to be between
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
.

•

Aggregates such as
#count
and
#sum
allows one to count and sum the elements, respectively.

These constructs allow for compact and readable encodings of complex search spaces, making ASP particularly well-suited for formal explanations.
Moreover, the
#heuristic
directive provides solver-level preferences for atom selection.
For further details, see the
clingo
user guide.

3.3.1
Tree-specific explanation from a Boosted Tree

Tree-specific explanations for boosted trees identify minimal sets of literals that guarantee a prediction under best-case or worst-case scenarios.

Definition 6
(Best and Worst Instance
[
5
]
)

Let
BT=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{m}\}
be a boosted tree over Boolean variables
X_{n}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, and let
\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be instances with corresponding terms
t_{\bm{x}},t_{\bm{x}^{\prime}},t_{\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}}
, and assume
BT(\bm{x})=BT(\bm{x}^{\prime})=BT(\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})
.

•

The best instance extending
t
is any
\bm{x}^{\prime}
such that
t
is a subterm of
t_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}
,
BT(\bm{x}^{\prime})=BT(\bm{x})
, and
\bm{x}^{\prime}=\arg\max_{\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}:\,t\text{ is a subterm of }t_{\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}}}(\{w(BT,\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})\})
.

w_{\uparrow}(t,BT)
is the weight of any best instance, i.e.,
w_{\uparrow}(t,BT)=\max_{\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}:\,t\text{ is a subterm of }t_{\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}}}w(BT,\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})
.

•

The worst instance extending
t
is any
\bm{x}^{\prime}
such that
t
is a subterm of
t_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}
,
BT(\bm{x}^{\prime})=BT(\bm{x})
, and
\bm{x}^{\prime}=\arg\min_{\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}:\,t\text{ is a subterm of }t_{\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}}}(\{w(BT,\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})\})

w_{\downarrow}(t,BT)
is the weight of any worst instance, i.e.,
w_{\downarrow}(t,BT)=\min_{\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}:\,t\text{ is a subterm of }t_{\bm{x}^{\prime\prime}}}w(BT,\bm{x}^{\prime\prime})

The best (resp. worst) instances represent the most (resp. least) favorable scenarios, allowing reasoning about prediction guarantees under pessimistic conditions.

5
Related Work

The problem of explaining decision tree-based models has received significant attention, particularly through logical and symbolic methods.
Audemard et al.
[
3
]
proposed a SAT-based framework for generating formal explanations from decision trees by translating the model into CNF, supporting
direct
,
sufficient
, and
contrastive
that correspond to the explanation notions we adopt in this paper.

Izza et al.
[
16
]
addressed redundancy in path-based explanations by extracting abductive explanations as literal subsets, offering more concise explanations than path tracing.

Several SAT-based extensions have targeted ensemble models.
Audemard et al. extended their framework to compute
majority explanations

[
4
]
and
contrastive explanations

[
6
]
for random forests, demonstrating feasibility while acknowledging computational challenges in explanation enumeration.

Izza et al.
[
17
]
proposed an efficient SAT-based method to generate prime implicant (PI) explanations for random forests.
Their results showed improved performance over heuristic methods such as Anchors
[
23
]
, highlighting the advantages of logical methods in producing faithful explanations.

Takemura et al.
[
25
]
proposed an ASP-based method to derive rule-based explanations from decision tree models.
Their approach focused on extracting global and local rules through pattern mining from the structure of decision and ensemble trees.
However, their explanations lack the logical guarantees provided by our approach.

Beyond tree models, Dhurandhar et al.
[
9
]
developed the Contrastive Explanation Method (CEM) for deep neural networks, introducing the contrastive paradigm and emphasizing explanations aligned with human intuition through the presence or absence of signals.

In contrast to these works, our ASP-based approach provides a unified, extensible framework for generating multiple types of formal explanations for decision tree models and ensembles.
By leveraging ASP’s model enumeration capabilities, we enable various explanation types within a single encoding environment, improving flexibility and simplifying integration with domain-specific constraints.

6
Conclusion

This work presented an ASP-based approach for generating formal explanations for decision tree models.
We implemented encodings for sufficient, contrastive, majority, and tree-specific explanations, demonstrating that ASP can reproduce and sometimes outperform SAT-based methods, particularly for individual majority and contrastive explanations on random forests.

ASP’s enumeration capabilities offer practical advantages when generating multiple explanations by exploring the search space in a single solver call.
However, scalability challenges remain for large models, where long computation time limits practical applicability.
The primary bottlenecks are: (1) exponential memory growth during grounding when instantiating all possible combinations, and (2) the inefficient two-stage process requiring two distinct ASP systems.

Future work will focus on improving runtime performance through more efficient encodings, pruning, better heuristics, and optimization, while extending to boosted tree explanations and broader comparative analysis.
