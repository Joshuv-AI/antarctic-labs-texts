Monotone Multispecies Flows
Lauren Conger, Franca Hoffmann, Eric Mazumdar, Lillian J. Ratliff

Abstract
We present a novel notion of λ \lambda -monotonicity for an n n -species system of partial differential equations governed by mass-preserving flow dynamics, extending monotonicity in Banach spaces to the Wasserstein-2 metric space. We show that monotonicity implies the existence of and convergence to a unique steady state, convergence of the velocity fields and second moments, and contraction in the Wasserstein-2 metric, at rates dependent on λ \lambda . In the special setting of Wasserstein-2 gradient descent of different energies for each species, we prove convergence to the unique Nash equilibrium of the associated energies and delineate the relationship between monotonicity and displacement convexity. This extends known zero-sum results in infinite-dimensional game theory to the general-sum setting. We provide a number of examples of monotone coupled gradient flow systems, including cross-diffusion, gradient flows with potentials, nonlocal interaction, linear and nonlinear diffusion, and min-max systems, and draw connections to a class of mean-field games. Numerically, we demonstrate convergence of a four-player economic model for service providers and strategic users competing in a market, and a degenerately monotone game.

monotonicity, Wasserstein gradient flow, game theory, strategic learning, interacting species

Introduction

In the analysis of nonlinear dynamical systems, understanding the long-time behavior is important for modeling applications. When the dynamical system evolves via an operator on Banach spaces,
monotonicity
has proven to be a useful criteria under which convergence can be shown. In this work, we investigate mass-preserving flows, which are not in a normed space, and develop a notion of monotonicity in Wasserstein space which gives similar results to those for Banach spaces.
Mass-preserving flow equations are commonly used to describe physical systems, such as fluid flow, biological systems
[
43
, Ch 8]
, traffic
[
37
]
, and increasingly appear in emerging areas like generative modeling
[
34
]
, opinion dynamics
[
28
]
, and strategic learning environments
[

20

. Importantly, the monotonicity condition we propose has a structure that allows for systems where there is more than one entity evolving, such as in coupled flow equations. Understanding the long-time behavior of the coupled dynamics is a key question in these applications; in particular, monotonicity allows us to determine the existence of and convergence to a unique steady state.

Our notion of monotonicity is a natural extension of the well-studied condition for Euclidean dynamical systems in finite dimensions and Banach spaces in infinite dimensions, providing a condition for contraction of nonlinear dynamics. We show how this idea can be applied to velocity fields driving the evolution of probability measures. In the monotone flow setting, we consider an
n

\rho_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}})

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}})

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{i}v_{i}[\rho]\right)=0\quad\forall\,i\in[1,\dots,n]\,,

(1.1)

to contract in the joint Wasserstein-2 metric.

We additionally study a particular choice of velocity fields which results in a system of coupled gradient flows. In the Euclidean setting, all norms are equivalent, whereas in infinite dimensions, the choice of metric determines the topology and the gradient flow trajectory. We focus on the Wasserstein-2 metric, which results in a system of partial differential equations (PDEs) where the evolution of each species is written as a gradient flow with respect to its own energy. Definition 1.4

(Finite-Dimensional Monotonicity)
.

Let

\displaystyle\text{\emph{(first-order)}}\qquad\left\langle u(x)-u(x^{\prime}),\,x-x^{\prime}\right\rangle\geq\lambda\left\lVert x-x^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}\quad\forall\ x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,\text{ or}

\displaystyle\text{\emph{(second-order)}}\qquad J(x)+J(x)^{\top}\succeq 2\lambda\Id_{d}\quad\forall\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

In the finite-dimensional monotone setting, for dynamics

\displaystyle V_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert x-x^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\,,\qquad V_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert u_{i}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert x-x^{*}\right\rVert^{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho,\rho^{\infty})^{2}\,,\qquad\qquad\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert u_{i}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}}\left\lVert v_{i}[\rho](x_{i})\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(x_{i})\,,

1.1
Contributions

In this work, we define a notion of monotonicity for a system of coupled flows in Wasserstein space which results in convergence to a steady state. We then consider the setting of coupled gradient flows, and provide examples of systems that satisfy this monotonicity condition, both analytically for general functional classes and numerically for specific applications.

Monotone and Coupled Gradient Flows

In the monotone flow setting, we prove convergence in a weighted
L^{2}
-norm of the velocity fields, convergence of the second moments to a ball where they remain for all time, and convergence of solutions to the monotone flow in a joint Wasserstein-2 metric to a unique steady state. In the coupled gradient flow setting, we show that the steady state is a Nash equilibrium of the game specified by the cost functions encoded by the dynamics. We show that our infinite-dimensional generalization of monotonicity encapsulates the Euclidean definition, and prove that monotonicity of the full system implies displacement convexity of each energy functional with respect to its own species.

Applications

We include examples of monotone coupled gradient flow systems, with terms such as cross-diffusion, nonlocal interaction, and linear and nonlinear diffusion. We simulate a four-team game in which two service providers compete for users in two strategic populations, which manipulate their data over time. Then we draw a connection to multi-team mean-field games parameterized by a particular class of cost functions whose solution is a monotone coupled gradient flow. Finally, we empirically demonstrate convergence of a degenerately monotone game (a game which is not monotone, but almost monotone), using diffusion to ensure sufficient probability mass over the monotone region to still observe convergence. This suggests the possibility for our results to be extended to perturbations of monotone games.

Related Literature

This work draws from the optimization, game theory and PDE literature, applying convergence criteria and techniques from game theory to systems of flow equations. Contraction techniques for single-species PDEs fit elegantly into the multispecies framework, and the analysis of infinite-dimensional games utilizes classical PDE techniques.

Monotone Variational Inequalities in Optimization

The notion of (nonlinear) monotone operators in Banach spaces is well-studied; it was developed at least as early as 1965 in
[
6
]
. While the results include infinite-dimensional Banach spaces, it does not apply to the space of probability measures, as the Wasserstein-2 metric is not induced by a norm. Therefore, one can view our notion of monotonicity as an extension of classical monotonicity results to a metric space. For an overview of related results, see
[
32
, Ch 3]
,
[
21
]
. Other results detail algorithms for finding critical points, which are guaranteed to exist under monotonicity. The extragradient method
[
33
]
iterates by computing the gradient at a nearby point in the direction of the current gradient, and taking a step using the neighboring gradient. The results in
[
7
]
show that monotonicity implies weak convergence of a weighted history of a gradient descent trajectory, and the formulation of monotonicity in
[
17
]
hints at our formulation, by relating a monotone mapping on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to a set-valued mapping defined via the tangent space, which the authors use to derive a numerical method for finding solutions to a variational inequality. All of these works assume a Banach space structure; our work extends this to a non-Banach-space setting.

In the coupled gradient flow setting, each player

Definition 2.5

A set of measures

\displaystyle F_{i}[\rho_{i}^{*},\rho_{-i}^{*}]\leq F_{i}[\rho_{i},\rho_{-i}^{*}]\quad\forall\ \rho_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\,,\quad\forall\,i\in[1,\dots,n]\,.

A set of actions

3
Main Results

Our key result is convergence of the coupled flow system (

We prove a contraction result in

In finite-dimensional game theory, convergence to Nash equilibria can be proven using a Lyapunov function dependent on the velocity,

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}(\rho)\,,\quad D_{i}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\int\left\lVert v_{i}[\rho](x_{i})\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(x_{i})\,.

\displaystyle\leq-2\lambda\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\bar{\delta}_{0})^{2}+2\int\left\langle x,\,v[\bar{\delta}_{0}](0)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\rho(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq-2\lambda\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\bar{\delta}_{0})^{2}+2\left(\int\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\rho(t,x)\right)^{1/2}\left\lVert v[\bar{\delta}_{0}](0)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq-2\lambda\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\bar{\delta}_{0})^{2}+c\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\bar{\delta}_{0})\,,

where
c=2\left\lVert v[\bar{\delta}_{0}](0)\right\rVert<\infty
by assumption, and the Hölder inequality was used on the second line.
Then we have that
\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\bar{\delta}_{0})^{2}=2M(\rho(t))
exponentially converges to a ball with radius dependent on
c
and
\lambda
. Therefore
M(\rho(t))
is uniformly bounded for all time inside a ball of size
R
, where

\displaystyle R=\max\left\{\frac{c}{4\lambda},M(\rho(0))\right\}\,.

∎

While
Lemma

3.5
applies to the setting when
\mathcal{A}
contains a Dirac,
it is possible for the results to hold in greater generality. When Diracs are not included in
\mathcal{A}
, such as when
v
contains linear or nonlinear diffusion, applying monotonicity of (
1.1
) in
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{2}}
to control the second moment is not feasible; we provide an extension of the previous lemma below.
To show that the second moments are bounded under more general conditions, we will use a mollifier
\rho^{(\tau)}\in\mathcal{A}
as a proxy for
\bar{\delta}_{0}
, which satisfies
\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(\tau)},\bar{\delta}_{0})<\infty
. For example, when
\mathcal{A}=L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}})^{\otimes n}
, selecting

\displaystyle(\rho^{(\tau)}(x))_{i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{(2\pi\tau)^{d_{i}/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert^{2}}{2\tau}\right)\,,

results in
\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(\tau)},\bar{\delta}_{0})=\sqrt{d\tau}
, and allows us to apply the monotonicity inequality on
\rho^{(\tau)}
when it cannot be applied to
\bar{\delta}_{0}
.
This mollifier can be selected to have tails appropriate for the given velocity fields. The following lemma gives conditions under which
2
is satisfied.

Lemma 3.6

(Bounded Second Moments)
.

Let
v
be
\lambda
-monotone in
\mathcal{A}
,
\rho(t)\in\mathcal{A}
evolve according to (
1.1
) for all
t\geq 0
, and
1
hold. Fix
\tau>0
.
If there exists
\rho^{(\tau)}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

c_{\tau}\coloneqq\left(\int\left\lVert v[\rho^{(\tau)}](x)\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\rho^{(\tau)}(x)\right)^{1/2}<\infty\quad\text{ and }\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(\tau)},\bar{\delta}_{0})<\infty\,,

then

\displaystyle\sqrt{2M(\rho(t))}\leq\begin{cases}c_{1}+c_{0}e^{-\lambda t}&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0\\
c_{2}+c_{\tau}t&\text{if }\lambda=0\,,\end{cases}

where

c_{0}\coloneqq\max\left\{0,\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(0),\rho^{(\tau)})-\frac{c_{\tau}}{\lambda}\right\}\,,\quad c_{1}=\frac{c_{\tau}}{\lambda}+\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(\tau)},\bar{\delta}_{0})\,,\quad c_{2}=\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(0),\rho^{(\tau)})+\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(\tau)},\bar{\delta}_{0})\,.

In particular, if
\lambda>0
, then
\rho(t)
satisfies
2
.

Proof.

The second moment (
3.3
) can be upper-bounded using the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\sqrt{2M(\rho(t))}=\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\bar{\delta}_{0})\leq\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{(\tau)})+\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(\tau)},\bar{\delta}_{0})\,.

Let
\gamma_{i}(t)\in\Gamma^{*}(\rho_{i}(t),\rho^{(\tau)}_{i})
. Using
1
to apply
[
16
, Theorem 3.14]
for the time derivative of
\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{(\tau)})^{2}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{(\tau)})^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\left\langle x-y,\,v[\rho(t)](x)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{(\tau)})^{2}+\int\left\langle x-y,\,v[\rho^{(\tau)}](y)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma(t,x,y)\,,

where the last line follows from adding and subtracting
\int\left\langle x-y,\,v[\rho^{(\tau)}](y)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma(t,x,y)
and applying the monotonicity inequality. We use Hölder’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\int\left\langle x-y,\,v[\rho^{(\tau)}](y)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma(t,x,y)\leq\bigg(\int\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\gamma(t,x,y)\int\left\lVert v[\rho^{(\tau)}](x)\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\rho^{(\tau)}(x)\bigg)^{1/2}=c_{\tau}\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{(\tau)})\,.

Combining the last two estimates,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{(\tau)})^{2}\leq-\lambda\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{(\tau)})^{2}+c_{\tau}\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{(\tau)})\,.

When
\lambda\neq 0
,
we have

\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{(\tau)})\leq\frac{c_{\tau}}{\lambda}+c_{0}e^{-\lambda t}\,,

where
c_{0}=\max\{0,\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(0),\rho^{(\tau)})-\frac{c_{\tau}}{\lambda}\}
.
When
\lambda=0
,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{(\tau)})\leq\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(0),\rho^{(\tau)})+c_{\tau}t\,.

Using the triangle inequality, the second moments of
\rho(t)
satisfy

\displaystyle\sqrt{2M(\rho(t))}=\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\bar{\delta}_{0})\leq\begin{cases}\frac{c_{\tau}}{\lambda}+c_{0}e^{-\lambda t}+\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(\tau)},\bar{\delta}_{0})&\text{if }\lambda\neq 0\\
\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(0),\rho^{(\tau)})+c_{\tau}t+\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(\tau)},\bar{\delta}_{0})&\text{if }\lambda=0\end{cases}\,.

Setting
c_{1}=\frac{c_{\tau}}{\lambda}+\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(\tau)},\bar{\delta}_{0})
and
c_{2}=\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(0),\rho^{(\tau)})+\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(\tau)},\bar{\delta}_{0})
gives the desired inequality. From this inequality, we conclude that
if
\lambda>0
, then
2
is satisfied and if
\lambda\leq 0
, then
1
is satisfied.
∎

Lemma 3.7

(Uniqueness of Critical Points)

\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{A}

Proof.

Lemma 3.8

Lemma 3.9

Proof of

3.2
.

Recall the definition of the set

\displaystyle S=\left\{\rho\in\overline{\mathcal{P}_{2}}\ :\ \int_{\Omega}\left\|v[\rho](x)\right\|^{2}\mathsf{d}\rho(x)<\infty\quad\forall\ \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,\ \Omega\ \text{compact}\right\}\,.

Proof of (a): The proof follows from showing that the dynamics are a contraction in the joint Wasserstein-2 metric.
Let
\rho(t)
and
\eta(t)
be two solutions to (
1.1

We have from

\rho(t),\eta(t)\in\mathcal{A}
are absolutely continuous curves.
Hence, the time derivative of the joint Wasserstein-2 metric between the

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\eta(t))^{2}

\displaystyle=2\int\left\langle x-y,\,v[\eta(t)](x)-v[\rho(t)](y)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma(t,x,y)\,,

by
[
16
, Theorem 3.14]
, where
\gamma_{i}(t)\in\Gamma^{*}(\rho_{i}(t),\eta_{i}(t))
.
The right-hand side of the inequality is exactly the negative of the left-hand side of the monotonicity definition, so by

\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\eta(t))^{2}\leq-2\lambda\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\eta(t))^{2}\,.

By Grönwall’s inequality,
\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\eta(t))\leq e^{-\lambda t}\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(0),\eta(0))
, and therefore the dynamics are contractive in the joint Wasserstein-2 metric. From
[
1
, Proposition 7.1.5]
,

endowed with the Wasserstein-2 metric is complete because

is complete, and since

are absolutely continuous, we can apply

[

, Lemma 7.3]
to obtain the existence of a unique stationary point
\rho^{\infty}\in\overline{\mathcal{P}_{2}}
.
If
\mathcal{A}
is compact and the solution stays in
\mathcal{A}
for all time, the long-time limit
\rho^{\infty}
is also in
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof of (b): This step mirrors the convergence shown for
V_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert x-x^{*}\right\rVert^{2}
in the Euclidean setting of monotonicity. We use that
\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{\infty})^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{i}(t),\rho_{i}^{\infty})^{2}
and let

. Using

for the time derivative of the Wasserstein-2 metric, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{i}(t),\rho_{i}^{\infty})^{2}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\left\langle x_{i}-y_{i},\,v_{i}[\rho(t)](x_{i})\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{i})

Since
v_{i}[\rho^{\infty}](y_{i})=0
for
\rho_{i}^{\infty}
-a.e.

,

\displaystyle\left|\int\left\langle x_{i},\,v_{i}[\rho^{\infty}](y_{i})\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma_{i}(t,x_{i},y_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int\left\lVert x_{i}\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(t,x_{i})\int\left\lVert v_{i}[\rho^{\infty}](y_{i})\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}^{\infty}(y_{i})}=0\,,

and so we can add this term to the time derivative:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{i}(t),\rho_{i}^{\infty})^{2}=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\left\langle x_{i}-y_{i},\,v_{i}[\rho(t)](x_{i})-v_{i}[\rho^{\infty}](y_{i})\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma(t,x,y)\,.

Applying the monotonicity condition with the optimal plan, using that

, gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{i}(t),\rho_{i}^{\infty})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-2\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{i}(t),\rho_{i}^{\infty})^{2}\,,

which results in
\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{\infty})^{2}\leq-2\lambda\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho(t),\rho^{\infty})^{2}
. Exponential convergence follows from Grönwall’s inequality.

Part (c) follows from

.

4
Coupled Gradient Flow Setting

In this section, we present and discuss results for the setting where the dynamics are a coupled gradient flow.
Monotonicity can be viewed as a
extension of displacement convexity to the multispecies setting
, where instead of having a condition on a single energy functional, the condition holds for the set of energies
F=[F_{1},\dots,F_{n}]
. The notion of monotonicity also generalizes the results in
[
20
]
, which proves exponential convergence for a two-species min-max flow with
F_{1}=-F_{2}
. For a displacement convex-concave game
F=[F_{1},F_{2}]
with
F_{1}=-F_{2}
,
F
is an example of a
\lambda
-monotone set of energies.
Recall the dynamics (
1.2
), where each species
i
evolves in the direction of steepest descent for
F_{i}[\cdot,\rho_{-i}]
in the
\mathscr{W}_{2}
metric:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}(t)=-\nabla_{\mathscr{W}_{2},\rho_{i}}F_{i}[\rho(t)]=\mathrm{div}_{x_{i}}\left(\rho_{i}\nabla_{x_{i}}\delta_{\rho_{i}}F_{i}[\rho(t)]\right)\,,\quad\forall\,i\in[1,\dots,n]\,.

Recall also the definition of monotonicity for coupled gradient flows,
Definition

1.1
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a geodesically convex set. If for any
\rho^{(0)},\rho^{(1)}\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lambda\overline{\mathscr{W}_{2}}(\rho^{(0)},\rho^{(1)})^{2}\leq\int\left\langle x-y,\,\overline{\nabla\delta_{\rho}}F[\rho^{(0)}](x)-\overline{\nabla\delta_{\rho}}F[\rho^{(1)}](y)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma(x,y)\,,

for
\gamma\in\Gamma^{*}(\rho^{(0)},\rho^{(1)})
, then the set of
energy functionals

\{F_{i}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is
\lambda
-monotone
in
\mathcal{A}
.
In this section, we show how monotonicity implies convexity but convexity is does not necessarily imply monotonicity, because monotonicity accounts for dependencies among species. Like convexity, monotonicity is additive, and we show how to relate lower-bounds on Hessians of cross-interaction kernels to the monotonicity coefficient
\lambda
. The results are summarized in the following properties:

(C1)

Monotonicity implies convexity: if
F
is
\lambda
-monotone in
\mathcal{A}
, then each
F_{i}[\cdot,\rho_{-i}]
is
\lambda
-displacement convex in
\mathcal{A}_{i}
for all
\rho_{-i}\in\mathcal{A}_{-i}
. See
Lemma

4.4
,
Example

4.5
(
Section

4.2
).

(C2)

Additivity of monotonicity: if
F
is
\lambda
-monotone in
\mathcal{A}
and
E
is
\lambda
-monotone in
\mathcal{B}
, then
F+E
is
\lambda
-monotone in
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}
. See
Lemma

4.6
,
Example

4.7
(
Section

4.3
).

(C3)

Monotonicity of cross-interaction kernels: for
\mathcal{W}_{ij}
defined as
\mathcal{W}_{ij}=\int W_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(x_{i})\mathsf{d}\rho_{j}(x_{j})
,
F_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\mathcal{W}_{ij}
, let
\alpha_{ij}
be the Lipschitz coefficient of
\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},\cdot)
and
\sum_{i\neq j}\nabla^{2}_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\succeq c_{i}\Id_{d_{i}}
. Define

\displaystyle\Lambda:=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&-\alpha_{12}&\dots&-\alpha_{1n}\\
-\alpha_{21}&c_{2}&&-\alpha_{2n}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\alpha_{n1}&-\alpha_{n2}&\dots&c_{n}\end{bmatrix}\,.

Then

F
is
\lambda
-monotone in
\mathcal{A}
, where
\frac{1}{2}(\Lambda+\Lambda^{\top})\succeq\lambda\Id_{d}
. See

Lemma

4.8
,
Example

4.10
(
Section

4.4
).

To prove these properties, we use an equivalent, second-order notion of monotonicity. The second-order definition parallels the finite-dimension notion; we first present the second-order finite-dimension definition with the infinite-dimensional version following.
In a finite-dimensional game in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, each player selects a deterministic vector
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
minimizing a cost function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, which depends on the selections of all other players. When each player does gradient descent on its cost function, the dynamics are
\dot{x}_{i}=-\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(x)
. The definition of monotonicity for a finite-dimensional game is given in terms of the vector of gradients.

Definition 4.1

(Finite-Dimensional Monotonicity)
.

Let
f=[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, and define
\overline{\nabla}f(x)\coloneqq[\nabla_{x_{1}}f_{1}(x),\dots,\nabla_{x_{n}}f_{n}(x)]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\overline{\nabla}^{2}f(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that
(\overline{\nabla}^{2}f(x))_{ij}=\nabla_{x_{j}x_{i}}^{2}f_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times d_{j}}
.
A game
f
is monotone if it satisfies

\displaystyle\text{\emph{(first order)}}\qquad\left\langle\overline{\nabla}f(x)-\overline{\nabla}f(x^{\prime}),\,x-x^{\prime}\right\rangle\geq\lambda\left\lVert x-x^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}\quad\forall\ x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,\text{ or}

\displaystyle\text{\emph{(second order)}}\qquad\overline{\nabla}^{2}f(x)+\overline{\nabla}^{2}f(x)^{\top}\succeq 2\lambda\Id_{d}\quad\forall\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. The game is strongly monotone if
\lambda>0
.

Importantly,
\overline{\nabla}
is
not
a gradient; it is an entry-wise operator. In order to present the corresponding infinite-dimensional result, we define Hessian-like operators
\overline{\nabla^{2}\delta_{\rho}}
and
\overline{\nabla^{2}\delta^{2}_{\rho}}
.

Definition 4.2

(Matrix Operators)
.

For sufficiently regular
F
, the operators
\overline{\nabla^{2}\delta_{\rho}}
and
\overline{\nabla^{2}\delta^{2}_{\rho}}
are defined as follows, where
(\cdot)_{ij}
denotes the
i^{th}
row block and
j^{th}
column block:

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\nabla^{2}\delta_{\rho}}F[\rho](x)\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\nabla_{x_{i}}^{2}\delta_{\rho_{i}}F_{i}[\rho](x_{i})&\text{if }i=j\\

The second reformulation of monotonicity above provides a characterization that depends on only a single element

The key difference between convexity and monotonicity for both the Euclidean (

For more details and a summary of the relationship between monotonicity and convexity in finite and infinite dimensions, see

(Monotonicity and Convexity)

Next, we investigate monotonicity for a specific type of commonly-used functionals. In the PDE literature for biological models, interaction kernels are commonly employed to capture phenomena of interacting species. Our monotonicity condition provides convergence guarantees for systems driven by interaction kernels that have lower-bounded Hessians. To illustrate this, we draw a connection to the finite-dimensional contractivity literature;
[
24
, Theorem 41]
proves that when the convexity of cost functions dominate inter-species effects, the overall system is contractive. We extend this results the infinite-dimensional setting in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8

(Cross-Interaction Kernels)
.

Consider the interaction term
\mathcal{W}_{ij}:\mathcal{A}_{i}\times\mathcal{A}_{j}\to\mathbb{R}
, parameterized by the interaction kernel
W_{ij}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}},\mathbb{R})
, given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{ij}(\rho_{i},\rho_{j})=\iint W_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(x_{i})\mathsf{d}\rho_{j}(x_{j})\,.

Let
F_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\mathcal{W}_{ij}
for all
i\in[1,\dots,n]
. If for all
i\neq j
, there exist
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha_{ij}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})-\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},y_{j})\right\rVert\leq\alpha_{ij}\left\lVert x_{j}-y_{j}\right\rVert\,,\quad\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla_{x_{i}}^{2}W_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})\succeq c_{i}\Id_{d_{i}}\quad\forall\,x_{j},y_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}\,,\,x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\,,

and the matrix

\displaystyle\Lambda:=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&-\alpha_{12}&\dots&-\alpha_{1n}\\
-\alpha_{21}&c_{2}&&-\alpha_{2n}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\alpha_{n1}&-\alpha_{n2}&\dots&c_{n}\end{bmatrix}\,,

satisfies
\frac{1}{2}(\Lambda+\Lambda^{\top})\succeq\lambda\Id_{d}
, then
F
is
\lambda
-monotone in
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

The monotonicity condition to check is: for all
\rho^{(0)},\rho^{(1)}\in\mathcal{A}
and for all
\gamma\in\Gamma^{*}(\rho^{(0)},\rho^{(1)})
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\neq i}\int\left\langle\int\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},\,x_{j})\mathsf{d}\rho^{(0)}_{j}(x_{j})-\int\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(y_{i},y_{j})\mathsf{d}\rho^{(1)}_{j}(y_{j}),x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma_{i}(x_{i},y_{i})\geq\lambda\int\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\gamma(x,y)\,.

We use the optimal plans to write the inner product as

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\neq i}\int\left\langle\int\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},\,x_{j})\mathsf{d}\rho^{(0)}_{j}(x_{j})-\int\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(y_{i},y_{j})\mathsf{d}\rho^{(1)}_{j}(y_{j}),x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma_{i}(x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle=\int\sum_{i,j\neq i}\left\langle\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},\,x_{j})-\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(y_{i},y_{j}),x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma(x,y)\,,

and apply the following exact Taylor expansion

\displaystyle\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(y_{i},y_{j})=\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},y_{j})+(y_{i}-x_{i})^{\top}\nabla_{x_{i}}^{2}W_{ij}(\tilde{x}_{i},y_{j})

for some
\tilde{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
.
Then the integrand satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\neq i}\left\langle\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},\,x_{j})-\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(y_{i},y_{j}),x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}(x_{i}-y_{i})^{\top}\bigg(\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla^{2}_{ij}W_{ij}(\tilde{x}_{i},y_{j})\bigg)\cdot(x_{i}-y_{i})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i,j\neq i}\left\langle\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},\,x_{j})-\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},y_{j}),x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rangle\,.

Applying
\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla_{x_{i}}^{2}W_{ij}(\tilde{x}_{i},y_{j})\succeq c_{i}\Id_{d_{i}}
for the first term and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality plus the Lipschitz condition for the second term, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i,j\neq i}\left\langle\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},\,x_{j})-\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(y_{i},y_{j}),x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rangle&\geq\sum_{i}c_{i}\left\lVert x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rVert^{2}-\sum_{i,j\neq i}\alpha_{ij}\left\lVert x_{j}-y_{j}\right\rVert\left\lVert x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rVert\\
&=z^{\top}\Lambda z=\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}(\Lambda+\Lambda^{\top})z\,,\end{split}

(4.2)

where
z=z(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
z_{i}:=\left\lVert x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rVert
, and
\Lambda
is defined in the lemma statement. Plugging (
4.2
) into the monotonicity condition, we conclude

\displaystyle\int\sum_{i,j\neq i}\left\langle\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(x_{i},\,x_{j})-\nabla_{x_{i}}W_{ij}(y_{i},y_{j}),x_{i}-y_{i}\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int z(x,y)^{\top}(\Lambda+\Lambda^{\top})z(x,y)\mathsf{d}\gamma(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\int\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\gamma(x,y)\,.

∎

, using existing displacement convexity properties and the additivity property (

Lemma

4.8

5.2
Team Mean Field Games

Consider a setting with a single energy
F:\mathcal{A}_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
and single species
\rho=\rho_{1}
. We would like to solve a mean-field game parameterized by the energy functional
F
, viewing
\rho
as the density of particles participating in the game. Each particle aims to minimize a cost function, which includes a velocity penalty,
\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}
, as well as a penalty on the entire distribution, given in terms of
F[\rho]
. For long time horizons, a discount factor
\varepsilon
is used to weigh the cost at earlier times more heavily than the cost at later times and a terminal cost is given by
g_{\varepsilon}
. This model is a generalization of the model in
[
9
]
, which describes the density of a crowd of people with an intruder passing through. The optimization problem is

\displaystyle\inf_{v}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\int_{0}^{T}\int\left(\frac{\left\lVert v(t,y(t))\right\rVert^{2}}{2}e^{-t/\varepsilon}+f_{\varepsilon}(t,y(t),\rho(t))\right)\mathsf{d}\rho(y(t))\,\mathsf{d}t+g_{\varepsilon}(T,y(T),\rho(T))\\
&\qquad\quad\text{such that }\mathsf{d}y(t)=v(t,y(t))\mathsf{d}t\,,\ y(0)\sim\rho(0,\cdot)\,,\ \text{Law}\{y\}=\rho(t,\cdot)\end{aligned}\right\}\,,

where
f_{\varepsilon}(t,y(t),\rho(t))\coloneqq\frac{e^{-t/\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\delta_{\rho}F[\rho(t)]
and
g_{\varepsilon}(t,y(t),\rho(t))=\varepsilon f_{\varepsilon}(t,y(t),\rho(t))=e^{-t/\varepsilon}\delta_{\rho}F[\rho(t)]
.
The particle
y(t)
is sampled from the distribution
\rho(t)
, and driven by the velocity field
v
.
This control problem is equivalent to the mean field game

\displaystyle\inf_{v,\rho}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(\int\frac{e^{-t/\varepsilon}}{2}\left\lVert v(t,x)\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\rho(t,x)+\frac{e^{-t/\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}F[\rho(t)]\bigg)\mathsf{d}t+F[\rho(T)]e^{-T/\varepsilon}\\
&\qquad\quad\text{such that }\partial_{t}\rho(t)+\nabla\cdot(v(t)\rho(t))=0\end{aligned}\right\}\,,

This mean field game is studied in
[
50
]
, and it was shown that the optimal
(\rho,v)
results in a perturbation of a gradient flow
[
50
, Theorem 14, Remark 15]
. Here, we consider an extension to the setting where different particles have different cost functionals depending on their type, or species. Collecting the particles by species, the optimization problem becomes

\displaystyle\inf_{v_{i},\rho_{i}}\left\{\begin{aligned} &\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int\frac{e^{-t/\varepsilon}}{2}\left\lVert v_{i}(t,x_{i})\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(t,x_{i})+\frac{e^{-t/\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}F_{i}[\rho(t)]\right)\mathsf{d}t+F_{i}[\rho(T)]e^{-T/\varepsilon}\\
&\qquad\quad\text{such that }\partial_{t}\rho_{i}(t)+\nabla_{x_{i}}\cdot(v_{i}(t)\rho_{i}(t))=0\,,\quad\forall\,i\in[1,\dots,n]\end{aligned}\right\}\,,

Following a similar approach as the proof in
[
50
]
(equations 74-75), the optimal solution takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho_{i}&=\mathrm{div}_{x_{i}}\left(\rho_{i}\nabla_{x_{i}}\delta_{\rho_{i}}F_{i}[\rho]\right)+\varepsilon\mathrm{div}_{x_{i}}\left(\rho_{i}\nabla_{x_{i}}(\partial_{t}U_{i}-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla U_{i}|^{2})\right)\\
-\varepsilon\partial_{t}U_{i}&+U_{i}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla_{x_{i}}U_{i}|^{2}=\delta_{\rho_{i}}F_{i}[\rho]\,.\end{split}

(5.1)

For monotone
F
, (
5.1
) is a perturbation of coupled gradient flows with monotone energies. It is an interesting open problem to determine the sufficient conditions on
F_{i}[\rho_{i},\cdot]
, such as smoothness, under which we expect that the result in
[
50
, Theorem 14]
for the limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
can be shown in this multispecies setting. The long-time behavior of the solution in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
can then be analyzed using our monotonicity framework, since it follows the evolution (
1.2
).

5.3
Degenerately Monotone Game with Diffusion

For an energy functional that is "almost" strongly convex, exponential convergence of the corresponding gradient flow can still be shown if the energy functional also contains a linear entropy term. The notion of convexity used is called
degenerate
convexity. A potential function
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is degenerately convex if
\nabla^{2}V\geq\psi(x)
for some
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\psi(x)>0
if
x\neq 0
, and
\psi(x)
is uniformly bounded from below by some positive constant as
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\to\infty
. Exponential convergence for gradient flows with energies of the form

\displaystyle F_{i}[\rho_{i}]=\int V_{i}(x_{i})\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(x_{i})+\int W_{i}(x_{i}-y_{i})\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(x_{i})\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(y_{i})+\int\log\rho_{i}(x_{i})\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(x_{i})\,,

where
V_{i}
and
W_{i}
are degenerately convex, are given in
[
14
]
; this occurs because the entropy term ensures
\rho_{i}
has mass in regions of the
\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
where the Hessians of
V_{i}
and
W_{i}
are strongly convex.
In finite dimensions, a convex potential that is not strongly convex results in a polynomial rate, but not an exponential rate. Here we apply the results from
[
14
]
, Rule #5, to obtain a rate in combination with the effects of coupling between the species. From a PDE analysis perspective, it is interesting that the strength of the inter-species coupling does not necessarily change the exponential convergence rate. From a game theory perspective, our framework expands the set of games typically considered by including entropy in the cost function, and a framework for understanding why adding entropy, even though it is not strongly convex, enables an exponential rate.
The energy functionals are
F_{1}=F
and
F_{2}=-F
, with
F
given by

\displaystyle F[\rho_{1},\rho_{2}]=\frac{a}{2}\int\left\lVert x_{1}\right\rVert^{4}\mathsf{d}\rho_{1}(x_{1})-\frac{a}{2}\int\left\lVert x_{2}\right\rVert^{4}\mathsf{d}\rho_{2}(x_{2})+\frac{b}{2}\iint x_{1}^{\top}x_{2}\mathsf{d}\rho_{1}(x_{1})\mathsf{d}\rho_{2}(x_{2})+c(\mathcal{H}(\rho_{1})-\mathcal{H}(\rho_{2}))

where
\mathcal{H}(\rho_{i})=\int\log\rho_{i}(x_{i})\mathsf{d}\rho_{i}(x_{i})
, and
a,b,c>0
. In
[
14
]
, the authors illustrate how functionals with degenerate convexity can still have exponential convergence due to diffusion, which keeps mass in an area of the domain where the energy can be lower-bounded by a strongly convex function. In our example, the quartic term is not strongly convex; however, the diffusion term allows us to obtain a rate and we call this setting
degenerately monotone

Definition A.1

(Wasserstein-2 Metric)
.

The Wasserstein-2 metric between two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int\left\lVert z-z^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathsf{d}\gamma(z,z^{\prime})

where
\Gamma
is the set of all joint probability distributions with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
, i.e.
\mu(\mathsf{d}z)=\int\gamma(\mathsf{d}z,z^{\prime})\mathsf{d}z^{\prime}
and
\nu(\mathsf{d}z^{\prime})=\int\gamma(z,\mathsf{d}z^{\prime})\mathsf{d}z
.

If
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are such that at least one transport map
T
pushing forward
\mu
to
\nu
exists (written as
\nu=T_{\sharp}\mu
), we obtain the
Monge formulation
of the Wasserstein-2 distance:

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf_{T\ :\ \nu=T_{\sharp}\mu}\int\left\lVert x-T(x)\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\mu(x)\,.

Since this is not true for all pairs
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the Kantorovich formulation
Definition

A.1
is more general than the Monge formulation.
Another way of writing the Wasserstein-2 Metric is via the
Benamou-Brenier formulation
(also known as the
dynamic formulation
of
\mathscr{W}_{2}
), expressing the distance between two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as the path solving a continuity equation with minimal kinetic energy:

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf_{(\mu_{t},w_{t})}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\int\left\lVert w(t,x)\right\rVert^{2}\mathsf{d}\mu(t,x)\mathsf{d}t\,;\ \partial_{t}\mu_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\mu_{t}w_{t})=0\,\ \text{s.t.}\ \mu_{0}=\mu,\,\mu_{1}=\nu\right\}\,.

The Benamou-Brenier formulation is useful for geodesic calculations, as the optimal
(\mu,w)
are also solutions to the geodesic equation (
2.1
).

Lemma B.1

(Convexity of

Consider the functional

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{d}}{\mathsf{d}s}\mathcal{W}_{ij}(\rho^{(s)}_{i},\rho^{(s)}_{j})

In the setting where

Remark C.4
.

Corollary

C.3
does not necessarily hold for sets
\mathcal{A}
which do not contain
\overline{\mathcal{P}}^{(f)}
. For example, consider the single-species setting where
v[\rho]=-\nabla\log\rho
. The domain of
v:L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
excludes measures with finite support. Writing
v[\rho]=-\nabla\delta_{\rho}\mathcal{H}(\rho)
, where
\mathcal{H}=\int\rho\log\rho
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int\left\langle x-y,\,\nabla\log\rho(x)-\nabla\log\tilde{\rho}(y)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\gamma^{*}(x,y)\geq 0

for
\gamma^{*}\in\Gamma^{*}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})
, because
\mathcal{H}
is
0
-displacement convex over absolutely continuous measures. We check if the inequality still holds for the admissible plan
\gamma(x,y)=\rho(x)\tilde{\rho}(y)
:

\displaystyle\int\left\langle x-y,\,\nabla\log\rho(x)-\nabla\log\tilde{\rho}(y)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}\rho(x)\mathsf{d}\tilde{\rho}(y)=-\int\left\langle x,\,\nabla\rho(x)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}x-\int\left\langle y,\,\nabla\tilde{\rho}(y)\right\rangle\mathsf{d}y\leq-2d<0\,,

so the monotonicity inequality does not hold for general
\gamma\in\Gamma(\rho,\tilde{\rho})
.
