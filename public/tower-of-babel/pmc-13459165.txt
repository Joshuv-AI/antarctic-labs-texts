Study protocol for a quasi-experimental investigation of the effectiveness of the ‘CATCH My Breath’ vaping prevention curriculum at preventing vaping outcomes among high school students in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Adam G Cole, Lucas R W Fairs, Caroline Barakat, Dale Mantey, Marcella Bianco, Anne Philipneri, Celina Degano, Scott T Leatherdale, Steven H Kelder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaping is prevalent among youth in Canada, and youth who vape are at risk of becoming addicted to nicotine and continuing to vape. Given these risks, there is a need to identify effective prevention approaches. The goal of this study is to investigate the effectiveness of the ‘CATCH My Breath’ (CMB) vaping prevention curriculum at preventing vaping outcomes among a cohort of high school students in Ontario, Canada.MethodsThis quasi-experimental study will use a non-equivalent control group pretest-posttest design. A purposive sample of 28 high schools in Ontario, Canada will be recruited, and a trained presenter will deliver the CMB curriculum to grade 9 students during selected classes. CMB is a school-based vaping prevention curriculum with demonstrated success in the United States that incorporates theory to foster social competence and social influence resistance skills. Students in the intervention group will complete online surveys before exposure to the curriculum, and 3- and 12-months later to identify changes in vaping knowledge and attitudes, susceptibility to vaping among non-vapers, vaping initiation, and current vaping. These data will be compared to a matched sample of school- and student-level data from the COMPASS study, an ongoing longitudinal cohort study of youth health behaviours. Descriptive analysis of baseline data will produce a profile of demographics and baseline characteristics and identify any imbalances between the intervention and comparison groups that should be accounted for in later analyses. Multilevel regression models will test for intervention effects while controlling for baseline differences through the use of covariates.DiscussionTo our knowledge, there are no published studies demonstrating that a vaping program can prevent vaping initiation among high school students. As a result, there is not enough evidence for schools to confidently implement programs that address vaping. Given the demand from Canadian high schools for vaping prevention programming, there is a need to further examine the impact of CMB to inform program recommendations to schools.Trial registrationThis study has been registered with ClinicalTrials.gov on Feb 7, 2025 (NCT06825338).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28027-3.Keywords: Youth, Adolescent, Vaping, E-cigarette use, Prevention education, CATCH my breath, Quasi-experimental study

Background

While youth cigarette smoking is at a historic low, the prevalence of vaping (e-cigarette use) among Canadian youth has increased dramatically over the last decade [1] and continues to be high following the COVID-19 pandemic [2, 3]. According to recent nationally representative data, in 2023-24, 15% of students in grades 7–12 reported vaping in the past 30-days and 6.6% reported vaping daily [4]. There are possible negative health effects of vaping, including impacts on the respiratory and cardiovascular systems [5–8], and most vapes contain nicotine, which contributes to addiction and can alter adolescent brain development [9]. Interventions are urgently needed to prevent youth vaping, and the proposed research supports recent policy and position statements from Canadian organizations that emphasize the need to develop and evaluate programs that educate students about the risks of vaping [10–12].

Schools are an important and equitable setting for intervention because most youth spend the majority of their waking hours on weekdays in this environment. However, evidence indicates that many students witness their peers vaping at school, increasing their risk of experimenting with vapes [13, 14]. The school environment influences the likelihood that a student will vape [15, 16], and schools may take diverse approaches to prevent and address student vaping. School-based prevention programs are a common approach to motivate students to avoid harmful behaviours, and evidence indicates that well-designed tobacco prevention programs reduce the risk of smoking among students [17]. However, there are few school-based prevention programs targeting vaping and fewer that have undergone any evaluation of effectiveness [18, 19]. In Ontario, the current Health and Physical Education curriculum does not include any vaping-specific information, leaving it up to the discretion of educators to decide if vaping-related content should be covered, what content should be included, and how it should be taught. In the absence of evidence-informed programming, educators may implement programs that are not effective, or at worst, actually increase the risk of student vaping. It is evident that schools need urgent support in delivering evidence-informed interventions targeting vaping.

Two reviews of vaping prevention programs identified eight school-based programs targeting adolescents, all of which originated in the United States (US) [18, 19], and only some of these programs had been evaluated. Results of these evaluations indicate that students increased their knowledge of the risks of vaping after exposure to the programs [20–23]. Few studies have investigated the impact of vaping prevention programs on adolescent behaviours, such as vaping initiation. Furthermore, the programs that exist and the evaluations that have been conducted tend to target middle school students (grades 6–8). However, longitudinal data indicate that almost one-third of high school students (grades 9–11) initiate vaping one-year later [24], highlighting the need for prevention programs targeting high school students as well.

‘CATCH My Breath’ (CMB) is an evidence-informed prevention program developed in the US that targets middle and high school students with demonstrated success among youth in the US [23, 25]. CMB is unique from other prevention programs in that the entire program targets vaping, rather than including this topic as a supplemental component. Evaluations of CMB among US middle school students indicate that the program increases knowledge of the risks of vaping [23, 26, 27] and reduces perceived positive outcomes for vaping and the school-level prevalence of lifetime vaping [23]. A recent pilot study adapted CMB and tested the feasibility of implementing the program in a Canadian high school context [28, 29]. Informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [30], this pre-post investigation not only demonstrated that implementation of the program was feasible in Canadian high schools, but also there was a desire for vaping prevention programs among high schools in Ontario, Canada. After exposure to CMB, the average knowledge score of the risks of vaping increased significantly and fewer students agreed that most people in high school vape (a measure of perceptions of vaping norms) [28]. Despite these promising outcomes, the lack of a comparison group and the short follow-up period (1-month) limited the ability to assess changes in vaping behaviours. Such evidence is crucial for providing evidence-informed recommendations to schools in Canada and it is the focus of the proposed research.

Objectives and research questions

The objective of this study is to investigate the effectiveness of CMB at preventing vaping outcomes among a cohort of high school students in Ontario, Canada. This quasi-experimental investigation aims to answer the following primary research question (RQ):

RQ1: Do students exposed to CMB have a lower likelihood of initiating vaping compared to a matched comparison group of students?

RQ1: Do students exposed to CMB have a lower likelihood of initiating vaping compared to a matched comparison group of students?

In addition to preventing vaping initiation, the program may have positive impacts on the risk of future vaping among those who never vaped, and it may encourage students who currently vape to reduce or stop vaping. These research areas have been unexplored in previous studies. Additionally, few studies have considered gender differences in the impact of school interventions. Therefore, this study will also answer the following secondary RQs:

RQ2: Do students who never vaped who are exposed to CMB have a lower likelihood of being susceptible to future vaping compared to a matched comparison group of students?

RQ2: Do students who never vaped who are exposed to CMB have a lower likelihood of being susceptible to future vaping compared to a matched comparison group of students?

RQ3: Do students who currently vape (in the past 30-days) have a lower likelihood of continuing to vape compared to a matched comparison group of students?

RQ3: Do students who currently vape (in the past 30-days) have a lower likelihood of continuing to vape compared to a matched comparison group of students?

RQ4: How do vaping knowledge and attitudes change over time among students exposed to CMB?

RQ4: How do vaping knowledge and attitudes change over time among students exposed to CMB?

RQ5: Do effects of the intervention differ according to gender?

RQ5: Do effects of the intervention differ according to gender?

For this study, it is hypothesized that the students in the intervention group will be less likely to initiate vaping (RQ1), less likely to be susceptible to vaping (RQ2), and less likely to currently vape (RQ3) after exposure to CMB, compared to same-aged students in the comparison group. We also hypothesize that vaping knowledge will increase and pro-vaping attitudes will decrease following exposure to CMB content. We do not hypothesize any differences in intervention effects according to gender.

Methods

Study design

This quasi-experimental study will use a non-equivalent control group pretest-posttest design, which capitalizes on existing relationships with the ongoing Cannabis, Obesity, Mental health, Physical activity, Alcohol, Smoking, and Sedentary behaviour (COMPASS) study. School boards and schools may be less inclined to participate in a randomized controlled trial knowing that they could be randomly assigned to the control group rather than the intervention group. Collaborating with this pre-existing cohort study contributes to the feasibility of recruiting a comparator sample of schools and students and reduces the cost of the study. Therefore, a non-equivalent control group pretest-posttest quasi-experimental design is more feasible while providing rigour to the study design. COMPASS is a Canadian Institutes of Health Research-funded (2012–2027) prospective cohort study designed to evaluate how ongoing changes in programs, policies, and the physical environment affect youth health behaviours over time in over 200 schools in five Canadian provinces [31, 32]. The COMPASS study uses an online, anonymous, yearly survey to obtain student-level data [31, 33, 34]. The survey includes similar demographic questions (e.g., age, gender, ethno-racial identity, amount of weekly spending money) and core measures related to vaping to allow for comparisons.

Within the non-equivalent control group pretest-posttest design, there are two groups: intervention and comparison [35]. The intervention group (IG) will be comprised of students recruited from 28 high schools in Ontario, Canada who will receive the CMB vaping prevention curriculum. The comparison group (CG) will be a 1:1 matched sample of 28 Ontario high schools selected from a larger pool of schools participating in the COMPASS study who do not receive any vaping prevention programming [31]. These schools will be identified by matching specific school-level factors between the two groups: school median income, geographic location, school size, and time of year of data collection. For each IG school, a CG school(s) will be established as the closest match. Next, students within the matched pair of schools will be matched based on the following student-level factors: age/grade, gender, ethno-racial identity, amount of weekly spending money, and vaping status (non-susceptible never vaper, susceptible never vaper, lifetime vaping, current vaping). The CG student(s) that is the closest match will be identified for each IG student. If there is more than one possible match in the CG for schools or students, we will randomly select the match from the possible options. The most similar school or student will be used if no identical match can be identified.

Within the IG, students will complete a baseline survey prior to exposure to CMB, and follow-up surveys 3-months and 12-months after baseline. Within the CG, a prospective cohort of students complete a yearly survey. The effectiveness of CMB will be assessed through quantitative approaches that investigate the impact of CMB on student vaping initiation, susceptibility to future vaping, current vaping, and vaping knowledge and attitudes. This study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Board at Ontario Tech University (file #18093) on February 12, 2025. All parents/guardians will provide consent for their students to participate in the study and students will provide their own assent. This study has been registered with ClinicalTrials.gov on Feb 7, 2025 (NCT06825338). Appendix Table 1 provides trial registration information and Appendix Table 2 provides the SPIRIT checklist.

Any modification to the protocol which may impact on the conduct of the study, potential benefit to schools/students, or may affect student safety, including changes to study objectives, study design, population, sample sizes, study procedures, or significant administrative aspects will require a formal amendment to the protocol. Such amendment will be approved by the Ontario Tech University Research Ethics Board prior to implementation and notified to the appropriate school board review committees as required.

School recruitment

Intervention group

Figure 1 presents a summary of key events that will take place during the study. Recruitment will begin by obtaining commitments from Ontario-based Public Health Units (PHUs) to deliver the CMB curriculum in high schools within their catchment area. PHUs will be divided into four geographic areas of the province (i.e., northern, eastern, central, southwestern), and 1–2 PHUs in each geographic area will be recruited in order to maximize the diversity of the sample and the generalizability of the findings. After establishing partnerships with local PHUs, the Project Manager (PM) will acquire the necessary approvals from school boards to conduct research in schools in their district. Using input from the committed PHUs, the PM will submit research applications to 1–2 school boards in the PHU catchment area for review.

After receiving approval to conduct research in their school board, the PM will initiate contact with schools in the school board to participate in the study. School contact information (i.e., email of the principal or vice-principal) will be sourced through the school’s public website, staff at the school board (who may first contact schools on behalf of the study team), or staff at the partnering PHU. The PM will email an invitation letter to the school contact at schools that meet the study’s inclusion criteria (see Table 1) and where the local PHU has confirmed they can deliver the CMB curriculum. The PM will phone schools in follow-up if necessary. The aim is to recruit 2–4 schools from each participating school board to not overburden PHUs with curriculum delivery.

After obtaining permission from the approached schools, the final step will involve obtaining consent from the parents/guardians of eligible students in the schools. Either active consent or active-information passive-consent (passive consent) will be used depending on the requirements of the school board. Active consent process involves sending an electronic study information letter and consent form to parents/guardians of eligible students. Parents/guardians need to contact the research team if they do not want their student to receive baseline and follow-up surveys. Given that the CMB curriculum will be delivered during class time, students do not require parental permission to attend the lessons. Parental consent documents will be distributed using the normal channels used by the school to inform students and parents of a research study (e.g., information letter emailed to parents or posted in a virtual classroom). Online consent forms will be managed through LimeSurvey, and student email addresses will be received during the consent process. A school contact person will primarily be responsible for distributing the study information letter and consent materials to students and parents.

Comparison group

CG schools will be identified from the sample of schools participating in the COMPASS study. Recruitment of schools into the COMPASS study follows a similar process of obtaining school board approval, approaching individual schools, and obtaining parental consent for students to complete surveys using passive consent protocols [36, 37]. In 2023-24, 66 schools participated from Ontario [38]. While schools recruited into the COMPASS study are not randomly sampled or intended to be a provincially representative sample, the sample includes schools of differing sizes and in different geographic locations across Ontario, and estimates of lifetime and current vaping are consistent with those from other representative national and provincial surveys. Additional details about the COMPASS study sampling and recruitment methods can be found online (https://uwaterloo.ca/compass-system/).

In addition to collecting student-level data, the COMPASS study also collects data about school programs and policies targeting vaping and tobacco use using the School Policies and Practices survey. This survey is completed annually by a school contact knowledgeable about the school’s health-related programs and policies [31]. Responses to this survey will be used to verify that schools included in the CG have not implemented any vaping/tobacco use programs during the study period. Previous evidence indicates that many schools do not implement vaping or tobacco use prevention/cessation programs over one year [39–41].

Participants

Intervention group

All students who will be exposed to CMB at each participating IG school and have parental permission will be invited to participate in this study through email invitation. The PM will manage participant contact information in LimeSurvey to ensure that only students with consent receive a link to the online survey. It is anticipated that the majority of participating students will be in grade 9. The content of CMB aligns with expectations of the Ontario Health and Physical Education curriculum, and Health and Physical Education classes are most often taken by students at the start of high school when they are in grade 9. However, school administrators and PHU staff will provide their input regarding the best class subject for delivering the CMB program to students in their schools.

Students will be offered an incentive for completing surveys to improve student participation rates and achieve the target sample size. These incentives include a $10 digital gift card for completing the baseline survey, a $10 digital gift card for completing the 3-month follow-up survey, and a $15 digital gift card for completing the 12-month follow-up survey (up to $35 total). Inviting all eligible students within recruited schools to participate should provide gender and ethno-racial diversity in the sample. All students can decline to participate or withdraw from the study at any time.

Comparison group

Within the COMPASS study, all grade 9 to 12 students in sampled schools are eligible to participate. The COMPASS study selects schools that permit the use of passive consent [36], and students also have the opportunity to decline participating on the day of the survey. Students do not receive an honorarium for participating. In 2023-24, the average school participation rate was 77% [38].

Intervention description

The eligible schools that agree to participate in the IG of this study will receive the ‘CATCH My Breath’ (CMB) vaping prevention curriculum. This curriculum is based on best practices from earlier prevention studies [17, 42–45] that works by fostering social competence and social influence resistance skills. The curriculum design and content targets two key constructs of Social Cognitive Theory: self-efficacy and behavioural capacity (i.e., knowledge and skills) [46, 47]. The curriculum provides students with knowledge of why and how to resist vaping. Specifically, CMB lessons provide information about the physical, mental, and addiction risks associated with vaping; social norms of vaping (e.g., most youth don’t vape); media literacy (e.g., how to recognize and decipher marketing of vapes); strategies to resist and avoid vaping; and school and provincial policies about vaping [25]. To solidify the content, in-class activities allow students to discuss what they are learning and practice refusal skills.

The original CMB curriculum was designed to be delivered through four 25-minute lessons by a trained classroom teacher [25]. The original program underwent some minor revisions (e.g., including Canadian statistics and examples of advertisements) for a pilot study [29]. Informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [48], the pilot study examined the feasibility of implementing CMB in high schools, and ensured fit between intervention and context by examining the appropriateness and comprehensiveness of the content for Canadian high school students and the alignment of CMB with Ontario curriculum expectations. Additional feedback was received from students and curriculum presenters about components of the program that worked well (e.g., content addressing health risks, development of refusal skills, discussions about advertising techniques) and that needed to be revised (e.g., combine lessons to fit within high school class periods, update statistics, add personal testimonials) [29].

In collaboration with staff at CATCH Global Foundation, between August 2024 and March 2025 the curriculum was modified to reflect the feedback received during pilot testing. Notably, this included updating youth vaping prevalence statistics, adding content about the impact of nicotine on mental health, updating the examples of marketing ads, adding content about how to support those who want to quit vaping, and combining content so that there were two 60-minute lessons that would fit within longer high school class periods. In March 2025, the updated curriculum was tested with a sample of 22 grade 9 and 10 students to ensure it could be delivered within two 60-minute class periods.

The CMB curriculum will be delivered through two 60-minute lessons by a trained presenter between October 2025 and May 2026. While CMB was originally designed to be delivered by classroom teachers, we learned through the pilot study that it may be more feasible to have PHU staff deliver the curriculum instead. Not only does this reduce the challenge of finding a common time to train teachers who have busy schedules, but it could also provide more consistency in delivery across schools and further develop relationships between PHUs and schools. While PHU staff will be the primary presenters, in some areas the curriculum may be presented jointly by classroom teachers and PHU staff or by trained Ontario Tech University nursing students. Teachers may deliver the curriculum in collaboration with PHU staff due to staff restrictions that may be imposed by PHUs or school boards that limit the ability of PHU staff to deliver materials in a classroom setting. Nursing students may deliver the curriculum due to the sudden merger of several Ontario PHUs, which may limit the capacity of PHU staff to deliver the curriculum in schools in their region. Ontario Tech University nursing students need to complete a community health practicum placement as part of their degree, and their involvement in this research project offers them a school health experiential learning opportunity. Importantly, previous evidence from smoking prevention studies suggests that the presenter (Public Health Nurse vs. teacher) does not significantly impact delivery or the effectiveness of an intervention [49, 50], and all presenters will receive training prior to curriculum delivery.

To ensure standard delivery, curriculum presenters will be provided with a detailed curriculum/lesson plans, will attend a virtual 1-hour training webinar facilitated by staff at CATCH Global Foundation prior to delivering the curriculum, and will complete a short field notes survey after delivering each lesson to confirm the delivery of the lessons as designed and indicate whether there were any modifications or omissions. The data from the field notes survey will be reviewed and will be used in a sensitivity analysis to compare student outcomes in schools where there were changes to curriculum delivery. Curriculum presenters will access CMB program materials and resource through the CATCH digital access portal, which provides easy retrieval of multiple media (e.g., training videos, classroom handouts, etc.). The access portal also facilitates monitoring the fidelity of program delivery by providing presenter behaviour analytics (e.g., number of page views, average view time, click through rate, etc.). The lessons will be delivered within a span of two weeks at most. Requiring only two lessons reduces the burden on schools and PHUs, which have many competing demands, while also maintaining the integrity of the intervention.

Data collection

Intervention group

IG students will complete an online survey outside of school time at three timepoints: baseline (i.e., up to 2-weeks prior to lesson delivery), and 3- and 12- months after program delivery (see Fig. 1). Participating students will be sent an email with a unique ‘token’ to access the survey; this token will allow the research team to link participant data over time. The survey will be available for a 2-week period and will include demographic and behavioural questions consistent with other school-based studies in Canada and the US. The survey will be designed to be completed outside of school time within 15-minutes to reduce participant burden and will be collected through the online data capturing platform, LimeSurvey.

Comparison group

CG students enrolled in the COMPASS study complete an online, anonymous, yearly survey about their health behaviours [31, 33]. In 2023-24, 98% of schools allocated class time for survey completion. The survey includes similar demographic questions (e.g., age, gender, race/ethnicity, amount of spending money) and core measures related to vaping [33].

The COMPASS survey provides researchers with individual-specific, yet anonymous data required for linking individual student data over time. Consistent with previous research [51], the beginning of the COMPASS survey contains five questions required to create a unique self-generated code for each student in a school [52, 53]. This ensures the anonymity of the survey participants while still allowing researchers to link each student’s data over multiple years. Previous data suggest high linkage rates (> 75%) over one follow-up year [53]. Data are collected through Qualtrics [34].

Measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome that will be used to evaluate the effectiveness of the CMB program is vaping initiation. This measure will be collected at all three timepoints among students in the IG and at each yearly survey among students in the CG. To measure vaping initiation, one validated item [54, 55] will assess ever-vaping: “Have you ever tried a vape, also known as an e-cigarette?”, with response options ‘Yes’ and ‘No’. This item is consistent with other youth health surveillance studies in Canada and the US. The question will include a definition of vapes, including examples of common brands, to improve the validity of student responses [56, 57]. Students who respond ‘No’ at baseline will be coded as ‘Never vaped’. Students who never vaped at baseline who respond ‘Yes’ at follow-up will be coded as ‘Initiated vaping’ (outcome of interest), while those who respond ‘No’ at follow-up will be categorized as ‘Did not initiate vaping’ (reference group).

Secondary outcomes

Key secondary outcomes include susceptibility to future vaping and current vaping status. These measures will be collected at all three timepoints among students in the IG and at each yearly survey among students in the CG. Three validated items will assess susceptibility to future vaping among students who never vaped: [58, 59] (1) “Have you ever been curious about using a vape?”; 2) “If one of your best friends were to offer you a vape, would you use it?”; and (3) “At any time during the next year do you think you will use a vape?”, with response options ‘Definitely yes’, ‘Probably yes’, ‘Probably not’, and ‘Definitely not’. Consistent with previous studies [15, 58–60], students who never vaped who respond ‘definitely not’ to all three questions will be coded as ‘Not susceptible to future vaping’, while all others will be coded as ‘Susceptible to future vaping’. The analyses will explore whether IG students who are susceptible to future vaping at baseline are more likely to become not susceptible to future vaping at follow-up relative to CG students.

At each timepoint, one item will assess current (past 30-day) vaping: “On how many of the last 30 days did you use a vape?”. In the IG survey, students will input a whole number between 0–30, while the COMPASS study survey includes the response options: ‘None’, ‘1 day’, ‘2 to 3 days’, ‘4 to 5 days’, ‘6 to 10 days’, ’11 to 20 days’, ’21 to 29 days’, and ’30 days (every day)’. Students who report vaping at least one day in the last 30 days will be coded as ‘Currently vape’, while all others will be categorized as ‘Do not currently vape’. The analyses will explore whether IG students who report current vaping at baseline are more likely to report non-current vaping at follow-up relative to CG students.

Consistent with previous evaluations of CMB [23, 28], at each timepoint IG students will also answer questions assessing knowledge of harms of vaping (11 items related to content delivered in the lessons), perceived positive and negative outcomes of vaping (12 items), subjective norms of vaping (3 items), and vaping resistance self-efficacy (1 item). Data management

IG survey data will be stored on a secure server managed by LimeSurvey in Canada. After data collection is complete, the data will be downloaded from the LimeSurvey server and stored as encrypted digital files in an account associated with the Principal Investigator (PI) on an institution-approved secure cloud-based server. Consent information will be retained for 3-years and then deleted. Anonymized data for students who have given permission for their data to be used for secondary research purposes will be uploaded to Borealis to be stored indefinitely at the end of the project following data analysis and publication. Borealis is a Canadian-based research data repository. These anonymized data will be available only to specific individuals upon request. The requests will be reviewed and approved by the PI.

Paper copies of consent forms will be scanned and emailed to the PM, and the required participant information will be added to LimeSurvey to facilitate distribution of the online surveys to participants. The paper consent forms will be mailed to the PI, stored in a locked drawer in the PI’s office at Ontario Tech University, and shredded after 3-years.

Data from the COMPASS study are stored at the University of Waterloo on a secure server. Researchers interested in using COMPASS data need to complete a COMPASS Data Use Application Form, which outlines the research questions and specific variables requested for data analysis. Following review and approval of the COMPASS Data Use Application Form, the requested data are sent via secure file transfer.

Sample size determination

A sample size calculation [61] indicated 28 schools per condition and at least 60 students per school are needed to identify a 10% difference in the prevalence of vaping initiation between the IG and CG 12-months later, based on a two-sided test with alpha set at 0.05 and power set at 80%. A previous evaluation of multiple vaping interventions identified a 3–15% difference in vaping initiation between control and intervention groups [39]. It is believed that a 10% difference is reasonable based on previous evaluation data of school-based vaping interventions [23, 39] and would also represent a meaningful reduction in vaping initiation. It is not anticipated that school drop-out will be a significant concern due to the short duration of the study. The sample size parameters for this study are also based on an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.06 for ever vaping based on 2017-18 data [15], and a prevalence of vaping initiation of 30% based on 2018-19 data [24]. Unpublished data indicated that while the prevalence of vaping initiation was lower (17%) in 2020-21, the ICC for ever vaping was consistent (0.07). Even with a lower prevalence of vaping initiation, the target sample size will still provide sufficient power to identify a significant difference in the prevalence of vaping initiation between groups. To account for participant drop-out over time, the aim of this study is to recruit 100 students per school at baseline in order to have a linked sample of at least 60 students per school. The average school enrolment for COMPASS schools in Ontario is 656 students (or approximately 164 students/grade). The average school enrolment size for our pilot study was similar (576 students). Therefore, the proposed sample size is 28 schools per condition, for an estimated linked sample of 1680 students per condition.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive analysis of baseline data (e.g., frequencies, means, cross-tabulations) will produce a profile of demographics and behavioural characteristics of IG and CG students and schools. These results will verify that the matching procedures produced balanced groups. Any differences in group characteristics will be accounted for in regression models through the inclusion of covariates.

Consistent with our pilot study [62], McNemar’s Chi-square exact tests of paired proportions will assess significant differences in the proportion of IG students who correctly answer knowledge questions about vapes, who agree with statements about positive and negative outcome expectancies and subjective norms about vaping, and who report high self-efficacy (i.e., ‘easy’ and ‘very easy’) to resist vaping between baseline and 3- and 12-month follow-up as a measure of changes to knowledge, positive and negative outcome expectancies, subjective norms, and refusal self-efficacy following exposure to CMB (RQ4). It is expected that IG students will demonstrate higher knowledge of harms of vaping, reduced perceived positive outcomes of vaping, reduced subjective norms of vaping, and higher refusal self-efficacy after exposure to CMB.

The percentage of students who initiate vaping from baseline to 12-month follow-up will be calculated (overall and by gender). The difference between these two percentages will be calculated to identify whether a 10% lower vaping initiation rate was achieved in the IG relative to the CG. The proportion of students who never vape who are not susceptible to future vaping will be calculated in each group at baseline and 12-month follow-up (overall and by gender) to identify whether a higher proportion of students in the IG remain not susceptible to future vaping relative to the CG. The proportion of students who currently vape who do not report currently vaping at 12-month follow-up will also be calculated (overall and by gender) to identify whether a higher proportion of students in the IG stop vaping relative to the CG.

Next, multilevel regression models will test for intervention effects while accounting for clustering of students within schools. Multilevel models provide more precise parameter estimates, standard errors, confidence intervals, and significance tests because they account for clustering within the study design and data [63]. Separate multilevel regression models will test the hypothesis that after exposure to CMB, IG students will be less likely to initiate vaping (RQ1), less likely to be susceptible to vaping (RQ2), and less likely to currently vape (RQ3) compared to same-aged students in the CG. To answer RQ5, we will stratify Chi-square tests by gender to examine whether changes in vaping knowledge, positive and negative outcome expectancies and subjective norms about vaping, and resistance self-efficacy are different among gender subgroups. Regression models will include an interaction term for group by gender to explore whether the effectiveness of CMB differs according to gender. If the interaction term is significant, we will stratify regression models by gender to identify gender-specific effects. All regression models will account for clustering of students within schools and any baseline group differences identified previously. Analyses will use full information maximum likelihood estimation, which does not require complete data for each subject and provides robust estimates in the presence of missing data [64], however few students tend to be missing demographic data due to the placement of these questions at the beginning of the survey.

Sensitivity analyses

Some students who will complete the 3- and 12-month surveys may not be present for the CMB lessons which may bias the results towards a null effect. One question in the 3-month survey will ask students how many vaping lessons from the CATCH My Breath program they attended in the last 3 months (response options: 0, 1, 2). A sensitivity analysis will compare primary and secondary outcomes among students who report attending none and one lesson in the last 3 months to students who report attending two lessons in the last 3 months to investigate whether there are differences in outcomes based on program dose.

Data from the field notes survey which will be completed by curriculum presenters will help to identify instances where the CMB curriculum is not delivered as intended (e.g., content is omitted) which could impact program fidelity and study outcomes. A sensitivity analysis will compare primary and secondary outcomes between students that attend schools where there are significant diversions from curriculum delivery to students that attend schools where CMB is delivered as expected.

In 2023-24, the COMPASS study included 252 schools across five provinces (British Columbia = 9, Alberta = 7, Ontario = 66, Quebec = 134, and Prince Edward Island = 35). A sensitivity analysis will expand the CG to include (1) all other students from the matched sample of CG schools who were not matched to students in the IG and were not included in the original analyses (2), all other COMPASS schools and students from Ontario (not a matched sample), and (3) all other COMPASS schools and students from across Canada (not a matched sample), to investigate whether the results are consistent. The sensitivity analysis will follow a similar analytic process as described previously.

Missing data

Since students who use substances are less likely to be linked over time [53, 65], results may overestimate the impact of the intervention. Multiple imputation may be used to impute missing values on outcomes and demographic characteristics. Monotonic imputation or the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method may be used depending on the nature of the missing data [66]. Consistent with a previous study using COMPASS data [67], ten imputation datasets will be created, separate models will be run on each dataset, and final coefficient and variance estimates will be calculated. Multiple imputation results will be compared to the complete case analysis as a sensitivity analysis.

Discussion

This paper presents the protocol for a non-equivalent control group pretest-posttest quasi-experimental study evaluating the effectiveness of the Canadian high school version of the ‘CATCH My Breath’ (CMB) vaping prevention program at reducing vaping behaviours among a cohort of Ontario high school students. As noted previously, the prevalence of vaping has increased dramatically among youth in Canada [8, 18], and Canadian organizations have indicated a critical need to support schools in educating students about the risks of vaping [19–21]. However, there are few school-based prevention programs targeting vaping and fewer that have been evaluated [28, 29]. In fact, only one program has been shown to reduce vaping initiation among middle school students [35]. There is no evidence for effective prevention programs targeting high school students despite the fact that many students will initiate vaping during high school [37]. As a result, there is not enough evidence for schools to confidently implement programs that address vaping. This quasi-experimental study builds from a pilot study and will be the first to establish this much needed evidence in Canada. A plain-language summary of grouped survey data will be written and shared with PHUs, school boards, schools, and students. Results of this study may be presented in a recorded webinar for school, school board, and PHU staff, and will also be published as peer-reviewed research paper(s) and/or presented at research conference(s).

Strengths and limitations

One strength of this study is that it will obtain greater representation of the high school student population from across Ontario by targeting schools across 4 geographic regions. This process will assist in not only enhancing the generalizability of the findings but also improving the implementation of the CMB curriculum across the province. A second key strength is the use of PHU staff in delivering the lessons. Relative to school staff, PHU staff may be more knowledgeable on the subject matter due to the nature of their role and the associated experiences with vaping in their region. As a result, PHU staff may have greater knowledge and expertise to more confidently deliver the material and respond to student questions. Furthermore, the involvement of PHU staff may improve their connection with schools, which could facilitate future collaborations that would support youth health. A third strength of this study is the use of the data from the COMPASS study as a comparison group. These data contain a diverse sample from different areas of Ontario and questions that are comparable to those items found in this study’s survey, thus providing similar student data. Finally, the COMPASS study collects policy and program data that will be used to narrow the sample of CG schools to those that have not delivered any vaping-related programming.

One limitation of this study is the use of a quasi-experimental design. Due to the use of a non-equivalent control group pretest-posttest design, this study may be subjected to certain biases. For instance, selection bias may occur between the IG and CG because the participants were not assigned randomly. Schools that are selected into the IG may choose to participate because vaping is an issue among their students and they are interested in resources and programs to address vaping. Attempts have been made to reduce the effects of selection bias by recruiting schools from four regions across the province and through the use of a matched sample with COMPASS data. Another limitation involves the use of active consent which may also introduce selection bias. The requirement for active consent in many Ontario school boards and the subsequent lower student participation rate could negatively impact the study’s ability to achieve the projected sample for each school. Through a pilot study, the use of active consent resulted in 39% of eligible students having parental permission to complete the survey [28], which is consistent with other school-based research [68]. Active consent may bias the sample towards less risky students [69]. Offering an incentive to participating students and the matching process during analysis will be used to help offset this limitation. For schools with smaller populations, such as those in rural areas, it may be difficult to reach the sample size of 100 participating students at baseline. Delivering the curriculum in different class cohorts in the Fall and Winter semesters will help to increase the student sample size, particularly in smaller schools.

Implications for practice

Given the demand from Canadian high schools for vaping prevention programming, there is a need to examine whether CMB prevents vaping initiation among high school students. To our knowledge, there are no other evaluations of vaping prevention programs occurring in Canada. Thus, this research will offer timely, critical insights into the effectiveness of a vaping prevention program and has the potential to substantively inform school-based efforts.
