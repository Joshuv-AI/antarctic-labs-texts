A new high-pressure phase of n-butanol and its structural relationship with the low-temperature structure
Authors: Szymon Sobczak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe structural behavior and crystallization of n-butanol under low-temperature and high-pressure conditions have been systematically investigated by single-crystal X-ray diffraction. At ambient pressure and low temperature, n-butanol crystallizes in triclinic space group P1, denoted as phase α, with two crystallographically independent molecules per asymmetric unit (Z’ = 2). The structure of phase α is characterized by alternating polar and hydrophobic layers, stabilized by distinctive zig-zag hydrogen-bonded chains along [100], closely approximating twofold screw axis (21) symmetry. Under high-pressure, a new centrosymmetric phase β (space group P21/n, Z’ = 3) emerges. Phase β have similar aggregation motif stabilized by hydrogen-bonded zig-zag chains as one observed in phase α, but with oppositely oriented intermolecular vectors: (i) one chain relate the molecules by the 21 screw axis; and (ii) the other by glide-plane symmetry. Intriguingly, the recurrence of closely related structural motifs in both phases strongly suggests a common crystallization mechanism rooted in the formation of similar pre-associated molecular aggregates. These findings illuminate the sophisticated interplay between thermodynamic parameters and structure, advancing the understanding of linear monoalcohols aggregation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23729-x.Keywords: High-pressure, Butanol, Monoalcohols, Hydrogen bonds, Crystal engineering, CrystallizationSubject terms: Phase transitions and critical phenomena, Structure of solids and liquids, Structure elucidation, Carbohydrate chemistry

Introduction

n-Butanol (1-butanol, C4H9OH) is a linear, primary monoalcohol characterized by intriguing physicochemical properties that have secured its importance across a spectrum of industrial processes and fundamental scientific investigations1,2. This saturated C4 alcohol appears as a colorless liquid with a mild, characteristic sweet odor and exhibits pronounced amphiphilicity, which results in limited aqueous solubility while maintaining complete miscibility with numerous organic solvents1,3. This versatile amphiphilic nature underscores its extensive use in chemical synthesis, solvent applications, and as a precursor to functionalized derivatives, thereby substantiating its central role in numerous material and chemical industries1,4,5. Industrially, the large-scale synthesis of n-butanol is predominantly accomplished through petrochemical processes, specifically via the hydroformylation of propene, a route valued for its high efficiency and substantial yield1,2,6–8. Nonetheless, in the context of sustainable and renewable chemical production, biological pathways—namely the acetone-butanol-ethanol (ABE) fermentation employing anaerobic bacterial cultures (e.g. Clostridium species)—have garnered renewed interest despite historical economic limitations4,5,7,9. This revival stems from recent biotechnological advancements enabling improved process efficiency and increased commercial viability of biobutanol production4,7,9–11.

Beyond its classical utility as a solvent in surface coatings, paints, and adhesives, n-butanol serves as a crucial intermediate in synthesizing derivatives like butyl acetate and butyl acrylate, which are integral to the polymer and coatings sectors12,13. Its role extends to plastic manufacturing as a fundamental precursor in the synthesis of plasticizers, contributing significantly to polymer flexibility enhancement1,14. Further expanding its industrial relevance, n-butanol derivatives such as butylamines facilitate critical pathways toward agrochemicals and pharmaceutical agents. Significantly, recent attention has turned toward its application as a biofuel, driven by inherent advantages over ethanol, such as superior energy density, reduced hygroscopicity, lower volatility, and compatibility with conventional combustion engines and fuel infrastructures4,7,9–11,15–17. This profile not only facilitates seamless integration into existing fuel systems but also circumvents extensive infrastructural alterations, thereby positioning n-butanol as a viable sustainable fuel alternative. Despite its wide-ranging applications and industrial prominence, the solid-state characteristics of n-butanol have remained inadequately explored, with structural studies predominantly focused on low-temperature18–24. Previous studies, although limited, have recognized notable complexity of the crystallization and anisotropy within its low-temperature triclinic phase (space group P1), containing two symmetry-independent molecules per asymmetric unit (Z’ = 2)18,22.

Motivated by these prior findings, this work presents an extensive structural investigation into the crystallization behavior of n-butanol under both low-temperature and high-pressure conditions, employing single-crystal X-ray diffraction techniques complemented by theoretical calculations. The experimental findings confirm the existence of at least two polymorphs, α and β, characterized by Z’ values equal 2 and 3, respectively (Fig. 1), highlighting the complexity and adaptability of molecular aggregation patterns under varying thermodynamic constraints. Specifically, under low-temperature conditions, n-butanol crystallizes in a triclinic polymorph α with Z’ = 2, wherein molecules assemble into characteristic hydrogen-bonded chains interwoven with nonpolar alkyl domains. Intriguingly, under high pressure, n-butanol crystallizes in a distinct, monoclinic space group P21/n polymorph β with Z’ = 3. The differences in molecular arrangement between these two phases illuminate how subtle variations in molecular geometry and packing motifs arise from strain-induced deformation of intermolecular interactions, molecular conformation and spatial constraints.

Experimental methods

Single crystals of n-butanol suitable for low-temperature single-crystal measurements were obtained by temperature-induced crystallization of a drop of n-butanol confined within a glass capillary. The strong preference to form a supercooled liquid or glacial phase is a well-known characteristic of n-butanol, which, together with other larger linear alcohols H(CH2)nOH (n > 2), was long believed to preclude crystallization upon cooling or compression, invariably resulting instead in formation of amorphous phases23. Presently, to obtain a single crystal of n-butanol, a polycrystalline mass was first precipitated by cooling the capillary down to 120 K, followed by gradual heating to 170 K. A single crystal was then grown, by controlled temperature oscillations near the melting point, followed by slow cooling. The first structural experiment was conducted near to the butanol freezing point (Tc= 183.2 K)1 at 180 K, with additional measurements performed between 170 K and 100 K. The X-ray diffraction data for these experiments were processed similarly to the high-pressure measurements, with hydrogen atoms refined using the isotropic Uiso thermal parameter.

Single crystals suitable for the high-pressure single crystal experiments were grown in situ in a Merrill − Bassett diamond anvil cell (DAC)24, modified by mounting the anvils directly on steel backing plates with conical windows. A steel gasket with an initial thickness of 0.2 mm and a hole diameter of 0.45 mm was used to confine the sample. The DAC chamber was filled with n-butanol (Avantor) without any additional pressure-transmitting medium, and precipitation of the polycrystalline mass was induced isothermally by compressing the sample up to 1.30 GPa (Fig. 2a)25. Single-crystal of n-butanol at 1.25 GPa was obtained by gentle increasing and then gradually releasing the pressure until only one grain remained, as showed on Fig. 2b-d. Subsequent crystallizations at 1.35, 1.80 and 2.85 GPa were conducted under isochoric conditions, and single crystals suitable for X-ray diffraction experiments were obtained by the gentle heating and colling of the DAC25.

(a) Initial polycrystalline mass formed at 1.30 GPa; (b) gradual pressure release to 1.15 GPa results in the dissolution of excess grains; (c) growth of a well-formed single crystal upon recompression to 1.20 GPa; (d) n-butanol single crystal suitable for X-ray diffraction experiments. Note that the crystal volume in (d) is smaller than the DAC chamber volume, as the crystal remains in liquid–solid equilibrium just below the freezing pressure (pc = 1.24(2) GPa).

To further investigate the compressibility of n-butanol, additional measurements were conducted using a piston-cylinder press up to 1.7 GPa. The initial sample volume was 9.5 mL, and the molecular volume (Vm) for the liquid phase was calculated as the molar volume divided by Avogadro’s number. For the solid phase, Vm was determined as the unit cell volume divided by the number of formula units (Z)26. The critical pressure, pc​ =1.24(2) GPa, indicating the freezing point of n-butanol, was determined from the pronounced discontinuity in the pressure-dependent molecular volume evolution, indicating the transition from the liquid to the solid state (Fig. 3).

All of the X-ray diffraction data were collected using a single-crystal four-circle diffractometer equipped with a CCD detector and a MoKα X-ray source (λ = 0.71073 Å). The diffraction data were processed using the CrysAlisPro software suite (version 43.144a)27, and the crystal structure of n-butanol was solved using direct methods and refined with full-matrix least squares using SHELXS and SHELXL28,29, as implemented in Olex230. All hydrogen atoms were located based on molecular geometry and incorporated in the structural model using the isotropic Uiso thermal parameter. All structural drawings and estimations of intermolecular interaction energies, based on the UNI force field model, were performed using the Mercury version 4.0 software package31–33. The compressibility and thermal-expansion coefficients (Tables S4 and S5 in SI) were calculated with application of Principal Axis Strain Calculator (PASCal)34. Discussion

The properties and behavior of n-butanol at low temperature have been extensively investigated due to the intriguing nature of its liquid–glass transitions and the appearance of a ‘glacial’ state18,20–22. The ‘glacial’ state in n-butanol is a nonhomogeneous metastable phase that emerges during the isothermal transformation of the supercooled liquid. Such a transformation can be interpreted as an abortive crystallization process, resulting in the formation of highly strained microcrystallites, which emerge from the competition between different O–H⋯O associates characteristic of the liquid and glassy states and the uniform hydrogen bonds that prevail in the crystalline solid22.

To fully understand the implications of this complex crystallization pathway, it is essential to systematically investigate the nucleation processes and single-crystalline structures of n-butanol formed under both low-temperature and high-pressure conditions. At low temperatures, the crystallization process is primarily driven by a decrease in molecular kinetic energy, which reduces thermal motion and facilitates nucleation. Under these conditions, electrostatic attractions directional intermolecular interactions, such as hydrogen bonds, as well as van der Waals forces become increasingly significant, driving the formation of the most energetically favored molecular conformations. Conversely, crystallization under high-pressure conditions follows a distinct mechanism: increasing pressure systematically reduces volume and intermolecular distances, thereby decreasing the free volume available for thermal motion and enhancing packing efficiency35–37. As a result, crystals formed at high pressures are typically characterized high density and close molecular packing that their low-temperature forms35–49.

First report on the structure of n-butanol, based on powder X-ray diffraction data, was provided by Shmyt’ko et al. on data collected at 179 K and later refined by Derollez et al. using data collected at 120 K18,22. They suggested that at low temperatures, n-butanol crystallizes in the triclinic system, P1 space group, with two independent molecules (Z’ = 2)22. To confirm these observations and gain more information regarding the structure of n-butanol, a temperature-induced crystallizations experiments aimed at obtaining a single crystal were performed. Due to the complex behavior of n-butanol below its melting point at 183.2 K (cf. Experimental Methods)1, the first successful structural measurement was conducted at 170 K. Subsequently, the crystal was gradually cooled, and structural data were collected at 150 K, 120 K, and 100 K (Fig. 3). Next, the sample was gradually heated up and measured at 180 K, with an additional measurement done at 130 K.

The collected low-temperature single-crystal X-ray diffraction data confirm that n-butanol crystallizes in the P1 space group, hereafter denoted as phase α, consistent with the previous findings (Table 1). A detailed structure analysis of phase α reveals two crystallographically independent butanol molecules (labeled as A and B) located at the crystallographic plane (012). Molecules A and B are alternately bonded into polar hydrogen-bonded zig-zag chains extending along the [100] direction, closely approximating a twofold screw (21) symmetry broken by the different conformation and configuration of alkyl chains of adjacent molecules (Fig. 1a). Such a situation which leads to subtle but distinct deformations of O⋯O chain geometry preventing optimal close-packing alignment and consequently yielding in Z’>1 is a quite common among H(CH2)ₙOH (n > 2) and results from the competition between weak van der Waals interactions with significantly stronger directional H-bonds50,51. Additionally, the emergence of this pseudo-symmetrical interaction arrangement enable denser packing than would be achievable if all monomers would adopt identical conformations, as the combination of two independent molecules more frequently leads to formation of a less asymmetric aggregate52. Thus, at 180 K, just below the crystallization temperature, the OA⋯OB and OB⋯OA bonds are 2.788(2) and 2.801(1) Å and with O–H⋯O angles equal to 172.52(2)° and 171.15(2)°, respectively (cf. Tables S1 and S2 in SI). Quantitative interaction energy calculations indicate − 20.5 kJ/mol for OA⋯OB and − 20.2 kJ/mol for OB⋯OA bond.

The linear shape of n-butanol molecule and the presence of one functional group participating in formation of H-bonds drives the formation of parallel polar and nonpolar layers, regularly alternating in a clearly segregated fashion (cf. Fig. 1). The polar layers, include hydroxyl ‘head’ groups, whereas hydrophobic layers, comprising alkyl ‘tails’. Both layer types align parallelly to (001) plane leading to a strong anisotropy of phase α. Indeed, the least temperature-affected are unit-cell parameters a and b while the changes of parameter c, that corresponds mainly to the CH⋅⋅⋅HC interactions, are more pronounced. A detailed analysis of two labile torsion angles, τ₁ (O1–C4–C3–C2) and τ₂ (C4–C3–C2–C1), reveals deviations from the ideal gauche–gauche conformations typically expected for linear monoalcohols (Fig. 4). Although the τ₁ angle remains close to 180°, constrained primarily by the directional requirements of hydrogen bond formation, τ₂ deviates significantly, reflecting conformational adjustments necessary to balance steric hindrance and weak van der Waals interactions within the nonpolar regions. This effect indicates a significant conformational frustration required to optimize both intermolecular interactions and packing efficiency under low-temperature conditions.

Comparison of the molecular conformations observed in (a) phase α and (b) phase β of n-butanol.

Upon compression at room temperature, liquid n-butanol undergoes continuous densification up to 1.24(2) GPa, where a significant molecular volume reduction, of about 6 Å3 per molecule, indicate crystallization (Fig. 3). This significant volume reduction (by about − 13%) directly corresponds to the close molecular packing achieved under high-pressure conditions. The molecular volume in phase β at 1.24 GPa is Vm = 110.5 Å3, while at 180 K each molecule of butanol occupies by about 10% more volume (Vm = 121.87 Å3). Remarkably, isochoric and subsequent isothermal recrystallization experiments at high-pressure yielded another polymorph of n-butanol.

This high-pressure form crystallizes in the monoclinic system space group P21/n, maintaining a layered aggregation pattern closely analogous to one observed in phase α (Fig. 1). As anticipated, high-pressure phase β is circa sixfold less compressible than the liquid n-butanol implying its minimal void space and optimal close-packing arrangement (Fig. 3). The molecular layers in phase β align parallel to the crystallographic plane (101), with the H-bonded chains propagating along direction [010]. A particularly intriguing aspect of phase β is the crystallographic complexity arising from three symmetry-independent molecules per asymmetric unit (Z′ = 3). This indicates additional conformational frustration on the alkyl chain resulted from much more pronounced conformational adjustments than those observed for phase α (cf. Fig. 4). Interestingly, while the literature typically reports a preservation or even a reduction of the Z′ number under high-pressure conditions50,53—as observed, for example, in ethanol, in the high-pressure structure (space group P21/c, Z′ = 1) retains the same number of independent molecules as the low-temperature form (space group Pc, Z′ = 1)23, or in isopropanol, where the high-pressure form have four independent molecules (Z′ = 4) while the low-temperature structure has only three independent molecules (Z′ = 3)54, despite both adopting the same monoclinic space group P21/c — notable exceptions to this general trend do exist. A particularly interesting example is methanol, which below 175.37 K crystallizes in phase β (Cmc21 Z′ = 0.5) and subsequently in phase α (space group P212121, Z′ = 1) below 159 K; yet, its high-pressure polymorphs γ and ε, crystallizing in space group P-1 and P21/c respectively, both features three independent molecules55–57.

In n-butanol phase β, three molecules (A, B, and C) establish two distinct types of hydrogen-bonded chains: (i) a chain composed of alternating A and C molecules, and (ii) a second independent chain composed exclusively of molecules B. Such symmetry relation between hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor, including a 21 screw or glide plane relation, are among two major aggregation types typically found in not bulky hydroxyl-containing molecules characterized by an ability to form a strong, dominating directional interaction between equally good hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors51. All O—H⋅⋅⋅O chains in phase β propagate along this same crystallographic direction [010] however the A⋅⋅⋅C⋅⋅⋅A and B⋅⋅⋅B have opposite sense (Fig. 5). In the A⋅⋅⋅C chain, where the molecules are related by the glide-plane symmetry, the subtle yet measurable O⋯O bond asymmetries are noticeable (OA⋯OC = 2.757(2) Å while OC⋯OA = 2.755(2) Å), increasing to 2.71(1) Å and 2.69(1) Å at 2.85 GPa, respectively. On the other hand, the H-bonds between molecules B related through a 2₁ screw axis are significantly shorter and OB⋯OB is 2.735(2) Å (cf. Tables S3-4 in SI). Also the inclination angle between the alkyl parts of the alcohol molecules aggregated in the H-bonded chains strongly distinguishes the A⋅⋅⋅C⋅⋅⋅A and B⋅⋅⋅B chains (Fig. 5). In B⋅⋅⋅B chain molecules are aligned parallelly, with inclination angle smaller than 0.1°, while for the A⋅⋅⋅C⋅⋅⋅A molecules are inclined at 5.77°. A conformational analysis of butanol molecules present in phase β reveals substantial conformational variations across the three symmetry-independent molecules (A, B, and C), highlighting the adaptability of week van der Waals interactions under compression (cf. Fig. 4). The τ₁ angle in all three independent molecules assumes values below 177.0(1)° and experiences the most significant reduction at high pressure, reflecting a strong conformational adjustment toward the robust hydrogen-bond chains.

The interaction-energy analyses of the intramolecular contacts in phase β reveals only marginal differences in the strength of individual hydrogen bonds. Specifically, the interactions between hydroxyl groups OB—H⋯OB and OC—H⋯OA are comparable, with interaction energies of − 20.404 kJ/mol and − 20.390 kJ/mol, respectively. In contrast, the OA—H⋯OC hydrogen bond exhibits a slightly lower energy of − 20.247 kJ/mol, differing by only 0.16 kJ/mol from the OB—H⋯OB. Interestingly, the compression to 2.85 GPa leads to the most pronounced compression of OB⋯OB distance, by approximately 2.2%, whereas both OA⋯OC and OC⋯OA distances contract by about 1.7%.

The experimental observations reported by Hédoux et al. on the vitrification of liquid n-butanol at low temperature provide a compelling framework for discussing the crystallization pathways leading to its two distinct but closely related solid-state structures (Fig. 5)58. The spectroscopic investigations of n-butanol on decreasing temperature reveal the presence of two distinct OH stretching bands corresponding to two types of intermolecular O–H⋯O hydrogen associates present in the liquid and glassy states58. The lower-wavenumber band, corresponding to the stronger H-bonding interaction, becomes dominant in supercooled or amorphous n-butanol below the melting point, while the higher-wavenumber band, attributed to weaker H-bonds, predominates in the liquid state22. Importantly, during crystallization, only the weaker H-bond persist in the crystal structure, suggesting that the strong hydrogen-bonding interactions prevalent in the undercooled liquid are structurally incompatible with the periodic long-range order of the low-temperature crystalline phase α. Moreover, these same studies contrast the growth of the sharp 012 reflection with the slowly emerging, broadened 001 reflection at the early crystallization stages of phase α22,58. This observation indicates that the n-butanol crystal preferentially grows through the stacking of molecular (012) layers, rather than propagating along the [001] direction, which is hindered by frustrated hydrogen-bonding interactions. This is particularly notable, as such layered aggregation is characteristic of both phases α and β (Fig. 5a). As discussed by Kuleshova et al.59,60, appearance of similar aggregation motifs across different hydroxy-containing compounds that has Z′>1 may result from crystallization involving similar pre-associated aggregates, which subsequently undergo conformational adjustments to achieve more efficient packing at specific conditions. In this sense, the presence of similar packing arrangement in phases α and β, combined with the fact that the strongest H-bonds observed in the supercooled liquid are absent in the α phase while in phase β two energetically distinct H-bonded chains are present offers a plausible link between the crystallization at low temperature and high-pressure from the similar pre-assembled aggregates present in the liquid state.

In this context, the emergence of centrosymmetric phase β (P21/n) under high-pressure instead of crystallizing as polar P1 phase α can be interpreted as a structural adaptation to spatial confinement—satisfying both hydrogen bonding and steric constraints, while simultaneously enhancing packing efficiency. This preference agrees with the principles of crystal packing developed by Brock and Dunitz61. According to their observations unoccupied special positions—such as mirror planes, rotation axes, and centers of inversion—create “forbidden space” within the crystal lattice, representing regions that cannot be effectively utilized for molecular packing. Among these, unoccupied mirror planes generate the most extensive forbidden zones, followed by rotation axes, whereas unoccupied centers of inversion result in the least wasted space. Consequently, the aggregation of molecules A, B, and C in phase β—particularly the arrangement of molecules B along the 21 screw axis and the association of molecules A and C through glide-plane symmetry—reflects a pursuit for minimizing spatial inefficiency. Furthermore, the presence of inversion symmetry allows for a change of the direction, but not the orientation, of intermolecular vectors61, thereby preserving the general shape of the van der Waals surface, enhancing the potential for efficient packing arrangements. Therefore, the emergence of phase β with Z′ = 3 under high-pressure conditions can be understood as a crystallographically and thermodynamically driven response to the highly strained, contracted environment. These observations reinforce the broader statistical conclusions, that centrosymmetric space groups dominate among organic molecular crystals50,52,59,60–63.

Conclusions

The comprehensive structural analysis of n-butanol reveals two distinct structures, highlighting the adaptability of molecular aggregation in response to the external stimuli. At ambient pressure and low-temperature, n-butanol crystallizes in the triclinic α phase (space group P1), characterized by layered aggregation stabilized by polar O–H⋯O hydrogen bonds. The H-bonded chains in phase α closely approximate 21 axis symmetry, as their conformation results from the interplay between steric constraints and the intrinsic preference for forming strong, directional interactions leading to an increase of the Z′ number. Under high-pressure, a denser polymorph β (space group P21/n) crystallize with three independent molecules per asymmetric unit (Z′ = 3). This structure features two distinct hydrogen-bonded chains—one relating two independent molecules via glide-plane symmetry, and another aggregated through the 21 screw axis. These H-bonded patterns present in phase β has this same orientation but they are propagating in the opposite directions. The appearance of inversion centers allows to reduce repulsive like–like interactions and thus allow for much closer, efficient packing51,64. The difference in Z′ between low-temperature and high-pressure structure cannot be readily attributed to differences in interaction strengths or molecular geometry but could be explained by development of similar molecular associations in cooled and compressed liquid59. The presence of closely related structural motifs in phases α and β, may result from similar crystallization mechanism involving pre-associated aggregates, which subsequently undergo structural adjustments in response to the surrounding conditions.

Taken together, the α and β phases exemplify how polymorph formation reflects not only the intrinsic molecular potential but also the crystallization pathway, particularly the nucleation and growth under varying thermodynamic regimes. The emergence of Z’ > 1 in both phases of n-butanol appears to result more from crystal growth than from different interaction energies. These results underscore the role of crystallization conditions in stabilizing structures with an unexpected symmetry or/and high Z’ by modulating nucleation barriers and accommodating conformational strain. As such, they provide compelling empirical evidence for a nuanced understanding of the interplay among thermodynamics and packing efficiency in structures of monoalcohols.
