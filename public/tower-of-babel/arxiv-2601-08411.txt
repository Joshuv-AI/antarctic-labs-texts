Particle Filtering for a Class of State-Space Models with Low and Degenerate Observational Noise


Abstract
We consider the discrete-time filtering problem in scenarios where the observation noise is degenerate or low. We focus on the case where the observation equation is a linear function of the state and that additive noise is low or degenerate, however, we place minimal assumptions on the hidden state process. In this scenario we derive new particle filtering (PF) algorithms and, under assumptions, in such a way that as the noise becomes more degenerate a PF which approximates the low noise filtering problem provably inherits the properties of the PF used in the degenerate case. We extend our framework to the case where the hidden states are drawn from a diffusion process. In this scenario we develop new PFs which are robust to both low noise and fine levels of time discretization. We illustrate our algorithms numerically on several examples. Key Words : Filtering, particle filters, low and degenerate noise, Diffusions, time discretization.

Introduction

The discrete-time filtering problem considers an unobserved Markov chain
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
(the signal or hidden chain) and associated data

\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
. The data at any time
k
are assumed to depend on
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
only by the position
X_{k}
of the hidden state. The objective of filtering, assuming it is well-defined is to compute expectations with respect to (w.r.t.) the conditional distribution of the signal at each time
n
, given all the data observed up-to that time; we call such a distribution the filter.
This problem has numerous applications in statistics, applied mathematics, finance and beyond; we refer the reader to the texts
[
2
,
8
,
9
]
and the references therein for a more complete summary.

In most cases of practical interest, the filter is unavailable analytically and one must resort to numerical approximation of which there are plethora of methods; see
[
2
,
8
,
9
]
for some coverage of the subject. In this article we focus on the approach called particle filters (PF). These are Monte Carlo, simulation-based methods that simulate a collection of
N\in\mathbb{N}
samples in parallel and undergo what are called sampling and resampling operations. Such methods are very well understood from a mathematical perspective (e.g.
[
9
]
) and provide almost surely exact approximations of the filter as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Typically they are effective when the dimension of the hidden state,
d_{x}
say, is moderate, although there are several attempts to enhance the methodology to some special-case high-dimensional problems (e.g.
[
14
,
15
]
).

In this article we focus on the case where the observations have low or degenerate noise. For instance one could consider the case:

Y_{n}=A_{n}X_{n}+\Delta^{1/2}\epsilon_{n}

(1.1)

where
(Y_{n},X_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
,
\Delta\geq 0
,

\{\epsilon_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
a sequence of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}-
valued independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) noises
and for technical reasons
1\leq d_{y}<d_{x}
, and
A_{n}
a sequence of
d_{y}\times d_{x}
real matrices of full row rank.
The low noise case could be interpreted as
\Delta
small (e.g.
\Delta=10^{-6}
) and the degenerate case with
\Delta=0
. As was noticed by
[
21
]
, this scenario is
important in many applications where it is expected that the signal dynamics is strongly driving the observed data; see
[
3
,
12
]
for example. From a mathematical perspective, in the case
\Delta=0
one has to define the filter on a sequence of manifolds
\mathsf{M}_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}:y_{n}-A_{n}x=0\}
and indeed this has been explored, for instance, in
[
21
]
. In the context of fitting a single model for example the smoothing distribution (that is of
X_{0},\dots,X_{n}|y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
with
n
fixed) sampling on manifolds has been quite well investigated using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) in several interesting contexts; see for example

[
5
,
7
,
10
,
11
,
19
]
. The extension to the filtering problem seems only to be considered in
[
20
,
21
]
, although
[
20
]
do not consider a manifold that has been defined by the data such as
\mathsf{M}_{n}
. The approach of
[
21
]
is to leverage sequential MCMC and as such relies on the afore-mentioned MCMC literature. The methodology there is particularly useful in high-dimensions (
d_{x}
large) but can be computationally expensive for moderate dimensional problems that the PF itself is well-suited towards and it is this latter direction that we follow in this paper.

The application of PFs for low noise problems seldom works well: it is well-known and illustrated later in this article. In the worst case scenario the results can be very numerically unstable. The main idea of this article is to consider, even in the low noise case, sampling on a sequence of manifolds. For instance, in the context of (
1.1
)

\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{n}=\{(x,\epsilon)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}:y_{n}-A_{n}x-\Delta^{1/2}\epsilon=0\}
. The idea then is if one can define a PF on
\mathsf{M}_{n}
then the PF on
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{n}
will ultimately inherit the properties of the PF in the degenerate case (
\Delta=0
). In this paper we explore exactly this issue in the presence of linear observation models such as
(
1.1
) and our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We derive new PFs for the low noise and degenerate case for linear observation models. We place minimal assumptions on the hidden process
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
.

•

In the scenario of the previous bullet and under assumptions, we prove that the
\mathbb{L}_{r}-
error of the PF in the low noise case will converge as
\Delta\downarrow 0
to the
\mathbb{L}_{r}-
error of the PF in the degenerate noise case.

•

For additive Gaussian errors in the hidden chain and observations we derive the optimal proposal and prove that the weights
of the PF in the low noise case will converge as
\Delta\downarrow 0
to the weights of the PF in the degenerate noise case.

•

In the case where the hidden dynamics follow a diffusion process, we derive a new PF that is robust not only to low noise but also to fine time discretization error. This appears to be the first PF of its kind.

The significance of this work is not only for filtering. The new PFs in this work could be used for parameter estimation
associated to diffusion models, such as was done in
[
1
,
13
]
using the multilevel Monte Carlo method. We note in terms of the fourth contribution,
[
6
]
develop a methodology for the case of hypoelliptic diffusions and degenerate noise (with linear constraints), stating that PFs can be used in their context. However, they do not make the point that this enables the low noise case, which is one of the main messages of this work.

This article is structured as follows. In Section
2
we present the collection of models we will consider.
In Section

In Section
4
we show how to extend our methodology to the case where the hidden dynamics follow a diffusion process.
In Section
5

A

2
Modeling

2.1
State-Space Model

We present a large class of state-space models in the following section. The main idea is to relate what we call the
noise parameterization and degenerate noise case in full generality. As we stated in the introduction, however, we are only able to present computational methods for the case of linear observation regimes.

We consider the following state-space model (SSM) for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
given:

\displaystyle Y_{n}

\displaystyle=H_{n}(X_{n},\epsilon_{n})

\displaystyle X_{n}

\displaystyle=F_{n}(X_{n-1},\nu_{n})

where
(Y_{n},X_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}+d_{x}}
,
(d_{x},d_{y})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
,
(\epsilon_{n},\nu_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}+d_{x}}
are a sequence of independent noises (independent across
n
and of each other) with positive and smooth Lebesgue density,
H_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}+d_{y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
,
F_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{2d_{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
are a sequence of continuously differentiable functions and whose Jacobian is of full row rank.

2.2
Natural Parametrization

In the given scenario we can define the filtering densities w.r.t.
d_{x}-
dimensional Lebesgue measure as follows.
Denoting the positive conditional Lebesgue densities of
Y_{n}|X_{n}
and
X_{n}|X_{n-1}
as
h_{n}(y_{n}|x_{n})
and
f_{n}(x_{n}|x_{n-1})
respectively we have that the filter (see e.g.
[
2
]
and the references therein) at time 1 is

\pi_{1}(x_{1})=\frac{h_{1}(y_{1}|x_{1})f_{1}(x_{1}|x_{0})}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}h_{1}(y_{1}|x_{1})f_{1}(x_{1}|x_{0})\lambda_{d_{x}}(dx_{1})}

where
\lambda_{d_{x}}
is
d_{x}-
dimensional Lebesgue measure and we supress the dependence of
y_{1}
in the filter.
The filter at any subsequent time point
n\geq 1
has the following prediction-updating formula:

\pi_{n}(x_{n})=\frac{h_{n}(y_{n}|x_{n})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}f_{n}(x_{n}|x_{n-1})\pi_{n-1}(x_{n-1})\lambda_{d_{x}}(dx_{n-1})}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}h_{n}(y_{n}|x_{1})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}f_{n}(x_{n}|x_{n-1})\pi_{n-1}(x_{n-1})\lambda_{d_{x}}(dx_{n-1})\lambda_{d_{x}}(dx_{n})}.

In practice one cannot compute the filter and resorts to numerical methods. Consider when
d_{y}=1
and
\epsilon_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})

with
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})
the one-dimensional normal distribution of mean zero and variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}
very small
say
10^{-6}
we call this a special case of low observational noise. In such cases it can be difficult to design an efficient
numerical methods and to help deal with such a scenario we will consider a SSM under a noise-based parameterization which we now detail.

2.3
Noise Parametrization and Degenerate Noise

Define the manifold
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{n}=\{(x_{n},\epsilon_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}:y_{n}-H_{n}(x_{n},\epsilon_{n})=0\}
. We set
c_{n}(x_{n},\epsilon_{n})=y_{n}-H_{n}(x_{n},\epsilon_{n})
.
We shall suppose that
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
is equipped with a metric tensor with a fixed positive definite matrix representation
M
. Then it is simple to show that the filter density
of
x_{n},\epsilon_{n}
w.r.t. the Riemannian measure on
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{n}
denote
\mu^{M}_{\mathsf{M}_{n}}

is as follows (see e.g.
[
21
]
). We set

\widetilde{G}_{n}(x_{n},\epsilon_{n})=\textrm{det}(\partial c_{n}(x_{n},\epsilon_{n})M^{-1}\partial c_{n}(x_{n},\epsilon_{n})^{\top})^{-1/2}

where
\partial c_{n}
is the Jacobian matrix and
det
is the determinant.
For time 1 we have

\widetilde{\pi}_{1}(x_{1},\epsilon_{1})=\frac{\widetilde{G}_{1}(x_{1},\epsilon_{1})f(x_{1}|x_{0})p_{1}(\epsilon_{1})}{\int_{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{1}}\widetilde{G}_{1}(x_{1},\epsilon_{1})f(x_{1}|x_{0})p_{1}(\epsilon_{1})\mu^{M}_{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{1}}(d(x_{n},\epsilon_{n}))}.

and at any subsequent time
n\geq 2
:

\widetilde{\pi}_{n}(x_{n},\epsilon_{n})=\frac{\widetilde{G}_{n}(x_{n},\epsilon_{n})p_{n}(\epsilon_{n})\int_{\mathsf{M}_{n-1}}f(x_{n}|x_{n-1})\widetilde{\pi}_{n}(x_{n-1},\epsilon_{n-1})\mu^{M}_{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{n-1}}(d(x_{n-1},\epsilon_{n-1}))}{\int_{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{n}}\widetilde{G}_{n}(x_{n},\epsilon_{n})p(\epsilon_{n})\int_{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{n-1}}f(x_{n}|x_{n-1})\widetilde{\pi}_{n}(x_{n-1},\epsilon_{n-1})\mu^{M}_{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{n-1}}(d(x_{n-1},\epsilon_{n-1}))\mu^{M}_{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{n}}(d(x_{n},\epsilon_{n}))}.

Here
p_{n}(\epsilon_{n})
is the Lebesgue density of
\epsilon_{n}
.
We remark that one can work with the noise parameters of the hidden state as well. However, as we focus on observational noise, this representation will suffice. As we will establish in the next Section when the noise in the observation equation is low, one can consider numerical algorithms under the noise representation and ultimately, as the ‘noise disappears’ recover an algorithm associated to the degenerate noise filtering problem, the latter of which we now explain

2.3.1
Degenerate Noise

The approach here is easily adapted to the case of degenerate observation noise as we now explain. Writing the SSM as

\displaystyle Y_{n}

\displaystyle=H_{n}^{\star}(X_{n})

\displaystyle X_{n}

\displaystyle=F_{n}(X_{n-1},\nu_{n})

where
(Y_{n},X_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}+d_{x}}
,
(d_{x},d_{y})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
,
d_{y}<d_{x}
,
\nu_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
a sequence of independent noises (independent across
n
) with positive and smooth Lebesgue density,
H_{n}^{\star}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
,
F_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{2d_{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
are a sequence of continuously differentiable functions and whose Jacobian is of full row rank. Note that we require
d_{y}<d_{x}
as we aim to work with an embedded manifold.

As above, define the manifold
\mathsf{M}_{n}^{\star}=\{x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}:y_{n}-H_{n}^{\star}(x_{n})=0\}
. We set
c_{n}(x_{n})=y_{n}-H_{n}^{\star}(x_{n})
.
We shall suppose that
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
is equipped with a metric tensor with a fixed positive definite matrix representation
M
. We set

G_{n}(x_{n})=\textrm{det}(\partial c_{n}(x_{n})M^{-1}\partial c_{n}(x_{n})^{\top})^{-1/2}.

As before the filter densities w.r.t.
\mu_{M}^{\mathsf{M}_{n}^{\star}}
can be written down as

\pi_{1}^{\star}(x_{1})=\frac{G_{1}(x_{1})f_{1}(x_{1}|x_{0})}{\int_{\mathsf{M}_{1}^{\star}}G_{1}(x_{1})f_{1}(x_{1}|x_{0})\mu^{M}_{\mathsf{M}_{1}^{\star}}(dx_{n})}.

and at any subsequent time
n\geq 2
:

\pi_{n}^{\star}(x_{n})=\frac{G_{n}(x_{n})\int_{\mathsf{M}_{n-1}^{\star}}f_{n}(x_{n}|x_{n-1})\pi_{n-1}^{\star}(x_{n-1})\mu^{M}_{\mathsf{M}_{n-1}^{\star}}(dx_{n-1})}{\int_{\mathsf{M}_{n}^{\star}}\tilde{G}_{n}(x_{n})\int_{\mathsf{M}_{n-1}^{\star}}f_{n}(x_{n}|x_{n-1})\pi_{n-1}^{\star}(x_{n-1})\mu^{M}_{\mathsf{M}_{n-1}^{\star}}(dx_{n-1})\mu^{M}_{\mathsf{M}_{n}^{\star}}(dx_{n})}.

3
Particular Cases

In this Section we consider the case of general additive noise (Section
3.1
) and derive PFs in the low and degenerate noise cases. We then examine (Section
3.2
) the case where the hidden process has additive Gaussian noise along with the observation process as well and derive the optimal proposals for the low and degenerate noise models.

3.1
General Additive Noise

We consider the state-space model for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
given:

Y_{n}=A_{n}X_{n}+\Delta^{1/2}\epsilon_{n}\\

where,
Y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
,
1\leq d_{y}<d_{x}
,
A_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}\times d_{x}}
is of full row rank,
\epsilon_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
are a sequence of independent noises with positive Lebesgue density
p_{n}

and
\Delta\geq 0
. For the hidden dynamics, we do not make any constraints beyond the existence of a positive Lebesgue transition density which can be evaluated pointwise; it is written as
f_{n}(x_{n}|x_{n-1})
as before.
In order to write down a PF we will write the smoother (in the noise parameterization) w.r.t. Lebesgue measure as, due to the linear constraint, this is simple to do.

Remark 3.1
.

In this article we present noise degeneracy as the case that in the observation equation, as
\Delta\downarrow 0
there is no noise at all. However, one could consider, say
Y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and an observation equation of the type

Y_{n}=A_{n}X_{n}+\mathtt{P}(\Delta)\epsilon_{n}

where we define

\mathtt{P}(\Delta)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
1&1&0\\
\Delta&\Delta&\Delta\end{bmatrix}.

In this case one could send
\Delta\downarrow 0
which corresponds to removing the observation noise in the third observation component. This type of scenario could be dealt with in the forthcoming commentary, in the sense that one would define a filter (noise parameterization) and a corresponding algorithm in the low noise case and the case
\Delta=0
and that there is some convergence of the low-noise case to this limiting and degenerate case. The main changes would be mainly notational and hence, for ease of exposition, we keep the simple scenario that we have started with.

3.1.1
Low Noise Case

We begin in the low noise case. Let
x_{n}^{\star}
solve
y_{n}=A_{n}x
,
\text{ker}(A_{n})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}:A_{n}x=0\}
and
V_{n}
is the
d_{x}\times(d_{x}-d_{y})
matrix whose columns are an orthonormal basis of

\text{ker}(A_{n})
. In this case we will be filtering a random variable
\widetilde{Z}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and as it will prove useful below, we set
\widetilde{z}_{n}^{\top}=(z_{n}^{\top},\overline{z}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
, with
(z_{n},\overline{z}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}-d_{y}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
. Let
A_{n}^{\Delta}:=[A_{n},\Delta^{1/2}\mathtt{I}_{d_{y}}]
, with
\mathtt{I}_{d_{y}}
the
d_{y}\times d_{y}
identity matrix,
be a
d_{y}\times(d_{x}+d_{y})
matrix, define
\text{ker}(A_{n}^{\Delta}):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}+d_{y}}:A_{n}^{\Delta}x=0\}
, let
V_{n}^{\Delta}
be a
(d_{x}+d_{y})\times d_{x}

matrix whose columns are an
orthonormal basis of
\text{ker}(A_{n}^{\Delta})
and
(\widetilde{x}_{n}^{\Delta})^{\top}=((x_{n}^{\star})^{\top},0^{\top})
be a
(d_{x}+d_{y})-
dimensional vector that solves
y_{n}=A_{n}^{\Delta}\widetilde{x}_{n}^{\Delta}
.
For any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
set

u_{n}^{\Delta}(z)=\widetilde{x}_{n}^{\Delta}+V_{n}^{\Delta}z

with
u_{n}^{\Delta}(z)^{\top}=(u_{n}^{\Delta}(z,x)^{\top},u_{n}^{\Delta}(z,\epsilon)^{\top})
,

u_{n}^{\Delta}(z,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
,
u_{n}^{\Delta}(z,\epsilon)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}

and we note that
y_{n}=A_{n}^{\Delta}u_{n}^{\Delta}(z)
.

Given this construction, at any time
n\geq 1
we can write the joint smoothing density of
\widetilde{z}_{1:n}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd_{x}}
w.r.t.
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{d_{x}}(d\widetilde{z}_{i})
as

\pi_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{1:n})\propto\prod_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}(u_{k}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{k},\epsilon))f_{k}(u_{k}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{k},x)|u_{k-1}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{k-1},x))

where we set
u_{0}^{\Delta}(z,x)=x_{0}
, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
. We are assuming the probability density is well-defined for any
\Delta>0
, that is, the normalization is finite.

In such a scenario, one can use a standard particle filter, which we now detail. Let
\widetilde{q}_{n}^{\Delta}
be a sequence of positive Markov transition densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and set for any
(z^{\prime},z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d_{x}}

w_{n}^{\Delta}(z^{\prime},z)=\frac{p_{n}(u_{n}^{\Delta}(z,\epsilon))f_{n}(u_{n}^{\Delta}(z,x)|u_{n-1}^{\Delta}(z^{\prime},x))}{\widetilde{q}_{n}^{\Delta}(z|z^{\prime})}.

Note that
\widetilde{q}_{n}^{\Delta}
can depend on
\Delta
and when
n=1
we may (or may not) condition on
x_{0}

in
\widetilde{q}_{n}^{\Delta}
but write
\widetilde{q}_{n}^{\Delta}(\cdot|x_{0})
from herein.
Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be given and denote for any
n\geq 1
,
\widetilde{z}_{n}^{1:N}=(\widetilde{z}_{n}^{1}\dots,\widetilde{z}_{n}^{N})
which represent the particles of the PF. It will prove useful to use the ancestor representation of the resampling indices so we will also be interested in
(\widetilde{a}_{n}^{1},\dots,\widetilde{a}_{n}^{N})\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{N}
and we use the convention that
\widetilde{a}_{0}^{i}=i
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. We will also write
\widetilde{z}_{0}^{\widetilde{a}_{0}^{j}}=x_{0}
. The PF is then the following.

•

Initialize: For
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, sample
\widetilde{Z}_{1}^{i}|x_{0}
i.i.d. from
\widetilde{q}_{1}^{\Delta}(\cdot|x_{0})
. Set
n=2
.

•

Iterate: For
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, sample
\widetilde{a}_{n-1}^{i}|\widetilde{z}_{n-2}^{1:N},\widetilde{z}_{n-1}^{1:N}
i.i.d. from

\frac{w_{n-1}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n-2}^{\widetilde{a}_{n-2}^{i}},\widetilde{z}_{n-1}^{\widetilde{a}_{n-1}^{i}})}{\sum_{s=1}^{N}w_{n-1}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n-2}^{\widetilde{a}_{n-2}^{s}},\widetilde{z}_{n-1}^{s})}.

For
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, sample
\widetilde{Z}_{n}^{i}|\widetilde{z}_{n-1}^{\widetilde{a}_{n-1}^{i}}
independently from
\widetilde{q}_{n}^{\Delta}(\cdot|\widetilde{z}_{n-1}^{\widetilde{a}_{n-1}^{i}})
.
Set
n=n+1
and return to the start of the bullet point.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a bounded and measurable function (write such functions as
\mathtt{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}})
) then we can estimate:

\pi_{n}^{\Delta}(\varphi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{nd_{x}}}\varphi(\widetilde{z}_{n})\pi_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{1:n})\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{d_{x}}(d\widetilde{z}_{i})

by using the estimator, obtained just before the end of the iterate step

\pi_{n}^{\Delta,N}(\varphi):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi(\widetilde{z}_{n}^{i})w_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n-1}^{\widetilde{a}_{n-1}^{i}},\widetilde{z}_{n}^{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n-1}^{\widetilde{a}_{n-1}^{i}},\widetilde{z}_{n}^{i})}.

The convergence of this estimator and the choice of proposal has been extensively discussed in the literature; see for instance
[
2
,
9
]
and the references therein. We remark that the optimal, in terms of minimizing the variance of the weights conditional on
\widetilde{z}_{n-1}
, is

\widetilde{q}_{n}^{\Delta,\text{opt}}(\widetilde{z}_{n}|\widetilde{z}_{n-1})=\frac{p_{n}(u_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n},\epsilon))f_{n}(u_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n},x)|u_{n-1}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n-1},x))}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}p_{n}(u_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n},\epsilon))f_{n}(u_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n},x)|u_{n-1}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n-1},x))\lambda_{d_{x}}(d\widetilde{z}_{n})}.

This can only be used in certain scenarios, one of which we investigate below.

3.1.2
Degenerate Noise Case

The degenerate case can be handled in a similar manner, which we now describe.
For any
(n,z)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}-d_{y}}
set

u_{n}^{\star}(z)=x_{n}^{\star}+V_{n}z

where
V_{n}
is the
d_{x}\times(d_{x}-d_{y})
matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis of

\text{ker}(A_{n})=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{d_{x}}:A_{n}x=0\}
and
with the convention that
u_{0}^{\star}(z)=x_{0}
for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}-d_{y}}
.
At any time
n\geq 1
we can write the joint smoothing density of
z_{1:n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(d_{x}-d_{y})}
w.r.t.
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{d_{x}-d_{y}}(dz_{i})
as

\pi_{n}^{\star}(z_{1:n})\propto\prod_{k=1}^{n}f_{k}(u_{k}^{\star}(z_{k})|u_{k-1}^{\star}(z_{k-1}))

where we note that
u_{0}^{\star}(z_{0})=x_{0}
. Let
q_{n}^{\star}
be a sequence of positive Markov transition densities on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}-d_{y}}

3.1.3
Mathematical Result

We now consider the convergence, in a sense to be made precise below, of the PF in the low noise case to that of the degenerate noise case.
Recall that we use the notation for

, with
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}

We make the following assumption.

(
A1
)

For any

we have

\lim_{\Delta\downarrow 0}\widetilde{q}_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}|\widetilde{z}^{\prime})=p_{n}(\overline{z})q_{n}^{\star}(z|z^{\prime}).

This assumption is not overly restrictive as one chooses the proposal densities. The assumption means that as the noise disappears, one should propose the
d_{x}-d_{y}
random variable, just as in the in the degenerate noise case and the noise variable according to the noise density.

We denote by
\mathbb{E}^{\Delta}
as expectations w.r.t. the law of the PF in the low noise case and
by
\mathbb{E}^{\star}
as expectations w.r.t. the law of the PF in the degenerate noise case. We can now state our main result whose proof is in Appendix

.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume (A
(A1)
). Then for any
(n,r,\varphi)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\times\mathtt{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}-d_{y}})
we have

\lim_{\Delta\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}^{\Delta}\left[\left|\pi_{n}^{\Delta,N}(\varphi)-\pi_{n}^{\Delta}(\varphi)\right|^{r}\right]^{1/r}=\mathbb{E}^{\star}\left[\left|\pi_{n}^{\star,N}(\varphi)-\pi_{n}^{\star}(\varphi)\right|^{r}\right]^{1/r}.

Remark 3.2
.

The main purpose of Proposition
3.1
is to verify that the properties of the low noise case PF are inherited, as

\Delta\downarrow 0
to that of the degenerate noise case. In other words if one can design a good PF for the
degenerate noise case, then the low noise filtering problem can be effectively handled. For instance if one has that

\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{(z^{\prime}z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2(d_{x}-d_{y})}}w_{n}^{\star}(z^{\prime},z)<+\infty

and

\displaystyle\inf_{n\geq 1}\inf_{(z^{\prime}z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2(d_{x}-d_{y})}}q_{n}(z|z^{\prime})

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{(z^{\prime}z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2(d_{x}-d_{y})}}q_{n}(z|z^{\prime})

\displaystyle<+\infty

then by standard results for PFs (e.g.
[
9
]
) we have that

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}^{\star}\left[\left|\pi_{n}^{\star,N}(\varphi)-\pi_{n}^{\star}(\varphi)\right|^{r}\right]^{1/r}=\mathcal{O}(N^{-\tfrac{1}{2}})

and so the time uniform errors of the degenerate noise PF are then then obtained for the low noise PF.

We now consider the FitzHugh-Nagumo diffusion model with

is the noise level. This scenario leads to 20 discretization points between two consecutive observations and 2000 in total.

We consider an auxiliary process with linearized drift
\tilde{\mu}(x)=\mu(x_{\text{ref}})+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mu(x_{\text{ref}})(x-x_{\text{ref}})

A more interesting example is when the diffusion term in FitzHugh-Nagumo model depends on the state. We now revisit the previous model with exactly the same setting, except with the diffusion coefficient

The auxiliary process is as before except with
\tilde{\sigma}=\sigma(x^{\prime})
,
x^{\prime}
the end-point.

x^{\prime}
with deterministic

and using

. This incurs an extra correction term in the importance weights as now one needs to divide by the associated density. The ‘bootstrap’ and ‘optimal’ methods use this new density in lieu of the auxiliary process density used in the constant diffusion case. We run each PF with

particles.

(A.2)

Proof of Proposition
3.2

Proof.

The results under the natural parameterization are simple consequences of the choice of the proposal. For the low-noise case using simple linear algebra one can verify that

p_{n}(u_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n},\epsilon))f_{n}(u_{n}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n},x)|u_{n-1}^{\Delta}(\widetilde{z}_{n-1},x))\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}z_{n}^{\top}\widetilde{\Omega}_{n}^{-1}z_{n}+\widetilde{\mu}_{n}^{\top}z_{n}\right\}.

From there one has the optimal proposal and the normalizing constant, which is (up-to a constant)
w_{n}^{\Delta,\text{opt}}(\widetilde{z}_{n-1})
can be obtained by completing the square and using Gaussian integrals. For
the degenerate case we have similarly that

f_{n}(u_{n}^{\star}(z_{n})|u_{n-1}^{\star}(z_{n-1}))\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}z_{n}^{\top}(\Omega_{n}^{\star})^{-1}z_{n}+(\mu_{n}^{\star})^{\top}z_{n}\right\}

and the proof is similar to the low-noise case. The final limit is a direct consequence of (
A.1
)
and the dominated convergence theorem.
∎
