The chain mediating effect of mindfulness and self-regulation in the relationship between parental warmth and adolescent study engagement
Authors: Xiangli Guan, Yaqi Zhang, Jingjing Wang, Xuejiao Li, Lina Ma, Md Zahir Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Grounded in motivational dynamics theory, this research investigates how parental warmth within the family education environment influences adolescents’ engagement in learning. It also examines the role of adolescents’ self-cognition and regulation, focusing on mindfulness and intentional self-regulation. A sample of 1255 Chinese adolescent students was recruited. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to examine the relationships among parental warmth, mindfulness, intentional self-regulation, and study engagement. A strong, positive relationship was found among parental warmth, mindfulness, intentional self-regulation, and study engagement (0.29 ≤ r ≤ .48, p < .01). Parental warmth was a positive predictor of adolescents’ study engagement, with a direct effect of 0.34, accounting for 48.57% of the total effect. Additionally, parental warmth influenced study engagement indirectly through mindfulness and intentional self-regulation. The chain-mediated role of mindfulness and intentional self-regulation explained 51.43% of the total effect, with an indirect effect size of 0.36. The chain-mediating effect of mindfulness and intentional self-regulation underscores the importance of both parental warmth and adolescents’ self-cognition and regulation in shaping adolescents’ learning behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32733-0.

Introduction

Theoretical background

Adolescents’ study engagement has a significant impact on both academic performance and subsequent career progression1. Schaufeli and colleagues2 demonstrated that learning engagement is an enduring, positive, and fulfilling emotional and cognitive experience within the learning process. This engagement involves three key dimensions—vigor, dedication, and absorption, characterized by energy, persistence, recognizing learning’s value, embracing challenges, focused attention, and enjoying the learning process3,4. A high level of learning engagement is pursued through academic problem-solving with positive emotional perceptions, where encountering unpleasant experiences is common5. Students often enjoy socializing with peers and other indoor and outdoor activities rather than academic engagement6. Motivational dynamics theory7 is the key theoretical foundation of this present study. It proposed that any individual engagement can be shaped by the interactions between multiple internal cognitive processes and the environmental factor or external motivations. In this regards the self-cognition framework emphasizes on how individual level self-awareness regulates behavioral outcomes8.Individual motivation and behaviors are both regulated by the self-cognitive mechanisms, e.g., mindfulness (practice to pay attention to the present moment on purpose without any judgment) and self-regulation (ability to manage own thoughts and behavior to achieve goal adapting any situation) which have been hypothesized as mediator of the relationship between environmental factors including the parental warmth which may resulting in the study engaged of the children. Existing literature also support the role of parental warmth fostering children’s study engagement9 and in the process mindfulness and intentional self-regulation (how an individual plans for the action to achieve the goals and execute the actions through regulation) can predict the relationship significantly10,11. Previous study also indicates that some students face challenges with study engagement, such as poor learning efficiency, lack of motivation, and weak persistence in their studies12. Furthermore, these students are more susceptible to future issues related to vocational choices and depression compared to their peers13,14.

To address the question regarding the sources that influence study engagement, Bandura15 suggested that an individual’s attitudes and behaviors may not be directly shaped by objective reality but are instead influenced by how they perceive and interpret their experiences. Dewey16 posited that students’ cognition and their evolving attitudes are shaped through interactions with their environment. Considering the study engagement, mindfulness, and self-regulation play the key cognitive role to influence the motivation and learning engagement among the students17. Increasing evidence has indicated that family education is related to adolescents’ learning engagement18. Moreover, scholars have long focused on the issues and causes of student learning12, including the negative effects of unfamiliar parenting methods on adolescents’ learning behavior19. Existing literature also highlighted the impact of perceived parenting style on adolescent’s learning engagement, where some parenting styles hinder the academic engagement and success20. China is deeply rooted in collective values, prioritizing interpersonal relationships and interdependence between individuals and their communities. Fei21 described Chinese people as ‘people of relationships,’ with the self-existing within different hierarchies. Chinese people align their own role and relationships with social relations, which subsequently play a crucial role in their personal growth22. Family occupies the central place in the Chinese sense of identity23, where parents value family relationships—offering continuous support to their children. Existing literature shows that perceived parental warmth and accepting parents tend to engage more in the learning process20,24. Moreover, parents with higher educational qualifications who provide warmth and support create an encouraging and safe environment that enhances adolescents’ learning behavior18. The traditional Chinese strict parenting style has shifted to one that is more amenable, with room for autonomy and emotional support, due to the growing cultural exchange between China and Western cultures.

The role of parental warmth and mindfulness in study engagement

This study focused on positive psychology within the context of parental warmth and aimed to explore the impact of parental warmth on youth’s study engagement in the collectivist culture of China. However, studies have shown that a favorable home environment does not inevitably result in high study engagement. Parental warmth is associated with individual cognitive factors25,26. More importantly, individual cognitive factors are crucial in influencing study engagement. This present study explores whether the parental warmth influences children’s study engagement through individual cognitive process, e.g. mindfulness and self-regulation. As a key aspect of the self-cognition framework, mindfulness refers to the cognitive ability to engage attentively with any form of experience—whether positive, negative, or neutral—with calmness27. Historically, for over a century, learning has often been associated with hardship, and students rarely perceive their academic experiences as enjoyable compared to socializing with peers, engaging in physical activities, or watching television6. The renowned Chinese poet Han Yu, from the Tang Dynasty, famously remarked, “there are paths in the mountain of books, and diligence is the way; there are no shores in the sea of learning, and hardship is the boat”. However, for young students, the mindfulness of balancing negative thoughts and emotions during the learning process is particularly important due to their frequent experiences of boredom and confusion28. This focus on balance contrasts with the broader concept of mindfulness, which is typically defined as ‘the ability to calmly observe positive, negative, or neutral experiences’27. Brown and Ryan29 suggested that mindfulness involves being consciously present in the current moment with clarity and balance, as opposed to disregarding or overemphasizing pain27. Studies have shown that mindfulness can reduce the negative impact of unfavorable parenting methods on adolescents30 and influence study engagement by affecting academic work31. Furthermore, a previous study examining the correlation between mindfulness and academic self-efficacy suggests that mindfulness can enhance positive self-awareness and performance by facilitating positive reappraisal32.

Mindfulness and Self-Regulation in educational setting

Metacognitive regulation within the self-cognitive system also positively impacts students’ learning, playing a crucial role in their academic progression7. Research indicates that the capacity for intentional self-regulation is influenced by positive parenting styles33,34, and environmental factors may affect both intentional self-regulation and its association with study engagement35. Intentional self-regulation is an active process wherein individuals concentrate on developmental objectives and proactively reconcile the demands of their environment with the correlation between available resources and their aspirations for achieving success. This form of regulation primarily empowers individuals to select, enhance, and compensate for three goal-related strategies, aiding in the coordination between individuals and their environment, as well as managing external and internal resources for improved development36. Significantly, research has demonstrated that mindfulness, as a regulatory aspect of self-compassion, affects an individual’s intentional self-regulation37. Research also shows that mindfulness has a positive impact on medical students’ learning engagement38. Therefore, it is plausible that a family’s positive atmosphere influences study engagement through mindfulness and intentional self-regulation. Research on the factors influencing study engagement has garnered significant attention39. However, studies on the internal mechanisms of these influencing factors are not particularly extensive, and this also holds true for research exploring the impact of parental warmth on study engagement through internal self-cognition. Therefore, might parental warmth influence study engagement via mindfulness and intentional self-regulation?

Hypotheses

This study proposes four hypotheses: parental warmth positively influences academic engagement (Hypothesis 1), parental warmth influences academic engagement through mindfulness (Hypothesis 2), parental warmth affects academic engagement through intentional self-regulation (Hypothesis 3), and parental warmth influences study engagement through the sequential mediation of mindfulness and intentional self-regulation (Hypothesis 4). Grounded in motivational dynamics theory7, the conceptual framework of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. A positive family climate provides students with emotional warmth that satisfies their fundamental psychological needs. Both mindfulness and intentional self-regulation within the student’s self-cognitive system are influenced by parental warmth, which lays the groundwork for students to engage in scholarly endeavors and enhance academic achievement.

Method

Participants

Using a convenience sampling approach, 1,400 students were selected from 31 classes across two junior high schools, three senior high schools, and three universities in Lanzhou, located in Western China. The survey began in October 2020 and ended in January 2021. After a comprehensive review of the completed questionnaires and the removal of invalid responses, 1,255 questionnaires were deemed valid, yielding an effective response rate of 89.64%. Among the valid participants, 1,157 were Han students, and 98 belonged to ethnic minorities, reflecting the actual ethnic composition of the survey population. The participants’ ages ranged from 12 to 22 years (M = 16.81 years, SD = 2.38 years), with the sample divided into three school levels: junior high 30.6% (M = 14.2 years, SD = 1.1 year), senior high 35.2% (M = 16.8 years, SD = 1.2 year), and college students 34.2% (M = 19.3 years, SD = 1.4 year). The mean age for junior high students was 14.2 years (SD = 1.1), for senior high students was 16.8 years (SD = 1.2), and for college students was 19.3 years (SD = 1.4). Among the valid participants, 1,157 were Han Chinese students, and 98 were from ethnic minority groups, reflecting the actual ethnic composition of the survey population. The ethnic minority students made up 7.8% of the sample. Additional demographic factors, such as parental education background, and family structure were included as covariate variables in the data analysis. Measures

Parental warmth

The assessment of parental warmth in this study employed two subscales from the Short-Egna Minnen av Barndoms Uppfostran-China (s-EMBU-C), which is an adapted version of the original EMBU scale used to measure perceived parental warmth, emotional support, and control. The s-EMBU-C scale has been specifically developed and validated for use in Chinese populations, offering a reliable means of assessing adolescents’ perceptions of emotional warmth from both fathers and mothers. Each subscale includes seven items, such as ‘My father/mother gives me compliments’. The scale has demonstrated strong reliability and validity in previous studies40, with Cronbach’s α values of.81 for maternal emotional warmth and.82 for paternal emotional warmth in this study. This scale was chosen because it provides a culturally relevant measure of parental warmth, which is central to the positive family environment discussed in positive psychology.

Mindfulness

Grounded in the understanding that mindfulness is vital for managing academic stress, this study employed an adapted version of the Mindfulness Subscale from the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS)41. This approach aligns with a growing body of research demonstrating mindfulness’s efficacy in educational settings, where it has been linked to improved academic outcomes, reduced stress, and enhanced emotional regulation27,38. Previous studies have specifically leveraged the subscale to assess students’ capacity to regulate negative emotions and sustain focus during learning31. The use of this adapted scale in the current study is consistent with these prior applications and reflects the growing recognition of mindfulness as a critical factor in students’ academic engagement and performance. To measure this construct within the current learning context, adolescent participants rated four items (e.g., “When I’m feeling down about my learning, I try to face it with curiosity and openness”) on a 5-point Likert scale. An individual’s mindfulness score was calculated as the mean of these four items, with higher scores reflecting greater mindfulness. The adapted scale demonstrated good internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) and has established reliability and validity42. Given the historical tendency for youth learning experiences to be characterized by boredom or suffering, this mindfulness-based measurement provides a more appropriate framework for understanding adolescent engagement.

Intentional self-regulation

This study utilized the “Intentional Self-Regulation Scale,” developed by Freund and Baltes36, which consists of nine items across three dimensions: selection (Sample item: I will invest corresponding time and effort according to the importance of the matter), optimization (Sample item: I organize my time and resources effectively to meet my goals), and compensation(Sample item: In situations where solutions elude me, I collaborate with peers or mentors for guidance.). Participants rated each item using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (completely inconsistent) to 5 (fully consistent). The total score for intentional self-regulation was computed by averaging the responses across all nine items, higher scores indicate a higher level of mindfulness. The reliability and validity of this scale have been shown to be satisfactory43, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.86 in the present study.

Study engagement

This study employed the “Study Engagement Scale,” developed by Schaufeli and colleagues2, which includes 17 items across three dimensions: vigor(Such as: When I was studying, I felt energetic and enthusiastic), dedication(Such as: I felt that my study activities were very clear and meaningful.), and absorption(Such as: I felt that my study activities were very meaningful). Participants rated each item using a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (always). The total study engagement score was calculated by averaging the responses across the 17 items, higher scores indicate a higher level of study engagement. The reliability and validity of this scale have been confirmed as high13,44, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.94 in this study.

Procedure

Professionally trained psychology graduate students ensured anonymity within the classes. The questionnaires were screened, and invalid ones were discarded according to standard criteria. With the permission of their guardians, a professionally trained psychology graduate student conducted anonymous on-site paper tests on a class-by-class basis. Upon review of the completed questionnaires, 145 invalid questionnaires with missing answers or patterned responses were removed. A total of 1,255 valid questionnaires were obtained, yielding an effective response rate of 89.64%.

To ensure the reliability of the study data, a series of parameters were implemented to identify and disregard invalid survey questionnaires. First, the questionnaires were checked for completion. Any survey booklet missing a significant number of questions (more than three unanswered questions) was considered invalid. Second, we addressed inconsistent or contradictory responses. Contradictions between answers indicated a lack of thoughtfulness in the response. Third, we excluded logical inconsistencies, such as implausible age or grade. Additionally, response patterns, such as selecting the same options or following a repetitive pattern, were flagged as signs of potential inattentiveness, leading to the exclusion of those responses. Finally, we ensured that respondents followed the instructions strictly. If a question required a single answer, but participants selected multiple options, the questionnaire was regarded as invalid and excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

First, before formally starting the data analysis, the issue of common method bias was tested using Harman’s one-factor test. If the number of extracted factors is greater than one in exploratory factor analysis and the variance explained by the first factor is less than the critical threshold, it indicates that the scientific validity and credibility of the study are upheld. This means that there is no significant methodological bias in the data. The results show that nine factors with eigenvalues greater than one account for 60.96% of the total variance, with the first factor explaining only 27.77%. Since this percentage is significantly below the 40% threshold, it suggests that the potential impact of common method bias is minimal in this study45.

Secondly, correlation analyses were conducted on the relevant variables from the valid questionnaires, and the mean values of the variables were calculated. Thirdly, the hypothesis model was tested. The regression analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0 and Mplus 9.0. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and bootstrap analysis. For the mediation effect analysis, we employed a bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method, estimating 95% confidence intervals for various effects through 5,000 bootstrap replicates. If the bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CI) in the test results do not contain zero values, this indicates that the mediation effect is statistically significant. So, we employ the bootstrap test to validate the hypothesized relationships among the four variables, followed by regression analysis to confirm the validity of the hypothesis model.

In the SEM analysis, we controlled for several demographic covariates, including age, gender, and only-child status, as these variables have been shown to influence both parental warmth and adolescents’ study engagement. Age and gender were included to account for potential developmental and gender-related differences in study engagement. Only-child status was controlled because previous studies have demonstrated that parenting behaviors and expectations vary with the number of children which influences their self-regulation and study engagement46–48. Ethnicity was not included as a covariate, as the sample was predominantly Han Chinese, with only 7.8% of participants identifying as ethnic minorities. The inclusion of these covariates helps ensure that the observed relationships are not confounded by these demographic factors.

To support open science practices, the dataset for this study is publicly available as supplementary material. All SPSS variables have been clearly labeled to ensure the transparency and replicability of the findings. We encourage readers to access the supplementary dataset to further validate the results.

Results

Descriptive statistical analysis

Kim49 suggests that data are considered non-normal if the skewness value is equal to or exceeds 2, and the kurtosis value is equal to or exceeds 7. In the present study, the skewness of the variables ranged from − 0.90 to −0.02, while the kurtosis values varied from − 0.40 to 1.72, all falling below the critical thresholds recommended for assessing normality. A correlation test was initially conducted for each variable. Table 2 presents the mean values, standard deviations, and correlation coefficients for parental warmth, mindfulness, intentional self-regulation, and study engagement. The correlations between all variables were statistically significant, showing strong positive relationships between parental warmth, mindfulness, intentional self-regulation, and study engagement (p <.01).

Mindfulness and intent of self-regulation of intermediary role test

Independent samples t-tests were conducted to examine differences in key study variables (parental warmth, mindfulness, intentional self-regulation, and academic engagement) based on demographic factors such as gender, only-child status, and family location. These analyses revealed no statistically significant differences for any of the core variables across these demographic groups (p >.05). This suggests that these sociodemographic factors do not significantly influence student reports of these key psychological and behavioral attributes. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was subsequently performed to examine grade-level differences among junior high, senior high, and college students. Significant effects were identified for select variables: Senior high school students reported significantly lower levels of maternal warmth compared to their junior high school counterparts (p <.05). Scores for intentional self-regulation peaked in senior high school, where they were significantly higher than in both junior high (p <.05) and college students (p <.05). Academic Engagement: Senior high school students exhibited significantly higher academic engagement than both junior high (p <.001) and college students (p <.001).

The relationship between parental warmth and study engagement varied across school levels. Junior high students showed a stronger association between parental warmth and study engagement (r =.42, p <.01) compared to college students (r =.28, p <.01). Additionally, senior high students exhibited a significant mediating effect of mindfulness (indirect effect = 0.15, p <.01), while college students demonstrated a stronger mediating effect of intentional self-regulation (indirect effect = 0.25, p <.01). These findings suggest that school level influences the mediating pathways between parental warmth and study engagement.

The PROCESS macro plugin in SPSS was used to conduct the mediation effect analysis with Model 650,51, incorporating grade level, gender, status as an only child, and family geographic location as covariates. The results showed that these variables had no significant impact on the relationships between parental emotional warmth, mindfulness, deliberate self-regulation, and academic engagement (p >.05). This approach served three purposes: first, to preliminarily validate the hypothesis model; second, to assess the influence of covariates on the model; and third, to establish a foundation for the subsequent validation of a mixed structural equation model using MPlus 7.0 software. So, we employed MPlus 7.0 to develop a hybrid structural equation model integrating both measurement and path analysis components. Both the measurement model and structural model were tested, and the fit indices are reported below. For the measurement model, the fit indices were as follows: χ²/df = 2.65, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.04, SRMR = 0.03. For the structural model, the fit indices were: χ²/df = 2.49, CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.03, SRMR = 0.02. According to Hu & Bentler52, a CFI and TLI above 0.95, an RMSEA below 0.06, and an SRMR below 0.08 indicate an acceptable fit. Both the measurement and structural models met these criteria, indicating that the models fit the data well. The standardized loadings of all observed variables on their corresponding latent variables ranged from 0.74 to 0.90, suggesting that the measurement model meets ideal criteria, the observed variables adequately represent their respective latent variables, and the structural model is suitable for further testing.

To ensure the reliability of the mediation effects, we employed a bias-corrected non-parametric percentile bootstrap test for evaluation. In the analysis, the skewness-corrected percentile Bootstrap method was used for testing, with 5,000 repeated samples drawn to estimate the 95% confidence intervals for various effects. Since the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the bootstrap does not include zero, the mediation effect is statistically significant.

Discussion

Cultural context and parental warmth

In childcare literature, Chinese parents are often referred to as “tiger dads and tiger moms,” a label that reflects their parenting approach characterized by strictness and control20. This study reveals that youths’ assessments of parental warmth closely align with their frequent experiences of parental warmth and support, while occasional feelings of parental rejection surface. This may substantiate the notion that as Eastern and Western cultures increasingly interact, the contemporary parenting model of Chinese parents has progressively exhibited traits of emotional warmth and independent support. In recent years, the intersections between Western and Eastern models of parenting have gained global attention significantly through the increasing of cultural exchanges between those distinct cultures. Despite having a long tradition of Chinese strict parenting controlled by discipline, major shifts have been observed with those cultural exchanges where the soft shift towards emotional support is notable from parents. Mostly, this shift is due to the growing societal expectations, economic freedom, and the influence of Western modes of education and perspectives53.With the rapid urbanization, parents in China are adopting the global trends of fostering emotional warmth, still emphasizing the traditional way of emphasizing academic achievement. This evolving parenting practice values both family cohesion and societal conformity, while prioritizing autonomy at a personal level and emotional conformity54. This shift is driven by the growing global attention on the emotional well-being and self-regulation of children through the influence of modern-day psychological theories that suggest supportive and balanced parenting. This mechanism ensures the supportive learning environment where the adolescent’s engagement is enhanced through motivation55.

Within the Chinese educational framework, the emphasis on academic endeavor among the youth appears to surpass that of older generations, particularly for senior middle school students, due to the imperative nature of the college entrance exam for university admission. In this context, the aim of our study was to investigate the direct and indirect links between positive family emotions and adolescent engagement in their studies, considering these as aspects of the family environment. We also sought to understand the roles of mindfulness and intentional self-regulation in this relationship. In this present study, we hypothesized the unidirectional model where mindfulness is the precedent to intentional self-regulation; however, we acknowledge the bidirectional relationship between those two constructs. Existing literature suggests the influencing role of self-regulation on mindfulness, with individuals with better emotional regulation having the likelihood to engage in mindful practices56. However, considering our study perspective, we regard mindfulness as the precedent to self-regulation, grounded in the theoretical framework to comprehend that mindfulness complements emotional development and balance, which ultimately ensures more effective self-regulation57. This study emphasizes this unidirectional model to explore the role of mindfulness in the setting of self-regulation in adolescents’ academic performance and behavior.

The role of parental warmth in learning engagement

This study delves into the connection between parental warmth and learning engagement, revealing a positive correlation. The findings indicate that parental warmth can positively predict learning engagement; the greater the perceived emotional warmth in the family environment from parents, the higher the adolescents’ learning engagement, aligning with prior literature18,24. Parents serve as children’s primary mentors, and their emotional warmth fosters a sense of security and self-assurance in adolescents, thereby encouraging active academic exploration. Adolescents who experience high levels of parental warmth display greater learning engagement than those with lower levels of parental warmth. While the study found that maternal warmth was perceived as higher than paternal warmth, we did not directly test the differences between maternal and paternal warmth in our analysis. Therefore, this interpretation should be considered speculative. It aligns with traditional cultural stereotypes of the ‘strict father’ and ‘kind mother’ in Chinese society58, but future research should aim to conduct more direct analyses of maternal vs. paternal warmth to examine whether these differences hold true in adolescent development. Testing such differences could provide valuable insights into how different parental roles influence adolescents’ study engagement. Perceptions of maternal emotional warmth surpass those of paternal warmth. Meanwhile, young individuals’ assessments of their fathers closely match the emotional warmth and support provided by the fathers, suggesting a possible transition from China’s “strict father” to a more “kind father.” Furthermore, “red face” emphasizes respect and friendliness, while “white face” emphasizes norms and strictness58. These two concepts do not conflict with each other; rather, they complement and support one another. Thus, the conventional “red and white face” dynamic in Chinese parenting—where the mother-child relationship is perceived more positively than the father-child relationship, which is comparatively more negative—may also be evolving. Practically, they are speculative and imaginary rather than tested empirically in this current study and they are focusing on the evolving parenting style and roles in Chinese family education setting. Future research could further investigate the characteristics of parental roles in Chinese family education and their influence on learning engagement.

Exploring the mediating role of mindfulness

Secondly, this study investigates the role of mindfulness in the relationship between parental warmth and learning engagement. The findings suggest that parental warmth affects learning engagement not only directly but also indirectly through the mediating role of mindfulness. This outcome is consistent with research on the correlation between parenting style and mindfulness, as well as the connection between mindfulness and learning engagement30,31. Adolescents’ perceptions of their parental warmth help them develop a balanced state of mindfulness to manage negative thoughts and emotions, which subsequently impacts their learning activities. The family unit is the environment where an individual is born and nurtured, and parents are the child’s primary caregivers. Familial relationships and parental interactions provide individuals with early emotional experiences. When adolescents achieve academically, emotionally warm parents offer pride and praise; during academic challenges, these parents provide companionship and support, allowing teens to feel emotional security. Therefore, when faced with mundane and tedious studies, adolescents can approach the experience with clarity and balance, effectively dealing with negative encounters27. Research also shows that mindfulness can affect learning engagement by influencing academic diligence31 and that interventions based on mindfulness may enhance learning performance59. In completing their education, adolescents can concentrate solely on their studies by calming any negative experiences. Moreover, they can recognize the value and importance of learning, actively engage in educational activities, and derive pleasure from the learning process.

Mindfulness and intentional self-regulation as sequential mediators

This study examines the roles of mindfulness and intentional self-regulation in linking positive family emotions to learning engagement. The findings suggest that these two factors act as sequential mediators, connecting positive family emotions to learning engagement. Adolescents’ perceptions of positive family emotions can influence their learning engagement through individual cognitive factors, with positive family emotions affecting mindfulness, which then impacts intentional self-regulation and learning engagement. This research broadens our understanding of the relationship between positive family emotions and learning engagement and is the first to reveal the sequential mediating functions of mindfulness and intentional self-regulation. Parental warmth helps children develop effective ways of managing emotions and behaviors by fostering a positive parent-child relationship. Studies have also shown that mindfulness affects intentional self-regulation37. Students with higher levels of mindfulness often exhibit greater self-regulation. Importantly, research has found that training can enhance these abilities. For example, Phang et al.60 implemented mindfulness-based cognitive interventions with college students to help them manage stress. The results showed significant reductions in perceived stress and increases in mindfulness levels. Additionally, Lerner and colleagues61 designed and launched the GPS project (Goal Selection, Pursuit of Strategy, Shifting Gears), aimed at promoting adolescents’ intentional self-regulation. This project helps adolescents set goals, devise plans to achieve them, and learn to tackle obstacles encountered during implementation, thereby increasing their interaction with the environment. This enhances their intentional self-regulation skills, which in turn boosts their learning and behavioral outcomes.

Research limitations and future implications

This study offers empirical evidence supporting further investigation into the relationship between positive family emotions and learning engagement, as well as the complementary relationship between mindfulness and intentional self-regulation. These factors may enhance both academic performance and healthy development in adolescents. As Eastern and Western cultures increasingly intersect, there has been a noticeable shift in Chinese parents’ approaches to family education. Future research could delve deeper into this shift, examining the unique characteristics of the parental role in Chinese family education and their impact on learning engagement.

It is imperative to underscore a fundamental limitation of this study pertaining to its sample and design. The participants consisted solely of adolescents from northwestern China, a region that lags in development relative to other areas within the country. Consequently, the generalizability of the study’s findings to youth in the more economically advanced regions of central and eastern China remains uncertain. Especially, adolescents in the developed region are highly privileged for the educational resources, support, and the extracurricular activities—which certainly have significant effects on the parental warmth and self-regulation of study engagement compared to the less developed areas. Future research should endeavor to expand the sample size and juxtapose the learning engagement of young individuals residing in the northwest with those in the central and eastern regions.

Predominantly, this present study included 92.2% of Han Chinese with 7.8% participation from the ethnic minority. Future research should focus on the significant differences in study variables across the Han and ethnic minorities, and also between secondary and higher education students. Although parental emotional warmth can predict learning engagement, the study’s cross-sectional design does not allow for a thorough discussion of the causality between the two, which may lead to misinterpretations of the results. Future research should consider longitudinal designs to better explore the causal relationship between parental emotional warmth and learning engagement. Furthermore, mindfulness was considered as a unidimensional subscale comprising four items from the Self-Compassion Scale, and we recognize this methodological limitation. Future studies should explore mindfulness as the latent factor to strengthen the psychometric properties.

Additionally, this present study included a wide range of participants from early adolescence to young adulthood, which warrants further studies that will include the analysis of developmental variations. Moreover, as participants were nested within schools and classes, there is the potential for non-independence of observations, which could have influenced the standard errors and the results. In this study, we used single-level SEM, which does not account for the hierarchical structure of the data. The lack of multilevel modeling or cluster adjustments may have led to underestimation of the standard errors. Future research should apply multilevel modeling or hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) to account for the nested structure of the data, allowing for more accurate estimation of the relationships between parental warmth, mindfulness, self-regulation, and study engagement. Another important statistical aspect is, although the Harman one-factor test suggested minimal common method variance in this study, we acknowledge that the use of self-report measures for key constructs (i.e., parental warmth, mindfulness, self-regulation, and study engagement) from a single informant may still introduce the risk of shared method bias. Self-report data can be subject to social desirability and cognitive biases, which could inflate the observed relationships between variables. To address this potential limitation, future studies could integrate multi-informant measures, such as reports from both parents and adolescents, or behavioral assessments to more objectively evaluate the constructs of interest. This would help to reduce the potential for shared method variance and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationships between parental warmth, mindfulness, self-regulation, and study engagement.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study has demonstrated that parental warmth directly enhances learning engagement. Furthermore, it strengthens learning engagement indirectly through the mediating roles of mindfulness and intentional self-regulation. Parental warmth also indirectly boosts learning engagement by sequentially mediating the effects of both mindfulness and intentional self-regulation.
