The predictive performance of NEWS, MEWS, SOFA and SAPS II in outcomes of bacteremic and non-bacteremic sepsis
Authors: Manish Anant Talkar, Durga Shankar Meena, Deepak Kumar, Gopal Krishana Bohra, Vibhor Tak, Amit Kumar Rohila, Ankur Sharma, Mahendra Kumar Garg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study evaluates the prognostic accuracy of sepsis scores and inflammatory biomarkers in predicting mortality, bacteremia, and septic shock among sepsis patients, with comparisons across bacteremic and non-bacteremic groups.MethodsThis prospective observational study was conducted in a tertiary care center in western India. A total of 196 sepsis patients were enrolled and categorized into bacteremic (n = 95) and non-bacteremic (n = 101) groups. Key sepsis scores analyzed included Sequential Organ Failure Assessment Score (SOFA), Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS), National Early Warning Score (NEWS), and Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS II). Outcomes measured included 30-day mortality, bacteraemia prediction, and septic shock. Diagnostic performance was assessed using area under curve (AUCs), sensitivity, specificity, and odds ratio (OR) analysis.ResultsThe overall 30-day mortality rate was 27.0%. Among all patients, NEWS (AUC: 0.861, 95% CI: 0.81–0.92, p < 0.001) and SOFA (AUC: 0.738, 95% CI:0.65–0.82, p < 0.001) demonstrated strong predictive capabilities for 30 days mortality. Subgroup analysis revealed that, in bacteremic sepsis, NEWS (AUC: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.99, p < 0.001) and ferritin (AUC: 0.804, 95% CI: 0.71–0.90, p < 0.001) showed the highest predictive values, while SOFA (AUC: 0.852, 95% CI: 0.76–0.94, p < 0.001) and HsCRP (AUC: 0.623, 95% CI: 0.51–0.74, p = 0.033) were most predictive for mortality in non-bacteremic sepsis. MEWS (≥ 7) demonstrated the strongest association with bacteremia (OR: 5.1, 95% CI: 2.4–11, p < 0.001, adjusted for age, and comorbidities), followed by SOFA (≥ 6), (OR: 2.4, 95% CI: 1.3–4.2, p = 0.005). For septic shock prediction, SOFA (≥ 6) (OR: 5.7) and procalcitonin (≥ 2.5 ng/mL) (OR: 4.9) were the strongest predictors.ConclusionsNEWS and SOFA demonstrated strong predictive accuracy for 30-day mortality, with ferritin providing additional value in bacteremic patients. For bacteremia prediction, MEWS (≥ 7) showed the highest association. These tools can provide critical support for clinicians in risk stratification and early intervention in severe sepsis.Clinical trial numbernot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12046-3.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, leading to organ dysfunction and substantial mortality [1]. Globally, it accounts for around 11 million deaths annually (about 19.7% of all deaths) [2]. Early recognition and timely management are critical; delays in identification and treatment are consistently associated with worse outcomes. Importantly, sepsis encompasses both bacteremic and non-bacteremic phenotypes. While bacteremia is common, non-bacteremic sepsis (up to 30% of cases) poses diagnostic challenges, often requiring imaging and biomarker support for confirmation [3].

Several bedside scores are used to standardise severity assessment and estimate mortality risk. The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), introduced in 1996, evaluates six organ systems and is validated primarily in ICU settings, with higher scores correlating with increased mortality [4]. NEWS (National Early Warning Score) and MEWS (Modified Early Warning Score), developed to detect early physiological deterioration, are widely used on general wards and in emergency settings [5, 6]. SAPS II (Simplified Acute Physiology Score II) provides comprehensive risk stratification but can be less practical during initial resuscitation. Despite widespread adoption, performance varies across hospitals settings, and the utility of these scores within bacteremic vs. non-bacteremic subgroups remains incompletely defined [7, 8]. Prior studies also suggest that these subgroups differ in presentation and outcomes, raising the possibility that prognostic discrimination may not be uniform across them [7, 8].

Alongside scores, inflammatory biomarkers including high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (HsCRP), procalcitonin (PCT), ferritin, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) are frequently used to support clinical assessment. These measures can complement physiological scores by reflecting immune activation and disease trajectory. However, their prognostic and diagnostic performance has been inconsistent, particularly when analyses are stratified by bacteremia status [8–11]. Early identification of high-risk patients is crucial, as bacteremic and non-bacteremic sepsis may differ in severity and outcomes. Evaluating prognostic scores and biomarkers across these subgroups can aid timely intervention and enhance risk stratification especially in resource-limited settings. We aimed to assess the ability of sepsis scores and biomarkers to predict 30-day mortality, bacteremia, and septic shock, and to compare their performance between bacteremic and non-bacteremic sepsis.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This prospective observational study was conducted in the Department of Internal Medicine at a tertiary referral center in Western India between December 2022 and November 2024. The study included patients aged ≥ 18 years with clinically diagnosed sepsis, as defined by the Sepsis-3 guidelines [12], who were admitted to the Emergency Department (ED), ICU, or general ward. Patients with confirmed fungal, viral, or tubercular sepsis were excluded. Bacteremia was defined by the isolation of a recognised pathogen in one or more blood cultures (CDC/NHSN surveillance criteria) set consistent with sepsis [9]. The non-bacteremic group included sepsis patients with sterile blood cultures or cultures growing only contaminants. Septic shock was defined by persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥ 65 mm Hg and a serum lactate level > 2 mmol/L, despite adequate fluid resuscitation [12], Blood samples were simultaneously collected for inflammatory biomarkers (PCT, HsCRP, and ferritin), and microbiological analysis. In cases of persistent bacteremia, only the first episode was considered. Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) and other common skin contaminants were included only if isolated from at least two blood cultures consistent with sepsis.

Patients were enrolled at the moment Sepsis-3 criteria were first met (index assessment). For community-onset presentations (Emergency department or direct admission), the index assessment was performed within 6 h of triage; for hospital-onset sepsis (≥ 48 h after admission), it occurred within 6 h of first fulfilling Sepsis-3 on the ward/ICU. All prognostic scores were calculated from vital signs and laboratory results available at the index assessment, before vasopressor initiation or invasive ventilation whenever feasible. Blood cultures obtained within ± 24 h of the index assessment were used to classify bacteremia status. We included patients who provided consent and had blood cultures in the specified window; we excluded those who did not meet Sepsis-3 or lacked essential score components despite attempts to retrieve them. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (AIIMS/IEC/2023/4484) and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1964). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Outcome

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality from the index assessment (the time Sepsis-3 criteria were first met). Vital status was determined by: (i) review of the electronic/hospital records for in-hospital deaths and discharges, and (ii) structured telephone follow-up at day 30 with the patient or a designated caregiver. The secondary outcomes included evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of sepsis scores and inflammatory biomarkers in predicting bacteremia. Additionally, the study assessed the duration of ICU stay, the need for vasopressor support, and the incidence of septic shock.

Data collection and scores analysed

All eligible patients diagnosed with sepsis were enrolled, and the following data were collected: demographic information, clinical and laboratory findings, comorbidities, infection focus, microbiological data (etiology and antimicrobial susceptibility), baseline PCT, HsCRP levels, ferritin and NLR. In this study, we applied and analyzed the performance of SOFA, qSOFA (quick SOFA), NEWS, MEWS, SAPS II, and Pitt Bacteremia scores (PBS) in predicting outcomes in patients with sepsis. Each score was calculated at admission based on clinical and laboratory parameters specific to their respective scoring criteria. Septic shock status was recorded at admission and throughout the index hospitalisation via daily chart review; analyses refer to shock at any time during hospitalisation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis for this study was conducted using standard methods to assess the predictive performance of sepsis scores and inflammatory markers. Continuous variables were presented as Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD) or as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), depending on the data distribution. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric data and the Student’s t-test for parametric data. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and analyzed using the chi-square test. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the diagnostic performance of sepsis scores and biomarkers, with area under the curve (AUC) values used to measure discrimination. AUCs were estimated on continuous scores with 95% CIs by the DeLong method. Pairwise comparisons of AUCs was performed by DeLong test. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for each score and marker at the predefined thresholds. Additionally, logistic regression analysis was performed on different cut-off of sepsis scores to identify independent associations of sepsis scores and inflammatory markers with 30-day mortality. Each score/biomarker was entered dichotomised at the listed descriptive cut-point (e.g., NEWS ≥ 12, SOFA ≥ 6). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all analyses were conducted using SPSS and Datatab statistical software.

Results

This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic utility of various sepsis scoring systems in predicting mortality among bacteremic and non-bacteremic sepsis patients. The key scores analyzed included the SOFA score, qSOFA, MEWS, NEWS, SAPS II, and Pitt Bacteremia Score. Additionally, inflammatory biomarkers such as HsCRP, PCT, ferritin, and the NLR were also evaluated. The study was conducted over two years, including 196 patients, with a prospective 30-days follow-up for mortality outcomes.

Baseline characteristics

Of 290 adults screened, 196 were included. We excluded 47 patients not meeting Sepsis-3 criteria, 31 with no blood cultures within ± 24 h, 6 who declined consent, and 10 with missing essential score components (Fig. 1). The final cohort comprised 95 (48.5%) bacteremic and 101 (51.5%) non-bacteremic cases. ICU admission during hospitalisation occurred in 112/196 (57.1%) patients. Forty seven cases (24%) were hospital-acquired sepsis. The median age of the cohort was 54 years (IQR: 36–64), with 55.6% male participants. Comorbidities were broadly similar in both groups, with two exceptions: hypertension was more frequent in non-bacteremia 34.7% vs. 17.9%; p = 0.013), while immunocompromised status was more frequent in bacteremia (25.3% vs. 9.9%; p = 0.008). Bacteremia was associated with longer ICU stay and more days of vasopressor support compared with non-bacteremia, whereas the distribution of septic shock was similar between groups (Table 1). Median SOFA, and MEWS scores at admission were significantly higher in bacteremic patients (p < 0.05, Table 2). Among inflammatory markers, ferritin levels were notably higher in non-bacteremic patients (1200 vs. 640 ng/ml, p < 0.001).

Role of sepsis scores and inflammatory biomarkers in mortality prediction

The 30-day overall mortality rate in the study cohort was 27.0%. Among sepsis scoring systems, NEWS emerged as the most predictive tool for mortality, achieving an AUC of 0.861 (95% CI: 0.81–0.92, p < 0.001, Fig. 2A) with a cut-off of ≥ 12, providing a sensitivity of 81.6% and specificity of 70.7% (Table 3). SOFA (AUC: 0.738, p < 0.001) and SAPS II (AUC: 0.715, p < 0.001) showed higher predictive values compare to PBS (AUC: 0.685). Among inflammatory markers, ferritin demonstrated higher accuracy in mortality prediction compare with PCT (AUC: 0.714 vs. 0.601; Fig. 2B). We also examined a sensitivity-prioritised point (lowest score achieving ≥ 0.90 sensitivity). In this cohort, it resulted in relatively low specificity across scores; therefore, we emphasise AUC and treat thresholds as descriptive only.

Logistic regression analysis for mortality prediction

Logistic regression analysis (adjusted for age, gender, and comorbidities) revealed that a SOFA score (≥ 6) was independently associated with mortality (OR 4.7, 95% CI: 2.0-10.6, p < 0.001). Moreover, NEWS (≥ 12) also demonstrated association with mortality (OR 4.9, 95% CI: 2-12.1, p < 0.001). Among biomarkers, ferritin (≥ 650 ng/mL) was significantly associated with mortality (OR 5.4, 95% CI: 2.2–13.6, p < 0.001). In contrast, PCT (≥ 2.5 ng/mL) and HsCRP (≥ 250 mg/L) were not independently associated with mortality. Supplementary Table 3 provides a comprehensive analysis of logistic regression findings. These findings underscore the utility of NEWS, SOFA, and ferritin as key tools in predicting 30-day mortality in sepsis, particularly in bacteremic cases, while highlighting the limited utility of inflammatory biomarkers in non-bacteremic sepsis.

Role of sepsis scores and inflammatory biomarkers in predicting bacteremia

Sepsis scoring systems and inflammatory markers were analyzed for their ability to predict bacteremia. MEWS showed the highest discrimination for bacteremia (AUC 0.735; accuracy 63.1%, Fig. 5). SOFA showed a diagnostic accuracy of 59.7% and an AUC of 0.619 (p = 0.003). In contrast, NEWS demonstrated a lower discrimination with an AUC of 0.304 (p = 0.001), indicating its limited role in identifying bacteremia. Among inflammatory biomarkers, ferritin showed the highest discrimination for bacteremia (AUC 0.689), indicating that lower ferritin values were associated with bacteremia; (Supplementary Fig. 1). NLR (≥ 7.5) showed the sensitivity of 68.4% and an AUC of 0.621 for bacteremia prediction (p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 2). The other markers like HsCRP and PCT also lacked strong predictive associations (Supplementary Fig. 3, 4). Sensitivity and specificity values for all evaluated scores and biomarkers are provided in Table 4.

Logistic regression analysis (adjusting age, gender and comorbidities) further reinforced these findings. MEWS (≥ 7) was independently associated with bacteremia, showing an odds ratio (OR) of 5.1 (95% CI: 2.4–11.1, p < 0.001), while SOFA (≥ 6) also demonstrated a significant association (OR 2.4, 95% CI: 1.3–4.2, p = 0.005). Inflammatory markers like PCT and HsCRP did not reveal significant correlations with bacteremia (Supplementary Table 4). PCT, in particular, had an OR of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.53–1.7, p = 0.861), suggesting its limited diagnostic relevance in this context. Overall, In this cohort, MEWS and SOFA showed measurable discrimination for bacteremia, while NEWS and biomarkers like PCT demonstrate limited diagnostic utility. These results may inform initial risk stratification alongside standard microbiological testing.

Diagnostic utility of inflammatory biomarkers in septic shock prediction

The predictive performance of various inflammatory markers for septic shock was analyzed. Among these, PCT and ferritin showed the highest predictive values, with AUCs of 0.709 (Fig. 6) and 0.698, respectively (p < 0.001, Supplementary Table-5). PCT demonstrated a sensitivity of 75% and specificity of 58%, while HsCRP (≥ 250 mg/L) offered superior specificity (71.2%) alongside a sensitivity of 68%. These findings suggest that inflammatory markers, particularly PCT and HsCRP, reliably identify patients at risk of septic shock, facilitating early recognition and targeted therapeutic interventions.

Discussion

In this single-centre prospective cohort of 196 adults with sepsis, we found significant utility of NEWS and SOFA in predicting 30-day mortality. NEWS ≥ 12 achieved the highest overall diagnostic accuracy and ROC (70.1%; AUROC: 0.861), followed by MEWS and SOFA. NEWS ≥ 12 showed comparable sensitivity (81.6%) to SOFA and MEWS but achieved higher specificity (70.7%), making it the most balanced score for mortality prediction in this study. Previous studies also reported the high accuracy of NEWS score for mortality prediction (Suttapanit et al., AUC 0.770, Boekhoud et al., AUC 0.752) [13, 14]. Similarly, studies by Goulden et al. and Moreno-Torres et al. have reported superior performance of NEWS for mortality prediction compared to qSOFA and SOFA, with NEWS ≥ 5 demonstrating comparable sensitivity but higher specificity (40–50%) in similar populations [15, 16].

In the bacteremic cohort, NEWS performed even better with a AUROC of 0.940. Similarly, SAPS II ≥ 56 also showed a good diagnostic accuracy of 58% and 91% sensitivity, aligning with previous data showing SAPS II as a robust mortality predictor in critically ill patients [17]. For the non-bacteremic cohort, SAPS II ≥ 56 achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy (71.3%), MEWS ≥ 7 demonstrated the highest sensitivity (100%), equalling qSOFA, while SAPS II had the highest specificity (70.7%). A study by Colussi et al. also highlighted SAPS II as an effective tool in non-bacteremic sepsis, reporting lowest false positive rate and highest PPV (80%) [18].

For bacteremia prediction, MEWS and SOFA scores showed good diagnostic accuracies. Despite limited positive predictive values (< 60%), their negative predictive values were higher, with MEWS (80.9%) outperforming SOFA (69%). In contrast, NEWS and SAPS, which performed well for mortality prediction, showed poor predictive power for bacteremia. These findings align with previous studies which described the poor diagnostic accuracy of NEWS, and qSOFA for bacteremia prediction (AUROC of NEWS: 0.65, qSOFA: 0.60) [19].

Among biomarkers, the NLR demonstrated modest discriminative power at a cut-off ≥ 7.5, with a sensitivity of 68.4%. These findings align with a study from Sweden, reported similar AUROC values for NLR (0.68; 95% CI 0.65–0.71) [20]. Another report also showed comparative efficacy of NLR with culture positivity (NLR: >4.63 predicts bacteremia with an accuracy of 84.9%) [21]. Conversely, PCT performed poorly in current study, which was consistent with prior reports with poor NPV and sensitivity even at lowest cut off (0.1 ng/ml), highlighting its limited utility for bacteremia prediction [22–24].

In this study, we found a varying predictive efficacy of biomarkers for septic shock. PCT emerged as the most robust marker in bacteremic sepsis (AUROC: 0.731), with a sensitivity of 73.8% and specificity of 52.9%. Ferritin and HsCRP followed, with moderate predictive accuracies. In non-bacteremic sepsis, HsCRP outperformed other markers (AUROC: 0.828), with superior specificity (83.6%) and PPV (84.2%). Ferritin and PCT demonstrated consistent sensitivity and diagnostic accuracy across subgroups, with ferritin offering an NPV of 91.6% in bacteremic sepsis and nearly 100% in non-bacteremic cases at a cut-off ≥ 650 ng/mL. These findings are in line with Schupp et al., who reported strong predictive power for PCT (AUC: 0.861) in septic shock [25], and Fang et al., who described ferritin’s utility as a reliable marker for risk stratification in septic shock [26–28]. Clinically, our findings suggest that bedside scores such as NEWS and SOFA can be effectively used for early risk stratification and prognostication in sepsis. MEWS may help identify patients with a higher probability of bacteremia, prompting early blood culture collection and escalation of antimicrobial therapy if indicated.

The strength of this study is its prospective design: it applies Sepsis-3 definitions with systematic baseline capture and 30-day follow-up. Commonly used scores (NEWS, MEWS, qSOFA, SOFA, SAPS II, PBS) and biomarkers (CRP, PCT, ferritin, NLR) were evaluated head-to-head in the same patients. We also stratified all analyses by bacteremia versus non-bacteremia. These features provide robust, context-specific estimates relevant to ED and ICU practice. However, this study also has some important limitations. This single-center study’s findings may have limited generalizability due to its relatively small sample size. The static measurement of sepsis scores and biomarkers, without accounting for dynamic changes over time, may have restricted predictive accuracy. The predominance of ICU and ED patients (62.8%) in the cohort may introduce selection bias, limiting generalizability to less severe sepsis cases in non-ICU settings. Although contaminants were handled per protocol, misclassification of bacteremia cannot be entirely excluded. Management pathways were not standardised, so residual confounding is possible. Finally, thresholds for prognostic scores were cohort-derived; because sensitivity-prioritised cut-points markedly reduced specificity, we report them as descriptive only and recommend local validation.

Conclusion

This study underscores the role of sepsis scores and inflammatory biomarkers in predicting mortality and bacteremia. Among all sepsis scores, NEWS showed the highest discrimination for 30-day mortality, while MEWS perform better as a predictors of bacteremia. Among biomarkers, ferritin and NLR showed measurable prognostic value, whereas CRP and PCT had limited utility in predicting 30-day mortality. These findings may inform early risk stratification; however, external validation, calibration, and prospective impact studies are needed before clinical use.
