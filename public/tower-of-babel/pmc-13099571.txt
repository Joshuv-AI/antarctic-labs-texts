How to destroy “forever chemicals” for good
Authors: Amy McDermott
Published: 2026

From 2010 through 2019, researchers tracked all the births in New Hampshire. What they found, published last December, was alarming: families downstream of groundwater sites known to be contaminated with so-called “forever chemicals” saw a threefold increase in low birth weights and deaths among infants, compared to families upstream of the same sites (1).

Utilities are seeking effective, scalable methods to remove and ultimately destroy persistent and potentially dangerous forever chemicals. Image credit: Shutterstock/kittirat roekburi.

It’s one of many studies sounding alarms about the serious health risks posed by forever chemicals—the infamous molecules also known as perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS). Such findings don’t just make clear the dangers; they also underscore the urgent need for robust, practical, and scalable methods for removing these insidious chemicals.

Most of the roughly 14,000 forever chemicals go unregulated and untreated. Filtering is possible, but even if filters could capture every chemical, the concentrated waste left behind on the filters themselves presents a problem. Tossing highly concentrated PFAS into landfills just prolongs and exacerbates the issue.

What’s needed is a way to destroy PFAS permanently. Unfortunately, the chemistry of forever chemicals—their incredibly robust carbon–fluorine bonds—presents a challenge. Several technologies in development aim to break those bonds, whether by blasting them with ultraviolet radiation, using hungry microbes to chew them up, vaporizing them with plasma beams, or degrading them using water’s supercritical phase.

These approaches have shown promise in the lab, and some are being scaled up to destroy PFAS at contaminated sites—for example, military bases or airports where firefighters once practiced with chemical foams. But technical and financial hurdles remain. It could take another several years before one or more of these technologies reach anything close to commercial scale.

A Growing Problem

Once touted for their convenience in nonstick cookware coatings, rain-resistant fabrics, and fire-smothering foams, PFAS are extremely stable chemicals, typically composed of multiple super-strong carbon–fluorine bonds. “They’re widely used because they don’t degrade, but once released in the environment, they’re difficult to destroy,” explains University of Arizona hydrologist Bo Guo, a coauthor of the recent infant study (1).

As these chemicals swirl down drains, or leach out of trash at the dump, they contaminate soil, groundwater, and, ultimately, drinking water. Once people drink them, a growing number of studies suggest that the chemicals turn up in the liver, blood, and reproductive organs (2).

“These are extremely persistent chemicals,” says Lida Chatzi, a professor of population and public health sciences at the University of Southern California (USC) in Los Angeles and director of USC’s ShARP Center for PFAS remediation. “We can detect them in the blood of almost anyone in the US,” she says, where they’re likely affecting “literally every organ and system.”

The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has set regulations on just two forever chemicals, PFOS (perfluorooctanesulfonic acid) and PFOA (perfluorooctanoic acid), limiting them to four parts per trillion in drinking water in 2024. Utilities have five years, until 2031, to comply. The EPA proposed enforceable standards for four more PFAS in 2024, but with the change in administration, the agency recently has been trying to roll those additions back. Still, thousands of US drinking water utilities are now preparing to remove at least PFOS and PFOA from their water, using, for example, activated carbon or resins to absorb and remove the chemicals. Proven technologies will be essential.

Shining a Light

One of the most promising approaches is also among the most familiar: ultraviolet (UV). UV-based systems can irradiate and break apart PFAS. Physical chemists first started using UV light and various photosensitive ions to do chemical reduction in the 1960s, notes chemist and environmental engineering researcher Jinyong Liu at the University of California, Riverside. As early as 2009, scientists at Caltech in Pasadena, California, applied UV methods to destroy forever chemicals (3). Efforts have ramped up in the last 10 years. Typically, a UV bulb shines on a water tank containing sulfite and PFAS. When the UV hits the sulfite, it excites and shears individual electrons, known as free radicals, off the sulfite. The unpaired electrons then attack the PFAS.

Liu’s lab works to optimize the UV setup, tinkering with the pH of the water bath and the cocktail of chemicals added to the water, to see how many kinds of PFAS they can degrade and how quickly they can do it. He led a 2022 study that combined sulfide and iodide in the water bath to shorten the reaction time (4). With this setup, he says the team could destroy 99% of most types of PFAS within 24 hours.

Because UV destruction has been used for decades in water treatment to clean up other chemicals, the approach should be relatively straightforward to scale up for PFAS, says Adam Smith, a microbial ecologist and civil and environmental engineer at the USC ShARP Center. Already, treatment plants use stainless steel pipes studded with UV bulbs to destroy other chemicals as the water flows through. Unfortunately, it takes 24 hours to destroy most of the PFAS in a sample. A flowing drinking water setup wouldn’t work, as the water would rush by too quickly. And for any UV-based technology, the water needs to be clear for high transmissibility of the light, Smith says. Coffee-colored wastewater or industrial water would need some cleanup steps first.

A more practical application, Liu says, would be treating a few liters of water at a time in holding tanks—for example, to destroy concentrated PFAS clinging to saturated absorbent resins or carbon. This approach could help when cleaning up PFAS after they’re removed from industrial sources of wastewater.

A national company called Haley & Aldrich already has a field setup, EradiFluor, deployed at different Department of Defense sites. Right now, it’s a mobile unit (a trailer). When the team arrives at a given site, they target wastewater concentrates in a holding tank or drum. They hook up a series of hoses that pump the wastewater through the EradiFluor. UV lamps click on inside the setup while researchers add sulfite and other nontoxic chemicals to the water. Results from the initial field tests showed that EradiFluor destroyed more than 99% of PFAS in the concentrated wastes. The team is now working on a much larger UV setup to treat 100 gallons per day; the wastewater would be clean enough for industrial reuse, or disposal off-site.

Garbage Lunch

Seeking a more energy-efficient approach to destroy forever chemicals, some researchers are trying to recruit microbes to chew up the stubborn bonds of PFAS, much as they do in oil spills. In principle, PFAS-hungry microbes could work on the spot, to clean up soils contaminated by firefighting foams, for example.

“There’s been a lot of work on degradation of PFAS under aerobic and anaerobic conditions in microbial communities,” explains environmental biogeochemist Peter Jaffé at Princeton University in New Jersey, whose lab is hunting for PFAS-eating bacteria.

So far, they’ve found several bacteria that can break carbon–fluorine bonds, Jaffé says, but just one that can attack perfluorinated compounds such as PFOS and PFOA. His lab was already studying the organism, an Acidimicrobium known as strain A6, in 2019, because it was known to remove halogen atoms from various compounds. The fluorine compound work began with rust-orange vials of liquid, containing ammonium and iron to feed the bacterium. The researchers created tiny artificial worlds in the sealed vials, with nitrogen and carbon dioxide as a makeshift atmosphere above the liquid. Then, they pipetted two types of PFAS into the vials and left them on a gentle shaking rack. Every two weeks, they opened a vial and analyzed the remaining concentration of PFAS. It took 60 days to decrease the PFAS concentration by 50%. A6 can chew up PFAS, removing fluorine (5). Then, other microbes cleave the carbon–carbon bonds of the remaining compound, ultimately producing fluoride and carbon dioxide as harmless by-products.

Biodegradation, so far, is probably the least effective destruction technology; it took two months to remove just half of the PFAS concentration. But in the long run, Smith at USC believes biodegradation is “the best option we have”—if researchers can find the ideal microorganisms and conditions. In principle, water treatment plants could inoculate carbon filters or absorbent resins with the microbes, Smith says. Microbial biofilms could trap and destroy the PFAS as the water flows through.

Jaffé says the next step is to learn more about the microbe’s basic biology. The Department of Defense recently awarded him a small grant to gather basic parameters about the bacterium, including its optimal growing temperature and whether it might be hungry (or inert) in open air—for instance, in topsoil enriched with iron at military bases. Jaffé recently found the gene that A6 turns on when it’s chowing on PFAS. He’s now investigating the enzyme that breaks those tough bonds. If researchers can produce such enzymes cheaply on commercial scales—no easy feat—they might spread them on contaminated sites for clean-up.

Plasma Rays and Super Water

Other methods can more effectively destroy PFAS in short order, not just slowly mitigate them—but these approaches also have big drawbacks.

The hot ionized gas of plasmas—often called the fourth state of matter—is great at obliterating forever chemicals. “In plasma systems, you can essentially break a molecule into its constituent atoms,” says analytical chemist Steven Ray at the State University of New York at Buffalo. But their energy costs are substantial—an order of magnitude higher than other methods.

Ray, however, hopes his new plasma technology will be more affordable. Researchers create plasma in the lab by using electricity to zap gas or steam. The material becomes ionized, a sea of positive and negative charges that reacts like an extremely hot flame with temperatures ranging from 5,000 to 50,000 Kelvin. “When the PFAS goes in, it vaporizes, then atomizes,” Ray explains.

By running air through a microwave field to create a plasma, Ray’s method is less energy-intensive than other approaches. He uses a magnetron from a microwave oven to create an enormous oscillating microwave field inside a long ceramic tube. The waves flow down to the far end of the tube, where they accelerate electrons and form a plasma that looks like a bright pink flame. He then injects heated, volatile, airborne PFAS into the tube, where they flow down into the plasma, vaporize, and atomize. The approach is half the price of other plasma setups, Ray says, in part because the capital costs are lower and the efficiency is higher. The idea is not to directly treat PFAS chemicals in drinking water, but rather, vaporize them once they’re filtered out by carbon or resins.

Plasma isn’t the only promising avenue. Another effective, but costly, approach: supercritical water oxidation (SCWO). Engineers heat water to the supercritical phase, above 374 degrees Celsius. At that temperature, oxygen reacts to rip PFAS chemicals apart, breaking the carbon–fluorine bond.

Chemical engineer Marc Deshusses led pilot research at Duke University in Durham, North Carolina, superheating as much as one ton of water per day. He found PFAS were degraded down to carbon dioxide and fluoride component parts. Deshusses co-founded a startup company called 374Water that has field-tested SCWO systems for PFAS cleanup. The setup looks like a bunch of long pipes, he says, some heated, through which the waste is continuously pumped. As it travels through the SCWO system, the waste reaches supercritical conditions, reacts, is cooled, and is depressurized. The effluent is very clean water, sometimes with dissolved salts such as fluoride. The goal, he says, is ultimately to feed drums of firefighting foam or bags of used filters into the system at industrial scales. “I think SCWO is really up there in terms of technology readiness,” he says.

“There’s a lot of promise in SCWO,” agrees Ryan Capelle, the global PFAS leader at Stantec, a design and consulting firm that’s working with utilities to help them become EPA-compliant by 2029. But it can require a lot of energy. “In general, destructive technologies are in the earlier stages of achieving municipal-scale deployment,” he says.

High-frequency sound can also break apart forever chemicals, a process called sonolysis. Like other techniques, it also capitalizes on high temperature and pressure. When high-frequency sound waves oscillate through water, gas bubbles can destabilize and collapse. The collapse creates high temperatures and pressures that can break PFAS apart, yielding fluoride. Already, a company called Mantisonix is using sonolysis to destroy PFAS on a pilot scale. The sound moves through water in a system the size of a large cupboard, according to cofounder Madeleine Bussemaker, a chemical engineering professor at the University of Surrey, in the United Kingdom.

Leading the Way

All of these approaches show potential. All of them will face challenges to scale. And all of them have teams of researchers and engineers keen on applying their tech to a huge public health challenge. It will take time for different towns and regions to discern which approaches work best.

PFAS present a huge challenge for utilities. Drinking-water treatment facilities clean 10 million gallons a day; PFAS destruction technologies need to destroy 100% of targeted compounds within minutes to hours, not days to weeks. And they need to do it without increasing water treatment costs by an order of magnitude.

Already, some utilities have spent years testing PFAS levels in the environment. Thousands of others are just getting ready to test for the first time this year or next. “It’s a whole spectrum of preparation,” says John Kmiec, director of the municipal water utility for Tucson, Arizona.

Any new treatment that utilities are considering for community drinking water supply “has to be known and reliable,” Kmiec says. The decision comes down to the quality of water output from the treatment process, how much the tech costs to build, and how much it costs to maintain. For that reason, utilities will probably stick with what’s been proven: chemical absorption, either using activated carbon or ion exchange media to remove and concentrate PFAS from water streams.

The focus on filtering also makes PFAS destruction technologies more viable sooner, Capelle at Stantec adds, because you have less volume to treat. But “you don’t want to limit yourself,” he says. It’s a good idea to keep destruction in mind, too, he notes, as technologies are evolving quickly. “Destruction is the holy grail,” Capelle says. “We are all trying to destroy PFAS, not just pass it down the line.”
