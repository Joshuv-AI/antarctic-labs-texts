Beyond the Clinic: How Stroke Survivors Navigate Secondary Prevention Through Situated Agency
Authors: Charlotte Lee, Hilary Hwu, Shoilee Khan, Lucy Frankel, Raza Noor, Amy Y X Yu, Seema Marwaha, Gustavo Saposnik, Moira K Kapral, Manav V Vyas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSecondary stroke prevention is critical to reduce recurrent strokes; however, stroke survivors’ real-world engagement with secondary preventive care remains poorly characterized.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study to understand the barriers and facilitators faced by stroke survivors in the Greater Toronto Area, Canada. Adult stroke survivors with low or moderate disability, who had a first-ever stroke in the year prior, were recruited from five stroke prevention clinics. Semi-structured interviews were conducted in three different languages. Recordings were transcribed, and the transcripts were coded and analyzed using interpretive descriptive methodology. We compared experiences by immigration status during coding; overlapping patterns led us to present integrated themes and note where intensity differed.ResultsTwenty-five stroke survivors (40% women, mean age 70.3 years) were interviewed. We demonstrate how actions typically framed as patient behaviour were shaped by, and in turn shaped, broader structural and relational conditions, which we interpret through the lens of situated agency. We identified seven core themes: (1) Self-reliance as a default mode of recovery; (2) Gaps in understanding of stroke risk and prevention; (3) Barriers to engaging in preventive health behaviours; (4) Intersectional vulnerability in system navigation; (5) Family and social networks: source of strength or silent gaps; (6) Structural assumptions about self-management; (7) Desire for dignity and agency.DiscussionStroke survivors’ engagement with stroke preventive care is shaped by external circumstances and the ways they act within and respond to their contexts. Using a situated agency framework to design interventions could improve engagement and reduce stroke recurrence.Keywords: stroke, secondary preventive care, situated agency, interview, qualitative analyses

Introduction

Secondary stroke preventive care plays a vital role in reducing the risk of stroke recurrence; however, the burden of recurrent stroke has largely remained unchanged over the years. 1 In high-income countries, secondary preventive care is typically delivered in outpatient settings, with a primary focus on medication adherence, risk factor control and lifestyle modifications. 2 While these services are intended to be accessible, their uptake and effectiveness may vary across populations. In the US, access to a family physician was less common among Black and Hispanic stroke survivors than their White counterparts. 3 In contrast, a population-based study in Canada found comparable or better ambulatory secondary stroke preventive care in immigrants compared to long-term residents, despite an overall suboptimal stroke preventive care at a population level.4,5 Given that there are differences in stroke recurrence based on ethnicity and immigration status,1,6-8 these findings raise questions about how survivors engage with preventive services day to day and how they navigate systems, information, and follow-up over time.

Existing models of secondary stroke prevention centre on individual responsibility and adherence to recommendations, adopting changes such as medication use, diet, and exercise. 9 However, these frameworks rarely account for the relational, cultural, and structural conditions that shape survivors’ capacity to act. A study of stroke survivors in the US highlights the emotional, relational, and practical disruptions experienced by stroke survivors and their caregivers, revealing how recovery extends beyond clinical guidance. 10 Further, Latour’s actor-network theory reminds us that human behaviour is shaped by a constellation of actors, including people, technologies, institutions and policies. 11 Thus, secondary stroke prevention is not enacted by individuals alone; it is co-produced through complex interactions between survivors and the systems surrounding them.

Our study examined the barriers and facilitators that influence stroke survivors’ participation in secondary stroke preventive care, with particular attention to the social, structural and cultural factors that shape these experiences across immigrant and long-term resident participants.

Methods

Study Design

This qualitative study employed an interpretive descriptive methodology to explore how immigrant and long-term resident stroke survivors engaged in secondary stroke prevention. 12 Interpretive description generates practice-relevant insights from patterns in experience, while remaining situated in the real-world complexity of healthcare systems. This approach supports a contextual understanding of participants’ perspectives, acknowledging the influence of structural conditions, relational dynamics, and cultural values on health behaviour. Rather than imposing a pre-existing theory, interpretive description allows concepts to be drawn from the data during analysis to derive meaningful explanations of participants’ experience. Maximum variation sampling was used to ensure diversity in ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and stroke severity. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached, consistent with interpretive descriptive methodology. 12

Setting and Participants

Participants were recruited from five stroke prevention clinics across the Greater Toronto Area (GTA), Canada, as well as through community partner organizations. The inclusion criteria were: (1) adults aged 40 years and older, who (2) experienced a first-ever ischemic stroke within the previous year, and (3) were residing in the community (not a long-term care home) and had a 4) modified Rankin Scale score < 4 (i.e., could walk without support). We included people aged 40 years and older because strokes occuring before age 40 often does are less attributable to vascular risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes; therefore, preventive care focused on managing these risk factors may be less applicable to younger individuals. We excluded people who could not communicate in an interview for 60 minutes, and those who did not speak one of the most commonly spoken languages in the GTA: English, French, Mandarin, Cantonese, Hindi, Punjabi and Tagalog. In our study, “immigrant” was defined as someone who was born in another country and moved to Canada, becoming a permanent resident or a Canadian citizen. Those born in Canada were considered non-immigrants. Maximum variation sampling was used to ensure diversity in ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and stroke severity.

Recruitment

Participants were referred by clinicians at participating stroke prevention clinics or self-identified in response to recruitment flyers posted in clinics or community health centres. Interested individuals underwent a 10-minute screening to determine eligibility. Written informed consent was obtained in participants’ preferred language, and ethical approval was granted by the Unity Health Research Ethics Board (on March 15th, 2023) and Toronto Metropolitan University Research Ethics Board on July 22nd, 2025 (REB# 4138 and REB# 2025-290). Screening and informed consent were conducted by research staff not within the participants’ circle of care.

Data Collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted by trained study personnel in person, by telephone, or via video conferencing, depending on participant preference, between October 2023 to May 2025. Interviews lasted approximately one hour in length and followed a guide designed to elicit experiences related to three domains of stroke preventive care: risk factor management, medication adherence, and healthcare utilization (Appendix A). Demographic information was collected at the start of each interview. Participants received a $50 honorarium upon completion of the interview, recognizing their time and contribution.

Data Management and Analysis

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English as necessary, with all identifying information removed. After verifying the accuracy of transcription and translation (where applicable), transcripts were read multiple times to facilitate immersion in the data, and coded inductively using open, descriptive codes. These codes were then grouped and refined through an iterative process of interpretation to develop broader thematic insights. We attended to patterns and variations across immigration status, gender identity, and perceived acculturation, seeking not only commonalities but also meaningful divergences. To support reflexive thematic analysis, transcripts were managed and coded using NVivo (QSR International). We compared coded data by immigration status, gender and language concordance. Where experiences differed in strength or complexity, we highlighted these differences within the themes. Because overall patterns were shared, we present integrated themes rather than group-specific ones.

Two researchers independently coded transcripts and met regularly to discuss discrepancies and ensure interpretive consistency. Through collaborative analysis, the lead author constructed conceptual maps to explore connections across themes, staying attentive to the social and structural contexts shaping participant experiences. 12 Rather than viewing barriers and facilitators as fixed or external categories, we interpreted them as conditions that expanded or constrained participants’ ability to engage in care. Through this lens, behaviours such as resisting assistance, modifying routines, or selectively following advice were not signs of non-adherence but expressions of agency within constraint. These patterns led us to draw on the concept of situated agency to interpret our findings.

Constrained agency refers to how individuals make choices and assert autonomy within conditions that limit their range of possible actions. In this study, participants’ responses were shaped not simply by personal preference but by structural limitations, social roles, and cultural expectations that narrowed what was practically or acceptably possible.

Situated agency(Hillier et al, 2020) highlights how individuals act in ways that are locally meaningful and contextually shaped; meaning their actions emerge not from innate personal traits, but through ongoing negotiation with social, structural, and cultural environments. 13

We adopted strategies to ensure trustworthiness, including analyst triangulation, audit trails, and thick description. 14 Credibility and plausibility were further supported by comparing findings with concurrent quantitative data. 15 Transferability was addressed through detailed contextual descriptions and sampling across a diverse metropolitan region. 16 We followed the Standards of Reporting Qualitative Research to ensure transparency and comprehensiveness. 17 The research team brought interdisciplinary expertise in neurology, nursing, and qualitative methods, as well as lived experiences of immigration. Interviews were conducted by trained personnel with cultural and linguistic competencies. Regular team discussions ensured reflexivity throughout data collection and analysis.

Participant Demographics

Interviews showed that stroke survivors’ engagement with secondary prevention was shaped less by isolated knowledge or motivation and more by a shifting interplay of structural conditions, relationships and cultural expectations. We set out to identify factors influencing engagement and found that care was continually negotiated by survivors’ actions, decisions and daily contexts.

We identified seven core themes that reflect how participants navigated secondary stroke prevention within complex systems of care, relationships, and expectations. Rather than static categories, these themes illustrate the ways survivors actively made sense of and engaged with prevention. Together, they demonstrate how actions typically framed as patient behaviour were shaped by, and in turn shaped, broader structural and relational conditions, which we interpret through the lens of situated agency. The themes include: (1) self-reliance as a default mode of recovery, (2) gaps in understanding of stroke risk and prevention, (3) barriers to engaging in preventive health behaviours, (4) intersectional vulnerability in system navigation, (5) family and social networks as sources of strength or silent gaps, (6) structural assumptions about self-management, and (7) desire for dignity and agency. We discuss each theme in detail below, with attention to the interplay between individual agencies and the contextual conditions that shaped it.

Self-Reliance as a Default Mode of Recovery

Participants frequently framed their recovery in terms of independence, resilience, and minimal reliance on others. Many emphasized that they were managing well, even when describing fatigue, pain, or limitations. These expressions of self-reliance often masked unmet needs and reflected constrained forms of agency shaped by cultural norms, caregiving histories, and personal pride.

Statements such as “I live alone… I can do it myself” (ID-13, woman, immigrant) and “I am okay, I don’t need any support” (ID-11, woman, immigrant) illustrated this pattern. However, later accounts sometimes contradicted these claims. One woman described significant physical pain that made it difficult to cook, yet she continued to prepare meals independently: “I can’t stand up even for 15 minutes… so I’m just cooking very fast foods” (ID-11, woman, immigrant). She also switched to a doctor closer to home despite losing language concordance, explaining, “It was very far… I changed to one here, which is very convenient for me” (ID-11, woman, immigrant). These decisions prioritized logistical feasibility over relational or linguistic continuity, reflecting the trade-offs of constrained autonomy.

Many participants also hesitated to involve family, expressing concern about burdening adult children. One woman noted, “If I ask them to help me, they have to leave their children… I don’t want that” (ID-11, woman, immigrant). And said, “My son is busy with his own life… I don’t want to bother him” (ID-11, woman, immigrant). These accounts reveal a protective stance, where silence about needs preserved family harmony and upheld cultural expectations of non-interference. The tendency to avoid burdening family was especially emphasized among immigrant participants, who framed silence about needs as culturally appropriate.

Medication adherence emerged as the most common form of preventive care, often described as straightforward and achievable. “I take my pills. I’m fine,” said several participants. Lifestyle changes were viewed as vague or burdensome, particularly when unsupported by clear guidance. “They told me not to drink. I don’t know why, but I’m doing it” (ID-17, man, immigrant). Others developed their own interpretations of prevention. One participant reflected, “Getting anxious will just increase your blood pressure… and that’s probably not good for not having a stroke” (ID-23, woman, immigrant). These accounts highlight how survivors simplified prevention to what was most manageable on their own. Relying on medication, resisting burdensome changes, or substituting personal logic all reflected efforts to preserve independence and control, which are forms of self-reliance enacted within the limits of available knowledge and support.

Self-reliance was both a meaningful identity and a response to structural constraints. Participants navigated recovery in ways that preserved dignity and control, even when it meant foregoing help or simplifying prevention to medication alone. Their actions reflected not disengagement, but agency shaped by context, priorities, and limited alternatives.

Gaps in Understanding of Stroke Risk and Prevention

Although most participants reported attending medical appointments and taking prescribed medications, many lacked a clear understanding of what had caused their stroke or how to prevent another. This disconnect between health system expectations and patient knowledge was especially pronounced among immigrant participants navigating unfamiliar systems and languages. Participants often engaged in preventive care without fully grasping the rationale for specific behaviours, reflecting a form of situated agency enacted within conditions of limited and contextually inappropriate information.

Several participants expressed confusion or surprise about having had a stroke at all. “I don’t know, I can’t think of anything I do that would prevent a stroke. I couldn’t understand why I had it,” said one participant (ID-1, woman, non-immigrant), who also did not consider smoking to be a risk factor. Many others had difficulty recalling guidance from healthcare providers, noting that recommendations were vague, inconsistent, or entirely absent. “No one has said to me, these are the changes you need to make in your life” (ID-9, man, non-immigrant). One participant reflected, “You are sent home and the recommendations are made etcetera. But there’s really no follow up as to, well, how, you know, how are you doing with that” (ID-19, man, immigrant).

Even when provided with written resources, participants described them as generic or irrelevant. “I did get a big stroke book when I was sent home… but a lot of it didn’t really pertain to me because I didn’t identify with a lot of it” (ID-15, man, non-immigrant). Some struggled to remember or interpret dietary guidance: “My doctor didn’t say anything, but just not to eat… uh, what is it… saturated fat?… or, I don’t know… processed meat” (ID-11, woman, immigrant). Others reported contradictory advice, leading to confusion and ambivalence. One participant, unsure about alcohol restrictions, recounted different instructions from his doctor, pharmacist, and hospital staff, ultimately deciding on his own limits based on convenience and upcoming holidays (ID-4, man, immigrant).

Several participants adhered to medications or lifestyle suggestions without knowing their purpose. This passive adherence often stemmed from trust in healthcare providers, but also revealed a gap between action and understanding. “One of my medications apparently takes care of cholesterol… but nobody ever said I had a cholesterol problem” (ID-23, woman, immigrant). Another explained, “They told me… not to take alcohol… I’m not really sure why… but that’s what I’m doing” (ID-17, man, immigrant). These accounts suggest that adherence, while seemingly positive, may mask gaps in self-efficacy and informed engagement.

In the absence of adequate follow-up, participants frequently turned to informal sources to fill knowledge gaps. They searched online, asked friends, or used personal logic to interpret symptoms and risk. “We’re not dumb. You can just Google everything if you want to learn it yourself” (ID-4, man, immigrant). Another noted, “The doctor? No, I’m just watching on the TV and YouTube” (ID-11, woman, immigrant). While some participants were confident in their ability to find and assess information, others expressed ambivalence. “I understand I can’t trust everything online” (ID-4, man, immigrant, later in the interview). This “do-it-yourself” knowledge work revealed both resourcefulness and vulnerability, highlighting the risks of unsupported learning in a system that assumes health literacy and cultural fit.

Taken together, these accounts illustrate how survivors engaged in secondary prevention under conditions of uncertainty. Their agency was evident, not in the absence of knowledge gaps, but in how they adapted, complied or improvised in the face of those gaps.

Barriers to Engaging in Preventive Health Behaviours

While most participants expressed a desire to prevent future strokes, many encountered significant obstacles to adopting recommended lifestyle changes or maintaining follow-up care. These barriers extended beyond individual motivation, encompassing physical limitations, emotional distress, and systemic inaccessibility. For immigrant participants in particular, these challenges were often compounded by financial strain, language barriers and limited awareness of available services, as described in the accounts that follow. Notably, all participants had at least one stroke prevention clinic visit; the issue was not initial access but sustaining engagement amid physical, informational, and logistical constraints.

Engagement in secondary stroke prevention was shaped not only by knowledge and intention but also by the conditions under which participants were expected to act. Although participants demonstrated agency, their ability to follow through with prevention strategies was often narrowed by broader social, economic, and institutional factors.

Many participants described both physical and emotional symptoms that impeded behaviour change. Post-stroke fatigue, low mood, and reduced confidence undermined their ability to exercise, lose weight, or cease addictive behaviours. One participant, reflecting on smoking, said, “I’ve [quit smoking] before. I know how to do it. I’m just not interested right now… it helps me relax” (ID-1, woman, non-immigrant). Others expressed difficulty with motivation. “I do have a small gym down in the basement, but no inclination to go down there” (ID-3, man, non-immigrant).

Participants described getting “used to just recovering,” feeling stuck in a passive mode of convalescence. One participant explained, “When you’re trying to recover, you get used to just recovering, as opposed to, hey, you’ll feel better if you go outside” (ID-19, man, immigrant). Even when participants felt motivated, their bodies often imposed limits. “I feel so lethargic… the effort that I’m putting forth is the one that I’m struggling with since the stroke” (ID-1, woman, non-immigrant). Another shared, “I just really… find it too demanding” to resume sessions with a personal trainer (ID-3, man, non-immigrant). Medication side effects also played a role. “Since I’m taking these special pills I have fatigue now… I wish I could exercise more but I just can’t” (ID-16, man, non-immigrant). In several cases, participants lived with chronic conditions or mobility challenges that predated their stroke but still limited their ability to engage in preventive health behaviours. These factors collectively reflected constrained agency: a desire to act was present, but capacity was diminished by illness, fear or psychosocial stressors.

In addition to physical and emotional barriers, participants faced difficulties accessing rehabilitation, dietitians, and follow-up care. Several described long wait times, cancelled appointments, or unclear referrals. One participant shared, “The occupational therapy… there was no follow-up… we had to fight to make sure that is put in place” (ID-19, man, immigrant). Others were never referred to needed services. An 84-year-old woman who lived alone recalled being told she was ineligible for help because she could brush her own teeth. “I beg your pardon, I do… I have lost my confidence completely” (ID-22, woman, immigrant). Another participant noted, “No dietitian. We do it all on our own” (ID-4, man, immigrant).

Some lacked a family physician or had difficulty obtaining timely appointments, disrupting care continuity. One woman worried about her expiring prescriptions: “During COVID, this doctor left… now I go to a walk-in… there is nobody assigned to you… they told me go back to your hospital because we can’t deal with you… my neurologist gave me a big prescription… but said you’re going to have to work it out after that. I’m not sure how that’s going to happen” (ID-2, woman, immigrant). Others secured care only through persistent advocacy. One spouse recalled, “They said, you need to have a family doctor… it was a nightmare… our whole neighborhood was trying to help” (Spouse of ID-18, woman, non-immigrant). Another participant was forced to go two weeks without blood thinners because his doctor refused to fax a prescription. “I had a stroke. I need to get pills… I was out of the blood thinners for two and a half weeks” (ID-20, woman, non-immigrant).

Many participants reported that expected services never materialized, or that they were left to navigate recovery on their own. This suggests a breakdown in the continuity of care, particularly after discharge. Without proactive follow-up, participants were less likely to engage in preventive behaviours beyond taking medication.

Even when services were technically available, participants described practical obstacles that limited their ability to access or benefit from them. These included transportation difficulties, cost of nutritious food, and language barriers. One participant said, “The expense has tripled” when trying to eat healthy (ID-20, woman, non-immigrant). Another described the stress of daily appointments without reliable transportation: “I’m getting kind of nervous because I don’t have a driver” (ID-20, woman, non-immigrant). One participant was again left without blood thinners due to a breakdown in both provider communication and logistical access: “I had to wait two weeks without [blood thinners] because my GP wouldn’t refill it” (ID-20, woman, non-immigrant).

While challenges with booking or attending appointments appeared in earlier accounts, the examples here underscore how barriers rooted in personal circumstances (e.g., fixed income, lack of transit, or language difficulties) intersected with systemic gaps. These structural determinants of health shaped not only what care was available, but whether it could realistically be accessed. The issue was not participants’ willingness to engage in prevention, but whether the material and logistical conditions of their lives made such engagement feasible.

Together, these accounts demonstrate that stroke prevention does not take place in a vacuum. Behaviour change after stroke is influenced by a web of physical, emotional, social, and structural factors. Survivors enacted agency within these constraints, but their efforts were shaped by fatigue, fragmented care systems, and barriers embedded in their everyday realities.

Intersectional Vulnerability in System Navigation

Participants’ ability to access and engage with secondary stroke prevention was shaped by the intersection of multiple structural vulnerabilities, including immigration status, language barriers, age, physical mobility, and limited financial resources. These overlapping conditions created cumulative disadvantages, particularly for immigrants with fewer social supports, and contributed to a healthcare system that many experienced as fragmented, inconsistent, and difficult to navigate, as described in the participant accounts below.

System navigation emerged as a site of constrained agency. While participants demonstrated resourcefulness, their choices were shaped and often restricted by institutional design, social positioning, and a lack of culturally responsive care, such as receiving generic stroke materials they could not relate to (ID-15, man, non-immigrant) or losing language concordance when switching to closer providers (ID-11, woman, immigrant). The barriers participants faced rarely occurred in isolation; instead, they compounded and interacted in ways that reinforced inequities in recovery, as illustrated in participants’ accounts below of overlapping challenges with mobility, language and access to support.

Several participants encountered difficulty understanding instructions, completing paperwork, or communicating with healthcare providers. Those with limited English proficiency often operated without the support of interpreters, and unfamiliarity with the system further constrained access. In some cases, physical limitations added another layer of complexity. One participant described changing doctors from one who spoke her first language to a physician closer to home because they could no longer manage the distance by themselves: “I changed doctors even though the first one spoke [my language] — the new one is closer, and I can walk” (ID-11, woman, immigrant). What appeared to be personal preference was, in effect, a practical response to intersecting constraints.

In the absence of strong family networks or access to private services, many relied on community programs, neighbours or privately arranged personal support workers (PSWs) to fill gaps in care and transportation. While community programs are part of the broader health system, enrolment is discretionary and not guaranteed; several participants described relying on friends or privately arranged PSWs when referrals did not materialize. These supports were therefore experienced as essential yet precarious. As one participant explained, “I get to my appointments by relying on friends or walking paths, not on family” (ID-22, woman, immigrant). These improvised strategies reflected both persistence and the fragility of supports available to those excluded from more structured or guaranteed care pathways.

Participants also described receiving health information that felt generic or irrelevant to their lived experience. Educational materials often failed to account for cultural values, dietary practices, or socioeconomic realities, limiting their resonance and uptake. One participant reflected, “They handed me a list. No follow-up. It’s tough when you’re alone” (ID-3, man, non-immigrant). Survivors did not passively accept recommendations; many critically assessed their applicability. However, in the absence of tailored guidance or follow-up, these acts of discernment sometimes led to disengagement rather than adaptation.

Collectively, these accounts underscore how system navigation is shaped by intersecting social positions and institutional barriers. Survivors acted within their means, but access to care remained contingent on navigating complex systems without the necessary structural or cultural supports. When care is not contextually relevant or logistically feasible, even well-intentioned guidance can reinforce exclusion, as illustrated earlier when participants described generic stroke books that “didn’t really pertain” to them (ID-15) and dietary advice they could not follow (ID-11, woman, immigrant).

Family and Social Networks: Source of Strength or Silent Gaps

Family was commonly described as a source of emotional strength and a motivator for recovery. Participants often cited their loved ones as reasons to maintain independence and persevere through the challenges of post-stroke life. However, the actual role of family in stroke prevention varied widely. Some received meaningful assistance, while others experienced minimal support or chose not to seek help, revealing a tension between valuing family as a source of emotional strength and managing recovery needs independently.

Participants frequently expressed a desire not to burden adult children or disrupt the lives of younger family members. One noted, “My son is busy with his own life… I don’t want to bother him” (ID-11, woman, immigrant). These expressions of restraint reflected agency enacted through silence, which we interpreted as being shaped by cultural values around intergenerational respect, reciprocity and non-interference, consistent with prior research on aging and caregiving norms. 18 This protective stance, while preserving relational harmony, also left some participants to manage their recovery needs alone.

In several cases, participants lived with family but described little caregiving involvement. The presence of others in the home did not always translate into practical support. As noted in field notes, “The assumption is there’s someone available… but what if that’s not true?” This discrepancy between proximity and participation complicates assumptions about the reliability of informal caregiving networks, especially among immigrant families where shared living arrangements may mask unmet needs.

Even when emotional closeness was present, it did not always correspond with tangible assistance. Participants frequently described strong relational ties while simultaneously emphasizing their self-sufficiency. “They love me but can’t help with daily things. I just do it myself” was a sentiment echoed across multiple interviews. This dynamic illustrates a form of relational autonomy in which participants prioritized emotional connection while managing the practical aspects of care independently.

Structural Assumptions About Self-Management

Participants’ experiences revealed a healthcare system that places the burden of recovery on individuals, assuming they will coordinate follow-up care, understand treatment plans, and adopt lifestyle changes after discharge. This expectation of autonomous self-management often failed to account for the structural and social conditions that limit patients’ capacity to do so, particularly among those with limited resources, health literacy, or confidence navigating the system.

Many participants reported that care was initiated only in response to acute events, with little emphasis on prevention or continuity. “Only if it’s necessary. Sometimes shit happens,” one participant explained (ID-15, man, non-immigrant). Another added, “I’ve not yet been told anything specific” (ID-9, man, non-immigrant). These accounts highlight a reactive system, where the responsibility for maintaining engagement rests on the patient, rather than being proactively supported by healthcare providers.

This fragmented model was particularly evident at the point of discharge. Several participants described a lack of follow-up. “You’re sent home and that’s it. No check-in” (ID-19, man, immigrant). Participants commonly perceived gaps in transition planning between acute treatment and ongoing secondary prevention. Although all were scheduled for at least one stroke prevention clinic visit, many described limited coordination and proactive follow-up across services (e.g., rehab, primary care, community supports), which left them shouldering much of the self-management and system navigation.

Participants also noted that the information provided was often generic or disconnected from their lived experience. Some reported receiving advice or resources that failed to reflect their cultural, financial, or social realities. “I didn’t identify with the stroke book. It didn’t feel like it was for me” (ID-9, man, non-immigrant). Another shared, “No help unless you can’t brush your teeth — that was their bar for care” (ID-22, woman, immigrant). These examples illustrate both structural mismatches, such as educational materials that do not speak to younger stroke survivors, and participants’ frustrations with navigating a complex system. While some accounts pointed to clear service gaps, others reflected perceptions of misalignment or lack of follow-up, underscoring the importance of tailoring education and clarifying care pathways.

Together, these accounts underscore how assumptions about self-management, such as expecting survivors to independently arrange follow-up care or modify lifestyle, can disadvantage those with mobility limitations, language barriers, or limited resources, thereby amplifying existing inequities in recovery.

Desire for Dignity and Agency

Across interviews, participants emphasized the importance of maintaining dignity, autonomy, and a sense of control during their recovery. Even when accepting assistance or facing limitations, they resisted being seen as passive or dependent. Their engagement with care was not marked by refusal, but by selective participation shaped by personal values, cultural expectations, and emotional needs.

Participants often pushed back against advice they perceived as infantilizing or overly directive, asserting their competence and self-awareness. One participant remarked, “I didn’t use the shower chair. I’m fine now” (ID-17, man, immigrant), while another explained, “I don’t need anyone to tell me not to eat… I did everything. Do you understand me?” (ID-11, woman, immigrant). These responses reflected a desire to remain self-determining within a system that could at times feel disempowering.

Many grounded their recovery in lifelong habits and identities, including discipline, cultural pride, and intellectual engagement. “I cooked meals before I left, so they wouldn’t go to waste” (ID-22, woman, immigrant), said one participant. She also described translating articles to keep her mind sharp: “If I lost that ability, that would be the end” (ID-22, woman, immigrant). Others framed themselves as capable and progressing steadily, even when receiving support: “I’ve just got progressively stronger… I don’t really need a lot of help. I can do things” (ID-21, woman, non-immigrant).

Expressions of competence and self-reliance were not only identity-affirming but also practical. Several participants lived alone or had limited support, making independence a necessity as much as a value. One woman described her detailed plan to walk to a clinic alone, arranging backup support in case she could not manage the return trip: “I said, no, I will walk… I don’t want to become dependent on everybody. If I can’t walk back, I’ll call you” (ID-22, woman, immigrant). These contingency plans reflect intentional, proactive efforts to preserve agency while navigating real physical and logistical limitations.

Spirituality and emotional meaning-making also shaped how participants approached prevention and recovery. Some expressed gratitude for having survived and described their motivation to avoid another stroke in deeply personal terms. “I was fortunate… I don’t want to have another stroke” (ID-8, man, immigrant). This internal motivation may not be easily captured by conventional behaviour change models, but it played a powerful role in shaping engagement with care.

Together, these accounts demonstrate how stroke survivors navigated recovery with a strong desire to preserve identity, autonomy, and self-worth. Their agency was not always visible through formal metrics, but it was embedded in everyday decisions to adapt, persist, and define support on their own terms.

Discussion

This study sought to identify the barriers and facilitators influencing stroke survivors’ participation in secondary stroke preventive care, with attention to how these experiences may differ in intensity or complexity for immigrants and long-term residents. We present themes in an integrated way because experiences were largely shared across participants, with immigrant survivors describing more layered challenges in some areas. While the original intent was to examine individual- and system-level factors impacting access, what emerged was a deeper recognition of how survivor engagement with prevention is shaped not merely by external circumstances, but by the ways survivors themselves act within and respond to their contexts. Survivor actions were not simply responses to care but expressions of agency constrained and co-produced by cultural, social, and structural conditions.

Self-reliance emerged as both a strategy and a burden; system navigation required relational resources and health literacy; and generic health messaging often missed the mark. Across these themes, survivors maintained a consistent desire for dignity, respect, and control over their recovery. These are not static factors but situational conditions within which their agency was enacted. At the same time, many participants expressed trust in clinicians and pride in gains made, particularly with medication adherence and gradual functional recovery, underscoring that gaps coexisted with positive experiences.

Situated agency offers a conceptual lens through which to understand how survivor actions emerge not as isolated decisions, but as embedded responses shaped by relationships, structures, and cultural values. In health research, situated agency has emerged as a conceptual alternative to explain how individuals navigate, resist, and adapt within social and structural constraints.13,19 This perspective moves beyond simplistic binaries of adherence versus non-adherence, emphasizing that agency is not exercised in isolation, but is embedded in cultural discourses, relational dynamics, personal histories, and spatial contexts. In other words, drawing on previous work,13,19,20 we view agency as an enacted process conditioned by context. Rather than simply measuring adherence or non-adherence, this lens helps illuminate how survivors adapt, resist, or selectively engage with care in ways that are meaningful to them. We extend this framework to stroke recovery, showing how survivors exercised agency by, for instance, prioritizing family caregiving, relying on spiritual interpretations of illness, or declining services that felt irrelevant or unhelpful. Their actions were shaped by more than personal preference; they were embedded in social worlds and institutional contexts.

Traditional models of secondary prevention often assume a divide between “me” (the patient) and “them” (the provider or system). Self-management is positioned as an individual task, disconnected from systemic or relational enablers. Our findings challenge this framing. Participants’ ability to engage with prevention was deeply tied to continuity of care, access to culturally relevant information, and support from trusted networks. In this way, agency becomes a shared space, neither fully individual nor entirely system-driven. Stroke prevention emerges as a co-constructed practice, shaped by survivor-system interactions. This reframing invites a shift in how health systems understand their role; that is, from dispensing information to co-producing conditions that support meaningful action.

Although themes were shared across immigrant and long-term resident survivors, some challenges were described as more layered by immigrants, particularly when navigating language barriers and unfamiliar systems. This pattern indicates that constrained agency is a universal feature of stroke survivorship but is magnified where structural and cultural barriers intersect. Recognizing this gradient avoids over-simplification while underscoring the need for supports tailored to survivors’ systemic and cultural contexts.

A cross-cutting finding across themes was the absence of tailored, contextually relevant information. Survivors often received advice that was vague, generic, or misaligned with their cultural practices, social conditions, or daily realities. This gap was evident in how participants described medication as the most feasible preventive strategy, in the confusion surrounding dietary and lifestyle recommendations, and in the reliance on self-sourced or improvised knowledge. While participants acted resourcefully, their agency was constrained by the inadequacy of information that could translate into meaningful action in their own contexts. This highlights that effective prevention depends not only on access to services but also on the provision of guidance that resonates with survivors’ lived experiences and priorities.

Implications for Practice and Policy

Our findings suggest the need to reconceptualize secondary stroke prevention as a co-constructed process, rather than a one-sided, individual responsibility (i.e., patient versus the system or provider). Community-engaged approaches offer a promising path forward. For instance, the Stroke Ready program, 21 and community-driven initiatives19,22 demonstrate how health systems can collaborate with local organizations to tailor stroke education and support. Studies by Janssen et al (2025) and Balakrishnan et al (2017) further emphasize the value of culturally safe, narrative-based, and participatory strategies that prioritize survivor voice and build trust.23,24 These approaches align with the concept of situated agency, which recognizes that meaningful engagement with care emerges from survivors’ embedded contexts. Policies and programs that enable relational continuity, contextually adapted education, and culturally responsive infrastructures are essential to supporting equitable recovery and long-term prevention, particularly for immigrant and racialized populations.

In addition, the burden of navigating fragmented systems was a recurring theme among participants, who often undertook complex tasks such as scheduling, interpreting care plans, and seeking referrals without sufficient and consistent structured support. This echoes broader calls for stroke care navigators or community-based coordination roles to bridge systemic gaps and reduce inequities.22,25 Navigation was not merely logistical but also emotional and relational, requiring survivors to advocate, interpret, and persist, often in isolation. Integrating formal navigation roles into stroke prevention programs could reduce this burden and enable survivors to focus on recovery rather than system management. When culturally grounded and community-embedded, navigation support can enhance relational continuity, improve trust, and strengthen survivors’ situated agency. Integrating a navigator or coordination role at stroke preventive clinics (or partnering with community agencies) could address the specific tasks survivors described (i.e., clarifying care plans, arranging referrals, and ensuring language-concordant follow-up) without shifting responsibility onto individual clinicians alone.

Implications for Research

Prior research has catalogued a wide range of barriers and facilitators to stroke self-management, including physical limitations, cognitive impairments, mood disorders, medication adherence, and resource access.26-29 Some studies have also noted cultural or faith-based influences. 10 However, these factors are typically treated as static variables, portrayed as either obstacles or enablers, rather than as dynamic and contextually negotiated elements of survivor experience.

Our study contributes a novel perspective by showing that cultural values, social roles, and relational obligations are not background influences but integral to how agency is enacted. Survivors described patterns such as self-reliance, spiritual meaning-making and continuation of caregiving roles despite limitations, which we interpreted as being shaped by cultural expectations and gendered caregiving norms. These factors influenced both their health behaviours and their understanding of what self-care meant in their lives. This aligns with prior findings that cultural identity can both enable and constrain chronic illness management, including among immigrants, 9 but extends this by illustrating the interactional and situated nature of these processes. Future research should (a) adopt frameworks that account for the situated and adaptive nature of agency, (b) examine how social positioning, including migration histories, shapes engagement with prevention, and (c) design interventions that respond to survivors’ everyday contexts rather than assuming uniform pathways of self-management. Future mixed-methods work could quantify the prominence of themes identified here (e.g., frequency of language-concordance trade-offs), complementing our interpretive findings.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in one metropolitan area; however, we had participants who lived in rural areas but were transferred to and hence received care in the metropolitan area. While the interviews were conducted in multiple languages by research assistants with culturally similar backgrounds, there may be difficulties in translating information to English, and the context of the discussion could be different. We also recruited participants from clinics, which means that those who may be socially vulnerable and not connected to formal pathways may be missed. The use of interpretive description prioritizes depth and contextual understanding but may limit theoretical generalizability. As with all qualitative research, the researcher’s interpretation may be influenced by positionality, though steps were taken to ensure reflexivity and analytic rigour.

Conclusions

In conclusion, survivor engagement with secondary prevention is shaped by external circumstances and the ways survivors act within and respond to their contexts, suggesting the need to develop interventions that empower survivors with the aim of improving secondary stroke prevention and reducing stroke recurrence.
