Origin of the two-armed vertical phase-spiral in the inner Galactic disk 2025 Origin of the two-armed vertical phase-spiral in the inner Galactic disk – D
Rimpei Chiba, Neige Frankel, Chris Hamilton

Abstract
Gaia recently revealed a two-armed spiral pattern in the vertical phase-space distribution of the inner Galactic disk (guiding radius R g ∼ 6.2 ​ kpc R_{\rm g}\sim 6.2\,{\rm kpc} ), indicating that some non-adiabatic perturbation symmetric about the mid-plane is driving the inner disk out of equilibrium. The non-axisymmetric structures in the disk (e.g., the bar or spiral arms) have been suspected to be the major source for such a perturbation. However, both the lifetime and the period of these internal perturbations are typically longer than the period at which stars oscillate vertically, implying that the perturbation is generally adiabatic. This issue is particularly pronounced in the inner Galaxy, where the vertical oscillation period is shorter and therefore adiabatically shielded more than the outer disk. We show that two-armed phase spirals can naturally form in the inner disk if there is a vertical resonance that breaks the adiabaticity; otherwise, their formation requires a perturber with an unrealistically short lifetime. We predict analytically and confirm with simulations that a steadily rotating (non-winding) two-armed phase spiral forms near the resonance when stars are subject to both periodic perturbations (e.g., by spiral arms) and stochastic perturbations (e.g., by giant molecular clouds). Due to the presence of multiple resonances, the vertical phase-space exhibits several local phase spirals that rotate steadily at distinct frequencies, together forming a global phase spiral that evolves over time. Our results demonstrate that, contrary to earlier predictions, the formation of the two-armed phase spiral does not require transient perturbations with lifetimes shorter than the vertical oscillation period.

Asano et al. 2025

These simulations demonstrate a clear causal link between the two-armed phase spiral and the internal disk perturbation. However, the underlying physical mechanism remains puzzling. While it has been known that bars and spiral arms induce breathing motions in the disk
(

;

;

, it is unclear how they could generate phase spirals, which specifically require a
non-adiabatic
perturbation to the disk, i.e., the timescale of the perturbation must be short compared to the vertical oscillation period – otherwise, the star’s vertical action would be conserved.
Li et al. 2023
provide an example of a disk perturbed by a bar and a spiral arm which exhibits a breathing motion, but no phase spirals.
Banik et al. 2022
;
Banik et al. 2023
conducted a linear analyses of the disk response to various perturbations and suggested that the formation of a two-armed phase spiral requires the internal perturber to rapidly grow and decay over a timescale comparable to the vertical oscillation period, which is as short as
70\,{\rm Myr}
in the inner disk.

This paper provides a theoretical explanation as to why two-armed phase spirals can form in the inner disk using a combination of linear analysis, nonlinear analysis, and test-particle simulations. The key ingredients are resonance and diffusion. It is well known that resonances can break the adiabaticity of the system even in a slowly varying potential [see
Weinberg 1994
for a general discussion]. However, while resonant perturbations can temporarily generate an open phase spiral, resonant forcing alone will eventually lead to a closed ring-like structure composed of a chain of resonant islands, as stars near resonances librate and gradually phase mix within the resonances. When these stars are also subject to small-scale stochastic kicks, which inevitably exist in both real and simulated galaxies, the phase mixing along the motion of libration remains incomplete, allowing the open spiral pattern to be preserved. As we elaborate in the main text, this competition between persistent resonant forcing and diffusion due to small-scale kicks results in an open two-armed phase spiral that rotates
steadily
without winding, with its shape set by the diffusion timescale rather than the timing of the perturbation, similar to the argument by
Tremaine
et al. 2023
. In the case of a transient perturbation, the phase spiral starts winding up once the perturbation has decayed.

2.3
Stochastic perturbation

In addition to the periodic perturbation by spiral arms, we subject the disk stars with small-scale stochastic kicks due to gravitational encounters with, for example, giant molecular clouds
(
Carlberg 1987
;
Jenkins &
Binney 1990
;
Aumer
et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
and dark matter substructures
(

;
Kazantzidis et al. 2009
, e.g.,)
. Following
Tremaine

, we model this process by a Gaussian random walk in
(q,p)
space, that is, the

of the displacement is zero,
\langle\Delta q\rangle=\langle\Delta p\rangle=0
, while that of the squared displacement over time
\Delta t
is

\displaystyle\langle(\Delta q)^{2}\rangle=D\Delta t,~~~\langle(\Delta p)^{2}\rangle=D\Delta t,

(13)

where
D
is the diffusion coefficient, which we assume to be constant. Under this process, it can be shown that the mean action of the disk changes at rate,
\mathrm{d}\overline{J_{z}}/\mathrm{d}t=D
(Appendix
C
). To determine the diffusion coefficient, we assume that the disk was initially razor thin (i.e., its initial mean action is zero,
\overline{J_{z}}=0
) and was gradually heated over time

to reach its present mean action,
\overline{J_{z}}(T)
. Then

\displaystyle D=\frac{\overline{J_{z}}(T)}{T}=\frac{1}{T}\int\mathrm{d}\theta_{z}\int\mathrm{d}J_{z}J_{z}f_{0}(J_{z})\sim\frac{1.80\sigma h}{T}.

(14)

By default, we adopt

, which gives
D\simeq 0.94\,{\rm kpc}^{2}\,{\rm Gyr}^{-2}
.

We note that subjecting stars to random walk in the
(q,p)

space results in little difference (Appendix
C
), provided we determine the diffusion coefficients in
(z,v_{z})
from their present mean-squared value

\displaystyle D_{z}=\frac{\overline{z^{2}}}{T}\sim\frac{3.62h^{2}}{T},~~~~D_{v}=\frac{\overline{v_{z}^{2}}}{T}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{T}.

(15)

Therefore, in our test-particle simulations (Section
5
), we apply kicks in the
(z,v_{z})
coordinates, which is computationally less expensive.

3
Linear analysis

In this section, we study the disk response to spiral arms under stochastic perturbations using linear theory. The general framework has been laid out in detail by
Banik et al. 2023
. Here, we examine the particular case where the disk is subject to
persistent
spiral arms in the presence of small-scale random kicks, which was not analysed in
Banik et al. 2023
. As we shall see, this leads to an intriguing result that a steady (non-winding) spiral pattern forms in phase space.

The evolution of the distribution function
f
with a stochastic process is governed by the kinetic equation:

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}t}+[f,H]=C[f],

(16)

where
H
is the Hamiltonian of the system,
[\cdot,\cdot]
is the Poisson bracket, and
C[f]
is the “collision” operator. The Gaussian random walk introduced in Section
2.3
is described by the following collision operator (Appendix
C
)

\displaystyle C[f]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}D\left(\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{{\partial}q^{2}}+\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{{\partial}p^{2}}\right)=D\left(\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}J_{z}}J_{z}\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}J_{z}}+\frac{1}{4J_{z}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{{\partial}\theta_{z}^{2}}\right).

(17)

As we will find, the perturbation by a persistent spiral arm is localized near resonances, so derivatives with respect to
J_{z}
dominate. Hence we may approximate

\displaystyle C[f]

\displaystyle\simeq DJ_{z}\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{{\partial}J_{z}^{2}}.

(18)

We expand the Hamiltonian
H=H_{0}(J_{z})+\Phi_{1}(\theta_{z},J_{z},t)
and the distribution function
f=f_{0}(J_{z})+f_{1}(\theta_{z},J_{z},t)
, where
f_{0}
is the initial unperturbed distribution. The linearized kinetic equation is

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}f_{1}}{{\partial}t}+[f_{1},H_{0}]+[f_{0},\Phi_{1}]=C[f_{0}]+C[f_{1}].

(19)

Fourier transforming the perturbations in
\theta_{z}

\displaystyle f_{1}(\theta_{z},J_{z},t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{z}}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}(J_{z},t)\,\mathrm{e}^{in_{z}\theta_{z}},

(20)

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(\theta_{z},J_{z},t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{z}}\hat{\Phi}_{n_{z}}(J_{z},t)\,\mathrm{e}^{in_{z}\theta_{z}},

(21)

we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}}{{\partial}t}+in_{z}\Omega_{z}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}-in_{z}\frac{{\partial}f_{0}}{{\partial}J_{z}}\hat{\Phi}_{n_{z}}=DJ_{z}\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{{\partial}J_{z}^{2}}\left(\delta_{n_{z},0}f_{0}+\hat{f}_{n_{z}}\right).

(22)

The equation for

describes the slow evolution of the angle-independent distribution due to diffusion. Here, we are interested in the evolution of the angle-dependent perturbation (
n_{z}\neq 0
), for which we have the following approximate solution, given the initial condition
\hat{f}_{n_{z}}=0
(Appendix
D
):

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{n_{z}}(J_{z},t)\!=\!in_{z}\frac{{\partial}f_{0}}{{\partial}J_{z}}\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathrm{e}^{-in_{z}\Omega_{z}(t-t^{\prime})-\left[(t-t^{\prime})/t_{\rm d}\right]^{3}}\hat{\Phi}_{n_{z}}(J_{z},t^{\prime}),

(23)

where

\displaystyle t_{\rm d}=[3/(n_{z}^{2}\Omega_{z}^{\prime 2}J_{z}D)]^{1/3}

(24)

is the diffusion timescale. The solution contains a super-exponential decay factor,
\exp\left[-(t/t_{\rm d})^{3}\right]
, arising from the joint effect of phase mixing and diffusion: phase mixing turns structures into increasingly smaller scale, while diffusion efficiently erases the resulting small-scale structures. Without phase mixing, the perturbation will undergo an exponential decay, which is much slower than super-exponential.

The Fourier coefficient of the spiral perturbation (equation
8
) is

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{n_{z}}(J_{z},t)=\hat{\Psi}_{n_{z}}(J_{z})\mathcal{T}(t)\sum_{l=\pm 1}\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-il\omega t},

(25)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{n_{z}}(J_{z})\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}\theta_{z}\Psi(z)\,\mathrm{e}^{-in_{z}\theta_{z}}.

(26)

Substituting (
25
) to (
23
), switching variable to
\tau=t-t^{\prime}
, and inverse Fourier transforming, we obtain the angle-dependent part of the response

\displaystyle f_{1}(\theta_{z},J_{z},t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n_{z},l=\pm 1}in_{z}\frac{{\partial}f_{0}}{{\partial}J_{z}}\hat{\Psi}_{n_{z}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-il\omega t}\,\mathrm{e}^{in_{z}\theta_{z}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathcal{T}(t-\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\Omega_{\rm s}\tau-\left(\tau/t_{\rm d}\right)^{3}},

(27)

where
\Omega_{\rm s}=n_{z}\Omega_{z}-l\omega
is the “slow” frequency, which is zero at the resonance,
(n_{z},l)=(\pm N_{z},\pm 1)
. In the time-asymptotic limit
(t\rightarrow\infty)
, the integral converges to a constant value since
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow 1
and
\exp[-(\tau/t_{\rm d})^{3}]\rightarrow 0
. This leaves the time dependence of equation (
27
) solely in the exponential function,
\exp(-il\omega t)
, indicating that long after the perturbation has emerged, the response rotates steadily in phase space at the forcing frequency
\omega
.

Figure 4:

) in the time-asymptotic limit. The disk is subject to a periodic vertical perturbation by galactic spiral arms in the presence of small-scale random kicks. The black curve marks the resonance.

We now examine the shape of the response in the time-asymptotic limit. Let us set
\mathcal{T}=1
(ignore the transient effects due to the growth of the spiral arm) and solve the time integral by Taylor expanding the integrand in
\Omega_{\rm s}
about the resonance
\Omega_{\rm s}=0
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\Omega_{\rm s}\tau-\left(\tau/t_{\rm d}\right)^{3}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\sum_{k=0}\frac{(-i\Omega_{\rm s}\tau)^{k}}{k!}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\left(\tau/t_{\rm d}\right)^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{\rm d}}{3}\sum_{k=0}\frac{(-i\Omega_{\rm s}t_{\rm d})^{k}}{k!}\Gamma\left(\frac{k+1}{3}\right),

(28)

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function. Since the response is localized at the resonance, we truncate the series up to
k=1
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\Omega_{\rm s}\tau-\left(\tau/t_{\rm d}\right)^{3}}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{t_{\rm d}}{3}\left[\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)-i\Omega_{\rm s}t_{\rm d}\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\Omega_{\rm s}^{2}t_{\rm d}^{2})\right]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{t_{\rm d}}{3}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\alpha\Omega_{\rm s}t_{\rm d}},

(29)

where
\alpha=\Gamma(2/3)/\Gamma(1/3)\simeq 0.51
. The diffusion timescale at the resonance is
t_{\rm d}\simeq 0.35\,{\rm Gyr}
, so the above truncation is valid only in the close vicinity of the resonance
|\Omega_{\rm s}|<1/t_{\rm d}\simeq 3\,{\rm Gyr}^{-1}
. Plugging the result back to equation (
27
), we have

\displaystyle f_{1}(\theta_{z},J_{z},t)\simeq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n_{z},l=\pm 1}in_{z}\frac{{\partial}f_{0}}{{\partial}J_{z}}\hat{\Psi}_{n_{z}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-il\omega t}\frac{t_{\rm d}}{3}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{i(n_{z}\theta_{z}-\alpha\Omega_{\rm s}t_{\rm d})}.

(30)

The factor,
\exp[i(n_{z}\theta_{z}-\alpha\Omega_{\rm s}t_{\rm d})]
, implies that the shape of the steadily rotating response winds with increasing distance from the resonance, indicating a steady phase spiral. The phase spiral is predicted to be trailing consistent with the observed pattern (Fig.
1
). The degree of winding is set by the diffusion time
t_{\rm d}
, similar to the prediction by
Tremaine
et al. 2023
, who explored the origin of the one-armed phase spiral in the presence of diffusion. The equation also exhibits a factor,
t_{\rm d}
, which implies that the response vanishes in the strongly diffusive limit (
t_{\rm d}\rightarrow 0
) as expected.

Our linear analysis demonstrates that a two-armed phase spiral can form even if the vertical oscillation period is much shorter than the lifetime of the galactic spiral arms, revising the conclusion of

4
Nonlinear analysis

The linear analysis in the previous section may break down near resonances if the response becomes nonlinear. As detailed in
Hamilton et al. 2023
, the validity of linear theory in the presence of diffusion is determined by the dimensionless diffusion parameter
\Delta
(equation
37
). In the collisionless limit
(\Delta=0)
, linear theory remains valid for less than a libration period before nonlinear effects, i.e., resonant trapping, become significant
(
Chiba &
Schönrich 2022
)
. In the ‘collisional’ regime
(\Delta\gtrsim 1)
, however, the stochastic perturbations can suppress the development of nonlinear structures, keeping the system in the linear regime
(
Pao 1988
;
Catto 2020
;
Hamilton et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. In this section, we examine how the disk response varies with
\Delta
by solving the nonlinear kinetic equation. This nonlinear (non-perturbative) approach also provides key insights into the formation mechanism of the steady phase-spiral.

Our analysis closely follows the work by
Hamilton et al. 2023
, who studied the impact of diffusion on the bar-halo resonant interaction. The basic strategy is to identify the Hamiltonian
H
that is conserved in the absence of diffusion and then investigate numerically how the distribution of stars evolve according to
H
when stochastic forces are added.

We begin by Fourier expanding the perturbation (
8
) in
\theta_{z}
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(\theta_{z},J_{z},t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}(t)\bigg[\sum_{n_{z}}\hat{\Psi}_{n_{z}}(J_{z})\,\mathrm{e}^{in_{z}\theta_{z}}\bigg]\bigg[\sum_{l=\pm 1}\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-il\omega t}\bigg],

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{T}(t)}{2}\sum_{n_{z},l=\pm 1}\hat{\Psi}_{n_{z}}(J_{z})\,\mathrm{e}^{i(n_{z}\theta_{z}-l\omega t)},

(31)

where
\hat{\Psi}_{n_{z}}
is defined in equation (
26
).
Since we are interested in the steady state response against a persistent spiral arm that had emerged in the distant past, we henceforth set
\mathcal{T}(t)=1
. Near a resonance,

\displaystyle N_{z}\Omega_{z}-\omega=0,

(32)

all terms in (
31
) except those with
(n_{z},l)=(N_{z},1)
and
(-N_{z},-1)
give rise to rapid oscillations which can be averaged out. Hence, while modelling the slow nonlinear dynamics in the vicinity of each resonance, it suffices to consider only the corresponding resonant term

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(\theta_{z},J_{z},t)

\displaystyle=|\hat{\Psi}_{N_{z}}(J_{z})|\cos\left(N_{z}\theta_{z}-\omega t+\arg\hat{\Psi}_{N_{z}}\right),

(33)

where we have used the relation
\hat{\Psi}_{-n_{z}}=\hat{\Psi}_{n_{z}}^{\ast}
since the function
\Psi
is real. For the two-armed phase-spiral
N_{z}=2
, the real part of
\hat{\Psi}_{2}
is negative, while its imaginary part is zero, so
\arg\hat{\Psi}_{2}=\pi
.

Figure 5:

), describing the evolution of the disk subject to both persistent spiral perturbations and stochastic perturbations. We plot the perturbation

increases from left to right, the perturbed distribution near the resonance becomes asymmetric about

, resulting in a spiral pattern in the
(z,v_{z})
space. The strength of diffusion in our standard model is
\Delta\simeq 0.2
(middle column).

We may simplify the equation by performing a canonical transformation to the now standard slow angle-action variable
(\theta_{\rm s},J_{\rm s})

(
Lynden-Bell 1979
;
Tremaine &
Weinberg 1984
, e.g.,)
:

\displaystyle\theta_{\rm s}=N_{z}\theta_{z}-\omega t+\arg\hat{\Psi}_{N_{z}},~~~J_{\rm s}=J_{z}/N_{z}

(34)

using the generating function
S(\theta_{z},J_{\rm s},t)=(N_{z}\theta_{z}-\omega t+\arg\hat{\Psi}_{N_{z}})J_{\rm s}
.
The new Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(\theta_{s},J_{\rm s})

\displaystyle=H+\frac{{\partial}S}{{\partial}t}=H_{0}(J_{\rm s})-\omega J_{\rm s}+|\hat{\Psi}_{N_{z}}(J_{\rm s})|\cos\theta_{\rm s},

(35)

which is time independent as we have moved to a frame rotating at the perturbing frequency
\omega
. We may thus obtain a comprehensive view of the dynamics by drawing the level curves of
H^{\prime}
in the slow angle-action space. The second row of Fig.
5
illustrates these curves with dashed black. As is typical of a resonant system, there are two distinct families of orbits near the resonance: trapped and untrapped. The trapped orbits exhibit oscillatory motion in the slow angle (libration), while the untrapped orbits freely explore the full
2\pi
range (circulation). We also plot the corresponding level curves in the
(z,v_{z})
space (Fig.
5
, fourth row), although we caution that
H
is not stationary here: the level curves continuously rotate. The configuration of these curves bears a close resemblance to the contours of the Jacobi integral in barred galaxies
(
Contopoulos 1978
, e.g.,)
.

Having modeled the Hamiltonian near the resonance, we now compute the evolution of the phase-space distribution in the presence of diffusion using the kinetic equation (
16
) introduced in the previous section. Substituting the Hamiltonian (
35
), the nonlinear kinetic equation reads

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}f_{1}}{{\partial}t}+\left(\Omega_{\rm s}+\frac{{\partial}|\hat{\Psi}_{N_{z}}|}{{\partial}J_{\rm s}}\cos\theta_{\rm s}\right)\frac{{\partial}f_{1}}{{\partial}\theta_{\rm s}}+|\hat{\Psi}_{N_{z}}|\sin\theta_{\rm s}\frac{{\partial}(f_{0}+f_{1})}{{\partial}J_{\rm s}}

\displaystyle=C[f_{0}]+C[f_{1}],

(36)

where
\Omega_{\rm s}=N_{z}\Omega_{z}-\omega
. We now assume that
C[f_{0}]
drives the flattening of the overall distribution on a timescale much longer than the evolution near the resonance. With this assumption, we may ignore
C[f_{0}]
and seek a steady state solution of the response
f_{1}
subject to the initial condition
f_{1}=0
and boundary condition
f_{1}=0
far from the resonance. In practice, we set the boundary at
(J_{\rm s}^{\rm min},J_{\rm s}^{\rm max})=(0,30)\,{\rm kpc}^{2}\,{\rm Gyr}^{-1}
and solve the equation on a
200\times 300
grid in
(\theta_{\rm s},J_{\rm s})
space using the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method.

We note that the kinetic equation analysed in
Hamilton et al. 2023
takes a simpler form as it is based on the pendulum approximation, that is, the Hamiltonian is Taylor expanded around the resonance to leading order. While the pendulum approximation provides a useful analytical treatment of the dynamics in certain limits
(
Chiba &
Schönrich 2022
;
Hamilton et al. 2023
)
, its accuracy is poor when the phase flow is significantly asymmetric about the resonance, as in our case (Fig.
5
, second row), where the resonance lies close to the origin. Since we are computing the kinetic equation numerically, we refrain from making the pendulum approximation and precompute the derivatives of the Hamiltonian at each grid points.

Fig.
5
shows the phase-space distribution of stars at
t=10\,{\rm Gyr}
, by which the response has reached a steady state. We plot the solution for three different values of the dimensionless diffusion parameter:

\displaystyle\Delta\equiv\left[\frac{DJ_{\rm s}}{N_{z}}\sqrt{-\frac{{\partial}\Omega_{\rm s}}{{\partial}J_{\rm s}}|\hat{\Psi}_{N_{z}}|^{-3}}\right]_{J_{\rm s}=J_{\rm s,res}},

(37)

which is the ratio of the timescale for stars to librate around the resonance and the timescale for stars to diffuse across the resonance [see
Hamilton et al. 2023
for a detailed discussion]. In our standard stochastic model (Section
2.3
),
\Delta\simeq 0.2
, corresponding to the middle column of Fig.
5
.

When diffusion is very weak
\Delta=0.02
(left), libration dominates over diffusion. In this case, stars phase mix along the dashed black curves, thereby forming a flat distribution within the trapped region. The resulting distribution is almost symmetric about
\theta_{\rm s}=0
, and the corresponding pattern in the
(z,v_{z})
space appears to be a closed ring composed of two resonant islands.

At the level of diffusion expected in our Galaxy
\Delta=0.2
(middle), phase mixing along the motion of libration gets appreciably disrupted, causing the system to settle into a steady distribution that is asymmetric about
\theta_{\rm s}=0
. When mapped to the
(z,v_{z})
space, this asymmetric distribution manifests as an open phase spiral, in qualitative agreement with linear theory, though quantitatively not identical, underscoring the limitation of linear theory in this regime. Physically, the periodic force by the spiral arm is constantly pushing stars at
0<\theta_{\rm s}<\pi
towards large
J_{\rm s}
, but these stars fail to librate back to their original position because of the stochastic forces. This results in an overdense arm (a phase spiral) emanating from the unstable fixed point at
\theta_{\rm s}=\pi
.

As diffusion strengthen further
\Delta=2
(right), the phase spiral becomes weaker and less tightly wound, approaching the linear prediction, which is valid for
\Delta\gtrsim 1

(
Hamilton et al. 2023
)
– strong diffusion has effectively “relinearized” the dynamics.

but the order of each set of resonances are reversed.

6
Discussion

6.1
Phase-space structure preserved by diffusion

An important physical insight from our work is the crucial role played by diffusion in
preserving
(rather than destroying) phase spirals. Persistent periodic perturbations initially drive phase spirals near resonances, but it is diffusion that maintains them over time by preventing near-resonant stars from phase mixing and making the distribution symmetric in the slow angle
(
Hamilton et al. 2023
, Section
4
, see also)
. This stands in contrast to the earlier work by
Tremaine
et al. 2023
, where diffusion acted to wipe out the phase spirals. The key difference is that they considered stochastic white-noise forcing — a series of uncorrelated large-scale impulsive kicks — rather than persistent periodic forcing. In their model, the phase spirals were generated by the impulsive kicks, and the role of diffusion was merely to destroy them. Our result suggests that, contrary to earlier expectations, diffusion may not always act to erase structures in phase space; instead, under certain conditions, diffusion can actively preserve phase-space structures, with implications beyond the inner-disk phase spiral studied here.

6.2
Two-armed phase spirals in the Milky Way

Since spiral arms are prevalent throughout the galactic disk and there are many resonances with similar strength (Fig.
3
), our results suggest that the two-armed phase spiral should be excited ubiquitously across the disk. Indeed,
Hunt et al. 2022
found in their high-resolution simulation of an isolated galaxy that two-armed phase spirals emerge over a range of radii as the disk undergoes secular evolution. This raises the question of why the two-armed phase spiral has been observed only in the inner disk and not in the Solar neighborhood and beyond. Gaia’s observation suggests that the overall amplitude of the combined spiral structure does not decline strongly with radius, even though individual arms may peak near their respective corotation radii
(
Eilers et al. 2020
;
Gaia
Collaboration et al. 2023
)
. Moreover, it is unlikely that the two-armed phase spiral is obscured by the more dominant one-armed phase spiral in the outer disk:
Frankel et al. 2023
found no clear evidence of a two-armed phase spiral in the residual between the data and the one-armed model. Furthermore, stellar diffusion is expected to be stronger in the inner disk, where the density of giant molecular clouds is higher. It is therefore nontrivial that we observe the two-armed phase spirals only in the inner disk. This remains a topic for future investigation.

6.3
Galactic bar

Similar to spiral arms, the bar in our Galaxy may also generate two-armed phase spirals through its resonances. However, within the framework of our simple model, the bar is disfavored as the main driver of the observed pattern because there are no strong vertical resonances near the orbits of stars forming the two-armed phase spiral. These stars have a guiding radius of
R_{\rm g}\simeq 6.2\,{\rm kpc}
, which is close to the bar’s corotation radius,
R_{\rm CR}=5.8-6.9\,{\rm kpc}

(
Binney 2020
;
Chiba &
Schönrich 2021
;
Clarke &
Gerhard 2022
;
Lucey
et al. 2022
;
Leung et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Dillamore et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. This means that the azimuthal frequency of these stars with respect to the bar (
\Omega_{\varphi}-\Omega_{\rm p}
) is very low, and hence to have a vertical resonance at a high vertical frequency, the vertical motion of stars must resonate with their radial motion. However, unlike spiral arms, the bar’s vertical potential does not have a strong radial dependence, so the resonances involving the radial motion (
N_{R}\neq 0
) are much weaker than that of the spiral arms (Appendix
B
). This renders the bar difficult to create a strong phase spiral at the observed phase space.

The consideration above relies on a variety of assumptions made in our model. Of these, we have assumed that the perturber has a fixed pattern speed. Using test-particle simulations,
Li et al. 2023
demonstrates that a bar can temporarily generate a pronounced two-armed phase spiral if its pattern speed decreases over time, causing the resonances to constantly move. A moving resonance indeed leaves behind a striated pattern in the slow angle-action space
(
Chiba 2023
)
, which may appear as a spiral in the
(z,v_{z})
space. It would be interesting to see whether and for how long these patterns survive in a realistic, noisy environment.

7
Summary

We studied the mechanism by which galactic spiral arms induce two-armed phase spirals in the vertical motion of the stellar disk. While past theories predict that the formation of such a phase spiral requires a non-adiabatic (impulsive) perturbation that grows and decays over a timescale much shorter than the vertical oscillation period (
\lesssim 0.1\,{\rm Gyr}
), we show that spiral arms with a realistically long lifetime (
\gtrsim 0.1\,{\rm Gyr}
) can in fact give rise to a two-armed phase spiral if there are (i) resonances, which break the adiabaticity, and (ii) stochastic kicks (e.g., due to molecular cloud scattering), which break the angular symmetry of the perturbed distribution, turning ring-like resonant structures into open spirals.

The formation process of the two-armed phase spiral is best understood by first considering an ideal case where the disk is persistently perturbed by a potential that is symmetric in
z
. In this case, stars with vertical oscillation frequencies close to the perturbing frequency become resonantly trapped and librate around the resonance. Over time, the distribution of these trapped stars phase-mixes along the perturbed orbit, forming a new equilibrium distribution with a ring-like structure that rotates steadily in the
(z,v_{z})
space at the resonant frequency. When a stochastic force is introduced, these stars diffuse through phase space, preventing them from completing a full cycle of libration. As a result, phase mixing remains incomplete. This leads to a steady distribution that is asymmetric in phase with respect to the center of libration (
\theta_{\rm s}=0
): due to the negative gradient in the initial distribution function, the density of stars pushed towards large action at
\theta_{\rm s}>0
is always higher than that of those pulled towards small action at
\theta_{\rm s}<0
. When this asymmetric distribution is projected onto the
(z,v_{z})
space, it manifests as an open phase spiral that rotates steadily without winding.

For transient spiral arms, the phase spiral induced near the resonance winds up and vanishes once the spiral arms disappear. In this situation, the role of diffusion in forming the phase spiral becomes minor. However, resonance still remains essential: for the phase spiral to form far from the resonance, the characteristic lifetime of the perturbation must be unrealistically short (Fig.
9
). Furthermore, the amplitude of the phase spiral generated by such an extremely short-lived spiral arm is too weak, since the total work done by the perturbation scales with its lifetime.

In realistic systems, we expect the vertical phase-space to host a high density of closely spaced resonances because there are possibly multiple spiral arms with different pattern speeds, and also because the temporal spectra of the spiral perturbation has large powers at high-order resonances when the orbit has a large radial motion (Fig.
3
). Since each resonance creates a local phase spiral, which rotates steadily at distinct frequencies, they give rise to a global spiral pattern in the
(z,v_{z})
space that appears to be shearing, in close analogy with the dynamics of the spiral arms themselves
(
Sellwood &
Carlberg 2019
)
.

We find that a spiral arm with surface density amplitude recently measured by
Eilers et al. 2020
and pattern speed
\Omega_{\rm p}=28\,{\rm Gyr}^{-1}
, corresponding to the Sagittarius-Carina arm, can naturally generate a two-armed phase spiral with the right level of amplitude as the observed data. More detailed modelling of the data would require careful treatment of selection effects
(
Frankel et al. 2023
)
, which can introduce
N_{z}=2
signals, as well as accounting for the amplification of the response by self-gravity
(
Widrow 2023
)
and the damping effects of horizontal mixing
(
Banik et al. 2022
;
Banik et al. 2023
)
.

Our study highlights the crucial role of resonances in the formation of two-armed phase spirals. Although phase spirals in real noisy environment may not show obvious signs of resonances, removing the effects of stellar diffusion reveals that they are in fact structured by discrete resonances. Uncovering these hidden phase-space structures can shed light into the nature of both the periodic perturbation and the stochastic perturbation.

Appendix A
Model of spiral arm

In this appendix, we describe our model for the spiral arm. We consider the following potential-density pair:

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}(R,\varphi,z,t)

\displaystyle=\xi(z)\Phi_{\rm a}(R)\cos\left[m(\varphi-\Omega_{\rm p}t+\cot\alpha\ln R)\right],

(38)

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(R,\varphi,z,t)

\displaystyle=\zeta(z)\Sigma_{\rm a}(R)\cos\left[m(\varphi-\Omega_{\rm p}t+\cot\alpha\ln R)\right],

(39)

where
\zeta(z)
describes the vertical density profile of the spiral, normalized such that
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\zeta(z)=1
.
\Phi_{\rm a}(R)
and
\Sigma_{\rm a}(R)
are the amplitudes of the potential and the surface density, respectively.
m=2
is the spiral’s azimuthal wave number,
\Omega_{\rm p}=28\,{\rm Gyr}^{-1}
is its pattern speed
(
Grosbøl &
Carraro 2018
;
Dias et al. 2019
;
Monteiro et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
, and
\alpha=12^{\circ}
is its pitch angle
(
Vallée 2015
;
Eilers et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. The radial wave number is
k(R)=m\cot\alpha/R\sim 1.5\,{\rm kpc}^{-1}
at
R=6.2\,{\rm kpc}
. The potential-density pair satisfies the Poisson’s equation
\nabla^{2}\Phi_{1}=4\pi G\rho_{1}
. We compute the Poisson’s equation using the tight-winding approximation, i.e.,
kR\gg 1
and
k|\mathrm{d}\ln\Phi_{\rm a}/\mathrm{d}R|^{-1}\gg 1
. In this regime, the Poisson equation reduces to

\displaystyle\left[{\partial}^{2}_{z}\xi(z)-k(R)^{2}\xi(z)\right]\Phi_{\rm a}(R)=4\pi G\zeta(z)\Sigma_{\rm a}(R).

(40)

In a razor thin disk, i.e.,
\zeta(z)=\delta(z)
, the vertical profile of the potential is exponential
\xi(z)=-\,\mathrm{e}^{-k|z|}

(
Binney &
Tremaine 2008
)
, which generates a discontinuous force at the mid-plane. In a disk with finite thickness, we expect the potential to be smooth, so that the force continuously switches sign at the origin. We therefore consider the following softened exponential

\displaystyle\xi(z)=-\,\mathrm{e}^{-k\sqrt{z^{2}+z_{\rm s}^{2}}},

(41)

where
z_{\rm s}
is the softening length. This functional form has an advantage over commonly used functions such as
\,{\rm sech}^{2}(kz)

(
Faure
et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
in that the degree of softening can be freely adjusted. Substituting this to the Poisson’s equation yields

\displaystyle\zeta(z)=\frac{\Phi_{\rm a}k^{2}}{4\pi G\Sigma_{\rm a}}\left(\frac{z_{\rm s}^{2}}{z^{2}+z_{\rm s}^{2}}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{k\sqrt{z^{2}+z_{\rm s}^{2}}}\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{-k\sqrt{z^{2}+z_{\rm s}^{2}}}.

(42)

Since
\zeta>0
at
z=0
,
\zeta=0
at
|z|\rightarrow\infty
, and
\zeta^{\prime}<0~(\zeta^{\prime}>0)
at
z>0~(z<0)
,
\zeta
is nowhere negative. We next determine
\Phi_{\rm a}(R)
from the normalization condition:

\displaystyle 1=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}z\zeta(z)=\frac{\Phi_{\rm a}k}{2\pi G\Sigma_{\rm a}}P(a),

(43)

where

\displaystyle P(a)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\frac{a^{2}}{x^{2}+a^{2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}+a^{2}}}\right)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{x^{2}+a^{2}}},

(44)

and
x\equiv kz
and
a\equiv kz_{\rm s}
. To compute the integral, we first consider the following function

\displaystyle I(a)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{x^{2}+a^{2}}}=aK_{1}(a),

(45)

where
K_{1}(a)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. Equation (
45
) is obtained by changing variables
x=a\sinh(t)
. Taking the second derivative of
I
with respect to
a
, we find

\displaystyle I^{\prime\prime}(a)

\displaystyle=a^{-1}I^{\prime}(a)+P(a),

(46)

\displaystyle\therefore P(a)

\displaystyle=aK_{1}^{\prime\prime}(a)+K_{1}^{\prime}(a)-a^{-1}K_{1}(a).

(47)

Using the relation
K_{\nu}^{\prime}=-K_{\nu-1}-\nu a^{-1}K_{\nu}
and
K_{-\nu}=K_{\nu}
, we get

\displaystyle P(a)=I(a)=aK_{1}(a).

(48)

Hence, from equation (
43
), we finally have

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm a}(R)=\frac{2\pi G\Sigma_{\rm a}(R)}{k(R)}aK_{1}(a).

(49)

In the limit
z_{\rm s}\rightarrow 0~(a\rightarrow 0)
, the equation recovers the solution for the razor thin disk, i.e.,
\Phi_{\rm a}=2\pi G\Sigma_{\rm a}/k
.

We assume that the amplitude of the spiral’s surface density has a Gaussian profile with a peak at the corotation radius
R_{\rm CR}\equiv v_{\rm c}/\Omega_{\rm p}
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\rm a}(R)=\Sigma_{\rm max}\,\mathrm{e}^{-(R-R_{\rm CR})^{2}/(2R_{\beta}^{2})},

(50)

where
R_{\beta}\equiv\beta\sqrt{2}R_{\rm CR}/m
is the width of the radial profile, which is controlled by the parameter
\beta
defined as the ratio between
R_{\beta}
and the distance between the corotation resonance and the Lindblad resonances,
\sqrt{2}R_{\rm CR}/m

(
Hamilton
et al. 2024
)
.
Note that our model differs from that of
Hamilton
et al. 2024
, who assume a Gaussian profile for the potential, rather than for the surface density. The potential amplitude of our model is

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm a}(R)=\frac{2\pi G\Sigma_{\rm max}aK_{1}(a)}{m\cot\alpha}R\,\mathrm{e}^{-(R-R_{\rm CR})^{2}/(2R_{\beta}^{2})},

(51)

which similarly decays away from the corotation radius, although the peak is shifted to

\displaystyle R_{\rm peak}=R_{\rm CR}\frac{1+\sqrt{1+8\beta/m^{2}}}{2}.

(52)

In our model with
m=2
and
\beta=0.5
, we have
R_{\rm peak}\sim 1.2R_{\rm CR}
.

Appendix B
Resonances of galactic bar

Our study demonstrates that two-armed phase spirals can be generated by the combined effect of periodic resonant perturbations and stochastic perturbations. We attributed the former to galactic spiral arms, though, in principle, the galactic bar could play a similar role if it can strongly resonate with the vertical motion of stars in the disk. Here, we investigate whether such a strong resonance exists.

Fig.
13
shows the spectra of the bar’s potential presented in
Chiba
et al. 2021
along an in-plane unperturbed orbit with
(J_{R},J_{\varphi},J_{z})=(80,1440,0)\,{\rm kpc}^{2}\,{\rm Gyr}^{-1}
, which is the action of stars from which the two-armed phase spiral was found. We show results for three different bar pattern speeds favoured by recent measurements
(
Binney 2020
;
Chiba &
Schönrich 2021
;
Clarke &
Gerhard 2022
;
Lucey
et al. 2022
;
Leung et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Dillamore et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. As in Fig.
3
, the brackets denote the set of integers
(N_{R},N_{\varphi})
corresponding to the frequency
\omega=N_{R}\Omega_{R}+N_{\varphi}(\Omega_{\varphi}-\Omega_{\rm p})
. The spectra are significantly biased toward small
\omega
, indicating that strong resonant perturbations are only expected at low vertical frequency (
\Omega_{z,{\rm res}}=\omega/N_{z}
), i.e., far from the origin of the
(z,v_{z})
space. The strongest resonances
(N_{R},N_{\varphi})=(0,\pm 2)
, directly coupling the vertical motion with the azimuthal motion, occur at very small
\omega

(<10\,{\rm Gyr}^{-1})
because the guiding radius of the orbit
(R_{\rm g}=6.2\,{\rm kpc})
is close to the bar’s corotation radius

\Omega_{\varphi}-\Omega_{\rm p}
is small. The resonances involving the radial motion of stars

Dehnen &

Appendix C
Choice of coordinate system for small-scale kicks

In our kinetic analyses (Sections
3
and
4
), we modeled the effect of small-scale stochastic kicks from, e.g., giant molecular clouds, as a random walk of stars in the
(q,p)
space. Meanwhile, in our test-particle simulations (Section
5
), we applied the stochastic kicks in the
(z,v_{z})
space for the sake of computational efficiency. In the epicycle limit, the two coordinate systems are equivalent up to a dimensional constant. However, they are generally not identical, so we must check if subjecting stars to random walk in
(q,p)
or
(z,v_{z})
leads to any significant differences.

The second and third columns of Fig.
14
compare test-particle simulations with small-scale kicks applied in
(q,p)
and
(z,v_{z})
. In the former, we transform coordinates from
(z,v_{z})
to
(q,p)
every
\Delta t=10\,{\rm Myr}
, add random kicks
(\Delta q,\Delta p)
drawn from a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and variance
D\Delta t
(section
2.3
), and then transform back to
(z,v_{z})
for further integration. In the latter, we directly apply Gaussian-random kicks in
(z,v_{z})
with zero mean and variance
(D_{z}\Delta t,D_{v}\Delta t)
, where the diffusion coefficients are determined from the present mean-squared height and velocity dispersion (section
2.3
). We employ the transient spiral model (Section
5.2
) in order to highlight the effect of diffusion, which becomes prominent once the spiral arm has decayed. Comparison between the second and third columns confirms that whether stars are kicked in
(q,p)
or
(z,v_{z})
makes negligible difference.

Throughout the paper, we applied stochastic kicks in both
q
and
p
(or both
z
and
v_{z}
). However, encounters with, e.g., molecular clouds, are expected to be local, suggesting that only their velocity is affected. Thus, it may be more appropriate to scatter stars in momentum space rather than in the full phase space. In what follows, we show that this results in little difference so long as the rate at which the mean action of the disk changes is kept the same.

Let us first examine the rate of change in the mean action when stars undergo random walk in both
p
and
q
, or only in
p
. Let
\langle\Delta q\rangle
denote the ensemble average of the change in
q
over time
\Delta t
, with an analogous expression for
p
. We assume that

\displaystyle\langle\Delta q\rangle=\langle\Delta p\rangle=\langle\Delta q\Delta p\rangle=0,~~\langle(\Delta q)^{2}\rangle=D_{q}\Delta t,~~\langle(\Delta p)^{2}\rangle=D_{p}\Delta t,

(53)

for some constants
D_{q}
and
D_{p}
. For many uncorrelated weak kicks, the collision operator for a DF
f({\bm{w}},t)
takes the Fokker-Planck form
(
Binney &
Tremaine 2008
)

\displaystyle C[f]=-\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}{\bm{w}}}\cdot\left[{\bm{B}}({\bm{w}})f({\bm{w}},t)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}{\bm{w}}}\cdot\left[\mathsf{D}({\bm{w}})f({\bm{w}},t)\right]\right],

(54)

where

\displaystyle B_{i}({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle=\int(\mathrm{d}\Delta{\bm{w}})P({\bm{w}},\Delta{\bm{w}})\Delta w_{i}=\frac{\langle\Delta w_{i}\rangle}{\Delta t},

(55)

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{ij}({\bm{w}})

\displaystyle=\int(\mathrm{d}\Delta{\bm{w}})P({\bm{w}},\Delta{\bm{w}})\Delta w_{i}\Delta w_{j}=\frac{\langle\Delta w_{i}\Delta w_{j}\rangle}{\Delta t},

(56)

with
P({\bm{w}},\Delta{\bm{w}})
denoting the transition probability that a star at
{\bm{w}}
is scattered by
\Delta{\bm{w}}
over time
\Delta t
.
From equation (
53
), it follows that

\displaystyle C[f]=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{q}\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{{\partial}q^{2}}+D_{p}\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{{\partial}p^{2}}\right).

(57)

Transforming to the angle-action coordinates (equation
7
), we have

\displaystyle C[f]=\frac{D_{q}+D_{p}}{2}\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}J_{z}}+J_{z}\left(D_{q}\sin^{2}\theta_{z}+D_{p}\cos^{2}\theta_{z}\right)\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{{\partial}J_{z}^{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\cos\theta_{z}\sin\theta_{z}}{2J_{z}}\left(D_{q}-D_{p}\right)\frac{{\partial}f}{{\partial}\theta_{z}}+\frac{D_{q}\cos^{2}\theta_{z}+D_{p}\sin^{2}\theta_{z}}{4J_{z}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{{\partial}\theta_{z}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\cos\theta_{z}\sin\theta_{z}\left(D_{q}-D_{p}\right)\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{{\partial}J_{z}{\partial}\theta_{z}}.

(58)

When
D_{q}=D_{p}=D
, the equation reduces to equation (
17
).

We now use this collision operator to calculate the rate at which the mean action changes. We have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{J_{z}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\mathrm{d}\theta_{z}\int\mathrm{d}J_{z}J_{z}f=\int\mathrm{d}\theta_{z}\int\mathrm{d}J_{z}J_{z}C[f].

(59)

Substituting equation (
58
) and integrating by parts, we find, after a tedious but straightforward calculation,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{J_{z}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{D_{q}+D_{p}}{2}.

(60)

The result suggests that the change in the mean action in case
(D_{q},D_{p})=(0,D)
is exactly half of that in case
(D_{q},D_{p})=(D,D)
. In other words, if we set
(D_{q},D_{p})=(0,2D)
, the rate of change in the mean action would be the same as
(D_{q},D_{p})=(D,D)
. Since the typical orbital period (
T_{z}=2\pi/\Omega_{z}\sim 0.1\,{\rm Gyr}
) is much shorter than the diffusion timescale, which is of order the age of the disk (
T\sim 10\,{\rm Gyr}
), equation (
60
) implies that as long as the diffusion coefficients are determined based on the present mean action of the disk (i.e.,
D_{q}+D_{p}
remains constant), the choice of kicking in
q
and
p
, or only in
p
has little effect on the evolution of
f
.

It is difficult to derive an analytical expression for
\mathrm{d}\overline{J_{z}}/\mathrm{d}t
when stars undergo random walks in the
(z,v_{z})
coordinates. However, we expect a similar conclusion, since kicks in
(z,v_{z})
and
(q,p)
result in little difference, as demonstrated earlier (second and third columns of Fig.
14
). We verify this numerically by running simulations where stars are only kicked in
v_{z}
(forth and fifth columns of Fig.
14
). Compared to the standard model, where stars are kicked in both
z
and
v_{z}
(3rd column), removing kicks in
z
(4th column) results in a reduced diffusion. However, when the strength of kick in
v_{z}
is increased by a factor of two (5th column), the degree of diffusion is almost the same as the standard model (3rd column). These results confirm that the consequence of small-scale kicks depends little on whether stars are kicked isotropically in phase space or only in
v_{z}
, provided the strength of kick in
v_{z}
is doubled, keeping the rate of change in the mean action roughly the same.

Appendix D
Solution to the linearized kinetic equation

This appendix details the derivation of the solution to the linearized kinetic equation (
22
):

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}}{{\partial}t}+in_{z}\Omega_{z}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}-in_{z}\frac{{\partial}f_{0}}{{\partial}J_{z}}\hat{\Phi}_{n_{z}}=DJ_{z}\frac{{\partial}^{2}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}}{{\partial}J_{z}^{2}}.

(61)

Following
Tremaine
et al. 2023
, we seek an approximate solution by Taylor expanding the equation around a reference action
J_{z0}
, i.e.,
J_{z}=J_{z0}+\Delta J_{z}
and
\Omega_{z}=\Omega_{z0}+\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}\Delta J_{z}
. Then the equation becomes

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}}{{\partial}t}+in_{z}(\Omega_{z0}+\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}\Delta J_{z})\hat{f}_{n_{z}}-D(J_{z0}+\Delta J_{z})\frac{{\partial}^{2}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}}{{\partial}(\Delta J_{z})^{2}}=g(J_{z0},t),

(62)

where

\displaystyle g(J_{z},t)=in_{z}\frac{{\partial}f_{0}}{{\partial}J_{z}}\hat{\Phi}_{n_{z}}.

(63)

The order of the last term in the left hand side of equation (
62
) compared to the third term is

\displaystyle\frac{D\Delta J_{z}{\partial}^{2}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}/{\partial}(\Delta J_{z})^{2}}{n_{z}\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}\Delta J_{z}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}}\sim\frac{D}{n_{z}\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}J_{\rm h}^{2}},

(64)

where
J_{\rm h}
is the scale over which
\hat{f}_{n_{z}}
varies. It is reasonable to associate
J_{\rm h}
with the half-width of the resonant island in
J_{z}
space,
J_{h}=2\sqrt{-|\hat{\Psi}_{n_{z}}|/\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}}

(
Hamilton et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. Using equation (
37
), it follows that the ratio of the two terms scales as
\epsilon^{1/2}\Delta/4
, where
\epsilon\sim\hat{\Psi}_{n_{z}}/H_{0}
. Since
\epsilon\sim 0.1
and
\Delta\sim 0.2
in our model, we will neglect the last term in the left hand side of equation (
62
):

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}}{{\partial}t}+in_{z}(\Omega_{z0}+\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}\Delta J_{z})\hat{f}_{n_{z}}-DJ_{z0}\frac{{\partial}^{2}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}}{{\partial}(\Delta J_{z})^{2}}=g(J_{z0},t).

(65)

We caution though that, strictly speaking, linear theory is only valid for
\Delta\gtrsim 1

(
Hamilton et al. 2023
, see)
.
We look for a solution with the initial condition
\hat{f}_{n_{z}}(J_{z},t=0)=0
by Laplace transforming the equation in time. Multiplying by
\,\mathrm{e}^{-pt}
and integrating over
t\in[0,\infty]
, we have

\displaystyle[p+in_{z}(\Omega_{z0}+\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}\Delta J_{z})]\tilde{f}_{n_{z}}-DJ_{z0}\frac{{\partial}^{2}\tilde{f}_{n_{z}}}{{\partial}(\Delta J_{z})^{2}}=\tilde{g}(J_{z0},p),

(66)

where the tilde denotes functions in Laplace space
\tilde{f}_{n_{z}}(J_{z},p)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{-pt}\hat{f}_{n_{z}}(J_{z},t)
and is similarly defined for
\tilde{g}
. To simplify the equation, we define a new dimensionless variable

\displaystyle x\equiv-t_{0}[p+in_{z}(\Omega_{z0}+\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}\Delta J_{z})],

(67)

where
t_{0}
will be determined later. The equation then reads

\displaystyle-x\tilde{f}_{n_{z}}+n_{z}^{2}{\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}}^{2}DJ_{z0}t_{0}^{3}\frac{{\partial}^{2}\tilde{f}_{n_{z}}}{{\partial}x^{2}}=t_{0}\tilde{g},

(68)

We now choose
t_{0}
to be
t_{0}\equiv(n_{z}^{2}{\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}}^{2}DJ_{z0})^{-1/3}
such that

\displaystyle-x\tilde{f}_{n_{z}}+\frac{{\partial}^{2}\tilde{f}_{n_{z}}}{{\partial}x^{2}}=t_{0}\tilde{g},

(69)

which is an inhomogeneous Airy equation. In the absence of the potential perturbation (
\tilde{g}=0
), the solution is given by the Airy function
(
Tremaine
et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. For the general inhomogeneous case, the solution is given by the Scorer function
(
Scorer 1950
;
Gil
et al. 2001
)
:

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{n_{z}}=t_{0}\tilde{g}\pi Hi(x),~~~{\rm where}~~~Hi(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{xs-s^{3}/3}.

(70)

To obtain
\hat{f}_{n_{z}}
, we substitute
x
(
67
) back into the equation and apply the inverse Laplace transform:

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{n_{z}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{-1}[\tilde{f}_{n_{z}}]

\displaystyle=t_{0}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{-in_{z}(\Omega_{z0}+\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}\Delta J_{z})t_{0}s-s^{3}/3}\mathcal{L}^{-1}[\tilde{g}(p)\,\mathrm{e}^{-t_{0}sp}]

\displaystyle=t_{0}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{-in_{z}(\Omega_{z0}+\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}\Delta J_{z})t_{0}s-s^{3}/3}\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}g(t^{\prime})\delta(t\!-\!t_{0}s\!-\!t^{\prime}).

(71)

Performing the integral over
s
using the delta function, we get

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{n_{z}}(J_{z0}\!+\!\Delta J_{z},t)\!=\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\!\,\mathrm{e}^{-in_{z}(\Omega_{z0}+\Omega^{\prime}_{z0}\Delta J_{z})(t-t^{\prime})-[(t-t^{\prime})/t_{\rm d}]^{3}}\!g(J_{z0},t^{\prime}),

(72)

where
t_{\rm d}\equiv 3^{1/3}t_{0}
is the diffusion timescale (equation
24
). It can be verified that the above solution satisfies equation (
65
).
Finally, taking the limit
\Delta J_{z}\rightarrow 0
, we obtain the following approximate solution (equation
23
)

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{n_{z}}(J_{z0},t)\simeq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathrm{e}^{-in_{z}\Omega_{z0}(t-t^{\prime})-[(t-t^{\prime})/t_{\rm d}]^{3}}g(J_{z0},t^{\prime}).

(73)
