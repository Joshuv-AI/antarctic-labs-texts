Prostate MRI Quality Assessment: PI-QUAL and Factors Influencing Image Quality
Authors: Ji Soo Song, Weon Jang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince the introduction of Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS), prostate MRI has become an essential tool for detecting clinically significant prostate cancer, determining tumor stage, and guiding targeted biopsy. However, variability in image quality across institutions continues to affect diagnostic accuracy. To address this issue, the Prostate Imaging Quality (PI-QUAL) scoring system was developed as a standardized framework for evaluating the quality of prostate MRI. PI-QUAL v1 established the first quality control system for multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) but had limitations, including the inability to assess biparametric MRI (bpMRI) and a strong reliance on biopsy correlation. The updated PI-QUAL v2 simplified technical requirements, incorporated bpMRI assessment, and introduced a reproducible 3-point scale with adjustment rules that balance quality assurance with the diagnostic advantages of mpMRI. Factors influencing image quality include magnetic field strength and susceptibility- and motion-related artifacts. Complementary approaches, such as the Prostate Signal Intensity Homogeneity Score and artificial intelligence–based evaluation tools, have also been proposed. Overall, PI-QUAL v2 enhances reproducibility and broadens clinical applicability, providing a practical framework for ensuring high-quality prostate MRI. Future progress is expected through standardized data collection, structured training, and multidisciplinary collaboration.Keywords: Prostate, Diagnostic Imaging, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Image Quality

INTRODUCTION

Since the first reports on prostate MRI in the early 1980s, rapid advances in MRI technology have markedly expanded its clinical applications (1). Before the widespread use of systematic biopsy based on prostate-specific antigen (PSA), prostate MRI was primarily used for staging after histologic confirmation of prostate cancer (2,3). As concerns about overdiagnosis grew, prostate MRI took on a new role in pre-biopsy lesion localization and characterization to enable targeted biopsy for clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa), thereby reshaping traditional diagnostic pathways (4,5).

As clinical utilization increased, the need for unified guidance on image acquisition and interpretation emerged. In 2012, the Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System version 1 (PI-RADS v1) was introduced, providing fundamental recommendations for acquisition and reporting of multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) based on three key sequences: T2-weighted image (T2WI), diffusion-weighted image (DWI), and dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging (DCE). In 2015, PI-RADS v2 established minimum acceptable technical standards for mpMRI acquisition (6,7). The current version, PI-RADS v2.1 (2019), promotes standardized data collection and monitoring to improve consistency in image acquisition and interpretation, while facilitating multidisciplinary communication between radiologists and urologists (8).

Because mpMRI provides essential information for csPCa detection, staging, and targeted biopsy planning, maintaining adequate image quality is critical. However, the rapid proliferation of MRI has introduced heterogeneity across vendors, scanners, and protocols, leading to inter-institutional and international variability in image quality (9). Even when PI-RADS technical requirements are met, substantial quality gaps may persist (10,11). Degraded image quality directly compromises diagnostic accuracy, potentially causing unnecessary biopsies, treatment delays, or understaging, and reduces the utility of PI-RADS. These concerns underscore the need for a standardized framework to objectively assess and manage image quality. In this context, consensus-based quality criteria proposed in 2020 (12) led to the development of the Quality Control Scoring System for mpMRI of the Prostate version 1 (PI-QUAL v1), which has since been updated to PI-QUAL v2.

In this review, we introduce the updated PI-QUAL system for prostate MRI quality assessment and describe the key determinants of image quality.

DEVELOPMENT OF PI-QUAL

PI-QUAL v1 was the first scoring system designed to evaluate the quality of prostate mpMRI. It assigned a 5-point Likert score to each of the three PI-RADS sequences (T2WI, DWI, and DCE) based on PI-RADS v2 technical requirements, the presence of artifacts, and the conspicuity of anatomic structures. A score of 1 indicated that all sequences were nondiagnostic, whereas a score of 5 indicated that all sequences were of optimal diagnostic quality. The scoring framework also stratified the likelihood of lesion detection and exclusion at each level and aimed to support quality control and reliability in multicenter research (13).

PI-QUAL v1 nevertheless had several limitations. A major constraint was its inapplicability to biparametric MRI (bpMRI), which is performed without contrast and consists only of T2WI and DWI (14). Additionally, because it was developed at a single institution, its external reproducibility and generalizability required further validation. Moreover, image quality was assessed based on whether it was sufficient to detect a biopsy-proven lesion rather than by sequence-specific technical criteria. This approach hindered the intrinsic assessment of individual sequence quality and limited its applicability across broader clinical contexts such as staging, screening, and surveillance (15). To address these limitations, an international working group involving the European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR) and the European Association of Urology Section of Urological Imaging (ESUI) revised the system in 2024, yielding PI-QUAL v2 to overcome prior constraints and promote broader clinical use (Table 1) (16,17).

OVERVIEW OF PI-QUAL V2

The revised PI-QUAL v2 incorporates assessment items for bpMRI and simplifies scoring to provide a reproducible quality-assessment framework applicable across diverse patient populations (18).

TECHNICAL RECOMMENDATIONS

PI-QUAL v2 refines the PI-RADS v2.1 acquisition guidelines by focusing on essential elements, reducing the 20 technical parameters of v1 to seven (Table 2). For T2WI, a slice thickness of 3 mm is required. For DWI, a slice thickness ≤4 mm and acquisition or synthesis of a high b-value ≥1,400 s/mm2 are required. Apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps should be generated using at most two b-values ≤1,000 s/mm2. For DCE, requirements include a 3 mm slice thickness, temporal resolution ≤15 seconds, and fat suppression.

ASSESSMENT METHOD

For examinations that satisfy the PI-QUAL v2 essential criteria, T2WI and DWI are each scored across four items (maximum 4 points each), and DCE is considered optimal only when both of its two items are fulfilled (Table 3). The assessment criteria for each sequence consider whether images demonstrate adequate signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast, clear visualization of the prostate and surrounding structures, and absence of artifacts that significantly impair diagnostic quality.

Final scoring differs according to contrast use (Table 4). For bpMRI, if either T2WI or DWI scores ≤2, the overall PI-QUAL is 1. If both sequences score ≥3 but at least one fails to reach 4, the overall score is 2. Only when both sequences score 4 is the overall score 3. For mpMRI, if either T2WI or DWI scores ≤2, the default overall score is 1; however, if the other sequence scores 4 and DCE fulfills both of its criteria, the final score can be upgraded to 2 (Fig. 5). Conversely, even if both T2WI and DWI score 4, if DCE fulfills only one or none of its criteria, the final score is downgraded to 2 (Fig. 6).

RATIONALE AND CAUTIONS REGARDING SCORE ADJUSTMENT

In mpMRI, even if both T2WI and DWI receive the maximum score of 4, the overall PI-QUAL score is downgraded when the quality of DCE does not meet the required standard, implying that the additional sequence may negatively influence the overall image quality assessment. However, PI-QUAL v2 does not permit selective adjustment based solely on the DCE score, thereby enforcing a more stringent criterion for assigning a score of 3 in mpMRI. Conversely, when either T2WI or DWI scores ≤2, high-quality DCE can prevent the overall score from dropping to 1 by maintaining it at 2, functioning as a compensatory factor. This adjustment is based on the premise that mpMRI provides more comprehensive diagnostic information than bpMRI by incorporating additional sequences. Accordingly, the system awards the highest score only when all sequences meet a defined quality threshold, reflecting the potential diagnostic superiority of mpMRI.

PI-QUAL v2 avoids composite (summed) scores; instead, it records per-sequence ratings and encourages explicit documentation of causes of quality degradation. This approach allows operators to identify problematic sequences and repeat them immediately if degradation is detected during scanning. Nevertheless, PI-QUAL should guide rather than dictate repeat-scan decisions. For example, when MRI is performed in a patient with elevated PSA to aid in screening and guide targeted biopsy, repeat imaging may be unnecessary even if the PI-QUAL score is 1, as long as a lesion is clearly identifiable and can be targeted for biopsy (Fig. 2).

It is recommended not to assign a PI-RADS score to examinations rated PI-QUAL 1. In particular, lesions should not be interpreted as PI-RADS 3 solely because image quality is insufficient for confident assessment. In such cases, the PI-QUAL score should be clearly stated in the report to document the diagnostic limitation, and repeat imaging may be advised depending on the clinical context. For PI-QUAL 2 images, repeat scanning is not mandatory; however, in patients undergoing active surveillance or postoperative follow-up, acquisition of optimized images corresponding to PI-QUAL 3 is recommended.

DETERMINANTS OF PROSTATE MRI QUALITY

FIELD STRENGTH

PI-RADS v2.1 recommends the use of 3T MRI, as it generally provides a higher SNR than 1.5T systems. However, depending on factors such as the scanner’s years in service and the degree of protocol optimization, 1.5T MRI may occasionally yield superior image quality. In particular, in patients with hip arthroplasty, severe image distortion can occur due to susceptibility artifacts, and some studies have suggested the use of 1.5T MRI in such cases (19). Nevertheless, studies directly comparing image quality between 1.5T and 3T MRI in prostate imaging are limited, and some reports have shown no significant differences between the two field strengths (20).

ARTIFACTS

Beyond susceptibility from hip arthroplasty, intrarectal gas can generate susceptibility artifacts at the interface with the posterior peripheral zone, and rectal distention may produce motion artifacts. Because the peripheral zone is a common site of prostate cancer, these artifacts can limit screening assessments (21). Rectal cleansing or catheterization can reduce intrarectal gas. Alternatively, intrarectal gel can displace gas with water-based material and reduce susceptibility mismatches, although excessive gel may induce rectal distention and paradoxically increase motion artifacts (22,23).

BOWEL PERISTALSIS

Periprostatic bowel peristalsis causes motion artifacts that blur lesion margins and affect staging assessments, including extraprostatic extension (24). Enemas may reduce rectal distention and motion, likely by promoting rectal contraction (25). Antiperistaltic agents can improve T2WI quality and reduce rectal motion, but they increase preparation time and cost and may cause adverse effects such as dry mouth, blurred vision, or dizziness. Some studies found no significant improvements in image quality or csPCa detection, so their use should be individualized according to patient status and local protocols (26,27,28).

RELIABILITY AND VALIDITY OF PI-QUAL

Since the update to PI-QUAL version 2, studies have assessed its reliability and validity. In a multicenter study of 3T mpMRI, inter-reader agreement for PI-QUAL v2 was moderate and tended to improve with reader experience; variability was greater for T2WI than for DWI or DCE (29). Another study comparing PI-QUAL v1 and v2 demonstrated comparable inter-reader agreement for individual sequences and reported that the inclusion of DCE improved inadequate bpMRI quality to an acceptable level for mpMRI interpretation (17).

Additional studies have explored the clinical validity of PI-QUAL v2. An analysis of bpMRI acquired across multiple institutions showed that PI-QUAL scores affected diagnostic accuracy for csPCa and emphasized the importance of adequate spatial resolution in T2WI and the use of at least three b-values in DWI to ensure optimal image quality and diagnostic performance (16). Another study reported that higher image quality was associated with a lower proportion of equivocal PI-RADS 3 lesions, although gains in overall diagnostic accuracy were limited (30). Further study is needed to define the impact of PI-QUAL across a wider range of clinical scenarios, including staging.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND ENHANCEMENTS

Although PI-QUAL v2 provides a standardized framework for quality assessment, certain limitations remain. In particular, background signal heterogeneity in the peripheral zone due to inflammation or fibrosis may not be fully captured by PI-QUAL alone. To complement this, the Prostate Signal Intensity Homogeneity Score (PSHS) has been proposed (23). PSHS is a 5-point visual scale of peripheral zone background homogeneity on axial T2WI, based on PI-RADS v2.1 acquisition recommendations; a score of 1 indicates diffuse uniform low signal, whereas a score of 5 indicates completely homogeneous high signal. Lower scores imply greater heterogeneity from inflammatory or fibrotic change that may obscure lesions, whereas higher scores facilitate lesion conspicuity. Used alongside PI-QUAL, PSHS may jointly reflect acquisition quality and interpretability.

Additionally, AI–based approaches have been proposed to automate and objectify image quality assessment. Recently, an AI-based software system was introduced that determines in real time whether the DCE sequence is necessary during mpMRI acquisition. This system analyzes T2WI and DWI using a convolutional neural network to automatically evaluate image quality and immediately provides feedback to the technologist during scanning, thereby assisting in the decision of whether to proceed with contrast-enhanced imaging. The system demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity and showed strong agreement with radiologist assessments (24). However, a key limitation was that image quality was classified only dichotomously as adequate or inadequate. Future applications incorporating standardized, multi-tier image quality metrics such as PI-QUAL are expected to enable more systematic and objective external validation.

The simplified scoring system introduced by PI-QUAL v2 has provided a foundation for broader participation in quality assurance and research across institutions. In the future, the shared use of a unified image quality scale among physicians, radiologic technologists, researchers, and between institutions is expected to facilitate more efficient communication in both clinical practice and research settings. To achieve this, the establishment of structured training programs is essential, along with sustained efforts toward consistent and stable quality management.

Although the revised PI-QUAL v2 has improved the reproducibility of image quality assessment and broadened its clinical applicability, variability due to differences in scanner performance, magnetic field strength, and imaging protocols, as well as limitations in standardization across institutions, still remains. To overcome these challenges, additional multicenter studies involving diverse scanners and institutions are essential.

CONCLUSION

This review outlines the background, structure, and scoring methodology of PI-QUAL, a standardized quality assessment system for prostate MRI, and examines factors affecting image quality, evidence on its reliability and validity, and future directions and clinical applicability. PI-QUAL v2 improves upon the limitations of v1 by simplifying technical parameters and incorporating the assessment of bpMRI, thereby enhancing reproducibility and broadening its clinical applicability. Additionally, by introducing score adjustment rules, PI-QUAL v2 reflects the diagnostic strengths of mpMRI while providing a compensatory mechanism against image quality degradation. Looking forward, the clinical and academic value of PI-QUAL is expected to increase further with the establishment of standardized datasets, structured training programs, multidisciplinary collaboration, sustained quality assurance, and multicenter research.
