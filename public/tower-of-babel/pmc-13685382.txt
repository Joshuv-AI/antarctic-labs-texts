Genome-wide analysis of the soybean GmGRP gene family identifies putative cold-responsive genes
Authors: Xinyu Wei, Wencheng Lu, Hongchang Jia, Xiaoguang Yu, Xiaofei Yan, Henan Diao, Ying Liu, Zhibin Gao, Dezhi Han, Honglei Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glycine-rich proteins (GRPs) play essential roles in plant growth, development, and stress responses, but systematic studies in soybean remain limited. In this study, using the soybean reference genome, 11 GmGRP gene members were identified with tools such as Pfam, SMART, CDD, and HMMER, and their physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, gene structure, conserved motifs, and promoter cis-acting elements were systematically analyzed. Phylogenetic analysis showed that GmGRPs can be divided into multiple subgroups and share conserved biological functions with GRPs in Arabidopsis, rice, and maize. Gene structure and motif analysis revealed significant structural differences across subgroups, whereas members within the same group are highly conserved. Promoter element analysis showed that GmGRPs are extensively enriched for ABA-, JA-, SA-, GA-, and cold-responsive regulatory elements, suggesting that these genes may participate in regulating multiple hormonal and stress signals.Collinearity results showed that two pairs of homologous genes may have arisen from gene duplication. Tissue expression analysis indicated that GmGRPs exhibit significant tissue specificity, with different members potentially performing different biological functions.Promoter analysis identified low-temperature response (LTR) cis-elements in the promoters of GmGRP7 and GmGRP8, suggesting these two members are candidates for cold-responsive regulation, pending experimental validation. Expression patterns varied substantially among family members and across time points. GmGRP8, GmGRP9, and GmGRP10 displayed pronounced induction, reaching peak fold-changes of approximately 11-, 13-, and 14-fold relative to the 0 h control at 6–12 h, respectively. In contrast, GmGRP3, GmGRP11, and several other members showed a progressive decline in transcript abundance under sustained cold. These findings are consistent with the promoter-based prediction that GmGRP7 and GmGRP8 are cold-responsive candidates, and suggest that additional GmGRP members may also participate in low-temperature responses through distinct expression dynamics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09623-2.

Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) is one of the most widely cultivated legume crops worldwide, contributing approximately 30% of global plant-based edible oil and 69% of dietary protein [1, 2]. Its nutritional value, combined with the agronomic benefits of biological nitrogen fixation via the soybean–rhizobium symbiosis, makes it an economically and ecologically important crop [3].

Despite its global significance, soybean growth is highly sensitive to environmental conditions, and low-temperature stress is a primary abiotic constraint on production [4–6]. Cold stress impairs germination, inhibits seedling establishment, disrupts reproductive development, and can reduce yield by approximately 24% [7, 8]. In China’s Northeast, one of the world’s major soybean-producing regions, spring cold events during sowing represent a persistent challenge to yield stability. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying cold tolerance in soybean is therefore a research priority with direct relevance to crop improvement.

Glycine-rich proteins (GRPs) were first identified in plants as structural components of the petunia cell wall [9]. The GRP superfamily is defined by repetitive glycine-rich motifs and is classified into five structural classes based on domain architecture [10]. Classes I and II comprise predominantly cell wall structural proteins; the function of Class III members remains unclear; Class IV (GR-RBPs) members carry RNA Recognition Motifs (RRM) or Cold Shock Domains (CSD) and are implicated in RNA binding and post-transcriptional regulation; and Class V members have structurally diverse, poorly characterized architectures [10]. GRP genes show tissue- and developmental stage-specific expression and respond to a range of hormonal and environmental signals [11]. Several members have been associated with stress adaptation: in Arabidopsis, AtGRP2 and AtGRP7 are cold-induced and enhance freezing tolerance, likely through RNA chaperone activity [12]; rice OsGRP3 modulates defence responses and heat and oxidative stress tolerance [13]; and 37 RB-GRP genes in cotton show stress- and tissue-specific expression patterns [14]. Collectively, these findings suggest that GRPs function as multifunctional regulators at the intersection of development and environmental stress response.

Despite these advances, a systematic genome-wide analysis of the GRP gene family in soybean has not been reported. Existing work on plant GRPs has focused predominantly on individual members or specific subfamilies in model species, with limited characterisation in major crop plants. This gap is notable given that soybean is both cold-sensitive and agriculturally important.

The soybean genome has undergone at least one paleopolyploidy event, generating a complex paralogous gene structure that warrants systematic investigation. Furthermore, identifying GRP members with promoter-level evidence of cold-responsive regulation could provide a basis for future functional studies targeting cold-tolerance mechanisms.

The present study addresses this gap through an in-silico characterization of the GRP gene family in soybean. The specific aims were to: (1) identify GRP family members genome-wide; (2) analyse gene structure, conserved protein domains, and motif architecture; (3) infer phylogenetic relationships among GmGRP proteins and orthologues from Arabidopsis, rice, and maize; (4) characterise promoter cis-regulatory elements; (5) examine chromosomal distribution and collinearity to assess duplication history; and (6) profile tissue-specific expression patterns.

Together, these analyses provide a genomic reference for the soybean GRP family and identify GmGRP7 and GmGRP8 as candidate cold-responsive members that warrant experimental validation.

Materials and methods

Identification and physicochemical property analysis of soybean GRP gene family

The complete genome sequence and annotation files (GFF3 format) for Glycine max (Williams 82, JGI v2.1) were obtained from Phytozome v13.0 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/). GRP family genes were identified using a dual approach: HMM profiling and BLASTP similarity searches. The GRP domain profile (PF07172) was retrieved from Pfam v35.0 and used for HMM-based searches with HMMER v3.3.2 integrated into TBtools v1.120 [15], with an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10 − 5. In parallel, BLASTP searches were conducted against the soybean proteome using known Arabidopsis thaliana GRP sequences as queries, applying an E-value threshold of 1 × 10–10, word size of 6, and the BLOSUM62 substitution matrix. Candidate sequences from both approaches were merged and de-duplicated.

All candidate proteins were validated using SMART v9.0 and InterProScan v5.59-91.0 to confirm the presence of complete GRP domains, including both nucleotide-binding and substrate-binding regions. Sequences with premature stop codons or incomplete domain architecture were excluded. Where multiple splice isoforms were annotated for a single locus, only the longest predicted protein was retained. This yielded a final curated set of 11 GmGRP genes. Physicochemical parameters were calculated using ExPASy ProtParam, and subcellular localization was predicted with Cell-PLoc 2.0.

Phylogenetic tree analysis of soybean GRP gene family

GRP amino acid sequences from Arabidopsis, rice, and maize were obtained from Phytozome.

Multiple sequence alignment was performed with MAFFT v7.490. The phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using a maximum-likelihood (ML) approach implemented in MEGA11. The JTT + G+I substitution model was applied, and branch support was assessed with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The resulting tree was visualized using iTOL (http://itol.embl.de).

Motif analysis and gene structure analysis of soybean GRP gene family

The MEME website (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) was used to perform motif analysis (MEME Suite v5.5.4; maximum 10 motifs; minimum width 6 aa; maximum width 50 aa; occurrence model: zero or one occurrence per sequence [ZOOPS]). Protein conserved domains were analyzed on NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi).

Soybean genome annotation files were downloaded from the Phytozome database. TBtools software was used to visualize motif and gene structure results.

Promoter cis-acting element analysis of soybean GRP gene family

Promoter sequences 2000 bp upstream of the start codon (ATG) of members of the GmGRP gene family were downloaded from the Phytozome database. The PlantCARE website (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) was used to analyze cis-acting elements in promoter sequences. TBtools [15] Software was used to visualize and analyze promoter cis-acting elements.

Distribution and collinearity analysis of GmGRP gene family in soybean genome

The positions of GmGRP gene family members on chromosomes were determined from the GFF3 file of the soybean genome. TBtools software was used to map the distribution of GmGRPs across chromosomes and to visualize intra-species collinearity relationships within the soybean GRP gene family. Intra-species collinearity analysis was performed using MCScanX with default parameters (minimum match score ≥ 5 gene pairs per syntenic block). Tandem duplicates were defined as paralogous gene pairs located within 200 kb of each other on the same chromosome. To assess the selective pressure on syntenic gene pairs, pairwise non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates were estimated using the Yang-Nielsen (YN) method as implemented in Ka/Ks Calculator v2.0. CDS sequences were aligned using the protein-guided codon alignment approach in MAFFT v7.490 prior to Ka/Ks calculation.

Tissue-specific expression analysis of soybean GRP gene family

Transcriptome data from nine tissues of Williams 82 (roots, root hairs, stems, leaves, flowers, root nodules, pods, seeds, and shoot apical meristem) were retrieved from the Phytozome database (dataset accession: G max_275_Wm82.a2.v1). Expression values are reported in FPKM units; the source dataset includes three biological replicates per tissue. For visualization, expression values were log2-transformed (FPKM + 1) and z-score-normalized per gene across tissues. TBtools (v2.056) was used to generate the expression heatmap.

Plant material and cold-stress treatment

Soybean seeds of the cultivar Dongsheng17 from the Soybean Research Institute, Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Harbin, China, were surface-sterilized and germinated on moistened filter paper at 25 °C in the dark for 48 h. Uniform seedlings were transferred to a hydroponic solution (Hoagland’s No. 2 basal salt mixture) and grown at 25 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod (150 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) for 7 days. For cold-stress treatment, seedlings at the V1 growth stage were transferred to a growth chamber pre-equilibrated to 4 °C under otherwise identical light conditions. Whole-seedling aerial tissue (cotyledons and primary leaves combined) was harvested at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h after the onset of cold treatment. Three independent biological replicates were collected at each time point, with each replicate consisting of pooled tissue from five seedlings. Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted using the CTAB-based method with lithium chloride precipitation, following established protocols for soybean tissue. RNA quantity and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically (A260/A280 ≥ 2.0; A260/A230 ≥ 1.8); RNA integrity was verified by denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis. Genomic DNA was removed by on-column DNase I digestion (37 °C, 30 min). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of DNase-treated RNA using a reverse transcriptase kit with oligo(dT)₁₈ and random hexamer primers, following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was diluted 1:10 in nuclease-free water prior to qPCR.

Primer design and qRT-PCR

Gene-specific primers for all 11 GmGRP genes were designed using Primer3 (v2.6.0), targeting amplicons of 100–200 bp that span exon–intron boundaries where possible to detect genomic DNA contamination. Primer sequences and amplicon characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Primer specificity was confirmed by BLAST against the Glycine max Williams 82 reference genome (JGI v2.1) and by the presence of a single melting-curve peak. The soybean housekeeping gene GmEF1α (Glyma.02G244200) was used as the internal reference.

qRT-PCR was performed on a real-time PCR system using a SYBR Green master mix in a total reaction volume of 20 µL, containing 10 µL of 2× SYBR Green mix, 0.4 µM each primer, and 2 µL of diluted cDNA. The thermal program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, with a final melting-curve acquisition from 65 °C to 95 °C. Each biological replicate was assayed in technical triplicate. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method, with the 0 h time point serving as the calibrator. Fold-change values ≥ 2 (i.e., ΔΔCt ≤ − 1) or ≤ 0.5 (i.e., ΔΔCt ≥ 1) were considered biologically significant. Statistical comparisons between each time point and the 0 h control were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, in R v4.3.0.

Results

Identification and physicochemical characterization of the soybean GRP gene family

Whole-genome screening of Glycine max using integrated domain-detection tools (Pfam, HMMER, CDD, and SMART) against the reference genome identified 11 candidate sequences containing the conserved GRP domain (PF07172) and showing high amino acid sequence similarity to characterized Arabidopsis GRP proteins. These 11 genes were named GmGRP1–GmGRP11 based on their chromosomal locations (Table 1). Physicochemical parameters calculated using ExPASy ProtParam and subcellular localization predictions from Cell-PLoc 2.0 are summarized in Table 1. The 11 GmGRP proteins vary significantly in size, ranging from 79 amino acids (aa) (GmGRP6) to 227 aa (GmGRP1), with corresponding molecular weights of 8,591.98 Da and 25,130.22 Da, respectively. Their theoretical isoelectric points (pI) cover a broad spectrum from 4.55 for GmGRP6 to 9.36 for GmGRP1, indicating that the family includes both acidic and basic proteins. The instability index (II) analysis classified six proteins (GmGRP4, GmGRP5, GmGRP6, GmGRP9, GmGRP10, and GmGRP11; II < 40) as stable, while the other five were deemed unstable. The aliphatic index ranged from 41.5 (GmGRP7) to 109.89 (GmGRP9), reflecting differences in their thermostability. The grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) scores were negative for GmGRP1, GmGRP2, GmGRP7, GmGRP10, and GmGRP11 (ranging from − 0.699 to − 0.071), indicating that these proteins are hydrophilic.

Phylogenetic analysis of GmGRP proteins

To examine the evolutionary relationships of soybean GRPs with homologues in other species, we aligned protein sequences from 11 GmGRPs, 14 Arabidopsis GRPs, 23 rice GRPs, and 10 maize GRPs and constructed an ML phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1). The 58 proteins resolved into five major phylogenetic groups (Groups I-V). Group I was the largest, comprising 21 members across three species; Group II contained 17 members; Groups III, IV, and V contained 4, 5, and 11 members, respectively. Soybean GmGRPs were distributed across Groups II, IV, and V, with no members in Groups I or III. It should be noted that these five phylogenetic groups (as designated by the tree topology) do not correspond directly to the five classical GRP structural classes (I-V) defined by domain architecture. The co-clustering of GmGRPs with Arabidopsis, rice, and maize orthologues within the same phylogenetic groups suggests evolutionary conservation of the GRP family across angiosperms and is consistent with the possibility that members within each group share conserved biological functions. The absence of GmGRPs from Groups I and III may reflect lineage-specific gene loss, the relatively small size of the soybean GRP family compared with Arabidopsis, or the exclusion of non-canonical members during domain-based screening. Resolving this pattern would require broader phylogenomic sampling.

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis

To further investigate the evolutionary and structural characteristics of the GmGRP family, phylogenetic relationships, conserved protein motifs, and exon–intron organization were analyzed comparatively (Fig. 2). The phylogenetic tree grouped the 11 GmGRP proteins into several distinct subgroups (Fig. 2A). Members within the same subgroup generally clustered together and exhibited similar structural features, suggesting close evolutionary relationships.

MEME analysis identified seven conserved motifs (Motifs 1–7) among the GmGRP proteins (Fig. 2B). Motif 2 was present in all eleven GmGRP members, indicating that it represents a highly conserved feature of the family and likely corresponds to the core glycine-rich region characteristic of GRP proteins. In contrast, the remaining motifs displayed subgroup-specific distributions. Proteins belonging to the same phylogenetic subgroup generally shared similar motif compositions, whereas motif patterns varied substantially among different subgroups. These differences suggest that diversification of motif architecture may have contributed to the functional specialization of individual GmGRP members during evolution.

Analysis of exon–intron organization revealed substantial variation in gene structure among family members (Fig. 2C). GmGRP1 exhibited the most complex structure, containing five exons and four introns, whereas the remaining ten genes possessed a conserved organization consisting of two exons and one intron. Despite these differences in gene structure, conserved domain analysis confirmed the presence of the characteristic GRP domain in all identified family members, indicating a shared evolutionary origin and conserved functional core. The strong correspondence among phylogenetic relationships, motif composition, and gene structure supports the reliability of the subgroup classification and suggests that structural diversification has contributed to the functional differentiation of the GmGRP gene family.

Promoter cis-acting element analysis

To explore potential regulatory mechanisms of GmGRP genes, the 2,000-bp promoter regions upstream of the translation start site were analyzed using the PlantCARE database (Fig. 3). A diverse array of cis-acting regulatory elements associated with phytohormone signaling, environmental responses, and plant development was identified in the promoters of all GmGRP family members.

Hormone-responsive elements were widely distributed throughout the GmGRP promoters, including abscisic acid-responsive elements (ABREs), auxin-responsive elements (AuxREs), gibberellin-responsive elements (GAREs), salicylic acid-responsive elements (TCA elements), and methyl jasmonate-responsive elements (CGTCA motifs and TGACG motifs). The widespread occurrence of these elements suggests that GmGRP genes may be regulated by multiple phytohormone signaling pathways.

In addition to hormone-related elements, several stress- and environment-responsive cis-elements were detected, including low-temperature response elements (LTR), drought-responsive elements (MBS), anaerobic induction elements (ARE), light-responsive elements, wound-responsive elements (WUN-motif), and circadian regulatory elements. Among the identified family members, GmGRP7 and GmGRP8 contained LTR elements in their promoter regions, suggesting their potential involvement in responses to low-temperature stress. It should be noted that the presence of cis-acting elements alone does not demonstrate transcriptional regulation or functional involvement in specific stress responses. Rather, these elements provide preliminary evidence for potential regulatory mechanisms and serve as useful indicators for identifying candidate genes for future experimental investigation. Therefore, GmGRP7 and GmGRP8 are proposed as candidate cold-responsive genes, the roles of which under low-temperature conditions require further validation through gene expression and functional analyses.

Chromosomal distribution and collinearity analysis

Gene duplication is an important mechanism driving gene family expansion and functional diversification in plants. Chromosomal mapping revealed that five GmGRP genes (GmGRP1–GmGRP5) are clustered on chromosome Gm02 within a genomic interval of approximately 53.2 kb (46,834,145–46,887,358 bp) and are transcribed from the same DNA strand. The physical proximity of these genes, together with the relatively short intergenic distances between adjacent members (2.1–19.1 kb), is consistent with their origin through tandem duplication events. To evaluate the evolutionary constraints acting on this genomic cluster, Ka/Ks ratios were calculated for duplicated gene pairs within the region. All analyzed pairs exhibited Ka/Ks values below 1.0, with an average of approximately 0.42, indicating that these genes have predominantly undergone purifying selection following duplication. This pattern suggests that functional constraints have contributed to the retention of duplicated GmGRP members during soybean genome evolution. The presence of a tandemly duplicated GmGRP cluster on chromosome Gm02 indicates that local duplication has contributed substantially to the expansion of the soybean GRP family. Although the duplicated genes retain considerable sequence similarity, differences in gene structure, conserved motifs, and expression profiles suggest the potential for functional divergence among family members. Together, these findings indicate that tandem duplication, followed by purifying selection, has played an important role in shaping the evolutionary history of the GmGRP gene family in soybean (Fig. 4).

Evolutionary selection pressure analysis of duplicated GmGRP genes

To investigate the evolutionary constraints acting on duplicated GmGRP genes, the nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka), synonymous substitution rate (Ks), and Ka/Ks ratio were calculated for the identified syntenic gene pairs (Table S2). The two duplicated gene pairs identified through collinearity analysis, GmGRP1/GmGRP8 and GmGRP6/GmGRP10, exhibited Ka/Ks ratios of 0.897 and 0.379, respectively.

The Ka/Ks ratios of both duplicated gene pairs were less than 1, indicating that these genes have predominantly undergone purifying (negative) selection during evolution. This suggests that amino acid-altering mutations have been selectively constrained, thereby preserving protein function following duplication events. The relatively lower Ka/Ks value observed for the GmGRP6/GmGRP10 pair indicates stronger evolutionary conservation compared with the GmGRP1/GmGRP8 pair. These results suggest that the duplicated GmGRP genes have retained important biological functions after duplication and that purifying selection has played a major role in maintaining the structural and functional stability of the GmGRP gene family during soybean evolution.

Tissue-specific expression profiling of GmGRP genes

To characterize the spatial expression patterns of the GmGRP family, we analyzed publicly available transcriptomic data from nine tissue types of the Williams 82 cultivar roots, root hairs, stems, leaves, flowers, root nodules, pods, seeds, and shoot apical meristem and visualized the results as a heatmap (Fig. 5). GmGRP genes displayed pronounced tissue specificity, with distinct expression profiles across the family.

GmGRP5 showed the highest expression in flowers and root nodules, suggesting roles in reproductive development and symbiotic nitrogen fixation. GmGRP9 was preferentially expressed in pods, suggesting a possible role during seed pod development. GmGRP11 exhibited predominant expression in roots, consistent with a role in root growth or root–soil interactions. GmGRP7 and GmGRP10 were most highly expressed in seeds, suggesting possible involvement in seed filling or maturation. The remaining members, GmGRP1, GmGRP4, GmGRP6, and GmGRP8, showed comparatively lower and broader expression across multiple tissues.

Collectively, the tissue-specific expression patterns of GmGRP genes support functional diversification within the family and provide a basis for prioritizing candidates for further experimental characterization.

Expression Profiles of GmGRP Genes under Cold Stress

To assess whether GmGRP members respond to low temperature at the transcript level, and to provide experimental context for the promoter-based predictions, qRT-PCR was used to profile all 11 GmGRP genes in soybean seedlings exposed to 4 °C for 0–24 h. Expression patterns varied substantially among family members and across the cold-treatment time course (Fig. 6).

Three genes showed clear and statistically supported transcript induction. GmGRP10 was the most strongly cold-responsive member: transcript levels increased approximately 4-fold at 1 h, rose to 9-fold at 3 h, and reached a peak of 14-fold at 6 h (all significant at the ≥ 2-, ≥ 4-, and ≥ 8-fold thresholds, respectively), before declining to 5-fold at 12 h and 3-fold at 24 h. GmGRP9 showed a distinct temporal profile, with a relatively modest early induction, followed by a large increase at 12 h (13-fold; *** threshold), then a partial decline at 24 h ( 3-fold; * threshold).

GmGRP8 also exhibited progressive induction, with significant increases from 3 h (7-fold; **) through 6 h (1-fold; ***), followed by a return toward baseline by 12–24 h. Among the remaining members, GmGRP1 showed moderate upregulation at 3–6 h (2.7- and 4-fold, respectively; * threshold at 6 h) followed by a significant decline below baseline at 12–24 h. GmGRP2 displayed a similar biphasic pattern, with peak induction at 6 h (2.7-fold) and suppression at 12 h (0.4-fold; *). GmGRP4 was significantly induced at 1 h (3.3-fold; *) but showed a return toward baseline and partial suppression at 12 h. GmGRP5, GmGRP6, and GmGRP7 each showed modest, non-significant increases in expression during the early phase of cold treatment (1–6 h) that did not reach the ≥ 2-fold threshold, followed by a partial decline at 12–24 h. GmGRP7 showed no significant fold-change at any measured time point under the thresholds applied here. Two members showed predominant suppression. GmGRP3 transcript levels declined progressively from 6 h onwards, reaching approximately 0.4-fold at 12 h and 0.3-fold at 24 h (* at both time points). GmGRP11 exhibited significant induction at 1 h (1.5-fold; non-significant by threshold) but was markedly suppressed at 12 h (0.05-fold; ***) before partial recovery at 24 h. Collectively, these data indicate that cold stress elicits diverse transcriptional responses across the GmGRP family, with GmGRP8, GmGRP9, and GmGRP10 showing the most substantial induction under low-temperature conditions.

Discussion

This study presents a genome-wide identification and in silico characterization of the GRP (glycine-rich protein) gene family in the soybean genome. From an evolutionary perspective, the 11 GmGRP members identified here co-cluster with GRPs from model plants in a subset of the five phylogenetic groups (Groups II, IV, and V) resolved by ML analysis, with notable absence from Groups I and III. The co-clustering of GmGRPs with orthologs from Arabidopsis, rice, and maize within shared subgroups suggests broad conservation of the GRP family across angiosperms [16]. Previous reviews of plant GRPs have also emphasized the five-class division and their typical structural features (such as the combination of RRM or CSD structures with glycine-rich regions), which are closely related to functions (structural support, RNA binding/post-transcriptional regulation, stress response) [12]. This co-clustering is consistent with broad conservation of GRP gene function across angiosperms, though functional equivalence among members within each group would require experimental confirmation.

Differences in gene structure and conserved protein motifs suggest functional differentiation. We observed that GmGRP1 has more exons and introns, whereas all other members have a simpler structure with two exons and a single intron; all GmGRPs contain the typical GRP domain, but motif distribution shows subfamily specificity. This pattern is consistent with reports in other species: structurally more complex GRPs are generally associated with post-transcriptional regulation or multifunctional interactions, whereas short GRPs dominated by glycine repeats tend to function as structural proteins in cell wall-related processes or stress responses [17–19].

Variations in gene structure may also reflect historical processes of gene duplication and neofunctionalization or sub-functionalization, especially given soybean’s complex whole-genome duplication history, in which gene duplication is a key driver of functional diversification [20].

Promoter cis-elements and expression profiles suggest potential roles for GmGRPs in hormone signaling and stress-related regulation. Our promoter analysis shows that most GmGRPs contain ABRE (abscisic acid response elements), LTR (low-temperature response elements), and JA/SA/GA-related elements, suggesting potential involvement in multiple phytohormone signaling pathways and stress responses, although the co-occurrence of these elements does not establish pathway integration without transcriptional or functional validation. Previous studies have shown that Class IV GRPs (i.e., glycine-rich RNA-binding proteins, GR-RBPs) are induced by ABA or cold and enhance stress tolerance by regulating downstream response gene expression through RNA binding, mRNA stability, or splicing [21–23]. In particular, studies of Arabidopsis AtGRP2 and AtGRP7 show that these GRPs can respond to cold and possess RNA chaperone activity, thereby regulating mRNA fate and translation efficiency during cold adaptation [22, 24]. On this basis, the presence of LTR elements in the promoters of GmGRP7 and GmGRP8 identifies these two members as promoter-based candidates, warranting experimental validation of cold-responsive expression before functional conclusions can be drawn.

Collectively, the available evidence indicates that GRPs participate in various stresses (cold, drought, salt, heavy metals, etc.) and developmental processes (flowering, seed development, root nodule symbiosis). For example, rice OsGRP3 can improve drought tolerance by altering the mRNA stability of stress-related genes; soybean-derived GmGRPL can alleviate aluminum-induced damage in Arabidopsis, suggesting that different GRP members can participate in distinct stress responses [11, 13, 25]. Given the tissue-specific expression observed in this study (e.g., GmGRP5, highly expressed in flowers and root nodules; GmGRP11, highly expressed in roots), different GmGRPs may have distinct functions in soybean reproductive development, nitrogen fixation, and root biology. These expression patterns suggest specific candidates for follow-up functional studies targeting these processes.

At the molecular level, GR-RBPs are reported to mediate stress responses through several mechanisms: (1) acting as RNA chaperones to facilitate mRNA folding and nuclear-cytoplasmic transport; (2) modulating the stability and translation efficiency of specific mRNAs; and (3) participating in alternative splicing and small RNA metabolism [26, 27]. None of these mechanisms have been directly demonstrated for any GmGRP in the present study; however, GmGRP7 and GmGRP10, which carry RRM domains, are plausible candidates for RNA-binding functions analogous to those reported for AtGRP7. Future work combining RNA-seq, RIP-seq/CLIP-seq, and protein interactomics (Co-IP/MS) would be required to determine whether GmGRP-driven regulatory networks operate in soybean. Specifically, future investigations targeting GmGRP7 and GmGRP10 may clarify their roles as RNA-binding regulators of cold- and stress-responsive gene networks.

From a breeding perspective, if molecular validation confirms that certain GmGRPs (such as GmGRP7/GmGRP8) exhibit cold-responsive expression and contribute to low-temperature tolerance, they would merit consideration as candidates for molecular breeding. Approaches such as marker-assisted selection or CRISPR-based gene editing may be applicable, contingent on phenotypic validation. Because GRP-mediated responses may involve hormone signaling and ROS homeostasis, multi-target strategies could be explored, though the added benefit of such approaches over single-gene modification would require empirical assessment [13, 28].

In summary, this study provides a genome-wide characterization of the soybean GRP family and identifies candidate genes for further experimental investigation of hormone signaling and cold-responsive regulation.

Conclusion

This study systematically identified 11 GmGRP genes across the soybean genome and characterized their physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, gene structure, conserved motifs, promoter cis-elements, chromosomal distribution, and tissue expression patterns. GmGRP members are evolutionarily conserved and subdivide into multiple subgroups, with significant structural and motif divergence between subgroups. Promoter analysis revealed broad involvement of GmGRPs in hormone and stress response regulation; notably, GmGRP7 and GmGRP8 harbor low-temperature response (LTR) cis-elements in their promoters, identifying them as candidates for cold-responsive regulation pending experimental validation.

Tissue expression profiling demonstrated distinct spatial expression patterns among GmGRPs, consistent with functional divergence within the family. These findings provide a genomic reference for investigating the biological functions of GmGRP, with an emphasis on cold-responsive candidates that require experimental validation before any applied use can be considered.
