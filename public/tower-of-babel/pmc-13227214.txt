Training for complexity: Fostering interdisciplinary thinking in One Health graduate education
Authors: Kristen Sullivan, Meredith R Spence Beaulieu, Jade Lyons, Emily Floess, Alexandria P Nelson, Caitlin G Niven, Savanna K Smith, Chenxing Xie, Michael Reiskind
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGraduate training in One Health must equip students to think across disciplines and address complex health challenges through integrative approaches. While case-based and project-based learning are increasingly used to support these goals, there is limited research on how graduate students engage in interdisciplinary exchange, particularly through peer feedback. This study examined structured peer interactions in a graduate-level One Health seminar at North Carolina State University. Six doctoral students from diverse disciplines – ranging from engineering to ecology to technical communication – shared their research projects and provided written and verbal feedback on each other's work.Using qualitative and quantitative methods, we analyzed how peer engagement shaped interdisciplinary thinking. Students consistently identified connections across One Health domains and offered insights that expanded the scope, setting, or methods of their peers' work. Notably, students contributed more ideas outside of their own disciplines than within them and frequently generated new research questions for themselves in the process. Thematic analysis revealed shared interests in environmental and ecological impact, sociocultural context, epidemiological pathways, genetic research, and policy relevance.Findings highlight the value of structured peer feedback as a tool for developing core One Health competencies, particularly systems thinking and interdisciplinary communication, and for fostering collaboration. Students demonstrated readiness to engage across disciplinary boundaries and apply One Health principles in thoughtful, practice-oriented ways. This study contributes to growing efforts to design graduate education that helps students engage meaningfully with the complexity at the heart of One Health work.Keywords: One health education, Interdisciplinary collaboration, Graduate training, Systems thinking, Peer feedback, Workforce development, Interdisciplinary learning

Introduction

There is a growing need for graduate students to be trained in One Health [1], an integrated and unifying framework designed to sustainably balance and promote the health of humans, animals, and ecosystems [2]. Such training must prepare students to work adeptly across disciplines and to employ systems thinking to co-create innovative solutions to complex problems. As the demand for such abilities rises, educational programs designed to cultivate these skills are emerging. However, well-established curricular models are lacking [1], [3], [4], [5], [6].

Key competencies in One Health include interdisciplinary collaboration, systems thinking, effective communication, and the integration of diverse knowledge domains [7], [8]. These competencies are essential for addressing global issues, including emerging infectious diseases, antimicrobial resistance, sustainable food production, and the long-term health impacts of climate change [9]. One Health education literature regularly describes the use of active-learning strategies [10], including case-based approaches to develop these skills. For example, a multidisciplinary One Health course for undergraduate students emphasized small-group activities and summative discussions of instructor-led topics, resulting in increased perceived ability to define One Health, describe its benefits, and explain its utility for addressing high-priority health issues [11]. Case-based approaches have been integrated into interprofessional training programs for medical and veterinary students, leading to increased appreciation of interdisciplinary One Health approaches [12]. Another summer intensive course that featured in-country community engagement experiences for early-career professionals and students reported broadened perspectives and increased recognition of the importance of collaboration after course completion [13]. Others have leveraged the virtual learning environment for project-based Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) that enable global team learning on One Health topics [14]. However, the nature and content of interdisciplinary interactions among students in One Health training programs remain largely unexplored, particularly for students at the graduate level.

Peer feedback is a well-documented best practice in interdisciplinary education. It enhances learning by providing students with diverse perspectives, encouraging critical thinking, and supporting a collaborative learning environment [15], [16], [17]. In the context of One Health education, peer feedback can simulate real-world interdisciplinary interactions, helping students learn to communicate across disciplines and integrate multiple perspectives into their research. While a central goal of graduate-level One Health training programs is to prepare students for interdisciplinary work, less is known about how students actually engage across disciplines; characterizing these interactions can provide insight to inform graduate training and educational approaches. This is important given that difficulties in collaboration and communication across disciplines and stakeholders are frequently cited as challenges to effective One Health implementation [18]. Exploring interdisciplinary student interactions can provide valuable insights to inform curricular design and enhance the effectiveness of One Health education, with the ultimate goal of developing a highly skilled workforce and bolstering long-term One Health implementation.

This study investigates peer-to-peer feedback among graduate students in a Global One Health seminar as a mechanism for building skills in interdisciplinary collaboration, examining how students from diverse disciplines contributed to each other's research projects. By analyzing the research summaries and feedback provided by students from different colleges and programs at North Carolina State University (NC State), this study explores how structured engagement can foster interdisciplinary thinking in One Health graduate education.

Methods

Study design and context

This exploratory, qualitative study examined how graduate students from diverse disciplinary backgrounds engaged with and contributed to one another's research in a One Health seminar course. The study employed a participatory approach in which all six doctoral students enrolled in the course were invited to serve as members of the research team after the course concluded. The research team also included the course instructor and two affiliated faculty and staff from the NC State Global One Health Academy.

The course, Seminar in One Health Problem Solving, focused on the foundational principles of Global One Health and the value of interdisciplinary collaboration in addressing complex health challenges. It was designed to help students identify and articulate how One Health connects to their own research, while engaging with perspectives from other disciplines. In the first half of the course, students explored the history and core concepts of One Health through readings, presentations, and discussion. In the second half, they each presented one of their research projects and received structured peer feedback on how disciplinary perspectives outside their own could inform or strengthen their work. This format was deliberately designed to foster integrative thinking and interdisciplinary exchange.

Participants

All six students enrolled in the graduate seminar course were invited to participate in the study as both members of the research team and as study participants. Five of the students were Global One Health Fellows, selected through a competitive application process to participate in a year-long training program offered by the NC State Global One Health Academy. The sixth student joined the course independently.

All students were pursuing doctoral degrees in different disciplines: applied ecology; civil, construction, and environmental engineering; communication, rhetoric, and digital media; forestry and environmental resources; and genetics and genomics. They represented a range of program stages: one second-year, one third-year, two fourth-year, and two fifth-year students. All six identified as female. Four students identified as white, one as Asian, and one as Black/African-American. Five students were US-based, and one was an international student.

Ethical review

The study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of North Carolina State University and received an exempt determination. The research team was assembled following the conclusion of the seminar course. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Given the participatory nature of the study and small cohort size, complete participant anonymity was not possible.

Data collection

Each student submitted a written research summary before presenting their research in class. These summaries varied in format and length, including dissertation chapters, published papers, and one-page overviews explicitly tailored for the course. Students then gave a 10-min in-class presentation, followed by 15 min of discussion. Peers were asked to provide feedback on how their own disciplinary expertise could contribute to or intersect with the presenter's work.

Following each presentation, students submitted written reaction papers capturing their feedback. A total of 6 research summaries and 30 reaction papers were analyzed in this study.

Data analysis

Qualitative analysis

A codebook was collaboratively developed by the research team, incorporating both a priori codes aligned with the research questions and emergent codes. The codebook contained categories for 53 research areas organized under five One Health domains: 1) human health; 2) animal health; 3) plant health; 4) environmental health/climate change and ecosystem health; and 5) intersections of human, animal, plant, and environment domains. The codebook captured information on research focus, scope, setting, and continent. Reaction papers were coded for types of contributions, including new research questions, methodological suggestions, applications, and potential interdisciplinary synergies.

ATLAS.ti [19] was used for data analysis. Initial coding was conducted jointly by three researchers on a subset of the data (20%) to establish intercoder reliability. The remaining data were double-coded, and discrepancies were resolved through team discussion until consensus was achieved. Data display matrices were utilized to facilitate comparisons within and across participants and to identify overarching themes. These themes were refined through group discussion to ensure they accurately reflected the experiences of class participants. The refined themes are presented in the results.

Quantitative analysis

To explore how students' disciplinary background influenced their peer feedback, we compared the research areas present in each student's research summary with those present in their cumulative reaction papers for all other students in the class. Areas overlapping with the student's own research were classified as within-discipline, whereas new areas were considered outside their disciplinary knowledge. We also examined whether feedback expanded the presenting student's research in terms of research areas, scope, setting, geographic region (i.e., continent), or One Health domain. Comparisons were made between the content of the research summary and the cumulative feedback received from peers. Finally, we assessed whether feedback introduced new research questions relevant to the reaction paper writer's own field, in addition to the presenter's. Given the small sample size (n = 6 students), all results are reported descriptively using averages and ranges to show trends rather than statistical significance.

Results

Graduate student participants consistently extended both their own research and their peers' work beyond disciplinary boundaries, identifying connections across One Health domains. In their research summaries, all six students integrated multiple intersecting domains within the One Health framework, with every project drawing connections between at least two core areas – human, animal, environmental, or plant health. This initial level of interdisciplinarity in the students' research is expected in this context, given that five of the six students were competitively selected for participation in a One Health fellowship program and that all students showed a keen interest in One Health by virtue of their enrollment in the course. The range of research topics was wide and reflected the diversity of disciplines represented in the class. Projects included investigations of microbial communities in pit latrines; comparative analyses of COVID-19 news coverage between the U.S. and China; effects of weather extremes on fecal contamination in rural Bangladesh; genetic and biotechnological approaches for crop improvement; snake and parasite biodiversity dynamics; and environmental risks, trade-offs, and co-benefits of fuel sources for cooking and water boiling in low and middle-income countries.

The peer feedback process demonstrated an active and thoughtful interdisciplinary exchange. Students applied their disciplinary training to generate new insights and broaden existing intersections between domains, and they also extended beyond the bounds of their disciplinary expertise to offer interdisciplinary perspectives. Specifically, they explored interactions between humans and animals, and between the environment/ecosystem and each of the other domains – humans, animals, and plants. Feedback commonly took the form of proposed research questions. Some research questions were directly relevant to the presenter's study; others were applicable to the feedback provider's own research, and many were aimed at potential collaborations spanning fields. In addition to new questions, students suggested alternative study designs, analytical approaches, data collection methods, and research settings (Fig. 1). Occasional challenges were raised, including implementation issues and research risks.

Quantitative findings mirrored these patterns and offered further insight into the breadth and direction of student engagement. Students provided feedback that included more research areas (mean = 14.83, range 3–21) than those present in their own research summaries (mean = 9.67, range 6–15). They were also more likely to contribute ideas from outside their primary discipline (mean = 10.33, range 2–18) than from within it (mean = 4.5, range 1–7), suggesting that students were not only engaging across domains but also building confidence in applying their thinking in unfamiliar disciplinary spaces.

Likewise, even after accounting for a relatively high level of interdisciplinarity in the initial research summaries, most students received feedback that expanded the One Health scope of their work. Four students had at least one additional domain identified in peer feedback that was not present in their summary, and two received feedback incorporating two or more new domains. While research summaries reflected a mean of 3.33 One Health domains (range 3–4), the collective peer feedback introduced an average of 4.17 domains (range 3–5). This broadening of conceptual scope reflects the value of peer dialogue in expanding both individual and collective understandings of complex health challenges.

In addition to thematic content, students offered a range of specific suggestions to strengthen the technical and methodological components of their peers' projects. These included the use of enhanced sampling strategies, integration of interdisciplinary datasets, application of genetic or biotechnological tools, and more innovative uses of systems frameworks. This reflected not only intellectual engagement but also a shared readiness to apply these ideas in collaborative and practical ways.

Crucially, the feedback process appeared to benefit both those giving and receiving input. Students generated new research questions for their own disciplines (mean = 2.33, range 0–6) just as often as for their classmates' fields (mean = 2.33, range 1–4). This reciprocity suggests that interdisciplinary peer engagement can be generative in both directions, encouraging broader thinking and new connections across the group.

Discussion

This study offers a close look at how structured peer engagement in a graduate seminar can help students operationalize One Health principles through interdisciplinary collaboration. By integrating insights from both qualitative and quantitative data, we find clear evidence that, when given the opportunity and structure, graduate students can contribute meaningfully across disciplinary boundaries – even in areas where they have little prior expertise. Rather than limiting themselves to their own research frameworks, students embraced complexity and demonstrated the integrative thinking that One Health education seeks to foster.

A key finding is the extent to which students broadened their thinking beyond their home disciplines. The feedback they provided often introduced new research questions, study settings, and conceptual domains absent from their original project summaries. This pattern suggests not only a willingness to think broadly but also an ability to view research through a systems lens. Students frequently identified points of intersection between disciplines and proposed ways to deepen or expand projects at these junctures. The reciprocity of this exchange – students generating questions for their peers and themselves – reflects the mutual benefit of interdisciplinary peer learning [15].

The increase in the number and range of One Health domains represented through peer feedback supports growing calls in the One Health education literature to invest in pedagogical strategies that go beyond knowledge transmission. Scholars have emphasized the need for educational approaches that promote collaborative learning, critical reflection, and problem-solving across disciplines [20]. Our findings support this shift. Students did not merely identify connections; they used those connections to generate meaningful insights and new research directions grounded in One Health thinking. These findings also align with updated One Health core competencies, which emphasize systems thinking, interdisciplinary communication, and collaboration – central skills for the next generation of One Health professionals [7], [21]. Student contributions in this study reflected precisely these abilities, especially in their readiness to engage across conceptual and methodological boundaries.

This study does have limitations. The small cohort size limits the generalizability of findings, and students' self-selection into the seminar and research project indicates a predisposition toward interdisciplinary thinking. Even so, the patterns observed here provide a valuable look at how structured peer feedback can foster collaborative habits that support both individual growth and collective problem-solving. Future larger-scale or longitudinal studies could explore how such engagement affects research directions over time, or how similar models perform across different institutional or disciplinary contexts.

Despite these limitations, the qualitative and quantitative findings of this study highlight the potential of a structured peer feedback process to serve as a powerful tool for operationalizing One Health principles in graduate education. Students demonstrated core competencies such as systems thinking, interdisciplinary communication, and collaborative readiness – skills essential for working across human, animal, plant, and environmental domains [13]. This study offers a view of what interdisciplinary exchange can look like when intentionally cultivated within a cohort setting, and how such engagement can meaningfully expand the way students think about their work and its potential impact.

Ultimately, this study contributes to an emerging understanding of how One Health education can move from abstract ideal to practical application. When students are supported in structured, reflective environments, they strengthen one another's work and broaden their thinking about their own. As One Health continues to evolve as both a field and a framework, building these habits of interdisciplinary collaboration is essential to preparing the next generation of researchers and practitioners.
