A signature constructed with circadian rhythm-related genes to predict the prognosis and therapy response for low-grade gliomas
Authors: Yiqiang Zhou, Ruwei Ke, Ziliang Hu, Zhepei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Recent studies suggest circadian rhythm-related genes are crucial for immune balance and potential therapeutic targets, but their role in low-grade gliomas (LGG) is not well understood. We analyzed their prognostic significance in glioma progression using predictive models. TCGA-LGG cohort data was analyzed utilizing univariate Cox regression and LASSO to create a strong prognostic model. Kaplan–Meier and ROC analyses validated the model’s predictive accuracy, revealing significantly poorer survival outcomes in high-risk patients relative to those at low risk. Cox regression identified our gene signature as an independent prognosistic predictor. Decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration supported the nomogram’s predictive power. Further analyses, including gene alteration, functional enrichment, tumor immune microenvironment, and chemotherapy sensitivity, highlighted differences between risk groups. We also conducted immunohistochemical analyses on clinical samples and in vitro experiments with glioma cell lines. Our findings highlight the clinical importance of these genes and confirm the prognostic utility of the survival analysis model, suggesting its promise for guiding outcome prediction and immunotherapy strategies in LGG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04052-3.

Introduction

Low-grade gliomas (LGGs) are a subset of primary brain tumors that arise from glial cells [1]. While these tumors are typically classified as benign, certain low-grade gliomas demonstrate infiltrative growth patterns and possess biological characteristics indicative of malignant transformation [2]. To date, treatment of LGGs primarily relies on achieving maximal resection, supplemented by adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy to improve clinical outcomes [3]. The clinical outcomes for patients diagnosed with low-grade gliomas exhibit considerable variability [4], underscoring the pressing need for the identification of reliable biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets specific to LGGs.

Circadian rhythm-related genes (CRRGs) are integral to a variety of biological processes, encompassing metabolism, regeneration, immunology, and endocrinology [5]. Disruptions in biological rhythms [6] have been implicated in the promotion of cancer marker establishment [7]. Xiao and colleagues performed an analysis of CRRGs and ERα protein levels in ERα-positive and ERα-negative breast cancer tissues. Their findings indicated that CRRGs were increased in ER-α-positive breast cancer samples [8]. Their findings indicated that ERα-mediated circadian clock gene expression could facilitate the proliferation of ERα-positive breast cancer cells. Similarly, Wang’s team showed a significant increase in CRRGs in colorectal cancer tissues, as determined by qPCR and immunohistochemical assays [9]. High expression of CRRGs is significantly correlated with advanced TNM staging and lymph node metastasis. Furthermore, the Relles team utilized qPCR in order to analyse CRRGs expression in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma samples. The results of this investigation revealed significantly lower levels (P < 0.025) of circadian clock gene expression in tumour samples compared with adjacent non-tumour tissue samples. This study also established a significant association between reduced circadian clock gene expression in tumors and diminished survival rates, indicating a potential tumor-suppressive role for these genes in pancreatic cancer [10].

Despite increasing research, the clinical implications of CRRGs in low-grade gliomas remains inadequate. To enhance the understanding of patient prognoses and to inform tailored treatment approaches, this research focuses on CRRGs linked to patient outcomes and to construct a robust prognostic model. Additionally, our findings will be validated through comprehensive in vivo and in vitro experimental methodologies.

Independent prognostic analysis of the model

To verify if the risk score’s prognostic independence and to enhance high-risk model clinical relevance, we utilized Cox regression (univariate and multivariate), along with PH hypothesis testing, on the validation cohort data. First, we included both the clinical features of the patients and the previously calculated risk scores in univariate Cox analysis, filtering for results with p < 0.05, followed by PH hypothesis testing. Finally, we conducted multivariate Cox analysis to obtain the final factors with p < 0.05, which were considered as independent prognostic factors. Using these factors, we constructed a nomogram with ‘RMS package’ in R software (Fig. 3A) to analyze potential 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS. Next, we plotted calibration curve utilizing survival ROC package (Fig. 3B), which revealed that the predicted 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS closely matched the observed results (Fig. 3C–E), indicating the reliability of the nomogram. Finally, we utilized “ggDCA package” in R software to plot decision curve analysis (DCA) to evaluate the nomogram (Fig. 3F). The DCA results verified that nomogram yielded a greater net benefit within threshold probability range compared to using a single predictive variable alone. A significant correlation between tumor grading and risk scoring was found (R = 0.35, Fig. 3G).

Correlative assessment between risk scores and clinical pathological features

The comprehensive analysis was conducted on 245 high-risk samples and 253 low-risk samples, revealing mutation rates of 92.65% and 98.38%, respectively (Fig. 4A, B). Genetic mutations can be categorized as missense and nonsense mutations, frameshift insertions and deletions, intron deletions, splice site variations, and multiple mutations, with missense variants being predominant. Significant differences were observed between two risk categories; low-risk group exhibited a greater mutation frequency, with IDH1 and TP53 mutations detected in 91% and 40% of cases, respectively. Within high-risk group, 64% of cases had IDH1 mutations, and 52% had TP53 mutations. IDH1 missense mutations appeared in 64% of high-risk samples, implying their suitability for targeted therapy.

Tumor immune microenvironment and immune profiling

By evaluating immune scores, cell and checkpoint expression levels across two risk categories, we demonstrated that this prognostic model has significant implications in tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). As expected, high-risk patients exhibited higher ESTIMATE, stromal, and immunological scores than low-risk patients (Fig. 6A). Next, we examined the immune cell infiltration differences. The two groups showed no differences in aDCs, mast cells, Tfh, and Th1 cells. Infiltration of macrophages, neutrophils, B cells, dendritic cells (iDCs and pDCs), helper T cells (Th2), TILs, and Tregs, was higher in high-risk group, whereas NK cell infiltration was more prominent in low-risk group. Antigen-presenting cells (APCs), chemokine receptors (CCRs), checkpoints, cytotoxic activity, human leukocyte antigens (HLAs), pro-inflammatory factors, major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC class I), parainflammatory factors, T cell co-inhibition, T cell co-stimulation, type I interferon responses, and type II interferon responses were generally more significant within high-risk patients (Fig. 6B, C). Higher NK cell infiltration across low-risk group indicates stronger tumor-suppressive immunity, while the widespread immune cells and function factor enrichment within high-risk group suggests more active immune infiltration but potentially lower immunotherapy benefit.

Discussion

Low-grade gliomas (LGGs) constitute a prevalent form of primary malignant neoplasm within central nervous system [11]. While the amalgamation of surgical resection with postoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy may enhance clinical outcomes to a certain degree, the heterogeneity [12] of the condition can lead to recurrence and progression to high-grade gliomas in some patients [13]. To date, a comprehensive understanding of the etiology and progression of LGGs has remained elusive. Identification of innovative prognostic markers in patients with LGG and the elucidation of the underlying mechanisms are crucial. The domain of research incorporating CRRGs and tumor biology is burgeoning, given the mounting evidence indicating a profound correlation between the disruption of CRRGs and the onset, progression, as well as therapeutic response of tumors. Disruption of biological rhythms may facilitate establishment of cancer biomarkers. Up-regulation of CRRGs in tumor tissues is correlated with onset and progression of breast and colorectal cancers, whereas reduced expression in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma tissues appears to inhibit these processes. Nonetheless, the precise role of circadian clock genes in lower-grade gliomas (LGGs) remains inadequately defined. We developed a CRRG-based prognostic model and performed an integrative analysis to evaluate its effectiveness in risk classification, chemotherapy sensitivity, and assessing immune landscape alterations in LGG patients. We conducted immunohistochemical analyses on clinical samples and performed in vitro experiments using glioma cell lines. The results underscored the potential clinical significance of these genes and confirmed the prognostic value of the survival analysis model.

Initially, we conducted a comprehensive survey of 42 CRRGs with prognostic significance using single-variable Cox regression analysis to develop predictive features. LASSO Cox regression analysis selected 8 genes (CIART, CSNK1E, HDAC3, LGR4, PTEN, SERPINE1, SF3A3, and SIX3) to establish a prognostic model. The analysis revealed SIX3 functioned as the sole protective factor, whereas the remaining genes acted as risk-associated factors in LGG. SIX3 is lowly expressed in most malignant tumors evidence from lung cancer studies suggests that it can downregulate Wnt/β-catenin target genes pathway [14] functioning as a negative regulator of this pathway [15]. CIART gene plays a key role in cell apoptosis. CIART gene encodes a protein that can interact with tumor suppressor gene P53 [16] and inhibit its function, thereby reducing P53-mediated cell apoptosis and promoting tumor development [17]. Studies have shown that patients with lower CSNK1E expression in breast, colorectal cancers, and oral squamous cell carcinoma tend to have significantly better overall survival rates [18]. Likewise, we also found that CSNK1E expression increase was associated with poorer overall survival in LGG patients. HDAC3 expression is elevated in a variety of malignancies, such as lung [19], breast [20], liver [21], pancreatic [22], melanoma [23], ovarian [24], colorectal [25], and prostate cancers [26]. LGR4 is a member of G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family and possesses a leucine-rich repeat sequence of G protein-coupled receptor, and has various regulatory functions in many cell types and numerous target sites [27]. IQGAP1, ELF3, MMP2, and HOXA9 proteins can be regulated by LGR4 to contribute tumor progression and activate Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/Akt, and MEK1/erk1/AP-1 pathways [28] to stimulate malignant tumors development and metastasis [29]. PTEN, an acronym for Phosphatase and Tensin Homolog, functions as a tumor suppressor gene [30]. PTEN can encode a phosphatase that has the ability to dephosphorylate phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3), acting as a negative regulator of the PI3K-AKT axis to cause cells to excessively proliferate and form tumors. SERPINE1, or plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI-1), serves as a principal regulator of plasminogen-plasmin system within human body. It is recognized as the main inhibitor of both urokinase-type and tissue-type plasminogen activators (uPA and tPA), functioning through inhibition of plasminogen activation [31]. Elevated SERPINE1 expression is strongly associated with reduced fibrinolysis [32], implying its involvement in promoting tumor cell proliferation and migration. SF3A3 is one of regulatory subunits of serine/threonine protein kinase ste20-like kinase 1 (SPK1), which is known to participate in regulating cell cycle process in plants, but the interaction between SPK1 and SF3A3 in animals has been studied less. Studies showed that SF3A3 may induce breast cancer occurrence by affecting gene expression [33]. Here, our study demonstrates that SIX3 expression is markedly decreased in tumor cell lines, whereas the remaining genes incorporated in the model exhibit upregulation. This suggests their potential as effective biomarkers for prognostic prediction. The clinical implications of these genes in cancer, along with their underlying mechanisms, warrant further investigation in future research.

Subsequently, LGG patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk categories according to the median risk score. The high-risk group exhibited both a reduced survival rate and a shorter survival duration relative to the low-risk group. The model’s predictive strength was further validated by ROC curve analysis, which demonstrated excellent discriminative capacity. Prognostic predictor factors, including age, grading, and risk score, were depicted in line charts, and calibration plots for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS validated the model’s predictive precision for LGG prognosis, thereby affirming the line chart as an effective prognostic tool. Subsequently, we assessed the prognostic model’s relevance to the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Consistent with expectations, elevated immune, stromal, and composite ESTIMATE scores were observed in high-risk group. Furthermore, high-risk group showed increased M2 macrophage levels and resting natural killer (NK) cells, while infiltration level of eosinophilic cells was decreased [33]. CCGs expression was found to be elevated, and a high TMB was linked with poor survival prognosis. Additionally, we assessed the efficacy of specific chemotherapeutic agents across various subtypes of LGG. The findings revealed that the estimated half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for sorafenib and vinorelbine was lower in patients classified as low-risk compared to those classified as high-risk. Furthermore, high-risk patients exhibited increased sensitivity to AKT inhibitor VIII, CGP-60,474, dasatinib, gemcitabine, lapatinib, paclitaxel, sorafenib, rapamycin, and vinorelbine. This suggests that these chemotherapeutic agents may provide therapeutic benefits to high-risk patients. Collectively, these insights may contribute to the refinement and personalization of therapeutic approaches for individuals with low-grade glioma.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study offers valuable evidence supporting the prognostic and therapeutic relevance of CRRGs among LGG. However, further investigation through clinical trials is essential to establish its practical feasibility.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The Cancer Genome Atlas TCGA provided RNA-seq and clinical data for 509 LGG samples (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), which were evenly split into training and test groups. Circadian clock genes were selected from Molecular Signatures (MSigDB) [34], repository (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/) as depicted in Supplementary Data 1. Eighty-five GSE4412 samples were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus GEO database. The batch effect present in GEO data was addressed through normalization procedures. Transcriptome profiling was quantified in terms of fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) and subsequently integrated with clinical data for subsequent analysis.

Developing and validating an LGG prognostic model based on CRRGs

Univariate Cox regression analysis of the CRRGs dataset identified genes associated with prognosis, with a P-value threshold of < 0.05. A prognostic model was built utilizing the training cohort. Following the application of the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis, implemented via R package’s glmnet, eight genes were identified as significantly correlated with overall survival (OS) in low-grade glioma (LGG patients) [35]. Each LGG patient’s risk score (RS) was computed utilizing the formula below:

(“Coef” denotes coefficient and “Expr” represent CCG expression)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) reduces dimensionality by projecting correlated features into orthogonal principal components (PCs), without supervision. Visualizing PC scores (e.g., PC1 vs. PC2) reveals clusters, outliers, or trends in the data.

PCA-based classification separated LGG patients into risk groups. Kaplan–Meier (K-M) survival and ROC curves confirmed the model’s prognostic accuracy [36]. This same methodological approach was replicated with the GEO validation cohort to affirm the predictive prowess of our model.

Construction and evaluation of the predictive nomogram

Cox regression—(univariate and multivariate)—was conducted to meticulously identify relevant prognostic variables. Utilizing rms R package, we developed a comprehensive nomogram to incorporate our model with critical clinical variables, including patient’s age and LGGs grading. To assess the nomogram’s accuracy, a calibration curve was crafted to measure any discrepancies. In the final analysis, the reliability of the nomogram was validated through ROC curve construction, ensuring its prognostic precision.

Immune profiling and tumor microenvironment analysis

Cellular and immune response enrichment levels between groups, as determined by immune checkpoint signature, were precisely measured utilizing advanced bioinformatics tools like ESTIMATE [37], CIBERSORT [38], QUANTISEQ [39], and TIMER [40]. A bubble plot was employed to elucidate the diversity in immune responses as predicted by these algorithmic approaches. To conduct comparative analyses of immune checkpoint expression levels across the different groups, we leveraged powerful R packages [41] such as ggplot2 and reshape2 for their robust visualization and data manipulation capabilities [11].

Analysis of functional enrichment

Utilizing gene ontology (GO) and KEGG [42] pathway analyses [41], we delved into the functional roles of genes exhibiting differential expression among diverse risk groups, pinpointed through limma R package. A comparative GSEA [43] was performed across various risk subgroups, with the top five pathways being visually represented. Furthermore, single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) was conducted, with enriched pathways for each risk category illustrated in a heatmap.

Drug sensitivity prediction

We utilized the Genomics of Cancer Drug Sensitivity database to predict LGG patients’ sensitivity to different chemotherapy agents. The IC50 values, representing the concentration required to achieve half-maximal inhibition, were determined for a range of drugs including AKT inhibitor VIII, CGP-60,474, dasatinib, gemcitabine, lapatinib, paclitaxel, sorafenib, rapamycin, and vinorelbine. These calculations were performed using the pRRophetic algorithm in conjunction with ridge regression [11]. Boxplot visualizations were produced utilizing pRRophetic and ggplot2 R packages.

Analysis of mutation status

Somatic mutation profiles were analyzed utilizing maftools R package, and waterfall plots were created to illustrate the most frequently mutated genes.

Collection of clinical samples

This study included 10 LGG patient-derived clinical samples, encompassing both neoplastic and neighboring non-tumorous tissues. Tissue quality variations resulted in only 8 samples fulfilling the inclusion criteria. Following surgical resection, the samples were immediately preserved at − 80 °C for immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University approved the study, and informed consent was secured from each participant or guardian following the Declaration of Helsinki (Clinical trial No.: not applicable).

Immunohistochemically (IHC) staining and evaluation

Surgically excised LGG samples were collected and preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin and paraffin-embedded. The sections underwent deparaffinization using xylene and were rehydrated through sequential alcohol gradients in preparation for staining. Antigen retrieval was conducted by incubating formalin-fixed tissues in a citrate buffer at 95 °C for 20 min to restore protein antigenicity. To block non-specific binding, sections were treated with a methanol solution containing 3% H2O2 for 30 min, quenching endogenous peroxidase activity. Overnight incubation at 4 °C in a humidified chamber was performed using primary antibodies specific to CIART, CSNK1E, HDAC3, LGR4, PTEN, SERPINE1, SF3A3, and SIX3. Following this, sections were treated with biotinylated secondary antibodies and subsequently with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin. The chromogenic reaction was initiated with a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB)-containing substrate solution, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin to visualize nuclei. Sections were dehydrated with graded alcohol, cleared with xylene, and mounted before being examined under a light microscope to evaluate aforementioned protein expression. A 4-point scale (0–3) was utilized to assess protein expression intensity, with scores ≥ 3 classified as positive and 0–2 as negative.

Cell lines and cultivation

Five human glioma cell lines (U251, LN229, SHG44, U118, and U87) were grown in DMEM, and normal astrocyte HMO6 cells in RPMI-1640, under 37 °C and 5% CO₂, with medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

Cell transfection

ShRNA specific for HDAC3 and a non-specific control were provided by Suzhou GenePharma Co., Ltd., Ltd. (Shanghai) and transfected using polybrene transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions, when cells reached 30–40% confluency. Six hours following transfection, the medium was refreshed with fresh medium. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection. The shRNA sequences are outlined below: HDAC3 (homo) shRNA-1–296: 5′-AGGCTTCACCAAGAGTCTTAA; HDAC3 (homo) shRNA-2–934: 5′-TGCTGGGTGGTGGTGGTTATA; sh-NC sense: 5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT.

Total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

RNA extraction was performed utilizing TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa, Dalian, China), and its concentration and purity were then assessed. Upon verification that the RNA quality met the required standards, cDNA was synthesized from RNA templates utilizing reverse transcription kit. The resultant cDNA solution was employed for real-time PCR with fluorescence quantification (RT-qPCR). The RT-qPCR reaction mixture comprised the following components: 10µL of SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix, 7µL of nuclease-free water, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers, and 1 µL cDNA.

GAPDH served as an internal control gene for this study. The target gene’s expression was normalized utilizing ΔCT = (CT(gene) − CT(GAPDH)), and quantified via 2−ΔΔCT method.

The primer sequences are described below: GAPDH-F: 5′-GCACCGTCAAGGCTGAGAAC; GAPDH-R: 5′-TGGTGAAGACGCCAGTGGA; HDAC3-F: 5′-GATGACCAGAGTTACAAGCACC; HDAC3-R: 5′-TTGAAGCAGCCTAATCGATCAC.

Western blot

Proteins were isolated and quantified from both clinical specimens and cell lines, then resolved via SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Following a 10% milk/TBST block, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-HDAC3 and GAPDH (1:1000), followed by a 1-hour incubation with Goat Anti-Mouse/Rabbit IgG H&L secondary antibodies (1:1000). ECL was utilized for detection; band intensities were measured in ImageJ.

Colony formation assay

The U251 and U87 cells (5 × 10^3 per 6-cm dish) were cultured in DMEM over a period of 14 days. Throughout this period, the DMEM medium was replenished every three days. Upon completion of the 14-day culture period, Colonies were fixed (4% PFA), stained (0.1% crystal violet), imaged, and counted with ImageJ software.

Wound healing assay

Cells (2 × 10⁵ U251 or U87) were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated for 12 h. Upon confluence, a linear scratch was introduced utilizing a sterile 20-µL pipette tip. Following two PBS washes to eliminate detached cells and debris, cultures were maintained in serum-free DMEM at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ (humid atmosphere). Scratched regions were imaged at 0 and 24 h utilizing inverted microscopy.

Transwell assay

To evaluate invasive capacity of U251 and U87 cell lines, we employed Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers with an 8.0-µm pore size. Glioma cells (5 × 10⁴) in 200 µL serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers; lower chambers contained 500 µL DMEM with 10% FBS. After 48 h incubation at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, non-invading cells were removed from the upper membrane. Migrated cells on the underside were fixed (4% PFA), stained (crystal violet), imaged, and, imaged microscopically and evaluated with ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were conducted utilizing R software. Spearman’s rank correlation assessed the link between two continuous variables. Categorical comparisons applied chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was utilized for paired continuous data. A p-value < 0.05 was regarded statistically significant.
