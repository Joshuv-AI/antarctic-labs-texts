Evaluating the potential of factor XI/factor XIa inhibitors to address unmet anticoagulation needs in critical patient populations
Authors: Tzu-Fei Wang, Melissa F. Glasner, Rasha Al-Lamee, María Cecilia Bahit, Jonathan M. Coutinho, David Gailani, S. Claiborne Johnston, Carolyn S.P. Lam, Wolfgang C. Winkelmayer, Jeffrey I. Weitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Factor [F]XI/XIa inhibitors have emerged as a promising class of investigational anticoagulants that aim to preserve hemostasis while attenuating thrombosis. By selectively targeting the intrinsic pathway, FXI/XIa inhibition may provide effective antithrombotic protection with a lower risk of bleeding compared with conventional therapies. Early clinical studies across a range of indications suggest that FXI/XIa inhibition is associated with favorable safety profiles and predictable pharmacokinetics. Ongoing phase 3 trials are still evaluating the role of FXI/XIa inhibitors in multiple settings. If efficacy and safety are confirmed in larger studies, FXI/XIa inhibitors may represent an evolution in anticoagulant therapy, potentially expanding access to effective thromboprophylaxis for patients currently underserved by existing options. Continued investigation will be essential to define optimal dosing strategies, identify patient populations most likely to benefit, and establish how these agents integrate into evolving standards of care.

Introduction

Anticoagulant therapy is indicated for the prevention and treatment of thrombotic disorders, including venous thromboembolism (VTE), atrial fibrillation (AF)-related stroke, postoperative thromboprophylaxis, and secondary prevention of arterial and venous thromboembolic events [1]. Approved agents, such as vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), heparins, and oral direct thrombin and factor [F]Xa inhibitors, target critical proteins involved in coagulation and have substantially improved clinical outcomes for patients at risk for, or after having experienced, thrombotic events. Nevertheless, these agents have limitations, including increased risks of life-threatening bleeding, limited utility in certain high-risk populations, and the need for careful dosing or laboratory monitoring in specific clinical contexts [2].

Therapeutic decision making is especially complex in frail and elderly patients and those with comorbid conditions such as advanced chronic kidney disease, prior intracranial hemorrhage, thrombocytopenia, or concurrent antiplatelet drug use [2]. Many of these individuals are underrepresented in large anticoagulation trials, and their management is often guided by extrapolated data or expert consensus rather than high-certainty evidence. Moreover, some patients remain untreated or undertreated due to their or their health care providers’ concerns about bleeding, frailty, fall risks, or inability to access routine laboratory testing or drug-level monitoring [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. These limitations highlight the need for safer, more selective anticoagulants (compared with currently available options) that offer robust antithrombotic effects with reduced hemorrhagic risk.

Background

FXI/XIa inhibitors have emerged as a novel class of anticoagulants with the potential to address this unmet need (Table 1) [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [16], [17], [18], [19], [11]]. Factor XI plays a secondary role in hemostasis under physiological conditions but contributes significantly to thrombus propagation under pathological circumstances. Factor XI inhibitors reduce thrombosis by blocking intrinsic-pathway thrombin amplification (FXI/FXIa-mediated FIX activation), while preserving tissue factor–initiated hemostasis, thereby lowering bleeding risk. Individuals with congenital FXI deficiency typically experience mild bleeding, usually only when exposed to trauma. Additionally, epidemiology studies show that these individuals also have a reduced risk of venous and arterial thrombosis. Potential of FXI/XIa Inhibitors for Patients with Cancer

ML is an 80-year-old female who presented with abdominal pain and weight loss. Workup revealed an ulcerating mass in the gastric body, confirmed by biopsy as a gastric adenocarcinoma, with metastasis to the peritoneum and liver. Palliative chemotherapy was promptly initiated. However, within 4 weeks of starting chemotherapy, she presented to the emergency room with an acute onset of chest pain and shortness of breath. Computer tomography of the chest revealed bilateral pulmonary embolism. Given the presence of an intact intraluminal tumor and concern of high risk of bleeding with an oral anticoagulant, treatment with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was suggested, but she had difficulties administering daily injections. She asked if there was an alternative anticoagulant that she could tolerate better.

Cancer-associated thrombosis (CAT) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, necessitating anticoagulation despite an elevated bleeding risk [22]. Patients with cancer exhibit a 2- to 3-fold higher bleeding risk, while receiving anticoagulation therapy relative to those without cancer [23]. This stems from cancer-related factors such as vascular invasion, bone marrow suppression, and treatment-induced tissue damage [22]. Traditional anticoagulants, including LMWH and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), effectively reduce the risk of recurrent VTE in CAT, but there are always concerns of bleeding with anticoagulation, especially in those with unresected luminal gastrointestinal (GI) or genitourinary (GU) tumors or recent GI surgery [23]. In a large cohort study, the most frequent sites of clinically significant bleeding in patients with cancer on anticoagulation therapy were the GI tract (>50%) and GU tract (∼20%), with intracranial hemorrhage accounting for ∼10% of events [24,25]. Anticoagulation-related bleeding is associated with an increased risk of mortality, especially after intracranial and GI bleeding. In addition, impaired liver or kidney functions and drug–drug interactions between anticoagulants and anticancer therapies occur frequently in patients with cancer, which can alter the metabolism, efficacy, or safety of anticoagulants, further complicating treatment decisions [26,27].

Initial clinical trials in patients without cancer have demonstrated that FXI/XIa inhibitors are associated with a lower risk of bleeding, including GI bleeding, compared with standard agents, mainly in the setting of thromboprophylaxis after hip or knee arthroplasty (Figure 2) [7,8,[28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33]]. These early data provided the impetus for testing FXI/XIa inhibitors in patients with CAT, especially those with high bleeding risks. Therefore, the ASTER and MAGNOLIA trials were designed to investigate abelacimab (a monoclonal antibody against FXI) for CAT [7,8]. The ASTER trial compared abelacimab with apixaban in patients with cancer-associated pulmonary embolism and/or proximal lower-extremity DVT, in whom a DOAC is considered the standard of care, while the MAGNOLIA trial compared abelacimab with dalteparin in patients with unresected GI or GU cancer, where LMWH remains the standard of care (Table 2) [7,8,22,23]. However, in late July 2025, the Independent Data Monitoring Committee observed inferior efficacy of abelacimab compared with that of apixaban in preventing recurrent VTE and recommended pausing enrollment in the ASTER trial and transitioning all patients on abelacimab to standard-of-care anticoagulation. In December 2025, the sponsor decided to terminate both the ASTER and MAGNOLIA trials. The decision to stop MAGNOLIA was reported to be strategic and operational. Detailed data are not available yet, but this new development has raised concerns about the efficacy of FXI/XIa inhibitors in this population. Potential of FXI/XIa Inhibitors for Patients with Cardiovascular Disease

TJ is a 78-year-old female with persistent atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 3 (estimated glomerular filtration rate, 38 mL/min/1.73 m2). She was prescribed apixaban for stroke prevention, but over the past year, she required 2 hospitalizations for overt GI bleeding from colonic angiodysplasia, each with a hemoglobin drop of > 2 g/dL requiring transfusion support. Endoscopic treatment was performed, but the risk of recurrence remains high. Her congestive heart failure, hypertension, age ≥75 years, diabetes mellitus, prior stroke of thromboembolism, vascular disease, age 65-74 years, sec category, scale score is 5 (annual stroke risk ≈ 7%), and her hypertension, abnormal renal/liver function, stroke, bleeding, labile INR, elderly, drugs/alcohol, scale score is 4 (high bleeding risk due to age, hypertension, CKD, and prior major bleeding). Anticoagulation was stopped due to bleeding concerns, and she is referred to consider left atrial appendage occlusion.

Patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD) and their physicians often face complex therapeutic dilemma when anticoagulation is indicated. Despite well-established benefits in preventing thromboembolic events such as stroke in AF, a significant number of patients remain untreated or undertreated due to concerns of bleeding, particularly those with a history of intracranial hemorrhage, advanced age, frailty, or renal impairment [3,5]. Registries show that 30% to 40% of eligible patients with AF remain untreated with anticoagulation, often due to bleeding concerns [5,34].

Antithrombotic treatments are especially challenging in patients who require concurrent antiplatelet agents, such as patients with AF who undergo percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), where dual or triple therapies with anticoagulants and single or dual antiplatelet therapies (DAPTs) are often necessary [35]. However, evidence showed that dual therapy nearly doubles the risk of bleeding compared with monotherapy, and triple therapy results in a >3-fold increased risk for nonfatal and fatal bleeding in patients with AF (Table 3) [[36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41]]. In the AUGUSTUS trial, apixaban plus a P2Y12 inhibitor significantly reduced bleeding complications compared with DAPT plus a vitamin K antagonist, but the overall clinically relevant bleeding rate remained substantial at 10.5% over 6 months (Table 3) [37]. In the What is the Optimal antiplatElet & Anticoagulant Therapy in Patients With Oral Anticoagulation and Coronary StenTing (WOEST) clinical trial, dual therapy (anticoagulant + clopidogrel) reduced bleeding events compared with triple therapy (anticoagulant + clopidogrel + aspirin [ASA]) in patients undergoing PCI but was limited by a small sample size and underpowered to assess thrombotic outcomes [38,43]. In the PIONEER AF-PCI clinical trial, rivaroxaban-based therapy (15 mg once daily plus a single antiplatelet agent or 2.5 mg twice daily plus DAPT) reduced bleeding risk compared with warfarin-based triple therapy, but the study was not powered to assess cardiovascular event efficacy, leaving uncertainty about ischemic outcomes [39]. The RE-DUAL PCI clinical trial demonstrated less bleeding with dabigatran-based dual therapy compared with warfarin-based triple therapy, but the study was limited by insufficient power to evaluate some ischemic endpoints [44]. These findings established the current approach to managing patients with AF undergoing PCI, including using a DOAC instead of a VKA, shortening DAPT duration if possible, stopping ASA early, and continuing single antiplatelet therapy with a P2Y12 inhibitor. However, despite that, the best current strategy remains associated with a significant risk of bleeding, underscoring its limitations. As a result, clinicians continue to make trade-offs between thrombosis prevention and bleeding risks.

In the Anti-Xa Therapy to Lower Cardiovascular Events in Addition to Standard Therapy in Subjects with Acute Coronary Syndrome 2 – Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (ATLAS-ACS 2–TIMI) 51 trial, adding very low–dose rivaroxaban (2.5 mg twice daily) to DAPT after acute coronary syndrome (ACS) reduced the risk of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, or stroke by 20%. Although rates of fatal or intracranial bleeding were not increased, major bleeding was more frequent with rivaroxaban [40]. In the Cardiovascular OutcoMes for People Using Anticoagulation StrategieS (COMPASS) trial, among patients with stable coronary artery disease or peripheral artery disease, rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily combined with ASA reduced cardiovascular events by 24% and lowered rates of all-cause death and stroke. This regimen did not significantly raise the risk of intracranial hemorrhage, but increased GI bleeds [45]. Both trials support dual-pathway inhibition (low-dose rivaroxaban plus antiplatelet therapy) to reduce recurrent cardiovascular events. Although the risk of bleeding improved compared with the traditional antithrombotic agent combination, it remains elevated. Factor XI inhibitors combined with antiplatelet agents may offer dual pathway–like benefits with potentially safer bleeding profiles, making them a natural next step in cardiovascular prevention.

Patients with heart failure (HF) and other comorbid conditions such as AF represent another high-risk group, due to increased thrombotic risks from blood stasis, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [46]. However, therapeutic options are limited because of comorbid renal or hepatic dysfunction, which complicates dosing and increases bleeding risks with VKAs or DOACs [47]. In the COMMANDER-HF trial, rivaroxaban (2.5 mg twice daily) was compared with a placebo in patients with HF, coronary artery disease, and recent decompensation but without AF. While rivaroxaban reduced the rate of thromboembolic events such as stroke (hazard ratio [HR], 0.66) relative to placebo, it did not significantly reduce the composite primary outcome of all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, or stroke and was associated with increased bleeding risks, including fatal bleeding, compared with placebo [48]. The limitations of current anticoagulants in these complex CVD populations highlight an unmet need for safer agents that can dissociate thrombosis prevention from bleeding risks.

Two phase 2 clinical studies investigated the efficacy and safety of novel FXI inhibitors in patients with AF who require oral anticoagulation: Program of Anticoagulation via inChibition of FXIa by the Investigational Compound Asundexian (PACIFIC)-AF with asundexian and AZALEA-TIMI 71 with abelacimab. They both suggest that, compared with DOACs, FXI inhibitors are associated with significantly lower rates of bleeding in patients with AF, potentially enabling safer anticoagulation in patients previously deemed unsuitable for therapy. Specifically, the AZALEA-TIMI 71 study was stopped early due to a greater-than-expected reduction in bleeding with abelacimab compared with standard-of-care rivaroxaban in patients with AF. Major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding occurred at rates of 3.2 and 2.6 events per 100 person-years with 150 and 90 mg abelacimab, respectively, compared with 8.4 events with rivaroxaban. This corresponded to an HR of 0.38 (95% CI, 0.24-0.60) and 0.31 (95% CI, 0.19-0.51), both of which were statistically significant (P < .001) [30]. However, these phase 2 studies were not powered for thrombotic complications [[49], [50], [51]]. Subsequently, the phase 3 Oral faCtor Eleven A iNhibitor asundexian as novel antithrombotiC (OCEANIC)-AF trial, which also enrolled patients with AF, was stopped early because asundexian was associated with a higher risk of stroke or systemic embolism than apixaban (1.3% vs 0.4%; HR, 3.79; 95% CI, 2.46-5.83). Most recently, the LIBREXIA ACS study was also terminated. The study evaluated the efficacy and safety of adding milvexian to standard-of-care antiplatelet therapies in patients with a recent ACS [52]. Following a preplanned interim analysis by the Independent Data Monitoring Committee, it was determined that the trial is unlikely to meet the primary efficacy endpoint, leading to the decision to terminate the trial. Potential of FXI/XIa Inhibitors for Preventing Recurrent Stroke in Patients with Ischemic Stroke

RB is a 72-year-old male with hypertension and type 2 diabetes who had an ischemic stroke 1 month ago. He started standard antiplatelet therapies but developed some nose bleeding and easy bruising. He not only remains worried about the risk of another stroke but also wants to avoid further bleeding. He asked his neurologist if there were any other therapy options.

Anticoagulation in patients with ischemic stroke is challenging due to the need for secondary prevention of stroke and the elevated risk of intracranial hemorrhage [55,56]. In individuals with cardioembolic sources of stroke, such as AF, anticoagulation is the cornerstone of secondary prevention, which significantly reduces the risk of recurrent stroke [55,56]. However, many patients remain untreated or undertreated because of concerns for intracranial bleeding, especially in older adults and those with cerebral amyloid angiopathy, microbleeds on magnetic resonance imaging, or prior hemorrhagic transformation [5].

The timing of anticoagulation initiation in those with AF-related stroke after an acute ischemic stroke is critical. Initiating therapy too early (within 48-72 hours) in patients with large infarcts or hemorrhagic transformation can increase the risk of intracranial hemorrhage. Conversely, delaying anticoagulation beyond 2 weeks may increase the risk of recurrent thromboembolism, particularly in patients with AF or other high-risk cardioembolic sources [5,57]. The early versus lLate initiation of direct oral randomized controlled trial demonstrated that in patients with ischemic stroke and AF, early initiation of DOACs was as safe as late initiation and may reduce the risks of recurrent ischemic stroke [58]. Similarly, the Optimal Timing of Anticoagulation After Acute Ischaemic Stroke (OPTIMAS) trial enrolled patients with AF and acute ischemic stroke, also supported early DOAC initiation (≤4 days) as a safe and effective strategy for secondary stroke prevention [59]. Overall, there was no increase in major bleeding or symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage with early anticoagulation [58,59].

Factor XI/XIa inhibitors offer a new approach that may reshape anticoagulation strategies in stroke prevention, in both cardiac (AF-related) and noncardioembolic stroke [60]. Antithrombotic use in AF-related stroke was summarized in the previous section on CVD, and in this section, we will focus the discussion on noncardioembolic stroke. Preclinical studies and early-phase clinical trials suggest that FXI/XIa inhibitors do not impair primary hemostatic mechanisms and may be well-suited to patients with prior stroke, cerebral small-vessel disease, or high bleeding risks, to provide additional risk reduction for recurrent stroke on top of standard antiplatelet therapy in patients with noncardioembolic stroke, without increasing the risk of bleeding [51,61,62]. A meta-analysis of 4 phase 2 randomized controlled trials included 4732 patients treated with FXI/XIa inhibitors in addition to standard antiplatelet agents and 1798 controls (placebo plus standard antiplatelet therapy or standard DOAC) for primary and secondary stroke prevention [50]. The analysis found that FXI/XIa inhibitors did not increase the risk of major bleeding (relative risk [RR], 1.19; 95% CI, 0.64-2.22), major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding (RR, 1.19; 95% CI, 0.65-2.16), or intracranial hemorrhage (RR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.26-3.19) compared with controls. However, there were no differences in the risk of symptomatic ischemic stroke (RR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.67-1.17), composites of symptomatic stroke and covert infarcts (RR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.87-1.18), or all-cause mortality (RR, 1.21; 95% CI, 0.77-1.90) from these smaller (likely underpowered) phase 2 studies.

Therefore, results from large phase 3 trials will be instrumental (Table 5 [52]). The results of the OCEANIC-STROKE trial were recently revealed at the 2026 International Stroke Conference. A total of 12,327 adults with acute noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or high-risk transient ischemic attack were randomized to asundexian 50 mg once daily vs placebo, in addition to standard-of-care antiplatelet agents for secondary prevention of stroke, with an intervention period up to 31 months [53,63]. Asundexian significantly reduced the risk of ischemic stroke compared with placebo (6.2% vs 8.4%; stratified Cox HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.65-0.84), without a difference in the risk of major bleeding (1.9% vs 1.7%; stratified Cox HR, 1.10; 95% CI, 0.85-1.44), clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding, or symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. The benefit was seen in all subgroups, including different stroke types such as atherosclerotic, lacunar, or stroke of undetermined etiology. The absolute risk reduction in ischemic stroke in 1 year was 1.9%, with the number needed to treat of 53. A second phase 3 trial, LIBREXIA-STROKE, evaluating another oral FXI/XIa inhibitor, milvexian, is ongoing, and results are anticipated within the next year (Table 5). Potential of FXI/XIa Inhibitors for Patients with Chronic Kidney Disease

JS is a 76-year-old male with stage 4 CKD secondary to diabetic nephropathy. He presented with left lower-extremity swelling and pain, and compression ultrasound confirmed an extensive femoral-popliteal deep vein thrombosis. He was initially started on apixaban, but his nephrologist raised concerns about the appropriateness of a DOAC at his level of kidney function (estimated glomerular filtration rate, ∼23 mL/min/1.73 m2). Transition to warfarin was discussed, but the patient expressed frustration with the need for frequent international normalized ratio monitoring and dietary restriction. In addition, his physician was concerned that warfarin would accelerate this patient’s kidney function decline. He asked if there was a safer or more convenient anticoagulant that could be used in the setting of advanced CKD.

Patients with reduced kidney function face several challenges: both elevated baseline risk of thrombosis and of bleeding, and thus a greater potential for anticoagulant-related harm [64]. The kidneys play a critical role in drug clearance and metabolism of many anticoagulants. Therefore, anticoagulation therapy can present substantial challenges in patients with advanced CKD [65]. Additionally, clinical trials have typically excluded patients with advanced CKD and/or kidney failure requiring dialysis, leaving significant evidence gaps in the management of this population [4,66]. Commonly used anticoagulants, including LMWHs and DOACs, are partially or predominantly excreted through the kidneys (Table 6 [[67], [68], [69], [70], [71], [72], [73]]). In patients with reduced kidney function, these agents can accumulate in the bloodstream, increasing the risk of overanticoagulation and serious bleeding events [[64], [65], [66],74,75]. These uncertainties contribute to a cautious approach among clinicians, leading to undertreatment or the avoidance of anticoagulation altogether, even when indicated. Many FXI/XIa inhibitors currently under development (monoclonal antibodies, antisense oligonucleotides, and small-interfering RNA) are not metabolized through the kidneys and represent a promising class of anticoagulants for patients with kidney failure receiving hemodialysis Table 6). A systematic review and meta-analysis of 5 phase 2 randomized trials involving 1270 participants with kidney failure receiving hemodialysis found that FXI/XIa inhibitors (including gruticibart, IONIS-FXIRx, osocimab, and fesomersen) did not significantly increase bleeding risks compared with placebo, with odds ratios of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.47-1.35) for clinically relevant bleeding and 0.51 (95% CI, 0.21-1.28) for major bleeding [76]. Thromboembolic events and all-cause mortality were also not significantly different, with odds ratios of 0.66 (95% CI, 0.28-1.56) and 0.46 (95% CI, 0.15-1.40), respectively [76]. However, these findings were limited by the small sample size and the narrow scope of phase 2 studies. While these studies are not powered for thrombosis outcomes, data from some in vivo and ex vivo studies suggest that FXI/XIa inhibition may reduce dialysis filter clotting or catheter-related thrombosis [76,77]. Larger phase 3 trials are needed to clarify the clinical role of FXI inhibitors in patients with kidney failure receiving dialysis and to establish optimal dosing and management strategies [76].

Discussion

The emergence of FXI/XIa inhibitors marks a potential inflection point in anticoagulation therapy, particularly for populations in whom bleeding risks, comorbidities, or pharmacologic constraints limit the use of current agents. These novel agents offer a paradigm shift by targeting FXI/XIa, which are dispensable for physiological hemostasis but critical in pathologic thrombosis, enabling antithrombotic efficacy with reduced hemorrhagic complications. If proven effective and without safety concerns in large clinical trials, this could have clinically significant implications across high-risk cohorts, including patients with cancer, cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, and renal insufficiency, allowing anticoagulation in those previously deemed at too high risk for anticoagulation.

Multiple phase 2 studies have demonstrated the superior safety of FXI/XIa inhibitors compared with current standard-of-care anticoagulants across different populations (thromboprophylaxis after joint arthroplasty and AF) but are typically underpowered for efficacy. Several large phase 3 trials have already provided substantial knowledge about the efficacy and safety of this novel anticoagulant class, and more trials are underway. All trials to date have consistently demonstrated improved or similar safety of FXI inhibitors compared with the current standard of care. However, with respect to efficacy, the results become more complex with both wins and losses. The explanations of these different outcomes continue to evolve as we accumulate more data and experience, including the type of FXI/XIa inhibitors, their indications being tested for, their comparators, and more. The inferior efficacy of asundexian against apixaban found in OCEANIC-AF for stroke prevention and ASTER for treatment of cancer-associated thrombosis revealed the concern about the efficacy of FXI/Xia inhibitors relative to that of a highly effective anticoagulant such as apixaban. However, the LIBREXIA-AF study comparing milvexian with apixaban in AF is still ongoing. If successful, these studies could enable safer anticoagulation use in patients with AF. The results of several ongoing phase 3 trials are anxiously awaited and will continue to shape our understanding and improve our knowledge of the populations they are most beneficial for and how to best use FXI/XIa inhibitors.
