scBalFlow: a staged flow matching framework for imbalanced single-cell drug perturbation prediction
Authors: Hanwen Lyu, Jiawei Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMotivationConventional drug perturbation prediction models typically employ end-to-end encoder-decoder architectures, directly mapping control samples and perturbation conditions to post-perturbation gene expression profiles. However, these approaches widely overlook the severe class imbalance inherent in perturbation datasets, leading to a predictive bias toward weakly responsive samples.ResultsTo address this bottleneck, we propose scBalFlow, a decoupled two-stage training framework. The first stage predicts the perturbation response intensity under given conditions, employing a Gaussian-Augmented Inference (GAI) strategy to counteract data imbalance. Crucially, the second stage bypasses weakly responsive conditions, while utilizing a Flow Matching model to synthesize highly responsive samples. Comprehensive evaluations on large-scale benchmarks, including SciPlex3 and McFarland, demonstrate that scBalFlow effectively overcomes the imbalance issue and significantly outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods on imbalanced datasets, particularly in capturing complex distribution shifts and maintaining single-cell distributional consistency.Availability and implementationThe source code and datasets are available at GitHub https://github.com/hanwenlv-cmd/scBalFlow and Figshare with doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.33137447. The datasets of SciPlex3, ComboSciPlex, and McFarland underlying this study are available via the pertpy package. Alternatively, they can be downloaded manually from https://exampledata.scverse.org/pertpy/srivatsan_2020_sciplex3.h5ad for SciPlex3, https://exampledata.scverse.org/pertpy/combosciplex.h5ad for combosciplex, and https://exampledata.scverse.org/pertpy/mcfarland_2020.h5ad for McFarland.

1 Introduction

High-throughput drug screening facilitates the identification of potential therapeutic candidates and genetic targets by analyzing cellular gene expression profiles across diverse combinatorial perturbation conditions (Carnero 2006). However, the vast combinatorial space of these conditions makes exhaustive experimental exploration prohibitively expensive and time-consuming (Menden et al. 2019). Consequently, deep learning approaches have emerged to predict the effects of unseen perturbations by training on existing datasets (Aliper et al. 2016).

Current methods typically employ encoder-decoder architectures, such as Conditional Variational Autoencoders [CVAEs; Kingma and Welling (2014] or Conditional Autoencoders [CAEs; Lopez et al. (2018)], which utilize control profiles as inputs and perturbation conditions as covariates to predict post-perturbation states. Despite achieving reasonable accuracy in predicting mean gene expression levels, these models often fail to capture the detailed distribution of individual samples. To overcome this issue, generative models based on multi-step iterative refinement, such as diffusion models (Ho et al. 2020) and conditional flow matching (Lipman et al. 2023), have gained significant traction. However, these methods sharply increase training and inference costs. More crucially, they share a fundamental vulnerability: they are highly prone to overfitting weakly responsive gene expression profiles.

This vulnerability stems from a core characteristic of drug perturbation data: class imbalance, where only a small fraction of candidate compounds triggers significant biological responses. To characterize the imbalance in drug perturbation responses, we visualize the similarity between perturbed and control expression profiles across different doses in Fig. 1. The distributions show that most perturbation conditions remain highly similar to their corresponding controls, revealing a strong prevalence of weakly responsive conditions. Because previous approaches predominantly rely on end-to-end training paradigms, the models are implicitly forced to map a weakly responsive sample to another nearly identical one. This leads to model degradation, where the network simply converges to predicting the global mean expression of the control group. While standard imbalance-mitigation techniques (e.g. resampling or weighted loss functions) can partially alleviate this, end-to-end models inherently require massive parameter capacities to reconstruct high-dimensional gene expression profiles. Consequently, they remain highly susceptible to overfitting despite these corrections.

Rsim2 close to 1 indicates that the perturbed profile remains globally similar to the control, whereas a lower value indicates a stronger global transcriptomic departure. Notably, dose alone does not explain the imbalance: although higher doses in SciPlex3 slightly increase the fraction of responsive conditions, many perturbations remain near-control. McFarland does not permit a clean within-drug dose-effect analysis because most drugs were measured at only one nominal dose, making dose and drug identity inseparable.

To address these challenges, we propose scBalFlow, a staged training framework that decouples the prediction task into response identification and expression synthesis. The framework consists of two primary stages: Response Prediction and Gene Expression Prediction. In the first stage, we quantify the cellular response level by calculating the Rsim2 score between the control and post-perturbation gene expression profiles. Conditions less than a predefined Rsim2 threshold are classified as “highly responsive” (the minority class). Simultaneously, we implement Gaussian-Augmented Inference Loss [GAILoss; Ren et al. (2022)], a specialized weighted loss function designed to handle severe class imbalance. The second stage employs a self-attention-based Flow Matching generative model. Guided by perturbation conditions to control the generation trajectory, the model learns to transform standard noise into the target perturbed gene expression distributions. Since the post-perturbation distribution for weakly responsive conditions is virtually identical to the already-known control group, our staged framework effectively eliminates this unnecessary computation. Consequently, the generative model can dedicate its entire representational power exclusively to modeling the complex distribution shifts of genuinely responsive samples, thereby improving both generation quality and computational efficiency.

1.1 Related works

Traditional methods primarily rely on end-to-end training paradigms, typically employing lightweight encoder–decoder architectures. For instance, PRnet (Qi et al. 2024) utilizes a CAE framework, while scGen (Lotfollahi et al. 2019) and biolord (Piran et al. 2024) leverage CVAEs to learn latent representations of cellular states, achieving perturbation synthesis via linear vector arithmetic in the latent space. Similarly, CPA (Lotfollahi et al. 2023) and chemCPA (Hetzel et al. 2022) employ Adversarial Autoencoders to disentangle perturbation covariates, enabling counterfactual prediction.

Recently, generative models based on Flow Matching, such as CellFlow (Klein et al. 2025) and scDFM (Yu et al. 2026), have been introduced to achieve superior distributional alignment. These methods estimate a continuous vector field that transports samples between source and target distributions, improving the distributional alignment between predicted and observed perturbed cell populations. Despite their modeling differences, these approaches are commonly trained across the full set of perturbation conditions. In contrast, scBalFlow adopts a staged strategy that first identifies perturbation conditions requiring generative modeling and subsequently applies flow matching only to these selected conditions.

2 Materials and methods

The overall architecture of scBalFlow is illustrated in Fig. 2. The framework consists of two main stages: (i) Response Prediction in the first stage, which utilizes a Residual Multi-Layer Perceptron (ResMLP) to predict the Rsim2 score, using a concatenated vector consisting of the encodings of the drug, cell type, and dosage as its input. (ii) Expression Prediction in the second stage, which employs a Flow Matching generative model constructed with multi-layer self-attention blocks. This stage uses Gaussian noise as the latent space and perturbation conditions as control signals via Adaptive Layer Normalization [AdaLN; Peebles and Xie (2023)] to iteratively generate perturbed gene expression profiles through ODE integration.

The overall architecture of scBalFlow framework. scBalFlow adopts a two-stage strategy to address the imbalance between weak and strong perturbation responses. The data are divided into matched control and perturbation groups. Cell-type representations are obtained by linearly projecting the mean expression profile of the corresponding control cells, drugs are represented using PubChem fingerprints, and dosages are encoded through a linear layer. In Stage 1, the precomputed coefficient of determination between the mean control and perturbed expression profiles, denoted as Rsim2, serves as the response-similarity label. The three embeddings are concatenated and fed into an MLP-based response predictor, which estimates Rsim2 and is optimized using GAI Loss. In Stage 2, the real Rsim2 values are used during training to route highly responsive conditions to the flow-matching generator, whereas weakly responsive expression profiles are generated by sampling from the matched control group. During inference, the predicted Rsim2 from Stage 1 is used for routing because post-perturbation expression profiles are unavailable for unseen conditions. The two stages use the same encoding schemes but do not share encoder parameters.

2.1 Response prediction stage

2.1.1 Training data

In this stage, the model does not directly process high-dimensional gene expression data. Instead, it takes the perturbation condition cond as input, which is composed of the drug molecule (SMILES), the target cell type (cell), and the drug dosage (dose). Given Nchem drugs, Ncell cell types, and Ndose dosages, the total number of unique perturbation combinations is Ncond=Nchem×Ncell×Ndose. We pre-calculate the Rsim2 ∈RNcond between the mean expression of the control group and the mean expression of the perturbed group for each condition (details are provided in Section 3, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online). The dataset is thus defined as (cond,Rsim2). Notably, Rsim2 is used to determine whether a perturbation condition requires flow-based generation, whereas evaluation R2 measures the agreement between model-generated expression profiles and the observed perturbed profiles in Stage 2.

2.1.2 Condition encoding

For drug molecular encoding, we selected PubChem as the primary method, representing molecules as 881-dimensional binary vectors where each dimension indicates the presence or absence of a specific chemical substructure. To enable the model to generalize to unseen dosage levels and cell types, dosages are normalized using a reference dosage of 10 μM (i.e. xdose/10) and transformed into an embedding of dimension ddose through a linear layer. Cell types are encoded using the mean gene expression profile of their corresponding control cells and transformed into a cell-type embedding of dimension dcell. The resulting embeddings are concatenated with the drug representation to form the conditional input embedding hcond∈Rddrug+ddose+dcell.

2.1.3 Response predictor architecture

We compared the performance of MLP and Transformer architectures. Results indicated that the ResMLP architecture is more suitable for this task, as excessively high model capacity can lead to overfitting on minority classes in imbalanced datasets. Our Response Predictor consists of multiple residual blocks, each containing a linear layer and a SiLU activation function. 2.1.4 Imbalance training

To determine the optimal strategy for mitigating the severe data imbalance in the dataset, we evaluated several established techniques, including Oversampling, Balanced MSE, and GAILoss. Specifically, the Oversampling strategy operates at the data level by selectively duplicating minority-class instances—namely, those exhibiting low Rsim2 values, which correspond to strong biological responses, to construct more balanced training batches. Alternatively, balanced MSE addresses imbalanced regression from a probabilistic perspective, modifying the standard mean squared error into a statistically principled, weighted loss function that accommodates skewed target distributions. GAI serves as an advanced refinement of this concept; it incorporates Gaussian priors to smooth the target label space, thereby reducing generalization errors in sparse, high-response regions.

2.2 Gene expression prediction stage

2.2.1 Training data

For the generative stage, we exclusively retain perturbed gene expression samples where Rsim2 <threshold, denoted as xp∈RNgene.

2.2.2 Condition encoding

The same drug encoding is reused in the prediction stage and transformed by a linear layer into hdrug∈Rd, where d denotes the target dimension. To support generalization to unseen dosages, the dosage value is normalized using a reference dosage of 10 µM (i.e. xdose/10) and projected into a dosage embedding hdose∈Rd through a linear layer. 2.2.3 Flow matching model

While conventional approaches typically utilize pre-perturbation samples from the same cell type as the initial state, our empirical observations during training suggest that the marginal differences between pre- and post-perturbation profiles result in excessively slow model convergence. To address this, we instead employ a standard Gaussian distribution x0∈RNgene as the initial state for the generation process. The model defines a path between x0 and the target distribution (perturbed expression xp) by learning a time-dependent velocity field vt over the interval t∈[0,1].

2.2.4 Datasets and preprocessing

The single-drug perturbation datasets include SciPlex3 and McFarland. SciPlex3 contains 9-drug OOD split and two dosage splits, while McFarland includes five drug splits and three cell-type splits. The combinatorial drug perturbation dataset, ComboSciPlex, is a scRNA-seq subset derived from the combinatorial perturbation experiments in SciPlex3 and includes one drug combination split. More details are provided in Section 1, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online.

Gene expression values were normalized using log(x+1) and library size normali zation. We select the top 2000 Highly Variable Genes (HVGs) for SciPlex3 and McFarland dataset, and 1000 for ComboSciPlex dataset.

For each condition, we selected the top 50 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) as a subset for additional testing (detailed selection methods are provided in Sections 1 and 2, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online).

3 Experiments

3.1 Results and discussion

We benchmarked our method against state-of-the-art models on the SciPlex3, McFarland, and ComboSciPlex datasets, complemented by comprehensive ablation studies. The evaluation focuses on the accuracy of predicted gene expression values, the distribution alignment of generated samples under conditions with significant perturbation effects, and the biological validity of the generated responses through enrichment pathway analysis, overlap analysis, and perturbation direction consistency assessment. Furthermore, we systematically evaluate the computational efficiency of our method by analyzing training and inference costs in terms of both time and memory consumption. The detailed calculation procedures for all metrics are provided in Section 2, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online.

3.2 Generalization to unseen drugs

On the SciPlex3 dataset, we compared scBalFlow with chemCPA and biolord, using the optimal hyperparameters reported in their respective original studies. Details of the parameter selection strategy of scBalFlow are provided in Table 4, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online. We established an unpredicted baseline (Identity), which directly outputs the control group expression as the prediction. Furthermore, two variants were included for ablation: (i) scBalFlow flow from ctrl, an end-to-end flow matching model that generates expression directly from control samples and conditions, and (ii) scBalFlow w/o threshold, which trains on all conditions without filtering for weakly responsive samples in the second stage.

The drug perturbation prediction results on the SciPlex3 dataset evaluated on DEGs are presented in Fig. 3, while the evaluation results on the whole gene set are provided in Figs 2 and 3, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online. The advantage of scBalFlow becomes increasingly evident as the dosage increases, and it achieves significantly better performance than all baseline models at the highest dosage level. In terms of precision, at low dosages (e.g. 0.01 μM), most methods perform similarly to the baseline with high R2 scores. However, as the dosage increases, the prediction error of traditional methods escalates, often falling below the baseline. In contrast, scBalFlow maintains superior performance, with its advantage over the baseline widening at higher doses. These results highlight the advantage of scBalFlow in handling perturbation imbalance and confirm the necessity of its two-stage design.

3.5 Ablation study

To optimize the Stage-1 perturbation predictor, we compared three imbalance-handling strategies (Oversampling, Balanced MSE, and GAI Loss) with the standard MSE baseline (Fig. 11, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online). Standard MSE collapsed to predicting Rsim2≈1 for all samples under severe imbalance. Oversampling showed limited generalization due to overestimation of response intensity, while Balanced MSE produced unstable low-Rsim2 predictions. In contrast, GAI Loss achieved a better balance between detecting active perturbations and maintaining accurate response estimation.

We further evaluated the sensitivity of GMM components, initial noise scale, noise learning rate, Stage-1 learning rate, and early-stopping patience across 30 configurations and 12 OOD tasks (Fig. 12, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online). The initial noise scale was the most influential factor, with σ0=0.3 achieving the lowest validation MSE, whereas other parameters showed limited effects. The selected model hyperparameters are summarized in Table 4, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online.

We evaluated SciPlex3 thresholds of 1.0 (no filtering), 0.9, 0.8, 0.7, and 0.6. As shown in Table 5, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online, the threshold 0.8 gives the best joint result: R2=0.891, Energy Distance =0.234 and MMD =0.0167.

3.6 Computational cost analysis

We benchmarked computational efficiency on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, including model size, peak GPU memory usage, and inference time (Table 2 and Fig. 13, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online). Overall, computational efficiency depends on representation and workload design. While CellFlow benefits from compact features, scBalFlow achieves scalability through effective workload reduction in the generative stage.

Despite operating in a higher-dimensional gene-expression space, scBalFlow achieves favorable inference efficiency through imbalance-aware routing rather than feature compression. As shown in Table 3, available as supplementary data at Bioinformatics online, threshold filtering substantially reduces the number of conditions and cells processed by Stage-2 without changing the generator architecture, thereby avoiding unnecessary generation for near-control perturbations.

4 Discussion

scBalFlow addresses the severe imbalance in single-cell perturbation datasets by separating response estimation from expression generation. This design prevents the flow model from being dominated by weakly responsive conditions and reduces computation by applying generative modeling only to conditions predicted to exhibit substantial transcriptomic changes. Ablation and threshold-sensitivity analyses further demonstrate that response-aware routing improves both prediction accuracy and efficiency.

However, the current hard routing strategy relies on dataset-specific threshold selection, and Rsim2 may overlook subtle but biologically meaningful responses. Future work could develop uncertainty-aware soft routing and incorporate complementary signals, such as pathway activity or differential-expression patterns, to better capture weak responses while maintaining the efficiency benefits of routing.

5 Conclusion

In this study, we identified severe response imbalance as an important challenge that can limit the ability of generative models to capture strong perturbation responses in single-cell drug perturbation datasets. To address this challenge, we proposed scBalFlow, a decoupled two-stage framework that separates response estimation from gene expression generation. The first stage employs a GAI strategy to estimate perturbation response intensity, while the second stage applies Flow Matching selectively to conditions with substantial transcriptional changes.

Comprehensive evaluations on SciPlex3, McFarland, and ComboSciPlex demonstrate that scBalFlow consistently improves distributional alignment across different perturbation generalization scenarios. The improvements are observed across multiple evaluation metrics and held-out settings, indicating the robustness of the proposed response-aware modeling strategy. The proposed response-aware routing strategy also reduces unnecessary computation associated with near-control perturbations. These results suggest that explicitly modeling response imbalance provides a promising direction for scalable and biologically meaningful single-cell perturbation prediction.
