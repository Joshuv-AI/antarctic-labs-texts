Emergent Interacting Phases in the Strong Coupling Limit of Twisted M M -Valley Moiré Systems: Application to SnSe 2
Ming-Rui Li, Dumitru Calugaru, Yi Jiang, Hanqi Pi, Ammon Fischer, Henning Schlömer, Lennart Klebl, Maia G. Vergniory, Dante M. Kennes, Siddharth A. Parameswaran, Hong Yao, B. Andrei Bernevig, Haoyu Hu

Abstract
We construct an interacting Wannier model for both AA-stacked and AB-stacked twisted SnSe 2 \text{SnSe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} , revealing a rich landscape of correlated quantum phases. For the AA-stacked case, the system is effectively described by a three-orbital triangular lattice model, where each orbital corresponds to a valley and exhibits an approximate one-dimensional hopping structure due to a new momentum-space non-symmorphic symmetry. By exploring the interacting phase diagram using a combination of theoretical methods, including Hartree-Fock mean-field theory and exact solutions of the spin model in certain limits, we identify several exotic quantum phases. These include a dimerized phase with finite residual entropy, valence bond solids, and quantum paramagnetism. In the AB-stacked case, the system realizes an interacting kagome lattice model, where the Wannier orbitals associated with the three valleys form three sublattices. In the strong coupling regime, we use cluster mean-field methods to demonstrate the emergence of a classical spin liquid phase due to the frustrated lattice structure. The high tunability of the moiré system, which allows control over both the filling and interaction strength (via twist angle), renders twisted SnSe 2 \text{SnSe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} a versatile platform for realizing a wide range of exotic correlated quantum phases.

Summary and discussion.

In this work, we construct an interacting Wannier model for both twisted AA-stacked and AB-stacked
\text{SnSe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. In the AA-stacked configuration, the system is described by an interacting three-orbital triangular lattice model, where each orbital corresponds to a valley and develops an approximate 1D hopping structure. We explore the strong-coupling interacting phase diagram at zeroth order (meaning with only the most important terms in the Hamiltonian) and identify various nontrivial quantum phases, including a dimerized phase with non-zero entropy, valence bond solids, and quantum paramagnetism.
Further results will be presented in Refs.
Călugăru et al. 2025
;
Han et al. 2025b
.
In the AB-stacked configuration, the system forms a kagome lattice model, where three sublattices originate from three valleys. In the strong coupling regime, we demonstrate the existence of a “classical spin liquid” phase.

The high tunability of the moiré system allows us to control both the filling and the interaction strength (by tuning the twist angles), enabling a systematic exploration of the phase diagram of correlated systems. Our study provides the first theoretical framework for twisted
\text{SnSe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and predicts a range of exotic quantum phases that could be realized in experiments.

Appendix II
Wannier orbitals

We focus on the interacting physics of the lowest six conduction bands (
6=3\times 2
, with three corresponding to the valleys and two to the spin indices). Since the lowest band for each valley and spin possesses trivial topology
Călugăru et al. 2024
, Wannier orbitals can be constructed by smoothening the gauge
Soluyanov and Vanderbilt 2012
.

II.1
Smooth gauge

We adapt the method introduced in
Soluyanov and Vanderbilt 2012
to smooth the gauge and construct Wannier orbitals. In this section, we provide a brief review of the procedure.
We begin by discussing gauge smoothening for a one-dimensional (1D) system, achieved by constructing a parallel-transport gauge. Starting with the Bloch function:

\displaystyle\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{a}u_{a}(\mathbf{k})\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},a}^{\dagger},

(S2.14)

where
\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
is the electron operator in the band basis with wavefunction
u_{a}(\mathbf{k})
, and
\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},a}^{\dagger}
represents the electron operator with momentum
\mathbf{k}
and orbital
a
. The embedding matrix
Wang et al. 2016
is defined via

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}+\bm{G},a}=\sum_{b}V^{\bm{G}}_{ab}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},b}.

(S2.15)

Our goal is to perform a
U(1)
transformation on the wavefunction
u_{a}(\mathbf{k})
such that
u_{a}(\mathbf{k})
becomes a continuous function throughout the entire Brillouin zone. As the bands are topologically trivial, this is possible.
We first smooth the gauge for
\mathbf{k}\in\{k_{j},{k_{j}=j\frac{2\pi}{N_{1}}}\text{ for }{j=0,...,N_{1}}\}
by requiring the overlap
\langle u(k_{j})|u(k_{j+1})\rangle
to be real and positive, where
|u(k_{j})\rangle=(u_{1}(k_{j}),u_{2}(k_{j}),\dots u_{M}(j_{j}))^{T}
and
M
is the number of orbitals. This is achieved by applying a
U(1)
phase rotation,
|u^{\prime}(k_{j+1})\rangle=e^{-i\beta_{j+1}}|u(k_{j+1})\rangle
, where
\beta_{j+1}=\text{Arg}(\langle u^{\prime}(k_{j})|u(k_{j+1})\rangle)
, for each
|u^{\prime}(k_{j+1})\rangle
sequentially. Such procedure corresponds to unwinding the Berry phase. It starts with the initial condition
|u^{\prime}(0)\rangle=|u(0)\rangle
. By this method, all discontinuities are transformed to the boundary of the Brillouin zone

\displaystyle\langle u^{\prime}(2\pi)|V^{2\pi}|u^{\prime}(0)\rangle=\sum_{a,b}u^{\prime*}_{a}(2\pi)V_{ab}^{2\pi}u^{\prime}_{b}(0)=e^{i\phi_{c}}

(S2.16)

where
\phi_{c}
will be the Wannier center.
We can then remove this discontinuity by letting

\displaystyle|\tilde{u}(k_{j})\rangle=|u^{\prime}(k_{j})\rangle e^{i\phi_{c}k_{j}/(2\pi)},

(S2.17)

which leads to a continuous Bloch wavefunction
\tilde{u}(\mathbf{k})
. We can show this by noticing that the overlapping matrix satisfies

\displaystyle M(k_{j})=\langle\tilde{u}(k_{j+1})|\tilde{u}(k_{j})\rangle=e^{-i\phi_{c}/N_{1}}\approx 1+O(\frac{1}{N_{1}})

(S2.18)

\displaystyle M(k=2\pi)=\langle\tilde{u}(0)|V^{2\pi}|\tilde{u}(2\pi)\rangle=e^{-i\phi_{c}}e^{i\phi_{c}}=1.

(S2.19)

However, we note that after smoothing the gauge, the Wilson loop, which can be evaluated as

\displaystyle W=\sum_{j}(-1)\,\arg\left[\langle\tilde{u}(k_{j+1})|\tilde{u}(k_{j})\rangle\right]=\sum_{j}\frac{\phi_{c}}{N_{1}}=\phi_{c},

(S2.20)

can still be nonzero as long as
\phi_{c}\neq 0
. Each discrete
k
-point contributes
\phi_{c}/N_{1}
to the Wilson loop, and summing over all contributions yields the total Wilson loop, which corresponds to the Wannier center
\phi_{c}
.

U(1)

\displaystyle|u^{\prime}(k_{1}=0,k_{2,j+1})\rangle=e^{-i\beta_{j+1}}|u(k_{1}=0,k_{2,j+1})\rangle,

(S2.21)

where
\beta_{j+1}=\text{Arg}\left(\langle{u}(k_{1}=0,k_{2,j})|{u}(k_{1}=0,k_{2,j+1})\rangle\right)
, and we set the initial point as
|u^{\prime}(k_{1}=0,k_{2}=0)\rangle=|u(k_{1}=0,k_{2}=0)\rangle

\displaystyle|\tilde{u}(k_{1}=0,k_{2})\rangle=|u^{\prime}(k_{1}=0,k_{2})\rangle e^{i\phi_{c0}k_{2}/(2\pi)}

where
\langle u^{\prime}(k_{1}=0,k_{2}=2\pi)|V^{\textbf{b}_{2}}|u^{\prime}(k_{1}=0,k_{2}=0)\rangle=e^{i\phi_{c0}}

(S2.23)

Appendix III
Interacting Wannier Models

In this appendix, we discuss the construction of an interacting model from the Wannier orbitals obtained in
appendix

II
.

III.1
Tight-binding model

Since the bands under consideration are topologically trivial, we construct the Wannier orbitals by smoothing the gauge as discussed above. Consequently, the band basis and the Wannier basis differ by a
U(1)
phase. Then the tight-binding model can be expressed as

\displaystyle H_{t}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}\epsilon_{\mathbf{k},\eta}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}

(S3.43)

with
\epsilon_{\mathbf{k},\eta}
the dispersion of the lowest band obtained from the continuous model. The real-space hopping can be obtained by the following Fourier transformation

\displaystyle t_{\Delta\mathbf{R}}^{\eta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\epsilon_{\mathbf{k},\eta}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{R}}

(S3.44)

III.2
Interactions

We consider the screened Coulomb repulsion with
Lian et al. 2021

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{q})=2\pi\xi^{2}U_{\xi}\frac{\tanh(\xi|\vq|/2)}{\xi|\mathbf{q}|},

(S3.45)

where
\xi
the screening length and
\epsilon
the dielectric constant, and
U_{\xi}=e^{2}/(\epsilon\xi)
. We take the following setup

\displaystyle\xi=10\text{nm}\,,\quad\quad\epsilon=12\,,

(S3.46)

which leads to
U_{\xi}=e^{2}/(\epsilon\xi)=12
meV.
The screened Coulomb repulsion reads

\displaystyle H_{I}=\frac{1}{2\Omega_{tot}}\sum_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{q}}V(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\delta\rho_{-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G}}\delta\rho_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}

(S3.47)

where
\Omega_{tot}
denotes the size of the sample and

\displaystyle\delta\rho_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}=\rho_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}-\langle 0|\rho_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}|0\rangle

(S3.48)

We define the vacuum state
|0\rangle
as the charge neutrality where all Wannier orbitals are empty. To obtain the interactions for the Wannier model, we introduce the density operator and project it onto the Wannier basis

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{G})=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,s,l}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},s,l}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{G},s,l}\approx\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},\eta,s}u_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\eta,*}(\mathbf{k})u_{\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{G}}^{\eta}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\eta,s}

(S3.49)

where
\mathbf{q}\in MBZ,\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{Z}\bm{b}_{M,1}+\mathbb{Z}\bm{b}_{M,2}
,
and the form factor

\displaystyle M^{\eta}_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{Q}}u_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\eta,*}(\mathbf{k})u_{\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{G}}^{\eta}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q})

(S3.50)

where the summation of the
\mathbf{Q}
has been taken with respect to the
\mathbf{Q}
-lattice of the corresponding valley.

The density operator can then be written as

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{G})=\sum_{\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{k},\eta,s}M^{\eta}_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{k})\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\eta,s}

(S3.51)

We notice that,

\displaystyle\rho(-\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{G})=\rho(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{G})^{\dagger}=\sum_{\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{k},ij,\eta,s}M^{\eta,*}_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{k})\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}

(S3.52)

which indicates the hermiticity relation of
M^{\eta}

\displaystyle M_{-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G}}^{\eta}(\mathbf{k})=M^{\eta,*}_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})

(S3.53)

Then we are able to project the interaction onto the
\hat{d}^{\dagger}
electron basis. Combining
eqs.

S3.51
and
S3.47
, we obtain

\displaystyle H_{I}\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\Omega_{tot}}\sum_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{q},\eta,\eta^{\prime},s,s^{\prime},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}V(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})M^{\eta}_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{k})M^{\eta^{\prime}}_{-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\eta,s}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\mathbf{q},\eta,\eta^{\prime},s,s^{\prime},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\eta,s}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k}\textquoteleft-\mathbf{q},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}

(S3.54)

where we have introduced the momentum-space interaction in the
d
electron basis

\displaystyle V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\Omega_{0}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}V(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})M^{\eta}_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{k})M^{\eta^{\prime}}_{-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\Omega_{0}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}V(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})M_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}^{\eta}(\mathbf{k})M_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}^{\eta^{\prime},*}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q})

(S3.55)

It is more convenient to define

\displaystyle{V}_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})=\tilde{V}_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}-\mathbf{q})

(S3.56)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})=\frac{1}{\Omega_{0}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}V(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})M_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}^{\eta}(\mathbf{k}_{1})M_{\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}}^{\eta^{\prime},*}(\mathbf{k}_{2})

(S3.57)

Then we have

\displaystyle H_{I}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{\mathbf{q},\eta,\eta^{\prime},s,s^{\prime},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\tilde{V}_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q},\eta,s}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}+\mathbf{q},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}

(S3.58)

Finally, we perform Fourier transformation which gives the following interaction in the real space

\displaystyle H_{I}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\eta,\eta^{\prime},s,s^{\prime},\Delta\mathbf{R},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{r}_{1},\Delta\mathbf{r}_{2})\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{r}_{1},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{r}_{2},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}

(S3.59)

The real-space interaction tensor is defined as

\displaystyle V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{r}_{1},\Delta\mathbf{r}_{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{3}}\sum_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\tilde{V}_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})e^{i\mathbf{k}\Delta\mathbf{r}_{1}-i\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\Delta\mathbf{r}_{2}+i\mathbf{q}\Delta\mathbf{R}}

(S3.60)

The dominant interactions take the form of density-density interactions and can be written as

\displaystyle H_{V}=\sum_{\eta\eta^{\prime},\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},ss^{\prime}}\frac{V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})}{2}n_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}n_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}},

(S3.63)

where
n_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}=\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}

Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},ss^{\prime}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})n_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}n_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},ss^{\prime}}V_{\eta^{\prime}\eta}(-\Delta\mathbf{R})n_{\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime},s}n_{\mathbf{R}-\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,s^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},ss^{\prime}}\bigg[V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})+V_{\eta^{\prime}\eta}(-\Delta\mathbf{R})\bigg]n_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}n_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}\,,

(S3.64)

we could therefore take
V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})=V_{\eta^{\prime}\eta}(-\Delta\mathbf{R})
.

The density-density interactions are approximately
U(6)
symmetric and can be approximately expressed as

\displaystyle H_{V,U(6)}=\sum_{\eta\eta^{\prime},\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},ss^{\prime}}\frac{V(\Delta\mathbf{R})}{2}n_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}n_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}},\quad V(\Delta\mathbf{R})=\frac{1}{9}\sum_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})

III.3.3
Symmetry

The AA-stacking SnSe
2
has

\displaystyle C_{3z},\quad C_{2x},\quad\mathcal{T}

(S3.73)

symmetries. The system also has an approximate zero-twist symmetry

\tilde{M}_{z}

Călugăru et al. 2024
.
In addition, for each valley, the system also develops
U(1)\times SU(2)=U(2)
symmetry, which leads to a

\displaystyle U(2)\times U(2)\times U(2)

(S3.74)

symmetry. Moreover, the interaction term of the system has an approximate
U(6)
symmetry with a small symmetry breaking of around
3\%
of the on-site Hubbard interactions.

Finally, if we only keep the nearest-neighbor 1D hopping (
eq.

S3.62
) and the density-density interaction (
eq.

S3.63
), which are the two dominant terms in the model, the system exhibits additional effective symmetries. First, such a system possesses particle-hole symmetry, characterized by the transformation

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{C_{3z}^{\eta}(n\bm{a}_{M,1}+m\bm{a}_{M,2}),\eta,s}\mathcal{P}\rightarrow(-1)^{m}\hat{d}_{C_{3z}^{\eta}(n\bm{a}_{M,1}+m\bm{a}_{M,2}),\eta,s},\quad n,m\in\mathbb{Z}

(S3.75)

The additional
(-1)^{m}
is introduced to keep the sign of the hopping term during the particle-hole transformation. This can be easily seen with the hopping term (with only 1D nearest-neighbor hopping) given as

\displaystyle H_{t}=t\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\sum_{n=\pm 1}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R}+nC_{3z}^{\eta}(\bm{a}_{M,2}),\eta,s}

(S3.76)

Under particle-hole transformation, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}H_{t}\mathcal{P}=t\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\sum_{n=\pm 1}(-1)\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}+nC_{3z}^{\eta}(\bm{a}_{M,2}),\eta,s}=t\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\sum_{n=\pm 1}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}+nC_{3z}^{\eta}(\bm{a}_{M,2}),\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}=H_{t}

(S3.77)

The density-density interaction term under particle-hole symmetry becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}H_{V}\mathcal{P}=\sum_{\eta\eta^{\prime},\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},ss^{\prime}}\frac{V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})}{2}(1-n_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s})(1-n_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{V}-\sum_{\eta,s,\mathbf{R}}\bigg[\sum_{\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime},s^{\prime}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})\bigg]n_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}+\sum_{\eta\eta^{\prime},\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},ss^{\prime}}\frac{V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})}{2}

(S3.78)

We observe that
\mathcal{P}H_{V}\mathcal{P}
and
H_{V}
differ only by a chemical potential term and a constant term.
Thus, the model has a particle-hole symmetry.
Under the particle-hole transformation, the filling of the system becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}n_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}(1-n_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s})=6-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}n_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}

(S3.79)

Due to the particle-hole symmetry, it is sufficient to consider the ground states at fillings
\nu=1,2,3
. The ground states at fillings
\nu=4,5,6
can be obtained by applying a particle-hole transformation to the ground states of
\nu=1,2,3
, respectively.

Besides particle-hole symmetry, the system with nearest-neighbor 1D hopping and density-density interactions
eq.

S3.64
exhibits a

\displaystyle U(2)^{L_{0}}\times U(2)^{L_{1}}\times U(2)^{L_{2}}

(S3.80)

symmetry, where
L_{\eta}
denotes the size of the system along the
C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,1}
direction. For valley
\eta
, hopping occurs exclusively along the
C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,2}
direction. Consequently, for each valley, the system can be represented as multiple 1D chains, where electrons are restricted to hopping within a chain and cannot hop between chains.
This restriction gives rise to a
U(2)
symmetry for each chain of electrons within a given valley. As a result, the system exhibits a total symmetry of
U(2)^{L_{0}}\times U(2)^{L_{1}}\times U(2)^{L_{2}}
, where
L_{\eta}
denotes the number of one-dimensional chains of valley
\eta
.

III.4.1
Tight-binding model

The tight-binding model for AB stacking can be written as

\displaystyle H_{t}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}\epsilon_{\mathbf{k},\eta}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}=\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,s}t_{\Delta\mathbf{R}}^{\eta}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,s}

The tight-binding model also exhibits an additional inversion symmetry. Due to time-reversal symmetry, the energy dispersion satisfies

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathbf{k},\eta}=\epsilon_{-\mathbf{k},\eta}

(S3.83)

We can define the following inversion transformation

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\eta,s}\mathcal{I}^{-1}=\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},\eta,s}

(S3.84)

With this symmetry, the tight-binding Hamiltonian exhibits the symmetry of the kagome lattice, corresponding to the space group SG 191 (

Interactions

\displaystyle-O_{\Delta\mathbf{r}_{1},i_{1}j_{1}}O_{\Delta\mathbf{r}_{2},i_{2}j_{2}}+O_{\Delta\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{r}_{2},i_{1}j_{2}}O_{\Delta\mathbf{r}_{1}-\Delta\mathbf{R},i_{2},j_{1}}\bigg]

(S4.96)

\displaystyle M_{(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{1},i_{1},j_{1}),(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{2},i_{2},j_{2})}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\Delta\mathbf{R}}\bigg[V(\Delta\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{x}_{1},i_{1},j_{1},\Delta\mathbf{x}_{2},i_{2},j_{2})-V(\Delta\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{x}_{2}+\Delta\mathbf{R},i_{1},j_{2},\Delta\mathbf{x}_{1}-\Delta\mathbf{R},i_{2},j_{1})\bigg]

(S4.97)

The Hartree-Fock Hamiltonian now reads

\displaystyle H_{int}^{MF}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{x}_{1},\Delta\mathbf{x}_{2},i_{1}j_{1}i_{2}j_{2}}M_{(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{1},i_{1},j_{1}),(\Delta\mathbf{x}_{2},i_{2},j_{2})}

\displaystyle\bigg[O_{\Delta\mathbf{x}_{1},i_{1}j_{1}}d_{\mathbf{R},i_{2}}^{\dagger}d_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{x}_{2},j_{2}}+O_{\Delta\mathbf{x}_{2},i_{2}j_{2}}d_{\mathbf{R},i_{1}}^{\dagger}d_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{x}_{1},j_{1}}-O_{\Delta\mathbf{x}_{1},i_{1}j_{1}}O_{\Delta\mathbf{x}_{2},i_{2}j_{2}}\bigg].

(S4.98)

When
\Delta\mathbf{x}_{1}
or
\Delta\mathbf{x}_{2}=0
, the Hartree-Fock Hamiltonian gives density-hopping terms as

\displaystyle H_{dh}^{MF}=\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta x,i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}}\left[M_{(0,i_{1},j_{1}),(\Delta x,i_{2},j_{2})}n_{i_{1}}\delta_{i_{1},j_{1}}d_{\mathbf{R},i_{2}}^{\dagger}d_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{x},j_{2}}+M_{(\Delta x,i_{1},j_{1}),(0,i_{2},j_{2})}n_{i_{2}}\delta_{i_{2},j_{2}}d_{\mathbf{R},i_{1}}^{\dagger}d_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{x},j_{1}}\right],

(S4.99)

which effectively enhanced the electron hopping amplitude
t
. Moreover, the antiferromagnetic order arises from the superexchange mechanism, with a spin–spin coupling
J\sim\frac{t^{2}}{U}
, where
U
is the onsite Hubbard interaction. Consequently, the density-hopping terms serve to stabilize the AFM phase. Since the strength of the effective hopping in
eq.

S4.99
is proportional to the particle number
n_{i}
, we expect that larger filling has a stronger tendency towards AFM order.

The phase diagram of the momentum-space in
fig.

S15
and that of the real-space in
fig.

S16
exhibit similar structure but with different phase boundaries locations
. As discussed above, the interactions will stabilize the AFM phase. In particular, including the full long-range interaction in the momentum-space calculation shifts the balance in favor of the AFM phase and pushes the onset of ferromagnetism to stronger coupling. The phase boundary is sensitive to the specific interactions due to the large quasi-degeneracy of the mean-field states near the phase boundary.

For convenience, we define the spin operator as

\displaystyle S^{\mu}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}=\sum_{ss^{\prime}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\frac{\sigma^{\mu}_{ss^{\prime}}}{2}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s^{\prime}}

(S5.129)

We have the following relation for the spin operators (Fierz identity)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu}S^{\mu}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}S^{\mu}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta}=\sum_{\mu}\sum_{s_{1}s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}s_{2}^{\prime}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s_{1}}\frac{\sigma^{\mu}_{s_{1}s_{1}^{\prime}}}{2}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s_{1}^{\prime}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,s_{2}}\frac{\sigma^{\mu}_{s_{2}s_{2}^{\prime}}}{2}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,s_{2}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ss^{\prime}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s^{\prime}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,s^{\prime}}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,s}-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{ss^{\prime}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,s^{\prime}}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,s^{\prime}}

(S5.130)

Combining
eqs.

S5.128
and
V.1.1
, we obtain the effective spin-spin coupling
\nu=1

\displaystyle J\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s,s^{\prime}}\sum_{\Delta\mathbf{R}=\pm C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,2}}P_{L}^{\nu=1}\bigg[\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\cdot\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta}+\frac{1}{4}n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}n_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta}\bigg]P_{L}^{\nu=1}

(S5.131)

which is consistent with the Heisenberg exchange coupling derived for multi-orbital Hubbard models, where the valley index plays the role of an orbital degree of freedom.
In addition, we note that for the single-orbital Hubbard model at half-filling in the strong-coupling limit, the density–density interaction term becomes a constant, as each site is constrained to have exactly one electron from a fixed orbital.
with Heisenberg coupling strength

\displaystyle J=2t^{2}/E_{H}

(S5.132)

The full Hamiltonian now reads

\displaystyle H_{J}^{\nu=1}=

\displaystyle P_{L}^{\nu=1}\bigg\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,\eta^{\prime}}\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}n_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+J\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s,s^{\prime}}\sum_{\Delta\mathbf{R}=\pm C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,2}}\bigg[\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\cdot\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta}+\frac{1}{4}n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}n_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta}\bigg]\bigg\}P_{L}^{\nu=1}

(S5.133)

We observe that we have coupling in both spin channel
\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}
and density channel
n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}
. For the spin channel, each spin operator
S^{\mu}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}
at valley
\eta
only couples to the spin operator at the same valley. For valley
\eta
, the effective spin coupling is one-dimensional antiferromagnetic coupling along direction
C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,2}
.
For the couplings in the density channel, we have two types of density-density interactions. One has interaction strength
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
, which comes from projecting the Coulomb repulsions to the Wannier orbital; the other one has interaction strength
J
and comes from the Schrieffer-Wolff transformation. Since the Coulomb repulsion is proportional to
1/\epsilon
, then
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})\propto 1/\epsilon
and
J\propto\epsilon
. Therefore, types of interactions have different behaviors as we tune
\epsilon
, and we thus separate the density-density interactions into two parts.
In the limit where
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})=0
, we only have 1D coupling for both spin and density channels. As a result, the effective spin model
H_{J}^{\nu=1}
possesses an additional
C_{2y}
symmetry defined as

\displaystyle C_{2y}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R},\eta=0}C_{2y}^{-1}=\bm{S}_{C_{2y}(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{r}_{0})-\mathbf{r}_{0},\eta=0}

\displaystyle C_{2y}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R},\eta=1}C_{2y}^{-1}=\bm{S}_{C_{2y}(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{r}_{1})-\mathbf{r}_{2},\eta=2}

\displaystyle C_{2y}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R},\eta=2}C_{2y}^{-1}=\bm{S}_{C_{2y}(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{r}_{2})-\mathbf{r}_{1},\eta=1}.

(S5.134)

In addition, each valley and each chain possesses a
U(2)
symmetry, as discussed in near
eq.

S3.80
.

Ground states of the effective spin model at
\nu=1

To solve the

(

\displaystyle n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}=\sum_{s}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}\hat{d}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s}

(S5.135)

In other words,

\displaystyle[n_{\mathbf{R},\eta},H_{J}^{\nu=1}]=0

(S5.136)

Therefore, we could assume the eigenstates of the
H_{J}^{\nu=1}
are also the eigenstates of the density operator
n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}
. We could characterize the eigenstate
|\psi\rangle
of
H_{J}^{\nu=1}
via the

which are defined as

\displaystyle n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}|\psi\rangle=N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}|\psi\rangle

(S5.137)

with

.
In addition, we also require there to be only one electron per unit cell which indicates

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta}N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}=1,\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{R}

(S5.138)

For a given set of quantum numbers
\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}
with

\displaystyle N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\in\{0,1\},\quad\sum_{\eta}N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}=1\,

(S5.139)

with the corresponding Hilbert space defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}}=\bigg\{|\psi\rangle\bigg|\forall(\mathbf{R},\eta),n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}|\psi\rangle=N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}|\psi\rangle\bigg\}\,,

(S5.140)

we now show that the lowest-energy state within
\mathcal{H}_{\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}}

can be obtained.
For convenience, we consider the system with open boundary conditions.

To obtain the ground state, we first decomposed our Hamiltonian into two parts: the density-density interactions
H_{Charge}^{\nu=1}
and the spin-spin
H_{spin}^{\nu=1}
interactions

\displaystyle H_{J}^{\nu=1}=H_{Charge}^{\nu=1}+H_{Spin}^{\nu=1}

\displaystyle H_{Charge}^{\nu=1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,\eta^{\prime}}\bigg[\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})+\frac{J\delta_{\Delta\mathbf{R},\pm C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,2}}\delta_{\eta,\eta^{\prime}}}{2}\bigg]P_{L}^{\nu=1}n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}n_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime}}P_{L}^{\nu=1}

\displaystyle H_{Spin}^{\nu=1}=J\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s,s^{\prime}}\sum_{\Delta\mathbf{R}=\pm C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,2}}P_{L}^{\nu=1}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\cdot\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta}P_{L}^{\nu=1}

(S5.141)

Here, we also comment that the density operator and spin operator commute with each other

\displaystyle[n_{\mathbf{R},\eta},\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}}]=0

(S5.142)

which suggests

\displaystyle[H_{Charge}^{\nu=1},H_{Spin}^{\nu=1}]=0\,.

(S5.143)

We now aim to find the state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}}
that minimize the energy of the system

\displaystyle E=\langle\psi|H_{Charge}^{\nu=1}|\psi\rangle+\langle\psi|H_{Spin}^{\nu=1}|\psi\rangle

(S5.144)

The energy contribution from the
H_{Charge}^{\nu=1}
term for a given configuration
\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}
can be obtained directly, where
\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}
denotes the set of all
N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}
obtained by varying
\mathbf{R}
and
\eta

\displaystyle E_{Charge}=\langle\psi|H_{Charge}^{\nu=1}|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,\eta^{\prime}}\bigg[\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})+\frac{J\delta_{\Delta\mathbf{R},\pm C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,2}}\delta_{\eta,\eta^{\prime}}}{2}\bigg]N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}N_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime}}

(S5.145)

with
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}}
.

We now discuss the energy contribution from the spin-coupling term
H_{Spin}^{\nu=1}
. For a given configuration
\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}
, we first classify the unit cells into different groups.
Here, we note that each unit cell can be regarded as a “site" of the system. Although a randomly generated configuration may break translational symmetry, we continue to use the terminology of unit cells, each labeled by a lattice vector
\mathbf{R}
.

We notice that, if we only consider
H_{Spin}^{\nu=1}
, spins at
\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2}
interact with each other if and only if there exists
\eta
with
\mathbf{R}_{1}-\mathbf{R}_{2}=\pm C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,2}
and
N_{\mathbf{R}_{1},\eta}=N_{\mathbf{R}_{2},\eta}=1
. Based on this fact, we could use the following rules to classify the unit cells into different groups

•

Unit cell at
\mathbf{R}
and unit cell at
\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R}
belong to the same group if there exists
\eta\in\{0,1,2\}
such that
\Delta\mathbf{R}=\pm C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,2}
and
N_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta}=N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}=1
.

•

If
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}
belong to the same group, and
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{R}^{\prime\prime}
belong to the same group, then
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{R}^{\prime\prime}
also belong to the same group.

Based on the above rules, for given
\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}
, we could classify the unit cells into several groups. Only spin operators within the same group interact with each other through
H_{Spin}^{\nu=1}
.
Two unit cells from different groups are then decoupled if only
H_{Spin}^{\nu=1}
is considered, since they are not interacting with each other directly and are not coupled through other sites.

One particular example for a randomly generated
\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}

We use
\mathcal{S}=\{(\mathbf{R}_{1},\eta),(\mathbf{R}_{2},\eta),...\}\in\mathcal{N}_{\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}}
to denote the group formed by a series of unit cells. The number of unit cells of each group will then depend on the values of
\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}
.
We also comment that a single site could also form a group as shown in
fig.

S21
(left).
In addition, we also let
\mathcal{N}_{\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}}
denote the set of all groups of unit cells we constructed for
\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}

\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}

Since unit cells from different groups are decoupled, we can solve each group of unit cells separately.
Each group of unit cells (
\mathcal{S}

H_{Spin}^{\nu=1}
.

For a spin chain with the open boundary and length
n

\displaystyle nE_{n}

(S5.146)

where

denotes the average energy of each site.
Therefore the energy contribution from

\displaystyle E_{Spin}=\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}}n_{\mathcal{S}}\text{ }E_{n_{\mathcal{S}}}

where
n_{\mathcal{S}}
denotes the number of unit cells of the spin chain described by set
\mathcal{S}

Since

\displaystyle E=E_{Charge}+E_{Spin}

(S5.148)

The corresponding state that minimizes the energy for a given configuration of
\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}

\displaystyle|\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}\rangle=\prod_{\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{N}_{\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}}}|\text{Ground state of spin chain labeled by $\mathcal{S}$ }\rangle

(S5.149)

\{N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}

\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})=0

|\Delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})|\gg J

\nu=1

\displaystyle\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})=0

\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}

\delta V_{\eta,\eta^{\prime}}
.
Numerically, we compare the energies of all charge configurations at filling
\nu=1
with translational symmetry defined by a
3\times 3
supercell.
We find that the energy of the
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
term

\displaystyle E_{\delta V}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,\eta^{\prime}}\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}N_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime}}

(S5.160)

will be minimized by developing valley-polarized states characterized by

\displaystyle N_{\mathbf{R},\eta}=\delta_{\eta,\eta_{0}},\quad\forall\mathbf{R}

(S5.161)

with a given valley number
\eta_{0}
(see
fig.

S21
right panel, for
\eta_{0}=0
).
We note that
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
is always repulsive (
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})>0
), as it originates from the projected Coulomb interaction. This term captures the charge repulsion between electrons, and thus tends to favor more uniform charge distributions. Note that the Wannier centers of the three valleys are close to each other but do not exactly coincide. As a result, one natural way to achieve a more uniform charge distribution is to develop a valley-polarized state, as we have also confirmed numerically.

We note that the
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
term energetically favors valley polarization, while the Heisenberg-type terms proportional to
J
in Eq. (
section

V.1.1
) favor the dimer phase. These two contributions compete: if the energy gain from valley polarization due to
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
exceeds the energy cost from the
J
-terms, the system tends to develop valley polarization. However, since the exact ground state at finite
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
is hard to determine analytically, it is difficult to find the threshold value of
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
at which the valley-polarized state becomes the ground state. We expect, with large enough
|\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})|
(
\gg J
), system tends to develop a valley-polarized state.
After the valley polarization, the system forms infinite spin chains, as illustrated in the right panel of
fig.

S21
.
The ground state of an infinite spin chain is a quantum paramagnetic state, where long-range spin order is suppressed by strong quantum fluctuations.

In summary, the system exhibits different ground states depending on the interaction strength. In the limit of small
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
, it forms a highly degenerate dimer phase. In contrast, for large
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
, the system favors a valley-polarized paramagnetic state.

V.1.3
Comparison between exact solutions of spin model and Hartree-Fock simulations

In this section, we discuss the consistency between our exact results in the spin model and our HF results. We first note that, the spin coupling
J
and the density-density interaction
\delta V_{\eta,\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
scale differently as we tune
\epsilon
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle J\propto\epsilon,\quad\delta V_{\eta,\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})\propto\frac{1}{\epsilon}.

(S5.162)

Therefore, within the strong coupling region, a relatively small
1/\epsilon
corresponds to the limit of small
\delta V_{\eta,\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
, while a relatively large
1/\epsilon
corresponds to the limit of dominant
\delta V_{\eta,\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
. Here, we also comment that
\delta V_{\eta,\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
is much smaller than the on-site Hubbard interactions (see
eq.

S5.102
). Even though we focus on the strong coupling limit (with large on-site Hubbard interaction), the energy contributions from
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})
could still be much smaller than the Heisenberg term that is proportional to
J
, when a relatively large
\epsilon
is considered.
Within the Hartree-Fock simulations, we indeed observe that a valley polarization develops at a relatively large
12/\epsilon
with
12/\epsilon\gtrsim 0.68
for
\theta=3.89^{\circ}
(see also
appendix

IV
).

We now discuss the case where the Heisenberg coupling dominates and the system does not develop valley polarization.
In this region, HF simulations have found an AFM ground state without valley polarization.
For the spin model (
section

V.1.1
),
exact solutions demonstrate a degenerate dimer phase in this limit (
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R}))\approx 0
). We first notice that the dimer state of the spin models is formed by the following spin-singlet state

\displaystyle|\uparrow,\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow,\uparrow\rangle\,.

(S5.163)

Such spin-singlet formation cannot be accurately captured by the HF approximation, within which the ground states are represented by Slater determinant states. However, the antiferromagnetic interactions that drive spin-singlet formation will favor an antiferromagnetic order, which has been observed in HF simulations.

To better understand the Hartree-Fock results in the strong coupling regime, we now analyze the corresponding spin model by treating spin operators as classical spins. This approach is expected to yield consistent results with Hartree-Fock simulation of the electronic model, as both approximations similarly underestimate quantum fluctuations of the spin degrees of freedom.
The energy of the spin model can be approximately written as (at
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})=0

\displaystyle E_{J}^{MF}=

\displaystyle+J\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s,s^{\prime}}\sum_{\Delta\mathbf{R}=\pm C_{3z}^{\eta}\bm{a}_{M,2}}\bigg[\langle\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\rangle\cdot\langle\bm{S}_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\langle n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\rangle\langle n_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta}\rangle\bigg]

A_{l=(\mathbf{R},1)}
: the constraint is violated if both dimers
\phi_{\mathbf{R},1}
and
\phi_{\mathbf{R}+2\bm{A}_{2},1}
are occupied. In this case, we have
\phi_{\mathbf{R},1}=\phi_{\mathbf{R}+2\bm{A}_{2},1}=1
, so
A_{(\mathbf{R},1)}=1
, and the configuration is eliminated by the
1-A_{(\mathbf{R},1)}
.
Then, calculating the degeneracy
\Omega
of the ground states of the dimer phase in our model
section

V.1.2
with
\delta V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})=0
is equivalent to calculating

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega}{\Omega_{dimer}}=\frac{1}{Z}\int D[\psi]\prod_{l}(1-A_{l})e^{-S}=\langle\prod_{l}(1-A_{l})\rangle

(S5.180)

where
\langle\rangle
denotes the expectation value taken with respect to the action
S
and
\Omega_{dimer}
is the ground state degeneracy of the normal triangular lattice dimer model.
Since
S
denotes a non-interacting fermionic system, the expectation value can be calculated via Wick’s theorem. However, for a system with
N
sites, one needs to evaluate the expectation values of the product of at least
N
fermionic fields.
For the product of
N
fermionic fields,
when evaluating its expectation value with Wick’s theorem, one needs to contract the fermionic fields in pairs.
There will be approximately
\sim\frac{N!}{(N/2)!2^{N/2}}\sim N^{N/2}
ways to perform such partitions/contractions.
Therefore, we could only perform a direct exact evaluation of
\Omega/\Omega_{\text{dimer}}
for small system sizes.

Fortunately, an estimation of
\Omega/\Omega_{\text{dimer}}
for an arbitrary system size can be performed. We observe the following exact relation

\displaystyle\prod_{l}(1-A_{l})=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}A_{l}A_{l^{\prime}}+...

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N_{l}}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\bigg[\sum_{l}A_{l}\bigg]^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{l}}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\bigg[A\bigg]^{n}

(S5.181)

where we have used the fact that
A_{l}A_{l}=0
due to the anti-commutating property of the fermions (from
eqs.

S5.170
,
S5.178
and
S5.179
). We also define

\displaystyle N_{l}=\sum_{l}1

\displaystyle A=\sum_{l}A_{l}\,\quad l\in(\mathbf{R},n).

(S5.182)

In addition, since
A_{l}A_{l}=0
and we have at most
N_{l}
distinct
A_{l}
fields,

\displaystyle\bigg[\sum_{l}A_{l}\bigg]^{m}=0,\quad m>N_{l}

(S5.183)

We can therefore extend the summation to infinity and find:

\displaystyle\prod_{l}(1-A_{l})=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{l}}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\bigg[\sum_{l}A_{l}\bigg]^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\bigg[A\bigg]^{n}=\exp[-A\bigg]

(S5.184)

We now have

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega}{\Omega_{dimer}}=\frac{1}{Z}\int D[\psi]e^{-A}e^{-S}=\langle e^{-A}\rangle

(S5.185)

where
A
is the summation of the products of four-fermion operators (
eq.

S5.179
). We can then conclude that calculating the degeneracy is equivalent to calculating the partition function of an effective interacting fermionic system with
A
being quartic in
\psi
. We note that
A
is quadratic in
\phi
(see
eq.

S5.179
), and
\phi
is quadratic in the
\psi
(see
eq.

S5.178
). Therefore,
A
is quartic in the fermionic fields
\psi
.
This result is exact so far.

We can use the cumulant expansion to approximately calculate such a partition function.

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega}{\Omega_{dimer}}=\langle e^{-A}\rangle\approx\exp[-\langle A\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\langle A^{2}\rangle-\langle A\rangle^{2}\bigg]+...\bigg]

(S5.186)

where we have truncated the cumulant expansion to the second order. The corresponding entropy is then:

\displaystyle S=\frac{k_{B}}{N}\log(\Omega)\approx S_{approx}=k_{B}\frac{\log(\Omega_{dimer})}{N}-\frac{k_{B}}{N}\langle A\rangle+\frac{k_{B}}{2N}\bigg[\langle A^{2}\rangle-\langle A\rangle^{2}\bigg]

(S5.187)

with
N
the number of triangular sites of the system.
The first term

k_{B}\log(\Omega_{dimer})/N

is the entropy of the conventional dimer model without the second constraint (
section

V.2
).

-k_{B}\langle A\rangle/N
denotes the first-order contribution, and
k_{B}\bigg[\langle A^{2}\rangle-\langle A\rangle^{2}\bigg]/2N
is the second-order contribution. In principle, we could also systematically refine the estimation of entropy by introducing high-order contributions. However, higher-order contribution is small as we will show in the following section, so we only consider the contribution up to second order.

V.2.5
Review of the dimer model on the triangular lattice

In this section, we review the mapping between the dimer model and the effective fermionic system, for the conventional dimer model without the second constraint introduced at the beginning of
section

V.2
. Further details on the dimer model can also be found in Refs.
Kasteleyn 1963
;
Kasteleyn 1961
;
Fendley et al. 2002
.

First, we aim to prove that calculating the number of dimer configurations (without imposing the dimer constraint
•
‣
section

V.2
)
is equivalent to calculating the partition function of a non-interacting fermionic system (
eq.

S5.171
).

We first discuss the Pfaffian of the matrix. For an antisymmetric
n\times n
matrix
M
, the Pfaffian is defined as

\displaystyle\text{Pf}[M]=\sum_{p}{}^{\prime}(-1)^{p}M_{p_{1},p_{2}}M_{p_{3},p_{4}}\dots

(S5.214)

where
\sum_{p}^{\prime}
denotes the summation over all the permutations with constraints
p_{1}<p_{2},p_{3}<p_{4},p_{2i-1}<p_{2i}
, and
p_{1}<p_{3}<...<p_{n-1}
.
(-1)^{p}
is the parity of the permutation.
Equivalently, we could also define the Pfaffian via

\displaystyle\text{Pf}[M]=\frac{1}{(n/2)!2^{n/2}}\sum_{p}(-1)^{p}M_{p_{1},p_{2}}M_{p_{3},p_{4}}\dots

(S5.215)

\sum_{p}
denotes the summation over all the permutations.
We aim to prove that, by choosing a specific matrix
M
,
one can establish a one-to-one correspondence between the dimer configurations and the terms in the summation of

eq.

S5.214
.

To establish this correspondence, we assume that each row/column index of the matrix corresponds to a lattice site.
Thus, the
n\times n
matrix describes the system with
n
sites.
We further require
M_{ij}\neq 0
if and only if the corresponding bonds are allowed to form a dimer. For our current model,
M_{ij}\neq 0
if and only if the two sites labeled by
i,j
are nearest neighbors.
Therefore, each dimer configuration corresponds to a unique non-zero term in the summation of
eq.

S5.214
.
However,
(-1)^{p}M_{p_{1},p_{2}}M_{p_{3},p_{4}}\dots
is not necessarily equal to 1 for all configurations.
If one can construct
M_{ij}
such that
(-1)^{p}M_{p_{1},p_{2}}M_{p_{3},p_{4}}\dots=1
(or equivalently
-1
) for all the corresponding dimer configurations, then

\displaystyle|\text{Pf}[M]|=\text{ number of dimer configurations}

(S5.216)

One particular construction of the
M
matrix that satisfies our requirements has been discussed in Ref.
Kasteleyn 1963
;
Kasteleyn 1961
;
Fendley et al. 2002
. We have shown it in
fig.

S27
.
Written explicitly, we have

\displaystyle M_{(\mathbf{R},1),(\mathbf{R}^{\prime},1)}=\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}+\bm{A}_{2}}-\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}-\bm{A}_{2}}

\displaystyle M_{(\mathbf{R},2),(\mathbf{R}^{\prime},2)}=-\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}+\bm{A}_{2}}+\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}-\bm{A}_{2}}

\displaystyle M_{(\mathbf{R},1),(\mathbf{R}^{\prime},2)}=\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}}-\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}+\bm{A}_{2}}-\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}-\bm{A}_{1}}-\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}-\bm{A}_{1}-\bm{A}_{2}}

\displaystyle M_{(\mathbf{R},2),(\mathbf{R}^{\prime},1)}=\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}+\bm{A}_{1}}+\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}+\bm{A}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2}}-\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}}+\delta_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}-\bm{A}_{2}}

(S5.217)

Note that we have used
(\mathbf{R},\alpha)
to label the positions of sites instead of the index
i,j
in the above equation
eq.

S5.217

(see also discussions near
eq.

S5.167
). In what follows, we will always use
i
and
j
to label the sites of the system.
.

Via
M
defined in
eq.

S5.217
, it is proved
Kasteleyn 1963
;
Kasteleyn 1961
that calculating the numbers of dimer configurations
\Omega_{dimer}
is equivalent to calculating the Pfaffian of the matrix
M_{ij}
.

\displaystyle\Omega_{dimer}=\text{Pf}[M]

(S5.218)

The Pfaffian of the matrix can be calculated using Grassmann numbers. We introduce fermionic fields to facilitate the computation

\displaystyle\psi_{i},\quad i=1,...,n

(S5.219)

with

\displaystyle\{\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\}=0,\quad\psi_{i}=[\psi_{i}]^{\dagger}

(S5.220)

We define

\displaystyle Z=\int\prod_{i=n,n-1,...,1}d\psi_{i}e^{-S},\quad S=-\sum_{ij}\frac{1}{2}\psi_{i}M_{ij}\psi_{j}

(S5.221)

Using Grassmann number integral

\displaystyle\int d\psi\psi=1

(S5.222)

we find

\displaystyle Z=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n/2}{n/2}!}\int\prod_{i}d\psi_{i}\bigg(\sum_{ij}\psi_{i}M_{ij}\psi_{j}\bigg)^{n/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n/2}{n/2}!}\sum_{p}(-1)^{p}M_{p_{1},p_{2}}...=\text{Pf}[M]

(S5.223)

Therefore, we can calculate the partition function of the effective fermionic system to obtain the ground state degeneracy.

We next show that, from the current Pfaffian technique, we can also calculate the multi-dimer correlations. We use
\phi_{ij}
to denote the dimer connecting two nearest-neighbor sites
i
and
j
. For a given dimer configuration
c
, we define
\phi_{ij}^{c}=1
if the configuration
c
contains the dimer
ij
, and
\phi_{ij}^{c}=0
otherwise. The
m
-dimer correlators are defined as:

\displaystyle F_{i_{1}j_{1},..,i_{m}j_{m}}=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{c}\phi_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{c}\phi_{i_{2}j_{2}}^{c}...\phi_{i_{m}j_{m}}^{c}

(S5.224)

where
\sum_{c}
runs over all the dimer configurations. We now aim to use the fermionic action to calculate the dimer correlators.
Building on the one-to-one correspondence between dimer configurations and the terms in the summation of
eq.

S5.214
,
we can extract the
m
-dimer correlation functions by focusing on the terms on the right-hand side of
eq.

S5.214

that include the product of
M_{i_{1}j_{1}}
(or
M_{j_{1}i_{1}}
), …,
M_{i_{m}j_{m}}
(or
M_{j_{m}i_{m}}
). To extract such terms, we take the derivative with respect to
M_{ij}
. Only terms containing
M_{ij}
in the product survive under the derivative. Thus, we have:

\displaystyle F_{i_{1}j_{1},...,i_{m}j_{m}}=\frac{1}{Z}\bigg[\prod_{l=1}^{m}\bigg(M_{i_{l},j_{l}}\frac{d}{dM_{i_{l},j_{l}}}+M_{j_{l},i_{l}}\frac{d}{dM_{j_{l},i_{l}}}\bigg)\bigg]\text{Pf}[M]

(S5.225)

Since the derivative removes
M_{i,j}
(or
M_{j,i}
), we introduce an additional
M_{i,j}
(or
M_{j,i}
) such that all the terms that survive under the derivative retain the same sign.
In addition, we notice that:

\displaystyle\bigg[\prod_{l=1}^{m}\bigg(M_{i_{l},j_{l}}\frac{d}{dM_{i_{l},j_{l}}}+M_{j_{l},i_{l}}\frac{d}{dM_{j_{l},i_{l}}}\bigg)\bigg]\text{Pf}[M]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg[\prod_{l=1}^{m}\bigg(M_{i_{l},j_{l}}\frac{d}{dM_{i_{l},j_{l}}}+M_{j_{l},i_{l}}\frac{d}{dM_{j_{l},i_{l}}}\bigg)\bigg]\int D[\psi]e^{-S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int D[\psi]\prod_{l=1}^{m}M_{i_{l},j_{l}}\psi_{i_{l}}\psi_{j_{l}}e^{-S},

(S5.226)

where
\psi_{i,l}\psi_{j,l}
comes directly from the derivative of the action
eq.

S5.221
. Therefore,

\displaystyle F_{i_{1}j_{1},...,i_{m}j_{m}}=Z\langle\prod_{l=1}^{m}M_{i_{l},j_{l}}\psi_{i_{l}}\psi_{j_{l}}\rangle

(S5.227)

where the expectation value
\langle\rangle
is taken with respect to the action
S
.

i_{1},j_{1},...,i_{m},j_{m}
are site indices where
i_{l},j_{l}
labels the
l
-th dimer we are interested in.
We thus conclude that calculating the dimer correlators is equivalent to calculating the fermion correlators, which can be done using Wick’s theorem.

3\times 3

This depth-first search approach employs two key optimizations: (1) dynamic prioritization of lattice sites based on their remaining configuration possibilities, and (2) maintenance of a backtracking stack for efficient decision-tree navigation. The algorithm proceeds as follows:

Initialization begins with the central lattice site, enumerating all its possible configurations that are consistent with local bonding constraints and add it to the backtracking list. At each step, the algorithm selects the most constrained site - defined as having the minimal number of permissible valley configurations - from the current backtracking list. For a chosen site, we test each possible bond configuration sequentially. If a configuration is permissible, we commit it and proceed. If it is not, we test the next one. If no configurations are allowed, the algorithm backtracks to the previous decision point.
The newly added valid bond configurations trigger constraint propagation to adjacent sites, where we:

1) Fully determine the valley states and bonds for that site if its bonding configurations can be uniquely specified,

2) Update remaining configuration possibilities for partially constrained sites,

3) Re-sort the backtracking list based on updated permissible configuration counts.

When encountering forbidden configurations, the algorithm immediately prunes that branch of the search tree and retreats to the previous decision point. This constraint-driven approach ensures efficient exploration of the configuration space by focusing computational effort on the most critically constrained regions of the lattice.

The practical implementation of this algorithm requires a finite lattice, which introduces boundary effects. While certain boundary conditions can prevent a perfect tiling of the lattice with the six ground-state patterns, the energy contribution of the boundary is negligible relative to the bulk energy in the thermodynamic limit.
We therefore
focus on the bulk pattern in this study.

To mitigate finite-size effects, we perform our simulations on a large circular lattice that includes all sites satisfying
|\mathbf{R}|\leq 14|\bm{a}_{M,1}|
.
We refer to the central circular region with
|\mathbf{R}|\leq 10|\bm{a}_{M,1}|
as the bulk, and focus solely on its properties.
This setup effectively isolates the bulk properties from edge interference.
Within the central circular region (
|\mathbf{R}|\leq 10|\bm{a}_{M,1}|
), we numerically identify exactly six distinct ground state configurations, modulo lattice translations.
As shown in
fig.

S33
, this result is in perfect agreement with our analytical predictions.

Appendix VI
AB stacking SnSe

We now discuss the phase diagram of AB-stacked SnSe

The system can be separated into two limits: the strong-coupling limit, where interactions dominate the physics, and the weak-coupling limit, where the interaction strength is smaller than or comparable to the bandwidth, making the Fermi surface more relevant to the low-energy behavior.
It is difficult to quantitatively distinguish between the strong-coupling and weak-coupling regimes based on the angles of the system, as the interaction strengths also depend on the value of the dielectric constant

However, numerically, we have observed that (

S5
), for small angles

\epsilon=12
is around
8
. However, at large angles,

, the interaction over bandwidth at
\epsilon=12
is smaller than
4
.
Therefore, qualitatively, the strong-coupling limit is more likely to be realized at smaller twist angles (e.g.,
\theta=3.89^{\circ}
,
4.41^{\circ}
), while the weak-coupling regime is expected to occur at larger angles (e.g.,

,

).

and
7.34^{\circ}
.
Due to the large on-site interactions(
table

S5
), the system does not favor double-occupied states, and we could assume

\displaystyle n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})\in\{0,1\}

(S6.274)

To make the mapping clearer, we also introduce the following Ising variables

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}=2n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})-1\in\{-1,1\}

(S6.275)

Then the charge-charge interaction model can be mapped to an effective Ising model with a magnetic field.
The Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H_{Ising}=\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta,\eta^{\prime}}\frac{V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})}{8}\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime}}+h\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}

(S6.276)

where

\displaystyle h=\frac{-\mu}{2}+\frac{1}{12}\sum_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})

(S6.277)

The effective magnetic field
h
tunes the total magnetization of Ising variables which is equivalent to the filling of the system

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{M}}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\langle\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\rangle=\frac{1}{N_{M}}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\langle 2n_{\mathbf{R},\eta}-1\rangle=2\nu-3

(S6.278)

To understand the properties of the Ising model, we perform the following Fourier transformation

\displaystyle V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\Delta\mathbf{R}}e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{R}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})

(S6.279)

In the momentum space, the Hamiltonian can also be written as

\displaystyle H_{Ising}=N\bigg[\sum_{\mathbf{q},\eta,\eta^{\prime}}\frac{V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q})}{8}\sigma_{-\mathbf{q},\eta}\sigma_{\mathbf{q},\eta^{\prime}}+h\sum_{\eta}\sigma_{\mathbf{q},\eta}\bigg]

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{q},\eta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{R}}

(S6.280)

We show the eigenvalues of
V(\mathbf{q})
in
fig.

S37
(a). Due to the kagome structures, we observe a flat band, which also indicates strong fluctuations in the charge channel.
Qualitatively, the flat eigenvalues of
V(\mathbf{q})
indicate that various types of long-range order, associated with different wave-vectors
\mathbf{q}
and characterized by the condensation of specific
\sigma_{\mathbf{q},\eta}
fields, are energetically degenerate. This suggests strong thermal fluctuations in the charge degrees of freedom at finite temperature and leads to ground state degeneracy at zero temperature.

We also note a key difference between our model and the Ising model (with Ising-Ising interactions and external magnetic fields): in our case, each Ising configuration can exhibit additional degeneracy, whereas in the usual Ising model, each Ising configuration corresponds to a unique state without further degeneracy.
This can be seen from the following mapping between Ising variables and the density operators:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}=-1\Leftrightarrow n_{\eta\uparrow}(\mathbf{R})=n_{\eta\downarrow}(\mathbf{R})=0

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}=+1\Leftrightarrow n_{\eta\uparrow}(\mathbf{R})=1-n_{\eta\downarrow}(\mathbf{R})=1\quad\text{or}\quad n_{\eta\uparrow}(\mathbf{R})=1-n_{\eta\downarrow}(\mathbf{R})=0

(S6.281)

We can observe that singly occupied spin
\uparrow
and spin
\downarrow
states of the original
d
electrons correspond to the same Ising state with
\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}=+1
.
Therefore, each configuration of Ising variables, characterized by
\{\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\}_{\mathbf{R},\eta}
, corresponds to

2^{\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta}(\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}+1)/2}
configurations of density operators due to the additional spin degeneracy of
d
electrons.
Since our strong-coupling model (
eq.

S6.272
) does not introduce interactions in the original physical spin channels (as opposed to the effective Ising spins) and depends only on the charge configuration
{n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})}_{\eta,\mathbf{R}}
, all states sharing the same charge distribution but differing in spin configurations are energetically degenerate. As a result, this additional spin degeneracy does not influence the ground-state energy but contributes to the entropy of the system at the strong-coupling limit.
In addition, we comment that a perturbative term arising from finite hopping leads to a Heisenberg-type interaction between spins, with strength proportional to
t^{2}/U
. This additional coupling can further stabilize long-range spin order and quench the entropy associated with the spin degrees of freedom.

We also comment that, in what follows, we will use density operators
n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})
instead of Ising variables
\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}
. In this subsection, we introduce Ising variables to more clearly illustrate the mapping between the charge-charge interaction model and the Ising model.

VI.1.2
Ground state of the classical charge models

Since the model (
eq.

S6.272
) is classical, it can be solved exactly under certain assumptions. For any given charge configuration, the energy of the system can be computed directly. However, the total number of possible charge configurations scales as
2^{3N}
, where
N
is the number of moiré unit cells and
3
comes from three valleys.
To simplify the problem, we restrict our attention to configurations that respect the translational symmetry of a supercell, as defined in
fig.

S37
(b).
In general, larger supercells allow for a more comprehensive exploration of competing states, leading to more reliable results.
Notably, within our current choice of supercell, the resulting ground state still preserves the translational symmetry of the original moiré unit cell. We thus expect that enlarging the supercell further is unnecessary in this case.

To be more specific, we use
C_{\mathbb{\mathcal{R}}}
to denote the supercell characterized by
\mathbb{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathbb{Z}\bm{a}_{SC,1}+\mathbb{Z}\bm{a}_{SC,2}
with
\bm{a}_{SC,i=1,2}
the lattice vectors for the supercells (
\bm{a}_{SC,1}=2\bm{a}_{M,1},\bm{a}_{SC,2}=2\bm{a}_{M,2}
).
For a given super-cell

,
we use
\{n_{i}=0,1\}_{i=1,...,12}
to characterize the corresponding charge configurations, where
i
is the index of the site within the supercell with position
\mathbf{r}_{i}
and valley
\eta_{i}
correspondingly, and
n_{i}=0,1
denote the sites are filled by 0 or
1
electrons respectively.
Due to the strong on-site repulsion, we do not consider the doubly occupied states, which are high-energy states. Therefore, for a given configuration
\{n_{i}\}_{i=1,...,12}
, we could construct the following state, which has translational symmetry characterized by the supercell in
fig.

S37
(b)

\displaystyle\prod_{\mathcal{R}}\prod_{i}(d_{\mathcal{R}+\mathbf{r}_{i},\eta_{i},s_{\mathbf{r}_{i},\eta_{i}}}^{\dagger})^{n_{i}}|0\rangle

(S6.282)

with arbitrary spin indices
\{s_{\mathbf{r}_{i},\eta_{i}}\}_{i}
.
Since the Hamiltonian we considered only has density-density interactions, the state given in
eq.

S6.282
is also the eigenstate of the Hamiltonian given in
eq.

S6.272
.
Also, with only density-density interaction, the energy of the state will not depend on the spin indices
\{s_{\mathbf{r}_{i},\eta_{i}}\}_{i}
, and only depends on
\{n_{i}\}_{i=1,...,12}

We now aim to obtain the ground state at integer filling
\nu=1,2,3
. To do so, we first generate all possible characteristics of
\{n_{i}\}_{i=1,...,12}
. There will be
2^{12}
of them (
n_{i}\in\{0,1\}
). We then pick out the configurations with the filling we considered. In other words, we only consider the configurations with
\sum_{i}n_{i}=4\nu
(the area of the supercell is 4 times the area of the original unit cell). We then calculate the energy of all configurations that we picked out, and find the configurations with the lowest energy, which corresponds to the ground state.

By comparing the energy of all possible configurations with translational symmetry characterized by the supercell (
fig.

S37
b), we identify the corresponding ground state.
At filling
\nu=1
, the ground states we found are valley-polarized states with

\displaystyle\prod_{\mathbf{R}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta,s_{\mathbf{R}}}|0\rangle

(S6.283)

for given
\eta
and arbitrary spin configurations
s_{\mathbf{R}}
. Here,
\mathbf{R}
characterizes the position of the original unit cell instead of the supercell.
Therefore, the valley-polarized state preserves the translational symmetry of the original moiré unit cell.

At filling
\nu=2
, the ground states are valley-polarized states with

\displaystyle\prod_{\mathbf{R}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta_{1},s_{\mathbf{R}}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\eta_{2},s^{\prime}_{\mathbf{R}}}|0\rangle

(S6.284)

for given
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
with
\eta_{1}\neq\eta_{2}
, and arbitrary spin configurations
s_{\mathbf{R}},s^{\prime}_{\mathbf{R}}
.
Here,
\mathbf{R}
characterizes the position of the original unit cell instead of the supercell. Therefore, the valley-polarized state preserves the translational symmetry of the original moiré unit cell.

At filling
\nu=3
, the ground states are valley-polarized states with

\displaystyle\prod_{\mathbf{R}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},0,s_{\mathbf{R}}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},1,s^{\prime}_{\mathbf{R}}}\hat{d}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},2,s_{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime\prime}}|0\rangle

(S6.285)

with arbitrary spin configurations
s_{\mathbf{R}},s^{\prime}_{\mathbf{R}},s^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{R}}
.
Here,
\mathbf{R}
characterizes the position of the original unit cell instead of the supercell. Therefore, the valley-polarized state preserves the translational symmetry of the original moiré unit cell.

Finally, beyond the mean-field results, we also show that with only nearest-neighbor interactions and at integer fillings, the ground states given in
eqs.

S6.283
,
S6.284
and
S6.285
are the exact ground state of the charge-charge interaction Hamiltonian (
eq.

S6.272
).
We now aim to prove that states in

S6.283
,
S6.284
and

H_{charge}
(

S6.272
).
We first consider the simplest case, which is
\nu=3

(
eq.

S6.285
). The state given in
eq.

S6.285
has a charge configuration
{n}_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})=1
for all
\eta
and all
\mathbf{R}
. Since doubly occupied states are excluded due to the strong on-site Hubbard repulsion, this is the only allowed charge configuration and therefore corresponds to the ground state. However, we note that the spin degeneracy discussed below
section

also results in a trivial ground-state degeneracy.

We next consider the case of
\nu=1
. Due to the equivalence of the three valleys, it suffices to consider the representative state in
eq.

S6.283
, where valley 0 is occupied.
This then leads to the following charge distribution

\displaystyle n_{\eta=0}(\mathbf{R})=1,\quad n_{\eta=1}(\mathbf{R})=n_{\eta=2}(\mathbf{R})=0,\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{R}

(S6.286)

We next consider an arbitrary nearest-neighbor bond connecting two sites, labeled
(\mathbf{R},\eta)
and
(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime})
. For nearest-neighbor bonds on the kagome lattice, we have

Since the same argument applies to any nearest-neighbor bond, the total energy contribution from the charge-charge interaction term

\displaystyle H_{V,int}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta\eta^{\prime}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})n_{\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R})

(S6.288)

is also minimized. In addition, the chemical potential term

is a constant for the fixed total filling.
Therefore,

S6.283
is the ground state at
\nu=1
.

We now consider the case of
\nu=2
. Due to the equivalence of the three valleys, it suffices to consider the representative state in
eq.

S6.284
, where valley 0 and 1 are occupied.
This then leads to the following charge distribution

\displaystyle n_{\eta=0}(\mathbf{R})=1,\quad n_{\eta=1}(\mathbf{R})=1,\quad n_{\eta=2}(\mathbf{R})=0,\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{R}

(

S6.272
) at fixed total filling

\displaystyle\tilde{n}_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})=1-n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})

(S6.290)

The Hamiltonian can then be written as

\displaystyle H_{V}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta\eta^{\prime}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})\tilde{n}_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})\tilde{n}_{\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\bigg[\mu-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime}}\bigg(V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})+V_{\eta^{\prime}\eta}(-\Delta\mathbf{R})\bigg)\bigg]\tilde{n}_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})-\sum_{\mathbf{R}}3\mu+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta\eta^{\prime}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})

(S6.292)

which is a constant at a fixed total filling. Therefore, it is sufficient to consider

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{V,int}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta\eta^{\prime}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})\tilde{n}_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})\tilde{n}_{\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R})

(S6.293)

The charge configuration that minimizes

We now show that the charge configuration given in

. Since we only consider nearest-neighboring coupling, we take an arbitrary nearest-neighbor bond connecting two sites, labeled

and

S6.289

From

which leads to

(S6.294)

This ensures that the energy contribution from the given bond is minimized (given that the nearest-neighbor charge-charge interaction is repulsive). Since the same argument applies to any nearest-neighbor bond, the energy from

VI.1.3
Finite-temperature behavior

The eigenvalues of the interaction matrix show a relatively flat band
fig.

S37
(a). This indicates strong charge fluctuations.
For the kagome Ising model with nearest-neighbor coupling and zero magnetic field, it has been shown
Kanô and Naya 1953
that the strong fluctuations lead to
a non-zero entropy at zero-temperature indicating a classical spin-liquid phase.
Here, our charge-charge interaction model can also be mapped to a Kagome Ising model. To investigate the fluctuations of our model, we investigate the entropy of the system using a cluster mean-field calculation.

We first discuss the cluster mean-field solution. We consider the following density-density interaction terms

\displaystyle H_{V}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta\eta^{\prime}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})n_{\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R})+\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta}(-\mu)n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})

(S6.295)

We introduce supercell denoted by
C_{\mathbb{\mathcal{R}}}
with
\mathbb{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathbb{Z}\bm{a}_{SC,1}+\mathbb{Z}\bm{a}_{SC,2}
and
\bm{a}_{SC,i=1,2}=2\bm{a}_{i=1,2}
the lattice vectors for the super-cells. The choice of one supercell has been shown in
fig.

S37
(b) with 12 sites.
We then separate the Hamiltonian into intra-supercell contributions and inter-supercell contributions

\displaystyle H_{V}=H_{intra}+H_{inter}

\displaystyle H_{intra}=\sum_{\mathcal{R}}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(\mathbf{R},\eta)\in C_{\mathcal{R}},(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime})\in C_{\mathcal{R}}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})n_{\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R})+\sum_{(\mathbf{R},\eta)\in C_{\mathcal{R}}}(-\mu)n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})\bigg]

\displaystyle H_{inter}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathcal{R}\neq\mathcal{R^{\prime}}}\sum_{(\mathbf{R},\eta)\in C_{\mathcal{R}},(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime})\in C_{\mathcal{R^{\prime}}}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})n_{\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R})

(S6.296)

We treat the inter-supercell coupling
H_{inter}
at mean-field levels, which leads to

\displaystyle H^{MF}_{inter}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathcal{R}\neq\mathcal{R^{\prime}}}\sum_{(\mathbf{R},\eta)\in C_{\mathcal{R}},(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R},\eta^{\prime})\in C_{\mathcal{R^{\prime}}}}V_{\eta\eta^{\prime}}(\Delta\mathbf{R})\bigg[\langle n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})\rangle n_{\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle+n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})\langle n_{\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R})\rangle-\langle n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})\rangle\langle n_{\eta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}+\Delta\mathbf{R})\rangle\bigg]

(S6.297)

where the expectation value is taken with respect to the mean-field Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{MF}=H_{inra}+H_{inter}^{MF}

(S6.298)

After the mean-field decoupling, each cluster is decoupled from all others (in the sense that the density operators of one cluster only couple to the expectation value of the density operators of another cluster), allowing it to be solved exactly by directly diagonalizing the Hamiltonian. In other words, each cluster is still influenced by the surrounding clusters, but this effect is treated only at the mean-field level.
The mean-field
\langle n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})\rangle
will be determined self-consistently.

We now describe the calculation of entropy within the mean-field approximation. In this framework, the clusters are decoupled at mean-field level, allowing the partition function to be expressed as:

\displaystyle Z=\bigg[Z_{SC}\bigg]^{N_{SC}},\quad Z_{SC}=\sum_{\mathcal{N}}d[\mathcal{N}]e^{-\beta\mathcal{N}}

(S6.299)

where
N_{SC}
is the number of supercells considered. The partition function for each supercell,
Z_{SC}
, is computed using the mean-field approximation. For each given charge configuration
\mathcal{N}=\{n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})\}_{\mathbf{R}}
of a single cluster, we can solve the mean-field Hamiltonian (
H_{{MF}}
) of that cluster and obtain the corresponding energy
E_{\mathcal{N}}
. However, for each charge configuration
\mathcal{N}
, there exists an additional degeneracy due to the doubly degenerate singly-occupied states (see the discussion near
section

VI.1.1
). The degeneracy factor is given by:

\displaystyle d[\mathcal{N}]=\prod_{\mathbf{R},\eta}2^{n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})}

(S6.300)

We only consider the singly occupied and empty site. States with doubly occupied states are not included in the summation of the charge configurations (
\sum_{\mathcal{N}}
) due to the large on-site Hubbard repulsion.
Then the partition function
Z_{SC}
now is

\displaystyle Z_{SC}=\sum_{\mathcal{N}}d[\mathcal{N}]e^{-\beta\mathcal{N}}

(S6.301)

The entropy of the system is then

\displaystyle S=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\frac{1}{\beta}}\bigg[-\frac{1}{\beta}\log(Z)\bigg]=N_{SC}\sum_{\mathcal{N}}\frac{d[{\mathcal{N}}]e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{N}}}}{Z_{SC}}\bigg[\log(Z_{SC})-\beta E_{\mathcal{N}}\bigg]

(S6.302)

We observe that the entropy calculated above also includes contributions from spin degeneracy.
Since the Hamiltonian we considered at the strong coupling limit (
section

VI.1.3
) does not have any spin-spin couplings, such a degeneracy is trivial. We are mostly interested in the entropy contribution from the strong fluctuations in the charge degrees of freedom.
To isolate the entropy arising from the charge degrees of freedom, we introduce the following quantity to characterize the degeneracy associated with the charge configurations

\displaystyle S_{charge}=N_{SC}\sum_{\mathcal{N}}\frac{e^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{N}}}}{Z_{charge}}\bigg[\log(Z_{charge})+\beta E_{\mathcal{N}}\bigg],\quad Z_{charge}=\sum_{\mathcal{N}}e^{-\beta\mathcal{N}}

(S6.303)

S_{charge}
is constructed by setting the spin degenerate factor
d[\mathcal{N}]
to
1
in the definition of entropy
S
(
eq.

S6.302
).

VI.1.4
Numerical results

We illustrate the entropy calculated using the cluster mean-field approximation in
fig.

for the two smallest angles (
\theta=3.89^{\circ},4.41^{\circ}
), where the strong coupling limit is more likely to be realized.

Enhanced entropy is observed near the filling
\nu=3/2
. This enhancement occurs because
\nu=3/2
corresponds to the kagome Ising model at zero magnetic field, where frustration increases the degeneracy. This mapping is given by
eq.

S6.275

At filling

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\langle\sigma_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\rangle=\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{\mathbf{R},\eta}\langle 2n_{\eta}(\mathbf{R})-1\rangle=\frac{2\nu-3}{3}=0

(S6.304)

This results in zero magnetization in terms of the Ising variables, corresponding to the zero magnetic field in the kagome Ising model. At low temperatures, where
T/V_{1}\lesssim 0.1
, the entropy is observed to approach zero. This behavior arises because the realistic model includes longer-range density-density interactions, such as next-nearest-neighbor terms, that cause deviations from the ideal nearest-neighbor kagome Ising model. Within our mean-field solutions, these longer-range interactions stabilize a long-range ordered state and give a vanishing entropy at low temperatures.

Near integer fillings (
\nu=1,2
), the system tends to develop a valley-polarized state (see
section

VI.1.2
). This state suppresses the system’s entropy. At
\nu=0
and
\nu=3
, all three valleys are either entirely empty or completely filled with one electron each, respectively. This quenching of charge fluctuations reduces the entropy at these fillings.
