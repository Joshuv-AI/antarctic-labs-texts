A meshless MUSCL method for the BGK-Boltzmann equation
Klaas Willems, Axel Klar, Giovanni Russo, Giovanni Samaey, Sudarshan Tiwari

Abstract
We present a numerical method for simulating rarefied gases that interact with moving boundaries and rigid bodies. The gas is described by the BGK equation in Lagrangian form and solved using an Arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian method, in which grid points move with the local mean velocity of the gas [ 47 ] . The main advantage of the moving grid is that the algorithm can deal well with cases where the domain boundaries are time-dependent and the simulation domain contains rigid objects. Due to the irregular nature of the grid, we use a novel meshless MUSCL-like Moving Least Squares Method (MLS) for spatial discretisation coupled with a higher-order Implicit-Explicit Runge-Kutta method. To avoid spurious oscillations at discontinuities, we use the so-called Multi-dimensional Optimal Order Detection (MOOD) method [ 20 ] with an adapted criterion to relax the discrete maximum property. Finally, we employ a new implementation of the boundary conditions that requires no iterative or extrapolation procedure. The method achieves fourth-order in 1D and second-order in 2D for simulations with moving boundaries. We demonstrate the method’s effectiveness on classical test cases such as the driven square cavity, shear layer, and shock tube.



Keywords.
Rarefied gas dynamics, BGK model, gas rigid body interaction, meshfree method, IMEX, MOOD

1
Introduction

The field of rarefied gas dynamics concerns the study of gases in regimes where the mean free path of gas particles is comparable to a characteristic macroscopic length scale of the system. In this regime, classical fluid dynamical models break down and a kinetic description is required. In the case of rarefied gases, the appropriate kinetic model is the Boltzmann equation, a seven-dimensional kinetic integro-differential equation
[
11
]
. Historically, the main application of rarefied gas dynamics was in aerospace engineering, where flow is characterized by high Mach numbers and the mean free path of gas molecules in the outer atmosphere is equal to or larger than the size of man-made objects. Simulations in this field are usually performed with the Direct Simulation Monte Carlo method (DSMC)
[
8
]
, a stochastic particle method for the Boltzmann equation. More recently, the field of rarefied gas dynamics has found applications in micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS)
[
30
]
. In these applications, a gas interacts with small sensors such as accelerometers, micro pumps and micro engines whose characteristic size is comparable to the mean free path of the gas. The simulation of MEMS is a truly multi-physics problem that requires modelling of structural dynamics, electrical properties and gas dynamics
[
40
]
. Especially relevant for the gas dynamics is that the gas flows in MEMS require precise treatment of solid-boundary motion. Furthermore, they are characterized by low Mach numbers, therefore DSMC simulations require a significant amount of particles to be simulated to reduce statistical noise. In
[
55
]
, a parallelized DSMC method for moving boundaries was combined with a fluid solver to deal with this issue. Alternatively, deterministic, grid-based, simulation methods for the Boltzmann equation can be used, of which the most well-known is the spectral method from
[
24
,
36
,
37
]
.
On the other hand, a significant amount of deterministic numerical methods rely on a simplified model known as the BGK-Boltzmann equation
[
7
]
. In this model, the collision integral of the Boltzmann equation is replaced by a relaxation towards the steady-state solution. This model is cheaper to work with and considered good enough for flows that are not too rarefied. Numerical methods based on the BGK equation were first applied to MEMS in
[
25
,
26
]
.

Several classes of numerical methods for the BGK-Boltzmann equation to treat low-Mach flows and moving boundaries have been published. A first class of methods are the so-called semi-Lagrangian methods
[
41
]
. These schemes use the method of characteristics to treat the transport term in the BGK equation. Since the transport is linear, its treatment reduces to an interpolation problem of the distribution function at the previous time step. The BGK relaxation term is treated implicitly to avoid a time step restriction due to the stiffness. However, due to the special structure of the BGK operator, the implicit treatment can be explicitly solved, leading to a very stable and efficient treatment of the collision term. These schemes have been extended to higher-orders
[
41
,
27
]
, strict conservation when using nonlinear reconstructions
[
17
,
16
]
, and do not have a time step restriction. Semi-Lagrangian methods have been extended to moving boundary problems in 1D with one-way coupling from the motion of the rigid body to the gas
[
40
]
. The method relies on a so-called immersed boundary method: the Cartesian grid does not change during computation and the equation is only solved in the grid points in the gaseous domain. Since the exact location of the gas-rigid body is not tracked, this methods seems unpractical to extend to higher dimensions with two-way interaction of the gas and rigid body. Another class of methods are the cut-cells methods from
[
21
,
22
]
. These are first-order conservative finite volume type methods for Cartesian grids. Along the moving boundary, grid cells are “cut” and the scheme is applied to the now polygon-shaped cell. Additionally, we also mention the Unified Gas Kinetic Schemes (UKGS)
[
54
]
. These are finite volume schemes that time integrate the kinetic distribution function and its first three macroscopic moments together. In addition, the distribution function is evaluated at the cell interfaces using an analytical solution that takes into account both transport and collision processes. In
[
14
]
, this method was extended to moving meshes and adaptive velocity discretisations. Finally, it should be noted that deterministic methods for rarefied gases are mostly limited to mono-atomic single species cases. When gas mixtures and complicated particle interactions are required, DSMC methods remain very relevant
[
44
]
. Finally, there exists a class of meshfree methods. Meshfree methods don’t rely on fixed meshes. Instead, they use an arbitrary (potentially moving) set of points without any information on the connectivity, making them ideal for simulations involving deformable domains, rigid body interactions, and free surfaces.

In this work, we improve upon a meshfree ALE method from
[
47
]
in several aspects. This method moves spatial points with the mean macroscopic velocity of the gas. As a consequence, the gas-rigid body boundary is tracked exactly, however, the grid becomes irregular. The transport step is then discretised using a meshless Weighted Essentially Non Oscillating (WENO) method from
[
3
]
. The meshless WENO method computes a non-linear weighing of the divergence on several stencils. The BGK-collision operator is treated implicitly like in the semi-Lagrangian methods. The result is a higher-order method that works on arbitrary domains. Recent work on meshfree methods
[
51
]
has shown that the meshless WENO method suffers from stability issues and is potentially very unconservative. We replace the meshless WENO method with a novel meshless MUSCL method that uses only one central stencil. We combine this with the Multidimensional Optimal Order Detection (MOOD)
[
20
]
method to avoid spurious oscillations at discontinuities. Finally, we use a new boundary routine that does not rely on an iterative procedure or extrapolation. We apply the new method to several test cases in one and two spatial dimensions.

This text is organised as follows. In section
2
, we discuss the BGK model for the rarefied gas in Lagrangian formulation and the Newton-Euler equations for rigid body motion. In section
3
, we discuss the velocity, space and time discretisation. Finally, in section
4
, the scheme is applied to several well-known test cases.

2
The model

In section
2.1
, we give a summary for the BGK model for rarefied gas. In section
2.2
, we rewrite the BGK equation in the so-called Arbitrary-Lagrangian-Eulerian form. This formulation allows for easy treatment of moving boundaries later on. In this manuscript, we limit ourselves to 1D and 2D simulations. In section
2.3
, we use the so-called Chu-reduction to reduce the dimension of the velocity variable to the same dimension of the space variable. This dramatically decreases the computational time. In section
2.4
, we describe the model for the solid objects and discuss the coupling with the rarefied gas.

2.1
The BGK model for a rarefied gas

We consider the BGK-Boltzmann equation for rarefied gas dynamics
[
7
]

\displaystyle\partialderivative{f(x, v, t)}{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(x,v,t)=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\mathcal{M}(f)-f(x,v,t)\right),

(1)

with
f(x,v,t)
the population density of the gas as a function of time
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, space
x\in D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
and kinetic velocity
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The quantity
\tau
is the so-called relaxation time. Compared to the Boltzmann equation, in the BGK equation, the Boltzmann collision operator is replaced by a relaxation operator in which the population density relaxes towards the local Maxwellian distribution

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(f)=\frac{\rho(x,t)}{\left(2\pi R_{s}T(x,t)\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left|v-U(x,t)\right|^{2}}{2R_{s}T(x,t)}\right).

(2)

The macroscopic quantities
\rho(x,t)\in\mathbb{R},U(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
T(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}
are the density, mean velocity and temperature of the gas. These quantities relate to the first three moments of the population density
f(x,v,t)
with respect to
v

\displaystyle\left(\rho(x,t),\rho(x,t)U(x,t),E(x,t)\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\phi(v)f(x,v,t)dv

(3)

with
\phi(v)=\left(1,v,{|v|^{2}}/{2}\right)
. The total energy
E(x,t)
is the sum of the internal energy and the kinetic energy

\displaystyle E(x,t)=\frac{3}{2}\rho(x,t)R_{s}T(x,t)+\frac{1}{2}\rho(x,t)|U(x,t)|^{2}.

(4)

The quantity
R_{s}
is the specific gas constant, which is determined by the universal gas constant and the molar mass of the gas of interest as
R_{s}=R/m_{A}
, where
R
is the universal gas constant, and
m_{A}
is the atomic weights, expressed in Kg/mole. The elements in
\phi(v)
are referred to as the collision invariants because due to (
2
) and (
3
)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(\mathcal{M}(f)-f(x,v,t)\right)\phi(v)dv=0.

(5)

Consequently, by integration of equation (
1
) with respect to the collision invariants
\phi(v)
, a system of conservation laws are obtained

\displaystyle\begin{split}t\partialderivative{\braket{f}}{t}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\braket{vf}&=0,\\
t\partialderivative{\braket{v f}}{t}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\braket{v\otimes vf}&=0,\\
t\partialderivative{\braket{ \frac{|v|^2}{2} f }}{t}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\braket{v\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}f}&=0.\end{split}

(6)

As the relaxation time
\tau
tends to zero, the population density
f(x,v,t)
tends to the local Maxwellian
M
. In this limit, the density
\rho(x,t)
, mean velocity
U(x,t)
and energy
E(x,t)
satisfy the compressible Euler equations. The derivation can be formalised using the so-called Chapman-Enskog expansion, in which the population density is written as a Hilbert expansion in the so-called Knudsen number Kn
[
5
]
. A zero-th order expansion then yields the compressible Euler equations and a first-order expansion yields the compressible Navier-Stokes equations with the wrong Prandtl number Pr. The ellipsoidal statistical (ES)-BGK model was introduced to solve this problem
[
29
]
. Finally, we also note that the BGK and ES-BGK model satisfy the H-theorem, also known as the entropy inequality
[
1
]
. In the remainder of this text, we will focus on the simplest form of the BGK equation (
1
). However, we note that the extension to the ES-BGK model and other more complex BGK models like the multi-species BGK model
[
50
]
are straightforward, see for example
[
9
]
.

2.2
The ALE formulation

The formulation of the BGK-Boltzmann equation (
1
) that we will discretise in section
3
is the so-called arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian formulation
[
47
]

\displaystyle\derivative{x}{t}

\displaystyle=U_{ALE}

(7)

\displaystyle\derivative{f}{t}

\displaystyle=(U_{ALE})-v)\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\frac{1}{\tau}(\mathcal{M}(f)-f),

(8)

where

\derivative{f}{t}:=\partialderivative{f}{t}+U_{ALE}\cdot\nabla_{x}f

and

f=f(x,v,t)
. In equation (
7
), we have introduced motion to the the grid with a velocity
U_{ALE}
that is yet to be defined. To compensate for this motion, the term
U_{ALE}\cdot\nabla_{x}f
is added to the population dynamics in equation (
8
). For all grid points that lie inside the domain, we will set
U_{ALE}
to the local mean velocity of the gas
U(x,t)
. In the case of grid points that lie on the boundary, we distinguish between two cases: points that lie on the outer boundary of
D
, and points that lie the boundary of a rigid object. For the former we set
U_{ALE}=0
assuming the outer boundary is a wall which does not move, and for the latter we set
U_{ALE}=U_{w}
, with
U_{w}
the velocity determined by the translation and rotation of the rigid body, see section
2.4
.

Remark 1
.

The term
U_{ALE}\cdot\nabla_{x}f
in equation (
8
) is a non-conservative flux. Thus, any numerical scheme for (
8
) cannot conserve
f(x,v,t)
and thus also not the density
\rho(x,t)
, mean velocity
U(x,t)
and mean temperature
T(x,t)
.

2.3
Chu-reduced form

We limit ourselves to simulations in one and two spatial dimensions with some symmetry such that we can reduce the dimension of the equation using the so-called Chu-reduction
[
19
]
. In the one-dimensional case, we consider a slab symmetry. We write the space vector and velocity vector as
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
and
v=\left(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\right)
. We assume that
v_{1}
is aligned with the symmetry axis, and define the velocity
v_{\perp}=\sqrt{v_{2}^{2}+v_{3}^{2}}
. Then, if the initial condition only depends on
x_{1},v_{1}
and
v_{\perp}
, the solution at time
t_{f}
is a function of
x,v_{1}
and
v_{\perp}
. In this case we can define

\displaystyle g_{1}(x,t,v_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(t,x,v)dv_{2}dv_{3},\;g_{2}(x,t,v_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(v_{2}^{2}+v_{3}^{2}\right)f(t,x,v)dv_{2}dv_{3},

(9)

where now by
x
we mean
x_{1}
.
Multiplying equation (
8
) by
1
and
v_{2}^{2}+v_{3}^{2}
and integrating with respect to
\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we obtain two equations for
g_{1}=g_{1}(x,v_{1},t)
and
g_{2}=g_{2}(x,v_{1},t)

\displaystyle\derivative{g_1}{t}+(v_{1}-U(x,t))\partialderivative{g_1}{x}=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(G_{1}-g_{1}\right),\hskip 9.24994pt\derivative{g_2}{t}+(v_{1}-U(x,t))\partialderivative{g_2}{x}=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(G_{2}-g_{2}\right)

(10)

with

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{M}(f)dv_{2}dv_{3}=\frac{\rho(x,t)}{\left(2\pi R_{s}T(x,t)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left|v_{1}-U(x,t)\right|^{2}}{2R_{s}T(x,t)}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(v_{2}^{2}+v_{3}^{2}\right)\mathcal{M}(f)dv_{2}dv_{3}=2R_{s}T(x,t)G_{1}.

(12)

The gain in computational efficiency is clear. If we denote
N_{x}
and
N_{v}
as the amount of degrees of freedom in one space and velocity dimension, then, a 1D simulation using (
1
) has
N_{v}^{3}N_{x}
points in phase space. When the Chu-reduction is used (
9
), this reduces to
2N_{x}N_{v}
, thereby significantly decreasing the computational time. The same can be achieved in two space dimensions by assuming 2D geometry, i.e. that the system does not depend on one of the coordinates, say
x_{3}
, and defining

\displaystyle g_{1}(x,t,v_{1},v_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t,x,v)dv_{3},\hskip 9.24994ptg_{2}(x,t,v_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{3}^{2}f(t,x,v_{1},v_{2})dv_{3}.

(13)

Then, one obtains

\displaystyle\derivative{g_1}{t}+(v-U(x,t))\nabla_{x}f=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(G_{1}-g_{1}\right),\hskip 9.24994pt\derivative{g_2}{t}+(v-U(x,t))\nabla_{x}f=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(G_{2}-g_{2}\right)

(14)

with

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{M}(f)dv_{3}=\frac{\rho(x,t)}{2\pi R_{s}T(x,t)}\exp\left(-\frac{\left|\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{bmatrix}-U(x,t)\right|^{2}}{2R_{s}T(x,t)}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}v_{3}^{2}\mathcal{M}(f)dv_{3}=R_{s}T(x,t)G_{1}.

(16)

2.4
Newton-Euler equations for a rigid body

Consider a rigid body with centre of mass
X_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and mass
m
upon which a total force
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and torque
\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
act. Then, the velocity
V
of the centre of mass is described by the Newton’s second law of motion

\displaystyle m\derivative{V}{t}=\mathcal{F}.

(17)

The fundamental law describing the rotation of a rigid body is Euler’s second law, or the balance of angular momentum,

\displaystyle\derivative{t}L=\derivative{t}\left(I(t)\omega\right)=\mathcal{T},

(18)

where
L\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the angular momentum,
I(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3\crossproduct 3}
the moment of inertia, and
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
the angular velocity, all in a fixed (lab) frame of reference. The moment of inertia can be written as

\displaystyle I(t)=R(t)I^{b}R(t)^{T},

(19)

with
R(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3\crossproduct 3}
the rotation matrix mapping the rigid body coordinate system to lab coordinates. The moment of inertia
I^{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the (fixed) inertia tensor expressed the rigid body coordinate system. Using these definitions, equation (
18
) can written in the following form,

\displaystyle I(t)\derivative{\omega}{t}+\omega\crossproduct\left(I(t)\omega\right)=\mathcal{T}.

(20)

The total velocity of a point on the rigid body due to translation and rotation is given by
U_{w}=V+\omega\crossproduct(x-X_{c})
. The total force
\mathcal{F}
and torque
\mathcal{T}
are the result of the gas exerting pressure on the surface of the rigid body. We denote
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{3\crossproduct 3}
the pressure tensor, which is given by

\displaystyle\psi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(v-U_{w})\otimes(v-U_{w})f(t,x,v)dv.

(21)

The force
\mathcal{F}
and torque
\mathcal{T}
can be computed from the pressure tensor
\psi

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\int_{\delta S}\left(-\psi\cdot_{s}\right)dA,\ \mathcal{T}=\int_{\delta S}(x-X_{c})\times(-\psi\cdot_{s})dA,

(22)

with
s
the normal unit vector pointing outward with respect to the rigid body, and
\delta S
the surface of the rigid body.

3
Numerical scheme

In this section, we discuss the numerical discretisation method of the Chu-reduced form of the ALE formulation of the BGK-Boltzmann equation. The velocity discretisation method is discussed in section
3.1
. In section
3.2
, we discuss a
meshless
spatial discretisation method that can deal with the “Lagrangian” nature of the grid. We use a splitting method to time integrate the transport step explicitly and the relaxation step implicitly. To avoid spurious oscillations at discontinuities due to the transport step, we use an
a posteriori
limiting method called Multi dimensional Optimal Order Detection (MOOD). The splitting method and MOOD are discussed in section
3.3
. In section
3.4
we discuss the implementation of the diffuse reflective boundary conditions. Finally, in section
3.5
, we discuss some implementation aspects related to the irregular nature of the spatial grid.

We use
i\in\{1,\dots,N_{x}\}
, to denote the index of the grid point at position
x_{i}
. The index
k\in\{0,\dots,N_{v}-1\}
is used to identify the velocity variable
v_{k}
. Finally, the index
n
is used for the time variable. We will avoid writing the subscripts “1” and “2” in
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
since the algorithm for
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
is exactly the same. Using this notation, we write
g_{i,k}^{n}\approx g(x_{i},v_{k},t^{n})
.

3.1
Velocity discretisation: discrete velocity method

The velocity variable is discretised according to a simple discrete velocity model (DVM). Consider the one-dimensional Chu-reduced form of the Lagrangian BGK equation (
10
). We truncate the only remaining velocity variable
v_{1}
to the domain
[-v_{\rm max},v_{\rm max}]
, which we discretise using
N_{v}
equispaced points. The two equations for
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
become a set of
2N_{v}
equations for

\displaystyle\derivative{g_{i, k}}{t}+(v_{k}-U_{i}(t))\partialderivative{g_{i, k}}{x}=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(G-g_{i,k}\right)

(23)

where
v_{k}=-v_{\rm max}+2\,k\,v_{\rm max}/(N_{v}-1),\;k=0,\dots,N_{v}-1
and with
U_{i}(t)
an approximation for
U(x_{i},t)
. All integrals of
v
can then be approximated using the midpoint rule. In the 2D case (
14
), we use a square velocity grid
[-v_{\rm max},v_{\rm max}]^{2}
, and discretise velocities
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
in the same way as in the one dimensional case.

Remark 2
.

We note that the conservation property (
6
) and the entropy inequality do not hold for the simple DVM used here
[
35
]
.

Remark 3
.

If
v_{\rm max}
is taken large enough such that the distribution functions
g_{1,k}
and
g_{2,k}
are flat at
k=0
and
k=N_{v}-1
, then the midpoint rule is spectrally accurate
[
49
]
.

3.2
Space discretisation: meshless MUSCL

Due to the moving nature of the spatial grid, we avoid the use of grid based methods that would require remeshing at every time step. Instead, we discretise the spatial variable using a meshless MUSCL scheme
[
51
]
. This method relies on a Moving Least Squares (MLS) approximation of the spatial derivatives
[
43
,
32
,
34
]
. These discrete approximations of the derivative are then substituted into equations (
9
) or (
13
) to obtain a numerical scheme. We will give the method in one spatial dimension and then briefly discuss the extension to two spatial dimensions.

Remark 4
.

We note that these methods do not require a grid with cells and in theory should work with any set of points covered in the domain. As a result, they are extremely flexible when it comes to complex simulation domains, moving boundaries and free surface flows. However, we emphasize that these spatial discretisations are not conservative.

3.2.1
Moving least squares approximation

Consider an irregular set of points
x_{i},i=1\dots N
inside the simulation domain. Each point is assigned a set of neighbours which contains the indices of all the points that lie within a distance
h_{\rm max}
from
x_{i}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{j|\Delta x_{ij}|<b_{r}\Delta x=h_{\rm max}\text{ and }i\neq j\},

(24)

with
\Delta x
the initial spacing of points and

\Delta x_{ij}=x_{j}-x_{i}
. We refer to
b_{r}

as the “radius factor”.
Using the stencil
\mathcal{C}_{i}
,
we compute a local least squares polynomial fit of a test function

u_{i}\approx u(x_{i})
.
This routine will then be used in section
3.2.2
to approximate spatial derivatives of
f(x_{i},v,t)
.
We will give a third-order MLS method in 1D and discuss the generalisation to 2D below. We write down a Taylor polynomial approximation at point
x_{i}
to
x_{j}

\displaystyle u_{j}=u_{i}+\Delta x_{ij}\derivative{u}{x}+\frac{\Delta x_{ij}^{2}}{2}\derivative[2]{u}{x}+\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta x_{ij}^{3}\right),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}.

(25)

Then, by minimizing the
L^{2}
error of the fit, an approximation for the spatial derivatives can be obtained

\displaystyle\left(\derivative{\Tilde{u}}{x},\derivative[2]{\Tilde{u}}{x}\right)_{x=x_{i}}=\argmin_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{j}w_{ij}\left(u_{j}-u_{i}-\Delta x_{ij}\alpha-\frac{\Delta x_{ij}^{2}}{2}\beta\right)^{2}.

(26)

For stability reasons, we introduce a weight function

\displaystyle w_{ij}=w(x_{i},x_{j})=\exp\left(-\gamma\Delta x_{ij}^{2}\right).

(27)

We set
\gamma
to
{6}/{h_{\rm max}^{2}}
so that all points are within the support of the Gaussian. Note that other choices of basis functions and weight functions exist. For an overview of different MLS methods, see
[
46
,
45
]
. The solution of the minimisation problem (
26
) can be written in the so-called normal equations

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}^{2}&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}^{3}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}^{3}&\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}^{4}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\derivative{\Tilde{u}}{x}\\[5.69054pt]
\derivative[2]{\Tilde{u}}{x}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}\Delta u_{ij}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}^{2}\Delta u_{ij}\end{bmatrix},

(28)

with
\Delta u_{ij}=u_{j}-u_{i}
. The solution of the normal equations can then be written explicitly as

\displaystyle\derivative{u}{x}\approx\derivative{\Tilde{u}}{x}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\alpha_{ij}(u_{j}-u_{i}),\hskip 9.24994pt\derivative[2]{u}{x}\approx\derivative[2]{\Tilde{u}}{x}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\beta_{ij}(u_{j}-u_{i}),

(29)

where the coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
and
\beta_{ij}
are rational functions of
\Delta x_{ij}
. We will use the coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
and
\beta_{ij}
in the meshless MUSCL method to obtain a stable discretisation for the linear transport term in the BGK-Boltzmann equation. In 1D, this MLS method based on a
k
-th order Taylor expansion, yields a
k-\ell
-th order approximation for the
\ell
-th order derivative in space (
k>\ell
). The method can be easily extended to higher dimensions in space by using multivariate Taylor expansions.

It should be noted that due to the use of a Taylor expansion in (
25
), the least squares problem can become ill-conditioned, especially for higher-order approximations. Moreover, by solving the normal equations directly, the condition number of the original least squares problem squares. In practice, the least squares problem should be solved using a QR or an SVD decomposition
[
48
]
. In addition, the distances
\Delta x_{ij}
can be scaled with
{1}/{h_{\rm max}}
, to improve the conditioning further. Using these two improvements, we have achieved fifth-order approximations in 1D and third-order approximations in 2D.

3.2.2
Meshless MUSCL scheme in one space dimension

In this section, we will use the MLS coefficients to obtain a stable meshless discretisation of the transport term in the Lagrangian form of the BGK-Boltzmann equation. We distinguish between a first-order scheme, that is specifically designed to be positive, and higher-order schemes. We will use the higher-order scheme as a default, and switch to the first-order scheme when spurious oscillations are detected. This is explained in detail in section
3.3
.

3.2.2.1
First-order scheme

For the first-order scheme in one space dimension we can make use of a simple generalisation of the classical upwind scheme to the irregular grid case. We define
\mathcal{U}_{i,k}
the set of neighbours of point
i
that lie in the upwind direction, i.e.,
\mathcal{U}_{i,k}=\{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}|\left(v_{k}-U(x_{i},t)\right)\Delta x_{ij}<0\}
. Using the set
\mathcal{U}_{i,k}
and a first-order MLS approximation of the spatial derivative, a simple expression for the transport term in (
10
) can be found

\displaystyle\left(v_{k}-U(x_{i},t)\right)\partialderivative{g(x_i, v_k, t)}{x}\approx\left(v_{k}-U_{i}(t)\right)\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i,k}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}\left(g_{j,k}(t)-g_{i,k}(t)\right)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i,k}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}^{2}}.

(30)

If we only consider transport and thus ignore the BGK-collision operator, we can obtain a first-order scheme using a forward Euler time integrator

\displaystyle g_{i,k}^{n+1}=g_{i,k}^{n}\left(1+\Delta t(v_{k}-U_{i}^{n})\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i,k}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i,k}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}^{2}}\right)-\Delta t\left(v_{k}-U_{i}^{n}\right)\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i,k}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}g_{j,k}^{n}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i,k}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}^{2}}.

(31)

Due to the choice of upwind stencil, this scheme is
L^{\infty}
-stable under the generalized CFL condition
[
51
]

\displaystyle\Delta t\leq\min_{k,i}\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i,k}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}^{2}}{\left|\left(v_{k}-U_{i}^{n}\right)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{i,k}}w_{ij}\Delta x_{ij}\right|}.

(32)

We note that every stencil has a different configuration of points and therefore every stencil has its own stability condition. The time step is thus determined by the strictest condition. Moreover, because the grid moves, the stability condition changes at every time step. However, for simplicity, we fix the time step using a CFL number for the initial grid.

Remark 5
.

For a regular grid
\Delta x_{ij}=\Delta x
and the weight function set to unity, the CFL condition (
32
) simplifies to the classical CFL condition.

3.2.2.2
Higher-order meshless MUSCL scheme

The upwind scheme from section
3.2.2.1
requires a different upwind stencil for every transport velocity. Such a procedure is cumbersome and computationally expensive, especially in 2D. For the higher-order methods we thus use the central meshless MUSCL scheme that was developed in
[
51
]
. The first-order schemes will act as a fallback method in the case that oscillations are detected. We will illustrate the idea of the meshless MUSCL scheme using as an example a second-order variant. The extension to higher orders and two space dimensions are discussed below. Given the coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
and
\beta_{ij}
associated to the third-order MLS from section
3.2
, we can write the transport term in (
10
) as

\displaystyle\left(v_{k}-U(x_{i},t)\right)\partialderivative{g(x_i, v_k, t)}{x}\approx\left(v_{k}-U_{i}(t)\right)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\alpha_{ij}\left(g_{j,k}(t)-g_{i,k}(t)\right).

(33)

The discretisation (
33
) corresponds to a central discretisation, which produces an ordinary differential equation with unstable modes
[
51
]
. Instead, we introduce a numerical approximation for the midpoint
g\left(\frac{x_{i}+x_{j}}{2},j,t\right)\approx g_{i,j,k}(t)
, which we define later, and write the transport term as

\displaystyle\left(v_{k}-U(x_{i},t)\right)\partialderivative{g(x_i, v_k, t)}{x}\approx 2\left(v_{k}-U_{i}(t)\right)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\alpha_{ij}\left(g_{i,j,k}(t)-g_{i,k}(t)\right).

(34)

Because the stencil “size” reduces by a factor two due to the use of the midpoints, a factor two must be introduced in the spatial derivative (
34
). The midpoint
g_{i,j,k}(t)
is reconstructed either from point
i
or from point
j
in an upwind manner

\displaystyle g\left(\frac{x_{i}+x_{j}}{2},v_{k},t\right)\approx g_{ij,k}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\bar{g}_{i,j,k}(t),&\text{if }\left(v_{k}-U(x_{i},t)\right)\Delta x_{ij}>0\\
\bar{g}_{j,i,k}(t),&\text{else }\end{cases},

(35)

with
\bar{g}_{ij,k}
and
\bar{g}_{ji,k}
the reconstructed values (ignoring the time dependence for brevity)

\displaystyle g\left(\frac{x_{i}+x_{j}}{2},v_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=g_{i,k}+\frac{\Delta x_{ij}}{2}\partialderivative{g_{i, k}}{x}+\frac{\Delta x_{ij}^{2}}{8}\partialderivative[2]{g_{i, k}}{x}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta x_{ij}^{3})

\displaystyle\approx\bar{g}_{ij,k}

\displaystyle=g_{i,k}+\frac{\Delta x_{ij}}{2}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\alpha_{il}(g_{l,k}-g_{i,k})+\frac{\Delta x_{ij}^{2}}{8}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\beta_{il}(g_{l,k}-g_{i,k}),

(36)

\displaystyle g\left(\frac{x_{i}+x_{j}}{2},v_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=g_{j,k}-\frac{\Delta x_{ij}}{2}\partialderivative{g_{j, k}}{x}+\frac{\Delta x_{ij}^{2}}{8}\partialderivative[2]{g_{j, k}}{x}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta x_{ij}^{3})

\displaystyle\approx\bar{g}_{ji,k}

\displaystyle=g_{j,k}-\frac{\Delta x_{ij}}{2}\sum_{l\in C_{j}}\alpha_{jl}(g_{l,k}-g_{j,k})+\frac{\Delta x_{ij}^{2}}{8}\sum_{l\in C_{j}}\beta_{jl}(g_{l,k}-g_{j,k}).

(37)

Note that the coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
and
\beta_{ij}
must only be computed once per time step. They can then be reused for every discrete velocity in the computation of the gradient (
34
) and the reconstruction of the midpoint (
36
) (
37
). The extension of this scheme to higher-orders is straightforward. Using a higher-order MLS procedure, a higher-order approximation for the first-order derivatives can be obtained. Then, using all available derivatives from the MLS approximation, the reconstruction can be performed to higher-order. In
[
51
]
, a fourth order scheme was obtained and the stability properties of the meshless MUSCL methods were investigated. Finally, we note that due to the higher-order nature of the scheme, it cannot be TVD stable
[
33
]
. In section
3.3
, we discuss a procedure to deal with this issue.

3.3
Time discretisation: IMEX and MOOD

We discretise time using
IMEX Runge-Kutta schemes
in which the transport term is discretised explicitly and the BGK collision term is discretised implicitly by a Diagonally Implicit (DIRK) scheme. We denote
\tilde{a}_{sp}
and
\tilde{w}_{s}
the coefficients associated to the explicit Runge-Kutta scheme, and
a_{sp}
and
w_{s}
, the coefficients associated to the implicit Runge-Kutta scheme. The equation that describes the motion of the particles (
7
) is discretised using the explicit scheme of the IMEX Runge-Kutta method. Using the notation
g(t^{n},x_{i},v_{k})\approx g^{n}_{i,kl}
, we obtain the following fully discrete scheme

\displaystyle\tilde{g}^{(s)}_{i,k}

\displaystyle=g^{n}_{i,k}-\Delta t\sum_{p=1}^{s-1}\tilde{a}_{sp}{\tt transp}(g_{i,k}^{(p)})

(50)

\displaystyle\bar{g}^{(s)}_{i,k}

\displaystyle=\tilde{g}^{(s)}_{i,k}+\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\sum_{p=1}^{s-1}a_{sp}\left(G^{p}_{i,k}-g^{(p)}_{i,k}\right),\hskip 9.24994ptx^{(s)}=x^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{p=1}^{s-1}\tilde{a}_{sp}U_{i}^{p},

(51)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994ptg^{(s)}_{i,k}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}^{(s)}_{i,k}+\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}a_{ss}\left(G^{s}_{i,k}-g^{(s)}_{i,k}\right),

(52)

\displaystyle g^{n+1}_{i,k}

\displaystyle=g^{n}_{i,k}-\Delta t\sum_{s=1}^{\nu}\tilde{w}_{s}{\tt transp}(g_{i,k}^{(s)})+\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\sum_{s=1}^{\nu}w_{s}\left(G^{s}_{i,k}-g^{(s)}_{i,k}\right),\hskip 9.24994ptx^{n+1}=x^{n}+\Delta t\sum_{s=1}^{\nu}\tilde{w}_{s}U_{i}^{s},

(53)

with
{\tt transp}
a meshless MUSCL discretisation of the transport operator. Notice that, because of the DIRK structure of the implicit scheme, all terms in Eq.(
51
) can be computed explicitly. The implicit step (
52
) can be computed in an explicit fashion taking advantage of the special structure of the BGK model, by using a technique that was first published in
[
39
]
and is often used for discrete velocity models of the BGK equation
[
15
,
18
]
. The idea behind the technique is very simple. We multiply (
52
) by
(1,v_{k},|v_{k}|^{2})
and sum over
k

\displaystyle\sum_{k}g^{(s)}_{i,k}(1,v_{k},|v_{k}|^{2})=\sum_{k}\left(\bar{g}^{(s)}_{i,k}+\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}a_{ss}\left(G^{s}_{i,k}-g^{(s)}_{i,k}\right)\right)(1,v_{k},|v_{k}|^{2}).

(54)

By definition of local Maxwellian,

\displaystyle\sum_{k}(G^{s}_{i,k}-g^{(s)}_{i,k})(1,v_{k},|v_{k}|^{2})=0

and therefore the moments of
g^{(s)}_{i,k}
in (
54
) are computed explicitly as moments of
\bar{g}^{(s)}_{i,k}
. Once the moments are computed, we can explicitly compute the Maxwellian
G_{i,k}^{s}
, and finally

g^{(s)}_{i,k}=\frac{\tau\bar{g}^{(s)}_{i,k}+\Delta ta_{ss}G^{s}_{i,k}}{\tau+\Delta t\,a_{ss}}.

For the numerical test in section
4
, we will use the ARS(2, 2, 2) scheme
[
2
]
and the IMEX-SSP2(3, 3, 2) scheme
[
38
]
.

To avoid spurious oscillations at discontinuities due to the discretisation of the transport term, we employ the Multi-dimensional Optimal Order Detection algorithm, commonly referred to as MOOD
[
20
]
. MOOD uses an
a posteriori
check to detect oscillations. Upon detection of an oscillation, the order of accuracy of the spatial discretisation method is reduced to one. The criterion we use to detect oscillations is the discrete maximum property (DMP)
[
10
]
. This is a natural choice since the transport in the BGK equation is scalar and linear, thus, for the solution to be a weak solution, it must satisfy the DMP. Numerically, we check if the solution after transport
\tilde{g}_{i,k}^{(s)}
(
50
) does not exceed the minimum and maximum values from the previous stage

\displaystyle\min_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}(g_{i,k}^{(s-1)},g_{j,k}^{(s-1)})\leq\tilde{g}_{i,k}^{(s)}\leq\max_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}(g_{i,k}^{(s-1)},g_{j,k}^{(s-1)}).

(55)

If this criterion is not satisfied, the order of the method is reduced, and the solution
\tilde{g}_{i,k}^{(s)}
is recomputed. However, in
[
23
]
, it was shown that the DMP is too strict at smooth extrema, thereby needlessly reducing the order of the method. We solve this problem by supplementing the DMP condition with the so-called u2 detection criterion. This criterion relaxes the DMP at local extrema to prevent losing the order of the method. First, we define the following smoothness indicators

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i,k}^{\rm min}

\displaystyle=\min_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\left(\left|\partialderivative[2]{\Tilde{g}_{i, k}}{x}\right|,\left|\partialderivative[2]{\Tilde{g}_{j, k}}{x}\right|\right)\text{ and }\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i,k}^{\rm max}=\max_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\left(\left|\partialderivative[2]{\Tilde{g}_{i, k}}{x}\right|,\left|\partialderivative[2]{\Tilde{g}_{j, k}}{x}\right|\right),

(56)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{i,k}^{\rm min}

\displaystyle=\min_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\left(\partialderivative[2]{\Tilde{g}_{i, k}}{x},\partialderivative[2]{\Tilde{g}_{j, k}}{x}\right)\text{ and }\;\mathcal{X}_{i,k}^{\rm max}=\max_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\left(\partialderivative[2]{\Tilde{g}_{i, k}}{x},\partialderivative[2]{\Tilde{g}_{j, k}}{x}\right).

(57)

The smoothness indicators are based on the minimal and maximal values of the curvatures in a stencil. Then, the u2 detection criterion consists of three conditions

\displaystyle\left|\max_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}(g_{i,k}^{(p-1)},g_{j,k}^{(p-1)})-\min_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}(g_{i,k}^{(p-1)},g_{j,k}^{(p-1)})\right|

\displaystyle<\delta

(58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{i,k}^{\rm min}\mathcal{X}_{i,k}^{\rm max}

\displaystyle>-\delta

(59)

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i,k}^{\rm min}}{\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i,k}^{\rm max}}\geq 1/2\text{ or }\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i,k}^{\rm max}

\displaystyle<\delta

(60)

The first condition (
58
) prevents MOOD events in regions where the solution is flat. Criterion (
59
) checks if a point is at a local extremum by checking the signs of the minimum and maximum curvatures. Finally, criterion (
60
) is used to distinguish between discontinuities and smooth extrema. All criteria contain an additional parameter
\delta
to avoid micro-oscillations triggering MOOD events and thus resulting in unnecessary loss of accuracy. In two space dimensions, the criteria (
60
) and (
59
) are checked in both space dimensions, see appendix
A
.

The full MOOD procedure can be summarised as follows. First, the transport step (
50
) is computed using a higher-order method. Then, the discrete maximum property (
55
) is checked. If this criterion is satisfied, the implicit part of the IMEX stage is computed according to (
51
-
53
). If the discrete maximum property is not satisfied, the u2 conditions are checked according to (
58
-
60
). If all three conditions are satisfied, the solution is still accepted, and the implicit part of the IMEX scheme is computed. If one of the u2 criteria fails, the solution
\tilde{g}^{(s)}_{i,k}
is recomputed using a forward Euler time integration method and a first-order method in space

\displaystyle\tilde{g}^{(s)}_{i,k}

\displaystyle=g^{s-1}_{i,k}-\Delta t\left(w_{s}-w_{s-1}\right){\tt transp}(g_{i,k}^{(s-1)}).

(61)

Now, the remainding computations in the IMEX stage can be computed using again (
51
-
53
).

Remark 6
.

We note that it is possible to use a Strong Stability Preserving (SSP) IMEX Runge-Kutta method such that it is not necessary to switch to explicit Euler time integration when a MOOD event occurs. We did not achieve as good results using an SSP IMEX method versus switching to forward Euler.

3.4
Diffuse-reflective boundary conditions

In all the numerical examples we will consider diffuse reflective boundary conditions. Let us denote by

\partial D
the boundary of the domain occupied by the gas, and
\hat{n}
the unit normal to the boundary pointing inside the gas domain. At the particle level, diffusive reflective boundary conditions imply that upon collision with a wall, the particle is reflected back into the gas with a velocity sampled from a Maxwellian with mean velocity
U_{w}
and temperature
T_{w}
. The boundary condition reads

\displaystyle f(x,v,t)=\frac{\rho_{w}}{\left(2\pi R_{s}T_{w}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{|v-U_{w}|^{2}}{2R_{s}T_{w}}\right),\hskip 9.24994ptx\in\partial D,\;(v-U)\cdot\hat{n}\geq 0.

(62)

In the case of a moving object, the velocity of the wall
U_{w}
is determined based on the translation and rotation of the object, see section
2.4
. The density of the gas
\rho_{w}
that is reflected back into the domain is determined by mass conservation at the surface of the wall

\displaystyle\int_{(v-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}>0}M_{w}(v-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}\;dv+\int_{(v-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}<0}f(x,v,t)(v-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}\;dv=0.

(63)

The first term in equation (
63
) represents the particles that have been reflected off the wall, the second represents the particles that are headed towards the wall. In
[
28
]
, an algorithm is discussed for numerically imposing this boundary condition in the context of semi-Lagrangian methods. They enforce condition (
62
) after the collision step, which ultimately requires an iterative procedure to be used. Alternatively, they present an algorithm in which the distribution function is extrapolated from points inside the domain to grid points on the boundary, after which the “wall density”
\rho_{w}
(
63
) can be computed. We perceive both options to be suboptimal: the iterative procedure is cumbersome and can be computationally expensive (especially for small Knudsen numbers), and the polynomial extrapolation procedure is potentially unstable. We therefore propose an alternative procedure to implement diffuse reflective boundary conditions that neither requires an extrapolation procedure nor an iterative procedure. We propose to enforce the boundary condition (
62
) after a transport step and thus before the collision step. After all, gas particles interact with the wall during the transport step. The algorithm consists of three steps that are applied in each Runge-Kutta stage to points that lie on the boundary of the gas domain. For simplicity, we give the numerical procedure using a first-order time integration algorithm and we leave the space and velocity variable continuous. First, we compute the transport step for all “outgoing” characteristics

\displaystyle\bar{f}=f^{n}-\Delta t(v-U_{w})\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{n}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for}\hskip 9.24994pt(v-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}<0,

(64)

where, as in section
3.3
, the quantity
\bar{f}
is the distribution function after advection at time
n+1
, but before collisions have been taken into account. The density
\rho_{w}
that, together with the boundary temperature
T_{w}
and velocity
U_{w}
, characterizes the Maxwellian distribution of particles reflected back into the gas domain, can be computed imposing that the net mass flux across the boundary is zero
\forall x\in\partial D
, using (
63
). Hence, numerically this becomes

\displaystyle\int_{(v-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}>0}(v-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}\;M^{n+1}(\rho_{w},U_{w},T_{w})dv+\int_{(v-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}<0}(v-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}\;\bar{f}dv=0

(65)

Finally, we assign the “incoming” half of the distribution function as

\displaystyle\bar{f}=M(\rho_{w},U_{w},T_{w})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for}\hskip 9.24994pt(v-U_{w})\cdot\hat{n}>0,

(66)

after which the implicit step of the Runge-Kutta scheme can be applied to both the grid points inside the domain and on the boundary. This boundary procedure is not limited to meshless discretisations and could also be applied to semi-Lagrangian schemes.

Implementation aspects

An important aspect of meshless methods is the so-called grid management
1
1

1

Although the methods adopted in this paper are denoted as “meshless” or “gridless”, we still denote by “grid” the set of points, to emphasize the idea that the smoothness of their distribution is relevant for the spacial accuracy of the method. For this reason we still use the term “grid”.

[
45
,
42
,
47
]
. Grid management involves creating the stencils
\mathcal{U}_{i,k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{i}
. Moreover, the stencils
\mathcal{U}_{i,k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{i}
must be recomputed regularly since the grid moves. If these stencils are not updated regularly enough, the spatial discretisation errors may become very large. A grid management routine also regularly adds or removes points from the domain to avoid that the grid becomes too irregular. For example, in case there is part of the domain that is void of grid points, a new point is inserted. Similarly, when multiple points are too close together, they are replaced by a point in the middle. As was investigated in
[
51
]
, this improves the stability of the schemes. When a new point is added or removed, the stencils
\mathcal{U}_{i,k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{i}
of all points should be recomputed.

In one spatial dimension, the grid points are stored in an array that is sorted using the position. In this way, it is trivial to efficiently find neighbouring points that are close, and to add and remove points. In two spatial dimensions the grid points are stored in an array in arbitrary order. To find neighbouring points, we create a uniform background square grid with spacing
b_{v}\Delta x
,
b_{v}\geq 1
, with
\Delta x
the initial grid spacing. We refer to
b_{v}
as the “voxel factor”. Using the uniform background grid, we generate a table that contains the indices of the grid points per background cell. When the stencil
\mathcal{C}_{i}
must be recomputed, we search
\left\lceil{b_{r}}/{b_{v}}\right\rceil
cells in all directions for neighbouring points (see figure
2
), thereby avoiding a costly
N_{x}^{2}
search. Using the neighbour information of every point, two neighbouring points are removed and replaced with a point in the middle if the distance between the points is less than
b_{\rm minDist}\Delta x
with
b_{\rm minDist}<1
. Finally, when a background cell is void of grid points, a new grid point is placed at its cell centre, to avoid gaps in the domain.

When a new point is added to the domain, the solution value at that point is interpolated using a second-order MLS interpolation procedure. When MOOD is used, the discrete maximum property (
55
) is checked for the newly interpolated value. In case the DMP criterion is not satisfied, we recompute the solution value using a first-order interpolation routine.

5
Conclusion

We have presented a numerical method for simulating rarefied gases that interact with moving boundaries and rigid bodies. The gas is described by the BGK equation and solved using an Arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian method in which grid points move with the local mean velocity of the gas. This formulation allows for easy treatment of irregular domains and moving boundaries. We use higher-order meshless MUSCL schemes with MOOD to discretised the equation on the irregular, moving grid. Diffusive-reflective boundary conditions on the walls are treated using a new procedure that avoid an iterative solver or extrapolation procedure. We perform several tests and comparisons with different methods to validate the performance of the numerical method. In future work, we would like to tackle three-dimensional problems and mixtures of gases.
