The “salted pretzel” sign: A radiological indication of a calcified amorphous tumor as a rare cause of embolic stroke
Authors: Helena Herm, Thomas Borgmann, Manfred Berger, Hendrik-Johannes Pels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcified amorphous tumors (CAT), also known as toothpaste tumors, are associated with a high prevalence of cerebrovascular events. Present literature provides limited information on conservative treatment strategies, as most published cases had undergone surgical treatment. Hence, this report demonstrates a conservative treatment of right middle cerebral artery syndrome in a 76-year-old woman due to a CAT. Cause for the syndrome was a high-density plaque occluding the inferior branch of the right middle cerebral artery (MCA) on the computed tomography (CT). The occlusion was resolved by only thrombolytic therapy, therefore additional mechanical thrombectomy was not necessary. Follow-up CT showed a small area of ischemic demarcation as well as a “salted pretzel” sign created by multiple calcified emboli within the distal MCA territory. Furthermore, transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a CAT of the mitral valve accompanied by severe calcified degeneration of the mitral annulus as the cause for the calcified cerebral emboli. Instead of surgical removal of the CAT the patient was started on a vitamin K antagonist for secondary prevention. This case suggests initial thrombolytic therapy can be an effective reperfusion strategy in patients presenting with calcified cerebral emboli. Moreover, treatment with an oral anticoagulant might be a conservative strategy for secondary prevention in selected cases.Keywords: Calcified amorphous tumor, Toothpaste tumor, Salted pretzel sign, Embolic stroke, Thrombolytic therapy

Introduction

Calcified amorphous tumors (CAT) are rare non-neoplastic tumors most commonly affecting the mitral valves and annuli. They were first described in 1997 as nodular calcium deposits surrounded by eosinophilic amorphous material consisting of degenerative fibrin and cholesterol components [1]. The intraoperative appearance of a toothpaste-like content drained from this encapsulated mass [2] formed the term “toothpaste tumor” to describe this entity. The prevalence of cerebrovascular events in published cases of patients with CAT has been reported to be as high as 19% [3]. During stroke workup calcified cerebral emboli can be visualized by the “salted pretzel” sign as in this case. This sign was first illustrated by Christian et al in 2008 as the calcified peripheral emboli shown on CT seemed like salt on a pretzel [4]. To our knowledge, the relationship between this radiological phenomenon and the calcified peripheral emboli caused by a CAT has not been described before. Different treatment options for CAT remain cause for discussion in present literature, since most events are only described in case reports and stronger evidence by randomized controlled trials is not provided. Primarily surgical treatment is recommended in cases of complicated CAT with severe mitral valve dysfunction or systemic emboli [5]. A systematic review even demonstrated surgery was performed in most reported cases (up to 93%) [6]. However, the absence of histological evidence with conservative treatment might lead to a publication bias with disproportional representation of surgical cases. As a result, only very limited information on conservative treatment strategies regarding acute reperfusion therapy as well as secondary prevention exists. This case demonstrates the effectiveness of thrombolytic therapy for calcified cerebral emboli, thus suggesting conservative secondary prevention should be considered in eligible cases with CAT.

Case report

This case presents a 76-year-old woman who was admitted to the emergency department with acute onset left-sided weakness and hypoesthesia, multimodal neglect, as well as dysarthria (National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) 9, modified Rankin Scale (mRS) 4) 50 minutes prior. Native images of the CT as well as angiography showed a high-density plaque resulting in the occlusion of the inferior branch (M2 segment) of the right middle cerebral artery (MCA) (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3) with lack of ischemic demarcation. Having excluded contraindications (e.g., no fever, cardiac murmur or skin changes indicative of endocarditis), thrombolytic therapy using 81 mg of recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rtPA) was started. The patient was intubated and transferred to the angio suite for endovascular mechanical thrombectomy. However, further angiography showed the calcified embolus had already broken up and moved distally into 2 peripheral M3 segments resulting in only a minor loss of perfusion (Figs. 4 and 5). Thus, under consideration of risk-benefit assessment additional mechanical thrombectomy was omitted and the patient was soon extubated [7]. Only mild hemiparesis and dysarthria persisted (NIHSS 3). The follow-up CT the day after showed hypoattenuation of a right precentral ischemic area as well as the “salted pretzel” sign [4] within the distal MCA territory (Figs. 6 and 7). Sonography of the carotid arteries revealed only very little atherosclerotic changes, atrial fibrillation was not detected. However, transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiogram showed a round hyperechogenic structure of 12 millimeters at the posterior mitral leaflet (PML) of the mitral valve resulting in the immobilization of the PML (Figs. 8 and 9). In addition, severe calcified degeneration of the mitral annulus without mitral valve stenosis was described. The mass was assessed by the cardiac imaging department to be a “toothpaste tumor” because of its pathognomonic echocardiographic characteristics. Vegetations suspicious of endocarditis or thrombotic adherences were not present. As the calcified properties of CAT suggested a causal relationship with the calcified cerebral emboli and no other source of calcified emboli was detected, the CAT was found to be the source of cardiogenic embolism resulting in M2 occlusion. The absence of mitral valve dysfunction, suspicion of malignancy or signs of recurrent ischemic events were reasons to omit from surgical treatment. As thrombolytic therapy had demonstrated its effectiveness in targeting the fibrinous component of the CAT-related embolus, further conservative management of this case was proposed. Based on the success of the few conservatively managed cases reported in the literature the patient was started on phenprocoumon (most commonly used vitamin K antagonist in Germany) with a target INR range of 2-3. With an LDL of 115 mg/dL and frequently recorded high blood pressure the patient was also prescribed Rosuvastatin in addition to adjustments of the antihypertensive therapy. The patient was transferred to a rehabilitation ward (NIHSS at discharge 3, mRS 2) and scheduled for an outpatient follow-up including transthoracic echocardiogram after 3 months. During this follow-up as well as a check in phone call 1 year after the event no further symptomatic ischemic events were reported.

Discussion

Reported cases of ischemic strokes by CAT often failed to describe clinical features as well as neuroradiological imaging [8]. Our finding of the “salted pretzel” sign in the serial neuroimaging contributed to a better understanding of the pathophysiology of this phenomenon. Possible sources of the “salted pretzel” sign include atherosclerosis of carotid and vertebral arteries, calcification of the aorta, the aortic valve, and the mitral valve, as well as complications of coronary or carotid intervention [4]. There were only very few atherosclerotic changes in carotid and vertebral arteries on sonography, no calcifications of the aortic valve on echocardiography, and no history of a coronary intervention in this case. Therefore, the neuroradiological finding of the “salted pretzel” sign in our case corroborates this phenomenon being a radiological indication of calcified cerebral emboli by CAT.

As the majority of reported cases of CAT had undergone surgical treatment, limited evidence is available regarding conservative treatment strategies preventing further ischemic events in patients with a toothpaste tumor [6]. Surgery should be strongly considered when severe mitral valve dysfunction, suspicion of malignancy or further ischemic events in spite of best medical treatment exist. After exclusion of these criteria conservative strategies can be utilized. The few conservatively managed cases reported in present literature had been treated mainly with warfarin or other oral anticoagulants not specifically named. There was no explanation in previous reports as to why warfarin had been preferred over direct oral anticoagulants [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Broader effects of warfarin on the coagulation cascade being an advantage in the context of strongly thrombogenic valvular pathology and fibrin-rich thrombi were reasons considered. Previous studies argue that because of the calcified nature of the emboli strong effectiveness of thrombolytic therapy should not be expected [12]. Our case shows thrombolytic therapy using rtPA had been successful in breaking down the calcified embolus occluding the M2. This effect is likely due to the fibrinolytic components acting on the degenerated fibrin and cholesterol matrix around the calcified deposits of the CAT. Therefore, the primarily solid appearance of the embolus should not prevent clinicians from withholding potentially beneficial therapy with rtPA whilst evaluating the option of mechanical thrombectomy. Furthermore, secondary prevention with only a vitamin k antagonist and no surgical treatment showed no further ischemic event, thus corroborating the effectiveness of conservative treatment. In future cases the use of direct oral anticoagulants instead of vitamin K antagonists should also be considered for secondary prevention.

However, the composition of CATs is very heterogenous indicating emboli with a lower share of fibrinous components might benefit less from thrombolytic therapy. Additionally, in the follow-up time frame of 1 year no further cerebral imaging was conducted. Hence, asymptomatic ischemic events with conservative treatment cannot be ruled out. Above that, this is only a single case, therefore further studies are needed to underline our findings.

In conclusion, the “salted pretzel” sign can be a radiological indication of calcified cerebral emboli by CAT. Consequently, CAT should be considered as a cardiogenic embolic source especially with radiological proof of calcified emboli. This underlines the importance of routine echocardiogram as part of the stroke workup. Our case suggests conservative treatment can be an effective reperfusion strategy as well as a successful secondary prevention in selected cases with CAT associated cerebral emboli.
