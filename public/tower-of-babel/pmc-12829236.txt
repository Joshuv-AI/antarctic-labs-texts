Trends in income-related concentration and inequality in health in Japan: evidence from population-based National surveys from 2001 to 2022
Authors: Takashi Oshio, Ruru Ping, Ayako Honda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJapan has experienced widening income disparity in recent years, raising concerns about income inequality in health. This study aims to investigate trends in income-related concentration and inequality in key health outcomes between 2001 and 2022.MethodsThis study utilized repeated cross-sectional data from 500,580 individuals (238,746 men and 261,834 women) aged ≥ 6 years, obtained from eight waves of population-based national surveys conducted between 2001 and 2022. The study examined trends in the concentration index and the relative and slope indices of inequality for key health outcomes, including self-rated health, subjective symptoms, limitations in undertaking activities of daily living, and experience of stress/anxiety, as well as the number of physician visits and incidence of selected non-communicable diseases (NCDs). All measures were standardized by age and sex.ResultsIncreasing concentrations of poor health status among low-income individuals and rising income-related health inequality were observed over the study period, although a greater pro-poor concentration was noted for physician visits. Additionally, income-related inequality increased for persons with hypertension, diabetes, and at least one type of NCD.ConclusionsThe results indicate persistent income-related inequalities in health within a context of universal health coverage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12963-025-00443-4.

Background

Widening income disparity raises concerns about increasing health inequality in Japan [1–3]. A prolonged period of sluggish economic growth and an unstable labor market over the past three decades, characterized by a rising reliance on precarious employment (in part-time and fixed-term positions), may have exacerbated income-related health inequality [2, 4, 5]. Given the positive correlation between income and health status, widening disparity is expected to concentrate poor health status among low-income individuals and steepen the income gradient in health outcomes.

However, studies examining trends in health inequality in Japan have demonstrated mixed and inconsistent results, even when using the same dataset from the Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions (CSLC), which is conducted by the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare (MHLW). For example, one study reported a modest increase in the concentration of poor self-rated health (SRH) among low-income groups between 1986 and 2007 [6], but two similar studies covering the same period had slightly different findings, one reporting narrowing or unchanged trends in income- and social class-based inequality in SRH [7] and the other found a decrease in income-related inequality in SRH for those aged 20–59 years [8]. Additionally, a recent study found no increase in health inequality in SRH between 1992 and 2013 [9] while another highlighted growing inequality in SRH by education level between 1986 and 2016 [10].

The inconsistency in the results of previous studies can be attributed to various factors, including differences in the study periods and targeted age groups. Moreover, previous studies used different methods to measure health inequality, such as the concentration index (CI) [7, 8] and the relative index of inequality (RII) and the slope index of inequality (SII) [6, 9] Additionally, most previous studies have relied solely on SRH to determine health outcome and have not examined other dimensions of health.

To address these limitations, this study aims to examine trends in income-related health inequality between 2001 and 2022. Over this period, the economic growth in Japan stagnated at a low level, averaging approximately minus 0.6% per year (standard deviation: 1.9%) [11]. Concurrently, the proportion of people in precarious employment as a percentage of total employment increased from 27.4% to 36.9% [12], and the proportion of individuals aged ≥ 65 years in the total population increased from 18.0% to 29.0% [13]. Given these demographic and economic shifts, it is crucial to reassess trends in income-related health inequality, as new patterns not captured by previous studies may emerge.

The findings from this study are expected to add to the existing literature on income-related health inequality. A study using cross-sectional data from 33 countries found that physical functional disability among middle-aged and older adults was more prevalent in low-income groups [14]. A Brazilian study showed that individuals with higher socioeconomic status had a lower prevalence of multi-morbidity of chronic conditions [15], and a study from China found a pro-rich concentration of diabetes among the middle-aged and elderly population [16]. Another study examining the impact of COVID-19 on health inequality in South Africa found that the pro-poor concentration of poor health was six times higher during COVID-19 [17]. An European study examined income-related health inequalities before and after the Great Recession in Europe [18]. It found that old-age pension benefits helped prevent these inequalities from increasing, whereas he market incomes of younger groups did not. The results of this study are expected to have valuable implications for public health policies in a society experiencing widening income disparity.

In addition, this study has three notable features that distinguish it from most preceding research. First, in addition to SRH, to enhance the generalizability and robustness of the findings, the study considered three general health indicators—subjective symptoms, limitations in activities of daily living (ADL), and stress/anxiety. Furthermore, building on studies that examined income and other socioeconomic determinants of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) [19–21], the study explored trends in income-related inequalities for seven doctor-diagnosed NCDs that are known to contribute significantly to disease burden in Japan (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, angina pectoris, stroke, cancer, and dementia) [22]. Together with the health outcome indicators, physician visits were also examined as a proxy for healthcare utilization.

Second, unlike many previous studies that use single measures to assess health inequality and to achieve robust measures, this study assessed both concentration of health outcomes, measured using the CI [23, 24], and inequality, measured using the RII and SII [25, 26], with respect to pretax household income. For each health variable, the study examined whether these indices changed consistently over time.

Third, unlike many previous studies that compared results for specific age groups, an entire population sample (aged ≥ 6 years) was used and health variables were systematically standardized by age and sex [27]. If not fully acquitted, the effect of population aging may overestimate income-related health inequality, as older individuals generally have lower incomes and poorer health than the rest of the population. The effect of population aging on health inequality was captured by comparing original and standardized health variables.

Methods

Study sample

This study used large-scale data obtained from the CSLC conducted by the MHLW. The CSLC has been conducted since 1986 and involves an annual household survey, as well as health, income/savings, and long-term care surveys conducted every 3 years. The samples were collected nationwide using a two-stage random sampling procedure. First, approximately 5,400 districts were randomly selected from approximately 940,000 national census districts. Second, approximately 290,000 households were randomly chosen from each selected district based on population size. Information about each co-residing household member was collected from the selected households.

Repeated cross-sectional data collected from the most recent eight waves of the CSLC, conducted every 3 years from 2001 to 2022, was analyzed. Restricting the sample to individuals aged ≥ 6 years, this study analyzed data of 500,580 individuals (238,746 men and 261,834 women), obtained with permission from the MHLW. The CSLC is authorized by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, which oversees all government surveys in Japan from statistical, legal, ethical, and other viewpoints, in accordance with the Statistics Law in Japan. Therefore, ethical approval was not required for this study.

Variables

Binary variables were defined for four health outcomes: (i) poor SRH, (ii) subjective symptoms, (iii) ADL limitations, and (iv) stress/anxiety, all derived from survey responses. Regarding SRH, respondents were asked, “What is your current health status? Is it excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?” Poor SRH was defined by assigning 1 to fair or poor and 0 otherwise. Physician visits based on self-reported visits in the previous month, were considered an indicator of healthcare utilization. Further, based on the self-reported reasons for physician visits, six types of NCDs (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, angina pectoris, stroke, and cancer) were examined as well as having at least one of these six types of NCDs.

In addition to these health variables, pretax income was considered in the computation of income-related concentration and inequality for each health variable [6]. Pretax income, which consists of wages and other earned income, social security benefits, and intra-family transfers, was used to measure the total ability to pay and to capture the impact of taxation on concentration and inequality in health. Household-size-adjusted pretax income was calculated by dividing the reported household pretax income by the square root of the number of household members. A set of age dummy variables and a male dummy variable were created to standardize health outcome variables by age and sex.

For both years, the standardized curves were closer to the line of equity (45° line) than the original curve, underscoring the role of population aging in amplifying the concentration of poor SRH among low-income individuals. Moreover, both original and standardized concentration curves shifted outwards from the 45° line from 2001 to 2022, indicating a growing concentration of poor SRH among low-income individuals.

Figure 3 compares the percentage prevalence of poor SRH by pretax income quartile in 2001 and 2022. In both years, the curves exhibit negative slopes, indicating that higher income corresponded to better health, while the standardized curves were flatter than the non-standardized ones, suggesting that population aging amplifies the negative relationship between income and health. Both original and standardized curves shifted upwards from 2001 to 2022, indicating a general deterioration in population health. Moreover, for both non-standardized and standardized curves, the income gradients for health became somewhat steeper and straighter over the study period. The steeper gradient points to widening income-related inequality in SRH. The straighter curve suggests that RII and SII analyses became more valid in 2022, because they assume a linear relationship between income level and health status.

Discussion

Trends in income-related concentration and inequality in key health outcomes between 2001 and 2022 were examined using nationwide repeated cross-sectional data obtained from eight waves of population-based national surveys. Specifically, trends in the CIs, RIIs and SIIs for key health outcomes, physician visits, and selected NCDs, were examined, with all results standardized by age and sex.

A decline in CI and increases in RII and SII for all health problems has been observed in recent years, even after standardization by age and sex. These findings are indicative of the widening health inequality over the past two decades, which is in line with a modest upward trend in income inequality as shown in the Gini coefficient of pretax income. Although further investigation is needed, the results are consistent with the view that stagnant economic growth and an increasing proportion of precarious workers have exacerbated health inequality, as suggested in previous studies [1–5].

The findings suggest that the levels of CI, RII and SII for most health variables were amplified by age, likely due to the fact that older individuals generally have a lower income and poorer health status. This results in a concentration of poor health among low-income groups. In fact, the average annual income of older households was approximately half that of other households [34]. The stress/anxiety variable differed from other variables, indicating that younger individuals were more likely to experience stress/anxiety than older individuals. Analysis of the association between health and socioeconomic status, while also accounting for age, revealed that factors correlated with both health and socioeconomic status can provide a more refined understanding of income-related health inequality.

Regarding visits to physicians, a proxy for healthcare utilization, the CI has declined while the RII and SII have risen over the study period from 2001 to 2022. Income-related inequity in healthcare utilization has not increased as much as the growing concentration of health problems in low-income individuals or the rising income gradient for health. This observation suggests that Japan’s universal health coverage, available through the mandatory health insurance system, may have helped reduce financial barriers for low-income groups accessing healthcare and prevented horizontal inequity in healthcare utilization from widening, that is, reduced inequality in healthcare utilization after controlling for healthcare needs. This is an important issue warranting further investigation [8, 23, 35].

Furthermore, the study revealed mixed trends in income-related concentrations and inequality across the selected NCDs. However, hypertension and diabetes, both of which have a relatively high prevalence in Japan, have become more concentrated among low-income individuals. Consequently, the concentration of having at least one NCD has become more prevalent in the poor. Further study is required to examine the underlying factors influencing the concentration of hypertension and diabetes in the low-income population group in Japan and to identify measures that may help to prevent these diseases. No improvement in equality was observed for other diseases examined in the study. Together with the trends of concentration and inequality in more comprehensive health problems including poor SRH, subjective symptoms, and ADL limitations, caution should be exercised regarding the risk of widening health inequality.

Three indices, CI, RII, and SII, were used in the study, which enhanced the robustness of the methodological approach. The CI measures health disparities across the entire socioeconomic gradient by ranking individuals from poorest to richest and quantifying the extent to which health outcomes are concentrated among specific socioeconomic groups. It captures both the magnitude and direction of inequality and is sensitive to changes in population composition [23, 24]. In contrast, the RII and SII derived from regression models, estimates the relative and absolute risks of a health outcome between the most and least advantaged groups. While the RII and SII provide intuitive measures of inequality, they assume a linear relationship between socioeconomic rank and health outcomes and may overlook nuanced gradients, as they are sensitive to how socioeconomic status is categorized [25, 26]. This study leverages the CI’s ability to capture distributional patterns across the entire socioeconomic spectrum and the RII/SII’s capacity to model relative disparities between extremes. By combining these indices, which are both indicative of the widening health inequality over the past two decades, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of health inequality.

This study also has some limitations. First, the study relied on self-reported health condition. Furthermore, as the analysis utilized repeated cross-sectional data, the causal relationship between income and health could not be precisely determined. A possible feedback loop from low income to poor health may amplify the concentration of health problems among low-income individuals and the income gradient of health outcomes. Second, socioeconomic status other than pretax household income was not considered, because focuses were put on income-related concentration and inequality in health outcomes. Notably, education or job status, both of which are closely associated with income, may affect income-related concentration and health inequality, as indicated by some previous studies [4, 5, 36]. Third, the study did not control for cohort or calendar year fixed effects. Health variables were standardized by age and sex; however, the results may have been confounded by the cohort effects. Some studies have revealed poorer health conditions in individuals who entered the labor market during the “employment ice age” in Japan (1993–2004), a period when young people faced difficulty finding jobs after graduating from high school or college [37]. Additionally, the factors specific to each calendar year other than the inequality of pretax income were not considered [38]. Finally, this study was descriptive in nature and causal inference was beyond the scope of the study. The long-term, descriptive monitoring undertaken in this study provides an empirical foundation for future exploratory studies.

Conclusions

The findings from this study indicate widening health inequality in Japan during the period of economic stagnation from 2001 to 2022. Increasing concentrations of poor health status among low-income individuals and rising income-related health inequality were observed over the study period, although a greater pro-poor concentration was noted for visits to physicians. Additionally, income-related inequality increased for persons having hypertension, diabetes, or at least one NCD. These results indicate enduring income-related inequalities in health within a universal coverage context.
