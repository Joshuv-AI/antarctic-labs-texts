Salicylic acid and silicon cross talk influence physiological and biochemical attributes of sorghum under salinity stress
Authors: Manish Jangra, Sarita Devi, Satpal Satpal, Yogesh K Ahlawat, Nita Lakra, Pankaj Kumar, Sonu Langaya, Azam Seyedi, Sachin Gupta, Nittin Sharma, Leena J Patel, Anurag Malik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalinity stress is a major abiotic constraint limiting crop productivity worldwide. Sorghum bicolor is an important cereal crop cultivated in semiarid regions and exhibits moderate tolerance to salinity stress. Therefore, the present study evaluated the effects of foliar application of salicylic acid (SA) and silicon (Si) on the physiological, biochemical, and yield attributes of sorghum under saline conditions. Greenhouse pot experiments were conducted during Kharif 2020 and 2021 at CCS Haryana Agricultural University using sorghum cultivar HJ 513. The experiment was conducted in a factorial completely randomized design with three replications under three salinity levels (0, 7.5, and 10.0 dS m⁻¹ NaCl). Salicylic acid (0, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mM) and silicon (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mM) treatments were applied separately as foliar sprays at the flowering stage, and pooled data from two years were statistically analyzed. Salinity stress significantly reduced plant height, leaf area, biomass accumulation, relative water content (RWC), chlorophyll content, SPAD value, and seed yield compared with the control treatment (untreated), while malondialdehyde (MDA) content and electrolyte leakage increased under saline conditions. Foliar application of SA (1.5 mM) and Si (1.5 mM) significantly improved RWC, chlorophyll content, SPAD value, and yield traits while reducing MDA accumulation and electrolyte leakage under salt stress conditions. Significant positive correlations were observed among physiological, biochemical, and yield parameters. The results indicate that exogenous application of SA and Si alleviated salinity-induced damage and improved stress tolerance in sorghum under greenhouse conditions. Therefore, SA and Si may serve as promising management strategies for improving sorghum performance under saline environments. Further studies under field conditions and at the molecular level are needed to validate the long-term effectiveness of SA and Si application for improving salinity tolerance in sorghum.Keywords: Salicylic acid, Silicon, Sorghum bicolor, Salinity stress, Crosstalk, Antioxidant defense, Physiological responses, Salt tolerance.IntroductionSubject terms: Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor L. Moench) is an important cereal crop cultivated widely in semiarid and arid regions because of its adaptability to harsh environmental conditions. It contains a high carbohydrate content (70–80%) and is utilized in the production of several commercial products, including starch, dextrose, edible oil, paste, and alcoholic beverages1,2. Sorghum is considered one of the most resilient cereal crops; however, its productivity is still significantly affected by several biotic and abiotic stresses.

Among abiotic stresses, salinity and drought are major environmental constraints limiting agricultural productivity and crop quality worldwide. Salinity stress adversely affects plant growth by disturbing water uptake, ion homeostasis, photosynthesis, and metabolic activities, ultimately resulting in reduced yield. Under saline conditions, plants experience osmotic stress and ionic toxicity, which reduce chloroplast activity, stomatal conductance, leaf water potential, photosynthetic rate, and other growth parameters3. Salt stress also negatively influences seed germination and seedling establishment in sorghum by reducing germination percentage and delaying germination initiation4. Therefore, improving crop productivity in salt-affected regions is essential to meet future food and fodder demands.

Plants have evolved several adaptive mechanisms to tolerate salt stress. In sorghum, improved Na+ exclusion ability helps maintain relatively low sodium accumulation in shoots. In addition, various transcription factors, including NAC, bHLH, and MYB, are activated under salinity stress and regulate the expression of stress-responsive genes such as SOS1, HKT1, NHX, aquaporins, ion channels, and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging proteins5. Different mitigation strategies, including soil flushing, breeding salt-tolerant cultivars, and exogenous application of stress-alleviating compounds, have been suggested to enhance plant salt tolerance6.

Among these approaches, the application of phytohormones and beneficial elements has gained considerable attention for improving stress tolerance in crops. Salicylic acid (SA), an endogenous phenolic phytohormone, regulates several physiological and biochemical processes in plants and plays a significant role in resistance against biotic and abiotic stresses. Previous studies have demonstrated that SA enhances photosynthetic efficiency, antioxidant enzyme activity, chlorophyll biosynthesis, and osmolyte accumulation under stress conditions. For example, SA treatment improved the photosynthetic performance and enzymatic activity of Brassica juncea under cadmium toxicity7. Similarly, SA and Si have been reported to regulate morphophysiological traits and enhance stress tolerance in crops such as Vicia faba8.

Silicon (Si), the second most abundant element in soil, is also known for its beneficial role in improving plant tolerance to drought and salinity stress. Silicon enhances plant growth, biomass accumulation, photosynthetic pigments, relative water content, and ionic balance under saline conditions9. Furthermore, Si reduces oxidative damage by enhancing antioxidant defense systems and maintaining cellular membrane stability. Under salinity and drought stress, silicon application has been reported to improve photosynthesis, preserve water balance, and stimulate enzymatic activity10.

Although the individual effects of SA and Si have been extensively studied in several crops, information regarding their combined influence on sorghum under salinity stress remains limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the synergistic effects of salicylic acid and silicon on the physiological and biochemical attributes of sorghum under salt stress. We hypothesized that the combined application of SA and Si could enhance sorghum tolerance by maintaining water balance, stabilizing cellular membranes, and reducing oxidative damage under saline conditions.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The experiments were conducted under greenhouse conditions during Kharif 2020 and 2021 at CCS Haryana Agricultural University (29.14°N, 75.70°E). The average greenhouse conditions were 25–35 °C during the day, 15–25 °C during the night, and 60–70% relative humidity. Plastic pots (32 cm height × 12 cm base diameter) were filled with sandy loam soil having EC 0.49 dS m⁻¹, pH 7.6, and organic carbon 0.41%.

Treatments

Before sowing, the soil-filled pots were saturated with the required concentrations of NaCl solution to develop salinity levels of 7.5 and 10.0 dS m⁻¹, whereas control pots were irrigated with distilled water. Seeds of Sorghum bicolor cultivar HJ 513 were surface sterilized with 1% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution for 5 min, washed thoroughly, dried on blotting paper, and sown in the prepared pots. A total of 63 pots were maintained, with three plants per pot. The experiment was arranged in a factorial completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications. Three salinity levels (0, 7.5, and 10.0 dS m⁻¹ NaCl) were maintained throughout the experiment. Salicylic acid (SA) treatments consisted of 0, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mM concentrations, while silicon (Si) treatments consisted of 0, 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mM concentrations supplied in the form of ortho-silicic acid.

The SA and Si treatments were evaluated in separate experiments. Plants were regularly supplied with 250 mL of Hoagland nutrient solution at two-day intervals throughout the experimental period. Foliar application of SA or Si treatments was carried out manually using a hand sprayer at 60 days after sowing (DAS), corresponding to the flowering stage. After the initial establishment of salinity levels, the respective saline solutions were used during subsequent irrigations to maintain constant salinity levels (7.5 and 10.0 dS m⁻¹) throughout the experimental period. Control plants were irrigated with distilled water. Soil salinity levels were monitored periodically by EC to ensure uniform salt stress conditions in all treatments.

Plant samples were collected after foliar treatment application at the flowering stage. Fully expanded young leaves were sampled for physiological and biochemical analyses, including chlorophyll content, antioxidant enzyme activity, and osmolyte estimation. Growth-related observations were recorded at the respective sampling stage according to standard protocols.

Plants were harvested at maturity, and whole-plant fresh biomass was recorded immediately after harvesting. Samples were then oven-dried at 70 °C until constant weight for determination of dry biomass.

Two independent experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of salicylic acid (SA) and silicon (Si) under different salinity levels. In each experiment, sorghum cultivar HJ 513 was subjected to three salinity levels along with different concentrations of either SA or Si separately using a factorial completely randomized design (CRD). Data obtained from two consecutive years were pooled for statistical analysis.

Growth parameters

At 67 DAS (soft dough stage), plant height, leaf number, leaf area (LI-3000 m), and fresh and dry biomass were recorded. Plants were separated into shoot and root portions, and fresh biomass was recorded immediately after harvesting. Dry biomass was determined after oven-drying the samples at 70 °C until constant weight.

Physiological parameters

Relative water content (RWC)

RWC was calculated via the following formula (Weatherley 1950): The fresh 200 mg leaf discs were removed from the water after 3–4 h, and moisture was swiftly removed via filter paper before being weighed to produce a fully turgid weight (TW). These leaf discs were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h (after cooling in a desiccator) to determine their dry weight (DW).

Electrolyte leakage (%)

Plasma membrane injury in the form of electrolyte leakage was analyzed by11. A conductivity meter was used to determine the conductance of a decanted liquid (ECa) containing a leaf disc after 3–4 h of incubation at room temperature. These samples were then boiled in a water bath for 30 min at 100 degrees Celsius, and the electrical conductivity of the solutions was measured as ECb.

Malondialdehyde content

Lipid peroxidation was measured in terms of the MDA content (µM g−1 DW) of the fresh leaf sample with minor modifications to the methodology; i.e., the absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 532 nm, and the value for nonspecific absorption at 600 nm was subtracted. The concentration of MDA was calculated via its extinction coefficient of 155 m M−1 cm−112.

Total chlorophyll content (TCC)

TCC was estimated via the dimethyl sulfoxide method13, i.e., leaves were washed, blotted dry in oven, cut into 50 mg discs and dipped in test tubes containing 5 ml of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) overnight. The amount of chlorophyll (mg g−1 FW) extracted in DMSO was estimated by recording its absorbance at 663 and 645 nm via a spectrophotometer.

SPAD value

The chlorophyll content in the form of a SPAD (Soil Plant Analysis Development) unit was also determined with the help of a SPAD 502 plus instrument to calculate a numerical SPAD value that is directly proportional to the amount of chlorophyll present in the leaf.

Yield parameters

Seed yield per plant (g) was recorded by taking the seed weight in grams from each plant, and the harvest index is represented as a percentage. The harvest index for each plant was calculated by dividing the economic (grain) yield by the biological yield (seed + stover yield) of the same plant and multiplying by 100.

EC Value of soil

Before sowing and after harvest, the soil samples were analyzed via a conductivity meter to determine the electrical conductivity of the saturation extract, EC(1;2), which was expressed as dS m−1.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data of combined two years were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using a factorial completely randomized design (CRD). Treatment means were compared using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT)/Least Significant Difference (LSD)/Tukey’s HSD test at P ≤ 0.05. Significant main effects and interactions among salinity levels and SA or Si treatments were interpreted accordingly.Correlation analysis was performed among physiological, biochemical, growth, and yield-related traits using pooled data across all treatments and replicationsvia GraphPad Prism. Heatmaps were generated with ClustVis.

Discussion

Growth characteristics are impacted by various factors other than genotype, and these parameters help analyze crop yield. Under salt stress, sorghum shows a progressive decline in plant height, leaf area, leaf number, and fresh and dry weight. Similar results were obtained and confirmed by14. These authors reported that, in sorghum, salinity lowered growth due to decreased water intake, shoot cell toxicity from salt and chloride, and reduced photosynthesis. Salts in the soil decreased root water uptake due to the substrate’s low solute potential, which changed cell metabolism and reduced plant growth and development—exogenously applied salicylic acid enhanced vegetative growth by increasing fresh and dry biomass in sorghum under salt stress. These findings correlate with those of14 who reported that foliar application of SA enhanced sorghum biomass production. The increased biomass in response to SA under salinity stress may be due to the protective role of SA on membranes, which might be responsible for improving plant salt tolerance. Similar results were obtained in cowpeas15. Silicon is essential for increasing wheat plant growth16. The enhancement of morphological features such as leaf area, stem length, root length, stem number, and root, shoot, and leaf biomass by Si treatment of cotton under Ni, Zn, and Pb stress is particularly effective at reducing toxicity17. Si-NP application to basil and lentil provides tolerance against salinity; it increases growth and biomass18. These results showed that SA and Si improved plant growth under saline conditions.

The relative water content (RWC%) of the leaves revealed that the salt treatments had a progressively decreasing effect on sorghum. Under salt stress, the hydrolysis of macromolecules into smaller molecules such as mono- and disaccharides and the biosynthesis of amino acids, particularly proline, are the primary sources of high osmotic potential19. It was proposed that the osmotic potential value is reduced because less water is available to the roots, eventually lowering the leaf relative water content (LRWC) in sorghum. Our results indicated that the relative water content increased upon foliar application of SA and Si in sorghum under saline conditions, which shows that the addition of SA may protect plants against the decrease in leaf osmotic potential. The authors of14 reported that 50 ppm SA mitigated the effect of salinity on sorghum by increasing the leaf relative water content. This increase in relative water content after the application of salicylic acid was probably due to membrane stability and an increase in the concentration of osmolytes14. Which allows the roots to absorb water and maintain the desired level of water content in the tissue. Si treatment under normal and salt-stress conditions increases the water status of maize seedlings20. In addition, the Si deposited in plant tissues helps relieve the water stress caused by salinity by lowering transpiration21. Silicon may actively mediate several crucial metabolic processes that improve stress tolerance in many silicon accumulator plants because it increases root hydraulic conductivity and modulates polyamine metabolism in cucumbers22. These results showed that both application treatments increased the relative water content.

Electrolyte leakage and lipid peroxidation increased with increasing malondialdehyde (MDA) content under salt stress. According to23 electrolyte leakage and the MDA content increase with salt stress due to increased H2O2 concentrations24 reported that the displacement of Ca2+ membrane proteins and changes in the composition of membrane lipids, which affect membrane permeability, caused an increase in the percentage of electrolytes during salt stress. The accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced under stressful conditions led to an increase in the MDA concentration. By oxidizing membrane lipids, these ROS cause damage to the membrane. The primary product that resulted from this reaction was mixed with thiobarbituric acid to make the final product, MDA. Exogenous application of salicylic acid reduces electrolyte leakage and the MDA content under control and stress conditions25 confirmed that SA caused an overall decline in electrolyte leakage and the MDA content, indicating that SA functions as a regulator of membrane integrity through electrostatic attachment to the negatively charged phospholipid head. In pomegranate, SA increases the osmolyte content, which protects it from salt stress26,27 reported a similar effect in maize. However, Si treatment decreased the MDA levels in sweet paper plants subjected to salt stress, suggesting that Si can act as an effective antioxidant21. It has also been reported that Si application reduces electrolyte leakage because of reduced H2O2 and lipid peroxidation in wheat under cadmium toxicity28. In the present investigation29, confirmed that the application of Si to cucumber decreased the MDA content through increased antioxidant activity and osmolyte accumulation. The total chlorophyll and chlorophyll contents (SPAD units) decreased with increasing salt level from the control to 10 dS m−1 salt stress in sorghum. The oxidation of chlorophyll and other chloroplast pigments, associated with the instability of the pigment‒protein complex, may be responsible for the decrease in total chlorophyll and SPAD units under salt stress30 These results agree with the observations of31 who reported that salt stress reduced SPAD values and chlorophyll content in wheat. A reduction in chlorophyll content, i.e., total chlorophyll content and SPAD values, has also been recorded in sorghum under salt stress30. These observations also agreed with the results of14. Since protochlorophyllide reductase and aminolevulinic acid dehydratase are both involved in chlorophyll production, the decrease in chlorophyll concentration under saline, alkaline stress may be caused by the decreased enzymatic activity of these enzymes32 and the increase in oxidative stress that causes chloroplast injury33. Exogenous application of SA reduces the damage induced by salt stress. Similar results were also reported by34 for wheat and28,35 for tomato. Silicon application to sorghum also increased the chlorophyll content and SPAD values in stressed and unstressed environments. The same results were observed in maize36, mung bean37 and sorghum38. Silicon is essential for enhancing photosynthetic pigments. Increasing chloroplast ultrastructure also improves mesophyll cells and the appearance of large starch grains39. Its use greatly enhances the level of chlorophyll, the activity of the enzyme ribulose biphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco), the effectiveness of PSII, and the ultrastructure of the chloroplast. Si application also improved the expression of photosynthetic proteins such as PsaA, the PS I core protein, and PsbA, the PS II D1 protein40. Similar results have been reported for maize36 mung bean37, and sorghum38. Exogenous application of SA and Si reduces the damage induced by salt stress.

Salt stress significantly reduced yield, such as the seed yield or harvest index per plant. The yield increased with increasing levels of salt (10 dS m−1) due to unfavorable effects on physiological processes, accumulation of dry matter, and, ultimately, a reduction in plant seed yield. The results are consistent with the findings of41,42. Reduced rates of photosynthesis and metabolite translocation from source to sink, which together operate as a source for yield (sink), affect yield production. The harvest index showed a proportional transfer of assimilates into economic yield, although salt stress negatively impacted the harvest index. After foliar application of SA, there was a corresponding increase in yield attributes and yield under salt stress. Similar results were reported by43,44 after the foliar application of SA and Si. Compared with the control (untreated), the application of silicon increased the rice harvest index and resulted in a significant increase in the grain and straw yields. The improvement in rice growth and production characteristics and the stimulating effects of Si in lowering biotic and abiotic stressors may be responsible for the increase in grain and straw yields45. The greater photosynthetic efficiency may have contributed to these results, but Si addition promoted plant development, as indicated by the higher seed output and harvest index. This is confirmed by the findings of46. The yield attributes increased significantly after foliar application of salicylic acid and silicon.

The selected concentrations of salicylic acid (SA) and silicon (Si) were based on previous studies in sorghum reporting their effectiveness in improving physiological and biochemical tolerance under abiotic stress conditions2,47–51.

Further research will elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms involved in the SA- and Si-mediated alleviation of salinity stress in sorghum. Field trials under diverse environmental conditions are also needed to validate the efficacy and practical applicability of this approach on a larger scale. Furthermore, investigating the long-term effects of SA and Si application on soil health and ecosystem sustainability will be crucial for developing sustainable agricultural practices. The integration of omics approaches, such as genomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics, can provide valuable insights into the intricate signaling pathways and metabolic networks underlying the response of sorghum to SA and Si under salinity stress.

Although the present study demonstrated the beneficial effects of salicylic acid and silicon under salinity stress, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted under greenhouse conditions, which may not fully represent field environments. In addition, ion accumulation, antioxidant enzyme activities, and molecular mechanisms associated with salt stress tolerance were not investigated. The relatively limited sample size and controlled experimental conditions may also influence the broader applicability of the findings. Therefore, further studies involving field validation, ion-homeostasis analysis, antioxidant assays, and molecular approaches are required to better understand the mechanisms underlying SA- and Si-mediated salinity tolerance in sorghum.

The incorporation of these advancements into breeding programs aimed at developing salinity-tolerant sorghum varieties can contribute significantly to ensuring food security and agricultural sustainability in regions affected by salinity stress. Ultimately, the successful implementation of SA and Si application strategies holds promise for increasing crop productivity and resilience under increasingly challenging environmental conditions.

Conclusions

This study investigated the impact of salicylic acid and silicon on the physiological and biochemical responses of sorghum under saline conditions. In conclusion, the application of salicylic acid and silicon represents a promising strategy for improving salinity tolerance in sorghum, with potential implications for enhancing crop productivity in saline-prone agricultural regions. Future studies should focus on understanding the molecular mechanisms behind these beneficial effects and exploring their applications under field conditions. Foliar application of salicylic acid (1.5 mM) and silicon (1.5 mM) at 65–70 days after sowing improved fresh and dry biomass under the imposed salinity treatments (7.5 and 10.0 dS m⁻¹ NaCl irrigation levels) compared with untreated plants under greenhouse conditions.The performance of the forage sorghum cv. HJ 513 in terms of seed yield increased upon the application of SA and Si under moderate and high salinities. The sorghum cv., i.e., HJ 513, fits well in arid environments after the application of salicylic acid (1.5 mM) and silicon (1.5 mM), which helps alleviate the yield penalty caused by salt stress.
