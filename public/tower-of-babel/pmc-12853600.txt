Initial different human papillomavirus infection statuses and subsequent infection risk: a decade-long longitudinal study
Authors: Han Zheng, Mengdan Gao, Hongyan Xu, Jingfen Cai, Zhenhong Wu, Yanfang Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHPV infection is a major risk factor for cervical cancer. HPV vaccine coverage remains low. Early identification of high-risk individuals with some characteristics is crucial for those with limited vaccination access.ObjectiveTo estimate the impact of different HPV infection statuses on subsequent infection, infection type, and coinfection. Influencing factors of long-term HPV infection status and clearance were also explored.MethodsData from Wuxi’s cervical cancer screening (2013–2022) was used. Multivariate and multinomial logistic regression models were used to evaluate the relationship between different HPV infection statuses in the first four rounds and subsequent (the fifth round) infection, infection type, and coinfection, and the influencing factors on different HPV infection statuses during the five rounds, respectively. Survival analysis was applied to explore the HPV clearance.ResultsA total of 20,817 females were included. Those experienced two or more HPV infections had the highest likelihood of being subsequent infection (Two or more vs. One: OR = 2.11, 95%CI: 1.61 ~ 2.76, P < 0.001). The risk of subsequent infection increases progressively (versus sustained infection-free) in one classification of HPV infection: a single transient infection (OR = 5.72, 95%CI: 4.66 ~ 7.01, P < 0.001), persistent infection (positivity in ≥ 2 consecutive rounds; OR = 10.78, 95%CI: 8.04 ~ 14.44, P < 0.001), reinfection (reversion to positivity after prior clearance; OR = 18.51, 95%CI: 11.58 ~ 29.57, P < 0.001), and lowest among those who cleared infection with no recurrence (OR = 2.99, 95%CI: 2.32 ~ 3.84, P < 0.001), highest among those with unsolved infection (OR = 23.21, 95%CI: 18.29 ~ 29.45, P < 0.001) or reinfection (OR = 18.79, 95%CI: 11.51 ~ 30.04, P < 0.001) in another classification. HPV infection status seemed not to associate with subsequent infection types and coinfection. Baseline age, at least one coinfections and HPV 16/18 infection may influence long-term HPV infection status. The median duration for HPV clearance is 1.98 years (95%CI:1.96 ~ 2.00), which significantly affected by baseline age (HR = 0.99, 95%CI: 0.98 ~ 0.99, P < 0.001) and history of coinfection (HR = 0.50, 95%CI: 0.80 ~ 1.02, P < 0.001).ConclusionsWhen HPV vaccination coverage remains suboptimal, early identification of high-risk cervical cancer populations should prioritize characteristics including multiple/persistent/recurrence infection, coinfection, high-risk types infection, advanced age, and prolonged viral clearance time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01202-9.Keywords: Women, Human papillomavirus, Persistent infection, Reinfection, Coinfection, Clearance

Background

Cervical cancer remains the fourth most common cancer and the second largest cause of cancer death in women worldwide [1]. Notably, with a world-age-standardized incidence rate of 10.7 per 100,000 women, cervical cancer in China resulted in an estimated 10,600 cases in 2018, posing a substantial public health burden [2].

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is a well-established etiological factor in cervical carcinogenesis, accounting for 99.7% of all cases [3–5]. Beyond cervical cancer, HPV is also implicated in other anogenital and oropharyngeal malignancies, including vaginal, penile, vulvar, and head and neck cancers [6]. Over 150 HPV types have been identified. These are categorized by oncogenic potential into high-risk (HR-HPV, including 16, 18, 31, 33, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, and 58) and low-risk (LR-HPV) groups [7]. HR-HPV types, particularly 16 and 18, are responsible for approximately 70% of global cervical cancer cases [8]. Multiple HPV infections confer a substantially higher risk of cervical cancer than single-type infections [9].

Although the HPV vaccine serves as an effective preventive measure against cervical cancer [10], it does not cover all high-risk HPV genotypes and carries explicit age restrictions. Approved for market launch in China in 2016 [11], the vaccine initially targeted women aged 9–45 years. Nevertheless, domestic adoption rates have remained persistently low: coverage was merely 0.30% in 2018 and increased only marginally to 2.24% by 2020 [12]. Multiple factors influence HPV vaccination rates. Coverage is positively correlated with higher education levels and socioeconomic status, whereas inverse associations exist with older age and marital status (married/divorced) among adult populations [13]. Thus, early identification through assessment of HPV infection status becomes especially vital for those with limited vaccination access. Wuxi’s cervical cancer screening program targets women aged 35–64. Then it can be reasonably inferred that among participants in Wuxi’s cervical cancer screening between 2013 and 2022, those who had completed the full vaccination course constituted a minority. Thus, research must delve into analyzing variations in HPV infection and natural clearance patterns. This understanding will enable the development of scientifically grounded health guidelines.

HPV, a small double-stranded DNA virus, constitutes one of the most prevalent sexually transmitted infections globally. Epidemiological studies indicate that over 70% of sexually active individuals acquire HPV during their lifetime [14]. This infection typically resolves spontaneously within several months, with approximately 90% of cases clearing within two years [1]. However, some individuals may experience recurrent infections more than once [14], which means permanent immunity is not established, reinfection remains a possibility. The World Cancer Reports indicated that persistent epithelial infection with HPV substantially elevates the odds of contracting invasive cervical cancer, as opposed to a transient infection [15]. Although current research has extensively focused on the association between HPV infection and cervical cancer, the fundamental strategy for reducing the burden of cervical cancer lies in the early identification of high-risk populations. Those with persistent or recurrent positivity carry a higher risk of malignant transformation. However, how different aspects of an initial HPV infection—such as whether it is a single infection, persistent infection or reinfection—affect subsequent infection remains to be fully elucidated.

Therefore, the primary aims of this study are to (1) investigate how initial different HPV infection status influence subsequent infection, infection type, and coinfection; (2) analyze natural clearance patterns and trajectories of HPV infection among women in Wuxi City over the decade from 2013 to 2022.

Methods

Study population

Following China’s inclusion of cervical and breast cancer screening in major national public health services in 2009, Wuxi City began offering free screenings for these conditions to women in 2010. This screening program targets local women aged 35–64, excluding those who have already been diagnosed with cervical cancer or who have no sexual experience. This program is implemented biennially to balance screening effectiveness with the accessibility of medical resources. The process of collecting HPV specimens entails positioning a specialized cervical brush at the cervical os and inserting it into the cervix until only the bristles at the distal end of the brush head remain visible externally; depressing the brush handle while slowly rotating the cervical brush three to five times in a single direction; immersing the cervical brush in cell preservation medium, rinsing it thoroughly, breaking it at the score line on the brush handle, and securing the cap. High-throughput sequencing technology was employed for HPV deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) typing, enabling the detection of 14 h HPV types: 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 44, and 68. For this study, we collected HPV test data from women participating in cervical cancer screening between 2013 and 2022. This ten-year period encompassed five rounds of screening, based on the implementation cycle of the Wuxi City Cervical Cancer Screening Program. For individuals who underwent multiple screenings within any given round, only the most recent result was retained for analysis.

Study variables

The baseline age was defined as the age at the first HPV screening. HPV infection was defined as the detection of any of the following types: 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, or 68. HPV infection type was categorized into two groups: those positive for HPV 16 or 18, and those positive for other types. Coinfection classification accounted for the number of co-occurring types (single-type versus multiple-type infections). Three distinct HPV infection status definitions were established to capture different longitudinal patterns across screening rounds.

Status 1 was subclassified by the total number of unique infections detected during follow-up: no infection, one infection, or two or more infections. Status 2 distinguished between sustained infection-free status, a single transient infection, persistent infection (defined as positivity in ≥ 2 consecutive rounds), and reinfection (reversion to positivity after prior clearance). Status 3 comprised sustained infection-free status, unresolved infection (no clearance during follow-up), cleared infection with no recurrence (clearance followed by no further infection), and reinfection. These definitions are detailed in S-Table 1. Two composite variables were derived: at least one coinfection or at least one HPV 16 or 18 infection during the screening period. The data for this study were derived from a routine screening registry system, which did not allow for the collection of certain important behavioral and clinical information, such as HPV vaccination history, detailed sexual behavior patterns, smoking status, socioeconomic status, and contraceptive use. The absence of this information is a primary limitation of our study design and may lead to residual confounding.

Outcomes

The fifth-round HPV infection, infection type, and coinfection were analyzed as primary outcomes to investigate their relationship with three HPV infection states observed in the first four rounds. Additionally, the three HPV infection states across all five rounds (positive ≥ 1) served as key outcomes when examining their influencing factors.

Statistics

We have gathered the raw data of HPV test results spanning from 2013 to 2022. Participants with incorrect (non-18-digit) ID card numbers or logical inconsistencies were excluded. Subsequently, the data were consolidated every two years into one screening round, with participants who underwent screening in both years retaining only the results from the most recent year. Based on their unique ID card numbers, we horizontally merged the data and retained only those individuals who participated in all five rounds of HPV screening. A flow chart illustrating the HPV data screening process was presented in S-Figure 1. Ultimately, 20,817 participants were included in this study.

Survival curve among participants with HPV cleared without recurrence, reinfection and their subgroups

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation ( ±SD), while categorical variables were reported as frequency and percentage (N, %). Multivariate logistic regression models assessed associations between three distinct HPV infection statuses observed during the first four screening rounds and three outcomes in the fifth round: HPV infection, HPV infection type, and HPV coinfection. Each model adjusted for baseline age, history of at least one coinfection, and at least one detection of HPV 16 or 18 during the first four rounds. Additionally, multivariate and multinomial logistic regression models evaluated whether baseline age, history of at least one coinfection, or at least one HPV 16 or 18 detection across all five rounds influenced the three HPV infection statuses (from round 1 to 5), conditional on at least one positive HPV test during the study period.

A survival analysis was performed to estimate the median time to HPV clearance among individuals with resolved infections showing no recurrence, and the time to HPV reinfection among those who retested positive following prior negativity. The survival time was calculated based on sampling time between screening rounds. Subgroup Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated based on HPV infection type or coinfection status at enrollment. Differences between subgroups were evaluated using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was employed to assess the association of baseline age, HPV infection type, and coinfection status with both outcomes.

The semiparametric group-based trajectory model (GBTM) was applied to identify distinct subgroups exhibiting similar longitudinal patterns [16, 17]. We analyzed both HPV infection status trajectories across five screening rounds and HPV infection type trajectories among individuals with at least two positive tests during this period. Detailed methodologies could be found in the supplementary document. Furthermore, we organized a graphical summary to highlight the key findings of the article. A two-tailed P value was considered statistically significant.

The impact of the initial different HPV infection status on the subsequent HPV infection type and coinfection

The relationships between different HPV infection statuses across the first four screening rounds and subsequent HPV infection type and coinfection in the fifth round were presented in Table 3. Regarding HPV infection status 1, participants with one infection (OR = 2.74, 95%CI: 1.33–5.62, P = 0.006) or two or more infections (OR = 2.74, 95%CI: 1.33–5.62, P = 0.006) had significantly higher odds of acquiring other HPV types infection in subsequent screenings compared to those without infection. However, no significant difference was observed between the one-infection and multiple-infection groups. For HPV infection status 2, all infection categories—single transient infection (OR = 2.74, 95%CI: 1.34–5.64, P = 0.006), persistent infection (OR = 4.37, 95%CI: 1.61–11.84, P = 0.004), and reinfection (OR = 7.46, 95%CI: 1.71–32.54, P = 0.008)—were associated with elevated odds of other HPV types infection in the fifth screening round relative to uninfected participants, but no significant differences existed among these three categories. In HPV infection status 3, only cleared infection with no recurrence (OR = 7.91, 95%CI: 2.62–23.94, P < 0.001) and reinfection (OR = 6.27, 95%CI: 1.47–26.68, P = 0.013) showed significantly higher odds of other HPV types infection compared to sustained infection-free subjects. Notably, cleared infection with no recurrence (OR = 3.87, 95%CI: 1.36–11.05, P = 0.011) conferred significantly higher odds of other HPV types infection than unresolved infection. No significant association of different HPV infection statuses across the first four screening rounds and subsequent HPV coinfection in the fifth round were found.

Survival analysis

The median times to HPV clearance without recurrence or reinfection in the total population and subgroups were presented in S-Table 2. Among individuals with HPV clearance and no recurrence, the median time was 1.98 years (95%CI: 1.92, 2.02). For those experiencing HPV reinfection, the median time was 4.26 years (95%CI: 4.16, 4.52). Kaplan-Meier survival curves for the two observation endpoints in the total population and subgroups were shown in Fig. 1. A significant difference was observed only in baseline HPV coinfection status among those achieving clearance without recurrence (P < 0.001). Cox regression results (Table 4) indicated that both baseline age (HR = 0.92 95%CI: 0.88, 0.96; P < 0.001) and a history of at least one HPV coinfection prior to the observation endpoint (HR = 0.78 95%CI: 0.74, 0.82; P < 0.001) were associated with time to HPV clearance.

Trajectories of HPV infection and infection type

Four distinct HPV positivity trajectories were identified during the five screening rounds in the total population (A in Fig. 2), namely Group 1 (negative transformed positive, n = 353, 1.70%), Group 2 (persistent negative, n = 19,291, 87.20%), Group 3 (positive transformed negative, n = 95, 0.46%), and Group 4 (low possible positive turned negative, n = 1,078, 5.18%). Additionally, four different trajectories of HPV positive types were found in the screening period among those who underwent two or more HPV positivity (B in Fig. 2), namely Group 1 (negative transformed other types positive, n = 194, 28.96%), Group 2 (persistent 16 or 18 positive, n = 176, 26.27%), Group 3 (other type positive turned negative and then recurrence, n = 30, 4.48%), and Group 4 (16 or 18 and other types mutually transformed and then turned negative, n = 270, 40.30%).

Discussion

This extensive longitudinal study, utilizing balanced panel HPV test data, has yielded several pivotal findings into the HPV infection. The risk of subsequent HPV infection may be linked to the number of prior infections and to persistent or reinfection status. Long-term infection status may be influenced by baseline age, coinfection history, and prior HPV 16 or 18 infection. The median time to HPV clearance may be about two years, with reinfection may typically occur at approximately four years. HPV infection status and type may both be dynamic over time.

Our study prompts that more times of initial HPV infections correlate with an elevated risk of subsequent infection, which may suggest the cumulative effect of repeated HPV exposure on future infection status. However, the data presented in this study could not establish a precise dose-response relationship between the two variables at present, underscoring the need for further research. The observation from the Ludwig-McGill cohort study that naturally acquired IgG antibodies after HPV16 infection correlated positively with subsequent redetection of HPV16 or other types provides indirect evidence that prior infection elevates the risk of future reinfection, reflecting the limited protection offered by natural immunity. This aligns with our study’s result, where a history of a single HPV-positive event, as opposed to a consistently negative status, was linked to a greater probability of subsequent HPV positivity [18]. As a non-cytolytic virus, HPV can induce a humoral immune response. However, the resulting antibody levels are often low and short-lived [19]. With over 150 known types, the extensive genetic diversity of HPV limits cross-protection between different strains [20], meaning long-term immunity cannot be guaranteed. Furthermore, HPV employs multiple immune evasion strategies that diminish the host’s opportunity to mount an effective immune response [21]. Initial multiple HPV infections may indicate underlying immunosuppression or chronic inflammation, which impair viral clearance and promote infection persistence in the future.

HPV infection dynamics significantly influence the risk of subsequent infections. The ascending order of subsequent infection risk in one infection mode, from lowest to highest, is as follows: a single transient infection, persistent infection, reinfection. Reinfection may involve a shift in HPV genotype compared to persistent infection, potentially impairing the host’s ability to clear virus and thereby elevating the risk of subsequent infection. In another infection mode, cleared infections without recurrence conferred lower susceptibility to subsequent infections compared to both unresolved infections and reinfections, which indicates that clearing an HPV infection, even temporarily, can have a protective effect against future positivity. While some studies have explored the factors influencing HPV persistence [22, 23], few have specifically compared the risks among these different infection patterns. Our results suggest that reinfection may be a more critical factor to consider in predicting future HPV infection. We also found that among different HPV infection statuses, there is no obviously significant difference in the risk of subsequent infection with HPV 16 or 18 versus other HPV types, or the risk of coinfection among individuals versus a single type infection. Presently, most studies have found that persistent HPV infection or coinfections elevated the risk of adverse outcomes, like cervical cancer [24, 25]. Our study investigated the association between different HPV infection statuses and subsequent HPV infection types, along with co-infections, which may help to fill a void in the research on HPV infection to some extent.

Furthermore, we identified several factors that may influence long-term HPV infection status, including baseline age, the history of at least one coinfection and infection with HPV 16 or 18. It is reported that a “U” shaped curve was exhibited in the age-specific prevalence of HPV. The HPV infection rate was lowest at the age of 41 to 45 years, after which it began to increase [26]. A hospital-based investigation in China showed that the HPV infection rate was significantly higher among women over 50 years of age than among women under 50 years of age [27], which may result from poor immune function among elderly women [28]. In our study, the average baseline age of women was 46 years, indicating that by the fifth screening round, their average age would be over 50 years. This may cause persistent HPV infection or reinfection at this period. A study underscored the dominant oncogenic potential of HPV16 [29]. The DNA load of HPV 16 or 18 was significantly associated with persistence of type-specific infection in the first six months [30]. Hence, it can be deduced that a longer period of high HPV DNA load will prolong the duration of HPV infection. Research showed that the proportion of cervical cancer in HPV multiple infection groups was significantly higher than that in single infection groups (relative risk was 1.43, 95% CI: 1.34–1.53) [9]. HPV coinfection may interact and affect virus clearance [31]. These factors can serve as important predictors for clinicians to assess an individual’s risk of long-term HPV infection and tailor appropriate prevention and management strategies.

Additionally, meta-analysis indicates that the median duration of HPV infection is less than one year in cytologically normal women, regardless of infection type or high-risk status [32]. In contrast, our survival analysis revealed a median clearance time of approximately two years among HPV-positive individuals, which is somewhat longer than previous estimates. This discrepancy may arise because our screening program conducts tests biannually. Thus, transient infections cleared between screenings could be misclassified as persistent if reacquired subsequently, potentially inflating estimated clearance times. Data on the median interval for HPV reinfection remain scarce. Our study also observed a median recurrence period of approximately 4 years, suggesting that regular cervical cancer screening might have certain public health value. Additionally, coinfection with multiple HPV genotypes appears to prolong viral clearance, as different genotypes exhibit variable elimination kinetics. Advanced age further delays clearance, likely due to immunosenescence impairing antiviral immune responses.

Finally, the mutual changeability of HPV infection status and type throughout the five screening rounds highlights the dynamic nature of HPV infections. A clustering tendency among HPV types, driven by the similarity of their L1 genomic regions [33], may result in a transformation in HPV type during the infection period. Meanwhile, preexisting HPV-16 was generally associated with an increased risk for subsequent acquisition of other types [34]. The transition between various HPV infections implies that a negative result on a specific type test, but does not guarantee the complete eradication of HPV DNA from the body. Multiple HPV DNA viruses could coexist within an individual. When a person’s immune system is weakened, the likelihood of reinfection with any HPV type rises. This emphasizes the need for regular and long-term follow-up to accurately monitor the progression and resolution of HPV infections. However, the precise underlying mechanism remains elusive and warrants additional research.

HPV vaccination has been demonstrated to significantly reduce HPV infection rates [35] and is currently recommended for use in multiple countries [11, 36, 37]. However, this study lacked data regarding participants’ HPV vaccination status, as a principal limitation of our study. With the widespread adoption of the HPV vaccine, vaccination history has become a central factor influencing HPV infection dynamics. Future research should incorporate vaccination data to more accurately assess the natural history of infection. Given the contextual description in the background section, which suggests that few women participating in Wuxi cervical cancer screening had completed full HPV vaccination series, the findings retain reasonable validity. Nevertheless, subsequent research should account for HPV vaccination status as a critical confounding factor.

Our research findings carry several important implications for clinical practice. When assessing a patient’s future risk of HPV infection, clinicians may refer to factors such as the number of prior infections, history of reinfection, age at first screening, history of coinfection, and prior infection with HPV 16 or 18 to inform the development of more individualized and risk-stratified screening strategies, for instance, determining whether to increase the frequency of Pap smears or colposcopic examinations. From a public health perspective, given the dynamic nature of HPV infection status and types, widespread vaccination may play a crucial role in preventing initial infections and reducing the overall burden of HPV-related diseases. Regular HPV screening can be valuable for early detection and intervention. However, given the observational design of our study and the potential for residual confounding, our findings are not yet sufficient to directly support modifications to existing screening interval guidelines and should be considered as a reference only. Several main limitations of the current study should be considered. Firstly, the strong association observed in this study between a history of prior infection and subsequent HPV positivity may reflect a confluence of biological susceptibility, residual confounding, and screening interval bias. For instance, certain host genetic or immunological factors could simultaneously predispose individuals to both difficulty in clearing prior infections and a higher risk of acquiring new ones. Concurrently, despite adjusting for several covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured behavioral factors like changes in the number of sexual partners may persist. Furthermore, the two-year screening interval could have led to the omission of certain transient infections and may have underestimated the duration of what was classified as “persistent infection”. Therefore, caution is warranted when making causal inferences from our regression results. Secondly, the study lacked key covariate data, including sociodemographic factors, health status, medication use, sexual behaviour, and HPV vaccination history, hindering the assessment of these factors’ potential impact on the results. Thirdly, we employed survival analysis to assess the clearance and reinfection of HPV. As participants provided samples only at biennial screenings, the exact timing of the event (clearance or reinfection) was unknown; it could only be determined to have occurred between two consecutive screening visits. Therefore, our data are interval-censored. This data structure limits the precision of our estimates of the time-to-event, but it nevertheless provides valuable macro-level insights into infection dynamics. Fourthly, selection bias may affect the study subjects. Participants diagnosed with cancer during screening are highly likely to withdraw from subsequent rounds. Those who complete all screenings may possess specific characteristics (like better overall health), leading to an unrepresentative sample. Finally, due to the absence of critical clinical outcome measures such as pathological changes or cervical cancer incidence, exploring the association between HPV infection status changes and disease progression was not feasible. Nevertheless, we can identify high-risk groups in advance by considering any instances of HR HPV positivity as the end result. The study presents several strengths. Notably, its extensive sample size and trustworthy data sources are major assets. Additionally, the variables utilized in this article are either calculated or detected, thereby eliminating any potential for recall bias.

Conclusions

In summary, the likelihood of subsequent HPV infection may rise with increasing infectious number and may be associated with persistent infection or reinfection. No obviously significant differences exist in the risk of infection types or coinfection across different HPV infection states. Long-term HPV infection status may be influenced by baseline screening age, coinfection history, and HPV16 or 18 infection history. The median duration for HPV clearance may be approximately two years, while reinfection may typically occur around four years. HPV clearance may be significantly affected by baseline age and history of coinfection. Both HPV infection status and viral type may undergo dynamic transitions over time.
