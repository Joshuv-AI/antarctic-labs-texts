From Berzelius to Hyperspace: Previously Unrecognized Network of Reactivity in Textbook Brominations
Authors: Yankai Jia, Wai‐Shing Wong, Yasemin Bilgi‐Gadina, Louis P. J.‐L. Gadina, King‐Lam Kwong, Yanqiu Jiang, Nikolaos Vagkidis, Benito Marazzi, Peter R. Schreiner, Bartosz A. Grzybowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChemical reactions are traditionally depicted as linear equations optimized toward a single product, with other outcomes treated as minor side products. However, advances in reaction automation now allow systematic exploration of reaction “hyperspaces”—that is, multidimensional landscapes of conditions—showing that even archetypal transformations can yield previously unrecognized dominant products. Using the Robowski robotic platform with low‐cost NMR detection, we scanned nearly 1000 conditions for the bromination of 1,1‐diphenylethylene (DPE) and benzylideneacetone (BDA). These maps revealed new major products, condition‐dependent switchovers between addition and substitution pathways, and novel substitution patterns with potential for derivatization into biologically active scaffolds. In turn, correlations and anticorrelations in product distributions across the hyperspace facilitated reconstruction of mechanistic networks connecting different outcomes. Together, our findings show that even foundational reactions, long considered mechanistically complete, harbor hidden complexity and behave as dynamic, switchable networks embedded in conditions’ space.

Introduction

Recent advances in reaction automation, coupled with algorithms capable of deconvoluting complex reaction mixtures [1], have made it possible to map the outcomes of organic reactions exhaustively over multidimensional “hyperspaces” of reaction conditions. In this hyperspace perspective, reactions are no longer viewed as simple, “linear” conversions optimized toward a single desired product but as intricate, dynamic networks embedded in the hyperspace and potentially switchable between different major products (Figure 1a,b). In our recent studies [1, 2, 3, 4], hyperspace scanning revealed unexpected complexity even in classical transformations long presumed to be mechanistically settled. For example, in the Pechmann coumarin synthesis (1884), the scan uncovered 11 previously unreported products, including biologically active benzofurans formed through non‐classical pathways [2]. In the Biginelli reaction (1891), hyperspace exploration revealed a previously unknown seven‐component transformation yielding scaffolds capable of chirality‐specific dimerization and selective metal binding [3]. Likewise, in multicomponent reactions involving canonical 1,3‐dicarbonyl substrates, the scans exposed new cyclization modes inaccessible through traditional one‐variable‐at‐a‐time experimentation [1, 4]. However, while such multicomponent systems are inherently rich in reactivity, it remains unclear whether simpler, foundational transformations harbor comparable hyperspace diversity. To probe this question, we turned to olefin bromination—a cornerstone of organic chemistry first reported by Berzelius in 1846 [5] and mechanistically dissected for more than a century [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. On one hand, we did not expect the classic olefins (e.g., aliphatic alkenes, stilbenes, or styrenes) to support reactivity channels alternative to classic electrophilic addition yielding vicinal dibromide products. On the other hand, we hypothesized that olefins prone to the addition‐elimination process may support more intricate mechanistic networks leading to condition‐dependent switchability between different major products. For instance, it is well‐documented that 1,1‐diphenylethylene (DPE; Figure 1c) can react with Br2 to afford predominantly either the vicinal dibromide P1 (the addition product) or the vinyl bromide P2 (the substitution product), depending on conditions [12]. Whether this system supports additional major products elsewhere in hyperspace has never been examined. Similarly, the bifunctional substrate benzylideneacetone (BDA; Figure 1d) yields the addition product Q1 in chloroform [13], whereas in acetic acid it precipitates as the methyl‐substitution product Q2 [14]. Whether other major products exist—and whether they can arise within the same solvent under different hyperspace coordinates—remains unknown. Here, we address these questions using the Robowski platform. In contrast to our previous implementations relying on UV–Vis detection [1, 2, 3, 4], we integrate Robowski with a custom, low‐cost NMR module operating in a fully automated closed loop of sample preparation, transfer, spectrum acquisition, and tube washing/drying. Using this system, we scan the hyperspaces of DPE and BDA bromination across nearly 1000 conditions. In both cases, we uncover regions of previously unrecognized reactivity, including major products not described in the literature, and substitution patterns that are synthetically useful for derivatization into biologically active compounds [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. We then use the correlations (or anticorrelations) of product distributions across the hyperspace to help reconstruct the mechanistic networks leading to different products. These findings highlight the power of hyperspace‐guided discovery and demonstrate that even archetypal reactions—long considered mechanistically complete—can harbor rich, condition‐dependent reactivity. More broadly, this study serves as a proof of principle: hyperspace scanning, enabled by accessible robotic platforms, can redefine the boundaries of reaction‐space exploration and establish reactions as dynamic, switchable networks embedded in conditions’ space.

Results and Discussion

Construction of the Robotic Platform

For the preparation of all reaction mixtures, our studies relied on the Robowski system described in detail, along with replication blueprints, in ref. [1]. This in‐house‐built, inexpensive (∼$25K) platform supports various organic solvents and harsh reagents and can be programmed to execute desired sequences of reagent addition, mixing, and temperature control over 54‐vial plates of 2 mL vials (Figure 2a). In previous works, we used Robowski in conjunction with optical detection to approximate reaction yields based on the so‐called unmixing of UV–Vis spectra of reaction crudes. While powerful in scope, we also noted that the unmixing approach is not without limitations and is ill‐suited to quantify reaction outcomes, in which UV–Vis spectra of multiple products are highly correlated or weak (for a detailed discussion, see Supporting Information of ref. [1]). This is the case in olefin bromination reactions, calling for an alternative detection modality. Accordingly, we first set out to construct a Robowski‐NMR system combining several components illustrated in Figure 2b, Section 2 of the Supporting Information, and Movie S1. First, a pipetting module was used to transfer the reaction crudes from the 54‐vial plates (prepared by traditional Robowski from Figure 2a) to the NMR tubes. Once the tube was filled, a robotic arm (Meca500, Mecademic) transferred it to an 80 MHz benchtop NMR (Spinsolve 80, Magritek). After the spectrum was acquired (∼7 min per sample), the robot removed the tube, turned it upside down, and threaded it onto a nozzle supplying a high‐velocity stream of ethanol as a rinsing solvent for 30 s. After this first washing step, the tube was rinsed for the second time for 30 s at another washing station (Figure 2b) and was finally carried to a drying station under a stream of hot air (2 min). As these washing/drying operations took place, the robot was already filling in another NMR tube and acquiring its NMR spectrum. The control hardware (utilizing an Arduino microcontroller, see Supporting Information, Section 2) and house‐written software orchestrated the entire process such that only these two tubes were in constant, alternating use—as one was being cleaned/dried, the other was being filled in/measured. At specified intervals (every 5 samples), the robot also inserted into the NMR a standard sample (95% D2O with 5% H2O) for shimming to ensure magnetic field homogeneity. In this manner, the system's throughput was limited only by the supply of samples to measure. The closed‐loop design allowed us to process one 54‐vial plate within ∼6 h—this was significantly shorter than for using a carousel system, in which cleaning of the NMR tubes after each run added considerable time (∼2 h per plate) and required additional manual operations of transferring reaction mixtures from vials into NMR tubes and reinserting tubes into the carousel. The longest continuous run we performed was 72 h. From an engineering point of view, we found this setup easier to operate and more adaptable to different types of samples/solvents than the flow NMR technique, which we had initially considered but which involved multiple operational challenges (e.g., time‐consuming optimization of flow injection, material carryover for concentrated samples, and careful flow control) [20, 21]. In parallel, all NMR spectra acquired were processed by house‐written scripts that perform spectral phasing, baseline correction, and peak fitting with Lorentzian/pseudo‐Voigt functions. All codes for running the robot and for spectra processing as well as blueprints for system replication are deposited at Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17080099).

Background Experiments With Simple Olefins

As a benchmark, we first performed brominations of three classic olefins: cis‐stilbene [8], styrene [22], and 2‐vinylpyridine. For each olefin, we tested four conditions with [olefin]0 and [Br2]0 each set at either 75 or 300 mM. The NMR spectra (see Supporting Information, Section 3.1) of all these reaction mixtures indicated the formation of only the expected electrophilic addition product (i.e., the vicinal dibromide). Given no other reactivity channels and additional products, we did not investigate in more detail the hyperspaces for such simple olefins.

Hyperspace of DPE Bromination

Instead, to probe the potential for expanded reactivity, we considered a four‐dimensional space of reaction conditions defined by various initial concentrations of 1,1‐diphenylethylene (DPE), Br2 and an ionic additive, tetra‐n‐butylammonium bromide (TBABr), tetra‐n‐butylammonium tetrafluoroborate (TBABF4), tetra‐n‐butylphosphonium bromide (TBPBr), or tetra‐n‐butylammonium tribromide (TBABr3). Compared to TBABr, TBABF4 represents the use of a non‐nucleophilic counteranion (BF4 −) instead of Br−; TBPBr represents the use of a countercation (TBP+) larger than TBA+; and for TBABr3, the Br3 − ion is less basic than Br− and can perturb the complexation equilibria of Br2 differently than Br− [23, 24]. The concentration boundaries for each three‐dimensional DPE–Br2–additive subspace were chosen based on several criteria. For the upper boundary, we considered the maximal solubility of these reactants as well as the problems associated with distortion of NMR signals in the regime of excessively high concentrations (which made quantification of yields problematic). For the lower boundary, we wished the concentrations to remain high enough to allow for reliable quantification of even minor products. We also wished to keep the concentration ranges synthetically relevant—wide enough to produce variations in reaction outcomes but narrow enough to avoid unrealistic and wasteful stoichiometric ratios. For TBABr, the three‐dimensional subspace was probed at seven concentrations of [DPE]0 (75 and 300 mM in even intervals), seven concentrations of [Br2]0 (70–300 mM), and five concentrations of an ionic additive (0, 75, 150, 225, 300 mM), translating into 7 × 7 × 5 = 245 conditions. For other salts, the zero‐salt condition was not repeated, and each space spanned 7 × 7 × 4 = 196 conditions. Altogether, the four‐dimensional space was probed at 245 + 3 × 196 = 833 conditions. Robowski prepared all reaction mixtures by pipetting into each 2 mL vial the desired volume of the 1,2‐dichloroethane (1,2‐DCE) solvent followed by the desired volumes of stock solutions of salt (1.20 M for TBABr, TBABF4, and TBPBr; 939 mM for TBABr3), Br2 (876 mM), and DPE (876 mM), such that the total volume of each mixture was 800 µL. The plates were then sealed and shaken to homogenize all reaction mixtures, incubated in an oven at 26 °C in darkness for 20 h, and then opened and transferred to the Robowski‐NMR station to acquire 1H NMR spectra of all reaction crudes.

Mapping of Product Yield Distributions

Our exploration of product yield distributions started with TBABr. For this particular system, Bellucci and Chiappe [12] have previously reported 11 experiments evidencing formation of the addition product P1 and the substitution product P2, with the latter favored for decreasing reactant concentrations. We also see these products in our spectra with diagnostic, cleanly resolved singlets at 4.51, 5.44, and 6.80 ppm assigned to P1, DPE, and P2, respectively (see Supporting Information, Section 3.2). These signals were fitted with Lorentzian/pseudo‐Voigt functions, integrated, and converted to absolute concentrations using external calibration curves for DPE (423, 211, 106, 53, and 26 mM) and TBABr (0, 75, 150, 225, and 300 mM; see Supporting Information, Section 5.4). The yields for P1 and P2 were then quantified with respect to the limiting reactant, Yi=[Pi]final/min([DPE]0,[Br2]0+[TBABr3]0), where we account for the fact that Br3 − anion from TBABr3 is also a source of Br2 equivalent. Yield distributions were plotted using our HyperspaceViewer software [1] (https://gitlab.com/az‐steak/hyperspace_viewer) and are shown in Figure 3a,b. As seen, for the addition product P1, the yields are maximal (60%) for {[DPE]0, [Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 262.5, 262.5, 0 mM and gradually decrease away from this corner along all three axes (i.e., with decreasing [DPE]0, decreasing [Br2]0 and increasing [TBABr]0). By contrast, the substitution product P2 forms in > 90% yields at high values of [TBABr]0 = 225 and 300 mM regardless of [DPE]0 and [Br2]0.

These outcomes are in line with the prior work [12], but they do not account for all possible products. To see this, we quantified the fraction of “unaccounted” DPE consumption—that is, the fraction of DPE that was converted but could not be explained by the formation of the already‐identified products. For stoichiometric 1‐to‐1 conversions of DPE into products, defined the Residual Unaccounted Fraction of DPE as RUF=1−∑known,i[Pi]final/([DPE]0−[DPE]final). As illustrated in Figure 3c, the RUF map (based on the knowledge of only P1 and P2) features maxima near two corners, namely (A), corresponding to excess of DPE and low [TBABr]0, and (B), corresponding to excess Br2 and low [TBABr]0. The RUF values at corners A and B are 0.4 and 1.0, respectively. Hence, we proceeded to inspect the spectra collected at these two corners.

The 1H NMR spectra of the crudes near corner A feature a singlet at δ = 2.46 ppm. This product P3 could not be isolated in pure form because of its lability upon aqueous workup, during which it eliminates to regenerate DPE [25, 26] (Figure 3g). However, the singlet's chemical shift and lack of spin–spin coupling partners are consistent with the structure of 1‐bromo‐1,1‐diphenylethane, which we indeed confirmed in situ by 1H NMR titration of DPE with HBr (Figure 3g and details in the Supporting Information, Section 3.5) The highest NMR‐based yield of P3 was estimated at 74% under two conditions of {[DPE]0, [Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 300, 75, 0 mM and 262.5, 75, 0 mM, as illustrated in the yield map in Figure 3d. We observe that the yield for P3 decreases moving away from corner A—gradually as [DPE]0 decreases and [Br2]0 increases, but rapidly as [TBABr]0 increases.

In turn, crude 1H NMR spectra near corner B feature a singlet at δ = 6.73 ppm, which is slightly higher field than that for P2 at δ = 6.80 ppm. Upon aqueous workup and preparative thin‐layer chromatography, we isolated 2,2‐dibromo‐1,1‐diphenylethan‐1‐ol (P4′) and confirmed this assignment by 2D NMR spectroscopy (see Supporting Information, Section 3.4). However, this compound featured a singlet at δ = 6.77 ppm, suggesting the possibility of hydrolysis (during purification) of the parent 1,1,2‐tribromo‐2,2‐diphenylethane (P4) formed in situ (Figure 3h). To verify this hypothesis, we performed a separate set of experiments in which robot‐prepared samples featuring the highest intensity of the P4‐indicative signal were subject to hydrolysis—this diagnostic peak gradually disappeared while the P4′ signal at δ = 6.77 grew in intensity (for details, see Supporting Information, Section 3.6). Across the space of conditions studied, the highest NMR‐determined yield of P4 was 91% for {[DPE]0, [Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 75, 300, 75 mM (see yield map in Figure 3e).

Having quantified the distributions of products P1 through P4, we repeated the RUF analysis of the DPE substrate that reacted but was unaccounted for by the knowledge of these four products. As illustrated in Figure 3f, the values of RUF were now only a few percent, with the exception of a region near corner B, at which RUF was up to 0.27. Since this region overlaps with the region over which P4 forms, we hypothesized it may be due to the presence of 1,1‐dibromo‐2,2‐diphenylethylene P5 (Figure 3i), which could result from the dehydrobromination of P4. To test this hypothesis, we repeated 16 conditions of high [Br2]0 around corner B and acquired their crude 13C NMR spectra (100 MHz) after 24 h (13C NMR spectra were preferable since P5 lacks any characteristic olefinic or aliphatic protons and can be easily overlooked by 1H NMR). These spectra were compared against the authentic sample synthesized separately by the treatment of benzophenone with carbon tetrabromide and triphenylphosphine [27]. Six out of 16 spectra showed all six 13C resonances due to P5, including the three diagnostic peaks of the non‐protonated carbon atoms (see Supporting Information, Table S2 for the 16 conditions and Figure S20 for the spectra). However, this product was formed only in trace amounts, and yield quantification by quantitative 13C NMR spectroscopy in the presence of a paramagnetic relaxation agent [28, 29] was not pursued.

We note that hyperspace maps for the three other salts (TBABF4, TBPBr, and TBABr3) generally resembled those obtained with TBABr, though with subtle differences attributable to the unique properties of their cations. For TBABF4, the maximal, 63% yield of P1 is similar to that with TBABr (63% vs. 60%), but the region of appreciable (30%–50%) yields is broader (Figure 4a). At the same time, yields of P2 are systematically lower (40%–85% vs. 85%–100% for TBABr) (Figure 4b). This increase in the P1/P2 ratio upon changing the salt's counteranion from Br− to BF4 − can be reasonably ascribed to much slower elimination of P1 to P2 by the much weaker base BF4 −. The maximal yields of P3 and P4 are similar to those with TBABr but the regions of appreciable yield are broader, for P4 extending from the B corner toward high values of [TBABF4]0 (Figure 4c,d). Based on the mechanistic network from Figure 5b, this can be attributed to the inability of TBABF4 to form the Br2–Br− complex, increasing steady‐state concentration of Br2 and, in effect, promoting conversion of P2 to the π‐complex P2•2Br2 and the ion pair C3 +•Br3 −, eventually leading to more P4. For TBPBr, the maximum yield of P1 is also ∼60%, but the distribution of P1 has an additional shoulder along the [TBPBr]0 = 150 mM plane where a local, 47% yield maximum is located (Figure 4e). The yields around this local maximum remain fair, 25%–40%, compared to 0%–30% over the same regions for TBABr and TBABF4. At the same time, yields of P2 are slightly but systematically lower than for TBABr (Figure 4f). This trend may be due to a higher extent of Br2−Br− complexation to give Br3 −, which may originate from a tighter ion pairing of the larger TBP+ cation and Br3 − anion. Consequently, a higher steady‐state concentration of Br3 − suppresses dissociation of the ion pair C1 +•Br3 −, limiting the formation of P2. The stronger Br2−Br− complexation also implies a lower steady‐state concentration of Br2, which is manifest in the slightly decreased yields of tribromide P4 in the same region (Figure 4h). For P3, the 1H resonance (δ ∼ 2.68 ppm) is not observed because of the overlap with the broad multiplet due to the α‐CH2 group of TBP+ (Figure 4g). For TBABr3, the most striking feature is that yields of P2 are low, < 50%, over more than half of the space, while the regions of good yield for both P1 and P4 are broader than for other salts (Figure 4i–l). This can be explained by the Br3 − inhibiting the dissociation of the ion pair C1 +•Br3 − (thus favoring the formation of P1). In addition, Br3 − serves as an additional source of molecular Br2 such that a greater portion of P2 formed can be further brominated to give P4 via the intermediates P2•2Br2 and C3 +•Br3 −.

In accordance with the generally accepted mechanism [30], a rapid pre‐equilibrium between DPE and Br2 in 1:2 ratio yields the π‐complex DPE•2Br2, which subsequently undergoes a rate‐limiting ionization to give, in solvents of moderate polarity, a tight carbenium–tribromide ion pair C1 +•Br3 −. These initial steps are supported by the second‐order dependence of Br2 on the reaction rate for various olefins [31, 32, 33] and DPE [12] in chlorinated solvents even at [Br2]0 as low as 10−4 M. For the ion pair C1 +•Br3 −, the constituent β‐bromocarbenium ion C1 + is proposed based on Hammett plots constructed from kinetic data of substituted 1,1‐diarylethylenes and the fitted reaction rate constant (ρ) [34, 35]. The cyclic bromonium ion C2 + (i.e., the bridged analogue of C1 +) [10, 22] and the ion pair (C2 +•Br3 −) are in light gray because DFT calculations show that C2 + always converges to the open carbenium ion C1 + (see caption to panel b). In the accepted mechanism, the vicinal dibromide P1 can be furnished from the ion pair C1 +•Br3 − when a bond is formed between the benzhydrylic carbocationic center and the terminal Br atom of Br3 − with the elimination of Br2 [33, 36]. This pathway is consistent with the occurrence of P1’s maximum yield in our hyperspace at the corner with high [DPE]0 and high [Br2]0 because the extent of C1 +•Br3 − ion pair dissociation is minimal and favors such a spontaneous collapse. In order to form the vinyl bromide P2, the ion pair C1 +•Br3 − must undergo dissociation to give free ions of C1 +. The β‐deprotonation of C1 + by a Br− anion leads to P2 and the by‐product HBr. As concentration decreases from 300 to 75 mM along the equimolar diagonal ([DPE]0 = [Br2]0) on the zero‐salt plane ([TBABr]0 = 0), the yield of P2 increases from 32% to 70%. This is in accord with the necessity of the dissociation of C1 +•Br3 − which happens to a larger extent upon dilution. For the discussion of the lower portion of the network, leading to unprecedented products P3–P5, see main text. Note: As in the upper portion for the network, the cyclic bromonium ion C4 + (i.e., the bridge analogue of C3 +) and the ion pair (C4 +•Br3 −) are in light gray because DFT computations show that C4 + always converges to the open carbenium ion C3 + (for further theoretical details, see Supporting Information, Section 6.2).

Mechanistic Network of DPE Bromination

Analysis of yield maps for the major products P1–P4 (Figure 3a–e) and minor product P5, together with chemical reasoning, facilitated reconstruction of a mechanistic network for DPE bromination (Figure 5c). The elements of this network leading to the known products P1 and P2 are well established [31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36] and are narrated in the caption to Figure 5b. Regarding the unprecedented products P3–P5, we first recall that the maximal yield of P3 is observed at corner A of the hyperspace (excess DPE with low [TBABr]0) and drops gradually when [DPE]0 decreases and [Br]0 increases. In fact, a similar local yield trend around corner A is noticeable for the vinyl bromide P2. In other words, the yield gradients of P2 and P3 roughly correlate at the vicinity of corner A on the zero‐salt plane. This observation corroborates the proposal that the Markovnikov hydrobromination of DPE to afford P3 consumes the HBr by‐product accompanying the formation of P2. As for the new tribromo derivative P4, the high‐yielding region is confined near corner B (excess Br2 with low [TBABr]0) of the hyperspace, and the yield generally decreases as [DPE]0 increases, [Br2]0 decreases and [TBABr]0 increases. Interestingly, we observe a rough anti‐correlation of such a yield gradient between P4 and the vinyl bromide P2, where the latter's yield is zero at corner B and gradually increases along the three directions away from the corner. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that the formation of the tribromo derivative P4 takes place at the expense of vinyl bromide P2. Mechanistically, P2 may react further with excess Br2, leading to the formation of the π‐complex P2•2Br2 and the β,β‐dibromocarbenium–tribromide ion pair C3 +•Br3 − [30]. The spontaneous collapse of C3 +•Br3 − can yield the new tribromo derivative P4. Finally, the trace vinylidene dibromide P5 can be afforded either from the dissociated β,β‐dibromocarbenium ion C3 + via proton loss to a Br− anion, or from the elimination of HBr from the tribromo derivative P4. We note that DFT calculations (Figure 5b and Supporting Infornation, Section 6.2) are consistent with the proposed open‐carbocationic structures of C1 + (shown in the upper part of Figure 5c) and C3 +. Overall, while the majority of the connections in the network are inferred, they are congruent with the hyperspace product distributions and accepted mechanistic knowledge; two transformations, DPE + HBr → P3 and P4 → P5 + HBr, are experimentally validated by 1H NMR titration results (Figure S13) and 13C NMR spectroscopy (Figure S21).

Hyperspace of Benzylideneacetone, BDA, Bromination

As the second case study, we explored the four‐dimensional hyperspace of the bromination of an electron‐poor BDA. This space was probed at four initial concentrations of BDA (75, 150, 225, and 300 mM), four initial concentrations of Br2 (75, 150, 225, and 300 mM), and three initial concentrations of TBABr (0, 75, and 150 mM) at two reaction times (24 and 48 h). All reaction mixtures were prepared on the Robowski platform by sequentially pipetting 1,2‐DCE solvent and three stock solutions of TBABr, Br2, and BDA (each with a concentration of 876 mM) and were then subjected to homogenization and incubation at 26°C in darkness.

This time, the hyperspace was much more complex than for DPE and housed no less than 12 products shown in Figure 6 and isolated by scaling up specifically selected conditions on Robowski and then purifying the different crudes by HPLC (for details, see Supporting Information, Section 4.1). Using their reference 1H NMR spectra to define diagnostic peaks, all crude spectra acquired at each point of the hyperspace were processed with our in‐house, fully automated pipeline, which performed spectral phasing and baseline correction, fitted peaks using pseudo‐Voigt functions, and assigned these fitted peaks to product‐characteristic signals (see examples in the Supporting Information, Section 5.3). Product yields across the hyperspace were then computed from these assigned signals using external calibration. Repetitions under four representative conditions were performed to establish the reproducibility of our yield quantification protocol (see Supporting Information, Section 4.4).

Three species (anti‐Q1, Q2, and Q4) were major products in different regions of the hyperspace (maximum yield of each > 60%), five (syn‐Q1, Q2′, Q5, Q6, and Q7) had maximum yields between 10% and 50%, and the remaining four (Q3, Q3′, Q7′, and Q8) were not quantified in terms of yield distributions either because of severe spectral overlap or because of their minute quantities. All of the products were stable in the same solvent, including the Q1 and Q2 pair, which, as we narrated in the introduction, was previously obtained only in two different solvents (CHCl3, AcOH) with Q2 precipitating in AcOH. Three products (Q4, Q5, and Q7) were never reported before, whereas the chlorinated one (Q2′) has been prepared from BDA under different conditions, that is, using TMSCl/DMSO [37] or BnMe3N+•ICl2 − [38] as the chlorinating agent.

Figure 6a–h show individual yield distributions of the quantified products at 24 h (for anti‐Q1, syn‐Q1, Q4, Q2, Q6, and Q7) or 48 h (Q5 and Q2′, for which appreciable yields became available at longer times). For the known addition product anti‐Q1 in Figure 6a, the global maximum (96%) is located at {[BDA]0, [Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 75, 225, 75 mM. Notably, a local maximum (82%) is located at {[BDA]0, [Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 225, 75, 0 mM and is surrounded by nine conditions with yields > 60% for [BDA]0 ≥ [Br2]0 on the zero‐salt plane—these conditions correspond to the typical, no‐salt literature conditions [13, 39] for the preparation of Q1. Within this same region, the diastereomer syn‐Q1 is found as a minor product with a maximum yield of 19% (Figure 6b). Next, the major product Q4 is formed in up to 81% yield at {[BDA]0, [Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 75, 225, 0 mM at 24 h (Figure 6c). Q5 is also produced within this region albeit in much smaller yields, up to 30% at {[BDA]0, [Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 75, 300, 0 mM at 48 h (Figure 6d). In turn, the known product Q2 shows appreciable yields when [Br2]0 = 75 mM and when [TBABr]0 is non‐zero, with its yield maximized (69%) at {[BDA]0, [Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 150, 75, 75 mM (Figure 6e). Along the edge of {[Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 75, 150 mM, three out of four conditions give yields of 67%–69%. Interestingly, the related chloro‐substituted product Q2′ is colocalized with Q2 and at 48 h reaches 57%–60% yields at four conditions along the said edge (Figure 6f). The minor product Q6 has the yield maximum (14%) at {[BDA]0, [Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 150, 75, 75 mM (Figure 6g), whereas the 20% yield maximum of Q7 is located at {[BDA]0, [Br2]0, [TBABr]0} = 75, 150, 150 mM (Figure 6h). The various regions occupied by the eight quantified products in the 3D subspace are schematically summarized in Figure 6i—this map emphasizes multiple switchovers between different products, and the striped regions denote the presence of two non‐isomeric products.

Mechanistic Network of BDA Bromination

These distributions and the hyperspace colocalization of different species allowed for inferring connections between the species involved and supported the reconstruction of a plausible mechanistic network for BDA bromination. In Figure 6k, the products identified are grouped into three categories: (1) the olefin of BDA undergoes electrophilic addition, (2) the olefin remains intact, and (3) the olefin undergoes substitution. In each category, the methyl group of BDA is halogenated to different extents. Regarding group (1), BDA reacts with Br2 to generate the open β‐bromocarbenium ion C5 + (in DFT computations, the plausible cyclic bromonium ion C6 +, shown in light gray in Figure 6k, always converges to C5 + upon geometry optimization). DFT computations further show that the anti‐attack of the C5 + by Br− is barrierless to furnish anti‐Q1. However, the syn‐attack of C5 + by Br− always converges to regenerate BDA and Br2 instead of leading to syn‐Q1 (for details, see Supporting Information, Section 6.3). These theoretical results are in agreement with the preference for anti‐Q1 over syn‐Q1 in this corner of the hyperspace. As mentioned, syn‐Q1 is absent in the vicinity of anti‐Q1’s global maximum where Br2 is in excess and [TBABr]0 = 75 or 150 mM. The exclusive formation of anti‐Q1 in this region is attributed to the anti‐attack of C5 + by the large excess of Br3 − which, according to DFT computations, is also barrierless. Lastly, when Br2 is the excess reactant and TBABr is absent, the methyl group of anti‐Q1 can be further brominated to give Q4 as the major product and Q5 as the minor product.

For product category (2), the methyl group of BDA is brominated via the enol tautomer to afford the monobromide Q2 (major product) and dibromide Q3 (trace product). However, Q2 was found to also undergo substitution by Cl− to yield the corresponding monochloride Q2′. In control experiments, the solvent 1,2‐DCE was found to react with TBABr to give 1‐bromo‐2‐chloroethane. This suggests that Cl− released from the solvent molecules underlies the unexpected bromine‐to‐chlorine substitution observed for Q2 (see Supporting Information, Section 4.5).

For product category (3), the keto form C6 + must tautomerize to the enol form C7 + which is then deprotonated by Br− to give the known, minor product Q6. The methyl group of Q6 can be further mono‐ and dibrominated to give Q7 (minor product) and Q8 (trace product), respectively. Again, Q7 was found to be substituted by free Cl− to give the trace product Q7′.

Finally, since we performed our analyses at two time points (24 and 48 h), we were also interested in whether and how the disappearance of some products may be related to the formation of others—such correlations could further corroborate the reaction mechanism. Accordingly, we performed Pearson correlation analysis for the changes in concentration of the eight measurable products between 24 and 48 h. In particular, if one product is converted into another in this time period, their concentration changes should be strongly anti‐correlated. As shown in the correlation matrix (Figure 6j), only two product pairs give such anti‐correlations, namely the Q2/Q2′ pair (r = −0.68) and the syn‐Q1/Q6 pair (r = −0.63). Both anti‐correlations have p values < 0.01 and are thus statistically significant (for details, see Supporting Information, Section 4.6 and Figure S65). First, r = −0.68 between Q2 and Q2′, which is consistent with the proposed mechanism whereby Q2 is slowly displaced by Cl− (which, in turn, is slowly liberated from 1,2‐DCE by TBABr) to produce the chloro‐substituted derivative Q2′. Second, syn‐Q1 and Q6 have r = −0.63, pinpointing the possibility that syn‐Q1 is slowly eliminated to Q6 even in the absence of added Br−. In contrast, the major diastereomer anti‐Q1 lacks correlation with Q6 (r = 0.10), suggesting a difference in reactivity between the two diastereomers toward dehydrobromination. Strictly speaking, these two transformations are merely reasonable hypotheses based on the correlation analysis. In principle, anti‐correlations can also arise when two independent product formation pathways respond oppositely to condition variables. Mindful of this, we plotted the maps of concentration changes of syn‐Q1 and Q6 from 24 to 48 h, focusing on a region along the salt‐free plane where six conditions exhibit negative values of Δ[syn‐Q1] and also positive values of Δ[Q6] (Figure S66). For all these six conditions, the decrease in [syn‐Q1] is roughly compensated by the increase in [Q6] (Table S8). This material balance further supports the hypothesis of an overall syn‐Q1 → Q6 conversion. Analogous analysis of concentration changes and material balance was performed for the Q2/Q2′ pair, also corroborating the proposed conversion from Q2 to Q2′ (Figure S67 and Table S9) under specific conditions of the hyperspace. In the future, it would be desirable to validate these mechanistically inferred transformations experimentally by kinetic measurements. All in all, this type of “global” analysis of yield data enabled the uncovering of not only the differential reactivity of diastereomers, but also the participation of solvent molecules in the reaction network. In the broad sense, this implies chemists should pay attention to potential indirect involvement of solvents (even halogenated solvents that are generally considered inert), particularly within “extreme” regions of the conditions’ hyperspace.

Conclusions

Our exploration of hyperspaces in olefin bromination demonstrates that even “textbook” reactions may conceal unexpected diversity. Simple olefins behaved predictably, but substrates such as DPE and BDA produced multiple condition‐dependent outcomes—including previously unreported major products and mechanistic pathways—highlighting how comprehensively our workflow can elucidate complex reaction systems. Here, this comprehensiveness was in part due to the systematic grid search of the hyperspace. Naturally, this is a resource‐intensive strategy, and one may legitimately envision the use of gradient‐based and adaptive sampling methods, which would be more effective in optimizing yields of products detected across broad regions of the hyperspace—it should be remembered, however, that such methods could also miss products that form in low yields only within small hyperspace regions (e.g., Q2′ here). Looking forward, two aspects are critical. First, automation of hyperspace exploration is indispensable for fundamental discovery: without robotic preparation and high‐throughput detection, the multidimensional hyperspaces cannot be reconstructed, and the hidden mechanistic networks remain inaccessible. Automation here is not about accelerating known chemistry, but about opening new windows onto reactivity. Second, the synergy between hyperspace mapping and correlation analysis of yield distributions, interpreted alongside widespread computational approaches such as DFT, transforms descriptive product maps into mechanistic reconstructions. With these components in place, hyperspace scanning can be generalized to other transformations, redefining reactions as dynamic, condition‐embedded networks and establishing a method for mechanistic discovery at scale.
