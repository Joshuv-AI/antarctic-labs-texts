Clarifying Potential Candidates for Extended Oral Antibiotic Prophylaxis: A Retrospective Analysis of Risk Factors for Infection Following Revision Hip and Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Mia J Fowler, Allina A Nocon, Yu-fen Chiu, Kathleen Tam, Alberto V Carli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtended oral antibiotic prophylaxis (EOAP) has gained popularity after a study showed an 81% reduction in periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) among “high-risk” patients undergoing total joint arthroplasty (TJA). However, subsequent studies report inconsistent efficacy, particularly in revision TJA (rTJA) where PJI risk is higher. EOAP also conflicts with antibiotic stewardship principles. To optimize EOAP use, consensus on “high-risk” patients is needed. This study determined which preoperative comorbidities from the Inabathula comorbidities (IC) actually increase PJI risk in aseptic rTJA patients.Methods1995 consecutive aseptic rTJAs (1014 hips, 981 knees) met criteria for retrospective analysis. International Classification of Diseases-10 codes identified IC comorbidities, including autoimmune diseases, smoking, among others. Reoperations for PJI at 90 days and 1 year were recorded. Chi-squared/Fisher’s exact tests analyzed associations between IC and PJI. Multivariable logistic regressions evaluated specific comorbidities’ contributions to PJI risk.Results502 (50%) revision hips and 580 (60%) revision knees had at least 1 IC. Having at least 1 IC did not significantly increase the risk of developing PJI for revision hips. Only body mass index >35 kg/m2 (90-day RR = 3.5; P = .011; 1-year RR = 3.0; P = .014) and chronic kidney disease (90-day RR = 4.1; P = .016; 1-year RR = 4.0; P = .006) were the comorbidities significantly associated with developing PJI for revision hip cases. Meanwhile for knees, having at least 1 IC produced a 3.6x-fold increased relative risk for developing PJI at 1 year (P = .010). However, on multivariate analysis, diabetes mellitus was the only comorbidity significantly associated (90-day odds ratio = 3.5; 95% confidence interval [1.3-9.4]; 1-year odds ratio = 3.3; 95% confidence interval [1.4-7.7]) with developing PJI for revision knee cases. In hips and knees, having >3 IC comorbidities conferred the highest odds of PJI.ConclusionsAlthough over half of rTJA patients meet IC and could be eligible for EOAP, very few comorbidities significantly confer increased PJI risk. Prospective EOAP studies should consider using indications that are stricter than the IC to avoid unnecessary antibiotic use.Keywords: Extended oral antibiotic prophylaxis, Revision hip arthroplasty, Revision knee arthroplasty, Extended antibiotics

Introduction

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) following total joint arthroplasty (TJA) is a devastating complication that represents a leading cause of revision arthroplasty procedures in North America [1,2]. Although PJI occurs rarely, approximately 0.5-2% following primary TJA [3], its prevalence is much greater following revision TJA (rTJA), ranging from 3 to 8% of aseptic rTJA cases [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. The consequent physical, mental, and financial burden of PJI is immense [9], leading to tremendous efforts within the orthopaedic community to develop perioperative strategies to decrease the risk of PJI occurring. Such strategies include preoperative skin decolonization, tranexamic acid administration [9] intrawound application of antiseptic solutions [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]], and the use antibiotic cement [14]. Yet although such strategies do collectively decrease the incidence of PJI, patients with underlying comorbidities such as diabetes and morbid obesity continue to be at elevated risk for PJI.

An increasingly popular prevention strategy involves prescribing extended oral antibiotic prophylaxis (EOAP) for a week postoperatively in patients with comorbidities that are believed to predispose to PJI. Yet despite promising results from 2 retrospective studies in primary TJA [15,16], the benefits of EOAP have not been consistently reproduced in the revision arthroplasty setting [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. Furthermore, concerns for excessive EOAP use have been voiced [23], specifically because of possible adverse side effects and lapses in antibiotic stewardship that could preferentially select out resistant organisms if PJI does occur.

A significant limitation in how EOAP has been studied thus far is the somewhat arbitrary way by which high-risk comorbidities were originally selected. EOAP use was first reported contemporarily in a retrospective analysis by Inabathula and colleagues (2018), who listed 24 comorbidities as being indications for a 7-day course of EOAP, with 9 more conditions subsequently added, bringing the total to 33 [15]. Despite the widespread attention that these studies have gathered in the United States, it is important to note that many of their indicative comorbidities for EOAP have never been independently associated with PJI. A rigorous analysis of the proposed comorbidities and their individual contribution to PJI risk is urgently needed to clarify the indications for prescribing EOAP.

Therefore, the purpose of the current study is to determine if the presence of the 32 comorbidities listed by the Inabathula group (referred to Inabathula comorbidities, or “Inabathula comorbidities [IC]”) confer a higher risk of PJI or surgical readmission in patients undergoing aseptic rTJA. Study goals included: 1) calculating the prevalence of aseptic rTJA patients who have one or more IC on presentation, 2) determining if the presence of any IC, IC category, or specific IC(s) is associated with a higher rate of PJI post-rTJA, and 3) determine if having an increasing number of comorbidities is associated with a higher rate of PJI. Revision hip and revision knee cases were investigated separately to determine if PJI risk factors differed between the 2 surgical procedures.

Material and methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to study initiation. All consecutive rTJA procedures performed in a single tertiary orthopaedic institution from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2020, were identified using a prospectively maintained institutional database. In our institution, serum C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate levels are drawn preoperatively, with preoperative aspiration performed if levels are elevated without an alternative clinical explanation. Furthermore, for all aseptic revision procedures (other than periprosthetic fracture or recurrent instability), 3 or more intraoperative tissue cultures are collected. Infectious disease consultations are ordered for unexpectedly positive intraoperative cultures. Infection was deemed present if 2 or more cultures are positive. Consequently, revision procedures for known or surprise septic indications were diagnosed utilizing the 2018 International Consensus Meeting criteria and were subsequently excluded.

Preoperative variables retained for analysis included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), joint, laterality, medical comorbidities as defined by International Classification of Diseases-10 definitions and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score. Patients were screened for having one or more of the preoperative medical conditions described by Inabathula [15] and Kheir [16] (referred henceforth as “Inabathula conditions” or “IC”): BMI >35 kg/m2, diabetes mellitus, active smoker, chronic kidney disease (CKD), presence of autoimmune disease (rheumatoid/psoriatic/inflammatory arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, inflammatory bowel disease/ulcerative colitis/Crohn’s disease, multiple sclerosis, psoriasis, Graves’ disease/hypothyroidism, Hashimoto disease/thyroiditis, myasthenia gravis, vasculitis, celiac disease, pernicious anemia, vitiligo, scleroderma/systemic sclerosis, Addison disease/adrenal hormone insufficiency, and Sjogren/Sicca syndrome), and “other” high-risk indications (history of sepsis, hepatitis C, chronic recurrent cystitis, stasis dermatitis, and traumatic catheterization with hematuria). For patients with diabetes mellitus, preoperative hemoglobin A1C levels were retained for analysis. Patients with at least one IC subsequently categorized as being “IC positive.” If IC-positive patients had more than one IC (eg, having diabetes and rheumatoid arthritis), the number of ICs was also recorded.

Our institution has robust preoperative guidelines for primary and nonseptic revision arthroplasty with regard to optimizing preoperative comorbidities. Patients with autoimmune conditions taking antirheumatic or immunosuppressive medications are counseled to halt medications in accordance with guidelines established by the American College of Rheumatology/American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons [24]. Patients with a history of type 2 diabetes have surgery postponed for further optimization if 1) their hemoglobin A1c < 8 kg/m2 and/or 2 kg/m2) they have a random glucose measurement exceeding 180 mg/dL. Finally, we have no formal BMI cutoff for revision surgery. Patients with BMI ≥35 kg/m2 are referred, if time permits, for preoperative nutritional counseling and BMI ≥45 kg/m2 are referred for a preoperative anesthesia consult and echocardiogram.

Procedures with at least 90 days of follow-up or occurrence of infection prior to 90 days were retained for analysis. Patients who received more than 2 doses of postoperative antibiotics were excluded. No patients received resorbable depots of antibiotics (such as beads) within the periprosthetic space. The study outcome was the occurrence of PJI 1) within the first 90 days and 2) within 1 year following rTJA. PJI was defined according to the 2018 International Consensus Meeting definition. The 3 most common organisms responsible for PJI in our cohort were Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and methicillin-sensitive S aureus, respectively. To minimize the risk of loss-to-follow-up, all rTJA patients within our institution are contacted via telephone at postoperative days 10, 28, 90, and at 1 year to answer questions regarding presence of infection symptoms, occurrence of readmission, and occurrence of reoperation at any hospital.

Statistics

Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentiles. Descriptive statistics were used to determine the proportion of IC-positive patients undergoing aseptic revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) and revision total knee arthroplasty (rTKA) and how many had multiple IC conditions (categorized ordinally from 0 to 4). Subsequently, a chi-squared/Fischer’s exact test was used to determine if the presence of at least one IC was associated with occurrence of 90-day and 1-year PJI. Univariate analysis was conducted to determine if the presence of specific comorbidities was associated with PJI at 90 days and 1-year. A multivariable logistic regression was performed to determine if significant associations existed between IC categories (defined as they were in the original articles [15,16]: BMI >35 kg/m2, diabetes mellitus, active smoker, chronic kidney disease, autoimmune disease, and other conditions) and PJI development. Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated to determine if an increasing number of IC conferred increasing odds of PJI at 90 days and 1 year. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 17.0 (Stata Corp LLP, College Station, TX) and the level of significance was set at P < .05.

Given the relatively low incidence of PJI, we performed a sensitivity analysis to assess the statistical power of nonsignificant comparisons. For all comparisons that did not reach statistical significance, we calculated the minimum detectable effect size (Cohen’s h) that could have been detected with 80% power at an alpha level of 0.05 using post hoc power analysis. These included comparisons between IC-positive and IC-negative patients as well as individual comorbidity subgroups. This analysis was performed to determine whether the lack of significance reflected a true absence of association or a possible limitation in statistical power. A small Cohen’s h indicated that a lack of meaningful difference was more likely, while a large Cohen’s h meant we could only have detected fairly large differences, therefore increasing the chance that our study was too small to identify a modest but true effect.

We identified 2256 rTJA procedures at our single institution during the study time frame. Of these, 1995 met inclusion criteria and were retained for analysis. The cohort included 1014 rTHAs (50.8%) and 981 rTKAs (48.2%). Preoperative aspirations were ordered in 1058 (53%) of cases with no surprise infections (2 or more positive cultures) identified.

Results

In our study of 1995 rTJAs, 1082 (54.2%) were IC positive. When comparing hips and knees, a significantly greater portion of rTKA patients (580, 53.6%) were IC positive, compared to rTHA patients (502, 46.4%; P < .0001).

IC conditions and PJI following revision total hip arthroplasty

Patients undergoing rTHA with at least one IC condition did not have a significantly increased risk of PJI at 90 days (P = .08) or within 1 year (P = .127) compared to patients without an IC (Table 2). IC conditions and PJI following revision total knee arthroplasty

Patients undergoing rTKA with at least one IC condition did not have a significantly increased risk of PJI at 90 days (P = .076) but did have a significantly higher risk at 1 year (RR = 3.6; P = .010; Table 2). When broken into IC subgroups, univariate analysis determined that BMI >35 kg/m2 (90-day RR = 2.6; P = .033; 1-year RR = 2.4; P = .028) and diabetes (90-day RR = 3.9; P = .001; 1-year RR = 3.6; P = .001) were significantly associated with a higher likelihood of PJI occurring within 90 days and 1 year, compared to IC-negative patients, P < .05 (Table 3).

When multivariate analyses were completed, only diabetes mellitus remained an independent predictor of PJI at 90 days (OR = 3.5; 95% CI [1.3-9.4]; P = .011) and 1 year (OR = 3.3; 95% CI [1.4-7.7]; P = .008 (Table 4). Comorbidities of BMI >35 kg/m2, chronic kidney disease, active smoking, autoimmune disease, and all other IC conditions were not associated with a significant increase in PJI risk at any time point.

Is having multiple IC conditions worse?

Having an increasing number of IC conditions was associated with an increasing risk of postoperative PJI at both 90 days and 1 year in both rTHA and rTKA patients.

For rTHA cases, every additional IC condition doubled the OR of developing PJI at 90 days, but this only achieved significance for 3-or-more vs less-than-3 conditions (OR = 6.21; P = .001). For risks at 1 year, every additional IC condition significantly increased the odds of developing PJI (1 v 0: OR = 2.56; P = .029; 2 v < 2: OR = 2.32; P = .027; 3 v < 3: OR = 5.23; P = .001), until 4 or more conditions (4 v < 4: OR = 3.87; P = .206) (Table 5).

Discussion

rTJA is projected to rise in prevalence over the next 10 years [25,26], meaning that the number of periprosthetic joint infections, currently estimated to occur in 3-8% of revision procedures [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]], will also rise. This increased risk of infection and its devastating consequences have spurred arthroplasty surgeons into employing as many preventive efforts as possible, regardless of their level of evidence. The use of EOAP following revision surgery has consequently surged by over 300% in the past decade [27,28]. Despite this popularity, EOAP directly contradicts current antibiotic stewardship efforts [29,30] and does little to mitigate concerns regarding the number of multidrug-resistant bugs in the United States [31]. The current study was therefore undertaken to clarify which revision arthroplasty patients truly are at increased for infection and could possibly benefit from interventions such as EOAP.

The primary finding from our study is that previously recommended comorbidity-based criteria for EOAP are too broad, applying to over 50% of the revision patients. Through multivariable analyses, we determined that only a few comorbidities (diabetes in knee patients, BMI >35 kg/m2, and chronic kidney disease in hip patients) are responsible for conferring increased infection risk for revision patients. Therefore, these results suggest that universal prescribing of EOAP to revision patients is unnecessary. Inconsistent benefits from EOAP in the literature support this recommendation, with 3 out of 6 retrospective studies finding no benefit with routine use of EOAP after rTJA [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]] and 2 out of 5 studies finding no benefit after primary TJA [15,16,[32], [33], [34]]. Furthermore, the potential perils of ignoring tenets of antibiotic stewardship are becoming apparent. In a retrospective study of 119 primary and rTJAs, Carender and colleagues found that EOAP was associated with an increased frequency of gram-negative infections and an increased rate of resistance in patients that developed coagulase-negative staphylococci infections [35]. Furthermore, Jenkins and colleagues found that TJA patients prescribed additional perioperative antibiotics were at increased risk of Clostridium difficile-associated disease [36]. Outside of orthopaedics, a study of 80,000 patients undergoing elective surgery determined that giving antibiotics for over 24 hours increased the risk of postoperative acute kidney injury and C difficile infection, with minimal improvements in infection prevention [37]. These reports along with our study findings strengthen the assertion that EOAP use should be avoided when unnecessary. However, we acknowledge that the absence of association for some comorbidities may reflect insufficient statistical power rather than a true lack of risk. Our current results support the need for further investigation into appropriate indications for EOAP and enhance the literature that asserts EOAP may not be the ideal course of treatment for all patients.

A secondary finding from our study is that most comorbidities indicated for EOAP use confer no increased risk of infection and should be revised. Specifically, we determined that 28 of the 33 comorbidities listed by Inabathula and colleagues led no independent increase in infection. The largest category of comorbidities that did not confer infection risk was “autoimmune disease,” which comprised 15 different conditions and was present in 30% of our revision population. Although associations among certain autoimmune conditions and PJI have been made in the primary TJA literature [[38], [39], [40]], these publications are limited to small case series and do not involve revision arthroplasty. We therefore do not find merit in routinely recommending EOAP for patients with these comorbidities. Furthermore, our study found that active smoking and “other conditions” (6 comorbidities) were not associated with increased infection risk following aseptic revision. There are conflicting results in literature on the impact of smoking on PJI after primary TJA [41,42], however there continues to be a paucity of literature in the revision setting. We interpret our results cautiously as rates of active smoking were low in our study, and there remains the possibility that we were not sufficiently powered to detect a difference in this group. We do certainly acknowledge that smoking is extremely deleterious to patient health and is associated with other complications such as delayed wound healing, thromboembolic disease, and cardiopulmonary complications. Given a lack of clear evidence in current revision literature, we recommend that prospective trials be completed before mandating universal prescription of EOAP to smokers undergoing revision surgery.

Another secondary finding from our study is that comorbidity-associated risk profiles for revision patients differed between hips and knees. In rTKA patients, IC-positive patients were at increased risk of PJI at 1 year, while in the THA cohort, IC-positive patients were at no greater risk of developing PJI at either 90 days or 1 year than those without. Additionally, the specific comorbidities that were independently predictive of PJI were different between hips and knees, with BMI >35 kg/m2 and chronic kidney disease in hips, and only diabetes mellitus in knees, associated with increased risk of PJI after rTJA. This suggests that hips and knees should be considered separately when determining risk factors associated with PJI. This is supported in the literature, with studies by Bukowski and colleagues finding benefit of EOAP in an rTKA-only cohort [18], but no benefit in an rTHA-only cohort [19]. While there is less literature on patient-specific risk factors for PJI risk in revisions than in primaries, Klemt and colleagues found diabetes mellitus was associated with an increased risk of PJI following aseptic rTKA [43]. In hips, diabetes mellitus has been associated with PJI following THA [44]. Our study is the first to specifically identity BMI >35 kg/m2 as a risk factor for PJI in the revision THA population, however, other studies have associated BMI >40 kg/m2 with infection following rTHA [45], and obesity with infection following primary THA [46,47]. Overall, the results from our multivariable analyses point toward diabetes, BMI >35 kg/m2, and chronic kidney disease as being the 3 comorbidities that deserve further consideration for routine EOAP use. Prospective evaluations scrutinizing these specific conditions should be undertaken in the future.

We do identify several limitations in our study. Firstly, despite routinely contacting our patients, we cannot rule out the possibility that patients may have sought care for complications at other out-of-state institutions, impacting our reported infection rates. Secondly, the retrospective nature of the study cannot account for possible selection bias due to individual surgeon preference. Specifically, if surgeons preferentially chose patients for surgery without specific comorbidities, this could have caused us to underestimate their prevalence and/or impact. Thirdly, our study intended to evaluate PJI risk in patients eligible for EOAP based on previous studies, and therefore excluded patients who had received more than 2 doses of antibiotics. Such patients may have been more likely to have high-risk conditions and therefore may underestimate the rate of high-risk conditions in the rTJA population. Furthermore, although this large cohort is from a single institution with guidelines for presurgical antibiotics, other clinical practices, which could have impacted the likelihood of PJI, but were specific to the over 40 surgeons in the department, could not be controlled. These practices, such as the use of tranexamic acid, postoperative transfusions and thromboprophylaxis, wound closure methods, surgical approach, and the treatment of concerning wounds (cellulitis, drainage) should be documented and their impact considered in future prospective studies. Moreover, we did not stratify the complexity of revision surgery (by time or by components revised: liners, single, or both components) which may intrinsically affect infection rates. Due to our focus on EOAP-based literature, we did not evaluate comorbidity-based calculators such as the American Society of Anesthesiologists score or Charlson comorbidity scores, which have been reported to increase PJI risk [[48], [49], [50], [51], [52], [53]]. Finally, while our study included nearly 2000 revision procedures, the overall rate of PJI remained low, which limited statistical power for thorough subgroup comparisons. This reinforces the need for larger or prospective studies to validate our findings.

Conclusions

Current indications for EOAP use apply to over 50% of rTJA patients and we consequently recommend that such indications be revisited and revised. We determined that not only do the majority of EOAP indications not confer an increased risk of infection, but that hip and knee revisions separately have different risk factors. For hip revisions, we found that “high-risk” patients had comorbidities including a BMI >35 kg/m2 and/or chronic kidney disease. Meanwhile for knee revisions, we found that “high risk” patients had comorbidities including a BMI >35 kg/m2 and/or diabetes. Our study did find that a higher number of comorbidities did confer a higher infection risk, suggesting that a comorbidity-based risk stratification does have merit when trying to identify patients who would benefit from infection-prevention techniques. Nevertheless, given that the benefit of EOAP following revision surgery is still debated [33], we caution arthroplasty surgeons from universally prescribing EOAP. We ultimately recommend that prospective studies of EOAP in the revision arthroplasty procedures be urgently undertaken.
