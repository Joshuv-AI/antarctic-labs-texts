Dissipative energy functionals of passive linear time-varying systems
Riccardo Morandin, Dorothea Hinsen

Abstract
The concept of dissipativity plays a crucial role in the analysis of control systems. Dissipative energy functionals, also known as Hamiltonians, storage functions, or Lyapunov functions, depending on the setting, are extremely valuable to analyze and control the behavior of dynamical systems, but in general circumstances they are very difficult to compute, and not fully understood. In this paper we consider passive linear time-varying (LTV) systems, under very mild regularity assumptions, and their associated storage functions, as a necessary step to analyze general nonlinear systems. We demonstrate that every passive LTV system must have at least one time-varying positive semidefinite quadratic storage function, greatly reducing our search scope. Now focusing on quadratic storage functions, we analyze in detail their necessary regularity, which is lesser than continuous. Moreover, we prove that the rank of quadratic storage functions is nonincreasing in time, allowing us to introduce a novel null space decomposition, under much weaker assumptions than the one needed for general matrix functions. Additionally, we show a necessary kernel condition for the quadratic storage function, allowing us to reduce our search scope even further.



1
Introduction

Dissipativity theory is extensively employed in the analysis and design of control systems
[
16
]
.
While it can be expressed in several different forms (e.g. port-Hamiltonian systems
[
18
]
, Kalman-Yakubovich-Popov inequality
[
11
]
, passivity
[
21
]
, positive realness
[
2
]
), the common idea is to focus on certain input-output properties of the system and their relation to the conservation, dissipation and transport of some energy quantity, typically known as total energy (in the case of physics), Hamiltonian (in the case of port-Hamiltonian systems), storage function (in the case of passive systems), or Lyapunov function. In this paper we focus on passive systems, thus we call such a functional “storage function”, consistently with the nomenclature used in control theory.

Although these concepts are deeply understood in the case of linear time-invariant (LTI) systems, the literature for linear time-varying (LTV) systems is rather scarce.
However, when working with general systems, a fundamental step is to consider LTV systems.
For example, LTV systems can emerge from the linearization of nonlinear dynamical systems along a reference solution
[
3
]
, or by approximating hard-to-model nonlinear systems with parametrized linear time-invariant systems, whose parameters vary with time.
Quite strong regularity and controllability assumptions are often taken to simplify the treatment of problems.

In this paper, we state results for more general assumptions. We assume very mild regularity on the coefficients of the LTV systems, with the minimal requirement of ensuring the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions following from the Carathéodory conditions
[
10
]
.
Even in the LTI case, constructing a storage function is not a trivial matter. Finding a storage function in the general case often involves solving a Lyapunov equation and a lot of computational work
[
8
]
.

In this paper, we offer valuable knowledge to simplify the search for a storage function in the LTV case. Crucially, we show that every passive LTV system has at least one quadratic storage function, which coincides with the available storage and is therefore minimal among all storage functions.

Moreover, we introduce an extension of the well-known null space decomposition results from
[
9
]
and
[
13
, Theorem 3.1.9]
. The null space decomposition in this paper holds for any generic weakly decreasing time-dependent pointwise Hermitian positive semidefinite matrix function (in the sense of the Loewner ordering), and extends to the case of quadratic storage functions, without requiring any rank restriction or additional regularity condition.
This allows us to split this matrix function into energy-storing parts and energy-independent parts. Furthermore, we present a necessary kernel condition for the quadratic storage function to ease the search for a storage function.

The paper is structured in the following way. We start by introducing functions of bounded variation and absolutely semicontinuous functions as preliminary results in
Section

2
.
We then focus on passive LTV systems’ properties and their corresponding time-varying storage functions.
In
Section

3
, we examine the regularity properties of quadratic storage functions.
In
Section

4
, we take a closer look at the available storage for LTV systems and introduce equivalent conditions connecting the available storage with the passivity of LTV systems. This helps us to show that every passive LTV system must admit a time-varying storage function that is quadratic and positive semidefinite in the state variable.
Then, in
Section

5
, we gain a deeper understanding of the necessary rank properties of the quadratic storage functions, which provides us with a null space decomposition, first for weakly decreasing pointwise Hermitian positive semidefinite matrices, then for the matrix functions inducing quadratic storage functions for LTV systems. This brings us, in
Section

6
, to extend a necessary condition for the kernel of the matrix function
Q
, which defines the quadratic storage function of a passive LTV system, a result that is well-known for LTI port-Hamiltonian systems
[
20
]
.

The null space decomposition of the storage function will be especially relevant in a follow-up work
[
6
]
, where we analyze other dissipativity concepts for LTV systems and their relations with passivity and among themselves.

1.2
Setting

We consider linear time-varying systems of the form

\begin{split}\dot{x}(t)&=A(t)x(t)+B(t)u(t),\\
y(t)&=C(t)x(t)+D(t)u(t),\end{split}

(1)

on a possibly unbounded open time interval
\mathbb{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
Here
x\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
is the state variable,
u,y\in\penalty\ L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
are the input and output variables, and
A\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n,n})
,
B\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n,m})
,
C\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m,n})
, and
D\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m,m})
are matrix functions.

Note that, since the time domain is 1-dimensional, it holds that
W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})\subseteq L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
(up to switching to the appropriate representative). Furthermore, for every input
u\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
, the system (
1
) satisfies the Carathéodory conditions for the existence and uniqueness of solutions (see also
Corollary

A.3
). Thus, for every pair
(t_{0},x_{0})\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
u\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
, there exists exactly one state solution
x\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
satisfying
x(t_{0})=x_{0}
.
Moreover, the choice of function spaces for
C
and
D
ensures that
y\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
, because of
Theorem

A.1
.
We call a triple
(x,u,y)\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})\times L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})\times L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
that satisfies (
1
) for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
a
state-input-output solution
.

We are particularly interested in systems which are passive in the sense introduced by Willems for LTI systems
[
21
]
.
The following definition, introduced e.g. in
[
12
]
, generalizes passivity to the case of time-varying systems.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that
V:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
storage function
for the LTV system (
1
) if it satisfies the following properties:

1.

V(t,x)\geq 0
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
and
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

2.

V(t,0)=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
,

3.

The
dissipation inequality

V\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)-V\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

(2)

holds for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
, along every state-input-output solution
(x,u,y)
of (
1

We call an LTV system

Sometimes passivity defined as in

1.1
is referred to as

, to distinguish it from definitions that use a different supply rate (which would replace

2

In this paper we focus on the properties of state-quadratic time-varying storage functions (from now on simply referred to as

We stress that in this paper we often switch between considering functions over the open time interval

Preliminaries

2.1
The fundamental solution matrix

Let us consider the homogeneous differential equation

\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)

(4)

for some
A\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n,n})
, which corresponds to (
1
) when fixing
u=0
.
As mentioned in
Section

1.2
, for every initial condition
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T},\ x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
there is a unique solution
x\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
satisfying
x(t_{0})=x_{0}
.
One convenient way to express
x
as a function of
x_{0}
is by using the
fundamental solution matrix

X\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
, defined as the solution of the homogeneous matrix differential equation

\dot{X}(t)=A(t)X(t),\qquad X(t_{0})=I_{n},

see also
Theorem

A.7
.
In particular, we can then write
x(t)=X(t)x_{0}
for all

and

. Note that it is still necessary to fix an initial time
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
.

2.2
Functions of bounded variation

In this section, we recall the class of functions of bounded variation, which will be used for studying the regularity of time-varying storage functions.

Given any nonempty compact interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]\subseteq\mathbb{T}
, we denote by

\mathfrak{P}_{[t_{0},t_{1}]}=\big\{\mathfrak{p}=\{\widetilde{t}_{0},\widetilde{t}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{t}_{K}\}\mid t_{0}=\widetilde{t}_{0}<\widetilde{t}_{1}<\ldots<\widetilde{t}_{K}=t_{1},\ K\in\mathbb{N}\big\}

the set of
partitions
of
[t_{0},t_{1}]
. Given a partition
\mathfrak{p}=\{\widetilde{t}_{0},\widetilde{t}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{t}_{K}\}\in\mathfrak{P}_{[t_{0},t_{1}]}
, we denote by
\lVert\mathfrak{p}\rVert\coloneqq\sup_{1\leq k\leq K}(\widetilde{t}_{k}-\widetilde{t}_{k-1})
its
norm
.
To keep the notation brief, we often write
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}
as shorthand for

\mathfrak{p}=\{\widetilde{t}_{0},\widetilde{t}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{t}_{K}\}\in\mathfrak{P}_{[t_{0},t_{1}]}
.
In particular, given a function
f:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{C}
, we denote its
total variation
as

\operatorname{Var}_{t_{0},t_{1}}(f)\coloneqq\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert f(\widetilde{t}_{k})-f(\widetilde{t}_{k-1})\rvert.

In particular, we say that
f:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{C}
is a function of bounded variation, or
BV function
, if
\operatorname{Var}_{t_{0},t_{1}}(f)<\infty
, and we denote by
\BV([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{C})
the vector space of BV functions on
[t_{0},t_{1}]
.
Furthermore, we say that
f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
is a function of locally bounded variation, or
locally BV function
, if
f|_{[t_{0},t_{1}]}:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{C}
is a BV function for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
, and we denote by
\BV_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C})
the vector space of locally BV functions on
\mathbb{T}
.

Analogously, given a matrix function
F:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{C}^{m,n}
, we define its
total variation
as

\operatorname{Var}_{t_{0},t_{1}}(F)\coloneqq\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lVert F(\widetilde{t}_{k})-F(\widetilde{t}_{k-1})\rVert,

and we say that
F
is a matrix function of bounded variation, or
BV matrix function
, if
\operatorname{Var}_{t_{0},t_{1}}(F)<\infty
.
We define analogously to before the matrix functions of locally bounded variation, or
locally BV matrix functions
, and the corresponding vector spaces
\BV([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{C}^{m,n})
and
\BV_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m,n})
.
Note that, since for every matrix function
F=[f_{ij}]:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{C}^{m,n}
and partition
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}
it holds that

\sup_{i,j}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert f_{ij}(\widetilde{t}_{k})-f_{ij}(\widetilde{t}_{k-1})\rvert\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lVert F(\widetilde{t}_{k})-F(\widetilde{t}_{k-1})\rVert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert f_{ij}(\widetilde{t}_{k})-f_{ij}(\widetilde{t}_{k-1})\rvert,

a matrix function is (locally) BV if and only if all of its entries are (locally) BV.

Remark 2.1
.

Let
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
.
Jordan’s decomposition theorem
[
4
, Corollary 13.6]
states that every real-valued BV function
f:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{R}
can be written as
f=g-h
, where
g,h:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{R}
are monotonically non-decreasing functions.
Many other properties follow: for example,
f
is quasi-continuous, i.e., its left- and right-hand limits
\lim_{t\to\widetilde{t}^{-}}f(t)
and
\lim_{t\to\widehat{t}^{+}}f(t)
exist and are finite at each point
\widetilde{t}\in(t_{0},t_{1}]
and
\widehat{t}\in[t_{0},t_{1})
; equivalently, functions of bounded variation only have jump discontinuities or removable discontinuities.
Furthermore,
f
has at most countably many discontinuities, it is bounded, Riemann integrable, and therefore Lebesgue measurable.
Additionally,
f
is differentiable at a.e.
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
,
\dot{f}\in L^{1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R})
, and
f
can be uniquely split into
f=f_{a}+f_{s}
, where
f_{a}\in W^{1,1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R})
with
f_{a}(t_{0})=0
and
\dot{f}_{s}=0
a.e.;
f_{a}
and
f_{s}
are sometimes called the
absolutely continuous part
and the
singular part
of
f
, respectively.

These properties extend entrywise to the case of complex-valued (locally) BV matrix functions (by identifying
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
).
In particular,
\BV([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{C}^{n,n})\subseteq L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{C}^{n,n})
and
\BV_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n,n})\subseteq L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n,n})
.
Furthermore, given any
F\in W^{1,1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{C}^{m,n})
and any partition
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{P}
, it holds that

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lVert F(\widetilde{t}_{k})-F(\widetilde{t}_{k-1})\rVert=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\lVert\int_{\widetilde{t}_{k-1}}^{\widetilde{t}_{k}}\dot{F}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right\rVert\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\widetilde{t}_{k-1}}^{\widetilde{t}_{k}}\lVert\dot{F}(t)\rVert\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\lVert\dot{F}(t)\rVert\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\lVert F\rVert_{W^{1,1}},

thus
\operatorname{Var}_{t_{0},t_{1}}(F)\leq\lVert F\rVert_{W^{1,1}}
.
In particular, we have that
W^{1,1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{C}^{m,n})\subseteq\BV([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{C}^{m,n})
and
W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m,n})\subseteq\BV_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m,n})
.

2.3
Absolutely semicontinuous functions

An intermediate regularity assumption that is stricter than bounded variation but weaker than absolute continuity is sometimes employed to study discontinuous differential equations.
Such functions appear in the literature with different names and equivalent definitions: upper (and lower) absolutely continuous, semi-absolutely continuous, absolute upper (and lower) semicontinuous, and of bounded variation with nonincreasing (and nondecreasing) singular part (see e.g.
[
15
,
17
,
14
,
19
]
).
We combine here several of these equivalent definitions under one.

Definition 2.2
.

We call a real-valued function
f:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{R}

absolutely upper semicontinuous
and we write
f\in\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R})
if any of the following equivalent definitions are satisfied:

(a)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that, for every choice of
r_{1},s_{1},\ldots,r_{K},s_{K}\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
with
r_{1}<s_{1}\leq r_{2}<s_{2}\leq\ldots\leq r_{K}<s_{K}
, it holds that

\sum_{k=1}^{K}(s_{k}-r_{k})<\delta\implies\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big(f(s_{k})-f(r_{k})\big)<\varepsilon.

(b)

There exists
g\in L^{1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R})
such that

f(s)-f(r)\leq\int_{r}^{s}g(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

holds for all
r,s\in[t_{0},t_{1}],\ r\leq s
.

(c)

The derivative
\dot{f}(t)
exists for a.e.
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
,
\dot{f}\in L^{1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R})
, and

f(s)-f(r)\leq\int_{r}^{s}\dot{f}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

holds for all
r,s\in[t_{0},t_{1}],\ r\leq s
.

(d)

f\in\BV([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R})
with monotonically nonincreasing singular part.

We call a function
f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}

locally absolutely upper semicontinuous
and we write
f\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
if
f|_{[t_{0},t_{1}]}\in\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{R})
for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
.

We extend now the concept of absolute upper semi-continuity to complex-valued pointwise Hermitian matrix functions. One possibility would be to apply the definition entrywise and identify
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
. However, to use the concept in the context of matrix inequalities in the Loewner ordering, we will proceed differently.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
is
weakly monotonically increasing
, or simply
weakly increasing
, if
Q(t_{0})\leq Q(t_{1})
for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
.
Analogously, we say that
Q
is
weakly monotonically decreasing
, or simply
weakly decreasing
, if
Q(t_{0})\geq Q(t_{1})
for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
.

Using the concept of weakly monotonically decreasing matrix functions, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
Q:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a pointwise Hermitian matrix function. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The function
V_{Q}^{x}:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{R},\ t\mapsto\frac{1}{2}{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)x
is absolutely upper semicontinuous for all
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

(ii)

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that, for every choice of
r_{1},s_{1},\ldots,r_{K},s_{K}\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
with
r_{1}<s_{1}\leq r_{2}<s_{2}\leq\ldots\leq r_{K}<s_{K}
, it holds that

\sum_{k=1}^{K}(s_{k}-r_{k})<\delta\implies\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big(Q(s_{k})-Q(r_{k})\big)<\varepsilon I_{n}.

(iii)

There exists
G\in L^{1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
such that

Q(s)-Q(r)\leq\int_{r}^{s}G(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

holds for all
r,s\in[t_{0},t_{1}],\ r\leq s
.

(iv)

The derivative
\dot{Q}(t)
exists for a.e.
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
,
\dot{Q}\in L^{1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
and the matrix inequality

Q(s)-Q(r)\leq\int_{r}^{s}\dot{Q}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

holds for all
r,s\in[t_{0},t_{1}],\ r\leq s
.

(v)

Q\in\BV([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
with weakly monotonically decreasing singular part.

Proof.

Note that, if
Q\in\BV([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))

\dot{Q}\in L^{1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
and the splitting of
Q
into its absolutely continuous part and its singular part can be explicitly constructed as

Q_{a}:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{C}^{n,n},\quad t\mapsto\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\dot{Q}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q_{s}\coloneqq Q-Q_{a},

and thus
Q_{a}
and
Q_{s}
are also pointwise Hermitian.

(v)
\implies

Since

\dot{Q}\in L^{1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
, and we can split

with
Q_{a}
absolutely continuous part and

For every

This implication is obvious by taking

.

Let
\mathbf{G}:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}),\ t\mapsto\int_{t_{0}}^{t}G(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

. Then for every

since functions in

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big(Q(s_{k})-Q(r_{k})\big)

Then it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}(s_{k}-r_{k})<\delta\implies\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big(V_{Q}^{x}(s_{k})-V_{Q}^{x}(r_{k})\big)

. Consider entrywise

for every

We then introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.5
.

We call a pointwise Hermitian matrix function
Q:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C})

absolutely upper semicontinuous
and we write
Q\in\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
if any of the equivalent statements in
Theorem

2.4
are satisfied.
We call a pointwise Hermitian matrix function
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C})

locally absolutely upper semicontinuous
and we write
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
if
Q|_{[t_{0},t_{1}]}\in\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
.

Note that for
n=1

Definition

2.5
is equivalent to
Definition

2.2
, since
\mathbf{S}^{1}(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{R}
.
Furthermore, from the definition we immediately deduce that the inclusion chains

\displaystyle W^{1,1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))\subseteq\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))\subseteq\BV([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))\subseteq L^{\infty}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))\subseteq\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))\subseteq\BV_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))\subseteq L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))

hold for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Note that, if the domain intervals are not degenerate, all inclusions are strict, as the following example shows.

Example 2.6
.

A classical example of a function of bounded variation that is not absolutely continuous is the
Cantor function
(see e.g.
[
4
]
for a precise definition).
More precisely, the Cantor function
\mathfrak{c}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is weakly monotonic increasing from
\mathfrak{c}(0)=0
to
\mathfrak{c}(1)=1
, in particular
\operatorname{Var}_{t_{0},t_{1}}(\mathfrak{c})=1
, but its derivative is
\dot{\mathfrak{c}}=0
a.e. on
[0,1]
.
In fact,
\mathfrak{c}\in\BV([0,1],\mathbb{R})\setminus\AUC([0,1],\mathbb{R})
, since
\mathfrak{c}
coincides with its singular part, and it is not weakly monotonic decreasing.
Note that, up to scaling or extending the definition interval appropriately, we can use
\mathfrak{c}\cdot I_{n}
as an example for a function in
\BV([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))\setminus\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
or
\BV_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))\setminus\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
.

It is also sometimes useful to consider the
reverse Cantor function

\breve{\mathfrak{c}}:[0,1]\to[0,1],\ t\mapsto\mathfrak{c}(1-t)
, which again is of bounded variation and coincides with its singular part, but is also weakly monotonic decreasing, thus
\breve{\mathfrak{c}}\in\AUC([0,1],\mathbb{R})\setminus W^{1,1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
. Analogously to before, one can use
\breve{\mathfrak{c}}
to construct examples of functions in
\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))\setminus W^{1,1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
and
\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))\setminus W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Note that absolutely upper semicontinuous functions need not be continuous. However, since they are of bounded variation, their discontinuities can only be of the jump or removable type, and one can deduce from the semicontinuity that actually they can only be of the decreasing jump type. We see this more precisely in the case of pointwise Hermitian matrix functions.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
. Then

\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q(t)\geq Q(t_{0})\geq\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}Q(t)

holds for all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Note that the left- and right-hand limits of
Q
exist because
\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}\subseteq\BV_{\mathrm{loc}}
.
Let us split
Q=Q_{a}+Q_{s}
into its absolutely continuous part and singular part, such that
Q_{a}(t_{0})=0
.
Since
Q_{a}
is continuous and
Q_{s}
is weakly monotonically decreasing, we have that

\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q(t)-Q(t_{0})=\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q_{a}(t)+\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q_{s}(t)-Q_{a}(t_{0})-Q_{s}(t_{0})=\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q_{s}(t)-Q_{s}(t_{0})\geq 0,

and hence
\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q(t)\geq Q(t_{0})
. We obtain analogously
Q(t_{0})\geq\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}Q(t)
.
∎

The following lemma shows that absolutely upper semicontinuous matrix functions are closed under congruence by absolutely continuous matrix functions.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
Q\in\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
and
V\in W^{1,1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbb{C}^{n,m})
. Then
\widetilde{Q}\coloneqq{V}^{\mathrm{H}}QV\in\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{m}(\mathbb{C}))
.
Furthermore, if
Q\in\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
, then
\widetilde{Q}\in\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{m}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Proof.

It is clear by definition that
Q(t)\in\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C})\implies\widetilde{Q}(t)\in\mathbf{S}^{m}(\mathbb{C})
and analogously
Q(t)\in\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})\implies\widetilde{Q}(t)\in\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
.
Let now
r,s\in[t_{0},t_{1}],\ r\leq s
. Because of
Theorem

2.4
(iv)
we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}(s)-\widetilde{Q}(r)

\displaystyle={V(s)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(s)V(s)-{V(r)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)V(r)\leq

\displaystyle\leq{V(s)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)V(s)+{V(s)}^{\mathrm{H}}\left(\int_{r}^{s}\dot{Q}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)V(s)-{V(r)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)V(r)\leq

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\lVert{V(s)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)V(s)-{V(r)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)V(r)\right\rVert+\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\int_{r}^{s}\lVert\dot{Q}(t)\rVert\,\mathrm{d}t\right)I_{n}.

Since

\displaystyle\left\lVert{V(s)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)V(s)-{V(r)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)V(r)\right\rVert=\left\lVert{\big(V(s)-V(r)\big)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)V(s)+{V(r)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)\big(V(s)-V(r)\big)\right\rVert=

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\lVert{\left(\int_{r}^{s}\dot{V}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)V(s)+{V(r)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)\int_{r}^{s}\dot{V}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right\rVert\leq 2\lVert Q\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\int_{r}^{s}\lVert\dot{V}(t)\rVert\,\mathrm{d}t,

we obtain

\widetilde{Q}(s)-\widetilde{Q}(r)\leq\int_{r}^{s}\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left(2\lVert Q\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert\dot{V}(t)\rVert+\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert\dot{Q}(t)\rVert\right)I_{n}\,\mathrm{d}t.

By taking

G:[t_{0},t_{1}]\to\mathbb{C}^{n,n},\qquad t\mapsto\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}}(2\lVert Q\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert\dot{V}(t)\rVert+\lVert V\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert\dot{Q}(t)\rVert)I_{n},

we deduce that
G\in L^{1}([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
does not depend on
r,s
, and that
\widetilde{Q}(s)-\widetilde{Q}(r)\leq\int_{r}^{s}G(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
.
We conclude with
Theorem

2.4
(iii)
that
\widetilde{Q}\in\AUC([t_{0},t_{1}],\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
.
∎

Finally, we characterize weakly decreasing matrix functions in terms of their absolute semicontinuity and classical derivative.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Then
Q
is weakly monotonically decreasing if and only if
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
and
\dot{Q}(t)\leq 0
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

If
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
and
\dot{Q}(t)\leq 0
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
, then
Q(s)\leq Q(r)
for all
r,s\in\mathbb{T},\ r<s
, because of
Theorem

2.4
(iv)
.

Suppose now that
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
is weakly monotonically decreasing.
In particular, by taking the constant zero matrix function
G\equiv 0\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
in
Theorem

2.4
(iii)
, we deduce that
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C}))
.
Let
\mathbb{T}_{d}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
be the set of points where
Q
has a classical derivative, thus
\lvert\mathbb{T}\setminus\mathbb{T}_{d}\rvert=0
.
Since

is weakly decreasing, for every

and

, and therefore

In this section we have summarized and extended some classical results to prepare for the subsequent analysis of the properties of storage functions.

3
Regularity of quadratic storage functions

In this section, we study the regularity of quadratic storage functions, as introduced in (
3
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
V:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a storage function for a passive LTV system of the form (
1
), and
let
X\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
denote the fundamental solution matrix associated to

for some fixed initial time

.
Then, for every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
r,s\in\mathbb{T}
with
r\leq s
, the inequality

V\big(s,X(s)x_{0}\big)\leq V\big(r,X(r)x_{0}\big)

(5)

holds.
Furthermore, if
V=V_{Q}
for some
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
, then the map

Q^{X}:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}),\qquad t\mapsto{X(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)X(t)

is weakly monotonically decreasing, in particular
Q^{X}\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
with
\dot{Q}^{X}\leq 0
.

Proof.

The state trajectory
x:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}^{n},\ t\mapsto X(t)x_{0}
is the unique solution of (
1
) with vanishing input signal
u=0
, such that
x(t_{0})=x_{0}
.
In particular, we obtain that

V\big(s,X(s)x_{0}\big)=V\big(s,x(s)\big)\leq V\big(r,x(r)\big)=V\big(r,X(r)x_{0}\big)

for all
r,s\in\mathbb{T},\ r\leq s
.
Suppose now that
V=V_{Q}
with
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
.
It is clear that
Q^{X}
is well-defined. Furthermore, because of (
5
) we have that

{x_{0}}^{\mathrm{H}}\big(Q^{X}(s)-Q^{X}(r)\big)x_{0}=2V\big(s,X(s)x_{0}\big)-2V\big(r,X(r)x_{0}\big)\leq 0

for all

and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, thus
Q^{X}(s)\leq Q^{X}(r)
, i.e.,
Q^{X}
is weakly decreasing.
Finally, by applying

, we conclude that
Q^{X}\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
with
\dot{Q}^{X}\leq 0
.
∎

We deduce the regularity of quadratic storage functions.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that, for some
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
,

is a storage function for a passive LTV system of the form (

). Then
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Proof.

Let
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
.
Because of
Lemma

3.1
, it holds that
Q^{X}\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
.
Let now
V:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}^{n,n},\ t\mapsto X(t)^{-1}
, which is well-defined and in

, because of
Theorem

A.7
. Then

{V(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q^{X}(t)V(t)={X(t)}^{-\mathrm{H}}{X(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)X(t)X(t)^{-1}=Q(t)

for all

We conclude with

.

Let us recall that
L^{p}
functions are identified when they coincide almost everywhere.
Although the definitions of BV functions and absolutely upper semicontinuous functions could be adjusted analogously, this is more challenging when considering the matrix function
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
inducing a quadratic storage function, unless we require the dissipation inequality (
2
) to only hold for a.e.
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
.

To address this problem, we look for a uniquely determined representative
\widetilde{Q}\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
, among the matrix functions that coincide with
\widetilde{Q}
almost everywhere, such that
\widetilde{Q}
also induces a quadratic storage function for the same passive LTV system.
Since in the points of continuity of
Q
it is straightforward to leave the function as it is, we analyze our freedom in the points of discontinuity, which form a set of measure zero, and can only be of the decreasing jump type, because of

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
V_{Q}
with
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
is a storage function for a passive LTV system (

), and let
\widetilde{Q}:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
such that

holds for all

.
Then
V_{\widetilde{Q}}
is a storage function for (
1
).

Proof.

Let
(x,u,y)
be any state-input-output solution of (
1
) and let
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}<t_{1}
, since for
t_{0}=t_{1}
the dissipation inequality (

.
For every
r,s\in\mathbb{T}
such that
t_{0}<r<s<t_{1}
we have

\frac{1}{2}{x(s)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(s)x(s)-\frac{1}{2}{x(r)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(r)x(r)=V_{Q}\big(s,x(s)\big)-V_{Q}\big(r,x(r)\big)\leq\int_{r}^{s}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t,

thus in the limit for
r\to t_{0}^{+}
and
s\to t_{1}^{-}
we obtain

\frac{1}{2}{x(t_{1})}^{\mathrm{H}}\left(\lim_{s\to t_{1}^{-}}Q(s)\right)x(t_{1})-\frac{1}{2}{x(t_{0})}^{\mathrm{H}}\left(\lim_{r\to t_{0}^{+}}Q(r)\right)x(t_{0})\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t.

We conclude that

\displaystyle V_{\widetilde{Q}}\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)-V_{\widetilde{Q}}\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)=\frac{1}{2}{x(t_{1})}^{\mathrm{H}}\widetilde{Q}(t_{1})x(t_{1})-\frac{1}{2}{x(t_{0})}^{\mathrm{H}}\widetilde{Q}(t_{0})x(t_{0})

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{2}{x(t_{1})}^{\mathrm{H}}\left(\lim_{s\to t_{1}^{-}}Q(s)\right)x(t_{1})-\frac{1}{2}{x(t_{0})}^{\mathrm{H}}\left(\lim_{r\to t_{0}^{+}}Q(r)\right)x(t_{0})\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t,

i.e.,
V_{\widetilde{Q}}
satisfies the dissipation inequality and is a storage function for (
1
).
∎

An immediate consequence of
Lemma

3.3
is that, if
V_{Q}
is a storage function for (
1
), then it admits an equivalent representative that is continuous from the right with respect to the time variable. We state this more precisely in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
V_{Q}
with
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
be a storage function for an LTV system (
1
).
Then the matrix function

\widetilde{Q}:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}^{n,n},\qquad t\mapsto\lim_{s\to t^{+}}Q(t)

is such that
\widetilde{Q}=Q
almost everywhere,
\widetilde{Q}(t)\leq Q(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, and
V_{\widetilde{Q}}
is a storage function for (
1
).

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{Q}
is locally bounded variation, it is continuous almost everywhere, thus
\widetilde{Q}=Q
almost everywhere by definition of
\widetilde{Q}
.
Because of
Lemma

2.7
, we have
\widetilde{Q}(t)\leq Q(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
and we can apply
Lemma

3.3
, concluding
V_{\widetilde{Q}}
is a storage function for (
1
).
∎

After introducing these properties we can use them in the following sections.

4
The available storage

We recall the definition of available storage for LTV systems from
[
12
,
16
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

The
available storage
of the LTV system (
1
) at time
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
and state
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is

V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(t_{1},u)\,:\,t_{1}\geq t_{0},\\
x(t_{0})=x_{0}\end{subarray}{}}\left(-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\right)=-\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(t_{1},u)\,:\,t_{1}\geq t_{0},\\
x(t_{0})=x_{0}\end{subarray}{}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t))\,\mathrm{d}t,

(6)

where the supremum (or infimum) is taken among all times
t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{1}\geq t_{0}
and inputs
u\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
, and
x\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
y\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
denote the state and output trajectories uniquely determined by
u
and by the condition
x(t_{0})=x_{0}
.

Since the supremum in
Definition

4.1
is taken over a nonempty set, it is always well defined.
Furthermore, since for
t_{1}=t_{0}
the integral in (
6
) vanishes, the available storage is always nonnegative.
We can then intepret it as a possibly infinite-valued function
V_{a}:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to[0,+\infty]
.
If we actually have
V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})<+\infty
for all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we say that the available storage is finite.

The finiteness of the available storage is closely related to the passivity of the system. In fact, we have the following equivalent statements.

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider an LTV system of the form (
1
). Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The system is passive.

(ii)

There exists
\beta:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for every
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
and state-input-output solution
(x,u,y)
, it holds that

\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\beta\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big).

(iii)

The available storage function is finite.

(iv)

The available storage function is of the form
V_{a}=V_{Q}
for some matrix function
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)

Let
V:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be any storage function for the system and let us define
\beta\coloneqq-V
.
Then for every
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
and state-input-output solution
(x,u,y)
it holds that

\beta\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)=-V\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)=V\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)-V\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)-V\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t,

because of the dissipation inequality (
2
) and of the non-negativity of storage functions.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)

The available storage satisfies

0\leq V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(t_{1},u):t_{1}\geq t_{0},\\
x(t_{0})\geq x_{0}\end{subarray}}-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq-\beta(t_{0},x_{0})<\infty,

for all
(t_{0},x_{0})\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
, thus it is finite.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv)

Let us define for simplicity

\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},x_{0},u)\coloneqq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
with
t_{0}\leq t_{1}
, initial state
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and admissible input
u
, and let us write in short
V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})=-\inf_{t_{1},u}\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},x_{0},u)
, where
t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{1}\geq t_{0}
and
u\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
are implicit in the notation.
Note that for every
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
with
t_{0}\leq t_{1}
, initial state
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, complex number
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, and pair of state-input-output solutions
(x,u,y),(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{u},\widetilde{y})
such that
x(t_{0})=x_{0}
and
\widetilde{x}(t_{0})=\widetilde{x}_{0}
, we have

\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},\lambda x_{0},\lambda u)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\left({\big(\lambda y(t)\big)}^{\mathrm{H}}\lambda u(t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t=\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}\cdot\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},x_{0},u)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},x_{0}+\widetilde{x}_{0},u+\widetilde{u})+\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},x_{0}-\widetilde{x}_{0},u-\widetilde{u})

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\left({\big(y(t)+\widetilde{y}(t)\big)}^{\mathrm{H}}\big(u(t)+\widetilde{u}(t)\big)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\left({\big(y(t)-\widetilde{y}(t)\big)}^{\mathrm{H}}\big(u(t)-\widetilde{u}(t)\big)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=2\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+2\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({\widetilde{y}(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}\widetilde{u}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t=2\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},x_{0},u)+2\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},\widetilde{x}_{0},\widetilde{u}),

since the state-input-output solutions of (
1
) form a linear subspace (see
Lemma

A.4
).

We show first that

V_{a}(t_{0},\lambda x_{0})=\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}\cdot V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})

(7)

holds for all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Suppose first that
\lambda\neq 0
: then for every
\varepsilon>0
there are
\underline{t_{1}}\geq t_{0}
and
\underline{u}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
such that

\inf_{t_{1},u}\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},\lambda x_{0},u)>\mathcal{V}(t_{0},\underline{t_{1}},\lambda x_{0},\underline{u})-\varepsilon
.
Furthermore, since
\lambda^{-1}\underline{u}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
, we obtain

\displaystyle V_{a}(t_{0},\lambda x_{0})

\displaystyle=-\inf_{t_{1},u}\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},\lambda x_{0},u)<\varepsilon-\mathcal{V}(t_{0},\underline{t_{1}},\lambda x_{0},\underline{u})=\varepsilon-\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}\mathcal{V}(t_{0},\underline{t_{1}},x_{0},\lambda^{-1}\underline{u})

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon-\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}\inf_{t_{1},u}\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},x_{0},u)=\varepsilon+\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0}).

Since the choice of
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we deduce that

V_{a}(t_{0},\lambda x_{0})\leq\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
In particular, by applying this property twice, be obtain

V_{a}(t_{0},\lambda x_{0})\leq\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}\cdot V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})=\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}\cdot V_{a}(t_{0},\lambda^{-1}\lambda x_{0})\leq\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}\lvert\lambda^{-1}\rvert^{2}V_{a}(t_{0},\lambda x_{0})=V_{a}(t_{0},\lambda x_{0}),

and therefore
V_{a}(t_{0},\lambda x_{0})=\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2}\cdot V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})
, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
To prove (
7
) for
\lambda=0
, we simply note that

V_{a}(t_{0},0)=V_{a}(t_{0},2\cdot 0)=4V_{a}(t_{0},0)\implies V_{a}(t_{0},0)=0.

We now show that

V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0}+\widetilde{x}_{0})+V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0}-\widetilde{x}_{0})=2V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})+2V_{a}(t_{0},\widetilde{x}_{0})

(8)

holds for all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
and
x_{0},\widetilde{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, so that we may conclude that
V_{a}(t_{0},\cdot):\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a quadratic form for every fixed
t_{0}
.
Analogously as before, for every
\varepsilon>0
there are
{t_{1}},{\widetilde{t}_{1}}\geq t_{0}
and
{u},{\widetilde{u}}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})
such that

V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})<\varepsilon-\mathcal{V}(t_{0},{t_{1}},x_{0},{u})

and

V_{a}(t_{0},\widetilde{x}_{0})<\varepsilon-\mathcal{V}(t_{0},{\widetilde{t}_{1}},\widetilde{x}_{0},{\widetilde{u}}).

Note that we can assume without loss of generality that
{t_{1}}={\widetilde{t}_{1}}
: if we had e.g.
{t_{1}}<{\widetilde{t}_{1}}
, we could replace
u
with

{\widehat{u}}(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}u(t)&\text{for }t\leq{t_{1}},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and observe that the corresponding outputs satisfy
{\widehat{y}}(t)={y}(t)
for all
t\leq{t_{1}}
, and therefore

\mathcal{V}(t_{0},{\widetilde{t}_{1}},x_{0},{\widehat{u}})=\int_{t_{0}}^{{\widetilde{t}_{1}}}\realPart\big({{\widehat{y}}(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}{\widehat{u}}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{t_{0}}^{{t_{1}}}\realPart\big({{y}(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}{u}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+0=\mathcal{V}(t_{0},{t_{1}},x_{0},{u}).

Then it holds that

\displaystyle V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0}+\widetilde{x}_{0})+V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0}-\widetilde{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},x_{0}+\widetilde{x}_{0},u+\widetilde{u})+\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},x_{0}-\widetilde{x}_{0},u-\widetilde{u})

\displaystyle\qquad=2\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},x_{0},u)+2\mathcal{V}(t_{0},t_{1},\widetilde{x}_{0},\widetilde{u})>2V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})+2V_{a}(t_{0},\widetilde{x}_{0})-2\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
can be taken arbitrarily small, we obtain

V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0}+\widetilde{x}_{0})+V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0}-\widetilde{x}_{0})\geq 2V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})+2V_{a}(t_{0},\widetilde{x}_{0})

for all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
and
x_{0},\widetilde{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
By applying this argument twice, we deduce that

\displaystyle 2V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})+2V_{a}(t_{0},\widetilde{x}_{0})=2\big(V_{a}(t_{0},\tfrac{x_{0}+\widetilde{x}_{0}}{2}+\tfrac{x_{0}-\widetilde{x}_{0}}{2})+V_{a}(t_{0},\tfrac{x_{0}+\widetilde{x}_{0}}{2}-\tfrac{x_{0}-\widetilde{x}_{0}}{2})\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\geq 4\big(V_{a}(t_{0},\tfrac{x_{0}+\widetilde{x}_{0}}{2})+V_{a}(t_{0},\tfrac{x_{0}-\widetilde{x}_{0}}{2})\big)=V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0}+\widetilde{x}_{0})+V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0}-\widetilde{x}_{0}),

and therefore (
8
) holds.

From (
7
) and (
8
) we conclude that
V_{a}
is a quadratic form with respect to
x
, i.e.,

V_{a}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)x

for a uniquely determined
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}(\mathbb{C})
, for every
t\in\mathbb{T}
and
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Furthermore, from the non-negativity of
V_{a}
we immediately deduce that actually
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i)

From the form of
V_{a}
, it trivially follows that it is finite and satisfies
V_{a}(t,0)=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
Let now
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
with
t_{0}\leq t_{1}
, let
(x,u,y)
be any state-input-output solution of (
1
), and let

\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\{\widetilde{u}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m})\mid\widetilde{u}(t)=u(t)\text{ for all }t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]\}.

For every
t_{2}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{2}\geq t_{1}
and
\widetilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}
it holds that

\mathcal{V}\big(t_{0},t_{2},x(t_{0}),\widetilde{u}\big)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{V}\big(t_{1},t_{2},x(t_{1}),\widetilde{u}\big),

because of the causality of the system (i.e., the fact that the values of
x(t),y(t)
for a fixed
t\in\mathbb{T},\ t\geq t_{0}
are uniquely determined by
x(t_{0})
and the restriction
u|_{[t_{0},t]}
, see e.g.
[
16
]
).
In particular, we deduce that

\displaystyle V_{a}\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)

\displaystyle=-\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2}\geq t_{0},\\
\widetilde{u}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{V}\big(t_{0},t_{2},x(t_{0}),\widetilde{u}\big)\geq-\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2}\geq t_{1},\\
\widetilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{V}\big(t_{0},t_{2},x(t_{0}),\widetilde{u}\big)

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t-\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2}\geq t_{1},\\
\widetilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{V}\big(t_{1},t_{2},x(t_{1}),\widetilde{u}\big)

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+V_{a}\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big),

where

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2}\geq t_{1},\\
\widetilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{V}\big(t_{1},t_{2},x(t_{1}),\widetilde{u}\big)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2}\geq t_{1},\\
\widetilde{u}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{V}\big(t_{1},t_{2},x(t_{1}),\widetilde{u}\big)

holds again because of causality, and therefore
V_{a}
is a storage function. ∎

Note that the proof of
Theorem

4.2
(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i) also shows that, if the LTV system (
1
) is passive, then the available storage is a storage function.
Not only this is a well-known fact, but it can also be observed that
V_{a}
is minimal among all storage functions of (
1
) (see e.g.
[
12
]
).
Furthermore,
Theorem

4.2
also adds that
V_{a}=V_{Q}
for some
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
. Combining these remarks with
Theorem

3.2
, we deduce the following corollary.

Corollary 4.3
.

The available storage of a passive LTV system of the form (
1
) is a quadratic form
V_{a}=V_{Q}
for some
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
and is minimal among all storage functions of (
1
), in the sense that
V_{a}(t_{0},x_{0})\leq V(t_{0},x_{0})
holds for every storage function
V:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

In particular, the following statement obviously holds.

Corollary 4.4
.

Every passive LTV system of the form (
1
) has a quadratic storage function
V_{Q}
for some
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
.

We stress that
Corollary

4.4
is an extremely relevant result, since it allows us to restrict the search of storage functions to the relatively contained class of quadratic forms.

5
Null space decomposition

By
null space decomposition
of a generic matrix function
B:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}^{m,n}
we understand a factorization of the form

B(t)={Z(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}\begin{bmatrix}B_{11}(t)&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}V(t),

(9)

where
B_{11}(t)
is an invertible matrix for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, and
Z:\mathbb{T}\to\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{C})
and
V:\mathbb{T}\to\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
are pointwise invertible matrix functions, often pointwise unitary.
When
B
is pointwise Hermitian, we can constrict ourselves to the case
Z=V
, since from (
9
) we deduce
BV_{2}=BZ_{2}=0
where
Z=[Z_{1},Z_{2}]
and
V=[V_{1},V_{2}]
, and therefore
{V}^{\mathrm{H}}BV
is also a null space decomposition for
B
.

In this section, we are interested in constructing a null space decomposition for the matrix function
Q
that induces a quadratic storage function.
In applications,
V
typically represents a time-varying change of variables, thus it is required to have a certain degree of regularity, which in our case would be
V\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
.
However, the construction of a null space decomposition (see e.g.
[
9
,
13
]
) in the general case is analytically hard and requires quite strong assumptions:
Q
would have to be as regular as
V
, and crucially it would have to be of constant rank.
This is in contrast with the increased generality that we achieved in
Section

3
, where
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
is not even required to be continuous, and no explicit restriction is taken on its rank.

Nevertheless, we will prove that the restrictive assumptions from
[
9
,
13
]
can be removed in the cases that we are interested in.
We start by showing that this can be done in the case of weakly decreasing Hermitian matrix functions.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
be a weakly monotonically decreasing matrix function.
Then the following statements hold:

1.

For every
t_{-1},t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
with
t_{-1}<t_{0}<t_{1}
we have

\ker\big(Q(t_{-1})\big)\subseteq\ker\left(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q(t)\right)\subseteq\ker\big(Q(t_{0})\big)\subseteq\ker\left(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}Q(t)\right)\subseteq\ker\big(Q(t_{1})\big).

(10)

2.

For every
t_{-1},t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
with
t_{-1}\leq t_{0}\leq t_{1}
we have

\rank\big(Q(t_{-1})\big)\geq\rank\left(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q(t)\right)\geq\rank\big(Q(t_{0})\big)\geq\rank\left(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}Q(t)\right)\geq\rank\big(Q(t_{1})\big).

(11)

In particular,
r\coloneqq\rank\circ\,Q:\mathbb{T}\to\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
is weakly decreasing.

3.

There exists
V\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\widetilde{Q}\coloneqq{V}^{\mathrm{H}}QV\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
is of the form

\widetilde{Q}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{Q}_{11}(t)&0\\
0&0_{n-r(t)}\end{bmatrix}

(12a)

and satisfies

\lim_{s\to t^{\pm}}\widetilde{Q}(s)=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{Q}_{11}^{\pm}(t)&0\\
0&0_{n-r_{\pm}(t)}\end{bmatrix},

(12b)

where
\widetilde{Q}_{11}(t)\in\mathbf{S}^{r(t)}_{++}(\mathbb{C})
and
\widetilde{Q}_{11}^{\pm}(t)\in\mathbf{S}^{r_{\pm}(t)}_{++}(\mathbb{C})
with
r_{\pm}(t)\coloneqq\lim_{s\to t^{\pm}}r(t)

for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

1.

We show the four inclusions separately.
If
x\in\ker(Q(t_{-1}))
, then

0\leq{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)x\leq{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t_{-1})x=0\implies{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)x=0\implies Q(t)x=0

for all
t\geq t_{-1}
. Thus,
(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q(t))x=\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}(Q(t)x)=0
, i.e.,
x\in\ker(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q(t))
.

If
x\in\ker(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q(t))
, then since
{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t_{0})x\leq{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)x
holds for every
t\leq t_{0}
, we obtain in the limit

0\leq{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t_{0})x\leq\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}\big({x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)x\big)={x}^{\mathrm{H}}\left(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}Q(t)\right)x=0\implies Q(t_{0})x=0,

i.e.,
x\in\ker(Q(t_{0}))
.

For
x\in\ker(Q(t_{0}))
we have that for all
t\geq t_{0}
,

0\leq{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)x\leq{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t_{0})x=0\implies Q(t)x=0,

and therefore
(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}Q(t))x=\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}(Q(t)x)=0
, i.e.,
x\in\ker(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}Q(t))
.

Finally, let
x\in\ker(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}Q(t))
. For every
t\leq t_{1}
we have
{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t_{1})x\leq{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)x
, and thus

0\leq{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t_{1})x\leq\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}\big({x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)x\big)={x}^{\mathrm{H}}\left(\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{+}}Q(t)\right)x=0\implies Q(t_{1})x=0,

i.e.,
x\in\ker(Q(t_{1}))
.

2.

Since
\rank(Q(t))=n-\dim(\ker(Q(t)))
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, we deduce immediately (
11
) from (
10
).
In particular, we have
\rank(Q(t_{0}))\geq\rank(Q(t_{1}))
whenever
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
, i.e.,
r=\rank\circ\,Q
is a weakly decreasing map.

3.

Because of
2
., the rank of
Q
drops only at
K\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
points
t_{1},\ldots,t_{K}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{1}<\ldots<t_{K}
.
Let us consider any two points
t_{0},t_{K+1}\in\mathbb{T}
with
t_{0}<t_{1}
and
t_{K+1}>t_{K}
.
Then the chain of inclusions

\begin{split}\ker\big(Q(t_{0})\big)&\subseteq\ker\left(\lim_{t\to t_{1}^{-}}Q(t)\right)\subseteq\ker\big(Q(t_{1})\big)\subseteq\ker\left(\lim_{t\to t_{1}^{+}}Q(t)\right)\\
&\subseteq\ker\left(\lim_{t\to t_{2}^{-}}Q(t)\right)\subseteq\ldots\subseteq\ker\left(\lim_{t\to t_{K}^{+}}Q(t)\right)\subseteq\ker\big(Q(t_{K+1})\big)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}\end{split}

holds, because of (
10
).

We then construct iteratively a sequence
\mathcal{B}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{1}^{-}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{2}^{-}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{K}^{+}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{K+1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
of sets of linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, such that
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is a basis of
\ker(Q(t_{k}))
for
k=0,\ldots,K+1
,
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{\pm}
is a basis of
\ker(\lim_{t\to t_{k}^{\pm}}Q(t))
for
k=1,\ldots,K
, and
\mathcal{B}
is a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
In particular, let
\mathcal{B}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
be such that
\mathcal{B}_{k}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{j_{k}}\}
for
k=0,\ldots,K+1
and
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{\pm}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{j_{k}^{\pm}}\}
for
k=1,\ldots,K
, for appropriate
j_{k}
and
j_{k}^{\pm}
.
The matrix
V\coloneqq[v_{n},\ldots,v_{1}]\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
then clearly satisfies the desired properties. ∎

It holds that

\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{Q}_{11}(t)&0\\

Applying a pointwise Gram-Schmidt procedure to the columns of

Remark 5.5
.

We stress that, since the proofs of
Theorem

5.1
and
Theorem

5.4
are constructive, these null space decompositions can in principle be numerically computed (see also
Corollary

5.2
). In the case of a general weakly decreasing
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
, it might be difficult to determine the points
t_{1},\ldots,t_{K}
at which
Q
decreases its rank without prior knowledge, although they could still be approximated using rank evaluations.
In the case of the
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
inducing a quadratic storage function, the computation of the fundamental solution matrix
X\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
is also necessary.

We illustrate the subtle properties of
Theorem

5.4
with an example.

Example 5.6
.

Consider the one-dimensional LTV system

\dot{x}(t)=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }t\leq 0,\\
-\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}x&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(13)

in the time interval
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}
.
Note that the solutions of (
13
) are of the form
x(t)=X(t)x_{0}
with
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
and

X(t)=\begin{cases}1&\text{for }t\leq 0,\\
\frac{1}{1+t^{2}}&\text{for }t>0.\end{cases}

Define
Q:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\ t\mapsto 1+t^{2}
, which is positive and continuously differentiable, and therefore
Q\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbf{S}^{1}_{++}(\mathbb{C}))
with
\dot{Q}(t)=2t
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
The storage function defined by
Q
has the form

V_{Q}\big(t,x(t)\big)=\frac{1}{2}(1+t^{2})x(t)^{2}=\begin{cases}\frac{(1+t^{2})}{2}x_{0}^{2}&\text{for }t\leq 0,\\
\frac{1}{2(1+t^{2})}x_{0}^{2}&\text{for }t>0\end{cases}

along every solution
x
of (
13
).
Since the system (
13
) has no input, and
V_{Q}
is decreasing along every solution, we conclude that
V_{Q}
is in fact a storage function and the system (
13
) is passive.

Because of
Theorem

5.4
, we expect to be able to find
V\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\operatorname{GL}_{1}(\mathbb{C}))
such that
\widetilde{Q}\coloneqq{V}^{\mathrm{H}}QV
is weakly decreasing.
In fact, by choosing
V=X
we obtain

\widetilde{Q}(t)={V(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)V(t)=\begin{cases}1+t^{2}&\text{for }t\leq 0,\\
\frac{1}{1+t^{2}}&\text{for }t<0,\end{cases}

and thus
\widetilde{Q}
is decreasing.
However, we cannot find a pointwise unitary
U\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\operatorname{GL}_{1}(\mathbb{C})))
that satisfies the same property. If there were such a
U
, then
\widehat{Q}\coloneqq{U}^{\mathrm{H}}QU\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbf{S}^{1}_{++}(\mathbb{C}))
would be weakly decreasing, but it would also satisfy
\lVert\widehat{Q}(t)\rVert=\lVert{U(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t)U(t)\rVert=\lVert Q(t)\rVert=1+t^{2}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\widehat{Q}(t)>0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we necessarily have
Q=\widehat{Q}
, and therefore
\widehat{Q}
cannot be weakly decreasing.

On the one hand, we observe that
Theorem

5.1
applies to all weakly decreasing pointwise Hermitian positive semidefinite matrix functions, regardless of their possible connection to a control system. Moreover, while
Theorem

5.4
requires the involvement of an LTV system, a more careful analysis of its proof reveals that we only need the associated homogeneous differential equation
\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)
(or equivalently the closed system obtained by setting
u\equiv 0
) to be passive with storage function
V_{Q}
.

On the other hand, we can actually show that a pointwise Hermitian positive semidefinite matrix function only admits such a null space decomposition if there exists a passive LTV system for which it is a storage function.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
Q:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
. Then there exists an LTV system (
1
) for which
V_{Q}
as in (
3
) is a storage function if and only if there exists
V\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
such that
\widetilde{Q}\coloneqq{V}^{\mathrm{H}}QV
is weakly decreasing.

Proof.

Because of
Theorem

5.4
, it is clear that the existence of such a
V
is a necessary condition.
Suppose now that such
V
exists, and consider the stationary system
\dot{\widetilde{x}}(t)=0
, for which
\widetilde{Q}
as in (
12a
) trivially defines a storage function
V_{\widetilde{Q}}
.
The change of variables
x(t)=V(t)\widetilde{x}(t)
then leads us to another passive system, which has the form
\dot{x}(t)=\dot{V}(t)V(t)^{-1}x(t)
and storage function
V_{{V}^{-\mathrm{H}}\widetilde{Q}V}=V_{Q}
.
∎

6
A necessary condition on the kernel of
Q

We aim to provide a necessary condition for the kernel of the
Q
matrix function defining quadratic storage functions of passive LTV systems, generalizing
[
5
, Proposition 11]
. For this, we start by proving the following two lemmas.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
be such that
\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0
holds for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
. Then
f(t)\geq 0
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{T}_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
for
N\in\mathbb{N}
be any sequence of compact intervals such that
\mathbb{T}_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{N+1}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{T}=\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{T}_{N}
, let
E\coloneqq\{t\in\mathbb{T}\mid f(t)<0\}
and let
E_{N}\coloneqq E\cap\mathbb{T}_{N}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let us fix any
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
E_{N}
is measurable, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists an open set
\Omega_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
E_{N}\subseteq\Omega_{n}
and
\lvert\Omega_{n}\setminus E_{N}\rvert<\frac{1}{n}
. We assume without loss of generality that
\Omega_{n+1}\subseteq\Omega_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, up to replacing
\Omega_{n+1}
with
\Omega_{n}\cap\Omega_{n+1}
.
For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the set
\Omega_{n}
is open, and therefore there are at most countably many disjoint intervals
I_{n,k}
such that
\Omega_{n}=\bigcup_{k}I_{n,k}
. In particular, we have that

\int_{\Omega_{n}}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{k}\int_{I_{n,k}}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq 0

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that

\int_{E_{N}}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\Omega_{n}}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{\Omega_{n}\setminus E_{N}}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq-\int_{\Omega_{n}\setminus E_{N}}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

and, therefore,

\int_{E_{N}}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq-\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega_{n}\setminus E_{N}}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=0,

because of the dominated convergence theorem. Since
f<0
on
E_{N}
, we conclude that necessarily
\lvert E_{N}\rvert=0
.

Thus, since
E=\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}E_{N}
, we deduce that

\lvert E\rvert=\left\lvert\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}E_{N}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\lvert E_{N}\rvert=0,

thus
f\geq 0
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
B\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n,m})
be such that
\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}B(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=0
for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
. Then
B(t)=0
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

If
B=[b_{ij}]\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R}^{2n,2m})
, then
b_{ij}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
satisfies
\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}b_{ij}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=0
for all
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
and all
i,j
. In particular, by applying
Lemma

6.1
to both
b_{ij}
and
-b_{ij}
, we obtain
b_{ij}(t)=0
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
, for all
i,j
.
We conclude that also
B(t)=0
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
∎

We can now prove a necessary condition for the kernel of the induced matrix function of a quadratic storage function of a passive LTV system.

Theorem 6.3
.

Suppose that
V_{Q}
as in (
3
) with
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
is a storage function for a passive LTV system (
1
).
Then

\ker\big(Q(t)\big)\subseteq\ker\big(Q(t)A(t)+\dot{Q}(t)\big)\cap\ker\big(C(t)\big)

(14)

holds for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Theorem

5.4
implies that the rank of
Q(t)
decreases at only a finite number of time instances.
In particular, we can write
\mathbb{T}
as the disjoint union of finitely many open intervals where
Q(t)
has constant rank, plus the points where the rank drops, which form a set of measure zero.
It is then sufficient to prove the statement of this lemma under the assumption that
Q
has constant rank on
\mathbb{T}
.

Let
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}
, let
V_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n,r}
be a constant matrix whose columns form a basis of
\ker(Q(t_{0}))
, let
X\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
be the fundamental solution matrix associated to
\dot{x}=Ax
and
t_{0}
, let
V\coloneqq XV_{0}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n,r})
, and let
Q^{X}\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbf{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
be defined as in
Lemma

3.1
.
Because of the null space decomposition in
Theorem

5.1
and of the constant rank assumption,

\ker(Q(t_{0}))=\ker(Q^{X}(t_{0}))=\ker(Q^{X}(t))
holds for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
. Therefore
Q(t)V(t)=Q(t)X(t)V_{0}={X(t)}^{-\mathrm{H}}Q^{X}(t)V_{0}=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
On the one hand, the columns of
V(t)
form a basis of
\ker(Q(t))
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
On the other hand, since the classical time derivative of
Q
exists a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, we deduce that

\big(Q(t)A(t)+\dot{Q}(t)\big)V(t)=Q(t)\dot{V}(t)+\dot{Q}(t)V(t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(QV)(t)=0

for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
Thus it follows that
\ker(Q(t))\subseteq\ker(Q(t)A(t)+\dot{Q}(t))
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Suppose now for the sake of contradiction that it is not true that
\ker(Q(t))\subseteq\ker(C(t))
, i.e., that it is not true that
C(t)V(t)=0
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
Due to
Lemma

6.2
there exist
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{T},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
such that
\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}C(t)V(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\neq 0
, and therefore there exist
u_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
v_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
such that

\alpha\coloneqq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{u_{0}}^{\mathrm{H}}C(t)V(t)v_{0}\,\mathrm{d}t={u_{0}}^{\mathrm{H}}\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}C(t)V(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)v_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

Let us now consider for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
the state solution
x_{\lambda}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
of (
1
), uniquely determined by the initial condition
x_{\lambda}(t_{0})=\lambda\overline{\alpha}V_{0}v_{0}
and the constant input
u\equiv u_{0}
, i.e.,

x_{\lambda}(t)=\lambda\overline{\alpha}V(t)v_{0}+b_{u}(t),\qquad b_{u}(t)\coloneqq X(t)\int_{t_{0}}^{t}X(s)^{-1}B(s)u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, where notably
b_{u}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
does not depend on
\lambda
, and
b_{u}(0)=0
(see also
Theorem

A.7
).
On the one hand, the difference of the storage function between
t_{0}
and
t_{1}
is

\displaystyle V_{Q}\big(t_{1},x_{\lambda}(t_{1})\big)-V_{Q}\big(t_{0},x_{\lambda}(t_{0})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\big(\lambda\overline{\alpha}V(t_{1})v_{0}+b_{u}(t_{1})\big)}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t_{1})\big(\lambda\overline{\alpha}V(t_{1})v_{0}+b_{u}(t_{1})\big)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}{v_{0}}^{\mathrm{H}}{V_{0}}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t_{0})V_{0}v_{0}=\frac{1}{2}{b_{u}(t_{1})}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t_{1})b_{u}(t_{1}),

in particular, it does not depend on
\lambda
.
On the other hand, the supply function is

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({y(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({u(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}C(t)x_{\lambda}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\big({u(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}D(t)u(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\realPart\left(\overline{\alpha}\lambda\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{u_{0}}^{\mathrm{H}}C(t)V(t)v_{0}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\realPart\left({u(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}C(t)b_{u}(t)+{u(t)}^{\mathrm{H}}D(t)u(t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\realPart(\lambda\alpha\overline{\alpha})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}c_{u}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\lambda\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}c_{u}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
c_{u}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
does not depend on
\lambda
.
Therefore, the dissipation inequality

\frac{1}{2}{b_{u}(t_{1})}^{\mathrm{H}}Q(t_{1})b_{u}(t_{1})\leq\lambda\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}c_{u}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

must hold for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. However, this inequality is false for arbitrarily large and negative
\lambda
which is a contradiction and we conclude that
\ker(Q(t))\subseteq\ker(C(t))
holds for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
∎

We conclude this section by presenting an example where the properties that we identified in this paper are relevant.

Example 6.4
.

Let us consider a mass-spring-damper system with time-varying stiffness on the time interval
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}
:

m\ddot{z}(t)=-k(t)z(t)-b\dot{z}(t)+u(t),

(15)

where
z\in\mathbb{R}
is the displacement of the mass,
k\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
with
k\geq 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}
is the time-varying spring stiffness,
b>0
is the damping factor, and the input
u\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
represents any external force.
By using the displacement
z\in\mathbb{R}
and the momentum
p=m\dot{z}
as variables, we can rewrite (
15
) as the first-order ordinary differential equation

\begin{split}\dot{z}(t)&=\frac{1}{m}p(t),\\
\dot{p}(t)&=-k(t)z(t)-\frac{b}{m}p(t)+u(t).\end{split}

By adding the measurement of the velocity
y=\dot{z}=\frac{p}{m}
as our output, we obtain then the LTV system

\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{z}(t)\\
\dot{p}(t)\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{m}\\
-k(t)&-\frac{b}{m}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}z(t)\\
p(t)\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}u(t),\\
y&=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{m}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}z(t)\\
p(t)\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

(16)

which has a unique solution
x=(z,p)\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{2})
for every initial condition
x(0)=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and input
u\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.

A natural choice of storage function for (
16
) would be its total energy, given by the sum of potential and kinetic energy, i.e.,

V(t,z,p)=\frac{1}{2}k(t)z^{2}+\frac{1}{2m}p^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}z\\
p\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}k(t)&0\\
0&\frac{1}{m}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}z\\
p\end{bmatrix}.

Equivalently, by defining the matrix function
Q=\operatorname{diag}(k,m):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbf{S}^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{C})
, we can write
V=V_{Q}
.

Suppose for now that
k\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
. We observe that, for every state-input-output solution
(x,u,y)
of (
16
) and every
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
, it holds that

\displaystyle V_{Q}\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)-V_{Q}\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}V_{Q}\big(t,x(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}{x}^{\mathrm{H}}\dot{Q}x+{x}^{\mathrm{H}}Q\dot{x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\dot{k}z^{2}+kz\dot{z}+\frac{1}{m}p\dot{p}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\dot{k}z^{2}-b\frac{p^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}yu\,\mathrm{d}t,

Even under these additional regularity assumptions, it is evident that
V_{Q}
is a storage function if and only if
\dot{k}\leq 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}
. In fact, because of
Lemma

6.1
,
V_{Q}
is a storage function if and only if
\frac{1}{2}\dot{k}z^{2}\leq b\frac{p^{2}}{m^{2}}
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}
along every solution.
Then, since the constant state trajectory
x\equiv[1,0]^{\top}
is a solution for
u=k
, we obtain
\frac{1}{2}\dot{k}\leq 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}
.

Let us now assume that
k
is decreasing due to wear and tear, until a certain time point where the spring breaks and
k
is set to zero. More precisely, consider the stiffness defined as

k(t)=\begin{cases}1&\text{for }t<0,\\
1-\frac{2}{3}t&\text{for }0\leq t<1,\\
0&\text{for }t\geq 1,\end{cases}

which clearly satisfies
k\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
and
k(t)\geq 0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
In fact, we also have
k\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
, since it can be split into
k=k_{a}+k_{s}
with

k_{a}(t)=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }t<0,\\
-\frac{2}{3}t&\text{for }0\leq t<1,\\
-\frac{2}{3}&\text{for }t\geq 1,\end{cases}\qquad k_{s}(t)=\begin{cases}1&\text{for }t<1,\\
\frac{2}{3}&\text{for }t\geq 1,\end{cases}\qquad\dot{k}(t)=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }t<0,\\
-\frac{2}{3}&\text{for }0<t<1,\\
0&\text{for }t>0,\end{cases}

in particular
k_{a}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
, and
k_{s}\in\BV_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
is the weakly decreasing singular part of
k
.
Since
m>0
is constant, we deduce immediately that
Q\in\AUC_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbf{S}^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
, thus
V_{Q}
is a suitable storage function candidate.
Note that
Q\notin W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbf{S}^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{C}))
, since
Q
is not even continuous.
Furthermore,
Q
admits a null space decomposition that can be obtained simply by swapping the two variables, i.e.,

U=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\text{ \quad which implies \quad}U^{\top}Q(t)U=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{m}&0\\
0&k(t)\end{bmatrix},

since
k(t)>0
for
t<1
and
k(t)=0
for
t\geq 1
. In particular, note that
Q(t)
has rank two for
t<1
, rank one for
t\geq 1
.

Moreover, we verify that the necessary kernel condition from
Theorem

6.3
is satisfied.
In fact, from the definition of
k
and the structure of the equation coefficients we have

\displaystyle\ker\big(Q(t)\big)=\begin{cases}\{0\}&\text{for }t<1,\\
\{(z,0)\mid z\in\mathbb{R}\}&\text{for }t>1,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\ker\big(Q(t)A(t)+\dot{Q}(t)\big)=\begin{cases}\{0\}&\text{for }t<1,\\
\{(z,0)\mid z\in\mathbb{R}\}&\text{for }t>1,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\ker\big(C(t)\big)=\{(z,0)\mid z\in\mathbb{R}\}\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R},

which indeed satisfy (
14
).

Finally, we show that
V_{Q}
is in fact a storage function for (
16
) for this choice of
k
.
Let
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\ t_{0}\leq t_{1}
; we distinguish three cases:

(
t_{1}<1
)

Since
k|_{(-\infty,1)}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((-\infty,1),\mathbb{R})
, we deduce like before that

V_{Q}\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)-V_{Q}\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\dot{k}z^{2}-b\frac{p^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}yu\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}yu\,\mathrm{d}t.

(
t_{0}\geq 1
)

Since
k|_{(1,\infty)}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((1,\infty),\mathbb{R})
, we deduce again that

V_{Q}\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)-V_{Q}\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}b\frac{p^{2}}{m^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}yu\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}yu\,\mathrm{d}t.

(
t_{0}<1\leq t_{1}
)

For every
\varepsilon>0
smaller than
1-t_{0}
we write

V_{Q}\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)-V_{Q}\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)=V_{Q}\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)-V_{Q}\big(1-\varepsilon,x(1-\varepsilon)\big)\\
+V_{Q}\big(1-\varepsilon,x(1-\varepsilon)\big)-V_{Q}\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big).

Since
1-\varepsilon<1
, we have

V_{Q}\big(1-\varepsilon,x(1-\varepsilon)\big)-V_{Q}\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{1-\varepsilon}y(t)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
t_{1}\geq 1
, we also have

\displaystyle V_{Q}\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)-V_{Q}\big(1-\varepsilon,x(1-\varepsilon)\big)=\frac{1}{2m}\big(p(t_{1})^{2}-p(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\big)-\frac{1}{2}k(1-\varepsilon)z(1-\varepsilon)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{m}\int_{1-\varepsilon}^{t_{1}}p(t)\dot{p}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{6}(1+2\varepsilon)z(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m}\int_{1-\varepsilon}^{t_{1}}p(t)\left(-k(t)z(t)-\frac{b}{m}p(t)+u(t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\leq-\frac{1}{m}\int_{1-\varepsilon}^{1}k(t)p(t)z(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{1-\varepsilon}^{t_{1}}y(t)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Combining the two terms, we deduce that

V_{Q}\big(t_{1},x(t_{1})\big)-V_{Q}\big(t_{0},x(t_{0})\big)\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}y(t)u(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{m}\int_{1-\varepsilon}^{1}k(t)p(t)z(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

holds for all
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.
Since the second integral converges to zero for
\varepsilon\to 0
, we conclude that once again the dissipation inequality holds.

Since these three cases are exhaustive, we conclude that (
16
) with this
k
is indeed passive, and the total energy
V_{Q}
is a storage function for the system.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, the properties of quadratic storage functions for linear time-varying systems with very mild regularity assumptions have been studied extensively.
The focus on quadratic storage functions has been justified by showing that the existence of any storage function implies the existence of a quadratic one, for example the available storage of the system.
This simplifies the notably very hard problem of determining a storage function, since the class of functions to consider is greatly simplified.
We have shown that their defining matrix functions
Q
have to be at least locally absolutely upper semicontinuous, in the extended sense given by the Loewner partial order. While storage functions are often assumed to be quite regular, this is a necessary step to be able to study the passivity of systems with discontinuous coefficients.

A null space decomposition for
Q
under state transformations has been proven to always exist, unlike in the case of general matrix functions, where stronger regularity assumptions are necessary
[
9
,
13
]
.
This decomposition delivers a better understanding on the structure of
Q
, in particular showing that the kernel of
Q
cannot reduce dimension in time. It also allows us to extend a necessary condition on the kernel of
Q
, previously known for linear time-invariant port-Hamiltonian systems
[
5
]
, to the case of passive linear time-varying systems.

Many of these properties will be fundamental tools in a followup work, where the differences and connections between dissipativity concepts for linear time-varying systems are analyzed.
Furthermore, the deeper understanding of time-varying storage functions achieved in this paper has the potential of being exploited to develop new techniques to determine Lyapunov functions and novel numerical methods.

The following matrix generalization of the Hölder inequality is central in our choice of function spaces.

Theorem A.1

(Generalized Hölder inequality)
.

Let
F\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{l,m})
and
G\in L^{q}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{m,n})
, where
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
and
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{r}
. Then
FG\in L^{r}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{l,n})
with

\lVert FG\rVert_{L^{r}}\leq\lVert F\rVert_{L^{p}}\lVert G\rVert_{L^{q}}
.

Proof.

By definition we have

\lVert FG\rVert_{L^{r}}=\big\lVert\lVert FG\rVert_{2}\big\rVert_{L^{r}}\leq\big\lVert\lVert F\rVert_{2}\lVert G\rVert_{2}\big\rVert_{L^{r}}\leq\big\lVert\lVert F\rVert_{2}\big\rVert_{L^{p}}\big\lVert\lVert G\rVert_{2}\big\rVert_{L^{q}}=\lVert F\rVert_{L^{p}}\lVert G\rVert_{L^{q}},

where we applied the better known scalar generalized Hölder inequality to
\lVert F\rVert_{2}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
and
\lVert G\rVert_{2}\in L^{q}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{R})
, see e.g.
[
1
, Chapter 4, Remark 2]

∎

x(t_{0})=x_{0}
can be expressed as
x(t)=X(t)x_{0}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

3.

X(t)
is invertible for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
. In particular
X^{-1}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n,n})
.

4.

For every
(t_{0},x_{0})\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
the unique solution
x\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
of the inhomogeneous differential equation (
17
) that satisfies the initial condition
x(t_{0})=x_{0}
, can be expressed as

x(t)=X(t)\left(x_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}X^{-1}(s)b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

(18)

for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

1.

The existence and uniqueness of the solution
X\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n,n})
follows immediately from
Theorem

A.2
, reinterpreting
\dot{X}=AX
as
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{vec}(X)=(I_{n}\otimes A(t))\mathrm{vec}(X)
under vectorization, where
\otimes
represents the Kronecker product, and applying
Theorem

A.2
.

2.

Let
x(t)\coloneqq X(t)x_{0}
. Then
\dot{x}(t)=\dot{X}(t)x_{0}=A(t)X(t)x_{0}=A(t)x(t)
and
x(t_{0})=A(t_{0})x_{0}=x_{0}
.

3.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that there exists
t_{1}\in\mathbb{T}
for which
X(t_{1})
is singular, i.e. there is
x_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that
X(t_{1})x_{1}=0
.
Because of
2
, both
x(t)\coloneqq X(t)x_{1}
and
\widetilde{x}(t)\equiv 0
are solutions of
\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)
satisfying
x(t_{1})=\widetilde{x}(t_{1})=0
.
It follows that
x=\widetilde{x}
, and therefore
x_{1}=X(t_{0})x_{1}=x(t_{0})=\widetilde{x}(t_{0})=0
, in contradiction with
x_{1}\neq 0
.
Therefore,
X(t)
is invertible for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, and by
Lemma

A.6
it follows that
X^{-1}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
.

4.

Define

f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}^{n},\qquad t\mapsto\int_{t_{0}}^{t}X^{-1}(s)b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

and
\widetilde{x}\coloneqq x-Xf
.
By the generalized Hölder inequality (
Theorem

A.1
),
X^{-1}b\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},C^{n})
. Thus,
f\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
with
\dot{f}=X^{-1}b
, by
Lemma

A.5
.
Since
W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C})
is an algebra, we get from
X\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
that
\widetilde{x}=x-Xf\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{T},\mathbb{C}^{n})
.
Furthermore, we obtain that

\dot{\widetilde{x}}=\dot{x}-\dot{X}f-X\dot{f}=Ax+b-AXf-XX^{-1}b=A(x-Xf)=A\widetilde{x}

and
\widetilde{x}(t_{0})=x_{0}
. Thus
\widetilde{x}(t)=X(t)x_{0}
for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
, because of
2
.
We conclude that

x(t)=\widetilde{x}(t)+X(t)f(t)=X(t)x_{0}+X(t)\int_{t_{0}}^{t}X^{-1}(s)b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=X(t)\left(x_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}X^{-1}(s)b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

holds for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
. ∎
