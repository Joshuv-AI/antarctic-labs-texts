Acute necrotising pancreatitis in a patient with Behçet’s disease
Authors: Abdullah Anwar Albadi, Amal Alharthi, Hezab Alrayes, Marzouq Albadi, Ali alrehaily, Abdulelah Alqwizani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryA man in his 40s of Middle Eastern descent, with no prior medical history, presented with severe abdominal pain that had progressed over 2 days. He was diagnosed with acute necrotising pancreatitis, multifocal venous thromboembolism and acute kidney injury, requiring admission to the intensive care unit. After exclusion of common causes of pancreatitis, a systemic inflammatory or vasculitic process was suspected. Treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by a tapering course of oral prednisone, led to clinical stabilisation. Given the clinical evolution and the patient’s ethnic background, Behçet’s disease was strongly suspected, and therapy with colchicine, adalimumab and mycophenolate mofetil was initiated. The patient improved significantly and was discharged with plans for outpatient rheumatology follow-up. This case highlights a rare but serious gastrointestinal manifestation of Behçet’s disease and underscores the importance of early recognition and immunosuppressive management in systemic vasculitides.

Background

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory disorder of unknown aetiology that can affect blood vessels of all sizes, both venous and arterial. The highest prevalence is reported in countries along the ancient Silk Road, particularly in East Asia, the Middle East and the Mediterranean region. The disease affects men more frequently than women, with a peak incidence in the third to fourth decades of life.1

BD was initially described as a triad of recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers and relapsing uveitis. However, it is now recognised as a multisystem disorder that may involve the skin, joints, nervous system, cardiovascular system and gastrointestinal tract.2

Pancreatitis associated with BD is extremely rare and often under-recognised. For example, an autopsy series of 170 cases in Japan identified five previously undiagnosed cases of pancreatitis related to BD,3 which reflects the prevalence of BD-associated pancreatitis.

Here, we present a case of acute necrotising pancreatitis occurring as a manifestation of Behçet’s disease.

Case presentation

A man in his 40s, with no history of alcohol consumption or substance use, presented with severe epigastric pain radiating to the back. He reported intermittent abdominal pain over the preceding month, associated with a 5-kg weight loss, culminating in an acute exacerbation 2 days prior to hospital admission.

On presentation, his vital signs were blood pressure 141/94 mm Hg, heart rate 113 beats per minute and temperature 36.5°C. Physical examination revealed an oral ulcer, Cullen’s sign (peri-umbilical ecchymosis) and erythema nodosum on the left leg.

No ocular involvement was identified, as the patient exhibited neither anterior uveitis (iritis/iridocyclitis) features—such as periocular pain, conjunctival injection, photophobia, blurred vision or epiphora—nor posterior segment findings (choroiditis or retinitis), which typically manifest as painless visual disturbances including blurred vision, scotomas or floaters.

Laboratory testing showed markedly elevated serum lipase and amylase (approximately 20-fold above the upper limit of normal).

Differential diagnosis

The diagnosis of acute pancreatitis was established according to the American College of Gastroenterology criteria,4 requiring at least two of the following: (1) characteristic abdominal pain, (2) pancreatic enzyme elevation ≥3× the upper limit of normal and (3) characteristic findings on cross-sectional imaging. All three criteria were fulfilled.

The diagnosis of Behçet’s disease was considered according to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD),5 which employs a weighted scoring system: oral aphthosis, genital aphthosis and ocular lesions each score 2 points; skin, neurological and vascular manifestations each score 1 point; and a positive pathergy test contributes 1 additional point. A total score of ≥4 establishes a definite diagnosis of Behçet’s disease, while a score of 3 suggests probable disease

Outcome and follow-up

The patient improved clinically and was discharged in stable condition, with ongoing rheumatology follow-up.

Renal impairment, which was related to acute pancreatitis and critical illness, was improved.

1 month later, a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis showed near-complete resolution of pancreatic collections (figure 5). Serum pancreatic enzyme levels had normalised. Venous thromboembolisms have resolved.

Discussion

Gastrointestinal (GI) involvement in BD, often referred to as intestinal Behçet’s disease, is most commonly reported in regions with high BD prevalence such as East Asia and the Middle East, and is less frequent in Western populations. GI manifestations occur in approximately 10%–15% of patients, although reported prevalence varies among studies.6

Pancreatitis as a manifestation of BD is extremely rare, with only a limited number of case reports published worldwide. Reported cases include O’Duffy et al’s study (USA, 1971),7 presenting with epigastric pain and iridocyclitis; Thi Du Le Huong et al’ss study (France, 1992),8 with fever, aphthous ulcers, superficial thrombophlebitis and pseudofolliculitis; Backmund and Schomerus’s study (Italy, 1999),9 with oral/genital ulcers, erythema nodosum and pericardial effusion; Alkim et al’s study (Turkey, 2001),10 with chronic pancreatitis and aortic pseudoaneurysm; Ben Yaghlène et al’s study,11 with pseudoaneurysms of the coeliac trunk; Hum et al’s study (Canada, 2017),12 with recurrent pancreatitis associated with mucocutaneous disease; Mahmoud et al’s study (Egypt, 2021),13 with acute necrotising pancreatitis; and Chaabi et al’s study (Morocco, 2023).14

Reported cases span a wide age range (second to fifth decades of life) and presentations vary from life-threatening necrotising pancreatitis (as in our case and that of Mahmoud et al) to milder forms without end-organ damage.

In our patient, treatment with high-dose intravenous corticosteroids resulted in rapid clinical and biochemical improvement. Azathioprine is a conventional first-line immunosuppressant for systemic BD; however, it is also known to cause idiosyncratic pancreatitis within 2–4 weeks of initiation.15 In our case, azathioprine was discontinued after the patient developed leucopenia and was replaced with mycophenolate mofetil.

Mycophenolate mofetil may have a role as a second-line or adjunctive therapy in more serious organ involvement (ocular, CNS, vascular), especially in patients who do not tolerate or respond to conventional agents (azathioprine, cyclosporine, TNF inhibitors, etc.) as per a retrospective study by Kerim et al.16

Human leucocyte antigen-B51 is a well-recognised genetic risk factor for BD, although its role as a marker of disease severity has been suggested; this association remains controversial and not consistently demonstrated.17 However, due to low specificity and a variable prevalence among ethnicities, it is not considered a diagnostic criterion in the ICBD.5
