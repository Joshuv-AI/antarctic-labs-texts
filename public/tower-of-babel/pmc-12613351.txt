MicroRNA-142 improves IL1RAP CAR-T cell activity in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Kaito Harada, Dandan Zhao, Miso Park, Fang Chen, Yongfang Xu, Yi Zhang, Yile Zhou, Bea Parcutela, Yead Jewel, Ashlie Munoz, Idoroenyi Amanam, Le Xuan Truong Nguyen, Jianjun Chen, Lucy Ghoda, Stephen J. Forman, John C. Williams, Bin Zhang, Guido Marcucci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterleukin-1 receptor accessory protein (IL1RAP) is selectively expressed on both bulk blasts and leukemic stem cells (LSCs) in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), while its expression is virtually absent on normal hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), making it an appealing target for chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy.MethodsWe developed a novel IL1RAP-targeting CAR-T cells using a single-chain Fab (24scFab) fused to CD28 and CD3ζ costimulatory domains. CAR-T cells with a mutated IL1RAP-binding paratope were also generated as a control by introducing two point-mutations in the complementarity determining region (CDR) loops of the 24scFab domain. We tested the CAR-T cells in cell line-derived (CD) and patient-derived (PD) xenografts (X). To address persistence and activity of IL1RAP CAR-T cells, we then tested two approaches. First, we mutated two of the three immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) within the CD3ζ domain (i.e., IL1RAP-1XX CAR-T). Second, we co-administered a synthetic miR-142 mimic (M-miR-142), previously shown to enhance T cell antileukemic activity, with IL1RAP CAR-T cells to AML xenografted mice.ResultsIL1RAP CAR-T cells demonstrated a potent antileukemic activity in both AML CDX and PDX models. Target specificity was confirmed by the complete loss of function of IL1RAP-mutated CAR-T cells. IL1RAP-1XX CAR-T cells improved T cell persistence in vitro but failed to demonstrate therapeutic benefit compared with IL1RAP CAR-T cells in vivo. We previously reported that leukemic cell growth suppresses miR-142 biogenesis, thereby hindering the metabolic switch and impairing host T cell antileukemic activity; this was rescued by administration of M-miR-142. Thus, we hypothesized a similar impact of leukemic cells on CAR-T and that M-miR-142 treatment could rescue it and enhance the IL1RAP CAR-T cell antileukemic activity. We showed that both CDXs and PDXs receiving M-miR-142 and IL1RAP CAR-T lived significantly longer than those receiving scrambled oligonucleotide and IL1RAP CAR-T or mutated CAR-T controls (median survival of PDX: 78 vs 51 vs 24 days).ConclusionsWe have identified a potentially novel strategy to enhance CAR-T cell persistence and efficacy in AML by counteracting a leukemia-induced, microRNA-deficiency mediated mechanism of immune suppression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13045-025-01755-6.

Background

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a genetically heterogeneous hematopoietic malignancy characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of clonally derived immature blasts [1, 2]. Despite the development of novel therapeutics, the prognosis for most AML patients remains poor, with an estimated 5-year overall survival rate of approximately 30% [1]. A key contributor to treatment failure is the presence of leukemia stem cells (LSCs), a primitive subpopulation of cells responsible for the initiation and maintenance of AML [3]. LSCs exhibit inherent resistance to therapy and are considered the source of disease treatment-refractoriness. While allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) offers a potential cure, its clinical benefit is often limited by post-transplant complications and disease relapse [4]. Moreover, many patients are ineligible for transplant due to advanced age and/or comorbid conditions [4]. Nevertheless, the graft-versus-leukemia (GvL) effect [5], wherein donor immune cells target and eliminate LSCs, underscores the therapeutic potential of harnessing adoptive immunity to achieve a cure in AML.

Tumor antigen-targeted immunotherapy has shown promising results in cancer treatment [6]. In lymphoid neoplasms such as B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) [7] and B-cell lymphomas (BCL) [8], CD19 has emerged as a clinically actionable tumor-associated antigen. Due to the remarkable clinical efficacy observed, CD19-CD3 bispecific T-cell engagers [9] and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells [7] have received FDA approval and are now integral to the standard of care for patients with B-ALL and BCL. In contrast, immunotherapeutics for AML and other myeloid neoplasms remain largely investigational. Although various CAR-T cells and bispecific engager strategies targeting AML-associated antigens, such as FLT3 [10, 11], CD33 [12, 13], CD38 [14], IL-10R [15], CLL-1 [16], CD123 [17], NKG2D ligands [18], GM-CSF receptor [19], and MPL [20], have been tested in clinical trials, none have replicated the clinical success achieved with CD19-directed therapies and thus none have garnered FDA approval. One major challenge is the lack of antigen specificity: many surface markers expressed on AML blasts and LSCs are also present on normal hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) [21], leading to on-target, off-tumor toxicity. Further, antigen escape [22] and the AML immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [23, 24] can impair CAR-T cell activity, persistence and fitness. In addition, cytokines released from T cells appear to promote AML proliferation which could undermine the therapeutic effect of the CAR-T [25]. To address these challenges, various strategies are being explored [26].

IL1RAP is a co-receptor that enhances signaling of inflammatory cytokine (e.g.,IL-1, IL-33, and IL-36), or aberrant FLT3 and c-Kit receptor tyrosine kinases involved in AML growth [27, 28]. IL1RAP is overexpressed on AML blasts and LSCs while being minimally expressed on normal hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) [29–31], making it a highly selective target for AML-directed immunotherapy with reduced off-tumor toxicity. Targeting IL1RAP as tumor associated antigen (TAA) has shown promising results, including antileukemic effects with IL-1 receptor antagonists [32] and monoclonal antibodies [28, 33, 34]. Notably, we previously reported that an anti-IL1RAP/CD3 bispecific T-cell engager effectively eradicated LSCs in AML patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models [30]. Given that IL1RAP is a specific TAA for AML LSCs and more differentiated blasts, herein we leveraged our IL1RAP antibody library and utilized a CAR that included the IL1RAP-specific single-chain Fab (24scFab) and the CD28 and CD3ζ intracellular signaling domains [30] to generate IL1RAP-targeted CAR-T cells (hereafter called IL1RAP CAR-T). These cells showed strong in vivo antileukemic activity; however, over time, as observed in the clinic, they became exhausted leading to disease relapse.

We recently showed that TME-derived cytokine signaling (e.g., IL-6) markedly reduces miR-142 levels, impairing T-cell differentiation and driving exhaustion in blast crisis (BC) chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) [35]. Loss of miR-142 disrupts a critical metabolic switch from oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos) to glycolysis that occurs in T cells upon antigen stimulation, thereby blunting activation and antigen-specific responses of these cells [36]. Administration of a synthetic miR-142 mimic (M-miR-142) restored T cells’ metabolic fitness and antileukemic activity [35].

Thus, we hypothesize here that blast-driven cytokine signals may also decrease miR-142 levels in CAR-T cells, leading to their impaired fitness, accelerated exhaustion, and functional decline, as observed clinically in AML patients, and that supplementation with M-miR-142 could then enhance CAR-T persistence and therapeutic efficacy. Of note, although a direct mechanistic link between IL1RAP biology and miR-142 modulation has not been established, we used our IL1RAP CAR-T to test this hypothesis as a proof-of-concept of an approach that could be eventually generalized to other anti-AML CAR-T products.

Methods

AML cell lines

MOLM13, KG-1a, THP-1, HL60, Kasumi, MV4-11, K562, 293-T cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, USA) or Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10–20% fetal bovine serum (Gemini Bio, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA), in accordance with supplier’s description. Luciferase (Luci)/green fluorescent protein (GFP)-expressing MOLM13, THP-1, K562 were produced by transduction with the lentivirus expressing Luci/GFP and packaged with pMD2.G, psPAX2 plasmids, and GFP+ cells were selected and expanded.

Patient-derived AML cells and healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells

All primary AML samples (Supplementary Table 1) and healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were obtained from the Hematopoietic Tissue Biorepository of the City of Hope National Medical Center (COHNMC). Specimens collected from patients registered at COHNMC were consented to an Institutional Review Board approved protocol (IRB#18,067); healthy donor specimens were collected from participants who consented to COH IRB#06229 or from discarded cones used for platelet enrichment from the Amini Transfusion Medicine Center, COHNMC. Patient and healthy donor sample acquisition was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRB) at COHNMC, in accordance with an assurance filed with and approved by the Department of Health and Human Services and met all requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki. Primary AML samples were cultured in RPMI or IMDM medium containing 20% FBS. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Details of the luciferase expressing patient-derived AML cells have been described previously [37].

Generation of CAR-T cells

293-T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS. To generate IL1RAP CAR lentivirus, 293-T cells were transfected with transgene plasmids containing IL1RAP 24scFab [30] (Fig. 1A) and packaging plasmids (pCMV-Rev2, pCHGP-2, and pCMV-G) using polyethyleneimine (MilliporeSigma, USA) [17, 38]. Plasmids with the same backbone as the IL1RAP 24scFab [30] but with point mutations in two of the three immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) of the CD3ζ domain (IL1RAP-1XX) or with two point mutations introduced in the CDR of the IL1RAP-targeting Fab (IL1RAP-mut) were also used to generate IL1RAP-1XX or IL1RAP-mut CAR lentivirus, respectively (Fig. 1A). On the day following transfection, medium was replaced with fresh DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 0.6 mM sodium butyrate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and cells were cultured for 72 h. Subsequently, lentivirus-containing supernatant was harvested from culture and centrifuged at 6080 g overnight at 4 °C. The lentiviral pellet was resuspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) with 4% D-lactose (Avantor, USA) and titered. IL1RAP, IL1RAP-1XX and IL1RAP-mut CAR lentivirus transduction of primary human T cells was carried out as previously reported with some modifications [17, 38]. Briefly, CD3+ T cells were isolated from healthy donor PBMCs using EasySep™ human T-cell enrichment kit (Stem cell Technologies, USA) and CD62L+ T cells were enriched using anti-human CD62L microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, USA). CD62L+ CD3+ T cells were cultured in TheraPEAK X-VIVO 15 (Lonza, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 U/ml of recombinant human IL-2 (PeproTech, USA), and 0.5 ng/ml of recombinant human IL-15 (PeproTech, USA), and Dynabeads™ Human T-Expander CD3/CD28 (Gibco, USA) at the ratio of 1:2 (beads:cells) was added to the media on day 0. On the following day, protamine sulfate (final concentration, 25 ug/ml, Fresenius Kabi, USA) and lentiviral supernatant was added to the media at a multiplicity of infection (m.o.i.) of 1—4. Half of the media was exchanged every 2 days until day 8. The transduction efficiency was evaluated by staining the cells with PE-conjugated anti-human Fab antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) followed by flow cytometry analysis. After Dynabeads were removed on day 8, Fab-expressing CAR-T cells were enriched by staining with PE-conjugated anti-human Fab antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) followed by selection using EasySep™ Release Human PE Positive Selection Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, USA) on day 9. Thereafter, T cells were maintained and expanded in cytokine-containing TheraPEAK X-VIVO 15 until day 16–17. Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry analyses were performed on LSRFortessa™ X-20 Flow Cytometer equipped with the FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo (Tree Star, USA). Antibodies used in the experiments are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Antibody cocktails were prepared in staining buffer, consisting of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (Gibco, USA) and 2 mM EDTA (Invitrogen, USA), and incubated with cells for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were stained with DAPI (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) and DAPI + dead cells excluded from the analysis. To determine IL1RAP expression on AML cells or PBMC from healthy donors, the cells were incubated with Human TruStain FcX (BioLegend, USA) for 10 min, and then stained with PE-conjugated anti-IL1RAP antibody (R&D systems, USA). PE-conjugated isotype control IgG was used as negative control (BD Pharmingen, USA).

T cell activation analysis

After co-culture of CAR-T or UTD-T cells with or without Luci/GFP-expressing MOLM13, THP-1 or K562 at effector to target (E:T) ratio of 1:1 for 48 h, cells were stained with BV510-conjugated anti-CD3, Brilliant Violet 421-conjugated anti-CD4, APC-conjugated anti-CD69, APC-Cyanine7-conjugated anti-CD25 (all from BioLegend, USA), and PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-CD8 antibody (BD Pharmingen, USA). Expression of T cell activation markers CD25 and CD69 was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cell proliferation and cell cycling assay

CAR-T or UTD-T cells were labeled with CellTrace Violet (CTV, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and cultured with or without Luci/GFP-expressing MOLM13 or K562 at E: T ratio of 1:2 for 4 days. Cell proliferation was assessed by flow cytometry, and every generation of cells appears as a different peak with lower fluorescent intensity of Violet on a flow cytometry histogram plot. For cell cycling analysis, CAR-T or UTD-T cells cultured with Luci/GFP-expressing MOLM13 or K562 at E: T ratio of 1:1 for 48 h were harvested and stained with Far red LIVE/DEAD fixable dead cell stain kit (Invitrogen, USA) and BV510-conjugated anti-CD3 antibody (BioLegend, USA) for 30 min. Subsequently, cells were fixed and permeabilized using cytofix/cytoperm fixation and permeabilization solution (BD Bioscience, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and then stained with PE-conjugated anti-Ki-67 antibody (BD Pharmingen) and DAPI. Ki-67+DAPIhigh population represents cells in G2/S/M phases via flow cytometry analysis.

Cytokine production assay

CAR-T or UTD-T cells were cultured with or without Luci/GFP-expressing MOLM13, THP-1 or K562 at E:T ratio of 1:1 in RPMI1640 (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1X Protein Transport Inhibitor Cocktail (Invitrogen, USA) overnight. As positive control, CAR-T or UTD-T cells were cultured with 1X Cell Stimulation Cocktail (Invitrogen, USA). Cells were harvested and stained with Far Red LIVE/DEAD fixable dead cell stain kit (Invitrogen, USA) and BV510-conjugated anti-CD3 antibody (BioLegend, USA) for 30 min, and then fixed and permeabilized by cytofix/cytoperm fixation and permeabilization solution (BD Bioscience, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, cells were stained with antibodies against IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2 or granzyme-B (BioLegend, USA; Supplementary Table 2) and analyzed by flow cytometry. Cells treated with the cocktail of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) and ionomycin were used as a positive control.

Cell killing and apoptosis assays

In vitro antileukemic activity of CAR-T cells was analyzed by the co-culture of target cells, i.e., AML cell lines or primary MNCs or CD34 + cells, with effector CAR-T or UTD-T cells at various E:T ratios. Briefly, 2 × 104 PKH67 (PKH67 fluorescent cell linker kit, Sigma-Aldrich, USA)-labeled target cells and effector T cells at E:T ratios of 1:16 to 1:1 were seeded into 96-well plate. Cells were harvested in Annexin V binding buffer after 48 h and stained with Pacific Blue-conjugated Annexin V (BioLegend, USA) and 7-AAD (BD Pharmingen, USA), according to the manufacturers’ instructions, and cell viability was measured by flow cytometry. To test the toxicity of CAR-T cells to normal cells, 2 × 104 PKH67-labeled PBMC or CD34+ cells isolated from heathy donors and enriched with CD34 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, USA) were co-cultured with UTD-T or CAR-T cells at E:T ratio of 1:4 or 1:1 in 96-well plate and then the percentages of Annexin-V+ and 7-AAD+ apoptotic cells were normalized by subtracting the baseline apoptosis rate, i.e., percentages of Annexin-V+ and 7-AAD+ apoptotic cells in the absence of effector T cells.

Real time cell analysis (RTCA)

Real time cell-killing activity of CAR-T cells was analyzed using xCELLigence RTCA eSight platform (Agilent, USA). Specifically, 2 × 104 Luci/GFP-expressing MOLM13 or THP-1 AML target cells and CAR-T or UTD-T effector cells at E:T ratios of 1:4 and 4:1 were seeded in 96 well (flat bottom) plates, and live cell imaging in bright-field and green fluorescence channels at 10 × magnification were recorded every 60 min for 48–72 h. GFP+ green object counts per well were counted and data were analyzed using RTCA eSight software (Agilent, USA). Time lapse images were concatenated to produce video using 5 × 104 target AML cells and effector T cells at E:T ratio of 1:1.

miR-142 lentiviral transduction of human T cells

GFP-expressing miRZip anti-miR-142-3p/5p (CS940MZ-1, a custom order from System Biosciences, with H1 promoter for anti-miR-142-3p and EF1a promoter for GFP-T2A-Puro expression) and non-target control (cat: SI506A-1, from System Biosciences) lentiviruses were produced and used for transduction of human T cells. Briefly, human T cells were activated using Dynabeads™ Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (11131D, ThermoFisher) with a ratio of 3:1 in 24-well culture plate in the presence of 30 μ/ml IL-2. On the second day, cells were transferred to the RetroNectin-coated plate followed by the addition of lentivirus with a MOI of 3 and TransDux virus transduction reagent (System Biosciences). The plate was then centrifuged for 90 min at 1500 × g for spinoculation. GFP + cells were sorted as the cells with miR-142 knockdown (KD).

Seahorse assay

An Agilent Seahorse XF Sensor Cartridge was hydrated in Agilent Seahorse XF Calibrant at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator overnight. On the day of assay, each well of a Seahorse XF-96-well cell culture microplate was seeded with 200,000 CAR-T cells in 180 μl of Seahorse assay medium, consisting of basal XF media, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 20 mM glucose, with a pH of 7.4. Wells containing only Seahorse assay medium, but no cells were used as controls. The cell culture plate was then placed in a non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 1 h prior to the assay. Stressor mix (1 μM oligomycin and 1 μM FCCP) was subsequently loaded into the hydrated sensor cartridge. The sensor cartridge was then mounted onto the cell culture plate and loaded into the Seahorse XF96 Analyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA), and Agilent Seahorse XF Cell Energy Phenotype Tests were performed with simultaneous injection of oligomycin and FCCP.

Gene expression by Q-RT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using QIAzol™ Lysis Reagent and miRNeasy mini kit (both from Qiagen, USA). For mRNA expression, total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript III First-Strand Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and then Q-PCR was performed using TaqMan™ Fast Advanced Master Mix on the Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 7 Flex PCR system (Applied Biosystems; Supplementary Table 3) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Beta-2-microglobulin (β2M) was used as internal controls. For miRNA expression, reverse transcription using MultiScribe™ Reverse Transcriptase and Q-PCR analysis using Taqman assays (Applied Biosystems; Supplementary Table 3) was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNU44 and snoRNA234 were used as internal controls for human and mouse miRNA, respectively. Results are presented as log2-transformed ratio according to the 2–ΔCt method (ΔCt = Ct of target –Ct of reference gene). Each reaction was performed in triplicate.

Oligodeoxynucleotide design and synthesis

MiR-142-3p and −5p mimics (collectively called M-miR-142) were designed and synthesized as previously reported [35, 39]. The double-stranded sequence of miR-142 mimic was conjugated through the 5′end of the passenger strand to the 3′end of a single-stranded, partly phosphorothioated oligodeoxynucleotide (ODN) using a synthetic carbon linker to obtain M-miR-142. To ensure the maximum activity and to confer nuclease resistance to M-miR-142, the passenger strand of the miR-142 moiety was 2’-O-methyl modified at the 3′end. The construct was also conjugated with Cy3 to track the internalization of M-miR-142 into the cells by flow cytometry. These sequences were previously reported [35, 39].

In vivo AML models

To generate xenograft models, age and sex-matched 8–16-week-old NOD/Scid/Il-2rg (NSG) or NSG-SGM3 (NSGS) mice (The Jackson Laboratory, Stock No. 005557 and 013062, respectively) were used. Mice were intravenously transplanted with 1 × 106 or 0.5 × 106 of Luc/GFP-expressing MOLM13 or primary AML blasts and were then given one or two doses of 3.0 × 106 CAR-T or UTD-T cells at designated time points. Subsequently, in vivo bioluminescent imaging (BLI) of the mice to assess the Luc-expressing leukemia burden was performed weekly using Lago X-Spectral and analyzed with Aura imaging software (Spectral Instruments Imaging). Human CD45 + or GFP + leukemic cell engraftment rates in peripheral blood (PB) were analyzed by flow cytometry biweekly. To test whether the lack of miR-142 impacts T cell antileukemic activity in vivo, we co-transplanted MllPTD/wt/Flt3ITD/ITD AML [39–41] Lin-Sca-1 + c-Kit + (LSKs) with Mir142−/− or Mir142+/+ T cells obtained from syngeneic Mir142 KO and wt mice into NSG recipients. We also transplanted BM cells from MllPTD/wt/Flt3ITD/ITD AML mice into Mir142+/+ or Mir142−/− recipients, or into Mir142flox(f)/f Lck-cre + (i.e., miR-142 KO in T cells only) or Mir142f/f Lck-cre − (wt) recipients. Further, we co-transplanted human AML blasts with WT or miR-142 KD human T cells, generated by miRZip anti-miR-142 lentivirus transduction, into NSGS mice. Next, we treated a cohort of MllPTD/wt/Flt3ITD/ITD AML mice with M-miR-142 (a synthetic miR-142; 30 mg/kg, iv, 6x/week) or scrambled control (SCR) for 3 weeks. BM cells from the treated primary mice were transplanted into congenic CD45.1 recipient mice to evaluate post-treatment LSC burden. Finally, a cohort of AML PDX mice were generated and were given 106 human T cells and then treated with M-miR-142 (30 mg/kg, iv, 6x/week) or SCR for 3 weeks. BM cells from the treated primary mice were also transplanted into NSGS mice to evaluate post-treatment LSC burden. To test if M-miR-142 could also enhance the antileukemic activity of the IL1RAP CAR-T cells, we performed three experiments. First, we engrafted IL1RAP+ primary AML blasts collected from a patient with relapse/refractory (R/R) disease (AML1160) into NSG mice and treated them with one dose of IL1RAP CAR-T cells on day 7 (3 × 106/mouse) and M-miR-142 or SCR starting on day10 (30 mg/kg, iv, 6x/week, 3 weeks). Next, we engrafted Luci-expressing IL1RAP+ AML blasts from a patient with R/R AML (AML1757) into NSG mice and treated them with one dose of IL1RAP CAR-T cells on day 3 and with M-miR-142 starting on day 4 (30 mg/kg, iv, 6x/week, 3 weeks). To assess the potential direct activity of M-miR-142 on AML cells vs that on IL1RAP CAR-T, we treated a third AML PDX model from a patient with R/R disease (AML-3) with none, SCR, M-miR-142, IL1RAP-mut CAR-T + SCR, IL1RAP-mut CAR-T + M-miR-142, IL1RAP CAR-T + SCR, or IL1RAP CAR-T + M-miR-142 (CAR-T: one dose of 3 × 106 on day 3 by intravenous injection; M-miR-142: 30 mg/kg starting on day 4, iv, 6x/week, 3 weeks). PB was collected from the above mice by retro-orbital bleeding biweekly and percentage and/or number of T and leukemic cells in PB biweekly and/or in BM post-treatment or at moribund were analyzed by flow cytometry (Supplementary Table 2). Epredia™ Shandon™ Wright-Giemsa Stain Kit (Fisher scientific, USA) was used for the staining of PB blasts. Mouse care and experimental procedures were performed in accordance with federal guidelines and protocols and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at City of Hope (IACUC 15005).

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as means ± standard deviations and were analyzed using the student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA analysis adjusted by the Bonferroni method as appropriate. The probabilities for overall survival were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the differences were compared using log-rank test. All P values were two-sided and values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 software program (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, www.graphpad.com).

Results

In vitro activity of IL1RAP CAR-T cells

To support IL1RAP as a suitable target in AML, we demonstrated a significant increase in membrane expression of IL1RAP on AML cell lines and primary patient blasts compared with normal HSCs (ref [30] and Fig. 1B and 2A). Notably, in contrast to other CAR-T-cell targets currently under clinical investigation (e.g., CLL1, CD33, CD123), IL1RAP expression was significantly lower in normal HSCs (ref [30]: Fig. 1E). To assess levels of anti-leukemic activity, we co-cultured IL1RAP CAR-T or UTD-T with IL1RAPhigh or IL1RAPlow AML cell lines at E:T ratios ranging from 1:16 to 1:1. E:T-ratio-dependent specific lysis and apoptosis were observed in IL1RAPhigh MOLM13, THP-1, and Kasumi cells co-cultured with IL1RAP CAR-T cells for 48 h, but not in those co-cultured with UTD-T (Fig. 1G-H; Supplementary Fig. 2E-F). In addition, real-time cell killing assay showed eradication of IL1RAPhigh MOLM13 and THP1 co-cultured with IL1RAP CAR-T for 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). IL1RAP CAR-T co-cultured with IL1RAPhigh MOLM13 cells proliferated quickly and killed GFP-expressing MOLM13 cells (Supplementary Video 1). By contrast, no E:T-ratio-dependent specific lysis and apoptosis were observed in IL1RAP CAR-T co-cultured with IL1RAPlow K562 (Fig. 1G-H).

Similar results were obtained when IL1RAP CAR-T were co-cultured with primary IL1RAP+ AML blasts (Fig. 2A). At E:T ratios ranging from 1:16 to 1:1, IL1RAP CAR-T, and not UTD-T, showed significant increase in T cell activation markers, cell cycling and cytokine production (Fig. 2B-E; Supplementary Fig. 3 C). We also observed E:T-ratio-dependent specific lysis and apoptosis in IL1RAP+ AML bulk blasts co-cultured with IL1RAP CAR-T, but not those co-cultured with UTD-T (Fig. 2F-H). Similar results were observed when we selected primary AML CD34 + blasts for co-cultures with IL1RAP CAR-T (Supplementary Fig. 4A-F).

IL1RAP CAR-T cells do not target normal HSCs

To test if IL1RAP CAR-T reacts to normal hematopoietic cells, we analyzed IL1RAP expression on healthy PBMCs. As previously described [42, 43], IL1RAP was highly expressed on CD14+ monocytes, but not on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, CD19+ B cells, CD3−CD16+CD56+ NK cells (Supplementary Fig. 5 A). IL1RAP expression was also not observed on activated T cells (results not shown). When IL1RAP CAR-T were co-cultured with autologous PBMCs at E:T ratio of 1:4 or 1:1, we observed a decrease in CD14+ monocytes, compared with co-culture utilizing UTD-T (Supplementary Fig. 5B-D). Previously, we demonstrated that IL1RAP expression was low on normal CD34+ HSCs [30]. Consistent with this observation, no specific killing of CD34+ HSCs from healthy donors co-cultured with IL1RAP CAR-T or UTD-T at E:T ratio of 1:1 was observed (Supplementary Fig. 5E-F). Of note, in immunohistochemical studies performed on healthy organ tissue arrays, we found no significant expression of IL1RAP in major tissues examined [30], supporting the likelihood of low risk for on-target, off-tumor toxicity.

To test the specificity of IL1RAP CAR-T, we used CAR-T cells harboring two Fab point-mutations that ablated IL1RAP binding as a control (i.e., IL1RAP-mut CAR-T; Fig. 1A and 3F). We observed T cell expansion and reduced tumor burden (Fig. 3G) on day 13 and 19 in the mice treated with one dose of IL1RAP CAR-T and not in those treated with IL1RAP-mut CAR-T or untreated controls. The IL1RAP CAR-T-treated mice survived significantly longer than those treated with IL1RAP-mut CAR-T or untreated controls (median: 49 vs 33 vs 31 days; p < 0.001; Fig. 3H). Of note, IL1RAP CAR-T rapidly decreased over time (Fig. 3G), and the leukemic cells from the relapsed IL1RAP CAR-T-treated mice still have high IL1RAP expression (Supplementary Fig. 6 A), suggesting that leukemia relapse was caused by the lack of persistence of CAR-T cells rather than antigen escape.

Since IL1RAP CAR-T rapidly decreased over time, to test if longer exposure to IL1RAP CAR-T could result in a more potent antileukemic effect, we engrafted NSGS mice with Luci/GFP-expressing MOLM13 and treated them with two (instead of one) separate doses of T cells on days 3 and 20 post-AML transplant, respectively (Fig. 3I). BLI signal demonstrated significantly lower tumor burden in the IL1RAP CAR-T -treated mice compared with UTD-T-treated or untreated controls (Fig. 3J-K). On day 28, we observed a significant reduction of circulating hCD45+hCD2−GFP+ AML blasts and increased T cells in the IL1RAP CAR-T- treated mice, compared with UTD-T-treated or untreated controls (Fig. 3L). The IL1RAP CAR-T-treated mice survived significantly longer than UTD-T-treated or un-treated controls (median: 96 vs. 35 vs. 34 days, p = 0.002; Fig. 3M). Of note, CDX mice treated with two doses of IL1RAP CAR-T appeared to live longer than those treated with only one dose and one mouse treated with two doses of IL1RAP CAR-T achieved long-term survival (≥ 200 days), with no signs of leukemia at the end of the observation period (Fig. 3M; Supplementary Fig. 6B). These results demonstrated that longer exposure to CAR-T cells can enhance antileukemic activity. Hence, we explored strategies to improve persistence of IL1RAP CAR-T cells.

ITAM mutated IL1RAP CAR-T cells fail to improve outcome in vivo

Feucht et al. previously reported that immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) mutations improved persistence and antileukemic activity of CD19-directed CAR-T cells [44]. Thus, to test whether these mutations could have a similar impact on IL1RAP CAR-T, we introduced point mutations in two of the three ITAMs of the CD3ζ domain of the IL1RAP CAR vector and generated IL1RAP-1XX CAR-T cells (Fig. 1A). IL1RAP-mut CAR-T cells were used as a negative control (Fig. 1A). We compared the in vitro and in vivo antileukemic activity of IL1RAP, IL1RAP-1XX, and IL1RAP-mut CAR-T co-cultured with IL1RAPhigh MOLM13 and IL1RAPlow K562. Both IL1RAP and IL1RAP-1XX CAR-T showed significantly higher levels of T-cell activation markers CD25 and CD69, cell cycling rates and cytokine (IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2) production in both CD4+ and CD8+ cell fractions than IL1RAP-mut CAR-T cells when co-cultured with IL1RAPhigh MOLM13, but not with IL1RAPlow K562 (Supplementary Fig. 7A-G). IL1RAP and IL1RAP-1XX CAR-T also showed similar E:T-ratio-dependent specific lysis and apoptosis in IL1RAPhigh MOLM13, THP-1, and Kasumi cells, and only limited activity on IL1RAPlow K562 cells (Supplementary Fig. 8A-B). Real-time cell killing assay also showed similar killing of IL1RAPhigh AML cell lines by IL1RAP CAR-T and IL1RAP-1XX CAR-T (Supplementary Fig. 8C-E). Similar E:T-ratio-dependent specific lysis and apoptosis was observed in IL-1RAP+ primary AML blasts treated with IL1RAP CAR-T or IL1RAP-1XX CAR-T (Supplementary Fig. 8F-G). By long-term killing assay, IL1RAP-1XX CAR-T seemingly persisted longer and showed a higher level of antileukemic activity than IL1RAP CAR-T (Supplementary Fig. 9A-D).

Discussion

IL1RAP is highly expressed on AML blasts and LSCs, but is missing or significantly reduced on normal HSCs and other normal tissues [29–31]. This selective expression makes IL1RAP an ideal and actionable target for CAR-T cell therapy for AML. Recently, IL1RAP monoclonal antibodies have entered clinical trials for both solid tumors [46] (NCT04452214, NCT04990037, NCT05181462) and leukemia (NCT06548230). One clinical trial investigating the safety and efficacy of an IL1RAP targeted CAR-T (NCT06281847) is also ongoing. In addition, we previously reported an IL1RAP/CD3 bispecific T cell engager, which demonstrated significant antileukemic activity and the ability to target LSCs in AML PDX models [30]. Building on these advances, we now report on the design and antileukemic activity of IL1RAP CAR-T.

The basic design of CARs typically involves an extracellular antigen-binding domain (e.g., scFv), a hinge, a transmembrane domain, and intracellular signaling domains [47]. While scFvs provide specificity, they can aggregate, leading to tonic signaling [48]. To address this potential concern, we created single chain Fab [49] using the same CDR sequences as in our bispecific T-cell engager [30]. The IL1RAP CAR-T also incorporated CD28 and CD3ζ intracellular co-stimulatory domains. This IL1RAP CAR-T exhibited high specificity and potent anti-leukemic activity, leading to prolonged survival of treated CDXs and PDXs, with no immediate toxicity observed. While a direct comparison between the IL1RAP CAR-T and our previously developed anti-IL1RAP/CD3 bispecific T-cell engager [30] was not performed, we anticipate that both products can be translated into the clinic and play complementary roles in different clinical settings, analogous to the way FDA-approved CD19-directed CAR-T cell therapies (tisagenlecleucel, brexucabtagene autoleucel and obecabtagene autoleucel) and the CD19-targeted bispecific T-cell engager blinatumomab are utilized.

A major challenge in CAR-T cell therapy for AML and other myeloid malignancies is the risk of on-target, off-tumor toxicity [50–52]. Many target antigens are shared between AML blasts and normal HSCs or progenitor cells, making it difficult to selectively target leukemia cells without harming normal hematopoiesis [21]. However, elevated IL1RAP expression is restricted to AML blasts, LSCs and CD14+ monocytes, thereby sparing normal HSCs and minimizing the potential for severe myeloablation [29–31]. Of note, in preliminary in vitro toxicity studies, we observed a transient decrease in CD14 monocytes, potentially due to their higher IL1RAP expression. This effect, however, would not lead to permanent monocyte ablation, as IL1RAP CAR-T spare HSCs, which can rapidly restore circulating monocytes. Furthermore, the transient depletion of monocytes might even prove beneficial by reducing symptoms of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and limiting T cell exhaustion, which is reportedly mediated by monocyte-secreted IL-1 and IL-6 [53].

Despite the promising preclinical activity of IL1RAP CAR-T cells, treatment failure could occur for various reasons as already observed for other CAR-T therapies tested in AML, including tumor antigen escape, CAR-T cell exhaustion, or CAR-T cell clearance [22]. Although IL1RAP is broadly expressed across AML subtypes, heterogeneous antigen density could allow for outgrowth of IL1RAPlow subclones, analogous to CD19-negative relapse observed in B-ALL [54]. In our models, mice that succumbed to disease continued to express IL1RAP on the AML cells, suggesting that antigen escape was not the cause of disease persistence or relapse. Nevertheless, it is possible that IL1RAP antigen escape may occur in humans. To this end, multiple approaches are being considered to circumvent this challenge [55, 56]. In our group, for example, we are pursuing dual-targeting IL1RAP CAR and combinatory regimens with immune checkpoint blockade, which may further reduce the likelihood of antigen-negative relapse and improve long-term efficacy.

Another major challenge in developing an effective CAR-T-cell therapy for AML is chronic TAA exposure in the immunosuppressive AML BM microenvironment that predisposes CAR-T cells to functional exhaustion and relatively short persistence, which leads to a gradual loss of effector T cells [57]. We observed this phenomenon in our mouse models. Although robust CAR-T-cell activation and proliferation occurred upon initial exposure to IL1RAP⁺ AML cells, CAR-T-cell numbers declined over time and disease progression inevitably followed. Because dose escalation in our xenograft models is confounded by graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) due to cross-species transplantation, we did not formally explore this approach to extend exposure to CAR-T cells. Rather, we proved that longer IL1RAP CAR-T exposure by administering a second infusion of IL1RAP CAR-T cells improves disease response. Of note, in the clinic, CAR-T cells are usually administered only in one dose to prevent toxicity, so strategies to increase CAR-T persistence with repeated cell administration may not be feasible and other approach must be explored.

Feucht et al. recently reported that introducing point mutations in two of the three ITAMs of the CD3ζ domain significantly enhanced CAR-T cell persistence and prolonged antileukemic activity [44]. Thus, we incorporated two ITAM mutations into the CD3ζ domain of our IL1RAP CAR-T construct. However, while we observed increase in numbers of CAR-T cells co-cultured with AML blasts, this strategy did not improve long-term survival, suggesting that an initially potent antigen-driven activation and proliferation could eventually lead to CAR-T exhaustion despite the CD3ζ ITAM mutations. The discrepancy between our results and those obtained with 1XX-modified CD19 CAR-T cells [44] may reflect fundamental differences between lymphoid and myeloid malignancies. Antigen density and distribution of IL1RAP on AML blasts and LSCs may also provide an initially strong and uniform signal that leads to T cell exhaustion despite ITAM truncation. Moreover, the AML TME is enriched for suppressive myeloid cells and inflammatory cytokines [57], which could attenuate the advantage eventually provided by partially mutated ITAM domain. These findings emphasize that the pharmacological impact of ITAM modulation may be context dependent and highlight the need for more disease-specific optimization of the CAR design.

To this end, building on our previous work [35], we considered if and how depletion of miR-142, a key non-coding RNA that regulates T cell differentiation and function, could impact CAR-T in the leukemic BM niche [58]. Our previous and current data support that miR-142 deficiency impairs T cell metabolic function, leading to exhaustion and a loss of antileukemic activity [35]. Administration of M-miR-142, a synthetic miR-142 mimic, successfully restored T cell fitness and prolonged survival in leukemia models [35]. Based on this observation, we hypothesized that M-miR-142 enhances fitness and persistence of IL1RAP CAR-T cells in AML. Indeed, when M-miR-142 was co-administered with IL1RAP CAR-T cells in our AML xenograft models, we observed reduced leukemic burden, increased CAR-T cells in BM, and longer survival compared to controls. Of note, even though, consistent with our previous findings [39],M-miR-142 alone has some direct antileukemic activity, a significantly greater therapeutic benefit was observed when it was co-administered with IL1RAP CAR-T, suggesting that this compound exerts additional effects on IL1RAP CAR-T functionality. The therapeutic benefit of adding M-miR-142 to IL1RAP CAR-T was consistently replicated across three independent AML PDX models.

Other reports have discussed the potential of miRNAs to improve CAR-T cell activity [59], however conferring drug-like properties to miRNAs for therapeutic administration has been a challenge. Progress in formulation and manufacturing of oligonucleotide-based compounds and RNA delivery technologies have advanced substantially in recent years [60]. The translational development of synthetic miRNA mimics such as M-miR-142 however will require a careful evaluation of pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, safety, and regulatory feasibility, which are beyond the scope of the present work. Nevertheless, we recently characterized the PK of a similar CpG oligonucleotide, miRisten (anti–miR-126) [41, 61–63], and demonstrated a favorable PK and toxicity profile that supported a successful IND application and initiation of a clinical trial in AML (NCT07025564). Based on these data, we anticipate that M-miR-142 will exhibit a comparable PK profile, characterized by rapid plasma clearance and preferential uptake by hematopoietic cells. Taken together, these findings suggest that clinical translation of M-miR-142 is feasible.

In summary, to our knowledge, this is the first report of a synthetic miRNA oligonucleotide used to functionally augment CAR-T cell antileukemic activity. This strategy may represent a paradigm-shifting approach that introduces small RNA therapeutics as precision tools to enhance adoptive immunotherapy. Importantly, the implications of this approach may extend beyond AML and could be considered for optimizing CAR-T cell therapy in other malignancies.
