Microfilarial morphometry and molecular characterization of Setaria spp. from naturally infected cattle in Thailand
Authors: Chattraporn Rungchalermlak, Ruttayaporn Ngasaman, Domechai Kaewnoi, Meayanee Kanjanaphan, Niorn Ratanapob, Pairpailin Jhaiaun, Apiraya Rudeekiatthamrong, Darunwan Chuensaengarun, Tanapat Mutchimadilok, Giang Thi Nguyen, Wissanuwat Chimnoi, Jumnongjit Phasuk, Tawin Inpankaew, Burin Nimsuphan, Ketsarin Kamyingkird
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFilarioid nematodes cause reduced livestock productivity and economic losses worldwide. In cattle, differentiation of microfilariae based on morphology is challenging due to overlapping morphological characteristics. This study aimed to identify microfilariae in cattle blood and determine the prevalence and risk factors of filarial worm infection in Thailand using two microscopic diagnostic methods and molecular techniques. A cross-sectional study was conducted from June 2023 to June 2024 across 15 provinces in Thailand. Cattle blood samples (n = 419) were collected, and microfilariae were examined using thin blood smears, the acetone-modified Knott’s technique, and conventional PCR targeting the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene, followed by sequencing and phylogenetic analysis. Parasitologically, microfilariae were detected in 10.28% of samples by the thin smear technique and 24.11% by Knott’s technique, with morphometry confirming sheathed microfilariae of the genus Setaria. Molecular screening revealed a 17.9% PCR positivity rate, with sequencing confirming Setaria digitata as the predominant species, alongside Setaria labiatopapillosa. The overall combined prevalence of Setaria spp. infection was 25.77%, with the highest prevalence in the central region (39.33%) and the lowest in the southern region (3.16%). Risk factor analysis revealed that beef cattle (OR = 8.336, P = 0.048) and dairy cattle (OR = 4.400, P = 0.004) had significantly higher infection risks than bullfighting cattle. At the farm level, geographical region (P < 0.001) and the presence of water ponds (OR = 5.885, P = 0.004) were significant risk factors. This study provides the first comprehensive report of Setaria spp. infection in Thai cattle and highlights the importance of combined diagnostic approaches for improved veterinary surveillance and targeted vector control.Keywords: Blood microfilaria, Cattle, Filarial nematode, Setaria spp., Setaria digitata, Setaria labiatopapillosa, Setariasis

Introduction

Filarioid nematodes are parasitic roundworms belonging to the superfamily Filarioidea. They infect a wide range of domestic animals and ungulates, including cats, dogs, cattle, buffaloes, sheep, and horses (Solismaa et al., 2008; Taylor et al., 2015). These parasites inhabit various sites within vertebrate hosts, such as the skin, eyes, heart, connective tissues, and body cavities (Eamsobhana et al., 2013). The early larval stage, the microfilaria, is commonly found in the bloodstream or subcutaneous tissue. Filarial nematodes have an indirect life cycle, where adult female worms produce microfilariae within the definitive host. The microfilariae are then ingested by hematophagous arthropod vectors during blood-feeding, where they subsequently develop into infective third-stage larvae (L3), which are transmitted to a new vertebrate host. The prepatent period for this infection is 8–10 months (Taylor et al., 2015).

Among various livestock species, cattle are important definitive hosts for several filarial species (Solismaa et al., 2008). Parasites belonging to five genera have been reported in cattle, i.e. Stephanofilaria (subcutaneous tissue), Onchocerca (connective tissue), Parafilaria (subcutaneous tissue), Setaria (body cavity), and Elaeophora (blood vessels) (Anderson, 1999; Hodda, 2022). The clinical signs of filarial infections vary based on the location of adult worms and microfilariae within the host, while disease severity depends on the parasite species and worm burden (Remya et al., 2025). Consequently, these infections can impair animal growth and productivity, leading to significant economic losses for farmers (Basude, 2023).

For the primary screening of filarial infections in cattle, diagnosis can be achieved through the morphological identification of adult worms obtained from biopsy specimens (Fujii et al., 1995; Galuppi et al., 2012) or by examining microfilarial morphology in skin tissues or lesion exudates, as reported for Onchocerca spp., Stephanofilaria spp., and Parafilaria spp. (Sundar et al., 2017; Hund et al., 2021; Lui et al., 2023). In addition, blood examination for circulating microfilariae, particularly Setaria spp. and Elaeophora spp., using parasitological techniques is commonly performed to detect and identify microfilariae at the genus or species level (Rajkumar and Sreenivasa Murthy, 2015; Basude, 2023).

Morphological features, including size, the presence or absence of sheath, and nuclear column pattern, vary among genera and serve as key diagnostic criteria for microfilarial identification (Mathison et al., 2019). However, some microfilariae exhibit highly similar morphological characteristics, making species differentiation challenging (Hanafiah et al., 2023). Additionally, morphometric data for microfilariae have mainly been reported in other host species, such as deer (Laaksonen et al., 2009), whereas comparable information in cattle remains limited. Therefore, morphological identification alone may not provide reliable differentiation among species. Moreover, comparative studies on the application of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) techniques for the detection of microfilariae in cattle in Thailand have not previously been reported. Consequently, there is an increasing demand for sensitive and specific diagnostic methods for accurate detection of microfilariae.

Combining microscopic and molecular techniques may improve the accuracy and sensitivity of microfilaria identification in blood samples (Mathison et al., 2019). Accurate identification of these filarial infections in cattle is essential for appropriate treatment, as well as effective disease control and prevention strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to identify microfilariae in cattle blood and determine the prevalence of Setaria spp. infection in Thai cattle. Additionally, the risk factors associated with Setaria spp. infection were evaluated using both microscopic and molecular techniques.

Materials and methods

Study areas, sample size calculation and study design

Fifteen provinces from four regions of Thailand were included in this study: the southern region (Songkhla, Phatthalung), the central region (Nakhon Pathom, Ratchaburi, Suphan Buri, Kanchanaburi), the northern region (Chiang Rai, Phrae, Lampang, Tak), and the northeastern region (Nong Khai, Surin, Si Sa Ket, Sakon Nakhon, Ubon Ratchathani). These provinces were selected to provide broad geographical coverage across Thailand and to represent different cattle production systems. Sample size was calculated using the formula described by Daniel (1999).

A cross-sectional study using a stratified random sampling approach was conducted from June 2023 to June 2024 to determine the prevalence of filarial infection, identify the species using morphometric and molecular methods, and evaluate the associated risk factors.

Data and sample collection

Cattle blood samples were obtained from small-scale farms enrolled in the integrative control approach project as previously described (Thi Nguyen et al., 2025). Briefly, approximately 5 ml of blood were collected from the jugular vein and stored in vacutainer tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (HeBei Xinle Sci&Tech Co., Ltd, Shijiazhuang, China) and kept at 4 °C for up to 48 h before morphological identification of microfilariae. Animal data, including age, sex, and type of cattle, were recorded. Information on farm management, ectoparasite prevention, water management and vector control was collected using a semi-structured questionnaire.

Morphological identification and morphometry of microfilariae

Morphological identification

Morphological identification of microfilariae was conducted using two parasitological techniques: (i) Dip-Quick staining of thin blood smears (Sathya et al., 2025): briefly, 5 μl of whole blood was smeared on a sterile glass slide, air-dried, fixed with methanol for 2 min, stained with the Dip-Quick staining kit and examined under a light microscope (Olympus CX31, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 1000× magnification with immersion oil; and (ii) Acetone-modified Knott’s concentration technique (Watanabe et al., 2004): briefly, 1 ml of whole blood was added into a 15-ml centrifuge tube (Corning Incorporated, NY, USA) containing 9 ml of acetone hemolytic solution and mixed gently by inverting the tube until homogenized. The tube was then centrifuged at 1500× g for 5 min. After decanting the supernatant, the resulting sediment was placed onto a sterile glass slide and examined under a light microscope (Olympus CX31, Olympus) at 400× magnification.

Morphometry

Microfilariae detected in the thin blood smear and via the acetone-modified Knott’s concentration technique were captured under a light microscope using a digital camera (Olympus EP50, Olympus). The following morphological characteristics were measured: (i) total body length excluding the sheath (TE); (ii) total body length including the sheath (TS); (iii) distance from the anterior extremity to the nerve-ring (NR); (iv) distance from the anterior extremity to the excretory pore (EP); and (v) distance from the anal pore to the tail tip (AP) (Fig. 1). The morphometric measurements were performed using EPview software v.1.4 (Olympus, Japan).

Morphometric parameters used for microfilarial identification: TE, total body length excluding the sheath; TS, total body length including the sheath; NR, distance from the anterior extremity to the nerve-ring; EP, distance from the anterior extremity to the excretory pore; AP, distance from the anal pore to the tail tip.

Molecular detection and bioinformatic analysis of microfilaria

Conventional PCR amplification of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene was conducted using COIintF (5′-TGA TTG GTG GTT TTG GTA A-3′) and COIintR (5′-ATA AGT ACG AGT ATC AAT ATC-3′) primers as previously described (Casiraghi et al., 2001). Briefly, the PCR master mix was prepared in a 20-μl reaction volume containing 14.7 μl of distilled water, 2 μl of 10× PCR buffer, 0.6 μl of MgCl2 (50 mM), 0.4 μl of dNTPs, 0.05 μl of each primer (20 pmol), 0.2 μl of platinum Taq DNA polymerase, 500 U (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and 2 μl of DNA template. Thermocycler conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 50 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 90 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min using a thermocycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The PCR products were resolved by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gel at 100 V for 30 min and visualized using a gel documentation system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Positive amplicons of approximately 680 bp were purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up Mini Kit (Macherey-Nagel GmbH & Co. KG, Düren, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified amplicons were submitted to U2Bio Sequencing Service Co., Ltd. (Bangkok, Thailand) for Sanger sequencing using the PCR primers. The newly generated sequences were compared with those available in the GenBank database using the NCBI BLASTn program to confirm species identification. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA 12 (Kumar et al., 2024). The sequences were aligned using the ClustalW algorithm, and evolutionary distances were calculated based on the Tamura-Nei model with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The genetic relationships between the obtained sequences and reference sequences from GenBank were then evaluated. Bootstrap values ≥ 60% were interpreted as providing moderate to strong support for the inferred phylogenetic relationships.

Statistical and risk factor analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test to assess the association between potential risk factors and filarial infection in cattle blood. Data derived from molecular detection were analyzed at both the animal level, combined with basic animal information (type of cattle and sex), and at the farm level, using farm management data (farming practices, water management systems, ectoparasite prevention, and province). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Variables significantly associated with filarial infection in the Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test (P < 0.05) were subsequently included in univariable logistic regression analysis to estimate crude odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Results

Molecular confirmation of microfilariae in cattle blood in Thailand

A total of 419 cattle blood samples comprising 204 dairy cattle, 181 beef cattle, and 34 bullfighting cattle were examined by PCR amplification targeting the cox1 gene, which yielded a single band of approximately 680 bp. PCR amplification revealed that 75 out of 419 samples (17.9%) were positive. Ten PCR-positive samples were randomly selected from across four regions and submitted for nucleotide sequencing. Sequence analysis confirmed S. digitata in six isolates from beef cattle and three isolates from dairy cattle samples from three regions (99.70–100% identity) and S. labiatopapillosa in one beef cattle sample collected in Surin Province (99.37% identity), compared with reference sequences available in the GenBank database (Table 1).

The characteristics of microfilariae in Thai cattle blood

Out of 419 cattle blood samples collected in this study, only 278 were used for the detection of microfilariae via parasitological techniques due to insufficient blood volume in some specimens. Parasitological examination revealed a microfilariae detection rate of 10.28% (29/278) using the thin blood smear technique (Fig. 3A), and 24.11% (68/278) using the acetone-modified Knott’s concentration technique (Fig. 3B).

Characteristics of microfilariae in Thai cattle blood observed under a light microscope at 400× magnification. A Sheathed microfilaria from a thin blood smear. B Sheathed microfilaria from the acetone-modified Knott’s concentration technique.

Characteristics of Setaria spp. microfilariae examined via the thin blood smear technique

Morphometric analysis of microfilariae obtained from thin blood smear preparations was conducted on a total of 32 microfilariae obtained from 29 samples. The morphometric analysis revealed variations in the measured structures, including the length of TE, NR, EP, and AP, with the detailed measurements presented in Table 2. TS length could not be measured in the thin blood smear due to unclear sheath boundaries caused by interference from red blood cells and insufficient staining visibility. Under light microscopy, microfilariae exhibit a slender, thread-like body enclosed within a lightly stained, nearly transparent sheath. The anterior extremity is rounded, with a cephalic space that is slightly longer than wide and devoid of nuclei. This is followed by the nuclear body containing densely packed, darkly stained, oval nuclei arranged along its length. A distinct unstained region corresponding to the nerve-ring was observed. The excretory pore appears as a small, triangular, unstained lateral opening, while the anal pore, showing a similar appearance, is located near the posterior extremity. The nuclei extend along most of the body; however, in the mid-body region toward the posterior, the nuclei that appeared loosely arranged were called the inner body (Fig. 4A). Notably, one atypical microfilaria exhibited a similar overall morphology, except that a single nucleus was observed in the cephalic space, followed by the main nuclear column (Fig. 4B). In addition, the compact column of nuclei did not reach the tip of the tail, resulting in a terminal region free of nuclei. The body tapered toward a pointed tail (Fig. 4B).

Characteristics of Setaria spp. microfilariae examined via the acetone-modified Knott’s concentration technique

Morphometric analysis of microfilariae obtained using the acetone-modified Knott’s concentration technique was conducted on a total of 217 individual microfilariae from the 68 positive samples. The morphometric measurements of the length of TE, TS, NR, EP, and AP are summarized in Table 2. The morphological characteristics of the microfilaria observed using this technique were consistent with those observed in thin blood smear. However, the acetone-modified Knott’s concentration technique enhanced the visualization of the microfilarial sheath. Microfilariae were frequently observed in a straightened shape with reduced coiling and minimal obscuration by erythrocytes, as a result of the red blood cell lysis occurring during sample processing. Consequently, while the acetone-modified Knott’s approach enhanced certain morphological features, the microfilariae obtained using this technique appeared to be slightly longer than those observed in thin blood smears (Table 2).

Prevalence of Setaria spp. infection in Thai cattle

Prevalence of filarial infection in Thai cattle was derived from three diagnostic approaches. Molecular detection was performed on all available samples (n = 419), whereas parasitological examination was conducted on a subset of samples (n = 278), selected based on sample availability and suitability for microscopic examination. Infection status was defined on a composite basis, whereby a sample was considered positive if microfilariae or DNA was detected by at least one of the three diagnostic methods (thin blood smear technique, acetone-modified Knott’s concentration technique, or PCR). Therefore, the reported prevalence reflects the proportion of animals positive by any of the applied diagnostic approaches. Parasitological examination demonstrated that all detected microfilariae were sheathed, consistent with the morphological characteristics of Setaria spp. Therefore, all microfilaria-positive samples were interpreted as Setaria spp. infections. The overall prevalence of Setaria spp. infection in Thai cattle was 25.77% (108/419) based on the composite diagnostic definition. The highest prevalence was observed in the central region, 39.33% (35/89), followed by 32.28% (41/127), 26.85% (29/108), and 3.16% (3/95) in the northeastern, northern, and southern regions, respectively. Setaria spp. prevalence ranged from 0% to 58.33% at the provincial level (Fig. 5). A high Setaria spp. infection (prevalence > 40%) was observed in Suphan Buri, Si Sa Ket, and Ubon Ratchathani provinces, whereas a moderate Setaria spp. prevalence (20–40%) was observed in Phrae, Nong Khai, and Surin provinces. In contrast, a low Setaria prevalence (< 10%) was found in Tak, Phatthalung, and Songkhla provinces. These findings indicate geographical variation in the distribution of Setaria spp. infections in cattle across the four regions of Thailand.

Risk factors associated with Setaria spp. infection in Thai cattle

At the individual level, sex (male vs female) and type of cattle (dairy, beef, bullfighting cattle) were analyzed. There were statistically significant differences in the prevalence of Setaria spp. infection among cattle types (P < 0.05). Beef cattle were 8.3 times more likely to be infected with Setaria spp. than bullfighting cattle (OR = 8.336, 95% CI: 1.93–35.94, P = 0.048). Similarly, dairy cattle had a significantly higher likelihood of Setaria spp. infection, being 4.4 times more likely to be infected than bullfighting cattle (OR = 4.400, 95% CI: 1.01–19.08, P = 0.004) (Table 3). However, there was no statistically significant difference in Setaria spp. infection between male and female cattle (P = 0.053) (Table 3).

At the farm level, statistically significant differences in Setaria infection were observed among the four geographical regions (P < 0.001). Cattle farms located in the northern, northeastern, and central regions were more likely to have Setaria spp. infections than those in the southern region (P < 0.001) (Table 4). Water management was also identified as a significant risk factor associated with Setaria spp. infection. Discussion

Microfilariae are the blood-circulating early larval stage of filarial nematodes and can be used to determine infection status through parasitological examination (Mohanty et al., 2000; Watanabe et al., 2004). Setaria spp. are among the most common filarial nematodes infecting cattle in Asia (Solismaa et al., 2008; Yu et al., 2021). Several species within this genus, including S. digitata, S. marshalli, and S. labiatopapillosa, have been reported in cattle (Nakano et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2010; Taylor et al., 2015; Sundar and D’Souza, 2015; Rodrigues et al., 2021; Kitajima et al., 2022; Hanafiah et al., 2023). Morphological examination of the microfilariae was performed using both the thin blood smear technique and acetone-modified Knott’s technique. The microfilariae detected in cattle blood in the present study were sheathed, which is consistent with previous reports describing Setaria spp. microfilariae in bovine blood (Ogbogu et al., 1990). The morphological features observed included a rounded anterior extremity, a slender body tapering toward the posterior extremity, rounded nuclei distributed throughout most of the body, and a tail terminating in a hyaline ovoid process. Additionally, the morphometric measurements obtained in this study were consistent with the previously reported size range of Setaria spp. microfilariae (240–260 μm) (Taylor et al., 2015). Although all examined microfilariae exhibited a similar sheathed morphology consistent with Setaria spp., species-level identification could not be reliably achieved based on morphometric characteristics alone. Therefore, the morphometric measurements presented in this study should be interpreted as representing Setaria spp. microfilariae rather than species-specific characteristics. The diagnostic approaches used in this study provided complementary information for the detection and identification of Setaria spp. infection. Thin blood smear examination was useful for direct observation of circulating microfilariae and their morphological characteristics, whereas the acetone-modified Knott’s technique improved visualization by concentrating microfilariae and enhancing observation of the sheath, thereby supporting more reliable morphometric evaluation. However, morphological methods alone have limitations for species-level identification because of overlapping characteristics among closely related Setaria species. Therefore, PCR amplification and sequencing were essential for confirming species identity and improving diagnostic accuracy.

In previous studies, morphological identification has been supported by DNA sequence analysis, which enabled reliable confirmation of Setaria spp. infection (Maharana et al., 2020). Closely related Setaria species may share overlapping morphological characteristics; therefore, morphology alone may not be sufficient for accurate species differentiation. Consequently, molecular analysis was necessary to validate the diagnosis in the present study. The cox1 gene, originally applied for molecular characterization and phylogenetic analysis of filarial nematodes by Casiraghi et al. (2001), has been widely used for the identification of filarial nematodes in several genera. This marker has also been applied for the detection of Setaria spp., yielding a PCR product of approximately 680 bp (Helmi et al., 2025). In this study, sequencing helped to confirm the presence of S. digitata and S. labiatopapillosa in cattle blood in Thailand. Our phylogenetic analysis based on cox1 gene sequences showed that the isolates obtained in this study clustered with either S. digitata or S. labiatopapillosa reference sequences. Most sequences obtained from cattle grouped with S. digitata and showed close genetic relatedness to isolates previously reported from cattle and mosquito vectors in Thailand and other Asian countries, suggesting that S. digitata was the predominant species detected in Thailand. In contrast, S. labiatopapillosa sequences also showed close genetic relatedness to reference isolates from multiple host species, although this species was detected less frequently than S. digitata. The detection of both species highlights the species diversity of Setaria spp. infecting cattle in Thailand and indicates the coexistence of multiple Setaria species within cattle populations, indicating a more complex epidemiological situation, which may be associated with differences in natural vector competence or transmission dynamics (Siriyasatien et al., 2023). The detection of S. labiatopapillosa in cattle blood provides additional evidence of Setaria species diversity in Thai cattle populations. Although S. digitata was the predominant species identified in this study, the presence of S. labiatopapillosa suggests that multiple Setaria species may coexist and circulate among cattle populations in Thailand. Since closely related Setaria species may exhibit overlapping morphological characteristics, the identification of S. labiatopapillosa further highlights the importance of molecular confirmation for accurate species-level identification. However, further studies involving larger numbers of molecularly characterized isolates are required to better understand the distribution patterns, host associations, and epidemiological significance of different Setaria species in Thailand.

The prevalence of microfilariae associated with Setaria spp. infection was evaluated using three diagnostic techniques, including parasitological and molecular techniques. The overall prevalence revealed in this study was higher than that reported in previous studies conducted in Thailand (Konghome and Kansan, 2019; Kanchanatemiya et al., 2020; Yumadeen et al., 2021; Senachai et al., 2024). High prevalence may be attributed to the use of more sensitive diagnostic and molecular techniques for species confirmation. To our knowledge, this is the first study in Thailand to confirm the presence of Setaria spp. using morphometry and molecular detection in cattle. The predominant microfilaria species in Thai cattle was S. digitata, whereas S. labiatopapillosa was also detected.

The analysis of risk factors associated with Setaria infection in cattle at the individual and farm levels revealed that the prevalence of infection was higher in beef and dairy cattle than in bullfighting cattle. Beef and dairy cattle in Thailand are commonly raised under semi-intensive management systems (Wiyabot and Manakit, 2021) and are often kept in open environments with prolonged exposure to mosquito vectors, which serve as biological intermediate hosts of Setaria spp. Furthermore, these animals may remain on farms for longer periods, resulting in increased cumulative exposure to infective larvae. In contrast, bullfighting cattle are generally managed under more intensive conditions and are considered high-value animals (Kaewnoi et al., 2020; Chimnoi et al., 2025). Such management practices may reduce exposure to mosquito vectors and consequently lower the risk of Setaria spp. infection.

At the farm level, infection prevalence differed significantly among geographical regions, with the southern region exhibiting the lowest prevalence. This variation may be associated with differences in environmental conditions, vector distribution, and cattle management practices across regions (Sukkanon et al., 2023). The lower prevalence observed in the southern region may also be related to the higher proportion of bullfighting cattle sampled in this area, which showed a lower risk of infection. Water management practices, particularly the presence of water reservoirs, were significantly associated with Setaria spp. infection in the present study. This association may be explained by the role of aquatic habitats in supporting mosquito breeding and increasing vector abundance. Stagnant water bodies containing organic matter provide favorable conditions for mosquito larval development, thereby potentially enhancing Setaria spp. transmission. Culex mosquitoes, which commonly develop in stagnant water contaminated with organic matter or wastewater, have been reported as potential vectors of filarial nematodes (Siriyasatien et al., 2023). In contrast, the presence or absence of drainage systems in animal pens was not significantly associated with Setaria spp. infection, possibly because temporary water accumulation or flowing water may not provide stable breeding habitats for mosquito vectors (Tung et al., 2003).

The findings of this study have practical implications for Setaria spp. surveillance and control strategies in cattle populations. The identification of circulating Setaria species and associated environmental risk factors provides baseline information for monitoring parasite distribution in Thailand. Since transmission depends on mosquito vectors associated with aquatic breeding habitats, improving water management practices and reducing potential mosquito breeding sites may be considered important components of parasite control programmes. In addition, integrating morphological examination with molecular confirmation may improve surveillance accuracy and facilitate a better understanding of Setaria spp. epidemiology. A limitation of the present study is that the risk factor analysis was based on univariable logistic regression; therefore, the observed associations represent unadjusted estimates and may be influenced by potential confounding factors. Future studies incorporating multivariable logistic regression are warranted to identify independent risk factors associated with Setaria spp. infection in cattle. Further investigations should also consider a broader range of potential risk factors at both animal and farm levels, including animal characteristics, management practices, parasite control measures, and environmental factors related to vector ecology. Such comprehensive approaches would provide a better understanding of the factors influencing Setaria spp. transmission and support the development of more effective surveillance and control strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study confirmed the presence of both S. digitata and S. labiatopapillosa infections in cattle blood in Thailand using morphological and molecular approaches. Setaria digitata was identified as the predominant species infecting cattle in Thailand. The combination of parasitological and molecular techniques enabled reliable identification, with the acetone-modified Knott’s technique improving microfilariae detection and increasing sensitivity, while highlighting differences in diagnostic performance among methods. Morphological approaches provide rapid screening but lack species-level resolution, whereas molecular techniques offer higher specificity and confirmatory identification. The significant association of infection risk with cattle type, geographical region, and water management practices highlights the need for targeted control programmes and improved management strategies. These findings have practical implications for disease surveillance, control measures, and cattle health management in affected regions.
