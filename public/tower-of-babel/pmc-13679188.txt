Cyanobacterial conversion of CO2 to bio-based diols and downstream valorization: biological basis, upgrading strategies, and applications
Authors: Changou Pan, Han Chen, Mingyue Zhang, Shaoxiong He, Zhiqun Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cyanobacteria have attracted growing interest as photosynthetic cell factories for the conversion of carbon dioxide (CO2) into renewable chemicals because they directly use solar energy and inorganic carbon under mild aqueous conditions. Recent advances in chassis development, genome engineering, carbon-partition control, and photobioreactor design have expanded the scope of cyanobacterial CO2-to-chemical biomanufacturing. Among the reported diols, 1,2-propanediol (1,2-PDO), 1,3-propanediol (1,3-PDO), and 2,3-butanediol (2,3-BDO) represent distinct development cases. In particular, 1,3-PDO provides an established benchmark for fermentative diol production from organic feedstocks, whereas 2,3-BDO offers additional opportunities for stereochemistry-sensitive upgrading but requires further validation in several downstream applications. These stages are often studied separately, and their interdependence remains a major challenge for process integration. This review summarizes the biological and engineering basis of cyanobacterial diol production, product recovery and purification, major upgrading strategies, and application prospects in pharmaceuticals, energy-storage materials, hydrogels, fuels, polymers, and related functional products. Key challenges in biological productivity, selective upgrading, catalyst stability, feed quality, and process sustainability are also discussed. The review distinguishes demonstrated cyanobacterial production from downstream transformations reported for other feedstocks and identifies the requirements for connecting them.

Introduction

Carbon dioxide (CO2) capture, conversion, and utilization have become central themes in sustainable manufacturing because they link carbon mitigation with the production of useful chemicals and materials. Although carbon capture and storage can reduce point-source emissions, it primarily functions as an end-of-pipe strategy. By contrast, carbon capture and utilization seeks to incorporate CO2 into value-added products, thereby connecting decarbonization with resource recovery and chemical production. In this context, cyanobacteria are attractive because they can directly harness solar energy and assimilate inorganic carbon through oxygenic photosynthesis, enabling the production of biomass and oxygenated intermediates for fuels, solvents, polymers, pharmaceuticals, and functional materials.1–4

Among the available CO2-conversion routes, photosynthetic biomanufacturing offers a route to chemical production under mild aqueous conditions. Thermochemical and electrochemical platforms provide alternative approaches, with performance governed by temperature, pressure, electricity supply, and feed preparation. Cyanobacteria, in contrast, combine direct light utilization with a prokaryotic cellular organization and an expanding genetic toolbox. They can grow rapidly and can be cultivated on non-arable land. Compared with heterotrophic industrial hosts, they can synthesize target molecules with reduced reliance on externally supplied organic feedstocks. These features make cyanobacteria promising hosts for the direct conversion of CO2 into renewable molecular precursors, although light delivery, nutrient supply, gas transfer, and downstream water removal remain important engineering requirements.5–7

The value of cyanobacterial production platforms, however, cannot be judged solely by whether a target compound can be detected. Practical deployment depends on the coordinated optimization of host physiology, metabolic rewiring, light and gas management, reactor operation, downstream separation, and product quality.8–10 From this perspective, bio-based diols are especially important because they combine biosynthetic accessibility with downstream synthetic versatility. Carbon fixed through the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle supplies triose-phosphate and pyruvate precursors for engineered diol pathways. Cyanobacteria have been engineered to produce 1,2-propanediol (1,2-PDO), 1,3-propanediol (1,3-PDO), and 2,3-butanediol (2,3-BDO), but the relative value of biological production differs among these three diols.11–14 1,2-PDO has well-established chemical production routes. Cyanobacterial synthesis offers a route to renewable carbon incorporation, although its competitiveness remains to be demonstrated.11,12,15 By contrast, 1,3-PDO represents one of the most successful examples of industrialized biological diol production, demonstrating that fermentation can provide a practical alternative to conventional petrochemical synthesis.16 2,3-BDO offers a different advantage: pathway enzymes can influence the relative formation of the meso and enantiomeric products, providing a route to stereochemically defined products.14,17 These complementary features make 1,3-PDO and 2,3-BDO particularly useful representative molecules for examining the relationship between upstream photosynthetic CO2 conversion and downstream upgrading chemistry.

Although 1,3-PDO and 2,3-BDO are both biologically accessible platform diols, their downstream development is at very different stages. Fermentative 1,3-PDO production from organic feedstocks has already been industrialized, and its derivatives have been widely explored in polymers, specialty chemicals, and synthetic intermediates. In contrast, the chemistry of 2,3-BDO remains less extensively explored across the application areas considered here, despite its vicinal-diol structure and stereochemical diversity. Accordingly, the downstream sections of this review use established chemistry and applications of 1,3-PDO, 1,2-PDO, glycerol, ethylene glycol, and related diol-derived intermediates as methodological precedents to identify plausible upgrading routes for 2,3-BDO. Results obtained with other diols are treated as methodological precedents rather than direct evidence for equivalent 2,3-BDO behavior. Direct 2,3-BDO transformations are considered separately under their reported conditions.15,18,19

Building on reviews of cyanobacterial engineering and diol chemistry, this review focuses on how cultivation and recovery determine feed quality for upgrading and application validation.1,2,4,15,19Fig. 1 summarizes this integrated perspective on cyanobacterial CO2 conversion to diols and their downstream valorization. It first summarizes the biological and engineering foundations of cyanobacterial production, including chassis selection and product spectrum, photosynthetic CO2 fixation and carbon allocation, genome engineering and pathway regulation, factors governing photosynthetic performance, and photobioreactor operation. Product recovery, concentration, separation, and purification are then considered as the critical interface between biological production and subsequent chemical processing. Building on this basis, the major upgrading routes of bio-based diols are examined, including carbonation, etherification, epoxide-mediated derivatization, oxidation, acetalization and ketalization, leaving-group installation, and click-enabled functionalization. The discussion then extends to application prospects in pharmaceutical intermediates, lithium- and zinc-battery electrolytes, hydrogel-related systems, fuels, polymers, and other functional products. Finally, the major biological, reactor-engineering, separation, catalytic, and application-level bottlenecks are considered together with the economic and environmental metrics needed to evaluate future CO2-based chemical production platforms. Biological and engineering foundations of cyanobacterial CO2-to-diol production

Cyanobacterial CO2 conversion is a systems-level process governed by the coupling of photosynthetic carbon fixation, intracellular carbon allocation, gene regulation, and bioprocess conditions. Cyanobacterial synthetic biology should therefore be viewed not simply as the introduction of heterologous pathways, but as the redesign of a photosynthetic chassis in which light-driven carbon assimilation is coupled more effectively to target-product formation.

Cyanobacterial chassis selection and accessible product spectrum

Cyanobacteria are also highly diverse in phylogeny, morphology, physiology, and environmental adaptation. Consequently, no single strain is suitable for all target products or cultivation modes, and chassis selection should be guided by the intended product class, engineering objective, and process conditions.

Among the available cyanobacterial hosts, the best-established model strains remain Synechococcus elongatus PCC 7942 and Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803. S. elongatus PCC 7942 has long been used for pathway construction, proof-of-concept production of ethanol and 2,3-butanediol, and carbon-export engineering.14,20,21Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803 is similarly important because of its natural transformability and extensive physiological characterization, which have made it a widely used chassis for volatile-product biosynthesis, lipid-related pathways, and genetic-tool development.22–24 More recently, fast-growing strains such as S. elongatus UTEX 2973, S. elongatus PCC 11801, S. elongatus PCC 11802, and Synechococcus sp. PCC 11901 have attracted increasing attention because they offer higher biomass productivity and greater compatibility with intensified cultivation.25–29

Biological basis of photosynthetic CO2 fixation and carbon allocation

Cyanobacterial photosynthetic CO2 fixation begins with light harvesting and photosynthetic electron transport. Upon illumination, photosystem II (PSII) oxidizes water and releases O2, while the photosynthetic electron-transport chain and photosystem I (PSI) generate the proton motive force and reducing equivalents required for ATP synthesis and NADPH production. These energy carriers subsequently support the Calvin–Benson–Bassham (CBB) cycle, in which ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCO) catalyzes the carboxylation of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate. The resulting fixed carbon enters central metabolism through triose-phosphate and sugar-phosphate pools, which connect photosynthetic carbon fixation to biomass formation, storage metabolism, and engineered product pathways.57

Because RuBisCO has relatively low catalytic efficiency and is susceptible to a competing oxygenation reaction, cyanobacteria have evolved a carbon-concentrating mechanism (CCM) that elevates the local CO2 concentration around RuBisCO. This system generally involves active inorganic-carbon uptake, intracellular bicarbonate accumulation, and carboxysome-based localization of RuBisCO. Within the carboxysome, bicarbonate is converted into CO2 by carbonic anhydrase. The CCM is therefore central to cyanobacterial carbon fixation because it increases the effective carboxylation efficiency of RuBisCO, suppresses wasteful oxygenation, and connects extracellular inorganic-carbon availability with intracellular metabolism.58–62

After entering central metabolism, fixed carbon is continuously partitioned among biomass formation, cellular maintenance, carbon storage, and engineered product synthesis. Glycogen biosynthesis, photorespiration-related metabolism, redox-balancing reactions, and stress-response pathways can all compete for carbon flux and reducing power, thereby limiting target-product formation. Accordingly, metabolic engineering in cyanobacteria requires not only the introduction of a product-forming pathway, but also control of flux through competing pathways, reinforcement of precursor supply, optimization of cofactor compatibility, and dynamic control of intracellular carbon partitioning.57,63

Storage metabolism can contribute to product synthesis as well as compete for fixed carbon. In engineered Synechocystis, disruption of glycogen formation reduced 1,2-PDO production. Subsequent isotope experiments attributed approximately one quarter of the product carbon to previously accumulated glycogen and the remainder to CO2 fixed during the production phase.12,64 This contribution reflects mobilization of an intracellular reserve of previously fixed carbon rather than utilization of an externally supplied organic substrate, illustrating why carbon allocation should be evaluated across both growth and production phases.

Pyruvate provides the direct link between central carbon metabolism and 2,3-BDO synthesis through the acetolactate–acetoin pathway. Kanno et al. enhanced glucose assimilation, deleted cp12, and modified Calvin-cycle regulation in S. elongatus PCC 7942 (Fig. 4).38 The optimized photomixotrophic strain produced a cumulative 12.6 g L−1 2,3-BDO over 12 days, with transfer into fresh medium on days 3, 6, and 9, corresponding to a reported productivity of approximately 1.1 g L−1 day−1. This cumulative production is distinct from the concentration in a single harvested culture. Short-term isotope experiments further showed increased incorporation of inorganic carbon following regulatory modification, although these labeling results do not represent the carbon-source distribution over the full production period. The study therefore demonstrates coordinated engineering of carbon supply, Calvin-cycle regulation, and product-forming flux under glucose-assisted conditions rather than fully photoautotrophic production. More broadly, comparisons among photoautotrophic, photomixotrophic, and two-stage processes should account for differences in carbon input and production interval rather than relying on final product concentration alone.37,53,55,56

Genome engineering and pathway control strategies

Cyanobacterial engineering has advanced substantially over the past decade. Early studies relied mainly on homologous recombination at chromosomal neutral sites, together with constitutive or inducible promoters and antibiotic selection markers. These approaches remain useful because cyanobacteria often possess polyploid genomes, and stable chromosomal integration is generally preferred over plasmid-based expression for long-term cultivation. However, conventional integration strategies can still be time-consuming, particularly when complete segregation of all genome copies is required.24,65,66

More recently, the cyanobacterial genetic toolbox has expanded to include CRISPR interference (CRISPRi), CRISPR-Cas-mediated genome editing, Cas12a-based platforms, base editing, promoter libraries, ribosome-binding-site tuning, and multiplex pathway assembly. These developments have improved the precision of gene knockout, transcriptional repression, gene activation, and sequence-level modification. They have also made it increasingly feasible to construct strains carrying multiple coordinated edits. Nevertheless, editing outcomes remain strongly host-dependent and must be interpreted in the context of polyploid genome segregation and long-term genetic stability. Recent studies further illustrate how CRISPR-based toolkits can accelerate multiplex pathway optimization in cyanobacteria.39,63,67–71

Dynamic regulation is becoming increasingly important in cyanobacterial metabolic engineering. In photosynthetic hosts, constitutive overexpression of heterologous pathways can impose a substantial metabolic burden, suppress cell growth, and compromise genetic stability during prolonged cultivation. Inducible systems, nutrient-responsive promoters, light-regulated modules, and growth–production decoupling strategies therefore offer potential advantages. Such approaches can temporally separate biomass accumulation from product formation or allow pathway activity to respond to the physiological state of the cells.63,70

Despite this progress, important challenges remain. Cyanobacteria are often more difficult to transform than established heterotrophic hosts, editing efficiency can vary substantially among strains, and complete segregation of engineered alleles in polyploid genomes may require prolonged selection. Pathway design must also account for the oxygen-rich intracellular environment, redox balance, limitations in product export, and the emergence of escape mutants under sustained metabolic burden. These limitations favor integrating genome-engineering tools with physiology-informed pathway design and cultivation objectives.

Factors affecting photosynthetic performance and carbon-conversion efficiency

The productivity of cyanobacterial cell factories is highly sensitive to the external physicochemical environment. Light intensity, spectral distribution, photoperiod, temperature, pH, salinity, nutrient availability, dissolved inorganic carbon, and hydrodynamic conditions can all affect photosynthetic performance, carbon-fixation efficiency, and the rate of product formation. Under low-light conditions, the supply of ATP and NADPH generated by the light reactions becomes limiting, whereas excessive irradiance can cause photoinhibition and reactive oxygen species formation, thereby damaging the photosynthetic apparatus. Even when the average incident light intensity appears appropriate, uneven light distribution in dense cultures can create substantial local heterogeneity, such that some cells remain light-limited while others are exposed to excess irradiance.72

Inorganic-carbon supply is another major determinant of productivity. Because CO2 gas–liquid mass transfer is inherently limited and dissolved inorganic-carbon speciation is strongly affected by pH, bicarbonate availability and gas-delivery strategy directly influence carbon-fixation efficiency. Insufficient inorganic-carbon supply can result in carbon limitation even under adequate illumination, whereas poorly controlled operation at high CO2 concentrations may cause unnecessary carbon loss, local acidification, and increased process cost. The operation of the carbon-concentrating mechanism (CCM) further shows that carbon supply cannot be considered independently of cellular physiology, because inorganic-carbon uptake and concentration consume ATP and reducing power that might otherwise support product formation.58,59,73

Nutrient balance introduces an additional trade-off in cyanobacterial production systems. Nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, iron, and trace metals are required for photosynthetic machinery, enzyme activity, and biomass formation. Although deliberate nutrient limitation can, in some cases, redirect carbon toward selected metabolites, it may also suppress growth, destabilize protein turnover, and reduce long-term reactor productivity. Similarly, oxygen accumulation in closed systems may inhibit oxygen-sensitive enzymes, intensify photooxidative stress, and alter gas-transfer driving forces. The optimal operating window therefore depends strongly on whether the process is designed for rapid biomass accumulation, continuous product formation, or staged growth–production operation.74

An additional but often underestimated issue is the mismatch between laboratory evaluation and process-scale operation. Many engineered strains perform well in shake flasks or small flat-panel photobioreactors but show lower or less stable productivity under high-cell-density, long-term, or outdoor-relevant conditions. Strain selection should therefore treat physiological robustness as a core criterion rather than focus solely on the maximum specific productivity obtained under idealized laboratory conditions. Practical cyanobacterial production platforms will require not only pathway optimization but also improved tolerance to realistic fluctuations in light, temperature, gas composition, nutrient availability, and hydrodynamic stress.

Photobioreactor engineering for cultivation, carbon supply, and product formation

Photobioreactor engineering is closely coupled to cyanobacterial productivity and metabolic performance. Because light serves both as the primary energy input and as a potential source of physiological stress, reactor design must simultaneously address illumination, heat dissipation, gas transfer, mixing, and fouling control. Common reactor configurations include open ponds, tubular systems, column reactors, flat-panel photobioreactors, and, more recently, internally illuminated or membrane-assisted systems. Each configuration represents a different balance among capital cost, contamination control, areal productivity, optical path length, and scale-up feasibility.75

Open systems remain attractive for low-value biomass production because of their relatively low capital cost, but they provide limited control over contamination, evaporation, gas transfer, and environmental fluctuations. Closed photobioreactors are generally preferred for higher-value products and mechanistic studies. Flat-panel and tubular systems provide higher surface-area-to-volume ratios, improved environmental control, and greater compatibility with defined gas-delivery conditions. However, these systems also introduce operational challenges, including oxygen accumulation, biofilm formation, pressure drop, and increasingly nonuniform photon distribution as culture density rises.

For CO2-to-diol production, gas-management strategy is particularly important. Carbon supply must be sufficient to support the CCM and sustain carbon fixation, whereas excessive bubbling may increase gas loss and operating cost. Mixing must maintain sufficiently uniform nutrient concentrations and temperature while avoiding damaging levels of shear. Dissolved oxygen may also need to be removed efficiently, particularly when product-forming pathways or product stability are sensitive to oxidative conditions. Process intensification therefore depends not only on strain performance but also on the coordinated optimization of light-path geometry, sparging, circulation, and gas composition.

Product properties and location also influence reactor operation and the composition of the harvested stream. Nonvolatile extracellular diols accumulate in the aqueous phase, so cell retention, medium replacement, harvest frequency, and product concentration must be considered together with biological productivity. By contrast, volatile products such as ethylene, isoprene, and isobutyraldehyde may require off-gas monitoring or in situ capture, whereas hydrophobic terpenoids and fatty acids may partition into an organic phase.30,33,44,47–49 These differences mean that reactor performance cannot be evaluated from liquid-phase concentration alone; the phase in which the product is recovered and the basis used for normalization should also be reported.

Future photobioreactor development is likely to move toward more modular and hybrid process configurations. These may include separate growth and production stages, advanced monitoring and control systems, internally illuminated reactors, intensified gas–liquid contactors, and integration with membrane or electrochemical modules. Reactor design and strain engineering should be developed together to match cultivation conditions with product formation and recovery. Such an approach allows strain engineering, carbon supply, light management, and product recovery to be optimized together.

For meaningful comparison among studies, photobioreactor reports should extend beyond nominal irradiance and final product titer. A process-relevant description should include incident photon flux and, where available, absorbed photon flux; optical path length; culture density; gas composition and flow rate; a mass-transfer descriptor such as kLa or an equivalent CO2-transfer proxy; dissolved O2 management; pH control; cultivation duration; volumetric and areal productivity; and a carbon balance that distinguishes inorganic carbon supplied, carbon retained in biomass, carbon recovered in product, and carbon lost in the outlet gas. These parameters are especially important for diols because they are low-volatility, oxygenated products for which downstream water removal can dominate the overall process burden. Diol recovery, concentration, separation and purification

Product recovery connects cultivation performance with the quality of the feedstock supplied to downstream chemical processing. For dissolved diols, the recovery sequence is governed primarily by product concentration, dissolved impurities, and the purity required for subsequent conversion. Clarification, concentration, purification, and, where necessary, stereoisomer separation can introduce product losses and additional utility demand. Separation studies using noncyanobacterial fermentation streams provide useful process benchmarks, although their performance depends strongly on feed composition and cannot be transferred directly to cyanobacterial cultures. The discussion below focuses specifically on dissolved diols, for which low volatility and high aqueous solubility make concentration and impurity removal particularly important.

Clarification removes cells and suspended solids while leaving dissolved diols in the liquid phase. Centrifugation or microfiltration may therefore be followed by ultrafiltration when removal of macromolecular impurities is required. Subsequent membrane selection must distinguish impurity removal from product concentration. In a cyanobacterial sucrose study, microfiltration and ultrafiltration allowed sucrose to pass into the permeate, providing clarification rather than concentration or desalting.76 Davey et al. investigated nanofiltration and reverse osmosis for the fractionation of 2,3-BDO, acetate, and ethanol from gas-fermentation streams.77 Their results support membrane screening within a multistep recovery process. For cyanobacterial cultures, membrane flux, diol transmission, salt rejection, fouling, and cleaning requirements should be evaluated together. A clarified permeate may still contain dissolved ions and low-molecular-weight organic compounds that affect subsequent chemical conversion.

Evaporation or reverse osmosis can reduce the volume entering final purification, although the associated process burdens differ. Evaporation consumes heat and may promote fouling or unwanted reactions, whereas reverse osmosis is constrained by osmotic pressure, solute transmission, membrane fouling, and cleaning requirements. Jeon et al. combined vacuum concentration, alcohol precipitation, and vacuum distillation to process a heterotrophic 2,3-BDO broth.78 The reported recovery was based on the treated BDO rather than the original broth, and the relatively concentrated feed limits direct extrapolation to dilute photosynthetic cultures.

Liquid–liquid extraction and aqueous two-phase extraction provide alternatives to extensive thermal water removal. Li et al. used an ethanol/ammonium-sulfate aqueous two-phase system to extract 2,3-BDO from fermentation broth.79 Extraction recovery describes partitioning of the product into the extract phase rather than the purity of a final solvent-free product. Process evaluation must therefore include solvent regeneration, salt recycling, residual solvent removal, and treatment of the aqueous waste stream. Reactive separation provides another option by temporarily converting a diol into a more readily separable acetal or ketal, followed, when recovery of the parent diol is required, by hydrolysis and regeneration of the derivatizing reagent. Kubic and Tan evaluated such an approach for 2,3-BDO using process modeling.80 The predicted costs and energy requirements depend on the assumptions of the model and have not been validated for cyanobacterial cultures.

Final purification must address impurities generated during processing as well as those present in the original feed. Zhang et al. found that organic-acid ester formation reduced the quality of 1,3-PDO produced by Clostridium pasteurianum. Their downstream process combined ultrafiltration, evaporation, glycerol-assisted vacuum distillation, alkaline ester hydrolysis, rectification, and activated-carbon treatment.81 This example illustrates why product specifications should include relevant acids, esters, colored impurities, and residual reagents in addition to the diol assay. Additional polishing or stereochemical separation may be required for applications with more stringent specifications.

The required extent of purification should ultimately be determined by the downstream chemistry or end-use application. Complete isolation may be necessary when stringent feed specifications are required, whereas compatible aqueous processing could reduce the need for extensive drying. Such compatibility, however, must be demonstrated using the actual feed composition. Recovery comparisons should therefore report overall diol recovery, final purity, stereoisomer composition where relevant, solvent and reagent consumption, water use, thermal and electrical energy demand, waste generation, and operating duration. A defined functional unit, such as one kilogram of isolated diol at a specified purity, is needed for meaningful process comparison. Table 5 summarizes representative separation benchmarks.

Main upgrading strategies for bio-based diols

The downstream development of 2,3-BDO remains less extensive than that of several other established diol platforms. This section therefore examines major diol transformations from the perspective of their potential relevance to 2,3-BDO product development. Carbonation, etherification, epoxide-mediated derivatization, oxidation, acetalization and ketalization, leaving-group installation, and click-enabled functionalization are considered because they provide different routes for converting hydroxyl functionality into chemically and functionally diverse products. Where direct studies of 2,3-BDO are limited, reactions reported for 1,2-PDO, 1,3-PDO, glycerol, ethylene glycol, and related diol-derived intermediates are used as chemical precedents to identify transferable reaction principles and potential limitations.

Such transfer is not necessarily straightforward because hydroxyl-group position, substitution pattern, steric environment, and stereochemistry can substantially affect reaction outcome. In particular, the vicinal secondary hydroxyl groups and stereochemical diversity of 2,3-BDO distinguish it from linear primary diols such as 1,3-PDO and may influence reaction selectivity and stereochemical transfer. Accordingly, the following discussion distinguishes transformations demonstrated directly with 2,3-BDO from those established with related substrates. Reaction performance is considered in terms of conversion, selectivity, isolated yield, catalyst requirements, and compatibility with biologically derived feedstocks, rather than assuming that a successful transformation of one diol can be transferred directly to another.

Carbonation to cyclic carbonates

Carbonation converts hydroxyl-containing substrates into cyclic carbonates and related oxygen-rich intermediates. This transformation is attractive from a green-chemistry perspective because cyclic carbonates often combine useful solvent properties with broad synthetic utility. For vicinal diols, intramolecular carbonate formation is particularly valuable because it provides intermediates for ring-opening reactions, polymer synthesis, and electrolyte-related applications.

In practice, the efficiency and selectivity of diol carbonation depend strongly on substrate structure and activation mode. Routes based on dialkyl carbonates, carbonate-forming reagents, and catalytic CO2 utilization have all been explored. For bio-based diol upgrading, the most attractive methods are those that provide good selectivity while avoiding highly hazardous reagents, minimizing stoichiometric waste, and simplifying downstream purification.15,18

Direct carbonation of bio-based diols provides access to carbonate products, although its practical value depends on both reaction performance and process requirements. Honda and co-workers showed that CO2 and 1,2-propanediol could be converted to propylene carbonate over a CeO2/2-cyanopyridine carboxylation–hydration cascade, demonstrating that high cyclic-carbonate selectivity can be achieved from a diol and CO2 when water is actively managed.83 Bobbink et al. addressed the thermodynamic and kinetic difficulty of converting diols and CO2 directly into cyclic carbonates using an N-heterocyclic carbene catalyst (Fig. 5).84 Under the optimized conditions, catalyst 2c, 1-bromobutane, and Cs2CO3 were employed in DMF at 90 °C and atmospheric CO2 pressure, affording the model carbonate in up to 81% GC yield (61% isolated yield). Carbon-13 labeling confirmed that CO2 was the main source of the carbonate carbonyl carbon. In the proposed catalytic sequence, the carbene captures CO2, the resulting adduct reacts with an alkoxide and the alkyl halide, and subsequent intramolecular substitution closes the carbonate ring while regenerating the catalyst. The method accommodated both 1,2- and 1,3-diols, giving five- and six-membered cyclic carbonates. Avoiding elevated CO2 pressure and transition metals is advantageous; nevertheless, the 20 mol% catalyst loading, stoichiometric alkyl halide and Cs2CO3, DMF solvent, and salt-containing waste substantially limit the green-chemistry benefit. Future development should therefore prioritize catalytic promoter turnover, safer solvents, and more efficient management of the water-forming equilibrium. The CeO2/nitrile strategy was subsequently extended from small cyclic carbonates to CO2/diol copolymerization and alternating polycarbonate formation, broadening the product range from solvent-like molecules to polymeric carbonates.85,86

Alternative carbonate routes provide useful comparison cases because they illustrate different reagent, catalyst, and separation requirements. Ochoa-Gómez and co-workers optimized glycerol transesterification with dimethyl carbonate, showing how catalyst basicity and reaction conditions influence a phosgene-free cyclic-carbonate process.87 Bian et al. further demonstrated that CO2 and diols could be coupled using a Cs2CO3/CH2Cl2 system to form polycarbonates and poly(ether carbonate)s, although the use of dichloromethane and base-promoted activation makes this route more useful as a demonstration of route diversity than as a benchmark for green processing.88 Taken together, these studies support carbonation as a valuable upgrading direction while identifying water removal, promoter loading, solvent choice, and molecular-weight control as important limitations.

For 2,3-BDO upgrading, the practical value of carbonation depends not only on carbonate yield but also on management of the water-forming equilibrium, promoter consumption, and reagent recovery. Routes based on dialkyl carbonates should be distinguished from direct CO2 incorporation because the carbonate reagent itself contributes to the overall material and energy balance. Studies on glycerol and other diols provide useful processing precedents, but the corresponding selectivity and catalyst performance should ultimately be verified with 2,3-BDO.

Etherification for tuning polarity and transport properties

Etherification provides a direct means of tuning the polarity, flexibility, and interfacial behavior of hydroxyl-containing molecules. Monoetherification retains a hydroxyl group for subsequent functionalization, whereas dietherification removes hydrogen-bond donor sites. Cyclic and acyclic ethers therefore provide different opportunities for downstream synthesis and property control.89

Selective etherification can be complicated by competing mono- and disubstitution, elimination, rearrangement, or changes in stereochemical composition. The reaction outcome depends on the substrate, base, electrophile, solvent, and leaving group. Although etherification can alter polarity and transport behavior, properties such as lower viscosity, improved electrochemical stability, or enhanced compatibility with polymer matrices must be demonstrated for the actual derivative and formulation.90

Glycerol provides a useful comparative substrate, although it is a triol. Gu et al. prepared monoalkyl glyceryl ethers over acid-functionalized silica,91 while Selva et al. used dialkyl carbonates to alkylate glycerol formal and solketal.92 These studies provide information on selectivity and reagent choice without establishing equivalent performance for 1,3-PDO or 2,3-BDO. Yu et al. produced dimethoxyethane from crude ethylene glycol and methanol in continuous flow over SAPO-34.93 This result applies to the specific feed and catalyst tested. Describing a feed as crude does not establish cyanobacterial or fermentation provenance, and transfer of the process to the intended biological stream requires a measured impurity profile and catalyst-lifetime testing.

For 2,3-BDO, etherification must additionally address the competition between mono- and diether formation, possible elimination, and retention of stereochemical integrity. Process evaluation should therefore include alkylating-reagent consumption, salt generation, solvent recovery, and any drying required before subsequent use. Reaction conditions established for other glycols should be regarded as starting points rather than directly transferable protocols for 2,3-BDO.

Epoxide-mediated derivatization to β-amino alcohols

Epoxide-mediated chemistry provides a versatile route for diol upgrading. Conversion of a vicinal diol into the corresponding epoxide transforms a relatively unreactive substrate into a more reactive electrophilic intermediate. Subsequent ring opening with amines, alcohols, thiols, halides, or carbon nucleophiles provides access to a wide range of functionalized products.

This route is particularly relevant to 2,3-BDO derivatives because stereochemical information can be transferred or reorganized during both epoxide formation and nucleophilic ring opening. When the starting diol is meso or enantiomerically enriched, different epoxide isomers and downstream products may be obtained. This feature is especially important for β-amino alcohols, which are widely used as pharmaceutical intermediates, ligands, organocatalysts, and functional additives.94–96

The outcome of these transformations depends strongly on catalyst selection and reaction design. Chiral metal complexes, Lewis acids, organocatalysts, and carefully selected bases can influence the efficiency and selectivity of both epoxide formation and ring opening. Jacobsen-type catalysts and related chiral salen systems remain representative examples of stereoselective epoxide chemistry, and recyclable polymeric and macrocyclic Cr(iii) salen catalysts have also been investigated.94,97–100

Connecting cyanobacterial diol production to epoxide chemistry requires validation of the complete sequence from diol activation to epoxide formation and subsequent conversion. Studies using purchased or independently prepared epoxides establish the ring-opening chemistry but do not demonstrate the preceding conversion of cyanobacterially produced 2,3-BDO.95,101

Epoxide-mediated upgrading is attractive because it provides access to β-amino alcohol motifs that are widely encountered in medicinal chemistry. Arai et al. developed a chiral Lewis-acid system based on Nb(OMe)5 and a tetradentate BINOL-derived ligand for the desymmetrative opening of meso-epoxides with anilines (Fig. 6).98 The resulting anti-β-amino alcohols were obtained in up to quantitative yield, with enantioselectivity reaching 96% ee. At higher substrate concentration, a separate experiment using 0.25 mol% catalyst afforded 86% yield and 88% ee. The catalyst also differentiated unsymmetrical cis-epoxides, providing regioselectivity of up to 18 : 1 and enantioselectivity exceeding 99% ee. Extension to meso-aziridines furnished vicinal diamines in up to 95% yield and 84% ee; recrystallization could increase the enantiomeric excess to above 99% ee. This work provides a useful stereochemical benchmark for converting diol-derived three-membered-ring intermediates into β-amino alcohols. However, preparation of the multidentate ligand, the use of an uncommon metal, solvent demand, and stereoisomer purification must be considered when this route is compared with biocatalytic or heterogeneous alternatives. Azoulay et al. showed that meso-epoxides could undergo asymmetric ring opening with aromatic amines in water under chiral Lewis acid catalysis, demonstrating how stereochemical information can be translated into enantioenriched β-amino alcohols.99 Lizza and Moura-Letts reported a complementary metal-free strategy in which solvent choice directed epoxide opening with primary amines, shifting selectivity control from a metal catalyst to the reaction medium and substrate structure.96

For a cyanobacterial diol platform, an important future direction is to integrate epoxide chemistry into continuous or tandem processing. Du and co-workers used continuous-flow biocatalysis for epoxide opening with amines, suggesting a modular downstream option if inhibition by crude-stream components and mass-transfer limitations can be controlled.101 Rajendran later combined nitroarene transfer hydrogenation with epoxide ring opening over Pd/C to form β-amino alcohols in a tandem sequence, illustrating how amine generation and C–N bond formation can be consolidated.102 These studies support the β-amino alcohol pathway while also showing that stereochemical control, catalyst cost, amine excess, and work-up remain decisive process variables.

Evaluation of the complete diol-to-β-amino-alcohol sequence must therefore include diol activation, epoxide formation, nucleophilic ring opening, isolated recovery, and catalyst or ligand removal. Results obtained with cyclic, aromatic, or otherwise structurally different epoxides provide methodological precedents, but direct transfer to a 2,3-BDO-derived epoxide requires experimental verification. Importantly, stereochemical changes should be measured at each stage rather than inferred solely from the stereochemical composition of the starting diol.

Selective oxidation to carbonyl and carboxyl derivatives

Selective oxidation provides access to carbonyl- and carboxyl-containing products with broader synthetic and functional value. Depending on substrate structure and oxidation level, diols can be converted into hydroxy ketones, diketones, aldehydes, and carboxylic acids. These products can serve as intermediates, monomers, heterocycle precursors, and components of redox-active or other functional molecular systems.103–105

The principal challenge in diol oxidation is selectivity. Overoxidation, C–C bond cleavage, and poor functional-group compatibility can reduce synthetic efficiency and increase waste generation. Catalytic aerobic oxidation, acceptorless dehydrogenation, and electrochemical approaches that avoid stoichiometric heavy-metal oxidants are therefore particularly relevant to greener oxidation of renewable diols. Oxidation is most useful when it forms part of a defined upgrading sequence rather than being treated as an isolated conversion step.

Selective oxidation can also be viewed as a branching strategy that converts relatively unreactive diols into carbonyl-containing intermediates suitable for subsequent heterocycle, solvent, flavor-chemical, or polymer synthesis. Sato and co-workers established a general catalytic approach for dehydrogenating 1,2-diols to α-hydroxy ketones over Cu-based catalysts.106 Al-Auda subsequently examined gas-phase dehydrogenation of 2,3-BDO to acetoin over supported copper catalysts and identified support acidity, copper loading, temperature, and hydrogen as variables affecting selectivity.107 Wang et al. produced acetoin from all three 2,3-BDO stereoisomers using Gluconobacter oxydans DSM 2003, showing that whole-cell oxidation can achieve high product concentrations under mild aqueous conditions.108

Carbon-retaining oxidation and oxidative cleavage address different product objectives. Luo et al. used a cobalt catalyst for aerobic cleavage of 1,2-diols,109 whereas Chakrabarti et al. coupled alcohol dehydrogenation with N-heterocycle formation in water using cooperative iridium catalysis.104 Assessment of selectivity must also consider oxygen or oxidant supply, gas transfer, catalyst stability, and separation of unreacted diol from carbonyl products. Hydrogen management is an additional consideration where relevant. Successful operation in water alone does not establish compatibility with a biological broth.

Acetalization and ketalization for temporary protection and property modulation

Acetalization and ketalization are classical strategies for controlling diol reactivity, tuning molecular stability, and generating protected intermediates for multistep synthesis. Cyclic acetals and ketals can modify lipophilicity, reduce the number of hydrogen-bond donors, and provide access to intermediates that are difficult to obtain through direct functionalization of the parent diol.110,111

From the perspective of green manufacturing, these transformations are most useful when they simplify subsequent chemistry or improve selectivity in later steps. Their practical value therefore depends on whether the protection step reduces the overall complexity of the synthetic route rather than merely introducing an additional operation. For medicinal-chemistry and advanced-material applications, temporary masking of diol functionality can nevertheless be strategically useful when selective downstream transformation would otherwise be difficult.

Acetalization and ketalization can also modify molecular properties rather than serving only as protecting-group reactions. Deutsch et al. showed that acid-catalyst properties governed glycerol conversion to cyclic acetals and played a central role in determining reaction selectivity.112 Vicente et al. converted refined, technical, and crude glycerol with acetone to solketal over sulfonic mesostructured silicas. The crude-feed experiments are particularly informative because inorganic impurities deactivated acid sites even when the target ketalization reaction itself remained efficient.113 Li et al. improved glycerol-to-solketal conversion over mesoporous Lewis-acid silicates such as Hf-TUD-1, linking catalytic performance to acidity, pore accessibility, and the surface environment.114

These protected intermediates can also be used directly in subsequent upgrading. Dodson and co-workers acetylated solketal and glycerol formal over heterogeneous acid catalysts to obtain oxygenated biodiesel additives, showing that acetal and ketal intermediates can be valorized directly rather than necessarily being deprotected.115 Ilgen et al. evaluated solketal, prepared using Amberlyst-46, as an oxygenated gasoline additive, thereby connecting ketalization with subsequent blending performance and fuel standards.116 These examples illustrate that each additional protection or acylation step must provide a sufficient advantage in separability, compatibility, or downstream value to justify the extra operation.

For 2,3-BDO, acetalization and ketalization may serve not only as synthetic transformations but also as reactive-separation strategies that temporarily reduce the hydrophilicity of the parent diol. The overall benefit therefore depends on the carbonyl reagent, equilibrium control, reagent regeneration, and whether the protected intermediate is used directly or hydrolyzed to recover 2,3-BDO. These factors should be considered together when comparing reactive separation with conventional concentration and purification.80

Halogenation and leaving-group installation for further substitution

Halogenation and related leaving-group installation convert relatively unreactive diol-derived substrates into more reactive electrophilic intermediates. Alkyl halides, sulfonates, and other activated derivatives substantially expand the scope of subsequent substitution chemistry, enabling coupling with amines, phenols, thiols, carbon nucleophiles, and polymerizable units. Hydroxyl activation therefore provides an effective bridge between bio-based diol platforms and broader synthetic transformation networks.117–120

The practical value of this strategy depends strongly on reaction selectivity and reagent choice. Competing side reactions, loss of stereochemical integrity, and the use of corrosive or waste-intensive activation reagents can all reduce synthetic efficiency and limit sustainability. Future development should therefore emphasize milder and more selective activation methods and, where possible, integration with subsequent substitution steps to reduce intermediate isolation and purification burdens.

Leaving-group installation remains useful despite these environmental limitations. Morita developed aqueous tosylation and mesylation of primary alcohols promoted by KOH and catalytic amines, showing that sulfonate installation can be performed under more benign reaction conditions while still relying on sulfonyl chloride reagents.118 Petten et al. used ionic liquids as recyclable reagent–solvents for alcohol halodehydroxylation, reducing the use of volatile solvents but introducing life-cycle considerations associated with ionic-liquid synthesis, viscosity, and recovery.119

Classical activation chemistry also provides reliable access to alkyl halides, although reagent hazards and waste formation remain important limitations. De Luca et al. used a TCT/DMF complex to convert alcohols into alkyl chlorides.120 For 2,3-BDO, leaving-group installation is complicated by the presence of two adjacent secondary hydroxyl groups. Selective monoactivation or diactivation must therefore be balanced against competing substitution, elimination, and possible changes in stereochemical configuration. The usefulness of halide- or sulfonate-based intermediates will depend not only on activation yield but also on whether the subsequent substitution can be carried out selectively without excessive salt formation or loss of stereochemical information.

Click-enabled late-stage functionalization

Click reactions provide a modular strategy for incorporating diol-derived molecules into larger functional systems. Once an azide, alkyne, thiol, allyl, or another clickable group has been introduced into a diol-derived substrate, the resulting molecule can be coupled efficiently to polymers, surfaces, biomolecules, or crosslinking units. These reactions are attractive because they are often high yielding, broadly tolerant of functional groups, and well suited to late-stage diversification.121,122

For bio-based diols, click chemistry is generally a downstream derivatization strategy rather than the first upgrading step. It allows a relatively simple platform molecule to be incorporated into a more complex molecular or polymeric architecture. This modularity makes click chemistry particularly relevant to hydrogels, polymer networks, responsive coatings, and advanced biomedical or electrochemical interfaces in which the diol-derived unit serves as one component of a larger structure.123

Click-enabled functionalization therefore usually follows the installation of allyl, vinyl, norbornene, azide, alkyne, or thiol groups. Reviews by Hoyle and Lowe provide the general chemical background for these reactions but do not establish the performance of any particular diol-derived material.121,124 Du et al. used oligo(ethylene glycol)-derived precursors and thiol–ene chemistry to form fully biodegradable antibacterial hydrogels under physiological conditions.125 He and co-workers combined thiol–ene click chemistry with borax–diol interactions to generate self-healing hydrogels.126

Post-functionalization studies further illustrate the value of preserving reactive groups after carbonate or epoxide formation. Li et al. prepared functional polycarbonates through CO2/limonene dioxide copolymerization while retaining pendent epoxide groups for subsequent modification with thiols or carboxylic acids.127 Bailey et al. used thiol–ene modification of hydroxypropylmethylcellulose to tune polymer–nanoparticle hydrogels for therapeutic delivery, demonstrating how renewable hydroxyl-rich scaffolds can be converted into modular materials with adjustable erosion and mechanical properties.128 These examples identify the functions enabled by the specific scaffolds and reactions tested.

For 2,3-BDO, click chemistry would normally require prior installation of a reactive handle such as an alkene, azide, alkyne, or thiol group. Its value should therefore be assessed over the complete sequence from diol functionalization to final coupling rather than from the click step alone. The PEG-, limonene-, and cellulose-based studies provide useful precedents for late-stage functionalization and network construction, but translation to 2,3-BDO will depend on the efficiency of handle installation, purification, and retention of the desired stereochemical information.

Application prospects of upgraded diol derivatives

The value of diol upgrading ultimately depends on whether the resulting derivatives can meet the performance, processing, and cost requirements of specific applications. Diol-derived molecules have been investigated in medicinal chemistry, electrochemical energy-storage systems, polymeric and gel materials, fuels, and other functional products. Because direct application studies of 2,3-BDO derivatives remain limited, these established systems provide useful structure–property relationships and performance benchmarks for guiding 2,3-BDO product development.129–133

The discussion below therefore focuses on the application principles that can be extracted from these studies rather than assuming direct transfer of performance from one molecular scaffold to another. Examples involving related diols and their derivatives are used to identify the molecular features, processing requirements, and performance criteria relevant to each application. Their relevance to 2,3-BDO depends on whether the same functional principle can be reproduced within its C4 vicinal-diol framework while retaining acceptable synthetic efficiency, purity, and application performance.

Chiral pharmaceutical intermediates and medicinally relevant scaffolds

β-Amino alcohols are among the most important high-value products accessible through diol upgrading. These motifs are widely found in bioactive molecules and serve as key intermediates in the synthesis of β-blockers, central nervous system agents, antimalarial candidates, and chiral auxiliaries. Diol-derived epoxides provide an efficient route to such structures because nucleophilic ring opening can introduce nitrogen-containing functionality while generating stereochemically differentiated products.134,135

The pharmaceutical relevance of this route becomes particularly important when the starting diol is stereochemically defined. Enantiomerically enriched or meso diols can be directed through selective ring-closing and ring-opening sequences to generate distinct β-amino alcohol stereoisomers, which may differ in biological relevance or downstream synthetic value. This feature makes 2,3-butanediol particularly interesting because its stereochemical diversity can serve as a design variable rather than merely a structural characteristic.94,97–100

More broadly, renewable chiral intermediates may reduce dependence on petroleum-derived feedstocks while providing opportunities to combine biocatalysis, asymmetric catalysis, and green synthetic design. Their practical value, however, depends on comparison with established industrial standards in terms of step economy, stereoselectivity, purification burden, solvent demand, and overall cost. Fig. 7 compares transition-metal-catalyzed precedents (Fig. 7a and b) with the NaOH-mediated hydrogen-transfer strategy developed by Wang et al. (Fig. 7c). The latter was used to synthesize quinoxalines directly from o-nitroanilines and vicinal diols or α-hydroxy ketones.136 Oxidation of the alcohol component generates a 1,2-dicarbonyl intermediate while simultaneously supplying the reducing equivalents required to reduce the nitro group and form an o-phenylenediamine. Subsequent condensation and aromatization furnish the quinoxaline without a transition-metal catalyst or an externally added oxidant or reductant. The redox-balanced cascade tolerated a broad range of substituents and afforded functionalized quinoxalines in 44–99% yield. In this metal-free route, the diol provides the carbon framework and also serves as the internal hydrogen donor. This combination of redox economy and bond construction improves step efficiency and is particularly relevant to high-value heterocycle synthesis. Nevertheless, the use of strong base and a heated organic reaction medium remains important when assessing the complete environmental profile; solvent substitution, base recovery, and reduced thermal demand would support a more favorable environmental assessment. Related redox-neutral alcohol-functionalization chemistry also provides a precedent for minimizing external oxidants or reductants.137

Epoxide aminolysis provides useful medicinal-chemistry precedents. Yadav et al. applied samarium triflate-catalyzed epoxide opening in syntheses of propranolol, atenolol, and RO363.135 These examples establish reaction methods and medicinally relevant motifs, but they do not demonstrate direct conversion of 2,3-BDO into the named drugs. In particular, an epoxide derived from 2,3-BDO has a different carbon skeleton from the aryloxypropanolamine precursors used for several β-blockers. Any proposed connection therefore requires an explicit synthetic route and accounting of the additional carbon-containing reagents.

Practical application of these routes also depends on scalability and stereochemical control. A practical acetic-acid-mediated epoxide ring-opening synthesis of propranolol and esmolol emphasized high yield, simplified work-up, and reagent recycling rather than methodological novelty alone.138 For a 2,3-BDO-derived chiral feedstock, the closest methodological precedents remain asymmetric epoxide-opening studies because they demonstrate how stereochemical information in a vicinal-diol-derived epoxide can be transferred into a medicinally relevant scaffold.98,99 Xu et al. developed a modular strategy for converting the bulk diols ethylene glycol and 1,3-propanediol into stereodefined, higher-value 1,2- and 1,3-diols.139 As illustrated in Fig. 8a, optically pure diols are established intermediates for pharmaceuticals and chiral ligands, whereas conventional routes commonly rely on asymmetric alkene oxidation, asymmetric epoxide hydrolysis, carbonyl reduction, or resolution of racemic products (Fig. 8b).

To enable direct C(sp3)–H functionalization, the authors temporarily masked the hydroxyl groups as an acetonide. This protection suppressed competing Ni-catalyzed O-arylation and provided the selectivity required for decatungstate-mediated hydrogen-atom transfer and chiral Ni-catalyzed C–C bond formation. Protection, asymmetric arylation, and deprotection were subsequently integrated into a one-pot sequence, furnishing 1-aryl-1,2- and 1-aryl-1,3-diols from the corresponding bulk diols (Fig. 8c). A representative arylation of a preformed acetonide afforded the protected product in 71% yield and 90% ee, while gram-scale transformations retained both efficiency and stereoselectivity. The method therefore demonstrates a plausible route from commodity diols to pharmaceutical building blocks, although the protecting-group operations, nickel and photocatalyst requirements, fluorinated solvent, and photon-transfer limitations remain relevant to its overall green-chemistry assessment.

Taken together, these studies identify two complementary medicinal-chemistry directions relevant to 2,3-BDO: direct transformation of the vicinal-diol framework and conversion through stereodefined epoxide or higher-value diol intermediates. For 2,3-BDO, the main opportunity lies in exploiting rather than merely preserving its stereochemical information. Route development should therefore track stereochemical changes through activation, ring formation or opening, and subsequent functionalization, while evaluating the isolated yield and step economy of the complete synthetic sequence.

Molecular design precedents for lithium-metal battery electrolytes

Electrolyte design provides an important application precedent for diol-derived molecules because etherification, fluorination, and related transformations allow oxygen-donor spacing, molecular polarity, and coordination strength to be adjusted systematically. In lithium-metal batteries, these structural variables influence ion transport, Li+ solvation, oxidative stability, desolvation behavior, and interphase formation.129,140–143

Transfer of these design principles to 2,3-BDO requires attention to the actual carbon skeleton and substitution pattern. Ethylene glycol-, 1,3-PDO-, 1,4-butanediol-, and 2,3-BDO-derived ethers are not structurally interchangeable, even when they contain similar ether functionality. Relevant application benchmarks therefore include ionic conductivity, viscosity, salt solubility, oxidative and reductive stability, Li-metal coulombic efficiency, and full-cell cycling under clearly stated areal capacity and electrolyte loading.

Previous glycol-ether studies establish chain length, ether-oxygen spacing, terminal substitution, and fluorination as key variables linking molecular structure with ion transport and interfacial stability.144–147

Fluorinated ether electrolytes provide the clearest molecular precedents for translating diol-derived ether chemistry into lithium-metal batteries. Amanchukwu et al. showed that fluorinated ethers can combine ionic conduction with improved oxidative stability.129 Related work on sterically tuned ether solvents showed that replacing 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) with the bulkier 1,2-diethoxyethane (DEE) increases the anion-to-solvent ratio in the Li+ solvation shell and improves high-voltage Li-metal cycling.142 Yu et al. further demonstrated a fluorinated 1,4-dimethoxybutane analogue capable of operating with Li metal and high-voltage cathodes under practical cell conditions.148

Yu et al. subsequently used a structure-guided design sequence to balance ion transport, Li+ solvation, and electrode stability in fluorinated ether electrolytes.146 The starting solvent, fluorinated 1,4-dimethoxybutane, showed favorable compatibility with lithium metal but coordinated Li+ too weakly to sustain rapid ionic conduction. Shortening the distance between the ether oxygen atoms produced a 1,2-diethoxyethane framework capable of forming a more favorable chelating environment around Li+ (Fig. 9a–c). Subsequent β-fluorination introduced electron-withdrawing –CF3 groups, giving F3DEE and F6DEE with improved interfacial and oxidative stability. Extensive fluorination, however, weakened ion transport. Replacing selected –CF3 groups with locally asymmetric –CHF2 groups introduced both overall and local dipoles, producing F4DEE and F5DEE as a more balanced compromise between solvation and stability (Fig. 9d and e). With 1.2 M LiFSI, the optimized electrolytes achieved Li‖Cu coulombic efficiencies above 99.5%, including values of 99.9 ± 0.1%, and supported approximately 270 cycles in thin-Li, high-loading NMC811 cells. They also enabled more than 140 cycles in anode-free pouch cells. These results identify fluorine position and local molecular polarity, rather than total fluorine content alone, as important electrolyte-design variables. Nevertheless, fluorinated-solvent synthesis, material intensity, persistence, and end-of-life management remain relevant to any broader sustainability assessment. Lin et al. subsequently compared F1F0, F1F1, and F1F2 ether analogues, showing that increasing fluorination can improve cycling stability but may reduce solvation strength and ionic conductivity.147 More recently, Choi et al. used asymmetric ethers, including fluorinated asymmetric analogues, to accelerate Li redox kinetics; the optimized F3EME electrolyte formed a compact SEI and supported high-rate Li-metal and anode-free pouch-cell cycling.149

Reproduced with permission from ref. 146, copyright 2022 Springer Nature.

Together, these studies identify fluorination and molecular asymmetry as tunable variables in electrolyte design. Huang et al. further paired a fluorinated-ether electrolyte with a salt-philic/solvent-phobic polymer coating, showing that electrolyte formulation and artificial-interphase engineering can be combined.150 Zhang et al. introduced a monofluorinated acetal electrolyte that reduced fluorine content while retaining favorable oxidative stability, Li deposition morphology, and ion transport.151 For electrolyte candidates derived from bio-based diols, these findings indicate that ethers, acetals, and dioxolanes should be evaluated in terms of solvation structure, interphase chemistry, rate capability, and practical cell lifetime rather than renewable-carbon content alone.

A complementary molecular-design example is provided by the C1–C5 dichloroalkane series studied by Wang et al., which showed that carbon-chain length alters Li+ coordination geometry and electrolyte behavior (Fig. 10).152 The selected 1,3-dichloropropane formulation combined weak coordination with a broad liquid range and supported lithium-metal pouch-cell operation. Although these chlorinated diluents are not direct diol derivatives, the study illustrates how relatively small changes in carbon skeleton and donor-site geometry can strongly affect electrolyte performance.

For a proposed renewable electrolyte solvent, the final product specification should define acceptable water content, ionic residues, residual metals, organic contaminants, and solvent assay according to the intended cell formulation. These requirements are distinct from the specification of the upstream diol feedstock, which should instead be defined by the tolerance of the synthetic steps and the ability of subsequent purification to deliver electrolyte-grade material. Material and energy inputs should therefore be evaluated per kilogram of final solvent meeting the required specification rather than by assuming that the incoming biological diol must already be battery-grade.

Diol and polyol design precedents for zinc-ion batteries

A further application opportunity for diol-derived molecules lies in zinc-ion batteries, where aqueous and quasi-aqueous electrolytes often suffer from parasitic reactions, dendrite growth, hydrogen evolution, and interfacial instability. Polyhydroxy compounds can modify the hydrogen-bonding network of water, influence the Zn2+ solvation environment, reduce water activity, and regulate interfacial Zn deposition. Diols and related polyols are therefore being investigated as electrolyte additives, solvent modifiers, or structural components in zinc-based energy-storage systems.153–158

Compared with lithium-based systems, aqueous zinc-ion batteries provide a different environment in which hydrophilic, hydrogen-bond-active molecules can directly affect electrolyte structure. Renewable diol derivatives may therefore function as solvation regulators, components of gel electrolytes, or interfacial modifiers. Further progress requires a clearer structure–property framework that relates molecular structure to Zn2+ solvation, water activity, interfacial reactions, and metal-deposition behavior rather than relying only on empirical additive screening.159

The appropriate validation criteria also differ from those used for lithium-metal batteries. Relevant measurements include Zn/Zn symmetric-cell lifetime, Zn/Cu coulombic efficiency, suppression of hydrogen evolution, corrosion behavior, resistance to electrolyte freezing or drying, full-cell capacity retention, and compatibility with realistic cathode loadings. These measurements are more informative than additive performance under a single mild condition and help assess the relative contributions of solvation/interfacial regulation, viscosity, and water activity.

Aqueous Zn-ion batteries provide a relatively direct setting in which bio-derived alcohols can influence electrolyte behavior because polyols can modify water activity and Zn2+ solvation. Wei et al. used a 1,2-propanediol-containing electrolyte to reduce water activity, regulate Zn2+ solvation, suppress dendritic deposition, and improve low-temperature operation.160 Lin and co-workers combined PEG-200 with benzylidene acetone to suppress zinc dendrites and hydrogen evolution, illustrating how a polyether additive can contribute to interfacial regulation within a formulation containing more than one functional additive.161 Quan et al. used food-grade sorbitol to regulate water activity, suppress corrosion, and improve antifreezing behavior, extending the same general design concept from simple diols to higher polyols.162

The broader additive literature helps distinguish hydrogen-bond effects from coordination and adsorption effects. Gou et al. introduced glycine as a bio-inspired zincophilic additive that reorganizes hydrated Zn2+ solvation and preferentially adsorbs on Zn, providing a non-diol comparator for interfacial stabilization.163 Peng et al. reported methoxy polyethylene glycol as a multifunctional additive whose terminal ether and hydroxyl groups jointly regulate solvation and interfacial stability.164 These studies illustrate how different molecular interactions can contribute to electrolyte performance, while viscosity, molecular-weight distribution, and long-term compatibility remain important variables. Liu et al. interpreted the low-temperature behavior of aqueous Zn-ion battery electrolytes through the coupled regulation of tetrahedral and cation entropies (Fig. 11).165 In this interpretation, tetrahedral entropy reflects disruption of the water hydrogen-bond network and is therefore related to freezing resistance, whereas cation entropy is associated with Zn2+ solvation and desolvation. Introducing 1,3-BDO at an optimized cosolvent mole fraction, xBDO = 0.27, disrupted the extended hydrogen-bond network and decreased the solid–liquid transition temperature from −16.4 to −101 °C. The cosolvent also reorganized the Zn2+ solvation environment, reduced the population of active water molecules, and suppressed hydrogen evolution, corrosion, and dendritic deposition, as represented in Fig. 11. The reported Zn‖Zn voltage profiles further demonstrate the dependence of the interfacial response on electrolyte composition. At 25 °C, with a current density of 1 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 1 mA h cm−2, the formulation with xBDO = 0.27 sustained markedly longer cycling than both the additive-free aqueous electrolyte and the xBDO = 1.0 formulation. In the wider study, the optimized electrolyte enabled Zn‖Zn cycling for more than 4000 h at −40 °C under 0.1 mA cm−2/0.1 mA h cm−2 and supported PANI‖Zn full-cell operation from −40 to 60 °C. Thus, in the tested formulation, 1,3-BDO functions as both an antifreezing cosolvent and a regulator of Zn2+ interfacial chemistry. Its relatively high concentration, viscosity, separation requirements, and feedstock origin nevertheless remain relevant when assessing the broader environmental implications of using the molecule in this role.

Regulation of Zn deposition by 1,3-butanediol in aqueous Zn-ion battery electrolytes: proposed interfacial processes without and with 1,3-BDO at high and low temperatures. Reproduced with permission from ref. 165, copyright 2025 Wiley-VCH.

The cosolvent in Fig. 11 is 1,3-butanediol, a constitutional isomer of 2,3-BDO. The study demonstrates a diol structure–property relationship for use as an electrolyte cosolvent, but it does not establish cyanobacterial production of 1,3-butanediol or equivalent behavior of the two isomers. Any proposed substitution of one isomer for the other therefore requires direct comparison of concentration, viscosity, solvation behavior, and impurity effects.

Diol-related hydrogels and hydrogel-supported photosynthesis

Hydrogels and related soft materials represent another important application direction for diol-derived chemistry because hydroxyl-rich building blocks can participate in hydrogen bonding, dynamic covalent interactions, supramolecular assembly, and polymer-network formation. Diol derivatives can be incorporated into such systems as crosslinkers, flexible spacers, pendant functional groups, or precursors to clickable monomers. Depending on molecular architecture and network design, these materials can be developed for biomedical devices, ionic conductors, and drug-delivery systems.125,126,128,159,166

The appeal of renewable diol feedstocks in this area arises from the combination of biogenic origin and structural tunability. Through oxidation, etherification, click-type modification, or protection–deprotection strategies, properties such as water affinity, mechanical compliance, dynamic reversibility, and ionic transport can be adjusted systematically. When combined with electrolyte salts, such networks may also function as gel electrolytes.159 Conducting polymers such as PEDOT provide related precedents for soft bioelectronic materials.167,168

These hydrogel studies illustrate distinct roles for hydroxyl-rich components: PEG-derived networks provided biodegradable antibacterial materials,125 whereas reversible borax–diol interactions supported self-healing behavior.126 Fu et al. prepared injectable hyaluronic acid/PEG hydrogels through strain-promoted azide–alkyne cycloaddition, showing how hydroxyl-rich biopolymers and PEG segments can be incorporated into bioorthogonal gel networks.166

Recent studies on delivery systems and elastomeric materials also help define the relevant validation criteria for soft materials. Bailey et al. modified hydroxypropylmethylcellulose through thiol–ene chemistry to tune polymer–nanoparticle hydrogels for therapeutic delivery, connecting renewable hydroxyl-rich scaffolds with changes in erosion, mechanical behavior, and in vivo retention.128 Studies of poly(glycerol sebacate) further show that glycerol-based ester networks can combine elasticity, degradability, and tissue-compatible mechanical properties.131 These polyol- and polymer-based examples motivate comparisons of modulus, swelling, degradation products, sterilization stability, and biological response, but they do not by themselves establish a route from the cyanobacterial diols considered here.

In a distinct use of hydrogels as cultivation matrices, Taylor et al. investigated whether photosynthetic cyanobacteria could remain viable and productive after immobilization in mechanically robust, printable hydrogel matrices (Fig. 12).169 Among the three candidate networks evaluated, S. elongatus PCC 7942 remained viable and metabolically active only in the bovine-serum-albumin-conjugated PEGDA material, showing that matrix compatibility cannot be inferred from hydrogels developed for heterotrophic organisms. When a 2,3-butanediol-producing strain was encapsulated in this network, the resulting cell-laden hydrogel generated 719 mg L−1 2,3-butanediol over four days under continuous illumination. Fluorescence imaging qualitatively indicated retained chlorophyll fluorescence and confirmed physical retention, while mechanical measurements showed that incorporation of the cells increased the compressive modulus and that subsequent accumulation of 2,3-butanediol reduced it. The cell-laden hydrogel therefore functioned as an immobilized photosynthetic biocatalyst and also exhibited a mechanical response to product accumulation. Its practical sustainability will depend on construct reuse, photon and CO2 transport, product diffusion, volumetric productivity, polymer synthesis, and recovery of the hydrogel components after loss of biological activity.

For hydrogels, the most useful benchmarks are application-specific and include swelling ratio, modulus, toughness, fracture behavior, self-healing kinetics, degradation or erosion profile, ionic conductivity where relevant, cytocompatibility for biomedical applications, and long-term stability in the intended medium. Diol-derived building blocks should therefore be evaluated as modular components of the network rather than assumed to be intrinsically superior hydrogel precursors.

Fuels and fuel-precursor molecules from bio-based diols

2,3-BDO and related alcohols have long been considered as fuel candidates or as precursors to hydrocarbons, olefins, and oxygenated fuel additives. Through dehydration, oligomerization, hydrogenolysis, and related catalytic transformations, these molecules can be converted into products with improved volatility, higher energy density, or more favorable combustion behavior. Although fuel-oriented applications generally offer lower margins than pharmaceutical or energy-storage materials, they remain relevant because they can accommodate large production volumes and may contribute to the development of sustainable aviation fuels and other transportation fuels.19,170–172

For cyanobacterial production platforms, however, direct fuel applications need to be considered in the context of the stringent techno-economic requirements of large-volume fuel markets. Volumetric productivity and separation efficiency become particularly important at this scale. In many cases, a more realistic near-term option may be selective upgrading of biologically produced diols into higher-value intermediates that subsequently enter fuel-oriented catalytic processing, rather than direct use of the diols as fuels. Such a staged valorization strategy may provide greater economic resilience while renewable production systems continue to improve in efficiency and maturity.173

The fuel relevance of 2,3-BDO depends on the sequence of reactions used to generate hydrocarbons and related products. Harvey et al. converted 2,3-BDO into renewable gasoline-range products, solvents, and fuel additives, providing a direct product-level demonstration for this C4 diol.132 Adhikari et al. later converted 2,3-BDO directly to butene-rich C3+ olefins over Cu-modified 2D pillared MFI, showing that catalyst architecture can control both olefin selectivity and catalyst stability during the conversion process.174 Cordon et al. used reversible dioxolane formation to address the difficult separation of 2,3-butanediol from dilute aqueous streams (Fig. 13).175 Condensation of 2,3-butanediol with an aldehyde first generates a more hydrophobic dioxolane intermediate, thereby coupling reactive separation with subsequent catalytic upgrading. Conversion over Cu/ZSM-5 at 448–523 K proceeded through a reaction network involving ether cleavage, hydrogenation, and dehydration. At 523 K, the process afforded a 96% yield of C3+ alkenes, compared with 89% for direct 2,3-butanediol conversion over the same catalyst. Complete dioxolane conversion and maximum alkene selectivity were achieved between 498 and 523 K without extensive over-hydrogenation, while aldehyde identity and reaction temperature enabled partial control over the resulting alkene distribution. Catalyst deactivation was also minimized when the complete reaction network was operated above 498 K. This study shows how temporary derivatization can reduce the need to isolate a highly hydrophilic diol completely; however, the overall benefit depends on aldehyde recovery, hydrogen consumption, catalyst lifetime, and closure of the reactive-separation loop.

Studies using real fermentation streams provide stronger evidence for process integration than model-substrate catalysis alone. Affandy et al. demonstrated an integrated route from lignocellulosic biomass to a sustainable aviation fuel blendstock through a 2,3-butanediol intermediate (Fig. 14).170 A broth containing approximately 90 g L−1 2,3-butanediol was produced at 100 L scale and conditioned by nanofiltration, which reduced the measured impurity content from 4.6 to 0.6 wt%. Additional application prospects of diol-derived molecules

Beyond the major application areas discussed above, diol-derived molecules can also serve as specialty solvents, electronically active monomers, coating precursors, and other functional intermediates. Such applications are particularly relevant to 2,3-BDO when they exploit its vicinal-diol structure or stereochemical information sufficiently to justify the additional upgrading and purification steps.176

Cyclic-carbonate functionality provides additional opportunities for solvent applications. Parker et al. showed that ethylene and propylene carbonate could replace dipolar aprotic solvents in Heck reactions, providing a specific solvent-replacement role for cyclic carbonates beyond their use in batteries and polymers.177 Beattie et al. demonstrated proline-catalyzed amination reactions in cyclic carbonate solvents, further showing that carbonate derivatives can function as reaction media as well as synthetic intermediates.178 In both cases, environmental performance depends on solvent recovery, process compatibility, and the complete material balance rather than on classification as a green solvent alone.

Activation of diol feedstocks also provides access to electronically active and coating-related materials. Caras-Quintero and Bäuerle developed a Mitsunobu cyclization between 3,4-dihydroxythiophene-2,5-dicarboxylate and substituted vicinal diols to prepare structurally diverse EDOT derivatives (Fig. 15).176 Diisopropyl azodicarboxylate and tri-n-butylphosphine promoted closure of the alkylenedioxy ring in THF at 40 °C for 48 h, furnishing mono- and disubstituted EDOT dicarboxylates. The EDOT dicarboxylates were isolated in 20–80% yield, with the disubstituted derivatives giving 20–67% yield. Polymerizable EDOT monomers required subsequent saponification and decarboxylation. The method also provided the first chiral EDOT derivatives and transferred the stereochemical information of the starting diol without detectable racemization in the reported study. This result establishes a direct connection between stereodefined diol substrates and electronically active monomers whose substituents can be used to tune the solubility, processability, and electronic behavior of the resulting conducting polymers. Related double-Mitsunobu routes to EDOT and ProDOT derivatives were reported by Zong et al.179 The approach is most credible for high-value, low-volume applications because the stoichiometric azo and phosphine reagents, THF, and associated by-products impose substantial material and waste burdens.180 Catalytic, recyclable, or electrochemical activation could support a more sustainable large-scale process. Key challenges and future directions for integrated CO2-to-functional-molecule platforms

Despite advances in cyanobacterial CO2 conversion to diols, the stages linking photosynthetic carbon fixation to specification-defined functional products remain largely disconnected. Upstream productivity, recovery, upgrading chemistry, and application performance are often optimized separately, even though constraints at one stage determine the requirements of the next. The central challenge is therefore not only to improve individual operations, but also to establish compatibility across the complete CO2-to-product sequence.

Improving upstream carbon fixation and diol productivity

The first major challenge remains upstream diol productivity. Current cyanobacterial systems are still constrained by modest titers, low volumetric productivity, limited long-term stability, and insufficient robustness under process-relevant conditions. Further improvement will require coordinated advances in chassis selection, carbon allocation, cofactor balance, product export, and long-term pathway stability rather than pathway overexpression alone.

Productivity must also be evaluated under realistic cultivation conditions. Final titer alone is insufficient because broth concentration, cumulative production, interval productivity, and whole-run productivity describe different aspects of process performance. Future studies should therefore report cultivation duration, carbon input, gas utilization, reactor scale, and consistent volumetric or areal productivity metrics. Fast-growing strains and high-cell-density cultivation strategies are most relevant when product-specific gains can be maintained under realistic light distribution, gas transfer, oxygen removal, and contamination constraints.

For diol production, pathway design should additionally address cofactor demand, competing carbon sinks, by-product formation, and, where relevant, stereoisomer composition. This is particularly important for 2,3-BDO, for which the relative formation of meso and enantiomeric products directly affects downstream value. Dynamic regulation of intracellular flux may therefore provide advantages over constitutive overexpression by allowing growth, carbon storage, and product formation to be balanced across the cultivation cycle.

Developing selective upgrading chemistry and greener processing routes

The second major challenge lies in translating established diol chemistry into selective and practical upgrading routes. For 2,3-BDO in particular, reactions demonstrated with other diols or prepared intermediates provide useful chemical precedents, but their value depends on whether comparable selectivity can be achieved with the actual C4 vicinal-diol substrate. Downstream development should therefore focus not only on identifying feasible transformations, but also on defining which reaction principles can be transferred without excessive activation, protection, or purification requirements.

Route evaluation should consider the complete synthetic sequence rather than an isolated reaction step. Relevant criteria include conversion, selectivity, isolated recovery, catalyst loading and lifetime, solvent and reagent consumption, impurity tolerance, and the material requirements of work-up and purification. Selective catalytic transformations that minimize stoichiometric activating reagents and tolerate biologically derived feed streams are particularly desirable because dilute aqueous diol streams can otherwise transfer a substantial burden to separation and conditioning.

Process intensification can further improve route efficiency. Telescoped reaction sequences, reactive separation, in situ product removal, and continuous-flow upgrading may reduce repeated isolation and solvent use when the individual operations are mutually compatible. Catalytic selectivity, feed quality, solvent choice, and separation requirements should therefore be developed together rather than optimized independently.

Strengthening end-use validation and supporting engineering scale-up

A third major challenge is the gap between molecular design and application-level validation. For 2,3-BDO, application studies based on other diols, constitutional isomers, or related functional molecules are valuable for generating structure–property hypotheses, but they do not establish equivalent performance of a 2,3-BDO-derived product. Promising candidates should therefore be synthesized through explicit routes and benchmarked directly against relevant incumbent materials or molecular comparators.

The required validation depends on the intended application. Pharmaceutical intermediates require defined molecular structure and stereochemistry; electrolyte molecules require controlled purity together with transport, interfacial, and cell-level measurements; hydrogel or polymer systems require mechanical, transport, and stability data; and fuel-oriented products require sustained catalytic performance, product separation, and compliance with. Relevant fuel specifications manufacturability, impurity tolerance, regulatory constraints, and supply-chain compatibility should be considered alongside laboratory performance when assessing scale-up potential.

From fragmented case studies to integrated value-chain design

A further challenge is the limited integration of biological production, separation, synthetic chemistry, and application development. Cyanobacterial biologists, reactor engineers, synthetic chemists, and application researchers often optimize different parts of the same value chain independently. As a result, upstream products may be developed without a defined downstream specification, while proposed derivatives may have no experimentally demonstrated route from the available biological feedstock.

A more integrated strategy can begin with the intended application and work backward to define the required molecular structure, stereochemical composition, purity, production scale, upgrading chemistry, recovery sequence, and cultivation conditions. Progress can then be assessed through explicit decision points based on sustained productivity, overall product recovery, catalyst durability, and application performance. Techno-economic analysis and life-cycle assessment should use the same process boundaries and carry uncertainty in these measurements into the final evaluation. Conclusions and outlook

Cyanobacterial photosynthetic biomanufacturing provides a route for converting inorganic carbon into a broad range of chemicals, including sugars, alcohols, organic acids, terpenoids, fatty-acid derivatives, and diols. Among the reported diols, 1,2-PDO, 1,3-PDO, and 2,3-BDO represent different development cases. 1,3-PDO provides an established example of biological diol production and downstream utilization. By contrast, 2,3-BDO combines a vicinal-diol framework with biologically accessible stereochemical diversity, highlighting the need for substrate-specific upgrading strategies. This review therefore treats 1,3-PDO as an important benchmark and uses established chemistry and applications of related diols as precedents for identifying development opportunities for 2,3-BDO.

At the biological level, sustained diol production remains limited by carbon allocation, cofactor demand, competing pathways, product export, and long-term strain stability. Reported performance must also be interpreted according to the actual cultivation mode because photoautotrophic production, photomixotrophy, and two-stage processes involve different carbon inputs and production intervals. For 2,3-BDO, future pathway engineering should improve not only titer and productivity but also by-product control and stereoisomer composition, since these attributes influence the value and suitability of the downstream feedstock.

Photobioreactor operation determines whether strain-level improvements can be maintained under process-relevant conditions. Light distribution, inorganic-carbon delivery, oxygen removal, temperature, pH, and hydrodynamics influence both cellular productivity and the composition of the harvested stream. Reactor studies should therefore report cultivation duration, gas conditions, photon supply, volumetric or areal productivity, and carbon balances with sufficient detail to distinguish genuine improvements in carbon conversion from differences in operating conditions.

Recovery provides the critical interface between biological production and chemical upgrading. Cyanobacterial diols are expected to enter downstream processing as dilute aqueous streams containing salts, nutrients, residual biomass components, and other low-molecular-weight species. Clarification, concentration, desalting, and purification can therefore introduce substantial product loss and utility demand. The required extent of purification should be defined by the tolerance of the subsequent chemistry or application rather than by an assumed universal purity target. Comparative testing of purified diol, representative model streams, and authentic biological material will be important for determining which impurities must be removed and whether partial purification is sufficient.

The upgrading literature shows that carbonation, etherification, epoxide-mediated derivatization, selective oxidation, acetalization or ketalization, leaving-group installation, and click-enabled functionalization can provide distinct routes from simple diols to higher-value products. Because direct 2,3-BDO studies remain limited for several reaction families, established chemistry of 1,2-PDO, 1,3-PDO, glycerol, ethylene glycol, and related intermediates provides a useful development map rather than direct evidence of equivalent 2,3-BDO behavior. Transferability must therefore be judged from hydroxyl-group position, substitution pattern, steric environment, stereochemistry, and the compatibility of the actual feed with the reaction system.

Application studies likewise provide design principles rather than automatic validation of a 2,3-BDO-derived product. Pharmaceutical applications highlight the value of stereochemical control; electrolyte studies show how oxygen-donor arrangement, polarity, and molecular substitution govern transport and interfacial behavior; hydrogel studies identify mechanical and transport criteria for functional networks; and fuel studies demonstrate the importance of catalyst lifetime, recovery, and final product specification. These precedents are most useful when they are translated into explicit structure–property hypotheses that can be tested directly with 2,3-BDO-derived molecules.

The environmental and economic performance of a cyanobacterial diol platform must ultimately be assessed across the complete sequence from carbon fixation to a specification-compliant product. High biological productivity can be offset by dilute product concentration, extensive water removal, solvent-intensive purification, catalyst replacement, or reagent consumption during upgrading, whereas an efficient chemical route may still be limited by insufficient biological supply or demanding feed specifications. Meaningful comparison therefore requires consistent functional units and reporting of carbon efficiency, overall recovery, solvent and reagent make-up, water and energy use, catalyst lifetime, waste generation, and final product quality within a common process boundary.

Progress toward an integrated CO2-to-functional-molecule platform will depend less on maximizing any single metric than on improving compatibility between successive stages. Chassis and reactor design should consider the requirements of product recovery; recovery should be matched to the tolerance of the downstream chemistry; and target applications should define the molecular structure, stereochemical composition, purity, and scale required from the upstream process. Within this framework, 1,3-PDO provides an established benchmark for biological diol utilization, whereas 2,3-BDO represents a stereochemically distinctive platform whose future value will depend on coordinated advances in production, recovery, upgrading, and application validation.
