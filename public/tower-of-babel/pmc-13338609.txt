Tall Cell Carcinoma With Reversed Polarity of the Breast: A Case Report
Authors: Yufei Xue, Weiwei Wang, Zhanguo Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tall cell carcinoma with reversed polarity (TCCRP) of the breast is a rare malignant neoplasm that shares morphological similarities with the tall-cell variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma, which may lead to diagnostic confusion. Immunohistochemical and molecular analyses are essential to confirm mammary origin and exclude metastatic thyroid carcinoma. Herein, we report a case of a 62-year-old woman, who was diagnosed with TCCRP based on characteristic histomorphological features and immunohistochemical findings in a breast mass. Despite exhibiting a triple-negative immunophenotype, TCCRP is associated with an indolent clinical course. Accurate diagnosis requires integration of morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecular features, which are critical for appropriate classification and may help support a more conservative management approach.

INTRODUCTION

Tall cell carcinoma with reversed polarity (TCCRP) is a rare subtype of breast cancer. In 2003, Eusebi et al. [1] first described five cases of breast tumors resembling the tall cell variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma. These tumors were characterized by mitochondria-rich columnar cells arranged predominantly in solid nests with delicate fibrovascular cores, resulting in a solid–papillary growth pattern. In the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Breast Tumours (2019), TCCRP was officially recognized as a distinct histological subtype of breast cancer [2]. Herein, we report a case of a 62-year-old woman with a breast mass detected on mammography, ultrasonography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which was subsequently diagnosed as TCCRP based on morphological and immunohistochemical evaluation.

CASE REPORT

A 62-year-old woman presented with a right breast mass that had been progressively enlarging over approximately two years. The patient initially noticed a painless, palpable, date-sized lesion. There was no associated skin erythema, ulceration, nipple discharge, nipple retraction, or systemic symptoms such as cough or bone pain.

Breast ultrasonography revealed an irregular hypoechoic nodule with spiculated margins located at the eight o’clock position in the right breast, approximately 1.4 cm from the nipple. The lesion measured 2.6 × 1.8 × 2.3 cm, extended posteriorly into the fatty layer, demonstrated heterogeneous internal echogenicity, and showed abundant vascularity on color Doppler flow imaging (Figure 1A). Based on these features, the lesion was classified as Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) category 4B, prompting hospital admission for further evaluation and management.

Mammography demonstrated an irregular high-density mass measuring 1.9 × 2.3 cm in the upper outer quadrant of the right breast, also classified as BI-RADS category 4B (Figure 1B). Breast MRI revealed a round mass with spiculated contours and obscured margins in the right upper outer quadrant. The lesion exhibited marked enhancement on post-contrast images and a washout kinetic pattern on dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging (Figure 1C), as well as restricted diffusion, appearing hyperintense on diffusion-weighted imaging and hypointense on the apparent diffusion coefficient map (Figure 1D and E).

Gross examination revealed a well-circumscribed, firm, gray-white tumor nodule measuring 2.2 × 2.0 × 1.5 cm within a 3.0 × 2.5 × 1.7 cm tissue specimen. Histological examination revealed a tumor with a nodular growth pattern, partially composed of cystic areas. The cystic spaces contained amorphous, eosinophilic, colloid-like material resembling thyroid follicular colloid and were lined by epithelium exhibiting papillary hyperplasia (Figure 2A). The tumor cells were tall columnar to cuboidal, with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. The nuclei were round to oval with smooth nuclear membranes. The papillary structures displayed reversed nuclear polarity, characterized by nuclear positioning away from the basement membrane toward the luminal surface (Figure 2B).

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed strong and diffuse positivity for cytokeratin 5/6 (CK5/6; Figure 2C), calretinin (Figure 2D), E-cadherin, as well as focal positivity for gross cystic disease fluid protein 15 (GCDFP-15; Figure 2E). The tumor cells were negative for p63 (Figure 2F), estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2. The Ki-67 proliferation index was 5%. Molecular analysis identified an IDH2 R172 mutation and PIK3CA mutations.

Collectively, the histological, immunohistochemical, and molecular findings supported a diagnosis of invasive breast carcinoma consistent with TCCRP.

The patient underwent surgical excision of the breast lesion with sentinel lymph node biopsy, which revealed no metastasis. Given the triple-negative immunophenotype, adjuvant chemotherapy was administered after multidisciplinary discussion. The patient was followed for 15 months, with no evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis.

DISCUSSION

TCCRP is a rare breast tumor originally described by Eusebi et al. [1] in 2003. Owing to its architectural and cytomorphological resemblance to the tall cell variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma, it was initially termed the tall cell variant of papillary breast carcinoma. However, affected patients lack a history of thyroid neoplasms, and subsequent studies have demonstrated distinct immunophenotypic and molecular profiles. Accordingly, in the 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Breast Tumors (2019), TCCRP was formally recognized as a distinct histological subtype of invasive breast carcinoma [2].

Clinically, TCCRP predominantly affects middle-aged to elderly women, with a reported mean age of approximately 64 years. Most patients present with a painless breast mass detected by palpation or imaging [3]. In the present case, multimodal imaging findings, including an irregular hypoechoic mass on ultrasonography and restricted diffusion with washout enhancement on MRI, were suggestive of malignancy. Although breast MRI has been reported less frequently in the literature [4], the imaging features in our case were consistent with previously described presentations, supporting a diagnosis of invasive carcinoma. Notably, the absence of axillary lymphadenopathy and the relatively well-circumscribed appearance are compatible with the indolent clinical behavior characteristic of TCCRP [567].

Histologically, TCCRP closely resembles the tall cell variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma, exhibiting solid and papillary growth patterns, delicate fibrovascular cores, and follicle-like structures containing eosinophilic, colloid-like material. Tumor cells are typically tall, columnar, or cuboidal, with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. The most distinctive diagnostic feature is reversed nuclear polarity, with nuclei oriented away from the basement membrane toward the luminal surface. Additional findings may include nuclear grooves and intracytoplasmic pseudoinclusions, whereas a desmoplastic stromal reaction is usually absent [8].

Immunohistochemically, TCCRP demonstrates expression of mammary epithelial markers, such as GCDFP-15, GATA-binding protein 3, mammaglobin, and E-cadherin, confirming its mammary ductal origin and helping to exclude invasive lobular carcinoma. Despite its consistent triple-negative immunophenotype, TCCRP shows a low Ki-67 proliferation index, reflecting its indolent biological behavior. Tumor cells frequently express CK5/6 and calretinin, the latter being an unusual finding in low-grade breast carcinomas and a useful ancillary diagnostic marker for this entity [910].

At the molecular level, TCCRP lacks RET rearrangements and BRAF mutations, further distinguishing it from papillary thyroid carcinoma [11]. Instead, most cases harbor IDH2 hotspot mutations, most commonly at codon R172, which are considered a defining molecular feature of this tumor [10]. IDH2 mutations are thought to play a role in the development of reversed nuclear polarity and often co-occur with alterations in the PIK3CA signaling pathway [12]. In this context, IDH2 immunohistochemistry may serve as a sensitive screening tool, whereas molecular testing provides definitive confirmation [8].

Notably, approximately 5% of patients receive neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). The limited use of chemotherapy reflects the indolent nature of TCCRP, although it is classified as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). NAC is administered based on individual tumor characteristics and clinical risk factors and is primarily indicated for patients with large tumors (> 2 cm), lymph node involvement, or histologically aggressive features, such as elevated Ki-67 expression (> 10%) [13].

Currently, there is no evidence supporting routine escalation (e.g., intensive chemotherapy for all TNBC subtypes) or consensus on uniform de-escalation (e.g., universal omission of chemotherapy) in the systemic management of TCCRP. Current practice relies on individualized decisions based on limited case experience.

In this context, NAC was administered to this patient after multidisciplinary team consultation, given the large tumor volume.

In summary, TCCRP is a rare and distinctive subtype of invasive breast carcinoma characterized by unique morphological, immunophenotypic, and molecular features, as well as a generally favorable clinical outcome. Recognition of this condition is essential to avoid diagnostic pitfalls and ensure appropriate clinical management.

TCCRP is a rare breast tumor with distinct pathological features. It is typically characterized by a triple-negative immunophenotype, reversed nuclear polarity, and IDH2 gene mutations. Despite being classified as TNBC, TCCRP generally exhibits indolent biological behavior and a favorable clinical prognosis. Accurate diagnosis requires the integration of histological features, immunohistochemical findings, and molecular testing to distinguish TCCRP from morphologically similar lesions, such as metastatic thyroid carcinoma. Recognition of this entity is important for clinical decision-making, as it may help avoid unnecessary systemic therapy and support a more precise and conservative management approach.
