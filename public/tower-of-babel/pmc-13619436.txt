Clinical features of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis: case reports and literature review
Authors: Tao Zhang, Hong Liang Gao, Hua Liu, Song Kai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to characterize Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis based on our experience and literature review.MethodsBy reviewing all reported cases of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis since the 21st century, we characterized the exposure history, clinical presentations, treatment strategies, and prognostic outcomes.ResultsFifty cases of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis were identified. The mean age was 63.4 ± 15.7 years, and 86% (43/50) of the participants were male. Of the 50 patients, 21 had a history of contact with cattle, sheep, or wild animals, and of the 43 patients, 26 had vascular comorbidities. Low back pain (39/45) was the most common symptom. The lumbar spine was the most susceptible region, with the L4–L5 segment being the most frequently involved site. Of the 47 patients, 39 had multivertebral infections, and the majority also had psoas abscesses (25/41). The positive rate of next-generation sequencing (NGS; 7/7) in spinal lesion tissues was the highest, followed by that of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection (26/30). The most common treatment regimen was doxycycline combined with hydroxychloroquine (31/49). The proportions of patients with fever (36% vs. 5.9%) and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP; 90% vs. 41.7%) levels were higher in the group of patients with vertebral osteomyelitis and those with concurrent vascular defects (p < 0.05). However, separate comparative analyses of the two subgroups showed no significant difference in overall outcomes (p > 0.05).ConclusionQ fever vertebral osteomyelitis should be included in the differential diagnosis, particularly in older men and patients with concurrent vascular defects. PCR or NGS performed on local specimens provides critical advantages for rapid pathogen identification. Doxycycline combined with hydroxychloroquine remains the most used treatment regimen at present.

Introduction

In recent years, the incidence of spinal infections has increased due to the growing number of older patients with chronic diseases, as well as those with immunocompromised conditions, those using steroids, drug abusers, and patients undergoing invasive spinal procedures (1, 2). The specific pathogens causing spinal infections include not only Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Brucella species but also common causative bacteria of pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis, such as Staphylococcus aureus (57–58%), Escherichia coli (1.05–16%), Streptococcus spp. (6.7–11%), and anaerobic bacteria (3%) (3, 4). However, cases of vertebral osteomyelitis caused by Coxiella burnetii are extremely rare.

Coxiella burnetii, a Gram-negative obligate intracellular bacterium, is the pathogen of Q fever. Typically, Q fever presents in two clinical forms: the acute disease, which is more common and often asymptomatic, and persistent focalized infection (chronic Q fever), which occurs in 4–5% of patients and is generally associated with a poor prognosis (5). The manifestations of chronic Q fever include endocarditis, vasculitis, and osteoarthritis (5, 6). Among the osteoarticular manifestations, spondylodiscitis or vertebral osteomyelitis is the most common form (7).

Coxiella burnetii infection is common in humans and various animal species; however, it has not been fully recognized in many countries, thereby posing a significant threat to human and animal health (8). Due to its rapid spread and high infectivity, C. burnetii infection is a public health, veterinary, and economic concern. At present, Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis may be an under-recognized clinical condition (9), and research on Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis is primarily based on individual case reports, with no case series studies. Therefore, we analyzed the clinical features of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis to enhance clinicians’ comprehensive understanding of this disease.

Methods

Case 1

A 66-year-old man, with no travel history, presented with a 6-month history of low back pain in the absence of constitutional symptoms such as fever, night sweats, weight loss, or nocturnal back pain. The comorbidities included hypertension, a history of left lower extremity deep vein thrombosis, and inferior vena cava filter implantation. The patient lived in an urban area and had no contact with cattle or sheep. The body temperature was 36.3 °C, and there was tenderness upon palpation of the lumbar spine. The visual analog scale (VAS) score for the intensity of lumbar pain, graded on a 0–10 scale, was 5 out of 10, indicating moderate pain. Laboratory investigations revealed a white blood cell count (WBC) of 2.81×109/L, C-reactive protein (CRP) of 22 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 37 mm/h, and procalcitonin (PCT) of 0.24 ng/mL. The serological test for Brucella and the interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) were both negative. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of chronic osteomyelitis at the L4–L5 level, accompanied by psoas abscesses. Computed tomography (CT) scans revealed bone destruction of the L4 and L5 vertebrae (Figure 1). No abnormalities were found on echocardiography and vascular ultrasound examinations. The preoperative diagnosis was lumbar osteomyelitis, although the specific pathogen remained unidentified. Due to severe low back pain that significantly affected his daily life activities and work, the patient underwent debridement and oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF) surgery. Histopathological examination revealed inflammatory infiltration accompanied by proliferative granulation tissue. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) detected C. burnetii DNA in the spinal lesion tissues; however, the sample culture results were negative. Although serological testing can provide valuable information on the stage of Q fever, this testing method was not available at our hospital during the diagnostic process. Eventually, the patient was diagnosed with Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis, and a chemotherapy regimen of doxycycline (200 mg/day) combined with ciprofloxacin (1,000 mg/day) was planned for at least 18 months. A follow-up Lumbar MRI examination after 6 months of chemotherapy confirmed significant improvement in the previous observed vertebral erosion, with no evidence of acute inflammation or masses. At the last follow-up, 6 months after the surgery, the patient’s clinical condition in terms of infection remained stable.

(D) CT scan at 6 months postoperatively revealed successful fusion of the intervertebral bone graft. (E) MRI at 6 months postoperatively showed normal isointensity of the L4 and L5 vertebrae.

Case 2

A 56-year-old man with no travel history presented with persistent low back pain for 12 months, accompanied by pain in the left lower limb and intermittent claudication, but without fever, night sweats, or weight loss. His comorbidities included a history of lumbar spinal stenosis. The patient was a cattle attendant (in contact with cattle and sheep) and lived in a rural area. The body temperature was 36.3 °C. Physical examination revealed lumbar tenderness, hypoesthesia of the left lower leg, and a positive Lasègue sign. The VAS scores for the intensity of pain in the lumbar region and the lower limbs were 6 out of 10 and 5 out of 10, respectively. Laboratory investigations revealed a WBC count of 18.2×109/L, CRP of 1.75 mg/L, ESR of 3 mm/h, and PCT of 0.4 ng/mL. The serological tests for Brucella and IGRA were both negative. MRI confirmed the presence of chronic osteomyelitis at the L4–L5 level, along with epidural and right psoas abscesses. CT scans revealed bone destruction at the endplates of the L4 and L5 vertebrae (Figure 2). Echocardiography ruled out endocarditis. The preoperative diagnosis was lumbar osteomyelitis, and because the patient presented with obvious low back and leg pain, debridement and transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) surgery was performed. Histopathological examination revealed necrotic foci in the L4–L5 intervertebral disc sample. To diagnose this unexplained infection, targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) testing and culture were performed on samples collected from the spinal lesion tissue. The tNGS test results revealed that C. burnetii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were detected in specimens collected from the spinal lesion; however, the culture results were negative. Eventually, the patient received a chemotherapy regimen of doxycycline (200 mg/day) combined with ciprofloxacin (1,000 mg/day) for a planned duration of at least 18 months. After 9 months of postoperative chemotherapy, the patient’s clinical symptoms had completely resolved, and he was still undergoing further treatment and follow-up.

(C,D) CT scans showed bone destruction at the endplates of the L4 and L5 vertebrae. (E) The postoperative X-ray imaging demonstrated proper positioning of the internal fixation device and bone graft.

Case 3

A 57-year-old man with a history of percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy presented with low back pain, night sweats, and nocturnal pain for the past 7 months, accompanied by pain and numbness in both lower extremities, but without fever or weight loss. Histopathological examination after lumbar discectomy at another hospital revealed chronic granulomatous inflammation; therefore, the patient received 5 months of empirical anti-tuberculosis treatment postoperatively. However, the low back pain and pain in the bilateral lower extremities did not show significant improvement. The patient was a sheep attendant who lived in a rural area. The body temperature was 36 °C. There was tenderness upon palpation of the lumbar spine, hypoesthesia of the bilateral lower legs, and a positive Lasègue sign. The VAS score for the intensity of lumbar pain indicated a moderate level of 6 out of 10. Laboratory tests revealed a WBC count of 5.37×109/L, CRP of 3.6 mg/L, ESR of 4 mm/h, and PCT of 0.02 ng/mL. The serological tests for Brucella and IGRA were both negative. No pathogenic microorganisms were detected in the blood culture. MRI revealed vertebral osteomyelitis and an epidural abscess at the L5-S1 segment. CT scans showed bone destruction at the posterior margin of the L5 vertebra and the superior margin of the S1 vertebra (Figure 3). No abnormalities were found on echocardiography or vascular echography. Due to ineffective anti-tuberculosis therapy and worsening low back pain, the patient underwent debridement and TLIF surgery. Histopathological examination showed chronic suppurative inflammation with necrosis, accompanied by proliferating granulomas. qPCR detected C. burnetii DNA in the spinal lesion tissues, but the culture results were negative. The diagnosis of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis was made, and treatment with doxycycline (200 mg/day) combined with ciprofloxacin (1,000 mg/day) was initiated for a period of 18 months. During the 6-month follow-up after chemotherapy and surgery, the patient’s clinical symptoms completely resolved, and the MRI demonstrated that the epidural abscess had completely disappeared. After the 24-month follow-up, the patient had fully recovered clinically and radiologically after discontinuation of the treatment.

Study design

The study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (10) to identify studies and describe the screening, inclusion, and exclusion processes in this review.

Literature search

A systematic literature search was performed in PubMed, MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Wanfang databases, using keywords such as “Coxiella burnetii,” “Q fever,” “Spinal infection,” “Vertebral osteomyelitis,” and “Spondylodiscitis.” The researchers independently conducted searches using the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” and screened the literature published between 1 January 2000 and 30 April 2026. Additional relevant studies were identified through manual screening of references.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The diagnostic criteria for Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis (11, 12) were based on the presence of any one of the following items: (1) a positive culture result from spinal lesion samples; (2) a positive PCR (or NGS) test result from spinal lesion samples; and (3) clinical and imaging evidence of spinal infection accompanied by positive serology with phase I IgG antibodies titers of ≥1:800.

This review included studies reporting demographic features, exposure history, symptoms, complications, diagnostic methods, treatment strategies, and outcomes of patients with Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis. Only articles written in English or Chinese with available abstracts were included. Expert opinions, clinical trials, animal studies, comments, letters, meta-analyses, practice guidelines, duplicate publications, and abstracts from scientific meetings were excluded.

Data extraction and quality evaluation

The two researchers independently screened the literature in strict accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus with the third author. The extracted information included the age and gender of the patients, exposure history, comorbidities, clinical presentations, the location of vertebral infection, related diagnostic tests, treatment strategies, and clinical outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical calculations were performed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were analyzed using independent samples t-tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The most common clinical symptom of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis was moderate or uncontrollable back pain (86.7%, 39/45), followed by fever. Eighty-three percent (39/47) of the patients had multivertebral infections, and the majority (61%, 25/41) also had psoas abscesses. Among the affected vertebrae, the L3 vertebra was the most common, but among the involved spinal levels, the L4–L5 level was the most common (Figures 5, 6). In the inflammatory marker test, elevated CRP and ESR were frequently observed, but abnormal WBC counts were relatively rare. The positive rates of blood culture and tissue culture for C. burnetii were extremely low. Thus, the diagnosis of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis was primarily based on PCR/NGS detection in the spinal lesion tissues or positive serology for phase I IgG antibodies; among these diagnostic methods, NGS detection had the highest positive rate (100%, 7/7). In terms of treatment strategies, the most common regimen was doxycycline combined with hydroxychloroquine (63.3%, 31/49), followed by doxycycline combined with ciprofloxacin (10.2%, 5/49). The mean follow-up time was 20.5 ± 22.6 (range 1–84) months, and a majority of the patients achieved favorable outcomes or recovery (Table 3).

Discussion

Q fever is a major global zoonotic disease that poses a threat to both public and veterinary health. C. burnetii has been detected in various hosts, including humans, domestic and wild animals, pets, birds, and arthropods (46). Given the threats posed by C. burnetii, it is crucial to have a comprehensive understanding of its transmission routes and potential sources of infection. In this study, 42% of the patients with Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis had a clear history of contact with cattle, sheep, or wild animals, with cattle and sheep being the most common, and 38% of the patients lived in the countryside. This is broadly consistent with the exposure risk history reported in the literature (47). Not all patients had direct contact with animals, but many lived near dairy farms (48, 49). In a majority of the Q fever outbreak incidents, livestock were the main source of C. burnetii infection, primarily transmitted through inhalation of contaminated particles (46). Therefore, even if most cases did not report contact with cattle, goats, or sheep, nor had any experience living around the farm, clinicians should still consider the possibility of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis.

Q fever can occur in people of all ages, and the age distribution varies slightly across different regions. However, in general, it is still primarily prevalent among the middle-aged and elderly population (47, 50). The average age of patients with Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis in this group is 63.4 ± 15.7 years, which is generally consistent with the mean age of 67 years reported in other studies (35, 42). Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis is more common in male patients, which may be related to occupational differences between genders. The common clinical symptoms of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis include low back pain and fever. However, clinical manifestations still lack specificity and are difficult to distinguish from spinal tuberculosis and brucellar spondylodiscitis. Moreover, Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis usually affects the lumbar spine, with the L4–L5 level being the most common. However, the most frequently involved vertebra is the L3 segment. Regarding the specific locations where Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis is prone to occur, there are no relevant reports in previous literature.

The clinical manifestations, imaging features, and common affected areas of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis are highly similar to those of spinal tuberculosis and brucellar spondylodiscitis. These three conditions are often difficult to distinguish from each other. In the past, serological testing has been the main method for diagnosing C. burnetii infection (51). However, in this study, the serological positive rate of C. burnetii in patients with Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis was not 100%. The indirect immunofluorescence assay for C. burnetii phase I IgG is the major criterion for Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis; however, it is not the definitive criterion (5, 11). During the effective treatment follow-up of patients with Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis, the serological titer is expected to gradually decrease. However, during the treatment and follow-up of chronic Q fever, serological testing for C. burnetii cannot reliably predict disease-related events or treatment failure (52).

The definite criteria for diagnosing C. burnetii osteoarticular infection include positive culture or PCR results from affected bone or biopsy tissue (5, 11). However, in this study, the positive rate of spinal lesion tissue culture was extremely low, at only 5.4%. Although culturing and isolating C. burnetii can provide definite evidence of infection, this process requires a biosafety level 3 laboratory. Due to the complexity of the technique, high infection risk, and low diagnostic rate, it leads to numerous logistical challenges in clinical practice, thereby limiting its application. Therefore, the main diagnostic criterion currently used in clinical practice continues to rely on the positive PCR results for C. burnetii in tissue samples. In this study, the positive PCR detection rate of C. burnetii in tissue samples was as high as 86.7%, while the positive rate of NGS was 100%. PCR is the preferred method for confirming C. burnetii in clinical samples (53, 54). Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) for clinical pathogen detection is becoming more widely available for a variety of sample types and patient indications. The majority of studies have demonstrated that the sensitivity and specificity of mNGS are comparable to those of specific PCR assays, and it can detect more potential pathogens than conventional methods (55). Especially for rare pathogenic bacteria (such as anaerobic bacteria) that cause spinal infections (56), when bacterial culture is difficult to provide a definitive diagnosis, PCR or NGS has significant advantages (55). Furthermore, tNGS is a new method that combines the advantages of PCR and NGS. Through a carefully selected primer pool, tNGS can selectively amplify the target genomic region, enabling the easier detection of target pathogens with high sensitivity while reducing background human DNA and simplifying data complexity. Thus, tNGS is a promising and important tool that can be used for the rapid detection of C. burnetii infection (53, 57).

Doxycycline combined with hydroxychloroquine is the gold standard treatment regimen for persistent focal infections caused by C. burnetii (5, 11, 58, 59). The number of cases of Q fever osteoarticular infections is limited, and the treatment plan is formulated based on expert recommendations; it has been reported that using doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine for 18 months is a good treatment option (29). The current recommendation is that chronic Q fever should be treated for at least 18–24 months (independent of the focus of infection) (9). Apart from the combination of doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine, doxycycline combined with ciprofloxacin is also an effective alternative option (59). The most common regimen in this study was doxycycline combined with hydroxychloroquine, followed by doxycycline combined with ciprofloxacin. Among them, the regimen of doxycycline plus ciprofloxacin was used more frequently in the surgical group. Of course, the three surgical cases in our study may have had a relatively significant impact on this result. Both regimens can achieve satisfactory therapeutic effects. As van Roeden reported, the efficacy of treating chronic Q fever with tetracyclines combined with quinolones is not inferior to that of tetracyclines combined with hydroxychloroquine (60). Currently, the regimen of doxycycline combined with hydroxychloroquine remains the most extensively studied clinical approach for treating chronic Q fever. However, retrospective studies have not corroborated a clear benefit of this combination, particularly in comparison with other regimens (61). At present, the medical community has not reached a consensus on this issue, so conducting high-quality controlled trials is extremely necessary. However, conducting prospective studies on rare diseases is extremely challenging. This challenge extends to the international level because of the rarity, heterogeneity, and the prolonged course of these diseases.

The indications for surgical treatment of spinal lesions include neurological deficits, spine instability and/or deformity, the presence of epidural abscess, and failure of conservative treatment (62). A review of the cases revealed that nearly half of the patients underwent debridement surgery, but there was no significant difference in prognosis and outcomes between the surgical group and the non-surgical group. For Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis, antibiotic treatment remains the cornerstone, while surgery serves as a necessary adjunctive measure. For patients with definite needs, surgical treatment is also a very important supplementary treatment option.

Furthermore, 60.5% of the patients with Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis also had vascular comorbidities, among which AAA was the most common. This is in complete agreement with the data reported by Alder and Liu (7, 42), which indicated that the proportion of patients with osteoarticular involvement due to C. burnetii infection and accompanied by aortic aneurysm was 60–61.5%. The case review in this study revealed that the majority of patients had a history of aneurysms or related surgical procedures before presenting with clinical manifestations related to C. burnetii infection. However, there is also a view that aneurysms are the most common complications of chronic Q fever (63). Currently, there are no clear research reports confirming that patients with aneurysms are more prone to infection with C. burnetii or that patients infected with C. burnetii are more likely to develop aneurysms. Therefore, for patients with spinal infections accompanied by aneurysms, if the specific type of infection cannot be determined, clinicians should highly suspect Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis caused by the rare pathogen C. burnetii as a possibility.

In the subgroup analysis of Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis with vascular lesions versus simple Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis, the results showed no significant differences in treatment strategies and outcomes between the two groups. However, all the deceased patients had vascular lesions. The reported mortality rate of Q fever was 2.0% (47), but the mortality rate of patients with infected aortic aneurysm due to C. burnetii can be as high as 25% (64). The findings of Botelho-Nevers indicate that surgery is crucial in the management of vascular infections caused by C. burnetii, and it has a particularly significant association with recovery (64). Therefore, for patients with Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis accompanied by vascular infections, in addition to antibiotic treatment, surgical treatment of the infected aneurysms or vascular grafts is also an essential management measure.

Limitations

This study is a retrospective study based on existing evidence, and it is subject to publication bias and reporting bias. The included cases are heterogeneous, and many published reports have omitted some clinical variables. Due to the rarity of the disease, robust statistical comparisons were not possible.

Conclusion

Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis should be included in the differential diagnosis, particularly in older men and patients with concurrent vascular abnormalities. PCR or NGS performed on local specimens provides critical advantages for rapid pathogen identification. Doxycycline combined with hydroxychloroquine remains the most used treatment regimen at present.
