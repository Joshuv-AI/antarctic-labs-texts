The experience of setting up midwife-led birth centers in Pakistan: a case study
Authors: Razia Naveed, Zainab Khawaja, Adnan Ahmad Khan, Tazleem Ayaz, Hania Rehman, Saad Ibrahim Rasheed, Ayza Sadaf Khawaja, Kirsty Hughes, Oliva Bazirete, Sofia Castro Lopes, Sabera Turkmani, Andrea Nove, Mandy Forrester, Caroline S. E. Homer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMidwife-led birth centers (MLBCs) exist in Pakistan in various financial and operational capacities. Some operate independently, providing maternity services for a small user fee. Others operate through public-private partnerships involving collaboration between the government and NGOs. Currently, there is insufficient evidence available on the experiences of community midwives as maternal health care providers in MLBCs. There is also a lack of evidence about how midwives can operate in the private sector, although MLBCs are becoming more widespread nationwide. The study aimed to explore how MLBCs operate, to identify innovative approaches to improving women-centred care services.MethodsIn-depth interviews were conducted with 6 stakeholders working in senior or leadership roles and 20 MLBC clients receiving maternity care services. Four Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) were organized with 25 individuals currently working as healthcare workers in MLBCs including midwives and nurses.ResultsFive key themes were identified to describe MLBCs in Pakistan, especially women’s experiences. The themes were (i) Having competent midwives, (ii) Providing comprehensive and accessible services that women need and want, (iii) Providing quality of care, (iv) Being disconnected from the healthcare system and (vi) Managing the cost.ConclusionThis study contributes to the understanding of MLBCs in Pakistan, their functioning, and providing quality, accessible, and affordable maternity care. Midwives provide humane and client-centered care at much lower prices, making their services accessible to a larger audience. The gap for women who want affordable high-quality care but cannot afford the premium fees of the private sector can be filled by establishing MLBCs and integrating them into the national health system. The policymakers and stakeholders should prioritize actionable steps in securing sustainable finance, continued medical, and integration of MLBCs into the larger public health system to harness the full potential of MLBCs in delivering high-quality, safe, and dignified maternity care to all, regardless of socioeconomic status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04703-9.

Introduction

Despite global efforts and commitment to improve maternal and child health, the overall rates of maternal and infant mortality remain high. It is estimated that every two minutes, a woman dies either during pregnancy, childbirth, or the postpartum period. This equates to approximately 287,000 women globally losing their lives due to complexities arising from pregnancy and childbirth [1]. The bulk of maternal mortality occurred within the African and Asian regions. The numbers and trend in maternal mortality indicate a slowdown in progress, jeopardizing the attainment of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by 2030 [1].

Pakistan continues to face high rates of maternal and infant mortality despite the efforts of the Government of Pakistan and international agencies over many decades. The maternal mortality ratio in Pakistan remains high at 186 per 100,000 live births [2]. Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey (PDHS) data show that 44% of births in the 5 years preceding the survey took place in private facilities, and only 22% took place in government facilities [2]. Overall, 69% of these births were attended by a skilled provider; most births were attended by doctors (60%), and only 9% by other skilled healthcare providers. The remaining births (24%) were attended by a traditional birth attendant (TBA) [2]. The Pakistan Social and Living Standard Measurement Survey (PSLM) also shows that 30% of all births in Pakistan happen at home and 38% occur at private facilities [3], a lower rate than in the PDHS, but still a high proportion.

The lack of skilled birth attendants, particularly in rural areas of Pakistan, is a major cause of concern as non-skilled attendant home births are still mostly the norm [4]. The comfort of being able to give birth at home in a supportive and familiar environment is the major reason women choose home births [4]. This boosts women’s confidence and sense of control over their care during the birth experience as evidenced by a critical interpretive synthesis. Other factors include the high cost of care and lack of access to healthcare facilities [5]. Births at public facilities can be unpleasant experiences for women. Poorer women, in particular, report mistreatment, verbal and physical abuse, ineffective communication, loss of autonomy, and lack of supportive care in public facilities [6].

Midwives are professionals who can provide care and support through pregnancy, labour, birth, and the postpartum period. Midwives who subscribe to the midwifery philosophy of care [7] provide care in the light of the ethical principles of justice, equity, and respect for human dignity while using the latest scientific research and evidence [8]. Midwifery-led care enables a convenient, cost-effective, and physiological birthing process. It emphasizes taking care of women’s physiological, psychological, and spiritual health while keeping social elements in consideration [7]. Substantial evidence supports the safety and benefits of midwife-led care throughout pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum phase [9, 10]. In addition, a systematic review showed that women receiving midwife-led continuity of care experienced lower rates of epidural analgesia, instrumental vaginal birth, preterm birth (less than 37 weeks), and miscarriages before 24 weeks of gestation. Moreover, they demonstrated a higher likelihood of having a spontaneous vaginal birth and a positive birth experience [10].

In 2006, the government of Pakistan – with the support of international agencies - introduced community midwives (CMWs) to help address the challenges in providing maternal and newborn care. The program was designed to extend maternal and newborn services to communities by selecting and educating community midwives from their local areas [11]. The thrust of the program aimed to establish home-based clinics to provide maternity services – including skilled birth attendance - to rural women [12]. In 2017 the CMW training program was extended from 18 months to 24 months. This is below the recommended three-year duration for a competency-based midwifery program, yet a CMW’s scope of practice includes a wide range of essential interventions [13]. The quality of care they provide has been found to be as good as or better than other providers of maternity services in Pakistan and better than the other options usually available to rural families [14]. Since 2014, Pakistan also has a two-year post-nursing midwife education program which awards a Bachelor’s degree in midwifery [15], but graduates of this program tend not to practise in rural areas.

Midwife-led units or birth centers are health facilities within which low-risk pregnant women can be cared for. These units are either ‘onsite’ (or ‘alongside’) or ‘freestanding’ (or stand-alone) birth centers [16]. The former co-exist within or beside a hospital or host obstetric unit while the latter are located on a separate site. The Maternal and Child Health Integrated Program (MCHIP) [17], a United States Agency for International Development (USAID) flagship program (2013–2018) focused on delivering comprehensive maternal and child health services in Pakistan’s Sindh province. Originally built around the concept of MLBCs, the program supported 32 MLBCs in the provision of on-the-job training, renovation of centers, and business skills training for midwives. However, the program expanded the MLBC concept to encompass other skilled birth attendants (SBA) including doctors, nurses, and LHVs due to a shortage of midwives. This expansion encouraged women to access quality maternity care across private and public facilities. Throughout the project, MCHIP engaged with 1,108 Maternal and Child Health centers (MNCHs) and 37 Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (CEmONC) centers in the province [17].

Midwife-led care in many high-income countries has been regularized within the healthcare system. However, many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) still lack effective midwife-led care [18]. Currently, MLBCs exist across Pakistan operating in various capacities. These MLBCs have diverse funding sources and operational strategies. Some function autonomously as private entities, providing maternity services to women at minimal fees. Others operate through public-private partnerships with a collaboration between the government and NGOs. These MLBCs tend to be integrated within existing health facilities, but even those that are located within or alongside a public sector facility sometimes depend on external funding and support from NGOs who are working towards making childbirth care more affordable and accessible to underserved communities. For some MLBCs there is direct government support consisting of financial backing to cover infrastructure, operational expenses, and healthcare services, which enables MLBCs provide care that is free at the point of use [11, 19, 20].

Currently, there is insufficient evidence available on the experiences of CMWs as maternal health care providers in MLBCs in Pakistan. There is also a lack of evidence about how midwives can operate in the private sector although MLBCs are becoming more widespread across the country. As part of a larger global study [21] on MLBCs, we conducted a study in four districts of Pakistan where MLBC sites were operating. The study aimed to explore how MLBCs operate, to identify innovative approaches promoting women-centered care services.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive case study was undertaken in four MLBC locations in Pakistan using an Appreciative Inquiry (Ai) Approach [22]. A case study allows an in-depth exploration of a phenomenon in a real-life context without manipulating the variable of interest. It involves data collection by multiple techniques but more commonly qualitative data collection methods such as interviews, focus groups, and observations [23].In our study, the case study approach helped to determine how specific national policies, and protocols on maternal and newborn health care are used, and the extent to which these resources assisted (or the lack of them hindered) the establishment and sustaining of MLBCs.

To explore participants’ experiences and perspectives an Ai approach was employed. This approach serves as an interactive technique designed to kickstart positive conversation surrounding experiences, needs, and proposed solutions [24]. It is a participatory approach that can be applied with study participants to initiate a positive conversation about their experiences, needs, and proposed solutions [25].

Ai is a qualitative research methodology with ‘naturally occurring’ characteristics, as people may naturally tend toward certain thoughts and images that stimulate their interest rather than being directed by the researcher, it is open-ended and allows for a flexible approach based on the needs of the participants [24]. The Ai framework or ‘4D model’ (Discovery–Dreaming–Designing–Destiny), according to McArthur-Blair [26] and Trajkovski [25], begins with appreciating or the ‘Discovery’ phase. This first phase is about learning and remembering the best moments and memories. The second phase, ‘Dreaming,’ expands on these moments to imagine how the situation of interest might look in the future. The Ai process then shifts its focus to ‘Designing’ the plan for future changes and what needs to be done to make their dream a reality (Phase 3), and finally, consenting to everyone’s role in achieving that plan, known as ‘Destiny’ (Phase 4). These four phases were applied to explore the experiences of decision-makers, healthcare workers, and women about MLBCs in the four sites. Participants were involved in all phases as the questions asked reflected all 4 phases of the Ai Process.

The sites were selected considering the following definition of an MLBC: “a dedicated space offering childbirth care, in which midwives take primary professional responsibility for birthing care” [19].

Before initiating the research process, ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) committee at Research & Development Solutions (RADS) Islamabad, Pakistan.

Data collection

To devise data collection tools, the country research team received virtual orientation training led by international consultants, focusing on implementing the Ai approach. The research team developed tools aligning with Networks of Care (NOC) framework [27]. The NOC framework consists of: agreement and enabling environment, operational standards, quality, learning, and adaptation domains which encompass accessibility, care quality, financial support, community engagement, referral systems, and the availability of health service supplies and resources. This framework directed the selection of sites, data collection methods, and initial stages of analysis.

Data collection tools reflected the 4 phases of Ai. The questions asked participants to reflect on their experiences using the 4Ds as a framework.

The study adhered to the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist [28]. In this regard, field notes were taken during the data collection process. The research teams kept daily field notes and reflective journals to record and contextualize information gathered during data collection. Additionally, data saturation was discussed and was reached after 15 interviews with women, however the research team decided to continue with the remaining five [6] clients to get insights from different perspectives.

The participants were purposively recruited through health facilities and healthcare professionals using the snowball sampling technique. Women attending the MLBC over the days the local research teams were present were invited to participate in an interview. Data were collected from three types of informants at each site. (Table 2).

Study participants

Key stakeholders were selected through the snowball technique. Staff and clients were nominated by the MLBC manager/administrator after carefully examining inclusion criteria. Clients (pregnant and postpartum women) interviewed were from the four MLBC sites.

At each site, a duo of researchers—one male and one female—were engaged in data collection. The teams were recruited from the same locality and selected for their proficiency in local languages and comprehensive familiarity with the context. The teams were supervised by a consultant with expertise in maternal health. A semi-structured interview guide was used and open-ended questions were asked to gain in-depth insights from respondents.

The key stakeholders, healthcare workers and clients were interviewed separately. In total, 25 MLBC staff participated in the in-person FGDs including midwives, their assistants, technicians, and dispensers1. All in-depth interviews with clients were conducted in person and in local languages. IDIs were done in the respondent’s preferred language, in-person and online through MS Teams. All key informant interviews, in-depth interviews, and focus group discussions were audio-recorded with the permission of the participants and later transcribed verbatim and translated from the original local languages into English to facilitate the analysis process. A back translation technique was used to identify any discrepancy in the translation of transcripts to avoid data from getting lost in translation. Additionally, for the facilitation of the consultant during the interview process, a translator (expert in the local language) was present to provide clarification for the data component “lost in translation”.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the inductive approach. The inductive approach is a systematic procedure to develop categories from raw data that captures key themes identified from a group of categories with common meanings [29]. As a first step, transcripts were read carefully multiple times by the research team. The team identified themes and subthemes based on the study objectives and the transcripts were then coded using NVivo, a robust software for organizing and categorizing the data, aiding in the systematic analysis [30]. The team independently identified the common meaning segments and labeled them with descriptive coding. Codes were organized into various categories. The research team then jointly reviewed their work and through a consensus identified overarching underlying emerging sub-themes and themes by reviewing coded segments. The team then generated reports and summaries of analysis findings. To promote transparency and collaborative reflexivity, multiple qualitative research analysts were engaged and had regular meetings to discuss differences in interpretations and personal biases during the analysis process. Moreover, other peer experts in social research were engaged to ensure the validity and reliability of the analysis process. The COREQ 32-item Checklist [28] was followed to ensure consistency in reporting.

Findings

The understanding of establishing MLBCs in Pakistan and women’s experiences with midwife-led maternity care are explained under five themes: (i) Having competent midwives, (ii) Providing comprehensive and accessible services that women need and want, (iii) Providing quality care, (iv) Being disconnected from the healthcare system and (vi) Managing the cost.

Having competent midwives

A successful MLBC had competent midwives working within a reliable and effective structure and system. This included midwives who had: a positive, confident, and supportive attitude toward their clients, access to continuing professional development (CPD), effective decision-making skills to refer women and babies when needed, the required competencies to manage complications, compliance with hygiene protocols, effective counseling skills, and up-to-date knowledge through technology inclusion. One midwife said:“A midwife [in an MLBC] should have these 7 skills; take the history, diagnosis, and medication, then she should know about delivery and aseptic techniques during the delivery otherwise the traditional birth attendant (dayi) can also do the delivery. The major thing is to save the patient from infections during the delivery by proper protocol and wearing gloves. She should use hand sanitizer and a midwife should know these things.” – Midwife (Private & on-site MLBC).

“A midwife [in an MLBC] should have these 7 skills; take the history, diagnosis, and medication, then she should know about delivery and aseptic techniques during the delivery otherwise the traditional birth attendant (dayi) can also do the delivery. The major thing is to save the patient from infections during the delivery by proper protocol and wearing gloves. In addition, midwives working in MLBCs required entrepreneurial skills to ensure that the MLBC could earn enough to be able to afford supplies and be sustainable. There is currently no financial or other type of support from the government to facilitate this development. However, some stakeholders reported that private organizations are training midwives to set up their units in addition to improving their clinical midwifery skills.

Providing comprehensive and accessible services that women need and want

Antenatal, labor and birth, and postnatal care services were provided at all four sites that were studied. During their pregnancy, clients were advised about Micronutrient supplements, ‘dos and don’ts’ of pregnancy, and provided dietary guidelines. Antenatal and postnatal care included counseling regarding diet, hygiene, infant feeding, lactation, and family planning. Counseling sessions were conducted with the client’s family and financial situation in mind. Staff tried to ensure the involvement of family in care during labor and birth. Clients valued this particularly as their family members became better equipped to care for the client upon going home.

“I was told to feed the child with my milk and to take care of myself. Regarding myself, I was told to eat, to have a nutritional diet, those sorts of things.” – Client (Private & freestanding MLBC).

“I was told to feed the child with my milk and to take care of myself. Regarding myself, I was told to eat, to have a nutritional diet, those sorts of things.” – Client (Private & freestanding MLBC).

“Compared to the other health facilities, this facilitation is good, because baji (midwife) supervises all the things in her clinic, and she facilitates her patients with all the services.” –Client (Private & freestanding MLBC).

“Compared to the other health facilities, this facilitation is good, because baji (midwife) supervises all the things in her clinic, and she facilitates her patients with all the services.” –Client (Private & freestanding MLBC).

The Bhong and Karachi sites also offered free obstetric ultrasound services, and the Mirpur Khas site offered laboratory tests in addition to the services mentioned earlier. Clients appreciated the fact that they were able to access all services under one roof. Clients at Khuzdar reported that basic equipment and medicines were available at the facility for post-birth care; they paid fees and trusted the midwives to provide services – sometimes even for free if needed. One client said:“The staff here (MLBC) and every service are very good, whether you are having an ultrasound, a checkup or an injection, or having urine tests.” – Client (Private & on-site MLBC).

“The staff here (MLBC) and every service are very good, whether you are having an ultrasound, a checkup or an injection, or having urine tests.” – Client (Private & on-site MLBC).

Physical proximity and accessibility were major reasons that clients chose the MLBCs. Being close to an MLBC saved transport costs not only at the time of birth but also for each antenatal and postnatal visit. At the Khuzdar site, in cases where clients were unable to reach the MLBC, midwives also provided a home birth service.“Even if you knock on the door of the midwife’s home at night, she will be available as the center is present in her home. So easy accessibility makes it preferable for women”. – Stakeholder.

“Even if you knock on the door of the midwife’s home at night, she will be available as the center is present in her home. So easy accessibility makes it preferable for women”. – Stakeholder.

Providing quality of care

Clients reported that they could openly communicate with staff without any fear or judgment and were encouraged to ask as many questions as they wanted; the midwives answered their queries politely and patiently without being rude. They were even encouraged to share personal problems with the staff if needed. They also emphasized the excellent listening skills of the midwives and the fact that midwives answered their questions in a dignified and polite manner. Clients stated that they were made to feel like they were talking to companions rather than healthcare workers. At the Bhong site, clients were even provided food and drinks (juice in the summer and tea in the winter) during labour. For example:

“The doctor [midwife] took care of me like my own mother or sister. She was consoling me. She was doing wazifa (prayer) for me too. She was praying to Allah.” – Client (Private & on-site MLBC).

“The doctor [midwife] took care of me like my own mother or sister. She was consoling me. She was doing wazifa (prayer) for me too. She was praying to Allah.” – Client (Private & on-site MLBC).

“During pregnancy, I consulted with a midwife, I was very satisfied with her treatment and behavior, so I decided to deliver my baby at their health facility.” – Client (Private & freestanding MLBC).

“During pregnancy, I consulted with a midwife, I was very satisfied with her treatment and behavior, so I decided to deliver my baby at their health facility.” – Client (Private & freestanding MLBC).

At all sites, clients were encouraged to go through labour and give birth in whatever position they preferred and felt comfortable in. They were able to walk, eat, and drink before birth. They were also able to have a companion who they were comfortable with during labour and birth. The staff’s behavior was reported to be positive, respectful, and polite during and after birth. In some special cases, the midwives went out of their way to ensure that the mother and child’s needs were met; this included buying sanitary items, clothes, fruits, and medicines for them. Trust was developed through empathetic care and effective communication strategies employed by the staff (including counseling). Most midwives reported communicating with patients in their local languages, which not only made communication more effective but helped in building trust. This was expressed in these quotes:

“… they can walk before the delivery, eat, and drink and bring in someone with whom they feel relaxed. Then it’s free of cost and we remain kind to people and respect them.” – Stakeholder.

“… they can walk before the delivery, eat, and drink and bring in someone with whom they feel relaxed. Then it’s free of cost and we remain kind to people and respect them.” – Stakeholder.

“When patients come here, we don’t treat them as patients rather we ask them to tell their queries to us like sisters. We ask them to trust us and tell us everything as we are their sisters.” – Midwife (Private & on-site MLBC).

“When patients come here, we don’t treat them as patients rather we ask them to tell their queries to us like sisters. We ask them to trust us and tell us everything as we are their sisters.” – Midwife (Private & on-site MLBC).

“In whatever way patients like, we compromise with them. In whatever position they want to deliver, we help them do that. We stay with them 24 hours and don’t leave them alone so that they don’t feel lonely. That’s why they trust us.” – Midwife (Private & on-site MLBC).

Clients appreciated the behavior of the staff and their approach to care throughout the pregnancy journey. To safeguard the privacy of other female clients, each birthing woman was restricted to one companion; no male attendant was allowed in the examination area adhering to cultural norm. Additionally, women in labour were offered tea by these midwives as a polite gesture and a part of cultural practice.

Although quality of care was shown through the attitude of midwives, barriers were reported in terms of maintenance of records of clients and services provided. Stakeholders suggested that sector partners (INGO/NGOs) could be engaged to support and facilitate capacity-building around data management and utilization. This would enable the tracking of clients in various stages of pregnancy and improve midwives’ decision-making and quality of care provision through pregnancy.“The data should be at the tips of their fingers, and they should know that a certain delivery was done at the 34th week how many checkups were done, and so on. It will improve their quality of care.” – Stakeholder.

Being disconnected from the healthcare system

MLBCs and CMWs are not connected to the national health system at any level, and function mostly in isolation. Hence, there are challenges to their ability to refer cases and manage complications.“Introduction of CMW and midwives is good on the ground level but somehow connecting them to the basic health unit, connecting them to secondary units, and then how to go from secondary to tertiary unit, all this needs to happen. Right now, it is all loose.” – Stakeholder.

“Introduction of CMW and midwives is good on the ground level but somehow connecting them to the basic health unit, connecting them to secondary units, and then how to go from secondary to tertiary unit, all this needs to happen. Right now, it is all loose.” – Stakeholder.

Referral mechanisms are not formalized and solely depend on personal connections; while integration was important, there were clear gaps identified in the MLBCs and the current system. Due to the lack of a formalized system of referrals, the onus lay with the midwives (especially when they have independent practices) to connect with nearby facilities for referring women with complications and having access to obstetric ultrasounds and laboratory tests. This personal network was important for facilities to accept referrals if complications arise. For example:“If I am a midwife working in a center not having a personal referral then my woman (client) will suffer.” – Stakeholder.

“If I am a midwife working in a center not having a personal referral then my woman (client) will suffer.” – Stakeholder.

In addition, referral records were not maintained in MLBCs; therefore, the trends were not identified to steer the referral mechanism. If referral records were maintained at MLBCs, it would guide the setup of a formalized channel of connection and facilitate a more effective referral mechanism.“They do not even have a record for the referral system. If the healthcare commissioner makes it compulsory for them to maintain a record about who, which, and where they refer and for what, then the midwives are going to maintain the record.” – Stakeholder.

Stakeholders also recommended that referral centers should be within 10–30 min of MLBCs. Road infrastructure must be maintained for smooth and timely referral and care provision. There was a need to develop formalized coordination with referral hospitals to combat medical emergencies and ensure the timely provision of maternity care.“We need to have a system where when we set up a facility, we need to visit the referral sites and tell them that we are opening a center, and we might be having referrals from you. Then we must ask them to take our cases and prioritize them.” – Stakeholder.

Managing the costs

The impact of costs was important for both the women and the service providers. The cost of food and transportation was a constant complaint, both in the responses of clients and midwives. The issue of affordability limited women’s access to MLBCs; sometimes, women were not able to afford MLBC costs, so they chose Traditional Birth Attendants (Dai) as highlighted by a few midwives. If stock-outs happened at MLBCs, patients were unable to access medicines. As a result, they would either purchase them from an external medical store or be forced to revisit the facility to obtain their prescribed medicines. Anemic women were identified by midwives, and counselled about nutritious diets; however, they were unable to implement dietary changes due to financial constraints. Moreover, malnutrition services were not available in the MLBC, and patients were referred to centers far away which added burden on women in terms of transportation and other costs.

None of the sites reported receiving government financial support, but all tried to access resources through networking and partnerships with donors or private funding agencies. To begin with, the Khuzdar site (where clients also pay for services) was reported to be facilitated by DKT2 Pakistan. DKT helped facilitate training and provides birth stations, delivery kits, newborn kits, and clothes for newborns. They also helped with facility infrastructure such as paint and curtains. The Bhong site was found to be a part of a larger hospital network and as such was dependent on donations; other than monetary donations, this also included medicines donated at medical camps. The Karachi site raised funds and donations to finance the running of the MLBC; the efforts of the founder of the facility in this regard were highlighted by staff members. At Mirpur Khas, the site existed as part of a public-private partnership; hence some government support was available. The building of the MLBC was financed by the Sindh government, but the ongoing operational costs were financially supported by an international NGO, which led to concerns about the long-term viability of the MLBC.“Things get better temporarily but on ground level, it is mostly the same and when the funds end their project ends, and then midwives are again on their own.” – Stakeholder.

Discussion

In Pakistan, there is a prevalent trend where women opt for private health facilities for childbirth. The significant reliance of expectant mothers on the private sector for the provision of care is highlighting the pressing need to create care models that enable private sector providers to safely cater to the needs of women and newborns at reasonable costs. MLBCs in Pakistan are offering maternity services for a nominal user fee. Presently, there’s a lack of comprehensive evidence regarding the experiences of community midwives as maternal healthcare providers within MLBCs. Additionally, the operational dynamics of midwives in the private sector remain underexplored, despite the growing prevalence of MLBCs throughout the country.

Our findings corroborate previous literature [8, 10, 21] that midwife-led care through an MLBC is highly appreciated by clients for being more humane and less medicalized. The public sector in Pakistan allows people to access care either free of cost or at highly subsidized rates. However the real and the larger barriers that clients bear are the mistreatment that they face at the hands of healthcare providers [6].

The quality of care in the public sector is so poor that many women prefer giving birth at home taking the services of traditional birth attendants (TBAs) – in potentially life-threatening circumstances – just to be able to have some control over their care and some autonomy during their labour and birth. TBAs are the preferred choice as they are more affordable, accessible, and available upon request [31, 32]. However, they have low levels of education and do not receive any formal training; they learn their skills from either older women in the community or from other TBAs [16]. In contrast, MLBCs bring about significant improvement in the safety and quality of care with humane and dignified maternity care. Midwives have enhanced clinical competency leading to comprehensive care that addresses the holistic needs of women. Therefore, MLBCs can address this preference because the midwives are usually local and known to the women and provide services with minimal user fees. MLBCs can provide a birthing environment to women that has many of the attractions of the home. Community midwives are usually known and acceptable by the communities as well. Therefore, midwives can leverage this connection and provide the comfort of home in an MLBC environment. The government must consider investing in these services considering midwife-led care’s benefits and positive outcomes. They can make investments in these services through subsidizing the fee charged at the facilities, or by providing them the key resources required at the facility to help cut costs. Where suitable, the facility’s management can be outsourced or through public-private partnerships, the costs can be managed.

In Pakistan women who can afford private healthcare services do not consider midwives as an option simply because of the widely accepted view of specialized doctors being a safer option despite evidence to the contrary [33, 34]. MLBC clients in high-income countries tend to belong to more wealthy demographics [35] whereas in LMICs, clients from low-income communities are catered to in MLBCs [19]. Because midwives have not been able to attract more affluent clients who would be willing to pay a high fee, they have been unable to make profits in LMICS [33]. Rich clients who can afford to pay the fees would rather go to a physician, leaving midwives to offer services to poor women [33]. Further, in low-income countries, MLBC services are predominantly offered by private, not-for-profit entities. Consequently, the accessibility of care for clients from underprivileged and marginalized backgrounds heavily relies on sustained support from these sources [19]. Evidence also shows that MLBCs provide quality services and could be implemented more widely in LMICs for women of all income levels [19]. Since midwifery services are not commonly used by the affluent in Pakistan, there needs to be more awareness regarding the topic. Using lessons learned from how midwifery has been widely accepted in other countries (including low- and middle-income countries), a similar, contextualized could work in Pakistan. Only then can MLBCs start to be integrated into the health system.

In addition, the lack of integration of MLBCs into the public health system also leads to poorer referral mechanisms. A robust referral system is necessary for successful MLBC operations where women receive the necessary and timely maternal care. Moreover, weak referral systems evident in many LMICs may compromise the safety of women and newborns who use MLBCs [19]. Midwife-led care in many high-income countries has been regularized within the healthcare system. However, in many LMICs, there is a lack of effective midwife-led care integration [18].

There is a dire need to formulate policies from the government to integrate MLBCs into the larger public health system. The integration will result in the general acceptability of midwives at the societal level leading to better trust in MLBCs and uptake in the service utilization in the wealthy section of society as well. By integrating MLBCs into the formal health structure referrals of pregnant women in need of maternity care will be made and timely care provision will be ensured.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the understanding of establishing MLBCs and the provision of quality, safe, accessible, and affordable maternity care. MLBCs provide a promising alternative for maternal and newborn health care emphasizing customized and client-centric approaches. It has been proven time and again that midwife-led care can avert preventable maternal deaths. The gap for people who want affordable high-quality and dignified care but cannot afford the premium fees of the private sector can be filled by midwife-led care. Midwives have been shown to provide humane and client-centered care at much lower prices, making their services accessible to a larger client base. However, to ensure MLBCs’ success and wider accessibility, key initiatives, and ownership by the government in policy interventions such as securing sustainable finances, continued medical education, and integration of MLBCs into existing healthcare systems are critical steps. Yet, ensuring their ongoing success and broader availability requires crucial actions such as securing financial stability, championing their advantages, and integrating them more efficiently into established healthcare systems. Moving forward, government, policymakers, and stakeholders should prioritize these actionable steps to harness the full potential of MLBCs in delivering high-quality, safe, and dignified maternity care to all, regardless of socioeconomic status. Additionally, future researchers can focus on evaluating the long-term impact of MLBCs on maternal and newborn health outcomes and indicators, to inform policy and practice in this vital area of healthcare delivery.
