Preoperative leptin levels were associated with postoperative acute kidney injury in patients with acute type A aortic dissection
Authors: Meiling Cai, Fei Jiang, Yanchun Peng, Sailan Li, Yuling Xie, Liangwan Chen, Yanjuan Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a common surgical complication of acute type A aortic dissection (AAAD) and strongly associated with an unfavorable prognosis. Although elevated leptin levels have been identified as a potential risk factor for chronic kidney disease in middle-aged and elderly individuals, their association with AKI following AAAD surgery remains unknown. This study investigated the association between preoperative leptin levels and postoperative AKI in patients with AAAD.MethodsThis study included 159 patients with AAAD at Fujian Heart Medical Center. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes standards were used to define and classify AKI. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the ability of leptin levels to predict AKI. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to study the association between leptin level and AKI.ResultsThe incidence of AKI was 33.96%. Leptin levels in the AKI group and stage 1, 2, and 3 AKI groups were higher than those in the non-AKI group (p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis revealed that elevated leptin levels were an independent risk factor for AKI (p < 0.001). ROC analysis of leptin detection for AKI showed an area under the curve of 0.71. The risk of AKI in the high-level group (leptin levels ≥3.62 ng/mL) was 3.87 times to the low-level group (leptin levels < 3.62 ng/mL).ConclusionElevated leptin levels are associated with AKI, and plasma leptin may serve as a potential early blood biomarker for AKI in patients with AAAD.Keywords: Acute type A aortic dissection, leptin, acute kidney injury, biomarkers, risk factors

Introduction

Acute type A aortic dissection (AAAD) is a cardiovascular disease with acute onset, rapid progression, and high mortality, posing a severe threat to patient’s life [1]. Surgery is the only effective radical treatment for AAAD [2]. However, due to the wide range of lesions involved and the complexity of surgery, the incidence of postoperative complications in AAAD is significantly higher than that in other cardiovascular surgical diseases. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is one of the most common and serious postoperative complications of AAAD, with an incidence of 40.1–86.67% [3–8]. Once AKI occurs, the short- and long-term mortality rates, length of ICU stay, length of stay (LOS), and medical expenditure of patients significantly increase [8,9]. Unfortunately, there is currently no effective treatment for AKI; therefore, prevention is crucial [10]. Early identification of patients at risk of AKI is vital for timely intervention and for informing them about potential postoperative outcomes.

Blood biomarkers are valuable supplementary tools for disease prediction and stratifying risks [11]. Adding specific blood biomarkers may help further improve the ability to identify patients at risk of AKI [12]. Currently, clinicians primarily rely on serum creatinine (SCr) levels to diagnose AKI [13]. However, since it could take hours or days for SCr levels to increase, there may be delayed AKI recognition. Neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin increases earlier than SCr and may help identify AKI; however, its reliability in predicting AKI in patients with AAAD is still unknown [13]. Therefore, new biomarkers need to be explored to improve the early identification of AKI risk.

Inflammation and oxidative stress play crucial roles in AKI development [10,14]. Leptin, an adipokine secreted by white adipose tissue, is involved in inflammation and immune response regulation [15,16]. In the kidney, leptin plays a biological role in the inflammatory response and participates in renal signaling pathways [17]. High leptin levels can cause pathological and physiological changes in the kidney and negatively affect renal function [18]. A previous study reported a link between elevated leptin levels and decreased kidney function in middle-aged and elderly individuals [19]. However, there is a lack of evidence regarding the association between levels of leptin and postoperative AKI in patients with AAAD. This study aimed to investigate whether leptin can be used as an early blood biomarker for AKI.

Methods

Protocol approval and informed consent of patients

This prospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [20]. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (Ethics approval no. 2022KY005), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Recruitment of research participants

This study included patients with AAAD admitted to the Fujian Heart Medical Center between February 2022 and November 2022. All the patients were diagnosed according to the 2014 Guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology for aortic dissection [21]. AAAD was defined as chest pain or other related symptoms that occurred no more than 14 days before the diagnosis of type A aortic dissection [22]. The inclusion criteria of this study were: (1) Age ≥18 years old, (2) AAAD confirmed by computed tomography angiography, magnetic resonance angiography, or ultrasound. (3) Performed ascending aorta replacement combined with improved three-branch stenting. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) related symptoms for more than 14 days before diagnosis, (2) severe renal insufficiency or chronic kidney disease before surgery. It was defined as patients with a history of chronic hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 who did not receive renal replacement therapy [23], (3) AAAD with pregnancy, (4) no emergency surgery within 12 h of admission, (5) death during surgery or within 24 h after surgery, and (6) previous cardiovascular surgery.

Sample size calculation

Based on the population rate estimation formula, where the population rate was denoted as π, the sample rate as P, and the required sample size as n, the formula for calculating the sample size was as follows: n=Zα/2 2π (1−π)δ2.

According to a literature review, the incidence of postoperative AKI in patients with AAD was 40% [3]. With a tolerance error of 0.2 times π, the calculated error margin was δ = 0.08. To account for potential loss to follow-up within the sample, the initial sample size was increased by 10%, resulting in a final cohort of 159 cases for investigation, with the significance level set at a = 0.05.

Outcome indicators

The primary outcome measure was AKI. Secondary outcomes included 30-day mortality rate, postoperative hypoxemia, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), mechanical ventilation time (MVT), length of ICU stay, and LOS.

Definitions

AKI was defined according to The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria as an increase in SCr of ≥26.4 μmol/L within 48 h or ≥ 50% baseline SCr within 7 days after AAAD surgery, or receiving CRRT treatment [24]. AKI stages were categorized as follows: Stage 1 (1.5–1.9 times baseline SCr), Stage 2 (2–2.9 times baseline SCr), and Stage 3 (3 times or more of baseline SCr or receiving CRRT treatment) [25]. Intraoperative hypotension was defined as a mean intraoperative arterial pressure < 65 mmHg [26]. Postoperative hypoxemia was defined as a PaO2-FiO2 ratio of < 300 mmHg [5]. The maximum value: PaO2-FiO2 ratio within 24 h after surgery was used to determine postoperative hypoxemia. Intraoperative fluid balance was defined as the value of intraoperative fluid intake minus the output, where fluid intake included crystals and colloids and fluid output included urine volume and blood loss.

Blood sample testing

With participants’ consent, about 5 mL of blood was collected before surgery and stored at 4 °C. Within 2 h after collection, the blood sample was centrifuged at 3000 g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the plasma was frozen at −80 °C. Plasma leptin concentrations were measured using an ELISA kit (Abcam, Cat No: ab108879) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Data collection

A questionnaire was designed to collect the demographic information, baseline vascular risk factors, and clinical information of the participants. The demographic information included age, sex, height, and weight. Baseline vascular risk factors included a history of hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, smoking, and drinking. Clinical information was obtained from medical records, including blood pressure, heart rate, preoperative medication, intraoperative clinical data (such as aortic occlusion time, surgical time, and cardiopulmonary bypass time), MVT, 30-day mortality rate, CRRT, length of ICU stay, and LOS. The initial blood test indicators after admission included white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), monocytes, lymphocytes, platelets (PLT), SCr, and procalcitonin (PCT) levels. The highest value–PaO2-FiO2 ratio within 24 h after surgery, and the highest SCr level was observed within 48 h and/or 7 days after surgery.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0, R 4.1.2, and GraphPad Prism 9.0 for statistical analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher tests. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test the normality of continuous numerical variables. Normal data are shown as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normal data are shown as median and interquartile range; the T-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used for inter-group comparisons. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the diagnostic value of leptin levels in AKI, with DeLong’s test used to compare ROC curves. Logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the relationship between plasma leptin levels and AKI. All tests were bilateral, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for differences.

Relationship between leptin levels and AKI

The AKI group had significantly higher median leptin levels than the non-AKI group (4.82 ng/mL vs. 3.26 ng/mL, p < 0.001) (Figure 2(A)). Leptin levels were also higher in the AKI groups at stages 1 (p < 0.05), 2 (p < 0.05), and 3 (p < 0.001) than in the non-AKI group, but there were no significant differences among the AKI groups at different stages (p > 0.05) (Figure 2(B)). We categorized the participants into four groups based on the leptin level quartiles and found a linear increase in AKI incidence with higher leptin levels (p = 0.049) (Figure 3).

Correlation between leptin and BMI

Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to investigate the correlation between the leptin levels and various preoperative clinical indicators. The results showed that the natural logarithm of leptin levels was positively correlated with BMI (r = 0.245, p = 0.002) (Figure 4(A)). However, there was no significant association between SCr, WBC count, PLT, neutrophil, PCT, urea, PT, and INR (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1). Considering the obvious sex dimorphism of leptin levels in the blood, we further explored the correlation between leptin and BMI in individuals of different sexes. The results showed that the natural logarithm of leptin positively correlated with BMI in both men (r = 0.262, p = 0.003) and women (r = 0.367, p = 0.028) (Figure 4(B,C)).

Value of leptin in the diagnosis of AKI

The ROC curve analysis results showed that compared with BMI and SCr parameters, leptin performed the best in predicting postoperative AKI in patients with AAAD, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.71 (Figure 5). The addition of leptin to BMI (AUC = 0.731) and SCr (AUC = 0.737) significantly improved the prediction of AKI. Notably, leptin combined with BMI and SCr level had the best predictive effect on AKI (AUC = 0.747) (Table 3).

Logistic regression analysis results of leptin and AKI

To investigate the potential utility of leptin as a standalone risk factor for AKI, variables with a significance level of p < 0.05, as shown in Table 1, as well as those that may affect AKI based on clinical experience, were incorporated into a multifactorial logistic regression model. These variables were further screened using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables with multicollinearity risk were excluded. Highly correlated variables (such as SCr and urea or PT and INR) were screened by VIF, SCr, and INR, and those with higher VIF scores were excluded (Supplementary Table 2). The results showed that the leptin level was an independent risk factor for AKI [OR = 1.41, 95%CI (1.17, 1.70), p < 0.001] (Table 4). We found that a leptin level of 3.62 ng/mL was the threshold for diagnosing AKI (Table 3). Participants were divided into low (leptin < 3.62 ng/mL) and high (leptin ≥ 3.62 ng/mL) level groups. The risk of AKI in the high-level group (leptin ≥3.62 ng/mL) was 3.87 times to the low-level group (leptin < 3.62 ng/mL) [OR = 3.87, 95%CI (1.53, 9.80), p = 0.004] (Table 4).

Logistics regression analysis results of BMI, SCr and AKI

Logistic regression analysis showed that neither BMI nor SCr level was an independent predictor of postoperative AKI in patients with AAAD. We further used the optimal cutoff value of the ROC curve to stratify BMI and SCr and found that BMI > 26.12 [OR = 3.469, 95% CI (1.301, 9.249), p = 0.013] was an independent predictor of AKI (Supplemental Table 3). However, SCr > 72 μmol/L [OR = 2.277, 95%CI (0.616, 8.413), p = 0.217] was not an independent predictor of AKI (Supplemental Table 4).

Discussion

This study of 159 patients with AAAD found that high plasma leptin levels were significantly associated with AKI and were an independent risk factor for its presence. Higher leptin levels are associated with a higher incidence of AKI. Patients with leptin levels ≥ 3.62 ng/mL had a 3.87 times risk of AKI compared to those with levels < 3.62 ng/mL. Patients with AKI also had higher rates of 30-day mortality, postoperative hypoxemia, longer MVT, and longer ICU stay.

In this study, the incidence of postoperative AKI in patients with AAAD was 33.96%, which was lower than 40.05% reported by Chen et al. [3]. Jiang et al. reported that a history of cardiovascular surgery is a risk factor of AKI [27]. This study excluded patients with a history of cardiovascular surgery, which may explain the low incidence of AKI. This study found that preoperative leptin levels were higher in patients with AKI than in those without AKI, but there was no significant difference in leptin levels among patients with different AKI stages, which is consistent with the report of Lousa et al. [28]. They found that in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), leptin levels were significantly increased compared to those in healthy controls, but there was no significant change among the different stages of CKD [28]. In this study, there was no difference in plasma leptin levels among the different AKI stages; therefore, a causal relationship between leptin and AKI could not be confirmed. However, consistent with the results of previous clinical studies, elevated leptin levels were associated with impaired renal function [29].

The association between leptin levels and impaired kidney function may be explained by the following mechanisms. Elevated leptin levels can induce renal oxidative stress, resulting in the production of reactive oxygen species via the activation of NADPH oxidase, ultimately leading to renal injury [17,30]. In contrast, leptin can cause inflammation [16], which can lead to renal vascular disorders, impair renal function, and promote the occurrence and development of AKI [10]. Additionally, leptin is cleared by the kidneys, and renal function can further affect leptin levels in the blood, leading to further kidney function impairment [30]. Although these studies support the adverse effects of leptin on the progression of renal dysfunction, leptin has also been shown to have a protective effect on renal function. In an animal model of AKI induced by ischemia/reperfusion, leptin alleviated AKI by inhibiting apoptosis and autophagy in renal tubular epithelial cells [31]. In another animal study, leptin provided significant protection against major renal dysfunction and damage in animal models of AKI via its effects on the central nervous system [32]. The results of existing studies are inconsistent, and further studies are needed to clarify the causal relationship between leptin and AKI.

Leptin, a hormone derived from fat cells, had been confirmed to be positively correlated with BMI, which was consistent with the results of this study [33]. In previous studies, BMI has been commonly used to measure the degree of obesity and has been proven to be a risk factor for AKI, which is consistent with the finding of this study that BMI was higher in AKI patients than in Non-AKI patients [34]. With the increasing incidence of AKI in patients with AAAD, the role of abnormal BMI in AKI has attracted increasing attention [8,9]. In obese patients, changes in the lipid profile may promote renal function impairment; however, the underlying mechanism remains unclear [17]. Changes in the lipid profile may increase the expression of the leptin gene in the adipose tissue, leading to an increase in leptin concentration [17]. Based on the results of this study, obesity may play a role in AKI through leptin, which requires further investigation.

It was well known that AKI was closely related to inflammatory response. PCT is a monocyte chemoattractant at the site of inflammation and contributes to inflammation-mediated cell damage [35]. In this study, elevated PCT levels were also observed in the AKI group, suggesting an important role for inflammation in AKI. Both WBC and neutrophil counts were significantly higher in the AKI group than in the non-AKI group. This may be related to the immune system activation caused by the inflammatory state of the body, resulting in an increase in leukocyte production. Increasing evidence suggests that WBC, primarily neutrophils, mediate tissue damage and play key roles in renal function impairment [36]. Severe renal dysfunction manifests as accumulation of creatinine in the blood. Li et al. [37] constructed and validated a risk prediction model for cardiac surgery-related AKI (CSA-AKI) based on machine learning methods. They detected a positive correlation between preoperative SCr levels and CSA-AKI. Consistent with our findings, we found that the preoperative SCr levels were significantly higher in patients with AKI than in those without. In addition, we also had findings consistent with Xin et al. [38]. Their results showed that WBC, neutrophils, PCT, SCr, PT, and INR were significantly higher in AKI patients than in non-AKI patients, whereas PLT was significantly lower. However, it is worth noting that SCr level was not an independent predictor of AKI in this study, which is consistent with the findings of Lyu et al. [39]. One possible explanation is that we used the SCr level measured at admission as the baseline value. Owing to the acute nature of AAAD, blood draws on admission are often completed shortly before AAAD repair surgery, and baseline SCr levels do not necessarily reflect true baseline renal function [40].

The results of this study also showed that the 30-day mortality rate of patients with AAAD was 6.9%, which was lower than the 46.67% reported by Zhang et al. [8]. This may be due to the differences in surgical methods. In this study, all patients underwent ascending aorta replacement combined with improved three-branch stenting. The application of this innovative technology can reduce the short-term mortality rate of patients with AAAD [41]. Moreover, Silachi et al. reported that the 30-day mortality rate for patients undergoing AAAD surgery under the care of experienced surgeons was 7.1%, which is similar to the results of our study [42]. In addition, the 30-day mortality, postoperative hypoxemia, MVT, and length of ICU stay were significantly higher in patients with AKI than in those without AKI, which is consistent with findings of previous studies [8,9,34].

However, this study has some limitations. First, the purpose of this study was not to determine the specific mechanism by which preoperative leptin leads to postoperative AKI in patients with AAAD but to explore the correlation between leptin and AKI. Although these findings suggest that leptin levels are associated with AKI, given the nature of single-center observational studies, the causal relationship should be interpreted with caution. Secondly, our findings showed that the high-level of leptin (≥3.62 ng/mL) significantly increased the risk of postoperative AKI in patients with AAAD compared to low-level of leptin (< 3.62 ng/mL). However, this only represents a possible range and not a definite cutoff value. In future research, we hope to conduct a large-sample multicenter study to further validate the predictive value of leptin for AKI.

Conclusion

The current study indicated a significant correlation between preoperative leptin elevation and postoperative AKI in patients with AAAD. The potential clinical significance of measuring plasma leptin levels for the early detection of AKI in patients with AAAD and its role in the prevention and treatment of AKI still needs to be further studied.
