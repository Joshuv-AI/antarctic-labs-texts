BN-NeRF: A fast 3D reconstruction and phenotyping framework for banana plants using handheld devices
Authors: Xing Xu, Shangcun Chen, Yu Liu, Jieli Duan, Mohui Jin, Han Fu, Zhou Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-fidelity 3D reconstruction and precise phenotypic parameter extraction of banana plants are critical for crop growth monitoring and yield estimation in precision agriculture. However, traditional methods encounter significant bottlenecks: LiDAR systems are cost-prohibitive for widespread adoption, while traditional photogrammetry often fails to handle the complex canopy structures, severe occlusions, and weak texture features characteristic of banana leaves. To address these limitations, this article proposes a novel framework for 3D reconstruction and automatic phenotyping based on multi-view images captured by mobile phones. We introduce BN-NeRF, an enhanced Neural Radiance Field method built upon Instant-NGP. Specifically, we integrate three key technical improvements: (1) frame-level geometric calibration to correct camera pose drift caused by handheld motion; (2) sparse geometric anchoring to explicitly constrain depth and scale using sparse point clouds; and (3) thin-leaf prior regularization to suppress artifacts and improve the geometric accuracy of leaf surfaces. Building on this reconstruction, we establish a complete pipeline to recover explicit metric geometry from implicit radiance fields. By combining mesh topological analysis with geodesic algorithms, we achieve automated and precise extraction of key morphological parameters. Extensive experiments were conducted on a dataset of 90 banana plants in a real-world orchard. The results demonstrate that BN-NeRF achieves superior rendering quality (PSNR of 32.4 dB, SSIM of 0.951, and LPIPS of 0.152) while maintaining inference speeds comparable to Instant-NGP. Furthermore, the extracted phenotypic parameters showed strong agreement with manual ground truth across both leaf-level and structural traits. In addition to trait-specific regression performance, the evaluation also includes normalized completeness analysis, calibration-cube-based scale validation, and Bland–Altman agreement analysis, supporting the measurement reliability of BN-NeRF for field phenotyping. This study demonstrates that low-cost smartphone-based acquisition, combined with BN-NeRF, can support accurate field phenotyping of banana plants. In addition, an implemented mobile-cloud system was functionally validated through repeated end-to-end runs on an iPhone 13 client and a cloud workstation.

Introduction

Bananas represent the fourth largest food crop globally, occupying a central position in the trade of tropical and sub-tropical fruits [1]. In the realm of precision agriculture, fine-grained phenotypic parameters of banana plants—such as plant height, leaf area index (LAI), leaf morphology, and growth rate—serve as critical agronomic indicators for assessing photosynthetic efficiency, monitoring health status, and predicting final yield [2], [3]. Traditional banana plantation management relies on manual field measurements using tape measures and leaf area meters. These methods are not only labor-intensive, time-consuming, and prone to subjective errors but also involve destructive sampling, failing to meet the demands of modern breeding and large-scale cultivation for high-throughput, non-contact data acquisition [4], [5], [6], [7]. Therefore, developing a method capable of rapidly reconstructing high-fidelity 3D models of banana plants using portable devices and automatically extracting phenotypic parameters is of significant importance for advancing the digital management of smart banana plantations.

In recent years, with the rapid development of computer vision, image-based 3D reconstruction technology has become a mainstream means of acquiring crop phenotypes [8]. Traditional Multi-View Stereo (MVS) and Structure from Motion (SfM) techniques recover scene geometry by matching image feature points, offering the advantages of low cost and ease of deployment [9], [10]. However, the geometric structure of banana plants is extremely complex: huge leaves often cause severe mutual occlusion, and the single leaf texture (abundance of weak texture regions), combined with micro-movements of branches and leaves caused by outdoor wind, poses significant challenges. Consequently, traditional methods based on explicit geometric matching (e.g., COLMAP, Pix4D) often produce missing point clouds, reconstruction holes, or severe noise, making it difficult to generate complete digital models [11], [12].

To overcome the limitations of traditional geometric modeling, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [13] have emerged as a novel implicit neural representation technology, demonstrating immense potential in synthesizing realistic novel views and recovering fine geometry in complex scenes. NeRF utilizes a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to represent the scene as a continuous volume density and color field, generating images through volume rendering techniques, which excel in handling complex occlusions and lighting variations [14], [15]. Recent studies, such as Instant-NGP [16] and Mip-NeRF 360 [17], have significantly improved reconstruction speed and rendering quality by introducing hash encoding and anti-aliasing mechanisms, making their application in agricultural scenarios possible.

Although NeRF and its variants have made significant progress in general scenes, directly applying them to banana plant reconstruction using handheld devices still faces three severe challenges. First, pose drift and motion blur. In uncontrolled field environments, handheld shooting with smartphones is prone to image motion blur. Furthermore, in the absence of high-precision Real-Time Kinematic Global Positioning System (RTK-GPS) assistance, the initial camera poses calculated by preprocessing tools like COLMAP often contain errors, leading to NeRF training divergence or ghosting artifacts [18], [19]. Second, the lack of physical scale. Standard NeRF models are trained in a normalized space, resulting in generated models that lack real physical metric units. This is a fatal defect for agricultural phenotypic analysis that requires precise measurements (e.g., leaf length, stem diameter) [20]. Third, thin-structure artifacts. Banana leaves are typical thin-shell structures. Lacking depth supervision, NeRF tends to deceive the rendering loss by generating "foggy" geometry, leading to blurred leaf edges and distorted thickness, which seriously affects the accuracy of leaf area extraction [21], [22], [6].

To address these challenges, this article proposes BN-NeRF, a framework for fast 3D reconstruction and phenotypic analysis of banana plants based on handheld mobile video streams. Unlike prior NeRF variants that typically emphasize pose refinement, sparse geometric supervision, or geometric regularization in isolation, BN-NeRF couples these directions in a banana-oriented manner so that the reconstructed radiance field is not only visually plausible but also metrically measurable for phenotyping. Specifically, FGC is introduced to correct frame-wise pose perturbations caused by handheld capture and unstable cross-view correspondence; SGA is introduced to recover metric scale and stabilize geometry in weakly textured regions using SfM sparse anchors; and TLP is introduced to preserve the laminar continuity of large banana leaves while suppressing off-surface density artifacts. In this sense, BN-NeRF should be understood as a task-oriented coupling designed for the joint biophysical and imaging challenges of banana phenotyping rather than as a simple aggregation of generic NeRF modules.

The main contributions of this article are summarized as follows:

We propose the BN-NeRF framework designed for handheld devices, enabling rapid reconstruction and automated phenotypic extraction of banana plants based on mobile multi-view imagery.

We propose a frame-level geometric calibration (FGC) strategy tailored to handheld banana capture, in which camera poses and scene parameters are jointly optimized to suppress pose perturbations caused by mild motion blur, weak texture, and unstable cross-view correspondence.

We introduce a sparse geometric anchoring (SGA) strategy that converts SfM sparse points into metric depth anchors, thereby reducing scale ambiguity and strengthening geometric support in weakly textured leaf regions for measurement-oriented reconstruction.

We design a banana-oriented thin-leaf prior (TLP) that combines anisotropic normal regularization and non-surface density suppression to preserve laminar continuity while limiting off-surface floaters and thickness inflation on large banana leaves.

We design a fully automated banana phenotyping pipeline capable of automatically segmenting stems and leaves from the reconstructed high-fidelity neural radiance field and calculating key agronomic parameters such as pseudostem height and leaf area.

Extensive experiments were conducted on a real banana plantation dataset. The results demonstrate that BN-NeRF outperforms existing NeRF variants and traditional MVS methods in rendering quality, normalized completeness, geometry-oriented scale validation, and phenotypic measurement agreement.

RELATED work

Crop 3D reconstruction technology

The acquisition of crop phenotypic information relies on high-quality 3D data sources. Traditional methods are primarily categorized into active sensing and passive vision. Active methods mainly rely on Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR), including Terrestrial Laser Scanning (TLS) [23] and handheld Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) devices [24]. LiDAR possesses extremely high ranging accuracy and can penetrate parts of the canopy to acquire internal structures, making it widely used in fruit tree branch reconstruction and forest biomass estimation [25]. However, although LiDAR excels in acquiring high-precision 3D structural information, high-precision equipment is cost-prohibitive. Furthermore, it is difficult for LiDAR to directly provide key information required for stress diagnosis, such as plant color and spectral attributes; therefore, it usually needs to be combined with RGB or multi/hyperspectral imaging for tasks such as pest and disease monitoring [26].

Passive methods are mainly based on MVS and SfM principles, utilizing sequence images captured by ordinary RGB cameras to reconstruct scenes [27]. Common software such as Agisoft Metashape and Pix4Dmapper has been widely applied in Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) field mapping [28], [29]. Rose et al. systematically evaluated the reconstruction accuracy of SfM + MVS in tomato organ-level phenotypic scenarios, indicating that under relatively stable acquisition conditions, this route can support plant 3D phenotypic measurement and scale assessment [30]. However, in field environments, thin leaf structures, wind-induced non-rigid motion, and dense self-occlusion significantly reduce reconstruction completeness [26]. Simultaneously, strong specular reflections from waxy leaf surfaces introduce appearance variations and "phantom features" that change with the viewing angle. This further destroys cross-view feature consistency, causing matching instability, which results in sparse point cloud missing, surface holes, and even geometric distortions [31], [32], [7]. Additionally, although Deep learning-based MVS (MVSNet) [33] improves completeness, its dependence on training data limits its generalization ability in unstructured wild environments.

Neural radiance fields

NeRF, as a revolutionary view synthesis technology, model scenes continuously through implicit neural networks. Unlike explicit point clouds or meshes, NeRF maps spatial coordinates and viewing angles to volume density and color, generating images through a differentiable rendering pipeline. This method can recover complex geometric details and view-dependent effects with extremely high resolution. To address the slow training issue of original NeRF (typically requiring hours), Müller et al. proposed Instant-NGP [16], utilizing Multiresolution Hash Encoding to shorten training time to the level of seconds, laying the foundation for the practical deployment of NeRF. For unbounded scenes and 360-degree capture, Barron et al. proposed Mip-NeRF 360 [17], resolving background blurring and aliasing effects through non-linear scene parameterization. However, NeRF is prone to overfitting in cases of sparse views [34] or inaccurate poses [35], producing geometric artifacts. Addressing this issue, BARF [18] and NeRF-- [36] attempted to jointly optimize camera poses during training. This shares a similar philosophy with this article's approach to solving handheld shooting jitter, but applying it to complex plant canopy structures still requires specific regularization constraints.

More broadly, NeRF variants relevant to BN-NeRF have evolved along three complementary directions. First, pose-refinement methods, such as BARF and SPARF, jointly optimize camera parameters and scene representation to improve robustness to imperfect initialization or noisy poses. Second, geometry-anchoring methods, such as DS-NeRF, exploit SfM-derived sparse 3D points as free depth supervision to constrain metric structure. Third, geometry regularization methods, such as RegNeRF, stabilize sparse-input reconstruction by discouraging degenerate density distributions and implausible geometry. These advances are highly relevant to our study, but none of them is directly tailored to the coupled challenges of banana plants, where handheld motion, severe self-occlusion, weak texture, large laminar leaves, and non-ideal pseudostem morphology coexist in the same capture sequence.

NeRF applications in agriculture and phenotyping

Given NeRF's high-fidelity expression capabilities for complex geometric details and texture information, recent research in agricultural engineering has applied it to the 3D reconstruction of crops/plants, further supporting non-destructive phenotypic information extraction [37]. Early attempts focused on plant reconstruction in controlled laboratory environments. For instance, Zhu et al. proposed a NeRF 3D phenotyping workflow for potted plants, combining path segmentation to achieve organ-level structural analysis and trait extraction [38]. Furthermore, Yang et al. proposed PanicleNeRF, utilizing mobile phone videos to achieve 3D reconstruction and key trait measurement of rice panicles under natural field conditions, demonstrating the feasibility of NeRF in high-throughput field phenotyping [39]. For orchard scenarios, Chen et al. proposed NeRF-F, achieving 3D reconstruction of orchard scenes based on UAV images and generating measurable point clouds/models, providing new in-sights for row structure and canopy-related analysis [40].

However, existing agricultural NeRF applications still face two unresolved issues for field phenotyping. The first is metric consistency. Most studies prioritize visual quality metrics, such as Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity (SSIM), and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS), while the recovery of real physical dimensions required for phenotyping remains limited. Although this problem can be alleviated by placing artificial Ground Control Points (GCPs) [41], doing so increases field workload. The second is thin-structure fidelity. For high-frequency details such as leaf edges, conventional radiance fields often produce bloated surfaces, foggy margins, or free-space floaters. Similar limitations have also been observed in 3DGS-based plant reconstruction when thin laminar organs are reconstructed without sufficiently strong geometric constraints [42], [43]. Compared with heavier alternatives, Instant-NGP still offers an attractive compromise between convergence speed and continuous surface modeling for mobile phenotyping workflows [6], [32], [44].

Beyond agricultural applications, NeRF-based improvements for thin or weakly textured geometry have mainly evolved along three directions. The first uses sparse or dense depth priors to stabilize geometry and reduce ambiguity [20], [45]. The second introduces geometric regularization, such as smoothness- or sparsity-based constraints, to discourage degenerate density distributions [34]. The third incorporates mask- or silhouette-assisted objectives to sharpen object boundaries, but these strategies generally rely on accurate foreground segmentation and are most suitable for object-centric reconstruction [46], [47]. These lines of work are highly informative, yet they are not directly tailored to field banana plants, where large laminar leaves, severe self-occlusion, weak texture, torn boundaries, and view-dependent reflections occur simultaneously. This gap motivates the banana-oriented thin-leaf prior developed in this study. Rather than introducing another generic thin-structure prior, our goal is to couple anisotropic normal regularization and non-surface density suppression in a banana-oriented manner so that thin-leaf continuity and measurement reliability can be improved simultaneously under handheld field acquisition.

Materials and methods

To achieve high-fidelity 3D reconstruction and automated phenotypic extraction of banana plants in uncontrolled field environments, this section proposes the BN-NeRF framework. Construction of the BN-NeRF framework

To address the coupled challenges of banana phenotyping under handheld acquisition, BN-NeRF is designed as a task-oriented extension of Instant-NGP rather than as a simple stacking of existing NeRF ideas. The design starts from three scene characteristics that frequently coexist in banana plants: (i) broad weakly textured leaves and unstable cross-view correspondence under handheld motion, which make the reconstruction highly sensitive to small pose perturbations; (ii) severe self-occlusion and insufficient geometric support on large leaf surfaces, which weaken purely photometric depth recovery and aggravate scale ambiguity; and (iii) the laminar yet highly curved morphology of banana leaves together with the semi-rigid pseudostem, which requires the reconstruction to preserve thin-surface continuity while remaining metrically reliable for subsequent phenotyping. Accordingly, BN-NeRF couples three modules with distinct but complementary roles: FGC corrects frame-wise pose perturbations under handheld capture, SGA injects sparse metric anchors from SfM geometry, and TLP imposes a banana-oriented thin-leaf prior on the reconstructed density field. Rather than relying on externally generated masks or edge maps, BN-NeRF adopts a 3D geometric regularization route, because reliable multi-view leaf boundaries are difficult to maintain under banana-canopy occlusion, specular reflection, torn leaf margins, and slight field motion. Through this module-to-challenge coupling, BN-NeRF aims to transform a visually plausible radiance field into a geometrically measurable representation for plant phenotyping. A more explicit view of the optimization architecture is provided in Fig. S1, which expands the core module in Fig. 1(b) by showing how COLMAP initialization and SfM sparse points are integrated with FGC, SGA, and TLP within the Instant-NGP backbone and coupled through a progressive warm-up-to-full optimization strategy.

Spatial representation based on multiresolution hash encoding

To achieve "fast" reconstruction and capture high-frequency details such as banana leaf veins, BN-NeRF adopts multi-resolution hash grids as the underlying encoding architecture, replacing the deep fully connected networks of traditional NeRF.

We map continuous spatial coordinates x into L layers of hash grids with different resolutions. Through trilinear interpolation, we obtain the feature vector Φ(x). We adopt lightweight MLP to predict volume density σ and view-dependent color c:(1)σ,h=MLPgeo(Φ(x))(2)c=MLPcolor(h,γ(d))Where d represents the viewing direction, and γ(·) denotes the positional encoding operator. This decoupled design ensures both the rigidity of the geometry and the accuracy of the photometric appearance.

FGC is specifically motivated by banana acquisition rather than arbitrary pose optimization. During handheld capture, broad leaf blades occupy large image regions with weak local texture, while self-occlusion and slight leaf motion reduce cross-view feature stability. Under such conditions, even geometrically plausible COLMAP poses may contain small frame-wise perturbations that are sufficient to produce ghosting or duplicated edges in NeRF reconstruction. FGC therefore assumes a reasonably initialized but imperfect pose trajectory and is intended to correct small-to-moderate perturbations rather than arbitrary pose failures. If the initialization contains large frame mismatches, abrupt viewpoint jumps, or strong non-rigid leaf deformation, the joint optimization may converge to local minima by absorbing dynamic appearance changes into geometry.

In implementation, the pose residuals were optimized jointly with the radiance-field parameters using Adam. The camera poses were initialized from the COLMAP reconstruction after global bundle adjustment, and the pose update variables were parameterized in the Lie algebra se(3). To avoid unstable early-stage drift, the first camera pose was fixed throughout training, and a smaller learning rate was assigned to pose variables than to the radiance-field parameters. The pose learning rate was set to 1×10−4, while the radiance-field learning rate was set to 1×10−2. Unless otherwise stated, these optimization settings were kept fixed for all experiments.

Sparse geometric anchoring based on rendering probability

To address the scale ambiguity of neural radiance fields in open orchard scenes, BN-NeRF leverages SfM sparse point clouds as geometric anchors. For a sparse point xi that is visible in image j, we project xi into camera Pj and use its reprojected z value in the camera coordinate system as the anchor depth Dij, following the uncertainty-aware sparse-depth supervision idea used in DS-NeRF. The expected depth of the corresponding rendered ray is calculated via volume rendering:(5)dˆij=∑k=1Kwijktkwhere the contribution weight is given by wijk=Tijkαijk, which depends on the accumulated transmittance (initialized as Tij1=1) and the opacity of the sampling point αijk=1−exp⁡(−σijkδk). To enhance robustness against outliers and stabilize the scale, we adopt a weighted Charbonnier loss function:(6)Ldepth=∑(i,j)∈Vwij(dˆij−dij)2+ϵ2where wij denotes the confidence weight of the sparse anchor associated with ray rij. In the revised formulation, δd,ij is not treated as the physical variance of depth. Instead, it is defined as a depth-uncertainty proxy derived from the reliability of the corresponding SfM point track. Specifically, for each sparse point xi, we read its average reprojection error e¯i from the COLMAP sparse reconstruction after global bundle adjustment and set δd,ij=e¯i for every image j in which xi is visible. The confidence weight is then computed as wij=1/(δd,ij+ϵw), so anchors with smaller reprojection errors contribute more strongly to the loss, whereas uncertain anchors are automatically down-weighted. Because the reprojection error provided by COLMAP is reported in pixels, δd,ij is used here as a reliability-related weighting term rather than a metrically calibrated depth variance. Accordingly, the same value is assigned to all image observations of the same SfM point track, and it should be interpreted as a track-level reliability weight rather than as a per-ray physical depth uncertainty. This uncertainty-aware weighting improves the robustness of sparse geometric anchoring to mismatched or weakly triangulated sparse points while preserving its role in absolute scale recovery.

SGA is introduced because banana phenotyping requires metric consistency and because broad weakly textured leaves often provide insufficient geometric support under photometric supervision alone. The module assumes that the SfM stage still retains a sufficient number of reliable sparse points on the pseudostem, leaf junctions, or major leaf surfaces. Under this condition, sparse anchors serve two functions simultaneously: they stabilize the depth distribution of the radiance field and recover an absolute measurement scale. However, if the sparse reconstruction is dominated by background points, contains too few plant points, or is weakened by severe blur and occlusion, the anchoring effect becomes less reliable and the resulting scale recovery may be unstable.

Progressive geometric regularization for thin structures

Banana leaves are large laminar organs with weak internal texture, severe self-occlusion, and very small physical thickness. Under photometric supervision alone, these characteristics tend to yield two coupled failure modes in radiance-field optimization: discontinuous leaf surfaces in poorly textured regions and non-surface density accumulation around leaf margins or background gaps. To address these banana-specific issues, BN-NeRF introduces a thin-leaf prior (TLP) composed of an anisotropic normal smoothness term and a spatial sparsity term.

For the normal smoothness constraint, we estimate the local surface normal n(x)=∇xσ(x)∥∇xσ(x)∥2+δ from the spatial gradient of the density field σ and apply the Total Variation (TV) regularizer in Equation (7) to adjacent samples on the reconstructed leaf surface:(7)LnTV=λn∑⟨u,v⟩∥nu−nv∥1where (u,v) denotes a pair of adjacent samples, nu and nv are the corresponding estimated normals, and λn controls the regularization strength. In implementation, because the multiresolution hash encoding in Instant-NGP is evaluated by trilinear interpolation, the density field is only piecewise smooth with respect to the spatial coordinate, and its gradients may change abruptly near grid-cell boundaries. Following the numerical-gradient rationale adopted in hash-grid-based neural surface reconstruction, we estimated the local normal using a central-difference approximation rather than relying on a single-point raw analytical gradient. Specifically, n(x)=∇σ(x)/(‖∇σ(x)||2+ϵ), with ∇σ(x)=[(σ(x+Δgex)−σ(x−Δgex))/(2Δg),(σ(x+Δgey)−σ(x−Δgey))/(2Δg),(σ(x+Δgez)−σ(x−Δgez))/(2Δg)]T. Here, Δg denotes the spatial discretization step used for numerical gradient evaluation in normalized scene space and was set to 5×10−4. The neighborhood used by the TV term was defined as Nr(x)={y∈S∣‖y−x||2≤rn}, where S denotes the surface-support sample set and rn was set to 2×10−3. In Eq. (7), adjacent sample pairs (u,v) were formed only when both samples belonged to S and satisfied ‖u−v||2≤rn. This definition confines the normal prior to locally supported laminar regions and avoids propagating smoothness across disconnected leaves or across large free-space gaps. Accordingly, the role of this term is not to oversmooth the whole plant. Instead, it stabilizes local normal variation on laminar leaf patches whose geometry should remain continuous while retaining leaf edges and vein-induced local shape variation.

For the spatial sparsity constraint, the penalty was not applied globally to the entire scene. Instead, it was evaluated on non-surface samples identified after the warm-up stage, specifically targeting the low-opacity portion of sampled rays with accumulated opacity below a threshold of 0.1. This design makes the sparsity term specifically target off-surface haze, free-space floaters, and thickness inflation around leaf margins, rather than uniformly shrinking all density features in the scene. Consequently, the sparsity term acts as a local off-surface regularizer that complements the on-surface continuity enforced by the anisotropic normal term.

We did not employ a per-leaf online adaptation of λn and λs during training. Instead, a single default pair (λn=5×10−4 and λs=1×10−5) was used in the main experiments. To evaluate its adaptability to different leaf scales, we further performed an offline sensitivity analysis on a representative subset composed of 3 small, 3 medium, and 3 large plants defined according to Table S3. These three groups also correspond to progressively increasing canopy complexity and top-leaf area, thereby providing a practical proxy for leaf-scale variability under field conditions. Rendering metrics were averaged over this 9-plant subset, whereas trait-level errors were computed on the selected top six functional leaves of each plant (N=54 leaves). One regularization weight was varied at a time while all remaining optimization settings were kept unchanged. Within the tested range {2.5×10−4,5×10−4,1.0×10−3} for λn and {5×10−6,1×10−5,2×10−5} for λs, the default pair provided the best overall trade-off between rendering quality and trait-level measurement accuracy. Reducing λn increased local normal jitter on weakly textured surfaces, whereas increasing it over-smoothed naturally curled leaf margins. Likewise, reducing λs left residual floaters and thickness inflation near leaf boundaries, whereas increasing it over-pruned weakly supported narrow edge regions. Accordingly, λn was first tuned to recover continuous laminar leaf surfaces without obvious edge collapse, after which λs was adjusted to suppress off-surface haze while preserving thin yet valid leaf structures.

Related NeRF studies have explored alternative geometric regularization routes, including sparse/depth supervision from SfM points or dense depth priors [20], [45], and geometry regularizers based on smoothness or sparsity [34]. Edge- or silhouette-assisted cues can also be beneficial when object boundaries are clean and segmentation is reliable; however, such methods typically depend on accurate foreground masks or stable multi-view boundaries [46], [47]. In field banana canopies, where self-occlusion, torn leaf margins, specular reflections, and slight wind-induced motion frequently coexist, these boundary cues are often less stable than 3D density-based constraints. For this reason, BN-NeRF adopts a banana-oriented 3D regularization route rather than relying on external edge or mask prediction. This choice should therefore be understood as a scene-dependent design decision rather than a claim that edge- or silhouette-assisted objectives are generally ineffective.

The applicability of TLP is bounded by the geometric and imaging support of the leaf structure. It assumes that most banana leaf regions can be locally approximated as thin, continuous surfaces with gradual normal variation, even though the global blade may be strongly curved or drooping. Under this assumption, TLP is effective for the dominant laminar blade regions that determine leaf length, width, and area. However, it is not expected to perfectly recover arbitrarily fine structures. Very narrow rolled edges, severely torn margins, highly specular tips, or motion-blurred leaf fragments whose image footprint approaches the effective sampling limit may remain under-resolved, and excessively strong regularization may over-smooth these local details.

Taken together, the methodological novelty of BN-NeRF does not lie in inventing pose refinement, sparse anchoring, or thin-structure regularization as isolated primitives. Instead, it lies in the banana-oriented coupling of these components within a single measurement-driven reconstruction pipeline. Relative to prior NeRF variants, FGC addresses pose instability under handheld banana capture, SGA converts sparse SfM geometry into metric anchors for phenotyping, and TLP imposes a biologically meaningful thin-leaf prior on large laminar organs. This combination is designed for quasi-static single-plant acquisition with sufficient view overlap; it is not intended for severe non-rigid motion, extremely sparse view coverage, or organ-scale details far below the effective image resolution.

Explicit Geometry Generation and preprocessing from implicit fields

Although BN-NeRF achieves high-fidelity visual synthesis via continuous volume density fields, its implicit representation is inherently a continuous function lacking explicit topological structure. Consequently, it is unable to directly support the quantitative measurement of agronomic phenotypes. This section elaborates on the mathematical process of transforming the implicit radiance field into an explicit point cloud model possessed of absolute physical metrics.

Iso-surface extraction and metric space recovery

To extract the explicit geometric surface of the banana plant, we employ the Marching Cubes algorithm to discretely sample the optimized volume density field σ(x), as illustrated in Fig. S2. As summarized in Fig. S2, this procedure consists of three steps: visualizing the implicit density field, extracting an explicit mesh via Marching Cubes at a chosen density threshold, and recovering absolute metric scale using a reference calibration marker. The iso-surface S is defined as the set of 3D points satisfying a density threshold τ:(9)S={x∈R3∣σ(x)=τ}

Let Vraw denote the set of vertices of the generated initial mesh. Due to the inherent scale ambiguity in monocular NeRF reconstruction, Vraw resides in a unitless normalized space with a random spatial pose. To address this, we introduce a reference calibration object with a known physical side length Lref. We automatically extract the point cloud of the calibration object via chromatic threshold segmentation and calculate the length of the main diagonal of its bounding box, denoted as Lˆ. Subsequently, we construct a similarity transformation model T mapping from the normalized space to the world metric space Ωworld:(10)Pworld=T(Praw)=LrefLˆ·RPraw+twhere R is the rotation matrix calculated based on Principal Component Analysis (PCA), designed to align the principal growth axis of the plant with the gravitational direction (Z-axis) of the world coordinate system; and t is the translation vector used to normalize the center of the plant's root to the coordinate origin.

To further evaluate the robustness of calibration-cube-based scale recovery, we conducted a dedicated factor-wise analysis with respect to three practical disturbance sources: cube position in the image, partial occlusion, and field illumination variation. For cube position, the 24 high-precision validation cases were grouped according to the normalized distance d between the cube-center projection and the image center into center (d<0.2), intermediate (0.2≤d<0.4), and edge (d≥0.4) groups. For partial occlusion, the visible cube region was grouped into no occlusion (>90% visible area), mild occlusion (70%–90%), and moderate occlusion (50%–70%) levels. For illumination, the cases were grouped into low (<80), moderate (80–170), and high (>170) illumination levels according to the mean 8-bit grayscale intensity of the cube region. For each condition, the relative scale error was computed as the absolute percentage deviation between the recovered cube edge length and the known physical reference. To quantify the influence of field conditions on cube extraction, we measured cube-segmentation accuracy using the 2D mask Intersection over Union (IoU) between the segmented cube mask and the manually annotated reference mask. For each case, the IoU was first computed on all retained cube-visible views and then averaged to obtain a case-level cube-segmentation accuracy value. In this study, we conservatively defined a tolerable scale drift threshold of <2.0% for banana phenotypic measurement, because this threshold is lower than the smallest one-dimensional trait MAPE (2.4%) reported in Section 4.3 and therefore prevents global scale miscalibration from dominating downstream phenotyping error.

Background filtering via geometric constraints

To address the ground background and outlier noise arising from the uncontrolled orchard environment, we propose a cascade filtering strategy based on geometric priors, as illustrated in Fig. S3. As summarized in Fig. S3, the filtering pipeline first constrains the reconstructed scene by plant-bounding-box and ground-plane priors, then removes background points through geometric filtering, and finally yields a clean segmented 3D plant model. First, the Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) algorithm is employed for robust ground plane fitting. Let the plane model be denoted as Π:nTx+d=0. We calculate the algebraic distance from each point pi in the point cloud to the plane Π. By setting a distance tolerance threshold δg, the set of ground inliers G is constructed and subsequently removed, as defined in Equation (11):(11)G={pi∈Pworld||nTpi+d||n|<δg}

Second, to mitigate the "floating" artifacts remaining in the aerial space of the radiance field, we utilize a Statistical Outlier Removal (SOR) algorithm. For an arbitrary query point pi, the mean Euclidean distance to its k-nearest neighbors, denoted as di¯, is computed. Assuming the neighborhood distances follow a Gaussian distribution N(μ,σ2), the retained set of valid plant points Pplant is defined as:(12)Pplant={pi|di¯<μglobal+α·σglobal}Where μglobal and σglobal represent the global mean distance and standard deviation, respectively, and α is a multiplier factor controlling the filtering sensitivity. Through the aforementioned processing, a banana plant point cloud model featuring precise scale and a clean background is ultimately obtained.

Automated phenotypic parameter extraction based on topological structure

Addressing the non-rigid growth and multiple curling morphological features of banana plants, traditional linear measurement methods struggle to characterize their true biomass. This paper proposes an automated phenotyping framework based on differential geometry and surface reconstruction, achieving precise calculation of key agronomic parameters from disordered point clouds.

Semantic part segmentation and pseudostem parameter inversion

As shown in Fig. 2, based on the topological connection characteristics of the "pseudostem-leaf" structure, a bottom-up hierarchical segmentation strategy is adopted. First, the Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) algorithm is utilized to separate individual plants. For the pseudostem part, we model it as a non-ideal cylinder. Leaf morphological measurement via geodesic integration

Following the removal of the pseudostem, individual leaf point clouds {Lk}k=1N are isolated using Euclidean clustering. To address the significant curvature of banana leaves caused by gravity and growth characteristics, we propose a medial-axis manifold-fitting method to replace the traditional Euclidean distance between endpoints, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The proposed geodesic phenotyping strategy assumes that the dominant blade region of a banana leaf is locally quasi-developable rather than strictly globally developable; that is, over short neighborhoods, the lamina can be approximated as a singly curved surface with near-zero Gaussian curvature and limited in-plane stretching, even though curled tips, torn margins, and locally twisted segments may violate this assumption. For this reason, geodesic integration is applied to smooth fitted curves extracted from the dominant blade region, whereas the effect of non-developable regions is evaluated separately in Section 4.3.2 and Supplementary Fig. S16.Fig. 3Workflow for precise leaf phenotyping using manifold fitting. Leaf Length Calculation (Lleaf): First, PCA is applied to determine the principal axis of the leaf. We then perform equidistant slicing along this axis using a step size of 1.0 cm, which was selected from the candidate set {0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0 cm} according to the parameter-sensitivity analysis reported in Supplementary Table S8. This step size provided a stable centroid sequence while avoiding excessive sensitivity to local high-frequency mesh noise. The resulting slice centroids were fitted using polynomial models of orders 2–6, and a quartic polynomial curve C(t) was retained because it provided the best trade-off between fitting residual and oscillatory overfitting on the validation subset. The leaf length was then defined as the arc-length integration of this fitted spatial curve:(14)Lleaf=∫01|C′(t)|dt=∫01(dxdt)2+(dydt)2+(dzdt)2dt

Leaf Width Calculation (Wleaf): For the width, a transverse point-cloud slice is extracted along the surface normal at the leaf's widest section. The transverse profile was sampled using the same step-size setting of 1.0 cm and fitted using polynomial models of orders 2–6. A quartic polynomial was retained for the transverse curve because it avoided underfitting of curled cross-sections by lower-order models while remaining more stable than higher-order fits on noisy leaf edges. The geodesic width was then calculated via curve integration along the fitted transverse surface curve. Because severely torn, locally twisted, or strongly rolled blade regions may violate the quasi-developable assumption, their error contribution was further analyzed separately in Section 4.3.2 and Supplementary Fig. S16.

Implemented mobile-cloud collaborative system for 3D reconstruction and phenotyping

The BN-NeRF workflow entails computationally intensive procedures, including radiance-field optimization, explicit geometry extraction, and phenotypic parameter estimation. Because these workloads are difficult to execute efficiently on a standalone mobile device, we implemented and functionally tested a mobile-cloud collaborative system based on a Client-Server (C/S) architecture. In this system, high-load reconstruction and phenotyping tasks are executed on a cloud-side workstation, whereas the mobile client is responsible for guided data acquisition, task submission, progress monitoring, and result visualization. Fig. S4 shows screenshots captured during actual end-to-end runs on an iPhone 13. As summarized in Fig. S4, the implemented mobile-side workflow covers guided video acquisition, asynchronous upload and task submission, cloud-side reconstruction monitoring, returned 3D visualization, phenotypic-analysis progress tracking, and final structured trait display. The purpose of this section is to document the implemented research system and its practical operating characteristics, rather than to imply a commercial-grade deployment.

System logical topology design

The overall system architecture is divided into a Mobile Acquisition Layer and a Cloud Computing Layer, with data exchange facilitated via 4G/5G/Wi-Fi wireless networks. The detailed software architectures for the cloud server and mobile client are illustrated in Fig. S5 and Fig. S6 in the Supplementary Materials, respectively. As summarized in Fig. S5 and Fig. S6, the supplementary diagrams further detail the server-side workflow from API-based task submission and asynchronous GPU processing to result persistence, and the mobile-side MVVM architecture that organizes sensing, networking, task polling, and phenotypic-result presentation.

Developed on the iOS platform (iPhone 13), this module integrates the AVFoundation framework to achieve adaptive image stream acquisition. To guarantee the robustness of SfM initialization, a shooting guidance module is embedded within the client. By utilizing IMU sensors to monitor the shooting angle in real-time, the system guides users to capture the banana plant along a spiral trajectory. This ensures data integrity and sufficient overlap at the acquisition source.

The cloud backend was deployed on the same workstation described in Section 4.1.3 (Intel Core i9-12900K CPU, 64 GB RAM, and NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU). Flask-based RESTful APIs were used for task submission, state query, and result delivery. The computational core integrates BN-NeRF training, explicit-geometry extraction, and phenotypic-parameter estimation, thereby forming a complete pipeline from raw imagery to structured trait outputs. Therefore, the interfaces shown in Fig. S4 should be interpreted as screenshots from an implemented research system captured during actual runs, rather than as conceptual interface mockups.

Data transmission and heterogeneous processing workflow

Upon user login, the mobile client packages the collected multi-view RGB image sequences together with the camera intrinsic metadata. The package is then uploaded asynchronously to the cloud server via HTTP POST requests.

Each uploaded job was assigned a unique task ID that remained unchanged throughout upload, queueing, reconstruction, phenotyping, and result return. The mobile client periodically queried the server for task states (uploading, queued, reconstructing, phenotyping, completed, or failed), and the returned 3D model and structured trait results were bound to the same task ID. This mechanism maintained synchronization between the mobile interface and the cloud-side workflow and prevented result mismatch across concurrent jobs.

Upon receiving the request, the server automatically triggers the BN-NeRF training task. Once training is complete, geometry extraction via Marching Cubes and phenotypic parameter calculation are executed immediately. This entire process is accelerated by GPU, effectively circumventing the memory constraints inherent to mobile devices.

The generated 3D point cloud model undergoes compression (e.g., using the Draco algorithm) and is encapsulated along with structured phenotypic parameters (such as pseudostem height and leaf area) into JSON format for transmission back to the mobile client.

Upon receiving the returned data, the mobile client uses the Metal graphics API to visualize the reconstructed 3D result and display structured phenotypic traits. In the present study, this workflow was functionally validated through repeated end-to-end runs using an iPhone 13 client and a cloud-side workstation, as illustrated by the screenshots in Fig. S4. A short demonstration video showing the actual operation of the implemented mobile-side app is also provided in the Data Availability Statement.

Practical validation of the implemented mobile-cloud system

To quantify the practical operating characteristics of the implemented system, we conducted 30 complete end-to-end runs using the iPhone 13 client and the cloud workstation under field 5G conditions at the South China Agricultural University (SCAU) plantation. These 30 repeated runs constituted a separate usability-oriented system-validation set and were not used in the wind-sensitivity analysis reported in Section 4.3.3. For each run, we recorded the uploaded package size, upload latency, server queueing latency, BN-NeRF reconstruction time, geometry and phenotyping time, result-return and mobile rendering time, total end-to-end latency, task success rate, and client-side crash count. Across the 30 runs, the uploaded package size averaged 69.2 ± 4.8 MB and contained approximately 108 selected RGB frames together with camera metadata. The mean upload latency was 19.5 ± 4.2 s, the mean server queueing latency was 3.5 ± 1.4 s, the mean BN-NeRF reconstruction time was 31.2 ± 2.6 s, the mean geometry and phenotyping time was 15.4 ± 2.1 s, and the mean result-return and mobile rendering time was 4.8 ± 1.2 s. The overall end-to-end latency averaged 74.4 ± 6.8 s. The task success rate was 93.3% (28/30), and no client-side crash was observed. The two failed runs were caused by orchard 5G signal interruption and by severe wind disturbance under >3.3 m/s conditions, respectively. The detailed runtime statistics and failure cases are summarized in Table S4 and Table S5. As summarized in Table S4, the implemented system completed 30 repeated end-to-end runs under field 5G conditions with an overall latency of 74.4 ± 6.8 s, a task success rate of 93.3% (28/30), and no observed client-side crash, indicating stable practical operation under the tested setting. As summarized in Table S5, the two observed failures were attributable to severe wind disturbance (>3.3 m/s), which caused SfM initialization failure, and to 5G signal interruption or handover failure, which caused upload interruption. Together with Fig. S4 and Supplementary Video, these results indicate that the current implementation supports stable end-to-end execution under the tested field conditions, while broader validation across more network settings and devices remains future work.

Results

Experimental setup and dataset Construction

Data acquisition and partitioning

The experimental data were acquired at the banana research plantation of SCAU, as depicted in Fig. S7. As summarized in Fig. S7, the site corresponds to a representative field banana-plantation environment, showing the target plant under natural orchard conditions together with the blue calibration marker used during data acquisition. To validate the robustness of the proposed algorithm across varying growth morphologies, a diverse dataset comprising 90 banana plants at distinct growth stages was constructed.

The dataset was categorized into three subsets according to pseudostem height (Hps), which was selected as the grouping criterion because it directly reflects canopy development and occlusion complexity in banana plants. For grouping, the Small Group was defined as Hps <85 cm, the Medium Group as 85 cm ≤ Hps <145 cm, and the Large Group as Hps ≥145 cm. Each group contained 30 plants, yielding a balanced total dataset of 90 individuals. The observed group-wise height range, mean pseudostem height, mean functional leaf count, and mean total area of the selected top six functional leaves are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. As summarized in Table S3, each size group contains 30 plants, and the mean pseudostem height, functional leaf count, and total area of the selected top six functional leaves all increase progressively from the Small to the Large group, confirming that the grouping also captures increasing canopy complexity.

The observed pseudostem height ranges were 40.2–84.8 cm for the Small Group, 85.2–144.6 cm for the Medium Group, and 145.1–212.3 cm for the Large Group, with corresponding mean pseudostem heights of 62.5 cm, 115.3 cm, and 178.6 cm, respectively. The mean functional leaf counts were 6.4, 8.9, and 11.6 for the three groups. These statistics confirm that the three groups represent progressively increasing canopy complexity and phenotyping difficulty.

To ensure the statistical significance of trait-level validation, the top six functional leaves of each plant were selected as the primary subjects for leaf-level measurement. For high-precision validation, a stratified random sampling strategy was adopted to construct the 24-plant subset used in Section 4.3, with 8 plants sampled from each size group. This balanced design preserves the structural diversity of the full dataset while avoiding over-representation of a single growth stage or canopy morphology.

Acquisition equipment and shooting strategy

An iPhone 13 smartphone served as the primary image acquisition terminal, utilizing its 12-megapixel wide-angle main camera to capture high-resolution imagery. During the acquisition process, the camera settings were configured to fixed white balance and autofocus mode, while HDR and flash were disabled to maintain photometric consistency across the dataset.

To address the view coverage challenges inherent in monocular reconstruction, a standardized multi-angle orbital acquisition strategy was designed, as illustrated in Fig. S8. As summarized in Fig. S8, the standardized acquisition setup combines a spiral handheld trajectory with three elevation angles (30°, 45°, and 60°) around the target plant to improve canopy coverage and cross-view overlap under field conditions. The operator handheld the device and captured images along a spiral trajectory around the plant at three distinct elevation angles (30°, 45°, and 60°), with an angular increment controlled at approximately 10°. Approximately 108 images with high overlap rates were accumulated per plant. This ensured comprehensive coverage of the canopy top and all sides of the pseudostem, providing dense visual priors essential for the implicit training of the BN-NeRF model.

For reconstruction, the handheld video stream was converted into a frame set through adaptive frame sampling followed by redundancy filtering, so that the final reconstruction set retained approximately 108 images per plant. This strategy preserved sufficient view overlap while avoiding excessive redundancy in video-based SfM reconstruction.

During acquisition, ambient wind speed was continuously monitored using a portable digital anemometer (Delixi Electric Co., Ltd., China) positioned at the average canopy height adjacent to the target plant. For each sequence, the representative wind speed was defined as the mean reading recorded over the approximately 12-s handheld capture window. Based on this value, each acquisition case was assigned to one of three wind-speed intervals: stable (<1.5 m/s), moderate (1.5–3.3 m/s), and high (>3.3 m/s), for the motion-sensitivity analysis reported in Section 4.3.3.

Experimental environment

All model training and inference tasks were executed on a high-performance workstation. The hardware configuration consisted of an Intel Core i9-12900K CPU, 64 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB VRAM). The software environment was built upon the Ubuntu 20.04 operating system, with algorithm logic implemented using the PyTorch deep learning framework.

For the SGA module, the SfM sparse reconstruction was exported from COLMAP after global bundle adjustment, and the per-point average reprojection error recorded in points3D.txt was used to compute the uncertainty term δd,ij.

The initial camera poses for BN-NeRF were also obtained from the COLMAP sparse reconstruction. In our implementation, COLMAP was used to perform feature extraction, feature matching, incremental sparse reconstruction, and global bundle adjustment, after which the optimized camera extrinsics were exported as the pose initialization for the FGC module. Because the input was collected as a handheld video sequence with strong frame-to-frame continuity, the reconstruction pipeline was configured to preserve high visual overlap while avoiding redundant neighboring frames. The optimized poses after COLMAP bundle adjustment were treated as the “reasonably initialized but imperfect” starting point for the subsequent joint pose-field optimization in BN-NeRF.

Ground truth acquisition

To facilitate quantitative accuracy assessment, manual on-site measurements were conducted synchronously. Prior to shooting, a blue cube was deployed as a calibration target for absolute physical scaling. Specifically, leaf area was measured using the grid paper method, pseudostem diameter using a vernier caliper, and pseudostem height using a high-precision laser rangefinder. These measurements served as the ground truth (GT) benchmarks for subsequent error analysis. In the revised analysis, the blue calibration cube was additionally used as a geometry-oriented reference for validating metric scale recovery by comparing the reconstructed cube dimension against its known physical size. In addition, the calibration cube was used in a dedicated robustness analysis to assess the sensitivity of scale recovery to cube position in the image, partial occlusion, and field illumination variation.

Validation of the point cloud denoising strategy

The initial point clouds generated by BN-NeRF inevitably contain environmental background artifacts and outlier noise. To validate the efficacy of the proposed cascaded denoising strategy based on geometric constraints, we quantified the variation in point cloud density before and after processing, as detailed in Table S1 in the Supplementary Material. As summarized in Table S1, the denoising procedure consistently reduced point-cloud density across small, medium, and large plants, with reduction rates ranging from 31.7% to 38.4%, indicating stable background and outlier suppression across different plant sizes.

Experimental data reveal that the algorithm achieved significant data reduction across all plant morphologies, with an average reduction ratio of approximately 34.7%. Specifically, the point count for the Small Plant decreased from 18,922 to 11,651 (a 38.4% reduction), while the Large Plant decreased from 96,342 to 63,577 (a 34.0% reduction).

It is crucial to note that this substantial reduction does not imply information loss, but rather an enhancement of the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR). Corroborated by visualization results, the excised 30%–40% of data points predominantly consist of airborne outliers and soil background noise. The algorithm successfully eliminates computational redundancy for subsequent phenotyping while preserving the core topo-logical integrity of the banana plants, such as leaf veins and pseudostem skeletons.

Geometry-oriented proxy validation of metric consistency

Because an independent scanner-grade reference mesh was not acquired for all 90 plants, geometric fidelity was further evaluated using a geometry-oriented proxy rather than an unsupported full-surface distance metric. Specifically, the blue calibration cube with a known physical side length of 10.0 cm was used as a metric reference. After scale recovery, the cube was segmented from the reconstructed geometry, and its recovered edge length was estimated from the corresponding explicit metric reconstruction. The relative scale error was then computed as the absolute percentage deviation between the reconstructed edge length and the known physical reference.

Across 24 cases in the validation subset, the mean cube scale error was 1.5% with a standard deviation of 0.3%, indicating that the proposed metric recovery pipeline preserves physical scale with low systematic bias. When interpreted together with the normalized completeness ratios in Table 2, this result provides a geometry-oriented validation of BN-NeRF beyond image rendering metrics alone. For the one-dimensional traits reported in Section 4.3, this mean scale error is lower than the corresponding MAPE values, suggesting that the remaining phenotyping errors are not dominated by global scale miscalibration alone. Accordingly, we define a conservative tolerable scale drift threshold of <2.0% for banana phenotypic measurement in this study, so that scale miscalibration remains below the smallest observed one-dimensional trait error and does not become the dominant source of downstream metric bias.

Robustness analysis of calibration-cube-based scale recovery

To move beyond the mean scale error reported in Section 4.2.5, we further analyzed the robustness of calibration-cube-based scale recovery under variations in cube position, partial occlusion, and field illumination based on the 24 high-precision validation cases. The detailed results are summarized in Supplementary Table S7. As summarized in Table S7, scale recovery remained stable under most nominal conditions, whereas edge positioning and moderate occlusion produced the largest error increase, and illumination variation mainly affected scale recovery through its impact on cube-segmentation accuracy. Because the same 24 validation cases were regrouped separately for each disturbance factor, these comparisons are intended to reveal practical sensitivity trends rather than to isolate all possible factor interactions in a fully factorial design. For position and illumination grouping, the case-level factor value was determined from the same retained cube-visible views used for IoU evaluation, so that the grouping rule remained consistent with the reported segmentation-accuracy statistics.

For cube position, the scale error remained lowest when the cube was located near the image center (1.2%±0.2%), and increased moderately when the cube appeared close to the image boundary (2.1%±0.3%). This result indicates that scale recovery is most stable when the calibration object is observed with sufficient projected area and limited perspective distortion.

For partial occlusion, the scale error remained within the tolerable threshold under no occlusion (1.2%±0.2%) and mild occlusion (1.6%±0.2%), but increased noticeably under moderate occlusion (2.4%±0.4%). This trend suggests that incomplete cube visibility reduces the stability of chromatic threshold segmentation and degrades the robustness of geometric scale estimation.

For illumination variation, the scale error remained comparatively stable under moderate and low illumination within the range of 1.3%±0.2% to 1.7%±0.3%, but the worst-case high illumination condition (e.g., specular reflection on the blue cube) produced a larger error of 1.9%±0.4%. The segmentation analysis further showed that mean cube-segmentation accuracy decreased from 95.2% under moderate illumination to 86.5% under challenging high illumination, and this degradation was accompanied by the aforementioned increase in scale error. This trend indicates that scale-recovery robustness depends not only on geometric visibility of the cube, but also on the stability of color-based cube segmentation under field illumination changes.

Overall, the proposed metric recovery pipeline remained below the predefined tolerable threshold of 2.0% under all nominal conditions, exceeding it only under adverse scenarios such as moderate occlusion (2.4%) and edge positioning (2.1%). These results suggest that keeping the calibration cube within the central-to-intermediate image region (d<0.4), preserving more than 70% cube visibility, and avoiding specular illumination can serve as practical acquisition guidelines for maintaining scale drift below the predefined 2.0% tolerance.

Extraction of leaf morphological parameters under non-rigid deformation

Banana leaves exhibit typical characteristics of large-scale curling and drooping. Traditional measurement methods based on Euclidean distance often yield significant negative biases (underestimation). The proposed method, based on medial axis manifold fitting and geodesic integration, demonstrates significant advantages in addressing this challenge.

As depicted in Fig. 6(a) and (b), the extraction results for leaf length achieved a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.9647, an RMSE of 3.44 cm, and a MAPE of 3.6%, while leaf width achieved an R2 of 0.9470, an RMSE of 2.03 cm, and a MAPE of 4.8%. The data points cluster tightly around the 1:1 reference line, indicating that the algorithm accurately tracks the geometric topology of severely curled leaves and restores the true physical dimensions along the leaf surface with limited scale distortion.

The regression results for leaf area (Fig. 6(c)) show an R2 of 0.8962 and a MAPE of 5.8%. Although the accuracy is slightly lower than that of the one-dimensional linear traits, this is primarily attributed to physical damage at the leaf edges, which leads to local boundary loss and consequently to slight underestimation during triangular mesh area integration. Nevertheless, the result remains sufficiently accurate for crop growth monitoring and trait analysis. This interpretation is further supported by the Bland–Altman agreement plots in Fig. S15, where leaf length and leaf width remain centered close to zero bias, whereas leaf area exhibits a mild negative bias together with wider limits of agreement, consistent with its higher sensitivity to boundary damage and local mesh incompleteness. As further analyzed in Section 4.3.2 and Supplementary Fig. S16, the remaining measurement bias is more pronounced in leaf regions that deviate strongly from the local quasi-developable assumption, such as curled tips, torn margins, and locally twisted blade segments.

Validation of the developability assumption and selection of geodesic parameters

To assess whether the local quasi-developable assumption used in Section 3.3.2 is supported by data, we further quantified leaf-surface developability on the 24-plant high-precision validation subset (N=144 leaves). For each metric leaf mesh after scale recovery, Gaussian curvature K was estimated by local quadratic surface fitting over the k=30 nearest mesh vertices, which provided a stable local patch size for curvature estimation without oversmoothing fine blade deformation. The developability ratio was defined as the area proportion satisfying |K|≤0.05cm−2. Leaves with a developability ratio ≥80% were categorized as quasi-developable cases (N=108), whereas the remaining leaves were treated as non-developable-dominant cases (N=36). The detailed results are summarized in Supplementary Fig. S16 and Table S8. The curvature threshold |K|≤0.05cm−2 and the 80% developability-ratio cutoff were selected empirically to obtain a stable two-group partition that remained visually consistent with the observed separation between dominant laminar blade regions and clearly curled/twisted leaf regions; they should therefore be interpreted as practical analysis thresholds adopted for this study rather than as universal physical constants.

As summarized in Fig. S16, most banana leaves remained dominated by near-developable surface regions in the central blade area, whereas non-developable regions were concentrated mainly at curled tips, torn margins, and locally twisted segments. Correspondingly, the geodesic measurement errors were lower in the quasi-developable group than in the non-developable-dominant group. Specifically, leaf length MAPE increased from 3.2% in the quasi-developable group to 4.8% in the non-developable-dominant group, and leaf width MAPE increased from 4.2% to 6.6%. This result indicates that the proposed geodesic phenotyping strategy is most reliable on dominant laminar blade regions and degrades mainly where local double curvature or severe edge damage becomes pronounced. The larger increase in width error is consistent with the fact that transverse geodesic paths are more sensitive to edge tearing, local twisting, and cross-sectional distortion than the fitted central-axis path used for leaf-length estimation.

We further evaluated the parameter selection basis of geodesic integration by sweeping the slicing step size ({0.5,1.0,2.0,5.0cm}) and polynomial orders (2–6). As summarized in Table S8, a step size of 1.0 cm and quartic fitting achieved the best overall trade-off between length/width accuracy and curve stability. Smaller step sizes (e.g., 0.5 cm) increased sensitivity to local mesh noise and tended to overestimate local geodesic fluctuation, whereas larger step sizes (e.g., 5.0 cm) reduced centroid resolution in strongly curved regions. Lower-order polynomials (orders 2–3) underfit the curved medial axis or transverse profile, whereas higher-order models (orders 5–6) introduced oscillatory overfitting artifacts near noisy leaf boundaries. These results support the final parameter choices adopted in Section 3.3.2.

Robustness of geometric fitting for plant structural parameters

Pseudostem diameter and pseudostem height are critical indicators reflecting banana growth vigor. The accuracy of these measurements hinges primarily on the precision of ground segmentation and the stability of the stem fitting process.

As illustrated in Fig. 7(a), the extraction of pseudostem diameter achieved an R2 of 0.9130, an RMSE of 0.67 cm, and a MAPE of 4.4%. Error analysis reveals that certain outliers originate from the non-ideal circularity of mature banana pseudostem cross-sections, which are often slightly elliptical. Consequently, a small deviation can arise between the mathematically fitted cylinder diameter and the arithmetic mean of the major and minor axes measured manually via calipers.

As shown in Fig. 7(b), pseudostem height measurement exhibited exceptional consistency, with an R2 of 0.9815, an RMSE of 1.25 cm, and a MAPE of 2.4%. This high precision is attributed to the implementation of RANSAC-based ground plane fitting, which effectively filters out interference from field weeds and soil clods and thereby establishes a precise Z = 0 reference datum.

Analysis of error sources

After trait-wise 3σ screening, a limited number of retained hard cases still exhibited relatively large residuals near the agreement boundaries. These cases did not exceed the formal exclusion criterion but remain informative for analyzing the practical error sources of BN-NeRF. These discrepancies can be primarily attributed to two factors.

Intermittent gusts during data acquisition caused minor displacements of leaf tips. While the pose refinement module of BN-NeRF effectively corrects global ghosting artifacts, the high-frequency jitter at the leaf tips may still induce local point cloud noise. This noise inevitably interferes with the precise determination of endpoints during leaf length measurement.

To rigorously quantify the operational envelope of BN-NeRF, we evaluated the impact of wind-induced motion across three wind-speed intervals: stable (<1.5 m/s), moderate (1.5–3.3 m/s), and high (>3.3 m/s). For this purpose, a dedicated evaluation subset consisting of 30 acquisition cases, independent of the 24-plant high-precision validation set, was analyzed, with 15, 8, and 7 cases assigned to the three intervals, respectively. In this dataset, 1.5 m/s was observed as a practical threshold beyond which reconstruction quality degraded markedly. Under stable conditions (<1.5 m/s), the framework maintained high geometric fidelity, with a Leaf Area MAPE of 6.5% and a PSNR of 31.2 dB. However, once the wind speed exceeded 1.5 m/s, a clear performance drop was observed: the Leaf Area MAPE increased to 22.1%, while the PSNR decreased to 23.5 dB. In the high-wind group (>3.3 m/s), substantial cross-view inconsistency frequently led to severe geometric ghosting and reconstruction failure (Leaf Area MAPE >45%). This degradation is attributable to the large surface-area-to-mass ratio of banana leaves, which makes them highly susceptible to non-rigid oscillation and thus violates the static-scene assumption required for radiance-field convergence. These results indicate that BN-NeRF remains reliable under mild field motion but requires relatively low-wind conditions to achieve research-grade phenotyping accuracy.

For gigantic leaves with complex morphologies, manual measurement using tape measures is prone to operational errors. This is particularly evident in scenarios involving high-position curling where the leaf surface is physically difficult to access. In such cases, the algorithmic extraction results may arguably be closer to the true physical dimensions than the manual measurements, highlighting the inherent uncertainty of manual fieldwork.

Agreement analysis of phenotypic measurements

To complement correlation-based evaluation, we further performed Bland–Altman agreement analysis for leaf length, leaf width, leaf area, pseudostem diameter, and pseudostem height, as shown in Fig. S15. For each trait, the paired difference was defined as di=xiBN-NeRF−xiManual, and the mean bias was computed as d¯. The 95% limits of agreement (LoA) were calculated as d¯±1.96sd, where sd denotes the standard deviation of the paired differences. Before calculating LoA, the paired differences were screened using the predefined 3σ rule and their normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; the corresponding results are summarized in Supplementary Table S9. Among the evaluated traits, leaf area showed the weakest normality support for the paired differences (SW-D = 0.089), but it still did not reject the normality assumption at the α=0.05 level and was therefore retained in the LoA analysis under the same predefined protocol. Under this definition, leaf length showed a mean bias of 0.34 cm, with 95% LoA from −6.40 to 7.08 cm; leaf width showed a mean bias of 0.12 cm, with 95% LoA from −3.86 to 4.10 cm; leaf area showed a mean bias of −30.0cm2, with 95% LoA from −659.2 to 599.2cm2; pseudostem diameter showed a mean bias of 0.14 cm, with 95% LoA from −1.17 to 1.45 cm; and pseudostem height showed a mean bias of 0.05 cm, with 95% LoA from −2.40 to 2.50 cm.

Across all traits, the mean biases remained small relative to the trait magnitudes. Notably, leaf area showed a slight negative bias and wider agreement limits than the one-dimensional traits, which is consistent with its higher sensitivity to boundary damage and local mesh incompleteness. By contrast, pseudostem diameter and pseudostem height exhibited the narrowest agreement intervals. These results indicate that the proposed framework is not only strongly correlated with manual measurements but also demonstrates practically acceptable agreement for field phenotyping applications.

Module effectiveness and ablation study

To rigorously investigate the individual contributions of each component within the BN-NeRF framework, we designed a hierarchical ablation strategy. The experiments aim to validate two primary hypotheses: (1) Whether the proposed FGC, SGA, and TLP effectively enhance 3D reconstruction fidelity in unstructured field environments; and (2) Whether the Medial Axis Manifold Fitting (MAMF) and Geodesic Integration (GI) strategies play a decisive role in extracting phenotypic traits from complex leaf morphologies.

Reconstruction fidelity analysis against environmental and data constraints

We conducted comparative experiments involving the Baseline model (i.e., Instant-NGP) and three variants obtained by removing FGC, SGA, and TLP, respectively. The detailed qualitative comparisons are provided in Fig. S10, while the main observations are summarized below.

For fairness, all ablation variants (w/o FGC, w/o SGA, and w/o TLP) were trained under exactly the same optimization schedule as the complete BN-NeRF model. Specifically, the Adam optimizer, the exponential learning-rate decay schedule, the batch size (262,144 rays per iteration), the total optimization steps (30,000), and the warm-up iterations (5,000) were kept strictly unchanged across all groups. In each ablation variant, only the corresponding module or loss term was removed, while all remaining optimization settings were fixed to the default configuration reported in Section 3.1.5 and Supplementary Table S2.

As summarized in Fig. S10, the three ablation variants exhibit distinct degradation patterns, whereas the complete BN-NeRF model recovers cleaner and more structurally faithful leaf geometry.

FGC and Pose Stabilization: As shown in Fig. S10(b), removing FGC (w/o FGC) leads to visible ghosting artifacts and blurred leaf margins under handheld acquisition. Quantitatively, the PSNR decreases from 32.4 dB for the complete BN-NeRF model to 31.9 dB for the w/o FGC variant. This result indicates that pose refinement remains an important contributor to rendering sharpness and structural stability under handheld capture. Notably, the w/o FGC variant still performs slightly better than the plain Instant-NGP baseline, because it continues to retain SGA and TLP even after pose refinement is removed. This indicates that the slight superiority of w/o FGC over Instant-NGP reflects the residual benefit of the remaining geometric constraints rather than the dispensability of FGC.

SGA and Weak Texture Regularization: Fig. S10(c) reveals that in the absence of SGA (w/o SGA), the leaf surfaces exhibit significant geometric collapse and airborne floaters. By leveraging sparse feature points as "geometric anchors", SGA provides explicit depth supervision to the neural network. Experimental results confirm that this module effectively prevents volumetric degeneration of the radiance field, ensuring the spatial continuity of the leaf surfaces.

TLP and Thin-Leaf Geometry Preservation: Fig. S10(d) indicates that removing TLP (w/o TLP) leads to leaf thickening, fragmented margins, and residual floating density around the blade, which together introduce systematic bias into subsequent leaf-area estimation. Importantly, TLP is not an SDF-based thickness penalty. Instead, it combines anisotropic normal smoothness on supported laminar surfaces with non-surface density suppression in surrounding free space. This coupling is particularly relevant for banana plants, where large thin leaves, weak texture, and self-occlusion make edge geometry prone to both discontinuity and volumetric thickening. As summarized in Table S6, the default TLP setting yields the best overall balance across representative small-, medium-, and large-plant subsets, whereas deviations from this setting lead to distinct failure modes, including local normal jitter, residual floaters and thickness inflation, over-smoothed curled margins, and over-pruning of narrow edge regions.

Effectiveness analysis of geometric measurement strategies

We decouple the phenotype measurement problem into three levels of geometric abstraction—topology, metric, and primitive—to systematically validate the necessity of the proposed post-processing pipeline, with the detailed visual examples provided in Fig. S11. As summarized in Fig. S11, the visual comparisons show that manifold fitting suppresses mesh-noise-induced overestimation, geodesic integration recovers intrinsic leaf length beyond Euclidean underestimation on curved leaves, and robust cylinder fitting reduces sensitivity to petiole-related and boundary outliers.

Manifold Fitting as a Geometric Low-Pass Filter. Direct measurement on the raw neural mesh (w/o MAMF) is highly susceptible to high-frequency topological noise. This causes geodesic paths to exhibit zigzag jitter on rough surfaces, artificially elongating the measurement path. The Manifold Fitting (MAMF) module functions as a geometric low-pass filter, eliminating topological anomalies and ensuring the measurement path lies on a mathematically continuous C1 surface. This regularization effectively eliminates overestimation errors caused by mesh discretization and noise.

Geodesic Integration Achieving Isometric Invariance. Banana leaves exhibit typical large-scale curling characteristics, which violate the linear assumption of Euclidean distance. Consequently, baseline Euclidean measurements exhibit significant systematic negative bias (underestimation) (R2 < 0.8), failing to capture the true physiological length. In contrast, GI adheres to the principle of isometric invariance for developable surfaces. By integrating along the manifold surface, the algorithm computationally "unfolds" the curled leaf, recovering its intrinsic geometric properties and boosting the measurement R2 to 0.95.

Cylinder Fitting for Outlier Rejection. For semi-rigid pseudostems, estimation methods based on statistical boundaries (e.g., AABB bounding boxes) are extremely sensitive to irregular cross-sections and protruding petioles, resulting in measurement values containing substantial outlier noise (RMSE = 2.25 cm). The proposed Least-Squares Cylinder Fitting (LSCF) leverages a cylindrical geometric prior to robustly fit the stem core. This primitive-based approach automatically filters out interference from non-stem artifacts, significantly reducing the RMSE to 0.67 cm.

Analysis of system operating efficiency and computational time

Quantitative analysis demonstrates that BN-NeRF achieves an order-of-magnitude acceleration in computational efficiency compared to implicit baseline algorithms. Regarding training convergence speed, as detailed in Table 4, under a high-resolution setting of 2048×2048, Vanilla NeRF requires up to 18 h 21 min, whereas BN-NeRF converges in merely 33 s. This represents a staggering speedup of approximately 2000 times. This efficiency is attributed to the implemented multi-resolution hash encoding technique, which effectively bypasses the massive computational overhead associated with deep MLPs. Furthermore, even when compared to Instant-NGP—currently the fastest SOTA method—BN-NeRF does not compromise computational speed despite the introduction of additional geometric anchoring and thin-leaf regularization constraints. Compared to the SfM-MVS pipeline commonly used in agriculture, BN-NeRF exhibits superior potential for real-time applications. Specifically, at a resolution of 2048×2048, SfM-MVS requires 1 h 20 min, while our method completes the task in just 33 s, significantly reducing the processing time. More critically, the computational time of SfM-MVS exhibits significant non-linear growth with increasing resolution (rising from 8 min to 1 h 20 min), whereas BN-NeRF demonstrates excellent resolution scalability, with a minimal time increase (from 26 s to only 33 s). This implies that our method possesses stronger adaptability for processing ultra-high-definition orchard inspection imagery in future applications.

Regarding hardware friendliness and deployment potential, experimental data indicate that BN-NeRF maintains 30 s-level rapid reconstruction while keeping video memory (VRAM) usage within the consumer-grade range. As shown in Table 4, the proposed system significantly outperforms other explicit or hybrid methods, such as Point-NeRF (which requires 38 min to 55 min) and DirectVoxGO (which requires 12 min 30 s to 18 min 17 s). These efficiency results indicate that BN-NeRF provides a favorable computational basis for future cloud-assisted or edge-assisted deployment.

The timings reported in Table 4 quantify the computational efficiency of the BN-NeRF reconstruction core on the cloud workstation. They should not be interpreted as end-to-end mobile-system latency, because practical turnaround additionally includes video upload, task scheduling, geometry extraction, phenotypic analysis, result compression, and result delivery. Therefore, system-level runtime statistics are reported separately in Section 3.4.3 and Tables S4 and S5. Taken together, these results indicate that BN-NeRF provides an efficient computational basis for cloud-assisted field phenotyping, rather than standalone real-time processing on a mobile device.

Discussion

Mechanism underlying the advantages of implicit neural representations in unstructured agricultural scenes

This study confirms the superior robustness of BN-NeRF over traditional explicit reconstruction methods (e.g., SfM-MVS) when processing complex banana orchard scenes. This performance disparity is not coincidental but stems from the fundamental differences between the two computational paradigms.

From "Feature Matching" to "Continuous Regression": Traditional MVS relies heavily on the explicit matching of feature points. In regions characterized by large-scale weak textures and high-frequency self-occlusions—such as banana leaves—feature detectors often fail, resulting in geometric holes or discontinuities in the point cloud. In contrast, BN-NeRF encodes the scene as a continuous implicit density field. Through the inductive bias of neural networks, particularly the TLP introduced in this work, it achieves reasonable geometric inference and completion for texture-less regions.

Endogenous Capacity for Dynamic Disturbance Resistance: Field environments inevitably involve wind-induced disturbances. Explicit methods demand strict multi-view geometric consistency, where even micro-displacements can lead to reconstruction failure. BN-NeRF, conversely, embeds dynamic pose optimization into the training process via FGC. This transforms rigid pose-sensitive reconstruction into a more robust joint optimization process and improves tolerance to mild field motion, although its reliability still degrades under stronger wind-induced non-rigid deformation.

Agronomic significance of automated phenotyping and digital twins

The "end-to-end" phenotyping extraction pipeline proposed by BN-NeRF marks a paradigm shift from "labor-intensive manual measurement" to "high-throughput digital monitoring."

Data Objectivity and Reproducibility. Traditional manual measurements are heavily susceptible to subjective operational factors (e.g., the reading angle of calipers) and often fail to perform non-destructive assessments on curled leaves. The proposed computational method based on geodesic integration not only resolves the systematic negative bias (under-estimation) caused by non-rigid leaf deformation but also provides an objective benchmark for establishing standardized crop growth databases.

Pathway towards Plant Digital Twins and Decision Support. Beyond outputting isolated phenotypic variables, BN-NeRF provides explicit 3D plant geometry together with metric trait estimates, which makes it a more suitable front-end for plant digital-twin workflows. In functional–structural plant modeling, 3D architecture is valuable because it can be linked to processes such as light interception, canopy microclimate, and growth-state updating, thereby supporting management-oriented prediction rather than static description.

In practical banana production, the outputs of BN-NeRF can support at least three types of agronomic decisions. First, repeated measurements of pseudostem height, pseudostem diameter, and leaf area can be used to identify plants with delayed vegetative growth or abnormal canopy expansion, thereby supporting targeted field scouting and growth monitoring. Second, the reconstructed 3D canopy geometry can serve as a structural input for subsequent light-distribution or ventilation-related analysis, which is relevant to spacing evaluation, pruning strategy assessment, and canopy management. Third, once temporally repeated observations become available, the reconstructed traits can be integrated into model-updating workflows to support growth prediction and management scheduling rather than one-time measurement alone.

Limitations and future prospects

Despite the strong performance of BN-NeRF in controlled field experiments, its practical applicability is still bounded by several conditions that should be made explicit. This practical limitation is also consistent with the motion-related error behavior discussed in Section 4.3.3.

First, wind disturbance remains an important limitation in outdoor banana phenotyping. Multi-view reconstruction methods are inherently sensitive to plant motion because cross-view inconsistency increases with wind speed, plant flexibility, and acquisition duration. In the present study, FGC improves tolerance to mild pose perturbation and moderate field motion, but it is not designed to fully compensate for strong non-rigid leaf deformation. Therefore, a practical mitigation strategy should combine both acquisition-side and model-side controls: on the acquisition side, capture should preferentially be performed under lower wind conditions, with shorter orbit duration and reduced frame redundancy; on the modeling side, future work should incorporate motion-aware temporal regularization or deformation modeling so that residual wind-induced motion is not absorbed as static geometry.

Second, the current framework is still focused on single-plant reconstruction rather than orchard-scale mapping. A feasible technical pathway for orchard-scale extension is to move from isolated per-plant optimization to row-level or block-level reconstruction with hierarchical registration. In practice, this would require three additional components: global camera-trajectory stabilization across adjacent plants, robust instance separation under inter-plant occlusion, and metric fusion of multiple local reconstructions into a larger field coordinate system. Such an extension would allow BN-NeRF to move from organ-level phenotyping toward plot-scale structural monitoring.

Third, the current study is essentially static, whereas a true 4D phenotyping workflow should explicitly model temporal change. Recent dynamic NeRF studies suggest that temporally conditioned radiance fields, canonical-space representations, and deformation fields provide a plausible technical route for extending static reconstruction to spatiotemporal modeling. For banana plants, this would make it possible to analyze not only static morphology but also organ-level growth trajectories, leaf unfolding, and disturbance recovery across time.

Finally, although the present manuscript discusses digital twins, the current system should still be regarded as a measurement-oriented front-end rather than a full digital twin in the strict sense. A more complete banana digital twin would require repeated structural observations, environmental sensing, and model updating to support prediction and management intervention. In that setting, BN-NeRF-derived traits could be used to update 3D canopy architecture, constrain FSPM-like growth models, and support decisions related to growth monitoring, canopy manipulation, and resource management.

Another limitation pertains to computational resources. Although multiresolution hash encoding has substantially accelerated optimization, the requirement for consumer-grade GPUs still constrains fully on-device execution and broader low-latency edge deployment. Future work will therefore focus on model lightweighting, faster geometry extraction, and more extensive mobile-cloud validation under practical field conditions.

Conclusion

This study presents BN-NeRF, a novel framework designed specifically for rapid 3D reconstruction and automated phenotyping of banana plants using handheld consumer-grade devices. To overcome the challenges posed by camera jitter during field acquisition, depth ambiguity in open orchard environments, and the typical thin laminar structure of banana leaves, we integrated FGC, SGA, and TLP strategies into the Instant-NGP architecture. Experimental results demonstrate that BN-NeRF significantly outperforms current SOTA neural rendering methods, achieving superior visual fidelity (PSNR = 32.4 dB) and effectively eliminating ghosting and background floating artifacts. Furthermore, by converting implicit radiance fields into explicit geometry with physical scale, the framework enables high-precision phenotyping. Beyond rendering quality, the evaluation also verified measurement-oriented reliability through normalized completeness analysis, calibration-cube-based scale validation, and trait-level agreement analysis. The extracted phenotypic traits—including leaf length, leaf width, leaf area, pseudostem diameter, and pseudostem height—showed consistent agreement with manual ground truth measurements, as supported by the trait-specific regression, MAPE, and Bland–Altman analyses reported in Section 4.3. In summary, this research bridges the gap between low-cost, rapid data acquisition and high-precision digital agriculture, providing a ro-bust and non-destructive tool for field-based crop monitoring. Future work will focus on three directions: improving robustness under stronger wind-induced non-rigid deformation, extending the current single-plant workflow toward orchard-scale hierarchical mapping, and integrating temporally conditioned reconstruction into a 4D phenotyping framework for dynamic growth analysis and model updating.
