Ultra‐Processed Food Consumption and Childhood Allergic Diseases: Increased Risk of Asthma Onset in the SENDO Project
Authors: O. Galindo, M. J. Goikoetxea, L. Moreno‐Galarraga, L. Argiz, J. M. Moreno‐Villares, Victor de la O, N. Martín‐Calvo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWhile the role of genetic predisposition in asthma and other allergic conditions is well established, the contribution of nutritional patterns is heterogeneous and has been demonstrated in cross‐sectional studies but not in prospective cohorts.MethodsWe analyzed data from 1546 participants enrolled in the SENDO cohort between January 2015 and June 2024. Children aged 4–5 years were prospectively recruited. UPF consumption was assessed at baseline and updated at the 3‐year follow‐up. Information on asthma and allergic diseases was collected at baseline and updated annually during the follow‐up. A final sample of 691 participants was classified into tertiles (T1, T2, T3) according to UPF consumption. Prevalent cases were excluded from the analysis to ensure that incident cases were included during the follow‐up. In the main analyses, we calculated the adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CI with survival analyses.ResultsAfter a mean follow‐up of 3.4 years, the adjusted risk for asthma in each tertile (T1, T2, T3) of UPF consumption was 2.6%, 9.9%, and 7.6% respectively (p for trend: 0.03). In the fully adjusted model of the survival analysis, children with greater UPF consumption (T2 + T3) showed a significantly higher risk of asthma (HR 3.76; 95% CI 1.15–11.51, p = 0.02) but not of AA, nor other allergic outcomes compared with their peers in the lowest tertile (T1) of UPF consumption.ConclusionHigher UPF consumption may be associated with an increased risk of developing asthma in school‐age children.

Introduction

Asthma and atopic diseases have become a growing public health issue in industrialized countries [1]. Prevalence of asthma and allergic diseases in childhood has risen significantly in developed regions [2, 3, 4]. However, trends in the burden of asthma and atopic conditions across the world are heterogeneous [4, 5, 6]. While genetic predisposition is a well‐established determinant of these conditions, growing evidence indicates that lifestyle factors such as diminished physical activity, tobacco exposure, and poor nutrition also play a significant role [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

Evidence suggests that children with healthier dietary patterns—characterized by lower fast food intake and greater consumption of fruits and vegetables—are less likely to exhibit symptoms of asthma, atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, and reduced allergic sensitization [12, 13, 14]. The association between dietary patterns and the risk of asthma and atopic diseases in children has attracted considerable research interest in recent years, with several studies focused on assessing the effect of maternal diet during pregnancy and the child's food diversity during the first years of life [12, 15].

Ultra processed food (UPF) consumption in childhood emerged as a potential risk factor for the onset and progression of asthma and wheezing through various mechanisms since UPF consumption may promote low‐grade inflammation, through activation of advanced glycation end (AGE) products leading to chronic inflammatory diseases of the airways [16, 17]. The pro‐inflammatory profile of UPFs, driven by high SFA and low fiber content, fosters systemic inflammation [18]. Specifically, SFAs activate Toll‐like Receptor 4 (TLR4) and the NF‐κB pathway, releasing cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐8) that increase airway hyperresponsiveness [19]. Concurrently, thermal processing generates advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs) which bind RAGE receptors. The convergence of SFA‐TLR4 and AGE‐RAGE signaling on NF‐κB synergistically amplifies inflammation and oxidative stress, promoting epithelial damage and bronchial remodeling [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Most of the evidence on the association of diet with atopic diseases and wheezing in children is based on cross‐sectional or case–control studies, which have critical methodological limitations that hamper the inference of a true biological association [13, 26, 27, 28].

Due to the lack of prospective studies in this area, this research aims to explore the association between the consumption of UPF in childhood and the risk of asthma and allergic diseases onset. This focus addresses a critical gap in the literature and contributes to a better understanding of the role of nutrition in asthma and allergic disease etiology.

Methods and Materials

Study Population

The Child Follow‐Up for Optimal Development (SENDO project: Seguimiento del Niño para un Desarrollo Óptimo) is an ongoing prospective pediatric cohort of Spanish children focused on understanding the effect of diet and lifestyle on children's health and development. Recruitment started in 2015, and it is permanently open.

Pediatricians from our team actively recruit participants aged 4 or 5 through schools and healthcare centers nationwide. Baseline information has been collected for 1546 participants enrolled between January 2015 and June 2024. The inclusion criteria were that participants fall within the specified age range and maintain residency in Spain for the duration of the study period. The sole exclusion criterion was the absence of an internet‐connected device, which was necessary to complete the questionnaires.

Participants recruited after March 2023 were excluded to ensure a minimum follow‐up period. This criterion guaranteed that all analyzed cases had sufficient follow‐up time. Additionally, participants lost to follow‐up, defined as those who completed the baseline questionnaire but no subsequent questionnaires, were also excluded.

To assess the incidence rate of asthma and allergic conditions associated with UPF consumption, we identified the cohort at risk for each outcome. To do so, prevalent cases of each outcome, defined as participants who reported asthma, allergic asthma (AA), atopic dermatitis (AD), aeroallergen sensitization (AS), food allergy (FA), or atopy in the baseline questionnaire, were excluded from the analysis of the corresponding outcome. After accounting for losses and missing data, the final sample was defined for each outcome. Figure 1 illustrates the recruitment and follow‐up of participants for all evaluated outcomes.

Flowchart of participant selection and inclusion for the analytic sample.

The SENDO study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee for Clinical Research of Navarra (Pyto. 2016/122). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or guardians of all participating children.

Questionnaire

Dietary information was collected using a previously validated 147‐item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) at baseline and at the third year of follow‐up [29]. A group of dietitians calculated the macro‐ and micronutrient content of each food item, including energy, using Spanish food composition tables. All food items were categorized based on their level of processing according to the NOVA classification system. The classification comprised four distinct groups: Group 1, unprocessed or minimally processed foods; Group 2, processed culinary ingredients; Group 3, processed foods; and Group 4, ultra‐processed foods (Table S1). The proportion of total energy intake (TEI) derived from each NOVA group was calculated as follows: (energy contribution of each group/TEI) × 100 [20]. UPF consumption was calculated at baseline, and the updated mean was used for participants diagnosed with any of the evaluated outcomes after the third year of follow‐up.

Case Definition for Data Analysis

The asthma definition derived from parental responses to specific questions, including: “Has your child been diagnosed with asthma by a physician?” or “Has your child received asthma‐related treatment (e.g., inhaled bronchodilators [IB] or inhaled corticosteroids [ICS])?” or “Has your child received treatment with inhaled combinations (IB + ICS), oral corticosteroids or leukotriene?” or “has your child been diagnose of either bronchitis or bronchial respiratory syndrome?”.

To establish a comprehensive and clinically relevant definition of AA, we considered the cases of asthma that additionally reported a positive answer to the question, “Has your child been diagnosed with atopic dermatitis or tested positive for aeroallergens?”

For the rest of allergic outcomes were taken from the annual questionaries under the label “has your child been diagnose by a doctor of AD, FA or AS in the last year?”

The definition of atopy included cases of AA, AD, AS, or evidence of FA.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on their potential as confounders and included sociodemographic variables (age, sex, and race), anthropometric measures (weight status), lifestyle variables (physical activity and screen time), breastfeeding practices, energy intake (continuous), parental knowledge about nutritional recommendations (low, medium, high score) for children, parental attitudes toward child's dietary habits (low, medium, and high score), and parent smoking status.

Family history of atopy was assessed through the baseline questionnaire considering parental/sibling aeroallergen sensitivity or allergic conditions (asthma, rhinitis or FA). Finally parental knowledge of nutritional recommendations was assessed by asking parents how often children should consume 16 food groups (e.g., fruits, vegetables, dairy). Responses were scored as a percentage and categorized as low (< 40%), average (40%–70%), or high (> 70%). Parental attitudes toward their child's dietary habits were assessed with eight yes/no questions on support for healthy eating. Scores were categorized as unhealthy (0–3), average [4, 5], or healthy [6, 7, 8], with unhealthy attitudes as the reference category.

Statistical Analysis

For descriptive purposes, we used mean and standard deviation (sd) for quantitative variables and percentages for qualitative ones. To assess the association between UPF consumption and the risk of asthma and allergic outcomes, we employed two analytical approaches. First, to evaluate the dose–response relationship, we introduced an ordinal variable corresponding to increasing tertiles of UPF intake into the regression models (p for trend). Second, based on the observed distribution, children in the second (T2) and third (T3) tertiles were combined into a single category representing high UPF consumption (participants consuming > 30% of total energy intake). This binary variable was compared against the first tertile (T1), which served as the reference category. To calculate the p‐value for the linear trend tests, we created a new variable by imputing the median UPF consumption in the corresponding tertile and treating it as a continuous variable. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using the Schoenfeld residuals test. The results showed no evidence of assumption violation, neither globally (Chi2 = 10.12; p = 0.86) nor for the ultra‐processed food consumption variable (p = 0.22). Consequently, we proceeded with the Cox model without including time‐dependent effects.

Hierarchical Linear Models were used to account for the potential correlation between siblings. This approach models the dependency within groups and adjusts the analysis for both individual‐ and group‐level factors. The model employs an exchangeable correlation structure. All the analyses were performed with Stata18.0, StataCorp LLC., and the statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All p‐values are two‐sided.

Association Between UPFs Consumption and Asthma and Allergic Diseases

We first calculated the marginal effect of UPF consumption on the risk of asthma and allergic diseases, that is, the adjusted risk of each outcome by tertile of UPF consumption in a model adjusted for age (continuous), sex (male vs. female), and energy intake (quintiles), parental knowledge of nutritional recommendations for children (low, medium, high score), and parental attitudes toward their child's dietary habits (low, medium, high score), screen time (continuous), and children's weight status (underweight, normal weight, overweight). To calculate the relative risk (RR) of each outcome associated with UPF consumption, we used the xtgee function with binomial family, logit link, exchangeable correlation structure, and robust variances, resulting in odds ratios. The method described by Zhang et al. [30], was then used to correct the result and obtain the relative risk (RR).

In the main analyses, we assessed the Hazard Ratio (HR) and 95% CI of each outcome associated with UPF consumption using Cox proportional hazard models and represented the cumulative hazard function using Nelson‐Aalen's curves. For these analyses, children in the second (T2) and third tertiles (T3) of UPF consumption were combined into a single category (children with UPF consumption over 30% of total energy intake), and T1 was used as the reference category.

Follow‐up was defined as the time difference between the date of completion of the baseline questionnaire and the date of diagnosis of the corresponding outcome or the date of the last questionnaire, whichever came first. If the diagnosis date was missing, the completion date of the questionnaire was used instead.

Crude and multivariable adjusted estimates were calculated. Multivariate analyses were progressively adjusted for key confounders [8] such as age (continuous), sex (male vs. female), and energy intake (quintiles), parental knowledge of nutritional recommendations for children (low, medium, high score), and parental attitudes toward their child's dietary habits (low, medium, high score), screen time (continuous), exclusive breastfeeding, and children's weight status (underweight, normal weight, overweight).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the association between the consumption of UPF in childhood and the subsequent development of asthma and allergic diseases, such as AS, AD, FA, and atopy in a cohort of Spanish children aged 4–5 years at recruitment. Our analysis revealed a positive linear trend on the risk of asthma, with an adjusted risk of asthma of 7.6% among children with high UPF consumption. Moreover, after a mean follow‐up of 3.4 years, we observed that children who consumed more than 30% of TEI from UPF had a 3.76‐fold higher risk of developing asthma than their peers with lower consumption (< 30% of TEI) after adjusting for potential confounders. The dose–response gradient observed in asthma cases suggests that the association may operate through gradual risk accumulation, which is frequent in nutritional epidemiology. These findings strengthen the evidence linking UPF consumption to chronic conditions, highlighting urgent concerns as global UPF consumption increases, particularly among young people [15].

Cross‐sectional studies have previously reported links between UPF consumption or proxies, such as Western dietary patterns, and respiratory symptoms [3] high UPF consumption (> 40% of TEI) was associated with significantly increased odds of wheezing respiratory diseases (OR: 1.87, 95% CI: 1.01–3.45) and, specifically, bronchitis/recurrent wheezing (OR: 2.12, 95% CI: 1.10–4.05) [19]. Similarly, analysis of NHANES data linked the highest quartile of UPF consumption in children to 76% increased odds of asthma (OR: 1.76, 95% CI: 1.10–2.82) [31]. Despite these findings, methodological limitations inherent to cross‐sectional studies hamper the inference of a causal association [20, 31]. In contrast, our longitudinal analyses guarantee the temporal sequence and add to the previous evidence that UPF consumption is associated with the risk of incident asthma in childhood.

However, our findings are not consistent with those of the Pelotas birth cohort in Brazil, which reported no significant association between UPF consumption at age 6 and risk of wheezing (OR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.54–1.34) or asthma (OR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.58–1.21) at age 11 [32]. Discrepancies between these studies and our findings could stem from various factors, including differences in population characteristics (age range, geographic location, baseline risk), methodologies for assessing UPF consumption (e.g., FFQ limitations, NOVA classification challenges) [17], definition of asthma and wheeze outcomes [33], residual confounding [34], or the specific types and amounts of UPFs consumed within different populations.

Asthma is recognized as a heterogeneous condition comprising different endotypes, broadly classifiable as allergic (high Th2 Response) and non‐allergic (non‐atopic, involving other pathways, or Type V defined in new nomenclature proposed by Jutel et al. [22]). Atopy represents the underlying genetic predisposition to mount IgE responses to common environmental allergens [35]. The finding of no significant association between UPF consumption and the development of AS, AD, FA, or atopy in our cohort is particularly intriguing when contrasted with the association between UPF consumption and asthma.

The lack of association of UPF consumption with strictly atopic outcomes such as AA, AS, FA, atopy or even protective association in AD in the late onset childhood IgE related phenotype [36], reinforces the theory that UPF‐driven inflammation operates through pathways distinct from the classic T2‐mediated/IgE cascade. This is consistent with the findings of Roduit et al. [37], who demonstrated that late‐onset atopic dermatitis is not a significant risk factor for the development of asthma after 6 years of age. These findings suggest that UPF consumption may promote a non‐atopic inflammatory endotype rather than driving traditional allergic sensitization.

It is worth noting that the proportion of children with a family history of allergy was inversely associated with UPF consumption, suggesting that the parents of children with patients with a higher genetically‐determined predisposition to atopy were more aware of their child's diet. Although this limitation, our findings suggest that the effect of diet on the risk of asthma may operate independently of atopic mechanisms, as it has been shown in other studies [5]. Venter et al. found in a study of 962 child–mother dyads followed out to 8 years, that there were no associations between maternal AGE intake in pregnancy and any of the atopic outcomes studied (food allergy, atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, and asthma), [15, 28], supporting the hypothesis that UPF may operate predominantly through non‐atopic mechanisms.

The fact that the children were followed from the age of 4–5 for an average of 3.4 years aligns the survey with the age range in which the highest incidence of asthma cases occurs. If the impact of UPF consumption is stronger in this age group, as suggested by studies in other populations [32], a synergistic effect could contribute to a higher incidence of asthma, particularly among children with elevated UPF intake. We acknowledge that our estimates may represent the upper limit of the real association between UPF consumption in childhood and the risk of asthma. However, the longitudinal design of our study, its consistency with previous evidence, the biological plausibility, and the dose–response relationship are important arguments in support of a real biological effect. If confirmed, our findings suggest that UPF consumption could represent a particularly potent modifiable risk factor for childhood asthma in specific populations or contexts.

Our findings must be interpreted with caution, as UPF consumption represents only one component of the complex gene–environment interactions driving asthma. Pathogenesis involves various exposures—including air pollution, tobacco smoke, and infectious agents [36, 37, 38]—that collectively drive systemic inflammation. Thus, UPF likely contributes to a cumulative inflammatory burden that precipitates asthma in susceptible individuals [16].

Our study represents the first longitudinal assessment of UPF consumption and asthma risk at school ages permitting reliable diagnosis. The prospective design establishes temporality—exposure preceding outcome—mitigating reverse causation and recall bias common in retrospective studies [39]. The robustness of our dietary data is supported by prior validation of the SENDO cohort [29]. Furthermore the prospective design establishes a clear temporal sequence, with UPF exposure preceding asthma diagnosis. This minimizes the risks of reverse causation and recall bias common to retrospective studies [30, 40]. Observing a dose–response relationship between UPF intake and asthma incidence provides additional evidence of a true association due to its biological plausibility of the association and suggests it is not an artifact of overfitting. The use of a well‐characterized cohort allowed for the adjustment of a wide range of potential confounders. Nevertheless, as an observational study, unmeasured factors could still influence the observed association. Finally, the application of Hierarchical Linear Modeling appropriately accounted for the statistical non‐independence of data arising from the inclusion of siblings, yielding more accurate effect estimates and standard errors [20, 23, 41].

We must acknowledge some limitations of our study. Although we used a previously validated FFQ, which is the preferred tool to measure usual dietary intake, it was not designed to specifically measure UPF consumption and may be less accurate than other methods, such as dietary records [14]. Similarly, the assessment of outcomes, even if based on clinical assessment or parental report following standardized criteria, can be challenging in children, and the lack of objective criteria for defining allergic asthma, FA (food oral challenge), or atopy (e.g., IgE thresholds) could influence the null finding for that outcome. These limitations could lead to a misclassification bias. However, considering the design of this study, if misclassification existed, it would be a non‐differential misclassification, which tends to bias the estimate toward the null value, which means that the real association would even be stronger [42]. The SENDO project predominantly comprises highly educated families. While this sociodemographic homogeneity limits the statistical representativeness of our sample relative to the general population, it simultaneously strengthens the study's internal validity. By restricting the cohort to a specific socioeconomic stratum, we significantly reduce the potential for residual confounding by socioeconomic status and unmeasured lifestyle factors often linked to it (the ‘restriction’ effect). Furthermore, it is important to distinguish between representativeness and generalizability; the latter relies on biological plausibility and universal pathophysiological mechanisms rather than solely on demographic similarity [19]. Although we acknowledge the lack of data on environmental pollutants as a limitation, our study design prioritized isolating dietary factors (UPFs) given the relatively limited evidence linking environmental exposures to new‐onset asthma compared to nutritional influences [43].

Finally, the number of incident cases was relatively small, which resulted in wide confidence intervals. However, the prospective study design, the rigorous adjustment for potential confounders, and the consistent dose–response signal support the robustness of our results and suggest a true causal association.

These findings should be confirmed before definitive conclusions can be drawn. Mechanistic studies are needed to investigate the underlying biological mechanisms in the association between UPF and asthma. This includes the measurement of systemic and airway inflammatory markers [25, 44] and a comprehensive analysis of the gut microbiome, encompassing metagenomics to describe changes in microbial composition and metabolomics to investigate the role of potential mediators, such as butyrate and propionic acid [45, 46]. Experimental studies, both in vitro and in vivo, are required to assess the independent effects of specific UPF additives, processing‐derived contaminants like AGE, and chemicals migrating from packaging on airway inflammation and immune responses [47]. Replicating these findings in diverse populations with different genetic backgrounds, dietary habits, and environmental exposures is essential to describing potential interactions between genes and environment [44].

In conclusion, we found a strong association between UPF consumption and asthma in school age patients, but not with AA, AD, AS, or FA, suggesting that low‐grade inflammation, rather than atopic mechanisms, may mediate the observed relationship. These findings highlight the need for public health policies aimed at limiting processed food consumption in children's diets as a preventive strategy.
