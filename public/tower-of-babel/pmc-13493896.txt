Behavioral and temporal dynamics of child pedestrian crash injury patterns: evidence from random parameter modeling
Authors: Sazzad Bin Bashar Polock, Michael Starewich, Swastika Barua, Anannya Ghosh Tusti, Shriyank Somvanshi, Tausif Islam Chowdhury, Nawaf Alnawmasi, Subasish Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the behavioral, environmental, and roadway-related determinants of injury severity in crashes involving child pedestrians (aged under 15), utilizing six years (2017–2022) of crash data from Texas. A random-parameter logit model is employed to account for unobserved heterogeneity in driver behavior, roadway context, temporal conditions, and environmental factors. The results reveal that higher injury severities are significantly associated with crashes occurring on two-lane, two-way roads, at curved alignments, and in areas lacking traffic control, where driver expectancy and pedestrian visibility may be compromised. In contrast, crashes involving backing maneuvers and those in large urban areas are more likely to result in no or minor injuries, possibly reflecting lower vehicular speeds and improved pedestrian-supportive infrastructure. The analysis also identifies year-to-year variability in the effects of key risk factors such as distraction, speeding, and roadway features, indicating the presence of temporal instability in crash dynamics. Crashes occurring during morning and late evening hours are consistently associated with greater injury severity, showing the role of visibility, traffic conditions, and behavioral factors such as driver alertness. These findings have important implications for child pedestrian safety programs and support targeted countermeasures, including improved lighting, traffic calming, signalization at uncontrolled sites, and expanded school-zone protections. By incorporating a behavioral safety lens and accounting for time-varying effects, this study contributes valuable insights for policymakers and practitioners aiming to reduce child pedestrian injuries through context-sensitive and evidence-based interventions.Keywords: Child pedestrian, Injury severity, Random parameter, Unobserved heterogeneity, Temporal instabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Pedestrian safety continues to pose serious challenges to public health and urban transportation systems, particularly for children. In 2022 alone, 1,105 children aged 14 and younger were killed in motor vehicle traffic crashes in the United States, including 165 child pedestrians, accounting for 15% of all child traffic fatalities and reflecting a 13% increase from the previous year1. In 2021, there were 1,093 fatalities among children under 14 years old, with 158 of them being pedestrians2. These figures highlight an unsettling trend: while overall child traffic fatalities have risen in recent years, child pedestrian fatalities remain a significant and persistent subset. Between 2012 and 2021, 1,746 child pedestrians lost their lives in traffic crashes nationwide, and many more sustained severe or life-altering injuries2. This underscores the vulnerability of child pedestrians in the current traffic environment and calls for urgent, targeted safety interventions.

This figure is intended to provide broader motivation for the study by showing that child pedestrian fatalities have remained a persistent safety concern over time. The trend shows that child pedestrian fatalities fluctuated during the period, increasing from 207 in 2014 to a peak of 248 in 2016, followed by a general decline through 2021, when fatalities reached 177. This post-2016 decline suggests that some progress may have been made in reducing child pedestrian fatalities during this period. Nevertheless, the increase to 187 fatalities in 2022 indicates that child pedestrian safety remains an ongoing concern and that recent improvements should be interpreted cautiously. While these national trends establish the broader relevance of the issue, the empirical analysis in this study focuses specifically on crash/person-level injury severity patterns among child pedestrians in Texas using the Texas Department of Transportation’s Crash Records Information System (CRIS) data from 2017 to 2022.

Numerous studies have investigated the underlying causes of child pedestrian crashes, identifying both behavioral and environmental contributors. Children often struggle with visual scanning, depth perception, and impulse control, all of which hinder their ability to make safe decisions in dynamic traffic situations3,4. Furthermore, infrastructural factors, such as the absence of sidewalks, inadequate signage, and high-speed road segments near residential areas or schools, exacerbate their vulnerability5,6. Time-of-day effects, particularly after-school hours, and poor visibility conditions further elevate crash risk for this age group7.

Child pedestrian safety is also shaped by structural inequities. Studies have shown that low-income neighborhoods and communities of color are often disproportionately affected due to disparities in road safety infrastructure and enforcement practices8. In such areas, pedestrian facilities are often poorly maintained, crossings are infrequent or unsafe, and driver compliance with pedestrian right-of-way laws is significantly lower.

While prior research has extensively examined pedestrian injury patterns, behavioral risk factors, and location-specific hazards, existing studies have paid comparatively less attention to child pedestrian injury severity using crash/person-level data while explicitly accounting for unobserved heterogeneity and year-to-year temporal instability9–11. This gap is important because the factors associated with child pedestrian crash outcomes may vary across roadway, traffic, environmental, and temporal contexts rather than remaining constant over time. Prior studies have also noted that safety interventions, such as school crossing guards, traffic calming infrastructure, and pedestrian education programs, are often implemented in specific locations or under limited program settings, and many lack systematic long-term evaluation, follow-up, or scalability assessment3,5,12. In this study, this intervention-related limitation is used as contextual motivation rather than as a direct evaluation objective. The present analysis does not evaluate the effectiveness of specific interventions; instead, it seeks to identify the behavioral, roadway, temporal, and environmental factors associated with child pedestrian injury severity and to examine whether these effects remain stable or vary across years. Such evidence can support the development and prioritization of more targeted safety countermeasures.

Building on this motivation, the current study analyzes child pedestrian-involved crashes in Texas from 2017 to 2022 using data from the CRIS. Specifically, this study applies random-parameter multinomial logit models with heterogeneity in means to examine the behavioral, roadway, traffic, temporal, and environmental factors associated with child pedestrian injury severity. Separate year-specific models are also estimated to evaluate temporal instability in the determinants of injury severity. National-level fatality trends are used only as background motivation, while the empirical analysis focuses on Texas crash/person-level injury severity outcomes.

Literature review

Child-specific vulnerability in pedestrian crashes

Child pedestrians represent a particularly vulnerable road-user group because their crash risk and injury outcomes are shaped by developmental, behavioral, and exposure-related factors. Unlike adult pedestrians, children often have limited visual scanning ability, immature hazard perception, reduced speed and distance judgment, and greater impulsivity when interacting with traffic environments, which can make it difficult for them to assess traffic gaps or anticipate driver behavior in complex roadway settings. Prior studies have shown that younger children frequently struggle to recognize hazardous traffic situations and may misjudge vehicle speeds, thereby increasing their crash risk13,14. Gender-based behavioral differences have also been reported, with boys more likely to engage in risky pedestrian behaviors, often influenced by peer pressure and lower perceived risk15. Socioeconomic conditions further shape child pedestrian safety because children from disadvantaged communities may experience higher exposure to unsafe walking environments, limited safety education, and inadequate pedestrian infrastructure16. Adult supervision is another important factor, particularly in residential streets, driveways, and parking lots, where unsupervised children may be more exposed to low-speed but frequent vehicle conflicts17. These findings indicate that child pedestrian injury severity cannot be understood solely through roadway design or traffic conditions. Child-specific developmental, behavioral, and social vulnerability also helps the interpretation.

Roadway, traffic, and environmental risk factors

The built environment plays a central role in shaping child pedestrian crash outcomes. Previous studies have identified roadway and traffic-related factors associated with more severe pedestrian injuries, including high-speed facilities, midblock locations, uncontrolled crossings, limited pedestrian infrastructure, and poor lighting conditions18–20. Rural roads may further increase injury severity because of higher operating speeds, limited sidewalks or shoulders, longer emergency response times, and reduced pedestrian accommodation21,22. At intersections and crossing locations, right-turn-only lanes, nearby nonresidential driveways, and commercial land uses have been associated with higher pedestrian crash risk23. Conversely, pedestrian-oriented treatments such as median refuges, pedestrian signals, and traffic calming installations have been shown to reduce severe injury risk in certain contexts9,24,25. Roadway geometry is also important, as curves, grades, and restricted sight distance may reduce drivers’ ability to detect pedestrians in time to avoid or mitigate a crash26–28. Environmental conditions can further modify injury severity by influencing driver perception, braking distance, and pedestrian conspicuity. For example, darkness, insufficient lighting, rainy weather, and reduced visibility conditions have been linked with higher pedestrian injury severity in prior study26. Together, these studies suggest that child pedestrian injury outcomes are shaped by multiple interacting roadway, traffic, and environmental conditions.

Temporal patterns and temporal instability in child pedestrian safety

Temporal characteristics are also important in understanding child pedestrian crash injury patterns. Prior studies have shown that child pedestrian crashes often cluster around school commute periods, after-school hours, evenings, weekends, and seasonal periods associated with higher outdoor activity16,29,30. Morning and afternoon peaks may reflect school-related exposure, while evening and nighttime crashes may involve reduced conspicuity, driver fatigue, higher travel speeds, and limited lighting31–35. Seasonal and special-event conditions may also influence child pedestrian safety through changes in daylight duration, weather, pedestrian activity, and driver expectancy36,37. Beyond identifying when crashes occur, recent crash-severity research emphasizes that the effects of contributing factors may not remain stable over time because roadway use, traffic volume, enforcement patterns, school attendance, and travel behavior can change across years38-41. This issue is particularly relevant for the 2017–2022 study period because pandemic-era disruptions may have altered traffic volumes, driver behavior, school travel, and child pedestrian exposure. Therefore, examining temporal instability is important for determining whether the factors associated with child pedestrian injury severity remain consistent or vary across annual periods.

Evolution of crash severity modeling approaches

Crash injury severity has traditionally been analyzed using discrete outcome models, including binary logit, multinomial logit, ordered logit, ordered probit, and related probability-based approaches40–42. These models provide interpretable estimates of the relationships between crash factors and injury outcomes and have been widely used in transportation safety research. However, conventional fixed-parameter models assume that the effect of each explanatory variable is constant across observations, which may be restrictive in pedestrian crash analysis because the effects of roadway, behavioral, temporal, and environmental factors can vary across crash contexts. Ordered models account for the natural ranking of injury severity but impose restrictive assumptions on the direction and magnitude of covariate effects across severity levels43. Multinomial logit models avoid the ordinal constraint by allowing separate estimation across injury categories, but standard specifications may still overlook unobserved heterogeneity44. Random-parameter and mixed logit models address this limitation by allowing selected coefficients to vary across observations, thereby capturing unobserved differences in crash circumstances, road-user behavior, and contextual risk45–49. Extensions such as random-parameter models with heterogeneity in means further allow observed variables to explain variation in random parameters, improving the interpretation of heterogeneous effects50–52. More recently, machine learning and deep learning approaches, including random forests, decision trees, gradient boosting, and TabNet, have been introduced for crash severity prediction because of their ability to capture nonlinear relationships and improve predictive performance53–55. However, statistical random-parameter models remain especially useful when the objective is to obtain interpretable estimates, quantify marginal effects, and assess temporal transferability in crash severity relationships.

Research gap and study objectives

Although prior studies have provided important insights into child pedestrian crash risk, several limitations remain. First, much of the child pedestrian safety literature has focused on crash occurrence, behavioral risk, or location-specific hazards, while fewer studies have examined crash/person-level injury severity outcomes among child pedestrians using a modeling framework that explicitly accounts for heterogeneous effects. Second, many previous studies rely on descriptive analysis or fixed-parameter regression models, which may not fully capture unobserved differences in driver behavior, roadway context, traffic conditions, environmental exposure, and child pedestrian characteristics. Third, although temporal patterns such as school-hour, nighttime, seasonal, and weekend effects have been documented, limited attention has been given to whether the effects of contributing factors remain stable across years, particularly during periods of changing travel behavior and exposure. This limitation is important because child pedestrian injury severity may vary not only by crash context but also by annual shifts in mobility, roadway use, and behavioral conditions. Therefore, the gaps identified in the Introduction and Literature Review are centered on the need for a child pedestrian injury severity framework that integrates crash/person-level data, unobserved heterogeneity, and year-to-year temporal instability.

To address these gaps, this study analyzes child pedestrian-involved crashes in Texas from 2017 to 2022 using random-parameter multinomial logit models with heterogeneity in means. The study examines behavioral, roadway, traffic, temporal, and environmental factors associated with child pedestrian injury severity and estimates separate year-specific models to assess temporal instability. By combining child-focused injury severity analysis with random-parameter modeling and annual transferability assessment, this study provides evidence that can support more targeted, context-sensitive, and data-driven child pedestrian safety interventions.

Methods

Details of modeling framework

This study initially employed the multinomial logit (MNL) methodology, an appropriate method for modeling categorical outcome variables with multiple discrete categories, to analyze child pedestrian injury severity outcomes. The choice of an unordered multinomial logit framework over an ordered probability model was deliberate and grounded in well-established methodological considerations. Ordered models impose a restrictive monotonic structure in which a covariate’s effect on the highest and lowest severity categories must move in opposite directions, leaving intermediate-category effects analytically ambiguous, a limitation particularly relevant when contributing factors exhibit non-monotonic effects across severity outcomes, as frequently observed in pedestrian crash data56. Ordered models are also more susceptible to bias from the underreporting of low-severity crashes in police-reported datasets, whereas unordered multinomial logit specifications retain consistent slope parameters under such conditions56. The random-parameter multinomial logit with heterogeneity in means (RPLHM) provides a flexible and widely adopted framework for accommodating unobserved heterogeneity in injury severity analysis, and recent pedestrian-severity studies on three-category outcomes have adopted this same specification, confirming its standing as a prevailing methodological choice for vulnerable road user crash severity analysis57–59. A detailed methodological comparison between the RPLHM and the Random Parameter Ordered Logit (RPOL) framework is provided in Appendix Table 7. The analytical framework employed in this study follows a four-stage sequential process, as illustrated in Fig. 2. In the first stage, child pedestrian crash data were extracted for 2017–2022, yielding 4,762 eligible crashes. Data preprocessing included cleaning and screening variables, reducing the dataset to 22 explanatory variables, recoding injury severity from the KABCO scale into fatal/severe injury (KA), moderate/possible injury (BC), and no injury (O) outcomes, annual segmentation into seven datasets, and dummy coding of categorical variables. In the second stage, three progressively flexible discrete choice models were estimated, including MNL, Random Parameter Logit (RPL), and RPLHM, with 1,000 Halton draws used for random parameter estimation and backward elimination applied at α = 0.10. Both overall and year-wise unconstrained analyses were conducted, with marginal effects computed for each severity outcome and temporal instability explored across yearly models. Model performance was assessed using likelihood ratio tests, AIC, AIC/N, and McFadden pseudo-R². In the third stage, transferability analysis was performed using pairwise likelihood ratio tests to identify transferable parameters and assess temporal stability across yearly models. In the fourth stage, the estimated results were interpreted through comparative assessment of temporal instability patterns and year-specific marginal effects, with model comparison representing the final output of the analytical framework.

Random parameter multinomial logit model

When analyzing child pedestrian injury data, key factors affecting injury severity outcomes may be overlooked based on police-reported data at the accident scene. The influence of particular factors may differ among the sample population being examined. Unobserved heterogeneity in latent factors influencing injury severity, may arise from these variables. Neglecting this issue may result in skewed parameter estimations and flawed model construction, potentially leading to erroneous findings and forecasts47. Advanced random parameter multinomial logit models have been created to overcome this constraint. These models aim to address these limitations by explicitly accounting for changes specific to each observation in the effects of significant factors. This study seeks to examine the probability of injury severity using a logit model with random parameters to account for differing levels of variability. Data

The crash data for this study was obtained from the Texas DOT’s CRIS database, a publicly accessible database that provides comprehensive details on crash events. This dataset includes information on individuals involved in crashes, vehicle types, crash locations, roadway characteristics, environmental and temporal factors, and the severity of injuries at both the crash and person levels. Given the study’s focus on child pedestrian safety, the dataset was filtered to include only crashes involving pedestrians under the age of 15. After applying this criterion, a total of 4,762 child pedestrian-involved crashes recorded between 2017 and 2022 were selected for analysis.

To examine temporal trends in child pedestrian crashes, distinct models were constructed for each year from 2017 to 2022, alongside a comprehensive model utilizing data from the entire six-year span. This annual modeling method allows for a more thorough examination of the temporal fluctuations in factors influencing crash severity. This strategy reflects annual variations in traffic conditions, travel behavior, and other contextual factors that may affect child pedestrian safety, rather than categorizing years into broader periods like pre-pandemic and pandemic eras. It also aids in retaining year-specific variations that could be obscured by combining data from time periods with distinct characteristics. The COVID-19 pandemic brought about profound changes in traffic patterns, with widespread lockdowns and school closures initially leading to a decrease in child pedestrian exposure62,63. However, as restrictions eased, there was a notable increase in walking and biking activity, particularly in residential areas, due to reduced reliance on public transportation and increased outdoor activities. Simultaneously, changes in driver behavior, including higher vehicle speeds on less congested roads, may have contributed to a heightened risk for child pedestrians. These evolving conditions highlight the importance of analyzing crash patterns before and during the pandemic to understand how child pedestrian safety has been affected. By analyzing each year independently, this study accounts for these evolving dynamics and provides a clearer understanding of how the relationships between crash-related factors and injury severity may have changed over time.

Variables were extracted and cleaned (e.g., data lumping) to ensure accuracy and completeness. Annual Average Daily Traffic (AADT) values were categorized consistent with standard TxDOT and HPMS traffic volume classifications; the category “20,001–40,000 vehicles per day” was retained in the final model based on its statistical significance at the 90% threshold through backward elimination. Crash data were primarily categorized based on severity using the KABCO scale: K (fatal injury), A (incapacitating injury), B (non-incapacitating injury), C (possible injury), and O (no injury). For analytical purposes, these categories were regrouped into three broader categories: KA , BC , and O. The categorization is based on the idea that the direct impact on fatal/severe injury can be measured for a deeper understanding of the factors. A similar severity classification has been adopted in previous traffic safety research59. Pedestrian-level injury severity was considered in the analysis to focus on the direct injury outcome of each child pedestrian involved in a crash, providing a more granular understanding of injury severity and its contributing factors. Results and discussions

Crash characteristics

Driver distraction showed a statistically significant negative association with severe injury outcomes. As reported in Table 2, the overall model produced a coefficient of − 0.512 for the KA category, indicating a reduced likelihood of fatal or incapacitating injuries. Marginal effects (Table 4) further show a decrease in KA probability by 0.0070, with corresponding increases in BC and O outcomes. This effect was more pronounced in 2020, where the KA probability declined by 0.0161, suggesting that distraction-related crashes during the pandemic may have occurred under lower-speed or reduced traffic conditions. This finding aligns with prior research indicating that distraction-related crashes often result in less severe outcomes in lower-speed or less congested environments38.

Heterogeneity in means

The heterogeneity-in-means analysis reveals that several contextual and demographic variables significantly influence the means of random parameters in child pedestrian crash severity models. Other crash contributing factors positively affect the mean of the random parameter for clear weather (mean = 0.532), indicating greater severity under clear conditions. Year-specific results show distinct patterns. In 2017, male gender increased the mean of the random parameter for straight-level roadways (mean = 0.983), pointing to elevated severity risk for male children on such segments. In 2018, the absence of traffic control devices raised the mean for straight-level roads (mean = 1.796), reflecting greater vulnerability in uncontrolled environments. For the 2019–2020 model, both higher posted speed limits (mean = − 1.971) and populations of 50,000–99,999 (mean = − 2.384) reduced the mean for clear weather, indicating mitigating effects on severity. In 2021, populations of 100,000–249,999 (mean = 2.332) and signal lights (mean = 1.759) increased the mean for clear weather, implying higher severity in larger urban areas and at signalized intersections. In 2022, the absence of traffic control devices (mean = − 1.002) and straight grades (mean = − 3.441) reduced the mean for clear weather, suggesting lower severity in these contexts.

These findings underscore how demographic, roadway, and environmental factors jointly shape the random parameters driving crash severity outcomes among child pedestrians.

Transferability tests

Likelihood ratio tests were performed to evaluate transferability of the models across multiple datasets. Results of the global transferability test using Eq. (5) yielded a test statistic of 20.06, corresponding to a distribution with 41 degrees of freedom. This test statistic falls well below the critical value, resulting in a high p-value (approximately 0.998) indicating that the null hypothesis fails to be rejected at the 90% confidence level. These results suggest that the estimated parameters are temporally stable and transferable across the datasets analyzed.

To further investigate the temporal stability of the models and address the potential for one-year interruptions as suggested by the reviewers, a cumulative transferability analysis was conducted. This approach evaluates stability by comparing each target year against a combined dataset of all preceding years. The results, presented in Table 5, show that the degree of transferability varies significantly over time. Notably, the comparison of 2018 data against the 2017 model yielded a value of 17.79 (11 df) with a confidence level of 91.35%. While this technically rejects the null hypothesis at the 90% level, it represents a much higher degree of stability compared to subsequent years. As the analysis moves into the 2020–2022 period, there is a sharp escalation in test statistics. For instance, testing 2020 data against the 2017–2019 baseline yielded a value of 221.25 (11 df, 99.99% confidence). These results suggest that while the pre-pandemic years showed a reasonable level of consistency, the 2020–2022 period introduced fundamental structural shifts in child pedestrian injury dynamics that cumulative historical data cannot account for.

Table 6 presents the results of the pairwise transferability tests, displaying test statistics and corresponding confidence levels for comparisons between yearly models. Most pairwise comparisons yielded large test statistic values with confidence levels exceeding 99%, indicating statistically significant differences in parameter estimations across years. For example, the 2021 model using 2017 data produced a value of 86.52 with 13 degrees of freedom and a confidence level of 99.99%, strongly rejecting the null hypothesis of transferability. In contrast, the 2019 model using 2020 data yielded a value of 14.03 (12 df) with a confidence level of only 70.13%, making it the only pairwise test that fails to reject the null hypothesis, suggesting potential parameter transferability between those two years. Some tests involving the 2021 data were excluded because of estimation issues caused by a singular matrix due to the complete absence of one variable in that year’s dataset. Overall, these findings underscore substantial temporal instability in model parameters and highlight the importance of accounting for year-to-year variation in crash severity modeling.

The pairwise and cumulative transferability tests indicate substantial year-to-year temporal instability in the model parameters. The cumulative tests reinforce this conclusion: with the exception of the 2018-versus-2017 comparison (χ² = 17.79, 91.35%), all subsequent target years produced sharply escalating test statistics, reaching χ² = 221.25 (99.99%) for 2020 and χ² = 242.30 (99.99%) for 2022. Conclusions

Child pedestrians under the age of 15 in Texas remain a vulnerable group, facing significant risks of severe and fatal injuries in motor vehicle crashes. To better understand the complex interplay of factors contributing to injury outcomes, this study employed a random-parameter logit model on 4,762 child pedestrian crashes recorded between 2017 and 2022. This modeling framework explicitly accounted for unobserved heterogeneity and interdependencies among driver behavior, roadway features, traffic control, lighting conditions, demographics, and temporal variables. The results revealed substantial variability in crash severity outcomes across different contexts, with distinct patterns emerging across years.

Crashes occurring on two-lane, two-way roads exhibited a consistently positive association with higher severity outcomes, with the overall model showing a significant positive likelihood, reinforcing the elevated danger posed by such roadway types. In contrast, straight-level alignments generally reduced injury severity, though their protective effect fluctuated over time, suggesting contextual instability. Crashes in areas with populations over 250,000 demonstrated lower probabilities of severe outcomes, likely reflecting the benefits of improved pedestrian infrastructure and lower speed limits in urban settings. Notably, crashes involving vehicles backing from parking spots were associated with significantly higher probabilities of no-injury outcomes, underscoring the lower kinetic energy involved in such low-speed events. Conversely, environmental related factors such as clear weather, and nighttime darkness involvement showed heterogeneous impacts across years, with effects modulated by other interacting variables, including posted speed limit and traffic control device presence. Temporal modeling further highlighted that morning (7 AM–12 PM) and evening (7 PM–12 AM) crashes were consistently more severe, and the absence of traffic control devices increased the risk of severe injuries. Importantly, the 2020 model revealed that distracted driving had a significantly reduced association with injury severity, likely a reflection of pandemic-era shifts in travel behavior, traffic volumes, and road usage patterns. These findings emphasize that risk exposure and severity are highly context-specific and vary meaningfully by year, geography, and environmental setting. From a transferability standpoint, the results distinguish between two levels of temporal stability: while the pooled overall model demonstrates broad aggregate-level stability across the six-year study period, the year-specific models are not generally transferable across individual years. Practitioners and policymakers should therefore exercise caution when applying parameters estimated from one year’s data to predict outcomes in another year, particularly during periods of structural behavioral or contextual change such as the COVID-19 pandemic.

From a policy standpoint, these results suggest that a system-based strategy is needed to mitigate child pedestrian injury risks, particularly by aligning interventions with the four tiered safe system roadway design hierarchy. At the Safe Roads level, targeted infrastructure enhancements such as raised crosswalks, curb extensions, refuge islands, and traffic signal installations should be prioritized on two-lane, two-way roads where severity risks are elevated. Improved lighting in high-incidence nighttime crash areas further aligns with creating forgiving roadway environments. Within the Safe Speeds element, the study highlights the value of enforcing low-speed zones (e.g., ≤ 25 mph) near schools, parks, and residential neighborhoods. Temporal risk patterns also support stricter speed enforcement and calming interventions during morning and evening hours, when severity is higher. For the Safe Road Users, behavioral interventions should include enhanced enforcement during peak child pedestrian activity (e.g., school hours), as well as culturally tailored public education campaigns targeting high-risk demographics, including communities with disproportionate involvement in severe outcomes. Incorporating Safe Vehicles strategies, such as promoting advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS) capable of pedestrian detection and automatic emergency braking, especially in family or school transportation fleets, may reduce crash severity. Furthermore, using crash data insights to proactively identify high-risk zones and deploy targeted countermeasures represents a data-driven application of the SSA framework, contributing to both proactive prevention and equitable safety outcomes. While Post-Crash Care factors were not directly measured, the spatial prioritization of EMS response and trauma care access in high-density child pedestrian areas remains a complementary policy priority.

Despite these contributions, the study is subject to several limitations. The use of police-reported crash data may exclude near-miss incidents and suffer from misclassification of crash conditions, thereby underestimating risk exposure. The analysis also lacked critical exposure variables such as pedestrian traffic volume, land use mix, and vehicle miles traveled, which limits rate-based inference. Behavioral dimensions such as adult supervision, alcohol use, and driver distraction were not fully captured due to data constraints. Environmental conditions like precipitation intensity and visibility were also inadequately detailed. Furthermore, focusing solely on Texas may limit generalizability to regions with different traffic regulations, infrastructure maturity, and child pedestrian exposure. The correlational nature of the modeling approach precludes causal inferences regarding specific interventions. Future research should integrate exposure-adjusted metrics, test the transferability of findings using multi-state or national datasets, and evaluate the real-world efficacy of interventions such as intelligent crosswalks, pedestrian flags, and behaviorally informed safety campaigns.
