brick-splitting Torsion Pairs and Trim Lattices
Sota Asai, Osamu Iyama, Kaveh Mousavand, Charles Paquette

Abstract
. We introduce the notion of brick-splitting torsion pairs as a modern analogue and generalization of the classical notion of splitting torsion pairs. A torsion pair is called brick-splitting if any given brick is either torsion or torsion-free with respect to that torsion pair. After giving some properties of these pairs, we fully characterize them in terms of some lattice-theoretical properties, including left modularity. This leads to the notion of brick-directed algebras, which are those for which there does not exist any cycle of non-zero non-isomorphisms between bricks. This class of algebras is a novel generalization of representation-directed algebras. We show that brick-directed algebras have many interesting properties and give several characterizations of them. In particular, we prove that a brick-finite algebra is brick-directed if and only if the lattice of torsion classes is a trim lattice. We also give a characterization of brick-directed algebras in terms of their wall-and-chamber structure, as well as of a certain Newton polytope associated to them. Moreover, we introduce an explicit construction of an abundance of brick-directed algebras, both of the tame and wild representation types.

1.1.
Brick-splitting torsion pairs

For an algebra
A

(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
in
\modu A
is said to be

if
\ind A\subseteq\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{F}
, that is, every indecomposable module in
\modu A
belongs to
\mathcal{T}
or
\mathcal{F}
. Several characterizations of splitting torsion pairs have appeared in the literature (see Proposition
2.4
).
As one of the main objectives of this paper, we introduce and study a modern analogue of splitting torsion pairs. First, recall that a module
X
is a
brick
provided
\End_{A}(X)
is a division algebra. By
\brick A
we denote the set of all (isomorphism classes) of bricks in
\modu A
.
Then, we say that a torsion pair
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
in
\modu A
is
brick-splitting
if
\brick A\subseteq\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{F}
, that is, every brick in
\modu A
belongs to
\mathcal{T}
or
\mathcal{F}
. A torsion class (or a torsion-free class) that belongs to a brick-splitting torsion pair is also called
brick-splitting
.
Each splitting torsion pair is obviously brick-splitting, but the converse is not true (see Example
3.5
). Henceforth, we refer to
(0,\modu A)
and
(\modu A,0)
as the
trivial
(brick-)splitting torsion pairs.

Our first theorem gives several characterizations of brick-splitting torsion pairs. Before stating these results, we recall some standard notation and terminology. In particular, for a lattice
(L,\leq)
, by
\Hasse(L)
we denote the Hasse diagram of
L
. Moreover, an element
x
in
L
is called
left modular
if for each
y\leq z
in
L
, we have the equality
(y\vee x)\wedge z=y\vee(x\wedge z)
.
For an algebra
A
, by
\tors A
we denote the set of all torsion classes in
\modu A
. It is known that, under the inclusion order,
\tors A
is a complete lattice which is weakly atomic semidistributive, and a labeling of the edges of

is given by bricks. For some details on the lattice theory and properties of
\tors A
, see Section
2
and references therein.

Theorem 1.1

(Prop.
3.12

\&

For an algebra

(2)

The next proposition provides further insights into the behavior of brick-splitting torsion pairs and shows how such pairs control some important properties of the algebras under consideration.
For a full subcategory

(Prop.

Let

Corollary 1.3

(Cor.

As our second novel generalization of a classical notion in representation theory, we give a brick-analogue of the family of representation-directed algebras (for the definition and some remarks on representation-directed algebras, see Section

For an algebra

, we say

In the following theorem, we give a conceptual characterization of brick-directed algebras in terms of brick-splitting torsion pairs.

Theorem 1.4

(Theorem

)

For an algebra

(1)

(2)

in

Similar to Proposition

.
We say that an algebra

Corollary 1.5

(Cor.
4.8

As noted above, through brick-directed algebras, we obtain a vast generalization of the classical family of representation-directed algebras. Unlike representation-directed algebras, which are always representation-finite, brick-directed algebras include various types of representation-infinite algebra. In fact, via a simple gluing construction recalled in Section

Before we state the next result, recall that by Drozd’s trichotomy theorem, each algebra

Corollary 1.6

(Cor.
4.16
)

For any positive integer

of any of the following types:

•

representation-finite algebra;

•

brick-finite tame algebra;

•

brick-infinite tame algebra;

•

brick-finite wild algebra;

•

brick-infinite wild algebra.

1.2.
\tau
-tilting finite algebras and trim lattices

In Section
5
, we use brick-directed algebras to give new characterizations of some lattice theoretical phenomena in the context of lattice theory of torsion classes.
Before stating some of our results, we remark that in the literature of lattice theory many important combinatorial properties of finite lattices are not defined for infinite lattices. Consequently, we restrict our attention to those algebras
A
for which
\tors A
is a finite lattice. This is the case if and only if
A
is
\tau
-tilting finite, which itself is equivalent to brick-finiteness of
A
(see
[
DIJ
,
DI+
]
). Hence, in this part we only consider brick-finite algebras.

Recall that a finite lattice
(L,\leq)
is said to be
extremal
if it has a maximal chain whose length is equal to both the number of join-irreducible elements and the number of meet-irreducibles. Extremal lattices were first introduced in
[
Ma
]
, as a generalization of distributive lattices. Another generalization of distributive lattices is given by the left modularity property:
(L,\leq)
is called
left modular
if
L
has a maximal chain of left modular elements. Note that these two generalizations of distributive lattices are independent, that is, neither of them implies the other (see Section
2
and references therein).
Following
[
Th
]
, a finite lattice
(L,\leq)
is said to be
trim
if it is extremal and left modular. Trim lattices were introduced as an analogue of distributive lattices, while they are not necessarily graded. In fact,
L
is distributive if and only if it is graded and trim (
[
Th
, Theorem 2]
).
As shown more recently in
[
TW
]
, trim lattices admit many interesting combinatorial properties.

To put the following theorem into perspective, let us make some observations. We first recall that for any algebra
A
, the lattice
\tors A
is always semidistributive (see
[
DI+
, Theorem 1.3]
and
[
RST
, Corollary 8.8]
). Meanwhile, an arbitrary finite semidistributive lattice need not be left modular nor extremal. Moreover, there are trim lattices which are not semidistributive (see
[
TW
, Figure 3]
).
Through some lattice-theoretical techniques, it has recently been shown that for semidistributive lattices, left modularity, extremality, and trimness are equivalent (see
[
TW
]
and
[
Mu
]
). However, there is no full classification of those algebras
A
for which
\tors(A)
is a trim lattice. The following theorem gives such a complete classification result. Moreover, we use some properties of brick-splitting torsion pairs to give new realizations of the above-mentioned lattice-theoretical properties in the more algebraic framework of representation theory (see Propositions
5.1
and
5.2
).
Before we summarize our results in the next theorem, recall that for an extremal lattice
L
, the
spine
of
L
consists of those elements that lie on a chain of maximal length.

Theorem 1.7

(Prop.
5.1

\&
Prop.
5.2

\&
Cor.
5.3
)

Let
A
be a brick-finite algebra. The following are equivalent:

(1)

A
is brick-directed;

(2)

\tors A
is left modular;

(3)

\tors A
is extremal;

(4)

\tors A
is a trim lattice.

Moreover, if
A
satisfies any of the above conditions, then the spine of
\tors A
consists of all brick-splitting torsion classes and it forms a distributive sublattice.

We note that the final assertion of the preceding theorem (i.e., the characterization of the spine), is a consequence of Theorem
1.1
, together with some earlier results on trim lattices (see
[
TW
, Prop. 2.6 and Theorem 3.7]
). We observe that from the aforementioned characterization of spine of trim lattices in our setting, one concludes that for a brick-finite brick-directed algebra

, each brick-splitting torsion class in
\modu A
lies on a chain of maximal length in
\tors A
, that is, a chain of length
|\brick A|
. From Theorem
1.7
, we also derive some interesting results.

Corollary 1.8

(Cor.
5.4
)

Let
A
be a brick-finite algebra. If
\tors A
is extremal (equivalently, left modular), then the following lattices are also extremal:

(1)

\tors A/J
, for any 2-sided ideal

in

;

(2)

\tors eAe
, for any idempotent element

in

(3)

\tors B
, for any algebra

-reduction of

Although some earlier studies of trim lattices in the context of lattice theory of torsion classes have already appeared in the literature, such results are primarily in the setting of representation-finite algebras. In
[

the authors showed that if

is representation-directed, then

Recall that for a finite lattice

,

of

is defined to be the length of a longest maximal chain in

Corollary 1.9

(Cor.
5.5
)

Let

be a brick-finite algebra. Then,

In Section

After a brief recollection of some standard tools from that setting, in the aforementioned section we introduce the notion of a (weakly) consistent sequence of elements in the Grothendieck group

Theorem 1.10

(Theorem

Let

Our next result states a remarkable property of bricks over brick-directed algebras. More precisely, from Theorems

and

, and some further observations, in Section

we show the following uniqueness property that generalizes some classical results. We particularly note that every representation-directed algebra satisfies the assumptions of the following statement.

Corollary 1.11

(Cor.

)

Let

be a brick-finite algebra. If

is brick-directed, then each brick in

is uniquely determined by its dimension vector, that is, for two distinct elements

in

We remark that the converse of the previous corollary is not true in general. Meanwhile, let us highlight two interesting consequences of the preceding corollary, which also motivate some related properties that can be studied for arbitrary brick-directed algebras (for details, see Remark
5.12
).
Working with a brick-finite algebra, say
A
, Corollary

implies the following.
On the one hand, it provides a simple necessary condition for the brick-directedness: If two distinct bricks have the same dimension vector, there exists a cycle of bricks, hence

On the other hand, this corollary implies a strong condition on the module varieties of brick-directed algebras: If

The last main result that we present here gives another characterization of brick-directed algebras in terms of a certain Newton polytope naturally associated to any brick-finite algebra. For undefined terminology and notation, we refer to Section

Theorem 1.12

(Theorem

)

Let

be brick-finite, and set

as the direct sum of

We end this section with a remark that puts our work in a broader perspective.

Remark 1.13

As observed above, using brick-finite brick-directed algebras, we gave a full characterization of trim lattices in the context of lattice of torsion classes. Meanwhile, we emphasize that some of our main results on brick-directed algebras are independent of the finiteness assumption on the lattice
\tors A
. In particular, there exist many examples of brick-directed algebras for which
\tors A
is an infinite lattice (see Corollary
1.6
).
Hence, in the context of lattice theory of torsion classes, the notion of brick-directed algebras can be seen as a generalization of trimness from the setting of finite semidistributive lattices to that of infinite semidistributive lattices.
Thus, our work can potentially pave the way for the study of some fundamental lattice-theoretical phenomena. More specifically, thanks to the recent results of
[
TW
]
and
[
Mu
]
, it is known that for any finite semidistributive lattice
(L,\leq)
, the notions of extremality and left modularity are equivalent (see
[
Mu
, Corollary 3.5]
). Hence, in light of Theorems
1.4
and
1.7
, it is natural to investigate the notions of extremality and left modularity over infinite semidistributive lattices.

2.
Preliminaries and Background

2.1.
Lattice theory

In this subsection, we recall only some facts from lattice theory that are used in our work. For the most part, we only provide references. For all the rudiments of lattice theory, we refer to
[
Gr
]
and
[
DP
]
.

Let
(P,\leq)
be a non-empty (possibly infinite) partially ordered set. Recall that
x<z
in
P
is said to be a
cover
relation, and it is denoted by
x\lessdot z
, provided that for each
y\in P
, if
x\leq y\leq z
, we have either
x=y
or
y=z
. Furthermore, the
Hasse quiver
of
P
, denoted by
\Hasse(P)
, is a directed graph which has
P
as the vertex set, and arrows
x\rightarrow z
for each cover relation
x\lessdot z
.

We also recall that
(P,\leq)
is said to be a
complete lattice
if for any subset
S
in
P
, there exists a unique element of
P
, smallest with the property of being larger than or equal to all elements of
S
, called the
join
of
S
and denoted by
\bigvee S
; as well as a unique element of
P
, largest with the property of being smaller than or equal to all elements of
S
, called the
meet
of
S
and denoted by
\bigwedge S
. We often use
(L,\leq)
to denote a lattice, and for
x
and
y
in a lattice
L
, the join and meet are respectively denoted by
x\vee y
and
x\wedge y
.

In the following,
(L,\leq)
always denotes a complete lattice, and
1
and
0
respectively denote the greatest element and the least element of
L
(also known as the maximum and minimum of
L
). Whenever there is no confusion, we suppress
\leq
in the notation and simply use
L
instead of
(L,\leq)
. Recall that a lattice
L
is said to be
weakly atomic
if for each
x<y
in
L
, there is at least one arrow in
\Hasse[x,y]
.

A sequence
x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{i}<\cdots
in
L
is called a
chain
, and is said to be finite if the sequence consists of only finite many elements. In particular, the
length
of
x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{r}
is defined to be
r
. Moreover, the length of the lattice
L
is defined as the maximum of the lengths of finite chains, if there exists such a maximum. Otherwise,
L
is said to be of infinite length. A chain
x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{i}<\cdots
is called
maximal
if it cannot be further refined to a chain by inserting more elements, which is the case if and only if
x_{j}\lessdot x_{j+1}
, for each
j
.

An element
x\in L
is called
join-irreducible
if, for each
y
and
z
in
L
with
y,z<x
, we have
x\neq y\vee z
. By convention,
0
is not considered as a join-irreducible element.
Provided
L
is a finite lattice,
x
is join-irreducible if and only if
it covers exactly one element of
L
. Moreover,
x\in L
is said to be
completely join-irreducible
if for any subset
S\subseteq\{y\in L\mid y<x\}
, we have
x\neq\bigvee_{y\in S}y
. By
\JI(L)
we denote the set of all join-irreducible elements in
L
, and
\JI^{c}(L)
denotes the subset of
\JI(L)
consisting of completely join-irreducible elements.
The notion of (completely) meet-irreducible elements, and consequently the sets
\MI(L)
and
\MI^{c}(L)
, are defined dually. Note that, by convention,
1
does not belong to
\MI(L)
.

A lattice
L
is called
semidistributive
if, for all
x,y,z\in L
, if
x\vee y=x\vee z
, then
x\vee(y\wedge z)=x\vee y
, and, furthermore,
x\wedge y=x\wedge z
implies
x\wedge(y\vee z)=x\wedge y
.
Consequently, for any cover relation
x\lessdot y
, the set
\{a\in L\,|\,x\vee a=y
} has a unique minimal element.
For a lattice
L
, and a pair of elements
y\leq z
in
L
, it is easy to see that the inequality
(y\vee x)\wedge z\geq y\vee(x\wedge z)
holds for all
x\in L
. This is known as
modular inequality
.
Motivated by this, an element
x
in
L
is called
left modular
if for each
y<z
in
L
, we have
(y\vee x)\wedge z=y\vee(x\wedge z)
. For more details on the left modular elements of lattices, see
[
LS
]
.
As shown in
[
TW
, Lemma 2.13]
, under the assumption that
L
is weakly atomic, to verify the left modularity for
x\in L
, one only needs to verify
(y\vee x)\wedge z\leq y\vee(x\wedge z)
for the cover relations
y\lessdot z
in
L
.

In the rest of this subsection, we restrict ourselves to the setting of finite lattices, particularly to recall some important generalizations of distributive lattices that play crucial roles in our studies. We note that, according to the current literature, many of these notions have been considered only for finite lattices.

Let
L
be a finite lattice of length
m
. Observe that
|\JI(L)|\geq m
and
|\MI(L)|\geq m
, because
L
must have at least
m
join-irreducible elements and at least
m
meet-irreducible elements. In fact,
L
is known to be distributive if and only if every maximal chain in
L
is of length
m=|\JI(L)|=|\MI(L)|
(see
[
Ma
, Theorem 17]
).

As a generalization of distributive lattices, following
[
Ma
]
, a finite lattice of length
m
is called
extremal
if
|\JI(L)|=|\MI(L)|=m
. In particular, unlike distributive lattices, an extremal lattice may admit a maximal chain of length less than
m
(see Example
3.5
). For an extremal lattice
L
,
spine
of
L
consists of all elements of
L
that lie on a chain of maximal length. The spine of an extremal lattice
L
is known to be a distributive sublattice of
L
(see
[
TW
, Prop. 2.6]
).

Another generalization of distributive lattices is given in terms of left modular elements. A finite lattice
L
is called
left modular
if it has a maximal chain of left modular elements. Such lattices, at least in this terminology, seem to have appeared first in
[
BS
]
. As shown later, left modular lattices can be characterized in terms of certain labelings (for details, see
[
TW
, Section 2.5]
and references therein).

Since the extremal and left modular lattices give two interesting generalizations of the finite distributive lattices, it is natural to ask about the intersection of these two families. Following
[
Th
]
, we say
L
is a
trim
lattice if
L
is simultaneously extremal and left modular. The family of trim lattices properly contains that of finite distributive lattices, yet they manifest some important properties similar to those of distributive lattices. In particular,
L
is distributive if and only if
L
is a graded trim lattice (
[
Th
, Theorem 2]
).
More recently, new properties of trim lattices have been studied in
[
TW
]
.

Before we recall some important results of
[
TW
]
and
[
Mu
]
that relate to our work, we note that the notions of extremality and left modularity do not imply each other. More explicitly, there exist extremal lattices that are not left modular, and there also exist left modular lattices that are not extremal (see
[
TW
, Figure 5]
). Moreover, as shown in
[
TW
, Figure 3]
, there exist trim lattices that are not semidistributive, and there also exist semidistributive lattices that are not trim.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
TW
, Theorem 1.4]
)

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. If
L
is extremal and semidistributive, then
L
is left modular. That being the case,
L
is a trim lattice.

Inspired by the above theorem, in
[
Mu
]
the author conducted a thorough study of left modular semidistributive lattices in a slightly more general setting. In particular, a consequence of his main theorem, the converse of Theorem
2.1
is obtained, which we state in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
Mu
, Theorem 3.2]
)

Let
(L,\leq)
be a finite lattice. If
L
is left modular and semidistributive, then
L
is extremal. That being the case,
L
is a trim lattice.

From the last two theorems and definition of trim lattices, it is immediate that if
(L,\leq)
is a finite semidistributive lattice, then the following are equivalent:

(1)

L
is left modular;

(2)

L
is extremal;

(3)

L
is a trim lattice.

Using a more algebraic approach, and via our new notion of brick-splitting torsion pairs, we give a new proof and a novel realization of the above equivalences in the context of lattice of torsion classes of a brick-finite algebras (for details, see Section
5
).
Meanwhile, for a more detailed treatment of the notions of semidistributivity, extremality, and left modularity of (finite) lattices, and their relationship with some other lattice-theoretical properties, we refer to
[
Mu
, Figure 2]
.

2.2.
Torsion theories

Throughout,
A
always denotes a basic connected
k
-algebra satisfying the assumptions from Section
1
. In the following, by a subcategory
\mathcal{C}
of
\modu A
, we always assume that
\mathcal{C}
is full and closed under isomorphism classes. Moreover, we set
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}:=\{X\in\modu A\,|\,\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(C,X)=0,\text{ for every }C\in\mathcal{C}\}
, and
{}^{\perp}\mathcal{C}
is defined dually.
Recall that a subcategory
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
extension-closed
if for each short exact sequence
0\rightarrow L\rightarrow M\rightarrow N\rightarrow 0
in
\modu A
, if
L
and
N
belong to
\mathcal{C}
, then so does
M
.

If
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{F}
are two subcategories of
\modu A
, then
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is called a
torsion pair
in
\modu A
provided the following conditions hold:

(1)

\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{F}
have only
0
in common.

(2)

\mathcal{T}
is closed under taking factors and
\mathcal{F}
is closed under taking submodules.

(3)

For each
M
in
\modu A
, there exists a unique submodule
t(M)
of
M
such that
0\rightarrow t(M)\rightarrow M\rightarrow M/t(M)\rightarrow 0
is exact with
t(M)\in\mathcal{T}
and
M/t(M)\in\mathcal{F}
.

If
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is a torsion pair in
\modu A
, the subcategories
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{F}
are respectively called the
torsion class
and the
torsion-free class
. Consequently, the modules in
\mathcal{T}
are called torsion modules and those belonging to
\mathcal{F}
are known as torsion-free modules. Note that both
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{F}
are extension-closed, and each one uniquely determines the pair, because
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
and
\mathcal{T}={}^{\perp}\mathcal{F}
(for more details, see
[
ASS
, Chapter VI.1]
).
Since
(0,\modu A)
and
(\modu A,0)
trivially form torsion pairs, we say
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is a non-trivial torsion pair in
\modu A
provided
\mathcal{T}\neq 0
and
\mathcal{T}\neq\modu A
.

For a set of modules in
\modu A
, the following lemma gives an explicit description of the smallest torsion class in
\tors A
containing them. Before we state this fact, let us remark that, for a subcategory
\mathcal{C}
of
\modu A
, the smallest extension-closed subcategory of
\modu A
that contains
\mathcal{C}
consists of all modules in
\modu A
which have a filtration by the objects in
\mathcal{C}
. Henceforth, we denote this category by
\Filt\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, for a subcategory
\mathcal{C}
of
\modu A
, let
\operatorname{Fac}\mathcal{C}
denote the full subcategory of
\modu A
consisting of all
N
in
\modu A
which are quotients of a finite direct sum of objects in
\mathcal{C}
.

For a subcategory
\mathcal{C}
of
\modu A
, we set

\mathcal{T(\mathcal{C}}):=\Filt(\operatorname{Fac}\mathcal{C})\ \mbox{ and }\ \mathcal{F(\mathcal{C}}):=\Filt(\Sub\mathcal{C}).

Thus each
M
in
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C})
has a filtration
0=M_{0}\subseteq M_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq M_{d-1}\subseteq M_{d}=M
such that for every
1\leq i\leq d
, there exists an epimorphism
\psi_{i}:C_{i}\twoheadrightarrow M_{i}/M_{i-1}
for some
C_{i}
which is a finite direct sum of objects from
\mathcal{C}
. The following lemma is well known; for instance see
[
MS
, Lemma 3.1]
. This lemma particularly implies that each torsion (and similarly torsion-free) class is uniquely determined by the indecomposable modules it contains.

Lemma 2.3

For a subcategory
\mathcal{C}
of
\modu A
, the smallest torsion class in
\modu A
containing
\mathcal{C}
is
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C})
, and the smallest torsion-free class containing
\mathcal{C}
is
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})
.

From the axioms of torsion theory, it is easy to see that for each torsion pair
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
in
\modu A
, every simple
A
-module belongs to either
\mathcal{T}
or
\mathcal{F}
. That is, each torsion pair divides the set
\{S_{1},\cdots,S_{n}\}
of all simple modules in
\modu A
into two disjoint subsets.
As recalled in Section
1
,
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is called a
splitting
torsion pair if for any
X\in\ind A
we have
X\in\mathcal{T}
or
X\in\mathcal{F}
.
In general, an algebra
A
may admit many (possibly infinitely many) non-trivial splitting torsion pairs. The following proposition lists some of the well-known characterizations of splitting torsion pairs.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
ASS
, VI. Prop. 1.7]
)

For a torsion pair
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
in
\modu A
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is splitting.

(2)

The canonical short exact sequence
0\rightarrow t(M)\rightarrow M\rightarrow M/t(M)\rightarrow 0
splits, for each
M
in
\modu A
.

(3)

\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(N,M)=0
, for all
M\in\mathcal{T}
and
N\in\mathcal{F}
.

(4)

\tau^{-1}M\in\mathcal{T}
, for each
M\in\mathcal{T}
.

(5)

\tau N\in\mathcal{F}
, for each
N\in\mathcal{F}
.

2.3.
Lattice of torsion classes

For an algebra
A
, by
\tors A
and
\torf A
, we respectively denote the set of all torsion classes and torsion-free classes in
\modu A
. These two sets actually form complete lattices with respect to the inclusion. More precisely, for a family of torsion classes
\{\mathcal{T}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
\tors A
(respectively,
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
\torf A
), the meet is given by
\bigwedge_{i\in I}\mathcal{T}_{i}=\bigcap_{i\in I}\mathcal{T}_{i}
(respectively,
\bigwedge_{i\in I}\mathcal{F}_{i}=\bigcap_{i\in I}\mathcal{F}_{i}
). Moreover, the join
\bigvee_{i\in I}\mathcal{T}_{i}
is defined as the intersection of all torsion classes
\mathcal{T}\in\tors A
that contain
\bigcup_{i\in I}\mathcal{T}_{i}
. One analogously defines
\bigvee_{i\in I}\mathcal{F}_{i}
.
Furthermore, there is an anti-isomorphism of lattices between
\tors A
and
\torf A
. More precisely,
\mathcal{T}\in\tors A
is sent to
\mathcal{T}^{\bot}\in\torf A
, and in the opposite direction
\mathcal{F}
is sent to
{}^{\bot}\mathcal{F}
.

It is known that
\tors A
, and thus
\torf A
, is always a semidistributive lattice (
[
GM
, Theorem 4.5]
), and they are weakly atomic (
[
DI+
, Theorem 3.1]
).
Moreover, as proved in
[
BCZ
]
and
[
DI+
]
, there is a brick labeling of
\Hasse(\tors A)
, that is, each arrow in the Hasse quiver of
\tors A
is uniquely labeled by an element of
\brick A
. However, we remark that a single element of
\brick A
may appear as the label of more than one arrow in
\Hasse(\tors A)
.
To briefly recall the idea of this labeling, we adopt the notation of
[
DI+
]
and, for
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
in
\tors A
, define

\brick[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]:=\{X\in\brick A\,|\,X\in\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\bot}\}.

Then, there is an arrow
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
in
\Hasse(\tors A)
if and only if
\brick[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]
has a unique element. We call this unique brick the
label
of the arrow
\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
.

We have the following important theorem. For the proof and more details, we refer to
[
DI+
, Section 3.2]
.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
DI+
, Theorem 3.4]
)

Let
A
be an algebra and let
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
in
\tors A
.

(1)

There is a bijection
\psi:\JI^{c}([\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}])\rightarrow\brick[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]
. In particular,
\psi
sends
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
to the brick that labels the unique arrow of
\Hasse[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]
that starts at
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.

(2)

There is a bijection
\phi:\MI^{c}([\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}])\rightarrow\brick[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]
. In particular,
\phi
sends
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
to the brick that labels the unique arrow of
\Hasse[\mathcal{U},\mathcal{T}]
that ends at
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
.

Consequently, the three sets
\brick A
,
\JI^{c}(\tors A)
, and
\MI^{c}(\tors A)
are in bijection.

The following property is well-known and immediately follows from the definition of the labels of arrows in
\Hasse(\tors A)
.

Proposition 2.6

If
\mathcal{T}_{l}\to\mathcal{T}_{l-1}\to\cdots\to\mathcal{T}_{0}
is a path in
\Hasse(\tors A)
and
X_{i}
is the label of
\mathcal{T}_{i}\to\mathcal{T}_{i-1}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
, then we have
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(X_{i},X_{j})=0
for any
i<j
.

Proof.

Since
i<j
, we have
X_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{j-1}
and
X_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{j-1}^{\perp}
.
∎

2.4.
Chain of bricks

As before,
\brick A
denotes the set of all isomorphism classes of bricks in
\modu A
.
For a totally ordered set
(I,\leq)
, a
chain of bricks
of
A
is an injective map
\Psi:I\rightarrow\brick A
such that if
i<j
in
I
, then
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(\Psi(i),\Psi(j))=0
.
We identify two chains
\Psi_{1}:I_{1}\to\brick A
and
\Psi_{2}:I_{2}\to\brick A
if there exists an isomorphism
\rho:I_{1}\to I_{2}
of posets satisfying
\Psi_{1}=\Psi_{2}\circ\rho
.
Let
\chbrick A
denote the set of all chains of bricks of
A
(after identification). We consider a partial order on
\chbrick A
, as follows: we write
\Psi_{1}\leq\Psi_{2}
if there exists an order preserving map
\rho:I_{1}\to I_{2}
satisfying
\Psi_{1}=\Psi_{2}\circ\rho
.

To every chain of bricks of
A
, one can associate a unique chain of torsion classes. Before we describe this correspondence, recall that a subset
J
of an ordered set
I
is called an
ideal
if
J
is down-closed, that is, for each
l\in I
, if
l\leq j
, for some
j\in J
, then
l\in J
. By
\mathfrak{J}(I)
we denote the set of all ideals of
I
and view it as a poset with respect to the inclusion of ideals.

Let
\Psi:I\rightarrow\brick A
be a chain of bricks of
A
. For
J\in\mathfrak{J}(I)
, let
T_{J}\in\tors A
be the smallest torsion class in
\modu A
that contains each brick
\Psi(j)
, for
j\in J
. Moreover, if
J\subsetneq J^{\prime}
are two ideals in
I
, for any
j\in J^{\prime}\setminus J
the brick
\Psi(j)
belongs to
\mathcal{T}_{J^{\prime}}\cap\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\perp}
.
As explained by Demonet, in
[
KD
, Appendix]
, this correspondence gives a strictly increasing map of posets
\mathfrak{J}(I)\rightarrow\tors A
, which sends
J
to
\mathcal{T}_{J}
.
Thus, any (equivalence class of) chain of bricks of
A
, say
\Psi
, induces a subposet of
\tors A
, which we denote by
\tors_{\Psi}A
.

The following result gives a beautiful and direct link between the chains of bricks and chains of torsion classes. Recall that
\chbrick A
is a poset consisting of all (equivalence classes of) chains of bricks of
A
, with respect to the order described above.
Similarly,
\chtors A
denotes the poset of all chains of torsion classes in
\tors A
, with the natural order given via refinement of chains.

Theorem 2.7

(Demonet
[
KD
, Appendix]
)

With the above notation, the map

f:\chbrick A\rightarrow\chtors A

that sends
\Psi
to
\tors_{\Psi}A
is an injective map of posets. In particular,
f
sends the maximal elements of
\chbrick A
to the maximal elements of
\chtors A
.

We note that the map
f
in the above theorem is almost never invertible. In fact,
f
is bijective if and only if
A
is local. Moreover, one immediately concludes that the length of each chain in
\tors A
is bounded by
|{\brick A}|
. Also, note that
A
is brick-infinite if and only if
\tors A
contains an infinite chain. For more details on the chain of torsion classes and their interactions with bricks, see
[
BCZ
]
,
[
DIJ
]
,
[
DI+
]
, and
[
KD
, Appendix]
.

3.
Brick-splitting torsion pairs

As a modern brick-analogue of the classical splitting phenomenon, we say that a torsion pair
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
in
\modu A
is
brick-splitting
if, for each
M\in\brick A
, we have either
M\in\mathcal{T}
or
M\in\mathcal{F}
. That being the case,
\mathcal{T}
is called a brick-splitting torsion class and
\mathcal{F}
is called a brick-splitting torsion-free class.
If
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is splitting torsion pair in
\modu A
, then evidently each brick in
\modu A
belongs to either
\mathcal{T}
or
\mathcal{F}
. However, as shown in the following, the converse is not true in general.

3.1.
Basic properties

We begin with the simple observation that if, for an algebra
A
, each indecomposable
A
-module is a brick, then the notions of splitting and brick-splitting torsion pairs coincide. Those algebras that satisfy the equality
\ind A=\brick A
are shown to be exactly the family of locally representation-directed algebras, which are known to properly generalize the family of representation-directed algebras (i.e., those algebras for which there is no cycle in
\modu A
). For different characterizations of locally representation-directed algebras, see
[
Dr
]
and
[
MP1
]
. In particular, if
A
is locally representation-directed, then every brick-splitting torsion pair in
\modu A
is in fact splitting.
We will frequently use the following lemma in some arguments. It is probably well known to experts, but we include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 3.1

Let
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
be brick-splitting.
If
X\in\mathcal{T}
and
Y\in\mathcal{F}
are bricks with
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(Y,X)=0
, then
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(Y,X)=0
.

Proof.

Since
\{X,Y\}
is a semibrick (i.e., a set of pairwise Hom-orthogonal bricks), we obtain a wide subcategory
\mathcal{A}=\Filt(X\oplus Y)
.
Assume that there is a non-split short exact sequence

0\to X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u}}{{\to}}E\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\to}}Y\to 0.

Then
E
is an indecomposable object of length
2
in the abelian category
\mathcal{A}
whose composition factors are non-isomorphic. Thus any non-zero endomorphism of
E
has to be an isomorphism, and hence
E
is a brick.
By the brick-splitting property, either
E\in\mathcal{T}
or
E\in\mathcal{F}
. In the first case, we get
v=0
, a contradiction. In the second case, we get
u=0
, a contradiction.
∎

In general, a brick-splitting torsion pair is not necessarily splitting (see Example
3.5
). For hereditary algebras, the following proposition shows that when the torsion pair is functorially finite, these two notions of splitting coincide. Before stating the proposition, we need the following lemma, which can be found in
[
Ha
, Chapter IV, Lemma 1.5]
.

Lemma 3.2

Let
A
be a hereditary
k
-algebra. Let
X,Y
be indecomposable rigid
A
-modules such that
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(X,Y)=0
. Then at least one of
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(Y,X)
and
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(Y,X)
is zero.

Proposition 3.3

Let
A
be a hereditary algebra and
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
be a functorially finite torsion pair in
\modu A
. Then,
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is splitting if and only if it is brick-splitting.

Proof.

We only need to prove one direction. Assume
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is brick-splitting.
Since
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
is functorially finite, by
[
As1
, Proposition 2.9]
, we take the left finite semibrick
\mathcal{B}
such that
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{B})
, and the right finite semibrick
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C})
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{B}
or
X\in\mathcal{C}
.
We show that
X
is rigid. By assumption, there is a left finite or right finite semibrick
\mathcal{S}
such that
X\in\mathcal{S}
. By
[
As1
, Theorem 2.27]
and its dual, we have that
\Filt\mathcal{S}
is equivalent to the module category of some finite dimensional algebra
\Gamma
. We can take an idempotent
e\in\Gamma
such that
\Filt X\simeq\modu\Gamma/\langle e\rangle
, since
\Filt X
is a Serre subcategory of
\Filt\mathcal{S}
. Denote by
Y
the indecomposable projective object in
\Filt X
. Then,
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(Y,X)=0
. Since
X
is a subobject of
Y
, we have a monomorphism
\iota:X\to Y
. Because
A
is hereditary, this gives rise to an epimorphism
0=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(Y,X)\to\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(X,X)
, showing that
X
is rigid.

Let us fix
B\in\mathcal{B}
and
C\in\mathcal{C}
. We have
\operatorname{Ext}_{A}^{1}(B,C)=0
, because
\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{C}[1]
is a 2-term simple-minded collection by
[
As1
, Theorem 3.3]
.
Then by Lemma
3.2
, we get
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(C,B)=0
or
\operatorname{Ext}_{A}^{1}(C,B)=0
. In the former case, since
B\in\mathcal{T}
and
C\in\mathcal{F}
, we have
\operatorname{Ext}_{A}^{1}(C,B)=0
by Lemma
3.1
. Hence, we have
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(C,B)=0
in both cases. This shows that
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{B})=0
.
Then, the hereditary property yields that
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{T})=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{A}(\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{C}),\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{B}))=0
.
Now, the desired result follows from Proposition
2.4
.
∎

Proposition 3.4

If
A
is hereditary of tame type then a torsion pair in
\modu A
is splitting if and only if it is brick-splitting.

Proof.

If
A
is a representation-finite hereditary algebra, it is well known that
\ind A=\brick A
. Hence, there is nothing to show.
Thus, henceforth we assume
A
is representation-infinite and let
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{F})
be a brick-splitting torsion pair.
Suppose
M
is an indecomposable which is not a brick. Then
M
lies in some stable tube in
\Gamma_{A}
. The quasi-simple modules of the tubes are all bricks. If one of those quasi-simples lies in
\mathcal{F}
and another lies in
\mathcal{T}
, then there is a short exact sequence

0\to L\to E\to N\to 0

with
L,N
quasi-simples where
L\in\mathcal{T}
and
N\in\mathcal{F}
. Lemma
3.1
, together with the assumption that
L
and
N
are Hom-orthogonal, leads to the desired contradiction.
Hence, for that tube, either all quasi-simples are in
\mathcal{T}
or they all belong to
\mathcal{F}
. This forces
M
to be in
\mathcal{T}
or in
\mathcal{F}
. Therefore, the pair

3.2.
Characterizations

In this part, we give several characterizations of brick-splitting torsion pairs.
We begin with the following proposition, which characterizes brick-splitting torsion pairs in terms of the intervals that each
\mathcal{T}\in\tors A
specifies. This particularly implies equivalence of the first two assertions of Theorem
1.1
.
For each
X
in
\modu A
, recall that
\mathcal{T}(X)
denotes the smallest torsion class
\mathcal{T}\in\tors A
with
X\in\mathcal{T}
. Dually
\mathcal{F}(X)
is defined.

Proposition 3.12

For an algebra
A
, and each torsion class
\mathcal{T}
in
\modu A
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\perp})
is a brick-splitting torsion pair;

(2)

Each
X\in\brick A
appears as an arrow in
\Hasse[0,\mathcal{T}]
or
\Hasse[\mathcal{T},\modu A]
.

Proof.

Assume that (1) holds. Consider
X\in\brick A
. Since
(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\perp})
is brick-splitting, we get
X\in\mathcal{T}
or
X\in\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
. In the first case,
\mathcal{T}(X)
is a torsion class in the interval
[0,\mathcal{T}]
, and we have
X
is the labeling brick of the minimal inclusion
\mathcal{T}(X)\cap^{\perp}X\subset\mathcal{T}(X)
. In the second case, if
X\in\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
, then
X
is the labeling brick of the minimal inclusion
\mathcal{F}(X)\cap X^{\perp}\subset\mathcal{F}(X)
of torsion-free classes, all included in
\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
. This translates to
X
being a brick-labeling of the corresponding inclusion
{}^{\perp}X\subset{{}^{\perp}(\mathcal{F}(X)\cap X^{\perp}})
of torsion classes in
[\mathcal{T},\modu A]
.

Now, assume that
(2)
holds. Let
X\in\brick A
. If
X
is a labeling brick between two torsion classes in
[0,\mathcal{T}]
, then obviously
X\in\mathcal{T}
. Suppose that
X
is the labeling brick between some two adjacent torsion classes
\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\mathcal{T}_{2}
in the interval
[\mathcal{T},\modu A]
. This implies that
X\in\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
.
∎

Remark 3.13

For brick-finite algebras, the above proposition gives an easy criterion to verify whether a given torsion class admits a brick-splitting torsion pair.

is not a brick-splitting torsion class. In particular,

nor

.

Corollary 3.14

Let

be a brick-finite algebra. If

has a path of length

, then every torsion class on that path is brick-splitting.

Proof.

, the following result completes the proof of Theorem

Proposition 3.15

For an algebra
A
, and each torsion class
\mathcal{T}
in
\modu A
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\perp})
is a brick-splitting torsion pair;

(2)

\mathcal{T}
is a left modular element of the lattice
\tors A
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathcal{T}
is left modular. Let

be not in
\mathcal{T}
. We need to prove that

. Since
L\not\in\mathcal{T}
, the smallest torsion class
\mathcal{T}(L)
containing

is not contained in
\mathcal{T}
. Since
\mathcal{T}(L)
is completely join-irreducible, there is a torsion class
\mathcal{Y}
with

such that every torsion class that is properly contained in
\mathcal{T}(L)
is contained in
\mathcal{Y}
. Using that

(\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{T})\cap\mathcal{T}(L)=\mathcal{Y}\vee(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}(L)).

Since
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}(L)
is properly contained in
\mathcal{T}(L)
, we see that
\mathcal{Y}\vee(\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{T}(L))=\mathcal{Y}
. Thus
(\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{T})\cap\mathcal{T}(L)=\mathcal{Y}\neq\mathcal{T}(L)
holds. This means that
\mathcal{T}(L)
is not contained in
\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{T}
, so that
L\not\in\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{T}.
Now, we consider the canonical sequence

0\to T\to L\to F\to 0

for

where

and

. Since
L
is a minimal extending brick for
\mathcal{Y}
, we know that every proper quotient of

has to be in
\mathcal{Y}
. So if

is non-zero (we know that

), then

, making

, a contradiction. Therefore,

and

.

For the other direction, assume that
\mathcal{T}
is brick-splitting. Observe that it follows from
[

, Theorem 1.3]
that
\tors A
is weakly atomic. It follows from
[

]
that in order to prove that
\mathcal{T}
is left modular, it suffices to prove that for two torsion classes

forming a cover relation, we have

(*):\quad(\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{T})\vee\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{U}\cap(\mathcal{T}\vee\mathcal{V}).

Observe that the containment from left to right is clear. Now, let

be the labeling brick corresponding to the cover relation
\mathcal{V}\subsetneq\mathcal{U}
. Since
\mathcal{T}
is brick-splitting, either

or

. If

, we see that

\mathcal{V}\vee\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{V}\vee(\mathcal{T}(L)\vee\mathcal{T})=(\mathcal{V}\vee\mathcal{T}(L))\vee\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{U}\vee\mathcal{T}.

In this case, the right-hand-side of
(*)
becomes
\mathcal{U}
. Since the left-hand-side contains both

and
\mathcal{V}
, then it has to contain
\mathcal{U}
. This proves that
(*)
holds. Assume now that
L\in\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
. Observe that
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{U}\cap{{}^{\perp}L}
. Hence,

\mathcal{V}\cap\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{U}\cap{{}^{\perp}L})\cap\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{U}\cap(^{\perp}L\cap\mathcal{T})=\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{T}.

In this case, the left-hand-side of
(*)
is
\mathcal{V}
. Now, observe that both
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{V}
are contained in
{}^{\perp}L
. Hence,

\mathcal{U}\cap(\mathcal{T}\vee\mathcal{V})\subseteq\mathcal{U}\cap{{}^{\perp}L}=\mathcal{V}.

Therefore,
(*)
also holds in this case.
∎

4.
Brick-directed algebras

For an algebra
A
, a
path
in
\modu A
is a sequence
X_{0}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}X_{1}\xrightarrow{f_{2}}\cdots X_{t-1}\xrightarrow{f_{t}}X_{t}
, where
t
is a positive integer,
X_{0},\cdots,X_{t}
are indecomposables, and for
1\leq i\leq t
,
f_{i}:X_{i-1}\rightarrow X_{i}
is a non-zero non-invertible morphism in
\modu A
. Such a path is called a
cycle
if
X_{0}
and
X_{t}
are isomorphic. We note that in a path in
\modu A
, some of the morphisms may belong to
\rad^{\infty}(A)
, that is to say, a path in
\modu A
is not necessarily a path in
\Gamma_{A}
.

Recall that
X\in\ind(A)
is said to be
directing
if it does not lie on any cycle in
\modu A
. Such modules are historically important. It is known that the preprojective components (similarly, the preinjective components) consist entirely of directing modules.
An algebra
A
is called
representation-directed
if all indecomposables are directed. Observe that if
A
is representation-infinite, then
\modu A
admits a cycle, hence every representation-directed algebra is representation-finite (for instance, see
[
ASS
, IX. Corollary 3.4]
).

4.1.
Characterizations of brick-directedness

As introduced in Section
1
, in this work we generalize the above classical notion of representation-directedness. In particular, a cycle
X_{0}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}X_{1}\xrightarrow{f_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{f_{m-1}}X_{m-1}\xrightarrow{f_{m}}X_{m}=X_{0}
in
\modu A
is called a
brick-cycle
if
X_{i}\in\brick A
, for all
0\leq i\leq m
. We say an algebra
A
is
brick-directed
if there exists no brick-cycle in
\modu A
.
From the definition, it is immediate that each representation-directed algebra is brick-directed. However, there are many brick-directed algebras that are not representation-directed.

Example 4.1

Let
A
be the algebra given in Example
3.5
. More specifically, we have
\brick A=\{{\begin{smallmatrix}1\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}2\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
2\end{smallmatrix}}\}
, and a simple computation shows that

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}({\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
2\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}2\end{smallmatrix}})=\operatorname{Hom}_{A}({\begin{smallmatrix}1\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
2\end{smallmatrix}})=\operatorname{Hom}_{A}({\begin{smallmatrix}2\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}1\end{smallmatrix}})=\operatorname{Hom}_{A}({\begin{smallmatrix}1\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}2\end{smallmatrix}})=0.

Hence,
A
is brick-directed. We observe that there exists a chain of bricks of
A
consisting of all bricks.
In contrast, we note that
A
is not representation-directed, simply because it admits indecomposables that are not bricks. In fact, one can easily check that every
X\in\brick A
lies on a cycle in
\modu A
.

Remark 4.2

The previous example shows that brick-directed algebras give a non-trivial generalization of representation-directed algebras. In particular, we note that for a brick-directed algebra
A
, all bricks of
A
may lie on a cycle in
\modu A
.

In the following, we give some characterizations of brick-directed algebras and study some of their fundamental properties.
The following lemma is handy for some of the following arguments.

Lemma 4.3

If
B
is an algebra such that
\modu B
is equivalent to a full subcategory of
\modu A
and
A
is brick-directed, then
B
is brick-directed.

Proof.

Observe that
\modu B
cannot have any brick-cycle, as otherwise, by fullness assumption, we obtain a brick-cycle in
\modu A
, hence a contradiction.
∎

As a consequence of the above lemma, one can show that brick-directedness is preserved under several algebraic operations. For the definition and properties of the
\tau
-tilting reduction (shortly,
\tau
-reduction), we refer to
[
Ja
]
.

Proposition 4.4

Let
A
be an algebra. Then,

(1)

If
A
is brick-directed, then so is
A/J
, for each ideal
J
in
A
.

(2)

If
A
is brick-directed, then so is
eAe
, for each idempotent
e\in A
.

(3)

If
A
is brick-directed, then so is every
\tau
-reduction of
A
.

Proof.

Parts
(1)
and
(2)
directly follow from Lemma
4.3
. In particular, we observe that the functor
Ae_{eAe}\otimes-
realizes
\modu eAe
as a full subcategory of
\modu A
. For part
(3)
, it follows from
[
Ja
, Theorem 3.8]
or
[
DI+
, Theorem 4.12 (b)]
that any
\tau
-tilting reduction
B
of
A
is such that
\modu B
can be realized as a wide subcategory of
\modu A
. In particular, if
A
is brick-directed, then there is no brick-cycle in any wide subcategory of
\modu A
, hence
\modu B
has no brick-cycle.
∎

As another useful consequence of Lemma
4.3
, we get the following result. For the definition and different characterizations of (strictly) wild algebras, we refer to
[
SS
, Chapter XIX]
.

Corollary 4.5

Strictly wild algebras are never brick-directed.

Proof.

Let
B
be the cyclic Nakayama algebra of rank
2
whose radical square is zero. If
A
is strictly wild, then there is a full and faithful functor
\mathcal{F}:\modu B\to\modu A
that respects indecomposables. Take the brick-cycle
P_{1}\to P_{2}\to P_{1}
in
\modu B
. Then
\mathcal{F}(P_{1})\to\mathcal{F}(P_{2})\to\mathcal{F}(P_{1})
is a brick-cycle in
\modu A
. Thus,
A
is not brick-directed.
∎

We now give a full classification of brick-directed hereditary algebras.

Proposition 4.6

Let
A
be a connected path algebra (i.e.,
A=kQ
). Then,
A
is brick-directed if and only if
Q
is Dynkin or the Kronecker quiver.

Proof.

First note that
A=kQ
is representation-finite if and only if
Q
is a Dynkin quiver. That being the case, it is well-known that
A=kQ
is representation-directed and therefore brick-directed. Moreover, if
Q
is the Kronecker quiver, then
\brick A
consists of all those indecomposables belonging to the preprojective component and preinjective component of
\Gamma_{A}
, together with the quasi-simple modules lying on the mouth of the tubes. It is well-known that tubes of
\Gamma_{A}
are homogeneous and pairwise hom-orthogonal, that is, there is no brick-cycle consisting of those bricks lying on the tubes. Moreover, morphisms between indecomposable modules in
\modu A
are directed, in particular, from the preinjective modules there is no non-zero homomorphism to the regular modules on the tubes nor to the preprojective modules. Moreover, there is no non-zero homomorphism from the regular modules on the tubes to the preprojective modules. Hence, for the Kronecker quiver
Q
, the path algebra
kQ
is brick-directed.

Suppose
Q
is an extended Dynkin quiver different from the Kronecker quiver. We show that
A=kQ
is not brick-directed. In particular, we know that
A=kQ
is tame and
\Gamma_{A}
has at least one stable tube of rank greater than
1
. In particular, there exists a tube
\mathbf{t}
in
\Gamma_{A}
such that the first two layers of
\mathbf{t}
consist of bricks. Thus, one can easily find a brick-cycle in
\mathbf{t}
. This shows that
A=kQ
is not brick-directed.

Finally, if
Q
is a quiver that is neither Dynkin nor extended Dynkin, the path algebra
kQ
is strictly wild, and hence Corollary
4.5
implies that
A=kQ
is not brick-directed. This finishes the proof.
∎

As recalled in Theorem
2.7
, from
[
KD
]
we know that each chain of bricks of
A
gives rise to a chain of torsion classes in
\modu A
, and this correspondence induces a bijection between the equivalence classes of maximal chains of bricks and the set of maximal chains of torsion classes in
\tors A
. In fact, an interesting characterization of brick-directed algebras can be given in terms of their maximal chain of torsion classes and the notion of brick-splitting torsion pairs.

Theorem 4.7

For an algebra
A
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

A
is brick-directed;

(2)

There exists a total order
J
on
\brick A=\{B_{j}\}_{j\in J}
with
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(B_{i},B_{j})=0
, whenever
i<j
in
J
.

(3)

There exists a maximal chain
\{\mathcal{T}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
\tors A
such that every
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is a brick-splitting torsion class.

Proof.

Assume that (1) holds. Consider the transitive closure
\leq
of the relation
\sim
in
\brick A
given by
X\sim Y
if
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(X,Y)\neq 0
. By the brick-directedness, the relation
\leq
is anti-symmetric, hence is an order relation. Now, it is possible to well-order
\brick A
by
\preceq
so that
X\leq Y
implies
X\preceq Y
. The opposite order to
\preceq
is the order that gives us (2).

Assume that (2) holds. We consider the set
\mathcal{I}
of all down-closed subsets of
J
. Then
\mathcal{I}
can be totally ordered by inclusion. For
I\in\mathcal{I}
, let
\mathcal{T}_{I}
be the smallest torsion class that contains all
B_{i}
for
i\in I
. If
j\in J
is such that
i<j
for all
i\in I
, then it is clear that
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(\mathcal{T}_{I},B_{j})=0
and hence that
B_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{I}^{\perp}
. This proves that each
\mathcal{T}_{I}
is a torsion class that is brick-splitting. We consider the chain
\{\mathcal{T}_{I}\}_{I\in\mathcal{I}}
. Assume that this chain is not maximal and let
\mathcal{T}
be a torsion class that can be added to this chain. Let
K\subseteq J
corresponding to the bricks in
\mathcal{T}
. Then
K
does not form a down closed set. Hence, there exist
B_{i},B_{j}\in\brick A
such that
B_{j}\in\mathcal{T}
while
B_{i}\not\in\mathcal{T}
, with
i<j
. Now, the torsion class
\mathcal{T}_{\leq i}
has to be comparable to
\mathcal{T}
. Since
B_{i}\not\in\mathcal{T}
, we have
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{\leq i}
, which leads to the contradiction that
B_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{\leq i}
. Hence
\{\mathcal{T}_{I}\}_{I\in\mathcal{I}}
is a maximal chain of brick-splitting torsion classes. This proves (3).

Finally, assume that (3) holds. Suppose to the contrary that (1) does not hold, and let
B_{1}\to B_{2}\to\cdots\to B_{n}\to B_{1}
be a brick-cycle, where
n\geq 2
. Consider
\mathcal{T}
the union of all torsion classes in the chain that contain no
B_{i}
. Clearly
\mathcal{T}
is again a torsion class that is brick-splitting, and is comparable to the torsion classes of the chain, hence has itself to be in the chain. Similarly, we let
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be the intersection of all torsion classes in the chain that contain all
B_{i}
. Then
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is a torsion class that is brick-splitting, and is comparable to the torsion class of the chain, hence has to be in the chain.
Now, we consider the inclusion
\mathcal{T}\subsetneq\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. If this is not a cover relation, there is a torsion class
\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}
in the chain that contains a non-empty proper subset of
\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\}
. In particular, there is
i
such that
B_{i}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}
while
B_{i+1}\not\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime}
, where indices are taken modulo
n
. By the brick-splitting property,
B_{i+1}\in(\mathcal{T}^{\prime\prime})^{\perp}
so that
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(B_{i},B_{i+1})=0
, a contradiction. Hence, the inclusion
\mathcal{T}\subsetneq\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is a cover relation. In particular, it follows from
[
DI+
, Theorem 3.3(b)]
that there is a unique brick in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
. Since
\mathcal{T}
is brick-splitting, we have
\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\}\subseteq\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
so that
n=1
, a contradiction.
∎

By the above theorem, a connected algebra
A
is brick-directed if and only if there is a chain of torsion classes where every
X\in\brick A
appears as a labeling of the cover relations. This particularly relates to some study of maximal green sequences. For more details, see
[
KD
]
and the references therein.

Using the above theorem, we obtain the following result, which gives some useful information on the bound quiver of arbitrary brick-directed algebras. In particular, we prove Corollary
1.5
.

Corollary 4.8

If
A
is brick-directed, then
A
is weakly triangular.

Proof.

Since
A
is brick-directed, by Theorem
4.7
, there is a maximal chain of brick-splitting torsion pairs in
\tors A
. Thus, we can consider a subchain

0=\mathcal{T}_{0}\subset\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\mathcal{T}_{2}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{T}_{n}=\modu A

of the aforementioned maximal chain such that
\mathcal{T}_{i}
contains exactly
i
non-isomorphic simple modules. Now, the result follows from Proposition
3.10
.
∎

Before we give a more combinatorial realization of brick-directed algebras, let us make a remark on weakly triangular algebras and their appearance in our work.

Remark 4.9

We remark that weakly triangular algebras appeared in the study and classification of
geometrically irreducible algebras
(over algebraically closed fields). These are the algebras for which every module variety
\modu(A,\underline{d})
is irreducible, where the dimension vector
\underline{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
varies. In fact, it is shown that every geometrically irreducible algebras is weakly triangular. For more details and new results, see
[
BZ
]
and the references therein.

Meanwhile, we note that brick-directed algebras and geometrically irreducible algebras are two different subfamilies of weakly triangular algebras.
On the one hand, the path algebra of the
3
-Kronecker quiver is clearly geometrically irreducible, but it is strictly wild, hence it cannot be brick-directed (see Corollary
4.5
). On the other hand, the local algebra
A=k[x,y]/\langle x^{2},y^{2},xy\rangle
is obviously brick-directed, but it is not geometrically irreducible (see
[
BZ
, Lemma 2.1]
). Nevertheless, the study of brick-directed algebras through the geometric lens is an interesting problem for future investigations (also see Remark
5.12
).

We now give a more combinatorial realization of brick-directed algebras. First, observe that, to any algebra, one can explicitly associate a quiver that captures the interactions between the bricks and hence the brick-directedness of the algebra under consideration. In particular, for a connected algebra
A
, by
Q^{b}(A)
we denote the
brick-quiver
of
A
, constructed as follows: The vertices of
Q^{b}(A)
are in bijection with elements of
\brick A
, and for each pair of distinct (hence non-isomorphic) modules
X
and
Y
in
\brick A
, we put an arrow from
X
to
Y
if
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(X,Y)\neq 0
. For an explicit example, see Example
4.14
.

The following proposition lists some properties of the brick quiver that follows from the above construction.

Proposition 4.10

Let
A
be a connected algebra. Then, the brick quiver
Q^{b}(A)
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

Q^{b}(A)
is connected and has no loops.

(2)

Q^{b}(A)
has finitely many arrows if and only if it has finitely many vertices. This is the case if and only if
A
is brick-finite.

(3)

Q^{b}(A)
is infinite if and only if it has at least two vertices of infinite degree.

(4)

Q^{b}(A)
has a cycle if and only if there is a brick-cycle in
\modu A
.

(5)

Every acyclic path in
Q^{b}(A)
gives a chain of bricks of
A
, provided no subpath of it can be completed to a cycle.

Proof.

Recall that
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
denote a complete list of (isomorphism classes of) simple modules in
\modu A
. In particular, each
S_{i}
appears as a vertex of
Q^{b}(A)
.

(1)

First, observe that the top and socle of each
A
-module are given as direct sums of simple modules. In particular, for
X\in\brick A
, and each simple module
S
appearing as a summand of
\topm(X)
, in the brick quiver
Q^{b}(A)
there is an arrow
X\to S
(dually, for every simple summand
S^{\prime}
of
\soc(X)
, there is an arrow
S^{\prime}\to X
). Thus, each non-simple
X
in
\brick A
is connected to at least one simple module in
Q^{b}(A)
.
Next, we note that for each arrow
\alpha\in\Omega_{1}
which is not a loop, say
\alpha:i\to j
, we have a brick
M
of length
2
given by the middle term of a non-split short exact sequence

0\to S_{j}\to M\to S_{i}\to 0.

Then, in
Q^{b}(A)
, we obviously have the arrows
S_{j}\to M
, and
M\to S_{i}
. Hence, using bricks of length
2
, we conclude that
Q^{b}(A)
is connected.
The fact that
Q^{b}(A)
has no loops immediately follows from the definition.

(2)

As explained in part (1), for each non-simple
X
in
\brick A
, in
Q^{b}(A)
there is at least one arrow from
X
to one of the simple modules
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
. Thus,
Q^{b}(A)
has infinitely many arrows if and only if
\brick A
contains infinitely many (non-simple) bricks.

(3)

If
Q^{b}(A)
is an infinite quiver, then obviously
\brick A
contains infinitely many (non-simple) bricks, because
Q^{b}(A)
has finitely many arrows starting at
i
and ending at
j
for any pair
(i,j)
of vertices. Consequently, for some
1\leq i\leq n
, the simple module
S_{i}
appears as a summand of the socle of infinitely many bricks in
\brick A
, say
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
. Hence, in
Q^{b}(A)
, there are infinitely many arrows outgoing from
S_{i}
, namely,
S_{i}\to X_{t}
, for each
{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
. Since every
X_{t}
is a brick, then
S_{i}
cannot be a summand of
\topm(X_{t})
, for any
{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
. Hence, there exists
1\leq j\leq n
, with
j\neq i
, such that the simple module
S_{j}
appears as a summand of the top of infinitely many bricks in
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}}
. Thus, in
Q^{b}(A)
, there are infinitely many arrows incoming to
S_{j}
. From this, we conclude that
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
in
Q^{b}(A)
are of infinite degree. The converse is trivial.

(4)

This directly follows from the definition.

(5)

We only prove the statements for finite paths in
Q^{b}(A)
, because a similar argument also works for the infinite paths.
Let
X_{0}\to X_{1}\to\cdots\to X_{m}
be an acyclic path in
Q^{b}(A)
, and assume that no subpath of it can be completed to a cycle. Namely, for each
0\leq i<j\leq m
, we have
X_{i}\not\simeq X_{j}
, and furthermore, there is no path in
Q^{b}(A)
from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
. In particular,
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(X_{j},X_{i})=0
. To show that
X_{0}\to\cdots\to X_{m}
gives rise to a chain of bricks, we consider the totally ordered set
(I,\leq)
, where
I:=\{0,1,\cdots,m\}
is the index set of the bricks
X_{i}
in the acyclic path
X_{0}\to\cdots\to X_{m}
, and the order in
(I,\leq)
is defined to be
s<t
if
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(X_{t},X_{s})=0
.
Then, it is immediate that the injective map
\Psi:I\rightarrow\brick A
, given by
\Psi(i)=X_{m-i}
, specifies a chain of bricks, particularly because, for all
s<t
in
I
, we have
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(\Psi(s),\Psi(t))=0
.

∎

As an immediate consequence of the preceding proposition, we obtain the following characterization of brick-directed algebras in terms of their brick-quivers.

Corollary 4.11

Let
A
be a connected algebra. Then,
A
is brick-directed if and only if the brick-quiver
Q^{b}(A)
is acyclic.

In the following remark, we highlight a property of the brick-quiver of algebras.

Remark 4.12

As observed in
[
MP2
]
, from some earlier results of
[
As1
]
, it follows that for any brick-finite algebra
A
of rank
n
, there is no set of pairwise Hom-orthogonal modules of size
n+1
. In particular, for such an algebra, the maximal size of an independent set in
Q^{b}(A)
is
n
, that is, the independence number of
Q^{b}(A)
is
n
and is attained exactly for the set of vertices in
Q^{b}(A)
corresponding to the simple
A
-modules (see
[
MP2
, Theorem A]
). In light of the Semibrick conjecture (see
[

, Conjecture 2.2]
), it is expected that the converse of the latter statement is true, that is, the independence number of

is
n
if and only if

is brick-finite. This is already known to be the case for various families of algebras, but it remains open in full generality (for more details, see
[

]
and references therein).

4.2.
Construction of new brick-directed algebras

We now give an explicit construction of new examples of brick-directed algebras via a process known as “gluing” two algebras. In particular, we closely follow the construction introduced in
[
MP1
, Section 7]
. This construction allows us to start from a pair of brick-directed algebras whose quivers satisfy some properties and construct a large family of new examples of brick-directed algebras.

Let
A=kQ/I
and
B=kQ^{\prime}/I^{\prime}
be two connected algebras, where
I
and
I^{\prime}
are assumed to be admissible ideals. Let
R
and
R^{\prime}
denote two sets of admissible relations, respectively, in
kQ
and
kQ^{\prime}
, which generate
I
and
I^{\prime}
.
Moreover, assume that
Q_{0}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and
Q^{\prime}_{0}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}
denote, respectively, the vertex set of
A
and
B
.
Provided that
x_{n}
is a sink in
Q
, and
y_{1}
is a source in
Q^{\prime}
, then by
\Lambda:=k\tilde{Q}/\tilde{I}
we denote the algebra obtained from
gluing
of
A
and
B
at the vertices
x_{n}
and
y_{1}
, with the bound quiver
(\tilde{Q},\tilde{I})
constructed as follows:

\mathbf{\tilde{Q}}
: The quiver obtained from
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
, by identifying the vertices
x_{n}
and
y_{1}
, and keeping the rest of
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
untouched. Hence, the set of vertices of
\tilde{Q}
is
\tilde{Q}_{0}=(Q_{0}-\{x_{n}\})\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}(Q^{\prime}_{0}-\{y_{1}\})\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}\{v\}
, where vertex
v
is obtained from the identification of
x_{n}
and
y_{1}
. Thus, we obtain
\tilde{Q}_{0}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},v,y_{2},\dots,y_{m}\}
, and the set of arrows is given by
\tilde{Q}
is
\tilde{Q}_{1}=Q_{1}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}Q^{\prime}_{1}
.

\mathbf{\tilde{I}}
: The admissible ideal in
k\tilde{Q}
is generated by the set
\tilde{R}:=R\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}R^{\prime}\mathbin{\mathaccent 0{\cdot}\cup}R_{v}
. Here,
R_{v}
denotes the set of all the paths of length
2
in
\tilde{Q}
that pass through vertex
v
. More specifically, each element of
R_{v}
is of the form
\beta\alpha
, for an arrow
\alpha\in Q_{1}
ending at vertex
x_{n}
, and an arrow
\beta\in Q^{\prime}_{1}
starting at vertex
y_{1}
.

From the construction, it is immediate that
(\tilde{Q},\tilde{I})
contains a copy of
(Q,I)
, as well as a copy of
(Q^{\prime},I^{\prime})
, as bound subquivers.
Moreover, other than vertex
v
in
\tilde{Q}
, any other vertex in
\tilde{Q}_{0}
belongs either to
Q_{0}
or
Q^{\prime}_{0}
, and not both.
We note that because
B
has a source, it is immediate that
\Lambda=k\tilde{Q}/\tilde{I}
is isomorphic to
A=kQ/I
if and only if the bound quiver of
B
consists of only one vertex, that is, if
B\simeq k
.

Some of the properties of the gluing process are listed in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.13

Let
A=kQ/I
and
B=kQ^{\prime}/I^{\prime}
be two connected algebras. If
\Lambda=k\tilde{Q}/\tilde{I}
is obtained from gluing
A
and
B
, then
\Lambda
is a connected algebra of rank
|A|+|B|-1
. Furthermore, the following hold:

(1)

If
M
is indecomposable, then
M
is either entirely supported over
Q
or entirely supported over
Q^{\prime}
.

(2)

\Lambda
is brick-directed if and only if both
A
and
B
are brick-directed.

Proof.

The first statement is clear. For the second statement, consider the brick quivers
Q^{b}(A)
and
Q^{b}(B)
of
A
and
B
, respectively. Let
S_{v}
be the simple module of
\Lambda
corresponding to the vertex
v
obtained from gluing the sink
x_{n}
in
A
with the source
y_{1}
in
B
.
We first observe that
S_{v}
is a source in
Q^{b}(A)
while it is a sink in
Q^{b}(B)
. Statement (2) follows from the fact that the brick quiver
Q^{b}(\Lambda)
of
\Lambda
is nothing but the gluing of
Q^{b}(A)
and
Q^{b}(B)
at
S_{v}
.
∎

Using the construction and proposition above, in the rest of this subsection we develop some tools to prove Corollary
1.6
. For that, we begin with some useful observations on some explicit examples of brick-directed algebras of smaller ranks that we use to construct brick-directed algebras of higher ranks. Meanwhile, let us recall that, by Drozd’s trichotomy theorem, each algebra
A
falls into exactly one of the three disjoint subfamilies, given by representation-finite, (representation-infinite) tame or wild algebras. For the definitions and further details on these subfamilies of algebras and the trichotomy theorem, see
[
SS
, Chapter XIX]
.

First, consider the local algebra
L_{n}=k[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]/\langle x_{i}x_{j}:1\leq i\leq j\leq n\rangle
. Because each local algebra is of rank
1
, the bound quiver of
L_{n}
consists of
n
loops that start and end at the same vertex, subject to all quadratic relations. It is well-known that
L_{1}
is representation-finite,
L_{2}
is (representation-infinite) tame, and
L_{n}
is wild for all
n>2
. Observe that
\brick L_{n}

4.3.
Wall-chamber structure of brick-directed algebras

Thanks to the rich connections between the study of bricks and the stability conditions, we can see some important properties of the brick-directed algebras through their
g
-vector fans. In particular, as the main result of this subsection, for brick-finite algebras we give an interesting characterization of brick-directed algebras in terms of their wall-and-chamber structures. For all the background, motivations, and undefined terminology on the
g
-vector fans and connections to stability conditions, we refer to
[
As2
,
AH+
,
BKT
,
BST
]
and the references therein.

As before, let
A
be an algebra of rank
n
, and consider the real Grothendieck group
K_{0}(\proj A)_{\mathbb{R}}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{R}[P_{i}]
.
We consider the
Euler bilinear form

K_{0}(\proj A)_{\mathbb{R}}\times K_{0}(\modu A)_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{R};\quad([P_{i}],[S_{j}])\mapsto\delta_{i,j}\dim_{k}\End_{A}(S_{j}).

Via this bilinear form, each
\theta\in K_{0}(\proj A)_{\mathbb{R}}
induces an
\mathbb{R}
-linear form
K_{0}(\modu A)_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
As in
[
Ki
]
, for
\theta\in K_{0}(\proj A)_{\mathbb{R}}
, a module

is said to be
\theta
-semistable
if
\theta([M])=0
and
\theta([N])\geq 0
for every quotient module
N
of
M
. If a
\theta
-semistable module
M
moreover satisfies
\theta([N])>0
for every non-zero proper quotient module
N
of
M
, then
M
is said to be
\theta
-stable
. Following
[
BST
]
, for each module
M\in\modu A
, we define

\Theta_{M}:=\{\theta\in K_{0}(\proj A)_{\mathbb{R}}\mid\text{$M$ is $\theta$-semistable}\}.

Recall that a
wall
in
K_{0}(\proj A)_{\mathbb{R}}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a set of the form
\Theta_{X}
, where
X\in\brick A
and
\Theta_{X}
has co-dimension one in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Every wall
\Theta_{X}
has a unique supporting hyperplane defined as
\mathcal{H}_{X}:=\{\theta\in K_{0}(\proj A)_{\mathbb{R}}\mid\theta([X])=0\}
. Observe that the converse is not true in general, that is, two distinct walls may have the same supporting hyperplane.

For each
\theta\in K_{0}(\proj A)_{\mathbb{R}}
,
[
BKT
]
associated two torsion pairs
(\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{\theta},\mathcal{F}_{\theta})
and
(\mathcal{T}_{\theta},\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta})
in
\modu A
by

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{\theta}

\displaystyle:=\{M\in\modu A\mid\text{$\theta([N])\geq 0$ for every quotient module $N$ of $M$}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\theta}

\displaystyle:=\{M\in\modu A\mid\text{$\theta([L])<0$ for every submodule $L\neq 0$ of $M$}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\theta}

\displaystyle:=\{M\in\modu A\mid\text{$\theta([N])>0$ for every quotient module $N\neq 0$ of $M$}\},

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}

\displaystyle:=\{M\in\modu A\mid\text{$\theta([L])\geq 0$ for every submodule $L$ of $M$}\}.

We always have
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{\theta}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}
. The difference between
(\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{\theta},\mathcal{F}_{\theta})
and
(\mathcal{T}_{\theta},\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta})
are expressed by the subcategory
\mathcal{W}_{\theta}:=\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{\theta}\cap\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{\theta}
, which is the subcategory consisting of all
\theta
-semistable modules. The subcategory

is known to be a wide subcategory of
\modu A
whose simple objects are exactly the
\theta
-stable modules. The following two results on brick-finite algebras are known and play important roles in our proofs below. For the details, we refer to
[

]
.

Proposition 4.17

Let

The following lemma collects some key properties of the wall and chamber structure of a brick-finite algebra.

Let

(1)

For each

5.
Trim lattices in representation theory

In this section, we use our results on brick-splitting torsion pairs and brick-directed algebras to give a new characterization of several lattice theoretical notions in the setting of lattice of torsion classes. In particular, we prove Theorem
1.7
and Corollaries
1.8
,
1.9
and
1.11
from Section
1
. More specifically, we fully determine for which algebras, the lattice of torsion classes are extremal, left modular and trim (for definitions, see Section
2
).

As remarked before, in the literature of lattice theory, the lattice theoretical notions considered in this work (i.e., extremality, left modularity and trimness) are only defined for finite lattices. Since we study such properties for the lattice of torsion classes, unless specified otherwise, throughout the entire section we only work with those algebras
A
for which
\tors A
is a finite lattice. This is known to be the case if and only if
A
is brick-finite (for details, see
[
DIJ
]
and references therein).

In what follows, we first prove Theorem
1.7
. This is obtained as a consequence of the next two propositions, which can be seen as the algebraic counterparts of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
, where we are concerned with the lattice of torsion classes of brick-finite algebras.

Proposition 5.1

If
A
is a brick-finite algebra, then the following are equivalent:

(1)

A
is brick-directed;

(2)

\tors A
is left modular.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.7
,
A
is brick-directed if and only if
\tors A
admits a maximal chain of brick-splitting torsion classes. Moreover, Proposition
3.15
asserts that brick-splitting torsion classes in
\modu A
are exactly the left modular elements in
\tors A
. Hence,
A
is brick-directed if and only if
\tors A
admits a maximal chain consisting of left modular elements. Therefore, by definition, this implies that
A
is brick-directed if and only if
\tors A
is left modular.
∎

In the next proposition, we consider the notion of extremality of
\tors A
. Before we state the following result, we note that the next proposition can be alternatively concluded from Proposition
5.1
, together with some recent results of
[
Mu
]
on semidistributive left modular lattices (for more details, see Section
2.1
). However, we use our results on brick-splitting torsion classes and brick-directed algebras to give a direct proof of this proposition.

Proposition 5.2

If
A
is a brick-finite algebra, the following are equivalent:

(1)

A
is brick-directed;

(2)

\tors A
is extremal.

Proof.

First, note that, by Theorem
2.5
, there is a bijection between
\brick A
and the set of join-irreducible (similarly, the set of meet-irreducible) elements of
\tors A
(also see
[
BCZ
, Theorem 1.5]
). Thus, to prove the proposition, we show that
A
is brick-directed if and only if
\tors A
admits a maximal chain of length
|{\brick A}|
.

To prove
(1)\to(2)
, we first note that, by Theorem
4.7
,
\tors A
admits a maximal chain
\{\mathcal{T}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of brick-splitting torsion classes. Moreover, by Proposition
3.12
, for each
\mathcal{T}_{i}
in this chain, every
X\in\brick A
belongs to
\brick[0,\mathcal{T}_{i}]
or
\brick[\mathcal{T}_{i},\modu A]
. Hence, each
X\in\brick A
appears as a label of the maximal chain
\{\mathcal{T}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, implying that
\tors A
has a maximal chain of length
|{\brick A}|
, thus
\tors A
is a extremal lattice.

To show
(2)\to(1)
, note that if
\tors A
is extremal, then
\tors A
admits a maximal chain of length
|{\brick A}|
. Then, by Theorem
2.7
, there is a chain of bricks of length
|{\brick A}|
, that is, there is a total order on the set of
\brick A
. This, by definition, implies that there is no brick-cycle in
\modu A
, hence
A
is brick-directed.
∎

As a consequence of the preceding propositions, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 5.3

Let
A
be brick-finite algebra. Then,
A
is brick-directed if and only if
\tors A
is a trim lattice. That being the case, the spine of
\tors A
forms a distributive sublattice of
\tors A
which consists of brick-splitting torsion classes.

Proof.

By definition,
\tors A
is a trim lattice if and only if
\tors A
is extremal and left modular. Hence, the first assertion follows from Propositions
5.1

and
5.2
. Moreover, from
[
TW
, Theorem 3.7]
, an element of a trim lattice is left modular if and only if it lies on the spine.
Hence, if
\tors A
is trim, then by Proposition
3.15
, a torsion class
\mathcal{T}
in
\modu A
belongs to the spine of
\tors A
if and only if
\mathcal{T}
is brick-splitting. Moreover, from
[
Th
, Lemma 7]
, it follows that the spine of every trim lattice is a distributive sublattice.
∎

From some standard algebraic arguments, together with Proposition
4.4
, we obtain Corollary
1.8
, which we prove below.

Corollary 5.4

Let
A
be a brick-finite algebra. If
\tors A
is extremal (equivalently, left modular), then the following lattices are also extremal:

(1)

\tors A/J
, for any 2-sided ideal
J
in
A
;

(2)

\tors eAe
, for any idempotent element
e
in
A
;

(3)

\tors B
, for any algebra
B
which is a
\tau
-reduction of
A
.

Proof.

By Corollary
5.3
,
A
is brick-directed if and only if
\tors A
is a trim lattice. Moreover, by Proposition
4.4
, the algebras
A/J
,
eAe
, and
B
described above are also brick-directed. Hence, the implications follow immediately from Propositions
5.1
and
5.2
.
∎

As noted earlier, the family of brick-directed algebras properly generalizes the classical family of representation-directed algebras. In fact, from the above lattice theoretical characterizations of brick-directed algebras, we also obtain a useful characterization of representation-directed algebras in terms of their lattice of torsion classes. In particular, we prove Corollary
1.9
.

Corollary 5.5

Let
A
be brick-finite algebra. Then,
A
is representation-directed if and only if
A
is representation-finite and length of
\tors A
is
|{\ind A}|
.

Proof.

If
A
is representation-directed, then we have
\ind A=\brick A
. In particular, every indecomposable module in
\modu A
is rigid, and therefore
A
is representation-finite (see
[
Dr
]
or
[
MP1
]
). Moreover,
A
is evidently brick-directed and therefore, by Proposition
5.2
,
\tors A
is extremal, that is,
\tors A
has a maximal chain of length
|{\brick A}|
. Thus, length of
\tors A
is obviously
|{\ind A}|
.

To show the converse, first observe that, for any algebra
A
, length of
\tors A
is always bounded by
|{\brick A}|
, and we obviously have
|{\brick A}|\leq|{\ind A}|
. Thus, if
A
is representation-finite and length of
\tors A
is
|{\ind A}|
, we must have
|{\brick A}|=|{\ind A}|
and that
\tors A
admits a maximal chain of length
|{\brick A}|
. These, in particular, imply that
\brick A=\ind A
, and
A
is brick-directed. By Theorem
2.7
, this further implies that there is a total order on
\ind A
, implying that
A
is a representation-directed algebra.
∎

Regarding the preceding proof, we note that the equality
\ind A=\brick A
implies that
A
is representation-finite. However, this does not necessarily imply that
A
is representation-directed (for more details, see
[
Dr
]
and
[
MP1
]
).

Remark 5.6

Let us briefly remark on the connection between our results on brick-directed algebras and the notion of (torsional) brick chain filtration, recently introduced in
[
Ri1
]
.
In particular, observe that a brick-finite algebra
A
is brick-directed if and only if a module in
\modu A
admits a (torsional) brick chain filtration that contains all elements of
\brick A
.

As another remarkable property of brick-directed algebras, below we state and prove Corollary
1.11
. To put this result in perspective, recall that each representation-directed algebra is representation-finite, and over any such algebra every indecomposable is uniquely determined by its dimension vector (see
[
ASS
, IX. Prop. 1.3]
). Our next result asserts the analogous property for brick-finite algebras which are brick-directed.

Corollary 5.7

Let
A
be a brick-finite algebra. If
A
is brick-directed, then each brick in
\modu A
is uniquely determined by its dimension vector, that is, for two distinct elements
X
and
Y
in
\brick A
, we have
[X]\neq[Y]
.

Proof.

Since
A
is brick-directed, we take a sequence
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{r}
of all bricks over
A
ordered so that
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(X_{i},X_{j})=0
for any
i<j
by Theorem
4.7
. As in the proof of Proposition
4.21
, we take
\theta_{i}\in K_{0}(\proj A)_{\mathbb{Q}}
for each
i
so that
X_{i}\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{\theta_{j}}
if
i\leq j
and
X_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta_{j}}
if
i>j
.

Now assume that there exist two distinct elements
X
and
Y
in
\brick A
such that
[X]=[Y]
. Without loss of generality, we may write
X=X_{l}
and
Y=X_{m}
with
l<m
. Then
Y=X_{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\theta_{l}}
holds, which gives
\theta_{l}([Y])<0
. On the other hand, we also have
X=X_{l}\in\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{\theta_{l}}
, which implies
\theta_{l}([X])\geq 0
. These contradict each other, because
[X]=[Y]
.
∎

Remark 5.8

Motivated by the uniqueness property shown in Corollary
5.7
, and our results from Section
4.3
on wall-and-chambers of brick-directed algebras, we pose the following question on brick-infinite algebras:
Let
A
be brick-directed, and assume that
X
and
Y
are bricks with
[X]=[Y]
in
K_{0}(\modu A)
. Then does there exist a brick
Z
such that
[X]=[Y]=[Z]
and
\Theta_{X}\subset\Theta_{Z}
and
\Theta_{Y}\subset\Theta_{Z}
?

We note that the uniqueness property considered in Corollary
5.7
can also be interpreted in terms of paths in a Newton polytope. To explain this point, let us first recall some terminology.

Let
M\in\modu A
. Then, the
Newton polytope

\mathrm{N}(M)
of
M
in
K_{0}(\modu A)_{\mathbb{R}}
is defined by
[
BKT
,
Fe1
]
as the convex hull of the subset
\{[L]\mid\text{$L$ is a submodule of $M$}\}
. For basic properties of Newton polytopes, see also
[
AH+
,
Fe2
]
.

For any two elements
v,w\in K_{0}(\modu A)_{\mathbb{R}}
, we write
v\leq w
if
w-v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}[S_{i}]
with
a_{i}\geq 0
for each
i
, and
v<w
means
v\leq w
and
v\neq w
. We call a sequence
v_{0}<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{l}

an increasing path
of the Newton polytope
\mathrm{N}(M)
if
v_{i}
is a vertex of
\mathrm{N}(M)
, for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,l\}
, and the line segment
[v_{i-1},v_{i}]
is an edge of
\mathrm{N}(M)
with
v_{i-1}<v_{i}
, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
.

Moreover, for any
\mathcal{T}\in\tors A
and
M\in\modu A
, we define
t_{\mathcal{T}}(M)\subseteq M
as the unique submodule
L\subseteq M
such that
L\in\mathcal{T}
and
M/L\in\mathcal{T}^{\perp}
.

In our context, it is enough to consider the case where
M
is the direct sum of all bricks in
\modu A
with
A
brick-finite. Then
\mathrm{N}(M)
and
\tors A
are related as follows.

Proposition 5.9

[
AH+
, Subsection 5.3]

[
Fe2
, Subsection 9.2]

Let
A
be brick-finite, and set
M
as the direct sum of all
X\in\brick A
.

(1)

There exists a bijection

\tors A\to\{\text{vertices of $\mathrm{N}(M)$}\}

given by
\mathcal{T}\mapsto[t_{\mathcal{T}}(M)]
.

(2)

The map in (1) induces a bijection

\{\text{maximal chains in $\tors A$}\}\to\{\text{increasing paths in $\mathrm{N}(M)$ from $0$ to $[M]$}\},

which preserves lengths.

(3)

Let
0=\mathcal{T}_{0}\subset\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{T}_{l}=\modu A
be a maximal chain in
\tors A
, and assume that it is sent to
0=v_{0}<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{l}=[M]
under the bijection (2). Then, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
, we have
v_{i}-v_{i-1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}[X_{i}]
for the label
X_{i}
of the arrow
\mathcal{T}_{i}\to\mathcal{T}_{i-1}
.

An increasing path
v_{0}<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{l}
in
\mathrm{N}(M)
is said to be
indivisible
if
v_{i}-v_{i-1}
is an indivisible element in
K_{0}(\modu A)
, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
.

Lemma 5.10

Let
A
be brick-finite, and set
M
as the direct sum of
X\in\brick A
. Assume that
0=v_{0}<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{l}=[M]
is an increasing path in
\mathrm{N}(M)
from
0
to
[M]
. Then the path is indivisible if and only if
l=|{\brick A}|
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.9
(3), we take a positive integer
m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that
v_{i}-v_{i-1}=m_{i}[X_{i}]
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
. Then we get

\sum_{X\in\brick A}[X]=[M]=v_{l}-v_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}m_{i}[X_{i}].

We first show the “if” part. The bricks
X_{1},\ldots,X_{l}
are distinct by Proposition
2.6
, so the assumption
l=|{\brick A}|
implies that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{l}
are all the bricks in
\modu A
. Thus
\sum_{X\in\brick A}[X]=\sum_{i=1}^{l}m_{i}[X_{i}]
above gives
m_{i}=1
, for any
i
. This implies that the increasing path
0=v_{0}<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{l}=[M]
is indivisible, because Lemma
4.18
(2) implies that each
[X_{i}]
is indivisible.

Next we show the “only if” part. Assume that
0=v_{0}<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{l}=[M]
is indivisible. Then we have
m_{i}=1
, for any
i
. Thus we have
\sum_{X\in\brick A}[X]=\sum_{i=1}^{l}[X_{i}]
. This implies
l=|{\brick A}|
, since
X_{1},\ldots,X_{l}
are distinct.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1.12
and give an interesting characterization of brick-directed algebras in terms of properties of the Newton polytope considered above.

Theorem 5.11

Let
A
be brick-finite, and set
M
as the direct sum of
X\in\brick A
. Then,
A
is brick directed if and only if
there exists an indivisible increasing path in
\mathrm{N}(M)
from
0
to
[M]
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
5.2
,
A
is brick directed if and only if
\tors A
has a maximal chain of length
|{\brick A}|
. The latter is equivalent to the existence of an increasing path of
\mathrm{N}(M)
of length
|{\brick A}|
from
0
to
[M]
, by Proposition
5.9
(2), and also to the existence of an indivisible increasing path of
\mathrm{N}(M)
from
0
to
[M]
, by Lemma
5.10
.
∎

We finish this section with a remark and further observations on Corollary
5.7
, including a question on the behavior of arbitrary brick-directed algebras.

Remark 5.12

To suitably put the important uniqueness property of Corollary
5.7
in perspective, particularly with regard to some open conjectures on bricks, we separately discuss the case for brick-finite as well as brick-infinite algebras.

(1)

Observe that if
A
is a brick-finite algebra whose bricks are uniquely determined by their dimension vectors, we cannot necessarily conclude that
A
is brick-directed. For instance, let
A=kQ/I
, where
Q
is a given by

\textstyle{2\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\beta}
\textstyle{1\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha}
\textstyle{3\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\gamma}

and
I=\langle\beta\alpha,\gamma\beta,\alpha\gamma\rangle
. Then,
\ind A=\{{\begin{smallmatrix}1\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}2\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}3\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
2\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}2\\
3\end{smallmatrix}},{\begin{smallmatrix}3\\
1\end{smallmatrix}}\}
,
and
\ind A=\brick A
. Obviously, each element of
\ind A
is uniquely determined by its dimension vector. However,
{\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
2\end{smallmatrix}}\rightarrow{\begin{smallmatrix}3\\
1\end{smallmatrix}}\rightarrow{\begin{smallmatrix}2\\
3\end{smallmatrix}}\rightarrow{\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
2\end{smallmatrix}}
is a brick-cycle in
\modu A
. Thus,
A
is not brick-directed.

(2)

As observed previously, there exist various families of brick-infinite algebras that are brick-directed (see Prop.
4.6
and Cor.
4.16
). We note that, in contrast to the uniqueness property in Corollary
5.7
, one cannot expect a similar statement for the brick-infinite brick-directed algebras.
More precisely, let
A
be a brick-infinite algebra over an algebraically closed field. Then, by the so-called “Second brick-Brauer-Thrall” conjecture (2nd bBT), first posed by the third-named author in 2019, it is expected that
\brick A
contains an infinite set of bricks with the same dimension vector. For the original motivations of the 2nd bBT, and the most recent state of art on this conjecture, respectively, see
[
Mo2
,
Mo3
]
,
[
MP2
,
MP3
]
, and the references therein. Meanwhile, one can naturally ask if, for an arbitrary brick-directed algebra
A
, and for each dimension vector
\underline{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
, the module variety
\modu(A,\underline{d})
always admits at most one brick component (for terminology and some motivations, see
[
MP1
]
).
