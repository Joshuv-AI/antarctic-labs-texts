Abstract
. A shuffle square is a word consisting of two shuffled copies of the same word. For instance, the Turkish word 𝚒𝚔 ​ 𝚒 ​ 𝚕𝚒 ​ 𝚔𝚕𝚒 \mathtt{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{ik}\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{i}\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{li}\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{kli}} ( binary in English) is a shuffle square, as it can be split into two copies of the word 𝚒𝚔𝚕𝚒 \mathtt{ikli} . We explore a representation of shuffle squares in terms of ordered nest-free graphs and demonstrate the usefulness of this approach by applying it to several families of binary words. Among others, we characterize shuffle squares with four and five runs, as well as shuffle squares with all 𝟷 \mathtt{1} -runs of length one (and with the 𝟷 \mathtt{1} ’s alternating between the two copies). In our main result we provide quite general sufficient conditions for a binary word not to be a shuffle square. In particular, it follows that binary words of the type ( 𝟷𝟶𝟶𝟷 ) n (\mathtt{1001})^{n} , n n odd, are not shuffle squares. We complement it by showing that all other words whose every 𝟷 \mathtt{1} -run has length one or two, while every 𝟶 \mathtt{0} -run has length two, are shuffle squares. We also provide a counterexample to a believable stipulation that binary words of the form 𝟷 m 𝟶 m − 2 𝟷 m − 4 ⋯ \mathtt{1}^{m}\mathtt{0}^{m-2}\mathtt{1}^{m-4}\cdots , m m odd, are far from being shuffle squares (the distance measured by the minimum number of letters one has to delete in order to turn a word into a shuffle square).



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Definitions

Let
k\geqslant 1
be an integer and let
A
be an alphabet with
|A|=k
. By a
k
-ary word
of
length
n
we mean any sequence
W=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
with
w_{i}\in A
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. For
binary
words we typically take the alphabet
\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}
or
\{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\}
, while for bigger alphabets we rather use the set
[k]:=\{\mathtt{1},\mathtt{2},\dots,\mathtt{k}\}
. The length of a word
W
will be denoted by
|W|
.

Any subsequence
W^{\prime}=w_{i_{1}}\cdots w_{i_{t}}
of a word
W=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
is called a
subword
of
W
. Its set of indices is denoted by
\supp(W^{\prime})=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{t}\}
and called the
support
of
W^{\prime}
. If the support of a subword
W^{\prime}
consists of consecutive integers, then we call
W^{\prime}
a
block
of
W
.

A pair of subwords
X,Y
of
W
with
X=Y
and disjoint supports is called
twins
. We will be using the shorthand

\supp(X,Y)=\supp(X)\cup\supp(Y)

wherever convenient.
The length of twins is defined as the length of either one of them,
|X|=|Y|
. We will occasionally refer also to the
double length
of twins which equals twice the length and measures how much of the word is taken by the twins. The letters of
W
which do not belong to either of the twins are called
gaps
(with respect to
X,Y
).

Example 1
.

Let

W=\mathtt{111001000110}

be a binary word of length
12
. One pair of twins
X,Y
of length 5 (so of double length
10
) in
W
is given by
\supp(X)=\{1,6,8,9,12\}
and
\supp(Y)=\{2,3,4,5,7\}
, as indicated by colors and under/overlines:

W=\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{1}}}\;\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{1}}}\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{1}}}\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{0}}}\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{0}}}\;\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{1}}}\;\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{0}}}\;\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{0}}}\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{0}}}\;\mathtt{1}\mathtt{1}\;\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{0}}}.

Indeed, we have
X=Y=\mathtt{11000}
, while
w_{10}
and
w_{11}
are gaps here.

Given two words
U={u}_{1}\cdots{u}_{n}
and
W={w}_{1}\cdots{w}_{m}
, their
concatenation
is defined as the word
UW={u}_{1}\cdots{u}_{n}{w}_{1}\cdots{w}_{m}
. This definition extends naturally to any finite number of words. In particular,
W^{r}
denotes the concatenation of
r
copies of the word
W
. For
w\in A
, we may write
{w}^{r}
instead of
{ww}\cdots{w}
. For instance,

W=\mathtt{111001000110}=\mathtt{1}^{3}\mathtt{0}^{2}\mathtt{10}^{3}\mathtt{1}^{2}\mathtt{0}.

This notation comes in handy when there are long blocks of identical letters.

For any letter
w\in A
, a
w
-run
in a word
W
is a maximal block
F
of
W
of the form
F={w}^{r}
for some
r\geqslant 1
. Here “maximal” means that no other block
F^{\prime}
of
W
which contains
F
is of the form
F^{\prime}=w^{r^{\prime}}
, with
r^{\prime}>r
. By a
run
we mean a
w
-run for any
w\in A
. Every word can be uniquely decomposed into a concatenation of its runs. For instance, in
\mathtt{011011100}
there are two
\mathtt{1}
-runs (
\mathtt{11},\mathtt{111}
) and three
\mathtt{0}
-runs (
\mathtt{0},\mathtt{0},\mathtt{00}
), and the corresponding decomposition is

\mathtt{0\;11\;0\;111\;00}=\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}^{2}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}^{3}\mathtt{0}^{2}.

For every non-empty word
X
we call the word
X^{2}=XX
a
square
. For instance, the English word
\mathtt{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{hots}\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{hots}}
is a square. This simple structure is a frequent theme of investigations since the celebrated 1906 result of Thue
[
26
]
(see the next subsection).
In this paper, however, we focus on a natural generalization of squares created more frivolously from two copies of the same word.
A
shuffle square
is a word
W
which contains twins of double length equal to exactly
|W|
; such twins will be called
perfect
, as they fill the whole word with no gaps. For instance,
\mathtt{00001001}
is a shuffle square with the perfect twins equal to
\mathtt{0001}
:

\mathtt{\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}0}}\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}0001}}\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}001}}}.

Obviously, a necessary condition for a word to be a shuffle square is that each letter in the word occurs an even number of times. We will call such words
even
(elsewhere, they are called
tangrams
, cf.
[
11
,
25
]
).

1.2.
Related results and conjectures

Shuffle squares were introduced in 2012 by Henshall, Rampersad, and Shallit
[
20
]
, and since then their various aspects have been intensively studied (see
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
15
,
19
]
).

The main challenge raised in
[
20
]
is to determine the number
S_{k}(n)
of distinct
k
-ary shuffle squares of length
2n
. The problem seems hard even in the most basic case of
k=2
. He, Huang, Nam, and Thaper
[
19
]
derived an asymptotic formula for
S_{k}(n)
, stipulated in
[
20
]
, and proved that
S_{2}(n)\geqslant\binom{2n}{n}
. They also posed an intriguing conjecture stating that almost all
even
binary words are shuffle squares. Since exactly a half of binary words of fixed even length are even, the conjecture is equivalent to

(1.1)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{S_{2}(n)}{2^{2n-1}}=1.

For a given binary word
W
, let us denote by
g(W)
the minimum number of gaps in
W
, that is, letters one has to delete from
W
in order to get a shuffle square. For instance,
g(\mathtt{0011})=0
, while
g(\mathtt{0110})=2
. Let
g(n)
be the maximum of
g(W)
over all binary words
W
of fixed length
n
.

By a sophisticated approach via a regularity lemma for words, Axenovich, Person, and Puzynina
[
3
]
showed that
g(n)=o(n)
. This means that every binary word of length
n
contains a shuffle square of length asymptotically close to
n
. On the other hand, by an explicit construction they showed that
g(n)=\Omega(\log n)
, which was improved by Bukh
[
6
]
to
g(n)=~\Omega(n^{1/3})
(see
[
4
]
for a proof). The exact asymptotic order of
g(n)
remains a mystery, though some intuition suggests that it should be
g(n)=\Theta(n^{1/2})
.

In the spirit of Thue, one may also investigate various
avoidability
properties involving shuffle squares. Generally, a set of words
\mathcal{F}
is
k
-avoidable
if there exist arbitrarily long
k
-ary words with no blocks belonging to
\mathcal{F}
. Thue proved in
[
26
]
that squares are
3
-avoidable. This result is considered the starting point of Combinatorics on Words, a wide discipline with plenty of connections and applications in various areas (see
[
5
]
,
[
23
]
).

What is the smallest
k
such that shuffle squares are
k
-avoidable? Using the probabilistic method, Currie
[
10
]
proved that
k\leqslant 10^{40}
. Later, this was improved to
k\leqslant 10
by Müller
[
24
]
, and to
k\leqslant 7
, by Guégan and Ochem
[
18
]
and, independently, by Grytczuk, Kozik, and Zaleski
[
14
]
. The last result is true in a more complex
list
variant, where the letters of constructed words have to be chosen from alphabets assigned individually to each position. Currently the best result is due to Bulteau, Jugé, and Vialette
[
8
]
, who proved that shuffle squares are
6
-avoidable. They also conjecture that
k=4
, which would be best possible.

On the algorithmic side, Bulteau and Vialette
[
7
]
proved that deciding if a given binary word is a shuffle square is NP-complete. The proof reduces the problem to
Bin-Packing
via graph structures similar to the ones we introduce and explore in this paper.

In the last section we present more open problems related to shuffle squares.

1.3.
Our results and the method

In this paper we first characterize shuffle squares in terms of ordered nest-free graphs (see Proposition
1
). Then, based on this characterization,
we establish conditions under which binary words belonging to specified classes are shuffle squares and conditions under which they are not.

In Section
3
, as a gentle warm-up, we consider words with four runs and five runs, as well as words with all
\mathtt{1}
-runs of length one (see, respectively, Propositions
2
,
3
and
4
).

In Section
4
, we prove our main result.

Theorem 1
.

For an integer
n\geqslant 1
, let
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n+1})
and
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
be two sequences of positive integers such that:

(1)

a_{1}
and
a_{n+1}
are odd, while
a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
are even,

(2)

for every
I\subset[n]
,
\sum_{i\in I}b_{i}\neq\sum_{i\not\in I}b_{i}
, that is, the sequence
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
cannot be split into two disjoint subsequences with the same sum.

Then the word

W=\mathtt{1}^{a_{1}}\mathtt{0}^{b_{1}}\mathtt{1}^{a_{2}}\mathtt{0}^{b_{2}}\mathtt{1}^{a_{3}}\cdots\mathtt{1}^{a_{n}}\mathtt{0}^{b_{n}}\mathtt{1}^{a_{n+1}}

is
not
a shuffle square.

It follows (see Corollary
3
with
r=2
in Section
4
) that the words
(\mathtt{1001})^{n}
, for
n
odd, are
not
shuffle squares. This was conjectured by Komisarski
[
22
]
, who used to call it the
odd ABBA problem
. Let us also mention that an alleged proof was presented by Fortuna
[
13
]
, though, up to our knowledge, it has not yet been written down. Actually, we show (see Proposition
5
) that the words
(\mathtt{1001})^{n}
,
n
odd, are the
only
binary words with all
\mathtt{0}
-runs of length two and all
\mathtt{1}
-runs of length at most two which are not shuffle squares.

Example 2
.

One class of instances which trivially satisfy assumption (2) of Theorem

is when
2\max_{i}b_{i}>\sum_{i}b_{i}
. Here is one example of such a word:

\mathtt{1}\;\mathtt{0}^{2}\;\mathtt{1}^{2}\;\mathtt{0}^{2}\;\mathtt{1}^{2}\;\mathtt{0}^{10}\;\mathtt{1}^{2}\;\mathtt{0}^{2}\;\mathtt{1}^{2}\;\mathtt{0}^{2}\;\mathtt{1}.

Example 3
.

Let us notice that conditions (1) and (2) of Theorem
1
do not characterize binary words which are not shuffle squares. Consider the word
W=\mathtt{101100110001}
. The related sequences of capacities are
(1,2,2,1)
and
(1,2,3)
, so condition (1) is satisfied while (2) is not, since
1+2=3
. Nevertheless,
W
is not a shuffle square, as it can be verified by inspection (or by the next-free graph method introduced in this paper).

In the last section, we tackle the question of how far a binary word may be from a shuffle square. Inspired by a personal communication with Boris Bukh and his notes
[
6
]
, in
[
4
]
the following conjecture has been implicitly stated.

Conjecture 1
.

There is a constant
c
such that for every
n
we have
g(n)\geqslant c\sqrt{n}
, that is, there exists a binary word
of length
n
with largest twins leaving out at least
c\sqrt{n}

elements.

In view of earlier constructions, we had thought for some time that a natural candidate to facilitate Conjecture
1
was the binary word

O_{m,r}=\mathtt{1}^{m}\mathtt{0}^{m-2}\mathtt{1}^{m-4}\mathtt{0}^{m-6}\mathtt{1}^{m-8}\cdots

consisting of

and
r\leqslant(m+1)/2
. Here we rebuke this belief by showing that
g(O_{m,r})\leq 23
, that is, by finding twins in
O_{m,r}
of double length at least
|O_{m,r}|-23
, for all
m
and
r
.

But, in our opinion, at least as important as the obtained results is the method we propose to prove them with. To each pair of
canonical
twins we uniquely assign an
ordered graph
(see the formal definitions in the next sections). Thus, instead of looking for twins in a word or deciding if it is a shuffle square, we construct a corresponding graph or show that such a graph does not exist. We believe that this approach is more transparent, insightful, and ultimately has some potential in this area of research.

1.4.
Canonical twins

Before we turn to graphic representation of shuffle squares and, more generally, twins in words, we need to introduce an important notion of canonical twins. But first we define monotone twins. Twins
X,Y
with supports
\supp(X)=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{t}\}
and
\supp(Y)=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{t}\}
are
monotone
if for every
h\in[t]
, we have
i_{h}<j_{h}
. By definition, among monotone twins there is always the “first” twin and the “second” twin, so we may now treat them as ordered pairs and denote by
(X,Y)
.

Observe that if in a word
W
there are twins
X,Y
of length
t
, then there are also monotone twins
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
of length
t

(and also with
\supp(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=\supp(X,Y)
and
X^{\prime}=X
).
We obtain them by swapping the elements which appear in
X
and
Y
in the wrong order.
More precisely, let
S=\{h:i_{h}>j_{h}\}
.
Then, the sets

I^{\prime}=\{i_{h}:h\notin S\}\cup\{j_{h}:h\in S\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad J^{\prime}=\{j_{h}:h\notin S\}\cup\{i_{h}:h\in S\}

support monotone twins.

For instance, in Example
1
from the previous section, the twins are not monotone, but with
S=\{2,3,4,5\}
, that is after swapping four pairs of elements between
X
and
Y
(all but the first one), the pair of twins
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
, indicated here:

W=\mathtt{\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}}\;\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}}\;\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}}\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}0}}\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}0}}\;\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}}\;\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}0}}\;\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}0}}\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}0}}\;11\;\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}0}}},

is monotone.

Besides swapping there are other operations which do not change the twins except for their location and mutual entanglement. Given
i_{g},i_{h}\in\supp(X)
and
j_{g},j_{h}\in\supp(Y)
,
g<h
, with
j_{g}<i_{h}
,
w_{i_{h}}=w_{j_{g}}
, and

\{j_{g}+1,\dots,i_{h}-1\}\cap\supp(X,Y)=\emptyset,

the
(g,h)
-rewiring
of the
monotone
twins
(X,Y)
in
W
is a new pair of twins,
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
with

\supp(X^{\prime})=(\supp(X)\setminus\{i_{h}\})\cup\{j_{g}\}

and

\supp(Y^{\prime})=(\supp(Y)\setminus\{j_{g}\})\cup\{i_{h}\}.

Note that
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
are still monotone.
Monotone twins
(X,Y)
for which a rewiring operation is no longer possible, enjoy the property that within each run all elements of
X
precede all elements of
Y
.

Finally, a trivial operation of
shifting
moves all the gaps to the right end of each run. It applies whenever there is a pair of consecutive positions,
\{i,i+1\}
, such that
i
is a gap while, say,
i+1\in\supp(X)
. Then we switch them around, that is, for the new
X^{\prime}
we have

\supp(X^{\prime})=(\supp(X)\setminus\{i+1\})\cup\{i\}.

When
i+1\in\supp(Y)
, the operation is analogous. If this operation is also no longer possible, then the resulted twins partition each run of
W
into three consecutive blocks (some might be empty) with the elements of
X
to the left, followed by the elements of
Y
, followed by gaps.
Such twins will be called
canonical
.
It is crucial for us to observe that among the longest twins in a word there is always (at least) one pair which is canonical.

2.
Graphic representations

Here we introduce our main tool—a representation of shuffle squares and, more generally, canonical twins in terms of ordered graphs with no nests.
A similar idea was utilized in
[
7
]
as a reduction tool in the proof of the NP-completeness of the decision problem for shuffle squares.

2.1.
Ordered graphs from words

An
ordered graph
is just a graph with its vertex set linearly ordered, say
from left to right
. We allow loops and parallel edges. A
nest
in an ordered graph consists of a pair of disjoint edges (including loops)
e
and
f
with
\min e<\min f
and
\max e>\max f
(see Fig.
3
). An ordered graph is
nest-free
if no pair of its edges forms a nest.

e
f

Figure 3.
A nest in an ordered graph.

Given an ordered graph
G
and a vertex
u\in V(G)
, the
right degree

\deg_{G}^{(\rightarrow)}(u)
of
u
in
G
is defined as the number of edges going from
u
to the right, and the
left degree

\deg_{G}^{(\leftarrow)}(u)
is defined analogously (each loop at
u
contributes 1 to
\deg_{G}^{(\rightarrow)}(u)
and 1 to
\deg_{G}^{(\leftarrow)}(u)
). Obviously, we have

\deg_{G}(u)=\deg_{G}^{(\rightarrow)}(u)+\deg_{G}^{(\leftarrow)}(u)

for every
u\in V(G)

We now describe a (unique) representation of canonical twins as a nest-free graph. Let a
k
-ary word
W=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
have canonical twins
(X,Y)
where
X=w_{i_{1}}\cdots w_{i_{t}}
and
Y=~w_{j_{1}}\cdots w_{j_{t}}
. In particular,

\{i_{1},\dots,i_{t}\}\cap\{j_{1},\dots,j_{t}\}=\emptyset,

and for all
h=1,\dots,t
, we have
i_{h}<j_{h}
and
w_{i_{h}}=w_{j_{h}}
. As an intermediate step, define an ordered graph
M=M_{X,Y}
on the set of vertices
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with edges
\{i_{1},j_{1}\},\dots,\{i_{t},j_{t}\}
. So,
M
consists of a
matching
of size
t
and
n-2t
isolated vertices. Clearly, there are no nests in
M
.

Let
W=U_{1}\cdots U_{m}
, where each block
U_{h}
is a run in
W
.
Contract each set
\supp(U_{h})
to a single vertex
u_{h}
, keeping all the edges of
M
in place and keeping the order
u_{1}<\cdots<u_{m}
. We denote the obtained ordered graph by
G=G_{X,Y}
.
Observe that
G
is still nest-free and, moreover, consists of
k
separated graphs on vertex sets
N_{w}=\{h:\text{$U_{h}$ is a $w$-run}\}
, one for each letter
w\in[k]
.
The degrees of vertices in
G
are

\deg_{G}(u_{h})=|\supp(U_{h})\cap\supp(X,Y)|

for
h=1,\dots,m
(see Examples
5
and
6
).

From
G
it is straightforward to uniquely reconstruct
(X,Y)
. The uniqueness is a consequence of the fact that we restrict ourselves to canonical twins. We simply scan the vertices

of

and left degrees
\deg_{G}^{(\leftarrow)}(u_{h})

\deg_{G}^{(\rightarrow)}(u_{h})

and the next
\deg_{G}^{(\leftarrow)}(u_{h})
to

But if we are only interested in the length of the twins, we may infer that there is a pair of twins in

of double length equal to

.

This allows us to apply this “graphic” approach also in situations when our goal is to find as large as possible twins in
W
. Then we simply attempt to construct a nest-free ordered graph
G
on vertices
u_{h}
,
h=1,\dots,m
, with no edges joining vertices from different sets

, and with degrees as close as possible to (but not greater than) the lengths of the corresponding runs
U_{h}
. We call
|U_{h}|
the

of vertex
u_{h}
. In particular, if we succeed to create such a graph
G
with all
\deg_{G}(u_{h})=|U_{h}|
, then we know that
W
is a shuffle square and we can produce a pair of perfect twins witnessing this property.

Let us summarize the above discussion in the following statement characterizing shuffle squares in terms of their ordered graph representations.

Proposition 1
.

Let
W
be a
k
-ary word and let
W=U_{1}\cdots U_{m}
be a decomposition of
W
into its runs. Then
W
is a shuffle square if and only if there exists an ordered graph
G
on the set
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
satisfying the following conditions.

1.

If
u_{i}
is joined to
u_{j}
by an edge, then the corresponding runs
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
are
w
-runs for the same letter
w
.

2.

The degree of each vertex
u_{i}
satisfies
\deg_{G}(u_{i})=|U_{i}|
.

3.

G
is nest-free.

Moreover, if there is a graph
G
satisfying properties 1-3, then one can construct (canonical) perfect twins in
W
by assigning the first
\deg_{G}^{(\rightarrow)}(u_{h})
elements of every run
U_{h}
to
X
and the next
\deg_{G}^{(\leftarrow)}(u_{h})
elements to
Y
.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}r_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(a_{0}+a_{1}-r_{1})&\\
r_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(a_{2}+a_{3}-a_{1}-r_{3})&\\
r_{4}=\frac{1}{2}(a_{4}-r_{3}).&\end{array}\right.

So, taking into account the additional constraints
0\leqslant r_{i}\leqslant a_{i}
,
i=0,1,2,3,4
,
the word

W=\mathtt{0}^{a_{0}}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{a_{1}}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{a_{2}}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{a_{3}}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{a_{4}}

is a shuffle square (with the
\mathtt{1}
’s alternating) if there exist integers
r_{1}
and
r_{3}
such that

a_{1}-a_{0}\leqslant r_{1}\leqslant\,a_{1}+a_{0},\;\;r_{1}\equiv\,a_{1}+a_{0}\quad(\!\!\!\!\!\!\mod 2)

and

(3.3)

\max(0,a_{3}-a_{2}-r_{1})\leqslant r_{3}\leqslant\,\min(a_{3}+a_{2}-r_{1},a_{3},a_{4}),\;\;r_{3}\equiv\,a_{3}+a_{2}-r_{1}\quad(\!\!\!\!\!\!\mod 2).

For

, we have

and the constraints simplify even further, while the result becomes stronger, as we may drop the restriction to alternating
\mathtt{1}
’s.

Corollary 1
.

An even binary word

W=\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{a_{1}}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{a_{2}}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{a_{3}}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{a_{4}}

with all
a_{i}\geqslant 0
,
i=1,3,4
, and
a_{2}\geqslant 1
is a shuffle square if and only if

(3.4)

a_{3}+a_{2}-a_{1}\geqslant 0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad a_{4}\geqslant a_{3}-a_{2}-a_{1}.

Proof.

First observe that
W
is a shuffle square with canonical twins of which the first twin takes the first two
\mathtt{1}
’s and the second twin - the last two
\mathtt{1}
’s
if and only if

a_{1}\leqslant a_{3}
and
a_{2}+a_{3}=a_{1}+a_{4}
. Further, this pair of conditions implies (
3.4
). Thus,
W
is a shuffle square if and only if
W
has perfect twins with alternating
\mathtt{1}
’s, which
by preceding discussion, holds if and only if

\max(0,a_{3}-a_{2}-a_{1})\leqslant r_{3}\leqslant\min(a_{3}+a_{2}-a_{1},a_{3},a_{4}),

and

r_{3}\equiv a_{3}+a_{2}-a_{1}(\!\!\!\!\mod 2).

We are going to show that this is equivalent to (
3.4
). The necessary condition is straightforward. Assume now that (
3.4
) holds. If
a_{4}=a_{3}-a_{2}-a_{1}
, then

\max(0,a_{3}-a_{2}-a_{1})=\min(a_{3}+a_{2}-a_{1},a_{3},a_{4})=a_{4}

and

r_{3}=a_{4}\equiv a_{3}+a_{2}-a_{1}(\!\!\!\!\mod 2).

Otherwise, owing to
a_{2}\geqslant 1
, we have

\max(0,a_{3}-a_{2}-a_{1})<\min(a_{3}+a_{2}-a_{1},a_{3},a_{4}),

and so there are at least two possible values for
r_{3}
, allowing for parity adjustment. ∎

We present the proof only in the case when

,

, leaving the remaining cases to the reader.

, we are looking at

with

and

of the same parity, and thus with

. Then, trivially,

is a shuffle square (in fact it is a square

).

For
m\geqslant 2
, we are going to check that the system of equations (
3.2
), with
a_{0}=r_{0}=0
and
r_{1}=a_{1}
, has integer solutions
r_{1},\dots,r_{2m}
with
0\leqslant r_{i}\leqslant a_{i}
. This boils down to choosing the values of variables
r_{2i-1}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, so that
r_{1}=a_{1}
, the quantities

a_{2i}+a_{2i+1}-r_{2i-1}-r_{2i+1},

i=1,\dots,m-1
, as well as
a_{2m}-r_{2m-1}
are all even, and
0\leqslant r_{i}\leqslant a_{i}
for all

.

a_{2i}+a_{2i+1}-r_{2i-1}-r_{2i+1}

, then so is
a_{2m}-r_{2m-1}
. Indeed, recalling that
r_{1}=a_{1}
and that
a_{1}
and
-a_{1}

\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}(a_{2i}+a_{2i+1}-r_{2i-1}-r_{2i+1})\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{2m-1}a_{i}-r_{2m-1}\equiv 0\pmod{2}.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{2m}a_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, we have
a_{2m}-r_{2m-1}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, as desired.

Now, fix
1\leqslant i\leqslant m-1
, assume that
r_{1},\dots,r_{2i-1}
have been chosen properly, and focus on the expression

\rho_{i}:=a_{2i}+a_{2i+1}-r_{2i-1}-r_{2i+1}.

Since
a_{2i},a_{2i+1}\in\{1,2\}
and

, we have

0\leqslant a_{2i}+a_{2i+1}-r_{2i-1}\leqslant 4.

so that
\rho_{i}
is even and nonnegative. Moreover, then

and

. To see the latter, note that if
a_{2i}=1
, then

a_{2i}+a_{2i+1}-r_{2i-1}\leqslant 3,

so

and

, while if
a_{2i}=2
, then
r_{2i}=\rho_{i}/2\leqslant 2
.

Finally, for
i=m-1
, we need to check that also

0\leqslant r_{2m}=\tfrac{1}{2}(a_{2m}-r_{2m-1})\leqslant a_{2m}.

The right-hand side inequality is obvious. As for the left-hand side, observe that by the above proof
r_{2m-1}\leqslant 1
, so
r_{2m-1}\leqslant a_{2m}
.

The proof of our main result, Theorem

Lemma 1
.

Let
H=P\cup C
be an ordered graph on the set of vertices
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, consisting of two vertex disjoint subgraphs: a path
P
with endpoints at
1
and
n
, and a cycle
C
. If
H
is nest-free, then
C
is an
even
cycle.

Proof.

Let a nest-free graph
H
satisfy the assumptions of the lemma and let
E(P)
denote the set of edges of the path
P
. Consider an order relation
\prec
on
E(P)
defined as follows: for each pair of distinct edges
e,f\in E(P)
,
e\prec f
if
\min e\leqslant\min f
and
\max e\leqslant\max f
. Since
P
is nest-free, this relation is a linear order and one can enumerate the edges of
P
accordingly to that order as

E(P)=\{e_{1}\prec e_{2}\prec\cdots\prec e_{p}\}.

Let
\pi(e_{i})\equiv i\pmod{2}
,
i=1,\dots,p:=|E(P)|
, be the parity of edge
e_{i}
.

Let
V(C)=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{q}\}
be the vertex set of the cycle
C
, where the vertices are numbered in the order they are traversed by
C
(we fix one of the two possible directions). We say that a vertex
x
of
C
is
hugged
by an edge
e
of
P
if
\min e<x<\max e
. For each
i=1,\dots,q
, let
X_{i}=\{e\in P:\text{$x_{i}$ is hugged by}\;e\}
and set
r_{i}=|X_{i}|
.

Observe that for each
i=1,\dots,q
the number
r_{i}
is odd, because path
P
, on the way from 1 to
n
, possibly zigzagging, “passes” each vertex of
C
an odd number of times. Moreover, by the definition of hugging, each set
X_{i}
consists of a block of consecutive edges of
P
under
\prec
. Finally, by the nest-freeness of
H
, for each
i=1,\dots,q-1
, the sets
X_{i}
and
X_{i+1}
are disjoint and positioned “side-to-side”, that is,
X_{i+1}
either directly follows or directly precedes
X_{i}
.

Thus, we may define a 2-coloring of

by assigning to

,

, color
\pi(e)
, where
e
is the first (or the last) edge in

. Clearly, adjacent vertices on

5.
Deletion distance to shuffle squares

Given a word
W
of length
n
, let
f(W)
be the length of the longest twins in
W
and set

g(W)=n-2f(W).

Further, let
g(n)=\max g(W)
over all
W
, with
|W|=n
. By a sophisticated approach via a regularity lemma for words, Axenovich, Person, and Puzynina
[
3
]
showed that
g(n)=o(n)
and, by providing a construction, that
g(n)=\Omega(\log n)
. The lower bound was improved by Bukh
[
6
]
to
g(n)=\Omega(n^{1/3})
by refining the construction from
[
3
]
(see
[
4
]
for a proof). In a personal communication Bukh has expressed his belief that, in fact,
g(n)=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
and that the real challenge would be to improve the upper bound, where one would need to refine the regularity approach from
[
3
]
. Focusing on the lower bound, in
[
4
]
we shared that belief and implicitly stated Conjecture
1
which, in our notation says that
g(n)=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
.

We first describe a general class of words whose two particular instances yielded lower bounds on
g(n)
in
[
3
]
and
[
6
]
. For positive integers
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}
, let

W(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})=U_{1}\cdots U_{r}

be the binary word whose consecutive runs
U_{i}
have lengths
|U_{i}|=n_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,r
. We adopt the convention that
U_{1}
is a
\mathtt{1}
-run. The main features of the words used in
[
3
]
and
[
6
]
were that the runs rapidly decreased in length and that their lengths were odd.

An easy generalization of these two special words led to an observation that if

n_{1}>n_{2}>\cdots>n_{r}

are all odd, then

(5.1)

g(W(n_{1},\dots,n_{r}))\geqslant\min\{r,\delta\},

where
\delta
is the smallest difference between two consecutive terms (see
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
). The idea of the proof is that if monotone twins take equally from each run, then they “lose” at least one element per run; otherwise, the leading twin is ahead at some point and the follower has to “jump” over the second next run, a loss that can never be made up, due to the run lengths getting smaller and smaller.

The particular words used in
[
3
]
and
[
6
]
were

A_{r}:=W(3^{r-1},\,3^{r-2},\,\dots,\,3^{2},\,3,\,1)

and

B_{r}:=W(r^{2}+1,\,(r-1)r+1,\,\dots,\,2r+1,\,r+1),

respectively. For both, the minimum in (
5.1
) is
r
. Simple
calculations reveal that for
A_{r}
we have
r=\Theta(\log|A_{r}|)
and for
B_{r}
,
r=\Theta(|B_{r}|^{1/3})
, both leading to the desired bounds. (For
n
not expressible as
|A_{r}|
or
|B_{r}|
for any
r
an obvious extrapolation has been applied.)

In view of the constructions from
[
3
]
and
[
6
]
a natural candidate to
facilitate Conjecture
1
seemed to be, for an odd integer
m
and

, a binary word

consisting of
r

consecutive
odd numbers. Indeed,

for
r=\Theta(m)
and thus, showing that
g(O_{m,r})=\Omega(m)
would do the job. However, inequality (
5.1
) yields only

, so a new approach would be needed.

6.
Open Problems

We conclude the paper with some directions and open problem for future consideration.

6.1.
Doubly binary words

Call a binary word
W=U_{1}\cdots U_{m}

doubly binary
if the lengths of runs,
|U_{1}|,\dots,|U_{m}|
, also form a binary sequence (over alphabet
\{1,2\}
). There exist many examples of such words, one notable instance being the famous
Thue-Morse word
,

T=\mathtt{01101001100101101001011001101001}...,

which is an infinite concatenation of two blocks,
A=\mathtt{0110}
and
B=\mathtt{1001}
, in the order designated by the sequence
T
itself, that is, by the sequence

ABBABAABBAABABBA...\ .

Note that the only prefixes of
T
which are even words are those of length
4k
for
k\geq 1
.
It turns out that for
k\geqslant 3
they are actually shuffle squares. We leave an easy proof to the interested reader, as a kind of exercise in applying the graphic method introduced in this paper. (As a hint: use induction on
k
and consider two subcases w/r to whether the
k
-th block is identical to the
(k-1)
-st block, or not.)

Another self-defining doubly binary sequence is the mysterious Kolakoski word (
[
21
]
, see also "Sequence A000002” in OEIS) which is equal to the sequence of the lengths of its runs:

K=\mathtt{122112122122112112212112122112112...}

The prefixes of
K
which are even words are rather rare and finding shuffle squares among them may be more challenging.
The first two even prefixes of
K
are

\mathtt{12211212}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathtt{1221121221221121},

and neither is a shuffle square (the largest twins in either prefix leave out each just two gaps).

Question 1
.

Is there at least one prefix of the Kolakoski sequence which is a shuffle square?

The most ambitious goal in this direction is the following.

Problem 1
.

Characterize all doubly binary even words which are
not
shuffle squares.

6.2.
Deletion distance to shuffle squares

It would be nice to know more about the maximum deletion distance of a binary word of length
n
to a shuffle square, which we denoted by
g(n)
. One natural try to get close to the conjectured value of
g(n)=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
failed, as demonstrated in Proposition
6
, but this does not mean that Conjecture
1
is false. In search for better constructions, one should perhaps allow varying differences between consecutive, still odd, terms.

A closely related is the following intriguing conjecture, mentioned in the introduction, posed by He, Huang, Nam, and Thaper in
[
19
]
.

Conjecture 2
.

Almost all even binary words are shuffle squares.

This means that the proportion of shuffle squares among all even binary words of length
n
tends to one with
n
tending to infinity. This conjecture implies that a random binary word can be made a shuffle square by deleting at most
two
letters. Indeed, for
n
even, construct a bijection between
even
and
non-even
words by flipping the last letter; for
n
odd, construct a bijection between

all
words of length
n
and
even
words of length
n+1
by adding an appropriate letter at the end. (This was already observed in
[
12
]
for
n
even, and in
[
19
]
for all
n
, but with distance at most three rather than just two.)

6.3.
Cutting distance to shuffle squares

Let
c(W)
be the minimum integer
c
such that there exists a partition
W=B_{1}\cdots B_{c+1}
into consecutive blocks and a permutation
\pi
of
[c+1]
for which
B_{\pi(1)}\cdots B_{\pi(c+1)}
is a shuffle square. In other words,
c(W)
is the minimum number of cuts applied to
W
after which it is possible to reassemble the resulting blocks to form a shuffle square. In particular,
c(W)=1
means that a shuffle square can be obtained from
W
by a cyclic permutation of its letters.

For instance, as discussed before (see Remark
1
after Proposition
2
), for every even binary word
W
with at most four runs we have
c(W)\leq 1
. On the other hand, as checked by a computer, the word

P=\mathtt{111110110000111100010000}=\mathtt{1}^{5}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}^{2}\mathtt{0}^{4}\mathtt{1}^{4}\mathtt{0}^{3}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{4}

is not a shuffle square, and the same is true for any of its cyclic permutations. However, it can be cut into three pieces,

\mathtt{111\;|\;1101100001111000\;|\;10000},

which may be rearranged to give a shuffle square:

\mathtt{1101100001111000\;|\;111\;|\;10000}=\mathtt{1}^{2}\mathtt{0}\mathtt{1}^{2}\mathtt{0}^{4}\mathtt{1}^{4}\mathtt{0}^{3}\mathtt{1}^{4}\mathtt{0}^{4}.

Thus,
c(P)=2
for the word
P
above. Is it true that two cuts are always sufficient for even binary words?

Conjecture 3
.

Every even binary word
W
satisfies
c(W)\leqslant 2
.

Let us remark that at present we do not even have a proof that
c(W)\leq C
for some constant
C
.
Also, Conjecture
3
does not hold for ternary words, as one may easily check that

c(\mathtt{122231113332})=3.

We dare, however, to formulate the following generalization.

Conjecture 4
.

For each
k\geqslant 2
, every even
k
-ary word
W
satisfies
c(W)\leqslant k
.

6.4.
Reverse shuffle squares

D. Henshall, N. Rampersad, J. Shallit in
[
20
]
defined also the notion of a
reverse shuffle square
, that is, a word which can be split into two subwords which are
reverses
of each other (cf.
[
19
]
). For instance,

\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{10}}}\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{011}}}\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{01}}}\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{0}}}\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{1}}}\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathtt{01}}}\underline{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathtt{0}}}

is a reverse shuffle square, consisting of two copies of
\mathtt{100110}
, one straight and one reverse.

We believe that a suitable modification of Proposition
1
will provide a strong tool to study the existence of and the deletion distance from reverse shuffle squares. Specifically, for binary
words it seems that condition 3 (nestlessness) should be replaced by
forbidding alignments, that is, pairs of edges
e,f
with
\max e<\min f
.

Finally, in the context of Conjecture
3
, one can easily deduce from the famous Necklace Splitting Theorem (see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
16
]
) that every even binary word is at cutting distance at most two from a
reverse shuffle square
.
