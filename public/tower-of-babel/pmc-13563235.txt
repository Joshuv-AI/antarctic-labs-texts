Impact of alcohol use on economy of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations member states in 2000–2023: a structural equation modelling analysis
Authors: Jürgen Rehm, Daniela Correia, Pol Rovira, Gianna Gayle H Amul, Sawitri Assanangkornchai, Surasak Chaiyasong, Ahmed S Hassan, Kyaw Ko Ko Htet, Jiraluck Nontarak, Kevin D Shield, Monsicha Sittisombut, Polathep Vichitkunakorn, Nyi Nyi Zayar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundEconomic growth in low- and middle-income countries is usually associated with reductions in mortality; however, it also correlates with increased alcohol use. This rise contributes to mortality and other disease burden, potentially hindering further economic growth. We quantified these associations for the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) countries for the years 2000–2023.MethodsStructural equation modelling (SEM) was used to analyse time-series data for nine ASEAN countries with full data on economic output (Gross Domestic Product [GDP] and Gross National Income per capita at purchasing power parity [PPP]), disease burden (mortality and disability-adjusted life years [DALYs]), and alcohol exposure indicators for adults aged 15 to 65. All data were obtained from publicly available international agency sources.FindingsSEM results indicate a positive association between alcohol use indicators and disease burden, which was, in turn, negatively associated with economic output. In 2021, for adults aged 15–65, alcohol-attributable mortality amounted to 5% of all-cause mortality in the region with three countries exceeding 10%; proportions for DALYs were similar. In the main model, the point estimates of potential impact on GDP PPP per capita were as high as 4%. The results remained highly consistent, irrespective of the indicators used.InterpretationAlcohol consumption in ASEAN countries over the study period hindered the ability to reap the full benefits of economic growth.FundingCentre for Alcohol Studies, Prince of Songkla University, Thailand, CAS-A1-67-002; WHO Western Pacific Region (2024/1505643-0; PO 203515367); Canadian Institutes of Health Research (FRN 181677, and FRN 203773).Keywords: Alcohol per capita consumption, Heavy episodic drinking, Gross domestic product at purchasing power parity, All-cause mortality, Disability-adjusted life years

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Systematic searches of Ovid Embase and MEDLINE were conducted on November 18, 2025, to identify reviews and primary research studies on the interrelationships between alcohol consumption and economic transition in low- and middle-income countries. The searches were not limited by language or date, and included MeSH/Emtree and truncated terms related to each concept, such as alcohol consumption, drinking, low- or middle-income country, economic development and transition (see Supplementary material 1 for the exact search strategy and results). Although no quantitative reviews were identified, one narrative review suggested that an increased and industrialised alcohol supply, though beneficial in some respects (e.g., revenue), can hinder economic development via unemployment and an increased disease burden. Primary research indicates that economic development increases consumption, which subsequently reduces life expectancy and increases alcohol-attributable deaths and disability-adjusted life years. Finally, a modelling study published after the systematic search estimated the macroeconomic impact of tax-driven price increases.

Added value of this study

This study is a longitudinal analysis that empirically estimates the macroeconomic consequences of alcohol consumption through health pathways in low- and middle-income countries, focusing specifically on the ASEAN region. Using structural equation modelling (SEM) applied to multi-country time-series data (2000–2023), we simultaneously estimate these complex interrelationships by including time lags, thus moving beyond narrative or cross-sectional analyses. To our knowledge, this is the first empirical quantification of the association between alcohol consumption levels and economic output; our results indicate a negative impact on economic output for low- and middle-income countries. For the highest-consuming nations, Lao PDR, and Viet Nam, the potential impact reached a 4.1% decrease in GDP PPP per capita, levels higher than the OECD findings for high-income countries with similar consumption. Heavy episodic drinking occasions appear to mediate these pathways. While the association was estimated for all nine countries with available data, the economic impact was lower in Muslim-majority countries and those with effective alcohol control policies.

Implications of all the available evidence

The observed association between alcohol consumption, increased health burden, and reduced economic output suggests that weak regulation may hinder countries from fully realising the benefits of economic growth. Policies such as alcohol taxation, minimum pricing, restrictions on availability and marketing, and measures targeting heavy episodic drinking can yield economic returns by reducing premature mortality and disability among the working-age population.

Introduction

Economic transition from low- to upper middle-income countries is often accompanied by increases in alcohol consumption.1,2 This is driven in part by increased affordability associated with rising purchasing power and by the intensified activities of transnational alcohol companies seeking new sources of growth and profit.3 The alcohol industry claims that even if this increase in consumption may have some negative consequences for people who develop alcohol use disorder, it stimulates the economic transition via the creation of jobs, supports other industries (such as the hospitality industry), and generates tax revenue.4 However, increased alcohol consumption also comes with attributable harms and costs,1,2 such as increased spending in the healthcare and law enforcement sectors, and productivity losses via premature mortality, absenteeism, and presenteeism.5,6

Based on Deaton’s theory,7 Rehm and colleagues developed a conceptual framework to examine the complex interactions between alcohol use and economic transition.8 Their model suggests a dual effect: while economic transition generally improves life expectancy and increases alcohol consumption, the resulting rise in alcohol use ultimately drives up mortality rates, hindering further economic growth (for a detailed overview of the framework, see Supplementary material 2).

To empirically assess these relationships, we formed a research partnership—comprising members from all Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) member states—to study the impact of alcohol consumption and alcohol control policies on economic development and health (study protocol:9). ASEAN member states were selected because they have experienced substantial economic growth since 2000, which was associated—as predicted by Deaton7—with substantial health gains. On average, between 2000 and 2023, these economies, measured by the Gross Domestic Product per capita at purchasing power parity (GDP PPP), grew at an annual rate of 6% (country GDP PPP per capita increased between 5% and 8%; see Supplementary Table 6.1).10 This growth, coupled with an annual increase in life expectancy of 0.3%,11 showed enough variability to make this region an ideal testing ground for the study.

This publication seeks to quantify the potential association between alcohol consumption and economic output in ASEAN member states, as mediated through health consequences (i.e., premature mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost). Both indicators are important, as alcohol use has become a significant risk factor for the region, with 3% of all-cause mortality (females: 1%; males: 4%) and 3% of DALYs lost (females: 1%; males: 4%) being attributable to alcohol. In some countries, attributable fractions for disease burden in males were as high as 10% (Thailand and Viet Nam; for exact values with 95% CIs, see Supplementary material 3). Alcohol-attributable costs amounted to 2.6% (95% CI 2.0–3.1%) of GDP in the latest meta-analysis, which included studies from middle-income countries (including two studies from Thailand).5

The high impact of alcohol use on disease burden has led to efforts to introduce alcohol control policies in several ASEAN member states,12 which were met with opposition from economic operators who rely predominantly on economic arguments (see above and4).

This manuscript attempts to empirically quantify the impact of alcohol on economic output in low- and middle-income countries (for high-income countries, see13), which is the main objective of this study. Furthermore, a recent modelling study14 on the macroeconomic impact of decreased consumption based on higher alcohol excise taxation found substantial increases in GDP PPP.

Methods

Comparable data were collected for all ASEAN countries, except Brunei Darussalam, from agencies within the United Nations, such as the World Bank (WB),10,11 or the WHO (for all data sources and detailed considerations of data quality, see Supplementary material 4). Brunei Darussalam was excluded because its adult alcohol per capita consumption (APC) data were not made available over the past 10 years; thus, the WHO APC values remained the same for these years.

Two key economic outputs modelled were sourced from the WB: GDP PPP per capita (in 1000 International $; indicator for the main model) and Gross National Income (GNI) PPP per capita (in 1000 International $); and the two key burden indicators age-standardised mortality (main model) and DALY loss rates per 100,000 were taken from the WHO. These key indicators can be considered to be of the highest quality and comparable. However, for other potential determinants of economic output, such as healthcare efficiency or culture, comparable indicators for all countries and years are more difficult to find (Supplementary material 4).

Annual data from 2000 to 2023 were compiled (2000–2021 for the predictor variables, and 2000–2023 for the economic output variables). Additionally, the above-described ASEAN project collected additional surveys for each country, which were integrated into the WHO estimates (for methodology, see15).

To quantify the impact of alcohol use on economic output via its detrimental health consequences, we applied structural equation modelling (SEM). Three interconnected equations represented the hypothesised pathways (Fig. 1; the formulas are detailed in Supplementary material 5).

Conceptual model on relations between alcohol exposure, disease burden and economic output tested. Disease burden variables: Age-standardised mortality rates per 100,000. Age-standardised DALY loss rates per 100,000. Economic output variables: GDP PPP per capita. GNI per capita.

The conceptual model for this study, shown in Fig. 1, examines the longitudinal pathways between alcohol consumption, disease burden, and economic output across ASEAN countries. The model specifically tests how the level of alcohol consumption at time T leads to heavy episodic drinking (HED), thereby indirectly affecting the national disease burden and impacting economic output two years later at time T + 2. While we acknowledge that cultural norms, alcohol availability, and environmental regulations are significant determinants of drinking behaviour,16 these variables were omitted to ensure model parsimony and data consistency across the diverse ASEAN region. Instead, education is used as the primary distal determinant, serving as a robust proxy for socio-economic status and health literacy. Furthermore, the model accounts for exogenous volatility by incorporating three major natural disasters—the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami, the 2008 Cyclone Nargis, and the 2009 Pacific typhoon season—which substantially increased mortality in ASEAN countries, along with the COVID-19 pandemic as a critical stressor on health and economic systems.

Both economic outputs were log-transformed based on their statistical properties. The explanatory variables included logarithms of age-standardised mortality and DALY rates among individuals aged 15–65 years, two dummy variables for the COVID-19 pandemic (one concurrent for the year 2020, and a lagged variable to capture the two-year economic rebound), and the average number of years of schooling in the adult population.17 The age range for the disease burden variables was selected to represent the population potentially participating in the formal economy.

Alcohol use indirectly affects economic output via disease burden with disease-specific lag times,18 ranging from immediate impact (injury) to lag times over 10 years (cancer). The correct overall lag time for our model is difficult to estimate a priori, but should be approximately 1–3 years, given the relatively young age groups of the formal economy. We used a 2-year lag time for the primary analysis and conducted sensitivity analyses for 1- and 3-year lag times (see Supplementary material 9 for the specification and results of sensitivity analyses).

In the second equation regarding disease burden, the logarithm of age-standardised mortality per 100,000 population (ages 15–65) was directly predicted by (1) the population prevalence of HED for that age group (for background on selection of the alcohol indicators, see19); (2) major natural disasters; and (3) the COVID-19 pandemic as measured in the respective countries.20

The third equation models the percentage of heavy episodic drinkers as a function of APC and the average years of schooling in each country (see background:21).

As indicated above, the ASEAN region is characterised by diverse policy and religious environments with respect to alcohol use. We therefore conducted sensitivity analyses on different groups of countries, based on a prior study classifying ASEAN countries into three groups with respect to alcohol use.22 This study distinguished three clusters: Muslim-majority countries with very low levels of alcohol consumption overall, irrespective of formal regulations, which ranged from a complete ban to tolerance for non-Muslim residents, countries with relatively strong alcohol policies (Philippines, Singapore, Thailand) over the past two decades, and countries with relatively weak alcohol policies (Cambodia, Lao PDR, Myanmar, Viet Nam).

The potential impact at the country level was estimated based on our main model as specified above, with GDP PPP per capita representing economic output and age-standardised mortality rates representing the disease burden. This deterministic scenario modelling operates at the country level using aggregate indicators. Country-level heterogeneity was captured through random intercepts, and no stratification by sex or age was applied. To estimate this impact, we derived three indicators by incorporating different values for the exposure variables: Model 1 assumed no alcohol consumption in 2021 (setting both APC and HED to 0) to estimate the overall impact of alcohol exposure for that year; Model 2 simulated a small alcohol policy intervention by decreasing the 2021 APC by 15%; and Model 3, representing a larger policy intervention directed at decreasing patterns of drinking (such as availability restrictions), assumed a 10-percentage-point decrease in 2021 HED. All three scenarios assume linearity of associations, the applicability of these associations beyond the observed data range, and representativeness of 2021 exposure values as a baseline. All models were estimated via SEM and quantified the difference in 2023 GDP PPP per capita between actual alcohol exposure and the respective modelled scenarios. However, it must be acknowledged that statistical power was determined for the overall model and not for the potential country-level impact; formal uncertainty intervals around scenario predictions would be wide given the small number of observations used for extrapolation (n = 9). Consequently, results should be interpreted as scenario estimates rather than exact outcomes.

Results

As indicated above, economic output increased almost monotonically from 2000 to 2023, with temporary declines observed during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021) and the global financial crisis (2008–2009); the latter mostly affected Singapore, Malaysia, and Thailand. On average, both GDP PPP per capita and GNI PPP per capita increased by around 6% annually (see Supplementary material 6 for trends in both economic outputs across all nine countries individually and combined).

Supplementary material 7 provides an overview of trends for the two health burden variables—mortality and DALY loss rates—in table and figure format. In short, as predicted by the increasing economic output, all-cause health burdens decreased over the observation period until the COVID-19 pandemic hit the region (most strongly in 2021 for most countries), with a more pronounced decrease in poorer countries.2,7 However, there were exceptions for the following middle-income countries: the Philippines, a lower middle-income country throughout the period, showed relatively stable mortality and DALY loss rates. A similar pattern applied to Malaysia, which remained an upper middle-income country for the duration of the observation period (for a visualisation of trends for all countries see Supplementary Fig. 7.1 and 7.2).

Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 8 show the trends for alcohol exposure variables. With respect to the APC among adults up to 65 years, as with APC in general,22 three clusters of countries can be distinguished: the Muslim-majority countries (Indonesia, Malaysia), where consumption stayed below one litre APC for the entire period, countries with relatively higher economic output, where implementation of strong alcohol control policies led to relative stability, especially over the past decade (Philippines, Singapore, Thailand), and countries with relatively lower economic output, where APC increased quite markedly, at least until the COVID-19 pandemic (Cambodia, Lao PDR, Myanmar, Viet Nam; see Fig. 2 for an overview and Supplementary material 7 for details).

Alcohol per capita consumption (APC) for ages 15–65 in ASEAN countries in 2021 (a) and the absolute change in APC between 2000 and 2021 (b).

The results of the structural equation models (Table 1) are highly consistent: regardless of the chosen indicators for health burden or economic output, alcohol use was negatively associated with economic output. Specifically, a higher APC generates a higher prevalence of HED in the population as well as a higher health burden, and all are associated with lower economic output. All covariates in the SEM were statistically significant in the predicted direction. The COVID-19 pandemic had a concurrent negative association with economic output and a positive association with mortality; for economic output, there was a significant rebound following the primary COVID-19 period. Education was positively associated with economic output, and negatively associated with health burden (irrespective of the indicators used). Finally, natural disasters had a significant negative impact on mortality and the burden of disease (see Table 1 and Supplementary Table 9).

The sensitivity analyses confirmed our choice of time lag length. Also, the results remained stable for all sensitivity analyses (Supplementary material 10). Excluding the two Muslim-majority countries led to a slightly larger impact of mortality on GDP PPP per capita. When basing the results of the two groups with stronger and relatively weaker alcohol policies, the effect sizes were about the same, but the effects were no longer significant. This loss of significance most likely reflects the substantially reduced statistical power, rather than a weaker underlying association. Overall, the sensitivity analyses are consistent with the main results.

Table 2 provides an overview of the indicators for potential impact of alcohol on GDP PPP per capita. Overall, as per Model 1, GDP PPP could have been more than 4% higher for the two countries with the highest level of alcohol exposure as measured by APC (Lao PDR and Viet Nam), and more than 2% higher for those with the next level of alcohol exposure (Cambodia, Philippines, Thailand). Surprisingly, Singapore would also experience a large increase in GDP PPP per capita by reduction of its high rate of HED, primarily due to its high rate of HED. Myanmar—which has limited exposure likely due to its comparatively low economic output and ongoing civil unrest—and the two Muslim-majority countries clearly showed the lowest impact on economic output.

Discussion

Alcohol use significantly burdens the ASEAN region, with 5% of all-cause mortality and 4% of DALYs lost attributable to this risk factor. This disease burden was found to be negatively associated with subsequent economic output, independent of the chosen indicators. Our findings show a clear negative trajectory for economic growth driven by alcohol consumption: as APC rises, it increases the prevalence of HED, which subsequently intensifies the national disease burden and ultimately reduces economic output. Over the past two decades in the examined ASEAN countries, rising alcohol consumption resulted in economic losses; for 2023, this amounted to a loss of GDP PPP per capita of up to 4.1% in countries with high levels of alcohol consumption, such as Viet Nam and Lao PDR.

However, in the region, there is high variability in the level of alcohol consumption and, thus, in the potential impact on disease burden and the economy. On the one hand, the two Muslim-majority countries, Indonesia and Malaysia, had APC values under one litre per year in each of the last 20 years, an attributable burden between 1% and 3% in 2021, and subsequently smaller negative associations with economic growth. On the other hand, countries like Lao PDR and Viet Nam increased their consumption markedly over the past two decades, with attributable disease burden between 9% and 12% and higher negative associations with economic growth.

Before discussing these results further, we highlight the strengths and limitations of the presented analyses. First, this study uses comparable international data from the WHO, the WB, and other United Nations agencies over a period of more than two decades, with WHO data being complemented by representative surveys integrated into the WHO data bank.15 Second, the ASEAN region is an ideal setting to examine the relationships between alcohol consumption indicators, attributable mortality in the working population, and economic output; this is due to the region’s characteristic stable economic growth and improving health outcomes across all countries. Third, the structural equation models demonstrated acceptable goodness-of-fit values overall, as evidenced by non-significant Fisher C values.

Several limitations must be taken into account. First, we cannot definitively establish causality based on our analysis of ecological trend data. However, given that the current analyses are well controlled, span a long period of time, and comprise nine countries where potential alternative explanations to fit all results would be difficult to identify, they provide strong indications of underlying causal relationships.23,24 Second, the use of comparable data, while a strength, also limited the variables included in our model, e.g., there are no comparable annual indicators for other potential influencing factors available for the entire ASEAN region.22 Third, our analyses show average effects drawn from all the country data, and therefore may not accurately describe the situation in individual countries. The variability of GDP PPP per capita is a case in point, fluctuating widely over a 20-year period, from 900 Int$ (Myanmar in the year 2000) to 141,900 Int$ (Singapore in 2022). While we attempted to mitigate this by log-transforming the main economic outputs to improve normality, the issue of high variability in many indicators persisted. Consequently, the predicted potential impact indicators can only be seen as indicative. While we had enough power to establish overall associations (Supplementary material 11), we lacked sufficient power to establish country-level parameters with small uncertainty intervals. Fourth, the current analyses only tested linear relationships, while some prior results (for high-income countries) indicated that the relationship between alcohol consumption and economic strength may be curvilinear: the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)13 estimated that alcohol consumption exceeding the 1/1.5 drinks per day threshold for females/males, respectively, led to an average 1.6% reduction in GDP in OECD countries due to alcohol-attributable diseases. Most non-Muslim ASEAN countries exceeded this threshold. Finally, our results provide insights into some of the negative impacts of alcohol consumption on economic output and growth. However, they neither estimate the full negative impact on the economy—which would include social impacts such as alcohol-related legal costs or productivity losses due to absenteeism or presenteeism5—nor do they take into consideration the industry’s claims on the positive impact of alcohol production and use on these outcomes via job creation or on other industries.6,25 Such comprehensive modelling to identify overall economic net benefits or net costs is yet to be undertaken, and is clearly necessary to establish a clear picture of the economic role of alcohol and associated industries. It is also necessary to reconsider the subsidies and support given to the alcohol industry by the World Bank and other international organisations.26 Consequently, such explorations of net economic effect of alcohol use in countries should be considered an important priority for future research.

Despite these limitations, we can already draw some conclusions. Alcohol consumption in ASEAN countries over the study period has been associated with slower economic growth. Thus, higher APC and HED were positively associated with higher mortality and disease burden during adulthood up to 65 years of age; these disease burden indicators, in turn, were negatively associated with economic output indicators, such as GDP PPP per capita. In other studies, alcohol-attributable premature mortality and burden of disease also caused reductions in life expectancy.2 Consequently, alcohol consumption prevented these countries from reaping the full benefits of economic growth (see also8). To counter these trends, governments should implement effective alcohol control policies. The effect of such policies can be seen in countries such as Singapore, where the level of alcohol consumption could be kept under 3 L APC.12,22

Recent evidence suggests three cost-effective measures16,27: increasing excise taxation, restricting availability (i.e., reducing purchasing hours without allowing customers to circumvent them via online sales), and banning all media marketing (for cost-effectiveness considerations, see28). To remain effective, these policies must be paired with measures to prevent the growth of the informal/unrecorded market (for proven effective measures, see29). Success stories in Thailand after 2008,27 or in the Philippines after taxation reform,30 demonstrate that rising levels of consumption can be halted via effective implementation of strong alcohol control policies. However, while the successful implementation of such policies stopped further increases in population drinking levels, the current levels in these two countries are still high, potentially having led to decreases in GDP PPP of between 2% and 2.5%. Consequently, new alcohol control policies should be considered in these countries.

However, the biggest changes seem necessary in Viet Nam and Lao PDR, the two countries which are now in the highest quartile with respect to alcohol consumption.31 While the most recent years saw some implementation of alcohol policy,12 much more is required. Both Thailand and the Philippines have inaugurated successful taxation reforms, including increases in tax rates,30,32 demonstrating that taxation can be crucial for reducing the level of consumption and heavy drinking, thus reducing the disease burden. In addition, availability restrictions have been shown to work in Thailand (as a key part of a policy package;27). While economic operators will oppose these measures, there is no strong empirical evidence that alcohol policy implementation ever hindered economic growth.
