A Myeloid-Specific Endogenous TRP1-CAR Knock-in Mouse Model for Melanoma-Targeted Immunotherapy
Authors: Yuxin Hao, Xin Chen, Min Wang, Jinxiang Han, Jihong Pan, Luo Zhang, Xiushan Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalignant melanoma is highly aggressive and prone to therapeutic resistance. Chimeric antigen receptor macrophage (CAR-M) therapy is promising for solid tumors, but conventional ex vivo-engineered CAR-M has limited in vivo persistence, complicating longer-term safety evaluation. This study established a myeloid-specific, endogenously expressed tyrosinase-related protein 1 (TRP1)-CAR mouse model to assess antitumor activity and safety.MethodsTRP1-CAR was conditionally knocked into the Rosa26 locus and crossed with Lysozyme 2 (Lyz2)-Cre mice to generate mice with myeloid-specific TRP1-CAR expression (TRP1-CARM). CAR signals and baseline observations were assessed by flow cytometry, tissue immunofluorescence, histopathology, and hematology. Phagocytosis, cytotoxicity, and inflammation-related gene expression were evaluated in vitro. A syngeneic B16-Fluc-GFP melanoma model was used in two independent cohorts to assess tumor growth, survival, intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and histopathology.ResultsCAR signals were detected in peripheral blood and in F4/80-positive macrophage-containing areas of the lung, liver, and spleen. No overt histopathological abnormalities or hematological changes were observed in the tissues examined under baseline conditions. TRP1-CARM-BMDMs showed enhanced phagocytic and cytotoxic activity against B16-Fluc-GFP cells, accompanied by altered inflammation-related gene expression in the co-culture system. In vivo, TRP1-CARM mice showed delayed tumor growth (P < 0.0001) and prolonged survival (P = 0.0005), with increased intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration.ConclusionThis myeloid-specific TRP1-CAR knock-in model showed in vitro antitumor activity and delayed melanoma progression in vivo. It provides a proof-of-concept framework for investigating antitumor activity and preliminary safety observations associated with sustained endogenous CAR expression.Keywords: chimeric antigen receptor, melanoma, macrophages, tyrosinase-related protein 1, gene-edited mice

Introduction

Melanoma is one of the most aggressive cutaneous malignancies. Its pronounced propensity for metastasis, together with intrinsic or acquired resistance to available systemic therapies, including dacarbazine-based chemotherapy1 and BRAF/MEK-targeted therapy,2 has left the 5-year overall survival rate for patients with stage IV melanoma below 25%.3,4 Although immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) targeting the programmed death-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) pathway5 and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4)6 has substantially improved clinical outcomes in a subset of patients, 40–60% still develop primary or acquired resistance, which may be driven by T-cell exhaustion, loss of tumor antigens, and remodeling of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.7 For instance, the KEYNOTE-006 trial8 reported an objective response rate (ORR) of only 33–37% with PD-1 monotherapy, underscoring the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies.

Adoptive cellular immunotherapy has recently offered new avenues for the treatment of malignant tumors. Among these approaches, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy involves the ex vivo expansion of autologous tumor-reactive T cells with intrinsic tumor antigen recognition capacity. It has shown distinct potential in addressing tumor heterogeneity and antigen escape and received FDA approval for advanced melanoma in 2024.9,10 However, the clinical application of TIL therapy is highly dependent on the availability of sufficient, high-quality resected tumor tissue, and the functionality of infused cells is often constrained by immunosuppressive signals within the tumor microenvironment.11,12 Meanwhile, chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has achieved landmark success in hematologic malignancies,13,14 but its clinical benefit in solid tumors remains substantially limited by insufficient tumor infiltration and tumor microenvironment-induced effector cell dysfunction.15,16 In addition, emerging chimeric antigen receptor natural killer (CAR-NK) cell therapy, despite its potential for “off-the-shelf” application and favorable safety profile, is likewise constrained by poor infiltration efficiency and inadequate microenvironmental adaptability in solid tumors.17,18

The collective limitations of these therapies underscore the need for a cellular therapy platform capable of efficiently infiltrating solid tumors and sustaining effector function within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Macrophages, as key innate immune effector cells, possess intrinsic tumor-homing properties and robust solid tumor infiltration capacity, making them a promising emerging platform for CAR engineering.19,20 CAR-M can directly eliminate tumor cells through CAR-mediated phagocytosis21,22 and promote CD8+ T-cell infiltration,22,23 thereby compensating for the limited solid tumor infiltration inherent to T-cell-based effector therapies.24,25 Current CAR-M approaches primarily rely on ex vivo viral transduction followed by adoptive transfer, with adenoviral and lentiviral vectors widely applied and shown to confer antitumor activity against targets such as human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) and CD19.21,26–29 Klichinsky et al further engineered anti-HER2 CAR-M using the chimeric adenoviral vector Ad5f35 and demonstrated antigen-specific phagocytosis and tumor control in a HER2-positive ovarian cancer model, laying the groundwork for the clinical translation of CAR-M.29

An ideal CAR target antigen should exhibit sufficient expression on tumor cells to enable effective recognition by effector cells, while showing relatively restricted expression in key normal tissues, thereby maximizing the therapeutic window and minimizing the risk of on-target/off-tumor toxicity. TRP1 is a melanocyte lineage-associated transmembrane glycoprotein localized predominantly to intracellular compartments such as melanosomes, where it participates in melanin biosynthesis; it is therefore primarily an intracellularly localized antigen. Owing to vesicular trafficking and membrane recycling, only a small fraction of TRP1 molecules is dynamically exposed on the cell surface, resulting in relatively limited surface epitopes available for CAR recognition and potentially insufficient CAR engagement on cells with low expression levels.30 From this perspective, TRP1 may be considered a CAR target antigen that is particularly sensitive to expression thresholds, whereby effective recognition is more likely to be achieved only when tumor cells express TRP1 at high levels. Clinical sample analyses have shown that TRP1 is positive in over 95% of primary melanoma lesions, with expression retained in approximately 40–45% of metastatic lesions,31 supporting its relevance as a melanoma-associated antigen and its potential as a target for TRP1-directed immunotherapeutic approaches. Furthermore, in normal tissues, TRP1 expression is largely restricted to melanocytes and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE),32,33 with no high-level expression detected in most other key organs. A prior clinical trial of the anti-TRP1 monoclonal antibody IMC-20D7S demonstrated a degree of disease control activity in relapsed/refractory melanoma, without dose-limiting toxicities or serious adverse events,31 providing preliminary clinical evidence for the feasibility and safety of TRP1 as an antigen target and offering important support for the design of the TRP1-directed CAR-M in this study.

Despite the promising antitumor potential of CAR-M therapy, current experimental systems exhibit notable shortcomings in long-term safety evaluation. Existing models based on ex vivo viral engineering followed by adoptive transfer can validate the antitumor activity of CAR-M, but are constrained by the number of infused cells, their in vivo persistence, and tissue distribution,34,35 limiting their ability to reflect sustained CAR expression in vivo or to enable chronic on-target/off-tumor toxicity assessment for targets such as TRP1 that are also physiologically expressed in melanocytes, including those of the hair follicle, and in the retinal pigment epithelium. A previous study reported that F4/80-targeted lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivery of TYRP1-CAR (ie, TRP1-CAR) mRNA could reprogram macrophages in situ within the eye and suppress choroidal melanoma; however, this strategy induces only transient CAR expression and is confined to the local ocular environment, limiting its utility for systemic long-term safety monitoring.36 Additionally, a myeloid-specific CAR knock-in mouse model enabling sustained endogenous CAR expression has been reported, but it has thus far been applied primarily in fibrosis settings and has not been extended to melanoma or to antigens such as TRP1 that are also physiologically expressed in normal tissues.37 Taken together, existing CAR-M research tools remain insufficient to simultaneously address both the validation of therapeutic efficacy in melanoma and the long-term systemic evaluation of on-target/off-tumor toxicity, highlighting the need to establish a myeloid endogenous CAR knock-in model tailored to the TRP1 target.

Based on these considerations, the present study established a myeloid-specific TRP1-CAR knock-in mouse model to achieve endogenous, sustained CAR expression in myeloid cells in vivo, and systematically evaluated its antitumor efficacy and potential safety in TRP1-positive melanoma. This study focused on addressing two key questions: first, whether TRP1-targeted CAR macrophages can phagocytose and eliminate TRP1-positive melanoma cells while promoting intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration; and second, whether this knock-in strategy can serve as a Proof-of-Concept (POC) model for evaluating the on-target/off-tumor risks that may arise from TRP1-targeted CAR-M cellular immunotherapy when the target antigen is also physiologically expressed in normal tissues. This system provides a physiologically relevant experimental platform for evaluating in vivo antitumor efficacy and facilitating longer-term safety observation of TRP1-targeted CAR-M, and offers POC support for CAR-M cellular immunotherapy directed against antigens with physiological expression in normal tissues.

Materials and Methods

Generation of Genetically Engineered Mouse Models

The genetically engineered mouse models used in this study, including conditional knock-in CARKI/+ mice and Lyz2Cre/+ driver mice, were generated or supplied by Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China; Production License Number: SCXK [Su] 2022–0016). The conditional knock-in CAR allele (CARKI/+) was generated by targeted integration of a donor cassette into the Rosa26 locus via clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9)-assisted pronuclear microinjection into fertilized C57BL/6J mouse embryos. The donor cassette contained a CAG promoter, a loxP-flanked PGK-Neo-6×SV40 polyadenylation sequence, and a Kozak-Myc tag-99-scFv-T2A-tdTomato-WPRE-BGH polyadenylation sequence. The encoded CAR included a Myc-tagged anti-TRP1 single-chain variable fragment (scFv), a human CD8 transmembrane domain (hCD8 TM), and an intracellular CD3ζ signaling domain.

To generate myeloid-specific TRP1-CAR mice, CARKI/+ mice were crossed with Lyz2Cre/+ driver mice. Mice carrying both the conditional CAR allele and the Lyz2-Cre allele (CARKI/+; Lyz2Cre/+) were designated as TRP1-CARM mice. CARKI/+ mice and Lyz2Cre/+ mice served as single-transgenic littermate controls.

All mice were maintained on a C57BL/6J background under specific pathogen-free conditions at 22 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle and free access to standard rodent chow and water. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong Medical Biotechnology Center, Shandong First Medical University (approval no. DW2025080702).

Genomic DNA Extraction from Mouse Tails

Genomic DNA was extracted from mouse tail tissue using a standardized alkaline lysis protocol. Using sterilized ophthalmic scissors, approximately 5 mm of mouse tail tip was clipped and placed into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube for sealed storage. The tissue sample was digested with 100 μL of freshly prepared alkaline lysis buffer A (containing 10 mM NaOH and 0.2 mM EDTA, prepared by dilution from stock solutions and brought to volume with ddH2O) at 95°C for 90 minutes. Subsequently, the lysate was neutralized with an equal volume of neutralization buffer B (40 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, also brought to volume with ddH2O). Following a brief centrifugation, the supernatant containing the genomic DNA was collected for subsequent experiments.

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

For genotyping of TRP1-CARM mice, we referred to previous studies.38 Each genomic DNA sample was analyzed in parallel using two independent allele-specific PCR assays. One assay used TRP1-KI_WT_F/R and TRP1-KI_Mutant_F/R primers to identify the CAR locus genotype (wild-type allele: 453 bp; CAR knock-in allele: 656 bp), whereas the other used Lyz2-Cre_WT_F/R and Lyz2-Cre_Mutant_F/R primers to determine the Lyz2-Cre genotype (wild-type allele: 346 bp; Cre allele: 721 bp), thereby enabling combinatorial determination of the genotypes listed in Table S1. PCR amplification was performed using 2× Rapid Taq Master Mix (Vazyme, P222-C2) with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30s, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10s, annealing at 62 °C for 30s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min/kb. A final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min was then performed. PCR products were resolved by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels.

Cell Culture

B16 and B16-firefly luciferase (Fluc)-green fluorescent protein (GFP) cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Corning), whereas HEK293T cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Corning). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; LONSERA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Sangon). Parental B16 cells were originally derived from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and subsequently provided to our laboratory by the School of Biomedical Sciences, Shandong First Medical University; HEK293T cells were obtained from the same institution. The B16-Fluc-GFP stable cell line was generated via lentiviral transduction. Briefly, B16 cells were infected with the supernatant of pCDH lentivirus carrying the Fluc-GFP fusion gene. At 72 hours post-infection, transduced cells were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin until all cells in the untreated control group had died. The surviving cell population was then collected and established as the stable line.

Isolation and Culture of Bone Marrow-Derived Macrophages (BMDMs)

BMDMs were isolated from 6-week-old female TRP1-CARM mice and their littermate controls. Mice were euthanized by isoflurane overdose followed by cervical dislocation, and femurs and tibias were aseptically dissected. The epiphyses of the bones were cut off, and the bone marrow was flushed out using DMEM medium (Corning) supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Sangon). The collected bone marrow suspension was centrifuged at 700 g for 5 minutes at room temperature. The cell pellet was then resuspended and treated with red blood cell lysis buffer at a 1:9 ratio (v/v, cell suspension to lysis buffer) for approximately 3 minutes on ice to remove erythrocytes. The lysis reaction was stopped by adding a 10-fold volume of PBS. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer to obtain a purified bone marrow cell population. Finally, the cells were resuspended in complete DMEM medium containing 10 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF; PeproTech) and seeded into culture dishes. Primary BMDMs were obtained following a 7-day induction period under these culture conditions.

Western Blot Analysis

As described in the preceding section, BMDMs were isolated from TRP1-CARM mice and their littermate controls. For tissue samples, lungs, livers, and spleens from TRP1-CARM mice and control mice were harvested and homogenized on ice. Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer, quantified using a BCA assay, and equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST for 2 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were anti-Myc Tag rabbit monoclonal antibody (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #2278), anti-β-actin mouse monoclonal antibody (1:1000, Beyotime, #AF0003), and anti-TRP1 rabbit monoclonal antibody (1:10,000, Abcam, #ab178676). Anti-Myc and anti-TRP1 antibodies were detected using HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000, Proteintech, #SA00001-2), whereas anti-β-actin antibody was detected using HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, Beyotime, #A0216). Protein signals were visualized using a standard enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Servicebio).

Flow Cytometry

Approximately 30 μL of peripheral blood was collected from the retro-orbital plexus using disposable capillary needles. Red blood cells were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (Beyotime, #C3702) at a 1:9 blood-to-buffer ratio on ice for approximately 3 min. Leukocytes were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #14-0161-82) at 4 °C for 30 min. Peripheral-blood myeloid subsets were analyzed in three independent staining tubes: macrophages were stained with APC-conjugated anti-CD11b (BioLegend, #101212) and FITC-conjugated anti-F4/80 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11-4801-82); neutrophils with APC-conjugated anti-CD45 (BioLegend, #147708) and FITC-conjugated anti-Ly6G (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11-9668-82); and monocytes with APC-conjugated anti-CD45 and FITC-conjugated anti-Ly6C (BioLegend, #128005). Surface staining was performed at 4 °C for 1 h in the dark. tdTomato fluorescence was detected in the PE channel. Macrophages, neutrophils, and monocytes were identified based on CD11b/F4/80, CD45/Ly6G, and CD45/Ly6C expression, respectively, and the proportion of tdTomato-positive cells within each subset was quantified. BMDM CAR expression was assessed using anti-G4S Linker (CST, #50515S). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1 software.

Phagocytosis Assay

Prior to co-culture, B16-Fluc-GFP cells and BMDMs were labeled separately with 1 μM CellTracker Green CMFDA dye (Invitrogen, #C7025) and 1 μM CellTracker Deep Red dye (Invitrogen, #C34565), respectively, in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Cells were then washed three times with PBS. For the phagocytosis assay, labeled B16-Fluc-GFP cells (5×105) were co-cultured with labeled TRP1-CARM-BMDMs or control BMDMs (1.5×106) at an effector-to-target ratio of 3:1 for 12 h. After co-culture, cells were collected, filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer, and analyzed by flow cytometry. BMDMs were identified by CellTracker Deep Red fluorescence in the APC channel, whereas B16-Fluc-GFP target cells were identified by the combined GFP and CellTracker Green signal in the FITC channel. The phagocytic rate was calculated as the percentage of FITC+APC+ cells among total APC+ BMDMs. Representative plots from one of the independent experiments performed are shown; this assay was not quantified across replicates and therefore no statistical comparison was performed.

Cytotoxicity Assay

B16-Fluc-GFP target cells (3 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and co-cultured with TRP1-CARM-BMDMs or control BMDMs at effector-to-target (E:T) ratios of 3:1 and 5:1 for 36 h or 48 h. Target cells cultured alone served as the viability control. At each time point, the culture medium was replaced with PBS containing 0.3 mg/mL D-luciferin, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. Bioluminescence signals from viable B16-Fluc-GFP cells were acquired using an in vivo imaging system. Normalized cytotoxicity was calculated as 1 − (bioluminescence signal of the co-culture group/bioluminescence signal of the target-cell-only control). Each experimental group at each E:T ratio and time point was assessed in five replicate wells (n = 5).

RNA Extraction and Quantitative PCR (qPCR) Analysis

B16-Fluc-GFP cells were co-cultured with TRP1-CARM-BMDMs in 6-well plates for 36 h at an E:T ratio of 3:1. Total RNA was extracted from the cells using the RNA Easy Fast Tissue/Cell Kit (TIANGEN, #DP451). Subsequently, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, #RR047A). qPCR was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) following the standard thermal cycling protocol. The sequences of the forward and reverse primers used to assess expression changes in inflammation-related genes in the co-culture system are listed in Table S2.

In vivo Studies

6–8-week-old TRP1-CARM mice and their littermate controls were subcutaneously inoculated with 3×105 B16-Fluc-GFP cells in the interscapular region on day 0. Under the same inoculation conditions, two independent cohorts of tumor-bearing mice were established for distinct endpoints. Both cohorts underwent bioluminescence imaging on days 17 and 21; one cohort was further imaged on days 27, 32, and 50 and followed for survival through day 50 (n = 8 per group), whereas the other cohort was euthanized on day 21 for tumor weight measurement and histological analyses (n = 7 per group). The two cohorts were inoculated and analyzed independently and were designed in advance for distinct experimental endpoints.

Mice were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane (3–4% for induction, 1.5–2.5% for maintenance) delivered in oxygen at 0.5 L/min. Once fully anesthetized, D-luciferin potassium salt (150 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally, and imaging was conducted 10 min later. Tumor size was measured 2–3 times per week using calipers from the time tumors became palpable (day 12 onward), and tumor volume was calculated as V = length × width2/2. When tumor volume reached ≥2000 mm3 or ulceration occurred, euthanasia was performed as a predefined humane endpoint; survival curves therefore represent time to humane endpoint rather than spontaneous death. Prior to euthanasia, mice were deeply anesthetized via isoflurane overdose, followed by cervical dislocation performed by trained personnel. Death was confirmed by sustained cessation of breathing and heartbeat and loss of all reflexes for ≥30 s. All anesthesia and euthanasia procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA).

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E)

Following fixation, mouse tissues were processed through a graded ethanol series for dehydration, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm. For staining, the sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a descending ethanol gradient. The staining procedure was performed as follows: sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated in acid ethanol, and rinsed in tap water for bluing. Subsequently, the sections were counterstained with eosin for 2 min. Finally, the stained sections were dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam for morphological examination under a light microscope.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Beyotime, #P0099) at 4 °C for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm. After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were subjected to citrate-buffer antigen retrieval, washed with PBS, and blocked with QuickBlock™ Immunostaining Blocking Solution (Beyotime, #P0260) for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies as required for the respective staining: anti-TRP1 (1:500, Abcam, #ab178676), anti-CD8 (1:2000, Abcam, #ab209775), anti-F4/80 (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, #70076), and anti-Myc (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #5605). After three washes with PBST, sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies, including Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:400, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A21206) and Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:400, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A21244). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #D1306). Images were acquired using a confocal microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.54).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5. Data are presented as mean ± SD, with the number of biological replicates (n) for each experiment stated in the corresponding figure legend. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests. Longitudinal tumor-volume data were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance. Survival was analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the inflammation-related gene panel, each gene was compared independently without correction for multiple comparisons; these results are therefore interpreted as exploratory.

Results

Generation and Baseline Characterization of TRP1-CARM Mice

The TRP1-targeted CAR construct comprised a Myc tag, an anti-TRP1 scFv, a hCD8 TM, and an intracellular CD3ζ signaling domain (Figure 1a). TRP1-CARM mice were generated by conditional knock-in of this CAR expression cassette at the Rosa26 locus followed by Lyz2-Cre-mediated recombination (Figure 1b). PCR genotyping detected the expected CAR knock-in allele and Lyz2-Cre allele in TRP1-CARM mice (Figure S1a).

In the lung, liver, and spleen, CAR fluorescence signals were observed in areas containing F4/80-positive macrophages in TRP1-CARM mice, whereas no corresponding signals were detected in controls. Quantification showed greater CAR-positive signal areas in all three tissues from TRP1-CARM mice than in controls (P < 0.001 for each tissue; n = 3 per group; Figure 1c–f). Western blotting further detected a Myc-tagged CAR band at approximately 50 kDa in the lung, liver, and spleen of TRP1-CARM mice (Figure 1g). In a representative 70-week-old TRP1-CARM mouse, CAR signals remained detectable in lung sections, including in F4/80-positive macrophage-containing areas (Figure S1b). Flow-cytometric analysis of tdTomato reporter expression detected tdTomato-positive cells in peripheral-blood macrophages (CD11b+F4/80+), monocytes (CD45+Ly6C+), and neutrophils (CD45+Ly6G+) from TRP1-CARM mice, with representative positivity rates of 49.6%, 40.3%, and 20.1%, respectively (Figure 1h–j).

Under baseline conditions, H&E staining of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney showed no overt histological abnormalities (Figure 1k). In addition, peripheral-blood hematological parameters did not differ significantly between TRP1-CARM mice and controls (n = 4 per group, Figure S1c).

In vitro Characterization and Antitumor Activity of TRP1-CARM-BMDMs, with Altered Inflammation-Related Gene Expression in Co-Culture

To characterize CAR expression in the effector cells and TRP1 expression in the target cells, TRP1-CARM-BMDMs and B16-Fluc-GFP cells were first evaluated. TRP1-CARM-BMDMs displayed tdTomato fluorescence and a Myc-tagged CAR protein band at approximately 50 kDa (Figure 2a and b). Flow cytometry further detected a tdTomato-positive cell population among TRP1-CARM-BMDMs, whereas no corresponding signal was detected in control BMDMs (Figure 2c). Western blotting detected TRP1 protein in B16 cells, B16-Fluc-GFP cells, and B16-Fluc-GFP tumor tissue; no corresponding band was detected in HEK293T cells used as the negative control (Figure 2d).

Following co-culture with B16-Fluc-GFP cells, representative flow-cytometric plots suggested higher phagocytic efficiency of TRP1-CARM-BMDMs toward B16-Fluc-GFP cells than of control BMDMs (Figure 2e). TRP1-CARM-BMDMs showed higher normalized cytotoxicity than control BMDMs at E:T ratios of 3:1 and 5:1 after both 36 h and 48 h of co-culture (all P < 0.05; Figure 2f–j, n = 5 wells per condition).

To further assess expression changes in inflammation-related genes within the co-culture system, qPCR was performed after 36 h of co-culture. Compared with control co-cultures, co-cultures containing TRP1-CARM-BMDMs showed increased expression of Ccl5, Cd80, Cxcl1, and Nos2, together with decreased expression of Cd206, Cd163, Il10, Arg1, Fizz1, and Vegfa (Figure 2k and l; n = 3). Representative bright-field images of cell pellets collected after co-culture showed visibly reduced dark pigmentation in the TRP1-CARM-BMDM co-culture group (Figure S2a).

TRP1-CARM Mice Delay B16-Fluc-GFP Melanoma Growth and Show Increased Intratumoral CD8+ T-Cell Signals

To evaluate the in vivo effects of TRP1-CARM mice in a B16-Fluc-GFP melanoma model, survival-follow-up and endpoint-analysis cohorts were established. In the survival-follow-up cohort (n = 8 per group), B16-Fluc-GFP cells were subcutaneously inoculated into TRP1-CARM mice and littermate controls, and longitudinal bioluminescence imaging was performed (Figure 3a). TRP1-CARM mice showed prolonged survival compared with controls (P = 0.0005, Log rank test; Figure 3b). In representative longitudinal bioluminescence images, tumor bioluminescence signals appeared lower in the TRP1-CARM group during the observation period (Figure 3c). After completion of the predefined 50-day study endpoint, visibly reduced pigmentation of the facial hair, whiskers, and coat was observed in two tumor-bearing TRP1-CARM mice; one representative example is shown (Figure S2b).

In the independent endpoint-analysis cohort (n = 7 per group), in vivo bioluminescence imaging was performed on days 17 and 21, followed by tissue collection and analysis on day 21 (Figure 3d). In representative in vivo bioluminescence images, tumor bioluminescence signals appeared lower in the TRP1-CARM group on day 21 (Figure 3e). Longitudinal tumor-volume monitoring showed lower tumor volumes in the TRP1-CARM group across the observation period (P < 0.0001, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA; Figure 3f). At the endpoint, tumor weights were lower in the TRP1-CARM group (P = 0.0003; Figure 3g), and representative gross images of excised tumors and ex vivo bioluminescence images were visually consistent with these observations (Figure 3h). Gross examination did not reveal obvious differences in the heart, liver, spleen, kidney, or lung between TRP1-CARM mice and controls (Figure S2c). Representative H&E sections appeared to display milder inflammatory-cell infiltration in TRP1-CARM mice (Figure S2d and e), whereas histological staining of major organs appeared to show no distinct histopathological disparities between the two groups (Figure S2f).

Representative H&E staining of tumor tissues revealed morphological differences between the two groups (Figure 3i). Control tumors appeared to show densely arranged tumor cells, more readily identifiable mitotic figures, limited inflammatory-cell infiltration, and large confluent necrotic areas. In contrast, tumors from TRP1-CARM mice appeared to show relatively looser cellular arrangement, multiple scattered small necrotic foci, and more prominent diffuse inflammatory-cell infiltration.

Immunofluorescence analysis of tumor tissues detected CAR fluorescence signals in tumors from TRP1-CARM mice, with partial overlap between CAR and TRP1 signals in selected areas; no CAR fluorescence signals were detected in control tumors (Figure 3j). Quantification showed a greater CAR signal area in tumors from TRP1-CARM mice than in controls (P < 0.0001, n = 3 per group, Figure 3k). In addition, tumors from TRP1-CARM mice exhibited stronger CD8+ T-cell fluorescence signals and a greater CD8+ signal area than controls (P < 0.0001, n = 3 per group, Figure 3l and m). In lung, liver, and spleen tissues collected after tumor establishment, CAR fluorescence signals remained detectable in TRP1-CARM mice but were not detected in controls (Figure 3n–p), and the CAR-positive signal areas were higher in all three tissues than in controls (P < 0.001 for each tissue; n = 3 per group, Figure 3q).

Discussion

Through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-in at the Rosa26 locus, we generated a myeloid-specific, TRP1-targeted CAR knock-in mouse model, which provides a valuable platform for systematically evaluating the antitumor efficacy and safety of TRP1-targeted CAR-M therapy in a physiologically relevant context. Under the baseline conditions examined, we did not detect overt histopathological or hematological abnormalities in the organs assessed. These baseline observations are preliminary, given the limited tissues and time points assessed. Using this model, we provided preliminary validation of the in vitro antitumor effects of TRP1-CARM, manifested as enhanced phagocytic activity and cytotoxic effects against tumor cells, accompanied by altered inflammation-related gene expression in the co-culture system. In the B16-Fluc-GFP melanoma model, TRP1-CARM mice showed delayed tumor growth, and increased intratumoral CD8+ T-cell signals were observed in the same tumors. By engineering endogenous myeloid cells to generate CAR-M, this work establishes a new POC framework for solid tumor immunotherapy, addressing in particular the limitations of conventional adoptive transfer models in evaluating the longer-term safety of CAR-M directed against antigens with physiological expression in selected normal tissues.

Notably, this study extends the CAR-M platform established in our previous work37 into a new therapeutic domain. Although both studies employ myeloid-targeted CAR engineering, they address distinct biological contexts and therapeutic objectives. The earlier study focused on a fibroblast activation protein (FAP)-directed CAR-M strategy for intervening in pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis, whereas the present study focuses on TRP1-targeted CAR-M immunotherapy for malignant melanoma. Correspondingly, the underlying scientific rationale and evaluation endpoints differ substantially: the former emphasized antifibrotic efficacy and restoration of tissue architecture, whereas the latter focuses on antitumor efficacy and CD8+ T-cell infiltration. Collectively, these two studies represent independent yet complementary lines of investigation that extend the translational application of the CAR-M platform from fibrotic disease to tumor immunology.

Conventional CAR-M research relies on ex vivo engineering followed by adoptive transfer of a finite number of cells, which fails to recapitulate the sustained, systemic presence of CAR-expressing cells in vivo.40,41 Such persistence is critical for evaluating both the durability of therapeutic efficacy and long-term safety. Our TRP1-CARM model addresses this limitation by achieving sustained endogenous CAR expression within myeloid cells. CAR signals were detected in F4/80-positive macrophage-containing areas of the lung, liver, and spleen (Figure 1c–g), confirming the multi-organ distribution of the endogenous CAR in vivo. CAR signals also remained detectable in the lung, liver, and spleen after tumor establishment (Figure 3n–q), supporting continued detectability of the CAR signal during tumor challenge. This sustained, systemic exposure addresses the shortcomings of conventional models, providing a basis for longer-term and more systematic safety assessment in vivo. Building on this platform advantage, we first systematically examined the baseline toxicity associated with endogenous CAR expression. No evident histopathological abnormalities were observed in major organs, and hematological parameters remained normal under steady-state conditions (Figure S1c), indicating that endogenous CAR expression did not elicit marked spontaneous systemic toxicity. In tumor-bearing mice, representative H&E findings in the heart, liver, spleen, kidney, lung, and tumor-adjacent skin did not reveal overt tissue injury in TRP1-CARM mice (Figure S2d–f). The less prominent pulmonary inflammatory-cell infiltration observed in the TRP1-CARM group should be interpreted cautiously because tumor burden differed between groups. Accordingly, these findings provide only preliminary histological observations rather than a definitive assessment of systemic or on-target toxicity. Notably, visible depigmentation of the facial hair, whiskers, and coat was observed in two TRP1-CARM mice during tumor-model follow-up (Figure S2b). Given the physiological expression of TRP1 in melanocytes, this observation may indicate a potential on-target effect in pigmented tissues; however, its incidence and temporal course were not systematically assessed in the present study. In addition, ocular tissues, another pigmented site with physiological TRP1 expression,32 were not examined. Future studies incorporating more refined immunopathological indicators and long-term monitoring of pigmented tissues, including skin and ocular tissues, will help further strengthen the safety evidence for this model.

Beyond these baseline observations, TRP1-CARM-BMDMs showed enhanced phagocytic and cytotoxic activity against B16-Fluc-GFP cells in vitro, accompanied by altered inflammation-related gene expression in the co-culture system. These findings are consistent with the involvement of inflammatory programs in CAR-M activity.42,43 In the B16-Fluc-GFP melanoma model, TRP1-CARM mice showed delayed tumor growth and prolonged survival (Figure 3b–h). Histological features of TRP1-CARM tumors, including more diffuse inflammatory-cell infiltration and scattered necrotic foci, provide qualitative pathological context for the observed antitumor response (Figure 3i). In addition, TRP1-CARM tumors exhibited increased intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration (Figure 3l and m). Although these findings suggest that CAR-M may exert antitumor effects partly through T-cell recruitment, this study did not directly establish a causal relationship between T-cell infiltration and tumor control. Future studies employing in vivo antibody-mediated depletion or transgenic models will be needed to clarify the causal contribution of distinct immune cell populations to the antitumor response.44 However, the in vivo antitumor effect may not be mediated by CAR-M alone. Flow-cytometric analysis detected tdTomato reporter-positive cells not only in macrophages but also in peripheral-blood monocytes (40.3%) and neutrophils (20.1%) (Figure 1h–j). Although monocytes are closely related to macrophages as their precursors,45 whether neutrophils directly participate in the antitumor response remains to be clarified. As cell lineage-specific depletion was not performed, the relative contribution of individual myeloid subsets to TRP1-CAR-mediated antitumor activity requires further investigation.46

Although the TRP1-CARM platform established in this study provides a powerful tool for systematically evaluating the antitumor efficacy and safety of TRP1-targeted CAR-M therapy, several limitations remain to be addressed. First, this model employs a constitutive CAR expression system, in which CAR is present continuously from early developmental stages. This differs from the clinical mode of CAR-M administration; future work could develop an inducible CAR expression system (eg, CreERT2)47 to enable induction of CAR expression in myeloid cells during adulthood, more accurately modeling clinical dosing regimens. Second, as noted above, the observed increase in T-cell infiltration represents only a correlative finding; the molecular mechanisms by which CAR-M cells recruit and activate T cells—such as chemokine secretion and antigen cross-presentation23—remain to be fully elucidated, and future studies could employ single-cell transcriptomic approaches to dissect this molecular network. With respect to safety evaluation, this study focused primarily on major organs under baseline steady-state conditions; future work should implement longer observation periods to systematically assess pathological changes in pigmented tissues such as the retina, further validating the long-term safety of TRP1 targeting. Finally, biological differences between murine and human myeloid cells may limit the direct extrapolation of findings from this model to human therapy. Future studies should generate macrophages expressing a humanized TRP1 CAR and evaluate their translational potential in humanized mouse models.

Conclusion

Through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-in at the Rosa26 locus, we established a myeloid-specific TRP1-CAR mouse model. Under the conditions examined, TRP1-CARM mice showed no overt histological abnormalities or hematological changes in the assessed tissues at baseline. TRP1-CARM-BMDMs showed enhanced phagocytic and cytotoxic activity against B16-Fluc-GFP cells in vitro, accompanied by altered inflammation-related gene expression in the co-culture system. In vivo, TRP1-CARM mice showed delayed melanoma growth and prolonged survival, together with increased intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration. These findings support the use of this model to investigate antitumor activity and preliminary in vivo safety observations associated with endogenous TRP1-CAR expression.
