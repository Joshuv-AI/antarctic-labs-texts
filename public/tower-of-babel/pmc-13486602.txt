Central guide–target pairing activates human Argonaute2 by uncoupling the N-PAZ and MID–PIWI lobes
Authors: Huaqun Zhang, Andrew Savidge, GeunYoung Sim, Mi Seul Park, Audrey C. Kehling, Vishal Annasaheb Adhav, Kotaro Nakanishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) are widely used to repress gene expression. For human Argonaute2 (AGO2) to cleave target RNA, siRNAs must be almost perfectly complementary to their targets, particularly in the central region, yet the mechanistic basis for this requirement has remained unclear. Here, cryo-EM structures and biochemical analyses show that centrally mismatched targets propagate along the nucleic acid–binding channel of bilobed AGO2, whereas centrally matched targets form a duplex that pries open the tapered channel, collapsing the bilobed architecture into a duplex-bound MID–PIWI lobe and an RNA-free N–PAZ lobe. This rearrangement generates a preactive open intermediate that accommodates the extensively paired duplex. The uncoupled N–PAZ lobe must then swing back and reassemble the bilobed architecture, thereby positioning the duplex in the PIWI catalytic center and promoting target cleavage.

Human AGO2 cleaves target RNAs that are fully complementary to a ~22-nucleotide (nt) guide RNA (1). The prevailing model proposes that, after seed pairing (guide nucleotides g2–g8; State II in Fig. 1A), the target RNA bypasses the central region and resumes duplex propagation in the 3′ supplementary region (g13–g16), forming two discontinuous duplex segments (State III) (2). Extension of the second segment into the tail region induces topological tension that releases the guide 3′ end from the PAZ domain (State IV), allowing rotation of the duplex segments and subsequent base pairing in the central region (g9–g12), which positions the scissile phosphate between target nucleotide position 10 (t10) and t11 for cleavage by the PIWI domain (State V). Crystal structures have captured States I–IV (3–5), while State V has been visualized by cryo-EM (6). Although this model assumes that centrally matched and centrally mismatched targets follow similar recognition pathways prior to cleavage (Fig. 1A), the underlying mechanism remains unresolved.

Previous studies have shown that target cleavage requires contiguous pairing across the central region but not the tail region (7–11), suggesting that the prevailing model does not fully explain how AGO2 achieves central duplex formation. Because the bilobed architecture forms a nucleic acid–binding channel that narrows toward the central region, how central pairing is accommodated remains unclear (Fig. 1A) (3). A recent cryo-EM study showed that siRNA-loaded AGO2 transitions from the canonical bilobed conformation to an open conformation as the guide pairs with progressively longer targets (12, 14, and 21 nt) (12). Notably, the PAZ domain becomes disordered upon pairing through g2–g12, and the entire N–PAZ lobe becomes disordered upon pairing through g2–g19, suggesting that continuous duplex propagation drives large conformational rearrangements, although their functional significance remained unclear.

Results

We determined the minimal guide length required for AGO2 activation. AGO2 programmed with truncated miR-20a guides (10 to 23 nt) was tested for cleavage of a 60-nt target fully complementary to the 23-nt guide. Catalytic activity emerged at 12 nt, and guides of 16 nt or longer exhibited cleavage comparable to full-length guides (Fig. 1B). Notably, a 16-nt guide is 3 nt shorter than 19 nt, which is the length of miR-20a required for stable engagement of its 3′ end by the PAZ domain (13). These observations confirm that robust AGO2 catalysis can occur even when the guide 3′ end is not anchored in the PAZ domain. At the same time, a 16-nt guide supports continuous Watson–Crick pairing from g2 to g16, corresponding to ~1.5 turns of an A-form helix. We therefore reasoned that formation of such an extended duplex could generate sufficient topological tension to displace the guide 3′ end from the PAZ domain. This logic could explain that even when AGO2 is programmed with a ~22-nt guide, perfect base pairing through g2–g16 alone would be sufficient to promote guide 3’ end release and trigger catalytic activation, without requiring prior duplex propagation into the tail region, as previous work supports (8, 11, 14).

To test this idea, we determined the cryo-EM structure of 23-nt miR-20a–loaded AGO2 bound to a target complementary from t2 to t16 (Fig. 1 C–F and Table 1). The 3D reconstitution shows only the duplex-bound MID–PIWI lobe because the departed N-PAZ lobe is bent through the Beam (15) and presumably free to move against the other lobe (Movie S1). This structure demonstrates that duplex formation across the seed, central, and 3′ supplementary regions alone collapses the bilobed architecture. In contrast, AGO2 bound to a target containing mismatches at t10–t11 retains the canonical bilobed conformation (Fig. 1 E and G), in which the duplex bypasses the central region and resumes pairing in the 3′ supplementary region (16). Thus, the ability to form stable central pairings determines whether AGO2 uncouples the two lobes.

In the open conformation, the guide 3′ end is solvent-exposed, permitting further duplex propagation into the tail region (Fig. 1F). The duplex is misaligned relative to that in State V, leaving the scissile phosphate distant from the active site (Fig. 1H). The N–PAZ lobe in State V contacts both major and minor grooves of the duplex (Fig. 1I) (6), indicating that the N–PAZ lobe of the open conformation must swing back to reconstitute the bilobed architecture. We hypothesized that the N-PAZ lobe positions the duplex relative to the PIWI domain to promote efficient cleavage. Consistent with this, an isolated MID–PIWI lobe (mini-AGO2) retained catalytic activity but exhibited markedly reduced cleavage rates compared with full-length AGO2 (Fig. 1J), indicating that the N–PAZ lobe is dispensable for catalysis but critical for efficient substrate positioning.

Discussion

During the revision of our paper, the MacRae group reported a cryo-EM structure showing that after State V, AGO2 deforms the guide-target duplex toward cleavage (17). Therefore, we designated State V (PDB: 9CMP) and the catalytically active state (PDB: 9HDX) as the early- and late-active states, respectively, and propose a revised model of AGO2 target recognition (Fig. 1K). Targets with central complementarity form a duplex that pries open the nucleic acid–binding channel, collapsing AGO2 into a preactive open conformation, whereas targets containing a central mismatch bypass the central region and retain the bilobed architecture. Only the bypass pathway would undergo target-directed miRNA degradation (TDMD) (5). These findings show that human AGO2 activation can be driven by duplex formation across the seed, central, and 3′ supplementary regions without requiring prior tail pairing, providing a mechanistic basis for target discrimination. Interestingly, Thermus thermophilus Ago, lacking α7, widens the channel and propagates the central-matched duplex without uncoupling the two lobes (18). The discrepancy may be explained by the existence of α7, which pushes the N-PAZ lobe outward when central-matched targets pair with the guide (Fig. 1L), suggesting the distinct mechanisms of AGO catalytic activation among species.

Materials and Methods

AGO2 used for cryo-EM was purified as previously reported (19). Micrographs were collected at OSU Center for Electron Microscopy and Analysis (CEMAS). Mini-AGO2 was designed, as previously reported (20). Details are provided in SI Appendix.
