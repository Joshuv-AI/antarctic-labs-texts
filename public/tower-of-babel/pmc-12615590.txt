The emission-reduction effects of green innovation in china’s heavily polluting industries under cost pressures
Authors: Xiaoqing Jiang, Ge Gao, Sang Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Green innovation is crucial for China, the “world’s factory”, to reduce the environmental impact of the high energy consumption and high emissions of heavily polluting industries. However, existing studies rarely investigate the emission-reduction effects of green innovation in heavily polluting industries in China. This study applies a dynamic panel model to explore how green innovation reduces pollutant emissions in these industries. Additionally, it examines the significant role of cost pressures in this process. The findings reveal three key insights: (1) Green innovation significantly reduces pollutant emission intensity in heavily polluting industries. However, cost pressures weaken the emission-reduction effects of green innovation. In contrast, efficiency-empowered advantages strengthen these effects. (2) Heterogeneity analysis reveals that the inhibitory effect of cost pressures primarily impacts small-scale and low-profit enterprises. This result highlights that the sensitivity of enterprises to costs is a key factor in reducing the effectiveness of green innovation. (3) Regional analysis indicates that cost pressures do not significantly affect the emission-reduction effects of green innovation in eastern and western enterprises. However, these pressures have a more pronounced inhibitory effect on central enterprises. This result could be due to increased competition for eligibility to receive industrial transfers from eastern coastal regions, which heightens the cost sensitivity of central enterprises. To address these challenges, this paper recommends improving the “cost-regulation matching mechanism” to resolve the paradox between green innovation and economic benefits, often referred to as the “weak Porter hypothesis”.

Introduction

In today’s world, economic development not only enhances people’s quality of life but also creates issues such as resource depletion, waste discharge, and environmental degradation. These issues pose serious threats to human health and stand in the way of achieving the collective goal of sustainable development for society. With its massive manufacturing sector, China is likewise facing significant environmental challenges. Government agencies foster green innovation through policy regulations aimed at alleviating the environmental pressures associated with the high energy consumption and high-emissions of heavily polluting industries. This strategic approach holds significant practical relevance for China, which is recognized globally as the “world’s factory”. However, the implementation of regulations does not always progress smoothly. Scholars have examined the economic consequences of guiding corporate green innovation through environmental policies and identified variations in the “Porter hypothesis”, distinguishing between “strong” and “weak” forms of the hypothesis. The “strong Porter hypothesis” posits that the benefits derived from green innovation can offset the associated costs; conversely, the “weak Porter hypothesis” suggests that these benefits may not necessarily cover the costs1,2, which could substantially weaken the actual emission-reduction effects of green innovations3–5. Heavily polluting industries are a primary focus of the environmental policies of China, which has devoted significant resources and implemented supportive measures to encourage green innovation among these industries. However, an important question arises: Do the green innovations adopted by China’s heavily polluting industries effectively contribute to emission reduction?

The major heavily polluting industries are primarily upstream raw material or energy industries. Unlike typical industries, they rely significantly on economies of scale and generally offer more homogeneous products. As a result, many of these industries focus on cost competition to achieve a competitive advantage through cost efficiency. This emphasis on cost can significantly influence their innovation choices and the effectiveness of those innovations. Some scholars argue that in the face of market competition, enterprises weigh the costs and benefits of green innovation. When cost constraints are pronounced, enterprises may resort to “pseudo-green” practices to superficially portray themselves as environmentally friendly6,7. However, the benefits of corporate green innovation are typically directly tied to actual emission-reduction outcomes. “Pseudo-green” and “green-washing” behaviors imply that even if environmental regulations encourage green innovation within enterprises, significant emission reductions are difficult to achieve, thus limiting the transition from the “weak Porter hypothesis” to the “strong Porter hypothesis”. Thus, do the cost pressures faced by heavily polluting industries hinder the environmental benefits of green innovation? Furthermore, does cost pressure have a more significant inhibitory effect on enterprises that are highly sensitive to cost fluctuations?

To answer these questions and bridge the analytical gap in current research, this study employs a dynamic panel model to examine the impact of green innovation by heavily polluting industries in China on pollutant emission intensity. By conducting a moderating effect analysis and exploring heterogeneity, we further investigate the role of cost pressure in this context. The findings of this study are instrumental for optimizing green industry policies, which have significant implications for global pollution control and sustainable development. Additionally, from the perspective of cost pressures, this paper examines the reasons behind the differentiation between the “strong” and “weak” versions of the Porter hypothesis, and it explores the potential for transitioning from the “weak” to the “strong” version, thus supplementing and enhancing related theoretical frameworks.

Literature review

The environmental effects of green innovation

In recent years, driven by the objective of sustainable economic and social development, scholars have conducted extensive and detailed research on the environmental performance of green innovation8–10. In terms of pollutant emissions, scholars have focused on verifying the impact of green innovation on carbon emissions. At the macro level, Xu et al.11 used city-level data from China and argued that green innovation can significantly reduce urban carbon emissions. On this basis, Zhao et al.12 employed data from 108 cities within China’s Yangtze River Economic Belt to validate the significant role of green innovation in carbon-emission reduction. Furthermore, they examined the moderating effects of formal and informal environmental regulations in this context. At the micro level, Ju et al.13, on the basis of data from Chinese manufacturing enterprises, discovered that enterprises could enhance their carbon performance through green innovation. Their findings indicated that ownership concentration and executives’ political connections had negative moderating effects, whereas board compensation had a positive moderating effect. Furthermore, some scholars have extended the scope of research on pollutants. For example, Dong et al.14 investigated the impact of green innovation on the emissions of major air pollutants.

In the realm of green development, existing studies have verified the significant role of green innovation in achieving economic green growth. Zhao et al.15 reported that green innovation could improve urban green total factor productivity; however, this effect is influenced by industrial structure upgrading and innovation agglomeration. Furthermore, an increase in urban green total factor productivity through green innovation exerts positive spillover effects on surrounding cities. Using provincial data from China, Jiang and Sun16 reported that digital innovation contributes to improving green development efficiency at the provincial level.

Overall, existing research on the environmental effects of green innovation tends to focus more on the macro level of urban areas, with relatively insufficient attention paid to the microlevel environmental effects at the corporate level. Notably, research on the environmental impacts of green innovation in heavily polluting industries is lacking. Furthermore, investigations into the environmental effects of green innovation often emphasize air pollutants such as greenhouse gases and sulfur dioxide, but comprehensive assessments that include the intensity of emissions related to water pollutants are lacking.

Factors influencing corporate emission reduction

Globally, numerous studies have explored the factors affecting corporate emission reduction through policy regulations, consumer behavior, and technological advancements. With respect to policy regulations, Lyu et al.17 discovered that environmental inspections incentivize enterprises to increase the quality and quantity of green innovations, thus reducing carbon dioxide emissions. This finding underscores the significant impact of environmental policies on corporate pollutant emissions. However, with alterations in environmental policies, the regulatory environment and the anticipated benefits of green innovation for enterprises also change. Consequently, policy uncertainty tends to be positively correlated with the intensity of corporate pollutant emissions18. In terms of consumer behavior, Xu et al. (2024) discovered that consumers’ environmental awareness can exert pressure on enterprises from the demand side, forcing enterprises to reduce their pollutant emissions19. With respect to technological advancements, researchers have reported that the application of artificial intelligence and industrial robots can significantly lower the intensity of corporate pollutant emissions20–22. Moreover, technological progress, as exemplified by green innovation, can decrease corporate pollutant emissions through various mechanisms, for example, by reducing energy consumption, optimizing the energy structure, and promoting industrial upgrading23–26.

With the development of the internet economy, some studies have identified a synergistic relationship between digitalization and the emission-reduction effects of green innovation. Liu et al.27 discovered that the integration of green innovation with digitalization can further decrease carbon emissions. On the one hand, digitalization provides enterprises with vast and efficient data resources, aiding in the precise assessment of market trends and boosting the returns from innovation28. On the other hand, the sharing model of digitalization offers enterprises multiple pathways for decision-making, assisting in reducing management and communication costs, which often translates into low-cost technological application and expansion29. Moreover, related research has illustrated from various perspectives that the cost characteristics of enterprises significantly impact the environmental performance of green innovation. For example, using data from Ethiopia’s cement and leather industries, Wakeford et al.30 reported that high technological costs and financial constraints are major inhibitors of corporate green innovation, whereas the effects of environmental regulations and concerns are relatively minor. Furthermore, studies by Jiang et al.31 and Zhang et al.32 have shown that limited profit margins and fragile financial conditions can affect the actual environmental performance of corporate green innovation. This review of the relevant literature reveals that the actual effectiveness of corporate green innovation is often associated with pressures on the cost front. However, existing studies have not directly examined the relationship between cost pressures and the emission-reduction outcomes of green innovation.

The innovations of this paper, compared with existing studies, are reflected in three key aspects: (1) Previous studies often neglect the fact that corporate pollutant emissions are easily influenced by individual inertia. The simplistic use of panel regression models may lead to biased regression results. Taking China as an example, this paper employs a dynamic panel model to re-examine the actual emission-reduction effects of green innovation in heavily polluting industries. Moreover, the measurement of pollutant emission intensity includes both water pollutants and air pollutants. (2) While many studies have explored the varied impacts of the “Porter hypothesis” on enterprises, this paper offers an explanation from a fresh perspective: cost pressures. We propose an improved “cost-regulation matching mechanism” to facilitate the transition from limited innovation compensation to systematic breakthroughs. (3) Heterogeneity analysis is used to delve deeper into how cost pressures affect outcomes. We find that the sensitivity of enterprises to their costs plays a crucial role in diminishing the effectiveness of green innovation in reducing emissions. Finally, we examine the diverse impacts during the industrial shift across different regions in China.

Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

The Porter hypothesis, theories of environmental coalitions, and the dynamic capabilities framework have each discussed the necessity of green innovation and its positive impacts on environmental improvements from various perspectives33–36. On the one hand, green innovation directly impacts the environment by enhancing production technologies. Through advancements in production technologies, green innovation facilitates the efficient use of energy and resources, thus reducing environmental consumption and achieving the goals of energy conservation and pollutant emission reduction37. Concurrently, by optimizing production processes, promoting the efficient utilization of production factors, and developing new green products, green innovation can positively impact pollutant emission reduction during the production process38. Bai et al.39 argued that by greening production processes, enterprises can realize resource reuse, thus alleviating the pressure on resource environments. Furthermore, the continuous improvements in institutional design and management concepts that accompany green innovation activities can lead to significant environmental performance40–42. On the other hand, there are indirect spillover effects of green innovation. Zhang and Zhu43 suggested that some products associated with green innovation drive the development of related industries, thus exerting positive environmental effects.

However, some studies suggest that the effectiveness of green innovation can be significantly influenced by the subjective behaviors of enterprises. According to Xing et al.44, when enterprises face environmental regulations, if they engage in “green-washing”, it becomes challenging for them to substantially contribute to reducing pollutant emissions. In general, according to the environmental Kuznets curve (EKC), economic growth initially leads to increased pollution, but as the economy further develops, pollutant emission levels eventually decline. Nevertheless, scholars have observed that heavily polluting industries incur substantial fixed costs, leading to a lock-in effect of production technologies once established, and the outcomes of environmental regulations can significantly impact their output45. As a developing country, China may still face technological and equipment disparities compared with developed Western nations. The “technology lock-in effect” induced by early investment in fixed assets increases the likelihood of “green-washing” among heavily polluting industries, resulting in uncertainties regarding the actual emission-reduction effects of their green innovations. On the basis of these insights, this paper proposes the following hypotheses:

H1a. Green innovation in China’s heavily polluting industries can significantly reduce pollutant emission intensity.

H1b. Green innovation in China’s heavily polluting industries cannot significantly reduce pollutant emission intensity.

Through advancements in production technology, green innovation not only contributes to reducing pollutant emission intensity but also decreases energy consumption, thus achieving cost savings46. However, given the high degree of product homogeneity among heavily polluting industries, many of these enterprises gain competitive advantages through cost optimization. This inclination may lead heavily polluting industries to prioritize cost control and avoid high-cost innovations and their application, potentially resulting in a crowding-out effect on innovation47. Consequently, there might be a certain degree of decline in the overall environmental performance of green innovations. Moreover, while initial investments in green innovation in heavily polluting enterprises are substantial, the benefits derived from these innovations and their acceptance in the market remain uncertain48. Consequently, cost pressures might restrict these enterprises’ capacity, prompting them to shy away from green innovation projects with higher expected returns but greater costs and risks, thus weakening the tangible emission-reduction effects of green innovations.

In contrast, enterprises with efficiency-empowered advantages typically achieve higher output efficiency and possess stronger market competitiveness alongside enhanced research and development capabilities49. These enterprises have more resources and more robust teams to pursue innovations that, although risky, offer considerable expected returns, thus solidifying their technological advantages and enhancing their market competitiveness50. Moreover, these enterprises often enjoy better short-term financial health, enabling them to pursue innovations within a more flexible framework51. In such scenarios, the synergy of relevant technological reserves, the support for scientific and technological innovation, appropriate institutional frameworks, and the development of innovative talent teams are more likely to act as positive factors promoting innovation52. Overall, efficiency-empowered advantages are conducive to reinforcing the actual emission-reduction effects of green innovation in heavily polluting industries.

H2a. Cost pressures weaken the emission-reduction effect of green innovation in heavily polluting industries.

H2b. Efficiency-empowered advantages strengthen the emission-reduction effect of green innovation in heavily polluting industries.

Cost pressures drive heavily polluting enterprises to avoid high-cost green innovations. The sensitivity to cost undoubtedly reinforces such avoidance behavior53, which inevitably impacts the actual environmental outcomes of green innovation in heavily polluting industries. Whether the actual benefits of green innovation induced by environmental regulations can outweigh the costs differentiates the “strong” and “weak” forms of the Porter hypothesis. Heavily polluting enterprises under significant cost pressures tend to have smaller profit margins and greater sensitivity to cost fluctuations, making them more susceptible to the “weak Porter hypothesis”. In terms of cost sensitivity, it is generally considered that small-scale heavily polluting enterprises are more likely to adopt price competition and strategies such as low-margin mass sales, showing greater sensitivity to cost changes54. Furthermore, heavily polluting enterprises with weaker profitability often face tighter cash flows, where even slight cost variations can significantly erode profits. Therefore, small-scale and low-profitability heavily polluting enterprises tend to adopt market survival models on the basis of cost competition, resulting in a more pronounced impact on the actual environmental effects of green innovation.

H3a. Cost pressures significantly affect the emission-reduction effect of green innovation for small-scale heavily polluting enterprises.

H3b. Cost pressures significantly affect the emission-reduction effect of green innovation in heavily polluting enterprises with weaker profitability.

Independent variable

Green Innovation (GInnos). This variable represents the green innovation activities of enterprises. It is defined on the basis of patent filings. Specifically, GInnos is the logarithm of the total number of green invention patents and green utility model patents filed by heavily polluting enterprise (i) in year (t).

Moderating variables

Cost Pressures (Oca). The operating costs of an enterprise directly reflect the expenses associated with its production and business activities. We also consider the scale differences among various heavily polluting enterprises. Thus, on the basis of the study conducted by Hu et al.59, we use the ratio of operating costs to total revenue as a proxy for cost pressures.

Efficiency-Empowered Advantages (TFP). Total factor productivity (TFP) assesses the comprehensive efficiency of an enterprise in terms of its production and resource allocation. Enterprises with higher total factor productivity typically possess more specialized research and development teams and stronger R&D capabilities, thus demonstrating greater efficiency-empowered advantages. Control variables

Drawing on the research of Liao et al.61, Wang et al.62, and Peng and Kong63, we select the following control variables: the cash-to-asset ratio (Cash), the total asset growth rate (Tagr), return on equity (Roe), Tobin’s Q (Tobin), the ratio of environmental investment to total revenue (Er), the current assets ratio (Curr), and total assets (Size). The environmental investment data are manually compiled from the “construction in progress” section disclosed in annual reports, including investments in desulfurization, denitrification, dust removal, wastewater treatment, energy conservation, and other environmental projects. To avoid excessively small figures in the regression results, we multiply the ratio of environmental investment to total revenue by 100, defining it as Er.

Sample and data source

This study utilizes data from heavily polluting industries listed in China from 2010 to 2019. The industry classification is based on the framework of the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC); the specific codes for heavily polluting industries are systematically presented in Table 1. The variable data are sourced from the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database and the China Research Data Service (CNRDS) platform. Pollutant emission intensity (LnP) and the ratio of environmental investment to total revenue (Er) are derived primarily through manual compilation. For variables with significant missing values, supplementary data are obtained from annual reports, corporate social responsibility reports, and official website information. Before the econometric analysis, listed companies with special designations such as ST and PT were excluded, and winsorization at the 1% level was performed on the variables to reduce the impact of extreme values. Additionally, for variables with standard deviations significantly larger than their means, we log-transformed these variables, including pollution intensity (LnP), green innovation (GInnos), and total assets (Size). The samples of heavily polluting industries were segmented into three equal groups on the basis of total asset size (Size): small-scale, medium-scale, and large-scale enterprises (Table 7). From Table 7, Model 1 shows that the regression coefficient of the interaction term (GInnos*Oca) is significantly positive. Conversely, in Models 2 and 3, it is not significant. The impact of cost pressures on medium-scale and large-scale heavily polluting enterprises is not significant. Small-scale enterprises are more constrained by costs, which amplifies the inhibitory effects of cost pressures. In contrast, for medium-scale and large-scale heavily polluting enterprises, financial resources tend to be more abundant, and the advantages of scale in production are more pronounced. As a result, cost pressures do not significantly affect the emission-reduction outcomes of their green innovations. Thus, cost pressures predominantly affect small-scale heavily polluting enterprises. Consequently, H3a is verified.

Profitability heterogeneity

According to the average return on assets (ROA), enterprises are categorized into two groups: those with an ROA above the average are considered to have strong profitability, whereas those with an ROA below the average are deemed to have weak profitability (Table 8). As shown in Table 8, in heavily polluting enterprises with weaker profitability, cost pressures tend to diminish the emission-reduction effect of green innovations. In contrast, for heavily polluting enterprises with stronger profitability, the impact of cost pressures on the emission-reduction effect of green innovation is not significant. This regression result corroborates H3b.

Both smaller heavily polluting enterprises and less profitable enterprises tend to be more sensitive to costs, as indicated by H3a and H3b. Our regression results show that cost pressures significantly weaken the emission-reduction effect of green innovation in these enterprises. Consequently, for them, the practical benefits of green innovation are greatly reduced. Under cost sensitivity, enterprises may struggle with environmental regulations, and they may resort to “green-washing” or deceptive practices. Although environmental regulations encourage green innovation, such innovation often fails to produce meaningful environmental benefits. It also does not generate enough profit to offset the costs, making it difficult for the “weak Porter hypothesis” to transition to the “strong Porter hypothesis”.

Regional heterogeneity analysis

On the basis of the research conducted by Yang and Peng64, we categorized enterprises into eastern, central, and western regions according to their geographical locations (Table 9). Table 9 shows that in Models 1 and 3, the coefficients of GInnos*Oca are not significant. This finding indicates that cost pressures do not affect the emission-reduction effect of green innovation in heavily polluting enterprises in the eastern and western regions. However, in Model 2, the coefficient of GInnos*Oca is significantly positive, suggesting that cost pressures markedly inhibit the emission-reduction effect of green innovation in heavily polluting enterprises in the central region. This finding aligns with the results of the research of Li et al.65, who noted that the outcomes of green innovation are more uncertain in developing regions than in developed regions. The regional disparities in China may be attributable to two primary reasons. First, the eastern coastal region is more developed, and its enterprises possess higher technological capabilities. In contrast, enterprises in the central region are less competitive, leading to differences between the central and eastern regions. Second, as industries in China’s eastern coastal areas relocate inland, the central region absorbs more production capacity from these heavily polluting enterprises than does the western region. Cost advantages help central region enterprises compete for this production capacity, but they also exacerbate cost pressures and increase cost sensitivity. These factors ultimately lead to disparities between the central and western regions.

Discussion

Heavily polluting industries often make substantial investments in fixed assets but suffer from low profit margins, making them more susceptible to pressures from the cost side. Moreover, as China is known as the “world’s factory”, these industries boast significant production capacity. Therefore, heavily polluting industries in China provide a crucial sample for this study. The empirical results indicate that cost pressures lead these industries to avoid high-cost, high-risk green innovations, despite the potential favorable environmental feedback that these innovations might offer. Nonetheless, enterprises with efficiency-empowered advantages exhibit a better reduction effect through green innovation. However, these findings are not contradictory because enterprises with efficiency-empowered advantages benefit from a higher synergistic utilization efficiency of innovation resources, which places less pressure on the cost side from green innovation. Additionally, these enterprises typically have better financial health and can withstand a certain degree of cost variation.

The core perspective of the “strong Porter hypothesis” posits that environmental regulation can stimulate green innovation within enterprises, achieving a “win–win” scenario of environmental protection and economic growth through compensatory effects. Nevertheless, traditional enterprises, particularly heavily polluting enterprises, may experience a “capability disparity” with high-tech enterprises because of the effect of technological lock-in. Consequently, the innovation achieved by these traditional enterprises often results in only “limited compensation”. In such instances, the economic returns from innovation are lower than its costs, making it challenging for these traditional enterprises to achieve the key leap prescribed by the “strong Porter hypothesis”.

The research framework in this paper suggests that environmental policy should shift from “unilateral regulation” to “cost-regulation matching” to transform limited innovation compensation under the “weak Porter hypothesis” into systematic breakthroughs under the “strong Porter hypothesis”. Furthermore, efficiency-empowered advantages are advantageous approaches to enhancing the environmental effects of green innovation.

Conclusion

In response to the challenges posed by climate change and the need for sustainable economic and social development, countries worldwide are formulating a series of environmental policies aimed at reducing corporate pollutant emissions. However, the cost characteristics of enterprises can constrain their decisions regarding green innovation, thus affecting the effectiveness of environmental policies. As the “world’s factory”, China faces significant environmental pressures, particularly from its heavily polluting industries, characterized by high costs, high energy consumption, and high emissions. This reality makes China’s heavily polluting industries a prime example of the friction between cost characteristics and environmental pressure, providing pivotal research samples for this study. This paper examines the emission-reduction effects of green innovation in China’s heavily polluting industries and compares the different impacts of cost pressures and efficiency-empowered advantages on these effects. We develop a theoretical framework to explain how cost characteristics could constrain the emission-reduction effects of green innovation, and this framework is empirically tested. Additionally, we explore the cost factors limiting the transition of the Porter hypothesis from its “weak” version to its “strong” version.

Drawing on data from listed heavily polluting enterprises in China, this study employs a dynamic panel model to investigate the impact of green innovation on pollutant emission intensity. Our findings demonstrate that green innovation effectively reduces the pollutant emission intensity of China’s heavily polluting industries. Specifically, cost pressures inhibit the emission-reduction effects of green innovation, whereas efficiency-empowered advantages enhance these effects. We find that cost pressures primarily weaken the emission-reduction effects of green innovation in small-scale or less profitable heavily polluting enterprises but do not inhibit the effects in medium- and large-scale or more profitable heavily polluting enterprises. These results suggest that the distinction between the “strong” and “weak” forms of the Porter hypothesis is often linked to enterprises’ sensitivity to costs. Furthermore, regional analysis reveals that cost pressures do not significantly affect the emission-reduction effects of green innovation in heavily polluting enterprises in the eastern and western regions but have a noticeable inhibitory effect on these enterprises in the central region. These findings have important implications for green industrial policies:

First, it is essential to reduce command-and-control policy interventions and to utilize market mechanisms for dynamic sector-specific planning. The advancement of environmental policies requires attention to the compliance pressures faced by cost-sensitive enterprises. Command-and-control interventions may lead to an excessive transmission of environmental pressures, which can compress the survival space of these enterprises and weaken overall emission-reduction efforts. This issue is particularly critical for many upstream enterprises, which operate on low-profit margins and form the backbone of the industrial chain. Excessive environmental pressures, either through rigid cost transmission or through passive survival space compression, may cause unexpected fluctuations in the industrial chain and the macroeconomy. In contrast, the use of market mechanisms to create differentiated environmental policies tailored to specific sectors, with dynamic adjustments based on market conditions, can convey appropriate compliance pressures to enterprises. This approach provides the necessary flexibility to manage the costs required to adapt to environmental regulations. Sector-specific dynamic planning through market mechanisms helps relevant departments advance environmental policies while minimizing the impacts on economic stability and maximizing the potential for green economic growth.

Second, enhancing market-driven advantages to guide corporate green demand is crucial. Environmental regulation should fully leverage the market mechanism’s strengths in price discovery and resource allocation. This strategy stimulates enterprises’ intrinsic compliance demands and internal motivation. Market mechanisms aid in scientifically determining and transmitting the costs and pressures associated with clean production, enabling the achievement of emission-reduction targets while maintaining economic stability. Additionally, green innovation and process optimization within enterprises are long-term investments with gradual returns. Market-based methods such as green bonds, green credit, carbon pricing, and carbon trading support sustained, long-term investments and backing for enterprises. These approaches foster compliance demands and motivation for green production while also mitigating the adverse effects of cost sensitivity.

Third, constructing an innovation-friendly society is essential. In the face of increasing resource consumption and environmental protection pressures, green innovation is vital for sustainable social development. However, green innovation involves high costs, long payback periods, significant uncertainty, and strong positive externalities. Therefore, it is necessary to build a society that supports innovation by promoting communication among enterprises, industries, and universities. Encouraging international competition and open cooperation will help create efficient platforms for transforming research into practical outcomes. This approach will allow for the scientific distribution of the risks and costs of innovation and the sharing of benefits. Additionally, it is important to support the full application of green innovation outcomes in actual production and to replace and eliminate outdated production capacities that do not meet current productivity standards.

This study is subject to certain limitations. First, it utilizes data from listed companies, and these data do not capture the emission-reduction characteristics of smaller heavily polluting enterprises. Although these enterprises are smaller in scale, their pollutant emission intensity per unit of production capacity may be larger. We encourage scholars to employ more diverse research samples to explore the impact of cost characteristics on these enterprises. Second, green innovation in this paper primarily refers to the application of green patents related to enterprises. However, the actual application of these patents in production also significantly affects the intensity of pollutant emissions, and determining how to scientifically construct measurement indicators requires further research. Third, the data on pollutant emission intensity are partially compiled from information disclosed by listed companies. Therefore, the influence of listed companies’ willingness to disclose information needs broader discussion.
