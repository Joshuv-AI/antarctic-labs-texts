Metagenomic insights into effect of pulse cell wall integrity on gut microbiota, CAZyme gene responses and starch/protein metabolism during in vitro fecal fermentation
Authors: Yilin Zhang, Qiang Huang, Yunus Emre Tunçil, Puspo Edi Giriwono, Zhuqing Xie, Bin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe physical structure of pulse cotyledon cells modulates gut microbiota by controlling starch and protein availability for colonic fermentation, yet the mechanisms governing the interplay between saccharolytic and proteolytic fermentation remain unclear. Here, enzymatically treated white kidney bean cotyledon cells with weakened cell walls (CWs) underwent in vitro fecal fermentations and shotgun sequencing. Impaired CWs enhanced fermentation, increased acetate and propionate production, and reduced branched-chain fatty acids (BCFAs) and ammonia. Damaged CWs upregulated CAZymes encoding genes GH4, GH15, GH126, CBM20, and CBM26, which are associated with amylase, α-glucosidases, and amyloglucosidase activities involved in starch degradation. Furthermore, amino acid pathway enrichment revealed that IhgO and csiD, involved in lysine degradation, as well as astA-E, PRODH, putA and E1.2.1.88, involved in the conversion of arginine and proline to glutamate, were upregulated. Instead, isolated protein showed the highest ammonia and BCFAs production, accompanied by elevated glutamate dehydrogenase (gudB, GLUD1_2, and E1.4.1.4), soxA and soxB, involved in serine metabolism, and DBT, involved in branched-chain amino acid degradation. These findings provide metagenomic insights into how pulse CW integrity regulates saccharolytic and proteolytic fermentation, deepening our understanding of whole pulse foods in supporting gut health. Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

The human gut hosts an intricate microbial community that plays a crucial role in influencing health1. A fundamental pathway by which gut microbes modulate host health involves their enzymatic breakdown of undigested dietary nutrients, including carbohydrates and proteins, to generate diverse microbial metabolites2. Abundant gut microbes in the proximal preferentially metabolize dietary fibers, producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) as main end-metabolites3. When readily fermentable carbohydrates are depleted, colonic microbial communities transit to proteolytic metabolism, which especially occurs in distal regions, generating not only SCFAs but also protein-derived products such as branched-chain fatty acids (BCFAs), ammonia, sulfides, phenol, and indole4. This dynamic balance between carbohydrate and protein fermentation in the gut profoundly impacts microbial composition and activity, with SCFAs particularly butyrate demonstrating significant benefits (e.g., fueling enterocyte metabolism, reinforcing epithelial integrity, and suppressing inflammation)5, while protein-derived metabolites are more frequently associated with adverse effects6.

Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), encoded extensively by the gut microbiome, represent diverse enzyme families critical for dietary fiber deconstruction7. The gut microbiome harbors over 136 glycoside hydrolase (GH) genes, compared to only 98 GH genes in the human genome, with only a small portion of the latter involved in dietary glycan processing8. The majority of CAZymes genes encode enzymes for glycosidic-bond cleavage, including GH and polysaccharide lyases (PLs)9. Additionally, carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs) assist CAZymes by binding to carbohydrate ligands and enhancing their enzymatic activity10. Elucidation of the CAZyme gene responses during colonic fermentation can facilitate comprehension of the mechanisms by which gut bacteria break down and utilize complex carbohydrates.

Natural fibers vary in structure due to their botanical origins and processing methods, containing complex mixtures of ill-defined glycans, as well as unspecified proteins, lipids, and small molecules11. Pulses serve as a collective term for non-oilseed legumes known for their rich nutritional profile (i.e., high starch, protein, fiber, and mineral levels). The fundamental structure of pulse seeds resides in their cotyledon cell, whose wall is primarily composed of cellulose, xyloglucan, and pectin. Within this architectural framework, cellulose-xyloglucan networks form load-bearing microfibrils that are firmly attached to the pectic matrix. Moreover, starch in pulses is enclosed within protein and further enclosed by cell wall (CWs)12. The structural properties and processing methods (such as milling and cooking) of pulses can affect cotyledon cell integrity, consequently altering the accessibility of starch and protein13. It has been reported that dietary patterns containing legume extracts and fibers have been linked to gut-modulating effects, notably an increased abundance of Bifidobacterium and Faecalibacterium14, as the intact CW structure serves as a physical barrier and resists digestion, leaving nutrients like starch and proteins to be delivered to the large colon for bacterial fermentation15. It has been found that the impaired CW integrity promoted the abundance of Blautia and Roseburia and butyrate production in vitro16. Metagenomic sequencing allows for the comprehensive analysis of microbial communities, deciphering the taxonomic structure and metabolic capabilities encoded within the microbiome’s functional gene repertoire. By applying shotgun sequencing to stool samples from obese individuals consuming pea or orange fiber snacks, the detailed response of microbial CAZyme genes GH and PL was revealed17. While the impact of CW integrity in regulating nutrient availability and its subsequent impact on microbiota and metabolite production is well established, the interplay between carbohydrates and protein fermentation in the context of whole pulse food along with the underlying mechanisms, remains to be elucidated.

This study aims to elucidate the mechanisms driving the fermentation outcomes of cotyledon cells with modified CW integrity, with particular emphasis on how alterations in microbial functional genes related to carbohydrate degradation and amino acid metabolism regulate the formation of SCFAs, ammonia, and BCFAs. We hypothesized that CW integrity changes modulate the expression of functional genes related to carbohydrate-degrading (e.g., GHs, CBMs) and amino acid metabolism, thereby shaping microbial communities and their metabolic activity during colonic fermentation. Intact cotyledon cells were separated from white kidney beans, and CW integrity was adjusted through enzymatic hydrolysis. All isolated cotyledon cells and purified components were subjected to dry heat treatment (180 °C, 20 min) prior to fermentation. Gas production, concentrations of SCFAs, BCFAs, and ammonia during fermentation were analyzed. Concurrently, metagenomic shotgun sequencing provides taxonomic and functional characteristics of gut microbial communities. To disclose the influence of fiber-protein interactions on microbiota and metabolism, the changes in microbial genes related to fiber degradation (GH and CBMs) and amino acid metabolism were characterized against the CAZymes and KEGG databases, respectively. The results provide mechanistic understanding of the interplay between pulse CW integrity and microbial fermentation, shedding light on the equilibrium between saccharolytic and proteolytic fermentation in the context of whole pulse food models.

Results and Discussion

Fermentation rate

Figure 1 presents the gas production of different samples and control groups (isolated starch, protein and CW). The gas production curves are typically used as indicators of fermentation rate18. Dry-heat-treated starch samples showed rapid gas production profiles throughout the fermentation period, which can be attributed to the relatively easily accessible nature of starch for microbial fermentation19. In contrast, dry heat-treated pulse proteins had the lowest gas production, indicating minimal fermentation by gut microbiota, which aligns with previous in vitro fermentation findings16,20. CW exhibited a restricted fermentation rate, likely due to its structural properties limiting microbial utilization21,22. The fecal inocula demonstrated limited fermentative utilization of IC, and its gas production was markedly lower than that of CW at 24 h of fermentation. In contrast, the formation of enzyme-generated surface micropores critically improved fermentation kinetics in SDC and HDC over IC. Enhanced microbial access to intracellular substrates and diffusion efficiency of secreted enzymes were achieved through targeted degradation of cellular structures16. Following dry heat treatment, the total starch, protein, and dietary fiber contents of the samples were determined, with the corresponding results presented in Table S1. The results indicate that dry heat processing disrupted the cell structure, leading to enhanced starch accessibility, as evidenced by an increase in total starch content compared with pre-treatment values. Furthermore, with increasing degrees of CW disruption, total starch content progressively increased (from 63% to 75%), whereas protein (from 13% to 6%) and total dietary fiber (from 23% to 17%) decreased accordingly.

Fermentation metabolite profiles

The concentrations of SCFAs, BCFAs and ammonia released from different tested samples are shown in Fig. 2 and Table S2. It was noteworthy that the heat-treated starch showed the highest total SCFA yield, characterized by the substantial production of acetate, propionate, and butyrate. This corresponds to previous reports that RS2 effectively produces butyrate which benefits colonocytes as an energy source and helps maintain intestinal health23–25. In contrast, protein fermentation resulted in limited SCFA production but yielded the highest levels of BCFAs, as evidenced by the elevated production of isobutyrate and isovalerate. SCFAs are primarily produced through saccharolytic fermentation, although certain amino acids or proteinaceous substrates can also contribute to their production. Isobutyrate and isovalerate, exclusive products of colonic fermentation of valine and isoleucine26, have not been directly reported with adverse effects. However, it was shown that the BCFAs produced from protein fermentation showed weaker abilities in modulating immune response relative to the SCFA butyrate27. Consistent with the gas production profiles, CW exhibited relatively lower SCFA production when compared to starch, further implying that the structure of purified pulse CW limited the microbial metabolism. IC showed lower total SCFA production (acetate and propionate) in comparison to CW, and with the increase of the impaired CW integrity, the production of acetate and propionate was promoted. This result suggests that the intact cellular structure restricted the fermentation and subsequent metabolites28. When CW integrity was impaired by enzymatic process, intracellular nutrients were more inclined to be exposed and utilized by gut bacteria for producing SCFAs. Instead, the lower BCFA production in HDC and SDC samples might be partly associated with the decreased protein levels resulting from the enzymic treatment16.

The ammonia production profiles were opposite to SCFAs, with protein fermentation producing the highest amount of ammonia in each stage. In contrast, heat-treated starch led to the lowest ammonia levels at 24 h across all samples. Ammonia production during fermentation gradually decreased as CW integrity diminished in IC, SDC, and HDC. As one of the major protein fermentation products, ammonia has been shown to reduce intestinal barrier function and drive dendritic cells into dysfunction29,30. Ammonia has also been associated with histological damage to the colonic mucus layer, inhibition of butyrate oxidation, and promotion of pro-inflammatory cytokines31. Hence, the downregulation of ammonia production through impairing the pulse cell integrity highlights the potential to mitigate the adverse effects of protein fermentation by enhancing microbial access to intracellular starch, which may shift fermentation pathways toward more SCFA production.

Microbial diversity and composition

Figure 3 visualizes the alpha diversity metrics that quantified microbial community richness and evenness in different substrates at 8 h and 24 h. At the 8 h of fermentation, IC, SDC, and HDC exhibited significantly higher observed species and Shannon indices than starch, indicating that the complex pulse components are more conducive to shifting the microbiota community compared to the single carbohydrate component. Protein fermentation exhibited relatively higher alpha diversity indexes compared to starch fermentation, suggesting the involvement of a more diverse bacterial community in protein metabolism within the colon, which aligns with the diverse range of metabolic outputs produced32. Notably, the Shannon and Simpson indices of CW significantly decreased by the end of fermentation, suggesting a substantial rise in the abundance proportion of certain bacterial genera at the end of fermentation.

Relative abundance dynamics of bacterial taxa at fermentation timepoints (8 h, 24 h) are presented in Fig. 4. Phylum-level analysis revealed the Firmicutes A, Firmicutes, Firmicutes C and Bacteroidota collectively account for over 80% of the composition (Fig. 4a, b). At the 8 h of fermentation, all groups exhibited elevated Bacteroidota abundance relative to baseline. The starch and CW samples exhibited the highest proportions of Bacteroidota at 8 h of fermentation (~30%), mainly due to the fact that Bacteroidetes are primary degraders of polysaccharides33. At the end of fermentation, the abundance of Bacteroidota further increased to 45% for the CW sample, which is consistent with the finding that Bacteroidetes species have large genomes and more GH for complex carbohydrate degradation33. Bacteroidota abundance rose at both 8 h and 24 h with compromised CW integrity, likely driven by enhanced fermentation of released intracellular starch.

PCA analysis revealing distinct clustering patterns among the substrates is shown in Fig. 4c, with results for a single sampling time presented in Figure S1. The PCA plot captured 61.87% of the multivariate variance (PC1:36.62% and PC2:25.25%) in microbial communities among the top 50 taxa across all substrates. Samples fermented at 8 h and 24 h exhibited a clear separation along the PC1 axis. At 8 h of fermentation, control groups starch, protein, and CW exhibited distinct clustering patterns. At 24 h, SDC and HDC clustered with starch in the first quadrant of the PCA plot, reflecting their shared microbial profiles. Figure 4d illustrates the changes in genus-level microbiota composition. Relative to baseline, starch fermentation enhanced the abundance of Clostridium, Blautia A, Fusobacterium A, Bacteroides, and Phocaeicola at 8 or 24 h. Conversely, protein fermentation samples promoted the abundance of Veillonella (8 h) and Lowsonibacter (24 h). CW especially increased the Choladousia, Bacteroides, and unclassified Bacteroidaceae at 8 or 24 h of fermentation, which is similar to IC (8 h).

LEfSe analysis with an LDA score >3.64 or 3.88 was applied to identify differentially abundant taxa. As shown in Fig. 4e, the starch group promoted the abundance of Clostridium sp000753455, Phacaeicola vulgatus, Mediterraneibacter faecis, and Bacteroides fragilis A at 8 h of fermentation. Some species of the genus Clostridium belong to Clostridium cluster IV and XIVa groups, which are good fiber utilizers34. Phocaeicola has been noted to be involved in breaking down complex carbohydrates in the gut35, which might be associated with the fast fermentation rate and high butyrate content after starch fermentation (Figs. 1 and 2). Bacteroides fragilis exhibits dual roles in host health, with recent studies highlighting its protective effects, such as ameliorating colitis in murine model36,37. As for protein, LEfSe analysis found that Veillonella was different at 8 h of fermentation, and Alistipes_A was different at 24 h of fermentation. Veillonella were considered potential pathobionts implicated in diverse pathologies38,39. Kaur et al.40 found that Alistipes exhibited the most extensive putrefactive metabolic capacity among commensals, contributing to both histidine degradation and tetrahydrofolate, indole, and phenol production. CW especially increased the Bacteroides at both 8 h and 24 h. Bacteroides were considered as a key polysaccharide degrader dependent on ecological cross-feeding41,42. At species level, the abundance of Choladousia sp902363135 was different at 8 h and Bacteroides xylanisolvens and Bacteroides ovatus were different at 24 h of fermentation of CW samples. B. xylanisolvens is known for its ability to metabolize hemicellulose into acetate, propionate, and succinate43, which is consistent with the high total SCFA production of CW compared to protein as observed in Fig. 2g.

IC presented a significantly increased abundance of Acetatifactor sp90006565 at 24 h of fermentation. Acetatifactor genus has been implicated in takeda G-protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) agonism via bile acid signaling, inducing glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) secretion that mediates anti-obesity effects through enteroendocrine axis modulation44. The LEfSe analysis further revealed that Blautia_A and Fusobacterium_A were significantly differential genera in SDC, consistent with the genus-level taxonomic composition analysis (Fig. 4d). In HDC, the Parabacteroides distasonis differed significantly. P. distasonis is characterized by succinate and secondary bile acid products. Oral supplementation of P. distasonis CGMCC 1.30169 ameliorated obesity-associated metabolic abnormalities in mice, as evidenced by suppressed body mass gain, reduced adipocyte hypertrophy and lowered hepatic lipid profiles45.

CAZymes encoding genes and correlation with metabolite concentration

The CAZyme encoding genes from microbial communities were characterized and annotated by family46. Differentially abundant GH or CBM genes across all substrates at either of the two time points were incorporated into Fig. 5. Combined with RDA analysis as seen in Figure S2, CW exhibited a distinct CAZyme-encoding gene profile among the tested substrates with the characterization of a high abundance of GH2, GH16, GH35, GH78, and CBM6 after 24 h of fermentation. The highly abundant genes GH2, GH16, GH35, and GH78 correspond to β-galactosidase/β-galactanase, α-L-rhamnohydrolase, and xyloglucanase—enzymes involved in pectin and hemicellulose degradation47–50. CBM6, which is commonly found in enzymes targeting xylan, mannan, β-glucans, and arabinans51, was markedly enriched in the CW group after fermentation, aligning with the presence of these polysaccharides in plant CWs. Further, the enrichment of these GH families aligns with the high abundance of B. ovatus and B. xylanisolvens (Fig. 4f). B. ovatus has been shown to exhibit GH2 activity and is capable of degrading galactomannan, glucomannan, and xylan52. B. xylanisolvens is linked to GH16 and GH35 which correspond to xyloglucanase and β-galactanase activities53.

In the IC group, GH families involved in fiber degradation remained highly abundant at 8 h but declined after 24 h of fermentation. Notably, GH12, which plays a role in processing xyloglucan and cellulose54, was detected at relatively high levels at 8 h, indicating sustained fiber degradation during the early fermentation stage. In contrast, SDC, HDC, and starch samples exhibited a pronounced upregulation of GH4, GH15, GH126, CBM20, and CBM26, most of which are associated with starch degradation55–58. The significant elevation in GH4 gene abundance showed potential co-occurrence with Fusobacterium A prevalence shifts, consistent with this taxon’s enriched abundance patterns in SDC, HDC, and starch groups (Fig. 4d, e). Prior studies have identified GH4-encoding capabilities within Fusobacterium A strains59. The increase in GH126 was potentially primarily driven by Phocaeicola vulgatus35 and Parabacteroides distasonis60 and both are known for their strong starch utilization capacity, which aligns with their enriched abundance in starch (8 h) and HDC (24 h), as shown in Fig. 4e-f. Notably, lower CW integrity corresponded to significantly higher levels of GH4 and GH15 after 24 h of fermentation, further suggesting the importance of these genes and related enzymes in intracellular starch degradation.

The correlation between CAZyme-encoding genes and metabolite production is depicted in Fig. 5b, c. At 8 h, GH2, GH16, GH78, GH15, GH126, CBM20, CBM26, and CBM6 exhibited strong positive correlations with acetate and propionate production, while butyrate levels were positively associated with GH4, GH15, GH126, CH126, CBM20, CBM26, and CBM5. By 24 h, GH4 and CBM26 showed the strongest positive correlations with acetate, propionate, and butyrate, whereas GH15, GH126, CBM20, and CBM5 also maintained significant associations with SCFA production. Notably, GH15, a key debranching enzyme, emerged as a potential predictor of SCFA production, highlighting its role in accessing recalcitrant starch fractions and facilitating fermentation57. Additionally, GH4 catalyzes hydrolysis of maltose 6-phosphate and sucrose 6-phosphate into glucose 6-phosphate and aglycones, supporting glycolytic energy production61. These findings underscore both different and shared CAZyme-encoding genes in the different fermentation stages, emphasizing their critical involvement in acetate, propionate, and butyrate production of these samples (Fig. 2a–c and Table S1). Although fiber degradation in the CW group sustained the proliferation of Bacteroides and the related CAZyme genes, its complex structure restricted the production efficiency of SCFAs (Fig. 2a–c).

Pathways of amino acid metabolism and the associated KO genes

A KO is defined as a functional unit that consists of a group of orthologous genes. Figure 6 illustrates the relative abundances of 59 significantly enriched (p < 0.05) KO genes associated with amino acid metabolism across all tested substrates, with the corresponding metabolic pathway modules shown in the right panel. Amino acid catabolism predominantly involves the transfer of amino groups to α-ketoglutarate via transaminases, leading to the formation of glutamate, which is subsequently deaminated by glutamate dehydrogenase, releasing ammonia62,63. Metagenomic analysis revealed that within the alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism pathway, the abundances of gudB, GLUD1_2, and E1.4.1.4 (glutamate dehydrogenase, K00260–K00262) were noticeably higher in the protein and IC groups relative to the SDC, HDC, and starch groups. This pattern is consistent with the elevated ammonia concentrations observed in Fig. 2h. Regarding serine metabolism, the protein group exhibited significantly higher abundances of soxA and soxB (K00302–K00303) than the IC group after 24 h of fermentation, further supporting the increased ammonia production. Additionally, the highest production of BCFAs was detected in the protein group (Fig. 2d–f). Correspondingly, a significant upregulation of DBT (K09699), a key gene in the valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation pathway involved in BCFA production, was observed in the IC and protein groups at 24 h of fermentation. White kidney beans are rich in lysine64, and the expression levels of KO genes associated with lysine degradation, such as K15736 IhgO and K15737 csiD, were noticeably enhanced in the cell groups relative to the starch group where the expression remained very low. Lysine degradation leads to the production of succinate which serves as an intermediate for further propionate and acetate production65. This process may partially contribute to the SCFA pool in the cell samples, although CAZymes and their corresponding genes likely play a more substantial role. In the SDC and HDC groups, the abundances of genes (astA, astB, astC, astD, astE, PRODH, putA, and E1.2.1.88) involved in the conversion of arginine and proline to glutamate were significantly increased. Previous studies have demonstrated that anaerobic bacteria can metabolize glutamate into acetate and butyrate66, which could partly account for the elevated acetate concentrations present in the SDC and HDC samples, as shown in Fig. 2a.

Methionine and cysteine degradation are the primary pathways for hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) production by gut bacteria67. Here, we observed a notable increase in K01251 AHCY in the protein and IC groups after 24 h of fermentation, suggesting a potential elevation in sulfide compound production. Regarding aromatic amino acid metabolism, the SDC and HDC samples particularly promoted the expression of tnaA (K09699), a key gene involved in tryptophan metabolism, as well as several genes, including K02609 and K02618, associated with phenylalanine metabolism68,69. Although this study did not detect the products from gut bacterial fermentation of sulfur- containing and aromatic amino acids, changes in the relevant pathways may imply alterations in the corresponding metabolites.

In this study, enzymatically treated cotyledon cells with varying degrees of CW disruption, along with isolated starch and protein fractions, were subjected to dry heat treatment and subsequently used to simulate colonic fermentation. It should be noted that the in vitro digestion may alter the structural integrity of the samples entering the colon, potentially affecting microbial composition and metabolite profiles. Nevertheless, the primary aim of this study was to specifically evaluate the impact of intrinsic cell structure on microbial fermentation. Further in vivo investigations, particularly in human subjects, are warranted to elucidate how microbial functional genes, particularly those involved in carbohydrate and protein metabolism, regulate the balance between saccharolytic and proteolytic fermentation in the colon and ultimately determine metabolite profiles.

Taken together, this study provides metagenomic insights into how pulse CW integrity regulates starch and protein fermentation. We find that enzymatic disruption of CWs accelerated fermentation rates, favoring saccharolytic metabolism with elevated acetate and propionate production while suppressing proteolytic pathways linked to ammonia and BCFA generation. Microbial community shifts—marked by the enrichment of Blautia A, Fusobacterium A, and Phocaeicola—clustered closely with starch-driven profiles, diverging from protein or intact CW groups. Functional analyses revealed that compromised CWs upregulated CAZymes encoding genes GH4, GH15, GH126, CBM20, and CBM26 involved in starch hydrolysis, alongside pathways for lysine degradation and arginine/proline-to-glutamate conversion. In contrast, protein fermentation elevated ammonia/BCFA levels and enhanced glutamate dehydrogenases (gudB, GLUD1_2), serine metabolism genes (soxA, soxB), and DBT involved in valine and isoleucine degradation. These findings underscore the role of pulse microstructure in directing microbial resource allocation, bridging dietary fiber complexity with microbiota functionality.

Methods

Materials

White kidney beans (Phaseolus vulgaris) were purchased from Chuxiong Trading Co., Ltd (Kunming, China). Viscozyme®L (V2010, 100 FBGU/g), a multi-enzyme blend (arabinosanase, cellulase, β- galactanases, hemicellulase, and xylanase) and 4-methylvaleric acid (No.277827) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Ltd (St. Louis, MO, USA). In all experimental setups, analytical-grade chemicals were utilized.

Preparation of white kidney bean cells with different degrees of cell wall integrity

Intact cotyledon cells were isolated from white kidney bean seeds using a modified method reported by Wu et al.70. Seeds were soaked in 0.1 M Na2CO3 solutions overnight at room temperature and dehulled. The dehulled pulse seeds were subjected to a mechanical grinding process using a blender, and the resultant paste was then filtered through sieves with mesh sizes of 54 and 150 μm to collect intact cell (IC) for the subsequent lyophilization process.

Cell wall integrity modulation followed an adapted method by Guan et al.16. Per gram of intact cell powder (dry weight), 10 mL of acetate buffer (pH 5.5) was added, followed by 500 μL of Viscozyme®L. The mixture was subjected to homogenization and subsequently placed in an water bath at 45 °C: 0.5 h for slightly damaged cells (SDC) or 1 h for highly damaged cells (HDC). Integrity was verified microscopically before collecting fractions on 54 μm sieves for lyophilization.

The physico-chemical properties of white kidney bean cells, including c-type crystalline structure, particle size (D50 124.3 μm), total starch content (60.5%), and CW galacturonic acid levels (28.2%), were well characterized in previous study70.

Isolation of cell wall, starch, and protein components

Cell wall, protein and starch components were purified using a method adapted from Guan et al.16. In brief, the decorticated cotyledons were pulverized into a paste using a blender and sieved (54 μm) to retain CW fragments, allowing starch and protein passage. The residue underwent a series of enzymatic treatments, initiated by thermostable α-amylase (0.05 mL/g DM) at 60 °C for 1 h, followed by amyloglucosidase (0.1 mL/g DM) at 60 °C for 3 h to hydrolyze starch. Subsequent protein removal was performed via protease digestion (0.1 mL/g DM) at pH 6.0 and 50 °C for 12 h. The resulting fraction passing 45 μm mesh was subjected to alkaline extraction by adjusting to pH 8.0–9.0 with continuous stirring for 1.5 h. The upper layer was harvested and changed to a pH of 4.5 to precipitate protein. The precipitated suspension was centrifuged at 4000 g for 10 min. The starch pellet was isolated by resuspension and repeated centrifugation under the same conditions, resulting in starch with a final purity of 96.8%. Proteins underwent dialysis prior to lyophilization. The extracted starch was composed primarily of α-1,4- and α-1,6-linked glucose polymers.

Dry heat treatment

White kidney bean cells with differing degrees of integrity and distinct isolated components (protein, CW elements, and starch) were subjected to an oven temperature of 180 °C for 20 min in order to simulate the baking condition.

The total starch content before and after dry heat treatment was determined by means of the Total Starch Assay Kit (K-TSTA, Megazyme, Bray, Ireland). The quantification of protein content was conducted through the utilization of an elemental analyser (vario EL cube; Elementar, Germany), employing a nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor of 6.25. Total dietary fiber content was analyzed using the Total Dietary Fiber Assay Kit (K-TDFR, Megazyme, Bray, Ireland). The determined components of different samples are shown in Table S1.

In vitro fecal fermentation

The fecal samples used in this study were collected with informed consent, and the privacy rights of participants were fully protected. The experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou First People’s Hospital (registration number K-2021-091-01) and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Three healthy volunteers meeting stringent inclusion criteria were enrolled, including normal body mass index values, no history of digestive system diseases or dietary taboos, probiotic/prebiotic abstinence ≥30 days prior, and antibiotic-free status for 90 days preceding the study.

In vitro fecal fermentation was conducted in an anaerobic chamber (HYQX-II, Yuejin Medical Devices Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) as described71. Briefly, the cell samples along with purified protein, CW fractions, and starch were accurately portioned as 50 mg aliquots (dry weight basis) into serum bottles, followed by addition of 4 mL sterile carbonate-phosphate buffer. A pooled fecal inoculum was prepared by homogenizing donor specimens (1:3 w/v in buffer) followed by four-layer cheesecloth filtration. Each serum bottle received 1 mL fecal inoculum, then underwent 37°C incubation with sampling at 4, 8, 12, and 24 h. Triplicate batches were maintained throughout. Gas production was quantified via syringe penetration through rubber septa at designated intervals. After manual agitation, the supernatants were separated by 12,000 g centrifugation for 10 min and analyzed for BCFA, ammonia, and SCFA. The pellets were preserved for subsequent DNA isolation and shotgun sequencing. All of the samples were frozen at −80 °C pending downstream analyses.

Determination of metabolites

800 µL of the supernatant obtained after centrifugation was mixed with 200 µL of internal standard solution (50 mM 4-methylvaleric acid dissolved in 5% (w/v) metaphosphoric acid), mixed thoroughly, and filtered into an amber sample vial for subsequent analysis. SCFA were quantified via gas chromatography (Agilent Technologies, Inc. Santa Clara, CA) using a ZB-FFAP capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Zebron). The oven temperature was programmed as follows: initial hold at 80 °C, followed by a gradient ramp of 8 °C/min to 192 °C, 3 min hold. N2 (1 mL/min) served as the carrier gas, with detector and injector temperatures maintained at 230 °C. Quantification was achieved using 4-methylvaleric acid as internal standard72. Ammonia contents were investigated using the method described by Galvão et al.73. The fermentation supernatant was combined with a solution containing 10 g/L phenol, 5.0 g/L sodium hydroxide, 0.42 g/L sodium hypochlorite, and 0.05 g/L sodium nitroprusside. Following a 30-min incubation period at ambient temperature, the absorbance was examined by spectrophotometric analysis at a wavelength of 625 nm.

DNA Extraction and shotgun sequencing

DNA were extracted from fermentation samples using the OMEGA Mag-Bind Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02, Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instruction. After assessing DNA concentration and integrity, qualified DNA underwent metagenomic shotgun library prep via Illumina TruSeq Nano Kit (Illumina, USA) with 400 bp insert size. Paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed on Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, USA) with 5.0 Gb raw data generation per sample.

Metagenome raw data quality control

Raw sequencing data were quality-filtered using fastp software to detect 3’ adapter sequences and truncate reads at splice junctions, ensuring analytical reliability for downstream studies74. Matches to the splice sequence (R1: AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCA; R2: AGATCGGAAGAGCGTCGTGTAGGGAAAGAGTGT) were required to possess a minimum length of 3 bp and to permit up to 20% base mismatch. Initial 3′ adapter sequences were trimmed prior to quality filtering via a 5-bp sliding window approach starting from the 5′ end. Reads were truncated at the first window position where average Phred scores fell below Q20 ( ≥ 99% accuracy). Subsequent filtering removed reads shorter than 50 bp and those matching to the human genome through Minimap2 (v2.24-r1122)75.

Taxonomic profiling

The taxonomy of quality-filtered reads was determined by the Kraken2 database76 against the GTDB database77. The metagenomic assembly was executed using the Megahit (v1.1.2) software with the meta-large preset78. Mmseqs2 with “easy-linclust” mode at 95% sequence identity and 90% coverage thresholds was utilized to process contigs exceeding 300 bp79. Non-redundant clusters were taxonomically annotated via mmseqs2 against the NCBI-nt database.

Functional profiling

Genes were predicted using Prodigal (v2.6.3)80. The filtered and processed protein sequences were compared with standard protein databases for functional analysis across all samples. The high-quality reads were aligned to the predicted gene sequences using Minimap275 with the “-ax sr--sam-hit-only” setting to ensure the most accurate alignment possible. FeatureCounts quantified read counts per gene, generating gene-level abundance profiles81. Non-redundant genes were functionally annotated using mmseqs2 against KEGG and CAZy databases, as well as Diamond (v2.0.15) against CARD and Metacyc databases. As a result, functional analyses identified 4,564,905 KEGG-annotated genes and 2921,944 genes were annotated with the CAZy database. Gene abundances were aggregated into 7242 KEGG Orthology groups, representing functional units defined by the KEGG database.

Microbiome analysis and statistics

Data shown in Figs. 1–3 were expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests. Data visualization was implemented through GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Significant changes were set to be those with p < 0.05. Microbiome analysis was conducted in R studio (v.4.3.1) using the Phyloseq82 and ggplot283. α-diversity metrics were calculated and compared between groups at different fermentation times. The ten most abundant phyla and twenty dominant genera were visualized through bar plots and heatmaps, respectively. PCA based on Euclidean distance was implemented via the ggplot2 package. To test significantly changed taxa and functional features among sample groups, LEfSe was employed under default settings84. GH or CMB genes with an abundance of 5% or higher and those that exhibited significant differences in at least one of the two fermentation stages were shown in heatmap. Redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed using the vegan package85. Correlations between CAZymes and the metabolites were examined by Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Amino acid metabolism pathway from KEGG database was enriched, and significant KO (KEGG Orthology) gene abundances was presented in heatmap. Pathway modules were adjusted from KEGG pathway maps, and those not attributable to bacterial synthesis or degradation processes were eliminated.
