Cu Anchored Carbon Nitride (Cu/CN) Catalyzes Selective Oxidation of Thiol by Controlling Reactive Oxygen Species Generation
Authors: Hyunwoo Choi, Sumin Kim, Minjoon Kwak, Yunki Gwak, Keunhong Jeong, Youngran Seo, Dongwon Yoo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProduction of H2O2 using heterogeneous semiconductor photocatalysts has emerged as an ecofriendly and practical approach across various applications, ranging from environmental detoxification to fuel cells and chemical synthesis. Extensive efforts have been devoted to engineering semiconductors to enhance their catalytic capabilities for H2O2 production. However, in chemical synthesis, the utilization of the potent oxidant H2O2 can present challenges in selectively oxidizing organic compounds. In this study, we introduce copper atoms into carbon nitride (Cu/CN), facilitating the generation of hydroperoxyl radicals (·OOH) as primary reactive oxidants and offering reaction conditions entirely devoid of H2O2 via the Fenton reaction. Cu/CN demonstrates selective oxidation of thiols to disulfides, in contrast to other current heterogeneous photocatalysts that yield undesired overoxidized side products, such as thiosulfinate and thiosulfonate. Cu/CN’s controllable capacity for specific ROS generation, broad substrate scopes, and recyclability empower greener and highly selective photooxidation of organic compounds.Keywords: photoaerobic oxidation, carbon nitride, specific thiol oxidation, disulfide formation, hydroperoxyl radical, oxygen reduction pathway

Introduction

Selective and partial oxidation reactions stand as cornerstone methodologies in chemical synthesis, serving as indispensable tools for modifying functional groups within molecules with precision, thus avoiding overoxidation and minimizing side reactions.1−8 This precision is pivotal in ensuring the efficiency of the synthesis and achieving the desired molecular structures. These reactions find widespread application across diverse domains, including pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, natural products, and other vital organic compounds, underscoring their importance in modern synthetic chemistry.9,10

In recent years, there has been a notable shift in focus toward photocatalytic oxidations utilizing heterogeneous semiconductors, driven by their ability to operate under mild reaction conditions. This aspect reduces the dependence on harsh reagents or elevated temperatures, aligning with the principles of green chemistry by minimizing environmental impact and waste generation.11−13 Extensive research has focused on enhancing the catalytic performance of semiconductors for reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, especially hydrogen peroxide (H2O2).14,15 Among these semiconductors, graphitic carbon nitride (CN) has garnered significant attention due to its proper band structure, stability, easy preparation, and high selectivity toward H2O2 production.16−24 Various approaches have been proposed to improve the catalytic performance of CN for H2O2 generation, including nanostructure tailoring,25 heterojunction construction,26−28 doping,20,29 or defect modifications.30 Despite achieving high H2O2 productivity, the direct utilization of this potent oxidant presents challenges in achieving selective oxidation without inducing further or complete oxidation of organic functional groups. For example, the oxidation of thiols produces a range of products, such as disulfides, thiosulfinate, thiosulfonate, and sulfonic acids.31,32 Among these, disulfides are especially noteworthy for their wide-ranging applications in pharmaceutical synthesis, the construction of functional materials, and for elucidating the biological functions of proteins.33−36 Given the versatile utility of disulfides, the selective oxidative conversion of thiols continues to be a topic of enduring interest.

To address this, various methods have been developed, including the use of homogeneous oxidants37 such as halogens,38 DDQ,39N-bromo-derivatives,40 and HNO3.41 While photocatalytic oxidations offer environmentally benign and energy-efficient alternatives to those traditional stoichiometric oxidants, it still requires additives37,42−44 or an oxygen-free environment45,46 to minimize the generation of significant amounts of overoxidized side products which limits their application in chemical synthesis (Figure 1 and Table S1). In addressing this challenge, we developed a carbon nitride system integrated with copper atoms (Cu/CN) tailored for the specific oxidation of thiols to disulfides. Through comprehensive experimental and computational analyses, we elucidated that Cu/CN facilitates the selective formation of hydroperoxyl radicals (·OOH) as primary reactive oxidants, providing reaction conditions entirely free from H2O2. This capability enabled Cu/CN to produce disulfides as the sole product, while other current photocatalysts (e.g., CdS, Eosin Y, and CN) demonstrated significant overoxidation due to the generation of H2O2. Cu/CN also exhibits a wide substrate scope and excellent recyclability with no copper leaching. The successful demonstration of Cu/CN′s photocatalytic ability for selective oxidations presents a promising pathway toward greener and more efficient catalytic systems for various chemical synthesis endeavors.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of Cu-Anchored Carbon Nitride (Cu/CN)

The synthesis of carbon nitrides involved a two-step protocol combining supramolecular preorganization followed by calcination under an Ar atmosphere (Figure S1). Initially, carbon nitride (CN) was synthesized through the formation of a supramolecular complex between melamine and cyanuric acid in a 1:1 ratio.47 Subsequently, the introduction of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O during the preorganization step led to the production of Cu-anchored carbon nitrides (Cu/CN).48 Two variations of Cu loading were investigated, resulting in the preparation of 0.4 and 2.4 wt % copper incorporated carbon nitrides (i.e., 4-Cu/CN and 24-Cu/CN, respectively), as confirmed by inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES) analysis (Table S2).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images showed the porous nanosheet structures of all synthesized carbon nitrides (Figure S2). Further characterization through transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) revealed ultrathin structures for both CN and Cu/CN, with no observable copper agglomerates on the latter (Figure 2a and Figures S3–S4). The absence of diffraction rings corresponding to Cu particles provides further evidence that the Cu atoms are uniformly distributed throughout the CN matrix (see the inset in Figure 2a). Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping in scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) mode confirmed the atomically well-dispersed nature of copper within Cu/CN (Figure 2b and Figure S5). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Cu/CN exhibited characteristic peaks corresponding to the CN structure, particularly the in-plane motif (100) at 12.8° and the interlayer stacking (002) at approximately 27.5° (Figure S6).49,50 Despite slight adjustments in interlayer spacing due to copper inclusion, the CN structure remained largely intact. Particularly, XRD patterns did not reveal peaks associated with Cu particles (e.g., Cu2O, CuO, or metallic Cu),51,52 affirming the uniform distribution of copper within the support material. Fourier transform-infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy displayed characteristic peaks indicative of heterocycles and the tri-s-triazine structure across all synthesized carbon nitrides (Figure S7).48

From X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis, the chemical environment and local structures of CN and Cu/CNs were thoroughly scrutinized by identifying C and N atoms, along with additional O atoms from adsorbed H2O (532.3 eV) on the catalysts (Figure S8).48,53 The C 1s spectrum of CN was divided into three peaks: one originated from the surface defects (284.7 eV), and two peaks from tri-s-triazine rings, representing sp2 C–NH on the periphery (286.6 eV) and N–C=N within the aromatic rings (288.1 eV). In the N 1s spectrum, three peaks were discerned from the tri-s-triazine rings: one from the periphery (400.9 eV), and two from the rings, corresponding to sp3 N–C3 (399.2 eV) and sp2 C–N=C (398.5 eV).54 Additionally, weak detection of π–π* excitation occurred at 403.6 eV in CN and 404.0 eV in 24-Cu/CN. The introduction of copper resulted in a subtle decrease in electron density at the sp2 C and sp2 N positions within the tri-s-triazine rings, indicating their interaction with Cu atoms. This suggests that Cu atoms are primarily stabilized by graphitic C and pyridinic N.48 The Cu 2p XPS spectrum of 24-Cu/CN exhibited two distinctive peaks at 932.7 and 952.3 eV, corresponding to the binding energies of Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2, respectively (Figure 2c).52 No satellite peak for CuII was observed around 945 eV, nor in 4-Cu/CN (Figure S8). Given the challenge of distinguishing between CuI and Cu0 peaks on the Cu 2p XPS spectrum, the Cu LMM spectrum was also analyzed (Figure 2d and Figure S8). The Cu LMM spectrum of 24-Cu/CN revealed a predominance of CuI at 571.7 eV, accompanied by a supplementary amount of Cu0 at 569.2 eV.55,56 To gain a deeper understanding of Cu chemical bonding states, the Raman spectrum of 24-Cu/CN was compared to that of CN (Figure S9). The Raman spectrum of 24-Cu/CN exhibited all characteristic CN peaks (590, 706, 769, 1213, 1231, 1311 cm–1)57,58 and along with additional peaks corresponding to Cu–C bonds at 251 and 621 cm–1,59 and Cu–N bonds at 494 cm–1,60 aligning with the XPS results (Figure S8). Moreover, the interaction between the Cu atom and other elements in the CN framework was analyzed through a comprehensive electron population analysis (Figure S10).61 The analysis indicated that Cu exhibited strong interactions with three atoms (two nitrogens and one carbon) across all three bond ordering methods (Figure S11 and Table S3). Both experimental and computational results confirm that the single Cu atom is coordinated with both carbon and nitrogen atoms within the CN matrix.

Based on the structural analysis of carbon nitrides described above, the incorporation of copper did not induce significant structural alterations but did influence the electronic structure and properties. This influence is evident in UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–vis DRS, Figure 2e), where both 4-Cu/CN and 24-Cu/CN exhibited expanded absorption in the visible light range. There was a noticeable increase in both π → π* and n → π* transitions around 350 and 500 nm, respectively, compared to copper-free carbon nitride (CN). This enhanced absorption of n → π* transitions is attributed to ligand–metal electron transfer states between Cu and the support.19,25,50,62 Furthermore, the optical absorption edge red-shifted from 383 to 408 and 453 nm with increasing copper content (dotted lines). Band gap energies for each carbon nitride were calculated from Tauc plots, revealing that 24-Cu/CN had a significantly smaller and red-shifted band gap energy (2.84 eV) compared to CN and 4-Cu/CN (3.00 and 2.98 eV, respectively) (Figure S12). Valence band maximum (VBM) energy was measured from XPS valence band (VB) spectra, and conduction band minimum (CBM) energy was calculated (ECBM – EF = EVBM – EF – Eg) (Figure S13).25,63 The calculated relative band positions of carbon nitrides showed that the higher the incorporation of copper into carbon nitride, the higher the VB and CB positions obtained (Figure 2f). Additionally, all prepared carbon nitrides exhibited the ability for single electron reduction of O2 (O2/O2•–: −0.33 eV vs NHE),64 indicating the feasibility of oxygen-involved transformations with all the catalysts during photoaerobic oxidation. Moreover, steady-state photoluminescence (PL) measurements demonstrated that charge-separation efficiencies were effectively promoted with higher copper incorporation, leading to decreased recombination rates (Figure S14).

Photoaerobic Oxidation for Disulfide

Photoaerobic oxidation of N-Boc-l-Cys-OMe (1) was chosen as a model reaction. The selective formation of disulfide between two cysteines has been a crucial step in enhancing the stability of proteins and forming antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs).65,66 Initially, a variety of hetero- and homogeneous photocatalysts were evaluated for their efficacy in converting 1 to the desired disulfide 2 under visible light irradiation at 456 nm (Figure 3a and Figure S15). Titanium dioxide (TiO2), known for its efficient absorption of ultraviolet light, demonstrated a modest 9% conversion of 1, albeit yielding disulfide 2 as the sole oxidized product (Figure 3b). Conversely, heterogeneous photocatalyst cadmium sulfide (CdS) and homogeneous organic dye Eosin Y achieved complete consumption of 1, but yielded disulfide 2 in moderate amounts, 40% and 38% respectively, due to the formation of significant overoxidized side products. When H2O2 was employed as the traditional oxidant (1.1 equiv) without light irradiation, disulfide 2 was obtained as a minor product (28%) alongside major overoxidized side products. Subsequently, newly prepared semiconductor photocatalysts, CN and 24-Cu/CN, were investigated under identical reaction conditions. CN demonstrated complete consumption of 1 with a similar yield of disulfide 2 (35%) compared to CdS and Eosin Y, as accompanying overoxidized side products. 24-Cu/CN, incorporating 2.4 wt % Cu atoms on CN, exhibited outstanding selectivity toward disulfide 2 (96%).

Several control experiments employing CN and 24-Cu/CN were conducted (Figure 3c and Figure S16). In the absence of photocatalysts or light, thiol 1 remained intact. The experiments inferred that the photogenerated excitons from the prepared carbon nitrides were involved in thiol oxidation. Furthermore, lower Cu wt % incorporated carbon nitride, 4-Cu/CN, also showed exclusive selectivity to disulfide 2 (83% of 2 with 17% unreacted 1) under the same conditions as 24-Cu/CN, although requiring a slightly longer reaction time (14 h) for full conversion (100% yield). The comparison between CN and Cu/CNs clarified that Cu incorporation onto carbon nitride was crucial for achieving selective thiol oxidation to disulfide under aerobic conditions. In addition, anaerobic reactions confirmed the oxidizing capability of photogenerated hole excitons on carbon nitrides.45 All carbon nitrides produced disulfide 2 as the sole product with reduced conversions: 60%, 52%, and 19% yields with 24-Cu/CN, 4-Cu/CN, and CN, respectively. These results correlated well with the recombination rates of photoexcitons observed in photoluminescence spectra (Figure S14), confirming that photogenerated hole excitons on carbon nitrides were capable of selective oxidation for disulfide synthesis, albeit requiring dioxygen (O2) for full conversion.

Major overoxidation side products from the reaction of disulfide 2 in the presence of CN were identified as thiosulfinate (2a) and thiosulfonate (2b) by separately synthesizing, isolating, and analyzing the mixture of 2a and 2b through 1H NMR analysis (Figure 3d). 2a and 2b were found to match the major overoxidation compounds observed under CN-catalyzed aerobic oxidation of thiol 1. When 24-Cu/CN was tested under the same aerobic conditions, no further oxidation of 2 was detected even after 12 h of photoirradiation.

Identification of ROS during Photoaerobic Oxidation with Cu/CN or CN

To explore the variance in oxidation behavior between 24-Cu/CN and CN under photoirradiation, we conducted an electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) experiment to capture reactive radicals using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO). As depicted in Figure 4a, 24-Cu/CN exhibited distinct peaks for DMPO-·OOH (red line, g = 2.0065), whereas CN displayed vague peaks with low intensities (black line).16,67 This suggests that the primary ROS on 24-Cu/CN is likely the hydroperoxyl radical (HOO·), potentially formed through single-electron reduction of O2 followed by protonation. Conversely, a significant ROS on CN may not be a radical species. Given carbon nitrides’ capacity to produce H2O2 via two-electron reduction of O2 and subsequent protonation, we conducted a colorimetric assay to monitor H2O2 generation during photoaerobic oxidation, using UV–vis spectrometry with 2,9-dimethyl-1,10-phenanthroline (DMP) (Figure S17).68,69 A progressive increase in H2O2 generation was observed with CN, evidenced by a change in solution color from green to orange, whereas the solution color with 24-Cu/CN remained consistently green. Quantitative analysis revealed that CN generated 8 mM of H2O2 within 2 h of light irradiation (black line in Figure 4b), whereas no H2O2 evolution occurred with 24-Cu/CN (red line in Figure 4b). From the EPR experiments and colorimetric H2O2 assay, it was found that the HOO· radical predominately generated on 24-Cu/CN, while CN primarily produced H2O2.

The impact of these ROS on our photoaerobic oxidation was further investigated through scavenging tests of 24-Cu/CN with benzoquinone (BQ), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), or NaN3, acting as superoxide (O2•–), hole (h+), or singlet oxygen (1O2) scavengers, respectively (Figure 4c).70N-Boc-l-Cys-OH (1′) was used as a substrate in the scavenging experiments. In the absence of any scavengers, the disulfide of 1′ was obtained in 87% yield with 24-Cu/CN after 8 h of irradiation, with no overoxidized side products observed as in the case of 1. When each scavenger was introduced, BQ and EDTA decreased the yields to 50% and 64%, respectively, while NaN3 treatment had no effect on the reaction. These scavenging tests revealed that photogenerated superoxide and hole were involved in thiol oxidation but 1O2 was not. Particularly, the role of superoxide (O2•–) was critical for the selective thiol oxidation with 24-Cu/CN, as indicated by the significant yield drop with BQ. This scavenging test result aligned well with EPR spectrometry analysis, considering the facile protonation of the superoxide radical to form the hydroperoxyl radical.15,71 In the scavenging test with CN, the concentration of H2O2 was measured (Figure 4d). When each scavenger, BQ, IPA, or NaN3, was introduced to the reaction mixture, the concentration of H2O2 over CN remained at a similar level. This suggests that one-step two-electron reduction of O2 for direct H2O2 formation is dominant on CN. If H2O2 were formed via a two-step sequential electron reduction of O2, H2O2 generation would be prevented by the superoxide radical scavenger BQ.17 From the above-described ROS investigation, it is suggested that the oxygen reduction pathways can be modulated by incorporating Cu atoms into carbon nitride, resulting in a difference in the selectivity for thiol oxidation to disulfide. While unmodified CN provides H2O2, Cu/CN generates the hydroperoxyl radical (HOO·) as a primary oxidant.

The Fate of HOO· on Cu/CN

Next, we considered the fate of the hydroperoxyl radical (HOO·) on Cu/CN, as H2O2 generation seemed inevitable through hydrogen atom abstraction (HAA) of thiol for disulfide formation. Another possibility is the disproportionation of HOO· or O2•– (Figure 5a). However, we confirmed the absence of H2O2 in our photoaerobic oxidation with 24-Cu/CN, as shown in Figure 4b. This absence could be attributed to an immediate Cu-catalyzed Fenton reaction, degrading H2O2.72,73 To verify this, 24-Cu/CN was exposed to a large amount of H2O2 solution in ethanol (9 mM) under light irradiation and monitored for 2 h, compared with CN (Figure 5b and Figure S18). H2O2 titration and UV–vis spectrometer analysis indicated a sharp decrease in H2O2 concentration with 24-Cu/CN, whereas the concentration steadily increased with CN. Impressively, 24-Cu/CN completely degraded H2O2 (red line in Figure 5b). Although the Fenton reaction can generate another ROS (i.e., hydroxyl radical), it is well-known that the hydroxyl radical is too unstable and immediately quenched by solvents such as ethanol.74 A Rhodamine B (RhB) degradation test was performed to support the short lifetime of the hydroxyl radical (Figure 5c). As expected, RhB degradation was not observed in ethanol (green line), even when excess H2O2 (40 mM, 3 mL) was added (yellow dotted line). This experiment indicates that the in situ generated hydroxyl radical from the Fenton reaction was effectively quenched by an excess amount of solvents, preventing dye degradation.

Elucidation of Divergent O2 Reduction Pathways

To elucidate the mechanism for the O2 reduction pathways, the activation and uptake capabilities for O2 were then investigated. After exposing all three catalysts to O2 for 1 h, the H2 temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) experiments demonstrated a shift in the reduction temperature from 125 to 103 °C with increasing Cu contents, indicating enhanced ability to activate O2 (Figure 6a). The O2 uptake capability were assessed following the exposure to O2 for 1 h and the subsequent removal of physisorbed O2 on the catalysts under N2 atmosphere for an additional hour (Figure 6b). The O2 temperature-programmed desorption (O2-TPD) depicts that 24-Cu/CN features the highest intensity of O2 desorption, indicating the superior chemical adsorption capacity for O2 due to the introduction of Cu atoms. Thus, we anticipate that the Cu sites in the Cu/CN catalysts to function as O2 adsorption sites, thereby improving the ability to activate O2 toward hydroperoxyl radical formation.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were conducted to elucidate the involvement of Cu/CN in photoaerobic oxidation processes. The protonated form of Cu/CN (Cu/CN*), designed to emulate photoexcited carbon nitrides based on prior computational investigations, was constructed (Figure 6c).16,70,75 Initially, the computation of metallic copper species Cu0 (Cu*-1) demonstrated the favorable energetics of photogenerated electron transfer to CuI on Cu/CN* (ΔG = −3.4 eV). Subsequently, the favorable energy of O2 adsorption on Cu in a Pauling-type configuration (Cu*-2) was also calculated (ΔG = −4.6 eV). Other potential sites for O2 adsorption, namely tri-s-triazine sites and cuprous species (i.e., CuI) on Cu/CN*, were investigated (CN*-1 in Figure 6c and Cu-3 in Figure S19a). It was found that Yeager-type O2 adsorption on tri-s-triazine in Cu/CN* (CN*-1 ΔG of 9.4 eV) compared to the O2 adsorbed Cu species (Cu*-2, Figure 6c), as was O2 adsorption on cuprous species on Cu/CN*, representing CuI species without accepting excited electrons, was unfavorable (ΔG = 5.5 eV for Cu-3 in Figure S19a). Previous studies have suggested the Yeager-type configuration of O2 on triazine (CN*-1) as an intermediate for H2O2 generation with CN, but its energy is significantly higher than that of Cu*-2, indicating a preference for O2 adsorption on Cu in a Pauling-type configuration.16,17 These findings suggest that the excited electron transferred to Cu tends to facilitate O2 adsorption, leading to the formation of CuI-superoxide species through internal electron transfer from Cu0 to O2 for superoxide formation. This interpretation is further supported by the calculation of the NPA (natural population analysis) charge of Cu*-2 (Figure S20).

The proposed mechanism for controlled reactive oxygen species generation with Cu/CN* is illustrated in Figure 6d. The aforementioned O2 adsorption on the copper site of Cu/CN*, involving electron transfer to CuI (a → b, ΔG = −3.4 eV) followed by O2 adsorption on Cu0 (b → c, ΔG = −1.2 eV), are exothermic processes. The NPA charge of Cu*-2, also denoted as c in Figure 6d, suggests superoxide formation through the internal electron transfer from Cu0 to O2 (Figure S20). Subsequent protonation of the superoxide (Cu*-2′, Figure S20) yields the hydroperoxyl radical (c → d, Cu*-4 in Figure S19b, ΔG = +2.4 eV), identified as the plausible rate-determining step. Following this, H2O2 produced during the reaction of HOO· for disulfide formation readily binds to Cu/CN* and undergoes a Cu-catalyzed Fenton reaction (Cu-5 → Cu-6, ΔG = −3.7 eV, Figure S19c), leading to H2O2-free conditions. The computational analysis indicates that the incorporated Cu acts as a site for O2 adsorption and serves as an electron conduit, transferring photoexcited electrons, thereby diverting the O2 reduction pathway away from pristine carbon nitride.

Based on experimental and computational results, we propose two divergent aerobic oxidation pathways for CN and Cu/CN (Figure 6e). In the case of CN, O2 adsorption on triazine facilitates H2O2 generation via a two-electron reduction of O2. The photogenerated H2O2 subsequently facilitates thiol oxidation, yielding a mixture of disulfide and further oxidized products (left, red circle and arrows in Figure 6e). Conversely, on Cu/CN, Cu accepts photoexcited electrons and O2, producing hydroperoxyl radicals through one-electron reduction process, selectively converting thiol to disulfide (right, blue circle and arrows in Figure 6e).

Substrate Scope

The substrate scope of our method was then investigated (Figure 7). A range of thiols underwent successful oxidation to form the corresponding disulfides with no further oxidation to thiosulfinate or thiosulfonate observed. Aliphatic thiols yielded the corresponding disulfides in yields ranging from 90% to 100% (2–8). Primary thiols were quantitatively converted to disulfides (2–4). Secondary and tertiary disulfides 5 and 6 were obtained in yields of 97% and 90%, respectively, from dimethyl mercaptosuccinate and d-penicillamine, a medication for Wilson’s disease. Glycosyl disulfide 7, mimicking O-glycosides, was formed in 92% yield without epimerization at the anomeric carbons, while oxidized l-glutathione (8) was prepared in aqueous reaction media. Furthermore, aryl disulfides were synthesized in satisfactory yields (71–98%) with various functional groups, including electron-donating (9, 10), electron-withdrawing (11, 12), and heteroaryls (13–15).

The synthesis of unsymmetrical disulfides was also explored using N-Boc-l-Cys-OMe (1). Initially, glycosylation of 1 via a disulfide linkage between cysteine and thioglucose was attempted by reacting it with 2 equiv of glycosyl thiol, yielding the corresponding unsymmetrical disulfide 16 in 81% yield. Aliphatic mercapto-esters and carboxylic acids, known as potent linkers for drug conjugation in drug delivery systems, were subjected to 24-Cu/CN catalyzed aerobic oxidation conditions with 1.76 This led to the formation of unsymmetrical disulfides 17 and 18 from methyl 3-mercaptopropionate and dimethyl mercaptosuccinate, respectively, in yields of 93% and 87%. Thiols bearing aryl (19) and heteroaryl (20, 21) functional groups also provided good yields 65%, 78%, and 67%, respectively.

Stability and Recyclability of Cu/CN

The recyclability of 24-Cu/CN was demonstrated upon disulfide formation. Analysis of the recycled 24-Cu/CN revealed its stability with no observable copper aggregation in TEM images (Figure S21). Furthermore, no copper leaching was detected in the supernatant of the reaction media after 8 h of aerobic oxidation, as confirmed by ICP-AES analysis (Table S2). The stable electronic structure of the copper on carbon nitride was confirmed through XPS analysis (Figure 8a and Figure S22). Across five cycles of recycling, disulfide formation remained consistent without any significant drop in the yield (Figure 8b).

Conclusion

In summary, our study illustrated the efficacy of copper-incorporated polymeric semiconductor carbon nitride (Cu/CN) in promoting selective thiol oxidation to disulfide, a stark improvement over pristine CN and other photocatalysts, which generated nonselective oxidized side products. Our experimental and computational analyses revealed that CN primarily produced H2O2 through a one-step two-electron reduction of O2, leading to a mixture of desired disulfide and overoxidized side products due to the strong oxidizing nature of H2O2. However, the integration of copper into the polymeric semiconductor matrix (Cu/CN) established a H2O2-free photoaerobic oxidation environment, enabling the high-yield formation of selective disulfide. This selectivity stemmed from the exclusive generation of ·OOH via the one-electron reduction of O2, followed by facile protonation. In this process, copper played pivotal roles as the O2 adsorption site, electron channel, and catalyst for the Fenton reaction, thus establishing the H2O2-free condition conducive to selective disulfide formation. The robustness and ability of Cu/CN to regulate reactive oxygen species during photoaerobic oxidation present a promising avenue for greener and more efficient synthetic catalysis across diverse applications.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of Cu/CN

Cu/CN was synthesized by a facile thermal polymerization method. Melamine (1.008 g) and cyanuric acid (1.032 g) were mixed in 50 mL of ethanol together with different masses of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O (4.0 mg for 4-Cu/CN or 24.0 mg for 24-Cu/CN) and hexamethylenetetramine (4.7 mg for 4-Cu/CN or 27.6 mg for 24-Cu/CN). The solution was stirred for 6 h, then washed and precipitated by centrifugation. The precipitates were dried at 60 °C overnight in vacuum oven. The white powders were gently grinded with mortar and moved to alumina crucible with a cover. Then it was heated to 600 °C under Ar atmosphere for 4 h with a ramping rate of 2.3 °C/min and maintained at 600 °C for 4 h. The content of copper in the obtained catalysts were estimated as 0.4 and 2.4 wt % by ICP-AES, and named as 4-Cu/CN and 24-Cu/CN, respectively

Photoaerobic Oxidation of Thiols to Disulfides

Symmetrical Disulfides

Thiol (0.4 mmol, 1.0 equiv) and 4,4′-difluorobenzophenone (8.7 mg, 0.1 equiv) were placed in a 2 mL volumetric flask and dissolved in ethanol to prepare a standard solution of the starting material. Subsequently, the standard solution was sonicated for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution of the materials. 24-Cu/CN (2.5 mg, 1.0 mol % Cu) was placed into a 5 mL vial, and 0.5 mL of standard solution was added using a Hamilton syringe. After it was sealed with a rubber septum, the reaction mixture was briefly sonicated to disperse 24-Cu/CN. Before the light irradiation, an 18 G needle was installed on the rubber septum to provide an air atmosphere, and the mixture was vigorously stirred under a Kessil LED at ambient temperature for 8 h. After that, the reaction mixture was centrifuged 3 times with EtOH (2 mL × 3). Then, supernatant was collected and concentrated in vacuo. The residue was analyzed by 1H NMR spectroscopy.

Unsymmetrical Disulfides

N-Boc-l-cysteine methyl ester (0.3 mmol, 1.0 equiv), coupling partner (0.6 mmol, 2.0 equiv), and 24-Cu/CN (12.5 mg) were placed in a 5 mL vial and dissolved with ethanol (3 mL). Subsequently, the reaction mixture was sonicated for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution of the materials. Before the light irradiation, 18 G needle was installed on the rubber septum to provide an air atmosphere, and the mixture was vigorously stirred under Kessil LED at ambient temperature for 12 h. After the reaction, the reaction mixture was centrifuged 3 times as diluted with EtOH (2 mL × 3). Then, supernatant was collected and concentrated in vacuo. The residue after evaporation was purified via column chromatography and characterized by NMR spectroscopy.

Catalyst Recycling Test

Thiol (0.4 mmol, 1.0 equiv) and 4,4′-difluorobenzophenone (8.7 mg, 0.1 equiv) were placed in a 2 mL volumetric flask and dissolved in ethanol to prepare a standard solution of the starting material. Subsequently, the standard solution was sonicated for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution of the materials. 24-Cu/CN (7.5 mg, 1.0 mol % Cu) was placed into a 5 mL vial, and 1.5 mL of standard solution was added using a Hamilton syringe. After it was sealed with a rubber septum, the reaction mixture was briefly sonicated to disperse 24-Cu/CN. The mixture was vigorously stirred under Kessil LED at ambient temperature for 8 h. Then, the supernatant was collected and concentrated in vacuo. The residue was analyzed by 1H NMR spectroscopy. The yield was calculated based on the ratio of 4,4′-difluorobenzophenone and the cystine (2). The recovered 24-Cu/CN was washed with EtOH (2 mL × 2) and H2O (2 mL × 2) and dried at 60 °C in a vacuum oven. The recovered 24-Cu/CN was measured, and the amounts of reagents were adjusted proportionally based on the amount of recovered catalyst.

ROS Verification

Radical Detection with EPR Spectroscopy

Photocatalyst (5.0 mg) and DMPO (10 mM) were dissolved in EtOH (1 mL) and stirred vigorously under 456 nm irradiation at room temperature. After 5 min, 0.5 mL of the reaction mixture was filtered through the cotton-plugged syringe and analyzed by EPR spectroscopy. The 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) was chosen as a spin trap for the detection of hydroperoxyl radical (·OOH). EPR spectra (X-band, 9.84 GHz) were recorded at room temperature by employing 100 kHz field modulation.

H2O2 Measurement

H2O2 evolution was carried out using a photocatalyst (12.5 mg) and EtOH (3 mL) under 456 nm irradiation at room temperature. The photogenerated H2O2 was measured by a colorimetric assay with a CuII based chromogenic agent. The chromogenic agent was prepared by mixing 2,9-dimethyl-1,10-phenanthroline (DMP) ethanol solution (1 g/L) and aqueous CuSO4 solution (0.01 M) in a 1:1 volume ratio. To monitor the H2O2 concentration over time, 50 μL of aliquots was added to 3 mL of the chromogenic agent. The mixture was then centrifuged to separate the photocatalyst, and the supernatant was measured by UV–vis spectrometry. H2O2 concentration was quantified by calculating the absorbance at 454 nm according to the Beer–Lambert law.

Rhodamine B Degradation

RhB degradation was carried out using RhB (0.001 mmol), a photocatalyst (8.0 mg), and H2O2 (40 mM) in EtOH (10 mL). RhB concentration was measured by a UV–vis spectrometry at 553 nm.

H2-TPD and O2-TPD Experiments

Pretreatment

30.0 mg of catalyst was preheated at 150 °C under N2 flow for 1 h and then cooled to 25 °C. The catalyst was subsequently exposed to pure O2 stream (50 mL/min) for 1 h at room temperature and flushed with Ar for 1 h to remove any physisorbed O2.

Reduction/Desorption

The reduction or desorption profile was recorded at a heating rate of 10 °C/min from room temperature to 200 or 350 °C and maintained for 10 min.
