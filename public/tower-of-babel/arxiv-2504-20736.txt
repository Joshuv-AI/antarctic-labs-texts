A Survey on Event-based Optical Marker Systems
Nafiseh Jabbari Tofighi, Maxime Robic, Fabio Morbidi, Pascal Vasseur

Abstract
The advent of event-based cameras, with their low latency, high dynamic range, and reduced power consumption, marked a significant change in robotic vision and machine perception. In particular, the combination of these neuromorphic sensors with widely-available passive or active optical markers (e.g. AprilTags, arrays of blinking LEDs), has recently opened up a wide field of possibilities. This survey paper provides a comprehensive review on Event-Based Optical Marker Systems (EBOMS). We analyze the basic principles and technologies on which these systems are based, with a special focus on their asynchronous operation and robustness against adverse lighting conditions. We also describe the most relevant applications of EBOMS, including object detection and tracking, pose estimation, and optical communication. The article concludes with a discussion of possible future research directions in this rapidly-emerging and multidisciplinary field.

Background

II-A

Event-based Cameras

Event (or event-based) cameras are bio-inspired sensors which detect brightness changes in a scene, instead of capturing frames at fixed rates. Each pixel in an event camera operates independently, and it is sensitive to light-intensity fluctuations occurring in the environment.
An event can be represented by the quadruple
(x,\,y,\,t,\,p)
, where
(x,\,y)
are the
coordinates
of the event,
t
is the
timestamp
, and
p=\pm 1
is the
polarity
of the event. If
p
is positive (negative), it means that the light intensity has increased (decreased) at location
(x,\,y)
in the last time interval.

Among the distinctive advantages offered by the sensing mechanism of event-based cameras, are asynchronous operation and high temporal resolution. Events are only generated when brightness changes occur in the scene, and this guarantees high speed and efficient calculation with low overhead. At the same time, with a response time in the microsecond range, event cameras are well equipped for sensitive applications requiring high accuracy and low run-times. The high dynamic range is another strength of event cameras, especially in outdoor environments with highly-variable or low light conditions, where conventional frame-based cameras usually struggle.

Finally, the event-based data collection mechanism, by only taking intensity variations into account and by disregarding those parts of the scene that do not change over time, guarantees high energy efficiency, which is crucial in applications involving embedded battery-powered devices.

II-B

Optical-Marker Systems

The use of optical markers for vision-based applications, such as tracking, localization, and data encoding has a long history. These systems rely on visible or infrared (IR) lights that act as reference points for the receiver systems. Visual fiducials can be categorized based on their energy behavior as either
active
or
passive
. Active markers emit their own light and require a power source, such as light emitting diodes (LEDs), while passive markers rely on reflecting light from external sources. Independent of this classification, many fiducials are designed with distinct geometric patterns, such as AprilTag, ARTag, ArUco, or QR (Quick Response) codes. These geometric markers can be implemented either actively (for example, using illuminated displays) or passively (using printed patterns). For specific applications, active and passive markers can be combined into hybrid systems, which often provide higher accuracy, improved robustness, and better adaptability to changing environmental conditions.

EBOMS go beyond frame-based systems and take advantage of flashing optical markers. Traditional frame-based optical communication systems are fundamentally limited by their frame rate, which typically ranges between tens and hundreds of frames per second (fps), with high-speed cameras reaching into the thousands of fps. This restricts their transmission rate to the kilobit-to-megabit per second range, depending on the specifications of the camera
[
55
]
. Other types of fast optical receivers, such as photodiodes, can operate at gigahertz frequencies: however, they function as single-point sensors and have poor spatial resolution, making them unsuitable for vision-based detection and communication tasks
[
50
]
. In contrast, EBOMS operate asynchronously, with bandwidths ranging from several megahertz to beyond a gigahertz, enabling transmission rates in the gigabit-per-second range
[
3
]
. The seamless fit between the high temporal resolution of event cameras, which selectively capture only rapid visual changes, and the capability of LEDs to operate at high frequency, plays a key role in the superior performance of EBOMS in numerous tasks.

III
Applications

Because of their low-latency response and resilience under extreme conditions, EBOMS are being used in a number of classical and emerging applications. Although, in principle, EBOMS could be classified according to the type of application (for instance, tracking, communication or pose estimation), the lines between them are often blurry. Some works have developed integrated frameworks that simultaneously alleviate the need for tracking or communication, or utilize pose estimation within an integrated localization and navigation pipeline. This convergence demonstrates the adaptability of EBOMS, since the same technology can be used for a variety of system functions.

III-A

Object Detection and Tracking

Detecting and tracking a moving target, is a fundamental task in a broad range of engineering applications, including robotics and autonomous vehicles
[
56
]
. In traditional systems, frame-based cameras are in charge of the detection task, which introduce latency due to their limited response time
[
57
,
58
]
. As a result, they are not well-suited for time-sensitive applications. In addition, usually the detector and the tracker aim at the whole target (for example, the airframe of a drone), which may lead to false positives and require substantial computational resources. Another option, is to detect only specific markers on the target, for instance, active markers and ArUco markers. This strategy has two main advantages: first, one should only focus on a narrow region of the scene (and not on the whole target). Second, the marker itself can convey additional information of use for pose estimation or communication.

One of the early works in this domain, is
[
33
]
. Here, the authors proposed to use event-based sensors to get past the limitations of traditional methods, for real-time 2D visual tracking. They proposed two algorithms to track the target objects (blinking LEDs). The first algorithm accumulates the events at each pixel over a fixed time window. Then, the pixel with the highest event count is selected as the estimated position of the LED. While this method is simple to implement and works reasonably well in practice, the size of the time window to update the target position, is fixed. To address this issue, a second algorithm, which asynchronously follows the events per pixel and detects event patterns that match the frequency of LEDs, is proposed. Experiments (autonomous robot homing and high-speed pan-tilt tracking) demonstrated the accuracy of the proposed algorithm. This pioneering work showed the potential of EBOMS for detection and tracking, laying the groundwork for subsequent studies in the field.

Other researchers have used geometric markers for object tracking and orientation estimation. In
[
34
]
, the authors used ArUco markers for fast continuous tracking (they achieved a frequency of 156 kHz and an end-to-end latency as low as 3 ms). The proposed approach consists of three main steps. A conventional ArUco detector based on the polarity map is used to detect the marker, followed by an update step of the pose which relies on the known 3D geometry of the marker. Finally, a two-way verification step is performed to backtrack the recent events and detect tracking failures by measuring the divergence between forward and backward pose estimates.

No matter the tracking algorithm used, the design of the markers themselves might have a strong impact on the accuracy of EBOMS. In this respect, the authors in
[
35
]
, introduced active ArUco markers integrating multiple LEDs, whose blinking patterns can be easily detected by an event camera in complex scenarios (fast motions, low-light and high-contrast conditions). The detection algorithm applies a mean shift filter for noise reduction, followed by a corner-refinement step using geometric consistency. The pipeline incorporates an error-detection strategy based on the Hamming distance, to improve accuracy. Adaptive thresholding with weighted statistics is used to handle motion blur and partial occlusions, more effectively. The promising results of this study show the advantages of active ArUco markers over traditional printed markers. Similarly, in
[
36
]
, the authors proposed EVNT-ArUco, a blinking ArUco marker specifically designed for event cameras. The main focus has been on the design of the marker, which guarantees better performances in static, dark environments, compared to standard passive markers.

III-B

Pose Estimation

EBOMS have a great potential for pose estimation, with a wide range of situations , including portable motion capture systems, augmented and virtual reality, and outdoor localization of mobile robots. Accurate and real-time pose estimation is crucial in these applications, as it offers the spatial information required for object tracking or trajectory planning in both physical and virtual environments.
However, conventional optical systems use frame-based cameras which capture scenes at fixed rates, which leads to motion blur during high-speed motions and performance degradations in low-light or high-contrast conditions
[
59
]
. In the context of pose estimation, such limitations can result in corrupted feature detection or loss of visual correspondences between frames. As a consequence, the estimated pose may drift over time, or become unstable during rapid head or object movements, which severely affects tracking accuracy in real world conditions. To get around these well-known limitations, one can combine event cameras with optical markers.

The authors in
[
37
]
were among the first to address the pose estimation problem using EBOMS, thus providing a baseline for subsequent studies. The proposed system consists of four LEDs, whose high-frequency light flashes are captured by an event camera which generates a stream of events. After a preprocessing step, a low-pass filter is applied to remove slow changes, followed by a clustering step to detect isolated events produced by the LEDs. In order to estimate initial camera pose, the well-known Perspective-
n
-Point (PnP) algorithm is employed, followed by an optimization step to minimize the reprojection error. Finally, the 6-DoF pose estimates are refined by applying a graph-based optimization method.

Inspired by
[
37
]
, an indoor positioning system including an event camera and multiple LEDs, is introduced in
[
38
]
. Each LED is assigned a unique frequency, which is detected using a Gaussian Mixture
Probability Hypothesis Density (GM-PHD) filter for tracking, along with a multi-LED fusion strategy. The pinhole camera model is used to estimate the camera’s position in an indoor setting.

Over the years, this solution has been fine-tuned to allow for faster responses and higher throughput. To this end, a low-latency algorithm tailored to rapidly-moving robots is proposed in
[
39
]
. They first perform bias tuning, and then frequency recognition for each LED. A low-pass filter is applied for tracking, and the PnP algorithm is utilized for pose estimation, by mapping the 3D LED positions to the corresponding 2D projections.

Along the same lines, the PnP algorithm has been applied for drone pose estimation in
[
40
]
. The authors proposed an event-based pipeline which achieves millimeter-level position accuracy with a pose update rate up to 1 kHz and latency as low as 2.5 ms, using four IR LEDs. This approach is particularly suited for small indoor environments and for agile robots (e.g. drones).

Going beyond traditional localization systems, EBOMS have also recently found application in aerospace robotics. For example, in
[
41
]
, the authors use blinking LEDs placed on the ground as reference points and an event camera mounted on a drone to solve relative localization problem. A Kalman filter is designed for detecting and tracking the LEDs, based on their unique frequencies. Finally, a real-time relative localization method is proposed, which relies on a Time-Delayed Kalman Filter (TDKF). The TDKF integrates the pose estimates of the target obtained from the solution of an PnP problem, with acceleration measurements from the onboard IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit). The PnP-based position is used as a correction input, whereas the inertial information is leveraged for motion prediction. The overall algorithm runs at 200 Hz and it guarantees real-time pose estimation in this study for the application of autonomous navigation and coordination between the Ingenuity helicopter and the Perseverance rover during planetary exploration missions.

III-C

Communication

Because of their inherent properties, such as robustness to motion blur, high dynamic range, and low latency, event cameras could significantly improve message transmission in terms of speed and error rate. They are thus well-suited for optical communication, especially in complex, unstructured environments, where traditional frame-based cameras provide relatively poor results. As far as traditional optical communication systems are concerned, Visible Light Communication (VLC) and infrared-based devices have been mostly explored for short-range data transmission
[
60
]
. These systems generally rely on photodiodes and frame-based cameras, as emitters and receivers, respectively
[
61
]
. However, they suffer from a number of weaknesses, such as a limited frame rate, motion blur in dynamic scenes, and sensitivity to ambient light
[
62
,
55
]
. Therefore, these systems are rarely used for real-time or high-speed applications, in outdoor, dynamic environments.

Besides point-to-point communication, EBOMS also open up new possibilities, such as inter-swarm communication in multi-agent systems, where multiple robots (securely) exchange messages via optical signals. This is particularly useful when radio-frequency communication is unreliable due to interference, jamming/spoofing, regulatory restrictions, or energy constraints. In all these cases, optical emitters in conjunction with event-based receivers, provide a fast, robust and energy-efficient communication channel. In the remainder of this section, we will provide an overview of existing EBOMS for optical communication. We will first analyze systems based on active optical markers like LEDs, we will then consider geometric and passive markers (cf. ArUco tags), and we will finally describe hybrid solutions combining passive and active markers.

1
Active Markers

Various types of active markers, such as visible-light LEDs or IR LEDs, have been explored in the literature, each one offering distinct advantages, depending on the specific application at hand. One of the foundational studies in this field, is
[
42
]
. It laid the groundwork for further research, by showing that event cameras lend themselves to high-speed transmission via modulated LED signals and by introducing a general framework for transforming raw event data into meaningful decoded messages. More specifically, in
[
42
]
the authors proposed a low-latency method to process event data and track IR LEDs blinking at different frequencies. They used evidence maps and a global particle filter, delivering superior performance compared to traditional cameras. Building upon this work, in
[
43
]
the authors designed an algorithm that employs a density-based clustering method to identify beacon signals from near-infrared LEDs. Event data is used to compute a sparse optical flow via spiking neural units, which estimate motion direction and speed. These estimates are then fed into a Kalman filter to track the beacon’s position over time. During decoding, a start code is searched for, followed by message extraction and parity bit checking for error detection. These steps guarantee robustness, as shown by the simulation results of the authors.

A significant departure from the state of the art is
[
18
]
, where a smart EBOMS is presented. Fig.
4
reports the pipeline proposed in
[
18
]
. Messages are converted into compact
11-bit
binary packets. Each packet includes a start code, a
7-bit
ASCII message, a parity bit for error checking, and an end code to wrap things up. For the decoding process, a blob-detection algorithm is used to pinpoint the location of LED signals. A high-pass filter is then applied to extract the relevant, high-frequency signals and a thresholding technique is employed to decode the original message. Thanks to the high-performance visible-light LEDs and optical lenses for longer distances, error-free data transmission at a frequency of 500 bps over a distance of 100 m is achieved, even under bright sunlight. This work demonstrated that active markers are effective for long-range optical communication in outdoor environments. Recent research has further explored the use of event cameras in the realm of optical communication. For instance, in
[
44
]
, the authors unveiled a high-speed Camera Communication (CamCom) system which ensured effective data transmission using IR LEDs, while their prototype design also allowed the use of RGB LEDs for potential multi-color signaling. The system delivered excellent performance with low error rates over a distance of 5 m, utilizing a technique known as binary pulse position modulation paired with a uniquely-designed start frame delimiter. In the same vein, a multi-agent framework that leverages event-based VLC is developed in
[
45
]
. This approach guaranteed an impressive success rate of 99.1% in communication, outperforming conventional radio-frequency and RGB-based methods. Its key strengths include robustness to occlusion and motion blur, with reliable communication even at moderate distances and in visually cluttered environments. This study suggests that event-based optical communication systems can correctly operate in harsh, cluttered environments, which are frequently encountered in many robotic applications.

In recent studies, more efficient encoding schemes for optical communication have been investigated. For example, in
[
46
]
, instead of using traditional methods to encode messages in LED-based systems (such as binary or ASCII code), a new approach, called
n
-pulse encoding, has been proposed. Information is conveyed by counting the number of pulses generated within a specific time window, which guarantees efficient data transmission. One of the key advantages of this solution, is that it fully benefits from the low latency of event cameras, and it can effectively work at high frequencies.

However, working with event cameras also poses several challenges. For example, for an object to be always visible by the camera, a continuous brightness change is indispensable. To tackle this issue, the authors in
[
47
]
proposed to use a spinning array of LED transmitters. The rotation of the LEDs, combined with the after-image effect, ensure that continuous brightness changes are perceived by the event camera, thereby ensuring constant visibility. While the rotating LED array transmits data by modulating the blinking pattern according to the angular position of the LEDs, the decoding process involves two main steps: noise filtering and signal demodulation. A sliding window is used to filter out noise, followed by signal demodulation to accurately reconstruct the transmitted data.

III-C
2
Geometric Markers

LEDs are commonly employed as optical markers, but an interesting alternative, especially for communication tasks, is geometric markers (generally, ArUco markers). These fiducial markers have distinctive black-and-white square patterns, which can be easily detected and decoded via conventional computer vision algorithms. Different supports exist for the ArUco markers: they can be displayed on digital monitors or printed on rigid substrates (in the latter case, an external source of light is necessary, for visibility). Their flexibility and variety of form factors, make them an excellent option in many innovative applications.

In
[
51
]
, Sun
et al.
proposed a novel transmission mechanism, in which binary data is encoded as dynamic visual markers on a screen and captured by an event camera through asynchronous brightness changes. To ensure accurate spatial localization, the authors proposed to use the Surface of Active Events (SAE). In this way, the markers maintain a high-contrast margin, which improves tracking stability. Data is transmitted as a video stream at 60 fps, and the decoding process involves binarizing the received information. The experimental results indicate that the system achieves a maximum transmission rate of 114 kbps under static conditions: the transmission rate drops to 7.68 kbps, in case of motion.

In
[
52
]
, the authors presented a cutting-edge method for real-time decoding of binary square fiducial markers, using an event camera. In a preprocessing step, a Gaussian filter is applied to the input data. Then, the Line Segment Detector (LSD) is used to accurately identify the boundaries of the markers. By forming candidate marker regions through the pairing of line segments extracted from temporally-segmented event frames, the detection process does not incur a high computational cost.
To extract the binary message embedded within the markers, a shift convolution method is utilized on the marker grid, by taking advantage of event distribution. This approach is not only robust against transmission errors, but also streamlines the extraction of encoded information.

III-C
3
Passive Markers

Passive markers, a distinct class of optical markers, are widely used in EBOMS. They do not emit electromagnetic waves, but, instead, reflect or modulate an external source of light. Reflective surfaces or Digital
Micromirror
Devices (DMDs) can be utilized as passive markers, especially in scenarios where adding an active light source is impractical due to design or environmental constraints, or energy limitations. This class of optical markers is particularly useful when an obstacle lies between the event camera and the target. In this case, the neuromorphic sensor generates events by indirectly capturing the light incident on a reflective surface. Thus, the obstacle is detected, event though it is outside the line-of-sight.

In
[
48
]
, the authors proposed the Selene framework, which is a high-speed, multi-channel EBOMS based on passive optical communication principles. Thanks to the DMDs, no modulated light signals are emitted, and the available ambient lighting, e.g. sunlight, is sufficient (see Fig.
5
). Firstly, a channel mapping strategy clusters data into independent mirror block positions. Next, a duplicate event removal step filters out redundant events caused by strong intensity changes, retaining only the first polarity transition, which represents the actual state change of interest. Finally, a data decoding step is performed. One of the key innovations of Selene is the use of a dual refresh rate: in fact, mirror blocks near the center are refreshed at a higher rate than those on the periphery. This is based on the observation that central pixels in event cameras exhibit lower timestamp latency than peripheral ones, enabling more precise event timing in those regions. By aligning the spatial refresh pattern of the DMD with the sensor’s non-uniform latency profile, Selene significantly improves throughput without sacrificing decoding accuracy. In
[
48
]
, the authors showed a peak data rate of 1.6 Mbps at distances up to 6 m, setting a new record for communication systems based on passive markers.

Recently, in
[
50
]
, the authors explored a novel approach for Non-Line-of-Sight (NLoS) communication, which uses passive light reflections from real-world objects. A modulated LED source and a neuromorphic camera are positioned orthogonally with respect to the target, and only the reflected light is captured by the camera. A periodic event-frame generation process is exploited to identify regions of interest (ROI), followed by contour-based refinement to extract the most informative pixels. Two modulation schemes are investigated: On-Off Keying (OOK) and an adaptive
n
-pulse encoding method that varies symbol mapping according to bit composition, to improve reliability. Demodulation is performed using a sliding-window approach, with guard intervals to handle noise and multi-path interference. Extensive experiments with a variety of object surfaces have shown that glossy and reflective materials significantly improve signal decoding. The system achieves a bit error rate as low as
4\times 10^{-4}
in dark conditions, supporting the use of passive markers for reliable NLoS communication, even with no direct visibility.

EBOMS based on the passive-marker technology have also found applications in various real-life scenarios, such as autonomous driving. For example, in
[
49
]
, a new visible light communication system, called NeuromorphicVLC, is proposed, which includes a retroreflective material and a liquid-crystal (LC) shutter to precisely modulate the reflected light. The detection algorithm begins by constructing an evidence map to isolate regions of high luminance. By applying an adaptive quantizer, the pixel intensities are then mapped into three levels: on, off, and noise. In this way, both the signal intensity and consistency across frames is evaluated. This allows to select key points (highly reliable pixels) for accurate message decoding. These key points are continuously tracked and they are used across 8 parallel channels for message transmission. This multi-channel communication system achieves high accuracy in dynamic scenarios, but the working range remains limited (up to 2 m).

Hybrid Markers

In order to enhance the accuracy and robustness of event-based optical communication systems, a possible solution consists in integrating different types of markers. For example, the Bicode system
[
53
]
, aptly combines blinking IR LEDs and 2D markers, to boost the signal recognition rate. It employs advanced techniques to process the event data, and it can robustly detect a signal up to 30 m away. Moreover, as shown in Fig.
6
, when it comes to pose estimation, it can operate with objects as far as 2.5 m, by leveraging the 2D markers. One of the distinguishing features of Bicode system, is a transmission frequency of 571 bps, with a low error rate.

IV
Conclusion

This survey paper bridges a critical gap in knowledge and provides a systematic literature review on Event-Based Optical Marker Systems (EBOMS), over the last 15 years. We described the general working principles of EBOMS, examined the emerging technologies, and discussed the latest applications and best practices. We hope that this survey may serve as a catalyst for further research in this area at the crossroads of computer vision, pattern recognition and robotics, setting the stage for innovative solutions to emerge.

V
Open Challenges and Future Research

Despite significant progress in EBOMS in the last decade, several open problems still exist, providing ample opportunities for future research.

One of the most urgent needs is the development of
standardized datasets and benchmarks
. In fact, the existing EBOMS are often evaluated in controlled laboratory settings, and there is a general lack of consistency in the environmental conditions or performance metrics. The field would greatly benefit from a unified, multi-modal dataset that includes synchronized event streams, IMU measurements, environmental metadata, and ground-truth pose estimates from an external motion-capture system, under diverse lighting and motion conditions. The existence of these resources is crucial to guarantee reproducibility, a fair comparative analysis, and the development of new data-driven methods.

Equally important is the lack of
standardized spatio-temporal encoding schemes
for event-based communication. Current systems rely on handcrafted or custom-designed modulation patterns, limiting scalability and interoperability. Future work should explore coding strategies which are adapted to the specificities of event cameras, such as spiking-based temporal encoding, frequency multiplexing with error correction, and more complex spatio-temporal marker layouts. A challenge will be to find the right balance between communication speed, robustness, and energy efficiency.

Another key direction is the
co-design of hardware and software
(detection algorithms). Most of existing EBOMS prototypes include off-the-shelf components and optimize software afterwards. A tighter integration between marker design and asynchronous processing pipelines, would contribute towards higher performance and lower latency. For example, adaptive markers that respond to feedback from the environment (such as ambient light or detection confidence) by adjusting their emission level in real-time, could significantly enhance robustness.

Scaling up EBOMS to
multi-agent systems
(e.g. swarms of drones) represents another major opportunity. While a few works have explored inter-agent optical links, large-scale asynchronous communication in swarms of autonomous vehicles, remains largely untapped. Future protocols could include event-based identification of team members, optical token-passing, and coordination mechanisms (e.g. formation control or chasing a moving target), that operate entirely without radio-frequency communication.

Security and privacy
are also primary concerns. As EBOMS move from research laboratories to public spaces, they meet new challenges, such as spoofing and jamming attacks, data leakage and confidentiality issues. Research into secure marker encoding, such as frequency hopping, temporal steganography, or lightweight encryption combined with local edge-based decoding could help build privacy-aware systems for smart cities, human-robot interaction, or surveillance.

There is also a surge in demand for
miniaturized and application-specific EBOMS hardware
. Potential use cases include smart glasses (eye or head-worn wearable computers) that simultaneously estimate their pose and communicate with their surroundings (e.g. for video games and AR), compact autonomous delivery robots moving in urban areas, and rovers for exploration of space or underwater environments, where radio frequency-based localization fails. In all these scenarios, form factor, power consumption, and resilience, are critical design requirements.

On the marker-design front,
more expressive and adaptable configurations
are worth exploring. These include 3D LED arrangements (not lying on the same planar surface), flexible LED arrays wrapped around rigid or deformable objects, and structured light emitted by DMDs or laser-based projectors to encode asynchronous patterns onto static scenes. The latter solution would enable passive communication or localization, without the need of physical markers attached to the objects in the environment. An especially promising idea is to develop advanced hybrid markers, combining both passive and active elements. For example, systems that automatically switch between IR LEDs and printed fiducials depending on the lighting conditions or power availability, would offer distinct advantages in terms of accuracy and robustness. Moreover, simultaneously using spatial, temporal, and frequency-based encoding, would open up richer, multi-layered communication channels, specially in cluttered environments.

Finally, one could take advantage of well-known
optical effects
, such as bokeh, for long-range EBOMS. In fact, under optical defocus, bright markers, like LEDs, produce large, easily detectable circular patterns on the images. These could be used to encode frequency, ID (identity), or motion information, even when the object is far way in the field of view of the camera. This is particularly useful for target detection, drone-to-drone communication, and cooperative robot exploration.

In summary, research on EBOMS is still in its infancy and it offers a rich set of opportunities across hardware, software, system design, and applications. To face the challenges ahead, researchers belonging to different communities (computer vision, robotics, machine learning, telecommunications, etc.) should work in close collaboration and channel their efforts to usher in a new era of intelligent and affordable event-driven perception systems.
