Could the evolution of stapling technology have influenced the observed reduction in postoperative air leakage?
Authors: Jiafu Wu, Yang Chen
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the article by Kim and Ryu entitled “Staple line coverage with suture fixation of polyglycolic acid patch for primary spontaneous pneumothorax” (1). The authors proposed reinforcement of the staple line using suture fixation of a polyglycolic acid (PGA) patch and demonstrated favorable outcomes in reducing postoperative air leakage following surgery for primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP). We commend the authors for their efforts to improve the surgical management of PSP.

Studies have confirmed that reinforcing staple threads can reduce postoperative gas leakage and recurrence in PSP, but the independent effects of each reinforcement method remain unclear (2-4). We believe that the continuous innovation in stapler technology and staple chamber design may be key factors that deserve further exploration of the interpretation of this study’s results.

Although the authors reported the number of cartridges used, details regarding cartridge models, staple configuration, and cartridge selection strategies were not provided. This issue is particularly relevant because the study covered nearly a decade, during which thoracoscopic stapling technology underwent substantial development. Earlier-generation devices differed from contemporary systems in staple configuration, tissue compression mechanisms, and staple-line stability. Some older cartridges employed two-row staple designs, whereas newer platforms increasingly adopted triple-row or graduated staple-height configurations intended to improve staple-line integrity and reduce tissue injury. Furthermore, even within the same period, differences in cartridge models and tissue-thickness matching strategies may influence staple formation quality and the incidence of postoperative air leakage. Recent evidence suggests that advances in stapling technology may independently contribute to reducing postoperative air leakage (5). Therefore, the observed reduction in postoperative air leakage may not be solely attributable to PGA patch fixation but may also partly reflect improvements in stapling devices.

We also observed a significant imbalance in the distribution of pulmonary blebs across study groups. The proportion of patients with multiple blebs was markedly higher in the group without patch suture fixation than in the suture groups. Although a dichotomous adjustment based on single versus multiple blebs was performed in the study, other clinically important parameters including bleb size, disease extent and total length of staple lines were not taken into account. Therefore, residual confounding bias related to disease burden cannot be fully ruled out.

In addition, because the study spanned nearly 10 years, temporal changes in surgical experience, perioperative management, and postoperative care protocols may also have influenced the incidence of postoperative air leakage. These time-related factors may introduce bias independently of both stapling technology and PGA patch fixation.

We believe that the technique described by the authors is innovative and clinically meaningful. Future prospective studies incorporating detailed information on cartridge models, staple configurations, cartridge selection criteria, lesion burden, and staple-line characteristics would help clarify the independent contribution of PGA patch suture fixation to the reduction of postoperative air leakage.
