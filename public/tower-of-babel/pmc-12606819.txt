Analysis of suspected serious adverse drug reactions reported with traditional medicines from the United Nations Asia region in WHO vigibase
Authors: Chandan N, Manish Barvaliya, Tejas Patel, Chetan AC, Harsha Hegde, Banappa Unger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditional medicines (TMs) are not regulated like conventional drugs in many countries, and they are often perceived as safe, which leads to large-scale self-medication. There is limited safety information available for TMs. Thus, including them in pharmacovigilance systems is crucial for systematically gathering and evaluating adverse drug reactions (ADRs) to enhance safety and prevent harm with their use. Therefore, this study analyzed the pattern of serious suspected ADRs reported with TMs to the WHO Uppsala Monitoring Center.MethodsIndividual Case Safety Reports (ICSRs) from January 1, 2016, to June 30, 2021, from the UN Asia region were included, which reported events suspected to be associated with TMs. ADRs resulting in hospital admission/prolongation of hospital stay, disability, life-threatening conditions, congenital abnormality, or death were considered serious ADRs. Demographic details, patterns of reported ADRs, and suspected TMs were analyzed along with the outcome of events.ResultsThere were 713 suspected serious ICSRs with at least one TM as a suspected drug reported, which accounted for 2.5% of the total reports. Skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders (19.8%), hepatobiliary disorders (15.3%), and gastrointestinal disorders (12.9%) were commonly reported system organ classes. Asymptomatic elevation of liver markers (8.8%) was the most common reaction, followed by vomiting (4.1%), dyspnoea (3.1%), drug-induced liver injury (3%), diarrhoea (3%), rash (2.8%), and urticaria (2.6%). Artemisia argyi (4.9%), Vitis vinifera (3.6%), Andrographis paniculata (2%), Ginkgo biloba (1.7%), Silybum marianum (1.5%), and Tinospora cordifolia (1.5%) were the most frequently suspected TMs. Seventeen (2.4%) ICSRs had a fatal outcome.ConclusionsThe present analysis revealed that various TMs have the potential to cause serious ADRs, particularly affecting the skin and subcutaneous tissue, the hepatobiliary system, and the gastrointestinal system. Their comprehensive list presented in the study will be valuable for promoting the safe use of TMs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05060-0.

Background

A suspected adverse drug reaction (ADR) is a harmful and unintended response to a medication that is classified as serious if it results in death, is life-threatening (requires immediate medical attention), requires or prolongs hospitalization, causes significant disability, or involves a birth defect [1–3]. Serious ADRs are a major cause of death and a public health concern [4, 5].

Traditional medicines (TMs) involve the use of plant-, animal-, and mineral-based substances to prevent, diagnose, treat, or improve physical and mental illnesses, as well as to maintain overall health [6, 7]. Globally, the use of TMs is increasing, especially for self-care, largely due to their easy availability over the counter and the perception that they are safe [8, 9].

A common misconception is that TMs are completely safe and can be used without a physician’s guidance, leading to widespread self-medication around the world. In many cases, these medicines are used without scientific evidence to support their claims [10–12]. The lack of research on the efficacy and toxicity of TMs—combined with limited regulatory oversight—raises safety concerns, particularly because they are often not quality-controlled for purity and potency like conventional drugs [9].

One approach to addressing these concerns is to integrate TMs into pharmacovigilance systems. These systems systematically collect and assess adverse drug reactions (ADRs), help to increase awareness, and improve safety [13]. Given that most TMs have not undergone clinical trials, post-marketing pharmacovigilance becomes a crucial tool for generating safety data necessary to ensure their safe and effective use [10].

Despite the widespread use of TMs, there is limited safety data on serious ADRs associated with their real-world use. Analyzing spontaneously reported suspected Individual Case Safety Reports (ICSRs) from the WHO global database is one way to raise awareness in this critical area. Therefore, the present study examined the pattern of serious suspected ADRs related to TMs reported by various United Nations Asia region member countries to the WHO Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC).

Methods

Data source

VigiBase is the WHO’s global database of ICSRs for medicines and vaccines. It is the world’s largest repository of drug safety data, containing over 30 million reports of suspected ADRs as a part of the WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring [14]. ICSRs from the United Nations Asia region were obtained from the WHO Uppsala Monitoring Centre in Sweden, following the required official procedures.

Selection criteria

All ICSRs reported between January 1, 2016, and June 30, 2021, from the United Nations Asia region were included in the assessment based on the following operative terminologies.

Traditional Medicines (TMs): Products derived from plants and animals in their natural state, whether used alone or in combination, were categorized as TMs. For this study, TMs listed under Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) code “V90: Unspecified Herbal and Traditional Medicine” and mentioned as the product type “herbal remedy-compositions of substances of natural origin (002)” were considered in the analysis. It included TMs from all countries under the United Nations Asia region.Serious ADRs: All suspected ADRs classified as “serious” in the dataset were considered serious ADRs. Criteria for such classification included events leading to hospitalization or prolongation of hospitalization, life-threatening conditions, disability, congenital malformation, and patient death. ICSRs, where the dataset labelled ADRs as serious without specifying the criteria, were excluded from consideration.Causative drugs: ICSRs in which at least one TM was identified as a “suspected” drug were considered causative. ICSRs mentioning TMs as “concomitant” or “interacting” drugs were excluded from consideration.

ICSRs with notifiers other than healthcare professionals and without seriousness criteria were excluded from the analysis.

Data extraction

Each ICSRs were provided with demographic details (age, gender), drug information (WHO preferred trade name, base name, salt name; dosage, frequency, route, indication), ADR data (event type, event as per MedDRA terminologies, time of onset, seriousness, de-challenge, re-challenge, outcome), and notifier information. The botanical drug names linked to ICSRs were validated using the Medicinal Plants Names services of Kew Science [15].

Data quality and uniformity

The event terms in the dataset were recorded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) classification system. MedDRA categorizes these terms from the System Organ Class (SOC) level to the Lowest Level Terms (LLT), enabling the direct assignment of MedDRA terms within a user database based on reported observations in practice. Each symptom, sign, diagnosis, therapeutic indication, inquiry, surgical or medical treatment, and medical social or family history characteristic is represented by a different descriptor (single medical concept) at the “Preferred Terms” (PTs) level. These related PTs are grouped into a High-Level Term (HLT), which is then grouped into a High-Level Group Term (HLGT). HLGTs are ultimately categorized into a broader group known as SOC. In this analysis, authors used SOC for describing ADRs related to organ systems and PTs for description of ADRs [16].

The dataset underwent a thorough review by two independent authors to ensure accuracy and to check for duplicate entries. Duplicate reports were identified and assigned the same report ID after running the VigiMatch tool in VigiBase. VigiMatch uses a statistical model to compare and match information between pairs of reports, identifying both matched and mismatched information [17]. Thus, it helps in identifying duplicate reports. Duplicate reports were excluded from the analysis.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed for summarizing the data. The data were presented in number and proportions for demographic details, commonly reported suspected serious ADRs with their SOC, suspected TMs as causative, and outcome of event. In the analysis of a large dataset, common adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and medicines (occurring in more than 0.5% of cases) were highlighted.

Data analysis was performed using the statistical software IBM SPSS version 25.0.

Fatal ADRs

Seventeen (2.4%) ICSRs had a fatal outcome. Fatal ADRs were more commonly documented amongst the 45–64 years age group (41.2%), followed by age < 2 years (23.5%) and > 65 years (17.6%). Their reporting was more common in females than males (58.8% vs. 35.3%). Table 4 summarizes the TM-ADR pair for fatal ADRs. Most frequently reported fatal reactions were liver disorders (14- including acute hepatic injury or hepatic encephalopathy), electrolyte disturbances (8- hypochloraemia/hyponatremia), coagulopathy (4), hepatorenal syndrome (4), and acute renal injury (3). The most frequently suspected TMs in fatal ADRs were Aphanizomenon flos-aquae (5), Aloe vera (4), Ganoderma lucidum (4), Citrus limon (2), and Elwendia persica (2).

Discussion

Analysis of the VigiBase database revealed a variety of suspected serious ADRs reported with TMs from the United Nations Asia region. A total of 1150 reactions were identified in 713 ICSRs involving 178 different TMs. In the present dataset, 23 different SOCs and 317 different PTs were reported. A sizable proportion of serious reactions to TMs are related to Skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders, hepatobiliary disorders, and gastrointestinal disorders.

Our findings suggest that adults account for not only a higher frequency of serious ADRs but also fatal outcomes of ADRs related to TMs. Six out of 10 serious ADRs and four out of 10 fatal ADRs were reported in the age group 18–65 years in this study. This finding is consistent with earlier Asian studies that have evaluated ADRs associated with herbal medicine [18, 19]. This has been attributed to the higher use of herbal medicine treatments among adults [19]. Female gender is predominantly reported as a risk factor for TM-related ADRs in the literature [13, 18, 19]. This has been attributed to the higher use of TMs in females than in males [19]. In this study, a slight female preponderance (51.8%) is observed over the gender distribution of the female population (48.8% female) in Asia [20]. It seems that gender minimally impacts the occurrence of serious ADRs related to TMs in the Asian population.

Skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders accounted for one-fifth of the serious ADRs due to TMs. Prior studies on the National Pharmacovigilance Centre of the Netherlands and Taiwan ADR reporting system for herbal medicine found that skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders accounted for 20% of ADRs due to herbal drugs [13, 19]. Rash, urticaria, and pruritus are the commonly observed cutaneous reactions in our study. One-sixth of skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders were classified as serious cutaneous adverse responses (SCARs), encompassing conditions such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, and drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms. Dermatologists should be aware of the potential TMs causing cutaneous ADRs, including SCARs [21–23].

Hepatobiliary disorders accounted for one-sixth of serious reactions, and more than half of the fatal reactions related to TMs. This aligns with a previous study based on data from the U.S. Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network, which reported that over 90% of the cases were severe, with half requiring liver transplantation or resulting in fatal outcomes [24]. Patients having the herbal and dietary supplements-induced liver injury group had a higher mortality rate than the Western conventional drug-induced liver injury group [25]. The use of herbal and dietary supplements may increase the risk of developing acute-on-chronic liver failure in patients with preexisting liver cirrhosis [25]. Early intervention and preventive strategies are warranted for hepatobiliary disorders, as asymptomatic elevation of liver enzymes/bilirubin is reported in almost one out of ten ICSRs in this study. Since the clinical characteristics of herbal-induced liver injury are not unique and can also be present in many other liver illnesses unrelated to the use of herbal remedies, it is a diagnosis of exclusion [26–28]. There is a need to have a regulatory framework to reduce the risk of herbal-induced liver injury. Enhancing public education and implementing a physician-supervised self-medication regimen are crucial steps in reducing its risk [24].

Gastrointestinal disorders accounted for one-tenth of the serious ADRs. Previous studies have shown that between one-third to half of the ADRs caused by herbal drugs were related to gastrointestinal issues [13, 19]. This suggests that gastrointestinal ADRs may represent a significant proportion of both non-serious and serious reactions associated with TMs. For ages, people have relied on herbal medicines to manage a variety of gastrointestinal disorders. TMs can have toxicological effects on the gastrointestinal tract in addition to their benefits in the prevention and treatment of medical conditions [29]. They should not be considered safer than conventional drugs in causing gastrointestinal ADRs [9].

Artemisia argyi, Vitis vinifera, Andrographis paniculata, Ginkgo biloba, Silybum marianum, and Tinospora cordifolia were the most frequently suspected TMs. Artemisia argyi and Ginkgo biloba are widely used Traditional Chinese Medicines. Artemisia argyi is widely used to treat various ailments, including bleeding disorders, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and skin conditions [30], whereas Ginkgo biloba is widely used for dementia, respiratory, and cardiovascular disorders [31]. Dyspnoea, pruritus, rash, anaphylactic reactions, angioedema, and dizziness were common reactions associated with Artemisia argyi, whereas angina pectoris and gastrointestinal hemorrhage were suspected with Ginkgo biloba. Vitis vinifera is a vine species indigenous to the Mediterranean region, central Europe, and southwestern Asia. It is recognized for its skin-protective, antioxidant, antibacterial, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and antidiabetic properties. Additionally, it exhibits hepatoprotective, cardioprotective, and neuroprotective effects [32]. Neutropenia, urinary tract infection, and diarrhea were the commonly reported reactions with Vitis vinifera. Andrographis paniculata, commonly known as green chiretta or creat, is native to South Asian countries such as India and Sri Lanka. It has been widely used as a TM, particularly in India and China, for the treatment of respiratory infections, liver disorders, skin diseases, dysentery, and malaria [33]. Allergic reactions such as anaphylaxis, angioedema, and urticaria were among the most frequently reported ADRs associated with Andrographis paniculata. Silybum marianum, a well-known hepatoprotective agent commonly used for various liver disorders [34], was often linked to elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels in the current dataset. Tinospora cordifolia, also known as guduchi or giloy, is a climbing shrub native to India and has been used for centuries in the Indian system of medicine as an immunomodulator, anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic [35]. In this study, liver injury and jaundice were frequently reported ADRs associated with Tinospora cordifolia. The frequent suspicion of these drugs in ADRs may be attributed to their widespread use.

The present study has several limitations. It retrospectively analyzed spontaneously reported suspected ADRs, which does not establish causality between the TMs and the reported reactions. However, it provides insights into patterns of reactions that should be considered during clinical practice for vigilance. The data source is VigiBase, which does not reflect the opinions of WHO UMC. Multiple factors may contribute to ADRs associated with TMs, including misidentification of medicinal plants, contamination/adulteration of herbal medicines, variations in phytoconstituents, herb-herb or herb-drug interactions, and faulty production methods. The methods of using TMs vary culturally both within and across countries, which may influence the development of ADRs in different ways. However, the authors were unable to assess the impact of these factors on the occurrence of ADRs. Furthermore, due to the lack of country-specific data, it was not possible to determine whether the reported suspected TMs were regulated or unregulated. Therefore, the findings of this analysis should be interpreted with caution and viewed as a means to generate hypotheses and identify patterns of suspected serious ADRs for monitoring purposes. This analysis does not establish a definitive causal relationship between any specific TM and the reported reactions.

Conclusion

Skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders, hepatobiliary disorders, and gastrointestinal disorders accounted for nearly half of the serious ADRs associated with TMs. Ensuring the safety of TMs requires robust and comprehensive pharmacovigilance systems. Clinicians should remain vigilant in recognizing ADRs linked to TM use and are encouraged to report such cases to pharmacovigilance programs. A comprehensive list of TMs and their associated ADRs in the present study would be a valuable resource for clinicians in identifying and suspecting ADRs related to TM use.
