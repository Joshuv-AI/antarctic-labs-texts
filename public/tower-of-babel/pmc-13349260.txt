Eating Epilepsy: Clinico-Electro-Imaging Profiles and Treatment Outcomes in Patients from Northeast India
Authors: Ruby Kasana, Mausumi Barthakur, Krishnapriya V Velayudhan, Amit Ranjan Barua, Nomal Chandra Borah, Krishna Undela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eating epilepsy (EE) is a rare type of reflex epilepsy that predominantly affects South Asian populations. Its pathophysiology involves activation of the insular-opercular and perisylvian networks during eating, although the clinical spectrum and treatment outcomes remain underrecognized in certain regions. This study aimed to describe the clinico-electro-imaging profile and treatment outcomes of patients with EE at a tertiary care hospital in Assam, Northeast India. This retrospective case series identified consecutive patients with EE between 2018 and 2025. Demographic and clinical information was collected from medical records and summarized. A total of 26 patients with EE, mainly young adults (mean age, 23.9 ± 6.1 years), experienced seizure onset during adolescence, and 65.4% were male. Rice-related triggers were identified in 30.8% of patients. Interictal electroencephalogram abnormalities were identified in 88.5% of cases, whereas magnetic resonance imaging abnormalities were observed in 23.1%. All patients presented with focal epilepsy, predominantly originating from the parieto-occipital and frontotemporal regions. At follow-up, 92.3% achieved seizure freedom while receiving appropriate antiseizure medications. These findings underscore the importance of region-specific, culturally aware assessment in the management of EE.

Introduction

Eating epilepsy (EE) is a subtype of reflex epilepsy.[1] It is more common in South Asian populations, possibly because of distinctive sociodemographic factors (eating habits) and genetic susceptibility.[234] EE is commonly triggered by stimuli associated with food intake during the preprandial, prandial, or immediate postprandial phases of eating.[5] Stimuli such as contemplating food, visual and olfactory cues related to food, and digestive and autonomic functions can act as potential triggers, especially with carbohydrate-rich meals.[4] These epileptic events may result from hyperactivation of primary gustatory regions, including the anterior insula and frontal operculum, in response to taste, olfaction, and anticipation of food. However, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms underlying EE remain unclear.

This case series aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, investigations, treatment approaches, and outcomes of patients with EE in Assam, located in the Northeast (NE) region of India. It offers a region-specific perspective on how local dietary and demographic factors influence the presentation of EE and guide tailored therapeutic strategies.

Methods

This retrospective case series was conducted at a tertiary care hospital and reviewed all patients registered in the neurology unit from January 2018 to December 2025. There is no widely accepted definition of EE in the literature; therefore, we employed an operational case definition.[46] EE was defined as reflex epilepsy in which seizures were consistently and reproducibly triggered by stimuli related to the act of eating during any phase of food intake (preprandial, prandial, or postprandial), consistent with the framework of Girges et al.[4] Patients were included if eating was the sole trigger or if it acted alongside other noneating stimuli, provided that eating consistently reproduced seizures. Cases in which eating appeared incidental were excluded.

Patients were eligible if they had: (i) a clinical diagnosis of EE as defined above; (ii) accessible interictal electroencephalographic (EEG) data; (iii) a brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan performed using an epilepsy protocol; and (iv) documented follow-up for at least three months after diagnosis to assess outcomes. Patients with incomplete records or those lost to follow-up were excluded. Seizure triggers were identified through structured history-taking during the initial clinical assessment. Patients and caregivers were specifically asked about food types, meal timing, trigger reproducibility, and other noneating stimuli. Rice-triggered seizures were defined as seizures that consistently and specifically followed rice consumption. This distinction is especially important in NE India, where rice is the main dietary staple and is consumed at nearly every meal, making it a frequent and culturally ingrained trigger exposure.[7] Family history was recorded as any seizure disorder in first- or second-degree relatives and was not restricted to EE specifically.

Seizure type and epilepsy classification followed the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) 2017 operational framework based on the synthesis of clinical semiology and interictal EEG findings.[8] Focal epilepsy was diagnosed when seizures began in a localized cortical area, with possible progression to focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures (FBTCS). Generalized epilepsy was identified when seizures involved both hemispheres from onset, confirmed by EEG evidence. When semiology and EEG localization were discordant, clinical semiology was given precedence, in accordance with ILAE guidance on the primacy of ictal event behavior.[8]

The institutional ethics committee approved the study; informed consent was obtained, and data were anonymized before analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means ± standard deviation (SD).

Seizure trigger and semiology

Rice triggered seizures in 8 patients (30.8%), whereas unspecified food triggered episodes in 18 patients (69.2%). Most triggers occurred mid-meal, aligning with prandial activation. Fourteen patients (53.8%) had seizures triggered by both eating and other stimuli, with sleep being the most common noneating trigger, reported in 9 patients. Aura was present in 20 patients (76.9%). Specific aura types included visual aura in 2 patients (7.7%), fear aura in 2 (7.7%), combined experiential and autonomic aura in 2 (7.7%), and staring aura in 1 (3.8%). The remaining 13 patients had nonspecific auras (giddiness or dizziness) that were not further characterized. Ictal semiology was predominantly FBTCS, observed in 21 patients (80.8%), with loss of consciousness (LOC) in 23 (88.5%). Lateralizing ictal features included head deviation in multiple patients and postictal aphasia in 4 (15.4%). Postictal vomiting was noted in 6 patients (23.1%), whereas nausea was reported in 3 (11.5%). Cluster seizures occurred in 10 patients (38.5%), and breakthrough seizures occurred in 3 patients. Seven patients (26.9%) had a family history of epilepsy, including any seizure type in relatives and not specifically EE.

Epilepsy classification

All 26 patients had focal epilepsy. The onset region was identified in 19 patients (73.1%) and was nonlocalized in 7 (26.9%). The most frequently observed localization was the posterior (parieto-occipital) region in 7 patients (26.9%), followed by the frontotemporal region in 6 (23.1%), temporal lobe in 3 (11.5%), central (rolandic) region in 1 (3.8%), and the insular-frontotemporal network in 1 (3.8%; Case 18, with hyperintensities in the left insular and right frontal regions on MRI). Bilateral synchronous epileptiform discharges were present in 4 patients (15.4%) with focal epilepsy. One patient (Case 23) showed discordance: EEG demonstrated left-hemisphere discharges, whereas MRI showed right-sided cortical atrophy. Classification was based on clinico-electrographic interpretation and semiology.

Interictal EEG findings

Interictal EEG was abnormal in 23 patients (88.5%). Findings included bilateral synchronous epileptiform discharges in 4 patients, bilateral independent focal discharges in 3 patients, and focal discharges involving the posterior (parieto-occipital), frontotemporal, central, insular-frontal, and temporal regions in the remaining patients. MRI brain findings

Brain MRI was abnormal in 6 patients (23.1%). Structural findings included left parietal encephalomalacia (Case 3); possible sequelae of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy affecting the bilateral thalami and parasagittal regions (Case 4); left insular and right frontal subcortical hyperintensities (Case 18); right middle temporal gyrus atrophy with gliosis (Case 23); right parietal gliosis (Case 25); and right mesial temporal sclerosis (Case 26).

Antiseizure medications

Sixteen patients (61.5%) received polytherapy, whereas 10 (38.5%) received monotherapy. Clobazam was prescribed for 13 patients (50.0%), typically as a premeal dose to target the predictable periprandial trigger window. Sodium valproate (VPA) was used in 11 patients (42.3%), levetiracetam (LEV) in 10 (38.5%), and oxcarbazepine (OXC) in 6 (23.1%).

Seizure outcomes

At the last follow-up, 24 patients (92.3%) achieved seizure freedom. Of these, 14 (53.8%) remained seizure-free while receiving medication, whereas 10 (38.5%) discontinued medication and remained seizure-free. One patient (Case 3, left parietal encephalomalacia) had a significant reduction in seizures but did not achieve complete seizure freedom. Another patient (Case 25, right parietal gliosis) had refractory seizures despite polytherapy, with only reduced severity and frequency. The median number of antiseizure medications (ASMs) was 2 (range, 1–4). The mean seizure-free duration at last follow-up was 3.0 ± 2.0 years (range, 0.4–7 years).

Discussion

This case series offers a detailed account of EE from NE India, covering clinical, electrographic, neuroimaging, and therapeutic aspects. Key findings include adolescent-onset focal epilepsy with frequent FBTCS, rice-specific and prandial triggers within a culturally unique dietary context, diverse yet network-coherent interictal EEG patterns, and seizure freedom in 92.3% of cases with targeted pharmacotherapy.

The predominantly male group with adolescent-onset disease aligns with the published literature across the Indian subcontinent. A North Indian case series reported seizure onset at 13.5 years, with 91.7% male predominance,[9] whereas a South Indian cohort reported a mean onset age of 11.9 years, with approximately 70% of patients being male.[3] These findings indicate a common neurobiological vulnerability during adolescence throughout the Indian subcontinent. The predominance of mid-meal seizures in this cohort provides important mechanistic insight into the pathophysiology of EE. During eating, particularly in the prandial phase, the anterior insular cortex and frontal operculum—the primary cortical recipients of gustatory, olfactory, and oromotor afferent inputs—undergo sustained activation in response to taste, chewing, and swallowing.[410] In a hyperexcitable cortex, periprandial activation may cross the seizure threshold and precipitate reflex ictal events. The predominance of parieto-occipital and frontotemporal interictal EEG findings in our cohort reflects engagement of the broader insular-opercular network: posterior regions modulate visuospatial and sensorimotor feeding responses. By contrast, frontotemporal discharges may reflect activation via olfactory and visual sensory food cues during meal anticipation and consumption.[310] The insular-frontal MRI hyperintensities in Case 18 directly support this anatomical substrate.

Phenotypic heterogeneity in seizure semiology is an anticipated characteristic of EE rather than a diagnostic inconsistency. Eating stimulates a widespread cortico-cortical circuit involving frontal, temporal, parietal, and insular regions.[410] The specific semiology in each patient reflects the location of the dominant epileptogenic node and its propagation pattern. The consistent and reproducible eating trigger remains the unifying feature of EE as a reflex epilepsy syndrome, regardless of variability seizure semiology.[46]

The bilateral synchronous discharges observed in patients with focal epilepsy likely reflect secondary bisynchrony, reconciling the high FBTCS rate with exclusively focal epilepsy classification across the cohort. This mechanism, focal frontal or parieto-occipital discharges propagating rapidly across the corpus callosum, may explain the apparent discrepancy between bilateral EEG appearance and focal onset, underscoring why EEG findings alone should not override clinico-electrographic classification in this setting.[11]

The apparent concentration of EE cases in NE India likely reflects a convergence of dietary habits and biological predisposition. Rice is consumed as the primary staple two to three times daily across communities in this region,[7] providing rice-sensitive patients with high-frequency and unavoidable trigger exposure, unlike in populations where rice consumption is intermittent. NE India also encompasses ethnic populations of South and Southeast Asian ancestry, in whom genetic susceptibility to reflex epilepsies has been proposed.[23] Rice’s carbohydrate density, prolonged oromotor demands, and postprandial glucose fluctuations may produce particularly potent network activation, explaining its emergence as a specific trigger in 30.8% of patients.

MRI abnormalities in 23.1% of patients align with the 28%–34% structural lesion rate reported in prior EE series.[410] The lesion distribution also corresponds to the proposed epileptogenic network: insular-frontal hyperintensities (Case 18), gliosis in the middle temporal gyrus (Case 23), and parietal lesions (Cases 3 and 25), all of which involve cortical nodes active during eating.[510] Both patients with unfavorable outcomes had structural parietal lesions, consistent with evidence that gliotic and encephalomalacic changes within the eating-related network may confer drug resistance by sustaining intrinsic epileptogenicity independent of the reflex trigger.[910]

The high overall seizure-freedom rate, with 38.5% of patients successfully discontinuing antiseizure medication, reflects the therapeutic advantage of trigger predictability in EE. Premeal clobazam, prescribed in 50.0% of patients, may transiently elevate the seizure threshold during the prandial trigger window, analogous to intermittent benzodiazepine use in catamenial or photosensitive epilepsy.[410] This mechanism-targeted strategy, combined with broad-spectrum ASMs (VPA, LEV, OXC), likely contributed to the high response rate. For the refractory structural subgroup, pharmacotherapy alone may be insufficient; presurgical evaluation, including advanced neuroimaging analysis, should be considered to delineate the epileptogenic zone, with surgical resection offering the potential for seizure freedom where medications have failed, as demonstrated in isolated case reports of lesional reflex epilepsy.[45]

The main strengths of this case series include the systematic, thorough evaluation of all patients using EEG and neuroimaging, along with ongoing clinical follow-up. This enabled consistent characterization of seizure patterns, treatment responses, and long-term outcomes, thereby enhancing the clinical validity of the findings. However, this study’s retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection and recall bias. The absence of extended EEG recordings with event capture and ictal analysis limits detailed electroclinical correlation. Moreover, advanced neuroimaging tools such as stereo-EEG, magnetoencephalography, positron emission tomography, and single-photon emission computed tomography were unavailable, limiting the precise localization of the epileptogenic network. Additionally, genetic testing was not conducted, which could have offered more insights into the observed familial susceptibility. Seizure histories relied on patient and caregiver reports, with telephone follow-up when needed; this may have affected the accuracy of semiologic details. Postictal amnesia was recorded only when spontaneously reported and was not systematically assessed, creating a risk of underreporting, especially in patient with frequent LOC.

Conclusions

This case series highlights EE in NE India as a distinct, culturally influenced form of focal epilepsy that predominantly affects adolescent males. The condition is closely linked to region-specific dietary habits, with rice serving as the primary staple and acting as a common prandial trigger, possibly interacting with a genetic predisposition prevalent among South and Southeast Asian populations. Most patients achieved sustained seizure remission with carefully customized antiseizure treatment. Notably, the presence of structural abnormalities within eating-related neural networks on MRI was associated with pharmacoresistance, suggesting a subgroup of patients who might benefit from presurgical assessment. Overall, these findings underscore the importance of culturally sensitive, region-specific strategies in the evaluation and management of EE.
