AI-driven fungicide design: From target identification to field application
Authors: Hong Hu, Zhiguang Qu, Yuanlong Liu, Lida Zhu, Zhinan Mei, Xiao-Lin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant pathogenic fungi pose a severe threat to global agriculture, causing substantial yield losses in staple crops and jeopardizing food safety through mycotoxin contamination. Conventional fungicide development is hindered by high costs, lengthy timelines, and the rapid evolution of fungal resistance, which outpaces conventional discovery workflows. Although artificial intelligence (AI) offers transformative potential to address these bottlenecks, its application in plant pathology remains fragmented and lacks integration of agriculture-specific constraints such as field stability, ecological safety, and resistance management. This review introduces the AI-driven fungicide design (AIFD) platform, a comprehensive framework comprising four interdependent components: a plant pathogen-specific data ecosystem, a modular microservice technical architecture, a linear multiphase development workflow, and a specialized resistance prediction workflow. We synthesize key technological advances across the fungicide development pipeline, from target identification and virtual screening to molecular optimization and field validation, with an emphasis on AI methodologies adapted to agrochemical requirements rather than pharmaceutical standards. Despite substantial advances, critical challenges persist, including scarce high-quality training data for understudied pathogens, limited model adaptability across diverse agroecosystems, poor interpretability that hinders stakeholder trust, and accessibility barriers for resource-constrained researchers. Future directions emphasize the integration of real-time field data, explainable AI to facilitate regulatory acceptance, and inclusive design strategies aimed at bridging the laboratory-to-field gap. By aligning computational innovation with agricultural priorities, AIFD platforms can accelerate the discovery of resistance-breaking, environmentally benign fungicides, thus offering a viable pathway toward sustainable crop protection and enhanced global food security.

Introduction

Plant pathogenic fungi are among the most destructive biotic stressors in agriculture, with impacts ranging from local crop failure to global food insecurity. Rice blast, caused by Magnaporthe oryzae, affects 85 countries and destroys enough rice annually to feed 60 million people, whereas gray mold, caused by Botrytis cinerea infects over 200 plant species, with annual losses exceeding US$10 billion globally (Dean et al., 2012; Singh et al., 2024). Fusarium head blight (FHB; Fusarium graminearum) not only reduces wheat yields but also contaminates grains with mycotoxins such as deoxynivalenol, jeopardizing human and livestock health (Chen et al., 2019, Kelly et al., 2015). The diversity of commercial fungicide chemistries is limited, being dominated by azoles, strobilurins, and succinate dehydrogenase inhibitors (SDHIs); the emergence of resistance further compromises disease control (Yin et al., 2023).

Conventional fungicide development follows a linear, resource-intensive workflow: manual target identification via fungal genetic screening, high-throughput screening of 106–108 compounds, iterative lead synthesis, and 3–5 years of field trials to validate efficacy across soils and climates (Yin et al., 2023). This process does not keep pace with the rapid evolution of plant pathogenic fungi, which adapt to new chemistries within 2–5 years (Yin et al., 2023; Islam et al., 2024). Additionally, modern agriculture demands fungicides that balance efficacy with environmental safety by minimizing toxicity to pollinators, soil microbes such as mycorrhizae, and non-target plants, while remaining compatible with integrated pest management (IPM) strategies such as rotation with biological control agents (Maltby et al., 2009; Jorgensen et al., 2017).

Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, including machine learning, deep learning (DL), and natural language processing, offer transformative potential through the integration of multidimensional agricultural data (e.g., fungal genomics, crop physiology, field weather, and Internet of Things [IoT] sensor data), which can streamline each stage of development (Mesías-Ruiz et al., 2023; Shastri et al., 2025; Yaganteeswarudu et al., 2025). However, current AI applications in fungicide development remain fragmented and disjointed. Existing efforts are predominantly confined to single stages of the discovery pipeline, such as virtual screening or target identification. These efforts operate in silos, failing to address agricultural requirements essential for successful field deployment. These fragmented approaches do not consider critical constraints, including field stability under environmental stress (e.g., UV degradation and rainfastness), the need for systematic resistance risk assessment, ecological safety for non-target organisms, and compatibility with IPM systems. Consequently, computationally optimized candidates often fail in subsequent development stages due to overlooked agricultural complexities, creating a pronounced translational gap between laboratory optimization and field efficacy.

A systematic, end-to-end AI framework that seamlessly integrates data ecosystems, computational architectures, and agricultural constraints is urgently needed to bridge this gap. Despite the transformative potential of AI in fungicide discovery, existing reviews exhibit critical limitations that impact their applicability to agricultural contexts. Reviews of AI in drug discovery predominantly focus on human therapeutics, prioritizing pharmacokinetics (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, toxicity [ADMET]) relevant to mammalian systems. This focus overlooks agricultural constraints such as field stability, rainfastness, pollinator safety, and soil microbiome impacts. Conversely, previous agricultural AI reviews address only single stages (e.g., virtual screening alone or resistance prediction alone) without encompassing the integrated, closed-loop ecosystem required for successful fungicide development from target identification to field deployment. This fragmentation creates a substantial knowledge gap: no comprehensive framework currently exists that specifically tailors AI-driven design workflows to the unique biological and ecological complexities of plant pathogenic fungi.

The present review addresses this critical gap by offering the first comprehensive framework specifically tailored to plant pathogenic fungi. It integrates resistance prediction, agroecosystem safety, and field applicability throughout the entire workflow, rather than treating these components as afterthoughts. We systematically explore the AI-driven fungicide design (AIFD) platform through four interdependent components: a plant pathogen-specific data ecosystem for aggregating and processing diverse data types to provide reliable model inputs; a modular technical architecture for delivering scalable computing resources and user-friendly interfaces; a linear development workflow for guiding fungicides from target identification to pre-registration, which ensures alignment with agricultural priorities; and a specialized resistance prediction workflow to assess long-term efficacy and prevent rapid fungal adaptation. By bridging computational innovation with agricultural realities, this review provides a comprehensive framework for translating AI advancements into practical, sustainable plant disease management solutions.

Overall architecture and workflow of AIFD

AIFD is a closed-loop system that integrates data, technology, and processes to accelerate fungicide development and ensure sustainability. Its architecture comprises four interdependent components, each addressing distinct yet complementary needs.

The foundational element is the plant pathogen-specific data ecosystem. It aggregates raw data from five key domains: pathogen multi-omics, chemical libraries, knowledge graphs, experimental/field trials, and safety/toxicity records (Figure 1). These data are cleaned, standardized, and integrated to generate high-quality inputs for AI models. Such biological and agriculture-specific data are critical for producing field-applicable candidates (Gaulton et al., 2012; Stajich et al., 2012; Gill et al., 2025). The modular microservice architecture serves as the technical backbone of AIFD (Figure 2). Its layers, ranging from infrastructure (cloud computing, distributed storage) to user interfaces (role-specific tools), fulfill core functions: handling computational tasks, managing data access and quality, hosting AI models, orchestrating workflows, and providing stakeholder-friendly interfaces. This design enables flexible updates, such as integrating new resistance prediction models without disrupting existing systems (Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). A further component is the linear multiphase workflow, which guides fungicide candidates from concept to pre-registration (Figure 3). Key phases include defining user requirements, target discovery, virtual screening, molecular optimization, resistance assessment, safety/efficacy validation, and delivering pre-registration candidates. User feedback refines subsequent iterations. The AI-driven resistance prediction workflow constitutes a critical sub-component (Figure 4). It specifically identifies resistance hotspots, models mutant target structures, assesses binding affinity changes, and simulates resistance spread. Plant pathogen-specific data ecosystem: The foundation of AIFD

The plant pathogen-specific data ecosystem serves as the fundamental prerequisite for AIFD. It determines whether computational innovations yield practical, field-applicable fungicides. Unlike generic AI data systems that prioritize volume over context, this ecosystem captures the complexities of plant–fungus interactions, ranging from global fungal genetic variability to fungicide–crop–agroecosystem dynamics. Operating as a closed-loop value chain, it encompasses raw data collection (diverse, context-rich inputs), unified integration (breaking data silos), systematic processing (refining AI-compatible data), and application to core workflows (target discovery, screening, and validation). Each step addresses agricultural data challenges: geographic bias, experimental inconsistency, and fragmentation. This ecosystem grounds models in the biological and environmental realities of crop protection (Figure 1). Without this ecosystem, AIFD risks generating laboratory-only candidates that fail in real fields, compromising the objectives of sustainable agriculture.

Raw data sources: Capturing agricultural context

Raw data collection is a critical first step in AIFD, shaping the relevance of subsequent processes. It aggregates five complementary, globally diverse data categories to address core fungicide development needs, ranging from pathogen biology to environmental safety.

Pathogen multi-omics data form the foundation of target identification. FungiDB provides fungal genomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic data, offering functional context to distinguish essential genes (Stajich et al., 2012). The AlphaFold Protein Structure Database presents 3D structures of key proteins for structure-based screening (Varadi et al., 2022). PhytoPath (Pedro et al., 2016) curates phenotype-genotype associations, ensuring that targets are relevant to pathogenicity.

Complementing genomic data, chemical data are tailored to agricultural demands. The Pesticide Properties Database (PPDB) documents field-relevant traits, such as soil persistence and foliar retention, of commercial fungicides (Lewis and Tzilivakis, 2017). ChEMBL, a manually curated chemical database of bioactive molecules with drug-like properties links compound structures to antifungal efficacy, enabling agriculture-specific structure–activity relationship (SAR) modeling (Zdrazil et al., 2024). Furthermore, COlleCtion of Open Natural ProdUcTs 2.0 curates natural antifungal products, supporting the development of eco-friendly formulations for low-input farming (Chandrasekhar et al., 2025).

In addition to chemical structures, knowledge graph data integrate disparate information via natural-language-processing-driven literature mining and expert curation. This approach captures context-rich associations (e.g., mutation-efficacy links and environmental impacts) (Lee et al., 2020), enabling the identification of real-world risks that can be overlooked in isolated laboratory data and thus helping to avoid costly errors.

Experimental and field data further bridge laboratory-to-field gaps through diverse data streams. Conventional field trial databases aggregate efficacy data across agroecosystems, reflecting environmental and crop-related effects (Mundt, 2025). Resistance databases document mutation frequencies in natural populations, guiding the prioritization of targets with lower resistance risk (Bédard et al., 2025). Complementing these aggregated datasets, IoT sensor networks—including spore counters, soil moisture monitors, and drone-based observations—provide real-time field data. Such systems represent current foundational connectivity for on-farm condition monitoring, rather than advanced autonomous platforms (Poniszewska-Marańda and Czechowska, 2021).

Finally, safety and toxicity data support both regulatory approval and IPM. These data quantify fungicide impacts on pollinators, soil microflora, and fauna, whereas phytotoxicity profiles document cultivar-specific symptoms such as chlorosis, safeguarding the ecosystem services that underpin productive agriculture.

Unified data integration hub: Eliminating silos

A data association engine extends beyond simple linking to establish causal and correlative relationships across data dimensions. For instance, it can identify how a mutation in the M. oryzae CYP51 enzyme (genetic data) reduces the binding affinity of a triazole compound (chemical data). This reduction in binding affinity then lowers field efficacy (field data) and may require increased application rates; these higher rates raise risks to non-target organisms (safety data) (Gill et al., 2025). Such multi-step relationships are critical for AI models to make holistic predictions, as they mirror the complexity of real agricultural systems.

A metadata management system ensures data reliability and security. Metadata include details such as fungal strain sources, experimental conditions, and access permissions. They track data lineage, allowing researchers to validate results and regulators to audit predictions. The system also enforces access control: proprietary compound structures from agricultural enterprises are encrypted and restricted to authorized users, whereas smallholder field data are anonymized to protect privacy (Dhaka et al., 2023; Ibrahim et al., 2025). This balance between openness and protection is critical for encouraging data sharing and addressing gaps in understudied regions.

Systematic data processing: Ensuring quality and compatibility

Systematic data processing transforms raw, heterogeneous data into standardized, high-quality resources suitable for reliable AI model use. It refines data while retaining agricultural context; even small inconsistencies, such as variation in assay conditions, can undermine model reliability.

Data cleansing addresses duplicates and sparsity. Duplicate entries (e.g., the same compound represented by different simplified molecular input line entry system(SMILES) strings) are removed via RDKit canonicalization (Chandrasekhar et al., 2023). Invalid or incomplete entries (e.g., protein sequences lacking key domains) are filtered via sequence alignment.

Data augmentation addresses chemical space imbalance and dataset sparsity through the algorithmic generation of biologically plausible synthetic data. Scaffold rotation generates structurally diverse analogs by modifying core molecular frameworks while preserving pharmacophoric features, thus expanding chemical coverage without experimental synthesis. SMILES enumeration produces multiple string representations of identical structures to improve molecular encoder robustness. These techniques serve as preprocessing steps to enrich sparse datasets—common for tropical pathogens and minor crops—and are distinct from quality control (QC)/data cleansing processes.

Data standardization eliminates inconsistencies regarding formats and metrics. Chemical structures are converted from 2D SMILES representations to 3D conformations via the experimental torsion-angle knowledge directed geometry algorithm (Riniker and Landrum, 2015). For bioactivity metrics, we support the use of 0–1 normalization in multimodal data integration workflows that combine disparate metrics—such as binding affinity, toxicity scores, and physicochemical properties—into unified optimization objectives. However, for single-parameter potency data, log transformation (pIC50, negative logarithm of IC₅₀) is more biologically meaningful in SAR modeling. Consequently, log transformation is utilized for SAR modeling, whereas 0–1 scaling is reserved for multi-objective optimization workflows. Environmental parameters (e.g., rainfall and soil pH) are standardized to consistent units, enabling cross-region data comparison.

Data fusion integrates cross-source data to capture context-dependent relationships that determine fungicide performance. For example, F. graminearum gene expression data (FungiDB) can be linked with IoT-derived weather records to explain reduced efficacy during wet seasons (Escribà-Gelonch et al., 2024). Similarly, crop variety data can be integrated with pathogen data to adjust for efficacy variability across cultivars.

Advanced preprocessing for sparse data uses transfer learning strategies that leverage external datasets for model pretraining, followed by fine-tuning on limited target-specific data. However, pretraining on mammalian pharmacological datasets (e.g., ChEMBL) can degrade performance for fungicide targets due to domain shifts in chemical space. For instance, lipophilicity distributions differ greatly between pharmaceuticals, which are typically hydrophilic for bioavailability, and agrochemicals, which are often lipophilic for cuticular penetration. Transfer learning should be avoided when the phylogenetic distance between source and target organisms is large or when chemical property distributions substantially diverge. Instead, we recommend fungicide-specific pretraining datasets (e.g., the PubChem agrochemical subset) to minimize negative transfer risks. This paradigm is explicitly framed as model training preparation, rather than data cleansing or augmentation.

QC ensures data consistency and relevance. Experimental data QC enforces standardized assay protocols (e.g., incubation time and nutrient medium); noncompliant data are flagged or excluded. Field data QC verifies the completeness of metadata (e.g., crop growth stage and application rate). QC procedures also exclude laboratory-only compounds with physicochemical properties unsuitable for agricultural use (Moermond et al., 2017).

Unlike pharmaceutical pipelines, which prioritize metabolic stability and blood–brain barrier penetration, fungicide design requires specific agricultural endpoint assessments. We replace vague physicochemical references with precise parameters, including UV absorption spectra (280–400 nm) for photostability prediction, soil organic carbon partition coefficients for environmental fate and groundwater contamination risk, Henry’s law constant for volatility and atmospheric drift potential, and rainfastness indices, including leaf-surface adhesion and resistance to wash-off. These parameters distinguish agrochemical optimization from drug discovery priorities; for instance, whereas pharmaceuticals are optimized for metabolic stability in liver microsomes, fungicides require controlled soil biodegradability to prevent environmental persistence. Data application: Powering core AIFD workflows

The final step, data application, ensures that processed data directly support each stage of the AIFD workflow, translating high-quality inputs into actionable outputs. In target discovery, standardized multi-omics data enable graph neural networks (GNNs) to identify genes that are conserved across fungal strains to ensure broad efficacy while remaining absent in host crops to minimize phytotoxicity. In virtual screening, standardized chemical libraries and 3D target structures guide equivariant GNNs (EGNNs) to predict binding poses and affinities, whereas processed safety data ensure that candidate compounds are not toxic to pollinators. In resistance prediction, processed mutation databases and population genetics data are used to train gradient-boosted models to identify resistance hotspots; processed structural data support molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to quantify binding changes. In safety validation, processed toxicity data inform DL models to predict non-target effects, ensuring alignment with IPM principles. Whereas the data ecosystem provides the foundational input for AI models, the modular microservice-based technical architecture serves as the engine that converts these data into actionable insights. This section outlines the technical infrastructure that operationalizes the data resources described above.

Modular microservice technical architecture: Enabling scalable AIFD

The modular microservice technical architecture of AIFD is purpose-built to address the unique complexities of agricultural data, including its diversity (spanning genomics, field observations, and chemical structures), variability across geographic regions and farming systems, and large scale (petabytes of multi-omics and trial data), while prioritizing decoupling, reusability, and stakeholder accessibility. Unlike monolithic architectures, which struggle to adapt to new data types or evolving user needs, this layered design separates technical concerns into distinct, interconnected layers, each optimized for a specific function. Bidirectional data flow ensures seamless integration: user requirements flow top-down from the interface to lower layers, whereas processed outputs (e.g., lead compounds and risk reports) flow bottom-up. The microservice model allows individual components to be updated, scaled, or replaced without disrupting the entire system (Figure 2; Table 2). Infrastructure layer: Computing and security foundation

The infrastructure layer provides computational resources and security frameworks tailored to agricultural AI workloads. Rather than prescribing specific hardware configurations or mandating particular architectural patterns, computational resource allocation should align with algorithmic requirements. Graphics processing unit (GPU) acceleration is essential for training DL architectures, such as EGNNs and AlphaFold-based structure prediction; central processing unit (CPU)-based computation is sufficient for gradient-boosted models (XGBoost) and data preprocessing tasks. This flexibility allows researchers to optimize resource allocation according to workflow demands, rather than arbitrary hardware assignments.

Regarding software architecture, whereas microservices offer modularity and independent component updates, they are not mandatory and may be suboptimal for certain applications. Monolithic architectures might be preferable for high-throughput screening tasks that require minimal inter-service communication latency and maximal computational speed. The choice between these approaches should be guided by specific computational priorities, balancing flexibility against performance requirements, rather than imposing a universal standard.

Security measures remain essential to protect proprietary agricultural data. AES-256 encryption safeguards sensitive chemical structures (e.g., unpublished compounds) and role-based access control ensures protection of restricted genetic information (e.g., endemic fungal strain genomes) (Selvi and Sakthivel, 2025).

Data service layer: Managing unified data access

Situated above the infrastructure layer, the data service layer addresses a core challenge in agricultural AI: data source fragmentation and inconsistency. Agricultural data are often distributed across smallholder records, research laboratory databases, and industry trials, with diverse formats (e.g., Excel spreadsheets for field notes and FAST-All files for genomic sequences) and inconsistent standards, including variable metrics, for disease control efficacy.

The primary role of this layer is to unify these data into a high-quality, standardized ecosystem that AI models can reliably use. This unification is achieved through a cluster of specialized, interconnected databases. Multi-omics databases, such as FungiDB, store fungal genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data (Stajich et al., 2012). Chemical libraries, such as ChEMBL, contain compound structures and bioactivity records (Gaulton et al., 2012). Knowledge graph databases integrate relationships among pathogens, crops, and compounds—for example, linking B. cinerea strains to strawberry varieties and effective fungicides (Gill et al., 2025). Safety and toxicity databases, such as SIDER-Agri, document impacts on non-target organisms and crops (Chen et al., 2022).

In addition to data storage, this layer provides critical data processing services. Integration tools merge disparate datasets, such as linking F. graminearum genomic data to field trial results for SDHI fungicides. Data cleansing tools remove duplicates through RDKit SMILES canonicalization and address gaps via transfer learning for sparse tropical pathogen data (Scalfani et al., 2022). Standardization tools convert data into consistent formats—for example, transforming 2D SMILES into 3D molecular conformations via the experimental torsion-angle knowledge directed geometry algorithm—and normalize metrics, such as scaling bioactivity values to a 0–1 range for cross-study comparisons (Das and Merz, 2024). This standardization ensures that AI models—whether predicting target binding or resistance risk—operate on unified datasets, thus eliminating errors related to incompatible formats or uncalibrated metrics.

AI model service layer: Specialized computations for plant pathogens

The AI model service layer serves as the engine of AIFD, hosting specialized algorithms tailored to the unique challenges of plant-pathogen-focused fungicide design. These challenges differ substantially from those encountered in pharmaceutical AI; for example, this domain prioritizes field stability rather than human pharmacokinetics. Each model is optimized for a specific task, with modifications that reflect agricultural priorities.

EGNNs are adapted to analyze plant–fungus interaction networks, preserving geometric invariance to accurately predict ligand–target binding poses (Veličković, 2023). This preservation of geometric invariance is critical for modeling 3D molecular interactions (Flores-Hernandez and Martinez-Ledesma, 2024). Transformer-based reinforcement learning (i.e., Transformer-RL) models incorporate reward functions that prioritize binding affinity along with field-relevant traits, such as UV resistance and rainfastness. Such traits are essential for foliar fungicides that must tolerate outdoor conditions (Wise et al., 2024). Latent diffusion models generate de novo compounds by learning SARs from agricultural chemical libraries, ensuring that novel molecules fit fungal target active sites while meeting agronomic physicochemical criteria, such as solubility for spray application (Luo et al., 2024). Gradient-boosted models such as XGBoost are trained on resistance mutation databases and field efficacy records to identify mutation hotspots in fungal targets, such as H134 in SDHI enzymes, thus integrating real-world resistance trends to avoid high-risk candidates (Silva et al., 2022). DL-based prediction models use multimodal data—including molecular descriptors, 3D structures, and crop response images—to assess both antifungal potency (e.g., minimum inhibitory concentration) and phytotoxicity (e.g., leaf chlorosis), ensuring that candidate compounds are safe for crops (Hong and Kwon, 2025; Li et al., 2025).

Recognizing that transparency is essential for stakeholder trust, an explainable AI (XAI) module is incorporated (Mohseni et al., 2021). Attention mechanisms highlight key molecular features driving predictions, such as a bromine atom at the R3 position enhancing binding to mutant targets, whereas natural language reports translate complex outputs into actionable insights.

The layer also supports continuous model improvement. Retraining pipelines update models with new data, such as emerging fungal strains and updated field trial results, thus ensuring that algorithms adapt to evolving agricultural threats.

Comparative methodological selection for agricultural applications

Selection of specific AI methodologies for fungicide development requires critical evaluation of trade-offs among computational demands, data requirements, and suitability for agricultural endpoints. EGNNs and conventional GNNs present distinct computational trade-offs. Whereas EGNNs capture 3D geometric invariance critical for accurate binding pose prediction, their computational intensity may be prohibitive for ultra-large library screening (> 108 compounds). In such cases, conventional message-passing GNNs offer sufficient accuracy with lower latency, making them more suitable for initial large-scale filtering.

XGBoost and DL exhibit complementary strengths for resistance prediction, depending on data availability. Although Transformer-RL excels in molecular optimization through sequential decision-making, XGBoost often outperforms DL in identifying resistance hotspots when training data are limited (< 1,000 samples)—a common scenario for emerging pathogens with sparse resistance mutation records. In contrast, DL architectures scale more effectively with large datasets (> 10,000 samples), capturing complex epistatic interactions that gradient-boosted models may overlook.

Domain-specific trade-offs further complicate model selection. Models developed for pharmaceutical applications optimize mammalian pharmacokinetics (e.g., blood–brain barrier penetration and metabolic stability in liver microsomes) that are not directly applicable to fungicide design, where cuticular penetration, rainfastness, and photostability are critical. For instance, models that prioritize high aqueous solubility for intravenous drug delivery may incorrectly penalize lipophilic fungicides required for cuticular penetration. Model selection should thus be guided by three criteria: data availability (small datasets favor XGBoost, large datasets favor DL), computational resources (limited GPU access favors conventional GNNs over EGNNs), and specific agricultural endpoints (e.g., cuticular penetration models require feature engineering distinct from that used for blood–brain barrier prediction).

Application service layer: Orchestrating end-to-end workflows

The application service layer functions as the orchestrator of AIFD, translating high-level user requirements into a sequence of executable tasks that leverage the data and AI model layers. Its primary role is to eliminate silos between technical components, ensuring that data flow seamlessly from target discovery to safety validation.

Key modules within this layer correspond to stages of the AIFD development workflow. The target discovery module uses GNNs from the AI model layer and multi-omics data from the data service layer to identify essential fungal targets. The virtual screening module coordinates EGNN-based docking and DL-based scoring to prioritize candidate compounds. The molecular generation module deploys Transformer-RL and latent diffusion models to optimize hits or design novel molecules. The resistance prediction module integrates gradient-boosted models and agent-based simulations to assess long-term efficacy. The safety validation module uses DL-based prediction models and digital twin frameworks to evaluate crop and environmental impacts.

Cross-module integration is enabled by application programming interfaces (APIs) that connect microservices, minimizing manual intervention (Guo et al., 2024). For instance, virtual screening hits are automatically transferred to the molecular generation module for optimization, and resistance risk scores from the resistance prediction module determine which candidates advance to safety validation. This orchestration accelerates development timelines while ensuring consistency across stages.

User interface layer: Stakeholder-centric interaction

The user interface layer represents the front end of AIFD and is designed to make advanced computational tools accessible to diverse stakeholders. We distinguish between primary users—computational chemists, toxicologists, and molecular biologists directly involved in molecular design—and secondary stakeholders, including smallholder farmers and agricultural extension officers, who participate through feedback loops rather than direct design activities.

For primary users, the interface provides advanced tools, including molecular visualization software such as PyMOL for analyzing predicted target structures (Verma et al., 2025), customizable virtual screening workflows that allow adjustment of parameters such as physicochemical filters, and access to raw model outputs (e.g., binding affinity scores) for in-depth analysis. For secondary stakeholders acting as intermediaries (i.e., agricultural extension officers who bridge developers and farmers), dashboards summarize localized insights, crop-specific fungicide recommendations , and alerts for emerging disease outbreaks. For field-level secondary stakeholders (i.e., smallholder farmers), the interface is simplified to prioritize usability, local language support, and audio-guided instructions for application. These users do not engage in molecular design but contribute critical field observations (e.g., efficacy reports, resistance symptoms, and wash-off incidents) that are incorporated into model retraining.

Role-based access control ensures the protection of sensitive data: researchers can access proprietary compound information, whereas farmers are limited to actionable recommendations. The platform operates as a lifecycle management system in which farmers participate through feedback loops without directly engaging in molecular design. Specifically, farmers report field efficacy, resistance emergence, and phytotoxicity symptoms via mobile interfaces. These data are transmitted through the field data module to the data service layer, where they are utilized to retrain models (e.g., by adjusting rainfastness filters based on reported wash-off incidents or updating efficacy predictions based on observed field performance). The feedback mechanism follows this pipeline: farmer observations → field data module → data service layer → model retraining pipeline → updated predictions. This architecture ensures that farmer input informs platform evolution and refinement while remaining distinct from the molecular design processes conducted by primary users.

Practical implementation barriers and accessibility

Bridging the gap between architectural vision and practical deployment requires addressing the “digital divide” between well-resourced industry laboratories and public-sector research institutions. Small research groups face considerable barriers in accessing the high-performance computing infrastructure necessary to train complex models such as EGNNs. To mitigate this limitation, the architecture supports hybrid cloud-computing strategies, allowing elastic scaling of computational resources without substantial local capital investment. Furthermore, the platform integrates open-source alternatives (e.g., DeepChem and RDKit-based workflows) along with proprietary solutions to reduce software dependency costs. Emerging federated learning approaches are particularly important because they enable small research groups to collaboratively train models on distributed datasets without centralizing sensitive data or requiring local petabyte-scale storage, thus democratizing access to AIFD capabilities.

Ensuring seamless interoperability between heterogeneous tools and datasets remains a fundamental technical challenge. The architecture adheres to Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable data principles and utilizes standardized APIs (e.g., OpenAPI specifications) to facilitate communication between cheminformatics tools and AI models. Standardization of chemical representation formats—SMILES for 2D structures, international chemical identifier for unique identification, and macromolecular Crystallographic Information File for macromolecular structural data—ensures compatibility across modules. To avoid vendor lock-in associated with proprietary AI platforms, the architecture promotes containerized microservices (e.g., Docker and Kubernetes), which encapsulate individual tools with their dependencies. This approach ensures reproducible deployment across diverse computing environments while maintaining interoperability.

Long-term sustainability of AI models requires rigorous machine learning operations protocols tailored to agricultural applications. The architecture incorporates model registries to track versioning and lineage, ensuring that predictions can be traced to specific algorithmic iterations. Automated monitoring of data drift—critical as new fungal resistance mutations emerge—triggers retraining pipelines to maintain predictive accuracy. Reproducibility standards are enforced through containerized environments and documented experimental protocols, ensuring that AI-predicted efficacy and safety endpoints remain reliable as underlying data distributions evolve.

To ground this architecture in current practice, several partial implementations demonstrate its feasibility. Public-sector examples include Joint Genome Institute fungal genomics pipelines integrated with machine-learning-based screening capabilities, as well as AgGateway’s analysis of microbiome differential abundance by pooling tobit models framework for agricultural data interoperability. Academic platforms, such as Eidgenössische Technische Hochschule Zurich’s cheminformatics screening services for plant protection applications, illustrate university-based implementations. Hybrid models, exemplified by collaborative AI research initiatives among major agrochemical companies (e.g., Syngenta, Bayer, and BASF) and academic institutions, further demonstrate the potential for effective collaboration while navigating proprietary constraints.

Linear multiphase development workflow: From target to pre-registration

The linear multiphase development workflow of AIFD is not merely a sequence of technical steps but a user-centric framework designed to align each stage of fungicide design with the practical priorities of agricultural systems: effective control of target pathogens, safety for crops and ecosystems, and long-term sustainability in the context of fungal adaptation. This workflow is intentionally integrated with the AIFD data ecosystem to ensure high-quality inputs and with the modular technical architecture to enable scalable computation and user accessibility. Consequently, decisions from target selection to pre-registration are grounded in real-world agricultural needs (Figure 3).

Input layer: Defining requirements and data support

The workflow begins with the input layer, a foundational stage that defines the problem space for AIFD by translating user needs into actionable, data-supported parameters. This step is critical to ensure that computational design aligns with agricultural requirements. Users specify not only the target plant pathogen and protected crop but also the intended application context. To reduce ambiguity, these requirements are standardized. For example, specifying the geographic region of application ensures that subsequent analyses consider locally adapted fungal strains. The input layer integrates data support from the AIFD ecosystem to ground the workflow in reliable information. Multi-omics databases, such as FungiDB, supply fungal genomic and transcriptomic data to inform target identification (Stajich et al., 2012). Chemical libraries, such as ChEMBL-Agri, provide compound structures and bioactivity data for virtual screening (Zdrazil et al., 2024). Knowledge graphs offer context on resistance trends, and safety databases (e.g., SIDER-Agri) establish benchmarks for non-target toxicity (Chen et al., 2022).

Target discovery and validation: Identifying essential, druggable targets

The target discovery and validation phase focuses on identifying molecular targets that are not only biologically essential to the pathogen but also suitable for fungicide development, while avoiding targets that are essential under laboratory conditions but irrelevant in the field. GNNs are central to this process because they excel at analyzing the complex, interconnected metabolic and pathogenicity networks of plant pathogenic fungi using multi-omics data (Veličković, 2023). GNNs utilize unsupervised learning to identify genes or proteins that meet three key criteria: phylogenetic conservation across global pathogen strains to ensure broad efficacy, essentiality for fungal survival or pathogenicity, and phylogenetic divergence from host crop orthologs to minimize off-target effects on the crop (Veličković, 2023). To further mitigate risk, knowledge graphs are utilized to exclude targets with documented resistance issues. For targets lacking experimental 3D structures—a common limitation for understudied pathogens—DL models such as AlphaFold generate high-fidelity structural predictions (Varadi et al., 2022). These predictions are validated against available experimental data, such as through alignment with wild-type SDHI structures in the Protein Data Bank (PDB), to ensure accuracy (Jumper et al., 2021). Furthermore, probe-based druggability assessment is conducted by screening a library of known fungicide probes against a single target to determine pocket accessibility and binding propensity. This approach evaluates whether the target’s binding pocket is accessible to small molecules and excludes structurally unsuitable targets. It is distinct from inverse or virtual screening (one ligand versus many targets) and conventional docking (many ligands versus one target for hit identification). The output of this phase is not merely a list of targets but a set of prioritized candidates, each accompanied by structural data—including 3D models and binding pocket coordinates—and a clear rationale for selection, providing a foundation for subsequent screening.

Virtual screening: Prioritizing high-potential compounds

Virtual screening builds on target validation to filter large chemical libraries and identify a focused set of hit compounds with strong potential for further development (Gentile et al., 2022). This phase balances computational efficiency with rigor by using a two-step filtering strategy to prevent downstream optimization from being overwhelmed with low-quality candidates. Primary screening first narrows the library using structural and physicochemical filters. Structural similarity filters retain compounds exhibiting motifs known to interact with the target, such as pyrazole scaffolds for SDHI enzymes or related fungal proteins. Agronomically tailored physicochemical filters ensure compatibility with agricultural application requirements. Secondary screening then refines these candidates using structure-based methods that address the dynamic nature of biological systems. EGNNs predict ligand-target binding poses while explicitly modeling active-site flexibility, such as side-chain movements within the SDHI binding pocket, to avoid false negatives caused by rigid structure assumptions (Matlock et al., 2021). DL-based scoring functions, such as DeepScore, rank compounds according to predicted binding affinity by incorporating complex molecular interactions, including hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, and electrostatic forces—interactions that conventional scoring functions often oversimplify (Wang et al., 2019). To ensure that safety is addressed early, ADMET prediction is integrated into this stage. Compounds with unfavorable profiles are excluded, including those classified as highly toxic (acute contact median lethal dose（Lethal Dose, 50%） < 2 μg/bee) and those failing to meet the “practically non-toxic” threshold (acute contact median lethal dose（Lethal Dose, 50%） < 100 μg/bee) according to U.S. environmental protection agency Tier I toxicity classification (U.S. environmental protection agency office of chemical safety and pollution prevention 850.3020) and european food safety authority guidance for bee toxicity testing. Compounds exhibiting excessive soil persistence (> 6 months), which could disrupt ecosystems or contaminate water, are also removed (Xiong et al., 2021). The output is a set of high-potential hit compounds, each annotated with binding scores and predicted bioactivity and safety profiles, ready for optimization.

Molecular generation and optimization: Refining hits or designing novel compounds

Molecular generation and optimization transform screening hits into lead compounds by addressing limitations of initial candidates, such as weak binding and poor field stability, or by designing de novo molecules to fill gaps in existing libraries. Hit optimization leverages Transformer-RL models, which support sequential, context-aware molecular modification. These models use reward functions calibrated to agricultural priorities, including enhancement of target binding affinity via reduced binding free energy, improvement of field stability through increased UV half-life, and maintenance of synthetic accessibility. Accurate prediction of photolysis for UV half-life assessment via quantum mechanics calculations (e.g., time-dependent density functional theory) is computationally prohibitive for the rapid iteration cycles required in RL workflows. Current AIFD platforms rely on surrogate models (e.g., GNNs trained on quantum mechanics-derived datasets) to approximate UV stability. This approach creates a trade-off between computational accuracy and throughput because surrogate-based RL prioritizes speed over precision. Consequently, experimental validation of photostability remains necessary for top-ranked candidates, rather than relying solely on computational predictions.

When existing hits are insufficient, de novo molecular generation utilizes latent diffusion models. These generative AI tools learn SARs from chemical databases, such as ChEMBL-Agri, and generate novel compounds that conform to target-specific structural constraints (Atance et al., 2022; Morehead and Cheng, 2024). It is important to distinguish between molecular graph generation and retrosynthetic pathway prediction. Although generative models can suggest synthetically tractable scaffolds, they do not produce complete, feasible synthetic routes. Retrosynthetic pathway prediction requires separate specialized algorithms (e.g., RetroPrime or neural-symbolic approaches); generated structures must subsequently undergo computational retrosynthesis planning to assess synthetic feasibility.

To address domain shift risk and database limitations, we critically evaluate the use of general chemical databases, such as ChEMBL, for generative model training. Models predominantly trained on pharmaceutical data exhibit inherent bias toward high-molecular-weight, hydrophilic compounds optimized for mammalian bioavailability—properties often unsuitable for agricultural field applications, where lipophilicity, cuticular penetration, and environmental stability are critical. We caution against direct transfer of pharmaceutical optimization models to fungicide design and recommend domain adaptation strategies, including pretraining on agrochemical-specific databases (e.g., PPDB and AGRITOX) followed by fine-tuning on curated fungicide datasets, to mitigate domain shift and align generated compounds with agricultural physicochemical requirements.

Three-dimensional (3D) quantitative SAR (QSAR) analysis further refines these lead compounds by identifying steric and electrostatic features that drive antifungal efficacy (Gupta et al., 2021). The output of this phase is a set of lead compounds, each supported by detailed 3D structural models, predicted efficacy against the target pathogen, and feasible synthetic routes, thereby facilitating translation from computational design to laboratory synthesis.

Resistance prediction and evaluation: Ensuring long-term sustainability

Resistance prediction and evaluation are essential to ensuring that lead compounds maintain efficacy across growing seasons and counter rapid fungal adaptation. This phase integrates an AI-driven workflow to assess risk across genetic, structural, and ecological dimensions. First, gradient-boosted models analyze resistance mutation databases (e.g., the Fusarium Resistance Database) and population genetics data to identify mutation hotspots—residues in target proteins that are prone to adaptive change. DL models then generate 3D structures of mutated targets, revealing active site alterations (e.g., H134R in SDHI enzymes) (Hervé and Michailides, 2021). MD simulations (e.g., implemented in assisted model building with energy refinement) quantify the impacts of mutations on ligand binding. Molecular mechanics/generalized Born surface area methods are used to calculate binding free energy changes; compounds with differences of < 2 kcal/mol between wild-type and mutant targets are classified as low risk (Wang et al., 2021). Finally, agent-based models simulate the spread of resistant strains by integrating spore dispersal, crop rotation, and fungicide application patterns to predict field longevity. Outputs include a refined set of lead compounds with low resistance risk and a comprehensive risk assessment report, ensuring that only sustainable candidates advance.

Safety and efficacy validation: Confirming field applicability

Safety and efficacy validation bridges computational predictions and real-world performance, ensuring that lead compounds meet agricultural application standards. This phase uses a hybrid approach that combines computational modeling with targeted experiments to narrow the laboratory-to-field gap. DL models predict key metrics (e.g., minimum inhibitory concentration for efficacy and leaf chlorosis for phytotoxicity) using multimodal data (molecular descriptors, 3D structures, and bioactivity records). Trained on datasets such as ChEMBL and the Phytotoxicity Database, these models enhance prediction accuracy (Gaulton et al., 2012).

In addition to direct toxicity endpoints, comprehensive safety validation must evaluate ecosystem-level impacts in alignment with One Health frameworks that connect plant, soil, and environmental health. First, the assessment of soil microbiome disruption requires AI models that integrate multi-omics training data (metagenomics and metatranscriptomics) to predict impacts on soil ecological networks, including arbuscular mycorrhizae, nitrogen-fixing bacteria, and decomposer communities. These models can flag compounds with a high risk of disrupting beneficial plant–microbe interactions or soil carbon cycling processes. Second, resistance spillover effects require explicit assessment through resistance ecology models that evaluate horizontal transfer of resistance genes and selection pressures on non-target fungal communities, including aquatic hyphomycetes, saprophytic soil fungi, and endophytes that provide essential ecosystem services. Finally, One Health integration warrants evaluation of bioaccumulation risks within food webs, impacts on pollinator gut microbiomes, and the long-term resilience of agroecosystems under repeated application of AI-designed fungicides. AIFD platforms should incorporate agroecosystem service metrics—such as maintaining > 85% of baseline microbial diversity—as optimization constraints along with fungicidal efficacy to ensure sustainable deployment.

Digital twin technology is used to simulate fungicide performance across diverse climatic and agroecological conditions (Escribà-Gelonch et al., 2024). Experimental validation includes in vitro assays (e.g., leaf disk tests) to confirm antifungal activity and crop safety, as well as small-scale field trials to assess yield protection and impacts on non-target organisms. Outputs include pre-registration candidate compounds and a validation report that synthesizes computational and experimental findings. This report provides regulators and researchers with the evidence needed to advance candidates to final development stages.

Output layer: Delivering results and enabling feedback

The workflow concludes with the output layer, which delivers actionable results to stakeholders and allows continuous platform improvement. Core deliverables include pre-registration candidate compounds, each accompanied by molecular structures, in vitro and field efficacy data, safety assessments (e.g., non-target toxicity and environmental fate), and detailed application guidelines, such as dosage, timing, and compatibility with other inputs. Equally important is the user feedback interface, which enables stakeholders to report field performance. For example, farmers might note reduced efficacy during heavy rainfall, whereas extension officers may identify challenges affecting application in smallholder fields. This feedback is systematically integrated to refine the AIFD platform. The resulting iterative cycle ensures that the workflow evolves to address emerging agricultural challenges, such as new pathogen strains and changing climate conditions, thus enhancing the relevance and impact of AIFD across diverse farming systems.

This workflow extends beyond isolated technical calculations to assess resistance risk across three interconnected dimensions: genetic, structural, and ecological (Figure 4; Table 3). It ensures that candidate fungicides not only perform well in laboratory assays but also maintain long-term efficacy under real-world agricultural conditions. Input data preparation: Standardizing resistance-related data

The workflow begins with input data preparation, a foundational step that directly influences the reliability of subsequent analyses. This stage involves not only data collection but also curation and standardization to eliminate heterogeneity, a common challenge in agricultural AI that can bias model outputs. Key inputs include wild-type target protein sequences from multi-omics databases, such as FungiDB (Stajich et al., 2012). Optimized lead compound structures, produced during the molecular generation phase, are also essential because they represent candidate molecules whose interactions with targets will be tested under mutant conditions. Resistance mutation databases, such as the Fusarium Resistance Database (Bédard et al., 2025), provide critical real-world context by cataloging known mutations (e.g., H134R in SDHI enzymes) and their documented effects on fungicide efficacy (Hervé and Michailides, 2021). Pathogen population genetics data from repositories such as PhytoPath (Pedro et al., 2016) reveal mutation frequencies in natural populations, helping to prioritize ecologically relevant risks.

Mutation hotspot identification: Locating resistance-prone sites

The mutation hotspot identification stage focuses on specific amino acid residues within the target protein that are most likely to drive resistance. This step improves efficiency by avoiding unnecessary analysis of low-risk sites. Gradient-boosted models, such as XGBoost, are utilized for their ability to process high-dimensional data; these models integrate mutation records, efficacy data, and protein sequence features to quantify associations between specific residues and reduced fungicide sensitivity (Silva et al., 2022). Resistance-focused knowledge graphs complement this analysis by linking known mutations to target proteins and related pathogens, ensuring consistency with field observations (Gill et al., 2025). The output of this step is a set of high-priority mutation hotspots that guides subsequent structural and binding analyses toward residues most relevant to resistance development.

Mutant target structure generation: Modeling structural changes

Mutant target structure generation translates genetic mutations into structural changes. This step is critical for predicting whether lead compounds will retain binding because it reveals how mutations alter the target’s active site. DL models, particularly adaptations of AlphaFold, generate high-fidelity 3D structures of mutation-bearing target proteins (Jumper et al., 2021). These models integrate evolutionary conservation signals (e.g., residue variation across fungal species) and biophysical constraints (e.g., bond lengths and hydrophobic packing) to produce thermodynamically stable and biologically realistic structures. For example, modeling of the H134R-mutant SDHI enzyme considers the larger size and positive charge of arginine, which can alter the shape and electrostatics of active sites. assisted model building with energy refinement-based energy minimization refines these models (Salomon-Ferrer et al., 2013) by correcting minor artifacts, such as strained bonds, and ensuring that active-site topology (e.g., pocket size, residue arrangement, and key interaction features) reflects in vivo conditions. Outputs include 3D models of wild-type and mutant targets, which form the structural basis for quantifying mutation impacts on compound binding.

Binding affinity change calculation: Quantifying mutation impacts

Binding affinity change calculation quantifies the effects of mutations on interactions between lead compounds and the target protein, yielding a direct measure of whether a compound might retain efficacy against resistant fungal strains. MD simulations are central to this process because they capture the dynamic behavior of ligand–target complexes over time, usually over 100–500 ns, rather than relying on static docking poses. These simulations reveal how mutations alter binding interactions; for example, whether the H134R mutation in SDHI disrupts hydrogen bonding or introduces steric clashes that displace the compound from the active site.

To translate these dynamic interactions into quantitative metrics, methods such as molecular mechanics/generalized Born surface area are used to estimate changes in binding free energy between wild-type and mutant complexes (Wang et al., 2021). Compounds that maintain stable binding—indicated by minimal changes in free energy, typically less than 2 kcal/mol—are classified as having low resistance risk because they retain the ability to engage the target despite adaptive mutations. This step is pivotal for distinguishing compounds effective only against sensitive strains from those that can tolerate fungal adaptation, thereby guiding candidate prioritization.

Resistance risk classification: Categorizing candidates

Resistance risk classification integrates multiple lines of evidence to assign each lead compound to a risk tier (low, medium, or high), ensuring that decisions are based on a comprehensive assessment rather than a single metric. Machine learning models, such as random forests or support vector machines, are trained on datasets that combine binding affinity changes from MD simulations, mutation frequencies in natural populations derived from population genetics data, and the evolutionary plasticity of the target protein (Li et al., 2015). Evolutionary plasticity refers to the capacity of a protein to tolerate mutations without loss of function.

A weighted scoring system further refines this classification. For example, a compound with a small binding affinity change (30% weight), low mutation frequency (30% weight), and a target protein with low evolutionary plasticity (40% weight) might achieve a high score and be classified as low risk. This objective, multifactorial approach avoids overreliance on a single parameter—such as ignoring rare but high-impact mutations—and ensures that risk assessments are grounded in molecular biology and ecological realities (Mohseni et al., 2021). The output, a clear risk classification for each lead compound, serves as a gatekeeper for progression to field validation.

Field resistance dynamics simulation: Predicting spread

Field resistance dynamics simulation extends resistance assessment beyond the molecular level to the agroecological scale, predicting how resistant fungal strains may spread in real-world farming systems and how management practices can mitigate this spread. Agent-based models (ABMs) are utilized for their ability to integrate complex (Stepanov and Ferson, 2024), interacting factors, including fungal population dynamics (e.g., growth rate and spore dispersal distance), crop characteristics (e.g., susceptibility and planting density), and fungicide application strategies (e.g., single vs. mixed chemistries and application frequency) (van den Bosch et al., 2014; Lehnert et al., 2015).

For example, an ABM might simulate that a lead compound applied alone will result in resistant strains reaching 50% of the population in 3 years, whereas combining it with a second fungicide with a different mode of action delays this outcome to 9 years. Such simulations not only predict resistance emergence but also provide actionable guidance for sustainable deployment, enabling growers and regulators to design strategies that extend the functional lifespan of the compound. The output—scenario-based resistance emergence timelines—bridges the gap between laboratory predictions and on-farm practice, ensuring that AIFD recommendations are both technically sound and agronomically feasible.

Output reporting: Guiding decision-making

The workflow culminates in output reporting, which synthesizes findings from all preceding steps into a comprehensive, stakeholder-oriented document. This report extends beyond a summary by incorporating data visualizations, such as heatmaps of mutation hotspots and line graphs of binding affinity changes, to communicate complex insights to non-experts (e.g., extension officers and regulators). It includes specific application strategy recommendations; for example, “rotate with a demethylation inhibitor fungicide every other application to reduce selection pressure.” The report explicitly identifies a final set of lead compounds with low resistance risk, supported by justification for their selection. For instance, “lead compound X is classified as low risk due to a binding free energy change of 1.1 kcal/mol and a predicted resistance emergence timeline of 8 years.”

This report serves as a critical decision-making tool. Researchers use it to select candidates for pre-registration, regulators rely on it to evaluate long-term safety, and growers reference it to design resistance management strategies. By linking computational predictions to practical actions, the report ensures that the resistance prediction workflow delivers tangible value across the fungicide development and deployment continuum.

Application cases of AIFD platforms

Real-world applications of AIFD platforms demonstrate how their integrated architecture addresses persistent challenges in agricultural fungicide development. This architecture—comprising data ecosystems, modular technical frameworks, and iterative workflows—prioritizes key agricultural needs, including overcoming pathogen resistance, minimizing ecological harm, and simplifying disease management for growers. The following section provides a detailed, process-oriented analysis of three representative applications, emphasizing how AIFD core components (target discovery, virtual screening, optimization, and validation) address context-specific challenges while supporting sustainable agriculture goals.

Resistance-breaking fungicides for wheat fusarium head blight (FHB)

Wheat FHB, caused by F. graminearum, is a global threat. It not only reduces yield but also contaminates grains with deoxynivalenol, a toxic mycotoxin. Conventional SDHI fungicides—once effective against FHB—have been compromised by widespread resistance, such as H134R mutations in the SDHI enzyme, creating an urgent need for resistance-breaking alternatives. AIFD platforms address this challenge through targeted, model-guided design to prioritize compounds that bind both wild-type and mutant SDHI enzymes while minimizing off-target effects on crops and beneficial organisms.

The workflow begins with target discovery, a critical step for avoiding high-risk targets prone to rapid resistance. GNNs analyze core metabolic and pathogenicity networks in F. graminearum by integrating multi-omics data. Through unsupervised learning, they identify enzymes that meet three criteria: phylogenetic conservation across global F. graminearum strains, essentiality for fungal viability and deoxynivalenol synthesis (to target growth and toxin production), and distinctness from wheat orthologs (to minimize phytotoxicity). This process identifies the SDHI enzyme as a top candidate. Knowledge graphs are used to validate target resilience by excluding alternatives with known resistance risks. For example, CYP51 is excluded because many field strains of F. graminearum harbor CYP51 mutations linked to azole resistance. In contrast, SDHI exhibits fewer resistance mutations and no cross-resistance with other chemistries.

Experimental 3D structures of mutant SDHI enzymes (e.g., the H134R variant) are limited. Thus, DL models adapted from AlphaFold2 generate high-fidelity structures by incorporating evolutionary signals from SDHI orthologs and wild-type SDHI experimental data to refine the active site. Analysis indicates that the H134R mutation introduces a positively charged arginine residue within the binding pocket, hindering conventional SDHI binding. This insight guides virtual screening by prioritizing compounds with smaller scaffolds or neutral/negatively charged groups. Virtual screening is conducted using a library of synthetic fragments and natural products through a two-step strategy. Primary screening utilizes EGNNs to retain compounds that fit the mutant binding pocket and meet foliar retention physicochemical criteria. Secondary screening implements DL-based scoring functions to quantify binding affinity, prioritizing compounds that bind both wild-type and mutant SDHI. This process refines the set of hit compounds for development.

Hit compounds are optimized via Transformer-RL models. Reward functions focus on three goals: enhancing binding to mutant SDHI (by modifying groups that interact with H134R), improving UV stability, and maintaining low toxicity to wheat and soil mycorrhizae. For example, a pyrazole-based hit may be modified by adding a bromine atom; 3D-QSAR analysis confirms that this change strengthens hydrophobic interactions with the mutant pocket without creating steric clashes. ABMs simulate the spread of resistant F. graminearum across seasons by integrating fungal dynamics, wheat phenology, and fungicide application timing. These simulations predict that the optimized compound will retain efficacy longer than conventional SDHIs by reducing selection pressure for resistance. Small-scale field trials conducted across wheat-growing regions confirm this prediction; its FHB control is better than that achieved with commercial SDHIs, deoxynivalenol levels meet food safety standards, no phytotoxicity is evident, and soil mycorrhizal colonization rates match those of untreated plots.

AI-generated antimicrobial peptides for rice blast

Rice blast, caused by M. oryzae, destroys substantial amounts of global rice yield annually. Conventional chemical fungicides often exhibit high environmental persistence or harm beneficial soil microbes, indicating a need for eco-friendly antimicrobial peptides (AMPs). However, natural AMPs lack stability and have narrow activity spectra. AIFD platforms address these issues by generating de novo AMPs targeting the M. oryzae cell membrane while ensuring crop safety and compatibility with organic farming.

The workflow begins with the construction of a specialized dataset for AMP design. This dataset integrates natural AMP sequences (with antifungal activity against plant pathogens), rice cell toxicity data (to minimize phytotoxicity), cell membrane structural data for M. oryzae (high levels of ergosterol and phosphatidylcholine, key AMP targets), and stability data (e.g., protease resistance).

This integrated dataset is used to train latent diffusion models—generative AI tools for learning AMP SARs—with two constraints: maximizing binding to the M. oryzae membrane (modeled through a membrane disruption surrogate) and minimizing interaction with rice cell membranes (via homology to low-toxicity rice peptides). Scaffold rotation and amino acid substitution are used to enhance latent space diversity, ensuring that the generated AMPs are novel.

The model generates novel AMP candidates, which are screened by graph attention GNNs for three key properties: antifungal efficacy, crop safety, and stability. Selected AMPs are synthesized and tested in vitro; several inhibit M. oryzae at practical concentrations comparable to those of commercial fungicides. The top candidate (e.g., AMP-32) effectively reduces spore germination, shows no phytotoxicity in rice leaf assays, and does not inhibit root growth at field-relevant concentrations.

Small-scale field trials further assess AMP-32, demonstrating effective blast control at seedling and booting stages (comparable to commercial triazoles). Soil samples from treated plots show no loss of beneficial microbes; mycorrhizal colonization matches that of untreated plots, and Trichoderma populations are unaffected. AMP-32 also degrades rapidly in rice tissues, lowering risks of food chain accumulation. This case illustrates the ability of AIFD to create eco-friendly alternatives to synthetic fungicides, supporting organic and low-input rice production systems.

Challenges in AIFD platforms

Despite technical progress and real-world potential, AIFD platforms face key challenges that limit global applicability, particularly in low-resource regions and diverse agroecosystems. Challenges extend beyond technical domains to encompass systemic inequities in agricultural research, data governance, and stakeholder engagement. This section provides an analysis of core barriers and their roots, impacts, and implications for sustainable, equitable plant disease management.

A key challenge in AI-driven plant pathogen management is the scarcity and inconsistency of high-quality training data, especially for understudied fungal pathogens affecting staple crops in tropical agroecosystems. Smallholder farmers rarely contribute field data, leading to underrepresentation of geographically distinct strains from low-resource regions. Inconsistent in vitro assay conditions—such as temperature, medium composition, and incubation duration—reduce comparability across studies and undermine dataset reliability. Furthermore, negative data (e.g., ineffective compounds or those with undesirable properties) are rarely documented. This publication bias skews datasets toward successful candidates, limiting the potential for models to learn from failures and develop robust SARs (Onaga et al., 2025).

Model adaptability to agroecosystem variability is another major limitation. Fungal pathogens show considerable geographic divergence, such that strains evolve unique genetic and phenotypic traits in response to local environmental conditions and host varieties. Models trained on temperate datasets often fail to generalize to tropical and subtropical agroecosystems. Environmental factors—soil pH, rainfall, humidity, and temperature—interact to affect fungicide performance, including stability, bioavailability, and mode of action. Most AI models rely on controlled laboratory data that neglect these real-world complexities. This reliance creates laboratory-to-field gaps, in which promising laboratory compounds may fail in the field due to poor rainfastness, rapid degradation, or reduced efficacy.

Poor AI model interpretability and lack of stakeholder trust hinder widespread adoption. AI models used for target identification or compound screening often function as opaque black boxes. Their complex decision-making processes are difficult to articulate. This lack of transparency makes it challenging to explain outputs to extension officers and smallholder farmers, who need clear links between AI recommendations and on-farm disease control. Without tangible explanations, adoption remains limited. Additionally, regulatory bodies require transparent documentation of toxicity and efficacy predictions for safety compliance. The lack of transparency in DL models can delay approval of AI-designed fungicides, slowing their transition to market (Gill et al., 2025).

Environmental safety in agroecosystems is often overlooked in AI-driven fungicide development. Current models focus on direct antifungal efficacy and acute toxicity to a narrow range of non-target organisms. They rarely consider indirect ecological impacts, such as effects on soil decomposer communities and beneficial plant-microbe interactions. Data regarding the effects of compounds on beneficial organisms (pollinators, natural enemies of pathogens, and soil microbes) are limited. This gap increases the risk of disrupting IPM systems, which rely on synergies among crops, pathogens, and beneficial organisms to sustain long-term productivity.

Regulatory science and AI-designed fungicides

The translational pathway from AI-designed candidates to regulatory approval and market entry presents distinct challenges in regulatory science that fundamentally differ from conventional discovery workflows. Current regulatory frameworks for accepting computational toxicology predictions, including QSAR models and DL-based toxicity classifiers, operate under established guidelines such as the organisation for economic co-operation and development Principles for QSAR Validation and the international council for harmonisation of technical requirements for pharmaceuticals for human use M7(R2) guideline for impurity assessment. These standards explicitly require models to demonstrate mechanistic interpretability and auditability. Specifically, XAI outputs must meet stringent transparency standards to support safety dossiers, ensuring that predicted endpoints are based on scientifically defensible structure–toxicity relationships rather than opaque correlations.

Dossier requirements for AI-designed fungicides greatly diverge from those used for conventionally discovered compounds. Regulatory agencies increasingly expect “algorithmic transparency” in submissions, including documentation of training data provenance, performance metrics on external validation sets (e.g., sensitivity, specificity, and predictive power), and uncertainty quantification for AI-predicted endpoints such as ecotoxicity and residue profiles. This shift extends beyond conventional submission packages that primarily rely on experimental data; current standards require comprehensive model development documentation, validation reports, and algorithmic bias assessments to establish complete evidentiary chains from data sources to predicted outcomes.

Public–private partnership frameworks are emerging as critical mechanisms to facilitate resource pooling and collaborative validation while navigating regulatory complexities. Data-sharing consortia—such as PPDB partnerships, precompetitive AI model development alliances, and tiered intellectual property agreements—enable stakeholders to share foundational algorithms and validation datasets without compromising proprietary chemical spaces. Existing initiatives, including the European Crop Protection Association’s digital transformation working groups, demonstrate the feasibility of collaborative validation workflows and provide models for establishing standardized regulatory pathways for AI-designed fungicides.

Future directions

In addition to current basic sensor connectivity, future AIFD platforms will utilize advanced IoT integration featuring three key innovations: edge computing for real-time model inference at the field level, which will enable autonomous decision-making without cloud latency; integration of drone-based hyperspectral imaging for automated disease detection and precision pathogen identification; and federated learning across decentralized IoT networks, which will allow models to update using diverse field data while preserving data privacy by avoiding centralization. These advances represent a transition from passive data collection to intelligent, autonomous agroecosystem monitoring.

Transfer learning will further enhance model adaptability across agroecosystems. Models pretrained on temperate-region datasets can be fine-tuned using smaller tropical datasets, whereas federated learning enables training across decentralized, region-specific datasets (e.g., wheat fields in India and Canada) without sharing sensitive data. These approaches ensure that models capture local fungal adaptations and maintain performance across diverse climates.

Model interpretability is expected to improve via the integration of XAI features. Attention mechanisms can highlight key target residues, and natural language reports can explain model outputs (e.g., “bromination at R3 enhances binding to the mutant target”). Such features build trust among stakeholders, accelerating adoption of AI-designed fungicides.

Agroecosystem safety remains a priority. Models predict indirect ecological effects; agent-based simulations assess beneficial microbial populations, and convolutional neural network analyze pollinator behavior to ensure IPM compatibility. Compounds that reduce mycorrhizal colonization by > 15% are excluded from further development, maintaining a balance between efficacy and ecosystem health.

Furthermore, long-term agroecosystem resilience will be systematically embedded within future AIFD optimization frameworks through advanced One Health integration. Next-generation platforms will model chronic, low-dose effects on soil food web stability and pollinator microbiome integrity across multiple growing seasons, moving beyond acute toxicity assessments to predict cumulative impacts on non-target fungal biodiversity and ecosystem service delivery. This approach includes the development of AI models that preserve natural disease suppression and nutrient cycling functions while controlling target pathogens. By treating agroecosystem health as a coequal optimization target along with immediate disease control and explicitly quantifying trade-offs between fungicidal potency and ecological resilience, AIFD will support the transition to regenerative agricultural systems that sustain productivity without compromising soil health or environmental integrity.

Smallholder accessibility will be enhanced through simplified, freely available platform versions offering local language interfaces, photo-based disease diagnosis, and cloud-based deployment without requiring high-performance computing. Partnerships with extension services will provide training to ensure that smallholder farmers can access AI-driven tools to protect crops and livelihoods.

Concluding remarks

In the context of global threats from plant pathogenic fungi—which disrupt yields, contaminate food, and undermine food security—AIFD platforms offer transformative solutions for sustainable plant disease management. Built on four interconnected pillars, a pathogen-specific data ecosystem, a modular technical architecture, a linear workflow, and a resistance prediction workflow, AIFD systematically addresses the limitations of conventional development approaches through integrated data utilization, computational efficiency, and proactive resistance management.

Key challenges persist, including the scarcity of high-quality, geographically diverse datasets for understudied pathogens and agroecosystems, as well as limited accessibility for resource-constrained smallholder farmers. Emerging technologies can address these gaps. IoT devices enrich training datasets with real-time field data, XAI enhances model transparency for stakeholder trust and regulatory compliance, and federated learning enables collaborative training without compromising data privacy.

By aligning AI methodologies with practical aspects of plant disease management—balancing efficacy, agroecosystem sustainability, non-target safety, and long-term resistance control—AIFD can develop next-generation fungicides for responsible agriculture. Through the maturation and integration of advances in computational biology, agronomy, and environmental science, AIFD will become an essential tool for mitigating fungal threats, supporting resilient crop production amid climate change and growing food demand, and enhancing global food security.
