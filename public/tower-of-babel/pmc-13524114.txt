Underweight Status is Associated with Prevalent Anemia in Postmenopausal Women with Osteoporotic Vertebral Compression Fractures: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Kexin Zhu, Yudie Hu, Yingying Ruan, Tingting Chen, Lili Feng, Fuqin Tang, Weifang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe association between body mass index (BMI) and anemia remains inconsistent across populations. This study aimed to examine the association between BMI categories and prevalent anemia in postmenopausal women with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs).Patients and MethodsIn this single-center, hospital-based cross-sectional study, 351 postmenopausal women with OVCFs admitted to a tertiary hospital in East China between July 2018 and June 2024 were included. BMI was categorized according to Chinese criteria. Anemia was defined as hemoglobin <120 g/L. Associations between BMI, hemoglobin levels, and prevalent anemia were assessed using correlation analyses, multiple linear regression, and sequential multivariable logistic regression models.ResultsOverall, 156 patients (44.4%) had anemia. The prevalence of anemia was highest in the underweight group (77.1%), followed by the normal-weight (42.9%), overweight (33.3%), and obese (31.3%) groups. Hemoglobin levels increased across BMI categories, and BMI was positively correlated with hemoglobin levels (rs = 0.317, P < 0.001). In multivariable analyses, underweight status was associated with lower hemoglobin levels and higher odds of prevalent anemia compared with normal weight. In the fully adjusted logistic model, underweight status remained significantly associated with prevalent anemia (aOR = 3.738, 95% CI: 1.648–8.483, P = 0.002), whereas overweight and obesity were not independently associated with anemia.ConclusionIn postmenopausal women hospitalized with OVCFs, underweight status was associated with lower hemoglobin levels and higher odds of prevalent anemia, while overweight and obesity were not independently associated with anemia. Underweight status may serve as a simple clinical flag prompting hemoglobin and nutritional assessment in this clinically vulnerable fracture population.Keywords: body mass index, anemia, underweight, osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, postmenopausal women

Introduction

Osteoporosis is characterized by systemic deterioration of bone mass and microarchitecture, leading to increased bone fragility and susceptibility to fracture.1 In postmenopausal women, estrogen deficiency accelerates bone resorption beyond formation, resulting in rapid bone loss, reduced bone mineral density (BMD), and an increased risk of fragility fractures.2 Among fragility fractures, OVCFs are particularly common and represent a major public health concern. It is estimated that approximately 16% of postmenopausal women will experience OVCFs, with an incidence of 10.7 per 1000 person-years.3 With population aging, the burden of vertebral fractures continues to rise globally, especially among women aged ≥ 65 years.4 Beyond localized pain, OVCFs are associated with progressive spinal deformity, impaired mobility, reduced quality of life, and increased frailty.5 Hospitalized postmenopausal women with OVCFs represent a clinically vulnerable subgroup in whom skeletal fragility, pain-related functional limitation, nutritional vulnerability, and age-related physiological decline may coexist. In this setting, anemia may further compromise peri-treatment status, physical performance, early mobilization, and rehabilitation potential.6–9 Therefore, examining anemia and nutritional vulnerability specifically in this fracture population may provide clinically relevant information that cannot be fully captured in general older adult or osteoporosis cohorts.

Anemia is a major global health problem, particularly affecting women and older adults.10 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), anemia is defined as a hemoglobin concentration < 120 g/L in women.11 Epidemiological studies have shown that anemia remains highly prevalent in China and worldwide, especially among older populations.12 Clinically, anemia has been associated with reduced physical performance, cognitive impairment, an increased likelihood of falls and hospitalization, and higher mortality.13,14 In older adults, anemia is also associated with functional decline and postoperative complications.8 Furthermore, anemia may contribute to tissue hypoxia by reducing oxygen delivery, thereby potentially affecting osteoblast differentiation, bone formation, and bone remodeling.15,16 Taken together, these considerations suggest that anemia may be particularly relevant to peri-treatment assessment and early rehabilitation planning in postmenopausal women hospitalized with OVCFs.

BMI is a simple and widely used anthropometric measure,17 but in older and clinically vulnerable populations, it reflects more than body size alone. Lower BMI, particularly underweight status, is commonly regarded as a pragmatic marker of reduced nutritional reserve and is closely related to protein-energy malnutrition, sarcopenia, frailty, and bone loss.18 Low BMI is also one of the phenotypic criteria included in contemporary malnutrition frameworks, although BMI alone cannot fully characterize nutritional status or body composition.19 This broader vulnerability has clinically meaningful skeletal consequences, as underweight status has been associated with an increased risk of fractures.20 These conditions may coexist with lower hemoglobin levels and a greater likelihood of anemia.21,22 In contrast, the interpretation of higher BMI in older adults is more complex. While a higher BMI may indicate greater nutritional reserve,23 it is also associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, altered iron metabolism, and metabolic disturbances, which may adversely affect hemoglobin levels.24,25 Taken together, BMI may be associated with anemia through overlapping pathways related to nutritional reserve, inflammation, and metabolic status. However, these pathways should be interpreted cautiously because BMI is a pragmatic but imperfect clinical indicator rather than a direct measure of nutritional status, iron metabolism, inflammatory mediators, or body composition. In addition, vertebral deformity, height loss, kyphosis, pain, and difficulty standing fully upright may affect height measurement and BMI interpretation in patients with OVCFs.26,27

Previous studies investigating the association between BMI and anemia have yielded inconsistent results. Some studies have reported that higher BMI is associated with higher hemoglobin levels and a lower likelihood of anemia,28,29 whereas others have suggested that overweight and obesity may be associated with a higher likelihood of anemia, possibly due to inflammation-related iron dysregulation.30–32 These discrepancies indicate that the relationship between BMI and anemia is likely population-specific and influenced by age, nutritional status, inflammatory burden, renal function, comorbidities, and underlying disease conditions.14,33 However, evidence regarding this association in hospitalized postmenopausal women with OVCFs remains limited. More importantly, the clinically relevant issue is not simply whether BMI is associated with anemia, but whether low BMI is associated with a higher likelihood of prevalent anemia in this clinically vulnerable population. Clarifying this issue may help clinicians recognize patients who warrant closer hemoglobin and nutritional assessment during hospitalization.

Therefore, the present single-center cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate hemoglobin levels, the prevalence of anemia, and their associations with BMI in postmenopausal women hospitalized with OVCFs. We specifically sought to determine whether underweight status represents a clinically meaningful marker of vulnerability, characterized by lower hemoglobin levels and higher odds of prevalent anemia in this population.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This single-center, hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Taizhou Central Hospital (Taizhou University Hospital), a tertiary medical center in East China. Consecutive postmenopausal women admitted with vertebral compression fractures between July 2018 and June 2024 were screened for eligibility. After excluding high-energy traumatic fractures and pathological fractures due to malignancy, infection, or other non-osteoporotic causes, potentially eligible patients with OVCFs underwent further eligibility assessment. The screening process, exclusion reasons, and final analytic cohort are shown in Figure 1. OVCFs were diagnosed based on clinical symptoms and radiographic evidence of vertebral compression fractures on X-ray, CT, or MRI, together with clinical assessment by orthopedic physicians. Index OVCFs were identified according to the admission diagnosis, clinical presentation, and available imaging findings. Pre-existing vertebral fractures unrelated to the index admission were excluded where identifiable from medical records or imaging reports.

Hematologic disorders affecting hemoglobin levels were defined as documented hematologic diseases that could directly alter hemoglobin levels, including aplastic anemia, hemolytic anemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, leukemia, lymphoma, or other clinically diagnosed blood disorders. Severe hepatic dysfunction was identified according to documented clinical diagnoses of severe liver disease or liver failure in the medical records. Severe renal dysfunction was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 or a documented diagnosis of severe renal insufficiency. Acute or severe inflammatory diseases were identified according to clinical diagnoses in the medical records rather than C-reactive protein (CRP) elevation alone. Patients with missing data on BMI, hemoglobin, or covariates required for the primary analyses were excluded. Specifically, three patients were excluded because of missing data on one or more of the following variables: hemoglobin, serum albumin, eGFR, and CRP.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Taizhou Central Hospital (Taizhou University Hospital) (Approval No. 2024K-01-11) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Because of the retrospective study design, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. All patient data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Definitions and Measurements

Anemia was defined according to the WHO criteria as hemoglobin < 120 g/L in non-pregnant women.11 BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2) and categorized according to the Chinese Working Group on Obesity as follows: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–23.9 kg/m2), overweight (24.0–27.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥ 28.0 kg/m2).34 Height and weight values recorded at admission were retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical records and used to calculate BMI. Because of the retrospective design, the specific height measurement procedures were not consistently documented. Postmenopausal status was defined as ≥ 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea not due to other causes.35 Osteoporosis was confirmed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), with all included participants having a documented T-score ≤−2.5 in accordance with current clinical guidelines.36 Although the diagnostic threshold was documented for all participants, complete individual numerical BMD and T-score values were not consistently available for extraction from the retrospective records and were therefore not included as analytical variables.

Data Collection

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records and the hospital laboratory information system. Data collected included age, height, weight, hemoglobin, serum albumin, prealbumin, total protein, serum calcium, serum phosphate, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), serum creatinine, eGFR, urea, CRP, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, treatment modality, and comorbidities. Comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, documented chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and cerebrovascular disease, and were identified from medical records and discharge diagnoses. According to the medical records, none of the included participants had a documented smoking history. Laboratory parameters were obtained from the first routine fasting blood sample after admission, before surgical or interventional treatment whenever applicable.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons across BMI categories were performed using one-way ANOVA, the Kruskal–Wallis test, the chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Comparisons between patients with and without anemia were performed using the independent-samples t test, Mann–Whitney U-test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni adjustment when applicable. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess associations between BMI and laboratory indicators.

Multiple linear regression was used to examine factors associated with hemoglobin levels, and multivariable logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with anemia. BMI was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable, with normal weight as the reference category. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, previous evidence, and data availability. The selected covariates were intended to account for demographic characteristics, renal function, inflammatory status, nutritional status, treatment modality, and comorbidity burden. Sequential logistic regression models were constructed with progressive adjustment for age, eGFR, CRP, serum albumin, treatment modality, and major comorbidities. Highly related variables were not entered simultaneously in the main models to reduce collinearity and model instability.

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). For linear regression models, model assumptions were evaluated using residual diagnostics and Durbin-Watson statistics. For logistic regression models, the linearity assumption in the logit for continuous predictors was assessed using the Box-Tidwell approach. Sensitivity analyses were performed by additionally including serum calcium, serum phosphate, and ALP in the fully adjusted models, with serum calcium and serum phosphate modeled per 0.1 mmol/L increase. Age-by-BMI interaction analyses and exploratory age-stratified analyses were also performed. A complete-case analysis was performed, and no statistical imputation was used. All 351 participants had complete data for the variables included in the primary regression models. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 351 postmenopausal women with OVCFs were included in the final analysis. All participants had complete data for the variables included in the primary regression models. The median age was 75.0 years, and the median BMI was 22.22 kg/m2. Among the participants, 48 (13.7%) were underweight, 196 (55.8%) were normal weight, 75 (21.4%) were overweight, and 32 (9.1%) were obese. Overall, 156 patients (44.4%) had anemia. Detailed baseline characteristics stratified by BMI categories are presented in Table 1. Age, hemoglobin level, and anemia prevalence differed significantly across BMI categories (all P < 0.001). Nutritional and protein-related indicators also differed across BMI categories, including serum albumin (P = 0.009), prealbumin (P < 0.001), and total protein (P = 0.037). Diabetes mellitus also differed significantly across BMI categories (P = 0.031). The underweight group was older and had the lowest hemoglobin level and the highest prevalence of anemia. In contrast, renal function indicators, inflammatory indicators, treatment modality, and most comorbidities did not differ significantly across BMI categories.

Hemoglobin Levels and Anemia Prevalence Across BMI Categories

Hemoglobin levels differed significantly among the BMI categories (P < 0.001), with the lowest median level observed in the underweight group. The median hemoglobin level was lowest in the underweight group (112.0 g/L), followed by the normal-weight group (121.0 g/L), the overweight group (124.0 g/L), and the obesity group (127.0 g/L) (Figure 2). Pairwise comparisons showed that hemoglobin levels were significantly lower in the underweight group than in the normal-weight, overweight, and obesity groups. Consistent with this pattern, anemia prevalence also differed significantly across BMI categories, as shown in Table 1. The prevalence of anemia was 77.1% in the underweight group, 42.9% in the normal-weight group, 33.3% in the overweight group, and 31.3% in the obesity group (P < 0.001), indicating that anemia was most frequent among underweight patients.

Correlation Between BMI and Laboratory Parameters

Spearman correlation analysis showed that BMI was positively correlated with hemoglobin level (rs = 0.317, P < 0.001). BMI was also positively correlated with serum albumin (rs = 0.182, P = 0.001), prealbumin (rs = 0.276, P < 0.001), total protein (rs = 0.140, P = 0.009), eGFR (rs = 0.128, P = 0.017), and WBC count (rs = 0.176, P = 0.001). No significant correlations were observed between BMI and serum calcium, serum phosphate, ALP, serum creatinine, urea, CRP, or neutrophil count (Table 2).

Factors Associated with Hemoglobin Levels

Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with hemoglobin levels (Table 3). When BMI was analyzed as a continuous variable, higher BMI was independently associated with higher hemoglobin levels after adjustment for age, eGFR, CRP, serum albumin, treatment modality, and comorbidities (β = 0.841, 95% CI: 0.457 to 1.224, P < 0.001). eGFR and serum albumin were also positively associated with hemoglobin levels. When BMI was analyzed as a categorical variable, underweight status was associated with lower hemoglobin levels compared with normal weight (β = −7.212, 95% CI: −11.379 to −3.045, P = 0.001). Obesity was associated with higher hemoglobin levels (β = 5.155, 95% CI: 0.229 to 10.081, P = 0.040), whereas overweight was not significantly associated with hemoglobin levels. Residual diagnostics did not indicate major violations of normality or homoscedasticity. The Durbin–Watson statistics were 1.974 and 1.971 for the continuous and categorical BMI linear regression models, respectively, indicating no obvious residual autocorrelation. All VIF values were below 2.0, suggesting no substantial multicollinearity.

Prevalence of Anemia and Univariate Logistic Regression Analysis

Compared with patients without anemia, patients with anemia were older and had lower BMI, serum albumin, prealbumin, total protein, serum calcium, and eGFR levels. They also had higher serum creatinine and urea levels. CRP and neutrophil count did not differ significantly between the anemia and non-anemia groups. The distribution of BMI categories differed significantly between patients with and without anemia (P < 0.001). Underweight patients accounted for 23.7% of the anemia group and 5.6% of the non-anemia group. In univariate logistic regression, underweight status was associated with higher odds of anemia compared with normal weight (OR = 4.485, 95% CI: 2.161–9.308, P < 0.001), whereas overweight and obesity were not significantly associated with anemia (Table 4).

Multivariable Logistic Regression Models for Anemia

Sequential multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to examine the association between BMI and anemia (Table 5). When BMI was analyzed as a continuous variable, higher BMI was consistently associated with lower odds of anemia across all models. In the fully adjusted model, BMI remained independently associated with lower odds of anemia after adjustment for age, eGFR, CRP, serum albumin, treatment modality, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and cerebrovascular disease (aOR = 0.869, 95% CI: 0.807–0.936, P < 0.001). When BMI was analyzed as a categorical variable, underweight status remained associated with higher odds of anemia in the fully adjusted model (aOR = 3.738, 95% CI: 1.648–8.483, P = 0.002), whereas overweight and obesity were not significantly associated with anemia. eGFR and serum albumin were also inversely associated with anemia in the fully adjusted models. Box-Tidwell tests showed no significant violation of the linearity-in-the-logit assumption for the continuous predictors included in the logistic regression models.

Sensitivity, Interaction, and Age-Stratified Analyses

Sensitivity analyses additionally including serum calcium, serum phosphate, and ALP in the fully adjusted models yielded results consistent with the main analyses (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Formal interaction analyses did not show statistically significant age-by-BMI interactions, whether BMI was modeled as a continuous variable (P for interaction = 0.162) or as underweight status (P for interaction = 0.320) (Supplementary Table S3). Therefore, age-stratified analyses were interpreted as exploratory. In exploratory age-stratified analyses, BMI and underweight status were significantly associated with anemia among women aged ≥75 years, but not among those aged <75 years (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of postmenopausal women with OVCFs, BMI was associated with hemoglobin levels and prevalent anemia. In particular, lower BMI, especially underweight status, was consistently associated with lower hemoglobin levels and higher odds of prevalent anemia. In addition, older age was associated with anemia in univariate and partially adjusted models, whereas higher serum albumin levels showed an inverse association with anemia in multivariable models. Although age-stratified analyses suggested that the BMI–anemia association was more evident among women aged ≥75 years, formal interaction analyses were not statistically significant; therefore, these subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Collectively, these findings indicate that, in this specific clinical population, BMI may provide a pragmatic indication of nutritional and physiological vulnerability in this population, although it is not a direct measure of either condition.19

Overall, our findings are broadly consistent with previous studies showing that lower BMI is associated with lower hemoglobin levels and a higher likelihood of anemia.28,29,33 However, the present study extends prior evidence by demonstrating that the clinically meaningful signal lies not in the potential protective effect of higher BMI, but rather in the adverse implications of underweight status. When BMI was analyzed as a continuous variable, higher BMI was associated with lower odds of prevalent anemia. In contrast, the categorical analysis revealed that neither overweight nor obesity was significantly associated with anemia compared with normal weight, whereas underweight status remained consistently associated with higher odds of prevalent anemia. These findings suggest that, in this population, underweight status could serve as a simple clinical flag for vulnerability rather than a sensitive screening marker. Unlike studies emphasizing obesity-related anemia, our findings suggest that low BMI may reflect poorer nutritional reserve, greater frailty, and increased susceptibility to anemia.33,37

Several possible explanations may underlie the observed association, although these mechanisms should be interpreted cautiously. First, nutritional vulnerability may play an important role. Although BMI is a simple anthropometric index, low BMI in older patients with osteoporotic fractures may coexist with protein-energy malnutrition, sarcopenia, frailty, and reduced physiological reserve.37–39 These conditions may impair erythropoiesis by limiting the availability of essential substrates required for red blood cell production and reducing bone marrow responsiveness.40,41 In the present study, serum albumin was positively associated with hemoglobin levels and inversely associated with prevalent anemia, further supporting the relevance of nutritional and systemic status. However, albumin is not a pure nutritional marker; it may also reflect inflammation, disease severity, hydration status, hepatic synthesis, and systemic physiological stress. Therefore, albumin should be interpreted cautiously rather than as direct evidence of nutritional causality.42,43 Beyond the BMI–anemia association, anemia itself may also be clinically relevant in patients with OVCFs. Reduced hemoglobin levels may contribute to tissue hypoxia, which may adversely affect physical performance and rehabilitation potential. In addition, anemia-related hypoxia has been suggested to affect osteoblast function and bone matrix formation.38,44,45

On the other hand, the relationship between overweight or obesity and anemia remains inconsistent. Although higher BMI is often associated with chronic low-grade inflammation and altered iron metabolism, which can contribute to anemia of inflammation through impaired iron utilization and hepcidin-mediated pathways,46,47 it may also reflect greater nutritional or physiological reserve. In older patients with OVCFs, who may experience frailty, reduced mobility, and inadequate dietary intake,3 undernutrition-related vulnerability may be more clinically apparent than obesity-related inflammatory pathways. Therefore, our findings support the interpretation that the inverse association between BMI and anemia may partly reflect differences in nutritional reserve, rather than indicating that obesity itself has a protective effect.29,48,49

Age may also be relevant to anemia in postmenopausal women with OVCFs. Although age was not consistently significant in fully adjusted models, patients with anemia were older than those without anemia, and age was associated with anemia in univariate and partially adjusted analyses. This association may reflect age-related physiological changes that predispose individuals to anemia, including reduced gastrointestinal absorption, increased chronic disease burden, persistent low-grade inflammation, renal function decline, and age-related changes in bone marrow hematopoietic function.50,51 In postmenopausal women with OVCFs, these effects could be further amplified by pain-related immobility, decreased dietary intake, and delayed functional recovery.52

From a clinical perspective, these findings have important implications. Underweight status, particularly when accompanied by low serum albumin, may provide a practical clinical flag for further hemoglobin and nutritional assessment. Given that BMI is readily available in routine clinical practice, its integration with laboratory indicators such as hemoglobin and albumin could facilitate early recognition of vulnerable patients. This approach may be particularly valuable in the peri-treatment management of patients with OVCFs, in whom early recognition of anemia and nutritional vulnerability may help inform individualized assessment, rehabilitation planning, and supportive care.53,54 Therefore, underweight status may be best interpreted as a clinical flag prompting further assessment rather than as a standalone screening tool for anemia.

This study has several limitations. First, because of the single-center cross-sectional design, temporal and causal relationships between BMI and anemia could not be clarified, and the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Second, although renal function, routine inflammatory markers, treatment modality, and major comorbidities were included in the analyses, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Data on iron metabolism, vitamin status, dietary intake, body composition, frailty, medication use, and detailed fracture characteristics were unavailable. Although the medical records confirmed that all participants met the DXA criterion of a T-score ≤−2.5, exact individual BMD values and T-scores were not consistently retrievable. Consequently, BMD could not be included in the baseline comparisons or multivariable analyses. Third, BMI classification may have been affected by vertebral height loss, kyphosis, pain, and difficulty standing fully upright.55 Moreover, the specific height measurement procedures were not consistently documented in the retrospective medical records. Therefore, potential measurement error and BMI misclassification could not be excluded. Finally, age-stratified analyses were exploratory, and the relatively small underweight and obese subgroups may have reduced the precision of subgroup estimates.

Future studies should validate these findings in larger, multicenter cohorts and examine whether similar BMI–anemia associations exist in patients with low BMD or other fragility fractures. More comprehensive assessments of nutritional status, iron metabolism, inflammatory biomarkers beyond routine clinical indicators, body composition, frailty, and fracture characteristics are also needed. Longitudinal studies should further clarify the temporal relationship between BMI and anemia and determine whether targeted nutritional and anemia-related management strategies can improve anemia outcomes and clinical recovery in this population.

Conclusions

In this single-center cross-sectional study of postmenopausal women hospitalized with OVCFs, underweight status was associated with lower hemoglobin levels and higher odds of prevalent anemia, whereas overweight and obesity were not independently associated with anemia. Lower serum albumin levels were also associated with anemia. Underweight status may serve as a simple clinical flag prompting hemoglobin and nutritional assessment in this clinically vulnerable fracture population, rather than as a standalone screening tool for anemia.
