Molecular mechanisms in diquat-induced organs injury：insights into cell signaling and potential therapeutic agents
Authors: Siying Han, Dan Dong, Zhenning Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diquat (DQ) is a potent, non-selective herbicide which can result in severe poisoning and a high mortality if ingested accidentally or intentionally. Diquat poisoning can cause extensive damage to multiple organs, including the intestines, liver, kidneys, brain, and other organs. As there are no specific antidotes available for DQ poisoning, the current therapeutic strategies are essentially restricted to blood purification therapy and supportive care. Numerous studies on the molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic agents have been conducted over the past few decades. However, there has been no comprehensive summary or analysis of these findings. This review extensively investigates the molecular mechanisms underlying DQ-induced organ injury, with a particular focus on the major signaling pathways. In addition, searches were conducted in PubMed and Web of Science using the following search terms: "diquat"[tiab] OR "diquat"[MeSH Terms]. A total of 166 eligible papers published over the past 35 years were selected. Consequently, more than seventy potential therapeutic agents with protective effects against DQ-induced toxicity are summarized and analyzed. Introduction

Diquat (DQ), 1,1'-ethylene-2,2'-bipyridylium, is a commonly used organic heterocyclic herbicide for crop protection and weed control in aquatic environments (Li et al. 2021). It is used in North America, Europe, Australia, and Japan, accounting for about 90% of its global usage (Karuppagounder et al. 2012). After application in agricultural fields to kill weeds, DQ levels in the environment usually remain between 0.1 and 1.0 mg/L (Bartell et al. 2000). The World Health Organization (WHO) has established an acceptable daily intake (ADI) of 0–0.006 mg/kg body weight (WHO 2016). The Food and Agriculture Organization concluded that the residual diquat in food and drinking water, arising from its routine application does not pose a threat to human health (FAO 2005). However, the misuse or improper application of DQ can exceed the recommended limits. Due to its long dissipation time (DT50) of 1000 days, DQ is persistent in water (European Food Safety Authority 2015). Consequently, DQ residues in aquatic environments are likely to have an adverse impact on organisms (e.g. Gasterosteus aculeatus L., Lymnaea stagnalis, and Xenopus metamorphosis) (Shi et al. 2024). Furthermore, long-term exposure to DQ has been linked to an increased risk of developing Parkinson’s disease (Zhou et al. 2025). Diquat may pose a significant threat to ecosystems and public health, due to its potential for bioaccumulation and long-term persistence in the environment.

The incidence of DQ poisoning has been rising globally in recent years due to the limitation of paraquat in some countries (Fan et al. 2023). According to the report of the National Poison Data System of the USA, 2,128 cases of DQ poisoning were recorded between 1998 and 2011 (Ling et al. 2025). In China, the in-hospital mortality rate for acute DQ poisoning is around 18.6%, whereas the proportion rises to almost 60% after follow-up (Ling et al. 2025). Overall mortality rates can range from 20 to 60%, especially in developing countries (Zhang et al. 2025). The majority of poisoning incidents arise from the ingestion of concentrated DQ formulations, typically in cases of attempted suicide (Magalhaes et al. 2018). In addition, occupation-related DQ exposures accounted for 8% – 29% of poisoning cases. The main causes of occupational poisoning are improper use of personal protective equipment, malfunctioning application equipment, and spillage or splash of DQ (Fortenberry et al. 2016).

The International Programme on Chemical Safety has estimated the lethal dose of DQ for humans to be approximately 6–12 g (HSG 1999). Diquat is mainly absorbed through the gastrointestinal tract (Ness-Cochinwala et al. 2022). Other rare routines of administration, including inhalation, mucosal contact (Basilicata et al. 2022), intramuscular or subcutaneous injection (Li and Zhou 2025), and intravaginal administration (Rudez et al. 1999), have also been reported in previous studies. Once ingested, DQ would be rapidly distributed to multiple organs via the bloodstream, contributing to multiple organ injury (Tang et al. 2025b). Following absorption, the kidney serves as the principal organ for DQ excretion and is particularly vulnerable to injury (Yu et al. 2025). Current therapeutic strategies aim to decrease absorption or enhance elimination and mainly include forced diuresis, blood purification, and supportive care (Li et al. 2024; Ness-Cochinwala et al. 2022). Regrettably, there are currently no specific antidotes available for DQ poisoning. Over the past few decades, extensive and in-depth research has been conducted on the molecular mechanism of DQ toxicity. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the potential therapeutic agents may protect against DQ-induced organ injury. However, a comprehensive evaluation of these findings has not yet been undertaken. In this review, the molecular mechanisms of DQ-induced organ injury and the potential therapeutic agents are comprehensively summarized.

Toxicokinetics of DQ

Diquat is primarily absorbed through the gastrointestinal tract, but the absorption rate is < 10% (He et al. 2025). Due to its hydrophilic nature, DQ can slowly traverse membranes primarily by diffusion and to a smaller degree by active transport via cation pumps (Abdollahi et al. 2004). Due to its large volume of distribution, DQ rapidly disseminates throughout the body in the aqueous phase(Rose et al. 1974). Unlike paraquat, DQ is not a substrate for the pulmonary polyamine uptake system and does not accumulate selectively in lung tissue (Dinis-Oliveira et al. 2006). Two hours after ingestion, peak blood and tissue levels are achieved, followed by a rapid decrease and excretion in urine (Magalhaes et al. 2018). Diquat is relatively resistant to metabolism. The minimal metabolic process of DQ is characterized by the oxidation of the pyridine rings, resulting in the formation of less toxic mono- and dipyridone derivatives. Cleavage of the pyridine rings produces TOPPS (Tetrahydro-2,2′-bipyridyl-1,1′-dioxide-5,5′-dicarboxylic acid), which is degraded to picolinamide and picolinic acid (Magalhaes et al. 2018). This process primarily occurs in the liver via cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes (FAO 2014). Approximately 94% of DQ is eliminated in feces within the initial 24 h. The absorbed DQ ions and its metabolites are primarily excreted via the kidneys within 48 h (Daniel and Gage 1966).

DQ-induced multiple-organ injury

Diquat can result in severe damage to multiple organs, including the intestines, liver, kidneys, lungs, brain, testes, and eyes (see Fig. 1). The clinical manifestations primarily include local mucosal injury, such as sore throat, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, which typically occur within minutes to hours after ingestion. Then, a series of complications develop subsequently, including paralytic intestinal obstruction, liver dysfunction, acute kidney injury, toxic encephalopathy, increased white blood cell counts, and rhabdomyolysis (Fan et al. 2023). The substantial alterations in both pathological and biochemical markers across various organs are shown as follows:

Intestine

Oral ingestion of DQ can cause corrosive injury to the oral mucosa, leading to hemorrhagic ulceration of the mouth and oedema of the mucosa (Magalhaes et al. 2018). Then, DQ can result in severe gastroenteritis manifesting as nausea and vomiting. The results of H&E staining reveal disorganized jejunal villi, mucosal edema, villus shortening, and reduced crypt depth at 24 h in DQ-poisoned mice (25 mg/kg, i.p.). Diquat can dramatically reduce the number of goblet cells and the expression of Mucin 2, which is crucial for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity (Qiao et al. 2020). Furthermore, on day 10 after administration of DQ (10 mg/kg, i.p.) in mice, it manifests as a marked reduction in the intestinal tight-junction proteins (e.g. zonula occludens-1, claudin-1, and occluding) and an elevation in plasma levels of D-lactate and diamine oxidase (Zhang et al. 2021). It shows that the activity of alkaline phosphatase is suppressed in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, as well as the activity of disaccharidases (e.g., lactase, sucrase, and maltase) on day 7 after DQ administration (10 mg/kg, i.p.) in piglets. Furthermore, DQ can reduce the mRNA levels of the SGLT1 (sodium-glucose cotransporter 1) and GLUT2 (glucose transporter 2) (Chen et al. 2022). Thus, DQ impairs the mucosal barrier, destroying intestinal permeability and causing absorptive dysfunction in the intestine.

Liver

Acute DQ intoxication can induce significant hepatic injury, as evidenced by markedly increased serum transaminase levels, although autopsy revealed only hepatic congestion (Yu et al. 2022). There is a significant increase in serum hepatic markers including AST (aspartate aminotransferase), ALT (alanine aminotransferase), and γ-GT (γ-glutamyl transferase) (Hua et al. 2022) 10 days after administration of DQ (10 mg/kg, i.p.) in piglets. Intraperitoneal administration of DQ (10 mg/kg) disrupts hepatic architecture, inducing irregular cords and markedly dilated sinusoids, as well as multiple inflammatory foci within the sinusoidal spaces in broilers at 24 h (Wang et al. 2025). Further pathological examination reveals focal destruction of hepatic necrosis, loosening of the connective tissue and ballooning degeneration, accompanied by prominent lipid vacuolation and significant infiltration of inflammatory cells on day 2 after DQ administration (20 mg/kg, i.p.) in broilers and White layers (Sun et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2023).

Kidney

Diquat is primarily excreted in the kidneys, potentially causing severe nephrotoxicity (Rogers et al. 2006). Patients with DQ poisoning typically develop acute kidney injury (AKI) characterized by anuria and oliguria, as evidenced by rising serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels within 6–24 h, peaking by day 3 after DQ ingestion (Tanen et al. 1999). Some patients may also develop varying degrees of proteinuria (Zeng et al. 2023), while ingesting large amounts of DQ can lead to acute renal failure (Qu et al. 2024). Renal biopsies have consistently identified proximal tubular injury as the predominant lesion, characterized by vacuolar degeneration and loss of tubular epithelial cells, tubular dilation, interstitial edema, and inflammatory infiltration (Zeng et al. 2023). Consistent with the clinical findings, the marked structural abnormalities in the kidneys are demonstrated on day 7 after administration of DQ (140 mg/kg, i.p.) in rats, including loss of tubular lumina, epithelial cell necrosis, and congestion (Huang et al. 2023). Similarly, renal injury markers such as serum creatinine, BUN, and the highly sensitive biomarker kidney injury molecule-1 were significantly elevated, while Aquaporin 1 (AQP1) expression is reduced at 24 h after administration of DQ (55 mg/kg, i.p.) in mice (Wu et al. 2024).

Lung

Although DQ is slowly absorbed into lung tissue and rapidly eliminated, it still induces lung injury manifested as pulmonary edema and respiratory failure due to its toxicity to the alveolar epithelium (Charles et al. 1978). Unlike paraquat, acute lung injury caused by DQ is not frequently reported. The deterioration of the lungs is probably associated with the dose of DQ (Wu et al. 2018). According to a previous case report, one patient who orally ingested 160 mL of enriched DQ died of severe respiratory failure rather than renal failure (Huang et al. 2021). The highest concentration of DQ was found in rat lung tissue on the first day after administration. Impairment of alveolar surfactant and increased capillary permeability have been observed in biopsy specimens, along with degeneration of alveolar epithelial cells. Alveolar wall dilation, congestion, mild emphysema, and significant infiltration, alongside ultrastructural damage in type II pneumocytes were demonstrated in DQ-poisoned mice (140 mg/kg, i.p.) (Wu et al. 2012, 2022a). Notably, the content of hydroxyproline (an indicator of pulmonary fibrosis) is not elevated in the early stage of DQ poisoning (Wu et al. 2022a). Slight pulmonary fibrosis is observed in rats after two weeks of DQ exposure. The absence of pulmonary fibrosis in DQ poisoning may be attributed to excessive oxidative stress and the activation of cell death programs in the acute phase (Chen et al. 2025). Furthermore, increased interstitial collagen deposition, fibroblast proliferation, and alveolar collapse are demonstrated in the lungs after 3 weeks in mice orally administered with 37.5 mg/kg of DQ. Meanwhile, the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), marked by decreased E-cadherin and increased α-smooth muscle actin and vimentin, is also confirmed (Xi et al. 2025). Overall, excessive DQ intake can cause acute lung injury in the acute phase, whereas prolonged exposure can lead to the development of pulmonary fibrosis.

Brain

Diquat can penetrate the blood–brain barrier and accumulates primarily in the midbrain and pons. Patients with DQ poisoning develop central nervous system injury characterized by agitation, delirium, seizures, and coma (Tang et al. 2025a). Autopsy results reveal that brainstem infarction and pontine hemorrhage are the causes of death (Ruha et al. 2001). Microscopic examination results reveal necrosis of the capillary walls with perivascular hemorrhages in the pons and multiple small areas of infarction in brain tissue. Exposure to DQ produces deficits in coordination and in spatial working memory on day 7 in mice (125 mg/kg, i.g.) (Zhou et al. 2025). Exposure to DQ results in significant neuronal loss and tissue oedema in the hippocampus, as manifested by morphological abnormalities and cell necrosis on day 3 after intraperitoneal injection with 200 mg/kg of DQ in mice (Wang et al. 2024). Moreover, a higher dose of DQ (346.5 mg/kg, i.g.) leads to the lysis and degeneration of nerve fibers, vacuolar-like changes, and moderate perivascular oedema, accompanied by increased levels of neuron-specific enolase in serum (an indicator of neuronal injury) on day 7 (Zhou et al. 2025). Long-term exposure to DQ mainly manifests as abnormalities in dopamine metabolism and motor dysfunction. It significantly reduces the levels of dopamine metabolite DOPAC (3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid) in the striatum of mice, thereby reducing dopamine turnover (Karuppagounder et al. 2012). Diquat can lead to neuronal fragmentation and deformation, triggering neurodegeneration in dopaminergic neurons (Bonneh-Barkay et al. 2005).

Testicle

Long-term exposure to DQ exerts toxic effects on the reproductive system. Diquat disrupts the integrity of the blood–testis barrier, which provides a protective microenvironment for germ cell survival and spermatogenesis (Yang et al. 2023). Histopathological results reveal testicular atrophy and impaired spermatogenesis, characterized by enlarged intertubular spaces and a significant loss of spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and spermatids on day 28 after administration of DQ (10 mg/kg/day, i.p.) in mice. Meanwhile, DQ markedly impairs spermatogenesis, characterized by a decrease in sperm density in the cauda epididymides and the testosterone levels in serum (Cheng et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2023). In yellow roosters exposed to DQ (15 mg/kg, i.p.), the expression of steroidogenic enzymes is markedly reduced on day 4, including StAR (steroidogenic acute regulatory protein), P450scc (cholesterol side-chain cleavage), and 3β-HSD (3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase) (Xiong et al. 2025). However, there have been no clinical case reports of DQ-induced testicular injury in humans.

Eye

Diquat can cause severe ocular damage. According to a previous clinical case report, a farmer accidentally splashed DQ into his right eye, resulting in extensive epithelial loss of the bulbar conjunctiva and cornea, accompanied by mild anterior uveitis (Swan 1968). Long-term exposure to DQ can lead to irreversible damage to the eyes, particularly the lens, ultimately resulting in cataract formation. Rat diet containing 0.05% DQ can induce bilateral lens opacities in rats within 12 months (Clark and Hurst 1970). Similarly, intravitreal injection of DQ (300 nmol in 30 μL saline) into the eyes of rabbits can result in the development of dense lens opacities over 3–4 weeks (Bhuyan et al. 1991). Furthermore, DQ significantly decreases the concentration of ascorbic acid in the aqueous humor, vitreous humor and lens, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress and accelerating cataract progression (Bhuyan et al. 1991).

Molecular mechanisms

Redox biochemistry

Compared with paraquat (E°′ = –446 mV), DQ with higher redox potential (E°′ = –349 mV) exhibits greater oxidative capacity. In redox cycling, DQ is 10–40 times more effective than paraquat at producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), and approximately 10 times more potent at producing H₂O₂ (hydrogen peroxide) (Djukic et al. 2012). Diquat can result in cellular impairment, whereby the generation of ROS is followed by the disruption of redox homeostasis (Fussell et al. 2011; Sun et al. 1990). The one-electron redox reaction triggered by DQ generates ROS, including superoxide anions (O2•⁻), H2O2, and hydroxyl radicals (•OH) (Mao et al. 2024). However, excessive ROS generation can contribute to the injury to proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (Fussell et al. 2011). Similar to paraquat, the role of DQ in the redox cycle is strongly dependent on NADPH (Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate). It facilitates the transfer of electrons from NADPH or NADH (Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) to oxygen (O₂) via cytochrome P450 reductase (CYP450), generating the radical cation (DQ+) (Circu et al. 2017). This radical then reacts rapidly with O₂ to form O₂•⁻and in turn regenerates the parent compound. The O₂•⁻ can be disproportionately converted by superoxide dismutase (SOD) to H₂O₂, which is then further transformed by catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GPX) to H₂O and O₂, thereby contributing to free radical scavenging (Fussell et al. 2011). Glutathione (GSH) can effectively scavenge ROS and protect cells from oxidative stress. Diquat stimulation can significantly decrease GSH levels in hepatocytes and generate glutathione disulfide (GSSG) (Lii et al. 1996). Additionally, the DQ-induced cytotoxicity is closely associated with the levels of stored iron in the body (Abe et al. 2006). The reaction between DQ•+ and O₂•⁻ reduces ferric iron (Fe3⁺) to ferrous iron (Fe2⁺), which then catalyzes the Fenton reaction to produce highly toxic •OH (Bouétard et al. 2013). Furthermore, aldehyde oxidase (AOX) can catalyze the reduction of DQ metabolites in the absence of NADPH. Aldehyde oxidase exerts a detoxifying effect by converting DQ compounds into the mono-pyridyl ketone form (Gupta et al. 2000). Diquat-induced hypoxia contributes to an increase of nitric oxide (NO) which is produced by NO synthase. Subsequently, NO and ROS interact to generate peroxynitrite (ONOO −), which is defined as one of the reactive nitrogen species (RNS). Signaling pathways

Excessive ROS generation is the initial factor of cellular damage caused by DQ. Subsequently, oxidative stress, autophagy and apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, inflammation, and ferroptosis are involved in DQ-induced toxicity (see Fig. 1). The molecular mechanisms of DQ-induced cellular injury and organ dysfunction are sophisticated. As summarized in Table 1 and Figs. 3 , 4, multiple signaling pathways are involved in DQ-induced cytotoxicity, including AHR/Nrf2, SIRT1/PGC1α, PERK/eIF2α, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, MAPK, NF-κB, NLRP3, and SLC7A11/GPX4. The SIRT1/PGC1α/Nrf1 axis is suppressed by DQ, thereby inhibiting the transcription of TFAM (a mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) binding protein). Through the AMPK/TFEB signaling pathway, DQ activates autophagic processes by altering the expression of the markers including SQSTM1/p62, LC3, and Beclin1. In addition, the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway is inhibited by AHR, leading to the downregulation of antioxidant genes, including NQO1, HO-1, CAT, and SOD2. DQ engages the PERK/eIF2α/CHOP axis of the unfolded protein response, contributing to ERS–associated apoptosis. Inflammatory responses are driven by the release of HMGB1, which binds to TLR4 and activates the MyD88/NF-κB pathway, thereby promoting transcription of NLRP3, pro-IL-18, and pro-IL-1β. The assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome contributes caspase-1 activation, further processing the precursors pro-IL-18 and pro-IL-1β into mature IL-18 and IL-1β, concurrently leading to GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis. Transferrin binds to Fe3⁺ ions and forms a complex with TfR1, which is then internalized by cells through endocytosis, followed by FTH1-mediated ferritin storage. Released iron ions (Fe2⁺) are then involved in the Fenton reaction and generation of reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH), leading to ferroptosis. Oxidative stress

AHR/Nrf2

Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR)/nuclear factor-erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) can regulate several genes encoding the detoxifying phase I and II enzymes. These enzymes exert protective effects against oxidative stress and apoptosis (Chen et al. 2023). Under oxidative stress, AHR is dislocated from the chaperone-associated complex and translocates to the nucleus to bind with AHR nuclear translocator. Subsequently, AHR then binds to the xenobiotic-response element located in the promoter region of target genes (e.g., CYPs), thereby activating transcription (Lawal 2017; Shaw and Chattopadhyay 2019). Meanwhile, Nrf2 can detach from Keap1 (Kelch like ECH-associated protein 1) and translocate to the nucleus (Zhang et al. 2022). Nrf2 then binds to antioxidant-responsive elements to upregulate the expression of antioxidant enzyme genes (SOD1, NQO1, CAT, and HO-1) (Bellezza et al. 2018). In fact, the changes in Nrf2 vary depending on the dose of DQ and the duration of exposure. In the initial phase of acute DQ poisoning, Nrf2 is upregulated to enhance the expression of the genes encoding antioxidative enzymes, aiming to counteract oxidative stress. The mRNA expression of Nrf2, NQO1, and HO-1 is significantly increased in the jejunum at 12 h after mice are administered with DQ (24 mg/kg, i.p.) (Wang et al. 2019). Similarly, DQ promotes Nrf2 expression, independent of Keap1, accompanied by an increase of GPX1 in the liver at 24 h after broiler chickens are intraperitoneally injected with DQ (20 mg/kg) (Chen et al. 2021b). It is regarded as an early adaptive response to oxidative stress. However, prolonged exposure to low-dose DQ can suppress the activation of the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway. One previous study showed that Nrf2 expression was decreased, followed by a decrease in the antioxidative enzymes and AHR/CYP1A1 (Cytochrome P450 family 1 subfamily a member 1) in the jejunum mucosa 7 days after DQ administration (10 mg/kg, i.p.) in piglets (Xun et al. 2021). The activity of Nrf2 is also inhibited in the mice jejunum 10 days after intraperitoneal injection of DQ (10 mg/kg) (Zhang et al. 2021). Moreover, DQ reduces the expression of Nrf2 and antioxidative enzymes in the jejunum and kidneys 7 days after DQ administration (10 mg/kg, i.p.) in piglets (Guo et al. 2020; Xun et al. 2021). Diquat-induced apoptosis in porcine enterocytes is primarily mediated by the Nrf2 signaling pathway, with significant increases in cleaved PARP (Poly (ADP-ribose) Polymerase) and caspase-3 (Jia et al. 2021) (see Fig. 3).

Mitochondrial dysfunction

SIRT1/PGC1α

The sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) protein, an NAD+-dependent deacetylase, can regulate oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and inflammation (Cui et al. 2022). SIRT1 can promote the deacetylation and transcriptional activation of PGC1α (Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1-α). Furthermore, PGC1α interacts with Nrf2 to activate TFAM (mitochondrial transcription factor A), thereby maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis (Chen et al. 2020, 2021a). However, DQ can inhibit the activity of SIRT1 and TFAM in the small intestine, leading to PGC1α acetylation (Cao et al. 2018). In addition to affecting SIRT1 and PGC1α, DQ can reduce the expression of Nrf1 (Nuclear respiratory factor 1), leading to the decreased levels of mitochondrial DNA, ATP (Adenosine triphosphate), and NAD+. This can further disrupt the activity of hepatocellular mitochondrial complexes and citrate synthase (Chen et al. 2020; Jia et al. 2020) (see Fig. 3).

Endoplasmic reticulum stress

PERK/eIF2α

Under normal physiological conditions, GRP78 (Glucose-regulated protein 78) can inhibit the unfolded protein response by binding to the ER (endoplasmic reticulum) transducers such as ATF6 (Activating transcription factor 6), IRE1α (Inositol-requiring enzyme 1α), and PERK (Protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase). In response to ER stress, GRP78 dissociates from PERK and binds to unfolded or misfolded proteins, which then undergo dimerization and cross-phosphorylation. The activated PERK can restrict the translation of eIF2α (Eukaryotic initiation factor 2 α) by phosphorylating eIF2α at Ser-51 (Qi and Chen 2019; Wang et al. 2022c). Diquat disrupts ER homeostasis in the small intestine, thereby triggering the unfolded protein response. The ER-resident molecular chaperone GRP78/Bip binds to misfolded proteins, resulting in the dissociation of PERK, ATF6, and IRE1α, and subsequently activating ER response (Gou et al. 2024a, 2024b). Meanwhile, DQ significantly increases the phosphorylated levels of PERK and eIF2α, upregulates the content of GRP78 and C/EBP homologous protein, ultimately leading to apoptosis (Gou et al. 2024a) (see Fig. 3).

Autophagy and apoptosis

PI3K/Akt/FOXO3a

The Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Protein kinase B (Akt) pathway plays a crucial role in the regulation of autophagy and apoptosis (Lapierre et al. 2015). Akt induces rapid translocation of FOXO3a (Forkhead box O3a) from the nucleus to the cytoplasm by its phosphorylation, accompanied by the reduced transcriptional activity of FOXO3a (Zhang et al. 2011). In IPEC-J2 cells, DQ exerts its toxic effects by promoting the phosphorylation of PI3K, Akt, and FOXO3a. Concurrently, DQ inhibits SIRT1 activity, disrupting the negative feedback of the Akt-FOXO3a axis, leading to the inhibition of autophagy. Diquat induces apoptosis by decreasing the ratio of Bcl-2/Bax and increasing caspase-8 expression (Tang et al. 2021; Wen et al. 2024a) (see Fig. 3).

Inflammatory Cascades

MAPKs

Mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), including extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1/2 (ERK1/2), p38 MAP kinase (p38 MAPK), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and Nemo-like kinase, regulate various cellular processes, including inflammation, apoptosis, and autophagy (Sun et al. 2015). In DQ-induced intestinal injury, phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and JNK2 increases in a time-dependent manner, accompanied by the promotion of autophagy through the disruption of the Bcl-2/Beclin-1 complex (Wu et al. 2019). Furthermore, activation of the ERK1/2 pathway promotes NF-κB (Nuclear factor-κB) transcriptional activity and enhances the production of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α) in DQ-stimulated IPEC-J2 cells (Xu et al. 2022a, 2023). Diquat triggers MAPK (ERK, JNK, and p38 MAPK) signaling pathways, which inhibit apoptosis and autophagic cell death by upregulating mTOR signaling in PC12 cells (Park and Koh 2019) (see Fig. 4).

NF-κB

Nuclear factor-κB comprises a family of dimeric transcription factors that regulate expression of multiple inflammatory genes (Lu et al. 2022). The activity of NF-κB is typically controlled by IκB (inhibitor of kappa B), which is kept in an inactive state in the cytoplasm (Ren et al. 2024a). Upon stimulation, the IκB kinase (IKK) complex phosphorylates IκB, permitting NF-κB dimers to translocate to the nucleus and initiate the transcriptional regulation (Lipniacki et al. 2007; Mitchell et al. 2016). DQ triggers massive ROS generation through redox reactions. ROS further promotes the nuclear translocation of NF-κB through the phosphorylation and degradation of IκB-α, accompanied by increased levels of the inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-8) in DQ-induced kidney and small intestinal injury (Huang et al. 2023). Similarly, DQ induces inflammation and apoptosis in PC12 (rat pheochromocytoma) cells by triggering the ROS-dependent NF-κB pathway (Choi et al. 2018). In addition, the NF-κB signaling pathway is initiated concurrently with the DQ-induced TLR4 (Toll-like receptor 4) activation (Cui et al. 2023). Furthermore, DQ induces cytotoxicity in PC12 cells via NF‐κB‐mediated p53 signaling, resulting in increased cyclooxygenase-2 expression (Choi et al. 2018) (see Fig. 4).

NLRP3

The NLRP3 (NACHT, LRR and PYD domains-containing protein 3) inflammasome, which is composed of NLRP3, ASC (Apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD), and procaspase-1, responds to stimuli like lipopolysaccharides and ROS. Following the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, caspase-1 cleaves the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18, and activates gasdermin D (GSDMD), thereby inducing pyroptosis (Wang et al. 2022a). Extracellular ATP can cause P2X4 (P2X purinoceptor 4) receptors to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome (Wang et al. 2022a; Yuan et al. 2022). Nuclear factor-κB triggers the transcription of NLRP3 and activates caspase-1 in HK-2 (human kidney-2) cells exposed to DQ, leading to the maturation of IL-1β and IL-18 (Qu et al. 2024). DQ significantly upregulates the P2X4/NLRP3 signaling pathway in microglial cells co-cultured with astrocytes. Inhibition of ATP release or silencing of P2X4 receptors significantly ameliorates the inflammatory response induced by DQ (Qi et al. 2024) (see Fig. 4).

Ferroptosis

SLC7A11/GPX4

Solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11)/Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) pathway exerts defensive effects against ferroptosis by promoting intracellular GSH formation and reducing lipid peroxidation (Zheng and Conrad 2020). In DQ-induced liver injury, DQ triggers lipid peroxidation and produces ROS via the Fenton reaction. Diquat promotes ferroptosis by downregulating GPX4 and upregulating the TFR1 (Transferrin receptor 1) (He et al. 2020; Hua et al. 2022). Diquat also facilitates the uptake of a significant quantity of trivalent iron ions into intestinal epithelial cells, while reducing the levels of SLC7A11. However, it is hypothesized that the increased levels of HSPB1 (Heat shock protein family B (Small) member 1), GSH, and GPX4 stimulated by DQ activates endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms (Xu et al. 2022b, 2022c). Diquat-induced lipid peroxidation promotes the upregulation of ACSL4 (Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4), resulting in an increase in PUFA-PE (Polyunsaturated fatty acids-phosphatidyl ethanolamine) content and subsequently triggering ferroptosis (Chen et al. 2023) (see Fig. 4).

Crosstalk between Nrf2 and NF-κB

Diquat generates large amounts of ROS through redox reactions, resulting in cellular homeostasis disruption, mitochondrial impairment, and cell death. The Nrf2 and NF-κB pathways are two critical signaling cascades that regulate cellular responses to oxidative stress and inflammation in DQ-induced cytotoxicity. While moderate levels of ROS can activate Nrf2 to restore redox balance, high levels of ROS can further activate IKK, which can then phosphorylate IκB, contributing to the nuclear translocation of NF-κB (Helou et al. 2019). Keap1, a cytosolic inhibitor of Nrf2, promotes the proteasomal degradation of IKK, ultimately suppressing NF-κB activation (Tian et al. 2012). ROS-induced Nrf2 activation increases HO-1 activity, which may inhibit the translocation of NF-κB. Nrf2 blocks IκB-α degradation and suppresses NF-κB-mediated transcription (Wardyn et al. 2015). Conversely, NF-κB p65 overexpression can promote the nuclear translocation of Keap1, thereby destroying the Nrf2-ARE interaction (Yu et al. 2011). In summary, Nrf2 counteracts NF-κB activation by promoting HO-1 activity and mediating IKK proteasomal degradation; conversely, NF-κB inhibits Nrf2 through p65-induced Keap1 nuclear translocation and p65-mediated CBP deficiency (Helou et al. 2019). Activation of Nrf2 inhibits the activation of NF-κB, accompanied by a reduction in the proinflammatory cytokines release (Gao et al. 2021). Conversely, Nrf2 deficiency increases NF-κB levels (Cuadrado et al. 2014). NF-κB negatively regulates Nrf2 by diminishing ARE transcription, forming a regulatory feedback loop (Cuadrado et al. 2018). The inhibition of NF-κB disrupts this feedback loop, leading to altered Nrf2 activity. The imbalance between Nrf2 and NF-κB is implicated in several diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune disorders, and tumors (Ben-Neriah and Karin 2011). Similarly, Nrf2/NF-κB balance in DQ-induced organ injury may critically influence therapeutic outcomes.

Potential therapeutic agents

The papers published between January 1990 and September 2025 were sought in PubMed and Web of Science using the following search terms: "diquat"[tiab] OR "diquat"[MeSH Terms]. A total of 814 records were initially identified. According to the exclusion criteria, the full-text articles with eligibility (N = 166) on potential therapeutic agents were included and summarized. The detailed flowchart is provided in the Supplementary Fig. 1. As shown in the Supplementary Table 1 and Fig. 6, more than seventy agents are summarized and classified as amino acids and amino acid derivatives, plant extracts, probiotics, nutritional supplements, and pharmaceuticals. Amino acids and amino acid derivatives

Among these potential therapeutic candidates, several amino acids with high bioavailability—arginine (68% ± 9%) (Bode-Böger et al. 1998), tryptophan (83% – 95%) (Sarwar and Botting 1999), L-theanine (47% – 74%) (Wang et al. 2022b), and serine (45% – 75%) (Bosley et al. 2021) —are attractive. They can restore redox balance and suppress inflammation in DQ-induced multi-organ injury.

Arginine alleviates DQ-induced oxidative stress by upregulating the AHR/Nrf2 signaling pathway and mTOR activity in the rat spleen, accompanied by a decrease of TNF-α (Tumor necrosis factor-α) and IL-1β, and an increase of the anti-inflammatory cytokine TGF-β1 (Transforming growth factor-β 1) (Mo et al. 2018). Tryptophan protects against mitochondrial biogenesis impairment via the Nrf1/PGC1α pathway in DQ-stimulated piglets jejunum (Liu et al. 2019). L-theanine suppresses the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in DQ-administered porcine jejunum through phosphorylation of ERK, JNK, and p38MAPK (Liu et al. 2021b). L-arginine with good safety and tolerability has been used to treat vascular functional disorders in humans (McNeal et al. 2016; Szlas et al. 2022). Similarly, clinical trials of L-tryptophan have demonstrated its efficacy in treating depression, insomnia, and premenstrual syndrome, although its therapeutic effects remain inferior to those of standard first-line treatments (Richard et al. 2009). L-theanine is well tolerated (LD₅₀ > 5000 mg/kg) and exerts anxiolytic, relaxing, and psychiatric symptom–relieving effects, supporting its neuroprotective potential in humans (Wang et al. 2022b). In addition, high-dose L-serine has demonstrated good safety and potential efficacy in treating amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy (Levine et al. 2017).

Glutathione is a tripeptide composed of cysteine, glutamate, and glycine. Glutathione counteracts oxidative stress, inhibits inflammation, and maintains cellular homeostasis. As an essential non-enzymatic antioxidant, GSH exerts therapeutic effects on several diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, cancer, autism, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (Santacroce et al. 2023). GSH supplementation enhances the antioxidant capacity of serum and jejunum in DQ-challenged weaned piglets. It increases the levels of intestinal tight junction protein and promotes mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC1α and TFAM) (Liang et al. 2023).

N-acetylcysteine is a powerful antioxidant derived from the amino acid L-cysteine. It is an approved drug for treating acetaminophen poisoning, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and other lung diseases. N-acetylcysteine exerts protective effects against DQ-induced cytotoxicity by markedly inhibiting ROS accumulation and blocking apoptosis and autophagy, as evidenced by reduced levels of cleaved caspase-9 and caspase-3, along with decreased LC3-II expression (Choi et al. 2018; Park and Koh 2019).

Plant extracts

Plant extracts possess notable antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties that protect against DQ-induced toxicity. Based on their relatively high bioavailability, hydroxytyrosol (75% – 99%) (Robles-Almazan et al. 2018) and pterostilbene (80%) (Kapetanovic et al. 2011) are considered promising candidates. Hydroxytyrosol, extracted from Olea europaea L., combats DQ-induced oxidative stress by enhancing Nrf2 activity and mitigating intestinal damage via the PI3K/Akt pathway (Wen et al. 2024a). Pterostilbene, mainly extracted from Pterocarpus santalinus, binds to SIRT1 at the Cys482 and Arg466 amino acid sites. This promotes ATP production and then alleviates mitochondrial dysfunction in the livers of piglets exposed to DQ. Moreover, pterostilbene prevents ferroptosis by suppressing ACSL4 and TFRC, increasing the levels of GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 (Ferritin heavy chain 1), and inhibiting the accumulation of excess iron and lipid peroxidation products (malondialdehyde and 4-hydroxynonenal) in the jejunum of DQ-poisoned broilers (Chen et al. 2023).

Probiotics

Some probiotics have emerged as high-priority candidates for mitigating DQ-induced organ injury. Bacillus amyloliquefaciens SC06 (Basc06) inhibits DQ-induced oxidative stress and autophagy in rat intestines by reducing JNK phosphorylation and promoting p38 phosphorylation (Wu et al. 2019). Furthermore, Basc06 alleviates oxidative stress and apoptosis via the PI3K/Akt pathway in DQ-stimulated IPEC-J2 cells (Tang et al. 2021). Pediococcus pentosaceus ZJUAF-4 alleviates oxidative stress by promoting Nrf2 activity and restoring the intestinal microbial balance in DQ-poisoned mice (Hao et al. 2021). Lactiplantibacillus plantarum P8 significantly reduces the phosphorylation of IκB-α and the levels of NF-κB p50, counteracting DQ-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis in the small intestine. Moreover, it mitigates DQ-triggered ferroptosis by suppressing ACSL4 expression while enhancing the expression of FSP1, GPX4, and SLC7A11 (Liu et al. 2024). Lactobacillus reuteri improves hepatic mitochondrial function and alleviates oxidative stress by activating the Nrf2/Keap1/HO-1 pathway, while simultaneously inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway to suppress inflammation in the DQ-challenged liver injury in hens (Zhan et al. 2025). Lactobacillus fermentum enhances antioxidant defence capacity and mitigates DQ-induced hepatic and muscular injury (Wang et al. 2013). Recent studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus plantarum P8 can mitigate stress and anxiety, as well as enhance memory and cognitive function in stressed adults (Lew et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2013). Lactobacillus fermentum is used as a nutritional supplement for healthy exercising males (West et al. 2011). Lactobacillus reuteri has been utilized clinically to treat gastrointestinal disorders, such as irritable complaints, diarrhea, and constipation (Peng et al. 2023). These probiotics have great potential for clinical translation.

Nutritional supplements

Nutritional supplements can protect against DQ-induced organ injury. Cellular ascorbic acid is rapidly consumed in DQ-stimulated rat hepatocytes. Supplementation with ascorbic acid in the incubation medium can attenuate DQ-induced lipid peroxidation and cytotoxicity (Nakagawa et al. 1991). Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) substantially reduces the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in plasma. Moreover, Vitamin C significantly enhances the activities of SOD and GPX, as well as the levels of GSH in plasma. It also effectively decreases the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) in plasma and the kidney. Vitamin C, with a bioavailability of 70% – 90%, protects against DQ-induced oxidative stress and inflammation (Lu et al. 2010). Although vitamin C is not authorized by the FDA for the treatment of DQ poisoning, it has been employed in the management of DQ poisoning in clinical practice. However, its efficacy has not yet been validated in well-designed clinical trials.

The absorption efficiency of vitamin D3 ranges from 55 to 99% (Simoliunas et al. 2019). Vitamin D3 ameliorates DQ-induced liver damage by modulating the Nrf2/Keap1 signaling pathway (Zhang et al. 2021). In addition, vitamin D3 significantly attenuates the inflammatory response by suppressing NF-κB p65 phosphorylation and diminishing the cytokines release in DQ-induced jejunal injury (Lu et al. 2010; Zhang et al. 2021). Squalene, a natural compound derived from shark liver, is widely used as a nutritional supplement, with bioavailability ranging from 60 to 85% (Ibrahim et al. 2020). Squalene mitigates DQ-induced hepatic oxidative stress and attenuates hepatic injury by further upregulating Nrf2 and its downstream target genes, such as NQO1 and GPX1 (Chen et al. 2021b). In addition, selenium-enriched yeast, with high absorption (89% ± 4%) and retention (74% ± 6%), attenuates DQ-induced oxidative stress and inflammation. This is achieved by activating Nrf2 and TLR4, while simultaneously inhibiting NF-κB activity, in the liver and thymus of piglets (Liu et al. 2021a). Selenium nanoparticles, with a bioavailability of 70% – 90%, upregulate Nrf2 in a dose-dependent manner and inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation followed by a decrease of IL-1β and IL-18 in the jejunal tissues of DQ-poisoned mice (Qiao et al. 2022). Selenoprotein P has been demonstrated to protect against oxidative damage and reduce the risk of morbidity from certain cancers in humans (Persson-Moschos et al. 2000). Selenoprotein P and Vitamin D3 therefore have great potential and are worthy of further research.

Pharmaceuticals

To date, no drugs have been authorized by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of patients with DQ poisoning. Soluble thrombomodulin (sTM), released from endothelial cells, is crucial for regulating coagulation and inflammation. Soluble thrombomodulin reverses the increased levels of HMGB1 and NF-κB induced by DQ in rats. Soluble thrombomodulin mitigates DQ-induced AKI via the HMGB1/IκBα/NF-κB signaling pathway (Huang et al. 2023). Recombinant human soluble thrombomodulin (rhsTM) has been approved and marketed in Japan as an anticoagulant therapy specifically for treating disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) associated with sepsis (Watanabe et al. 2017).

Dexamethasone is a glucocorticoid receptor agonist with a high bioavailability of 70% – 100%, exerting potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects (Spoorenberg et al. 2014). It effectively reduces the expression of NF-κB, TLR4 and MyD88 (Myeloid differentiation primary response 88) in the DQ-exposed rat kidney, accompanied by a decrease in the degree of renal tubular pathological damage and TNF-α levels. Dexamethasone exerts therapeutic effects on DQ-induced AKI in rats via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway (Cui et al. 2023).

Melatonin, a ROS scavenger and antioxidant, is a prescription drug used in the treatment of sleep disorders. It ameliorates hepatic and renal damage, evidenced by a significant reduction in serum levels of alanine aminotransferase and BUN. It also reduces the mortality rate of DQ-poisoned mice (Xu et al. 2006). Melatonin effectively counters DQ-induced oxidative stress by enhancing the expression of GPX1 (glutathione peroxidase 1) and SOD1. Melatonin mitigates DQ-induced spermatogonial apoptosis by regulating p53 and cleaved caspase-3. Moreover, melatonin restores the integrity of the blood-testis barrier. Overall, melatonin demonstrates protective effects against DQ-induced testicular damage in mice (Yang et al. 2023). In addition, dietary supplementation with melatonin enhances antioxidant capacity, thereby mitigating intestinal epithelial barrier injury in weaned piglets exposed to DQ (Zha et al. 2023). While the Chinese expert consensus on the diagnosis and treatment of acute DQ poisoning mentioned that melatonin may be attempted as a therapeutic option in patients with DQ poisoning, it should be noted that its bioavailability is low (3% – 15%) (Andersen et al. 2016; DeMuro et al. 2000).

Perspective and conclusions

The molecular mechanisms of DQ-induced multiple-organ injury are extremely complicated, although various signaling pathways in terms of oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular death are elaborated. While several potential agents can exert protective effects against DQ toxicity across multiple animal models, the extrapolation of these findings to humans should be approached with caution due to interspecies differences. Regrettably, no clinical trials are currently underway to evaluate these potential agents. Therefore, integrating available human case data is essential for validating animal-derived mechanisms and enhancing their translational relevance.

Future research should focus on the following areas: (1) The molecular mechanisms of DQ-induced cytotoxicity can be further investigated using multiple omics approaches. (2) Potential antidotes should be identified and optimized using structure-based drug design, computational chemistry, and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven strategies. (3) These potential therapeutic agents can be further evaluated in terms of efficacy and safety in DQ-administered animals. Notably, some candidates (e.g., vitamin C, vitamin D3, dexamethasone, N-acetylcysteine, glutathione, and melatonin) merit particular attention. These drugs, already used in treating other diseases, hold promise for clinical application in treating DQ-poisoned patients due to their established feasibility. Preclinical research and clinical trials should be conducted in the future to translate these findings into clinical applications.
