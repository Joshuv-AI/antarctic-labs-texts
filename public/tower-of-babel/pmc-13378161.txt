Background

Spinal cord injuries (SCIs) are complex disorders resulting from direct or indirect damage to the spinal cord and cause severe neurological deficits with a profound impact on patient outcomes. While the primary etiology of SCIs is acute mechanical trauma—frequently secondary to high-velocity motor vehicle accidents—the condition may also manifest through progressive pathological processes, including neoplastic infiltration or chronic infectious etiologies, such as Pott's disease (tuberculous spondylitis) [1, 2]. SCIs can be divided into traumatic and non-traumatic injuries. Traumatic spinal cord injury (TSCI) is a profoundly impactful condition that frequently leads to considerable morbidity, ongoing disability, psychological distress, and financial strain. In contrast, nontraumatic SCIs can result from various factors, including tumors, infections, and degenerative changes in the spine [1, 3]. Imaging plays a crucial role in the precise detection of injuries. Non-contrast computed tomography (CT) is the main imaging technique used, offering quick and detailed images of vertebral fractures and structural damage. CT has a sensitivity exceeding 98% for injuries to the cervical spine and nearly 100% for thoracolumbar injuries, making it highly effective in identifying fractures, dislocations, and bone-related issues [1]. The International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI), the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA), and the American Impairment Scale (AIS) are the most commonly utilized and well-validated clinical scoring systems for evaluating SCIs and determining prognosis with A being the most severe grade and grade E representing normal neurological function in a patient with prior deficits [2]. The management of SCIs starts right at the site of the trauma. Emergency medical services (EMS) staff implement spinal motion restriction (SMR) to minimize additional neurological harm during the transfer of the patient. SMR entails restricting unwanted movement of the spine through the use of equipment, such as backboards or scoop stretchers. When appropriate and possible, early surgical decompression—preferably within 24 h—can enhance neurological outcomes by alleviating pressure on the spinal cord [1]. This case highlights a remarkable full neurological and ambulatory recovery in a 15-year-old male following a C5 burst fracture and a 20.2 mm spinal cord contusion, despite an initial ASIA A classification. The outcome suggests that severe pediatric spinal trauma can achieve total functional restoration through timely surgical stabilization and intensive rehabilitation.

Case presentation

A 15-year-old Syrian male with no significant past medical history presented to the emergency department following a fall from height, sustaining multiple contusions primarily to the head and neck. The patient was transported to the hospital approximately two hours after the incident, and he was transferred by his parents’ private car rather than an ambulance, so no pre-hospital care was provided during transport. A Philadelphia tracheotomy cervical collar was applied immediately upon arrival. Upon initial assessment, the patient was hemodynamically stable with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 15/15 and no history of loss of consciousness, emesis, or seizures. The mechanism of injury was a fall from the first floor, estimated at 4–5 m in height. The patient reported preserved sensory function and sphincter control during the early posttraumatic period despite the loss of lower extremities’ motor function and the weakness in his upper extremities, which would hypothetically classify him as AIS grade B. However, upon arrival, the patient had predominantly abdominal breathing, and neurological evaluation revealed complete paraplegia (motor strength of 0/5), upper extremities motor weakness (motor strength of 1/5), and sphincters dysfunction; characterized by acute urinary retention, as a large volume of urine was drained immediately after Foley catheter insertion, and negative anal sphincter tone. Additionally, our patient had impaired motor and sensory function at S4-S5 levels; he had no voluntary anal contraction, no light touch or pin-prick sensation, and impaired deep sensation from anal canal. However, he had a preserved sensation in his face, neck and shoulders, but hypoesthesia below C5. Initial imaging was performed within ten minutes of arrival. The clinical picture strongly suggested the deterioration of the spinal cord injury, possibly due to secondary spinal cord injury caused by the evolving spinal cord edema, supported by the presence of radiological imaging via CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); which confirmed a C5 burst fracture with an associated 20.2 mm intramedullary spinal cord contusion. Surgery was performed immediately after imaging. An anterior approach was used to allow complete corpectomy of the injured vertebra, placement of a cylindrical cage, and fixation with an anterior plate secured to the adjacent superior and inferior vertebrae. The surgical approach was developed medial to the scalenus muscle and lateral to the visceral structures, dissecting between the omohyoid and sternocleidomastoid muscles, retracting the visceral compartment medially and the carotid sheath laterally to expose the cervical spine. A Cloward retractor was applied, the adjacent discs were removed, and the cage was positioned in direct contact with the endplates of the adjacent vertebrae. Adequate decompression of the spinal cord was confirmed before closure, a drain was placed, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit. No intraoperative complications were encountered. A cage was required because the vertebral body was completely removed, and anterior plating was added to ensure stability and prevent cage migration. Spinal cord decompression was achieved by removing the displaced vertebral body. Postoperatively, broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy was initiated and continued for 14 days, consisting of IV levofloxacin 750 mg once daily, IV vancomycin 1 g every 12 h, and IV ceftriaxone 1 g every 12 h. Additionally, the patient received analgesics, intravenous fluids, electrolyte replacement, and parenteral nutrition to prevent spinal shock and maintain physiological stability. Although he was hemodynamically stable before surgery, his condition deteriorated shortly afterward, with progressive respiratory compromise, tachycardia of 120 beats per minute, fever of 38 °C, and oxygen desaturation to 86%. However, these symptoms subsided gradually within the first two days after surgery. Postoperative monitoring focused on continuous assessment of vital signs, with standby mechanical ventilation and oxygen saturation measured every 30 min. The patient was monitored closely for seven days postoperatively, followed by regular follow-up for three months. Immediate postoperative findings showed persistent lower limb paralysis and diminished distal upper extremity strength (elbow extension 1/5; wrist flexion/extension 1/5), and sensory function and sphincter control were restored within 14 days of surgery, classified as AIS grade B. The patient followed a three-month structured physiotherapy regimen; he underwent three physiotherapy sessions a week, and an everyday regimen of gait training began following the initial recovery in his lower extremities. After the follow-up period, complete neurological recovery of muscle strength and full restoration of ambulatory function were noted. Rehabilitation consisted of physical therapy and muscle-strengthening exercises; psychological rehabilitation was not available at our institution. The patient is scheduled to continue rehabilitation for up to two years. A notable feature of this case is the remarkable rapid return to fully normal motor function.

Discussion

TCSCIs represent a profound clinical challenge characterized by debilitating morbidity and highly variable neurological prognoses. Globally, the epidemiological landscape of TCSCIs demonstrates significant geographic heterogeneity, with prevalence estimated between 250 and 906 cases per million and annual incidence ranging from 8 to 49.1 per million. Etiological drivers further diverge by region; while high-velocity motor vehicle collisions remain the predominant cause in developed nations, motorcycle-related trauma constitutes the primary mechanism of injury within the Southeast Asian corridor [4]. The ISNCSCI, the ASIA, and the AIS remain the most validated assessment tools for determining SCI severity. AIS grade A describes injuries in which motor and sensory function are entirely absent in the sacral segments S4–S5. Grade B is assigned when sensation is still detectable below the neurological level of injury, including the sacral region. Grades C and D indicate that motor function is preserved to a progressively greater extent below the neurological level. Grade E is used when both motor and sensory function are essentially normal in a patient with prior deficits [2]. In the present case, the patient presented with a C5 burst fracture associated with a neurological deficit involving the sacral pathways; although our patient’s injury was hypothetically classified as AIS grade B at the time of the incident, his injury was categorized as AIS grade A on arrival due to the absence of sensory and motor function at the sacral pathways, associated with sphincters dysfunction. Patients with TCSCIs often report neurological symptoms, such as weakness, tingling sensations, or loss of sensation, in certain regions of the body below the injury site. They may also experience pain that can vary from localized discomfort to more widespread, radiating pain, depending on the injury's severity and location. Neuropathic pain is often observed in cases of incomplete spinal cord injuries, potentially resulting from the transmission of signals through intact pathways within the spinal cord. Research suggests that neuropathic pain associated with spinal cord injury may arise from the spinal cord close to the injury site and may also involve secondary alterations in affected nerve roots and brain regions [1, 5]. In a physical assessment, medical professionals often detect neurological deficits below the injury location. These can manifest as diminished muscle strength, alterations in sensation (ranging from decreased sensitivity to total loss), and abnormal reflexes. Patients who have suffered acute trauma may also show signs of multiple injuries, such as fractures in the skull and limbs, lacerations, contusions, bleeding, changes in mental status, tenderness in the thoracoabdominal region, hypotension, and respiratory difficulties [1]. Our patient maintained hemodynamic stability and a GCS of 15/15, with no loss of consciousness or pupillary abnormalities. Despite this stable status, neurological assessment revealed complete paraplegia of the lower extremities, upper-limb weakness, urinary retention and loss of anal sphincter tone. The radiological findings showed an intramedullary lesion length of 20.2 mm. CT and MRI confirmed the diagnosis, consistent with CT being the main imaging modality in acute SCI evaluation [1, 6]. Stokum et al. reported that early deterioration in AIS grade may reflect evolving secondary injury, rather than irreversible tissue destruction, meaning that some cases of apparent worsening still have potential for recovery if treated promptly. He also outlined possible etiologies explaining the improvement in AIS grade; he reported that neuronal stunning, also known as spinal cord concussion or transient neuropraxia, is a reversible loss of axonal and neuronal function after spinal cord injury, driven by conduction block from edema, demyelination, neuroinflammation, early secondary axonal degeneration and oxidative stress, along with reduced neuronal excitability. In this state, the affected cells remain alive and potentially recoverable, but are temporarily unable to conduct normal neural signals. He also highlighted that neuronal stunning can occur both within MRI‑visible lesions and in nearby normal‑appearing tissue [2]. Consequently, it is concluded that progressive edema and other secondary injury cascades can degrade neurological function after the initial trauma, but these processes may improve with timely management, making early AIS worsening potentially reversible. Therefore, an initial shift toward a more severe AIS classification does not necessarily indicate permanent loss [2]. Ultimately, early surgical decompression has been proposed as a neuroprotective strategy, since evidence from studies on TCSCIs indicates that decompression performed within 24 h may enhance neurological recovery, particularly in cases of incomplete spinal injury [4]. Aarabi et al. documented improved outcomes following early surgery in severe presentations, albeit with limited strength of evidence. Reported complications included a higher incidence of neurological worsening (2.2% vs. 0.8%), wound infection, and mortality in the early cohort, whereas pulmonary embolism was more prevalent in the delayed group; cardiopulmonary complications were observed across both groups [7]. Cases documenting the deterioration in AIS grade or the improvement following AIS grade A injuries have been reported in the literature, such as the case by Semita et al. which documented a remarkable improvement within 3 months of an AIS grade A spinal cord injury due to C4-C5 dislocation in a 59-year-old female, despite the delayed management (20 h after the incident) [8]. Moreover, Stokum et al. reported that the AIS grade may worsen overtime in some cases, and in contrast, there are documented cases with “AIS grade conversion”. He added that “AIS grade conversion” refers to the improvement in the AIS grade of the patient; he documented an index case highlighting the improvement from AIS grade A to AIS grade C within a 6-month period in a 56-year-old male with a C5 tear-drop fracture [2]. In contrast, our 15-year-old patient underwent early fixation and decompression within 2 h after the incident, and demonstrated complete neurological recovery within three months of structured physiotherapy. This highlights an exceptional outcome compared to published data and the potential benefit of early intervention in cervical AIS grade A injuries, and underscores the resilience of younger patients. However, the limitations of this study include its single-case nature; therefore, its findings cannot be generalized, as well as the lack of follow-up MRI due to the financial constraints faced by the patient’s parents.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that an initial AIS Grade A classification in adolescent cervical trauma does not preclude a complete neurological recovery. Our case suggests that prompt surgical stabilization combined with intensive rehabilitation can overcome severe initial motor deficits. Ultimately, this report advocates for aggressive intervention in pediatric spinal injuries. However, further research into pediatric neuroplasticity is warranted to better define the clinical predictors of such full functional restoration.
