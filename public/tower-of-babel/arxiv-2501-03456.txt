Text to Band Gap: Pre-trained Language Models as Encoders for Semiconductor Band Gap Prediction
Ying-Ting Yeh, Janghoon Ock, Amir Barati Farimani

Abstract
In this study, we explore the use of a transformer-based language model as an encoder to predict the band gaps of semiconductor materials directly from their text descriptions. Quantum chemistry simulations, including Density Functional Theory (DFT), are computationally intensive and time-consuming, which limits their practicality for high-throughput material screening, particularly for complex systems. Shallow machine learning (ML) models, while effective, often require extensive data preprocessing to convert non-numerical material properties into numerical inputs. In contrast, our approach leverages textual data directly, bypassing the need for complex feature engineering. We generate material descriptions in two formats: formatted strings combining features and natural language text generated using the ChatGPT API. We demonstrate that the RoBERTa model, pre-trained on natural language processing tasks, performs effectively as an encoder for prediction tasks. With minimal fine-tuning, it achieves a mean absolute error (MAE) of approximately 0.33 eV, performing better than shallow machine learning models such as Support Vector Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost. Even when only the linear regression head is trained while keeping the RoBERTa encoder layers frozen, the accuracy remains nearly identical to that of the fully trained model. This demonstrates that the pre-trained RoBERTa encoder is highly adaptable for processing domain-specific text related to material properties, such as the band gap, significantly reducing the need for extensive retraining. This study highlights the potential of transformer-based language models to serve as efficient and versatile encoders for semiconductor materials property prediction tasks.

1
Introduction

The band gap of semiconductor materials is a fundamental property that directly impacts their electronic and optical behaviors. This parameter dictates crucial attributes such as conductivity, light absorption, and emission, making it essential for the performance of various electronic, optoelectronic, and photovoltaic devices. Therefore, the precise prediction and control of the band gap are vital for optimizing semiconductor applications in these fields
Kim et al. 2020
.

Band gaps are determined primarily through experimental methods, with UV-visible absorption spectroscopy and photoluminescence spectroscopy being the most commonly used techniques
Masood et al. 2023
. However, these experimental methods can only measure the band gaps of synthesized materials and are not applicable to new materials designed theoretically. On the computational side, Density Functional Theory (DFT) has been the primary tool for studying the electronic structure of materials
Koch and Holthausen 2015
;
Kohn and Sham 1965
. While DFT can accurately simulate electronic properties such as band structures and band gaps, its high computational cost and resource-intensive nature make it less practical for high-throughput material screening, especially for complex systems
Schleder et al. 2019
.

Machine learning (ML) methods have become powerful tools for addressing the computational challenges of Density Functional Theory (DFT). Shallow ML models, such as Random Forest (RF)
Wang et al. 2021
and Support Vector Regression (SVR)
Rajan et al. 2018
;
Zhuo et al. 2018
, are commonly used to predict band gaps efficiently by significantly reducing computational costs compared to solving complex quantum mechanics equations. However, these models often struggle with non-numerical features, requiring extensive preprocessing to convert material properties into numerical formats. This not only increases the complexity of feature engineering but also risks losing critical, hard-to-quantify information. Recent advances in deep learning, particularly Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)
Taniguchi et al. 2023
, have improved the ability to capture the interconnectivity of atomic structures by representing them as graphs. However, this approach requires the conversion of atomic arrangements into graph representations, which adds another layer of preprocessing. Furthermore, GNNs still face limitations in integrating non-numerical properties, such as compound names, into the training process without additional preprocessing steps. These challenges underscore the need for approaches that can seamlessly handle both numerical and non-numerical features in material property predictions while minimizing complex preprocessing requirements.

Language models offer unique advantages by directly utilizing human-readable text data, eliminating the need for elaborate pre-processing while preserving critical information embedded in material descriptions
Ock et al. 2023
;
Ock et al. 2024
. This streamlines the prediction process. Recent advances in natural language processing, particularly with large language models (LLMs), have introduced transformative possibilities for materials science. This simplifies the prediction process compared to conventional ML approaches, which rely on precise atomic coordinates or complex data preprocessing to obtain numerical features, LLMs can directly use text-based descriptions to predict material properties. This bypasses the need for detailed structural data and removes the necessity for elaborate feature engineering.

Recent advances in natural language processing, particularly with LLMs, have introduced transformative opportunities for materials science. For instance, AlloyBERT demonstrates the potential of transformer-based models to predict material properties from descriptive text
Chaudhari et al. 2024
. Similarly, AMGPT showcases the benefits of using composition-based input strings and fine-tuned LLMs, enabling accurate and efficient predictions for materials science tasks
Jacobs et al. 2024
;
Chandrasekhar et al. 2024
. Additionally, CatBERTa, a RoBERTa-based predictive model, has been developed to predict adsorption energy in catalyst systems
Ock et al. 2023
;
Ock et al. 2024
.

In this study, we leverage a transformer-based language model, RoBERTa (Robustly Optimized BERT Pre-training Approach), to predict the band gaps of semiconductor materials. This approach allows us to directly process text-based descriptions of material properties, such as chemical composition and crystal structures, without requiring extensive feature engineering. Our approach can handle both numerical and non-numerical features flexibly without complex data preprocessing, enhancing prediction accuracy and simplifying the input process. The inherent attention mechanisms of the transformer architecture also provide interpretability by identifying key material descriptors that contribute most to band gap predictions. By harnessing the power of language models, our approach captures subtle relationships and patterns within materials data, offering a robust framework for accurate and insightful band gap predictions.

2
Methods

2.1
Pre-trained Model

RoBERTa is an enhanced model based on Bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT), focused on improving language pre-processing performance
Liu 2019
. Widely applied across various natural language processing (NLP) tasks, RoBERTa is known for its strong ability to capture contextual relationships between tokens in text. We hypothesized that this capability would also be effective in identifying relationships between material features. Compared to BERT, RoBERTa omits the next-sentence prediction task, focusing solely on the masked language modeling task, and introduces multiple full sentences in each input during training to help the model understand long-range dependencies. RoBERTa also employs a byte-level Byte Pair Encoding tokenizer, which segments text into subword units at the byte level, allowing it to effectively handle rare and Unicode characters. These design choices, combined with dynamic masking, larger training batches, and improved sentence packing, enable RoBERTa to produce more representative embeddings, capturing both intra- and inter-sentence context
Liu 2019
.

2.2
Fine-tuning Model

Our model is based on the RoBERTa-base architecture, which comprises 12 transformer layers, each with 12 attention heads and a hidden size of 768. We fine-tuned this model for the regression task of band gap prediction on the AFLOW dataset. While AFLOW itself contains only numerical feature data, we generated textual descriptions by combining property values from the dataset and utilizing the OpenAI API to produce detailed text representations.

To adapt RoBERTa for regression, we implemented a custom regression head specifically designed to predict scalar band gap values. This regression head consists of two connected linear layers, each followed by ReLU activation to introduce nonlinearity, with dropout layers added between successive linear layers to mitigate overfitting. The final output layer produces a single scalar value corresponding to the predicted band gap. In our fine-tuning process, we employed the AdamW optimizer. The model parameters, including both the pre-trained weights and the regression head, were updated using the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function to optimize the prediction of band gap values. To prevent overfitting, we implemented a ReduceLROnPlateau learning rate scheduler that monitors validation loss. This approach enables the model to take advantage of the general knowledge encoded in RoBERTa while adapting to the specific characteristics of the AFLOW dataset.

2.3
Dataset

In this study, we utilized the AFLOW database, a comprehensive open repository for computational materials science that contains extensive information on inorganic crystalline materials and their properties
Taylor et al. 2014
. Band gap calculations in AFLOW combine first-principles methods with empirical corrections through an automated workflow. The framework uses VASP to perform DFT calculations with the GGA-PBE functional for standard compounds while applying the GGA+U method for strongly correlated systems containing d- and f-block elements. To address GGA’s tendency to underestimate band gaps, AFLOW employs an empirical correction scheme based on a linear regression model derived from 102 benchmark compounds with known experimental values
Setyawan et al. 2011
;
Gossett et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2022
. This systematic approach, along with the database’s vast size and rich feature space, makes AFLOW particularly well-suited for machine learning tasks aimed at material property prediction.

For our specific analysis, we selected a subset of 27,600 materials with band gap values ranging between 0 and 5 eV (inclusive). This range was chosen because it encompasses the most relevant band gap values for semiconductors, which are of particular interest in materials science and electronic applications. The lower bound of 0 eV represents materials with metallic behavior, where there is no electronic band gap. Materials that have band gap higher than 5 eV are insulating materials, which have too large band gaps and do not conduct electricity under normal conditions
Tripathy and Pattanaik 2016
;
Masood et al. 2023
. By focusing on materials within this range of 0-5 eV band gaps, we ensure that our model targets materials with practical applications in electronics and optoelectronics.

The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets to ensure reliable evaluation and optimization of the model. Specifically, 10% of the data was reserved for the test set to evaluate the final performance of the model. The remaining 90% was further split into 80% for training and 20% for validation, ensuring sufficient data for model training while retaining a representative validation set. This splitting strategy ensured the distribution of band gap values across all subsets, minimizing sampling bias and enhancing representativeness. This preprocessing and splitting steps not only improved computational efficiency during training, but also ensured the reliability and generalizability of the predictions, making the model robust for downstream applications.

2.4
Text Data Format

To investigate the impact of input data format on model performance, we utilized two different approaches to represent material property data. The first approach involved converting the data into a structured string format, where each entry followed a consistent template to standardize the presentation of material features. This format ensured that important characteristics, such as composition, crystal structure, and electronic properties, were uniformly highlighted, helping the model to better understand the relationships between these features and the corresponding band gap values.

The second approach utilized descriptive text generated by OpenAI’s GPT-3.5 Turbo model, which provided a more natural and detailed narrative of material properties. The generation process was configured with a maximum length of 512 tokens and a temperature parameter of 1.0, aligning with RoBERTa’s token limit to ensure efficient training and inference. This format aims to introduce greater variability and flexibility in feature representation, allowing the model to explore more human-like language representations. These descriptive texts captured key material features, including chemical composition, crystal structure, and known electronic behaviors, in a conversational style. We evaluated the performance of the model using raw data converted directly into different types of text without any pre-processing or additional modifications, to assess whether the language model could effectively process them.

In this study, we carefully selected features that capture both the chemical composition and structural properties of the materials, ensuring a comprehensive understanding of their electronic characteristics, especially the band gap. The selected features include chemical formula, atomic species, valence electron count, crystal symmetry, and magnetic properties, all of which are known to play critical roles in determining the electronic structure and band gap of materials. These features were selected based on previous research findings. The reduction in feature count significantly lowered computational complexity, improved model training and prediction efficiency, and allowed us to focus on the most crucial features for band gap prediction, enhancing the model’s practicality and efficiency.

4
Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated the effectiveness of using a transformer-based language model, RoBERTa, to predict the band gaps of semiconductor materials directly from text descriptions or strings, eliminating the need for complex feature engineering. Our results indicate that this approach outperforms shallow ML models, particularly when structured string formats are used, highlighting the potential of language models to provide an efficient and accurate alternative for predicting material properties from human-readable text.

A key finding is that RoBERTa’s pre-trained encoder already captures significant domain-specific knowledge, which can be leveraged with minimal fine-tuning to achieve high prediction accuracy. This is supported by our layer-freezing ablation study, embedding visualizations, and feature-wise self-attention score analysis, which collectively suggest that pre-trained language model encoders inherently recognize relevant domain-specific features. Fine-tuning further enhances these capabilities, refining the model for task-specific predictions. Overall, this study establishes that transformer-based language models like RoBERTa offer a robust and efficient approach for predicting semiconductor material properties, providing a compelling alternative to traditional methods reliant on detailed structural data and feature engineering.
