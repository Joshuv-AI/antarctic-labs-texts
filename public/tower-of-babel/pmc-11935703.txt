Incidence and Outcomes After Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest at Train Stations in Denmark
Authors: Annam Pervez Sheikh, Anne Juul Grabmayr, Julie Samsøe Kjølbye, Annette Kjær Ersbøll, Carolina Malta Hansen, Fredrik Folke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFollowing international guidelines, communities have deployed automated external defibrillators at train stations without substantive evidence.Methods and ResultsWe geocoded public out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrests (OHCAs) (2016–2020), automated external defibrillators, and train stations. The stations were divided into the following groups according to passenger flow: 1 (0–499), 2 (500–4999), 3 (5000–9999), and 4 (>10 000) passengers per day. Risk ratios (RRs) were calculated using Poisson regression of rates, and odds ratios (ORs) were analyzed through logistic regression. OHCAs at train stations accounted for 102 (2.3%) of 4467 public OHCAs. The incidence rate (IR) and RR for OHCAs were for group 1: IR, 0.02 OHCA per station per year, RR, 1.0 (reference); group 2: IR, 0.07, RR, 4.1 (95% CI, 2.3–7.3); group 3: IR, 0.25, RR, 12.7 (95% CI, 6.2–25.9); and group 4: IR, 0.34, RR, 16.3 (95% CI, 8.6–30.9).Compared with other public OHCAs, OHCAs at train stations were just as likely to receive bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (OR, 1.13 [95% CI, 0.60–2.12]). However, they had higher odds of bystander defibrillation (OR, 1.66 [95% CI, 1.06–2.58]), were more likely to achieve return of spontaneous circulation (OR, 1.88 [95% CI, 1.24–2.85]), and survive 30 days (OR, 2.37 [95% CI, 1.57–3.59]).ConclusionsThe incidence of OHCAs at train stations was associated with passenger flow, with the busiest stations having a 16‐fold higher risk of OHCAs than the lowest. OHCAs at train stations had better outcomes compared with other public OHCAs.Keywords: automated external defibrillation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, defibrillation, out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest, sudden cardiac death, train stationSubject Categories: Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiac Care

Train stations have a higher incidence of out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrests (OHCAs) compared with other public locations. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Furthermore, early defibrillation after OHCA is associated with survival rates of up to 56%. 6 , 7 , 8 Acknowledging these factors, earlier European resuscitation guidelines recommended deploying automated external defibrillators (AEDs) at high‐risk sites such as airports and train stations. 8 Many countries have deployed AEDs at train stations based on these guideline recommendations. 2 , 3 , 4 , 9 , 10 , 11 In Denmark, AEDs were deployed inside every train and at all major train stations between 2015 and 2020. However, train stations are heterogeneous in size and traffic, but the association between passenger flow and OHCA incidence remains unexplored. 1 , 2 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

Due to the lack of high‐quality data, current European guidelines have no specific recommendations on where to deploy AEDs in public locations. 13 Several studies have underlined the lack of an evidence‐based strategy for AED deployment, and the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation has recommended this subject be investigated. 12 , 14 As the first nationwide study, we aimed to investigate the incidence of OHCA at train stations and its association with passenger flow to guide strategic AED deployment. In addition, we investigated the likelihood of bystander interventions and survival after an OHCA at train stations in Denmark compared with other public OHCAs.

METHODS

This observational study analyzed OHCAs at all train stations in Denmark from 2016 to 2020. Our data include OHCAs geocoded to train station locations. The data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Setting

Train Networks

The Danish train network comprises 319 train stations, of which 189 are intercity and regional (long‐distance trains between cities and regions), 39 are underground metro stations located in the metropolitan area of Copenhagen, and 86 are suburban train stations.

The Danish train network transported 211 million passengers in 2021. 15 All train stations, except Copenhagen International Airport, were included in the study, because it was impossible to differentiate between OHCAs occurring at the airport compared with the adjacent train station.

In Copenhagen, 9 train stations have an overground station and an underground metro station in the same location. These stations were merged as 1 in this study.

Emergency Medical Services System

The emergency medical services (EMS) system in Denmark is a 2‐tiered system involving an ambulance and a physician‐staffed vehicle providing advanced life support. When someone calls the emergency number 1‐1‐2 in case of a suspected OHCA, the emergency dispatch center guides the caller in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and retrieval of the nearest AED, if possible. Furthermore, nearby volunteer responders are notified through the nationwide volunteer responder program. 16

Data Sources

OHCA Data

Data on OHCAs were obtained from the Danish Cardiac Arrest Registry (DCAR), a national quality registry that includes all verified OHCAs in Denmark. Thus, the validity of the registry is high and ensures minimal loss to follow‐up. 17 For OHCAs occurring between 2016 and 2020, we obtained information on exact geolocation (latitude and longitude), sex, bystander witnessed status, initial rhythm (shockable versus nonshockable), bystander CPR, bystander defibrillation, EMS response time, return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) at hospital arrival, and 30‐day survival. However, the registry has some limitations. It does not include data on AEDs, such as AED attachments, information about the first on scene, or differentiation between who initiated the resuscitation efforts (random bystander or volunteer responders).

Train Stations

The geolocations of train stations and data on passenger flow (2019) were directly collected from the Danish State Railway, Metro, Statistics Denmark and the Danish Traffic Authority. 15 , 18 Furthermore, the date of AED placement and precise location at the train station were collected from the national Danish AED network, an online AED registry including private and public AEDs. 19 This registry has been used in multiple studies. 20 , 21 The AED network is linked to all dispatch centers in Denmark. The locations of AEDs at train stations were verified by comparing them with data from the Danish State Railway.

Study Population

A total of 6457 public OHCAs were included from the DCAR from 2016 to 2020. Of these, 102 occurred at a train station in Denmark (Figure 1). The exclusion criteria were age <18 years, missing coordinates, or witnessed by EMS. Children (<18 years) were excluded to ensure a comparable population, because none of the OHCAs at train stations during our study period included children.

Geocoding

All OHCAs, AEDs, and train stations were geocoded using the geographical information system QGIS3 version 3.18.2. 22 OHCAs at train stations were identified by combining the coordinates of the OHCA location with coordinates of train stations and marked on a digital map. All OHCAs within a radius of 100 m from the center of the station were included by drawing a buffer from the center of the stations.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was OHCA incidence at the train station according to passenger flow. Secondary outcome measures were bystander CPR and defibrillation, ROSC, and 30‐day survival at train stations compared with other public locations.

Statistical Analysis

The association between passenger flow and incidence of OHCAs at train stations was calculated as a risk ratio using a Poisson regression. The passenger flow was calculated as the mean number of passengers per day. Stations that consisted of both a train and metro station were included by the sum of passengers. The passenger flow was divided into the following 4 groups by increasing the number of passengers: group 1, 0–499; group 2, 500–4999; group 3, 5000–9999; and group 4 ≥10 000 passengers per day.

A logistic regression model adjusted for age and time of day was used to examine the association between OHCAs at train stations compared with other public arrests, presented as odds ratios with corresponding 95% CIs. A directed acyclic graph approach was used to identify confounders, providing a structural and hypothesis‐driven method to identify the complex relationship between variables. 23 , 24 , 25 Based on the directed acyclic graph, we identified age and time of day as confounders and adjusted for these in our final analysis, ensuring that our model accounts for key variables that could bias the association between train stations and outcomes (Figures S1).

Descriptive statistics are absolute numbers and percentages, medians, and interquartile ranges.

A 5% significance level was applied. The statistical analysis was performed in R version 4.1.0 and RStudio version 1.4.717.

Incidence and Passenger Flow

OHCA incidence increased with increased passenger flow. Moreover, OHCA incidence in the 4 groups was as follows: group 1, 0.019; group 2, 0.07; group 3, 0.25; and group 4, 0.33 OHCAs per station per year. The risk of an OHCA was nearly 4‐fold higher in group 3, 12‐fold higher in group 2, and 16‐fold higher in group 4 compared with group 1 (Figure 2).

Bystander Interventions and Survival

Compared with OHCAs at other public locations, patients with OCHAs at train stations were just as likely to receive bystander CPR. However, they had a higher probability of bystander defibrillation to achieve ROSC and a significantly higher probability of 30‐day survival (Figure 3). DISCUSSION

This observational study, which investigates the incidence of OHCAs at train stations and its association with passenger flow, found that train stations accounted for 2.3% of all public OHCAs, with a strong association between OHCA incidence and passenger flow. A nearly 16‐fold higher risk of OHCAs was observed at the busiest train stations compared with stations with the lowest passenger flow. In addition, a higher probability of bystander interventions and survival was observed among patients at train stations compared with other public locations.

Our study shows that passenger flow is an important indicator for AED deployment at train stations, challenging earlier recommendations for deploying AEDs at all train stations. 8 Train stations can vary in size, passenger flow, and location, making it difficult to categorize them as 1 homogenous location. Instead, passenger flow can be used as guidance to prioritize which stations to cover with AEDs first.

We observed that only 2.3% of public OHCAs occurred at train stations, a finding that contradicts the observations of prior studies that showed incidence rates between 9% and 13%. 3 , 9 This inconsistency may be attributed to prior studies only including train stations in big metropolitan cities with a much higher passenger flow, leading to passengers spending more time at and around the stations. Our study included all train stations in Denmark and all non–EMS‐witnessed OHCAs. As a nationwide study with broad inclusion criteria, our study provides a more accurate picture of OHCA incidence at train stations in communities across rural and urban areas. A recent study showed that two‐thirds of all public OHCAs occurred in residential neighborhoods, indicating a lower incidence than expected at busy public locations such as train stations. 26 Train stations have often been compared with airports when deploying AEDs and patient outcomes. However, train stations are heterogeneous in terms of size and traffic compared with commercial airports. 6 , 8

A recent study on Copenhagen Airport found a much higher proportion of bystander defibrillation before EMS arrival (44%) compared with our findings from train stations (28%), as well as a higher survival rate (56% versus 45%, respectively). 6 Several factors can explain the lower defibrillation and survival found at train stations compared with airports. First, train stations vary considerably in terms of size and flow (some can be small), whereas airports have a high passenger flow around the clock, as reflected in the higher proportion of witnessed arrests (91% versus 69%). Second, the population at train stations might differ from that at airports, because the former includes a higher proportion of socially vulnerable people including individuals experiencing homelessness and substance use disorders. Furthermore, professional first responders arrive at the scene in Copenhagen Airport within 3 minutes in the case of a suspected OHCA, whereas train stations rarely have trained staff alerted in case of an OHCA. As expected, bystander defibrillation, ROSC, and survival probability were higher at train stations than at other public OHCAs. These higher probabilities are consistent with the findings of prior studies showing bystander defibrillation rates at train stations up 76% for witnessed arrest and ventricular fibrillation. 9 , 10 , 11

Several factors can explain the higher probability of bystander interventions and better outcomes at train stations. First, most train stations included in this study had on‐site AEDS, increasing the odds of bystander defibrillation. 20 , 27 , 28 Second, train stations often have numerous bystanders, which increases the odds of early bystander interventions. 29 Third, nearly one‐third of the patients at train stations had an initial shockable rhythm compared with one‐fourth of the patients with other public OHCAs, leading to better outcomes after the OHCAs. 30

Despite the availability of numerous bystanders and accessible AEDs, only 28% of patients were defibrillated by bystanders at train stations. To increase this, the staff can be trained in AED retrieval and CPR, and visible signs directing to the AED can be displayed in the stations to increase the percentage of patients who can be defibrillated. 31

Our study differs from previous studies, because it was conducted nationally. Furthermore, it was not restricted to witnessed OHCAs or OHCAs of cardiac origin and included all types of train stations. We also expanded earlier findings by showing an association between OHCA incidence and passenger flow at train stations.

The strong association between OHCA incidence and passenger flow questions earlier guidelines that categorize train stations as high‐risk sites for OHCAs equal to airports. Our findings recommend prioritizing the busiest train stations when deploying AEDs.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, as an observational study, our findings are associations, not casual relations. Second, our methods cannot guarantee that all included OHCAs occurred at train stations, because we included all public OHCAs within a radius of 100 meters from the center of the station. To minimize the risk of including OHCAs outside train stations, we included buffers around stations and excluded OHCAs that occurred in nearby buildings. Metro stations are located centrally, with a high risk of including OHCAs outside the station. However, we examined EMS charts for these OHCAs and excluded those that occurred outside the metro stations. The DCAR does not provide information on AEDs. Therefore, we do not have data on the number of AED attachments without delivering a shock, which is a limitation to this study. AED attachment could be expected to be higher at train stations due to a higher flow of people and AED availability. Third, due to the relatively small number of OHCAs at train stations, the results must be cautiously interpreted when generalizing the results. Nonetheless, our study included nationwide data from the DCAR, including urban and nonurban areas over several years.

Despite its relatively small size, Denmark stands out as a high‐performing nation in terms of its interventions and survival after OHCA due to widespread resuscitation training and accessible AEDs, which positively affect the transferability of the study. A lower incidence of bystander interventions and survival would be expected in less resourceful communities. However, other less resourceful communities could benefit from strategic AED placement at train stations, as shown in Brazil. 11

Due to the nature of the study, if any OHCA initially occurred in a train and then reported at a train station, they would be geocoded to the train station and be included in our study population. However, we expect this to be few cases. Despite this limitation, our study gives valuable insight into OHCA incidence and bystander interventions at train stations.

Traumatic OHCAs and suicides have been included in the study, because the DCAR records all OHCAs where there was a nonprofessional or professional attempt at resuscitation. However, cases involving major traumas, where CPR and AED use were not the primary treatments, are excluded from the registry. Despite this limitation, our study provides important insights into the significance of prioritizing AED distribution at train stations. Additionally, a recent study from Denmark showed survival rates of up to 8% for traumatic OHCAs, highlighting the potential benefits of including these cases in future research. 32

CONCLUSIONS

OHCA incidence at train stations was associated with passenger flow, with the busiest stations having a 16‐fold higher risk of OHCA than the lowest, making passenger flow an important indicator for AED deployment. OHCAs at train stations had a higher probability of bystander defibrillation, ROSC, and survival compared with other public OHCAs.
