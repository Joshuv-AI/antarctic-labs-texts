Abstract
. Unsupervised anomaly detection in time series has been a pivotal research area for decades. Current mainstream approaches focus on learning normality, on the assumption that all or most of the samples in the training set are normal. However, anomalies in the training set (i.e., anomaly contamination) can be misleading. Recent studies employ data augmentation to generate pseudo-anomalies and learn the boundary separating the training samples from the augmented samples. Although this approach mitigates anomaly contamination if augmented samples mimic unseen real anomalies, it suffers from several limitations. (1) Covering a wide range of time series anomalies is challenging. (2) It disregards augmented samples that resemble normal samples (i.e., false anomalies). (3) It places too much trust in the labels of training and augmented samples. In response, we propose RedLamp , which employs diverse data augmentations to generate multiclass pseudo-anomalies and learns the multiclass boundary. Such multiclass pseudo-anomalies cover a wide variety of time series anomalies. We conduct multiclass classification using soft labels, which prevents the model from being overconfident and ensures its robustness against contaminated/false anomalies. The learned latent space is inherently explainable as it is trained to separate pseudo-anomalies into multiclasses. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of RedLamp in anomaly detection and its robustness against anomaly contamination.

Time series anomaly detection (TSAD) is an important field in data mining and analytics.
Its goal is to detect unexpected patterns or values within sequences that are significantly dissimilar to the majority.
It has numerous real-world applications, including but not limited to robot-assisted systems

Anomalies are rare by nature, and labeling them from large quantities of data would be difficult and costly.
Therefore, TSAD is often formulated as an unsupervised problem with an unlabeled training set.

Unsupervised learning methods for TSAD, such as reconstruction-based methods

Consequently, as illustrated in Fig.

Recent studies, particularly in computer vision

This approach is based on the

Recently, CutAddPaste

Although existing binary anomaly assumption-based methods can mitigate the negative impact of anomaly contamination if pseudo-anomalies resemble contaminated anomalies, they face the following three issues:

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Anomaly Detection in Time Series

TSAD has been extensively studied over several decades
(
Blázquez-García et al. 2021
;
Schmidl et al. 2022
;
Braei and Wagner 2020
)
.
Initial machine learning methods include IsolationForest
(
Liu et al. 2008
)
and OCSVM
(
Schölkopf et al. 1999
)
.
Subsequently, many deep anomaly detection models have been proposed for capturing anomalous behavior in an unsupervised manner.
Most are based on the normality assumption that considers the majority of the samples in the training set to be normal.
As our model is partially based on reconstruction, we focus on reconstruction-based methods, which aim to learn the latent representation for the entire time series that can reconstruct normal samples while failing with anomaly samples.
Deep generative techniques, including autoencoders (AE)
(
Audibert et al. 2020
;
Abdulaal et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2021b
)
, GAN
(
Bashar and Nayak 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2019
)
, and diffusion models
(
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, are often used to build reconstruction-based methods.
For example, LSTM was incorporated into VAE to learn temporal features
(
Park et al. 2018
;
Su et al. 2019
)
.
IMDiffusion
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
integrates the imputation method with a grating masking strategy and employs diffusion models to generate masked sequences.
In addition to generative techniques, transformer architecture
(
Xu et al. 2022
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Tuli et al. 2022
)
and graph relational learning
(
Deng and Hooi 2021
;
Han and Woo 2022
)
have also been explored.
These methods control the trade-off between concise representation (i.e., regularization) and representation power (i.e., reconstruction).
Although they provide good results for multivariate time series benchmark datasets
(
Liu and Paparrizos 2024
)
, controlling such trade-offs remains challenging.
As a result, they tend to miss pattern-wise anomalies, especially when the differences from a normal sample are subtle.
Moreover, they suffer from overgeneralization under anomaly contamination as they are based on the normality assumption.

2.2.
Data Augmentation for Anomaly Detection

Data augmentation increases the diversity of the training set and improves the model’s ability to generalize to unseen data
(
Wen et al. 2021
;
Iwana and Uchida 2021
)
.
Generally, it assumes that the augmented samples cover unexplored input space while maintaining labels.
For example, BeatGAN
(
Zhou et al. 2019
)
is a reconstruction-based method proposed for detecting anomalies in ECG beats that uses time series warping for data augmentation and augmented samples as normal samples.

In anomaly detection, data augmentation is also used to handle data imbalance; that is, pseudo-anomalies are generated to replace an unsupervised task with a supervised binary classification task.
This approach relies on a binary anomaly assumption that learns the boundary that distinguishes between the training samples and the augmented samples.
The more the pseudo-anomalies mimic the nature of the true anomalies in the test set, the better the detection performance becomes.
Many augmentation techniques are proposed in computer vision
(
Zavrtanik et al. 2021
)
, such as cutout
(
DeVries 2017
)
and outlier exposure (OE)
(
Hendrycks et al. 2019
)
.
Li et al.
(
Li et al. 2021a
)
introduce CutPaste and CutPaste-Scar as data augmentations and demonstrate that conducting a 3-class classification using both augmentations performs the best.
However, these augmentations are tailored to the image and cannot be directly applied to time series.

The aim of several studies has been to generate time series-specific augmentations
(
Jeong et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
.
For example, NCAD
(
Carmona et al. 2022
)
designs data augmentation for TSAD utilizing OE, point outlier, and window mixup and then applies the hypersphere classifier.
Inspired by CutPaste, Wang et al.
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
propose CutAddPaste, which is designed to generate diverse anomalies by mimicking five different types of anomalies (e.g., global point, seasonal) based on the prior taxonomy
(
Lai et al. 2021
)
in a single data augmentation.
Unlike reconstruction-based methods, CutAddPaste can detect pattern-wise anomalies if suitable pseudo-anomalies are generated.
However, CutAddPaste requires many hyperparameters to be set to generate adequate augmentation for each dataset
(
Yoo et al. 2023
)
and is affected by anomaly contamination when there is a diversity gap.
To tackle the anomaly contamination issue, COUTA
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
employs data augmentation to create naive anomaly samples and penalizes irregular samples in the training set.
However, these binary anomaly assumption-based methods are overly confident as regards the generated pseudo-anomaly and ignore frequently occurring false anomalies, leading to degraded detection accuracy.

Proposed
RedLamp

3.1.
Problem Definition

In this paper, we focus on unsupervised anomaly detection for time series.
We adopt a local contextual window to model their temporal dependency, which is the common practice with most deep TSAD methods.
Consider a multivariate time series of
D
features collected over
T
timestep,

\bm{X}=\{\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\dots,\bm{x}_{T}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times T}
.
We define the training set
\mathcal{X}^{train}=\{\bm{X}^{(L)},\bm{X}^{(L+1)},\dots,\bm{X}^{(T)}\}
,
where
\bm{X}^{(t)}=\{\bm{x}_{t-L+1},\dots,\bm{x}_{t}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times L}
represents a
t
-th instance segmented by a sliding window of size
L
.
For simplicity, we consider that the stride is set at 1.

\bm{X}^{(t)}_{i,j}
indicates the element at the
i
-th feature and
j
-th timestep.
The goal of TDAD is to identify anomalous timesteps in a test set
\mathcal{X}^{test}
by training the model on an unlabeled training set.
The test set is also split into sub-sequences using the same window size
L
.
To detect the anomaly, the model aims to predict the anomaly scores
\bm{s}(t)
of the instance
\bm{X}^{(t)}\in\mathcal{X}^{test}
, where higher scores are more anomalous.

3.2.
Overall Framework

We propose
RedLamp
as the first multiclass anomaly assumption-based method for TSAD.

RedLamp
is robust against anomaly contamination and overcomes the challenges that face binary anomaly assumption-based methods, which are the diversity gap, false anomaly, and overconfidence in labels.
The core idea is to generate multiclass pseudo-anomalies through diverse data augmentations, enabling the model to learn a multiclass decision boundary.

An overview of the
RedLamp
framework is shown in Fig.
2
.
The proposed method begins by augmenting the training set to create an augmented instance set that includes normal instances and 11 types of pseudo-anomaly instances, as depicted in Fig.
2
(a).
Each augmented instance is accompanied by a one-hot label and an anomaly mask that indicates the location of the inserted anomaly (Section
3.3
).
Using the augmented training set, we train a model comprising a CNN-based encoder and decoder and a 2-layered MLP classifier.
Details of the model architecture can be found in Appendix
C
.
As shown in Fig.
2
(b), the augmented instances are first projected into embeddings by the encoder.
The decoder then generates reconstructions while the classifier predicts labels.
The decoder learns to reconstruct only the normal parts of the instances by utilizing the anomaly masks so that we can utilize the strong detection capability of a reconstruction-based approach.
To tackle the challenges of contaminated/false anomalies, we introduce backward correction and transform one-hot labels into corrected soft labels, which the classifier learns to predict (Section
3.4
).
Therefore, model parameters are learned via the loss function composed of masked reconstruction loss and cross-entropy loss (Section
3.5
).
Our anomaly score consists of reconstruction error and adjusted anomaly-class score that addresses the pseudo-normal class by considering a frequently observed class as normal (Section
3.6
).

3.3.
Time Series Anomaly Data Augmentation

Filling the diversity gap is important in terms of tackling anomaly contamination, and it also enhances detection performance (we discuss in Section
4.4
and
4.5
).
Instead of trying to mimic the unseen real anomalies, we aim to cover a wide range of time series anomalies.
We begin by introducing the algorithm and then elaborate on the data augmentations for time series-specific anomalies.

3.3.1.
Algorithm

3.3.2.
Data augmentation

Inspired by previous work
(
Goswami et al. 2023a
)
, we design 12 data augmentations, as illustrated in Fig.
2
(a).
These data augmentations, which consider time series-specific anomaly knowledge, generate anomalies of different traits, including five common types of time series anomalies
(
Lai et al. 2021
)
.
We describe them below using Python-format equations:

(1)

Normal: Uses the original data without modifications (
k=1
).

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim}=\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim}
.

(2)

Spike: Adds a sharp spike at a selected point.

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st}=\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st}+a
, where
a\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

(3)

Flip: Flips a selected range horizontally.

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed}[::-1]
.

(4)

Speedup: Changes the frequency of the data within a selected range by speeding it up (2x) or slowing it down (1/2x).

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed+length}[::2]
if 2x ,

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=Stretch(\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed-(length/2)};[length/2]\rightarrow[length])
if 1/2x .

(5)

Noise: Adds random noise sampled from a normal distribution.

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed}+\mathbf{a}
, where
\mathbf{a}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},0.1\bm{I})
.

(6)

Cutoff: Replaces data with a uniform value between the minimum and maximum values of a selected range.

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=Uni(\{min(\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed}),\dots,max(\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed})\},1)
.

(7)

Average: Applies a moving average of window size
w
.

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=MovingAverage(\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed},w=L/5)
.

(8)

Scale: Scales the values by a randomly generated factor.

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=a*\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed}
, where
a\sim\mathcal{N}(1,1)
.

(9)

Wander: Adds a linear trend and then shifts the baseline.

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed}+np.linspace(0,a,length)
,

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:}+=a
, where
a\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

(10)

Contextual: Scales a selected range and shifts the baseline.

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=a*\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed}+b
, where
a\sim\mathcal{N}(1,1),b\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

(11)

Upsidedown: Inverts a selected range.

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=2*Mean(\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed})-\bm{X}^{(t)}_{dim,st:ed}
.

(12)

Mixture: Swaps a selected range with another segment of the same length from a different timestep.

\bm{X}^{(t,k)}_{dim,st:ed}=\bm{X}^{(t^{\prime})}_{dim,st:ed}
, where
t^{\prime}\in\{L,...,T\}
,

where

is a selected feature,
st,ed

3.4.
Label Refurbishment

The definition of anomaly varies across datasets, making it difficult to design data augmentations that align with the real anomalies (see Appendix
E
).
Thus, pseudo-anomalies often include false anomalies, e.g., applying a short-range moving average to data with a longer periodicity may not be meaningful.
Additionally, anomaly contamination in the training set is inevitable.
These issues can be viewed as label noise;
that a potential anomaly instance is mislabeled as normal (i.e., contaminated anomaly) or a potential normal instance is mislabeled as pseudo-anomaly (i.e., false anomaly).

To address contaminated/false anomalies, we convert hard labels into soft labels by label refurbishment
(
Lu et al. 2023
)
, which is a technique often used to boost classification performance under label noise.
Since our pseudo-anomalies are generated from normal instances, we utilize backward correction
(
Patrini et al. 2017
)
, which can include the probability of misclassifying one class as another.
The corrected label is formulated as follows:

(1)

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{y}}^{(t,k)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha-K\beta)\bm{y}^{(t,k)}+\alpha+\beta&\text{if $k=1$},\\
(1-\alpha-K\beta)\bm{y}^{(t,k)}+\beta&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\alpha
represents the probability that a pseudo-anomaly is misclassified as normal,
and
\beta
denotes the probability that one class is misclassified as another.
The soft labels prevent the model from producing overconfident predictions, thus enhancing its robustness against false anomalies and anomaly contamination.

3.5.
Objective Function

We describe the loss function Eq. (
4

2
) and cross-entropy loss Eq. (

3.5.1.
Masked reconstruction loss

The decoder predicts the reconstruction
\bm{\hat{X}}^{(t,k)}
of augmented instance
\bm{X}^{(t,k)}
from the embedding.
The masked reconstruction loss is used to minimize the error in anomaly-free regions by removing inserted anomalies using an anomaly mask
\bm{W}^{(t,k)}
.
This loss allows the embeddings to capture the features of normal behavior in the latent space.
The masked reconstruction loss is defined as:

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{MSE}=||(\textbf{1}-\bm{W}^{(t,k)})\circ(\bm{X}^{(t,k)}-\bm{\hat{X}}^{(t,k)})||^{2}_{2},

where
\circ
denotes element-wise multiplication.

3.5.2.
Cross-entropy loss

The classifier predicts label
\bm{\hat{y}}^{(t,k)}
for
\bm{X}^{(t,k)}
from the embedding.
The cross-entropy loss is calculated by comparing the predicted label with the corrected label
\bm{\tilde{y}}^{(i,k)}
.
This loss helps prevent the model from overfitting contaminated/false anomalies and encourages the embeddings to separate into distinct classes in the latent space.
The cross-entropy loss is defined as:

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{CE}=-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\big(\bm{\tilde{y}}^{(t,k)}_{i}log(\bm{\hat{y}}^{(t,k)}_{i})\big).

3.5.3.
Total loss

Our loss function is the weighted sum of the above two losses and is defined as:

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\gamma\mathcal{L}_{CE}+(1-\gamma)\mathcal{L}_{MSE},

where
\gamma
is a hyperparameter that adjusts the weight of the cross-entropy loss.

3.6.
Anomaly Scoring

Finally, we propose an anomaly scoring function Eq. (
8
) that integrates reconstruction error Eq. (
5
) and adjusted anomaly-class score Eq. (
7
), which enables the detection of various types of anomalies.
The score is expected to be large when anomalies are input.

3.6.1.
Reconstruction error

As with a typical reconstruction-based approach, our model learns to reconstruct anomaly-free parts.
Thus, the model is expected to excel as regards reconstruction in normal parts but struggle in anomalous parts.
Reconstruction error is computed by comparing the input instance with its reconstruction:

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{MSE}(t)

\displaystyle=||\bm{X}^{(t)}-\bm{\hat{X}}^{(t)}||^{2}_{2}.

3.6.2.
Adjusted anomaly-class score

Some data augmentations may not disrupt the normality of the instance (check Fig.
6
(b) (i)).
To address such pseudo-normal classes, we consider anomaly classes that are frequently detected in the test set as normal.
We define frequent anomaly adjustment (FAA) in Eq. (
6
).
After applying FAA to the predicted label, the adjusted anomaly-class score is calculated as the sum of anomaly-class scores:

(6)

\displaystyle FAA(\bm{\hat{y}}^{(t)}_{i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\frac{1}{T-L+1}\sum_{j=L}^{T}\bm{\hat{y}}^{(j)}_{i}>\delta$},\\
\bm{\hat{y}}^{(t)}_{i}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(7)

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{CE}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{K}FAA(\bm{\hat{y}}^{(t)}_{i}),

where pseudo-anomalies detected above the threshold
\delta
across the entire test set are considered normal (we set
\delta=0.05
by default).

3.6.3.
Total score

Our anomaly score is calculated by adding the two scores together after applying min-max normalization to them:

(8)

\displaystyle\bm{s}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm MinMax}\limits_{T}(\bm{s}_{MSE}(t))+\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm MinMax}\limits_{T}(\bm{s}_{CE}(t)).

4.1.3.
Evaluation metrics

It is argued that the traditional metric for TSAD, namely the F1 score computed using the point adjustment (PA) protocol, overestimates model performance
(
Kim et al. 2022
;
Sarfraz et al. 2024
)
.
To ensure a more comprehensive evaluation, we adopt various threshold-independent metrics.
In particular, we employ five range-based measures designed to provide a robust and reliable assessment for TSAD:

(
Tatbul et al. 2018
)
, Range-AUC-ROC, Range-AUC-PR, volume under the surface (VUS)-ROC, and VUS-PR
(

Of these, VUS-PR is identified as the most reliable and accurate metric
(
Liu and Paparrizos 2024
)
.
For Range-AUC-ROC and Range-AUC-PR, we define the buffer region as half the window size.
Additionally, for UCR, we use accuracy as an evaluation metric, where an anomaly is considered detected if the highest anomaly score falls within the given anomaly range.

5.
Conclusion

We proposed the first multiclass anomaly assumption-based method,
RedLamp
, for unsupervised TSAD.
Our method overcomes the issues associated with binary anomaly assumption-based approaches: the diversity gap, false anomalies, and overconfidence in labels.
The core idea of
RedLamp
is to learn a multiclass boundary using multiclass pseudo-anomalies generated by diverse data augmentations.
The use of multiclass pseudo-anomalies prevents the diversity gap from occurring.
To mitigate the negative impact of anomaly contamination and false anomalies, we conduct multiclass classification via soft labels, preventing the model from being overconfident but ensuring its robustness against them.
Extensive experiments on real-world datasets demonstrated the effectiveness of
RedLamp
and its robustness against anomaly contamination.
We also showed that the learned latent space is explainable and provides insight into the real anomalies.
Future work will include the analysis of multivariate time series anomalies and the involvement of data augmentations suitable for multivariate time series.
We believe our work lays the foundation for a new approach to TSAD that exploits data augmentation techniques.

Appendix B
Baselines

We detail the baselines below.

•

IsolationForest
(
Liu et al. 2008
)
: constructs binary trees based on random space splitting.
The nodes with shorter path lengths to the root are more likely to be anomalies.

•

OCSVM
(
Schölkopf et al. 1999
)
: uses the smallest possible hypersphere to encompass instances.
The distance from the hypersphere to data located outside the hypersphere is used to determine whether or not the data are anomalous.

•

USAD
(
Audibert et al. 2020
)
: presents an autoencoder architecture whose adversarial-style learning is also inspired by GAN.

•

TranAD
(
Tuli et al. 2022
)
: is a Transformer-based model that uses attention-based sequence encoders to perform inferences with broader temporal trends, focusing on score-based self-conditioning for robust multi-modal feature extraction and adversarial training.

•

LSTM-VAE
(
Park et al. 2018
)
: is an LSTM-based VAE capturing complex temporal dependencies that models the data-generating process from the latent space to the observed space, which is trained using variational techniques.

•

BeatGAN
(
Zhou et al. 2019
)
: is based on a GAN framework where reconstructions produced by the generator are regularized by the discriminator instead of fixed reconstruction loss functions.

•

AT
(
Xu et al. 2022
)
: combines series and prior association to make anomalies distinctive.

•

IMDiffusion
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
: integrates the imputation method with a grating masking strategy and utilizes diffusion models to generate masked sequences.

•

D3R
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
: tackles the drift via decomposition and reconstruction, overcoming the limitation of the local sliding window.

•

NCAD
(
Carmona et al. 2022
)
: adapts OE, point outlier, and window mixup to generate pseudo-anomalies and then applies the hypersphere classifier discerning boundary between the normal and anomaly classes.

•

COUTA
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
: creates pseudo-anomalies and conducts classifications. It adaptively penalizes potential anomalies in the training set that have uncertain predictions to tackle anomaly contamination.

•

CutAddPaste
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
: focuses on generating diverse time series-specific anomalies. It then applies a TCN-based binary classifier to learn the decision boundary.

The baselines are implemented based on previously reported hyperparameters.
The batch size is 16, and the window size is 100.
Similar to
RedLamp
, we use
10\%
of the training samples as validation and apply early stopping.
While some methods, such as AT, introduce their own anomaly scoring mechanisms, we use reconstruction error as the anomaly score because it yields better accuracy across the board.
This is because of the inappropriate metrics used in their experiments.
To handle sequential information, we apply the moving average of half of the window size (50 timesteps) to the anomaly score.

Architecture.

Our architecture is based on SREA

.
The encoder is composed of 4 convolutional blocks, subsequently a maxpooling layer and a 1D-convolution layer.

The classifier includes a linear layer, batch normalization, ReLU activation, a dropout layer, a linear layer, and a softmax function.

Appendix F
Hyperparamter Sensitivity

Fig.
9
shows the effect of (a)
\alpha
, (b)

, and (c)

Effect of misclassified probability.

\alpha
represents the probability that a pseudo-anomaly is classified as normal,
and
\beta
denotes the probability that one class is misclassified as another.
We vary
\alpha
across
\{0.0,\dots 0.2\}
, and
\beta
across
\{0.0,\dots 0.04\}
.
The results in Fig.
9
(a) and (b) show that setting both misclassified probabilities is essential and probabilities that are too large or small limit the performance.

Effect of loss weight.

Fig.
9
(c) shows the results of varying the loss weight
\gamma
, which controls a balance between the reconstruction and cross-entropy loss.
We varied
\gamma
across
\{0.0,\dots 1.0\}
, where
\gamma=0.0
means the model is trained using solely
\mathcal{L}_{MSE}
and
\gamma=1.0
means it is trained using solely
\mathcal{L}_{CE}
.
This emphasizes the importance of utilizing both loss functions to achieve optimal detection performance.
