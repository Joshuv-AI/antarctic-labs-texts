Observations about left atrial tachycardias: Broadening our understanding
Authors: Steven M. Markowitz
Published: 2026

The techniques and outcomes of catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) have been well described for several decades. In contrast, less is known about the best approaches for patients who initially present for ablation with organized atypical flutters. Many of these patients also have AF, but in a substantial proportion atrial tachycardia (AT) is the only known arrhythmia. Such cases of so-called de novo atypical flutter present challenges for the clinician. For example, should an ablation procedure target only the presenting arrhythmia, or are more extensive lesion sets required to achieve optimal long-term outcomes? What is the appropriate endpoint for ablation of atypical flutters?

In recent years, efforts have been made to standardize the nomenclature of these arrhythmias, to replace the term “atypical flutter” with non–cavotricuspid isthmus AT.1 Subtypes of AT include macroreentry, localized reentry (which includes small circuit reentry and microreentry), and nonreentrant mechanisms, which differ in electrophysiological characteristics, dimensions, and mapping features. Because the term “atypical flutter” is still widely found in the literature, it will be used in this commentary to refer to organized atrial arrhythmias not involving the cavotricuspid isthmus.

Most of the experience with ablation of atypical flutters comes from patients who have had previous atrial interventions, such as catheter ablation or surgery.2 Many series that report locations and approaches for atypical flutter include a few patients without previous interventions, and a few cohorts are dedicated specifically to describing de novo atypical flutters.3, 4, 5, 6 The preponderance of evidence suggests that such patients have significant atrial myopathies and often develop recurrent atrial arrhythmias after ablation, such as other atrial circuits and AF.

In this issue of Heart Rhythm O2, Spahiu et al7 compared 32 patients who presented with de novo left atrial flutter (LAF) with 115 who had previous atrial ablation. They found useful distinctions between these groups, particularly with regard to tachycardia mechanisms. From a demographic standpoint, those with de novo LAF were older and more often female. The most interesting distinction was that patients with de novo LAF were more likely to have localized reentry (44%), and the preponderant location for these was a circuit in the anterior left atrium (LA). Other types of LAF were perimitral reentry (31%) and roof-dependent reentry (16%). Approximately 28% of these patients had more than 1 LAF mechanism including dual-loop reentry. The authors did not provide strict definitions for localized reentry and macroreentry, as were recently proposed in a consensus document, which recommended a circuit diameter of <2 cm to identify localized reentry.1 Regardless of this definition, the locations described in this paper distinguish reentry confined to 1 location (such as the anterior wall) from those involving multiple segments. The ablation approach was consistent with well-established techniques to treat atrial flutter, by targeting the critical isthmuses identified by activation, entrainment, and voltage mapping and ablating between unexcitable barriers. In addition, nearly all the patients in the de novo group had pulmonary vein isolation, except for 2 who had microreentrant mechanisms.

In patients who had previous ablation, perimitral reentry was the dominant mechanism, followed by roof-dependent reentry and anterior wall entry. Importantly, anterior wall reentry was strongly associated with previous linear ablation in the anteroseptal LA, highlighting the proarrhythmic effect of such linear ablation with radiofrequency energy. A major challenge of ablating in this location is the difficulty of creating durable transmural lesions because of the presence of Bachmann’s bundle, the septopulmonary bundle, and septoatrial bundles.8

It is worth noting that 72% of patients with de novo LAF also had a history of AF. Thus, most of this group might not be considered to have “pure” or isolated LAF, but this represents the real-world characteristics of such patients. Therefore, it is understandable that pulmonary vein isolation would be incorporated as an ablation target in such patients. There is a growing consensus that ablation in most patients with de novo LAF should involve not only treatment of a critical isthmus but also additional substrate ablation, particularly pulmonary vein isolation, as was done in this study.1,9 The rationale for these extended lesion sets is to minimize recurrences of AF but also to anchor linear lesions to a conduction barrier. Less commonly, patients with small localized circuits without extensive scar probably do not need more ablation beyond the targeted area during an initial procedure.

Several observations from this study support and extend findings that have been reported in other cohorts of de novo atypical flutter. In particular, a predisposition to voltage abnormalities and reentry in the anterior LA has been identified in other series.3,5,10,11 It is not clear why the anterior LA has a predisposition to develop fibrosis and discontinuous conduction, which promotes reentry. Some have postulated that these changes result from physical pressure exerted by the aortic root on the anterior LA.11 Particular effects of regional hemodynamics, myofiber composition, and neurohormonal sensitivities have not been well studied.

Long-term outcomes after ablation of de novo LAF show relatively high rates of recurrent atrial arrhythmias. In the present study, approximately half of patients had recurrent ATs or AF at 12 months after the index procedure, with comparable overall rates in the de novo and postablation groups. The de novo patients tended to have recurrent ATs rather than AF, whereas the postablation patients had both mechanisms with equal frequency. In the few patients who had remapping procedures, the recurrent arrhythmias were deemed to arise from other locations. The fact that index arrhythmias tended not to recur is a testament to the rigorous endpoint of conduction block pursued in the ablations. The diverse mechanisms of recurrent arrhythmias highlight that atypical flutter is a sign of a progressive atrial myopathy. It is not surprising that this would be the case because most patients in this group also had a history of AF, but similar observations about recurrences have been shown in patients with isolated flutter without a history of AF.5 A relevant question is whether the procedure endpoint should be satisfied with ablation of the presenting arrhythmia or whether complete procedural success should be pursued, defined by prevention of all inducible ATs.1

This study and others emphasize the continuum between AF and organized atrial flutters. It would be very interesting to investigate why some patients present with AF and others with organized AT. What allows patients to sustain complex and variable rotational activity characteristic of AF, whereas others anchor in consistent circuits? Do patients differ in the extent and location of atrial fibrosis? Do functional properties, such as restitution of conduction or refractoriness, play a role in determining whether a particular patient develops AF or AT? Answers to such questions will not only inform practical approaches to treating patients with atrial flutter but also provide further insights into the pathogenesis of AF and atrial flutter.
