Effects of surface finishing procedure, 3D printer type, and accelerated UV-aging on the surface roughness, translucency parameter, and color change of a 3D-printed permanent photopolymer resin
Authors: Özge Mine Yüceer, Ezgi Karaca, Beyza Güney, Sina Saygılı, Nagehan Aktaş, Merve Bankoğlu Güngör
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe objective of this study was to assess the effects of surface finishing procedure, 3D printer type, and accelerated UV-aging on the surface roughness (Ra), translucency parameter (TP00), and color change (ΔE00) of a 3D-printed permanent photopolymer resin.MethodsSpecimens (1 mm thick and A2 color) were fabricated using a permanent photopolymer resin and three 3D printers (Prusa Medical One, Form4B, and Asiga Max UV). Two surface finishing procedures (mechanical polishing and glazing) were applied to the specimen surfaces. UV-aging was applied using repeated cycles of light exposure and water spray at controlled temperatures for a total of 300 h. The Ra values and color coordinates were measured before and after accelerated UV-aging. The TP00 and ΔE00 values were calculated. The ΔE00 data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA, and the Ra and TP00 data were analyzed using three-way repeated-measures ANOVA. Pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni adjustment (α = 0.05).ResultsSurface roughness (Ra) was significantly influenced by the printer type, surface finishing procedure, and aging (P < 0.05). Mechanically polished groups showed higher Ra values, although all remained below the clinical threshold. TP00 values were affected by the printer type, surface finishing procedure, and UV-aging; mechanically polished specimens showed higher TP00 values and minor aging-related differences than glazed specimens in Medical One and Form4B groups. ΔE00 was influenced by both printer type and surface finishing, with all groups exhibiting ΔE00 values above the clinical acceptability threshold. However, the glazed Form4B group showed the smallest color change, indicating greater color stability.ConclusionsThe surface finishing procedure, 3D printer type, and accelerated UV-aging significantly influence the surface and optical properties of the tested 3D-printable permanent photopolymer resin.

Introduction

The development of subtractive and additive computer-aided design (CAD) and computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) systems has advanced digital dentistry by reducing restoration errors and improving predictability [1, 2]. While subtractive manufacturing (milling) is widely used, its limitations, such as material waste, restricted production, difficulty with complex geometries, and risk of crack formation, have increased interest in additive manufacturing (3D printing) [3, 4]. Photopolymer resins have traditionally been used for provisional restorations; however, they have been recently introduced for 3D-printed permanent restorations [5]. One of these permanent materials is VarseoSmile TriniQ (Bego, Bremen, Germany), which can be fabricated using stereolithography (SLA), digital light processing (DLP), and liquid crystal display (LCD) 3D printing technologies, offering enhanced mechanical strength and favorable optical properties for long-term clinical performance [6]. However, the printing technology, printing parameters, and post-processing procedures affect the surface, mechanical, and optical properties of the 3D-printed resins [7, 8], and there is limited evidence on the performance of this newly developed 3D-printed resin.

The surface characteristics and optical stability influence the clinical performance of 3D-printed permanent fixed restorations [9, 10]. 3D printing produces restorations layer by layer, often resulting in relatively rough surfaces that can negatively affect both the biological and esthetic compatibility of the restoration [9, 11]. Low surface roughness (Ra < 0.2 μm) produces more uniform, directional reflections, resulting in a more natural, esthetically pleasing appearance [12]. This threshold is also critical from a biological standpoint, as surfaces with Ra > 0.2 μm have been associated with increased bacterial adhesion and biofilm accumulation [13]. Furthermore, surface roughness facilitates the absorption and adherence of chromogenic agents found in common drinks. Consequently, inadequate polishing and surface finishing increase color change values, particularly compromising the color stability of photopolymer resins [14, 15]. Color changes of restorative materials are generally determined using the CIELab and CIEDE2000 formulas [16]. The CIEDE2000 formula aligns closely with how the human eye perceives color differences, making it particularly valuable for evaluating shades of both teeth and restorative materials. With established perceptibility and clinical acceptability thresholds of 0.8 and 1.8 [17], it has become increasingly common in color analysis. In addition to color, the translucency parameter (TP00) of restorative materials can change over time, affecting the esthetic outcome of the restorations. Translucency differences are evaluated using perceptibility (0.62) and acceptability (2.62) thresholds [18].

3D-printed restorative materials are constantly exposed to temperature fluctuations, moisture, and chemical agents in the oral environment, making them susceptible to structural and esthetic alterations. To replicate these long-term effects under laboratory conditions, artificial aging protocols have been developed [19, 20]. Storage in distilled water or staining solutions such as coffee and wine, microbial interaction in artificial saliva, immersion in water at 37 °C, and repeated thermocycling were used as aging protocols [21–23]. However, dental restoratives are constantly exposed to light, temperature changes, and mouth moisture. Accelerated ultraviolet (UV) aging encompasses these variables, reproducing the long-term effects of oral conditions in a shorter timeframe. Continuous exposure to UV light can induce photo-degradation processes, including oxidation and polymer chain breakdown, which are particularly important in resin-based materials and can impair their surface quality, color stability, and mechanical performance [24, 25]. Nonetheless, there is limited data on the combined effects of surface finishing procedures, 3D printer type, and accelerated UV-aging on the surface roughness, translucency parameter, and color change of the 3D-printed permanent photopolymer resins. Thus, the aim of the present study was to investigate the effects of surface-finishing procedure, 3D printer type, and accelerated UV-aging on the surface roughness (Ra), translucency parameter (TP00), and color change (ΔE00) of a 3D-printed permanent photopolymer resin. The null hypotheses of the study were that: (1) surface finishing procedure, 3D printer type, and accelerated UV-aging would have no effect on the surface roughness (Ra), (2) surface finishing procedure, 3D printer type, and accelerated UV-aging would have no effect on the translucency parameter (TP00), and (3) surface finishing procedure and 3D printer type would have no effect on the color change (ΔE00) of the tested photopolymer resin after accelerated UV-aging.

Materials and methods

Sample size calculation

A power analysis was performed using GPower (version 3.1.9.7, University of Kiel, Kiel, Germany). A repeated-measures ANOVA model was selected to evaluate Ra and TP00 values across two time points (before and after aging), two surface finishing procedures (mechanical polishing and glazing), and three 3D printers. With a significance level of α = 0.05, a desired statistical power of 0.90, an effect size of 0.40, and an assumed correlation coefficient of 0.5 between repeated measures, six specimens per group were required (total n = 36). For ΔE00, a two-way ANOVA was selected with two surface finishing procedures and three 3D printers, yielding six groups. With a significance level of α = 0.05, a desired power of 0.90, and an effect size of 0.40, 83 specimens (∼13.83 per group) were required. To meet and exceed the targeted power for all tested parameters (Ra, TP00, and ΔE00), the sample size was 15 per group (total n = 90). The experimental workflow is summarized in Fig. 1.

Experimental workflow

Specimen preparation

The specimens were designed in Tinkercad (Autodesk Inc., San Rafael, CA, USA) with standardized dimensions of 12 mm in diameter and 1 mm in thickness. The specimens were produced from an A2 colored, ceramic-filled, acrylate-based hybrid photopolymer permanent crown resin (VarseoSmile TriniQ, Bego, Bremen, Germany) using three different 3D printers. The properties of the 3D printers, as well as the curing and post-processing procedures of the tested 3D-printed resin, are presented in Table 1.

A total of 90 specimens were prepared, with 30 specimens fabricated for each of the three 3D printers. Each group was further divided into two subgroups based on surface-finishing procedures (mechanical polishing and glazing), yielding six experimental groups (n = 15 per group).

For mechanical polishing, polishing rubbers (G&Z Instrumente GmbH, CPD 14, Lustenau, Austria) were applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure standardized conditions. To minimize potential operator-dependent variability, all polishing procedures were performed by the same researcher. A low-speed micromotor (Strong Micromotor; Saeshin Precision Co., Daegu, South Korea) operating at 5,000 rpm was used under dry conditions throughout the process. Light, consistent pressure was applied to ensure a uniform surface finish and prevent surface damage during polishing. Specimens were ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 10 min and then dried before glazing. A ceramic primer (G-Multi Primer; GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was uniformly applied to the restoration surfaces before glaze application. A polymerizable resin-based glaze in clear color (Optiglaze; GC Corp, Tokyo, Japan) was then applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The glaze was carefully applied with a fine brush as a thin, homogeneous layer, ensuring no air bubbles were incorporated. Following application, the coated surfaces were polymerized for 40 s using a light-curing unit (Valo Cordless, Ultradent Products, IL, USA). All specimens were stored in a dry, lightproof container until further testing and were ultrasonically cleaned and dried before all measurements. The surface roughness (Ra) and color coordinates (L*, a*, and b*) of the specimens were measured before and after accelerated UV-aging.

Measurement of average surface roughness (Ra)

The average surface roughness (Ra) of all specimens was measured using a contact stylus profilometer (Marsurf M 300 C, MarSurf RD 18 C, stylus PHT 6–350/2 µm, Mahr GmbH; Göttingen, Germany) at a speed of 0.5 mm/s and a travel distance of 1.75 mm. Before measurements of each group, the device was calibrated using a calibration plate (PRN-10; Mahr GmbH, Göttingen, Germany). Measurements were performed three times in three different directions, and the mean Ra value was calculated for each specimen. The Ra values were compared with the clinically acceptable threshold value of 0.2 μm [13].

Analysis of Color Change (ΔE00) and Translucency Parameter (TP00)

Color coordinates (L*, a*, and b*) were measured at two steps: (1) after specimen preparation (baseline), and (2) following accelerated UV-aging. All measurements were performed using a spectrophotometer (Konica Minolta 2300d; Konica Minolta Inc., Tokyo, Japan) under standardized operating conditions. The spectrophotometer was calibrated using the manufacturer-provided white calibration plate before the measurements of each group. The device was set to the D65 standard illuminant, diffuse/8° measurement geometry, a 10° standard observer angle, and operated in the specular component-excluded (SCE) mode, with an 8-mm-diameter measurement aperture. For each specimen, three measurements were taken, and the mean color coordinates (L*, a*, and b*) were recorded.

Accelerated UV-aging

Accelerated UV-aging was performed in an aging test chamber (Atlas UV 2000, Material Testing Technology LLC, USA). The test device was programmed to operate in cycles consisting of 40 min of light exposure at 37 °C, 20 min of light combined with water spray, 60 min of light exposure alone, and 60 min of water spray in the dark, for a total of 300 h. The duration of this aging protocol was determined in accordance with ISO 4892-3 [30], which simulates aging for 12 months (1 year) [31, 32].

Statistical analyses

The Ra, TP00, and ΔE00 data were analyzed using SPSS 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of all data was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Ra and TP00 data were analyzed using three-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). The aging was defined as a within-subjects variable, while surface finishing procedures and printer types were defined as between-subjects factors. The ΔE00 data were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA, with surface finishing procedure and printer type as independent variables. Pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni adjustment. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

The three-way repeated-measures ANOVA results showed that there was no interaction among the surface roughness (Ra) data of the printer type, surface finishing procedure, and aging factors (P > 0.05). However, aging significantly increased the Ra values (P < 0.05). Furthermore, tests of between-subject effects revealed an interaction between the surface roughness data of the finishing procedure and printer factors (P < 0.05). The mean Ra of the mechanically polished specimens was found to be higher than that of the glazed specimens. Among the mechanically polished groups, the Asiga produced significantly smoother surfaces than the Medical One and Form4B (P < 0.05). In contrast, within the glazing groups, Medical One exhibited significantly lower surface roughness than both Form4B and Asiga (P < 0.05). However, all Ra values remained well below the clinical threshold of 0.2 μm, indicating that both surface finishing methods provided adequately smooth surfaces.

Before and after aging, mechanically polished specimens exhibited higher TP00 values than glazed specimens within each printer type (P < 0.05). Within each finishing procedure and aging group, Asiga consistently presented significantly lower TP00 values than both Medical One and Form4B (P < 0.05). Medical One and Form4B showed comparable TP00 values in the mechanically polished groups both before and after aging and in the glazing group before aging (P > 0.05); however, a significant difference emerged after aging in the glazing group (P < 0.05). All groups showed a significant decrease in TP00 following aging (P < 0.05). Although the decrease in TP00 varied among groups, the changes were statistically significant within each printer-surface finishing combination. Glazed specimens exhibited greater TP00 reduction compared with mechanically polished specimens in Medical One and Form4B groups. However, ΔTP00 values were in acceptable limits in all groups.

Discussion

The effects of surface finishing procedure, 3D printer type, and UV-accelerated aging on the surface roughness (Ra), translucency parameter (TP00), and color change (ΔE00) of a 3D-printed permanent photopolymer resin were investigated in the present study. The surface-finishing procedure, 3D-printer type, and accelerated UV-aging significantly influenced the Ra and TP00 of the tested photopolymer resin. The findings also demonstrated that both the surface finishing procedure and the type of 3D printer significantly affected color change after accelerated UV-aging. Thus, based on the results of the study, all null hypotheses were rejected.

The 3D printing technology, printer type, and surface finishing procedure of the restoration play essential roles among the factors affecting the discoloration of 3D-printed resins during intraoral use [23, 33]. Differences in light distribution, printing orientation, and curing efficiency across systems may lead to variations in translucency, color stability, and surface topography. Consequently, even when the same resin is used, the restorations can display noticeably different optical outcomes, depending on the printing technology and printing parameters [34, 35]. In the present study, one type of photopolymer resin was used to fabricate specimens with three different 3D printers: Prusa Medical One (LCD), Formlabs Form4B (LCD), and Asiga Max UV (DLP). Thus, the study was designed to compare two LCD-based printers with a third DLP-based printing system. In addition to printing parameters, post-processing conditions, particularly the cleaning procedure, may also influence the surface characteristics of the printed resin. Since the manufacturer provides multiple validated cleaning protocols without defining a fixed printer-washing unit combination, a standardized washing protocol was applied in this study to avoid an additional variable [36]. It should also be noted that each printer system in the present study was operated using its manufacturer-recommended post-curing protocol. Therefore, the observed differences among groups may reflect not only the influence of the printer technology itself but also the combined effects of printer-specific post-processing and final curing conditions. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as outcomes of complete printer workflows rather than purely isolated printer effects.

Photopolymer resins may exhibit changes in both their optical and mechanical properties during UV-aging. Prolonged exposure to UV light can induce polymer chain degradation or additional cross-linking, potentially leading to discoloration, reduced translucency, and increased surface roughness [24]. In the present study, an accelerated UV-aging protocol of 300 h was applied, corresponding approximately to 1 year of clinical service [32].

The Ra results showed that accelerated UV-aging led to a minor increase in the Ra values; the changes remained minimal, indicating that both finishing procedures provided stable surface characteristics under UV-aging conditions. Surface finishing had a more pronounced effect on the Ra than the type of printer used. The slight variation in the Ra values among printers may be related to differences in printing resolution and light-curing mechanisms. Glazing produced significantly smoother surfaces than mechanical polishing, allowing the glaze layer to fill surface irregularities and create a uniform, protective layer [33]. When compared with the clinically acceptable surface roughness threshold of 0.2 μm [13], all experimental groups remained well within this range both before and after accelerated UV-aging. This finding indicated that the tested 3D-printed resin maintained a clinically acceptable Ra value regardless of printer type, surface-finishing procedure, or aging conditions.

The TP00 outcomes obtained in the present study align with previous studies evaluating milled and 3D-printed resin-based restorative materials. Vichi et al. [37] reported a TP00 range of 8.96 to 15.75 for 1-mm-thick CAD-CAM and 3D-printed permanent composites, with Formlabs Permanent Crown Resin exhibiting a TP00 value of 12.52. Similarly, Espinar et al. [27] documented TP00 values ranging from 12.80 to 14.36 for the same material, depending on the printing angle and shade selection, while Lassila et al. [8] reported TP values between 11.0 and 11.6 for 2-mm-thick VarseoSmile Crown Plus specimens. The translucency values reported in the present study fall within this previously established range, supporting the material- and thickness-dependent nature of optical performance in 3D-printed resins. Before aging, the mechanically polished groups showed higher TP00 values than glazed specimens, regardless of the printer type. Lask et al. [33] similarly showed that certain surface treatments, particularly glazing or coating, modify the optical behavior of 3D-printed resins by altering their surface topography. In the present study, glazing consistently lowered TP00 values relative to mechanical polishing, likely due to the formation of a thin, less translucent surface layer that partially scatters light. Printer type also influenced the TP00. For all surface treatment groups, Asiga-printed specimens exhibited lower TP00 values, while Medical One and Form4B produced higher translucency, particularly after mechanical polishing. These differences corroborate Espinar et al. [27], who emphasized that material formulation, printing orientation, and printer-specific photopolymerization parameters affect optical outcomes. The variation observed across printers in the present study supported that light-curing characteristics, such as wavelength, irradiance uniformity, and exposure algorithms, play a significant role in resin polymer network formation, which ultimately determines translucency. The TP00 values of the tested 3D-printed resin decreased after UV-aging, indicating that UV exposure negatively impacted its optical stability. This reduction may be attributed to degradation of the polymer matrix, oxidation of surface components, and increased internal light scattering [38]. Although all groups showed a similar decrease, the extent of change varied depending on the printer type and surface finishing procedure. The Form4B specimens showed greater differences in translucency values than the Asiga and Medical One groups. Accelerated UV-aging significantly reduced TP00 values across all groups, with ΔTP00 ranging from − 0.82 to -1.52. However, the magnitude of change remained below the translucency acceptability threshold (TAT00 = 2.6) [27]. The findings suggested that the tested 3D-printed photopolymer resin maintained clinically acceptable translucency after UV-aging. The reduction observed in glazed groups may be attributed to the propagation of microcracks or partial degradation of the glaze layer during aging, which increases surface scattering [39]. Notably, mechanical polishing produced more stable translucency in LCD-based printer groups after aging, with ΔTP00 values remaining relatively small across printers (-1.09 to -1.24). Since polished surfaces lack an additional coating that is susceptible to degradation, they may age more uniformly, thereby maintaining their optical integrity. The results of the present study highlighted that both surface finishing procedure and printer type significantly influenced the TP00 values of the tested 3D-printed resin, while aging induced a moderate but clinically acceptable reduction in TP00. The findings emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate printing systems to achieve stable optical properties in 3D-printed photopolymer resins.

In the literature, the color stability of 3D-printed photopolymer resins has been compared with that of milled resin-matrix ceramics and conventional resin composites under various aging conditions. Koca et al. [40] assessed the long-term color stability of two 3D-printed resins (VarseoSmile Crown Plus, Crowntec), a milled resin nanoceramic (Cerasmart), and a conventional nanohybrid composite (Clearfil Majesty Esthetic) after daily coffee immersion for 30 days. Although early discoloration differed significantly, all materials showed similar color stability by day 30, with ΔE00 values exceeding clinical acceptability limits. Bozoğulları and Temizci [15] compared 3D-printed (Crowntec-DLP and Permanent Crown Resin-SLA) and milled (Cerasmart 270 and Vita Enamic) composites, and milled feldspathic ceramic (Vita Mark II) after thermocycling. Crowntec showed the highest discoloration (ΔE00) beyond clinical acceptability, whereas Permanent Crown Resin remained within acceptable limits and performed similarly to milled materials. Although surface roughness varied (lowest in Crowntec, highest in Vita Enamic), all materials stayed ≤ 0.2 μm with only slight, non-significant changes. These findings support the evidence that 3D-printed resins can achieve color stability comparable to that of CAD-CAM blocks and conventional composites, while also highlighting the need for further research on how factors such as printer type, post-curing protocols, and surface finishing methods affect the optical durability of printed restorations. In some studies [33, 36, 41], the color stability of 3D-printed resins with different surface treatments was compared after immersion in colorant liquids. The findings supported the need to implement appropriate surface finishing protocols, such as mechanical polishing or glazing, to maintain color stability and low surface roughness in 3D-printed restorations. However, the information on the effect of UV-aging on the color change of 3D-printed resins was limited. Perez et al. [23] investigated the impact of accelerated UV-aging on the color and optical properties of four resin-based restorative materials (Detax Freeprint Temp, Formlabs Temporary CB, Formlabs Permanent Crown, and GC TempPrint) produced using DLP and SLA 3D printing technologies (in light and medium shades at 0° and 90° of printing orientations). They found that all tested materials exhibited ΔE00 values above the clinical threshold, indicating unacceptable color changes after aging. Although the same resin was used across all groups in the present study, the results showed that both printer type and surface finishing procedure significantly influenced the color change of the tested 3D-printed resin. Among all experimental groups, the glazed Form4B group exhibited the lowest ΔE00 value (1.96 ± 0.46). This indicated that the glazing had a more protective effect on the color change of the Form4B group during accelerated UV-aging than in the other printers. Mechanical polishing generally resulted in comparable or slightly lower color changes in the Medical One and Asiga groups. Variations in printing technologies can explain the differences among printer types. Although both Form4B and Medical One use LCD technology, which cures each layer simultaneously with a uniform light source, differences in light distribution, UV intensity, and printing accuracy may lead to variations in polymerization efficiency. Form4B likely produced a more uniformly cured surface, which may explain its lower color change after glazing [23, 42]. In contrast, the Asiga uses DLP, in which each layer is cured with pixel-based light projection. Although DLP offers high accuracy, slight variations in light intensity and energy distribution can cause minor differences in polymerization and surface texture. The results indicated that the resin formulation did not solely determine the optical behavior of a 3D-printed restoration but was also highly dependent on the printing technology and subsequent surface treatment steps. Considering that a ΔE00 value of 1.8 represents the threshold for clinical acceptability [17], the color changes observed after UV-aging exceeded this threshold in all experimental groups, indicating perceptible, potentially clinically unacceptable color changes. This may be attributed to the accelerated UV-aging process of the tested photopolymer resin. UV-aging can cause photo-degradation of the organic matrix and of residual photo-initiators in the resin. During exposure to ultraviolet light, high-energy photons can break chemical bonds within the polymer network, leading to oxidation, chain scission, and structural changes in the resin matrix. These reactions often result in discoloration, surface roughening, and increased opacity. Additionally, unreacted monomers and photo-initiator residues may undergo further polymerization or degradation upon UV exposure, altering the material’s optical appearance [38]. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted in the context of the applied accelerated UV-aging conditions, which represent an intensified simulation of one year of intraoral exposure and may exaggerate color alterations compared to clinical reality. Furthermore, while the tested material is indicated for permanent restorations, its color stability may still be influenced by factors such as surface finishing procedures and printing techniques. Therefore, despite exceeding the clinical threshold, the material may remain clinically acceptable under intraoral conditions; however, further long-term clinical studies are required.

This study has several limitations. Only one type of permanent crown photopolymer resin (VarseoSmile TriniQ) was evaluated, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other 3D-printed permanent photopolymer resins. The specimens were fabricated using three 3D printers, each with its own printing technology and post-processing protocols. In addition, since each printer was used together with its corresponding manufacturer-recommended final curing protocol, the independent effects of printer technology and post-curing conditions could not be completely separated. Variations in curing unit type, exposure duration, wavelength, and temperature may have contributed to the differences observed among groups. Future studies should standardize post-curing conditions across different printer systems to better isolate the specific effect of printer technology. The use of flat, standardized, disk-shaped specimens, although advantageous for standardizing color-coordinate measurements and ensuring uniformity during UV-aging, does not fully replicate the anatomical geometry of clinical crowns. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and further studies using anatomically shaped restorations are recommended. Although the accelerated aging protocol simulated one year of clinical service, it cannot fully replicate the complex intraoral environment, including thermal fluctuations, moisture, pH variations, enzymatic activity, masticatory forces, mechanical wear, and long-term staining challenges. Longer aging periods that incorporate thermo-mechanical and chemical challenges, as well as clinical studies, are necessary to confirm the long-term surface and optical properties of 3D-printed photopolymer resins.
