Accuracy of recent intraocular lens power calculation methods in post-myopic LASIK eyes
Authors: Kate Gettinger, Sachiko Masui, Miki Omoto, Hidemasa Torii, Erisa Yotsukura, Yasuyo Nishi, Masahiko Ayaki, Ken Hayashi, Kazuno Negishi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This retrospective study compared postoperative prediction errors of recent formulas using standard- or total keratometry (K or TK) for intraocular lens (IOL) power calculation in post-myopic LASIK patients. It included 56 eyes of 56 patients who underwent uncomplicated cataract surgery, with at least 1-month follow-up at Keio University Hospital in Tokyo or Hayashi Eye Hospital in Yokohama, Japan. Prediction errors, absolute errors, and percentage of eyes with prediction errors within ± 0.25 D, ± 0.50 D, and ± 1.00 D were calculated using nine formulas: Barrett True-K, Barrett True-K TK, Haigis-L, Haigis TK, Pearl-DGS, Hoffer QST, Hoffer QST PK, EVO K, and EVO PK. Statistical comparisons utilized Friedman test, Conover’s all-pairs post-hoc, Cochran’s Q, and McNemar post-hoc testing. Root-Mean-Square Error (RMSE) was compared with heteroscedastic testing. Barrett True-K TK had the lowest median predicted refractive error (-0.01). EVO PK had the smallest median absolute error (0.20). EVO PK had the highest percentage of eyes within ± 0.25 D of the predicted value (58.9%), significantly better than Haigis-L (p = 0.047). EVO PK had the lowest mean RMSE value (0.499). The EVO PK formula yielded the most accurate IOL power calculation in post-myopic LASIK eyes, with TK/PK values enhancing accuracy.

Introduction

In cataract surgery, the primary aim is to remove the opacified lens and restore visual function. Modern surgical techniques now permit simultaneous refractive error correction with intraocular lens (IOL) implantation, and with this comes the need for accurate IOL power calculations. Previous studies have noted difficulties in achieving optimal post-surgical refractive status when using traditional IOL power formulas on post-refractive surgery patients1–3. As many of the traditional calculation formulas were based on assumptions of unmodified corneal measurements and structural properties, using these formulas on patients who have had LASIK can lead to “refractive surprise” after cataract surgery, resulting in unsatisfactory refractive error following lens implantation2. A recent report released by the American Academy of Ophthalmology found when using automated keratometry and IOL power theoretical formulas developed for eyes without previous corneal refractive surgeries, the mean predicted error in post-LASIK eyes trended hyperopic, with a large proportion falling outside 0.50 D of the target refractive power1.

Attempts have been made to establish more reliable IOL power calculation formulas for post-refractive surgery eyes, with some success4–11, but the accuracy of refractive prediction in post-refractive surgery eyes has not yet reached that in normal eyes. Accurate IOL power prediction also relies upon precise measurement of the corneal curvature. Traditionally, anterior corneal curvature measurements have been used to estimate corneal power using theoretical algorithms, but more accurate outcomes may be achieved if not relying solely on conventional K measurements. Recently, formulas using total keratometry (TK) measurements, which includes anterior and posterior corneal curvatures, as well as corneal thickness, have shown potential for providing more accurate IOL power calculations due to more accurate estimation of corneal refractive power. Additionally, some formulas utilize posterior keratometry (PK) measurements, which add the measurement of the curvature of the posterior side of the cornea to the base formula. Some studies have indicated using TK measurements over standard keratometry values can yield better refractive predictions in normal eyes12–14. There also have been some reports showing the accuracy of refractive prediction using TK in post-refractive surgery eyes6,15–18, however, more studies are needed to establish the advantage of using TK measurements. For example, in a study by Wang et al., the Haigis TK formula was shown to produce comparable estimates to the Haigis-L and Barrett True-K in patients with a history of corneal refractive surgery19. Notably, this study only considered the Haigis-L and Barrett True-K formulas with their TK counterparts. In contrast, our study compared several variations of modern formulas in addition to the Haigis-L and Barrett True-K formulas and we showed the Haigis-L performing notably worse than both the Haigis TK and Barrett True-K TK formulas, as well as compared to several other TK-utilizing formulas. Savini et al. found that utilizing total keratometry measurements produced the most accurate prediction option for the Barrett True-K formula, but failed to compare it to other formulas20. Additionally, neither of the above mentioned studies evaluated other modern post-myopic LASIK formulas like EVO K, Hoffer QST, and Pearl-DGS.

This study aimed to determine the accuracy of nine recent IOL power calculation formulas with conventional or total keratometry, including two formulas using machine-learning based prediction techniques, when used on post-myopic LASIK eyes.

Discussion

In this study, the accuracy of several IOL power calculation formulas, both with and without TK/PK measurements, and some utilizing machine-learning techniques, were compared on eyes that had previously undergone myopic LASIK surgery. A total of 56 eyes of 56 participants were included in the study, and all these eyes underwent an uncomplicated cataract surgery using IOLs with the same platform and completed a 1-month post-surgery follow-up.

Analysis of the data determined that the EVO PK formula yielded the most accurate overall results, with the lowest MAE and the largest percentage of eyes falling within ±0.25 D of the predicted refractive error. The Haigis TK and Barrett True-K had the next best prediction capability, with just shy of 50% of eyes ending within ±0.25 D of the predicted refractive error for each formula and the next lowest MAE values. When considering the RMSE, the EVO PK again had the lowest error value.

Most traditional IOL power calculation formulas’ inaccuracy in post-myopic LASIK patients stems from utilizing keratometry measurements that assume a consistent anterior and posterior corneal curvature ratio, which has obviously been modified in corneal refractive surgery, and from incorrectly predicting effective IOL position10,21. There have been several modified methods of IOL power calculation for post corneal refractive surgery eyes to solve this issue; however, the prediction errors have still remained insufficient5,8,10,22–24. As such, new formulas need to be developed that can account for post-LASIK and PRK eyes. The formulas included in this study are either formulas which try to specifically account for these modified corneal parameters resulting from corneal refractive surgery, or formulas which utilize artificial intelligence to achieve better accuracy. There is still debate, however, about which of these newly developed formulas is the most accurate in these situations7,25,26.

The Barrett True-K formula’s notable prediction accuracy has been demonstrated in several previous studies4,27. In a study by Ferguson et al., the Barrett True-K alone was shown to be as accurate as the American Society of Cataract and Refractive Surgery (ASCRS) average, a multi-formula average approach for post-refractive surgery eyes, and it was significantly more accurate when compared to the Haigis-L formula, which was the second-most accurate formula of those studied7. Similarly, the Barrett True-K performed well in our study, but the Haigis TK was more accurate than the Haigis-L. A study by Lanza et al., however, found that the Haigis-L gave the best overall predictions when compared to the Barrett True-K, EVO, Shammas, and the ASCRS average28. Other studies have similarly found the MAE of the Haigis-L to be highly accurate5. Our study showed relatively modest success with the Haigis-L, but other formulas were demonstratively better at predicting the IOL power. The Lanza et al. study also demonstrated that the prediction accuracy of all formulas degraded as axial length increased and corneal curvature flattened. In addition, it has been shown that some formulas may be more accurate for particular axial length measurements over others28,29. Within this study, the only case that had a corneal measurement less than 35D resulted in the extreme outlier prediction for the Haigis-L formula, indicating that the larger predicted error may have been due to the flat corneal curvature. This gives further indication of the need for careful consideration for IOL calculation formulas in post-myopic LASIK eyes.

In this study, using the TK/PK formula on post-myopic LASIK patients gave more accurate predictions when compared to the original version of the formula. These updated formulas were better able to predict IOL power in post-LASIK eyes than the traditional counterparts.

The formulas utilizing artificial intelligence algorithms yielded comparable results compared to traditional formulas. However, it became apparent that including the optional PK measurements notably improved the prediction capability of the Hoffer QST formula. There was not a statistically significant difference in the prediction accuracy of the Hoffer QST with optional PK values (Hoffer QST PK) compared to the Hoffer QST without PK values, but the overall absolute predicted error was notably improved with PK values. This suggests that even though it may be more time consuming, entering all available measurement data can be worthwhile in regards to the resulting prediction. As more studies are performed and additional data collected, improvement in prediction capability for post-myopic LASIK eyes may become even more pronounced when utilizing particular calculation formulas such as these.

This study had some notable limitations. The sample size was relatively small, with only 56 total eyes, all from a similar clinical setting. However, the authors believe the data was still meaningful considering similar published studies utilized similar sample sizes to this study30–32, and the recent recommendations in which a minimum sample size of 50 eyes for post-LASIK patient IOL studies, which the current study achieves33. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more expansive demographics would help confirm the findings and consistency of the predictions.

In conclusion, the EVO PK formula yielded the most accurate overall results, followed by the Haigis TK and Barrett True-K. In addition, the prediction accuracy of traditional theoretical IOL power calculation formulas for post-myopic LASIK eyes were improved by the use of TK/PK values, and artificial intelligence-based formulas improve when including optional measurement data. Utilizing TK/PK measurements for cataract surgeries on post-myopic LASIK eyes may help improve final refractive results and deliver more consistent, predictable outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study institutions and institutional review board approval

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional review board for human studies of the Keio University School of Medicine (approval No. 20180152). All patients provided written consent for the surgeries, however, for this retrospective study, patient consent to participate was waived. An opt-out approach was implemented in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects presented by the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology in Japan. This study abided by the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. There was no involvement of the patients or public in the design, management, or dissemination plans of the research.

Patients

The study participants were retrospectively recruited from patients who had received cataract surgery at Hayashi Eye Hospital and Keio University Hospital’s Department of Ophthalmology. Eligible patients included those who had previously received myopic LASIK surgery and underwent an uncomplicated IOL implantation and returned for follow-up at least one month after surgery. The cataract surgeries were performed by three surgeons at Keio University and four surgeons at Hayashi Eye Hospital. Consistency in surgical technique was maintained across the cohort: a 2.0 to 2.4-mm temporal clear corneal or superior limbal incision with phacoemulsification and IOL implantation in-the-bag under topical anesthesia.

Patients who underwent reoperation after surgery or who had ocular diseases other than cataract or systemic diseases were excluded from the study. When both eyes met the criteria for the study, the first treated eye was selected for inclusion. All patients provided written consent for the cataract surgeries. Patient consent to participate in this study was waived, and an opt-out approach was used according to the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects presented by the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology in Japan. The implanted IOL was either the TECNIS Monofocal clear (ZCB00) or yellow-tinted (ZCB00V) IOL, or the preloaded version of those IOL (DCB00/DCB00V) (all from Johnson and Johnson, Santa Ana, CA, USA). All the IOLs had the same IOL platform.

The sample size for a study of this nature was calculated and 130 observations were recommended. However, due to the difficulties in obtaining large numbers of patients that fit the study criteria, the sample size was determined according to the recent recommendations of a minimum sample size of 50 eyes for post-LASIK patient IOL studies, which the current study achieves33.

IOL power calculation

All patient biometric measurements were taken using the IOL Master700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) preoperatively. Using these parameters, the postoperative refraction was predicted using nine formulas: Barrett True-K, Barrett True-K TK, Haigis-L, Haigis TK, EVO K, EVO PK, Pearl-DGS, Hoffer QST, and Hoffer QST PK. The Barrett True-K formula is based on the Barrett Universal II formula34. It is a theoretical model that adjusts keratometry measurements for patients who have received LASIK or PRK and can be used even when no pre-operative refractive history is available to the surgeon4. The Haigis-L formula is another commonly used no-history formula for IOL power calculation35. The EVO (Emmetropia Verifying Optical) formula utilizes thick lens modelling to generate an “emmetropia factor” for each eye and calculate the appropriate IOL power36. The addition of posterior keratometry (PK) measurements distinguishes the EVO PK from the standard EVO K formula. The Pearl-DGS (Postoperative spherical Equivalent prediction using Artificial intelligence and Linear algorithms) is an open-source formula using artificial intelligence programming to attempt to improve IOL calculations36. The Hoffer QST is built upon the Hoffer Q formula and similarly utilizes machine-learning techniques to attempt better IOL power prediction37. The Hoffer QST formula allows for optional PK measurements to be included in the calculation. For this study, the predictions were made with and without these optional PK measurements to compare accuracy. PK is a measurement of the curvature of the posterior side of the cornea, and has been shown to be a notable contributor to total corneal astigmatism38. Past studies have indicated the significance of considering PK measurements when calculating IOL powers, but for many formulas it remains an optional component39. Within this report the formula calculations using PK values are designated as the Hoffer QST PK. For this study, IOL powers and refractive predictions were calculated with each formula, and the resulting estimates were compared through subsequent statistical analysis. The lens constants were set as 2.04(LF) and 4.0 (DF) for the Barrett True-K/TK, 119.3 for the EVO K/PK and Pearl-DGS formulas, -1.302 (A0), 0.210 (A1), and 0.251 (A2) for the Haigis-L/TK formulas, and 5.8 for the Hoffer QST formula. For the Barrett True-K/TK, Haigis-L/TK, and Hoffer QST, the constants were based on those found on the IOL Con Intraocular lens constant table. The EVO K/PK constant was based on that provided by the Emmetropia Verifying Optical Formula calculator36. The Pearl-DGS constant was based upon that provided by the IOL Solver calculator40.

The best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and subjective refractive error were measured at 1-month after cataract surgery. The mean prediction error was calculated by subtracting the predicted value with the IOL implant from the postoperative subjective spherical equivalent. The absolute prediction error was calculated as the absolute value of the prediction error. After determining the absolute prediction error, the percentage of eyes with a ± 0.25 D, ± 0.50 D, and ± 1.00 D predicted error was calculated for each formula.

Statistical analysis

Testing with Shapiro-Wilk normality test confirmed a non-normal distribution of the data. The median of the absolute value was chosen as the primary outcome. Using the Friedman test, the predicted and absolute prediction errors were compared between the formulas and further analyzed with Conover’s all-pairs post-hoc analysis with Holm adjustment. Cochran’s Q test with McNemar post-hoc testing and Holm adjustment was utilized to compare the percentages of eyes with ± 0.25 D, ± 0.50 D, and ± 1.00 D prediction error among the formulas. Root-mean-square error (RMSE) was calculated for predicted values and compared with heteroscedastic testing, as described by Holladay et al41. All analyses were performed using R software v.4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
