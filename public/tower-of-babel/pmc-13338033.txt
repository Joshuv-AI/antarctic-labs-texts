Radar-vision multimodal fusion for dynamic target trajectory prediction and threat assessment in power transmission corridors
Authors: Jun Zhang, Zhiwei Zhang, Xiao Tan, Jin Ling, Qing Jie Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPower transmission corridors face growing intrusion risks from unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), construction machinery, and wind-borne debris. Conventional single-sensor monitoring, however, covers less than 20% of corridor length and falls short of the sub-meter, real-time accuracy needed for rapid threat response. To close this gap, we present a radar-vision multimodal fusion framework that integrates and adapts established sensing and learning techniques into a coherent end-to-end pipeline tailored to the corridor protection domain. The framework makes three principal contributions at the system-integration level. First, a reliability-aware adaptive fusion strategy learns environment-dependent modality weights through sigmoid-gated quality indicators—signal-to-noise ratio, detection confidence, illumination variance, and a weather-condition index—enabling the network to shift reliance toward whichever sensor is more trustworthy rather than averaging degraded inputs. Second, a cross-modal attention module allows radar kinematic queries to interrogate vision semantic keys, uncovering complementary feature correspondences without manual supervision. Third, a category-specific hierarchical threat scorer combines distance, velocity, and trajectory-trend sub-scores with target-class multipliers and maps a continuous composite value onto five severity levels (Negligible, Low, Moderate, High, Critical) through field-calibrated thresholds. We validated the framework on 8,547 trajectory sequences collected over six months from twelve monitoring stations spanning suburban, mountainous, and agricultural terrains. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of predicted positions reached 1.62 m at a 3-second horizon and 3.21 m at 5 s—a 43% relative reduction compared with the radar-only baseline (2.84 m at 3 s). Threat-level classification achieved 89.6% precision, 87.3% recall, and an 88.4% macro-averaged F1 score across five classes, exceeding early-fusion and late-fusion alternatives by 7.4 and 9.8% points respectively. Deployment at three operational 500 kV / 220 kV sites detected 89 genuine threats with 17.8 s mean warning lead time, 3.2% false-alarm rate, and 99.4% system uptime. Introduction

Power transmission corridors are lifeline infrastructures for long-distance electrical energy transport. They encompass high-voltage lines, support towers, insulators, and 30–50 m safety buffer zones. These corridors form the backbone of modern grids, spanning hundreds of kilometers at 110 kV-1000 kV. A single 500 kV line serves millions of households; disruptions cascade through interconnected systems. Despite their criticality, corridors face increasing threats from UAVs, construction machinery, and airborne debris1.

Threat mechanisms vary but share catastrophic potential. UAVs pose collision risks severing conductors or damaging insulators, causing fires and multi-hour outages affecting 50,000 + customers with $200,000 + repair costs2. Construction machinery creates flashover conditions through close approach—electrical arcing occurs without contact, ionizing air gaps to carbonize insulation. Airborne debris (plastic sheeting, kites, metallicized balloons) causes phase bridging and short-circuit faults. Agricultural harvesting releases materials causing seasonal fault patterns. Each threat type exhibits distinct signatures: controllable drone trajectories, terrain-constrained machinery paths, wind-driven debris drift. All demand rapid detection for timely intervention.

Traditional monitoring relies on manual inspection with periodic visual surveys, supplemented by single-sensor systems3. This paradigm has fundamental limitations. Manual inspection provides only weekly/monthly coverage with temporal gaps for undetected intrusions. Single sensors face inherent constraints: radar measures range and velocity but struggles with classification; cameras provide visual detail but degrade under fog, rain, or darkness. Real-time response for fast-approaching targets exceeds manual monitoring capabilities. Accuracy requirements demand sub-meter precision that conventional approaches cannot deliver. These inadequacies motivate advanced multimodal sensing frameworks.

Radar and vision technologies have emerged as complementary sensing modalities for power transmission corridor monitoring. Radar systems provide all-weather detection capabilities and precise distance-velocity measurements, effectively tracking targets under adverse environmental conditions4. Vision systems offer rich texture and semantic information, enabling detailed target recognition and behavior analysis5. Recent studies have explored their integration in power system applications, demonstrating improved detection performance over individual sensors6. However, most existing implementations focus on simple data-level fusion or parallel processing, lacking sophisticated feature-level integration mechanisms that fully exploit the complementary characteristics of these modalities7.

Dynamic target trajectory prediction has received considerable attention in autonomous systems and intelligent surveillance domains. Model-based methods employing Kalman filtering and particle filtering have been widely applied but struggle with complex nonlinear motion patterns8. Deep learning approaches, particularly recurrent neural networks and attention mechanisms, have shown superior performance in capturing temporal dependencies and long-term motion trends9. In the context of power transmission corridors, several research efforts have investigated trajectory forecasting for aerial vehicles and ground equipment10. Nevertheless, these methods predominantly rely on single-modal data, underutilizing the spatial-temporal information available from multimodal sensors.

Threat assessment constitutes a crucial component of intelligent monitoring systems, enabling proactive risk management and decision support. Existing threat evaluation frameworks typically employ rule-based expert systems or fuzzy logic approaches, which depend heavily on manually designed features and predefined thresholds11. Machine learning-based methods have demonstrated improved adaptability but often treat threat assessment as a binary classification problem, providing insufficient granularity for operational decision-making12. The challenge of quantifying threat levels from heterogeneous sensor data while considering multiple risk factors remains inadequately addressed in current literature13.

The integration of radar and vision modalities for trajectory prediction and threat assessment in power transmission corridors presents several fundamental challenges. First, the heterogeneity in data characteristics, temporal resolution, and spatial reference frames complicates effective feature alignment and fusion. Second, the dynamic and unpredictable nature of target behaviors in complex environments demands robust prediction models capable of handling multimodal uncertainties. Third, comprehensive threat evaluation requires not only accurate trajectory forecasting but also contextual reasoning about proximity, speed, heading, and target type. These challenges necessitate innovative approaches that transcend conventional multimodal fusion paradigms14.

Against this backdrop, we put forward a radar-vision multimodal fusion framework for trajectory prediction and threat assessment in power transmission corridors. None of the individual techniques we employ—attention mechanisms, LSTM-based sequence modeling, sigmoid gating—are new in isolation. Our contribution lies in how these components are integrated, adapted, and validated for the specific demands of corridor protection, where sensor degradation is the norm rather than the exception and where operators require interpretable, graded alerts rather than opaque binary flags. The contributions of this work, each addressing a specific shortcoming identified in prior approaches, are outlined below.

First, unlike conventional fusion schemes that assign fixed or heuristic weights to each sensor channel, we integrate a reliability-aware adaptive fusion strategy in which sigmoid-gated quality indicators—radar SNR, detection confidence, illumination variance, and a weather-condition index—continuously modulate modality contributions. This mechanism lets the system shift reliance toward the more dependable sensor under fog, rain, or darkness rather than simply averaging degraded inputs.

Second, we adapt a cross-modal attention module to the radar-vision setting, where radar kinematic features serve as queries and vision semantic features serve as keys and values. Compared with early-fusion concatenation or late-fusion score averaging, this configuration discovers complementary correspondences between heterogeneous representations without hand-crafted alignment rules, reducing prediction error by 26.7% relative to early fusion at 5-second horizons.

Third, we develop a hierarchical, category-specific threat assessment framework that fuses distance, velocity, and trajectory-trend sub-scores with target-class multipliers and maps the resulting composite value onto five severity levels through field-calibrated thresholds. In contrast to binary threat classifiers reported in prior work12, this multi-level design delivers the graded situational awareness that field operators require for proportionate response—and, importantly, it remains fully interpretable and auditable, a property that power-grid regulators increasingly mandate for automated decision support.

Taken together, these three elements form an end-to-end pipeline from raw sensor input to actionable threat classification, validated on 8,547 real-world trajectory sequences and deployed at three operational monitoring sites over six months15.

Background and related technologies

Radar target detection and tracking technology

Radar systems extract target parameters by transmitting electromagnetic pulses and analyzing backscattered signals. They operate reliably under varying illumination and weather conditions16. Standard processing employs pulse compression, Doppler filtering, and coherent integration for enhanced sensitivity17. CFAR algorithms maintain consistent false alarm rates through adaptive thresholding18,19, while Kalman and particle filters estimate target states from sequential measurements20,21. For power corridor monitoring, radar delivers range, azimuth, elevation, velocity, and cross-section data for robust long-range tracking.

Visual target recognition and feature extraction technology

Vision systems convert optical signals into digital images preserving color, texture, and geometric details22. Deep learning detectors like YOLO and Faster R-CNN enable real-time recognition23,24, while feature extraction captures texture and motion for tracking25. Vision provides rich semantic context but degrades under poor lighting, rain, and fog, with shorter effective ranges than radar26.

Multimodal data fusion methods

Multimodal fusion seeks to combine heterogeneous sensor streams so that the resulting representation exceeds what any single modality can deliver alone27. The literature broadly groups fusion strategies into three tiers: data-level fusion, which concatenates raw measurements before any abstraction takes place; feature-level fusion, which merges learned intermediate representations; and decision-level fusion, which aggregates independent inference outputs28. Among these, feature-level approaches offer a pragmatic middle ground—they preserve richer information than decision-level methods while imposing lighter computational overhead than raw-data concatenation—and are therefore well suited to radar-vision integration where radar supplies kinematic state vectors and vision provides semantic classification cues.

In recent years, attention-based fusion has attracted considerable interest. Vaswani et al.29 introduced the scaled dot-product attention mechanism, which was originally designed for sequence-to-sequence language modeling but has since been widely adopted for cross-modal feature weighting. For instance, Liang et al.30 proposed a deep continuous fusion architecture that projects LiDAR bird-eye-view features into camera feature maps through continuous convolutions, demonstrating that spatially aware cross-modal attention substantially outperforms naive concatenation for 3-D object detection. Similarly, Chen et al.31 developed a multi-view 3-D detection network in which region-based fusion layers encode interactions between LiDAR point clouds and multi-view camera images, establishing an influential paradigm for heterogeneous sensor integration. More recently, Liu et al.32 presented BEVFusion, a unified bird-eye-view representation that fuses camera and LiDAR modalities in a shared spatial coordinate frame, achieving state-of-the-art results on the nuScenes benchmark with minimal latency overhead.

Trajectory prediction has likewise evolved rapidly. Classical model-based estimators—Kalman filters and their extended or unscented variants—remain effective for near-linear dynamics, yet they degrade when targets execute sharp turns, sudden stops, or erratic maneuvers8. Recurrent architectures, especially LSTMs33, capture longer-range temporal dependencies through gating mechanisms, and several studies have demonstrated their advantages for aircraft and vehicle path forecasting9. More recently, Transformer-based predictors have shown competitive results; Giuliari et al.34 applied a Transformer encoder-decoder to pedestrian trajectory prediction and found that self-attention over time steps naturally handles variable-length histories without the vanishing-gradient issues of recurrent cells. Yuan et al.35 introduced AgentFormer, a stochastic Transformer that models the joint distribution of multi-agent futures, capturing social interactions that recurrent baselines struggle to represent. In the specific context of power corridor monitoring, Zhao et al.10 demonstrated UAV tracking control strategies, while Li et al.6 explored visual matching for safety monitoring; however, neither study combined radar kinematic data with vision semantics in a learned fusion framework, leaving a gap that our work directly addresses.

Spatial-temporal alignment remains a foundational challenge when integrating sensors that operate in different coordinate frames and at disparate sampling rates. Coordinate transformation projects measurements into a common Cartesian space, while temporal interpolation resamples asynchronous observations onto a coherent time grid. Beyond fusion and prediction, threat assessment for critical infrastructure has received growing attention. Rule-based expert systems and fuzzy-logic classifiers11 remain common in operational deployments, yet they rely on hand-crafted features and fixed thresholds that generalize poorly across sites. Machine-learning approaches have improved adaptability, but many treat threat evaluation as a binary classification problem, sacrificing the granularity that operators need for graduated response12,13. Wang et al.37 proposed a probabilistic risk assessment framework for smart grid anomaly detection that integrates heterogeneous sensor streams, yet their work focused on static network faults rather than dynamic moving targets. Our hierarchical, category-specific scoring framework builds on these foundations while introducing target-class multipliers and field-calibrated severity thresholds designed for the moving-target domain.

Radar-vision fusion-based trajectory prediction and threat assessment model

This section presents our integrated framework addressing three core challenges: fusing heterogeneous radar-vision data, predicting target trajectories, and assessing threat levels. We first describe the overall architecture connecting five processing layers. Then we detail the multimodal fusion network with cross-modal attention. Finally, we explain the LSTM-based trajectory predictor and hierarchical threat assessment algorithm. Each component builds upon the previous, creating an end-to-end system from raw sensor input to actionable threat classification.

Overall model framework design

Our model processes multimodal sensor data through five hierarchical layers for end-to-end trajectory prediction and threat assessment38. Figure 1 shows the complete pipeline from raw data to threat classification. The five stages handle distinct tasks: data acquisition captures and synchronizes sensor streams; feature extraction transforms measurements into semantic representations; multimodal fusion integrates information through attention mechanisms; trajectory prediction forecasts future positions; threat assessment evaluates risk levels. This separation enables modular optimization while maintaining coherent information flow.

Overall architecture of the radar-vision fusion-based trajectory prediction and threat assessment model.

Figure 1 depicts the five-layer architecture. From bottom to top, the data acquisition layer synchronizes radar and camera streams at 20 Hz; the feature extraction layer encodes radar kinematics via a three-layer MLP and visual semantics via ResNet-50, each producing 512-dimensional vectors; the fusion layer applies cross-modal attention with sigmoid-gated adaptive weighting; the trajectory prediction layer forecasts future positions through a stacked LSTM; and the threat assessment layer maps composite risk scores onto five severity levels to guide graduated operator responses. Detailed descriptions of each layer follow in Sect. 3.2 and 3.3.

Radar systems continuously monitor the surveillance region with update rates of 10–20 Hz, capturing range, azimuth, elevation, and Doppler velocity for detected targets. Vision cameras operate at 25–30 frames per second, providing high-resolution imagery covering overlapping fields of view with radar coverage zones. Spatial-temporal registration modules within this layer perform coordinate transformation and time synchronization to align heterogeneous sensor data into unified reference frames39.

The feature extraction layer processes aligned multimodal data through parallel processing channels optimized for each modality. Radar feature extractors compute kinematic parameters, target trajectories, and motion patterns from sequential measurements, while vision feature extractors employ deep convolutional networks to generate semantic embeddings, appearance descriptors, and target classification probabilities. This layer outputs fixed-dimensional feature vectors encapsulating complementary information from both modalities, preparing data for subsequent fusion operations.

The multimodal fusion layer integrates radar and vision features through an adaptive attention-based mechanism that dynamically weights modality contributions according to environmental conditions and sensor reliability. Cross-modal correlation modules identify corresponding targets across different sensor streams, resolving potential ambiguities through joint analysis of spatial positions, motion characteristics, and appearance attributes. The fusion layer generates enriched feature representations that leverage radar’s precise kinematic measurements and vision’s rich semantic information40.

The trajectory prediction layer employs a temporal encoder-decoder architecture with long short-term memory networks to model target motion dynamics from fused multimodal features. Historical trajectory sequences spanning 3–5 s serve as input, enabling the prediction of future positions over 5–10 s horizons. Attention mechanisms within this layer selectively focus on relevant temporal patterns and multimodal cues, adapting predictions to diverse target behaviors including uniform motion, acceleration, and turning maneuvers.

The threat assessment layer evaluates predicted trajectories against multiple risk criteria—proximity to transmission lines, approach velocity, heading angles, and target classification—and maps weighted composite scores onto five severity levels whose thresholds and alert actions are detailed in Sect. 3.3 (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6)41.

Trajectory prediction and threat assessment algorithm

Why use LSTM instead of simpler methods? Target behaviors in power corridors are nonlinear and unpredictable33. Constant-velocity extrapolation and Kalman filtering work for straight-line motion but fail when drones turn sharply, vehicles decelerate, or debris tumbles erratically. Our 256-dimensional fused features contain three semantic categories: spatial position, kinematic trends, and classification probabilities. Each 3-second sequence provides 60 frames at 20 Hz—rich temporal structure that simple rules cannot exploit. LSTM gating mechanisms selectively preserve relevant history while forgetting noise. Ablation studies (Sect. 4.2) confirm this: replacing LSTM with linear regression increases 5-second error by 127%; Kalman filtering degrades accuracy by 89%.

We considered—and ultimately rejected—replacing the threshold stage with a learned classifier, for three reasons grounded in the operational realities of power-grid monitoring. First, regulatory and operational norms in the power sector increasingly demand that automated alert systems produce decisions traceable to interpretable physical quantities; a black-box classifier that outputs a threat label without transparent reasoning would face resistance from both grid operators and safety auditors. Second, high-severity events (Levels 3 and 4) are inherently rare in our dataset—they account for fewer than 6% of all labeled sequences—creating a severe class imbalance that degrades learned classifiers precisely for the categories where misclassification is most dangerous. During preliminary experiments, a three-layer MLP trained to predict threat levels directly from fused features achieved only 71.3% recall on Level 4 threats, compared with 94.2% under our threshold scheme, because the limited number of critical-event training samples was insufficient for reliable generalization. Third, threshold tables can be updated on site—when a new line configuration is commissioned or safety regulations change—without retraining the neural network, a practical necessity given that retraining requires GPU resources and validated data that field stations do not always have available. We acknowledge that a learned threat model could, in principle, capture nonlinear interactions among risk dimensions that fixed weights miss, and we discuss this as a concrete future research direction in Section VI.

Experiments and results analysis

Experimental environment and dataset

The experimental platform comprises a workstation equipped with dual NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs (24GB memory each), Intel Xeon Platinum 8358 processor (32 cores), and 256GB DDR4 RAM to support parallel processing of multimodal data streams. The software environment utilizes PyTorch 2.0 as the deep learning framework, CUDA 12.1 for GPU acceleration, and Python 3.10 for implementation49. OpenCV 4.8 handles vision processing operations, while NumPy and SciPy facilitate numerical computations and signal processing tasks.

We collected field data from 500 kV and 220 kV transmission lines across diverse conditions over six months. Twelve monitoring stations in suburban, mountainous, and agricultural regions captured synchronized radar-vision measurements. Each station used 77 GHz millimeter-wave radar (200 m range) and 4 K camera (90° FOV) mounted 25–45 m high, covering 150–200 m along corridors. Evaluation metrics quantify model performance across detection, prediction, and assessment tasks. Precision measures the proportion of correctly identified threats among all predicted threats, while recall quantifies the fraction of actual threats successfully detected. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) evaluates trajectory prediction accuracy by computing average Euclidean distance between predicted and ground truth positions across all time steps and sequences. Threat classification performance employs macro-averaged F1 scores across all threat levels to account for class imbalance. Processing latency metrics assess real-time feasibility by measuring end-to-end inference time from sensor input to threat assessment output.

Multi-target tracking metrics quantify identity consistency beyond detection accuracy. MOTA integrates false positives, false negatives, and identity switches51: MOTA = 1 - (FP + FN + IDSW) / GT. MOTP measures spatial accuracy via IoU between predicted and ground truth boxes51,52. IDSW counts incorrect label swaps during occlusions. These metrics capture tracking performance that precision/recall cannot—high detection accuracy may coexist with identity confusion degrading threat assessment utility.

Three mechanisms account for the accuracy gains observed in Table 3. The first, and most consequential, is the cross-modal attention module. By letting radar queries interrogate vision keys, the network discovers which semantic cues—object silhouette, orientation, articulated-part configuration—best disambiguate kinematic trajectories that raw Doppler alone cannot resolve. Removing this module inflated 3-second MAE from 1.62 m to 1.97 m, a 21.6% degradation that isolates the attention contribution. The second mechanism is adaptive weight learning: sigmoid-gated reliability scores suppress noisy modality channels before they contaminate the fused representation, trimming a further 13.4% from prediction error. The third is the stacked LSTM temporal encoder, whose gating cells selectively retain relevant motion history—velocity inflection points, curvature onset—while discarding sensor jitter that otherwise accumulates across autoregressive decoding steps.

Discussion

What accounts for the performance gains reported in Section IV? The answer is not that combining two data sources is generically better than one—naive concatenation (early fusion) also combines both modalities yet trails our method by 7.4 F1 points. Rather, the gains stem from a conditional routing of information. Radar anchors the spatial state of each target with range-rate measurements that remain stable under rain, dust, and darkness, while vision contributes texture, silhouette, and color cues essential for distinguishing, for instance, a hovering drone from a similarly sized bird at comparable range. The sigmoid-gated fusion learns when to trust which channel, re-weighting modalities on a per-frame basis. This conditional behavior is most visible during fog-to-rain transitions, where vision confidence drops abruptly and the gate must shift reliance within two to three frames to prevent trajectory drift. A fixed-weight scheme cannot react to such transient degradation, which explains why our degradation ratio under adverse conditions (0.071) is roughly one-third that of early fusion (0.194).

Inspecting cross-modal attention weights across the test set yields a finding that, in our view, carries implications beyond the immediate application. When a target accelerates or executes a sharp turn, the attention module assigns higher weight to radar velocity features—Doppler shifts encode instantaneous kinematics more faithfully than optical flow estimated from consecutive frames. During slow, multi-target scenes, such as two bird flocks crossing paths near a tower, the module shifts toward vision, exploiting appearance differences to maintain identity assignment. What strikes us as noteworthy is that this context-dependent routing emerges entirely from data-driven training with no hand-coded rules specifying which modality to trust under which condition. The network has, in effect, learned an implicit model of sensor trustworthiness conditioned on scene dynamics. This observation suggests a broader principle for heterogeneous sensor fusion: rather than prescribing fixed fusion rules based on engineering intuition, allowing the attention mechanism to discover modality preferences from data can yield more robust and generalizable routing policies. Whether this finding transfers to other sensor combinations—say, thermal-infrared paired with LiDAR—remains an open question, but the attention weight distributions we observe provide an empirical starting point for such investigations.

Category-wise analysis provides additional insight. UAV trajectories were predicted most accurately (1.41 m MAE at 3 s), likely because drones follow relatively smooth, pilot-controlled paths. Airborne debris, by contrast, exhibited the highest errors (2.37 m), reflecting wind-driven chaotic drift that the LSTM struggles to anticipate beyond two seconds. Construction machinery fell in between (1.58 m), benefiting from terrain-constrained motion yet occasionally confounded by articulated boom movements that resemble rapid approach toward conductors. These differences underscore the value of category-specific threat thresholds (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6): applying uniform thresholds across all target types would either over-alert on birds or under-alert on drones, neither of which is acceptable in practice.

Field deployment revealed key operational considerations:

Calibration

Multimodal fusion requires millimeter-level spatial alignment. Our semi-automated protocol using corner reflectors takes 2–3 h per station. Calibration drift averages 1.2 cm/month at windy sites, degrading accuracy 8%. We recommend quarterly recalibration, monthly for exposed locations.

Maintenance

Camera lenses accumulate debris causing 15–25% contrast reduction within two weeks. Heated enclosures eliminate 90% of condensation outages. Radar radomes require ice/snow removal—5 mm accumulation reduced range 22%. Lightning damaged two cameras; 120 km/h winds damaged one antenna.

Environmental limitations

Coastal deployment showed 15% false alarms from sea clutter until site-specific maps were implemented. Dense fog (< 50 m visibility) degraded vision 73%. EMI near substations required frequency selection and shielding. The framework generalizes within temperate environments; maritime/arctic/desert deployment needs dataset augmentation. Power (280 W/station) and bandwidth (4.2 Mbps) requirements constrain remote installations.

System reliability

Software exceptions occurred every ~ 48 h initially; watchdog timers achieving 15-second recovery enabled 99.4% uptime. Sensor failures required redundant installations. Network outages (23 incidents, 15–90 min each) were handled via backup cellular channels for local alerts. Practical deployment requires fault management, redundancy, and fallback communications.

Trajectory prediction errors originate from multiple sources including measurement noise, modeling assumptions, and inherent motion unpredictability. Quantization errors in sensor measurements introduce position uncertainty that accumulates through recursive prediction. The LSTM architecture assumes smooth, continuous motion patterns and struggles with abrupt maneuvers or erratic target behaviors that violate learned dynamics. Prediction uncertainty grows quadratically with forecast horizon as compound errors accumulate through autoregressive generation. Future improvements could incorporate uncertainty quantification through probabilistic prediction frameworks, enabling explicit representation of forecast confidence that adapts to motion complexity.

The hierarchical threat assessment framework delivers reasonable risk stratification in practice, but its threshold-based architecture brings trade-offs that deserve candid discussion. Fixed weight coefficients (, , ) were calibrated on our field data and may not transfer optimally to transmission lines with different voltage classes, span lengths, or terrain profiles. Distance-based metrics assume a uniform 30 m safety buffer, yet critical components—mid-span conductor sag points, insulator strings, damper locations—may warrant larger protective zones than standard tower segments.

A deeper limitation concerns what the threshold model cannot capture. Because the sub-scores combine linearly, the framework misses potential nonlinear interactions—for example, a slow-moving target on a direct collision course may pose greater risk than a fast target on a tangential path, yet linear weighting cannot fully distinguish these scenarios without manual threshold tuning. A learned threat classifier could, in theory, discover such interactions from data. As reported in Sect. 3.3, our preliminary comparison with a learned MLP classifier confirmed that the threshold design achieves substantially higher recall on rare high-severity events, which we consider non-negotiable in safety-critical infrastructure monitoring. The underlying cause—severe class imbalance in high-severity samples—is structural rather than incidental, and would require dedicated mitigation strategies (focal loss, cost-sensitive training, or synthetic augmentation) before a learned scorer could match threshold-based recall at the critical end of the severity spectrum. We view a hybrid architecture, in which a small neural network predicts residual corrections to the analytical sub-scores while preserving the interpretable threshold mapping for final severity assignment, as a promising path forward. We elaborate on this prospect in Section VI.

The model generalizes reasonably well across the three deployment locations, which share similar geographic and climatic characteristics—a result we attribute to the stratified training protocol and the adaptive fusion mechanism’s ability to compensate for site-specific sensor variability. However, preliminary coastal trials exposed an 18% performance degradation from sea clutter and salt-spray lens fouling, conditions absent from our inland training data. This sensitivity to distribution shift underscores a practical constraint: successful deployment at a new site presupposes either that the site falls within the training distribution or that site-specific fine-tuning data are collected beforehand. Additional operational prerequisites include careful sensor calibration, continuous power and network connectivity, and computational hardware at or above the Jetson AGX Orin class.

Optimization directions for future research include expanding training data diversity to improve generalization, incorporating physics-based motion models to constrain predictions, implementing online adaptation mechanisms for dynamic threshold adjustment, and developing lightweight model variants for resource-constrained deployment. Enhanced uncertainty quantification would enable risk-aware decision making, while integration of additional sensing modalities such as infrared or lidar could further improve robustness. Advanced data association algorithms could reduce identity switches in crowded scenarios, and hierarchical attention mechanisms might better capture multi-scale spatial-temporal patterns. These enhancements would strengthen the model’s applicability across broader operational contexts while maintaining real-time performance requirements.

The following paragraphs address two dimensions—security and reliability—that are essential for any operational infrastructure monitoring system but that we had not fully elaborated in the initial manuscript.

Regarding system security, three layers of protection are implemented. At the communication layer, all sensor-to-edge and edge-to-control-center data streams are encrypted with TLS 1.3, and each monitoring station authenticates via device-specific X.509 certificates to prevent spoofing. At the data-integrity layer, every transmitted frame carries a SHA-256 hash that the receiving node verifies before ingestion; corrupted or tampered frames are discarded and flagged for operator review. At the adversarial-robustness layer, we conducted preliminary testing with gradient-based perturbations (FGSM with ε = 0.03) applied to vision inputs. The adaptive fusion mechanism partially mitigated these attacks by automatically reducing vision weight when perturbed frames triggered anomalously low confidence scores, limiting F1 degradation to 4.2%—roughly half the degradation observed in the fixed-weight early-fusion baseline. We acknowledge, however, that dedicated adversarial defenses (e.g., adversarial training, input purification) have not yet been integrated and remain a priority for future hardening.

Regarding system reliability, our six-month deployment revealed several concrete findings. Mean time between software exceptions was initially 48 h; after deploying watchdog timers with 15-second automatic recovery cycles, effective uptime reached 99.4%. Hardware redundancy proved essential—two of the twelve stations experienced camera failures due to lightning, and one lost a radar antenna to sustained 120 km/h winds. In all three cases, the surviving sensor continued to operate in single-modal fallback mode, producing degraded but non-zero situational awareness until maintenance crews replaced the failed component (mean repair time: 36 h). Network connectivity, another reliability concern, suffered 23 outages averaging 42 min each; backup 4G LTE channels ensured that local threat alerts reached operators within the contractual 30-second latency even during primary link failures. Overall, we view the 99.4% uptime as adequate for supplementary monitoring but insufficient for safety-critical standalone operation; achieving the latter would require N + 1 sensor redundancy at every station and dual independent processing pipelines, both of which add cost that must be weighed against the value of the protected asset.

Conclusion

This study addressed the problem of detecting, predicting, and assessing dynamic threats—UAVs, construction machinery, birds, balloons, kites, and airborne debris—in high-voltage power transmission corridors, where conventional single-sensor monitoring left more than 80% of corridor length effectively unobserved. We developed a radar-vision multimodal fusion framework whose contribution resides not in inventing fundamentally new algorithmic primitives but in integrating, adapting, and rigorously validating established techniques into a cohesive pipeline purpose-built for the corridor protection domain. Three elements define this integration. First, a reliability-aware adaptive fusion strategy that learns per-frame modality weights from sigmoid-gated sensor quality indicators, shifting reliance toward the more trustworthy channel under fog, rain, or darkness instead of averaging degraded inputs. Second, a single-head cross-modal attention module in which radar kinematic queries interrogate vision semantic keys, allowing the network to discover complementary feature correspondences without hand-crafted alignment rules. Third, a hierarchical, category-specific threat assessment scheme—deliberately kept threshold-based for interpretability and on-site updatability—that combines distance, velocity, and trajectory-trend sub-scores with target-class multipliers and maps the resulting composite value onto five severity levels through field-calibrated thresholds.

Experimental evaluation on 8,547 field-collected trajectory sequences demonstrated that the proposed model achieved 1.62 m MAE at 3-second prediction horizons and 3.21 m at 5 s, representing a 43% relative reduction over the radar-only baseline. Threat classification reached 89.6% precision, 87.3% recall, and 88.4% macro-averaged F1 across five levels, surpassing early-fusion and late-fusion alternatives by 7.4 and 9.8% points respectively. Six-month deployment at three operational 500 kV / 220 kV monitoring sites confirmed real-world viability: the system detected 89 operator-confirmed threat incidents with 17.8-second mean warning lead time and 3.2% false-alarm rate while maintaining 99.4% uptime on edge hardware consuming 45 W per station. Operator workload, measured as person-hours of continuous manual surveillance, fell by 67%, and instrumented detection coverage expanded from 18% to 94% of corridor length.

Among the three integrated components, adaptive environment-dependent modality weighting proved most consequential in practice: it kept the degradation ratio under adverse conditions at 0.071, roughly one-third that of fixed-weight early fusion (0.194). The cross-modal attention mechanism automatically routed information according to context—prioritizing radar during rapid maneuvers and vision during multi-target disambiguation—without explicit supervisory signals. The threshold-based hierarchical threat framework, while not a learned model, provided the graduated and interpretable situational awareness that field operators required for proportionate response, addressing a practical gap left by prior binary-classification approaches.

Several limitations deserve candid acknowledgment. The most pressing is simultaneous dual-modality degradation: when dense fog (< 50 m visibility) coincides with heavy rain, both radar clutter and camera obscuration intensify at the same time, leaving the adaptive fusion mechanism with no reliable anchor. In our test set, such overlapping extreme conditions occurred in only 3% of sequences, but they accounted for 28% of all misclassified threats. A second limitation is distributional sensitivity—coastal deployment trials revealed 18% performance degradation from sea clutter and salt-spray lens fouling, conditions absent from our inland training data. Third, the 37.1 M-parameter model requires at least a Jetson AGX Orin-class GPU for real-time inference; sub-$100 platforms like Jetson Nano reach only 18 FPS after INT8 quantization, with a measurable accuracy penalty (MAE rising from 1.62 m to 1.89 m). Finally, the threat assessment weights (w_D, w_V, w_T) and class multipliers (γ_class) are currently fixed; different transmission-line configurations—a 1000 kV ultra-high-voltage line versus a 110 kV distribution feeder—may justify very different risk trade-offs that static coefficients cannot capture.

Looking ahead, we identify five concrete research directions, each paired with a specific technical approach. First, to address dual-modality degradation, we plan to integrate a thermal infrared channel as a third sensing modality; because mid-wave infrared penetrates fog more effectively than visible light and is unaffected by radar clutter, it can serve as a tiebreaker when both primary modalities weaken. The immediate next step is to deploy FLIR Boson 640 cameras at two existing stations and retrain the fusion network with a three-branch attention module. Second, to improve generalization across climates, we intend to adopt domain-adaptation techniques—specifically, adversarial feature alignment between inland and coastal feature distributions—so that models trained on one geography transfer to another with minimal site-specific fine-tuning. Third, to make the threat assessment weights context-sensitive rather than fixed, we will explore a meta-learning formulation in which line-specific parameters (voltage class, span length, minimum ground clearance) condition a small hypernetwork that outputs optimal , , , and for each installation. Fourth, we plan to investigate a hybrid learned-plus-threshold threat scoring architecture, in which a small neural network predicts residual corrections to the analytical sub-scores while preserving the interpretable threshold mapping for final severity assignment. Such a design could capture nonlinear interactions among distance, velocity, and trajectory trend that fixed linear weights miss, without sacrificing the auditability that power-grid operators require. Addressing the class imbalance inherent in high-severity events—through focal loss, cost-sensitive training, or synthetic data augmentation—will be a necessary component of this effort. Fifth, to enable deployment on ultra-low-cost hardware, we plan to apply structured pruning combined with knowledge distillation from the full 37.1 M-parameter teacher to a compact 4 M-parameter student network, targeting 30 + FPS on Raspberry Pi 5 with Coral TPU while maintaining MAE below 2.0 m.
