Proteomic analysis of Burkitt lymphoma tissues: insights into the impact of age and EBV infection on protein expression and molecular characteristics
Authors: Yu Huang, Shuang Zheng, Xinyi Huang, Shuqi Li, Wenhui Zhang, Man Shu, Qinghua Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aim to investigate the molecular heterogeneity of Burkitt lymphoma (BL) by analyzing the impact of age and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection on protein expression in tumor tissues. Using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), we performed proteomic analysis on 10 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) BL tissue samples (5 adults, 5 pediatric; stratified by EBV status) alongside clinical data from 273 BL patients. Clinically, pediatric patients showed higher lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels and advanced tumor stages (P < 0.05), while EBV-positive cases exhibited unique metastatic potential. Key findings revealed significant age-related disparities: adult BL patients exhibited 343 differentially expressed proteins (208 up-regulated, 135 down-regulated) compared to pediatric cases, predominantly enriched in energy metabolism (e.g., PFKM, PGM2) and oxidative stress response pathways (e.g., SOD1, IDH1). EBV-positive BL demonstrated distinct molecular signatures, with 176 differentially expressed proteins (93 up-regulated, 83 down-regulated) linked to purine metabolism (e.g., IMPDH2), apoptosis (e.g., MAPK13, BID), autophagy pathway (e.g., ATG3) and cytoskeletal remodeling (e.g., ARHGAP9, SCRIB). Our results provide novel insights into BL pathogenesis, emphasizing the interplay of age and EBV in shaping molecular landscapes. This study underscores the need for multi-omics integration to advance precision therapy strategies for BL subtypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03968-0.Keywords: Burkitt lymphoma, EBV infection, Age, Proteomics, Precision therapy

Introduction

Burkitt lymphoma (BL) is a highly aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Based on clinical and epidemiological features, it is classified into three subtypes: endemic, sporadic, and immunodeficiency-associated [1]. In China, sporadic BL is more common, mainly affecting children and adolescents, with a preference for the ileocecal region, often presenting as an abdominal mass [2]. Although significant progress has been achieved in comprehending the pathogenesis of BL, its exact molecular mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated.

Comprehensive analyses of whole-genome and transcriptomic data have indicated that EBV-positive tumors exhibit significantly elevated somatic hypermutations throughout the genome, along with fewer driver mutations, especially in genes related to apoptosis [3–5]. These findings highlight that the EBV status of tumors is a crucial determinant of specific BL phenotypes, molecular characteristics, and pathogenic mechanisms, regardless of geographical origin [6–8]. Based on these findings, the 2022 World Health Organization Classification of Tumours of Haematopoietic and Lymphoid Tissues (WHO-HAEM), 5th edition, introduced a classification system that divides BL into EBV-positive and EBV-negative subtypes. This revision further emphasizes the central role of EBV in BL pathogenesis and provides a foundation for targeted clinical research and therapeutic strategies. However, most existing BL studies rely on genomic and transcriptomic data or integrate cellular and plasma proteomic data. There is still a lack of research on the proteomics of tumor tissue samples. This study fills this gap by analyzing the proteomic differences between EBV-positive and EBV-negative BL.

Previous studies have revealed that BL patients in different age groups present distinct molecular characteristics [9]. The currently used BL International Prognostic Index (BL-IPI) incorporates four factors: age, disease stage, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, and central nervous system (CNS) involvement. Among these, age is recognized as a critical factor influencing prognosis [10]. Despite evidence of age-related impacts, the molecular regulatory networks associated with age remain poorly understood.

This study employs proteomic technologies to uncover molecular disparities between EBV-positive and EBV-negative BL and also makes comparisons between pediatric and adult groups. It is anticipated to provide a theoretical basis for understanding the pathogenesis of BL and offer novel directions for individualized clinical management.

Materials and methods

Patient specimens

Clinical and pathological data of BL patients diagnosed at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat - sen University from January 2004 to December 2024 were collected. Pathological specimens were derived from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues archived in the hospital’s pathology department. All samples underwent diagnostic review by pathologists via hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemical staining, in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) 2022 classification criteria for hematopoietic and lymphoid tumors. A total of 273 cases were enrolled for the analysis of clinicopathological features, and 10 tumor tissue samples were finally selected for proteomics analysis, comprising 5 adult cases (3 EBV-positive and 2 EBV-negative) and 5 pediatric cases (2 EBV-positive and 3 EBV-negative). EBV infection status was confirmed by EBER in situ hybridization.

EBER in situ hybridization (ISH)

EBER ISH was performed on 4-µm-thick (FFPE) tissue sections using an automated staining system (BOND-III, Leica Biosystems). The assay was carried out employing a commercially available EBER ISH kit (MaxVision Biosciences, Fuzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, after deparaffinization and epitope retrieval, sections were hybridized with a digoxigenin-labeled EBER probe. The hybridization signal was subsequently detected using an anti-digoxigenin antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase and visualized with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB), yielding a brown precipitate.

A known EBV-positive nasopharyngeal carcinoma tissue section was included in each run as a positive control. EBER positivity was defined by the presence of a distinct brown nuclear staining pattern within tumor cells. Any cytoplasmic or nonspecific staining was disregarded.

Protein extraction and digestion from FFPE tissue sections

Ten-micrometer-thick tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene and transferred to microcentrifuge tubes. Proteins were extracted using a lysis buffer consisting of 1% sodium deoxycholate (SDC) in 50 mM TEAB, pH 8.5, supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck Millipore). Tissues were disrupted by ultrasonication (5 cycles of 30 s on/30 s off at 80% amplitude). After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of proteins were adjusted to consistent volume with lysis buffer, reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol at 56 °C for 30 min, and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich) in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Reduced and alkylated samples were diluted with 50 mM TEAB to a final concentration of 4 M urea. Trypsin digestion was performed at 37 °C at a 1:50 (w/w) enzyme-to-protein ratio overnight followed by a second digestion at a 1:100 ratio for 4 h. Peptides were desalted using Strata X SPE columns and their concentration was quantified by UV absorbance [11, 12].

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis

After dissolving the peptides in the mobile phase of liquid chromatography, the Vanquish Neo UHPLC system was applied for separation. Then, the separated peptides were injected into the NSI ion source for ionization and subsequently analyzed by the Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. The data dependent acquisition (DDA) program was utilized for data collection. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pride/) partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD068020.

Database search

The raw MS/MS data were processed using Proteome Discoverer software (version 2.4). Searches were performed against a concatenated database consisting of the Homo sapiens reference proteome (UniProtKB, version dated January 2023, 20,389 entries) and a reverse decoy sequence database supplemented with common contaminants. Trypsin was specified as the protease, allowing up to two missed cleavages. The minimum peptide length was set to 6 amino acids, and a maximum of 3 modifications per peptide were permitted. The mass tolerance for precursor ions was set to 10 ppm, and for fragment ions, it was 0.02 Da. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification. Variable modifications included oxidation of methionine, N-terminal acetylation, methionine loss, and methionine loss with acetylation. False discovery rate (FDR) thresholds were set to < 1% at the protein, peptide, and PSM levels.

For quantification, label-free quantification (LFQ) was applied using the Minora feature detector in Proteome Discoverer. Normalization was performed based on total peptide amount. Missing values were handled by imputation from a normal distribution. Differential expression analysis was conducted using a t-test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons, and proteins with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |fold change| >1.5 were considered significant.

Protein annotation methods

Gene Ontology (GO) Annotation: The identified proteins were processed by the eggnog mapper software. Based on the EggNOG database (http://eggnog5.embl.de/#/app/home), the GO ID of each protein was extracted. Subsequently, the proteins were functionally classified and annotated according to cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes.

Protein Domain Annotation: Using the Pfam database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/entry/pfam/#table) and the corresponding PfamScan tool, the identified proteins were annotated for their protein domains.

The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Pathway Annotation: The proteins were annotatedusing KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/kaas/). The identified proteins were subjected to BLAST alignment. For the BLAST alignment results of each sequence, the alignment result with the highest score was selected for annotation.

Transcription Factor Annotation: Through the protein gene information obtained from the above annotations, in reference to the public databases TRRUST (https://www.grnpedia.org/trrust/) and GTRD (http://gtrd.biouml.org/) which are based on transcription factors and target genes, the transcription factor information corresponding to the proteins was annotated.

Protein functional enrichment

The KEGG database was used for pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed proteins. Fisher’s exact test was applied to assess the significance of pathway enrichment for differentially expressed proteins (using all identified proteins as the background), with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. GO annotations of proteins were classified into three main categories: biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions. Similarly, Fisher’s exact test was used for GO enrichment analysis, with P < 0.05 defined as significant.

Enrichment-based clustering

Functional enrichment clustering analysis of differentially expressed proteins across different groups was performed to investigate potential functional connections and differences in specific categories (e.g. GO terms, KEGG pathways). This involved: (1) collecting functional classification information and corresponding enrichment P values for all protein groups; (2) filtering functional categories that were significantly enriched in at least one group (P < 0.05); (3) transforming the P value matrix using a − log₁₀ transformation; and (4) performing one-sided hierarchical clustering on the transformed data using Euclidean distance and average linkage clustering. Clustering relationships were visualized as heatmaps using the Heatmap function from the R package ComplexHeatmap.

PPI network

To obtain protein-protein interaction (PPI) information, the interactive genetic database search tool STRING was utilized. The PPI network was constructed by inputting differentially expressed protein information into the String database (https://cn.string-db.org/), selecting the Homo sapiens species, and removing disconnected nodes in the network.

Results

Comprehensive overview of patients’ clinical and proteomic information

Among the 273 cases, 159 were adults (58.24%, aged > 18) and 114 were children (41.76%, aged ≤ 18) (Table 1). Clinical data analysis revealed significant differences (P < 0.05) between adults and pediatric in LDH level, tumor stage, and gender. However, no significant differences were observed in hemoglobin (HB), albumin (ALB), ECOG score, B symptoms, sites involved, or EBV infection status. Further EBV testing in 86 patients identified 30 EBV-positive (34.88%) and 56 EBV-negative (65.12%) cases (Supplementary Table 1), with B symptoms being the only significantly differing clinical parameter between these two groups. Proteomic analysis was subsequently performed on tumor tissue sections from 10 cases of BL patients.

Comprehensive overview of patients’ clinical information

Six distinct pairwise comparisons were performed based on patients’ age and EBV infection status to assess differential protein expression (Table 2). Notably, the most significant differences were found between adult and pediatric EBV-negative patients, with 857 differentially expressed proteins identified. K-means clustering was applied to analyze the expression levels of differentially expressed proteins (ANOVA P-value < 0.05) in four groups (Fig. 1a). The results revealed significant differences in enriched proteins of different ages and EBV infection statuses. Further KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that in EBV-negative pediatric (Fig. 1b), the differentially expressed proteins mainly affect the core cellular processes, such as the Spliceosome, mRNA surveillance pathway, and Ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes. For EBV-positive pediatric patients, the immune system and inflammation-related mechanisms are activated, and energy metabolism is also adjusted to respond to the infection. The differentially expressed proteins in these patients are enriched in pathways like Epstein-Barr virus infection, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, Neutrophil extracellular trap formation, Phagosome, Leukocyte transendothelial migration, IL−17 signaling pathway, Transcriptional misregulation in cancer, Citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and Oxidative phosphorylation. For adult patients, the differentially expressed proteins in EBV-negative cases were mainly enriched in pathways like NOD−like receptor signaling pathway and C−type lectin receptor signaling pathway. In EBV-positive adults, they were enriched in pathways such as Epstein-Barr virus infection, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, Ubiquitin mediated proteolysis, HIF−1 signaling pathway, PI3K−Akt signaling pathway, and Nucleotide metabolism. These results indicate that age and EBV infection status have a profound and specific impact on protein expression, leading to unique protein expression profiles in each group.

Adult versus pediatric BL

Previous research has highlighted that molecular characteristics and prognosis vary in BL patients of different ages [13]. By comparing the protein expression profiles of adult and pediatric BL patients, 343 differentially expressed proteins were identified, of which 208 were up-regulated and 135 down-regulated in adults (Table 2), which are showed in volcano plot (Fig. 2a).

Further analysis of the top 30 up-regulated and down-regulated proteins revealed that, compared to pediatric patients, the up-regulated proteins in adults were mainly involved in biological processes and signaling pathways such as energy metabolism (GPD1, GOT1, PGLS), gene expression regulation (ELOB, ADI, UBA1, TCEA1, ANP32B, RAD23A), stress response pathways (PPM1B, HSPH1, SOD1, SGTA), cytoskeleton regulation pathways (SLC9A3R1, TMSB4X, CUTA), and protein processing (JCHAIN, COG3, MRPL49, NPL, SH3BGRL, PARK7, MAT2B) (Fig. 2b). In contrast, the down-regulated proteins in adult BL patients were primarily involved in pathways such as gene expression regulation (TAF6, CBX8, H2BC1, H2BC21, ZC3H14, TAF15, SMARCA2, PBRM1, DHX40, SMG5), RNA metabolism and regulation (PPIG, RBM27, CDK13), cell cycle regulation (H3-7, SMARCA2, CACTIN, CENPV), and immune regulation (LILRB4, CUL7) (Fig. 2c).

Based on the identified differentially expressed proteins, a PPI network was constructed using the STRING database. Within the up-regulated protein group, key proteins such as AKT1, SOD1, TALDO1, IDH1, PFKM, and PGM2 were identified, occupying central positions in the PPI network (Fig. 2d). Extensive empirical research indicates that these proteins are involved in the mechanisms of various tumors, for example, SOD1, IDH1, and TALDO1 play crucial roles in protecting cells from oxidative damage [14–16], while PFKM and PGM2 are embedded in cellular energy metabolism pathways [17–21]. A comprehensive analysis of the differentially expressed protein data revealed that, although up-regulated proteins predominated in terms of quantity, down-regulated proteins exhibited a more critical central position within the complex PPI network (Fig. 2e). This finding underscores the need for future research to focus on the potential functions and regulatory mechanisms of down-regulated proteins.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that, compared to pediatric patients, the up-regulated proteins in adults were mainly enriched in key metabolic pathways such as Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis, Pentose phosphate pathway, and Purine metabolism, as well as important signaling and immune-related pathways like Cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway and Glucagon signaling pathway. Additionally, many pathways related to infection and immune responses were significantly enriched (Fig. 2f). The down-regulated proteins were primarily enriched in pathways such as Basal transcription factors, ECM-receptor interaction, Glycerophospholipid metabolism, Ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 2g).

EBV-positive versus EBV-negative BL

In the 2022 WHO 5th edition classification of haematolymphoid tumours, BL was divided into EBV-positive and negative subtypes, providing new insights and research directions. Our study compared protein expression in EBV-positive and negative BL patients, identifying 176 differentially expressed proteins, with 93 up-regulated and 83 down-regulated in EBV-positive patients (Table 2), as shown in volcano plot (Fig. 3a).

EBV-positive versus EBV-negative BL. a The volcano plot shows proteins with expression differences between EBV-positive and EBV-negative groups. b The chord diagram shows the related pathways and functions of the top 30 up-regulated proteins in the EBV-positive group. c The chord diagram shows the related pathways and functions of the top 30 up-regulated proteins in the EBV-negative group. d This chart shows pathways that are enriched in proteins up-regulated in the EBV-positive group. e This chart shows pathways that are enriched in proteins down-regulated in the EBV-positive group.

By analyzing the top 30 up-regulated and down-regulated proteins, we found that in EBV-positive patients, up-regulated proteins mainly participated in apoptosis pathways (MAPK13, GIMAP4, STAT6, BID), autophagy pathways (ATG3), cytoskeleton and cell signaling pathways (NCKIPSD, HGH1, SCRIB, ACTBL2, STK10, ARHGAP9), energy metabolism (RMND5A, FABP3, CYC1, PPA2, GPI) and purine metabolism pathways (IMPDH2) (Fig. 3b). Down-regulated proteins in EBV-positive patients were mainly involved in transcription regulation pathways (POLR2C, SMARCD1, ZNF800, HMGN5, BCL3), DNA damage repair pathways (RBBP6, YJU2, CIZ1, UNG, SETX), RNA metabolism pathways (RPP25, RBM42, QKI), cell signaling pathways (CSPG4, PDLIM1, C2CD5), and immune signaling pathways (FCRLA, NFKB2) (Fig. 3c).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis found that in EBV-positive patients, up-regulated proteins were mainly enriched in pathways like Apoptosis, Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, Nucleotide metabolism, Antigen processing and presentation, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, and Biosynthesis of nucleotide sugars (Fig. 3d). Down-regulated proteins were mainly enriched in RNA polymerase, N-Glycan biosynthesis, various types of N-glycan biosynthesis, and Other types of O-glycan biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 3e).

Discussion

Through a comprehensive analysis of clinical data and histoproteomic profiling in BL patients, we identified distinct clinical and molecular heterogeneity across different age groups and EBV infection statuses.

As a highly aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphoma, BL’s age-dependent clinical variability has long been a critical research focus. Our retrospective analysis of clinical datasets revealed significant intergroup differences (P < 0.05) across three parameters between adult and pediatric cohorts: serum LDH levels, tumor stage, and gender distribution. Notably, elevated LDH levels were observed in both cohorts. However, pediatric patients exhibited a more pronounced increase. Furthermore, pediatric patients demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of advanced tumor stages. These findings align with existing mechanistic insights. For example, Schmitz R et al. found that pediatric BL cells have shorter cell cycles, which lead to higher energy consumption per unit time and increased LDH release [22]. According to the theory of immune surveillance proposed by Dunn et al., the immune system in children is still developing, and functional differences exist in both adaptive and innate immunity compared to adults [23]. Furthermore, the function of key effector cells, such as Natural Killer (NK) cells, may be less mature, which could impair the efficiency of early tumor cell recognition and clearance [24, 25]. This potentially weakened surveillance capacity of the developing immune environment may theoretically render children more susceptible to tumor progression. Combined with the clinical trend of more advanced disease stage observed in pediatric patients in our cohort, this immunological evidence may provide a potential biological explanation for this phenomenon. The epidemiological profile of BL is characterized by a significant sex disparity, with global data consistently showing a 2 to 4-fold higher incidence in males compared to females [26, 27]. The underlying mechanisms for this bias are still under investigation but are thought to be multifactorial. Potential explanations may include sex-based differences in immune regulation, potentially influenced by sex hormones [28], or the differential expression of immune-related genes located on the X chromosome [29]. Collectively, our results augment prior research by clarifying age- and gender- related clinical patterns in BL.

Previous studies revealed that metabolic reprogramming, particularly glycolytic pathway activation, was the major molecular characteristic driven by MYC gene rearrangement, a core driver of BL [30–34]. Consistent with these findings, we found that differentially expressed proteins in adult patients were enriched in energy metabolism-related functions. Moreover, KEGG pathway analysis showed that up - regulated proteins were significantly enriched in the Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis pathway. Notably, adult cohorts exhibited marked up-regulation of PFKM (phosphofructokinase) and PGM2 (Phosphoglucomutase-2), both directly involved in glycolysis and glycogen metabolism [17–21], as well as GPD1 (Glycerol-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase 1), which links carbohydrate metabolism to lipid synthesis [35, 36]. Beyond metabolic reprogramming, BL cells face higher oxidative stress due to their rapid proliferation rate, driving survival strategies such as antioxidant enzymes upregulation [37]. Our findings provide probable mechanistic insights into this phenomenon: up-regulated expression of SOD1 (Superoxide Dismutase 1) and HSPH1 (Heat Shock Protein Family H Member 1) was observed in adult patients, suggesting enhanced antioxidant capacity and protein homeostasis [14]. Additionally, we found that IDH1 (Isocitrate Dehydrogenase (NADP(+)) 1) and TALDO1 (Transaldolase 1) are up-regulated in adult patients. Both are involved in the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), providing substrates for nucleotide synthesis and antioxidant stress [13, 14].

BL has traditionally been classified into three subtypes: endemic, sporadic, and immunodeficiency-related [1]. However, recent studies indicate that gene expression differences driven by EBV infection status far exceed those attributed to geographical location [8]. Consistently, molecular biology research indicates that, regardless of epidemiological and geographical factors, EBV-positive and EBV- negative BL exhibit distinct molecular and biological features [5, 7, 8, 32, 33]. In the present study, up-regulated proteins in EBV-positive BL patients mainly participated in the purine metabolism pathway, as well as apoptosis and autophagy signaling pathways, all of which have been proved to be crucial for EBV infection. Specifically: (i) the up-regulation of the purine metabolism pathway directly facilitates viral genome synthesis, which aligns with EBV’s high nucleotide demand during latency [38]. (ii) apoptotic cells can regulate the tumor microenvironment, thereby promoting tumor-cell growth, angiogenesis, tissue reconstruction, and tumor metastasis in aggressive B cell lymphoma [39]. (iii) EBV effectively utilizes autophagy to degrade host cell proteins, providing abundant substrates for viral replication [40], and simultaneously evading immune system attack and clearance by fine-tuning autolysosomes [41, 42]. Besides these established mechanisms, we identified significant up-regulation of cytoskeleton-related proteins and signaling molecules in EBV-positive patients. These proteins mediate critical cellular behaviors and processes including cell migration, adhesion, and polarity regulation [43–45], suggesting that EBV may significantly enhance the invasive capacity of tumor cells by cytoskeletal remodeling. For example, ARHGAP9, a Rho GTPase-activating protein, could promote tumor cell motility by regulating the dynamic remodeling of the actin cytoskeleton [46, 47]. Aberrant expression of SCRIB (scribble polarity complex) is closely associated with the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process [48], potentially explaining the heightened metastatic potential of EBV-positive tumors [49, 50]. These findings provide a foundation for future research to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying EBV-driven tumor progression.

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small, especially for patients tested for EBV infection. Second, the proteomic analysis only provides a static molecular snapshot of tumors, lacking investigations into dynamic changes and functional validation. Third, our proteomic analysis was based on a human-only database, which prevented the direct detection of EBV-encoded proteins and thus a formal characterization of the viral latency program. Despite this, our primary goal was to delineate the host proteome landscape associated with EBV status, an objective we have fulfilled. Future studies incorporating a combined human-viral database will be essential to directly link viral protein expression to the host pathways identified here. To address these limitations, future studies should expand the sample size and integrate multiomics technologies like genomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics. This will allow for a more comprehensive analysis of BL, helping to explore its pathogenesis and identify more effective therapeutic targets, ultimately improving patients’ prognosis and quality of life.

In summary, our study systematically analyzed the clinical characteristics, protein expression differences in BL patients, and the impact of age and EBV infection on the disease. It offers an in-depth exploration of the molecular characteristics of BL from a proteomic perspective, and thus provides a more comprehensive and essential theoretical basis and reference for future clinical research and the development of therapeutic strategies.
