The influence of tumor location on the onset of endocrine deficits in pediatric brain tumor survivors
Authors: Giorgio Attinà, Alberto Romano, Palma Maurizi, Tommaso Verdolotti, Stefano Mastrangelo, Antonio Ruggiero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric central nervous system (CNS) tumors are the second most common malignancy in children and are associated with significant long-term morbidity despite advancements in therapy. Endocrine dysfunction is one of the most frequent and impactful late sequelae, requiring timely detection and management. This study aimed to investigate the timing and risk factors associated with endocrine dysfunction in pediatric brain tumor survivors and to propose optimized follow-up protocols.MethodsA cohort of 33 pediatric patients with brain tumors, excluding craniopharyngiomas, pituitary adenomas, and germ cell tumors, treated at the Pediatric Oncology Unit of Fondazione Policlinico Universitario “Agostino Gemelli” IRCCS in Rome (2005–2015), was retrospectively analyzed. Endocrine dysfunction was assessed through clinical and laboratory evaluations, with a median follow-up of 114.1 months.ResultsEndocrine dysfunction was identified in a high percentage of patients, with hypothyroidism and panhypopituitarism being the most common. Age at diagnosis and treatment influenced the appearance and the timing of dysfunction. Hypothalamic and suprasellar tumors were associated with early onset, while tumors in posterior cranial fossa exhibited delayed onset.ConclusionsTumor location and patient age are critical determinants of the timing of endocrine dysfunction in pediatric brain tumor survivors. We propose a stratified follow-up based on tumor location, with immediate evaluation for hypothalamic and supratentorial tumors and periodic assessments for other regions. These tailored recommendations aim to ensure timely detection and management of endocrine complications, improving outcomes and quality of life. Future research with larger cohorts is needed to validate and refine these findings.Keywords: Childhood cancer, long term cancer survivors, endocrine sequelae, brain tumors, treatment late effects

Highlight box

Key findings

• Tumor location significantly influences the timing of endocrine dysfunction onset, with hypothalamic tumors causing early manifestation and other locations showing delayed onset. Patient age at diagnosis affects the chronology of endocrine deficiencies, with younger children experiencing earlier onset of thyroid dysfunction and panhypopituitarism. A stratified, location-specific follow-up protocol is proposed to optimize early detection and management of endocrine dysfunction in pediatric brain tumor survivors.

What is known and what is new?

• It is known that endocrine dysfunction is one of the most prevalent complications among pediatric brain tumor survivors with profound implications for growth, development, and quality of life. It is known that the hypothalamic-pituitary axis is particularly vulnerable to both direct tumor damage and therapeutic interventions, especially radiation therapy.

• This manuscript adds evidence that specific tumor locations (hypothalamic, suprasellar, pituitary) correlate with distinct temporal patterns of hormonal deficiencies onset. This manuscript adds a novel stratified follow-up protocol tailored to tumor location and patient age to optimize resource allocation while ensuring timely detection of endocrine dysfunction.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Implement location-specific and age-specific follow-up strategies: immediate evaluation for hypothalamic tumors; assessments at 1-, 2-, and 15-month for suprasellar, pituitary, or optic-diencephalic tumors; and evaluations at 12- and 36-month for other brain regions.

• Develop individualized care plans integrating tumor-specific, age-specific, and treatment-specific factors, with more frequent monitoring for patients who received craniospinal irradiation.

• Establish interdisciplinary collaboration between pediatric endocrinologists and oncologists while prioritizing patient and family education about potential endocrine complications.

Introduction

Central nervous system (CNS) tumors constitute the second most prevalent pediatric malignancy after leukemia, representing approximately 25% of all childhood cancers. Despite remarkable improvements in survival rates attributed to advancements in surgical techniques, innovative radiation therapy (RT) modalities, and enhanced chemotherapy (CT) protocols, substantial morbidity persists among survivors (1-5).

The therapeutic management of pediatric CNS tumors presents a complex clinical dilemma, requiring meticulous balance between implementing sufficiently aggressive treatment strategies to ensure long-term survival while minimizing treatment-associated morbidities (6,7). CNS neoplasms possess a distinctive capacity to disrupt vital neurological and endocrine pathways due to their anatomical proximity to the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. This disruption may occur through direct tumor infiltration or indirectly via iatrogenic damage during surgical intervention or radiation exposure. These endocrine perturbations typically manifest as progressive, cumulative, and frequently irreversible dysfunctions, necessitating lifelong surveillance and comprehensive management strategies (8-11).

Endocrine sequelae in pediatric CNS tumor survivors warrant particular concern given their profound impact on somatic growth, pubertal development, metabolic homeostasis, and reproductive potential—all fundamental determinants of physical and psychosocial wellbeing. The potential for delayed recognition of these complications emphasizes the critical need for structured, evidence-based follow-up protocols (12-14).

Importantly, the tumor’s intrinsic pathophysiology contributes significantly to endocrine dysfunction independent of therapeutic interventions. Merchant and colleagues demonstrated that 47% of patients with posterior fossa tumors exhibited endocrine abnormalities prior to initiating radiotherapy, highlighting the multifactorial etiology of these complications (15).

Contemporary therapeutic approaches have dramatically improved survival outcomes; however, each treatment modality carries inherent risks for late-onset complications that significantly impact survivors’ quality of life. Among these, endocrine and reproductive dysfunctions represent the most prevalent late sequelae in pediatric CNS tumor survivors (16-18).

Children at heightened risk for endocrine complications include those treated with cranial or craniospinal RT and/or high-dose alkylating agents such as cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, and busulfan (19). Delayed diagnosis and suboptimal management of hormonal dysfunctions can adversely affect linear growth, pubertal progression, and overall health status. Epidemiological data suggest that the cumulative incidence of endocrine and metabolic disorders among survivors may reach 62% with extended follow-up (20,21). This alarming statistic underscores the paramount importance of implementing comprehensive surveillance protocols for children treated for brain tumors.

Current recommendations suggest conducting endocrine evaluations at six-month intervals during adolescence and annually thereafter during adulthood (22). However, contemporary literature lacks precise guidelines regarding the optimal timing for initiating screening and establishing follow-up intervals specifically tailored for pediatric populations (23,24).

While previous investigations have emphasized the importance of endocrine monitoring in pediatric CNS tumor survivors, few have provided specific, clinically applicable recommendations regarding the optimal timing, frequency, and comprehensive scope of these assessments. Existing guidelines frequently present generalized approaches or rely on data extrapolated from heterogeneous patient populations, failing to account for the distinctive risk profiles associated with diverse tumor locations, histological classifications, and treatment regimens (25-29).

Our research aims to address these critical knowledge gaps by analyzing a well-characterized single-center cohort with comprehensive longitudinal follow-up data. We seek to establish evidence-based recommendations for endocrine surveillance protocols specifically tailored for pediatric CNS tumor survivors. By investigating correlations between the chronology and severity of endocrine dysfunctions with specific tumor characteristics and treatment modalities, we aim to refine and optimize clinical approaches to monitoring and managing these complications. Furthermore, our study emphasizes the fundamental importance of early detection and prompt intervention, as timely management of endocrine disorders has demonstrated significant mitigation of adverse effects on growth, development, and long-term health outcomes in this vulnerable patient population. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-908/rc).

Methods

Study design, inclusion and exclusion criteria

This is an observational and retrospective study involving pediatric patients diagnosed with brain tumors, treated at the Pediatric Oncology Unit of Fondazione Policlinico Universitario “Agostino Gemelli” IRCCS in Rome between January 2005 and December 2015, and subjected to regular endocrinological follow-up for at least 2 years after the end of treatment.

Inclusion criteria were: (I) diagnosis of brain cancer during paediatric age (0–16 years), excluding craniopharyngiomas, pituitary adenomas and germ cell tumors; (II) availability of data related to endocrinological follow-up for at least 2 years after the end of treatment; (III) absence of endocrinological disease before cancer diagnosis.

Exclusion criteria were: (I) diagnosis of craniopharyngiomas, pituitary adenomas and germ cell tumors; (II) endocrinological follow-up shorter than 2 years after the end of treatment; (III) presence of endocrinological disease before cancer diagnosis.

Patients affected by craniopharyngiomas, pituitary adenomas and germ cell tumors were excluded from the analysis because they are strongly associated with a high incidence of endocrine dysfunction due to their intrinsic nature and position.

Background information, disease-specific details, and treatment-related data were extracted from the electronic patients’ medical records. Endocrine evaluations were standardized based on institutional protocols, which were aligned with international guidelines for pediatric oncology follow-up. Endocrine dysfunction was evaluated based on clinical findings and laboratory results.

All patients alive at the time of the study were included in the analysis.

Disease and treatment-related data

For each patient, the following data were collected:

❖ Age and pubertal stage at diagnosis;

❖ Age and pubertal stage at diagnosis;

❖ Data related to the neoplastic disease (date of diagnosis, histology, and primarily affected site);

❖ Data related to the neoplastic disease (date of diagnosis, histology, and primarily affected site);

❖ Type and duration of the treatment [neurosurgery, radiotherapy (site and dose), CT].

❖ Type and duration of the treatment [neurosurgery, radiotherapy (site and dose), CT].

Endocrine assessment

For each patient, an annual endocrinological evaluation was performed including weight and height measurement, pubertal staging and dosage of plasmatic thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free thyroxine (fT4) deficiencies, cortisol, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and gonadotropins. The follow-up was stopped after 10 years from the end of the treatment (120 months).

Growth hormone (GH) stimulation tests were performed every 2–3 years based on clinical indications (if short stature or slow growth rate was detected). GH deficiency was defined as a GH response of less than 10 ng/mL following stimulation with clonidine hydrochloride (HCl) (0.15 mg/m2) or glucagon (30 µg/kg). GH testing was conducted in patients presenting with short stature [height below −2 standard deviations (SD)] or growth deceleration (growth velocity below the 25th percentile for age).

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis of all the parameters collected was performed. Quantitative variables are reported as means and SD. Qualitative variables are presented as frequencies and absolute percentages (%). Student’s t-test was used for comparison of continuous variables and the Chi-squared test was used for comparison of categorical variables. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with XLSTAT software.

Ethical considerations

The retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Università Cattolica Sacro Cuore-Policlinico Gemelli IRCCS (approval No. DIPUSVSP-25-06-2164) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Results

Patients’ characteristics, disease and treatment-related data

A total of 95 pediatric patients with brain tumors treated between January 2005 and December 2015 were initially evaluated. Of the 95 patients, 20 were excluded because they died due to their disease and 24 were excluded because diagnosed with craniopharyngiomas, pituitary adenomas, and germ cell tumors.

Of the remaining 51 patients, 18 were excluded due to incomplete endocrine evaluations at the study center. Consequently, 33 pediatric patients were included in this retrospective study. The mean age at diagnosis was 8.27 (SD 5.6) years, 59.6% were male and 40.4% were female. The mean follow-up duration was 114.1 (SD 17.6) months.

The tumor types included gliomas (69.7%), medulloblastomas (27.3%), and ependymomas (3%).

The tumor was located 36.4% in the optic-diencephalic region, 42.4% in the posterior cranial fossa and 21.2% in the hemispheric cortical area.

Treatment regimens consisted of neurosurgery, CT, radiotherapy or a combination of two or more of them: 9.1% underwent surgical resection only; 36.4% received surgery and CT; 6% underwent surgery and RT; 3% were treated with CT and RT; 45.5% received a combination of surgery, CT, and RT.

Endocrinological evaluation

During the follow-up, 11 patients developed an endocrinological deficit. Of the 11 patients, 5 (45.5%) had panhypopituitarism, 3 (27.3%) had only hypothyroidism, 1 (9%) had hypothyroidism and ACTH deficiency, 1 (9%) had precocious puberty and 1 (9%) had GH deficiency.

Hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis

Hypothyroidism was observed in 9 of the 33 patients (27.3%), with a mean onset of 7.65 (SD 9.5) months from diagnosis.

Among these cases, 8 were classified as secondary hypothyroidism, while 1 was primary hypothyroidism. The patient with primary hypothyroidism had undergone craniospinal RT, receiving doses of 54 Gy to the tumor site, 34.5 Gy to the brain, and 30 Gy to the spinal cord. Of the 9 patients, 3 had only hypothyroidism, 1 had hypothyroidism and ACTH deficiency, and 5 had a panhypopituitarism condition.

The timing of hypothyroidism onset varied according to tumor location (Figure 1). In patients with tumors involving the optic-diencephalic region, hypothyroidism was detected on average at 7.38 (SD 10.4) months from the diagnosis; in patients with tumor in posterior cranial fossa it was detected on average at 16.81 (SD 4.87) months from diagnosis of the tumor; in patients with tumors in hemispheric cortical area it was detected on average at 0.52 (SD 0.07) months from diagnosis of the tumor. The patients with hemispheric cortical area tumors and hypothyroidism were affected by high-grade gliomas and experienced the appearance of hypothyroidism in association with panhypopituitarism after surgery.

Time of appearance of hypothyroidism according to tumor localization.

According to age at diagnosis, we observed that in children younger than five years, hypothyroidism was detected after a mean of 6.26 (SD 6.9) months from diagnosis; in those aged 5–10 years, the mean onset was 14.76 (SD 12.4) months from diagnosis; only one children older than 10 years at diagnosis exhibited hypothyroidism after 1 month from diagnosis (Figure 2).

Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and panhypopituitarism

ACTH deficiency was diagnosed in 6 patients of the 33 patients (18.2%). In 5 patients, ACTH deficiency appeared in the context of panhypopituitarism and in 1 case, in association with hypothyroidism.

The group with panhypopituitarism experienced the deficit at a mean time of 0.67 (SD 0.96) months from diagnosis, immediately after undergoing surgery. All the patients had a glioma, 3 localized in hemispheric cortical area and 2 in optic-diencephalic region.

The age at diagnosis of patients with panhypopituitarism was on average 4.85 (SD 2.72) years. Children younger than five years at diagnosis exhibited deficiencies with a mean onset of 0.15 (SD 0.27) months from diagnosis and those aged 5–10 years, 6.97 (SD 9.1) months from diagnosis (Figure 3).

Time of appearance of panhypopituitarism according to age at diagnosis.

GH deficiency

GH deficiency was observed in 6 patients, 5 were patients with panhypopituitarism and 1 patient without other deficiencies. This patient with isolated GH deficiency was diagnosed with a medulloblastoma at the age of 5 years and developed GH deficiency after 47.1 months from diagnosis of tumor. All 6 patients received GH therapy without any problems during the treatment.

Discussion

Recent therapeutic advances for pediatric brain tumors have markedly improved survival outcomes, creating an imperative to enhance survivors’ quality of life. Among the spectrum of long-term sequelae, endocrine dysfunction emerges as one of the most prevalent complications with profound implications for growth, development, and overall well-being. This reality underscores the critical importance of implementing structured follow-up protocols that facilitate timely diagnosis and intervention while avoiding unnecessary medicalization in this vulnerable pediatric population (30-32).

The multifaceted nature of endocrine dysfunctions in pediatric CNS tumor survivors highlights the necessity for early, systematic hormonal evaluations. The observed prevalence of HPA dysfunction documented in our study aligns with previous research demonstrating the particular vulnerability of this neuroanatomical region to both direct tumor-related damage and therapeutic interventions, especially RT. Considering the substantial impact of these dysfunctions on linear growth, pubertal development, and metabolic homeostasis, prompt diagnosis and comprehensive management remain essential clinical priorities (33).

Our findings suggest that specific tumor locations, particularly those involving hypothalamic and suprasellar regions, correlate with accelerated onset of hormonal deficiencies (34,35). This observation carries significant implications for clinical practice by emphasizing the necessity for location-specific follow-up strategies. For hypothalamic tumors, comprehensive endocrine assessments should commence immediately following diagnosis and continue at regular intervals to detect early dysfunction. Conversely, for tumors in other intracranial locations, monitoring protocols can be tailored based on the characteristic delayed onset patterns observed. Additionally, the influence of patient age at diagnosis on the chronology of endocrine abnormalities warrants particular attention (36,37).

The earlier manifestation of deficits in younger children suggests that pubertal status and developmental stage may significantly modulate the response of the HPA to both tumor-related and treatment-induced insults. This developmental variability necessitates age-adapted follow-up protocols to ensure optimal surveillance and management of hormonal complications (38-40).

Current international guidelines provide the foundation for endocrine surveillance in pediatric brain tumor survivors. van Iersel et al. demonstrated that survivors of hypothalamic-pituitary region tumors and those receiving cranial radiotherapy require systematic follow-up beginning 1-year post-radiotherapy, with intervals of six months for pre- and peri-pubertal patients and annually for adults (41). The Children’s Oncology Group recommends similar surveillance frequencies but emphasizes risk stratification based on radiation dose and anatomical location. Our institutional protocol builds upon these established frameworks while incorporating specific considerations for our institutional experience and patient population characteristics.

Our investigation specifically identifies tumor location as a critical determinant influencing the temporal onset of endocrine deficits. In hypothalamic tumors, endocrine dysfunction manifests early in the disease course. Conversely, for tumors situated in other intracranial regions, the onset of endocrine dysfunction demonstrates significant temporal delay.

Age at diagnosis also emerges as a pivotal factor influencing the chronology of endocrine deficiencies. Younger children typically experience an earlier onset of these deficits compared to older counterparts. For TSH and fT4 deficiency, the mean onset is 6.26 months in children under five years of age, compared to 14.76 months in those over ten years. Similarly, panhypopituitarism appears at a mean of 0.16 months in children under five years, increasing to 6.97 months in those older than five years.

Based on these findings, we propose a stratified follow-up protocol. For hypothalamic tumors, immediate endocrinological evaluation is essential. For suprasellar, pituitary, or optic-diencephalic tumors, comprehensive hormonal assessments should be conducted at 1-, 2-, and 15-month post-diagnosis. For tumors located in other brain regions, endocrinological evaluations should occur at 12- and 36-month post-diagnosis. This differentiated approach aims to optimize resource allocation while ensuring timely detection and management of endocrine dysfunction.

The collective findings from this study support the development of individualized care plans that integrate tumor-specific, age-specific, and treatment-specific factors. Specifically, patients with a history of craniospinal irradiation, particularly those receiving high cumulative doses, should undergo more frequent and detailed endocrine evaluations. Furthermore, interdisciplinary collaboration between pediatric endocrinologists and oncologists is essential to ensure a coordinated approach to care that addresses both immediate and long-term needs of survivors.

Patient and family education should be prioritized to enhance awareness of potential endocrine complications and emphasize the importance of adherence to recommended follow-up schedules. Empowering families with knowledge regarding the clinical manifestations of hormonal deficiencies can facilitate early recognition and improve long-term outcomes.

A significant limitation of our study is the relatively modest sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings. Expanding the cohort and conducting extended longitudinal studies will be critical to validating and refining our proposed follow-up strategies.

Conclusions

The significantly improved survival rates among pediatric brain tumor patients necessitate enhanced focus on managing long-term complications, particularly endocrine dysfunction. Our findings demonstrate the substantial influence of tumor location and patient age on the temporal development of endocrine deficiencies. Hypothalamic tumors consistently result in early endocrine dysfunction, while deficits associated with suprasellar, pituitary, and optic-diencephalic tumors develop later in the clinical course. Younger children experience earlier onset of thyroid dysfunction and panhypopituitarism compared to older cohorts.

Based on these observations, we propose a location-specific tailored follow-up protocol to optimize early detection and intervention, thereby improving patient outcomes and quality of life. However, our conclusions are tempered by the limited sample size, and additional studies with larger, more diverse cohorts are essential to strengthen and expand these preliminary findings. Future research should focus on prospective validation of these targeted surveillance strategies and their impact on long-term endocrine outcomes in pediatric brain tumor survivors.
