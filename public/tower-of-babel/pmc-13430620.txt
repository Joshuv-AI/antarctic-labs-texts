A Melanoma Transplanted in Nude Mice Has Both Host‐ and Tumor‐Derived Blood Vessel‐ and Nerve‐Like Structures
Authors: Koya Obara, Azusa Sasaki, Yuko Hamada, Yutaro Kubota, Yusuke Aoki, Noriyuki Masaki, Michael Bouvet, Yasuyuki Amoh, Robert M Hoffman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNestin‐expressing stem cells located in the bulge area of the hair follicle can give rise to multiple cell types. In the present study, we subcutaneously transplanted red fluorescent protein (RFP)‐labeled SK‐MEL‐5 human melanoma cells into nestin‐driven green fluorescent protein (ND‐GFP)‐nude mice. Nestin‐GFP‐positive cells differentiated into CD31‐positive endothelial cells and αSMA‐positive pericytes, and βIII‐tubulin‐positive neurons, forming blood vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures within the transplanted melanoma. Bevacizumab, a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)‐targeting monoclonal antibody, suppressed nestin‐positive blood‐vessel structures and nerve structures from forming within the tumor but did not significantly inhibit tumor growth. Due to bevacizumab treatment, blood‐vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures expressing CD31, αSMA, and βIII tubulin formed without the expression of nestin in the transplanted melanoma but remained RFP‐positive, suggesting that they trans‐differentiated from melanoma cells. Relative mRNA expression of VEGFA also significantly decreased in the melanoma after bevacizumab treatment of the mice. In contrast, VEGFR2 and FGF6, markers that are related to blood vessels, significantly increased compared with the non‐treated mice. In the bevacizumab‐treated mice, mRNA expression of Tuj1, BDNF, and IGF1 markers related to nerves significantly increased within the melanoma. In the bevacizumab‐treated mice, nestin expression was not detected, but RFP was maintained in the blood‐vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures in the melanoma. Since nestin was not detected and RFP was maintained, it suggests these structures trans‐differentiated from the melanoma cells. The present study suggests that both the host and cancer cells can differentiate into blood‐vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures in the melanoma.Keywords: bevacizumab, HAP stem cells, melanoma, nestin, nestin‐driven‐GFP‐transgenic mouse

Introduction

Hair follicle‐associated pluripotent (HAP) stem cells, which were discovered by our laboratory, express nestin, originate in the bulge area of the hair follicle, and migrate within and outside the hair follicle [1, 2]. HAP stem cells can differentiate to multiple cell types, including keratinocytes, neurons, beating cardiac‐muscle cells, endothelial cells, and numerous other cell types [2, 3, 4, 5]. Amoh et al. showed that transplanted hair follicles formed nestin‐expressing blood vessels in host nude mice [2].

Previously, our laboratory demonstrated that color‐coded fluorescence imaging distinguished various cancer cells from induced host angiogenic vessels and stromal cells [6, 7, 8]. Yang et al. showed the interaction between cancer and stromal cells by using tumors expressing red fluorescent protein (RFP) growing in transgenic mice expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) [6]. Amoh et al. showed in nestin‐driven GFP (ND‐GFP) transgenic nude mice, ND‐GFP‐expressing endothelial cells vascularized a transplanted melanoma [8]. Hayashi et al. reported a color‐coded orthotopic nude‐mouse model of transplanted paralyzing spinal‐cord glioma that induced ND‐GFP‐expressing blood vessels and nerves in the spinal‐cord glioma [9].

In the present study, we show that a human melanoma expressing RFP, transplanted in ND‐GFP nude mice, formed nestin‐driven GFP‐expressing vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures in the melanoma. After bevacizumab treatment, the melanoma cells, not expressing nestin‐GFP but still expressing RFP, transdifferentiated into blood vessel‐ and nerve‐like structures.

Materials and Methods

Mice

ND‐GFP transgenic C57/B6 mice express GFP under the control of the nestin second‐intron enhancer with extensive expression in the hair‐follicle bulge area in HAP stem cells [1, 2].

In the present study, the ND‐GFP gene was crossed into nude mice on the C57/B6 background to obtain ND‐GFP nude mice (AntiCancer Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All surgical procedures and imaging were performed with an AntiCancer Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocol specifically approved for this study and in accordance with the principles and procedures outlined in the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Animals under Assurance Number A3873‐1. To minimize any suffering of the animals, anesthesia and analgesics were used for all surgical experiments. Animals were anesthetized by subcutaneous injection of a 0.02 mL solution of 20 mg/kg ketamine, 15.2 mg/kg xylazine, and 0.48 mg/kg acepromazine maleate. The response of animals during surgery was monitored to ensure adequate depth of anesthesia. The mice were observed on a daily basis and humanely sacrificed by over‐dosed anesthesia if they met the following humane endpoint criteria: Severe tumor burden (more than 20 mm in diameter), prostration, significant body weight loss, difficulty breathing, rotational motion, and body‐temperature drop.

Cell Line and Cell Culture

The SK‐MEL‐5‐RFP human BRAF‐mutant melanoma cell line with stable high‐RFP expression (AntiCancer Inc.) was maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; cat #10–103‐CV, Corning, NY, USA), supplemented with 1 IU/mL penicillin/streptomycin (cat #15–240‐062, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Access Biologicals, Vista, CA, USA). Cells were cultured at 37°C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Murine Cutaneous Malignant‐Melanoma Transplant Model in ND‐GFP Transgenic Nude Mice

ND‐GFP transgenic nude mice, 4 to 8 weeks old, male or female, were used. SK‐MEL‐5‐RFP melanoma cells (1 × 106 cells/mouse) in 50 μL of phosphate buffer saline (PBS; cat #MB1039‐1X, BioPioneer, San Diego, CA, USA) and 50 μL of Matrigel Matrix (Corning) were injected into the subcutaneous tissue in the left lateral abdomen of the mice. The resulting melanoma was measured every 7 days for a total of 14 days by an electronic skin caliper from the longest width and length in a treatment experiment with bevacizumab. Tumor volume was calculated with the following formula: Melanoma volume (mm3) = length (mm) × width (mm) × width (mm) × 1/2. Mice were placed into groups based on melanoma volume so that there was no statistical difference in mean melanoma volume between the two groups before treatment commenced. Bevacizumab (Avastin; Hoffman‐La Roche, Basel, Switzerland) was dissolved in 100 μL of PBS and administered to the mice in the treatment group by intraperitoneal injection twice a week at 10 mg/kg. Control mice received vehicle alone.

Fluorescence Imaging

The observation of fresh specimens of the tumor by fluorescence microscopy was done with an IMT‐2 inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a mercury lamp power supply. The microscope had a GFP filter set (Chroma Technology, Bellows Falls, VT, USA). Whole‐mouse‐body fluorescence imaging was done with a UVP ChemStudio (Analytik Jena, Upland, CA, USA).

Sample‐Size Determination and Tissues in Human Malignant Melanoma

The present clinical study was approved by the Kitasato University Medical Ethics Organization (KMEO B25‐131) on December 17, 2025, and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki Principles and Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects (Ministry of Education, Culture, Science and Technology and the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare in Japan) Deidentified formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded biopsy specimens of 28 primary melanomas with a Breslow depth ≥ 4 mm and 20 primary melanomas with a Breslow depth < 4 mm were obtained from the Kitasato University Department of Dermatology. Histological Analysis

The excised‐mouse and human‐tumor samples were formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded blocks (FFPB). The FFPB sections (4 μm each) were placed on slides for immunofluorescence staining. For immunofluorescence staining in mouse tumor samples, FFPB sections on slides were incubated with anti‐GFP rabbit polyclonal antibody (1:1000, cat #NB600, Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA); anti‐RFP (mCherry) rabbit polyclonal antibody (1 μg/mL, kindly provided by Dr. Sakagami, Department of Anatomy, Kitasato University School of Medicine) [10]; anti‐CD31 rat monoclonal antibody (1:200, cat #ab56299, Abcam, Cambridge, UK); anti‐neuronal class‐III beta‐tubulin (βIII tubulin) mouse monoclonal antibody (1:500, cat #MMS‐435P, Covance, Princeton, NJ, USA); and anti‐alpha smooth‐muscle actin (αSMA) mouse monoclonal antibody (1:200, cat #MS113P0, Lab Vision, Fremont, CA, USA). These sections were then incubated with goat anti‐rabbit IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (1:400, cat #A11008, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA); goat anti‐mouse IgG conjugated with Alexa Flour 568 (1:400, cat #A11004, Invitrogen); goat anti‐rat IgG conjugated with Alexa Flour 568 (1:400, cat #ab175476, Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA) and 4′,6‐diamino‐2‐phenylindole, dihydrochloride (DAPI) (1:5000, cat #SE196, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). The observation of tumor specimens and cultured cells with immunofluorescence staining was done with an IMT‐2 inverted microscope (Olympus), which had a GFP filter set (Chroma Technology). Quantitative analysis of the morphology of the tumor was performed histologically. The nestin‐GFP expressing area in fresh skin specimens was measured in one section randomly at 200× magnification. The total nestin‐expressing area in raw tumor specimens was measured in three sections randomly at 100× magnification, and the average value was calculated. The total number of nestin or RFP and CD31, αSMA or βIII‐tubulin double‐positive cells and nestin‐negative and CD31, αSMA or βIII‐tubulin‐positive cells, determined by immunofluorescence staining, was measured in two sections randomly per tumor specimen at 200× magnification, and the average value was calculated. For immunofluorescence staining in human‐tumor samples, FFPB sections on slides were incubated with anti‐human nestin rabbit polyclonal antibody (1:40, cat #18741, IBL, Gunma, Japan); anti‐human CD31 mouse monoclonal antibody (1:80, cat #M0823, DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark); anti‐neuronal βIII‐tubulin mouse monoclonal antibody (1:4000, cat #MMS‐435P, Convance); and anti‐αSMA mouse monoclonal antibody (1:200, cat #MS113P0, Lab Vision). These sections were then incubated with goat anti‐mouse IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 568 (1:400, cat #A11004, Invitrogen); goat anti‐rabbit IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (1:400, cat #A11008, Invitrogen); and 4′,6‐diamino‐2‐phenylindole, dihydrochloride (DAPI) (1:5000, cat #SE196, Dojindo). Human‐tumor samples were evaluated using the same methods as for mouse‐tumor samples. Quantitative analysis of the diameter and area of the skin and tumor specimens was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.52; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For area analysis, the threshold values were kept at the same level.

Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐PCR)

ND‐GFP mice treated with bevacizumab for 14 days and non‐treated mice, both with transplanted SK‐MEL 6‐RFP, were used for RT‐PCR analysis. Total RNA was extracted from FFPB sections using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and cDNA was synthesized with a GeneAce cDNA Synthesis Kit (NIPPON GENE, Tokyo, Japan) according to the instructions from the manufacturer. Pre‐amplification of cDNA was performed using a Prelude PreAmp Master Mix (TAKARA, Shiga, Japan) and pooling primers. GAPDH was used to normalize gene expression. Quantitative RT‐PCR was performed using the TB Green Premix Ex Taq (TAKARA) on a CFX96 Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and analyzed using the Delta Delta Ct method. Primer sequences were as follows: Vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) (forward, AACGATGAAGCCCTGGAGTG; reverse, GCTGGCTTTGGTGAGGTTTG) [11]; vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) (forward, ACTGTGGCGAAGATGTTTTTGAGC; reverse, CGGACTTGACTGCCCACT) [12]; fibroblast growth factor 6 (FGF6) (forward, CAGGCTCTCGTCTTCTTAGGC; reverse, AATAGCCGCTTTCCCAATTCA) [13]; Tuj1 (forward, TTTTCGTCTCTAGCCGCGTG; reverse, GATGACCTCCCAGAACTTGGC) [11]; brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (forward, CAAAGCCATCCACACGTGAC; reverse, TGAATGGACTCTGCTCTCCG) [11]; insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF1) (forward, CACTGACATGCCCAAGACTCAGA; reverse, TCCGAGTTGCCTCCGTTACC) [14]; hypoxia inducible factor 1α (HIF1α) (forward, TGACTGTGCACCTACTATGTCACTT; reverse, GGTCAGCTGTGGTAATCCACTC) [15]; bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) (forward, GGGACCCGCTGTCTTCTAGT; reverse, TCAACTCAAATTCGCTGAGGAC) [16]; and glyceraldehyde 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (forward, AGGTCGGTGTGAACGGATTTG; reverse, TGTAGACCATGTAGTTGAGGTCA) [17].

Statistical Analysis

All experimental data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data of the differences between groups with all histological analyses were analyzed with the one‐sided unpaired Student's t‐test (or Welch's t‐test for unequal variances). Two‐way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni's multiple comparisons post hoc test was used to examine the differences between groups in assessments of tumor volume. Data were analyzed by using Excel with Microsoft 365. A probability value of p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 is considered significant.

Results

Host HAP Stem Cells Form Blood‐Vessel‐Like and Nerve‐Like Structures in a Transplanted Melanoma

SK‐MEL5‐RFP melanoma cells were subcutaneously injected into ND‐GFP mice. ND‐GFP mice with SK‐MEL5‐RFP melanoma were observed by whole‐body fluorescence imaging 42 days after subcutaneous injection of the SK‐MEL5‐RFP cell line (Figure 1A). Fresh specimens of skin and melanoma removed from the mice were directly observed by fluorescence microscopy (Figure 1A). Reticular nestin‐GFP‐expressing areas were observed in the subcutaneous peripheral vessel walls in ND‐GFP mice. In addition, fibrous nestin‐GFP‐expressing areas were observed throughout the peripheral nerves. ND‐GFP‐expressing peripheral blood‐vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures in the subcutaneous tissue of the ND‐GFP mice had significantly enhanced expression of nestin at the margin of the melanoma compared to the tumor‐free side (Figure 1B, Table S1) (p = 2.90 × 10−2). The formed melanomas were excised and directly observed under a fluorescence microscope 42 days after subcutaneous injection of SK‐MEL5‐RFP cells (Figure 1C). The nestin‐GFP‐positive cells continuously extended from peripheral blood vessels and nerves of the host to the melanoma surface. Histologically, on the surface of the melanoma, nestin‐CD31‐double‐positive cells, nestin‐SMA‐double‐positive cells, and nestin‐ βIII‐tubulin‐double‐positive cells were observed within the melanoma (Figure 1D). The center of the melanoma specimen was observed directly by immunofluorescence microscopy (Figure 2A). Nestin‐GFP‐expressing cells, extending from blood vessels and nerves, formed tubular structures within the tumor, forming a mesh‐like network. The tubular structures comprised nestin‐CD31‐double‐positive cells, nestin‐αSMA‐double positive cells, and nestin‐βIII‐tubulin‐double‐positive cells, observed with immunofluorescence staining (Figure 2B). These results suggest that nestin‐expressing cells from the host extended into the melanoma, differentiating into endothelial cells, pericytes, as well as neurons, which formed new blood‐vessel‐like and new nerve‐like structures, respectively.

Nestin‐Expressing Blood‐Vessel‐Like and Nerve‐Like Structures in the Transplanted Melanoma Expanded With Tumor Growth

The melanoma volume was significantly larger at 42 days than at 21 days after melanoma‐cell injection (Figure 3A,B, Table S2) (p = 1.43 × 10−2). The total length of the nestin‐GFP expressing area in the melanoma specimens was significantly longer at 42 days than at 21 days after melanoma‐cell injection (Figure 3A,C, Table S2) (p = 5.58 × 10−3). The area of nestin‐CD31‐double‐positive cells (p = 7.60 × 10−3), nestin‐αSMA‐double‐positive cells (p = 1.44 × 10−3), and nestin‐βIII‐tubulin‐double‐positive cells (p = 1.60 × 10−3), seen with immunofluorescence staining, was significantly larger at 42 days than at 21 days after melanoma‐cell injection (Figure 3D–G, Table S2). These results suggest that as the melanoma grew, host‐derived nestin‐positive blood‐vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures increased within the tumor.

Bevacizumab Suppressed Nestin‐Expressing Blood‐ Vessel‐Like and Nerve‐Like Structures in the Transplanted Melanoma

Bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody against Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), was administered to the melanoma model. Changes in the distribution of nestin‐positive cells were compared between the bevacizumab‐treated and control mice and were observed at 14 days (Figure 4A). There was no significant difference in melanoma volume between bevacizumab‐treated and non‐treated mice (Figure 4B, Table S3) (p = 2.39 × 10−1 at day 1; p = 8.14 × 10−2 at day 7; and p = 7.90 × 10−2 at day 14). The total length of the nestin‐GFP‐expressing area in the margin of the melanoma specimens was significantly longer in non‐treated mice than in bevacizumab‐treated mice (Figure 4A,C, Table S3) (p = 2.92 × 10−3). The number of nestin‐GFP‐CD31‐double‐positive cells (p = 6.71 × 10−3), nestin‐GFP‐αSMA‐double‐positive cells (p = 1.58 × 10−2), and nestin‐GFP‐βIII tubulin‐double‐positive cells (p = 2.73 × 10−2) in the melanoma seen with immunofluorescence staining was significantly larger in the non‐treated mice than in bevacizumab‐treated mice (Figure 4D–G, Table S3). In contrast, the number of nestin‐GFP‐negative CD31‐positive cells (p = 3.24 × 10−3), nestin‐GFP‐negative αSMA‐positive cells (p = 4.97 × 10−2), and nestin‐GFP‐negative βIII‐tubulin‐positive cells (p = 1.51 × 10−2) in the melanoma observed with immunofluorescence staining was significantly larger in the bevacizumab‐treated mice than in non‐treated mice (Figure 4D–G, Table S3).

Bevacizumab‐Treated Mice Showed Altered Relative mRNA Expression of Angiogenesis‐ and Neurogenesis‐Related Markers in the Transplanted Melanoma

Relative mRNA expression of various angiogenesis and neurogenesis markers in the SK‐MEL 6‐RFP melanoma transplanted in ND‐GFP mice was examined by quantitative RT‐PCR analysis. For angiogenesis‐related genes, in the bevacizumab‐treated mice, relative mRNA expression of VEGFA (p = 0.017) significantly decreased. In contrast, the relative mRNA of VEGFR2 (p = 0.001) and FGF6 (p = 0.018) significantly increased, compared with non‐treated mice in the melanoma (Figure 5A–C, Table S4). For neurogenesis‐related genes, in the bevacizumab‐treated mice, relative mRNA expression of Tuj1 (p = 0.032), BDNF (p = 0.014), and IGF1 (p = 0.001) significantly increased, compared with non‐treated mice in the melanoma (Figure 5D–F, Table S4). Relative mRNA expression of HIF1α (p = 0.017), a major molecular mediator of the hypoxic response, and BMP2 (p = 0.011), related to the epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), also significantly increased in the melanoma of bevacizumab‐treated mice, compared with non‐treated mice (Figure 5G,H, Table S4). These results suggest that bevacizumab administration may reduce host‐derived nestin‐expressing blood‐vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures in the melanoma, leading to hypoxia within the melanoma and increased HIF1α expression which in turn may have resulted in an increase in BMP2 in the melanoma, which then led to increased expression of angiogenesis markers such as VEGFR2 and FGF6 [18, 19], and neurogenesis markers such as Tuj1, BDNF, and IGF1 [20], as measured by RT‐PCR, in the bevacizumab‐treated mice. The number of RFP‐CD31‐double‐positive cells (p = 2.34 × 10−2), RFP‐αSMA‐double‐positive cells (p = 1.72 × 10−3), and RFP‐βIII tubulin‐double‐positive cells (p = 4.62 × 10−2) in the melanoma, seen with immunofluorescence staining, was significantly larger in the bevacizumab‐treated mice than in the non‐treated mice (Figure 6A–I, Table S5). The fact that the blood‐vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures in the bevacizumab‐treated melanoma did not have nestin‐GFP expression of the host mice, but maintained RFP expression of the melanoma cells, suggests that the melanoma cells transdifferentiated into cells that can form blood‐vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures.

Nestin‐Expressing Blood‐Vessel‐Like and Nerve‐Like Structures in Human‐Patient Melanoma Expanded With Tumor Growth

Resected specimens were obtained from patients diagnosed with malignant melanoma at the Department of Dermatology at Kitasato University Hospital under written informed consent. The tumor specimens were observed by immunofluorescence microscopy. Histologically, nestin‐positive cells in tubular or cord‐like structures were observed between the melanoma cells (Figure 7A,B,D,E,G,H). The tubular structures comprised nestin‐CD31‐double‐positive cells and nestin‐αSMA‐double‐positive cells, as well as cord‐like structures comprising nestin‐βIII‐tubulin‐double‐positive cells (Figure 7A,D,H). These results were similar to those of the transplanted melanoma in the ND‐GFP mice. In the patient melanomas, nestin‐expressing cells from the host extended into the melanoma, differentiating into endothelial cells and pericytes, which formed apparent blood vessels, and neurons, which formed apparent nerves. The number of nestin‐CD31‐double‐positive cells (p = 5.71 × 10−3) and nestin‐αSMA‐double‐positive cells (p = 8.81 × 10−3) in the patient melanomas was significantly larger in the melanomas with tumor thickness < 4 mm than ≥ 4 mm (Figure 7A–F, Table S6). In contrast, the number of nestin‐βIII‐tubulin‐double‐positive cells (p = 7.49 × 10−3) in the tumor was significantly larger in the melanomas with tumor thickness ≥ 4 mm than < 4 mm (Figure 7G–I, Table S6). These results suggest that nestin‐positive blood‐vessel‐like structures initially proliferate within melanoma when the tumor thickness is < 4 mm, and then nestin‐positive nerve‐like structures proliferate when the tumor thickness progresses to ≥ 4 mm.

Discussion

In the present study, when SK‐MEL‐5‐RFP melanoma cells were subcutaneously transplanted in ND‐GFP nude mice, ND‐GFP‐expressing blood‐vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures were observed within the melanoma. Nestin‐GFP‐CD31‐double‐positive cells and nestin‐GFP‐αSMA‐double‐positive cells formed tubular structures extending into the melanoma from the nestin‐GFP expressing host [2].

Nestin‐GFP‐CD31‐double‐positive cells and nestin‐GFP‐αSMA‐double‐positive cells were observed in the melanoma, lining mesh‐like structures. These nestin‐GFP‐positive cells increased with melanoma growth. This suggests that for melanoma growth, nestin‐positive cells derived from the host formed blood‐vessel‐like structures by differentiating into endothelial cells and pericytes, as we previously showed in a transplanted‐spinal glioma [9]. We also observed nestin‐GFP‐βIII‐tubulin double‐positive nerve‐like structures in the transplanted melanoma in the present study, as we previously observed in the transplant model of spinal glioma [9]. Future experiments will confirm the functionality of the vessel‐like and nerve‐like structures expressing ND‐GFP in the transplanted melanoma. The present findings were obtained in immunodeficient nude mice. This model lacks the immune component of the tumor microenvironment, which may significantly alter tumor–stroma interactions. Validation in melanoma models in immunocompetent mice will be performed in the future.

In the present study, we showed that treatment with bevacizumab suppressed nestin‐GFP‐positive cells in blood vessel‐like structures and nerve‐like structures extending into the transplanted melanoma. Subsequently, in the bevacizumab‐treated mice, there were numerous nestin‐negative‐CD31‐positive cells, nestin‐negative‐αSMA‐positive cells, and nestin‐negative‐βIII‐tubulin‐positive cells in the transplanted melanoma. These results suggest trans‐differentiation of the melanoma cells may have taken place, as these cells maintained the melanoma RFP expression. The mechanism of the trans‐differentiation of the melanoma cells will be confirmed in future studies.
