Association between circulating metabolic biomarkers and the risk of inflammatory bowel disease: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Miaomiao Fan, Mengyang Wang, Huiying Chen, Shujuan Lin, Hao Bai, Hui Han, Liyong Chen
Published: 2026

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a complex, multifactorial disorder characterized by chronic and relapsing inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract. It is well-recognized that IBD is associated with a complex multifactorial etiology that includes genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, gut microbiota, and immune dysregulation [1]. Emerging evidence highlights that metabolic dysregulation is also a key player in IBD pathogenesis [2].

Circulating metabolites, which reflect the integrated influence of environmental, genetic, and microbiome factors, provide a powerful framework for uncovering mechanistic links between diverse biological pathways and disease phenotypes [3], thereby facilitating biomarker discovery and advancing the understanding of IBD pathogenesis and progression. Accumulating evidence from metabolomic analyses of both mouse models of IBD or from patients with IBD supports that differences in metabolite abundance are associated with pathological stages and subtype classifications. For example, a cross-sectional study included 40 IBD patients and 20 non-IBD subjects indicated that a number of lipid-, amino acid–, and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle–related metabolites were significantly altered in IBD patients, more specifically in CD [4]. Furthermore, the metabolic levels of lipids containing linolenic acid, arachidonic acid, and cholinergic phospholipids in serum are associated with the risk of CD [2]. In aggregate, most metabolomic studies to date have relied on post-diagnosis samples, where it is impossible to distinguish between metabolic signatures linked to IBD pathogenesis and those secondary to established disease. There have been few prospective cohort studies investigating the association between prediagnosis metabolites and risk of IBD.

This study used the UK Biobank data to comprehensively assess the association between circulating metabolic biomarkers, quantified using a high-throughput targeted nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), with the risk of incident IBD and its subtypes (UC and CD).

Materials and methods

This study utilized data from 501,950 participants in the UK Biobank, a large-scale prospective cohort that recruited over 500,000 individuals aged 40–69 across the UK between 2006 and 2010. At baseline, participants underwent comprehensive assessments including touchscreen questionnaires, physical measurements, and biological sample collection, with follow-up conducted via linkage to national health records. The outcome variables, IBD and its two subtypes UC and CD, correspond to the UK Biobank data field IDs: 161626-131629. Metabolomic profiling was conducted using Nightingale Health Ltd.’s high-throughput NMR platform, analyzing EDTA plasma samples to measure 249 metabolites across diverse pathways. Rigorous quality control (QC) procedures were implemented throughout the profiling process. This included sample randomization, use of internal standards, technical replicates, and batch correction based on QC samples [5]. Details of the assay procedure, including biomarker definitions and analytical performance metrics, are provided in the NMR metabolomics companion document (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/ukb/refer.cgi?id=3000). This study included 39 of the biomarkers, which were CE-marked and certified for diagnostic use and span most of the different metabolic pathways measured by the NMR platform [6]. We excluded participants with missing measurements on any of the 39 metabolites (n = 265,490) and with a prior IBD diagnosis at baseline (n = 3127) (Supplementary Figure 1). A total of 262,363 participants were included in this study. All the participants provided written informed consent, ensuring ethical compliance. Participants were followed from the date of their baseline assessment until the earliest of the following events: diagnosis of IBD, death, loss to follow-up, death, the end of the follow-up period (July 1, 2023). Person-time (in years) contributed by each participant was calculated accordingly.

The values of all metabolites were transformed using natural logarithmic transformation (ln[x+1]) followed by Z-transformation. Baseline characteristics were described by mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency (%) for categorical variables. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between per 1-SD increase in circulating metabolic biomarkers and risk of IBD and its subtypes (Table 1). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were adjusted for age, sex, race (White, Mixed, South Asian, Black, Chinese, Other, or missing), educational attainment (no qualifications, college or university degree, A-level/AS-level or equivalent, O-level/GCSEs or equivalent, CSEs or equivalent, other qualifications, or missing), socioeconomic deprivation index (quintiles or missing), BMI (< 18.5, 18.5 to < 25, 25 to < 30, ≥ 30 kg/m2, or missing), smoking status (never, former, current, or missing), alcohol consumption (never, former, current, or missing), leisure-time physical activity (< 500, 500 to < 1000, ≥ 1000 MET-minutes/week, or missing), healthy diet (yes, no, or missing), and inflammatory diseases. The false discovery rate (FDR) was computed using the Benjamini-Hochberg (B-H) method to address type 1 error inflation resulting from multiple testing. A P value < 0.05 after B-H FDR adjustment was deemed statistically significant [7]. Further details of the study design and methods are described in the Supplementary Methods. Results

During a median follow-up period of 14.34 years (interquartile range [IQR], 13.56 to 15.07 years), 1789 IBD cases were identified, comprising 707 cases of CD and 1368 cases of UC. The associations of IBD and its two subtypes for each circulating metabolic biomarker are shown in Fig. 1. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, 26 of the 39 circulating metabolic biomarkers were found to be significantly associated with future IBD events (P < 0.05). Of these, per 1-SD increase in 7 metabolomic biomarkers was associated with increased risk of IBD, including phenylalanine (HR = 1.04, 95% CI = 1.00 to 1.07), creatinine (HR = 1.04, 95% CI = 1.00 to 1.09), lactate (HR = 1.07, 95% CI = 1.02 to 1.12), pyruvate (HR = 1.06, 95% CI = 1.01 to 1.11), glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA) (HR = 1.25, 95% CI = 1.20 to 1.31), omega-6 to omega-3 ratio (HR = 1.05, 95% CI = 1.01 to 1.09), and monounsaturated fatty acid percentage (MUFA percentage) (HR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.10 to 1.22). Conversely, other 19 metabolomic biomarkers from amino acids (e.g., HRhistidine = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.89 to 0.98), fluid balance (e.g., HRalbumin = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.77 to 0.85), cholesterol (e.g., HRtotal cholesterol = 0.82, 95% CI = 0.78 to 0.86), apolipoproteins (e.g., HRapoB = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.83 to 0.92), fatty acid ratios (e.g., HRDHA percentage = 0.91, 95% CI = 0.86 to 0.96), and fatty acids (e.g., HRPUFA = 0.88, 95% CI = 0.84 to 0.92) showed a most significant protective effect on the incidence of IBD. Notably, glycolysis-related metabolites, such as lactate and pyruvate, were only positively associated with risk of UC, but not with CD. By contrast, cholesterol and fatty acid–related metabolites, such as very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) cholesterol, saturated fatty acids (SFA), and total fatty acids were negatively associated with risk of CD, but not with UC. Discussion

This study identified 26 of the 39 circulating metabolic biomarkers significantly associated with risk of incident IBD. Taken together, 7 circulating metabolic biomarkers (e.g., lactate, GlycA, MUFA percentage) were associated with increased risk of IBD, while 19 biomarkers (e.g., histidine, albumin, PUFAs) were associated with decreased risk of IBD. We further found that fatty acids and cholesterol-related metabolites were more strongly inversely associated with CD risk than with UC. Stratified and sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of the results.

Our study found that GlycA was positively associated with IBD risk, whereas albumin and cholesterol exhibited inverse associations. Consistent with prior study, GlycA level was significantly elevated in patients with active IBD compared to healthy controls and correlate well with mucosal healing in both CD and UC [8]. Study has showed that IgG Galactosylation in IBD patients has decreased galactose residues compared to healthy controls [9]. This change is more pronounced in active disease and may correlate with inflammation severity. Consistent with previous findings, cholesterol levels were inversely associated with the risk of IBD, likely attributable to bile acid malabsorption and subsequent alterations in cholesterol metabolism [2, 10]. High-density lipoprotein (HDL) and apolipoprotein A-I (ApoA1) may exert anti-inflammatory effects by modulating lipid metabolism and immune responses, highlighting their therapeutic potential. Notably, ApoA1 mimetic peptides have been shown to ameliorate intestinal inflammation by neutralizing pro-inflammatory lipids and suppressing inflammatory pathways [11]. Our analysis reveals the complex role of fatty acid metabolism in IBD pathogenesis. An elevated omega-6/omega-3 ratio and a higher percentage of monounsaturated fatty acids were associated with increased IBD risk. Conversely, polyunsaturated fatty acids and omega-3 fatty acids, particularly DHA, showed clear protective effects. A large prospective cohort study found that long-term higher intake of omega-3 fatty acids was associated with a lower risk of UC [12]. The protective mechanism is believed to involve the production of pro-resolving lipid mediators such as resolvins and protectins, which actively terminate inflammation and promote tissue repair [13]. Furthermore, we observed that amino acid metabolites such as histidine were associated with reduced IBD risk. This is consistent with previous findings that reported significant alterations in serum amino acid profiles in CD patients [3]. The protective role of histidine, an amino acid with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant potential, warrants further functional investigation. The consistent associations observed across stratified analyses by sex, age, BMI, lifestyle (smoking, physical activity), and socioeconomic status (TDI) suggest that the identified relationship is broadly applicable and not confined to specific population segments. These findings hold translational promise. The identified biomarkers, particularly those related to glycolysis, inflammation, and fatty acid metabolism, could be explored as components of a future risk prediction panel for early identification of high-risk individuals. Furthermore, metabolites such as histidine and polyunsaturated fatty acids represent potential targets for dietary or pharmacological strategies aimed at IBD prevention.

Our study has several strengths, including a prospective population-based design, a relatively longer duration and large sample size, and comprehensive ascertainment of potential confounders. This study also has several limitations that should be considered. First, the over-representation of Caucasian individuals of the UK Biobank cohort constrains the generalizability of our findings to other racial and ethnic populations. This is particularly relevant given known ethnic differences in lipid metabolism, which may influence biomarker performance and clinical applicability across diverse populations. Second, while we adjusted for key confounding factors, residual bias may persist due to unmeasured comorbidities that might alter metabolic profiles. Third, the study was observational in design, precluding direct causal inference. Human metabolism is highly sensitive to numerous factors, including diet and physical activity; the use of a single measurement of metabolites from long-term cryopreserved baseline blood samples may introduce measurement error and preclude assessment of changes over time. Future study should incorporate repeated measurements to better characterize temporal patterns.

Conclusion

This large-scale prospective study identified 26 circulating metabolic biomarkers significantly associated with risk of incident IBD, enhancing our understanding of IBD pathogenesis and suggesting potential opportunities for predictive and therapeutic applications. Further molecular and genetic studies are required to establish the underlying causal mechanisms.
