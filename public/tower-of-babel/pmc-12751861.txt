Evaluation of three obturation techniques in 3D-printed models of oval canals with standardized prepared morphology: a micro-CT study
Authors: Wenjun Xia, Qisheng Gu, Yingshuang Song, Yunjia Liu, Xuetao Deng, Wenhao Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe complex and irregular morphology of prepared oval root canals poses optimal sealing in oval canals as a key endodontic challenge. To address this, this study utilized five types of standardized 3D-printed models replicating prepared anatomy of extracted teeth with oval canal, to characterize the morphological diversity of oval canals after preparation and evaluate the efficacy of three obturation techniques across different morphologies.MethodsFive standardized 3-dimensional printed models replicating prepared oval canal morphology were produced using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)-derived data from sixty extracted premolars, which had undergone standardized collection, screening, and in vitro preparation procedures. Three obturation techniques (n = 15 each) were evaluated: (1) lateral condensation with AH-Plus sealer (AHP-LC), (2) continuous wave vertical compaction with AH-Plus sealer (AHP-CWC), and (3) single-cone technique with iRoot SP sealer (SP-SC). Micro-CT scanning and volumetric analyses were employed to quantify the obturation quality. Statistical analysis was performed using Mann–Whitney U test, Kruskal–Wallis H test, Dunn’s test or Spearman rank’s correlation depending on the experimental design.ResultsMicro-CT analysis revealed significant differences in the percentage of void volume (PVV) among the obturation techniques. Over the entire canal length, SP-SC demonstrated significantly lower PVV compared to AHP-LC and AHP-CWC (p < 0.01). In the apical third, canal morphology significantly influenced the PVV of AHP-CWC (p < 0.05), with a more circular cross-section (higher roundness) correlating strongly with a lower PVV (p < 0.001). No significant effects of morphology were observed in other canal segments or with the other obturation techniques.ConclusionsSP-SC achieved more complete three-dimensional adaptation regardless of morphological variations in oval canals including varied diameter ratios, isthmus and recess. Conversely, AHP-CWC's performance was significantly dependent on canal morphology in the apical third, where more circular cross-sections correlated with lower void volumes.

Background

Successful root canal treatment requires effective access preparation, precise canal shaping, thorough elimination of intracanal infections, and comprehensive three-dimensional obturation of the root canal system [1]. Complete eradication of root canal infections remains unattainable due to anatomical complexity and microbial persistence [2]. This challenge is especially significant in oval canal anatomies. Post-preparation, these canals often retain unprepared buccal and lingual recesses, resulting in irregular morphologies that complicate 3D obturation and lead to unsealed voids [3, 4]. These voids facilitate residual bacterial proliferation, compromising treatment success, making optimal sealing in oval canals a key endodontic challenge.​

Currently, the predominant clinical root canal obturation techniques include lateral condensation (LC) as the conventional benchmark [5], continuous wave compaction (CWC) utilizing thermoplasticized gutta-percha for enhanced adaptation in irregular canal spaces, and the single-cone (SC) technique which has gained increasing adoption due to its procedural simplicity and compatibility with modern nickel-titanium instrumentation systems [6]. Gutta-percha used in conjunction with root canal sealers remains the gold standard material for root canal obturation [7]. Among these, epoxy resin-based sealers are considered the reference standard due to their well-documented physicochemical properties and have been widely used for decades with both LC and CWC techniques [8, 9]. In contrast, calcium silicate-based sealers demonstrate superior flowability that enhances their ability to penetrate complex anatomical features such as isthmuses and recesses. This characteristic, combined with the fact that their chemical stability may be compromised by thermal compaction makes them particularly suitable for use with SC technique [10].

Existing oval canal obturation studies rely on pre-preparation morphology (typically based on initial maximum-to-minimum diameter ratios) [11–13], ignoring preparation-induced changes that bias results. To address this, we developed standardized 3D-printed replicas from prepared cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans, enabling micro-CT void quantification [14, 15] and morphology-adapted comparison of three techniques under controlled anatomical conditions.

This study therefore aimed to determine if there are any statistically significant differences in the sealing efficacy among the three obturation techniques (lateral condensation with AH-Plus sealer, continuous wave vertical compaction with AH-Plus sealer and single-cone technique with iRoot SP sealer) when applied to oval canals with standardized morphologies. To achieve this, the research first characterized the prepared morphological diversity of oval canals and, based on this, established standardized 3D-printed models replicating key morphotypes to serve as the experimental substrate for the subsequent micro-CT-based comparison.

Methods

Collection and preparation of extracted teeth

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Xuhui District Stomatological Hospital (Approval No.XYF AF/SC-08/01.0). Sixty extracted single-rooted premolars were collected, disinfected in 10% H₂O₂ for 72 h, and CBCT-scanned (NewTom VGi evo, Verona, Italy; 110 kVp, 6 mA, 6 × 6 cm HiRes FOV, 150 μm voxel). Teeth were included based on two primary criteria: a single canal anatomy and an oval canal morphology, defined as a maximum-to-minimum canal diameter ratio > 2:1 at all three measured cross-sectional levels (4, 8, and 12 mm from the apex). All selected teeth met this criterion at all three levels, with actual ratios ranging from 2.02 to 5.25. Specimens exhibiting C-shaped configurations, severe curvature, canal obliteration, or an unclosed apical foramen were excluded. Selected teeth (n = 10) underwent standardized preparation: access cavities, working length determination using K-files (ISO 3630–1, Mani Inc., Tochigi, Japan), F4/F5 shaping with ProTaper Gold (Dentsply Sirona Endodontics, Charlotte, NC, USA), saline irrigation, and paper-point drying.

Analysis of prepared oval root canal morphology

The 10 prepared teeth underwent a second CBCT scan with the above settings. The characterization of prepared oval root canal morphological diversity was conducted through a systematic analysis of CBCT data. The analysis was performed independently by two experienced endodontists who were calibrated on the assessment criteria. The analysis involved evaluating cross-sectional images of the prepared oval canals at 1-mm intervals from the apex to the coronal opening. The assessment criteria included the outline of the prepared canal, the presence of recesses, isthmuses, and irregular areas. Any discrepancies between the two evaluators were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. From this analysis, five recurring morphological patterns were identified as being representative of the prepared oval canals. 3D-printed models of prepared oval root canals

Five standardized 3D root canal models were designed based on CBCT-derived morphology in Geomagic Wrap software (v2013, Raindrop Geomagic, Morrisville, NC, USA). Standardization included a 20-mm canal length, no curvature, a 0.4-mm apical diameter (size 40 instrument), and a 0.06 taper (Fig. 1). 3D models were fabricated by a ProJet 3510 HD printer (3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA) with VisiJet Crystal resin (16 μm layer thickness; 3D Systems). Nine replicates of each morphology type were produced (45 models total) to ensure sufficient samples for statistical comparisons.

In vitro root canal obturation

Three obturation techniques were compared employing the 45 3D-printed models: lateral condensation with AH-Plus sealer (AHP-LC group), continuous wave vertical compaction with AH-Plus sealer (AHP-CWC group), and single-cone technique with iRoot SP sealer (SP-SC group). Each technique was applied to the 5 morphology models, with 3 replicates per model type.

Lateral condensation technique (AHP-LC group)

AH-Plus (Dentsply Sirona Endodontics, Charlotte, NC, USA) was introduced ulitizing a Lentulo spiral filler (#30, 21 mm length; Mani Inc.). A master gutta-percha cone (#40/0.04 taper; Meta Biomed Co., Ltd., Cheongju, South Korea), coated with AH-Plus, was inserted to the working length (20-mm canal length). Finger spreaders (#25, 21 mm length; Mani Inc.) created space for accessory cones until penetration was limited to the transition zone(5-mm canal length). Excess gutta-percha was removed with an EQ-V heat carrier (Meta Biomed Co., Ltd.) heated to 200 °C and vertically compacted with a cold plugger.

Continuous wave vertical compaction technique (AHP-CWC group)

AH-Plus sealer was introduced as above. A master gutta-percha cone (#40/0.06 taper), coated with AH-Plus, was inserted to the working length. Gutta-percha was down-packed by an EQ-V heat carrier heated to 200 °C, leaving 4 mm apically. Heated gutta-percha (200 °C) was injected (2–3 mm) by the EQ-V Backfill (Meta Biomed Co., Ltd.) unit and compacted with a stainless steel plugger until the transition zone was filled.

Single-cone technique (SP-SC group)

iRoot SP (Innovative BioCeramix Inc., Vancouver, BC, Canada) was delivered ulitizing its manufacturer-provided syringe, with the tip inserted deeply and withdrawn while injecting to the transition zone. A master gutta-percha cone (#40/0.06 taper) was inserted to the working length. Excess gutta-percha was trimmed at the transition zone ulitizing an EQ-V heat carrier and compacted with a cold plugger.

After obturation, models were stored in a 100% humidity environment for 1 week to ensure material setting.

The three segmented components (canal space, obturation material, and voids) were converted to 3D surface meshes and exported as STL files. These files were imported into Geomagic Wrap 2021 for quantitative analysis. The root canal was divided into three segments: apical third, middle third, and coronal third, each 5 mm in length, totaling 15 mm. Quantification and statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted by R statistical software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The normality of the data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since the majority of subgroups did not conform to a normal distribution, all subsequent analyses were conducted using non-parametric methods. The median and 95% confidence interval were obtained using Hodges-Lehmann estimator and Wilcoxon rank-sum test respectively. The statistical significance of differences was assessed using Mann–Whitney U test, Kruskal–Wallis H test, Dunn’s test and Spearman rank’s correlation depending on the experimental design. Error bars represent standard deviation, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Five recurring morphological patterns were identified as being representative of the prepared oval canals: (1) Uniform Expansion (UFE, canal walls uniformly enlarged); (2) Central Expansion (CE, enlarged at the long-axis center, with recesses at both ends); (3) Unilateral Expansion (ULE, enlarged at one long-axis end, with a recess at the opposite end); (4) Bilateral Expansion with Short Isthmus (BE-SI, enlarged at both long-axis ends, with a short central isthmus; and (5) Bilateral Expansion with Long Isthmus (BE-LI, enlarged at both long-axis ends, with a long central isthmus due to a large long-axis diameter and high initial maximum-to-minimum diameter ratio).

Micro-CT analysis revealed differences in PVV across the entire root canal and within individual segments among three obturation techniques (Fig. 3, Table 1). Across the entire canal length and in the middle third, SP-SC exhibited significantly lower PVV compared to AHP-LC and AHP-CWC, with no significant difference between AHP-LC and AHP-CWC. In the apical third, SP-SC showed the lowest PVV, followed by AHP-LC, while AHP-CWC exhibited the highest PVV, with significant differences observed among all groups. In the coronal third, PVV did not differ significantly between SP-SC and AHP-CWC, but both were significantly lower than that of AHP-LC. Discussion

Studies report conflicting results on obturation techniques (LC/CWC/SC) for oval canals [11–13, 18–21], likely due to morphological variability in extracted teeth. Our observations revealed substantial prepared alterations in oval canals, including non-uniform wall expansion, variably positioned recesses, and unpredictable isthmus formations—all of which resist standardization in conventional extracted-tooth studies. Such uncontrolled anatomical variations introduce baseline inconsistencies that ultimately compromise the reliability and cross-study comparability of obturation evaluations. To address these challenges, this study developed standardized models based on prepared oval canal morphologies, minimized the influence of initial variations in extracted teeth (canal length, curvature, and diameter), as well as operator-induced errors during root canal preparation, while preserving the diverse morphologies that increase obturation difficulty.

The derivation of the five standardized models was grounded in a rigorous process that integrated evidence from both the literature and empirical data. First, a review of micro-CT studies on oval canal preparation was conducted to identify the morphological profiles that result from instrumentation [22–25]. This informed our goal to create models that are representative of the major morphological types documented in the field, including the clinically relevant scenario of preparing a single oval canal as two distinct canals to improve debridement [26]. Crucially, the validity of these defined morphologies was confirmed by their presence within our own sample set of prepared oval canals. Thus, the final 3D-printed models, illustrated in Fig. 1, are not merely abstract representations but are firmly grounded in established theoretical principles, closely aligned with clinical reality, and empirically validated through the analytical process of this study.

3D-printed models have demonstrated widespread and successful application in dentistry, particularly in the accurate simulation of dental hard tissues. This includes, but is not limited to, the simulation of immature teeth for trauma-related procedures like revitalization or apical plug placement [27], calcified teeth with varying degrees of calcification [28], and C-shaped canals [29, 30]. In this study, five distinct cross-sectional morphology types were identified from prepared oval canals. High-precision 3D-printed models served as substitutes for extracted teeth, standardizing the baseline and enhancing the clinical relevance of the research by accurately reflecting natural morphological diversity.

Roundness serves as a critical metric for preparation efficacy, with values approaching 1.0 indicating optimal circularity for gutta-percha adaptation [31]. In oval canals, prepared cross-sections consistently exhibited low roundness indices (0.38–0.55 in our samples), reflecting prevalent non-circular morphologies. This geometric mismatch with circular gutta-percha points directly increased void formation, as quantified by micro-CT analysis of AHP-CWC group in the apical segments. AHP-CWC relies on heating gutta-percha to soften it, followed by vertical compaction to conform to the canal’s shape. However, in canals with large non-circular cross-sections, even well-fitted master gutta-percha points require excessive deformation, exceeding AHP-CWC’s deformation capacity and leading to persistent voids, as observed in micro-CT cross-sections of UFE and BE-LI models (Fig. 2C). Although some sealer and gutta-percha entered isthmus regions in BE-LI models, significant voids remained. Additionally, heated gutta-percha, while expanding, contracts by 1–2% upon cooling, contributing to voids and gaps within the obturation [32].

In this study, the significant differences in sealing efficacy observed among the three obturation techniques lead to the rejection of the null hypothesis that no difference exists in their performance within oval canal anatomies. SP-SC group demonstrated superior obturation outcomes. Calcium silicate-based sealers (CSBS), exemplified by SP, exhibit a slight volume expansion upon setting, enhancing adaptation [33, 34]. The biocompatible and bioactive properties of CSBS enable ion exchange and interaction with the surrounding dentin, transforming their role from mere sealers to integral filling materials [35]. Gutta-percha acts as a carrier, enabling delivery, compaction, and retreatment, while compensating for its lack of adhesion and thermal shrinkage [36]. Manufacturers recommend the single-cone technique with CSBS, as heat-induced chemical changes in CSBS, epoxy resin-based, and zinc oxide-eugenol-based sealers support the compatibility of the SP-SC approach [37]. Clinical translation requires comprehensive canal preparation, accurate assessment of prepared morphology, and adjunctive tools such as microscopes or ultrasonic activation to reduce voids and prevent sealer extrusion [38]. These technical refinements—particularly the integration of dynamic sealer monitoring with activation technologies—represent critical research directions for achieving predictable 3D obturation outcomes. While SP-SC and AHP-CWC showed comparable PPV in the coronal third, AHP-CWC had a lower mean void volume. 3D reconstructions revealed bubble formation in SP sealer at this level. Thus, for the coronal segment: consider warm gutta-percha backfilling, or add auxiliary cones without lateral compaction to increase gutta-percha volume and reduce sealer bubbles.

This study has several limitations. First, the findings reflect the performance of integrated sealer-technique systems rather than isolated effects, as combinations were based on clinical and manufacturer guidelines. Second, while 3D-printed models standardize morphology and approximate dentin mechanics [39], they cannot replicate dentinal tubules or true sealer-dentin interfaces. Third, although transparent models facilitate visualization, they may not fully simulate clinical opaque canal conditions. Finally, despite the high precision of the 3D-printed models reducing inter-specimen variability, the use of triplicate samples remains a limitation for statistical power. Future studies with larger replicates are warranted to strengthen the conclusions.

Conclusion

SP-SC technique yielded significantly fewer voids compared to both AHP-CWC and AHP-LC in oval-shaped root canals, suggesting its superior sealing performance. Cross-sectional canal morphology significantly influenced AHP-CWC’s apical third obturation, with more circular configurations associated with improved filling quality.
