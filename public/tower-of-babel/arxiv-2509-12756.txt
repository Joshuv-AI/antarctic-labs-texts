The Power Contamination Problem on Grids Revisited: Optimality, Combinatorics, and Links to Integer Sequences
El-Mehdi Mehiri, Mohammed L. Nadji

Abstract
This paper presents a combinatorial study of the power contamination problem, a dynamic variant of power domination modeled on grid graphs. We resolve a conjecture posed by Ainouche and Bouroubi (2021) by proving it is false and instead establish the exact value of the power contamination number on grid graphs. Furthermore, we derive recurrence relations for this number and initiate the enumeration of optimal contamination sets. We prove that the number of optimal solutions for specific grid families corresponds to well-known integer sequences, including those counting ternary words with forbidden subwords and the large Schröder numbers. This work settles the fundamental combinatorial questions of the power contamination problem on grids and reveals its rich connections to classical combinatorics. Key words : Power domination, Power contamination, Grid graphs, Contamination number, Combinatorial enumeration, Integer sequences, Schröder numbers, Pattern avoidance. MSC : 05C69, 05C57, 05A15, 05C30, 92D30, 05A05.

Introduction

In graph theory, domination is a central concept with a wide range of applications, particularly in network design and theoretical computer science. The study of domination and its related subset problems is a major axis in the field and has been extensively investigated by many researchers. For a detailed introduction to domination, we refer the reader to Haynes et al.
[
10
]
. Throughout the history of the domination set problem, numerous variants have been introduced in the literature, such as connected domination, total domination, independent domination, and Roman domination, to name a few. One such variant finds applications in electric power networks. Companies operating these networks monitor their system states by observing variables such as voltage, which is achieved by placing measurement units (MUs) at selected locations in the network. Due to their high cost, the number of MUs placed must be optimized, making this problem both a combinatorial optimization and a graph theory challenge. This variant, known as power domination, was introduced by Haynes et al.
[
9
]
and has since been the subject of a series of studies, including
[
11
,
6
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
7
,
2
]
. Like the original domination set problem, the power domination problem has been proven to be NP-complete
[
8
]
.

In 2021, Ainouche and Bouroubi
[
1
]
, building on the concept of power domination, introduced the notion of power contamination, a dynamic variant of the latter parameter, applied to a grid
G(n,m)
. Note that the classical power domination number for rectangular grids was first determined by Dorfling and Henning
[
6
]
.

The objective in
[
1
]
is to determine the optimal cardinality of a contamination set
S
which, through vertex propagation, contaminates the entire grid. As part of their investigation, the authors in
[
1
]
proposed the following conjecture concerning the contamination number
\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))
of a grid graph
G(n,m)
, which aims to characterize its exact value for all positive integers

and

.

Conjecture 1

(Ainouche and Bouroubi, 2021)
.

Let
G(n,m)
be the

grid graph. For all integers

, we have

\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))=\begin{cases}\max\left\{\left\lfloor\dfrac{m}{2}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\dfrac{n}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}+1,&\text{if $m$ and $n$ have the same parity},\\[10.00002pt]
\max\left\{\left\lceil\dfrac{m}{2}\right\rceil,\left\lceil\dfrac{n}{2}\right\rceil\right\}+1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Analogously, a grid graph
G(n,m)
can be viewed as a set of living plant cells. For example, one may consider an
n\times m
arrangement of cells on a tobacco leaf, where each cell is either
healthy
or
contaminated
by the
Tobacco mosaic virus
(TMV). TMV is one of the most extensively studied plant viruses. It infects a wide range of host plants, particularly tobacco and other members of the family
Solanaceae
. The term
mosaic
reflects the distinctive mottling and discoloration that develops on infected leaves, producing a mosaic-like pattern. TMV was first described in the late 19th century and later identified as a virus in 1930. The similarity between the power contamination process on grid graphs and the biological spread of TMV arises from their local transmission dynamics. In plants, TMV does not rely on insect vectors for long-distance spread, as is the case for many other viruses. Instead, it propagates directly from one cell to its immediate neighbors through microscopic channels known as
plasmodesmata
. In this sense, each infected cell can be thought of as "contaminating" the adjacent cells in the grid, while healthy cells remain unaffected until contact with a contaminated neighbor. This cell-to-cell spread is analogous to the rules of power contamination in graph theory
[
1
]
, where the infection propagates across edges to neighboring vertices. Hence, the power contamination variant provides a useful mathematical abstraction to capture the local, contact-driven nature of TMV infection on a leaf surface. For more comprehensive introduction on Tobacco mosaic virus biology, we refer the reader to
[
15
,
12
]
.

Remark 1
.

We observe the following:

•

If
n=1
, the only applicable rule is rule (d).

•

If
n=2
, rules (a), (b), and (c) are not applicable.

•

Corner cells cannot be contaminated solely by using rules (e)–(h). In fact, each of the four corner cells has exactly one rule that can be used to contaminate it.

•

A cell on an edge of the grid can be contaminated only by using rules (c)–(h). In particular, each such cell has exactly three applicable rules. For example, a cell on the left-most column that is not a corner cell can be contaminated only by rules (c), (g), and (h).

•

Two contaminated cells positioned according to rule (a) or (b) will lead to a fully contaminated
G(3,3)
.

•

Two contaminated cells positioned according to rule (c) (respectively, rule (d)) will lead to a fully contaminated
G(3,1)
(respectively,
G(1,3)
).

•

Two contaminated cells positioned according to any of the rules (e)–(h) will lead to a fully contaminated
G(2,2)
.

Lemma 1
.

Each uncontaminated cell can be contaminated only if there are exactly two contaminated cells satisfying one of the contamination rules
(a)
–
(h)
with respect to it.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the definition of the contamination rules
(a)
–
(h)
, each of which requires precisely two contaminated cells in specific relative positions to contaminate a new cell.
∎

Lemma 2
.

If there exists at least one consecutive pair of columns (resp. rows) containing no contaminated cells, then the power contamination process cannot result in a fully contaminated grid.

Proof.

Suppose there is a pair of consecutive columns (resp. rows) with no contaminated cells. By the contamination rules
(a)
–
(h)
, contaminating a new cell requires exactly two contaminated cells in specific relative positions. In this case, none of the rules can be applied to contaminate any cell within these two consecutive columns (resp. rows), even if both are adjacent to fully contaminated columns (resp. rows). Therefore, at least these two columns (resp. rows) will remain uncontaminated at the end of the process. An example of this case is illustrated in Figure

Lemma 3
.

If the set of contaminated cells forms a perfect rectangle, then no further cells can be contaminated, and the process terminates.

Proof.

When the contaminated cells form a perfect rectangle, every uncontaminated cell lies entirely outside this rectangle. According to the contamination rules
(a)
–
(h)
, each new contaminated cell must be adjacent to exactly two contaminated cells in a specific configuration. Since no such configuration exists outside the rectangle, no rule can be applied to contaminate an additional cell. Therefore, the process stops. An example of this case is illustrated in Figure

Lemma 6
.

Let
G(m,m)
be a square grid. If the initial contaminated set of cells lies on the main diagonal of the grid, i.e.,

S=\{(i,i)\mid 1\leq i\leq m\},

Proof.

Starting from the main diagonal, rules
(a)

(h)

Lemma 7
.

Let
G(n,m)
be a rectangular grid. Consider an initial contaminated set of cells
S
forming a zig-zag path that starts from the top-left cell, descends to the bottom row of the grid at an angle of
\pi/4
, then ascends to the top row at the same angle, and repeats this pattern across the grid. Such an initial configuration will lead to the entire grid becoming contaminated.

Proof.

We partition the grid into multiple square subgrids, each corresponding to a single “zig” or “zag” segment of the path.
By Lemma
6
, each such subgrid becomes fully contaminated, resulting in a completely contaminated rectangular block (a “perfect contaminated rectangle”).

If there remains at least one column to the right of the current contaminated rectangle that is not yet fully contaminated, we apply Lemma

Repeating this process step by step eventually contaminates all remaining columns, thereby contaminating the entire grid. An example of this case is illustrated in Figure

Lemma 8
.

If the number of columns
m
is odd, then in any optimal contamination
configuration all contaminated cells are located in columns of odd index.
Equivalently, no contaminated cell appears in a column with an even index.

Proof.

Due to the contamination rules, any contaminated cell in an even column can be shifted one step left to the adjacent odd column without reducing its effect. Repeating this for all such cells yields an optimal configuration supported entirely on odd columns.
∎

Proposition 2

For every integer

\gamma_{c}(G(1,m))=\gamma_{c}(G(1,m-4))+2,

The cells

Proposition 5
.

For all integers
m\geq n\geq 0
, we have

\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))\geq\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor+1.

(3)

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that

\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))<\gamma_{c}(G(1,m))=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor+1.

Without loss of generality, let

\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor.

By Corollary
1
, the first and last columns must each contain at least one contaminated cell in the initial set. Thus, the remaining number of contaminated cells to be placed in columns
2,\ldots,m-1
is

\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))-2=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor-2.

If each of these
m-2
columns contains at most one contaminated cell, then the number of columns among
2,\ldots,m-1
with no contaminated cell is

(m-2)-\left(\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor-2\right)=m-\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor.

By the pigeonhole principle (viewing each pair of consecutive columns as a “hole” and contaminated cells as “pigeons”), it is impossible to arrange
m-\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor
empty columns among the
m-2
columns without having at least one pair of consecutive empty columns.

By Lemma
2
, the presence of such a pair of consecutive uncontaminated columns implies that the process cannot lead to a fully contaminated grid.

This contradiction shows that our assumption

\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))<\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor+1

is false. Therefore, the stated lower bound holds.
∎

Corollary 2
.

For all integers
m\geq n\geq 0
, we have

\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))\leq\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor+1.

(4)

Proof.

In the construction of

,

It remains to treat the case

Proposition 8
.

For all integers
m\geq 1
, we have

\alpha_{1,m}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $m$ is odd},\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{m}{2},&\text{if $m$ is even}.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
m
is odd, there is only one optimal solution: the one where the cells in odd positions are contaminated and those in even positions are not.

If
m
is even, we observe that the leftmost and rightmost cells must both be contaminated. Therefore, we need to determine the positions of the remaining
\gamma_{c}(G(1,m))-2
contaminated cells among the
m-2
remaining positions, such that no two uncontaminated cells are adjacent.

Since
m
is even, we have

\gamma_{c}(G(1,m))=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor+1=\frac{m}{2}+1.

The number of uncontaminated cells is then
m-\gamma_{c}(G(1,m))=\frac{m}{2}-1
.

The count of ways to choose these uncontaminated cells with no two adjacent among the
m-2
available positions is

\binom{(m-2)-\left(\frac{m}{2}-1\right)+1}{\frac{m}{2}-1}=\binom{\frac{m}{2}}{\frac{m}{2}-1}=\frac{m}{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 9
.

For all integers
k\geq 0
, we have

\alpha_{2,2k}=k\cdot 2^{k}.

Proof.

Consider a grid of
n=2
rows and
m=2k
columns, with
k\geq 0
.
We call a column
clean
if it contains no contaminated cells.

From Lemma
2
, we know that no pair of consecutive columns can be fully clean in an optimal distribution of contaminated cells. Moreover, since
\gamma_{c}(G(2,2k))=k+1
, there must be exactly

2k-(k+1)=k-1

clean columns in an optimal distribution.

By Lemma
5
, the first and the last columns cannot be clean. Thus, the only possible clean columns are among the subcolumns
2,3,\dots,2k-1
.

Choosing
k-1
non-consecutive clean columns from these
2k-2
candidates is equivalent to counting the number of words of length
2k-2
over the alphabet
\{a,b\}
with exactly
k-1
occurrences of
a
and no two
a
’s consecutive.
This number is given by

\binom{(2k-2)-(k-1)+1}{k-1}=\binom{k}{k-1}=k.

On the other hand, since there are
k-1
non-consecutive clean columns, there must be exactly one pair of consecutive contaminated columns among the remaining
k+1
columns.

Each of these two consecutive contaminated columns contains exactly one contaminated cell. By Lemma
5
, both the upper and the lower row of the grid must contain at least one contaminated cell. Therefore, the two contaminated cells must be placed diagonally (upper cell in the left column and lower cell in the right column, or vice versa). This yields
2
possible configurations.

For each of the remaining
k-1
contaminated columns, the contaminated cell can be chosen arbitrarily (either the upper or the lower cell), giving
2^{k-1}
possibilities.

Consequently, the total number of optimal distributions is

\alpha_{2,2k}=k\cdot 2\cdot 2^{k-1}=k\cdot 2^{k}.\qed

.

\alpha_{2,0}=0,\alpha_{2,2}=2;\quad\forall k\geq 2:\quad\alpha_{2,2k}=2\alpha_{2,2k-2}+2^{k}.

(8)

Proof.

This follows directly from Proposition

.

Proposition 11
.

Consider a grid of
n=2
rows and
m=2k+1
columns, with
k\geq 0
.
Then, the number of optimal solutions with exactly
k

(k+1)\,2^{k}.

Proof.

Suppose first that the optimal solution contains
k
clean columns.
Similarly to the proof of Proposition
9
, the number of ways of choosing
k

2k-1

\binom{(2k+1-2)-k+1}{k}=\binom{k}{k}=1.

In this configuration, the
free cell
can only be placed in one of the

k+1

The column containing the free cell becomes fully contaminated (both upper and lower cells are contaminated).

Among the remaining
k
contaminated columns (those with exactly one contaminated cell), each can be chosen freely in the upper or lower row. Since the fully contaminated column already guarantees the condition of Lemma

, the other
k
columns can be chosen independently, giving
2^{k}
possibilities.

Consequently, the number of optimal solutions in this case is

1\cdot(k+1)\cdot 2^{k}=(k+1)2^{k}.\qed

Proposition 12
.

For every integer
k\geq 1
,

\alpha_{3,\,2k+1}\;=\;\texttt{A193519}(k+1),

where
A193519(n)
counts ternary words of length
n-1
over
\{1,2,3\}
that contain at least one of the subwords
13
or
31
.

Proof.

By Lemma
8
, when
m=2k+1
is odd, any optimal distribution on
G(3,m)
has exactly one contaminated cell in each odd column and none in the even columns. Hence an optimal distribution is determined by the row positions of the contaminated cells in columns
1,3,5,\dots,2k+1
.

Encode such a distribution by the ternary word

w=i_{0}i_{1}\cdots i_{k}\in\{1,2,3\}^{k+1},

where
i_{j}\in\{1,2,3\}
is the row index of the unique contaminated cell in the
(2j+1)
-st column.

Now, we prove the claim: A distribution is optimal in
G(3,2k+1)
for
k\geq 1
if and only if its associated word
w
contains
13
or
31
as a subword.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose the associated word
w
avoids both
13
and
31
. Then between any two consecutive odd columns the row index never jumps from
1
to
3
nor from
3
to
1
. In particular, all consecutive indices lie in
\{1,2\}
or all in
\{2,3\}
(or remain constant). In the

grid this forbids the creation of the necessary

“seed” (adjacent contaminated cells forming a rectangle) that triggers growth into the even columns; consequently no further contamination occurs beyond the initially marked cells, contradicting optimality. Hence every optimal distribution must yield a word containing
13
or
31
.

(
\Leftarrow
) Conversely, assume
w
contains
13
or
31
at positions
t,t+1
. Then in columns
2t+1
and
2t+3
the unique contaminated cells occupy rows
1
and
3
(in some order). This creates, together with the central even column
2t+2
, the configuration that initiates contamination to fill that even column and then propagates column-by-column. Because every odd column already has exactly one contaminated cell, this propagation completes the contamination of
G(3,2k+1)
using exactly
\gamma_{c}(G(3,2k+1))
initial cells, i.e., an optimal distribution.

Thus we have a bijection between optimal distributions on
G(3,2k+1)
and ternary words of length
k+1
that contain the subword

\alpha_{3,\,2k+1}=\#\{w\in\{1,2,3\}^{k+1}:\ w\text{ contains }13\text{ or }31\}=\texttt{A193519}(k+1).\qed

Proposition 16
.

For all integers
m\geq 1
, we have

\beta_{1,m}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\binom{\frac{m-1}{2}}{k}=2^{\frac{m-1}{2}},&\text{if $m$ is odd;}\\[11.99998pt]

Proof.

If

with exactly
\gamma_{c}(G(1,m))
contaminated cells. Any solution obtained from this optimal configuration by adding one or more contaminated cells is feasible.

In this optimal configuration, the number of uncontaminated cells is

m-\gamma_{c}(G(1,m))=m-\left(\frac{m-1}{2}+1\right)=\frac{m-1}{2}.

Choosing

uncontaminated cells to contaminate produces a distinct feasible solution.
Thus, the total number of feasible solutions is

\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\binom{\frac{m-1}{2}}{k}=2^{\frac{m-1}{2}}.

If
m
is even, then any optimal solution has exactly
\gamma_{c}(G(1,m))=\frac{m}{2}+1
contaminated cells, leaving

m-\gamma_{c}(G(1,m))=\frac{m}{2}-1
uncontaminated cells in the grid.

Let
k\in\{0,\dots,\frac{m}{2}-1\}
be the number of uncontaminated cells in a feasible solution.
For the grid to become fully contaminated, uncontaminated cells cannot be placed in the first or last columns, and no two uncontaminated cells can be consecutive.
Thus, we are counting the number of ways to place
k
non-adjacent uncontaminated cells in
m-2
available positions.

By a standard stars-and-bars argument, the number of ways to place
k
such cells is

\binom{(m-2)-k+1}{k}=\binom{m-k-1}{k}.

Summing over
k
gives

\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{m}{2}-1}\binom{m-k-1}{k},

which is well known to equal the Fibonacci number
F_{m/2}
.
∎

Proposition 17
.

For all integers
n,m\geq 1
, we have

\beta_{n,m}=2^{\,nm-\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))}\,\alpha_{n,m}.

(12)

Proof.

In any optimal distribution, there are exactly
\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))
contaminated cells, hence

nm-\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))

uncontaminated cells remain.
To form a feasible solution, we may add
k
extra contaminated cells, where
k
can range from
0
up to
nm-\gamma(G(n,m))
.

For each optimal distribution, the number of ways to choose which
k
of the uncontaminated cells become contaminated is

\binom{nm-\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))}{k}.

Summing over all possible
k
gives

\sum_{k=0}^{nm-\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))}\binom{nm-\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))}{k}=2^{\,nm-\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))}.

Since there are
\alpha_{n,m}
optimal distributions, multiplying yields

\beta_{n,m}=2^{\,nm-\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))}\,\alpha_{n,m}.\qed

5
Conclusion and Perspectives

This work has provided a complete combinatorial characterization of the power contamination problem on grid graphs. We conclusively disproved the existing conjecture regarding the contamination number
\gamma_{c}(G(n,m))
and determined its exact value for all grid dimensions. Beyond this, we developed recurrence relations for this parameter and initiated a systematic enumeration of optimal solutions, uncovering surprising and profound connections with classical combinatorial objects and sequences, such as the large Schröder numbers.

This analysis opens several compelling avenues for future research. A natural extension involves analyzing the contamination speed, which refers to the number of propagation steps required to fully contaminate the grid from an optimal set. This would add a dynamic, time-sensitive dimension to the problem. Furthermore, generalizing this analysis to other graph classes, such as tori, hexagonal grids, or hypercubes, or to weighted settings where contamination probabilities vary, could yield significant new insights. The intriguing combinatorial connections we uncovered, particularly the link to pattern-avoiding permutations described in our conjectures, demand further exploration through direct bijective proofs. Finally, while the problem is NP-complete in general, our exact results on grids may inform the development of efficient algorithms or approximation schemes for specific graph topologies. The interplay between this graph-theoretic model and other areas of mathematics and computational biology suggests that the power contamination problem is a fertile ground for further investigation.
