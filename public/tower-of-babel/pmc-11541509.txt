One-Pot Hybridization of Microfibrillated Cellulose and Hydroxyapatite as a Versatile Route to Eco-Friendly Mechanical Materials
Authors: Yui Okuda, Ryosuke Sasaki, Eiichi Kido, Ken Hirota, Tadashi Mizutani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Hydroxyapatite was crystallized in an alkaline dispersion of mechanically fibrillated cellulose to prepare their composites with hydroxyapatite contents of 26–86 wt %. The composite powder was uniaxially pressed at 120 °C and 300 MPa to obtain the compacts. Three-point bending tests revealed that the bending strengths of the compacts were 40–100 MPa, and the elastic moduli were 4–9 GPa. The composite containing 43% hydroxyapatite showed the largest bending strength and the largest work of fracture, and the composite containing 62% hydroxyapatite showed the largest elastic modulus. The composites, derived from the bioderived eco-friendly materials, showed the mechanical properties comparable to those of engineering plastics such as polyamide-6. Scanning electron microscopic observation of the fracture surface showed that the organic phase was discontinuous when the hydroxyapatite weight fraction was increased from 43% to 62%, and the compacts with a discontinuous organic phase lost toughness.

Introduction

Cellulose and bone are the two major biochemically produced structural materials. Bone is the composite of the nanocrystals of calcium phosphate and the collagen fibers, and its stiffness and toughness are benchmarks of future eco-friendly materials. A number of studies aim at the development of organic–inorganic composites showing both stiffness and toughness similar to those of bone and teeth.1−5 Bone consists of carbonated apatite (nanometer-sized calcium phosphate crystals), collagen I fibers, and water in a volume ratio of approximately 56–60:30–40:10.6−8 Carbonated apatite crystals have dimensions of 50 nm in length, 25 nm in width, and 1–4 nm in thickness.9 One of the key features of bone and teeth is the brick-and-mortar structure,10−16 where the brittleness of inorganic crystals (brick) is drastically improved by the continuous organic phase (mortar). Cooperation of the two phases can realize both stiffness and toughness of the composite using versatile constituents such as peptides, polysaccharides, calcium ions, and phosphate/carbonate ions. Materials consisting of such eco-friendly constituents would solve problems such as CO2 emission, fossil resource depletion, and microplastics in seawater.9

The composite of nanosized fillers and polymers has been found to exhibit excellent mechanical properties through numerous studies. It has been found that factors such as the fiber structure of the polymer, the orientation of the fibers, and the aspect ratio of the fillers are critical in determining the mechanical properties. For example, Zhu and co-workers reported that the composite of hydroxyapatite and aligned bacterial cellulose exhibited an elastic modulus of 10.9 GPa.17 Peng and co-workers prepared the composite film of aligned bacteria cellulose and HAP and showed that the bending strength and the elastic modulus were 1170 MPa and 38 GPa, respectively.18 Walther et al. prepared the composite film of various phyllosilicates, nanosheets, and hydroxyapatite. They demonstrated that the composite of mica and poly(vinyl alcohol) showed a tensile strength of 200 MPa and an elastic modulus of 40 GPa.19 In this study, the aspect ratios of the inorganic filler controlled the mechanical behavior of the composite film. Mica, a high aspect ratio filler, gave a stiff but fragile film, while hectorite, a low aspect ratio filler, gave a tougher film. There are several studies on nanocomposites exhibiting excellent mechanical properties.20−22

To prepare the composite of nanometer-sized hydroxyapatite (HAP) crystals and polymers, the bottom-up approach is efficient and promising. The polymer can act as a nucleation site for the HAP crystals and control the size and the orientation of the HAP crystals. One of the simple protocols for the preparation of HAP-polymer nanocomposite is the coprecipitation of polymer and HAP crystals from an aqueous solution.23 Previously, we reported that TEMPO-oxidized cellulose nanofibers (TCNFs) were coprecipitated with HAP to obtain the composite, which showed nonbrittle fracture.24 The compacts were bent but did not break into two pieces in the bending test. The composite displayed both stiffness (with an elastic modulus of 11.6 GPa)25 and toughness. TCNFs are chemically fibrillated cellulose and have a number of −COO– groups that can assist in the nucleation of the HAP crystals and also bind to the surface of the HAP crystals. If we can prepare a similar composite using more readily available mechanically fibrillated cellulose, the composite will be employed in wider applications such as substitutes for plastics and possibly artificial bones.

In this study, we used microfibrillated cellulose (MFC) as a starting material.17,26−28 Cellulose is a bioderived, biodegradable, and low carbon footprint material derived from renewable resources. The use of cellulose as a structural material is thus eco-friendly. Cellulose is potentially stiff: the longitudinal elastic modulus of crystalline cellulose is 137 GPa.29 The structural difference between MFC and TCNF is that the former has a diameter of 0.1 to several μm, while the latter has a diameter of 3–4 nm. The latter has a number of −COO– groups on the surface of the nanofibers, while the former has no ionic functionalities. In the hybridization process, the anionic functional groups of the polymer concentrate Ca2+, which assist in the nucleation of HAP and binding to HAP crystals. An important role of the anionic groups of the polymer in hybridization with hydroxyapatite has been documented in literature.30−35 Although MFC has no ionic functionalities, we expect that MFC can be a promising candidate for the polymer if the hydroxy groups of MFC are ionized under alkaline conditions of crystallization of HAP and act as a nucleation site of HAP crystallization.36,37 We performed a systematic investigation of hybridization of MFC and HAP, changing the HAP:MFC feed ratios from 30:70 to 96:4 and the reaction temperatures ranging from room temperature to 90 °C, and the mechanical properties of the resulting composites were evaluated.

Experimental Section

Materials

Microfibrillated cellulose, 10 wt % in water, Celish KY100G, was provided by Daicel Corporation, Japan. Calcium chloride dihydrate, disodium hydrogen phosphate, sodium hydroxide, and acetone were purchased from the FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation.

Instrumentation

Thermogravimetric and differential thermal analysis (TG-DTA) was performed with a Shimadzu DTA-60 thermal analysis instrument. The sample was heated in an alumina pan from 30 to 100 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min, kept at 100 °C for 10 min, heated to 1000 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min, and kept at 1000 °C for 10 min. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were carried out either with a Hitachi High-technology SU8020 field emission scanning microscope (Japan) or with a Hitachi TM3030Plus microscope.

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) of the composite powder was recorded on a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. Crystallite size of HAP, D, was calculated from the full-width of the diffraction peak at half of the maximum intensity (fwhm) of (002) and (310) peaks using the Scherrer equation: D = 0.9λ/(B cos θ), where λ is the wavelength of the X-ray, B measured in radians is the full-width at half-maximum of the 2θ peak, and θ is the Bragg angle for the peak.

Preparation of a MFC-HAP Composite with a Feed Ratio of HAP:MCF (50:50)

Microfibrillated cellulose 10 wt % in water (23.3 g) was mixed with 0.2 M Na2HPO4 (69.7 mL) and 1 M NaOH (18.6 mL), and the resultant suspension was stirred at room temperature, 50, 70, or 90 °C for 30 min. Then, 0.2 M CaCl2 (116 mL) was added dropwise at a rate of 0.06 mL/s at the same temperature, and the suspension was stirred for 1 h at the same temperature. After being cooled to room temperature, acetone (253 mL) was added, and the white precipitate was collected by suction filtration. The white powder was washed with water/acetone (1:1, 1 L). Finally, acetone (200 mL) was used to wash the powder. The obtained white powder was dried in vacuo at 80 °C for 2 h. The white powder was pulverized in a mortar.

MFC-HAP composites with the feed ratio of HAP (ICfeed) of 30% were similarly prepared using 54.36 g of 10 wt % aqueous dispersion of MFC, 0.2 M Na2HPO4 (69.7 mL), 1 M NaOH (18.6 mL), and 0.2 M CaCl2 (116 mL). MFC-HAP composites with ICfeed of 70% were similarly prepared using 10.00 g of 10 wt % aqueous dispersion of MFC, 0.2 M Na2HPO4 (69.7 mL), 1 M NaOH (18.6 mL), and 0.2 M CaCl2 (116 mL). MFC-HAP composites with an ICfeed of 96% were similarly prepared using 1.00 g of 10 wt % aqueous dispersion of MFC, 0.2 M Na2HPO4 (69.7 mL), 1 M NaOH (18.6 mL), and 0.2 M CaCl2 (116 mL). For the amount of acetone, 375 mL of acetone was used for the preparation of the composites with 30 wt % HAP.

Characterization of the Composite Powder

The inorganic content of the composite (ICobs), the weight fraction of HAP in the composite, was determined by TG-DTA. ICobs was calculated as a ratio of the weight at 1000 °C to that at 100 °C. Powder XRD patterns were recorded to determine the calcium phosphate crystal phase and the cellulose crystal phase.

Densification of the MFC-HAP Composite Powder

The MFC-HAP composite powder was placed in a tungsten carbide mold of 4 × 13 mm2 and pressed at 300 MPa at 120 °C for 5 min to obtain a compact of 4 × 13 × 1.5–1.8 mm3. The bulk density of the compacts (dbulk) was determined from the ratio of the sample weight and the volume of the rectangular parallelepiped. The compacts were subjected to three-point bending tests at a cross head speed of 0.5 mm/min. Results and Discussion

Synthesis of the Composites of Microfibrillated Cellulose and Hydroxyapatite

Hybridization of microfibrillated cellulose (MFC) and hydroxyapatite was carried out in a one-pot process. MFC was dispersed in an aqueous solution of Na2HPO4 and NaOH, and the dispersion was heated at 25–90 °C for 30 min. Then, aqueous CaCl2 was added dropwise. As shown in Figure 1, heating of MFC aqueous dispersion in alkali would lead to partial defibrillation, and the addition of calcium ions would induce formation of HAP on the surface of MFC. By adjusting the weight ratios of MFC and the inorganic ions, we prepared four composites with the feed inorganic contents of 30, 50, 70, and 96 wt %. These values of the inorganic content (ICfeed) are the feed ratios, and the actual values of the inorganic content (ICobs) were determined from the TG traces. As shown in Table 1, the values of ICobs were smaller than those of ICfeed, and the differences between them became larger as ICfeed values were larger. The smaller values of ICobs than the values of ICfeed at high ICfeed indicate that homogeneous nucleation of hydroxyapatite occurs at a high ICfeed. The particles of hydroxyapatite crystals formed by homogeneous nucleation were not bound to MFC and some of them were not collected by filtration due to their small particle size. The values of ICobs determined by TG were almost constant in the crystallization temperature range of 25–90 °C. We denote the composite with ICfeed of a % and the crystallization temperature of b °C as C-a%-boC.

Fabrication of the composites of hydroxyapatite and microfibrillated cellulose. As shown in Table 1, the isolated to theoretical mass ratios were almost 100%, showing that all of the added substances were quantitatively incorporated in the composites.

Characterization of the Composite Powder

The powder XRD patterns of the composite of C-50% are shown in Figure 2. The diffraction peaks of HAP were observed in the 2θ range of 25° to 40° as indexed in black, and the diffraction peaks of cellulose Iβ were observed at 2θ = 14.7° (1–10), 16.4° (110), and 22.6° (200) and are indexed in red.39 The diffraction peaks of HAP were observed even when crystallization was carried out at room temperature. As the crystallization temperature was higher, the diffraction peaks became sharper, showing that crystal growth was facilitated at higher temperatures. If cellulose II is produced by mercerization, diffraction peaks at 2θ = 12.0° and 20.0° should appear.39 We did not observe the diffraction peaks of cellulose II in any of the XRD patterns of the composites with feed ratios of 30, 50, 70, and 96% and crystallization temperatures ranging from room temperature to 90°. Therefore, alkaline treatment at various temperatures did not afford cellulose II in our experiments. The XRD patterns of C-30%, C-70%, and C-96% are shown in Figure S1.

Powder XRD patterns of C-50% and the starting material MFC. The indices of the diffraction peaks of HAP are shown in black and those of cellulose I are shown in red.

The degree of crystallinity of cellulose was estimated from the Segal crystallinity index (CI).40 CI was obtained from the XRD intensity at 23° (I23) of the crystalline cellulose and the XRD intensity at 18° (I18) of amorphous cellulose: CI = (I23 – I18)/I23. As shown in Figure 3, the CI values of the composites were smaller with increasing crystallization temperature, showing that alkaline treatment at higher temperatures caused partial amorphization of cellulose crystallite. However, for C-70%, the CI values increased from 30 to 50 °C. This peculiar behavior indicates that the crystallization of HAP leads to higher crystallinity of cellulose owing to the interaction between HAP and cellulose crystals.

Plot of the CI of cellulose against the crystallization temperatures. The CI of the starting MFC was 88%, and shown in the dotted line.

The crystallite sizes of hydroxyapatite in the composites were determined from the peak widths of (002) and (310) of XRD and plotted against the crystallization temperature in Figure 4. The crystallite sizes along the c-axis direction of C-30%-70oC, C-50%-70oC, and C-70%-70oC were smaller than that of HAP crystallized in the absence of MFC. Therefore, MFC suppressed crystallization of HAP along the c-axis.

We used the crystal density of HAP, dHAP, of 3.16 g/cm3 and that of cellulose crystal Iβ, dcellulose, of 1.63 g/cm3 to calculate ϕ.8,41 We found that the values of porosity increased with increasing ICobs. They also increase with increasing the crystallization temperatures. As the crystallization temperatures are higher, the HAP crystals will grow and the volume of the interfiber space will increase. The SEM image of the fractured surface of compact C-50%-70oC (Figure S8) shows that there are areas with voids between the cellulose fibers.

The compacts were subjected to three-point bending tests to evaluate the bending strength, the elastic modulus, the strain at failure, and the work of fracture. These mechanical properties are illustrated in Figure 6 as functions of ICobs. The mechanical properties of the compact of MFC alone are shown in Table S2. Comparison of these mechanical properties showed that the strength and elastic modulus increased due to the composite formation with apatite and that the apatite acted as a reinforcing material. The SEM images shown in Figure S8 illustrate that HAP is present between the cellulose fibers, and this structure explains how HAP functions as a reinforcing material. The bending strengths and the elastic moduli are comparable to those of carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC)-HAP composites (the bending strengths of 20–110 MPa and the elastic moduli of 4–8 GPa),42 although the densification conditions are not identical. Thus, cellulose with and without anionic functionality exhibited a similar strength and stiffness. The bending strengths of C-50% and C-70% were 60–100 MPa, which were smaller than that of polyamide-6 (118 MPa).43 The elastic moduli were 7–9 GPa, larger than that of polyamide-6 (2.8 GPa). Therefore, the composites were stiffer but more brittle than polyamide-6. The bending strength, the elastic modulus, the yield strain, and the fracture energy of bovine cortical bone were reported to be 57–308 MPa, 6.4–17.3 GPa, 1.03–1.68%, and 200–7000 J/m2.44−46 The properties of our composites shown in Figure 6 are thus comparable to those of the bovine cortical bone.

The mechanical properties did not depend strongly on the crystallization temperatures, but there is a trend that the bending strengths and the elastic moduli were largest for the composites crystallized at 50 or 70 °C (Figure S5). The mechanical properties of the composites were strongly dependent on the feed ratios of HAP. The bending strengths decreased in the order C-50% > C-70% > C-30% > C-96%. The elastic moduli decreased in the order C-70% > C-50% > C-96% > C-30%. This order indicates that HAP act as a filler to enhance the stiffness of the compacts when the HAP feed ratios are lower than 70 wt %, but further addition of HAP beyond 70 wt % did not enhance the stiffness. A large fraction of HAP in the composite would prevent the formation of the brick-and-mortar structure in the composite. The strains at failure and the work of fracture, a measure of the toughness of the compacts, decreased in the order, C-50% > C-30% > C-70% ≫ C-96%. It is noteworthy that the strain at failure of C-50% was larger than that of C-30%, despite C-50% being stiffer than C-30%. The compacts of C-96% exhibited the lowest toughness or pronounced brittleness. We can summarize the effects of IC and crystallization temperature on the mechanical properties as follows. For the crystallization temperature, the composites coprecipitated at 50 or 70 °C showed high stiffness and toughness. For IC, C-70% showed the highest stiffness, and C-50% also showed high stiffness. For the toughness, the composite C-50% showed the largest work of fracture, i.e., the highest toughness.

The porosity of C-50% changed from 14% to 33% when the crystallization temperature was varied from 25 to 90 °C (Figure 2). In contrast, the bending strength and the elastic modulus of C-50% ranged from 82 to 98 MPa and from 7 to 8 GPa, respectively, regardless of the crystallization temperature varying from 25 to 90 °C, indicating that they do not significantly depend on the crystallization temperature. We can conclude that the porosity of the molded compact is not a major factor in determining its mechanical strength.

The fracture surfaces of the compacts after the three-point bending tests are shown in Figure 7. For the fracture surfaces of C-30%, C-50%, and C-70%, fibrous structures of cellulose as seen from the carbon distribution shown in red are clearly observed. The fracture surface of C-96% (Figure 7d) was flat, reflecting its brittleness. As the HAP feed ratios increase, the fracture surface area containing calcium increased. In C-30% and C-50%, the cellulose phase seemed to be continuous, while in C-70% and C-96%, it seemed to be isolated. According to the brick-and-mortar model of bioceramics, a continuous organic phase is preferred. As the organic phase is isolated with increasing HAP weight fractions, brittleness of the composites is noted.

Cross-sectional SEM images (left) and elemental mapping (calcium in blue and carbon in red) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV (right) of the fracture surface of the compacts. (a) C-30%-70oC, (b) C-50%-70oC, (c) C-70%-70oC, and (d) C-96%-70oC.

In Figure 8, the atomic ratios of Ca/C of the fracture surface determined by EDS analysis are plotted against the crystallization temperatures. The Ca/C ratios agreed well to those calculated from the bulk HAP/cellulose ratios based on ICobs, that are shown in broken lines in Figure 8. For C-70%-70oC and C-70%-90oC, the ratios were larger than those in the bulk phase. This observation indicates that C-70% and C-96% tend to break preferentially at the inorganic phase. As shown in Figure 7, the organic phase becomes a discontinuous phase with increasing HAP fractions, and the brittle fracture of C-70% and C-96% can be attributed to this structure.

The dotted lines represent the calculated Ca/C based on the following equation: Ca/C = 10 AGU·ICobs /(6 HAP (100 – ICobs)), where AGU is the molecular weight of the anhydrous glucose unit (162), HAP is the formula weight of HAP (1005), and ICobs is the inorganic content (%) of the composites.

The MFC-HAP composites were not broken into two pieces in the three-point bending tests, a similar fracture mode to that of the TCNF-HAP composites.24 In contrast, the composite of carboxymethyl cellulose and HAP was brittle and broken into two pieces in the bending test.42 These observations suggest that the fibrous structure of cellulose rather than the ionic functionality of cellulose played an important role in the nonbrittle fracture of the composite.

The bending strengths and the elastic moduli of the composite compacts are compared with those of the composites prepared from chemically modified cellulose, such as TEMPO-oxidized cellulose nanofibers (TCNF) and carboxymethyl cellulose, in Table 2. The MFC-HAP composites showed similar mechanical properties to those prepared from other cellulosic substances, indicating that sufficient mechanical strength was achieved even with cellulose that was not chemically modified. The compacts of CMC-HAP were fragile, and the compacts were broken into two parts after the three-point bending test, while the compacts of TCNF-HAP and MFC-HAP were not broken into two parts.

Conclusions

We investigated the crystallization of HAP in the presence of microfibrillated cellulose under alkaline conditions, and the effects of the HAP:MFC ratios and the crystallization temperatures on the mechanical properties of the composites were evaluated. Although MFC has only OH groups as a polar functional group, hybridization occurred quantitatively and the inorganic content of the composites almost matched those calculated from the feed ratios. The mechanical properties such as bending strength and elastic modulus are also comparable to those of the composites of the anionic cellulosic materials such as TCNF-HAP and CMC-HAP. The bending strength and the elastic modulus were 98 MPa (C-50%-70oC) and 8.7 GPa (C-70%-50oC), respectively, and the former is comparable to that of polyamide-6 (118 MPa) and the latter is larger than that of polyamide-6 (2.8 GPa). In particular, MFC-HAP and TCNF-HAP underwent nonbrittle fracture, while CMC-HAP underwent brittle fracture. SEM observation of the fracture surfaces indicates that transition from the continuous organic phase to the isolated organic phase, as the inorganic content of the composite increased, led to brittleness of the composites.
