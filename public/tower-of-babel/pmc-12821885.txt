Progressive curriculum learning with Scale-Enhanced U-Net for continuous airway segmentation
Authors: Bingyu Yang, Qingyao Tian, Huai Liao, Xinyan Huang, Jinlin Wu, Jingdi Hu, Hongbin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractContinuous and accurate segmentation of airways in chest CT images is essential for preoperative planning and real-time bronchoscopy navigation. Despite advances in deep learning for medical image segmentation, maintaining airway continuity remains a challenge, particularly due to intra-class imbalance between large and small branches and blurred CT details. To address these challenges, we propose a progressive curriculum learning pipeline and a Scale-Enhanced U-Net (SE-UNet) to improve detail extraction, thereby enhancing segmentation continuity. Compared with previous connectivity-aware methods, our framework directly tackles the imbalance between large and small branches through end-to-end progressive learning, while balancing airway tree completeness and accuracy. Specifically, our curriculum learning pipeline comprises three stages. Stage 1 performs coarse learning to extract main airways. Stage 2 introduces a General Union Loss (GUL) to improve the identification of smaller airways. In Stage 3, we propose an Adaptive Topology-Responsive Loss (ATRL), which encourages the network to focus on preserving airway continuity. Throughout all stages, a crop sampling strategy is employed to reduce feature interference between airways of varying scales, effectively addressing the intra-class imbalance. The progressive training pipeline shares the same SE-UNet, integrating multi-scale inputs and Detail Information Enhancers (DIEs) to enhance information flow and effectively capture the intricate details of small airways. Additionally, we propose a robust airway tree parsing method and hierarchical evaluation metrics to provide more clinically relevant and precise analysis. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method outperforms existing approaches on the ATM’22 challenge dataset and achieves a 9.631% improvement in the Tree length Detection rate (TD) of small airways and a 4.622% improvement in the Branch Detection rate (BD) of the overall airway tree on our in-house dataset, thereby significantly enhancing small-airway accuracy and overall airway tree completeness.Keywords: CT image segmentation, Airway tree, Curriculum learning, U-Net

Introduction

Chronic Respiratory Diseases (CRD), such as Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), asthma, Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD), and sarcoidosis, are major global causes of mortality [1]. Advances in computer technology and medical imaging have made CT essential for diagnosing and evaluating these diseases [2]. Accurate airway segmentation and reconstruction from CT images are crucial for preoperative planning, real-time navigation, and detecting peripheral pulmonary lesions [3–8]. In clinical practice, bronchoscopy navigation typically uses the largest connected component of the segmented airway to ensure a continuous and accessible path. However, discontinuities in segmentation may cause peripheral bronchi to be disconnected and discarded, limiting access to distal regions. Therefore, both the continuity of the main airway and the extraction of peripheral branches are crucial for reliable navigation and lesion localization. However, manual segmentation of complex pulmonary airways in CT images is time-consuming and error-prone. Therefore, there is an urgent need for automated technologies to achieve accurate and continuous airway segmentation.

Recent deep learning methods [9–22] have demonstrated significant advantages over traditional approaches [23–28] in medical image analysis, achieving improved accuracy in tasks such as airway segmentation. However, two major challenges remain unaddressed towards achieving continuous airway segmentation: 1) intra-class imbalance between major and smaller branches, and 2) blurred details in CT imaging. As a result, despite good overlap with ground truth, existing methods often produce fragmented segments, restricting their clinical applicability [9, 12, 15, 16].

Intra-class Imbalance. The airway tree has a complex branching structure with significant volume and morphological differences between major and smaller airways, termed intra-class imbalance. Zheng et al. [15] showed that this imbalance causes smaller airway features to be overshadowed by major airways during training, making it difficult for models to focus on smaller structures. Despite their novel supervision approach and distance-based loss function, the issue of airway breakage is not addressed, and missed detections in peripheral small airways persist, leading to airway discontinuities.

Blurred Details in CT Imaging. In CT imaging, distal small airways are limited by resolution, noise, and motion blur, often showing low signal-to-noise ratios and blurred details. Fine branches and edge information are easily lost during downsampling, making it hard to restore their integrity during upsampling and causing airway discontinuities. Zhang et al. [9] proposed the Connectivity-Aware Surrogate (CAS) and Local-Sensitive Distance (LSD) modules, which effectively enhance airway connectivity and serve as a strong recent baseline for topology-aware airway segmentation. However, they still struggle to handle certain blurred airway walls (Fig. 1).

To address intra-class imbalance and reduce interference in feature learning, we propose a progressive curriculum learning pipeline for airway segmentation, inspired by curriculum learning [29]. We also present a Scale-Enhanced U-Net (SE-UNet) to improve the extraction of airway features across multiple scales, enhancing segmentation accuracy. Additionally, we present hierarchical evaluation metrics to assess performance, emphasizing segmentation continuity at different scales using a robust airway tree parsing method.

Motivated by the success of curriculum learning in improving segmentation by progressing from easy to complex tasks [30, 31], we propose a progressive curriculum learning pipeline for airway segmentation to address intra-class imbalance. Unlike conventional methods that treat all structures equally, our pipeline accounts for difficulty variations among branches and decomposes the task into three stages: (1) extracting main airways, (2) identifying smaller peripheral airways, and (3) repairing discontinuities. This stepwise design enables the model to build a stable foundation before handling harder cases, thereby alleviating intra-class imbalance. To support staged training, we design three crop sampling strategies. Random Crop provides diverse contexts for stable early learning, Hard-mining Crop targets regions where small airways are missed, and Breakage Crop focuses on disconnected branches. A dynamic training scheduler adaptively adjusts sampling ratios based on stage performance, ensuring attention to challenging structures while preventing overfitting. Finally, we propose an Adaptive Topology-Responsive Loss (ATRL) to enhance continuity. Unlike voxel-wise losses, ATRL enforces structural consistency by maximizing overlap between predicted and ground-truth centerlines, guiding the network to preserve topology, reduce breakages, and improve tree completeness.

To address discontinuities caused by blurred details in CT imaging, we propose Scale-Enhanced U-Net (SE-UNet). SE-UNet captures airway features across multiple scales by integrating pyramid pooling on the input image with residual multi-scale connections within the encoder-decoder structure. This design strengthens both global contextual representation and fine-grained detail restoration, alleviating the loss of small branches during downsampling. Furthermore, to enrich boundary information, we incorporate Detail Information Enhancers (DIEs) into SE-UNet. These modules highlight subtle structural cues in blurred regions, improving the recovery of fine airway edges and enhancing overall segmentation accuracy.

Existing metrics overlook the evaluation of continuity of segmentation. Current studies use Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), Precision (Pre), Tree length Detected rate(TD), and Branch Detected rate(BD) to evaluate segmentation performance [9, 15, 16, 32]. However, their reliability is limited. First, accurate airway tree parsing is essential for integrity assessment. Existing methods include skeletonization-based analysis [33] and deep learning-based classification [34]. The former generates inaccurate skeletons on irregular surfaces, requiring manual correction [35], while the latter misses high-order branches. Both reduce measurement accuracy and evaluation reliability. Moreover, they suffer from bias caused by class imbalance, as larger airways dominate metrics, obscuring deficiencies in small airway segmentation. To accurately evaluate segmentation performance, we present hierarchical evaluation metrics that assess topological completeness at different airway levels. The core is a robust airway tree parsing method that addresses false branches and handles individual differences by smoothing centerlines, pruning, and merging branches. Extensive experiments on public (499 cases) and in-house datasets (55 cases) demonstrate that our method significantly improves topological completeness compared to state-of-the-art methods, with notable advantages in small airway extraction.

Related work

Airway segmentation

Airway segmentation is crucial for the diagnosis and surgical planning of pulmonary diseases. Traditional methods [23–28] have been applied in this area. The EXACT’09 challenge [36] proposed 15 segmentation algorithms, but the results indicated that these methods are ineffective in extracting small airways.

In recent years, advancements in deep learning have significantly improved the performance of automated airway segmentation [9–19, 21, 22]. Charbonnier et al. [10] used 2D convolutional neural networks to address airway leakage, but the results were limited. Yun et al. [37] designed a 2.5D CNN that integrates adjacent slices in three orientations, enhancing tree length detection and reducing leakage. Juarez et al. [11] proposing a 3D U-Net that improved feature representation but struggled with small airway detection. This limitation arose due to CT noise, which caused the features of distal small airways to be blurred.

To enhance feature extraction against blurred details, Qin et al. [12] added feature recalibration and attention distillation modules, and Selvan et al. [13] treated the task as graph refinement. Their work improved the ability to extract small airways, but they did not address the challenges caused by the size differences between airway tree branches. Zheng et al. [15] defined this problem as intra-class imbalance, leading to missed detection of peripheral small airways and resulting in airway discontinuity.

To address intra-class imbalance and airway discontinuity, Qin et al. [14] proposed AirwayNet to enhance voxel connectivity, and centerline tracking algorithms [39] were also introduced. Wang et al. [40] proposed a spatial fully connected network with a radial distance loss to emphasize centerline consistency. Zheng et al. [15] introduced the General Union Loss (GUL) to reinforce centerline learning through distance loss. Nan et al. [16] introduced JCAM loss to enhance continuity. Wu et al. [41] introduced long-term slice propagation (LTSP) to restore missing branches by propagating contextual features across CT slices, thus preserving airway topology. Zhang et al. [9] introduced the Connectivity-Aware Surrogate (CAS) and Local-Sensitive Distance (LSD) modules. Although these modules enhanced connectivity, they also caused over-segmentation of large airways due to the failure to balance the learning weights between different types of airways. Thus, it is essential to reduce interference between features of different difficulties and automatically adjust sample weights during training. More recent advances, such as ExpTopo [22] and MGASM-Net [21], further integrate explicit topological modeling or morphology-guided Mamba mechanisms to enhance peripheral branch detection; however, the former is sensitive to skeleton inaccuracies and computationally intensive, while the latter relies on additional boundary annotations and still suffers from leakage, leaving room for a more efficient and annotation-robust solution.

Curriculum learning

Curriculum learning [29] is a training paradigm inspired by human learning processes, in which models are exposed to samples in an easy-to-hard order. By structuring training in this manner, models gradually build the capacity to handle increasingly complex patterns. In medical image analysis, curriculum learning has been validated across diverse segmentation and registration tasks [30, 31, 42–46], showing effectiveness in mitigating imbalance and improving robustness.

Li et al. [30] introduced a curriculum strategy to alleviate sample-level imbalance, demonstrating more stable convergence when minority classes were underrepresented. Liu et al. [31] further extended this concept by enforcing a progressive easy-to-hard scheme that exposed networks to adversarial or challenging samples, which improved generalization to complex medical data. More recently, Wang et al. [42] explored task-driven curricula for medical segmentation, highlighting the benefits of gradually increasing task complexity. Hu et al. [44] demonstrated the utility of progressive residual learning in brain lesion segmentation, where gradually incorporating more complex 3D features yielded significant efficiency gains. Zhang et al. [45] leveraged curriculum-guided self-training with data augmentation to bridge labeled and unlabeled domains, achieving state-of-the-art semi-supervised segmentation. Beyond segmentation, Burduja et al. [46] introduced curriculum strategies to deformable image registration, showing that a blurred-to-sharp progression improves both accuracy and convergence stability.

Despite these advances, most curriculum learning strategies in medical imaging focus on inter-sample difficulty, while overlooking intra-structure variation. In airway segmentation, large branches with clear boundaries are easier to delineate, whereas small peripheral airways suffer from weak contrast, noise, and higher variability. Discontinuities caused by blurred walls or artifacts further complicate segmentation, which sample-level curricula alone cannot resolve. Consequently, existing methods struggle with intra-class imbalance, often producing incomplete airway trees with missing or fragmented branches.

In this work, we propose a progressive curriculum learning pipeline that focuses step-by-step on large airways, small airways, and airway discontinuities to alleviate intra-class imbalance. Additionally, we present a Scale-Enhanced U-Net (SE-UNet) to effectively address the issues of blurred details and missing small airway features in CT imaging.

Methods

In this section, we describe the proposed approach in detail. First, the progressive curriculum learning pipeline is described in Sect. 3.1, where airway structures of increasing complexity are addressed in a staged training process. Then, the Scale-Enhanced U-Net (SE-UNet), designed to enhance information flow and recover fine structural details, is introduced in Sect. 3.2. Finally, in Sect. 3.3, hierarchical evaluation metrics are proposed to enable a continuity-aware and anatomically meaningful assessment of segmentation performance.

Progressive curriculum learning pipeline

Due to the large volume of 3D lung CT data and GPU memory limitations, airway segmentation networks are trained using sampling patches as inputs [11, 16]. When patches contain both large and small airways, intra-class imbalance causes loss gradients to be dominated by large airways, exacerbating gradient erosion in small airways [15]. To address this, we design a progressive curriculum learning pipeline to train the network and minimize interference during feature learning (Fig. 2). The pipeline consists of three stages: extracting main airways, identifying small airways, and repairing discontinuities. We develop three crop sampling strategies to effectively mine curriculum samples. The training scheduler dynamically adjusts sampling ratios based on the validation loss of each sample type. When the validation performance of a category converges, its ratio is gradually decreased while harder samples are emphasized. Weights are fully inherited between stages to preserve previously learned features and avoid catastrophic forgetting. The specific design and function of each stage are as follows:

Each ConvBlock [15] is composed of a convolutional layer followed by instance normalization and ReLU activation, as well as a spatial attention layer that generates a soft mask to modulate the convolutional response. It outputs features to the next block while generating an additional path through the upsampling layer, forming a group feature pyramid that is used for group supervision.

Compared with the original Inception module, which employs convolutions of different kernel sizes, DIE replaces those fixed-scale filters with attention-weighted ConvBlocks, allowing more flexible feature refinement. In contrast to standard ResNet blocks that perform element-wise residual summation, DIE concatenates the outputs from multiple attention-gated ConvBlocks to form a richer hierarchical representation. Hierarchical evaluation

Current airway segmentation research uses Tree length Detected rate (TD) and Branch Detected rate (BD) to assess airway completeness, Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and Precision (Pre) to measure accuracy [32]. However, these metrics are limited by false branches and intra-class imbalance, masking deficiencies in small airway segmentation. Thus, we propose hierarchical evaluation metrics based on Robust Airway Tree Parsing (Fig. 5) to evaluate topological completeness at different airway levels.

A qualitative example illustrating the topological parsing process of airway tree

Robust airway tree parsing

Existing methods include skeleton-based analysis [33] and deep learning-based classification [34]. However, the former often generates inaccurate skeletons, while the latter misses high-order branches. We propose a robust airway tree parsing pipeline that includes Skeletonization and Parsing, Pruning, Topology Grading, and Anatomical Matching. (a) Skeletonization and Parsing: Traverse the extracted skeleton to identify branch points and explore along the skeleton line from each branch point until the airway end or the next branch point is reached. Record parent-child relationships to complete skeleton parsing. (b) Pruning: Smooth the main airway segments, remove peripheral short segments, and merge single-segment parent branches to accurately divide airway branches. (c) Topology Grading: Recursively number branch points along the skeleton. (d) Anatomical Matching: Align the original numbering with anatomical grades based on hierarchical relationships and airway angles, considering common branch omissions and individual differences to achieve stable airway tree parsing. Experiment

Dataset

We evaluate our method on two datasets: in-house dataset used for training, assessment, and ablation studies; the ATM’22 challenge dataset [32] for fair comparative testing at https://atm22.grand-challenge.org.

ATM’22 challenge dataset: The ATM’22 dataset consists of 499 chest CT scans, including 299 scans for training, 50 for validation, and 150 for testing [14, 15, 51, 52]. The data were collected from three major sources: the EXACT’09 challenge [36], the LIDC-IDRI lung cancer screening dataset [53], and Shanghai Chest Hospital. Each scan was annotated by three radiologists, each with more than five years of clinical experience. The final ground truth labels were produced through consensus and adjudication to ensure high-quality airway annotations. The test set was kept hidden, and evaluation was conducted by the challenge organizers, ensuring an unbiased comparison with state-of-the-art methods.

In-house dataset: We further collected 55 chest CT scans from The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, encompassing both healthy subjects and patients with mild Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) or Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD). Each scan contains 256 to 612 slices, with spatial resolution ranging from 0.598 to 0.879 mm and slice thickness from 0.625 to 1.000 mm. All scans were anonymized to remove personal identifiers, and ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board of The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Coarse airway segmentations were generated using two recent deep learning models [9, 15] to provide initial guidance. Then, three pulmonologists with over five years of bronchoscopy and imaging interpretation experience carefully refined these predictions slice by slice. Ambiguous regions were discussed jointly, and final annotations were determined by majority voting. This procedure ensured both efficiency and annotation reliability. The dataset was split into training (35 cases), validation (10 cases), and test (10 cases) subsets, maintaining a balanced distribution of disease types and scan resolutions across splits.

Implementation details

In the preprocessing, we first truncate CT voxel intensities to two ranges, i.e., Hounsfield Units (HU) and HU, to suppress irrelevant tissues while preserving airway structures. The voxel values are then normalized into to stabilize the optimization process. All CT volumes are cropped into patches of size centered on sampled airway regions, which serve as the network input. For post-processing, Dual Threshold Iteration (DTI) [58] is employed to convert the probability map to a binary mask. Specifically, voxels with probability higher than are first labeled as airway, while those between and are further refined through connectivity propagation from high-confidence regions. Morphological hole filling is then applied to remove spurious cavities, and the largest connected component is extracted to suppress false positives.

The optimizer used is AdamW with a batch size of 2 and a learning rate of . The progressive curriculum training consists of three stages, corresponding to extracting main airways, identifying small airways, and repairing discontinuities, with training epochs set to 100, 50, and 50, respectively. The entire framework is implemented in PyTorch 2.0.0. Both training and inference are conducted on a workstation equipped with a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB memory). On average, our method achieves an inference speed of 73 ms per CT patch, enabling efficient processing of full CT volumes.

Firstly, we evaluated the impact of removing the Detail Information Enhancers (DIEs). Omitting DIEs (Ours w/o DIE) slightly affected the DSC and Pre but significantly reduced TD and BD by 4.426% and 7.640%, respectively. Secondly, the effectiveness of ATRL was explored. The model incorporating ATRL showed improvements in TD (2.666%) and BD (5.282%), confirming that ATRL effectively encourages the network to focus on the airway breakages, thereby enhancing the segmentation accuracy.

Thirdly, we evaluated the crop training scheduler, which adaptively adjusts the ratios of random, hard-mining, and breakage patches according to the validation loss of each sample type. Without this scheduler (Ours w/o crop scheduler), the network tends to overfit frequent easy samples and underperform on rare, thin airways. Incorporating the scheduler yields an additional 1.338% and 2.984% improvement in TD and BD, respectively, confirming that dynamic sample balancing significantly benefits small-airway learning and structural continuity.

Lastly, we compared the network trained through all three stages with those trained through only the first stage or the first two stages, with the results shown in the last three rows of Table 3. The second and third stages improved TD and BD by 16.146% and 4.716%, and by 21.264% and 8.490%, respectively. It shows that the second stage enhances peripheral airway detection, while the third stage further improves airway continuity by addressing discontinuities.

Discussion and conclusion

This study addresses the long-standing challenge of achieving accurate and continuous airway segmentation from chest CT images. The proposed approach integrates a progressive curriculum learning pipeline with a Scale-Enhanced U-Net (SE-UNet), demonstrating clear advantages in both segmentation accuracy and topological completeness, particularly for small and peripheral airways.

The progressive curriculum learning strategy plays a vital role in mitigating intra-class imbalance by decoupling the training process into three sequential stages: extracting main airways, identifying small airways, and repairing discontinuities. This staged learning process helps reduce feature interference between different scales, enabling the model to gradually learn more complex anatomical structures. Experimental results and ablation studies confirm the effectiveness of each stage, showing significant gains in Tree Detection (TD) and Branch Detection (BD) scores, especially in the later stages.

The SE-UNet architecture, enhanced with multi-scale inputs and Detail Information Enhancers (DIEs), further contributes to robust feature representation and recovery of fine anatomical structures. By integrating residual multi-scale inputs and Inception-style convolution modules in both the encoder and decoder, SE-UNet strengthens contextual awareness and preserves edge details even in blurred or low-contrast regions. This design proves particularly effective for restoring the integrity of distal airways that are often missed by existing methods.

To explicitly promote airway continuity, we introduce the Adaptive Topology-Responsive Loss (ATRL), which incorporates centerline distance and breakage-aware penalties. This loss function is specifically tailored to reinforce the learning of structurally coherent airway trees and has shown consistent improvements in maintaining branch connectivity.

Beyond conventional voxel-level metrics such as Dice and Precision, we propose a hierarchical evaluation scheme based on robust airway tree parsing, allowing for a more anatomically informed and clinically meaningful assessment of segmentation results. This not only reflects overall accuracy but also highlights performance differences across large and small airway branches.

It is worth noting that several peripheral branches predicted by our method were not included in the reference labels of the hidden test set. This discrepancy mainly arises from incomplete annotations of distal bronchi, a known limitation of the dataset also discussed in prior works. Visual inspection by clinical experts confirmed that these additional branches correspond to true anatomical structures. While no quantitative recalibration was performed, this observation indicates that the reported precision may be underestimated, and our method can potentially assist in discovering unannotated peripheral airways and improving the completeness of airway assessment.

While our method achieves state-of-the-art performance on both public and in-house datasets, several limitations remain. The model may be less robust under severe imaging artifacts or rare pathologies not well-represented in the training data. Additionally, the multi-stage training process introduces increased computational cost and complexity, which may limit deployment in real-time clinical environments. Future work will focus on improving efficiency through lightweight network design and knowledge distillation, as well as enhancing generalization across diverse imaging protocols using domain adaptation techniques.

In conclusion, we present a novel airway segmentation framework that combines progressive curriculum learning, a multi-scale enhanced U-Net, and a topology-aware loss to achieve highly continuous and anatomically faithful airway segmentation. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method outperforms existing approaches in small airway detection and overall tree completeness, offering a reliable solution for preoperative planning and real-time bronchoscopy navigation.
