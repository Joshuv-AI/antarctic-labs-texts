Composite shock indices with age, GCS, and SpO2 for trauma triage outcomes: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mehdi Torabi, Abolfazl Bagheri, Neda Naeemi Bafghi, Moghaddameh Mirzaee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe study evaluates the effectiveness of composite indices, incorporating age, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and oxygen saturation (SpO2) into shock indices, in predicting severe trauma patient outcomes in the emergency department (ED).MethodThe study was a retrospective, single-center, and cohort-based analysis of adult trauma patients (aged > 18 years) triaged as emergency severity index (ESI) levels 1 or 2 at a level 2 trauma center, conducted over 6 months. Data were collected encompassing vital signs, and composite shock indices from the shock index (SI), reverse shock index (rSI), and modified shock index (MSI) in combination with age, GCS and Spo2.ResultsA study of 437 participants revealed that composite triad-based shock indices such as rSI × SpO2 × GCS/Age, and Age SI/(SpO2 × GCS) significantly influence the differentiation of triage levels 1 and 2. The Age SI/ (SpO2 × GCS) predict in-hospital mortality, while Age MSI/ (SpO2 × GCS), predict blood transfusion necessity. The AUC for triage level discrimination was 1.00 (sensitivity 100%, specificity 100%), for in-hospital mortality 0.77 (sensitivity 69.3%, specificity 80.5%), and for blood transfusion need 0.75 (sensitivity 68.9%, specificity 70.6%).ConclusionSI, MSI, and rSI, when combined with age, GCS, and SpO2, are significant predictors of triage level discrimination, in-hospital mortality, and need for blood transfusion, suggesting that composite shock indices are more beneficial than indices alone.

Introduction

In the context of trauma care, timely intervention is critical, particularly in cases where patients are at risk of life-threatening conditions. It is imperative that trauma triage guidelines prioritize the risk stratification of trauma patients who may benefit from earlier and more intensive trauma team activation [1]. Although traditional vital signs are measured quickly and noninvasively and play an important role in the risk stratification of patients presenting to the emergency department (ED), they alone may not reliably indicate the severity of the disease in certain populations [2, 3]. However, when integrated with risk factors such as Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), vital signs can enhance the efficacy of predicting mortality in patients with severe injuries [4].

Shock indices have the potential to serve as valuable predictive tools in the ED triage, particularly in the more expeditious identification of trauma patients with higher injury severity. Given the simplicity and availability of these indices, it is advisable for trauma triage guidelines to prioritize the shock index in the risk stratification of trauma patients who may benefit from earlier and more severe trauma team activation. The shock index (SI) was initially developed as a tool for the detection of hypovolemic shock. It has since evolved into variants such as the modified shock index (MSI) and composites that incorporate age, GCS, and oxygen saturation (SpO2) to refine predictions across adult trauma, geriatric, and pediatric groups. The SI has been demonstrated to be a valuable predictor of in-hospital mortality and the necessity for blood products in trauma patients [5].

The reverse shock index (rSI), akin to the SI, facilitates the expeditious identification of adverse outcomes in trauma patients. The incorporation of risk factors, such as GCS, into the rSI (rSIG) has been demonstrated to enhance its predictive capacity, surpassing the performance of other indices [6]. This index may also assist emergency medical service providers in the early identification and triage of severely injured individuals. Abnormal rSIG in the ED has been associated with higher rates of in-hospital mortality, intubation, blood transfusion, and laparotomy, as well as admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) [7]. Therefore, the utilization of this metric has the potential to expedite the identification, management, and treatment of each injured individual. In addition, the rSIG has been demonstrated to exhibit superiority over the SI in terms of its capacity to predict mortality and coagulopathy in trauma patients [8].

The integration of additional risk factors, such as age, into these indices has been observed to enhance their diagnostic accuracy in predicting the prognosis of trauma patients, thereby surpassing the simplicity of indices like the SI in terms of prognostic accuracy. A study examined the predictive value of the SI multiplied by age and rSIG, adding the risk factors of age and GCS. It was observed that these composite indices had a substantial role in predicting mortality in trauma patients compared to the SI and MSI [9]. Additionally, the incorporation of SpO2 into the SI, as opposed to the SI alone, led to a substantial enhancement in the predictive capability of mortality in trauma patients by this composite index [10]. A further study revealed that substituting the SI, SpO2, and temperature as strong predictors of mortality instead of systolic blood pressure and respiratory rate in the Revised Trauma Score (RTS), a standard index of physiological injury severity, can provide a more accurate predictive model for mortality in trauma patients [11]. However, the existing composites generally incorporate only pairwise risk factors (e.g., age with SI or GCS with rSI), thereby leaving a gap in fully integrated triad models that combine age, GCS, and SpO2 for comprehensive ED triage.

Objectives

Considering the more accurate role of these composite indices, which were derived by incorporating age, GCS, and SpO2 into shock indices, the objective of this study was to assess the efficacy of novel triad-based indices in predicting the outcomes (triage levels 1 and 2 discrimination, in-hospital mortality, and the need for blood transfusion) of severe trauma patients (Emergency Severity Index level 1 or 2) referred to the ED triage.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

The present study aimed to investigate the accuracy of composite indices derived from the SI in predicting the outcome of severe trauma patients referred to the triage of the ED of …………… Academic Educating Hospital, a level 2 trauma and referral center for trauma patients. This hospital has 350 active beds in …., a large city with a population of nearly one million people located in southeastern …. The study design was retrospective, single-center, and cohort-based, and it was conducted on patients over a 6-month period from April 1, 2024, to October 1, 2024. Variables and outcomes were ascertained retrospectively from documentation, with triage variables sourced from ED triage sheets. Prior to the initiation of the study, the ethical code was obtained from the Ethics Committee of …..…. University of Medical Sciences (IR.KMU.AH.REC.1403.157), and all phases of the study were conducted under the committee’s comprehensive oversight. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, individual consent from participants was not obtained. The confidentiality of all patient information was maintained throughout the study. The study methods were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study participants

The study population included all trauma patients over the age of 18 who were classified as Emergency Severity Index (ESI) triage level 1 or 2 by the triage nurse. Patients younger than 18 years of age, pregnant women, individuals who did not have vital signs upon arrival at the ED, or who did not have sufficient information in their file were excluded from the study.

Data collection and variables

Study data were collected using a checklist from the data available on the triage form that was filled out and completed by the shift nurse. In instances where the data on the triage sheet was deemed inadequate, the initial vital signs documented in the shortest possible time in CPR were incorporated into the checklist. The patient checklist encompassed variables such as age, gender, GCS, traditional vital signs (systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate (HR), SpO2, mean arterial pressure (MAP), and pulse pressure (PP)), and indices.

Outcome measurement

The outcome included triage levels 1 and 2 discrimination, in-hospital mortality, and the need for initiation of blood transfusion of at least one red blood cell concentrate within the first 24 h of hospital admission.

Statistical analysis

The qualitative variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Quantitative data with a normal distribution were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The normality of the quantitative data distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The relationship between the qualitative variable and outcomes was assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and the relationship between outcomes and quantitative variables through the Student’s t-test, under the assumption of normal distribution. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Univariate logistic regression was then employed to examine the relationship and strength of association between patient outcomes and variables, calculating odds ratios and confidence intervals. Finally, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was calculated for indicators that demonstrated a significant relationship with outcomes and the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, predictive value, and accuracy were calculated at the best cut-off, determined using Youden’s J statistic (sensitivity + specificity—1) to maximize the net classification benefit. AUC less than 0.60 was considered very poor, 0.60–0.69 as poor, 0.70–0.79 as moderate, 0.80–0.89 as good, and greater than or equal to 0.90 as excellent discrimination. The analysis was conducted using SPSS software, version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Due to the presence of substantial collinearity among the independent variables, we implemented ridge regression as a method for performing multivariate regression. This regression method focuses exclusively on the regression coefficients, thereby neglecting to quantify the influence of the variables on the response variable. To assess the influence of each variable on the response variable, univariate logistic regression was employed. In ridge regression, variables that possess a larger standardized coefficient in terms of absolute value exert a greater influence on the response variable, a property that can be leveraged for prediction. Standardized coefficients facilitate a comparison of the relative influence of each independent variable and an analysis of their significance across different models. Negative coefficients suggest a direct relationship between an increase in the value of the independent variable and a decrease in the response variable. Conversely, positive coefficients signify that an increase in the value of the independent variable is associated with an increase in the response variable. The implementation of ridge regression analysis was conducted using Stata version 16.0 software (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Of the 2,054 trauma patients admitted during the study period, 636 (31%) met the initial eligibility criteria of triage level 1 or 2. The remaining 1,418 (69%) patients were triage levels 3–5 and were not part of the defined study population. The results of the univariate logistic regression analysis are shown in Tables 1, 2, 3. All indices were effective in differentiating between level 1 and 2 of triage and in predicting in-hospital mortality and the need for blood transfusion, except for DBP in all three outcomes and for SpO2 in differentiating between level 1 and 2 and in predicting the need for blood transfusion. Discussion

This study demonstrates that triad-based composite shock indices, incorporating age, GCS, and SpO2 into SI, rSI, and MSI, effectively predict triage level discrimination, in-hospital mortality, and blood transfusion need in severe adult trauma patients (ESI levels 1 and 2) referred to the ED triage. Specifically, rSI × SpO2 × GCS/Age and Age × SI/(SpO2 × GCS) showed perfect discrimination for differentiating triage levels 1 and 2; Age × SI/(SpO2 × GCS) had moderate predictive value for in-hospital mortality; and Age × MSI/(SpO2 × GCS) for transfusion need. These outperformed standalone indices in ridge regression, addressing a gap in integrated models beyond pairwise composites and traditional scores like RTS, which rely on less reliable parameters such as respiratory rate. This innovation underscores the potential for these indices to refine trauma protocols, though external validation remains essential.

Triage in the emergency department plays a vital role in prioritizing patients and facilitating swift diagnosis, particularly for those with critical conditions. The time spent in triage is crucial, especially for trauma patients, where rapid identification of life-threatening injuries can significantly impact outcomes. Research indicates a marked difference in hospital mortality rates between triage levels; level 1 patients face a substantially higher mortality risk compared to level 2 patients, while distinctions between levels 2 and 3 are less pronounced. This emphasizes the need for effective differentiation within levels 1 and 2, where nurses’ capability to rapidly assess and distinguish critically ill patients is vital [12, 13]. Tools like pulse oximetry are essential for monitoring SpO2 and heart rate, aiding nurses in evaluating patient severity through signs of hypoxia and tachycardia [14].

The SI, which highlights the relationship between blood pressure decline and heart rate, is particularly significant in triaging trauma cases, with an SI above 1 correlating to a fourfold increase in in-hospital mortality risk [15, 16]. However, its predictive value for outcomes like mortality and blood transfusion needs has been contested. A study revealed that the SI’s effectiveness as a single predictor is limited, suggesting that other risk factors, including patient age, must be incorporated into assessments for more accurate prognostication [17]. In their study, Iirol et al. demonstrated that incorporating age and GCS into the SI enhances its capacity to predict mortality in trauma patients. They concluded that the prognostic significance of the Age SI, SI/GCS, and Age SI/GCS indices significantly surpasses that of the SI alone in trauma patients [18]. Similarly, Lin et al. found that integrating SI with the AVPU scale yielded better outcomes than relying on SI alone [19]. Another risk factor that may increase the accuracy of composite indices derived from SI is SpO2. Another study by Qi et al. introduced SpO2 into the SI framework, resulting in the SI/SpO2 ratio outperforming SI by itself in predicting mortality. This finding signifies the potential of SI/SpO2 as a valuable tool for evaluating and triaging trauma patients in the EDs [10]. Consequently, the integration of key risk factors, including age, SpO2, and GCS, into the SI and rSI in trauma patients has been shown to enhance the predictive accuracy of these indices in predicting patient outcomes. This study found rSI × SpO2 × GCS/Age, and Age × SI/(SpO2 × GCS), respectively, to be more effective in differentiating between triage levels 1 and 2, and Age SI/(SpO2 × GCS) in predicting in-hospital mortality compared to other indices. Beyond trauma, these indices show promise in other critically ill populations, enhancing the generalizability of our findings. For instance, Uluç et al. in a retrospective ICU sepsis cohort found ASI with the highest discriminative ability for mortality (AUC 0.85), surpassing SI (AUC 0.69) and MSI (AUC 0.72) [20]. Similarly, Lee et al. reported Age-SI linked to in-hospital mortality in septic shock (OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03) [21]. These findings further highlight that vital sign-based indices can serve as strong prognostic tools beyond trauma populations.

The effectiveness of the rSI in predicting mortality and admission of trauma patients to the ICU was demonstrated by Chuang et al. They also noted that individuals with a rSI less than one had higher injury severity scores (ISS) and poorer clinical outcomes. The researchers also noted the importance of various parameters, including age and GCS, regardless of vital signs, in predicting the prognosis of trauma patients. This finding was subsequently confirmed by Vorbeck et al. in their study [22]. In their study, Wan-ting et al. examined the prognostic role of this index in predicting mortality by adding GCS to the rSI under the title of rSIG in patients with severe trauma. This study demonstrated that a value above 14 of this index increased mortality by more than 7 times. The study’s findings underscore the efficacy of this index in predicting hospital mortality, contrasting with the rSI’s ability to indicate the significance of GCS in trauma patients [23]. In addition to confirming the prognostic role of rSIG in trauma patients, Park et al. defined a cut-off of 16.5 for predicting in-hospital mortality [24]. Despite the validation of this index’s prognostic significance in trauma patients, contradictory results were reported by Yatsen et al., who found the role of shock index and rSIG in differentiating triage levels 1 and 2 from other levels to be ineffective in predicting hospital triage severity [25]. In a subsequent study, Kimura et al. investigated the role of age and added it to the rSIG index (rSIG/Age) in trauma patients. The researchers found that the rSIG/Age index exhibited a high degree of effectiveness in predicting in-hospital mortality [26].

It is crucial to utilize essential tools for the initial management of trauma patients effectively during the "golden hour". This strategy can significantly decrease immediate or early traumatic fatalities, particularly those that may be preventable. Timely identification of the need for blood transfusions is of substantial clinical importance in this context [27]. A study by Ozakin et al. demonstrated the efficacy of the SI in predicting the necessity of blood transfusion in trauma patients, particularly when augmented by additional risk factors such as serum lactate and base deficiency [28]. Rengifo et al. also confirmed the role of the SI for initiating blood transfusion in patients with penetrating chest trauma [29]. Furthermore, Powell et al. investigated the association between the SI and the necessity for blood transfusion, taking into account risk factors such as age and GCS. The conclusion drawn from this analysis was that the technology has the potential to serve as a valuable tool for predicting the necessity of in-flight blood transfusions, both in primary transfers and in inter-hospital transfers [30]. In addition to the SI, GCS, and age, the MSI has been demonstrated to serve as a valuable predictor of the necessity for massive blood transfusion in trauma patients5. In the clinical assessment of patients in the triage and management of trauma patients, the patient’s age should be considered because it may affect the accurate assessment and prediction of the need for blood transfusion [31]. In their study, Uemura et al. found that the role of rSIG in predicting the need for blood transfusion in trauma patients was significantly greater than that of SI [32]. While corroborating the significance of other indices, this study identified Age SI and Age × MSI/(SpO2 × GCS) indices as more substantial in predicting the necessity of blood transfusion in patients with severe trauma.

Limitations

The present study is not without its limitations. Despite the hospital’s designation as a referral center for trauma patients, its status as a single center potentially restricts the generalizability of the findings. The retrospective nature of the study and the deficiencies in patient records are further limitations. Therefore, prospective multicenter validations are essential to confirm applicability across diverse populations and mitigate confounding from incomplete records. The present study was constrained to patients with injuries classified as triage level 1 or 2, which may limit its generalizability to patients with less severe injuries. Triage levels 3–5 were not included in the defined study population and were therefore not incorporated into the analysis. While this approach ensures a focus on critically injured patients, it may overlook patterns relevant to moderate or minor trauma cases. Another limitation of the study was the exclusion of patients less than 18 years of age and pregnant women from the study population. Furthermore, while the extraction of data prioritized the triage form for initial vital signs, supplementation from CPR sheets in cases of incompleteness may introduce information bias due to potential temporal or measurement discrepancies between these sources. The necessity for transfusion serves as an indication for the initiation of a blood transfusion in these patients, thereby suggesting a haemodynamically unstable state. It is imperative that future studies consider the implementation of more inclusive criteria to assess outcomes across the entire spectrum of trauma severity. The study’s exclusive focus on vital signs precludes the examination of other variables, such as trauma severity indices.

Conclusions

The utilization of shock indices (SI, MSI, and rSI) combined with age, GCS, and SpO2 facilitates prediction of outcomes in severe trauma patients. Among these triad-based indices, rSI × SpO2 × GCS/Age and Age SI/(SpO2 × GCS) were most effective for triage level discrimination; Age SI/(SpO2 × GCS) for in-hospital mortality prediction; and Age MSI/(SpO2 × GCS) for blood transfusion need. This supports the efficacy of novel triad-based composites in enhancing ED triage and risk stratification over standalone indices, though external validation remains essential.
