Patterns in biomedical research funding in India: An analysis of ICMR small and intermediate grants (2023–2025)
Authors: Priyanka Verma, Atul Kumar, Deepika Mishra, Varun Surya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesThe Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) funds small and intermediate grants to help researchers conduct their own biomedical research. The study examined the ICMR-funded small and intermediate grant projects between 2023 to 2025 to evaluate institutional involvement, thematic concentration, and geographic dispersion.MethodsThe data were extracted manually from six publicly available ICMR funding lists for small and intermediate grants (2023–2025). Institutes were categorised as: medical and non-medical/technical. We used keywords to code project titles so we could find the main research themes. We also looked at the geographic distribution based on where the grantee institute was located.ResultsOut of 1,766 project grants, 1,220 (69.1%) of the projects that got funding were from government institutions, while 546 (30.9%) were from private, trust, or NGO institutions. Medical institutions received 1,084 (61.4%) of grants, while non-medical/technical institutions received 682 (38.6%) grants with technical institutions increasingly participating throughout the study period. Subjects receiving maximum funding were oncology, infectious diseases (especially tuberculosis), and non-communicable diseases. About 70% of the funded projects were from institutions in north and south India, combined.Interpretation and conclusionsThe analysis shows that medical and technical institutions are getting almost similar funding from ICMR between 2023 and 2025. Geographic and institutional concentration remains high in north/south India. These results shed light on recent trends in funding distribution and point out areas where research capacity could be improved.

India needs biomedical research funding to address its unique genetic diversity and extensive tropical diseases. Indigenous innovations are creating high-skilled personals and reducing dependency on expensive imported drugs, making healthcare more affordable. This research funding provides vital laboratory equipment and fellowships to decrease brain drain and help Indian researchers to transform clinical discoveries into community-wide, life-saving medicines and health care devices.

The Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) is the apex body for the formulation, coordination and promotion of biomedical research in India.1,2 Its extramural research programme3 is a critical driver of health innovation.4,5 In recent years, ICMR has categorised its investigator-initiated research into ‘Small Grants’6-8 (up to 2 Crore INR for typically pilot studies, high-risk/high-reward, or early-career researchers) and ‘Intermediate Grants’9-11 (between 2-8 Crore INR per project for established concepts requiring scale-up or clinical validation).

The objective of this study was to analyse the distribution of investigator-initiated Small and Intermediate grants funded by ICMR during 2023 to 2025 with respect to institutional participation, thematic focus, and geographic distribution.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study was a descriptive analysis of investigator-initiated research projects funded12 by the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) under the Small Grant (SG) and Intermediate Grant (IG) schemes during 2023–2025. Data were extracted from publicly available ICMR funding notification documents that list approved projects under these schemes. These documents provide information on the project title, principal investigator, and institutional affiliation of the grantee. Only projects listed as approved for funding in the official documents were included. No individual-level data were used in the study.

Institutional classification

Institutions were categorised into two broad groups based on their primary organisational structure:1.Medical institutions – including medical colleges, hospitals, clinical research centres, and institutes primarily involved in medical and health sciences education or clinical research.2.Non-medical/technical institutions – including engineering institutes, universities, CSIR laboratories, and pharmaceutical or biotechnology research institutes.

The present analysis was limited to projects approved for funding, as publicly available ICMR documents do not provide information on the total number of applications received. Therefore, analyses were conducted using the number of funded projects as the denominator.

Geographic categorization

The geographic distribution of funded projects was determined using the primary location of the grantee institute as reported in the ICMR funding documents. Institutes were assigned to one of six regions of India: (north, south, west, east, north-east, central). Regional categorisation was based on commonly used administrative regional groupings of Indian States used in national reporting and research analyses. Projects were attributed to the region in which the lead institute was located.

The study period (2023–2025) was selected to reflect the most recent funding cycles following the post-pandemic restructuring of ICMR extramural research calls, providing insight into contemporary funding allocation patterns rather than long-term temporal trends.

We then tallied the number of projects and distinct institutions in each category. We scanned the lists for repeat names of the principal investigator (PI) within each year to identify individuals receiving multiple awards. We also counted frequency of each institution name to identify the highest-awarded institutions.

Results

A total of 1,766 investigator-initiated research projects funded under the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) Small Grant (SG) and Intermediate Grant (IG) schemes during 2023–2025 were included in the analysis. The categorised distribution of grant is shown in Figure 1.

Categorised distribution of grant.

Across the three-year period, government-funded institutions accounted for the majority of approved projects (1,220; 69.1%), while private, trust, and NGO institutions accounted for 546 projects (30.9%). This proportional distribution remained consistent across individual years, with government institutions receiving approximately two-thirds of the funded projects annually.

When classified by institutional domain, medical institutions received 1,084 projects (61.4%), whereas non-medical or technical institutions accounted for 682 projects (38.6%). Although medical institutions constituted the majority overall, the relative contribution of non-medical institutions increased over the study period, reflecting greater participation from engineering- and science-based institutes in recent funding cycles.

Table I shows that the top seven institutions alone secure over one-third of all grant money, indicating ICMR’s strategy to channel resources into high-capacity central and national institutes capable of delivering large-scale, high-impact research. The inclusion of major medical centres [All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi; Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research, PGIMER, Chandigarh; Christian Medical College (CMC), Vellore and Tata Memorial Centre (TMC), Mumbai] alongside premier technical and science institutions (IITs, IISc) in the top ranks demonstrates the active support for translational research and Med-Tech integration across India’s leading research ecosystems. Government institutions (Central and State) secured the vast majority of research funding, receiving approximately 69.1% of the total grants ( Table II).

Participation of non-medical institutions

The year-wise grant distribution between medical institutions and others is shown in Figure 2. Projects from engineering and technical institutes predominantly addressed medical device development, diagnostic platforms, bioengineering applications, and artificial intelligence–based health solutions. Projects from CSIR laboratories and universities were largely focused on basic biomedical sciences, genomics, molecular biology, and drug discovery–related research. The share of non-medical Institutes increased from 35% in 2023 to 42.1% in 2025. This confirms up the idea of a ‘Med-Tech’ convergence, which is happening because there has been a rise in proposals from IITs (Delhi, Madras, Guwahati) and IISc for biomedical engineering, AI/ML diagnostics and biosensors. The 682 grants awarded to non-medical Institutes represent 38.6% of the total funding corpus. This breakdown highlights the primary academic and research sectors contributing to the Med-Tech convergence trend in India ( Table III).

Engineering institutes (mostly IITs, NITs and BITS) get the most grants, getting almost half of all non-medical funding. CSIR institutes and specialised universities (like IISc and IISERs) have a large and equal share, with a focus on upstream research areas like genomics, molecular biology, and new drug targets. Grants to NIPERs (National Institutes of Pharmaceutical Education and Research) and other pharmacy colleges focus on translational research, which is important for developing drug delivery systems and pharmaceutical products.

Principal investigator (PI) performance and repeat grants

Analysis of Principal Investigator data indicates a small subset of researchers who receive multiple grants within a single year, typically in the form of two small grants or a combination of Small and Intermediate grants. This pattern may suggest that ICMR creates a ‘research pipeline,’ where PIs who successfully execute Small Grants are often funded for Intermediate Grants to scale up their findings.

Research priorities of ICMR grants (2023–2025)

The distribution of research grants by priority area for ICMR grants is shown in Figure 3. Infectious diseases, non-communicable diseases, and oncology together accounted for over 50% of all funding, reflecting India’s ‘triple burden’ of disease. Within infectious diseases, tuberculosis was the single most funded specific pathology, with projects ranging from drug resistance to host-directed therapies. The combined share of bio-engineering and AI/digital health (21.2%) highlights the ‘Med-Tech’ convergence trend, with significant investment in indigenous diagnostic tools and smart therapeutics.

Geographic distribution of ICMR grants (2023–2025)

Geographic analysis based on the primary location of the grantee institute showed that the northern region accounted for the highest proportion of funded projects (n=671, 38%), followed by the southern region (n=565, 32%). The western region accounted for 274 (15.5%) grants, while the eastern region contributed 168 (9.5%) of projects. North-East and central India received 35 (2%), and 53 (3%) grants, respectively. At the State level, Delhi, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Chandigarh together accounted for a substantial proportion of funded projects, corresponding to the presence of major medical and technical institutions in these regions.

Northern regional share is driven primarily by a higher number of funded projects awarded to AIIMS New Delhi and PGIMER Chandigarh, along with strong contributions from IIT Delhi and CSIR laboratory in Lucknow. Southern region is characterised by a diverse mix of premier private institutions (CMC Vellore, Manipal) and public technical heavyweights (IISc, IIT Madras, NIMHANS). Western region is dominated by Tata Memorial Centre (Mumbai) for oncology research and IIT Bombay and Pune University clusters. Rajasthan contributes via AIIMS Jodhpur and IIT Jodhpur. Eastern region is driven largely by research hubs in Kolkata [Indian Institute of Chemical Biology (IICB), Kolkata; ICMR–National Institute of Cholera and Enteric Diseases (NICED), Kolkata; All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS) and Institute of Life Sciences (IILS), Bhubaneswar]. Central region is seeing targeted growth, particularly through AIIMS Bhopal. AIIMS Guwahati and IIT Guwahati are the leading institutes receiving ICMR funding in the North-East Region. Discussion

The investigation of ICMR-funded Small and Intermediate Grant projects from 2023 to 2025 elucidates contemporary trends in biomedical research funding in India. The results show that funded projects are spread out across many different types of institutions, research themes, and geographic areas. However, there is a concentration of projects in high-capacity institutions.

Non-medical and technical institutions are getting more and more involved, making up almost two-fifths of all funded projects. This shows that engineering, computer science and technology-based methods are becoming more important in solving problems in biomedicine and public health. Even though there are more types of projects being funded, medical institutions still get most of the money, especially for research that focuses on patients and clinical settings. Institutions like AIIMS, PGIMER and CMC are well-represented because they have a lot of clinical infrastructure, patient access, and research capacity. Concentration of funding within such institutions has been previously documented in analyses of national research funding systems13,14 and is frequently linked to enhanced efficiency in project execution, though it may also indicate disparities in research capacity across regions.

The thematic distribution of funded projects corresponds with India’s principal disease burden. A lot of research in oncology, cancer,15,16 diabetes,17,18 infectious diseases like tuberculosis,19 and non-communicable diseases is in line with national health priorities20-22 and past evaluations of ICMR-supported research areas. The increasing focus on technology-enabled diagnostics and devices indicates an endeavour to augment disease-centric research with innovation-driven solutions, especially in resource-constrained environments.

Geographic analysis showed that funded projects were clustered in the north and south of India, which is where the best medical and technical institutes are located. Even though the eastern, north-eastern and central regions had fewer funded projects, their inclusion shows that research participation is slowly spreading beyond traditional hubs. Enhancing research infrastructure and capacity in underrepresented regions is a crucial factor for achieving equitable national research development.

The current study has certain limitations. We could only look at the number of funded projects because there was not always public information about the total number of applications received and the amounts of grants that were granted. Also, the geographic attribution was based on where the grantee institute was located. It was not possible to analyse multicentric projects or fieldwork done in other areas separately.

In conclusion, the examination of ICMR Small and Intermediate Grant funding from 2023 to 2025 underscores the growing integration of medical and technical institutions, a persistent focus on nationally significant disease priorities, and a continued concentration of funded initiatives within established research centres. These results give us a quick look at how funding has been given out recently and they may help future efforts to find a balance between innovation, capacity building and fair access to biomedical research opportunities in India.
