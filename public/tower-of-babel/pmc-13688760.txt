Effectiveness of mHealth interventions on glycemic outcomes in adults with type 2 diabetes in Gulf countries: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yousif B. Hamadalneel, Tanzeel M. A. Omer, Toga K. Mohamed, Mai Elzain Omer, Abdulmoniem Elmansor, Muataz Khalil, Hifa O. Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) poses a major public health challenge in Gulf countries and has among the highest global prevalence; although mobile health (mHealth) interventions have demonstrated global benefits for diabetes self-management, region-specific evidence remains limited.ObjectiveTo assess the effectiveness of mHealth interventions compared with usual care for glycemic control among adults with T2DM in Gulf countries.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted following PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane CENTRAL were searched from inception to May 15, 2025. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and analytical observational studies comparing mHealth interventions with usual care in adults with T2DM in Gulf countries were included. The risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. A random-effects meta-analysis estimated pooled mean differences in HbA1c.ResultsThe search yielded 516 records, of which three studies involving 371 participants (mHealth n = 216; usual care n = 155) met the inclusion criteria. Two studies were conducted in Saudi Arabia, and one was conducted in the United Arab Emirates. Interventions included WhatsApp-based diabetes self-management education, mHealth-enabled health coaching, and hybrid care models with remote monitoring. The primary meta-analysis revealed a statistically significant reduction in HbA1c, favoring mHealth interventions compared with usual care (pooled mean difference − 0.86%; 95% CI: −1.48 to − 0.24; I² = 0%). However, sensitivity analysis including only RCTs yielded an inconclusive estimate, with a pooled mean difference of − 1.02% (95% CI: −6.34 to 4.29; I² = 49%).ConclusionsLimited evidence suggests that mHealth interventions may improve glycemic control among adults with type 2 diabetes in Gulf countries. However, the evidence should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of studies and the inconclusive findings of the sensitivity analysis. Larger, high quality RCTs with longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm their effectiveness and inform policy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03492-2.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) comprises a group of metabolic disorders characterized by chronic hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both [1]. DM, particularly type 2 diabetes, represents a major public health burden in Gulf countries, which rank among the highest globally for diabetes prevalence, with adult rates exceeding 20% in several nations, driven by rapid urbanization, sedentary lifestyles, and high obesity prevalence [2].

DM is challenging to manage, often leading to increased complications and substantial financial burdens for both patients and society [3]. Therefore, providing effective health care services that support patient self-management is essential, as behaviors such as maintaining a healthy diet and weight, engaging in regular physical activity, adhering to prescribed medications, regularly monitoring body weight and blood glucose, and supporting mental well-being are fundamental to effective diabetes control [4]. Evidence indicates that self-management support improves glycemic control, reduces the risk of serious complications, and enhances psychosocial and behavioral outcomes in patients with diabetes [5–8]. However, barriers such as limited individualized care, costly and inconvenient education programs, and poor patient–provider communication hinder sustained self-management, underscoring the need for effective and scalable self-management strategies to reduce health care costs and improve quality of life [9].

Emerging mobile health (mHealth) solutions offer the potential to address diabetes management challenges, as the rapid global adoption of mobile technologies has transformed daily life in both developed and developing countries [10]. Mobile technologies enable real-time access, mobility, and direct communication, facilitating timely health information exchange and efficient self-management support [11, 12]. They also improve access to affordable health services for low-income populations and underserved groups in middle- and high-income countries [13]. Mobile applications, in particular, can enhance adherence to clinical recommendations, strengthen patient–provider communication, and support sustained behavior change [14, 15].

Globally, systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that mHealth interventions significantly improve diabetes outcomes, achieving clinically meaningful reductions in glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) alongside improvements in self-management behaviors and quality of life [16, 17]. Global pooled analyses across type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and prediabetes populations report greater HbA1c reductions than usual care [18], findings that are consistent among older adults and in low-resource settings [19, 20]. Effective interventions span diverse mHealth modalities, including smartphone applications, short messaging services (SMSs), telemonitoring, and remote coaching [16–20], highlighting their scalability and relevance for diabetes management in high-burden regions such as Gulf countries.

Consistent with current international standards, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) emphasizes the growing role of diabetes technologies and digital health tools as integral components of diabetes self-management and care when combined with education and ongoing support [21]. However, the ADA also highlights that technology effectiveness is highly context dependent, requiring individualization, appropriate training, and health system readiness [21]. In Gulf countries, where the burden of type 2 diabetes is exceptionally high and digital adoption is widespread, evidence guiding the optimal use of mHealth applications remains fragmented and under-synthesized [2, 10]. The lack of region-specific evidence limits alignment between guideline recommendations and real-world implementation. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis examined the effectiveness of mHealth interventions on glycemic outcomes in adults with type 2 diabetes in Gulf countries to support the development of evidence-based, contextually appropriate digital diabetes care strategies that are consistent with contemporary standards of care.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22]. The review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251054897).

Search strategy and keywords

A systematic literature search of published studies was conducted across the following electronic databases from their inception to May 15, 2025: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Additional file 1). The PICO information consisted of population (P): patients with type 2 diabetes in Gulf countries (Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman); intervention (I): any intervention(s)/exposure(s) involving mHealth for managing and/or monitoring patients with type 2 diabetes; comparison (C): usual or standard care; and outcome (O): the outcome of interest is the change in HbA1c levels. The search strategy used a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to the impact of mHealth interventions on diabetes outcomes in adults with type 2 diabetes in Gulf countries (Additional file 1). An example of a PubMed search string is as follows: (((“diabetes mellitus, type 2“[MeSH Terms] OR (“diabet*“[Text Word] AND (“type 2“[Text Word] OR “type two“[Text Word]))) OR “hyperglycemia“[Text Word] OR “hyperglycem*“[Text Word] NOT (“child*“[Text Word] OR “teen*“[Text Word] OR “adolesc*“[Text Word] OR “juvenile“[Text Word] OR “pregna*“[Text Word] OR “gestation*“[Text Word] OR “animal“[Text Word])) AND (“mobile applications“[mh] OR “app“[tw] OR appli*[tw] OR “smartphone“[tw] OR “tablet“[tw] OR “mobile device“[tw] OR “mHealth“[tw] OR “m-Health“[tw] OR “DTx“[tw])) AND ((((((((((((“Gulf countries”)) OR (“GCC countries”)) OR (“GCC”)) OR (“Saudi Arabia“[MeSH Terms]))) OR (“UAE”)) OR (“United Arab Emirates”)) OR (“Qatar”)) OR (“Oman”)) OR (“Kuwait”)) OR (“Bahrain”)) (Additional file 1).

Eligibility criteria

This review included studies involving adults with type 2 diabetes whose condition was managed or monitored via mHealth application compared with usual or standard care. Eligible study designs were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental and analytical observational studies reporting changes in HbA1c levels. Only studies conducted in Gulf countries, published in English, and with no restrictions on publication date were included.

Studies were excluded if they focused on children, adolescents, individuals with type 1 or gestational diabetes, or animal subjects. Additional exclusion criteria included conference abstracts, case series, case reports, cross-sectional descriptive studies, editorials, incomplete studies, and publications in languages other than English.

Screening and study selection process

All identified studies were imported into Mendeley software to detect and remove duplicate records [23]. Two independent reviewers (TMAO and TKM) screened the titles and abstracts of all the retrieved articles to assess eligibility, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion within the research team. The same reviewers subsequently evaluated the full-text articles using Rayyan software [24], and disagreements were resolved through group consensus. The final list of included studies was verified and compiled by YBH.

Data extraction

The authors (TMAO and HOA) independently extracted the data using a standardized template in Microsoft Office Excel 2019, and the author (YBH) reviewed the extracted data for accuracy and consistency. The extracted data were summarized descriptively, and the outcomes were analyzed thematically. The data extracted included author name, year of publication, country, study design, follow-up duration, patient characteristics, descriptions of the intervention and comparator, measurement metrics, number of participants, and outcomes.

Operational definition

For the purposes of this review, mHealth was defined as medical and public health practices supported by mobile devices, including mobile phones, patient monitoring devices, personal digital assistants, and other wireless technologies [13]. Eligible mHealth interventions were predefined to include mobile applications, WhatsApp-based interventions, telecoaching delivered through telephone or video consultations, remote monitoring, and hybrid care models incorporating mobile technologies to support diabetes self-management and care.

Measures of effect

The measure of effect will be the improvement in glycemic control, expressed as the HbA1c levels between the intervention and standard care (or comparator) groups.

For interpretation of clinical relevance, an HbA1c reduction of ≥ 0.5% was considered the minimally important difference (MID) [18, 21].

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (YBH and TMAO) independently assessed the risk of bias (RoB) for the included studies. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2 (RoB 2) [25] was used for RCTs, while the risk of bias for case‒control studies was assessed using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) [26]. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consulting a third reviewer (HOA).

Data synthesis

Both qualitative and quantitative synthesis approaches were applied. A narrative synthesis was used to summarize the characteristics and outcomes of the included studies. Quantitative meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) software (Cochrane Collaboration) [27], which calculates pooled mean differences with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I² statistic, and a random effects model was applied to account for potential between-study variability.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the pooled effect estimate, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding the observational study and repeating the meta-analysis using only RCTs.

Certinity of evidence

The certainty of the evidence for the primary outcome (HbA1c) was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [28]. Evidence was evaluated across the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias and categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low certainty.

Results

Study selection

The initial search yielded 516 studies from four electronic databases. After 94 duplicates were removed, 422 records were screened on the basis of their titles and abstracts. This led to the exclusion of 402 records that did not meet the inclusion criteria. The full texts of the remaining 20 articles were assessed for eligibility, resulting in the exclusion of 17 studies. Consequently, three studies satisfied all eligibility criteria and were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Characteristics of the study participants

Across the three included studies, a total of 371 adults with T2DM were included in the analysis. Of these, 216 participants received mHealth-based interventions, while 155 participants received usual care [29–31]. All participants were adults aged 18 years and older who were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes [29–31]. In the study by Almulhim et al. (2024), participants had baseline HbA1c levels ≥ 7.0%, with 29 individuals randomized to the mHealth intervention (n = 14) or usual care (n = 15) [30]. Yagoob et al. (2024) included 80 adults with baseline HbA1c levels ≥ 9.0%, equally allocated to a WhatsApp-based diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES) intervention (n = 40) or usual care (n = 40) [29]. Zakaria et al. (2024) recruited 262 adults with HbA1c < 7.5% to ≥ 9.0%, of whom 162 received a WhatsApp-based hybrid diabetes care intervention and 100 received usual care [31] (Table 1).

Description of mHealth intervention

The included studies evaluated three distinct mHealth interventions with varying delivery models. Almulhim et al. implemented a 12-week mHealth-enabled health coaching intervention combining telephone and video consultations with asynchronous WhatsApp follow-up, focusing on individualized behavioral support and diabetes self-management [30]. Yaqoob et al. assessed a WhatsApp-based diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES) program that delivers standardized educational content, including text messages, images, and videos, to improve glycemic control among adults with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes [29]. Zakaria et al. evaluated a 90-day hybrid diabetes care model integrating routine in-clinic visits with continuous mHealth-supported remote monitoring, including continuous glucose monitoring, lifestyle tracking, and scheduled virtual interactions [31] (Table 1).

Impact of mHealth interventions on diabetes outcomes

Across all included studies, glycemic control was assessed using HbA1c, reported as the mean percentage values with corresponding standard deviations (means ± SDss) [29–31]. The intervention groups consistently demonstrated significantly lower final mean HbA1c levels compared with control groups (p < 0.001) [29–31]. Specifically, Almulhim et al. reported a final mean HbA1c of 7.29% ± 1.38 in the mHealth intervention group versus 8.93% ± 1.81 in the usual care group [30]. Similarly, Yaqoob et al. reported mean HbA1c values of 9.52% ± 0.98 in the intervention group compared with 10.26% ± 1.13 in the control group [29]. Zakaria et al. also reported superior glycemic outcomes in the mHealth exposure group, with a mean HbA1c of 6.58% ± 0.92, compared with 7.44% ± 1.80 in patients receiving usual care [31] (Table 1).

Quantitative analysis

A random-effects meta-analysis of three studies (mHealth n = 216; usual care n = 155) demonstrated that mHealth interventions were associated with a greater reduction in HbA1c than usual care was. The pooled mean difference was − 0.86% (95% CI: − 1.48 to -0.24), indicating that mHealth was significantly greater (T = 5.96, df = 2, P = 0.03). Study-level effects ranged from − 0.74% to − 1.64%, with all three studies showing clinically meaningful reductions. No heterogeneity was observed (I² = 0%, τ² = 0.00, P = 0.37) (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analysis

A random-effects meta-analysis including only the two RCTs [29, 30], yielded an inconclusive estimate, with a pooled mean difference of − 1.02% (95% CI: −6.34 to 4.29; P = 0.25). Moderate heterogeneity was observed (I² = 49%, τ² = 0.20, P = 0.16) (Fig. 5).

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach. For the primary analysis, which included two RCTs [29, 30], and one observational study [31], the certainty of evidence was judged to be very low because of the mixed study designs and imprecision resulting from the small number of studies and participants. For the sensitivity analysis including only RCTs [29, 30], the certainty of evidence was judged to be moderate and was downgraded because of imprecision related to the limited sample size and wide confidence interval (Table 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to specifically evaluate the impact of mHealth interventions on glycemic control among adults with type 2 diabetes in Gulf countries, with glycemic improvement assessed by differences in HbA1c levels between mHealth intervention groups and those receiving standard care.

The present findings demonstrate that mHealth interventions significantly improved HbA1c over 12 weeks, highlighting their potential role to enhance diabetes management during the study follow-up period. Importantly, the pooled HbA1c reduction exceeded the prespecified MID of 0.5%, indicating that the observed benefit is clinically meaningful. Although two RCTs [29, 30], and one observational study [31] met the predefined eligibility criteria and were included in the primary meta-analysis, differences in study design may have influenced the pooled effect estimate. Therefore, a sensitivity analysis excluding the observational study was performed. The analysis yielded a wide confidence interval that crossed the null effect, resulting in an inconclusive estimate despite a similar point estimate. This finding likely reflects the substantial reduction in sample size after excluding the observational study, which contributed more than half of the participants, thereby reducing the statistical precision of the pooled estimate. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as observational studies are inherently more susceptible to confounding and selection bias. Large systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs have consistently shown that smartphone- and mobile-based self-management interventions are associated with clinically meaningful reductions in HbA1c, alongside improvements in medication adherence, self-efficacy, self-care behaviors, and health-related quality of life [16, 17]. Notably, pooled evidence from global reviews encompassing diverse populations, including individuals with type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and prediabetes, revealed significantly greater HbA1c reductions among users of digital health interventions than among those receiving usual care, with reported mean decreases approaching − 0.90% [18]. These benefits have also been observed among older adults with type 2 diabetes, where mobile health interventions have been linked to improved glycemic control as well as favorable metabolic outcomes, including reductions in postprandial blood glucose and triglyceride levels [20]. Importantly, evidence from African settings further corroborates these findings, with systematic reviews and meta-analyses reporting statistically significant reductions in HbA1c attributable to mHealth-based diabetes management [19], reinforcing the generalizability and relevance of digital health interventions across diverse healthcare contexts. Collectively, these findings indicate that in Gulf countries, which are characterized by a high diabetes burden, widespread smartphone use, and expanding digital health infrastructure, mHealth interventions have strong potential for scalable integration into chronic care models. Sustained HbA1c improvements over 12 weeks suggest their capacity to address persistent challenges related to suboptimal self-management, treatment inertia, and fragmented follow-up care. Realizing this potential will require embedding mHealth solutions within national diabetes programs and primary care pathways, ensuring interoperability with electronic health records, and establishing supportive regulatory, reimbursement, and data governance frameworks, alongside culturally tailored design and sustained clinician engagement, to promote equitable and sustainable population-level impact.

This systematic review and meta-analysis included studies employing a broad range of m-health modalities, such as health coaching delivered via video consultations and telephone sessions [30]; hybrid continuous-care models combining in-clinic visits with remote monitoring and in-app communication [31]; and DSMES delivered through WhatsApp, covering core self-management domains [29]. These findings are consistent with evidence from multiple systematic reviews demonstrating the beneficial effects of diverse m-health interventions on glycemic control, including smartphone applications and text-, telephone-, and video-based communication [16]; SMS messaging and smartphone applications [17]; varied m-health intervention formats [18]; telemonitoring, telecommunication platforms, online educational programs, and wearable technologies [20]; and SMS messages, telephone calls, and customized mobile applications [19]. These findings are further supported by the development of the culturally tailored “DiabetesSutra” mobile application in India, which integrates personalized lifestyle advice, self-monitoring, and educational support to facilitate diabetes self-management, highlighting the growing role of context-specific mHealth solutions in chronic disease management [32]. In Gulf countries, where digital connectivity is high and diabetes prevalence is substantial, this heterogeneity suggests that effective m-health interventions can be flexibly implemented to match patient preferences and health system capacity. Both advanced app-based and telemonitoring solutions and simpler platforms such as SMS or WhatsApp may be leveraged to support scalable, patient-centered diabetes care within existing digital health infrastructures.

The certainty of evidence differed between the primary and sensitivity analyses. The primary analysis was judged to provide very low certainty evidence because it combined RCTs and observational studies and was limited by the small number of studies and participants. In contrast, the certainty of evidence from the sensitivity analysis, which included only RCTs, was judged to be moderate but remained limited by imprecision due to the small sample size and wide confidence interval. Therefore, although the primary analysis suggests that mHealth interventions may improve glycemic control, these findings should be interpreted cautiously until confirmed by larger, adequately powered RCTs.

This review is limited by the small number of eligible studies, reflecting the nascent state of mHealth research in the Gulf region. Nevertheless, the limited available evidence suggests that mHealth interventions may improve glycemic control among adults with type 2 diabetes in Gulf countries. However, given the small number of studies, modest sample sizes, and relatively short follow-up periods, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Although minor design-related concerns were noted, the risk of bias was generally low, and the observed low heterogeneity across studies suggests a consistent intervention effect across different populations and care settings. These findings indicate that mHealth interventions ranging from simple messaging-based programs to more comprehensive hybrid care models can be feasibly and reliably integrated into routine diabetes management in Gulf countries. In addition to their clinical benefits, mHealth interventions may offer economic value by reducing healthcare utilization and improving healthcare efficiency; however, robust cost-effectiveness studies are currently lacking. Although the included mHealth interventions differed in their delivery modalities, intervention intensity, technological sophistication, and level of healthcare professional involvement, they shared the common objective of supporting diabetes self-management, improving glycemic control and met the operational definition of mHealth used in this study. Therefore, we considered these interventions to be sufficiently comparable to evaluate the overall effect of mHealth. To account for the anticipated clinical heterogeneity among the included studies, a random-effects meta-analysis was employed. This approach assumes that the true intervention effects may vary across studies and provides a more conservative pooled estimate. Nevertheless, the observed heterogeneity in intervention characteristics should be considered when interpreting the pooled effect, and future studies with more homogeneous intervention designs are needed to better identify the effectiveness of specific mHealth approaches. Additionally, future large-scale RCTs in Gulf countries should therefore incorporate formal cost-effectiveness analyses alongside clinical outcomes to inform policy decisions, reimbursement strategies, and sustainable integration into national diabetes care programs.

Study limitations and strengths

This systematic review and meta-analysis has several strengths. The review followed PRISMA guidelines, was prospectively registered with PROSPERO, and applied a comprehensive search strategy across multiple major databases. The inclusion of both RCTs and an observational study allowed a broader assessment of real-world mHealth applications, while the use of standardized risk-of-bias tools and quantitative synthesis strengthened the methodological rigor. The consistency of the HbA1c reduction across all included studies and the absence of statistical heterogeneity further support the robustness of the findings.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Only three studies met the inclusion criteria, limiting their statistical power and generalizability across the diverse populations and healthcare systems of the Gulf region. The small sample sizes, relatively short follow-up periods, and inclusion of studies conducted only in Saudi Arabia and the UAE may limit the assessment of the long-term effectiveness and sustainability of mHealth interventions and restrict the generalizability of the findings across all Gulf countries. Variability in intervention design, intensity, and delivery platforms also precluded detailed subgroup or component-specific analyses. Additionally, publication bias could not be formally assessed due to the limited number of studies. Finally, the included studies primarily assessed HbA1c, while important patient-centered outcomes, such as medication adherence and self-efficacy, were not consistently reported. This limits a comprehensive evaluation of the overall effectiveness and patient-centered impact of mHealth interventions. Additionally, evidence on cost-effectiveness and broader health system impacts is scarce, highlighting priorities for future research.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides preliminary evidence suggesting that mHealth interventions may improve glycemic control among adults with type 2 diabetes in Gulf countries. However, this conclusion is primarily based on the overall pooled analysis, whereas the sensitivity analysis restricted to RCTs produced an inconclusive estimate because of substantial imprecision. Given the small number of studies, limited sample sizes, and short follow-up durations, the current evidence should be interpreted with caution. Larger, well-designed RCTs with longer follow-up are needed to confirm the effectiveness of mHealth interventions before firm conclusions regarding their routine implementation can be drawn.
