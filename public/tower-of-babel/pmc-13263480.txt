Tell me, gastric ultrasound: does this child have a full stomach?
Authors: Sarah Sintzel, Kelly Dilworth, Jean-Noël Evain
Published: 2026

The risk of pulmonary aspiration of gastric contents and the need for airway protection are frequent clinical concerns in acutely ill children who are unable to protect their own airway because they are comatose or sedated. In the operating room, procedures are most often scheduled, and children follow nil per os guidelines so that the stomach should be empty at the time of anesthesia (1). Consequently, the risk of pulmonary aspiration in this setting has been reported to be low [0.006% in the Wake-Up-Safe registry (2); 0.1% in the European Apricot study (3)]. However, the emergence of new medications that delay gastric emptying may challenge this apparent safety in the coming years (4). In critical care and emergency contexts, the absence of prior fasting—combined with conditions that may delay gastric emptying (e.g., intestinal disease, pain, morphine)—results in a higher prevalence of at-risk gastric contents. The reported incidence of pulmonary aspiration appears to be higher in this population [0.7% in the pediatric critical care Near-4-Kids registry (5)].

Point-of-care gastric ultrasound is a valuable tool for assessing gastric contents, predicting aspiration risk, and guiding airway management and anesthetic strategy in children (6). Initially described in adult anesthesia (7), this technique has rapidly been incorporated into pediatric practice and beyond the operating room, including in emergency settings (8). Using a paramedian sagittal view, the gastric antrum is identified and its contents qualitatively evaluated, allowing distinction between solid (or thick liquid) contents, clear fluid, and an empty antrum (9). Current European guidelines recommend qualitative ultrasound assessment of the gastric antrum in the right lateral decubitus position in children undergoing anesthesia for emergency procedures or when there is uncertainty regarding nil per os status (1). Excluding the patients in whom solid or thick liquid contents are clearly identified, this approach may be questioned for two reasons. Firstly, qualitative assessment raises concerns regarding subjectivity, which may persist even among experienced operators. To refine this assessment, a semi-quantitative approach has been proposed, using an ultrasound examination in the supine position followed by a second in the right lateral decubitus position (10). Quantitative estimation of gastric fluid volume—calculated from the antral cross-sectional area—is commonly used in research settings and has been advocated for routine clinical practice. Alternative formulas have been proposed in children (11), but Spencer’s formula—which incorporates the child’s age in months—remains the simplest for clinical use (12). Clinical algorithms combining qualitative and quantitative approaches have been proposed in both adults (13) and children (8). The second issue is that although the right lateral decubitus position increases sensitivity by allowing gastric fluid contents to pool in the antrum, this position can prove difficult to achieve due to pain or inadequate cooperation (14). The semi-recumbent position (45° head elevation) has been proposed as an alternative in adults (15).

We read with great interest the recent article by Cercueil et al. (16), which investigated the performance of gastric ultrasound in children examined in the semi-recumbent position. In this prospective randomized study, 90 healthy fasted children aged 1–10 years were randomized to ingest different predetermined volumes of water, generating a prevalence of at-risk gastric fluid volumes similar to that previously reported in children undergoing non-elective surgery (approximately 40%) (14,17). Blind evaluation of the diagnostic performance of gastric ultrasound for identifying an at-risk gastric fluid volume (>1.25 mL·kg–1) was subsequently performed using two approaches, namely (I) qualitative assessment performed in the 45° semi-recumbent supine position alone; and (II) use of a clinical algorithm combining qualitative assessment performed in the semi-recumbent supine position and the semi-recumbent right lateral decubitus position, supplemented—when necessary—by quantitative estimation of gastric fluid volume calculated from the antral cross-sectional area measured in the latter position. Diagnostic performance differed between the two approaches. Qualitative assessment in the semi-recumbent supine position alone identified gastric volumes >1.25 mL·kg–1 with 75% sensitivity and 85% specificity. Given the prevalence of at-risk gastric volumes in this sample, positive (77%) and negative (84%) predictive values were clinically meaningful. Using the algorithm increased sensitivity and negative predictive value to 86% and 89%, respectively, at the expense of reduced specificity and positive predictive value (78% and 72%, respectively). The quantitative volume estimation based on antral cross-sectional area measured in the semi-recumbent right lateral decubitus position yielded a similar performance to that reported for the classic right lateral decubitus position without head elevation (12). The primary concern with gastric ultrasound is incorrectly identifying an at-risk stomach as empty and consequently failing to implement preventive measures against aspiration. It therefore seems reasonable to prioritize approaches that maximize sensitivity and negative predictive value. The authors conclude that qualitative assessment alone in the 45° semi-recumbent supine position lacks sufficient sensitivity for clinical practice in children. When fluid is detected in the antrum, they recommend complementing the examination with quantitative analysis based on measurement of the antral cross-sectional area in the right lateral decubitus position. Although its performance remains limited, estimation of gastric fluid volume meaningfully improves the evaluation of stomach contents in pediatrics. Overall, these findings suggest that the semi-recumbent position does not replace the right lateral decubitus position for gastric ultrasound examination in children. However, when the right lateral decubitus position is not achievable, the semi-recumbent position appears preferable to the strict supine position. Future evaluation of a clinical algorithm incorporating quantitative gastric fluid volume estimation based on antral cross-sectional area measured in the 45° semi-recumbent supine position would be of interest.

Cercueil et al. should be commended for their study. However, their work—along with the broader literature on gastric ultrasound—raises several important issues that warrant discussion. As the authors themselves acknowledge, this was an experimental study with strictly-selected healthy child participants. This study population does not fully represent real-life pediatric patients, who may present uncertain fasting histories and variable gastric contents, or may be uncooperative, resulting in challenging ultrasound assessment. One might also criticize the study for utilizing and assessing clear liquids only, which does not forcibly represent everyday clinical practice, where gastric contents may include solid or thick liquid material. The authors deliberately chose to focus on the common yet challenging scenario in which clear liquid gastric contents are identified—situations in which aspiration risk varies widely depending on volume and is particularly difficult to estimate using qualitative assessment alone. This leads to the following question: above what threshold volume should liquid gastric contents be considered “at risk”? In the present study, the authors selected the commonly accepted threshold of 1.25 mL·kg–1, the value considered to significantly exceed the physiological volume of gastric secretions. Although clinical practice often requires a binary decision (empty versus full stomach), gastric volume is a continuous variable. The risk of aspiration likely follows this continuum rather than occurring only beyond a specific threshold. Finally, because pulmonary aspiration of gastric contents is a very rare complication, it seems difficult—if not impossible—to demonstrate that gastric ultrasound reduces this risk in pediatric practice. Significant gastric contents are a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for pulmonary aspiration to occur. In addition to gastric contents, other risk factors for aspiration must be considered, such as gastric insufflation during face-mask ventilation, inadequate depth of anesthesia during intubation, or a difficult airway. It would seem reasonable to assume that confirmation of an empty stomach at a given moment allows the risk of pulmonary aspiration to be considered close to zero. Rather than applying the simplistic rule that “emergency surgery equals full stomach”, gastric ultrasound allows risk to be assessed on a case-by-case basis and to tailor induction and airway management strategies accordingly. Conversely, at-risk gastric contents may be present even in children who are nil per os, particularly in the presence of certain medications or clinical conditions that delay gastric emptying. Rather than relying on the equally simplistic assumption that “Nil per os status equals empty stomach”, gastric ultrasound again allows a more rational individualized approach. Even when at-risk gastric contents are identified, a rapid sequence induction technique in young children must be adapted to minimize the risk of hypoxemia—which, unlike aspiration, is common in pediatric practice (18).

To conclude, let us return to our initial question: tell me, gastric ultrasound: does this child have a full stomach? For non-radiologists, the answer is becoming progressively clearer, and diagnostic accuracy continues to improve. Ultrasound is now widely available in pediatric departments in modern hospital settings, including the intensive care unit, operating theatre, and emergency department. Qualitative gastric ultrasound already offers a major contribution to assessing aspiration risk under anesthesia or sedation in pediatric practice. Further studies are still needed to enable more precise risk stratification, refine clinical algorithms, determine the most appropriate patient positioning, and clarify how and when quantitative assessment should complement qualitative evaluation. Point-of-care ultrasound has become our new stethoscope for the comprehensive assessment of children (19). Like a stethoscope, it does not provide answers in isolation, but it is a highly informative tool that complements clinical evaluation—and the more we use it, the more confident and nuanced our decision-making become. So, fire up your ultrasound.
