Associated factors and a prognostic model for 28-day mortality in Staphylococcus aureus bloodstream infection
Authors: Zhanpeng Yu, Zeyun Lin, Xiaoyan Tian, Zhenle Zhang, Chuyue Zhou, Yuntao He, Huiyan Wu, Yanling Zhang, Liang Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to identify factors associated with Staphylococcus aureus bloodstream infection (S aureus BSI), compare 28-day survival after propensity score matching (PSM), and develop a prognostic model for 28-day mortality. Adult intensive care unit patients with culture-confirmed BSI were identified in Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV. Factors associated with S aureus BSI were assessed in the full cohort by univariable logistic regression. Patients with S aureus and non-S aureus BSI were matched 1:1 using nearest-neighbor PSM without a caliper. Survival was examined using Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox models with matched-pair cluster-robust standard errors. Among patients with S aureus BSI, prognostic variables were screened by clinical domain and best-subset selection using minimum Bayesian Information Criterion; 10-fold cross-validated least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression served as a sensitivity analysis. Model performance was assessed by bootstrap internal validation and refitting in an external cohort. The cohort included 2221 patients, including 297 with S aureus BSI. Shorter time from admission to confirmed BSI, absence of tumor, shorter invasive ventilation and vascular catheterization, and fewer antibiotic types before BSI were associated with S aureus BSI. After 1:1 PSM, 28-day mortality was 26.9% versus 24.2%, with no significant survival difference (hazard ratio 1.11, 95% confidence interval 0.80–1.53; P = .539). The prognostic model included age, maximum red cell distribution width, minimum white blood cell count, minimum activated partial thromboplastin time, and maximum albumin. The apparent C-statistic was 0.816 (95% confidence interval 0.757–0.870), and the optimism-corrected C-statistic was 0.804 (95% confidence interval 0.752–0.861). After refitting in 32 external-cohort patients, the same 5 predictors yielded an apparent C-statistic of 0.778 (95% confidence interval 0.591–0.929). S aureus BSI was associated with a distinct prediagnosis profile, but pathogen group was not associated with 28-day survival after matching. The 5-variable model showed good optimism-corrected discrimination. The original prediction equation requires validation in larger external cohorts.

1. Introduction

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) are major causes of critical illness and death. Intensive care unit (ICU) patients are particularly susceptible because of comorbidities, organ dysfunction, and frequent exposure to invasive devices and antimicrobial therapy.[1,2] The pathogen distribution of ICU BSI is heterogeneous, and both host characteristics and healthcare exposures can influence the organism recovered and the subsequent clinical course.

Staphylococcus aureus is a frequent and clinically important cause of community- and healthcare-associated BSI. A systematic review reported substantial early and late mortality after S aureus bacteremia, while a contemporary prospective cohort emphasized that mortality also reflects acquisition setting, infection focus, host condition, and competing noninfectious causes.[3,4] These observations support separating 2 questions: whether patients with S aureus BSI differ from patients with BSI caused by other organisms, and which factors predict mortality within S aureus BSI.

Accordingly, this study aimed to characterize clinical factors associated with S aureus BSI in the full Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) BSI cohort, compare 28-day survival between patients with S aureus and non-S aureus BSI after propensity score matching (PSM), and develop and evaluate a parsimonious model for 28-day mortality among patients with S aureus BSI.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study used MIMIC-IV version 2.2 (MIT Laboratory for Computational Physiology, Massachusetts Institute of Technology), which contains de-identified electronic health records from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2019.[5] Eligible patients were adults aged 18 years or older with at least 1 positive blood culture, an identifiable organism, and an ICU admission during the corresponding hospitalization. Repeated antimicrobial-susceptibility records referring to the same microbiological event were collapsed. For patients with 1 or more S aureus-positive cultures, the earliest S aureus-positive culture was defined as the index culture, and the patient was classified in the S aureus BSI group. For patients without any S aureus-positive culture, the earliest culture positive for another organism was used as the index culture, and the patient was classified in the non-S aureus BSI group. Only 1 index BSI episode per patient was included. The external cohort comprised 32 adult ICU patients with S aureus BSI treated at the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University between 2020 and 2022. External-cohort eligibility, index date, predictor windows, and outcome ascertainment followed the same definitions as in the development cohort. The use of MIMIC-IV was approved by the institutional review boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. The external cohort was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (approval No. GYWY-K2025-64). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design; MIMIC-IV records were de-identified.

2.2. Definitions and data processing

Confirmed BSI was defined by the index eligible positive blood culture. The prognostic outcome was all-cause death within 28 days after confirmed BSI. Demographics, comorbidities, treatments, vital signs, and laboratory measurements were extracted using PostgreSQL. Prediagnosis exposures and prognostic measurements were defined during the 7 days before confirmed BSI. Time from admission to confirmed BSI, invasive ventilation duration, and vascular catheterization duration were measured in days; the number of antibiotic types was treated as a continuous count. When repeated measurements were available, the prespecified minimum or maximum was used. Candidate variables with more than 30% missingness at the cohort level were excluded. Data completeness was then calculated across the 76 retained candidate variables, and patients with <70% completeness were excluded. Remaining missing values in continuous variables were imputed using the mean before analysis; categorical variables used in the analyses had no missing values. Predictors in the final prognostic model were retained as continuous variables.

2.3. Descriptive and association analyses

Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range) and categorical variables as number (percentage). In the full pre-match cohort, separate univariable logistic regression models estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association of each prespecified clinical variable with S aureus BSI. These analyses were intended to describe organism-associated factors and were not interpreted as independent causal effects.

2.4. Propensity score matching and survival analysis

Propensity scores for S aureus BSI were estimated using logistic regression. Covariates were sex, age, time from admission to confirmed BSI, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, liver disease, diabetes, renal disease, tumor, surgery, invasive ventilation duration, vascular catheterization duration, dialysis before BSI, and number of antibiotic types during the 7 days before confirmed BSI. These variables were selected a priori because they could reflect host condition or prediagnosis healthcare exposure.[6,7] Nearest-neighbor matching targeted the average effect among S aureus BSI patients and used a 1:1 ratio without replacement or a caliper, with patients ordered by descending propensity score. Covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD of 0.10 used as the prespecified balance threshold.[6] Twenty-eight-day survival was compared by Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test. Marginal hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated with Cox regression using matched-pair cluster-robust standard errors.[8] Sensitivity models adjusted for age, sex, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, liver disease, diabetes, renal disease, and tumor; a complete-case model additionally adjusted for maximum Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score during the 7 days before BSI. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals.

2.5. Prognostic model development and validation

Among patients with S aureus BSI, the outcome was 28-day mortality from confirmed BSI. To limit the number of variables entered simultaneously with 80 events, bidirectional stepwise multivariable logistic regression was first performed within 7 prespecified clinical domains. Retained terms were entered into best-subset selection, and the subset with the minimum Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) was used for the final logistic model. ORs and 95% CIs were reported for final predictors. As a complementary sensitivity analysis, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation was applied to the 34 screened candidates, using the 1-standard-error penalty. Discrimination was quantified by the C-statistic. Internal validation of the fixed final 5-variable model was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism in the C-statistic. Calibration was displayed in predicted-risk groups with observed proportions and 95% Wilson CIs. In the external cohort, a logistic regression model was refitted using the same 5 prespecified predictors; all coefficients, including the intercept, were reestimated. The apparent C-statistic and Brier score of the refitted model were calculated, and uncertainty in the C-statistic was assessed using 10,000 stratified bootstrap resamples. Among the 214 patients with complete score data, separate univariable logistic regression models relating Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation III (APACHE III), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and SOFA to 28-day mortality were fitted. Predicted probabilities from these score models and from the nomogram were used for decision curve analysis (DCA). Reporting followed the TRIPOD + AI recommendations.[9] Analyses used R version 4.6.0, MatchIt version 4.7.2, and 2-sided P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Study population and propensity score matching

Among 2978 initially eligible patients, 445 were classified as S aureus BSI and 2533 as non-S aureus BSI. A total of 757 patients were excluded because data completeness across the 76 retained candidate variables was below 70%, including 148 patients with S aureus BSI and 609 with non-S aureus BSI. The final analytic cohort, therefore, included 2221 patients: 297 with S aureus BSI and 1924 with non-S aureus BSI. PSM retained all 297 S aureus BSI patients and 297 matched non-S aureus BSI patients. After matching, the maximum absolute SMD was 0.116; all covariates had an absolute SMD of 0.10 or less except for chronic pulmonary disease (SMD = 0.116). 3.2. 28-Day survival after propensity score matching

In the matched cohort, 80 of 297 patients with S aureus BSI (26.9%) and 72 of 297 patients with non-S aureus BSI (24.2%) died within 28 days. Kaplan–Meier curves did not differ significantly (log-rank P = .531; Fig. 1). The matched-pair cluster-robust Cox model yielded an HR of 1.11 (95% CI: 0.80–1.53; P = .539). Results remained nonsignificant after adjustment for age, sex, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, liver disease, diabetes, renal disease, and tumor (HR 1.15, 95% CI: 0.83–1.61; P = .401) and in a complete-case sensitivity analysis additionally adjusted for SOFA score (n = 470; HR 1.16, 95% CI: 0.78–1.72; P = .466). The proportional hazards test for the pathogen group was not significant in the primary model (P = .102).

Kaplan–Meier curves for 28-day survival after 1:1 propensity score matching. Shaded areas indicate 95% confidence intervals. The figure reports the log-rank P value and the marginal Cox hazard ratio with matched-pair cluster-robust standard errors. BSI = bloodstream infection, CI = confidence interval, HR = hazard ratio.

3.3. Factors associated with S aureus BSI

In the full cohort, 5 variables were associated with lower odds of S aureus BSI in univariable logistic regression: time from admission to confirmed BSI (OR per day 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.97; P < .001), tumor (OR 0.55, 95% CI: 0.38–0.81; P = .002), duration of invasive ventilation (OR per day 0.95, 95% CI: 0.91–0.99; P = .015), duration of vascular catheterization (OR per day 0.85, 95% CI: 0.80–0.90; P < .001), and number of antibiotic types used before BSI (OR per additional type 0.80, 95% CI: 0.72–0.89; P < .001). The complete univariable results are shown in Figure 2 and Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1. These associations are descriptive and do not establish independent or causal effects.

3.4. Development of the prognostic model

The 297 patients with S aureus BSI included 217 survivors and 80 patients who died within 28 days. Bidirectional stepwise multivariable logistic regression was conducted within 7 clinical domains and retained 34 candidate terms; the ORs and 95% CIs for these 34 retained terms are reported in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2. The clinical distributions of these 34 candidates are reported in Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 3.

Best-subset selection reached the minimum BIC at 5 variables: age, maximum red cell distribution width (RDW), minimum white blood cell (WBC), minimum activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and maximum albumin. As a complementary sensitivity analysis restricted to the 34 variables retained after domain-specific screening, LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation and the 1-standard-error penalty retained the same 5 variables. These predictors were entered as continuous variables in the final logistic model and nomogram (Fig. 3).

Nomogram for estimating 28-day mortality after confirmed S aureus bloodstream infection. APTT = activated partial thromboplastin time, RDW = red cell distribution width, WBC = white blood cell count.

In the final model, the odds of 28-day mortality increased with age (OR per year 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.06; P < .001), minimum APTT (OR per second 1.04, 95% CI: 1.00–1.07; P = .032), maximum RDW (OR per percentage point 1.20, 95% CI: 1.07–1.35; P = .002), and minimum WBC (OR per 109/L 1.09, 95% CI: 1.03–1.16; P = .004). Higher maximum albumin was associated with lower mortality (OR per g/dL 0.24, 95% CI: 0.13–0.44; P < .001). Table 2 presents the complete final-model coefficients.

3.5. Model performance and refitting in the external cohort

The apparent C-statistic was 0.816 (bootstrap 95% CI: 0.757–0.870). Internal validation of the fixed 5-variable model with 1000 bootstrap resamples yielded an optimism-corrected C-statistic of 0.804 (95% CI: 0.752–0.861). Grouped calibration estimates in the development cohort were close to the ideal line, with 95% Wilson CIs shown in Figure 4.

Development-cohort quintiles and external-cohort tertiles are overlaid in 1 coordinate system; points show observed mortality within groups ordered by predicted risk, and vertical bars show 95% Wilson confidence intervals. External-cohort probabilities were obtained after refitting a logistic model with the same 5 prespecified predictors and reestimating all coefficients, including the intercept. In the external cohort, a logistic regression model was refitted using the same 5 prespecified predictors; all coefficients, including the intercept, were reestimated. Among 32 patients (14 deaths), the refitted model had an apparent C-statistic of 0.778 (bootstrap 95% CI: 0.591–0.929) and an apparent Brier score of 0.183. Calibration estimates from the development model and the refitted external-cohort model are overlaid in Figure 4. This cohort-specific refitting analysis evaluated the prespecified predictor set rather than externally validating the original fixed prediction equation. 3.6. Clinical utility

In the 214 patients with complete APACHE III, SAPS II, and SOFA data, DCA showed that the nomogram provided greater net benefit than these severity scores over much of the evaluated threshold range, particularly at intermediate thresholds (Fig. 5A). The clinical impact curve displays the number classified as high risk and the number of true-positive deaths per 1000 patients across thresholds, with bootstrap confidence bands (Fig. 5B). The separation between these curves indicates that some false-positive classifications would occur, especially at lower thresholds; therefore, the nomogram should be considered a risk-stratification aid rather than a stand-alone treatment rule.

4. Discussion

This study separated organism-associated characteristics from prognosis after infection. In the full BSI cohort, S aureus BSI was associated with shorter prediagnosis healthcare exposure, absence of tumor, and fewer antibiotic types compared with a heterogeneous non-S aureus group. After 1:1 PSM achieved generally acceptable covariate balance, with minor residual imbalance in chronic pulmonary disease, S aureus BSI was not associated with 28-day survival. Within S aureus BSI, 5 routinely measured continuous variables formed a parsimonious mortality model that performed well internally. Refitting the same prespecified predictor set in the external cohort yielded an apparent C-statistic of 0.778.

The inverse associations in the full cohort should not be interpreted as evidence that invasive devices or antibiotic use protect against S aureus BSI. Rather, the non-S aureus comparator included organisms commonly selected by prolonged hospitalization, device exposure, and antimicrobial pressure.[1,2] The findings suggest that S aureus BSI occurred within a different exposure pattern in this dataset, but organism ecology, indication for treatment, and time-dependent confounding could not be resolved. The association with antibiotic count therefore describes pathogen distribution rather than the effect of antibiotic escalation.

The 26.9% 28-day mortality observed in S aureus BSI is compatible with previous pooled and prospective estimates.[3,4] However, the matched HR was close to 1 and remained nonsignificant after adjustment for age, comorbidities, and SOFA. Thus, these data do not support pathogen category alone as an independent determinant of short-term mortality among ICU patients with BSI. The CI nevertheless includes both a modestly lower and a potentially higher hazard, so absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as proof of equivalence.

Contemporary reviews emphasize the clinical heterogeneity of S aureus bacteremia and the importance of host condition, infection severity, and source control.[10] Recent cohorts have also associated age, comorbidity burden, acute illness severity, and albumin with mortality.[11,12] Prognostic models differ substantially in population, outcome, and predictor availability. A 2025 in-hospital mortality nomogram incorporated comorbidity and infection-related factors, procalcitonin, ICU admission, and polymicrobial BSI.[13] The multicenter FEN-AUREUS study identified clinical phenotypes with distinct 30-day mortality patterns,[14] whereas another recent multicenter model used age, red blood cell count, C-reactive protein, blood urea nitrogen, and platelet count.[15] The present model relies on 5 routinely available continuous variables, but direct performance comparisons are limited by differences in outcome definitions, case mix, and validation design.

The prognostic predictors are biologically and clinically plausible and broadly consistent with a systematic analysis of mortality predictors in methicillin-resistant S aureus BSI.[16] Albumin may integrate inflammation, capillary leak, nutrition, and hepatic synthetic function, and lower concentrations have also been associated with 28-day mortality in septic shock.[17] Increased RDW may reflect inflammatory disturbance of erythropoiesis and impaired red-cell homeostasis.[18,19] Higher WBC may indicate a stronger systemic inflammatory response; recent trajectory analyses also linked persistently high leukocyte levels with worse sepsis outcomes.[20] Prolonged APTT may reflect infection-associated coagulation dysfunction and factor consumption.[21,22] These variables should be viewed as prognostic markers rather than necessarily modifiable causes.

Methodological safeguards partly addressed the risk of overfitting. The minimum-BIC subset and cross-validated LASSO selected the same 5 variables, and bootstrap correction produced only a small decrease in the C-statistic. In the external cohort, refitting a logistic model with the same 5 prespecified predictors yielded an apparent C-statistic of 0.778. Because the intercept and coefficients were reestimated, this result pertains to the predictor specification and does not establish transportability of the original prediction equation. The small cohort may also produce optimistic and unstable estimates; larger external datasets are required for validation of the fixed model and, if necessary, model updating.

From a clinical perspective, the nomogram may help structure early discussion of prognosis by combining age with 4 routinely available laboratory measurements. It should complement, not replace, clinical assessment, evaluation of the infection source, source control, antimicrobial susceptibility, and serial organ-failure assessment. The DCA comparison with the established APACHE III, SAPS II, and SOFA scores[23-25] suggests potential internal net benefit at intermediate thresholds, but a prospective impact study would be required before the model could guide treatment decisions.

This study has several strengths, including use of a large ICU BSI cohort, explicit separation of organism-associated factors from within-S aureus prognosis, reproducible 1:1 PSM with balance diagnostics, matched-pair cluster-robust survival inference, reporting of HRs and CIs, a complementary penalized-regression sensitivity analysis, and availability of a separate clinical cohort for prespecified-predictor refitting.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design permits residual and unmeasured confounding despite PSM and adjusted sensitivity analyses. One matched covariate, chronic pulmonary disease, had a small residual imbalance, although additional covariate adjustment did not change the survival result. Second, the non-S aureus group was microbiologically heterogeneous, and the database did not permit consistent clinical adjudication of contamination, infection source, source control, antimicrobial appropriateness, or resistance phenotype. Third, excluding patients with <70% data completeness may have introduced selection bias, mean imputation may underestimate uncertainty, and SOFA data were incomplete. Fourth, stepwise screening and best-subset selection can overfit; agreement with cross-validated LASSO is reassuring, but bootstrap validation assessed optimism in the fixed final model and did not fully account for uncertainty introduced by the preceding variable-selection process. Fifth, the external-cohort refitting analysis included only 32 patients and 14 deaths. Reestimation of the intercept and 5 coefficients in this small cohort may be unstable, and the reported apparent performance may be optimistic; this analysis did not externally validate the original fixed prediction equation. Finally, the development data arose from a single US academic center and may not represent other settings.

5. Conclusion

Among critically ill patients with BSI, S aureus was associated with a distinct prediagnosis clinical profile, but 28-day survival did not differ from non-S aureus BSI after 1:1 PSM. A 5-variable model based on age, RDW, WBC, APTT, and albumin showed good internally corrected discrimination for 28-day mortality in S aureus BSI. The same predictor set showed apparent discrimination after refitting in the external cohort, but the original prediction equation requires validation in larger prospective multicenter cohorts before clinical use.
