Abstract
. In this paper, we study the existence of a dense orbit for the diagonal ℙ ​ GL ⁡ ( n ) \PGL(n) action on self-products of partial flag varieties. We determine when there exists a dense orbit for flag varieties of the form F ​ ( k 1 , … , k r , n ) m F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m} when k i = i k_{i}=i or when m = 3 m=3 and k i = ℓ + i k_{i}=\ell+i for some ℓ ≥ 0 \ell\geq 0 . 1.
Introduction

The problem of finding convenient coordinates for a collection of linear spaces is as old as coordinate geometry. The existence of easy to work with coordinates is often at the heart of many classical theorems. The prototypical result states that given two ordered sets
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n+2}\}
and
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n+2}\}
of
n+2
points in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
in general linear position, there exists a unique
M\in\PGL(n+1)
such that
M(p_{i})=q_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n+2
(see
[
Ha92
, Section 1.6]
). Consequently, the diagonal action of
\PGL(n+1)
on
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}^{n}
has a dense orbit if and only if
m\leq n+2
. In this paper, we consider generalizations of this fact to self-products of partial flag varieties.

Let
0<k_{1}<\cdots<k_{r}<n
be positive integers. For notational convenience, set
k_{0}=0
and
k_{r+1}=n
. Let
V
be an
n
-dimensional vector space. Let
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)
denote the partial flag variety parameterizing the set of partial flags

W_{k_{1}}\subset\cdots\subset W_{k_{r}}\subset V,

where
\dim(W_{k_{i}})=k_{i}
. The group
\PGL(n)
acts transitively on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)
. This action induces a diagonal action on products of the form
\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(k_{i,1},\dots,k_{i,r_{i}};n)
. We say that the action is
dense
if there exists a dense orbit of the action. Otherwise, we say the action is
sparse
. In this paper, we address special cases of the following problem.

Problem 1.1
.

Classify products
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}
for which
\PGL(n)
acts with dense orbit.

Our results on density can be summarized in the following theorems. We first fully resolve the case when
k_{i}=i
for
1\leq i\leq r
.

Theorem

(Theorem
3.1
)
.

The
\PGL(n)
action on
F(1,2,\dots,r-1,r;n)^{m}
is dense if and only if one of the following holds:

(1)

m\leq 2
; or

(2)

r=1
and
m\leq n+1
; or

(3)

m=3
,
r>1
, and
n\geq 3r-2
; or

(4)

m\geq 4
,
r>1
, and
n\geq 3r-1
.

As a natural generalization, we consider the case when
k_{i}=\ell+i
for
1\leq i\leq r
. We obtain a complete classification when
m=3
or when
m
is arbitrary and
n\geq(m-1)(\ell+r)
.

Theorem

(Theorem
3.8
)
.

The
\PGL(n)
action on the product
F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)^{3}
is sparse if
2\ell+r<n<2\ell+2r
. Assume that
n\geq 2\ell+2r
. Then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)^{3}
is dense if and only if one of the following holds:

(1)

r=1
; or

(2)

n\geq 3\ell+3r
or
n=3\ell+3r-2
; or

(3)

2\ell+2r\leq n\leq 3\ell+2r
and either
r=2
or
2n\geq 3\ell+3r-2
.

Remark 1.2
.

The cases when
\ell+r<n\leq 2\ell+r
are determined by the duality exchanging
k_{i}
with
n-k_{i}
and are listed in detail in Theorem
3.8
.

Proposition

(Proposition
3.9
)
.

Assume that
m\geq 4
and
n\geq(m-1)(\ell+r)
. Set

j=n-(m-1)(\ell+r).

Then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)^{m}
is dense if and only if one of the following holds:

(1)

n\geq m(\ell+r)
; or

(2)

r=1
and
m(\ell+1)(n-\ell-1)<n^{2}
; or

(3)

0\leq j<\ell
and either
r=2
and
m\leq\ell-j+r+1
or
r>2
and
\ell-j+r\geq mr-m-1
.

In the other extreme, we study the cases where the differences
k_{i}-k_{i-1}
are large. Our results in this direction include the following.

Theorem

(Proposition
3.2
and Corollary
3.5
)
.

The
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};(m-1)k_{r})^{m}
is dense if and only if the
\PGL(k_{r})
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r-1};k_{r})^{m}
is dense. In particular, if
k_{i}\geq(m-1)k_{i-1}
for
2\leq i\leq r+1
, then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}
is dense.

We also exhibit infinite families of products with sparse
\PGL(n)
action.

Theorem

(Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
4.4
)
.

(1)

The
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}
is sparse if there exists two indices
1\leq i<j\leq r
such that
k_{j}=n-tk_{i}
for some
t\geq 1
and
m\geq\max(4,t+2)
.

(2)

The
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{3}
is sparse if there exists three indices
1\leq h<i<j\leq r
such that
2k_{h}+k_{i}+k_{j}=2n
.

For example, we conclude that the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k,n-2k;n)^{m}
is dense if and only if
m\leq 3
. Similarly, given
k_{1}<k_{2}
such that
k_{1}+k_{2}
is even, the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},k_{2},n-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{2};n)^{3}
is sparse.

History of the problem and applications

The problem of classifying algebraic group actions with dense orbit has a long history. Special cases have played an important role in classical geometry. The systematic study of products of flag varieties with dense
\PGL
orbits was initiated by Popov
[
Po04
,
Po07
]
based on a question of M. Burger and further studied in
[
CEY23
,
CHZ15
,
De14
]
. We refer the reader to
[
Sm20
]
for a recent survey.

More generally, Popov studied the problem for connected algebraic groups and classified the simple linear algebraic groups
G
such that the action of
G
on
(G/P)^{n}
is dense when
P
is a maximal parabolic subgroup
[
Po04
,
Po07
]
. Devyatov has extended the classification to non-maximal parabolic subgroups
P
, except in type
A

[
De14
]
. When
G/P
is a type A partial flag variety, even the classification for
(G/P)^{3}
is unknown. The closely related but easier problem of characterizing products of partial flag varieties with finitely many
\PGL(n)
orbits was solved by Magyar, Weyman and Zelevinsky
[
MWZ99
]
.

The density of the
\PGL(n)
action on a product of flag varieties has many applications; we refer the reader to
[
CHZ15
,
Po07
]
for details. One of the main motivations for Popov’s work was to obtain bounds on certain Littlewood-Richardson coefficients and prove that they are multiplicity-free. More explicitly, let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}
be nonzero dominant characters of the maximal torus
T
in the semi-simple group
G
. Then
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
is called
primitive
if for every nonnegative
d
-tuple of integers
(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
, the Littlewood-Richardson coefficient
c^{0}_{n_{1}\lambda_{1},\dots,n_{d}\lambda_{d}}\leq 1
. Popov in
[
Po07
, Theorem 1]
proves that if
G
has an open orbit on
G/P_{\lambda_{1}}\times\cdots\times G/P_{\lambda_{d}}
, then
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
is primitive. Similarly, the density has geometric applications to the study of vector bundles, enumerative geometry and genus zero Gromov-Witten invariants (see
[
CHZ15
]
for more details).

Organization of the paper

In §
2
, after discussing basic facts concerning the geometry of partial flag varieties and group actions, we recall key results from
[
CHZ15
]
and
[
CEY23
]
concerning the
\PGL(n)

F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}
when
k_{i}=i
for
1\leq i\leq r
or when
k_{i}\geq(m-1)k_{i-1}
for
2\leq i\leq r
. Finally, in §
4
, we give further examples of sparse actions on products of partial flag varieties.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect basic facts concerning the diagonal action of
\PGL(n)
on products of flag varieties. We refer the reader to
[
CHZ15
,
CEY23
]
for proofs and additional details.

2.1.
Partial flag varieties

Let
V
be an
n
-dimensional vector space. For an integer
0<k<n
, let
\Gr(k,n)
denote the Grassmannian parameterizing
k
-dimensional subspaces of
V
. It is a projective variety of dimension
k(n-k)
.

More generally, let
0<k_{1}<\cdots<k_{r}<n
be a sequence of
r
integers. For notational convenience, set
k_{0}=0
and
k_{r+1}=n
. Let
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)
denote the flag variety parameterizing partial flags

W_{k_{1}}\subset\cdots\subset W_{k_{r}}\subset V,

where
\dim(W_{k_{i}})=k_{i}
. The flag variety
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)
is a projective variety of dimension

\dim(F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n))=\sum_{i=1}^{r}k_{i}(k_{i+1}-k_{i}).

The group
\PGL(n)
acts transitively on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)
. In this paper, we will be concerned with the induced diagonal action on
\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(k_{i,1},\dots,k_{i,r_{i}};n)
.

We say that the action is
dense
if there exists a dense orbit of the action. Otherwise, we say that the action is
sparse
. If the action of
\PGL(n)
on
\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(k_{i,1},\dots,k_{i,r_{i}};n)
is dense, then

\dim(\PGL(n))=n^{2}-1\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{i}}k_{i,j}(k_{i,j+1}-k_{i,j})=\dim\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(k_{i,1},\dots,k_{i,r_{i}};n)\right).

If this inequality is violated, we say that the action is
trivially sparse
. It is easy to give examples of actions that are sparse but not trivially sparse (see
[
CHZ15
,
CEY23
]
).

We will often prove the equivalence of the density of two different products of flag varieties. The following notation will be useful.

Notation 2.1
.

Given a variety
X
with a
\PGL(n)
action and a variety
Y
with a
\PGL(m)
action, we write
X\Leftrightarrow Y
to mean that the density of the action on
X
is equivalent to the density of the action on
Y
.

2.2.
Dense orbits and the dimension of stabilizers

Our analysis depends on an elementary observation. If
G
is an algebraic group acting on an irreducible projective variety
X
, then the orbit
Gx
of a closed point
x
under
G
is open in its Zariski closure
\overline{Gx}
by
[
Bo91
, I.1.8]
. Let
H
denote the stabilizer of
x
. Since
X
is irreducible, the orbit of
x
is dense if and only if

\dim(Gx)=\dim(\overline{Gx})=\dim(X).

Since
Gx
is isomorphic to
G/H
, we conclude that the orbit of
x
is dense if and only if

\dim(H)=\dim(G)-\dim(X).

Observe that since
\dim(Gx)\leq\dim(X)
, the inequality
\dim(H)\geq\dim(G)-\dim(X)
always holds. To verify the density of the orbit of
x
, one has to check the inequality
\dim(H)\leq\dim(G)-\dim(X)
. We say
\dim(G)-\dim(X)
is
the expected dimension
of the stabilizer.

Lemma 2.2
.

[
CHZ15
, Lemma 2.2]

Let
X
be an irreducible projective variety with a
\PGL(n)
action. Let
x\in X
be a closed point and let
H
denote the stabilizer of
x
. Then the orbit of
x
is dense in
X
if and only if

\dim(H)=n^{2}-1-\dim(X).

In particular, the diagonal action of
\PGL(n)
on
\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)
is dense if and only if there exists a closed point in
\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)
whose stabilizer has dimension (at most)

n^{2}-1-m\sum_{i=1}^{r}k_{i}(k_{i+1}-k_{i}).

2.3.
Classification of dense actions in special cases

The dense diagonal actions on products of at most four Grassmannians have been classified (see
[
CHZ15
,
SW98
]
).

Theorem 2.3
.

[
CHZ15
, Theorem 5.1]

Let
m\leq 4
and let
X=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Gr(k_{i},n)
be a product of
m
-Grassmannians. Then the
\PGL(n)
action on
X
is sparse if and only if
m=4
and
\sum_{i=1}^{4}k_{i}=2n
.

Similarly, the dense diagonal actions on self-products of Grassmannians have been classified by Popov
[
Po04
, Theorem 3]
and generalized to
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Gr(k_{i},n)
with
|k_{i}-k_{j}|<3
for all
i,j

[
CHZ15
, Theorem 7.2]
. We state a special case which will be sufficient for our purposes.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
CHZ15
, Theorem 7.2]

Let
k<n
be positive integers and let
a,b
be nonnegative integers. The
\PGL(n)
action on
\Gr(k-1,n)^{a}\times\Gr(k,n)^{b}
is dense if and only if it is not trivially sparse and
\Gr(k-1,n)^{a}\times\Gr(k,n)^{b}\not=\Gr(k-1,2k-1)^{2}\times\Gr(k,2k-1)^{2}
.

In the case of products of partial flag varieties much less is known. A complete classification is available only for triple self-products of two-step partial flag varieties.

Theorem 2.5
.

[
CEY23
, Theorem 5.10]

The
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},k_{2};n)^{3}
is dense if and only if
k_{1}+k_{2}\not=n
.

The following immediate corollary of Theorem
2.5
will be useful.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
m\geq 3
. The
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}
is sparse if there exists indices
i\not=j
such that
k_{i}+k_{j}=n
.

2.4.
Reduction lemmas

In this subsection, we collect several easy lemmas.

Consider the action of
\PGL(n)
on a product
\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(k_{i,1},\dots,k_{i,r_{i}};n)
.
If
n\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i,r_{i}}
, then we can choose a basis for the underlying vector space
V
so that the vector space
W_{i,k_{i,j}}
is the span of basis elements
e_{\ell}
with
\sum_{t=1}^{i-1}k_{t,j}<\ell\leq\sum_{t=1}^{i}k_{t,j}
. The stabilizer matrices are then of block diagonal form and the dimension of the stabilizer is the expected dimension.

Lemma 2.7
.

[
CEY23
, Lemma 5.5]

The product
\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(k_{i,1},\dots,k_{i,r_{i}};n)
is dense if
\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i,r_{i}}\leq n
.

There is a natural duality between
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)
and
F(n-k_{r},\dots,n-k_{1};n)
sending a vector space
W\subset V
to the annihilator of
W
in the dual vector space
V^{*}
. This action respects the natural
\PGL(n)
actions and yields the following lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

[
CEY23
, Proposition 2.7]

We have

\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(k_{i,1},\dots,k_{i,r_{i}};n)\Leftrightarrow\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(n-k_{i,r_{i}},\dots,n-k_{i,1};n).

Notation 2.9
.

Let
0<k_{1}<\cdots<k_{r}<n
be an increasing sequence of positive integers less than
n
. We will denote the sequence by
k_{\bullet}
. Let
n^{\prime}\leq n
and set
d=n-n^{\prime}
. Let
u
be the index such that
k_{u-1}\leq d<k_{u}
. Then for
1\leq i\leq r-u+1
, set
k_{i}^{\prime}=k_{u+i-1}-d
. Given a sequence
k_{\bullet}
and an integer
n^{\prime}<n
, we will call the sequence
k_{\bullet}^{\prime}

the sequence derived from
k_{\bullet}
with respect to
n
and
n^{\prime}
. Notice that this only depends on
n-n^{\prime}
, so if we do not wish to emphasize
n
and
n^{\prime}
, we will sometimes say
the sequence derived from
k_{\bullet}
with respect to
d
. Given a vector space
W
of dimension
n^{\prime}
and a general partial flag
F_{\bullet}
in an
n
-dimensional vector space with dimensions
k_{\bullet}
, the sequence
k_{\bullet}^{\prime}
denotes the dimension vector of the partial flag in
W
obtained by
F_{\bullet}\cap W
.

The following useful lemma will allow us to reduce the dimension.

Lemma 2.10
.

[
CEY23
, Lemma 5.6]

Suppose
k_{i,\bullet}
are
m
sequences of increasing positive integers less than
n
. Assume that
n^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}k_{i,r_{i}}\leq n<\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i,r_{i}}
. Let
k_{m,\bullet}^{\prime}
be the sequence derived from
k_{m,\bullet}
with respect to
n
and
n^{\prime}
. Then

\prod_{i=1}^{m}F(k_{i,\bullet};n)\Leftrightarrow\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}F(k_{i,\bullet};n^{\prime})\times F(k_{m,\bullet}^{\prime};n^{\prime}).

3.
Density results

In this section, we prove our main results on density. We first analyze the case where
k_{i}=i
. We then consider the other extreme when the difference between
k_{i}
and
k_{i+1}
is large.

3.1.
The case
k_{i}=i

We first study the case when
k_{i}=i
for
1\leq i\leq r
. For simplicity, we denote this partial flag variety by
F(1,2,\dots,r-1,r;n)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The
\PGL(n)
action on
F(1,2,\dots,r-1,r;n)^{m}
is dense if and only if one of the following holds:

(1)

m\leq 2
;

(2)

r=1
, and
n\geq m-1
;

(3)

m=3
,
r>1
, and
n\geq 3r-2
;

(4)

m>3
,
r>1
, and
n\geq mr-1
.

Proof.

Step 1:
m\leq 2
.
When
m\leq 2
, the action of
\PGL(n)
on
F(1,2,\dots,r-1,r;n)^{m}
has only finitely many orbits
[
MWZ99
, Theorem 2.2]
, hence the maximal dimensional orbit is dense. This is Case (
1
) of the theorem.

Step 2:
r=1
.
When
r=1
, the corresponding partial flag variety is
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. The action of
\PGL(n)
on
m
points in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
has a dense orbit if and only if
m\leq n+1
(see
[
Ha92
, Section 1.6]
). This is Case (
2
) of the theorem.

We may therefore assume that
m\geq 3
and
r>1
for the rest of the proof.

Step 3:
We now show that if
n\leq(m-1)r
, then
F(1,2,\dots,r-1,r;n)^{m}
is trivially sparse except when
m=3
and
r=2
. We have
that

(1)

\varphi(n,m,r):=\dim(\PGL(n))-\dim(F(1,2,\dots,r-1,r;n)^{m})=n^{2}-1-m\frac{(r-1)r}{2}-mr(n-r).

For fixed
m
and
r
, the function
\varphi(n,m,r)
is a quadratic function of
n
with leading term
n^{2}
. Hence, if for fixed
m
and
r
, the function
\varphi
takes negative values at
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
, it must take negative values for
n_{1}\leq n\leq n_{2}
as well. When
n=r+1
, we have

\varphi(r+1,m,r)=\left(1-\frac{m}{2}\right)r^{2}+\left(2-\frac{m}{2}\right)r.

This is negative since
m\geq 3
and
r>1
. When
n=(m-1)r
, we have

\varphi((m-1)r,m,r)=(m-1)^{2}r^{2}-1-m\frac{(r-1)r}{2}-m(m-2)r^{2}=\left(1-\frac{m}{2}\right)r^{2}+\frac{mr}{2}-1,

which is negative when
m\geq 4
and
r>1
or when
m=3
and
r>2
. We conclude that if
m\geq 4
and
r>1
or when
m=3
and
r>2
, then
\dim(F(1,2,\dots,r-1,r;n)^{m})>\dim(\PGL(n))
for
r+1\leq n\leq(m-1)r
. Hence, the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(1,2,\dots,r-1,r;n)^{m}
is trivially sparse if
n\leq(m-1)r
except when
m=3
and
r=2
. By Theorem
2.5
, the
\PGL(4)
action on
F(1,2;4)^{3}
is dense. This case is covered by Case (
3
) of the theorem. We may therefore assume that
n>(m-1)r
.

Step 4:
n\geq mr
.
If
n\geq mr
, then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(1,2,\dots,r-1,r;n)^{m}
is dense by Lemma
2.7
. Hence, we may assume that
(m-1)r<n<mr
.

Step 5:
(m-1)r<n<mr
.
Write
n=mr-j
for some
0<j<r
. By applying Lemma
2.10
repeatedly
m
times, we have

F(1,2,\dots,r-1,r;n)^{m}\Leftrightarrow F(1,2,\dots,j-1,j;(m-1)j)^{m}.

If
m\geq 4
, this is trivially sparse if
j>1
and dense if
j=1
by Steps 2 and 3. This yields Case (
4
) of the theorem. If
m=3
, this is trivially sparse if
j>2
and dense if
1\leq j\leq 2
by Steps 2 and 3. This yields Case (
3
) of the theorem and concludes the proof.
∎

3.2.
The case of sequences with large gaps

Next, we study the case when the sequence
k_{1},\dots,k_{r}
has large gaps. Our analysis is based on the following observation.

Proposition 3.2
.

We have

F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};(m-1)k_{r})^{m}\Leftrightarrow F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r-1};k_{r})^{m}.

Proof.

We first compute the expected dimension of the generic stabilizer in each case.

\displaystyle\dim(\PGL((m-1)k_{r}))-\dim(F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};(m-1)k_{r})^{m})

\displaystyle=(m-1)^{2}k_{r}^{2}-1-m\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}k_{i}(k_{i+1}-k_{i})+(m-2)k_{r}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=k_{r}^{2}-1-m\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}k_{i}(k_{i+1}-k_{i})

\displaystyle=\dim(\PGL(k_{r}))-\dim(F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r-1};k_{r})^{m}).

We conclude that the expected dimensions of the generic stabilizers are equal for both products. In particular, the action on one is trivially sparse if and only if the action on the other is.

We now give a construction of an element in
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r-1};k_{r})^{m}
given a general element in
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};(m-1)k_{r})^{m}
.
For
1\leq i\leq m
, let
W_{i,k_{1}}\subset\cdots\subset W_{i,k_{r}}
be general partial flags in
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};(m-1)k_{r})
. Let
U_{j}
be the span of
W_{i,k_{r}}
for
1\leq i\leq m-1
and
i\not=j
. Note that

\dim(U_{j})=(m-2)k_{r}.

For
1\leq j\leq m-1
and
1\leq\ell\leq r
, set
T_{j,\ell}
be the span of
W_{j,k_{\ell}}
and
U_{j}
. Observe that

\dim(T_{j,\ell})=(m-2)k_{r}+k_{\ell}.

Set
W_{j,k_{\ell}}^{\prime}=T_{j,\ell}\cap W_{m,k_{r}}
, which is a
k_{\ell}
-dimensional vector space. Finally, set
W_{m,k_{i}}^{\prime}=W_{m,k_{i}}
. Note that
W_{j,k_{r}}^{\prime}=W_{m,k_{r}}
for every
j
since
U_{j}
and
W_{j,k_{r}}
span the underlying vector space
V
.

It is easy to see that a general element in
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r-1};k_{r})^{m}
arises via this construction. Given
W_{m,k_{r}}\subset V
and general partial flags
W_{j,k_{1}}^{\prime}\subset\cdots\subset W_{j,k_{r-1}}^{\prime}
, fix
m-1
general
k_{r}
-dimensional vector spaces
W_{j,k_{r}}\subset V
. As in the previous paragraph, let
U_{j}
be the span of
W_{i,k_{r}}
for
1\leq i\leq m-1
and
i\not=j
. Set
W_{j,k_{\ell}}
to be the intersection of
W_{j,k_{r}}
with the span of
U_{j}
and
W_{j,k_{r}}^{\prime}
. This is clearly the inverse construction. Hence, if
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r-1};k_{r})^{m}
is not dense, then
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};(m-1)k_{r})^{m}
cannot be dense.

To prove the converse, we argue as follows. Let
S
denote the stabilizer of the vector spaces
W_{j,k_{i}}
in
V
. Let
S^{\prime}
denote the stabilizer of the vector spaces
W_{j,k_{i}}^{\prime}
in
W_{m,k_{r}}
. Since
W_{j,k_{i}}^{\prime}
are obtained from
W_{j,k_{i}}
by taking spans and intersections, any stabilizer of the vector spaces
W_{j,k_{i}}
in
V
also stabilizes
W_{j,k_{i}}^{\prime}
. We thus get a group homomorphism

f:S\to S^{\prime}.
By
[
CHZ15
, Lemma 4.5]
, the kernel of
f
is trivial. Hence, we obtain the inequality

\dim(S)\leq\dim(S^{\prime}).

If the
\PGL(k_{r})
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r-1};k_{r})^{m}
is dense, then

\dim(S^{\prime})=k_{r}^{2}-1-m\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}k_{i}(k_{i+1}-k_{i})\leq\dim(S).

We deduce that we must have equality in the dimensions and the
\PGL((m-1)k_{r})
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};(m-1)k_{r})^{m}
is dense by Lemma
2.2
.
∎

Definition 3.3
.

Given a sequence of positive integers
0<k_{1}<\cdots<k_{r}<n
and an integer
m>2
, we define integers
j_{\ell}(m)
inductively by descending induction starting with
\ell=r
as follows. We set

j_{r}(m):=n-(m-1)k_{r}.

Then for
0<\ell<r
, we inductively set

j_{\ell}(m)=k_{\ell+1}-(m-1)k_{\ell}+(m-2)\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{r}j_{i}(m).

If
k_{\ell}-\sum_{t=\ell}^{r}j_{t}(m)>0
, define
s_{\ell}
by

s_{\ell}:=\min\left\{0<i\leq\ell\ |\ k_{i}-\sum_{t=\ell}^{r}j_{t}(m)>0\right\};

otherwise set
s_{\ell}=\ell
. Let

\ell_{0}:=\max\{\ell\ |\ s_{\ell}\geq\ell-1\}.

With this notation, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
m>2
be a positive integer. Let
0<k_{1}<\cdots<k_{r}<n
be a sequence of integers such that
j_{\ell}(m)\geq 0
for all
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
. Then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}
is dense if one of the following holds:

(1)

j_{t}(m)\geq k_{t}-\sum_{\ell=t+1}^{r}j_{\ell}(m)
for some
t\geq\ell_{0}
; or

(2)

s_{\ell_{0}}\geq\ell_{0}
; or

(3)

s_{\ell_{0}}=\ell_{0}-1
and
m\left(k_{\ell_{0}-1}-\sum_{\ell=\ell_{0}}^{r}j_{\ell}(m)\right)(k_{\ell_{0}}-k_{\ell_{0}-1})<\left(k_{\ell_{0}}-\sum_{\ell=\ell_{0}}^{r}\ell_{t}(m)\right)^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
m
is fixed throughout the proof, we denote
j_{\ell}(m)
simply by
j_{\ell}
. We will prove by descending induction on
\ell
that if
\ell>\ell_{0}
, then either the action is dense or

F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}\Leftrightarrow F\left(k_{s_{\ell}}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u},\dots,\ k_{\ell-1}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u};\ k_{\ell}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u}\right)^{m}.

If
n\geq mk_{r}
, then
j_{r}\geq k_{r}
and the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}
is dense by Lemma
2.7
. Otherwise, by assumption
j_{r}\geq 0
, hence
mk_{r}>n\geq(m-1)k_{r}
. By
m
applications of Lemma
2.10
,we have

F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}\Leftrightarrow F(k_{s_{r}}-j_{r},\dots,k_{r}-j_{r};(m-1)(k_{r}-j_{r}))^{m}.

If
s_{r}=r
, then the action is dense by Theorem
2.4
. Otherwise, by Proposition
3.2
,

F(k_{s_{r}}-j_{r},\dots,k_{r}-j_{r};(m-1)(k_{r}-j_{r}))^{m}\Leftrightarrow F(k_{s_{r}}-j_{r},\dots,k_{r-1}-j_{r};k_{r}-j_{r})^{m}.

This proves the base case of the induction. If
s_{r}=r-1
, then the latter is an
m
-fold product of a Grassmannian and by Theorem
2.4
, the action is dense if and only if

m(k_{r-1}-j_{r})(k_{r}-k_{r-1})<(k_{r}-j_{r})^{2}.

The theorem thus holds for
\ell=r
.

Suppose that by induction we have

F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}\Leftrightarrow F\left(k_{s_{\ell}}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u},\dots,k_{\ell-1}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u};k_{\ell}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u}\right)^{m}.

If
j_{\ell-1}\geq k_{\ell-1}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u}
, then

k_{\ell}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u}\geq m\left(k_{\ell-1}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u}\right)

and the action on the latter is dense by Lemma
2.7
. Otherwise, by the assumption that
j_{\ell-1}\geq 0
, we have that

k_{\ell}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u}\geq(m-1)\left(k_{\ell-1}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u}\right).

By
m
applications of Lemma
2.10
,

F\left(k_{s_{\ell}}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u},\dots,k_{\ell-1}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u};k_{\ell}-\sum_{u=\ell}^{r}j_{u}\right)^{m}\Leftrightarrow

F\left(k_{s_{\ell-1}}-\sum_{u=\ell-1}^{r}j_{u},\dots,k_{\ell-1}-\sum_{u=\ell-1}^{r}j_{u};(m-1)(k_{\ell-1}-\sum_{u=\ell-1}^{r}j_{u})\right)^{m}.

If
s_{\ell-1}=\ell-1
, then the action is dense by Theorem
2.4
. Otherwise, by Proposition
3.2
, the density of the action on the latter is equivalent to the density of the action on

F(k_{s_{\ell-1}}-\sum_{u=\ell-1}^{r}j_{u},\dots;k_{\ell-1}-\sum_{u=\ell-1}^{r}j_{u})^{m}.

This concludes the inductive step. The induction stops when
\ell=\ell_{0}
. In which case, either the action is dense or we have the action on a product of Grassmannians whose density is determined by Theorem
2.4
. This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

In particular, we obtain the following easier to remember corollary.

Corollary 3.5
.

If
k_{i}\geq(m-1)k_{i-1}
for
2\leq i\leq r+1
, then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}
is dense.

Applying Theorem
3.4
when
r=2
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
m>3
and assume that
n\geq(m-1)k_{2}
. Then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},k_{2};n)^{m}
is dense if and only if one of the following holds:

(1)

k_{1}\leq j_{2}(m)
; or

(2)

0\leq j_{2}(m)<k_{1}
and
m(k_{1}-j(m))(k_{2}-k_{1})<(k_{2}-j(m))^{2}
.

Proof.

Set
j_{2}=j_{2}(m)
. By Lemma
2.7
, the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},k_{2};n)^{m}
is dense if
n\geq mk_{2}
. Otherwise, by assumption,
0\leq j_{2}<k_{2}
. If
k_{1}\leq j_{2}
, then by
m
applications of Lemma
2.10
,

F(k_{1},k_{2};n)^{m}\Leftrightarrow\Gr(k_{2}-j_{2},(m-1)(k_{2}-j_{2}))^{m}.

The action on the latter is dense by Theorem
2.4
. If
0\leq j_{2}<k_{1}
, then by
m
applications of Lemma
2.10
,

F(k_{1},k_{2};n)^{m}\Leftrightarrow F(k_{1}-j_{2},k_{2}-j_{2};(m-1)(k_{2}-j_{2}))^{m}.

By Proposition
3.2
,

F(k_{1}-j_{2},k_{2}-j_{2};(m-1)(k_{2}-j_{2}))^{m}\Leftrightarrow\Gr(k_{1}-j_{2},k_{2}-j_{2})^{m}.

The action on the latter is dense if and only if
m(k_{1}-j_{2})(k_{2}-k_{1})<(k_{2}-j_{2})^{2}
by Theorem
2.4
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Similarly, applying Theorem
3.4
when
r=3
and
m=3
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
n\geq 2k_{3}
. Then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};n)^{3}
is dense if and only if one of the following holds:

(1)

k_{1}\leq j_{3}(3)
; or

(2)

0\leq j_{3}(3)<k_{1}
and
k_{1}+k_{2}\not=k_{3}+j_{3}(3)
.

Proof.

For simplicity set
j_{3}(3)=j_{3}
. By assumption,
n\geq 2k_{3}
. If
n\geq 3k_{3}
, or equivalently, if
j_{3}\geq k_{3}
, then the action on
F(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};n)^{3}
is dense by Lemma

2.7
. Otherwise, we can write
n=2k_{3}+j_{3}
for some
0\leq j_{3}<k_{3}
. In this case, we apply Lemma
2.10
three times. If
j_{3}<k_{1}
, then

F(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};n)^{3}\Leftrightarrow F(k_{1}-j_{3},k_{2}-j_{3},k_{3}-j_{3};2k_{3}-2j_{3})^{3}.

By Proposition
3.2
,

F(k_{1}-j_{3},k_{2}-j_{3},k_{3}-j_{3};2k_{3}-2j_{3})^{3}\Leftrightarrow F(k_{1}-j_{3},k_{2}-j_{3};k_{3}-j_{3})^{3}.

By Theorem
2.5
, the action on the latter is dense if and only if
k_{1}-j_{3}+k_{2}-j_{3}\not=k_{3}-j_{3}
or equivalently if
k_{1}+k_{2}\not=k_{3}+j_{3}
. If
j_{3}\geq k_{2}
, then applying Lemma
2.10
three times,

F(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};n)^{3}\Leftrightarrow\Gr(k_{3}-j_{3},2(k_{3}-j_{3}))^{3}.

The action on the latter is always dense by Theorem
2.3
. If instead,
k_{1}\leq j_{3}<k_{2}
, then applying Lemma
2.10
three times,

F(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};n)^{3}\Leftrightarrow F(k_{2}-j_{3},k_{3}-j_{3};2(k_{3}-j_{3}))^{3}.

By Proposition
3.2
,

F(k_{2}-j_{3},k_{3}-j_{3};2(k_{3}-j_{3}))^{3}\Leftrightarrow\Gr(k_{2}-j_{3},k_{3}-j_{3})^{3}.

The action on the latter is dense by Theorem
2.3
. We thus conclude that if
j_{3}\geq k_{1}
, then the action on
F(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};n)^{3}
is dense.
∎

3.3.
The case
k_{i+1}-k_{i}=1

Let
k_{i}=\ell+i
for
1\leq i\leq r
. We will denote this partial flag variety by
F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)
for simplicity.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
k_{i}=\ell+i
for
1\leq i\leq r
. Then the action on
F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)^{3}
is dense if and only if one of the following holds

(1)

r=1
; or

(2)

3\ell+3r\leq n
; or

(3)

n=3\ell+3r-2
; or

(4)

2\ell+2r\leq n\leq 3\ell+2r
and either
r=2
or
3\ell+5\geq n
; or

(5)

n\leq 2\ell+r
and
2n\leq 3\ell+3

(6)

n\leq 2\ell+r
and
2n=3\ell+5

(7)

2n-2\ell-2\leq n\leq 3n-3\ell-r-3
and either
r=2
or
2n\geq 3\ell+3r-2
.

Proof.

Step 1.
The
\PGL(n)
action on the product
F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)^{3}
is dense if
r=1
by Theorem
2.3
. This is Case (1) of the theorem.
We may therefore assume that
r\geq 2
.

Step 2.
First, assume that
n-\ell-1\geq\ell+r
, equivalently,
n\geq 2\ell+r+1
. If
n=2\ell+t
for
r+1\leq t\leq 2r-1
, then the action on
F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)^{3}
is not dense by Corollary
2.6
. Hence, we may assume that
n\geq 2\ell+2r
. If
n\geq 3\ell+3r
, then the action on
F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)^{3}
is dense by Lemma
2.7
. This is Case (2) of the theorem.

If instead
2\ell+2r\leq n<3\ell+3r
, then let
j=n-2\ell-2r
.

Case 1:
j\leq\ell
.
If
j\leq\ell
, then by 3 applications of Lemma
2.10
followed by an application of Proposition
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)^{3}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow F(\ell+1-j,\dots,\ell+r-j;2(\ell+r-j))^{3}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow F(\ell+1-j,\dots,\ell+r-1-j;\ell+r-j)^{3}.

By Lemma
2.8
,

F(\ell+1-j,\dots,\ell+r-1-j,\ell+r-j)^{3}\Leftrightarrow F(1,\dots,r-1;\ell+r-j)^{3}.

By Theorem
3.1
, this is dense if and only if
r=2
or
r>2
and
\ell+r-j\geq 3r-5
. The latter inequality is
3\ell+5\geq n
and yields Case (4) of the theorem.

Case 2:
\ell+r>j>\ell
.
If
j>\ell
, then by 3 applications of Lemma
2.10
followed by an application of Lemma
2.8
, we have

\displaystyle F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)^{3}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow F(1,2,\dots,\ell+r-j;2(\ell+r-j))^{3}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow F(1,2,\dots,\ell+r-1-j;\ell+r-j)^{3}

The latter is dense if and only if
\ell+r-j=2
by Theorem
3.1
. This is Case (3) of the theorem.

Step 3.
If
n-\ell-1<\ell+r
, or equivalently,
n\leq 2\ell+r
, then by Lemma
2.8
we have

F(\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r;n)^{3}\Leftrightarrow F(n-\ell-r,\dots,n-\ell-1;n)^{3}.

Setting
\ell^{\prime}=n-\ell-r-1
, this becomes
F(\ell^{\prime}+1,\dots,\ell^{\prime}+r;n)^{3}
and we can apply Step 2. The remaining cases (5), (6), (7) follow by this duality.
∎

When

, we obtain the following.

.

Assume that

and

. Set

(2)

4.
Sparsity results

In this section, we discuss further examples of the density and sparsity of products of partial flag varieties.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
k_{i}<k_{j}
be two indices such that
k_{j}=n-s
, where
s=k_{i}t+u
for some
t\geq 1
and
0\leq u<k_{i}
. Then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}
is not dense if one of the following holds:

(1)

u=0
and
m\geq\max(t+2,4)
.

(2)

0<u\leq\frac{k_{i}}{2}
,
t\geq 3
and
m\geq t+2
.

(3)

\frac{k_{i}}{2}<u<k_{i}
,
t\geq 2
and
m\geq t+3
.

Proof.

There is a
\PGL(n)
-equivariant surjective morphism
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{m}\to F(k_{i},k_{j};n)^{m}
. If the action on the latter is not dense, then the action on the former cannot be dense either. Hence, for the rest of the proof we will assume that
i=1,j=2
.

Let
U_{i}\subset V_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq m
be
m
general partial flags of type
k_{1},k_{2}
.
Let
W
be the span of
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
. Observe that
W
is a
(2k_{1})
-dimensional linear space.

Case 1:
u=0
.
Assume
u=0
and
m\geq\max(4,t+2)
. In this case, let
S_{3}
be the span of
V_{3}
and
U_{i}
for
4\leq i\leq t+2
. Let
S_{4}
be the span of
V_{4}
,
U_{i}
for
5\leq i\leq t+2
and
U_{3}
if
t>1
. Let
U_{i}^{\prime}=S_{i}\cap W
for
i=3,4
. Observe that
U_{3}^{\prime}
and
U_{4}^{\prime}
are
k_{1}
-dimensional linear spaces in
W
.

It is easy to see that we can get any two general
k_{1}
-dimensional linear spaces in
W
in this way by choosing the flags
U_{i}\subset V_{i}
appropriately. Namely, let
U_{3}^{\prime}
and
U_{4}^{\prime}
be general
k_{1}
-dimensional linear spaces in
W
. If
t>1
, let
W^{\prime}
be a general
((t-2)k_{1})
-dimensional subspace; otherwise let
W^{\prime}=0
. Let
V_{3}
be a general
k_{2}
-dimensional linear space intersecting the span of
W^{\prime}
and
U_{4}^{\prime}
in a
k_{1}
-dimensional linear space
U_{3}
. Similarly, let
V_{4}
be a general
k_{2}
-dimensional linear space intersecting the span of
W^{\prime}
and
U_{3}^{\prime}
in a
k_{1}
-dimensional linear space
U_{4}
. Let
U_{i}
for
i>4
be general
k_{1}
-dimensional linear spaces in
W^{\prime}
and
V_{i}
for
i>4
be general
k_{2}
-dimensional linear spaces containing
U_{i}
. This reverses the construction. Since the action on
\Gr(k_{1},2k_{1})^{4}
is not dense, we conclude that the action on
F(k_{1},k_{2};n)^{m}
cannot be dense.

Case 2:
1\leq u\leq\frac{k_{1}}{2}
.
Assume
0<u\leq\frac{k_{1}}{2}
,
t\geq 3
and and
m\geq t+2
. In this case, let
S_{3}
be the span of
V_{3}
and
U_{i}
for
4\leq i\leq t+2
. Let
S_{4}
be the span of
V_{4}
,
U_{3}
and
U_{i}
for
5\leq i\leq t+2
. Finally, let
S_{5}
be the span of
V_{5}
,
U_{3},U_{4}
and
U_{i}
for
6\leq i\leq t+2
. Let
U_{i}^{\prime}=S_{i}\cap W
for
3\leq i\leq 5
. Observe that
\dim(U_{i}^{\prime})=k_{1}-u
. We can get any three general
k_{1}
-dimensional linear spaces in
W
by choosing the flags
U_{i}\subset V_{i}
appropriately. Namely, for
3\leq i\leq 5
, fix a general
(n-s+(t-1)k_{1})
-dimensional linear space
T_{i}
intersecting
W
in
U_{i}^{\prime}
. Let
U_{i}
for
3\leq i\leq t
be general linear spaces of dimension
k_{1}
contained in
\cap_{i=3}^{5}T_{i}
. For
3\leq i\leq 5
, let
V_{i}
be general linear spaces containing
U_{i}
and contained in
T_{i}
. For
6\leq i\leq t
. let
V_{i}
be general linear spaces containing
U_{i}
. With these choices, we recover
U_{3}^{\prime},U_{4}^{\prime},U_{5}^{\prime}
.

We have that

\dim(\Gr(k_{1},2k_{1})^{2}\times\Gr(k_{1}-u,2k_{1})^{3})=2k_{1}^{2}+3k_{1}^{2}-3u^{2}\geq\frac{17}{4}k_{1}^{2}>4k_{1}^{2}.

Hence, the action on
F(k_{1},k_{2};n)^{m}
cannot be dense.

Case 3:
\frac{k_{1}}{2}<u\leq k_{1}
.
Assume
\frac{k_{1}}{2}<u\leq k_{1}
,

and

. In this case, let

Hence, the action on

∎

Corollary 4.2
.

The
\PGL(n)

.

,
F(k,n-2k;n)^{m}

Corollary 4.3
.

Assume
m\geq 4
and
2t<n
. If the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(1,n-t;n)^{m}
is dense, then
m\leq t+1
. If
t\geq 3
and
n>2t
, then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(1,n-t;n)^{t+1}
is dense if and only if
n\geq t(t+1)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
, the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(1,n-t;n)^{m}
is not dense if
m\geq t+2
. Now suppose
m=t+1
. We have

\dim(\PGL(n))-\dim(F(1,n-t;n)^{t+1})=n^{2}-1-(t+1)((n-t-1)+t(n-t)).

When
n=2t+1
, this quantity is
-t^{3}+t^{2}+2t
, which is negative for
t\geq 3
.
When
n=t(t+1)-1
, this quantity is
(t+1)(2-t)
, which is negative for
t\geq 3
. We conclude that, under our assumptions, the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(1,n-t;n)^{t+1}
cannot be dense unless
n\geq t(t+1)
.

To prove the converse suppose that
n\geq t(t+1)
. By Lemma
2.8
, it suffices to prove the density of the action on
F(t,n-1;n)^{t+1}
. By Lemma
2.2
, it suffices to exhibit a set of
t+1
partial flags
U_{i}\subset V_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq t+1
with
\dim(U_{i})=t
and
\dim(V_{i})=n-1
such that the stabilizer of the configuration has dimension
n^{2}-1-(t+1)(t(n-1-t)+n-1).
Choose a basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
for the underlying vector space. Since
n\geq t(t+1)
, for each
1\leq i\leq t+1
, we can let the subspace
U_{i}
be the coordinate subspace spanned by the basis elements
e_{s}
with
(i-1)t<s\leq it
. We then let the
(n-1)
-dimensional flag element
V_{i}
be defined by the linear relation

\sum_{m=0}^{i-2}x_{mt+i-1}+\sum_{m=i}^{t}x_{mt+i}=0.

Matrices
M=(a_{u,v})
stabilizing this configuration satisfy the following conditions:

(1)

a_{u,v}=0
if
u\leq t(t+1)
and
v\not=u

(2)

Let
0<m\leq t+1
. Then
a_{mt+1,mt+1}=a_{t+1,t+1}
. If
1<s<m
, then
a_{mt+s,mt+s}=a_{s-1,s-1}
. If
m\leq s<t
, then
a_{mt+s,mt+s}=a_{s,s}
.

(3)

If
v>t(t+1)
, then for each
1\leq i\leq t+1
, we have
\sum_{m=0}^{i-1}a_{mt+i-1,v}+\sum_{m=i}^{t}a_{mt+i,v}=0.

As each of the equations in item (3) involve distinct variables, they are independent. Hence, it is easy to see that this subgroup of
\PGL(n)
has dimension
t+(n-t-1)(n-t(t+1))
. Observe that

\displaystyle\dim(\PGL(n))-(t+1)\dim(F(t,n-1;n))

\displaystyle=n^{2}-1-(t+1)(t(n-1-t)+n-1)

\displaystyle=n^{2}+t(t+1)^{2}-nt(t+1)-n(t+1)+t

\displaystyle=(n-t-1)(n-t(t+1))+t

This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Assume that there exist three indices
0<i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}\leq r
such that

2k_{i_{1}}+k_{i_{2}}+k_{i_{3}}=2n.

Then the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{3}
is not dense.

Proof.

There exists a
\PGL(n)
-equivariant surjective morphism

\phi:F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{3}\to F(k_{i_{1}},k_{i_{2}},k_{i_{3}};n)^{3}.

If the
\PGL(n)
action on
F(k_{i_{1}},k_{i_{2}},k_{i_{3}};n)^{3}
is not dense, then the action on
F(k_{1},\dots,k_{r};n)^{3}
cannot be dense either. Hence, it suffices to prove the proposition for
F(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};n)^{3}
when
2k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=2n
.

For
1\leq i\leq 3
, let
W_{i,k_{i}}
denote the elements of three general partial flags. For
i=2,3
, let
U_{i}=W_{i,k_{3}}\cap W_{1,k_{1}}
. Observe that

\dim(U_{i})=k_{1}+k_{3}-n,

which is positive since
k_{1}+k_{3}>k_{1}+k_{2}
and
2k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=2n
by assumption. Let

V_{2}=\Span(W_{2,k_{1}},W_{3,k_{2}})\cap W_{1,k_{1}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad V_{3}=\Span(W_{3,k_{1}},W_{2,k_{2}})\cap W_{1,k_{1}}.

Observe that

\dim(V_{i})=2k_{1}+k_{2}-n,

which is also positive since
n>k_{3}
and
2k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=2n
.
We thus obtain a collection of 4 linear spaces

(U_{2},U_{3},V_{2},V_{3})\in\Gr(k_{1}+k_{3}-n,k_{1})^{2}\times\Gr(2k_{1}+k_{2}-n,k_{1})^{2}.

It is easy to see that a general set of such 4-tuples arise via this construction. Namely, given general
U_{2},U_{3}
and
V_{2},V_{3}
in
W_{1,k_{1}}
, pick two general
k_{3}
-dimensional linear spaces
W_{2,k_{3}}
containing
U_{2}
and
W_{3,k_{3}}
containing
U_{3}
. Then pick two
(k_{1}+k_{2})
-dimensional linear spaces
T_{2}
containing
V_{2}
and
T_{3}
containing
V_{3}
. The intersection of
T_{2},T_{3}
and
W_{i,k_{3}}
has dimension
k_{2}
, hence we can choose a general
k_{1}
-dimensional subspace
W_{i,k_{1}}
in this intersection. Similarly, the intersection of
T_{2}
(respectively,
T_{3}
) with
W_{3,k_{3}}
(respectively,
W_{2,k_{3}}
) has dimension
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}-n>k_{2}
. Hence, we can choose a general
k_{2}
-dimensional linear space
W_{3,k_{2}}
containing
W_{3,k_{1}}
(respectively,
W_{2,k_{2}}
containing
W_{2,k_{1}}
) in this intersection. It is clear that applying the construction to these partial flags yields back
U_{2},U_{3},V_{2}
and
V_{3}
. We conclude that the action on
F(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};n)^{3}
is sparse if the action on
\Gr(k_{1}+k_{3}-n,k_{1})^{2}\times\Gr(2k_{1}+k_{2}-n,k_{1})^{2}
is sparse. Since

2(k_{1}+k_{3}-n)+2(2k_{1}+k_{2}-n)=2k_{1},

we conclude that the latter is sparse by Theorem
2.3
. This concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
a<b
be two positive integers such that
a+b
is even. Then
F(a,b,n-\frac{a+b}{2};n)^{3}
is not dense.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.8
,

F\left(a,b,n-\frac{a+b}{2};n\right)^{3}\Leftrightarrow F\left(\frac{a+b}{2},n-b,n-a,;n\right)^{3}.

The latter is not dense by Proposition
4.4
.
∎
