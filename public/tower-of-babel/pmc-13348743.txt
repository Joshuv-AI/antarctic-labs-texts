The evolving PI-RADS paradigm
Authors: Andrea Ponsiglione, Ivo G. Schoots, Anwar R. Padhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prostate MRI is central to the diagnostic pathway for prostate cancer (PCa), reducing unnecessary biopsies, improving the detection of clinically significant disease (csPCa), and limiting overdiagnosis of indolent tumours. The Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PIRADS) version 2.1 is the current international standard for MRI data acquisition and interpretation. This review synthesises the evidence for its use, its strengths and limitations, and future developments. PI-RADS v2.1 improved the assessments of multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) by clarifying technical requirements, zone-specific interpretation rules, and structured reporting recommendations. Its diagnostic performance is well established, particularly for ruling out csPCa and guiding risk-adapted biopsy strategies. However, significant gaps remain. MRI image quality varies substantially across centres, and PI-RADS lacks a mechanism to exclude non-diagnostic scans, resulting in inconsistent classification. Several scoring challenges persist, most notably the ambiguous definition and heterogeneous management of PI-RADS 3 lesions, imprecise lesion measurement standards, and limited guidance for central-zone and atypical lesions. Gaps include the absence of strategies for assessing background tissue changes and infiltrative patterns. Cancer detection specificity and inter-reader agreement remain moderate, with significant discrepancies in transition-zone assessments. Artificial intelligence (AI) holds promise for reducing variability, improving lesion detection, and optimising workflow, though rigorous validation and clear human-AI integration frameworks are needed. Advances in deep learning reconstructions improve image quality and enable shorter protocols, thereby supporting the adoption of biparametric MRI. A shift toward risk-based pathways, integrating MRI findings with PSA density and clinical parameters, is reflected in the forthcoming PI-RADS Pathway 2026, which aims to standardize global biopsy practice and reduce unnecessary interventions.

Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has become the cornerstone of the diagnostic pathway for prostate cancer (PCa), serving as the preferred imaging modality for men with a clinical suspicion of disease [1]. Incorporating MRI upfront into the diagnostic pathway before a biopsy reduces unnecessary biopsies, enhances the detection of clinically significant PCa (csPCa), and limits the overdiagnosis of indolent tumours [2, 3]. For these reasons, current international guidelines recommend MRI as the triage test for symptomatic or asymptomatic men, with a life expectancy of 10–15 years in a shared decision-making paradigm [4]. Eligible patients include average-risk and elevated-risk men clinically selected, and biopsy-naïve men with normal digital rectal examinations (DRE) and confirmed elevated and rising grey-zone prostate specific agent (PSA) (usually between 4 and 10 ng/mL). Other groups include men with a persistently elevated risk of having significant cancer despite prior negative or non-explanatory initial systematic biopsies, and men with concerning PSA kinetics (e.g., rising PSA levels or increased PSA velocity) or clinical findings in men on ‘safety net monitoring’ after prior negative MRI (avoided biopsy) or positive MRI with negative biopsy.

To ensure uniform interpretation and reporting within the diagnostic pathway, the Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) was jointly established by the American College of Radiology (ACR) and the European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR) [5]. Designed as a living framework, PI-RADS has undergone periodic revisions to integrate evolving clinical evidence. The most recent version, PI-RADS v2.1, released in 2019, introduced technical refinements and clarified interpretation criteria to enhance reproducibility and standardisation [6].

This review synthesises the current state and ongoing evolution of PI-RADS, focusing on recent updates, persistent challenges, and future directions. We summarize clinical adoption and diagnostic performance in practice, critically examine the limitations that hinder broader integration into routine practice, and highlight emerging developments, particularly artificial intelligence (AI), poised to influence the next generation of PI-RADS and prostate MRI interpretation (Fig. 1).

Where are we now?

Evolution and rationale for PI-RADS v2.1

Prostate MRI evolved from a staging tool to a multiparametric diagnostic modality by combining anatomical and functional imaging. Initially, MRI was used for locoregional staging in biopsy-proven cases because its ability to differentiate benign conditions from csPCa was limited [7]. Advances in hardware, software, and interpretive experience led to the development of multiparametric MRI (mpMRI), which integrated T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) mapping, and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) sequences. Early clinical adoption of mpMRI was hampered by variability in acquisition protocols, interpretation, and reporting across institutions. Recognizing the challenge, the ESUR introduced PI-RADS v1 in 2012 to standardize prostate MRI practice [8], building on earlier work by Padhani and Dickinson [9, 10]. While PI-RADS v1 represented a significant step, experience revealed limitations, including ambiguous criteria and moderate interobserver agreement. To address these issues and establish a global platform, the ACR, ESUR, and AdMeTech Foundation updated PI-RADS to v2 (2015), and its subsequent refinement, PI-RADS v2.1 (2019), which remains applicable to this day [5, 6]. In particular, PI-RADS v2.1 was developed to refine the consistency and clarity of prostate mpMRI interpretation while preserving the core principle of assigning sequence-specific scores that determine a final assessment category. Its main objectives remained the establishment of minimum technical standards for mpMRI acquisition, clarification of zone-specific interpretation criteria, enhancement of structured reporting and standardised terminology to improve interdisciplinary communication, and support for MRI-targeted biopsy and patient risk stratification.

Technical requirements and image optimisation

High‑quality image acquisition is fundamental to the diagnostic performance of prostate MRI. PI-RADS v2.1 reiterates that both 1.5-T and 3-T MRI systems are acceptable, but 3-T is preferred because of its superior signal-to-noise ratio, which can be leveraged to improve spatial and temporal resolution [6]. However, advances in external phased-array coil technology, optimized protocols, and deep-learning reconstructions (DLR) have narrowed the image quality gap [11, 12].

Although PI-RADS v2.1 does not mandate specific patient preparation, measures to reduce motion and rectal distention can improve image quality. A cleansing mini-enema before imaging may reduce rectal gas and DWI distortion, although evidence of benefit is mixed [13]. Antiperistalsis agents can reduce motion artifacts on T2WI, but should be used judiciously, given the cost and potential adverse effects in elderly patients [14]. Abstinence from ejaculation for several days before imaging has been proposed to optimize seminal vesicle distention, but the impact on cancer detection remains unproven.

Interpretation and reporting

PI-RADS v2.1 standardises the interpretation of prostate mpMRI by using a five-point category scale (PI-RADS 1–5) to indicate the likelihood of csPCa. PI-RADS defined csPCa as the presence of International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade group (GG) ≥ 2 (corresponding to a Gleason score of 3 + 4 or higher) on histology, and/or a tumour volume greater than 0.5 cc, and/or evidence of extraprostatic extension (EPE). The dominant scoring sequence for the peripheral zone (PZ) is DWI/ADC, whereas for the transition zone (TZ), it is T2WI. DCE plays a minor role, primarily upgrading PZ lesions from category 3 to 4. Note that there are no downgrading rules in PI-RADS.

Structured reporting is recommended, including image quality assessments, prostate volume, PSA density (PSAd), lesion mapping via sector diagrams, index lesion identification, and descriptions of EPE or seminal vesicle invasion (SVI). Standardized reporting templates support consistency and guide targeted biopsy and focal therapy.

PI-RADS performance benchmarks

Recent meta-analyses and large multicenter studies have evaluated the diagnostic performance of PI-RADS, confirming its sensitivity for detecting csPCa, particularly when a PI-RADS ≥ 3 threshold is used to guide biopsies [16–18]. In particular, Nedelcu et al. reported high patient-level sensitivity for GG ≥ 2 cancer for PI-RADS ≥ 3 (96%), and PI-RADS ≥ 4 (88%), although specificity was moderate at 43% and 66%, respectively [18]. As expected, cancer detection rates increase progressively with increasing PI-RADS suspicion category, from approximately 3% in category 1 and 6% in category 2 to around 20% in category 3, 53% in category 4, and reaching roughly 83% in category 5.

Integration into clinical pathways

The widespread adoption of PI-RADS v2.1 transformed prostate MRI from a purely diagnostic tool into a central component of the personalised PCa diagnosis pathway. By standardising acquisition, interpretation, and reporting, PI-RADS facilitates integration into clinical decision-making algorithms. Current guidelines endorse MRI supported by PI-RADS as the triage test to reduce unnecessary biopsies and improve detection of csPCa, while minimising overdiagnoses of indolent disease [4]. Current clinical implementation is risk-based, requiring the integration of PI-RADS categories with PSA metrics (particularly PSAd) [19]. Negative MRI findings allow deferral of biopsy in lower-risk men (with low PSAd) under robust safety-net protocols, including PSA monitoring and repeat imaging as needed [20]. Conversely, positive MRI findings can guide targeted biopsies, with PI-RADS 3–5 scores informing the method and extent of tissue sampling. While PI‑RADS 3–4 lesions may require a combination of targeted and systematic cores to achieve comprehensive evaluation, PI‑RADS 5 lesions could generally sufficiently sampled through targeted biopsy alone, potentially supplemented by perilesional cores [2, 21, 22].

Interpretation and scoring inconsistencies

Several scoring criteria remain ambiguous, leading to inconsistent clinical practice. PI-RADS category 3 lesions, defined as both focal lesions and “all others that do not qualify as 2, 4, or 5,” exemplify this challenge [23], with many clinical reports introducing terms such as diffuse PI-RADS 3 [24]. Over-classification of PI-RADS 3 findings may indicate limited reader expertise, poor adherence to guidelines, and such variability can increase unnecessary biopsies.

Another debated criterion is the size threshold used to distinguish PI-RADS 4 from PI-RADS 5 lesions. The current cut-off of 1.5 cm is based on expert consensus, and its clinical rationale remains unclear, although its prognostic value is emerging [25–28]. Notably, a 1.0 cm threshold may be more biologically appropriate, as it approximates the 0.5 mL Stamey definition of csPCa, a volume that corresponds to ~ 1 cm diameter on pathology [29–31]. Similarly, lesion measurement lacks standardization: PI-RADS v2.1 does not specify the imaging plane for size assessment, and measurements on diffusion-weighted imaging can be distorted by artifacts and large voxel sizes, further complicating reproducibility [32].

Assessment of CZ lesions remains unresolved. PI-RADS v2.1 provides limited guidance for categorising lesions in this anatomically complex region, but interpretations remain challenging even for experienced readers due to hypercellular signals on DWI. TZ upgrading rules also introduce subjectivity. While v2.1 allows atypical TZ nodules (T2WI score 2) to be upgraded to PI-RADS 3 if diffusion restriction is present (DWI score ≥ 4), a recent meta-analysis reported low csPCa detection rates for these “2 + 1” lesions, approximately 13%, suggesting that the incremental diagnostic value of this upgrading rule may be limited [15]. Similarly, upgrading PI-RADS 3 lesions in the PZ (to 3 + 1=4) results in modest increases in diagnostic yields [33]. In particular, the prevalence of GG ≥ 2 cancer among PI-RADS 3 lesions upgraded by DCE is intermediate in diagnostic cohorts (about 15–31%) [34, 35]. Although DCE upgrading helps identify a subgroup at higher risk than non-upgraded PI-RADS 3 lesions, its usefulness is limited by a high false-positive rate, largely due to chronic or acute prostatitis. As a result, many upgraded lesions represent low-grade, clinically insignificant cancer, raising concerns about over-diagnosis and unnecessary biopsies.

A risk-stratified strategy, incorporating PSA density, can improve decision-making on whether to biopsy upgraded lesions.

Reporting gaps

PI-RADS v2.1 has limitations in its approach to the background prostatic appearance. The system does not include assessments of findings such as prostatitis, atrophy, or post-biopsy changes, even though these processes can alter the gland’s appearance [36]. Such changes may mimic or obscure cancers by affecting T2 signal, ADC values, enhancement patterns, and zonal anatomy. As a result, radiologists handle them differently in practice, which affects lesion assessment and PI-RADS scoring [37]. In addition, PI-RADS does not offer specific categories for lesions that appear suspicious for malignancy but do not meet the strict criteria for PI-RADS 4 or 5 lesions, such as mucinous tumours [36]. Other organ-focused reporting systems include an intermediate category (e.g., LI-RADS-M) for observations that raise concern yet fall outside typical imaging patterns. Introducing a similar PI-RADS M (malignancy) category could improve flexibility when reporting atypical or infiltrative prostate lesions. In particular, rare mucinous adenocarcinomas, often showing deceptively high T2-weighted and ADC signals due to extracellular mucin, might be more appropriately flagged as PI-RADS M rather than being placed in the equivocal PI-RADS 3 group, reducing the likelihood of them being overlooked and preventing diagnostic delays [38, 39]. Additionally, PI-RADS does not provide explicit recommendations for reporting entities that mimic csPCa, such as ectopic BPH nodules or prostatic abscesses, which may fulfill imaging criteria for PI-RADS 4 or 5 [40, 41].

Unlike the BI-RADS system, there is no PI-RADS 0/X for non-diagnostic scans. As a result, many radiologists mistakenly report poor-quality MRI scans as PI-RADS 3 because of uncertainty rather than undertaking repeat scans. When biopsied, such poor-quality scans have been shown to have a lower prevalence of cancer within the PI-RADS 3 category [42].

There is also a pressing need to refine how PI-RADS defines and weighs invasive features. While extension across prostate zones (e.g., PZ into TZ) represents infiltrative tumour behaviour, it does not carry the same prognostic significance as true EPE or SVI. Future iterations of PI-RADS should therefore better differentiate between intra-prostatic infiltrative patterns and clinically meaningful extraprostatic invasion to ensure that reporting reflects their differing diagnostic and prognostic implications. The reliance on tumour size for categorization inadvertently downplays qualitative markers of aggressiveness, such as pronounced diffusion restriction, which can indicate high-grade cancer, including neuroendocrine or small cell variants [43]. Beyond qualitative visual assessment, PI‑RADS could be strengthened by incorporating quantitative diffusion metrics. Objective diffusivity measures, such as ADC values, and advanced multiexponential diffusion models provide additional information about tissue cellularity and microstructural complexity, including glandular, stromal, and cellular components, offering potential value for evaluating invasive tumour behaviour [44, 45].

Similarly, in the context of local staging, although PI-RADS recommends that radiologists assess EPE and SVI, it does not provide explicit criteria on how these assessments should be performed [6].

The current PI‑RADS sector map has anatomical gaps that may affect lesion localisation, including the absence of a clearly defined median‑lobe (intravesical) region. Although median‑lobe enlargement is common, and tumours can occasionally arise or extend into this area, PI‑RADS does not provide a dedicated sector for describing findings there. As a result, radiologists must reference adjacent sectors, thereby reducing precision and complicating communication with urologists during biopsy planning.

Inter-reader variability and performance gaps

Prostate MRI interpretation remains vulnerable to substantial inter-reader variability [46]. Several studies demonstrate that agreement among radiologists interpreting prostate MRI using PI-RADS v2.1 is only moderate for the PI-RADS 3–5 threshold, but improves for the PI-RADS 4–5 cut-off [47]. The PI-CAI Multi-Reader Multi-Case (MRMC) study found that 62 radiologists from 20 countries reading the same 400 MRI cases had an overall agreement of 73% for PI-RADS 3–5, compared with 78% for PI-RADS 4–5, indicating improved overall consistency by reducing the ambiguity associated with PI-RADS 3 lesions [48]. Furthermore, agreement is notably lower for negative cases (66%) than for positive cases (89%), indicating substantial human variability in distinguishing “normal” tissue from benign findings. PI-RADS 3 lesions contribute to variability because readers differ in their thresholds for upgrading based on DWI, DCE and lesion morphology [47]. Significant discrepancies are observed in TZ lesions when DWI upgrading rules should be applied to indeterminate T2WI findings [49]. Instead, DWI-identified lesions are downgraded using T2WI; an incorrect application of the PI-RADS v2.1 rules. It should be noted that there are no downgrade rules in PI-RADS; this reading method contributes to increased variability [33]. The inappropriate application of contrast medium to upgrade (PI-RADS 2 to PI-RADS 4) and downgrade PI-RADS 3–5 cases is another source of variability [33].

From a performance standpoint, PI-RADS v2.1 demonstrates excellent sensitivity for csPCa exclusion, approaching 96% at PI-RADS ≥ 3, supporting its role as a triage tool in diagnostic settings [18]. However, specificity remains modest, typically around 42–43% for PI-RADS ≥ 3, leading to variable positive predictive values (PPVs) and a high false-positive burden [18]. Up to 60% of PI-RADS ≥ 3 lesions yield negative biopsy results, reflecting unnecessary procedures, provoking patient anxiety, and increasing the risk of GG1 over-diagnosis. For the PI-RADS ≥ 4 cut-off, specificity improves to approximately 66%. Still, PPV remains heterogeneous across studies and clinical settings, partly due to differences in disease prevalence and the imaging thresholds used for biopsy decisions [50].

Protocol choice

As noted previously, PI‑RADS v2.1 does not provide detailed recommendations for patient preparation. Yet patient preparation is a key determinant of image quality, particularly when abbreviated or streamlined MRI protocols are used in selected patients. Factors such as bowel air management, bladder filling, and the use of antispasmodic agents can substantially influence artifact burden and lesion conspicuity [51]. For these reasons, there is a need for more prescriptive guidance on patient preparation and more precise, standardized requirements for compliant imaging protocols.

PI-RADS v2.1 acknowledges the increasing use of biparametric MRI (bpMRI), which omits DCE to reduce scan time, cost, and potential risks associated with gadolinium-based agents [6]. However, the current guidelines provide no explicit recommendations on when bpMRI may replace mpMRI, cautioning instead about a likely increase in PI-RADS category 3 cases and the associated diagnostic uncertainty. The PRIME trial results have intensified this discussion [52]. In a prospective study, PRIME demonstrated that bpMRI achieves csPCa detection rates comparable to those of mpMRI in biopsy-naïve men. Importantly, bpMRI was not inferior to mpMRI for detecting GG ≥ 2 cancers, supporting its potential role as a first-line imaging strategy in selected populations, such as low‑risk and very high‑risk patients [53], provided that image quality is high, and interpretation is performed by experienced readers [52]. Regardless, bpMRI is associated with a higher proportion of PI-RADS 3 lesions, which may lead to increased unnecessary biopsies if not combined with robust clinical risk stratification. The PI-CAI reader study also demonstrated that bpMRI is non-inferior to mpMRI for csPCa detection [33]. Decision curve analyses reveal context-dependent benefits of contrast medium use: mpMRI is slightly preferred over bpMRI in cancer-averse scenarios.

Integration of PSA density

Clinical guidelines emphasise the importance of interpreting MRI categories alongside PSAd to refine risk stratification for biopsy decisions [4, 54, 55]. Accordingly, the European Association of Urology (EAU) recommendations state that men with PI‑RADS 3 findings and low‑risk clinical parameters, including a PSAd below 0.10 ng/mL/cc, can safely omit biopsy (with a weak strength rating) [4]. The same framework suggests biopsy for PI-RADS 1–2 cases when PSAd is greater than 0.20 ng/mL/cc.

An open question is whether PSAd should be formally incorporated into the PI‑RADS scoring framework itself. Integrating PSAd into an imaging-based PI-RADS introduces conceptual challenges, especially regarding “double counting,” in which PSAd is combined with imaging findings that are themselves influenced by PSAd, undermining their independence. Without a validated risk‑model structure to guide how PSAd and imaging features interact, embedding PSAd directly into the scoring system may introduce unintended bias or reduce interpretative clarity.

Emerging paradigms and future directions

Integration of artificial intelligence and CAD systems

Predictive AI-based deep learning (DL) computer-aided detection (CAD) tools are poised to reshape prostate MRI interpretation and diagnostic workflows. Large-scale initiatives such as the PI-CAI Grand Challenge have demonstrated that a standalone AI model can outperform pooled radiologist performance in detecting GG ≥ 2 cancers [56]. However, AI was not found to be non-inferior to multidisciplinary care when considering the whole clinical picture, indicating that patient history, contextual data, and peer review remain essential for diagnosis. Multimodal AI, which incorporates image analysis, radiologic PI-RADS scores, PSA values/PSAd, and age, can further enhance AI performance [57]. Multiple large-scale studies show that AI, at its optimal operating point, effectively reduces false-positive rates, thereby reducing the risk of unnecessary, invasive biopsies [58], particularly for PI-RADS 3 cases. Using AI as a co-pilot significantly improves the clinical performance of non-experts, reducing inter-reader variability [59, 60].

However, AI integration into clinical practice requires robust validation, standardized reporting frameworks, and clarity on human-AI workflows [61, 62]. The PI-RADS Steering Committee has emphasized that AI development must follow structured guidelines to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and clinical relevance [63]. However, the committee has not commented on the complexities of introducing AI into diagnostic practice, including software capabilities, human-AI interactions, interfaces, reporting outputs, or post-marketing surveillance.

AI has also been deployed for image reconstruction. DL-based reconstruction (DLR) algorithms can significantly reduce scan times while maintaining, or even improving, image quality, addressing the growing demand for efficiency in PCa diagnosis [64]. Early studies suggest that AI-driven acceleration could enable high-quality bpMRI protocols in under 10 min, potentially expanding access and reducing costs without compromising diagnostic performance [65]. Nevertheless, DLR approaches require prospective validation on clinical endpoints, as there is no direct evidence linking DLR to altered biopsy decisions or patient outcomes from biopsies and staging.

Expanded clinical domains

The use of PI-RADS could be extended beyond initial cancer diagnosis to encompass the entire patient care continuum. Future directions envision a structured reporting framework integrating five major clinical domains: screening, diagnosis (the current focus of PI-RADS), staging, monitoring, and post-treatment assessment. This expansion includes specialized scoring adaptations, such as an adjusted PI-RADS for bpMRI in diagnostic settings, or a “screening PI-RADS” for population screening scenarios [66]. To refine risk stratification, PI-RADS should standardize the assessment of EPE/SVI rather than merely suggesting that these features be reported [67]. Monitoring tools, such as the PRECISE recommendations for assessing disease progression during active surveillance, could also be integral components of follow-up frameworks for men with prior negative MRI scans who avoided an immediate biopsy, as well as for men with positive MRI scans who had negative biopsies [68]. Post-treatment systems such as PI-RR, TARGET, and PI-FAB also require a unified diagnostic ecosystem [69–71]. Supporting these structural developments will be key for technological advancements in multimodal imaging, including Prostate-Specific Membrane Antigen Positron Emission Tomography and micro-ultrasound.

Quality assurance and control

Ensuring the integrity of the PI-RADS pathway requires rigorous quality assurance and control to standardise imaging, minimise heterogeneity, and reduce inter-reader variability. The quality of the acquired images should be systematically monitored and assessed, ideally utilising standardized tools such as the PI-QUAL version 2 scoring system [72]. Rather than relying solely on technical compliance, PI-QUAL grades the diagnostic usability of each sequence against predefined benchmarks, ensuring that non-diagnostic studies are identified and excluded from PI-RADS/DL-CAD categorisations [42, 73, 74]. This would prevent misinterpretation and inappropriate clinical decisions based on suboptimal imaging [75].

Pathway-related quality issues may arise across multiple stages, including patient preparation, acquisition, interpretation, and biopsy planning and execution. Radiologist experience is essential for reliable interpretation, and this is complemented by regular inter-reader comparisons and adherence to clinical benchmarks [76]. Advanced technologies, including DL-CAD systems and DLR, are expected to enhance standardization and quality assessment processes. Multidisciplinary collaboration, particularly pathology feedback, is critical for calibrating performance and maintaining continuous quality monitoring. Certification and accreditation programs are increasingly being developed in both Europe and the United States of America to help ensure radiologists maintain proficiency through structured training pathways, adherence to defined quality benchmarks, and ongoing continuing education [77, 78]. Similarly, ACR accreditation programs for prostate imaging centres are emerging [79].

Focus on risk-based clinical utility metrics

The MRI-driven biopsy paradigm demands new utility metrics that go beyond traditional sensitivity and specificity, which are inadequately assessed in an era that prioritises biopsy avoidance [80]. These indices should serve as Key Performance Metrics (KPMs) for pathway effectiveness and quality control. The goal is to balance timely detection of csPCa with minimizing unnecessary procedures and overdiagnosis of indolent disease [81, 82]. KPMs must reflect outcomes for all patients, including those who undergo biopsy and those who avoid it. Grade selectivity (GG ≥ 2/GG1 ratio) measures preference for detecting aggressive cancers over indolent ones, while biopsy efficiency (GG ≥ 2/(GG1 + benign biopsy)) evaluates efficiency relative to unproductive biopsies. Selective avoidance (avoided biopsies/unproductive biopsies) captures success in reducing unnecessary interventions among biopsy-avoiding patients. KPMs, when used to assess diagnostic strategies such as risk-based pathways, demonstrate a better benefit-to-harm ratio than MRI alone, supporting risk-based pathways as a more patient-centered and evidence-driven approach [83].

The PI-RADS pathway 2026

A significant development shaping future practice is the upcoming PI‑RADS pathway 2026, a position statement from the PI‑RADS Steering Committee. This updated pathway is expected to introduce a more structured, risk-based approach that integrates MRI findings, PSAd, and clinical history to inform biopsy decisions. Its goals include standardizing prostate MRI use worldwide, reducing unnecessary biopsies, and improving detection of clinically significant disease. The document emphasises high‑quality imaging standards and includes templates for recommendations to improve communication between radiologists and referring clinicians. As such, the PI‑RADS Pathway 2026 represents an important step toward creating a unified, data‑driven framework for prostate MRI interpretation, communication and patient management.

Future PI-RADS iterations

PI-RADS, conceived as a “living framework” that integrates emerging imaging technologies and evolving clinical evidence, must adhere to strict governance standards set by the ACR-RADS oversight committee. The scope of any revision determines the evidence threshold required for change: a minor update (e.g., version 2.2) typically requires at least one adequately powered single-center cohort study. In contrast, a version upgrade (e.g., PI-RADS 3.0) demands higher-level evidence, including at least one multicenter cohort study demonstrating significant clinical benefit. Crucially, this evidence must quantify net benefit by balancing improved detection of csPCa against reduced unnecessary biopsies and overdiagnoses, using robust clinical performance KPMs that additionally incorporate decision-curve analyses that weight benefits versus harms across a spectrum of urological preferences. The PI-RADS pathway 2026 document is an example document that meets the requirements of the ACR-RADS committee. Many other proposals lack these levels of evidence, leading the PI-RADS Steering Committee to conclude that a major version transition is not yet appropriate, despite the need. Consequently, interim progress is driven by position papers on the MRI pathway, AI integration, and image quality, which have higher levels of evidence regarding clinical utility; they are laying the groundwork for a robust, clinically informed future revision.

Conclusion

PI-RADS v2.1 has consolidated prostate MRI as a cornerstone of the diagnostic pathway for PCa by standardizing acquisition, interpretation, and reporting, thereby improving detection of clinically significant disease and supporting risk-adapted biopsy strategies. Its widespread clinical adoption has reduced unnecessary biopsies within structured pathways. However, significant limitations remain, including variability in image quality, persistent ambiguity in lesion categorisation, particularly for PI-RADS 3 lesions, moderate inter-reader agreement, and persistent high false-positive rates. Conceived as a living document, PI-RADS must continue to evolve in response to emerging evidence, technological advances, and changing clinical priorities. Future iterations should prioritise pathway-oriented clinical utility metrics, rigorous quality assurance, and evidence-based integration of AI systems/models. Sustained, governance-driven refinements will ensure that PI-RADS delivers measurable clinical benefits while maintaining simplicity, reproducibility, and patient-centered care.
