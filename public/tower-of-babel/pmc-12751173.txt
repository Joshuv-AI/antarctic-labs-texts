Ultrasound-guided needle localization in trans-mammary endoscopic thyroidectomy: a case report
Authors: Minghuang Zhang, Ning Meng, Xinjun Zhang, Jianhua Zhang, Supeng Huang, Kanghui Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThyroid nodules are relatively common, with a high incidence rate among adults, particularly women. With the widespread application of ultrasound imaging technology, the detection rate of thyroid nodules has significantly increased. However, traditional open surgery can lead to significant scar formation and potential thyroid dysfunction. Therefore, minimally invasive techniques that preserve thyroid function and aesthetic appearance are increasingly important.Case descriptionA 51-year-old Han Chinese female patient had nodules in both thyroid glands (right side C-TIRADS 4A, left side C-TIRADS 3). The patient was placed in a supine position with the neck extended (“human” position). Following ultrasound guidance, a localization needle was precisely marked at the nodule in the left lower pole, with the needle tip positioned at the deep margin of the lesion. The needle tail was secured to the skin with sterile tape. Trocar incisions were made at the margins of both areolas and the right upper outer quadrant. Carbon dioxide was insufflated to 6 mmHg to create the surgical space. The right thyroid lobe and isthmus were completely resected; subsequently, the surgeon shifted to the left thyroid lobe, identified the preplaced localization needle via endoscopy, and traced it to the nodule at the left lower pole. A circular dissection was performed around the needle tip with a 0.5-cm margin of normal tissue, precisely resecting the nodule. The localization needle enabled real-time, targeted resection under intraoperative guidance.ConclusionCombining ultrasound-guided localization needles with endoscopic thyroidectomy via the trans-breast approach offers a minimally invasive surgical option that preserves thyroid function and aesthetics while enabling precise resection of thyroid nodules, minimizing damage to normal tissue, and improving patient outcomes. This surgical method holds great potential in the field of thyroid surgery, achieving a balance between radical resection and functional preservation. Introduction

Thyroid nodules are among the most common lesions in thyroid disease. Epidemiological studies show that approximately 20–70% of adults worldwide have thyroid nodules, with a higher prevalence in women [1, 2]. With the widespread use of ultrasound imaging, the detection rate of thyroid nodules has steadily increased year by year [3]. While most nodules are benign, a small proportion may be malignant, particularly papillary thyroid carcinoma, which poses a significant threat to patient health [4]. Consequently, timely and accurate identification of the nature of thyroid nodules, followed by treatment tailored to their pathological features, has become a key issue in clinical practice [5]. In cases where the nature of the nodule remains uncertain or the risk of malignancy is high, surgical decision-making must balance both complete removal of the lesion and preservation of thyroid function [6]. Traditional thyroid surgery often involves extensive resection, which may compromise thyroid function and adversely affect the patient’s quality of life [7]. Therefore, determining how to achieve effective lesion removal while preserving as much thyroid function as possible has become a central focus in surgical planning [8]. Preserving thyroid function is crucial to long-term health, as the thyroid plays an essential role in endocrine regulation. By secreting thyroid hormones, it contributes to metabolism, thermoregulation, and multiple physiological processes [9]. Excessive thyroid resection can result in hypothyroidism, necessitating lifelong hormone replacement therapy, which significantly impacts patient well-being [10]. Thus, in thyroid surgery—particularly in cases involving benign nodules—ensuring complete removal of the lesion while maximizing preservation of thyroid function is a cornerstone of modern treatment strategies [11]. With ongoing advances in medical technology, minimally invasive endoscopic surgery has increasingly become the mainstream approach for managing thyroid diseases [12]. Endoscopic thyroidectomy is widely recognized for its advantages, including smaller incisions, reduced trauma, quicker recovery, and lower complication rates [13]. Compared with conventional open surgery, endoscopic approaches minimize postoperative pain and scarring, shorten hospital stays, and enhance patients’ overall quality of life [14]. In thyroid nodule surgery, precise localization of the lesion is pivotal to achieving surgical success [15]. Traditional approaches often depend on intraoperative palpation or incomplete imaging studies, whereas modern localization techniques have dramatically improved surgical accuracy [16]. Accurate localization not only facilitates effective lesion removal but also minimizes unnecessary tissue damage, thereby safeguarding thyroid function [17]. Ultrasound-guided needle localization provides real-time, highly accurate guidance for inserting the needle directly into the lesion, offering a precise intraoperative reference for nodule excision. Compared with conventional techniques, this approach enables preoperative confirmation of nodule position, reduces surgical uncertainty, and helps avoid inadvertent injury to vital structures such as the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands. In addition, ultrasound guidance offers advantages of noninvasiveness, repeatability, and real-time feedback, making it an indispensable tool in contemporary thyroid surgery.

This paper presents a case of areola endoscopic thyroid surgery employing ultrasound-guided needle localization to achieve both precise resection and functional preservation of thyroid tissue. Furthermore, it reviews the evolution of surgical techniques for thyroid nodules based on current literature. Through case analysis and literature review, the objective is to provide safe and effective strategies for clinical practice and to offer valuable references for the minimally invasive management of thyroid nodules.

Case report

A 51-year-old Han Chinese female patient was admitted to the hospital for evaluation and treatment due to a thyroid nodule discovered during an imaging examination 10 years ago. The patient had no significant past medical history or surgical history and was completely asymptomatic. She denied a family history of thyroid disease or a history of head and neck radiation therapy. Physical examination revealed a mass measuring approximately 1.5 × 1.5 cm in the upper pole of the right thyroid gland and a mass measuring approximately 1.0 × 1.0 cm in the lower pole of the left thyroid gland. Both masses were of moderate consistency, with clear borders, smooth surfaces, no tenderness, and moved up and down with swallowing. No other abnormalities were noted on physical examination. Chest x-ray showed no abnormalities. Thyroid plain scan + contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) findings: Thyroid density was unevenly reduced, with a round-shaped low-density shadow visible, the larger one located in the right lobe of the thyroid, measuring approximately 17 mm in diameter, with punctate calcifications inside, and the contrast enhancement degree was lower than that of the surrounding parenchyma; Thyroid ultrasound findings: On the left side, a mass measuring approximately 1.2 × 0.6 × 0.9 cm was located at the lower pole, with an aspect ratio less than 1, smooth margins, and uniform internal echoes, right side: approximately 1.3 × 0.5 × 1.1 cm in size, located at the upper pole, with an aspect ratio less than 1, irregular margins, and heterogeneous internal echoes, with coarse bright spots visible, and the remaining parenchyma showing coarse, heterogeneous echoes with a grid-like pattern. Diagnosis: bilateral thyroid nodules (right side C-TIRADS: Category 4A, left side C-TIRADS: Category 3), Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Preoperative thyroid ultrasound and CT images are shown in Fig. 1. Preoperative vocal cord examination was normal. Laboratory tests showed normal results except for thyroid peroxidase antibody at 278.48 ↑ (normal range < 5.61 IU/mL). In addition, calcitonin < 0.5 pg/mL; parathyroid hormone: 3.35 pmol/L, both within normal ranges. Following multidisciplinary discussion or decision by the attending physician: management plan for the right-sided nodule (Category 4A, high risk of malignancy): decided to perform right thyroid lobe and isthmus resection. Management plan for the left-sided nodule (Category 3, high likelihood of benignity): decided to resect only the nodule, maximizing preservation of normal left thyroid tissue (functional preservation requirements, nodule nature to be assessed via intraoperative frozen section). A preoperative ultrasound-guided localization needle was used for the left nodule (difficult to palpate intraoperatively, ensuring precise resection while minimizing the resection range and preserving function as much as possible. Surgical approach: under ultrasound guidance, the tip of the localization needle was placed at the lower pole of the left lobe nodule preoperatively (Fig. 2). The patient was placed in the “human” position under general anesthesia, and a three-incision approach through the chest and breast was performed. During the procedure, the right lobe and isthmus of the thyroid gland were completely resected. Under the guidance of the localization needle, the left lower pole nodule was precisely resected, ensuring that the resection margin was 0.5 cm of normal thyroid tissue. The remaining thyroid tissue was preserved. The intraoperative endoscopic findings are shown in Fig. 3. Surgical duration: 140 min; blood loss: 10 ml; postoperative vital signs were stable, and the patient returned to the ward safely. Postoperative hypothyroidism was treated with levothyroxine at a dose of 100 μg/day. Intraoperative frozen section: the right lobe lesion was suggestive of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis with atypical follicular epithelial hyperplasia, and papillary carcinoma could not be ruled out; the left side was an adenomatous nodule (hence, only the nodular lesion was resected). Postoperative pathology is shown in Fig. 4: right lobe diagnosis: papillary thyroid carcinoma, tumor diameter approximately 0.6 cm, with surrounding Hashimoto’s thyroiditis changes; Left lower pole nodular lesion: thyroid follicular tumor with atypical hyperplasia of follicular epithelium, considered to be a noninvasive follicular thyroid tumor with papillary nuclear features, approximately 0.5 cm in diameter. The patient was discharged on the sixth day postoperatively without complications such as hoarseness or hypocalcemic convulsions.

Discussion

Thyroid nodules are a common clinical condition, with their incidence rising annually owing to the widespread use of imaging techniques such as ultrasound [1, 2]. Surgical treatment has always been one of the main methods for treating thyroid nodules, but the approach to surgery has undergone continuous evolution and development.

In the past, traditional open thyroidectomy was the primary surgical method. The advantage of this approach is its clear and direct visualization, allowing the surgeon to observe and operate on the lesion and surrounding tissues. For larger or superficially located nodules, removal is relatively easy. However, this approach inevitably leaves a noticeable scar on the neck, which significantly affects the patient’s appearance. This is especially concerning for younger female patients, for whom the scar may have a substantial negative impact on their psychological well-being and quality of life [12, 14, 18–20]. In addition, open surgery can lead to complications such as postoperative neck discomfort, tension, and a relatively longer recovery time [13, 14, 21, 22].

With advances in medical technology and increasing aesthetic demands, endoscopic thyroid surgery has emerged. Endoscopic thyroidectomy via areola approach has gradually gained attention. This approach involves making an incision in the breast area, using natural skin folds to conceal the incision, thus improving the aesthetic outcome. At the same time, endoscopic thyroidectomy via areola approach provides sufficient operative space, reduces tension on neck tissues, and lowers the likelihood of nerve damage [19, 23–26]. Furthermore, the magnified view provided by the endoscope allows for more precise surgical operations, facilitating the accurate identification and protection of critical structures such as the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands [23, 25, 26].

As surgical techniques have advanced, the goals of thyroid nodule surgery have also shifted. In the past, the primary goal of surgery was to completely remove the lesion to treat the disease. However, with an improved understanding of thyroid physiology and higher patient demands for quality of life, the preservation of thyroid tissue and function, while ensuring the removal of the nodule, has become an important surgical goal. Compared with total or subtotal thyroidectomy, preserving more glandular volume can reduce the likelihood of postoperative hypothyroidism and the need for long-term levothyroxine replacement therapy. Retrospective studies and systematic reviews suggest that the incidence of hypothyroidism after partial thyroidectomy can reach approximately 20–30%, indicating that a smaller extent of resection may provide potential functional benefits under benign or low-risk conditions [27, 28].

Preoperative ultrasound-guided needle localization plays an increasingly important role in thyroid nodule surgery. During surgery, for small or deep-seated nodules that are difficult to palpate, a needle is accurately positioned at the nodule site under ultrasound guidance, allowing the surgeon to quickly and precisely locate the lesion along the needle, avoiding unnecessary time spent searching for the lesion, which could increase surgical time and tissue damage [20, 29–31]. This not only improves the efficiency of surgery but also enhances its accuracy. In particular, in endoscopic surgery, this technique helps overcome the limitations of two-dimensional imaging and the inability to directly palpate the lesion, reducing the risk of misresection and incomplete resection.

Studies have explored the use of ultrasound-guided needle localization in head and neck surgeries, yielding positive surgical outcomes. These studies provide theoretical support for our approach. For example, Fletcher et al. [20] reported the application of ultrasound-guided needle localization in the treatment of recurrent thyroid cancer, successfully identifying and excising recurrent papillary thyroid carcinoma. This study demonstrated that ultrasound-guided needle localization can improve the accuracy and safety of surgical resection. In addition, Duprez et al. [31] examined the use of ultrasound-guided hook-needle insertion in cervical lymph node dissection for recurrent thyroid cancer, showing that this technique assists surgeons in locating small, nonpalpable lymph nodes for removal. These findings are similar to our method and further validate the potential value of ultrasound-guided needle localization in head and neck surgery.

In this case, preoperative ultrasound-guided needle localization demonstrated significant advantages. First, it addressed the issue of accurately locating a small nodule in the lower pole of the left thyroid during endoscopic surgery. By placing the tip of the localization needle at the nodule site under ultrasound guidance, the surgical team was provided with a clear and intuitive surgical target, avoiding the time-consuming search for the lesion under the complex two-dimensional endoscopic view, which reduced both surgical time and anesthesia risk [29]. Moreover, the presence of the localization needle allowed real-time guidance during the surgery, ensuring the accuracy of the resection boundaries [30]. Although endoscopic surgery offers advantages such as magnified imaging, it still has certain limitations in spatial perception for deep or concealed lesions. The localization needle acts as a “navigation marker,” guiding the surgeon along the correct path to reach the lesion, ensuring precise removal while avoiding excessive tension or damage to the surrounding normal thyroid tissue, thus promoting functional preservation.

The surgical approach to thyroid nodules has evolved from traditional open surgery to modern endoscopic surgery, reflecting the continuous progress of medical technology and increasing attention to patient needs. The advent of endoscopic thyroidectomy via areola approach cleverly utilizes natural skin folds to conceal the incision, achieving an excellent cosmetic outcome [23, 32]. More importantly, this surgical method not only improves the appearance but also demonstrates unique advantages in safety and functional preservation. By providing sufficient operative space, reducing tension on neck tissues, and lowering the risk of nerve damage, combined with the magnified view and fine surgical instruments of the endoscope, it allows for more precise identification and protection of critical structures such as the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands [23, 25, 26]. In this case, during the resection of the right thyroid lobe and isthmus, the successful identification and preservation of the parathyroid glands, as well as the operation conducted after exposing the right recurrent laryngeal nerve, fully demonstrated the feasibility of this surgical method in protecting important tissues.

In addition, modern thyroid surgery increasingly emphasizes the balance between precise resection and functional preservation. In the past, thyroid nodule surgery might have involved a more extensive resection to ensure the complete removal of the lesion. However, this often resulted in partial or complete loss of thyroid function, requiring long-term or even lifetime thyroid hormone replacement therapy, which brought inconvenience and additional economic burden to the patient [27, 33]. Today, preoperative evaluation and surgical planning take into full consideration factors such as the nodule’s nature, location, size, and the patient’s thyroid function. In this case, the right thyroid lobe and isthmus were removed, while the left lobe only had the nodule located by the needle removed, with the remaining thyroid tissue preserved for functional purposes. Postoperatively, the patient’s thyroid function markers were largely maintained, demonstrating the successful implementation of the concept of precise resection and functional preservation.

The transareolar endoscopic thyroidectomy adopted in this case is a well-established option among various remote-access approaches. Numerous studies have reported similar breast-based approaches for thyroid surgery, all sharing the common objective of ensuring surgical efficacy while maximizing cosmetic satisfaction. Among these techniques, the transareolar endoscopic thyroidectomy is currently one of the most widely applied and extensively studied globally. Wang et al. reported as many as 1250 cases of transareolar endoscopic thyroidectomy, confirming the safety and feasibility of this technique in managing both benign and malignant thyroid diseases, while emphasizing its superiority in providing a clear surgical view for central compartment lymph node dissection and achieving excellent cosmetic outcomes [23]. In addition, Shan et al. [22] compared robot-assisted thyroidectomy via a trans-mammary incision with conventional open thyroidectomy and found no significant difference in postoperative complication rates between the two, whereas the robotic approach offered superior precision and safety during operation. In recent years, researchers have also focused on evaluating the long-term outcomes of this surgical approach. Kim et al. [25] compared trans-mammary endoscopic thyroidectomy with conventional open surgery in terms of functional preservation and found that the endoscopic approach was markedly superior in maintaining postoperative thyroid function, particularly in patients with a high demand for functional preservation. In our surgical strategy, the choice of incisions (bilateral areolar and right upper outer quadrant of the breast) was determined by specific case requirements and team experience. The core concept is consistent with the aforementioned literature—utilizing natural body folds to conceal scars. Compared with these classical techniques, the innovation in this case lies in the systematic integration of preoperative ultrasound-guided localization needle technology with the transareolar endoscopic platform. This combination specifically addresses the inherent challenge of “loss of tactile feedback” in conventional endoscopic surgery. Particularly for deeply located or very small nodules, the localization needle functions as a real-time, visible “lighthouse” for navigation, ensuring precise resection boundaries and thereby elevating the advantages of the transareolar endoscopic approach to a new level in both “precision” and “minimally invasiveness.”

In conclusion, this case and a review of relevant literature suggest that endoscopic thyroidectomy via areola approach combined with preoperative ultrasound-guided needle localization holds promising applications for the treatment of thyroid nodules. With continuous technological advancements and accumulated experience, it is expected that the surgical process will be further optimized, improving surgical outcomes and providing higher-quality medical services for patients with thyroid nodules.

Limitations

Although the endoscopic thyroidectomy via areola approach combined with preoperative ultrasound-guided needle localization offers numerous advantages, there are still some challenges and limitations in its clinical application. For instance, this surgical method requires a high level of technical skill and experience from the surgeon. It also carries risks such as the potential displacement or breakage of the localization needle, necessitating mastery of endoscopic techniques and a deep understanding of the thyroid anatomy to ensure the safety and effectiveness of the surgery. Furthermore, the cost of surgical instruments is relatively high, which may limit the widespread application of this approach. While the preoperative ultrasound-guided needle localization technique improves surgical precision, the placement of the needle requires strict procedural protocols to prevent risks such as damage to surrounding tissues and infection.

Conclusion

This case successfully demonstrates the significant advantages of preoperative ultrasound-guided needle localization combined with the endoscopic thyroidectomy via areola approach, effectively addressing the challenge of locating deep or small nodules during endoscopic procedures. This technique integrates the precise guidance of the ultrasound localization needle with the minimally invasive nature of endoscopic surgery: the preoperative needle provides a clear target for the operation, ensuring the accurate removal of the nodule (especially those in deep or unique locations), avoiding blind exploration, and enhancing efficiency while minimizing damage to normal thyroid tissue. This “precise resection + functional preservation” strategy ensures the radical treatment of the lesion while maximizing the preservation of normal thyroid tissue, thereby improving the patient’s postoperative quality of life. This technique represents the direction of thyroid surgery towards individualized, minimally invasive, and functional approaches. Its broader application requires standardized procedures and team collaboration, and further high-quality research is anticipated to provide evidence-based support. With technological advancements, it is expected to further optimize surgical safety and outcomes, offering patients higher-quality and more personalized treatment options in the future.
