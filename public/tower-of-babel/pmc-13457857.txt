AI image generation technology for visual communication design
Authors: Guohao Kong, Long Zhang, Junghyen Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study addresses two key challenges in applying artificial intelligence (AI) image generation to visual communication design: insufficient alignment with design requirements and unstable style controllability. It systematically explores the integration of AI technology with professional design practice. First, the current research and application status of AI generation in design is reviewed, and its technological positioning is clarified. Next, an integrated framework is constructed by combining diffusion models, CLIP-based cross-modal alignment, and ControlNet spatial constraints. This framework supports an AI-driven design workflow that incorporates requirement analysis, controllable generation, and multi-solution exploration. Multi-dimensional evaluation metrics, including generation quality, peak signal-to-noise ratio, and requirement matching, are also established. Experiments are conducted across the core design scenarios—posters, brand logos, and mobile interfaces—using public datasets such as DesignBench-20 K and a custom commercial test set. The model performs best in mobile interface scenarios. Across all scenarios, the design cycle is reduced by over 91%. The model reliably generates outputs under textual requirement noise ≤ 10% and non-design data distribution shifts ≤ 40%. Ablation studies highlight the critical and differentiated contributions of each core component in layout control and requirement matching. The study further clarifies the application boundaries of this technology in visual communication design. It proposes optimization paths, including enhancing CLIP’s abstract semantic feature extraction, building a ControlNet template library for design-specific constraints, and refining GAN style interpolation weight adjustment. Finally, the study identifies limitations in data adaptability and dynamic generation capability, providing directions for future improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55838-6.

Introduction

Visual communication design serves as a core medium connecting information with audiences. It is undergoing structural transformation driven by advances in artificial intelligence (AI)1. In recent years, AI image generation techniques, particularly diffusion models and generative adversarial networks (GANs), have achieved breakthroughs in high-fidelity image synthesis and stylized expression. These techniques have evolved from auxiliary tools into central components of creative production, shifting the workflow toward an intelligent “requirement–generation–optimization” paradigm2,3. Design requirements are diverse, encompassing functional constraints, spatial layout rules, semantic intent, style preferences, brand guidelines, and regulatory mandates. A single model cannot fully address all requirement types. This study therefore focuses on three key requirements in commercial visual communication design: semantic intent, style preference, and spatial layout rules. Regulatory and industrial functional constraints are excluded. This focus aligns with the core creative demands of commercial design while allowing technical development to target high-impact objectives, ensuring precise adaptation and efficient response within the intended requirement scope. Despite these advances, current AI techniques face several challenges. Textual semantic intent often fails to align with generated outputs, style controllability is unstable, spatial layout constraints are weak, and abstract semantic intent is difficult to translate into visual results. Complementary issues, such as designer role adaptation, integration into design education, and copyright or ethical compliance, also remain unresolved4,5.

To address these challenges, this study proposes an integrated framework combining diffusion models, CLIP-based cross-modal alignment, and ControlNet spatial constraints. The framework is validated using public datasets, such as DesignBench-20 K, and custom commercial datasets across five core design scenarios, including e-commerce promotional posters and brand logos. Objective metrics and subjective evaluations demonstrate its effectiveness. This study does not directly address issues related to changes in designers’ roles, educational integration, or ethics. The proposed human–AI collaborative workflow can serve as a technical reference. However, the practical application of these broader issues still requires dedicated investigation in future research.

Literature review

In recent years, deep learning–based image generation technologies, particularly diffusion models and GANs, have achieved breakthroughs in fidelity, style controllability, and cross-modal adaptability. Emerging techniques and paradigms, such as Flow Matching and ControlNet, along with iterative improvements in state-of-the-art models, have further advanced AI image generation toward precise control and efficient adaptation. These developments provide a robust and diverse technical foundation for deep applications in visual communication design. Among core generation models, diffusion models remain the dominant approach. Iterations from Stable Diffusion v1.5 to Stable Diffusion 3 have significantly improved cross-modal semantic alignment and generation efficiency. GANs, in contrast, have progressed toward fine-grained style control. StyleGAN3, the latest mature version, addresses the style bias present in earlier models and enables unbiased, continuous style interpolation. Its strengths in capturing and blending design styles remain unmatched, establishing GANs as a key technology for style-controllable generation in AI-driven design workflows.

From a technological application perspective, Shi (2024) and colleagues found through empirical studies that integrating diffusion models into design workflows could effectively expand the boundaries of creative possibilities6. Cooke (2024) further noted that AI-generated images could serve as visual stimuli during the conceptual development phase, promoting divergent thinking in design7. However, Hertz (2023) highlighted that the utility of AI in the design process exhibited clear stage-specific characteristics, with its contribution being relatively limited in convergent phases that required professional judgment, such as requirement definition and solution refinement8. Existing studies largely focus on handling a single type of design requirement or optimizing the application of a single technology. They have yet to develop an integrated framework that combines cross-modal semantic alignment, structured spatial constraints, and style-controllable generation. Moreover, research on adapting AI techniques to the composite requirements of commercial visual communication design remains limited. This gap is a key reason why AI-assisted design often fails to achieve effective requirement alignment in practical applications.

In addition, the introduction of large language models (LLMs) provides a new approach to scene parsing and layout planning in AI image generation. Koch (2025) et al. developed a two-stage system. In the first stage, the LLMs generates a structured layout from an object list. In the second stage, a layout-conditioned diffusion model synthesizes images according to the layout. The task decomposition strategy increases object recall in complex scenes from 57.2% to 99.9%. Their study also reveals a key trade-off. ControlNet preserves text-based style control but suffers from object hallucination. In contrast, GLIGEN achieves higher layout fidelity but reduces prompt-based controllability9.

At a more general scene level, Cao et al. (2024), in a review published in IEEE TPAMI, systematically examined advances in controllable text-to-image (T2I) diffusion models. From the perspective of condition injection, they categorized control methods into three groups: specific-condition generation, multi-condition generation, and general controllable generation. Their framework covers spatial control (including layout, pose, depth, and segmentation), image personalization, viewpoint geometry constraints, and attention guidance. This review provides important methodological support for the multi-condition collaborative generation framework adopted in this study10.

Multimodal prompt fusion mechanisms have attracted widespread attention in recent years. Wanaskar et al. (2025) proposed an open-source unified benchmarking framework for T2I generation models. Using the DeepFashion-MultiModal dataset, the framework evaluates generated outputs through a combination of weighted scores, CLIP similarity, LPIPS, FID, and retrieval-based metrics. The results indicate that structured metadata enhancement improves visual realism, semantic fidelity, and cross-architecture robustness11.

In the domain of layout control, the ContextGen framework proposed by Xu et al. employed a dual-context attention mechanism to achieve precise layout anchoring and high-fidelity identity separation. Existing studies show that this approach outperforms current methods in control accuracy, identity fidelity, and visual quality. It also provides a practical reference for transferring such advanced techniques to visual communication design tasks12.

Additionally, Jiang (2024) emphasized that designers must master the ability of “concept concretization” to translate abstract requirements into precise textual descriptions13. Kim (2024) highlighted the importance of maintaining cultural sensitivity and ethical awareness in AI-assisted design14. Siddharth (2025) examined the trustworthiness challenges of AI image generation in data-driven innovation contexts. Key issues include privacy risks in training data, limited robustness under input perturbations or distribution shifts, and the lack of interpretability and transparency in model outputs and decision-making. These factors directly impact the reliability and safety of AI-generated images in design applications15. In a related study, Siddharth (2024) proposed a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) method based on engineering design knowledge. This approach provides LLMs with precise contextual information from professional design knowledge bases. It effectively mitigates the models’ limitations in domain-specific knowledge and content accuracy, enabling LLMs to better meet the semantic and feature-mapping requirements of visual communication design16. Jiang (2024) further showed that LLMs could establish a new paradigm for semantic communication. Their strong contextual understanding improves both the efficiency and reliability of multimodal interaction. Combining LLMs with RAG methods allows researchers to explore their synergistic potential, while understanding these mechanisms is crucial for integrating AI deeply with visual communication design and enhancing the professionalism and precision of generated outputs17.

Overall, existing research provides a systematic framework for understanding AI image generation in visual communication design. Studies have produced valuable insights into technical implementation, designer role transformation, education systems, and ethical guidelines, laying a solid foundation for further investigation. However, gaps remain in requirement alignment research. First, most studies focus on a single requirement type or technology. Few offer integrated solutions that combine cross-modal semantic alignment with structured spatial constraints, limiting their ability to address the composite needs of commercial visual communication design. Second, existing methods struggle to translate abstract semantic or style-preference requirements. Research on multi-technology coordination and parameter optimization is also limited. Third, empirical studies on requirement alignment across the five core commercial design scenarios are scarce, leaving model adaptability and robustness unverified. This study addresses these gaps by focusing on three core requirements in commercial visual communication design: semantic intent, style preference, and spatial layout rules. This study constructs an integrated framework that combines diffusion models, CLIP-based cross-modal alignment, and ControlNet constraints. Its effectiveness and robustness are validated through multi-scenario experiments and ablation studies, filling the gap in achieving precise requirement alignment through multi-technology coordination. Future research can explore deeper AI integration with specific design scenarios and the generation and evolution of cross-cultural design languages. In this context, precise and personalized requirement alignment is expected to become a central focus.

Research model

Core technical model and theoretical basis

This study selects Stable Diffusion v1.5, StyleGAN3, CLIP ViT-L/14, and ControlNet v1.1 as the core technical models. The selection is guided by three principles: alignment with practical commercial visual communication design requirements, technical feasibility for implementation, and experimental reproducibility. This study selects Stable Diffusion v1.5, StyleGAN3, CLIP ViT-L/14, and ControlNet v1.1 as the core technical models. The model selection follows three principles: alignment with practical needs in commercial visual communication design, feasibility of engineering implementation, and experimental reproducibility. It also considers both task suitability and continuity in technological evolution. The choice of Stable Diffusion v1.5 is based on several key considerations. First, this version is widely used in controllable generation research, with a mature ecosystem and well-developed plugins. Its integration with ControlNet and CLIP has been extensively validated, making it a standard baseline for controllable generation in visual design. Second, it has a moderate parameter scale and computational cost, which supports multi-scenario and multi-round comparative experiments while ensuring efficiency and reproducibility. Third, many recent high-quality studies in this domain continue to build upon Stable Diffusion v1.5 for controllable generation and layout-constrained research, providing strong academic benchmarking support18.

The models were also evaluated for their adaptability to design tasks and continuity with recent technological developments. The integrated framework constructed in this study—combining diffusion models, CLIP-based cross-modal alignment, and ControlNet spatial constraints—is not limited to specific model versions. It represents a generalizable paradigm for technical integration. Its core methods for requirement quantification, multi-constraint collaborative generation, and parametric style exploration can be directly applied to newer models, such as Stable Diffusion 3 and Sora, providing a methodological foundation for iterative improvements in AI-driven design workflows.

Diffusion model

Diffusion models generate high-quality images from random noise by simulating a stepwise denoising Markov chain19. They form the core generative backbone of this study’s framework. The T2I workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1, which clearly depicts the two main stages: forward diffusion and reverse denoising. The figure also shows the complete technical chain from textual requirement input to final visual output, highlighting key components such as text feature encoding, noise initialization, multi-step denoising iterations, and image generation. Importantly, it emphasizes how textual conditions guide noise prediction during reverse denoising, providing the technical foundation for integrating CLIP-based cross-modal alignment and ControlNet spatial constraints in subsequent stages of this study. The process consists of two stages: forward diffusion and reverse denoising. The forward diffusion process involves gradually adding Gaussian noise to the original data over a fixed number of steps20. Although newer versions, such as Stable Diffusion 3, further improve semantic understanding and visual quality, their level of open-source availability remains limited, and the adaptation of controllable generation components is not yet fully mature. Moreover, with respect to the core issues addressed in this study—namely design layout constraints and multi-condition collaborative generation—no fundamental differences have emerged compared with version 1.5. Mohd et al. (2025), in their review of controllable T2I diffusion models, note that version 1.5 remained the most widely used experimental backbone for research on spatial constraints and multi-condition injection21. Similarly, the multimodal generation benchmark study by Jiang et al. (2025) adopts this version as the standard reference model. Therefore, selecting Stable Diffusion v1.5 ensures both the rigor and comparability of the research findings and aligns with prevailing experimental practices in the field22. GANs

GANs are trained through an adversarial game between a generator (G) and a discriminator (D)25. In this study, StyleGAN3, the latest mature version in the StyleGAN series, is selected as the core model for style generation and interpolation. StyleGAN3 resolves the style bias present in earlier models and enables precise exploration of continuous style spectra. It remains one of the leading models for controllable style generation in the visual design domain. Cross-modal alignment-based generation paradigm

Cross-modal learning methods, represented by the CLIP model, are key to achieving high-quality T2I generation26,27. In this study, CLIP ViT-L/14 is selected as the core model for cross-modal alignment. It provides a mature framework for constructing a shared embedding space for image and text features, enabling precise extraction of textual semantic features and visual characteristics in visual communication design. Combined with Stable Diffusion v1.5 and ControlNet v1.1, CLIP forms a highly compatible technical ensemble that underpins semantic alignment between design requirements and generated outputs. Trained to align image and text features in a shared embedding space, CLIP has broad applications in computer vision and generative AI, including cross-modal retrieval, text-guided image generation and editing, visual classification, and zero-shot recognition. It serves as a fundamental module for achieving requirement matching and style control in T2I generation models. CLIP provides the critical capability for mapping design text requirements to visual features with high precision. AI-driven design workflow reconstruction

The AI-driven design workflow reconstructed in this study builds upon the traditional workflow of commercial visual communication design. It targets the concept sketch generation and multi-solution creative exploration stages, applying intelligent technological enhancements to form a human–AI collaborative workflow, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The workflow consists of three core stages: requirement analysis and prompt engineering, controllable generation and iterative optimization, and multi-solution generation with style exploration. At each stage, design requirements are precisely transformed into model inputs, and controllable generation is achieved through quantitative formulations. Equations (8)–(10) serve as the computational foundation for these stages, supporting requirement analysis, spatial constraints, and style expansion. The following sections detail the core objectives of each stage, the logic behind the applied formulas, and their technical contributions.

Stage 1: Requirement Analysis and Prompt Engineering.

The main objective of this stage is to decompose textual design requirements into quantifiable representations. Key concepts representing semantic intent, style preferences, and other core requirements are extracted, and their influence on generated outputs is quantified. This process converts abstract textual requirements into weighted prompt features that the model can recognize, establishing the basis for precise generation. It addresses the common limitation in traditional T2I workflows where prompt concepts are ambiguous and core requirements may be overlooked.

Equation (8) serves as the core computational method in this stage, enabling quantitative analysis of the influence of each prompt concept. where I(c) is the average influence score of concept c in the prompt; T is the total number of diffusion steps; L is the total number of UNet cross-attention layers; H and W are the height and width of the attention maps (spatial dimensions); and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Attention-Ma}}{{\mathrm{p}}_{t,l}}(c,h,w)$$\end{document} is the activation of the cross-attention map corresponding to concept ccc at time step t, layer l, and spatial position (h, w).

In practice, the design requirement text is first decomposed into multiple core concepts. The cross-attention mechanism of the diffusion model is then used to extract the activation values of each concept across all feature dimensions. These values are substituted into Eq. (8) to compute the average influence score for each concept. Finally, the scores are used to assign weights to the corresponding prompt tokens, forming a weighted prompt that serves as the model input.

The technical contribution of this stage lies in the use of Eq. (8) to quantify and refine requirement analysis. This approach overcomes the empirical limitations of manually crafted prompts, enabling the model to focus on the core concepts of the design requirement. By doing so, it enhances alignment between generated outputs and textual requirements from the outset.

This stage primarily implements the extraction, quantification, and precise transformation of semantic intent and style-preference requirements. It converts the abstract semantics and style needs described in text into prompt features with weighted values recognizable by the model.

Stage 2: Controllable Generation and Iterative Optimization.

The primary objective of this stage is to integrate structured spatial requirements, such as layout rules, on top of the weighted prompts generated in Stage 1. This ensures precise control over image composition, layout, and element positioning. It addresses common issues in AI-generated images, such as chaotic layouts or misplaced key elements, achieving dual control over semantic requirements and spatial constraints. Iterative optimization is then applied to align the generated results with commercial design layout standards.

Equation (9) serves as the core computational framework in this stage. It combines the generative capacity of the diffusion model with the spatial control capabilities of ControlNet, enabling accurate responses to structured layout requirements. The core function of this stage is to respond to spatial layout requirements. Using ControlNet’s spatial constraint mechanism, layout rules—including composition, element positioning, and structural alignment—are transformed into visual control signals. This allows precise spatial regulation of the generated outputs, ensuring alignment with commercial design standards.

In this stage, style-preference requirements are explored along multiple dimensions. By adjusting parameters and leveraging GAN-based style interpolation, the framework generates multiple variants and combinations under the target style while maintaining consistency with semantic intent and compliance with spatial layout rules.

Performance evaluation

1. Definition of Evaluation Metrics.

This study evaluates model performance using a combination of objective metrics and subjective assessments across multiple dimensions: generation quality, requirement alignment, workflow efficiency, robustness, style controllability, and image information content. The requirement alignment dimension explicitly corresponds to the three core requirements defined in this study: semantic intent, style preference, and spatial layout rules. By coordinating multiple metrics, the framework enables quantitative evaluation of how well the model responds to each requirement type. All objective metrics are computed as the average of three experimental runs, while subjective evaluations are conducted by ten professional visual designers with over five years of experience using a 5-point scale (1 = poor, 5 = excellent). The evaluators are recruited through invitations issued via a design industry association and two commercial design companies. A total of 10 in-service visual communication designers are enrolled, each with more than five years of experience in commercial projects and all participating voluntarily. During the evaluation, each designer is required to rate the generated images independently without knowledge of their origin, that is, without being informed whether the images are created by AI or by human designers. A five-point Likert scale is adopted for scoring. Each evaluation dimension is accompanied by detailed textual descriptions and example references (as defined in Table 4). To assess rating consistency, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) is calculated for each dimension. The results show that all ICC values exceed 0.85 (95% confidence interval), indicating “good” to “excellent” inter-rater reliability. In addition, the deviation of each evaluator’s scores from the overall mean is examined using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W > 0.7), further confirming rating consistency.

The core evaluation metrics and their definitions are summarized in Table 4. For quantifying information content in images with respect to different target objects, this study leverages the feature-mapping characteristics of cross-modal embedding spaces. Specifically: (1) Using CLIP’s bidirectional image–text embedding capability, generated images are first embedded into the shared feature space. (2) Images are then converted into structured textual descriptions via a pretrained T2I generation model. (3) Topic modeling (e.g., LDA) is applied to extract core topic words from the generated text. The richness of these topics and the completeness of target object descriptions serve as quantitative indicators of information content. (4) Additionally, independent target object features are extracted from images and analyzed via clustering. Metrics such as the number of valid objects and feature distinctiveness provide complementary information content measures. Finally, leveraging the alignment property of text and image in the shared embedding space, the semantic similarity between the generated textual description and the original design requirement text is calculated. This provides an additional measure of how well the image information content aligns with the design requirements.

This metric exhibits a clear input–output correspondence: semantically similar design text inputs produce image outputs with similar feature vectors in the shared space, ensuring consistent semantic transfer from requirement to visual result.

Subjective evaluation was performed by 10 professional designers with over five years of experience in commercial visual communication design using a 5-point scale. The presentation order of each design task is randomized to mitigate potential order effects. Scoring criteria are provided in advance to all evaluators in written form, including operational definitions and positive/negative reference examples for each evaluation dimension (e.g., requirement compliance and commercial usability). Discussion

The qualitative visualization results in Table 5, together with the quantitative evaluation, jointly demonstrate that the proposed AI-driven visual communication design model has reliable image generation capability and strong adaptability to commercial scenarios. Across all test scenarios, the FID remains below 30, while the PSNR exceeds 28 dB, indicating consistently high generation quality. In all evaluated scenarios, FID remained below 30 and PSNR exceeded 28 dB, indicating high generation quality. Among the tested contexts, the mobile UI scenario achieved the best performance (FID = 25.9, SimCLIP = 0.84), benefiting from clearly defined layout rules that align well with ControlNet constraints. In contrast, full brand campaign scenarios showed slightly lower performance (FID = 29.3, SimCLIP = 0.77), reflecting the inherent difficulty of aligning abstract, high-level design requirements with generated outputs. Single-solution generation by AI required only 3.2–6.7 min, yielding a design cycle reduction rate greater than 91%, while maintaining stable performance under ≤ 10% requirement noise and ≤ 40% data distribution shifts. Ablation studies further confirmed that ControlNet, CLIP, and the diffusion model respectively govern layout control, requirement matching, and the balance between generation quality and computational efficiency, demonstrating that all three components are indispensable for robust performance. Nevertheless, certain discrepancies with expectations were observed. For example, SimCLIP in full brand campaigns did not reach 0.8, highlighting CLIP’s limitations in aligning abstract semantic concepts, while under 20% text noise, SimCLIP dropped to 0.69 due to the semantic corruption of critical keywords. Compared to existing approaches, the proposed model achieves lower FID, higher SimCLIP, and supports style-mixed generation across five distinct scenarios, illustrating its clear advantages in both technical performance and practical applicability. Overall, this study constructed an integrated AI-driven framework addressing the core technical challenges of visual communication design and demonstrated its advantages in generation quality, requirement alignment, efficiency, and robustness. However, it is important to clarify the research boundaries. This study does not directly provide solutions for designer role transformation, design education integration, or ethical issues such as copyright and style homogenization. Instead, it establishes a practical human–AI collaborative workflow—comprising manual requirement analysis → AI multi-solution generation → human refinement and verification → final output and delivery—which serves as a paradigm for designer role transition. In this workflow, designers can shift their core value from basic visual rendering to interpreting abstract requirements, curating and optimizing AI-generated outputs, and conveying brand-specific emotional messaging. Furthermore, the study quantitatively validates AI’s advantages in efficiency and creative quality during the design ideation phase, providing empirical evidence to inform design education curricula that incorporate AI tools, prompt engineering, and cross-modal semantic mapping. Ethical considerations, including copyright ownership and design style homogenization, remain unresolved and require interdisciplinary research integrating industry practice and legal frameworks. The framework proposed here can serve as a standardized AI-generated reference for such investigations. The study also identified limitations in abstract semantic alignment (e.g., emotional conveyance in full-brand visual systems) and in semantic recognition under high-noise conditions. These findings point toward future iterations that integrate cutting-edge models such as Stable Diffusion 3 or OpenAI/Gemini, while retaining the current study’s core methodologies—requirement quantification, multi-constraint collaborative generation, and parametric style exploration—and incorporating enhanced semantic understanding and precise cross-modal alignment to further improve responsiveness to complex design requirements. This study adopts Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the backbone model. Although it has limitations in abstract semantic alignment, it remains consistent with common selection standards in current controllable generation research. Compared with newer models such as Stable Diffusion 3, version 1.5 offers greater advantages in terms of open-source accessibility, compatibility with ControlNet, and experimental reproducibility, making it more suitable for multi-scenario quantitative evaluation. Future work will integrate the enhanced semantic capabilities of newer model versions while retaining the core methods proposed in this study, including requirement quantification, multi-constraint collaborative generation, and style interpolation. This integration is expected to further improve the model’s adaptability to complex design requirements.

The innovations of this study lie primarily in the construction of an integrated framework combining a diffusion model, CLIP cross-modal alignment, and ControlNet constraints, which explicitly defines the synergistic roles of each component, filling a notable gap in existing research on technical integration for AI-assisted design. Additionally, through empirical validation across five core design scenarios, the study provides the first quantified evidence of the model’s robustness to ≤ 10% requirement noise and ≤ 40% data distribution shifts. These findings not only offer a novel methodology for generating diverse and creative design outputs but also deliver actionable guidance for reconstructing AI-driven design workflows, supporting both efficiency and quality in real-world commercial applications.

Conclusion

Research contribution

This study investigated the application of AI image generation technologies in commercial visual communication design, with key contributions in three areas: innovative technical integration, empirical validation across design scenarios, and quantification of operational patterns. Instead of simply applying existing tools, the study customized and integrated CLIP, ControlNet, and diffusion models for the design domain, addressing gaps in scenario-specific integration, multi-constraint collaborative generation, and quantitative evaluation.

First, an integrated framework combining diffusion models, CLIP, and ControlNet was developed. Unlike standardized toolkits, this framework defines module-specific coordination and customization strategies: CLIP text features were optimized and weighted to capture key design concepts, improving semantic alignment; ControlNet spatial constraints were adapted to layout rules across scenarios, enabling dual control of semantics and spatial arrangements; and the three modules were coordinated within a unified workflow, establishing a domain-specific integration paradigm for visual communication design.

Second, empirical studies and ablation experiments across five core design scenarios provided the first multidimensional quantitative reference for AI applications in this field. Results delineated stable operational boundaries under ≤ 10% requirement noise and ≤ 40% data distribution shifts. Module contributions were quantified: ControlNet primarily governed layout constraints, CLIP ensured semantic alignment, and the diffusion model balanced generation quality and efficiency, offering practical standards for deployment.

Third, the study identified and validated quantitative patterns in style-controllable AI generation. For five mainstream design styles, it showed that higher style-feature similarity improves fusion outcomes and established α = 0.5 as the optimal weight for style blending, providing a practical method for multi-style creative exploration. Additionally, the AI-driven design workflow was reconstructed, integrating technical modules with professional design processes and clearly defining human–AI collaboration nodes. This transforms general-purpose AI tools into intelligent assistants tailored to design workflows, offering a practical paradigm for workflow automation and enhancement.

Future works and research limitations

This study has several limitations. It focused on a limited set of design scenarios, did not explore international or cross-cultural adaptation, relied on a single designer cohort for subjective evaluation, and currently lacks dynamic design generation and source file export. While models such as Stable Diffusion v1.5 are suitable for current commercial applications, they are limited in abstract semantic alignment and high-fidelity generation compared with cutting-edge models like Stable Diffusion 3 and OpenAI/Gemini.

Future research will pursue three directions: (1) Integration with advanced models: Incorporate semantic enhancement from Stable Diffusion 3 and precise cross-modal alignment from OpenAI/Gemini to improve translation of abstract design intentions. (2) Cross-cultural and multi-stakeholder evaluation: Expand datasets to include diverse cultural contexts and leverage LLMs to optimize textual representation and decomposition of abstract requirements. (3) Practical workflow enhancement: Develop lightweight model versions for seamless integration with mainstream design tools, and incorporate dynamic design generation and source file export via open-source ecosystems, enhancing real-world applicability and operational value.
