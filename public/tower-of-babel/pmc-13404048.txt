Novel Use of Intrapericardial Surgicel Injection to Manage Refractory Cardiac Tamponade After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement: A Case Report
Authors: Kameel Kassab, Valentino Bianco, Christian Torres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPericardial effusions and cardiac tamponade are common complications occurring during transcatheter aortic valve replacement and could be life-threatening. Management of tamponade involves emergent pericardiocentesis and addressing the underlying cause. We present a case of refractory cardiac tamponade with continuous hemorrhagic extravasation into the pericardial space in a nonoperative patient, which was managed by injecting Surgicel hemostatic agent into the pericardial space leading to hemostasis and patient stabilization.Key Words: cardiac tamponade, case report, Surgicel, transcatheter aortic valve replacement

Introduction

Cardiac tamponade is a common complication occurring during transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). In a contemporary registry involving 1247 TAVR patients, cardiac tamponade occurred in 1.7% of these patients with the most common cause of tamponade being left ventricular (LV) wire perforation (48%), pacemaker-induced right ventricular perforation (38%), and annular rupture (14%).1 Tamponade most commonly can be managed with emergent pericardiocentesis; however, conversion to surgery may be required depending on the cause of tamponade and the ability to control the source of bleeding. Here, we report a novel technique to control nonresolving tamponade percutaneously in a patient who was not a candidate for salvage surgery.

Case Presentation

An 88-year-old woman with a history of recent cerebrovascular accident, severe symptomatic aortic stenosis, and severe single-vessel coronary artery disease presented for elective TAVR. The procedure was performed under conscious sedation, with the right common femoral artery as the primary access site and the left common femoral artery as the secondary access site. A SENTINEL cerebral protection system (Boston Scientific) was placed via the right radial artery, given her recent stroke of undetermined etiology. The patient had been on aspirin 81 mg daily and received systemic heparin during the procedure. A temporary transvenous pacemaker was placed by the anesthesiology team via the right internal jugular vein. A 23-mm SAPIEN S3 Ultra Resilia valve (Edwards Lifesciences) was advanced under fluoroscopy. Two additional milliliters were added to the inflation balloon, targeting 1% oversizing. Following valve deployment, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated a well-seated valve without paravalvular regurgitation, a mean gradient of 5 mm Hg, normal LV ejection fraction, and no pericardial effusion.

The valve delivery system was then removed, and a pigtail catheter was advanced into the left ventricle for invasive transvalvular pressure measurement, at which point the patient was noted to be hypotensive. Repeat TTE revealed a moderate pericardial effusion with right ventricular collapse, consistent with tamponade physiology. Emergent pericardial access was obtained using a micropuncture kit via subxiphoid approach, and an 8.5F pericardial drain was placed. Initial aspiration yielded 400 mL of bloody effusion, confirmed to be arterial by blood gas analysis, which was autotransfused via the left femoral sheath. The patient received protamine 40 mg for heparin reversal, and her activated clotting time decreased to 150 seconds. She was emergently intubated, and transesophageal echocardiography was performed, demonstrating normal TAVR valve function with no evidence of annular or ventricular rupture (Fig. 1, Supplemental Video 1). Left ventriculography showed no evidence of LV perforation (Supplemental Video 2), and aortic root angiography showed no evidence of aortic root or sinus of Valsalva rupture (Supplemental Video 3).

Transesophageal echocardiogram demonstrates moderate pericardial effusion.

Despite full heparin reversal, the pericardial effusion reaccumulated, requiring continuous suctioning via the pericardial drain, with an additional 600 mL of bloody effusion drained. Prothrombin complex concentrate (4F-PCC; Kcentra, CSL Behring LLC) was administered; however, drainage persisted. Transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated the early formation of intrapericardial coagulum (Fig. 2), yet hemorrhage continued. After approximately 3 hours of ongoing autotransfusion, the surgery team deemed the patient not a candidate for open surgical repair. At this point, 10 mL of Surgicel absorbable hemostat (Ethicon US, J&J MedTech company) was injected through the 8.5F pericardial drain into the pericardial space, followed by a 20-mL saline flush. Foam-like material was visualized layering within the pericardial space on substernal TTE views (Supplemental Video 4), and drainage from the pericardial drain ceased, with resolution of tamponade.

Transesophageal echocardiogram demonstrates layering of coagulum formation.

The patient was successfully extubated later that day. The pericardial drain was removed on postoperative day 1 following serial TTE demonstrating no recurrence of significant effusion. The remainder of the hospital course was complicated by acute kidney injury, which subsequently improved, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 4.

Discussion

Post-TAVR tamponade is managed with immediate pericardiocentesis. If bleeding persists, reversal of anticoagulation and identification of the bleeding source are essential. Annular tears or rupture frequently require surgical repair, although coil embolization may occasionally be performed. LV perforation frequently resolves spontaneously but occasionally requires surgical repair. Right ventricular pacemaker lead perforation is typically managed conservatively. Frequently, however, the exact source of bleeding cannot be delineated, as in our case, and is often attributed to a microtear at the level of the annulus or sinus of Valsalva, although a small LV wire perforation can also be missed. Here, we managed refractory intrapericardial hemorrhage after exhausting all available options by injecting Surgicel into the pericardial space.

Surgicel is commonly used to manage surgical bleeding and cardiac device pocket hematomas. It is an oxidized regenerated cellulose hemostatic agent that provides a scaffold for clot formation and is absorbed within 1 to 2 weeks (Supplemental Video 5). It is used to control diffuse capillary and venous bleeding when conventional methods are ineffective or impractical and is available in sheet, fibrillar, and powder forms.2 Surgicel is bioabsorbable and generally safe, but may cause foreign body reactions or granuloma formation. A particular concern with its use in confined spaces such as the pericardial space is that its expansion upon contact with blood may potentially cause compressive symptoms. Additionally, long-term effects including theoretical concern for pericardial constriction could occur; therefore, it should be used with caution and in small amounts. Its use for controlling refractory tamponade after TAVR has not been formally described in the literature, although it has been employed by some operators in refractory cases. The use of other has been briefly described in small case series. The use of SURGIFLO Hemostatic Matrix, a gelatin-thrombin–based product, has been described in a recent case report for the management of LV perforation.3 Intrapericardial thrombin injection has been described in short case series of 5 patients with overall favorable results.4 The use of pericardial tissue adhesive or glue has also been described in cases of cardiac rupture in a series of 19 patients with only 5 patients surviving.5

Conclusion

Refractory cardiac tamponade after TAVR may occur without an identifiable source. This case highlights the potential role of intrapericardial injection of oxidized regenerated cellulose (Surgicel) hemostatic agent as a rescue strategy for refractory hemopericardium with cardiac tamponade following structural cardiac interventions such as TAVR. In the setting of ongoing pericardial bleeding despite prompt recognition and implementation of established management protocols, the administration of a readily available topical hemostatic agent through the existing pericardial drain was associated with effective, prompt bleeding control and clinical stabilization. Although this approach remains off-label, it may represent a simple, accessible, and potentially life-saving adjunct in carefully selected patients when conventional therapies fail. Further experience and systematic evaluation in a larger cohort of patients with longer follow-up duration are warranted to better define its safety profile, efficacy, and role within contemporary management algorithms.
