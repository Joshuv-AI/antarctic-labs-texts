Erythrocytic hemodynamics of human choriocapillaris observed by laser speckle flowgraphy: A case report
Authors: Hideo Akiyama, Junki Hoshino, Kazuma Saito, Shoji Kishi, Hidetaka Matsumoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a case in which the hemodynamics of the choriocapillaris (CC) could be observed by tracking the blood flowing through a short posterior ciliary artery (SPCA) using laser speckle flowgraphy (LSFG).ObservationA 28-year-old man with no significant medical history had a normal left eye with a refraction of −8 diopters. Best corrected visual acuity was 20/20 in the left eye. The prototype LSFG was able to visualize the hemodynamics of the CC in the video based on the flow of blood in the SPCA near the fovea, a location where the vortex veins did not overlap.Conclusions and importanceLSFG showed the precapillary arterioles of the CC do not appear to be terminal arterioles, but instead form local networks by anastomosis. The branches of the SPCA form the outer frame of the mosaic, and each mosaic does not seem to be an independent circulatory unit. This should help to improve our understanding of the perfusion of the outer retina in both health and disease conditions.Keywords: Choriocapillaris, Laser speckle flowgraphy, Short posterior ciliary artery

Introduction

Use of the laser speckle flowgraphy (LSFG) device enables non-invasive, two-dimensional mapping of the blood flow in the fundus of the eye, a technique that is based on the laser speckle phenomenon.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 A diode laser (830 nm) is projected on the moving red blood cells in the bloodstream, which then generates a speckle pattern from the reflected light, and from which the blood flow value is then calculated. Changes in the blood flow map during a single heartbeat rhythm are depicted by consecutive blood flow maps. Although utilization of LSFG makes it possible to observe the blood flow state of the retina and choroid in real time and in 2D images, this technique cannot distinguish between the arteries and veins. Using a prototype LSFG to visualize the hemodynamics of the choriocapillaris (CC), we were able to observe the short posterior ciliary artery (SPCA), which penetrates the sclera near the fovea without any overlapping with the vortex veins. The field of view of the commercially available LSFG (Softcare Co., Ltd., Fukuoka, Japan) is 8.6 × 4.6 mm, whereas the prototype of the narrow-field LSFG used in the present study has a field of view of 3.5 × 1.8 mm with 360 × 750 pixels (Fig. 1).

Case report

A 28-year-old man with no significant medical history and with a normal eye (left eye) was examined. His best corrected visual acuity was 20/20 and his refraction was −8.0 D. Intraocular pressure was 18 mmHg. His systolic and diastolic blood pressures were 126 mmHg and 81 mmHg, respectively. A muscarinic antagonist, 0.4 % tropicamide (Mydrin P; Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) was used for pupil dilatation. There were no abnormalities in the anterior segment or the intermediate opacities. As shown in Fig. 2A, the fundus showed a leopard-spot pattern, and the choroidal vessels could be seen in the fundus photograph (Canon CX-1; Canon, Tokyo, Japan). The en face image of the SS-OCT obtained by using a PLEX Elite 9000® system (Carl Zeiss, Dublin, CA, USA) revealed that there was a SPCA that penetrated the sclera near the fovea (white arrow). Furthermore, a 20 mm horizontal B-mode OCT (DRI OCT-1 Triton; Topcon Corp, Tokyo, Japan) image showed that the SPCA penetrated the sclera and entered the choroid near the fovea (Fig. 2C). The en face image obtained by segmentation that was more towards the Bruch's membrane than seen in Fig. 2B indicated that this SPCA was running towards the temporal area between the upper and lower vortex veins (Fig. 2D). The whole fundus image of the en face OCT revealed the watershed zone between the upper and lower vortex veins was distinct, and that the SPCA did not exhibit much overlap with the vortex veins (Fig. 2E). The false-color presentation of the composite map included the fovea and the starting point for the SPCA that penetrated the sclera (Fig. 3A). The panorama map of the posterior pole was created by combining fragments of the local composite maps (Fig. 3B). The composite map, which is indicated by the white broken line (Fig. 3B), presents the course of the SPCA that penetrated the sclera near the fovea (Fig. 4A). The map was created by averaging one heartbeat and then arranging it every 1/16th of a second, as shown in Fig. 4B. In this image, we can see the red mesh structure, which represents the fast blood flow, as it becomes thicker during systole and thinner during diastole. In addition, during diastole, the inside of the meshwork exhibited a cool color. The LSFG animation showed that the blood vessels that form the outer rim of the lobules look like the branches of the SPCA (Supplement 1). Thus, precapillary arterioles appeared not to be terminal arterioles, but instead formed local networks by anastomosis.

Discussion

In 1974, Hayreh performed fluorescein angiography (FA) on the eyes of monkeys with high intraocular pressure, and observed that the fluorescent dye oozed out from the center of the mosaic and then spread throughout the mosaic.6 The outer part of the mosaic had a dark outer frame, and each mosaic appeared to be an independent circulatory unit.7 Observations of the CC using the flat preparation previously reported by Torczynski et al. at the same time also supported these findings.8 The CC from the posterior pole to the slightly temporal side was made up of a collection of units with a diameter of 300–400 μm, which are called the lobular pattern. There was a round opening in the center of this lobule, from which the capillaries radiated outwards, and there were large blood vessels in the outer frame of the lobule. It was assumed that the arterial blood flowed in through the central opening (fenestration), filled the lobule, and then flowed out into the veins that formed the outer frame.

To investigate the circulatory dynamics of the CC, Hanyuda and Akiyama et al. performed FA and indocyanine green fluorescence angiography (IA) on the eyes of monkeys with high intraocular pressure.9 The experiment was carried out using a Constellation 25G System (Alcon Surgical, Fort Worth, TX, USA), with the intraocular pressure raised to 60 mmHg by infusion. When the fluorescent dye started to flow in at the very beginning of the infusion, the C-shaped arterioles appeared in IA, while for the FA, there were many fluorescent spots that appeared 13 seconds later, and which formed a mosaic with a dark outer edge. When the images of the FA and IA were superimposed during the very early stages, results showed that the C-shaped arterioles observed in IA corresponded to the dark outer edge of the mosaic observed in FA. These findings suggested that the outer frame of the lobule was an arteriole, and that blood filled the inside of the lobule from there. This was contrary to the conventional theory that states the center is an artery. Furthermore, the authors also reported that the fluorescence observed inside the mosaic in FA was thought to be free fluorescein that had not bound to the albumin that leaked through the fenestration.

In the present patient, the posterior pole CC exhibited a mosaic pattern based on the dynamics of the red blood cells that were obtained by LSFG, with the branches of the SPCA forming the outer frame of the mosaic. As the branches of the SPCA formed the outer frame of the mosaic, this indicated that each mosaic was not an independent circulatory unit. As the blood initially flowed into the mosaic from the branches proximal to the trunk of the SPCA, the filling of the mosaic progressed from the proximal to the distal region in the SPCA. This was almost identical to the filling pattern reported by Flower in high-speed ICG fluorescence angiograms.10

Using vascular casts and scanning electron microscopy techniques, Fryczkowski was successfully able to classify arteries and veins based on the shape of the endothelial cell nuclear indentations in the choroidal vascular mold.11 Anatomically, this method revealed that the lobular appearance of the CC was present only in part of the posterior pole. Furthermore, Fryczkowski also found that there were one or more collecting venules in the center of 86 % of the anatomic lobules, while the central feeding arterioles were observed in 14 %.

In 1994, McLeod and Lutty used combined alkaline phosphatase histochemical staining with flat embedding in glycol methacrylate to study the choroidal vascular pattern in the choroid from postmortem eyes.12 They reported that the posterior region revealed a lobular pattern with a centrally located venule along with arterioles that were located at the lobular periphery.

Several diseases exist that are caused by circulatory insufficiency in the choroid, which nourishes the outer layers of the retina. Accurately understanding the anatomy is extremely important when considering various macular diseases. We would like to think that the current information should help in our overall understanding of the perfusion of the outer retina in both health and disease conditions.

The limitations of this study are as follows. Although using LSFG enables observation of retinal and choroidal blood flow in 2D images, it is extremely rare to observe the SPCA supplying the macula, penetrating the sclera, and reaching the CC. The present case is thought to be due to the choroid being thin as a result of high myopia, which coincidentally then allowed the SPCA to be observed. In contrast, fundus examination of the fellow eye (−7.25 D high myopic eye) revealed no abnormalities such as a posterior staphyloma, lacquer cracks, or CNV, similar to the findings in the left eye. However, the SPCA, as described in this case report, was not observed. Since this study is based on a single case, it cannot be expected to provide complete verification. Furthermore, we cannot prove whether the LSFG pattern remained stable across multiple heart cycles, nor can we demonstrate the repeatability and reproducibility.

We recognize that the evaluation of a normal eye may differ from, or be less complex than, the evaluation of an eye with abnormalities. The hemodynamic observations of the CC presented in this study were made in normal eyes. However, further research is required to elucidate the clinical significance of these findings in eyes with disease, in which pathological changes may alter hemodynamic patterns and complicate interpretation.

Conclusions

By using LSFG with a narrow field of view, we were able to observe the hemodynamics of the CC by following the course of the SPCA, which does not overlap with the venules.
