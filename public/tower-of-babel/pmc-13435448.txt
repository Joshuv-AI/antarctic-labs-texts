Integrative bioinformatics and machine learning reveal an association of LTF and MMP8 with systemic inflammation and lung injury
Authors: Jutong He, Yanhong Wei, Yingjie Wu, Wen Wang, Ming Xu, Xuefeng Zhou, Shaoping Zhu, Xinyi Li, Hexiao Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute lung injury and its severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), represent life-threatening conditions with high mortality rates. Since the lung is the primary target organ in systemic inflammatory conditions like sepsis, this study aimed to identify key regulatory genes by analyzing systemic inflammation-related transcriptomic data, and to explore their roles in lung injury.MethodsWe performed bioinformatic screening on the GSE32707 dataset (systemic inflammation patients vs. controls) using differential expression analysis, WGCNA, and machine learning. Functional enrichment, PPI network, immune infiltration, ceRNA network, and molecular docking analyses were conducted. Validation was carried out using human samples, cell-based assays, and murine lung injury models.ResultsWe identified 506 differentially expressed genes, predominantly enriched in immune-related pathways. Machine learning algorithms prioritized lactoferrin (LTF) and matrix metalloproteinase-8 (MMP8) as key genes. Co-IP assays demonstrated a physical association between LTF and MMP8. A predicted ceRNA network suggested post-transcriptional regulation of LTF, while molecular docking indicated binding potential of LTF/MMP8 with methylprednisolone. Experimental validation confirmed that both genes are upregulated during lung injury and can be modulated by anti-inflammatory treatment.ConclusionLTF and MMP8 play critical roles in lung injury, potentially serving as molecular connectors between systemic inflammation and pulmonary damage, and represent promising therapeutic targets for further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03704-4.

Introduction

Lung injury is a complex condition involving diverse etiologies and pathological processes, frequently initiated by infections, trauma, inhalation of harmful substances, and other factors. It clinically presents as severe pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage, with a high mortality rate [1, 2]. Despite considerable advances in lung injury research over the recent decades, effective targeted therapies remain limited. Thus, identifying novel therapeutic targets and mechanisms is critical for improving patient outcomes.

Clinically, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), which represents a severe form of lung injury [3], is frequently triggered by systemic inflammatory conditions such as systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and sepsis. The lung is the most frequently affected organ in sepsis [4–6], and the inflammatory mediators released during SIRS can directly drive acute lung injury pathogenesis [7]. The profound inflammatory and injurious processes in ARDS represent an amplified model ideal for identifying central regulatory genes and pathways that are likely to be relevant across various etiologies of lung injury. Consequently, identifying early key molecules that become active during systemic inflammation and can predict or drive lung injury onset is crucial for early warning and intervention for ARDS.

However, identifying which patients with systemic inflammation will progress to lung injury prior to the manifestation of clear clinical signs of ARDS, such as hypoxemia, remains profoundly challenging. Direct and repeated lung tissue sampling from critically ill patients is nearly infeasible in clinical practice. In contrast, peripheral blood represents a routine and easily accessible sample in clinical settings, and its transcriptomic profile can sensitively reflect systemic inflammatory status and disease severity [8, 9]. We hypothesize that within the blood of patients across the systemic inflammatory spectrum, there exist biomarkers that can predict the onset of lung injury. These molecules emerge early in the systemic circulation and actively participate in distal lung tissue pathology.

With the rapid advancement of bioinformatics and artificial intelligence (AI), largely driven by advances in machine learning, we can now leverage these technologies to comprehensively analyze large-scale gene expression and protein interaction data, thereby identifying key molecules and signaling pathways [10–12]. In recent years, these technologies have been increasingly applied in lung injury research. For instance, Tao et al. demonstrated that exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells alleviate sepsis-induced ALI via miR-125b-5p, highlighting the critical role of non-coding RNAs and macrophage pyroptosis [13]. From a clinical prediction standpoint, Wang et al. identified early plasma proteomic biomarkers for predicting ARDS risk following cardiopulmonary bypass through a prospective nested cohort study [14]. Additionally, Levy et al. used electronic health record data to develop and externally validate a machine learning model retrospectively identifying ARDS patients, highlighting its potential for clinical decision support [15]. These pioneering studies have established the value of data-driven computational approaches for deciphering the complexity of lung injury from both mechanistic and clinical prediction perspectives. While these investigations provide invaluable insights into the mechanisms and clinical identification of lung injury, the pathological process remains highly complex, involving interactions among numerous genes and pathways. Therefore, this study aims to integrate WGCNA and multiple machine learning algorithms to identify key genes from datasets, followed by experimental validation to elucidate their significance in the pathogenesis of lung injury.

Based on this, we propose a central hypothesis that certain key molecules present in the blood of patients with systemic inflammation not only reflect the systemic inflammatory status but may also serve as “molecular bridges” that directly participate in and drive the pathological processes of distal lung injury. In this study, we integrated bioinformatics analyses with machine learning algorithms. We utilized a publicly available dataset (GSE32707) containing blood samples from patients with sepsis and SIRS. Using R software, we performed data preprocessing, differential gene expression analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, and support vector machine (SVM) analysis to identify and prioritize key genes associated with systemic inflammation. We subsequently conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, Disease Ontology (DO) enrichment analysis, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction, immune infiltration analysis, competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network analysis, and molecular docking to elucidate their potential molecular mechanisms. Finally, we validated the results using human samples, cell lines, and mouse models of lung injury. To bridge systemic inflammation with target organ injury, this study first used bioinformatics to analyze the GSE32707 blood transcriptomic dataset from GEO database, aiming to identify circulating key genes associated with systemic inflammation. We then experimentally validated their roles and mechanistic relevance in lung injury. We hypothesized that this two-stage approach, which begins with identifying blood-based biomarkers of systemic inflammation and proceeds to validate them in lung tissue, could identify and prioritize central hub genes that not only reflect systemic dysregulation but also demonstrate causal involvement and therapeutic relevance in lung injury.

Materials and methods

Data source and preprocessing

The GSE32707 dataset was downloaded from the NCBI GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). For bioinformatic discovery, we defined a cohort representing a spectrum of systemic inflammatory conditions, encompassing patients with SIRS, sepsis, and sepsis plus ARDS. This approach allowed us to identify genes central to systemic inflammation, with the subsequent experimental validation focusing on their role in the lung—the most frequently affected organ in sepsis. Untreated volunteers served as the control group. Gene expression data preprocessing was performed using R version 4.3.2 [16]. Raw expression values were first transformed using log₂(x + 1) to approximate normal distribution and reduce heteroskedasticity. Subsequently, quantile normalization was applied using the normalizeBetweenArrays() function from the limma package to eliminate technical variations and ensure inter-sample comparability. The normalized data was then used for subsequent analysis.

To further validate the expression of key genes in lung tissue, we incorporated an additional independent dataset GSE216943 from a murine model of LPS-induced acute lung injury. The FPKM values for Ltf and Mmp8 were extracted from each sample, transformed using log2(FPKM + 1) to approximate a normal distribution, and then compared between the LPS and control groups (Supplementary Table S1). For the GSE216943 dataset, the control group (labeled “NC” in the original metadata) is referred to as “Con” in this study.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the “limma” package in R, identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the systemic inflammation group and the control group [17]. The screening criteria were |log2(Fold Change)| > 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05.

GO, KEGG, and DO enrichment analyses

To elucidate the functional roles of the DEGs, we conducted a GO enrichment analysis using the GO database (accessed on June 16, 2024). Genes were categorized into three categories: Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). Enrichment of GO terms was assessed in R using the hypergeometric test, with multiple testing correction applied via the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Terms with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For a deeper understanding of the functional pathways associated with the DEGs, we conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. DEGs were mapped to KEGG pathways, and enrichment significance was evaluated using the hypergeometric test in R. Pathways were considered significantly enriched with adjusted P-value < 0.05 [17, 18]. Additionally, we performed Disease Ontology (DO) enrichment analysis to explore potential associations between the DEGs and human diseases. Using R, we mapped the DEGs to DO terms and conducted enrichment analysis with hypergeometric distribution, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Disease terms were considered significantly enriched with the adjusted P-value < 0.05.

WGCNA

WGCNA was performed using the “WGCNA” package in R to identify gene co-expression modules associated with systemic inflammation [19]. A co-expression network was constructed based on pairwise gene correlations, gene modules were identified using the dynamic tree cut algorithm, and the correlations between module eigengenes and the phenotype were analyzed.

Machine learning model construction

We utilized DEGs and key module genes from WGCNA as candidate genes to construct predictive models using LASSO regression, and SVM learning methods. These models were used to screen for key genes with high predictive value for systemic inflammation [20–23]. We assessed the predictive performance of the models using five-fold cross-validation. The overlapping genes between the LASSO‑selected and SVM‑RFE‑selected gene sets were defined as the final key candidates.

PPI network construction

We constructed a PPI network for the proteins encoded by the key genes using the STRING database [24].

Immune infiltration analysis

We assessed the immune cell infiltration in the samples using the CIBERSORT algorithm [25, 26]. We analyzed the correlation between key genes and immune cell infiltration to explore the role of key genes in the immune microenvironment of systemic inflammation.

Single-gene GSEA analysis

To explore the functional relevance of LTF, patients in the systemic inflammation group were divided into high- and low-expression subgroups based on the median expression level of LTF. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R. The GO and KEGG gene set were downloaded from the MSigDB database on June 16, 2024. All genes were ranked by |log₂FC| in descending order, and pathways with P < 0.05 were considered significantly associated with LTF expression.

ceRNA network prediction

We employed three databases (miRDB, miRanda, and TargetScan) to predict potential target miRNAs of the key gene. The intersection of the predictions was taken as the candidate miRNA set. Next, we used the spongeScan database to predict lncRNAs associated with these target miRNAs. Finally, a ceRNA network was visualized using Cytoscape [27].

Molecular docking prediction

Molecular docking prediction was performed to predict the binding affinity between the key gene-encoded proteins and methylprednisolone, a common drug used in inflammation treatment, to assess their potential as drug targets. Docking simulations were carried out using the “Docking Mode” workflow in PyMOL (Version 2.5.4), which employs the AutoDock Vina engine [28] for all core calculations. The crystal structures of LTF and MMP8 were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) under accession codes 1U62 and 1A85, respectively. Prior to docking, the protein structures were preprocessed, including the addition of hydrogen atoms, assignment of partial charges, and energy minimization. The structure of the methylprednisolone ligand was also subjected to geometry optimization and energy minimization. The binding site was defined around the known active region of each protein. A grid box with dimensions of 20 × 40 × 40 Å was set, with a maximum of 20 binding modes and an energy range of 5 kcal/mol. The resulting docking poses were ranked and evaluated based on the predicted binding affinity computed by the Vina scoring function. It is important to emphasize that the results of this molecular docking are purely computational predictions. They are intended solely to provide a testable hypothesis for future experimental validation.

Validation with human specimens

To validate the roles of the key genes lactoferrin (LTF) and matrix metalloproteinase-8 (MMP8) identified through bioinformatics analysis in lung injury, we first used Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) to measure the expression levels of LTF and MMP8 in human serum samples. We collected lung tissue specimens from the explanted lungs of lung transplant patients as the lung injury group, while the control group consisted of patients with benign lung tumors without pulmonary inflammation or other complications. All patients signed informed consent forms, and the study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (approval number: 2023138K). We then extracted total RNA and proteins from these lung tissue specimens. Specific primers for LTF and MMP8 genes were designed for real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) using the SYBR Green qPCR kit. The relative gene expression levels were analyzed using the ΔΔCt method, with β-Actin as the internal control. For protein expression analysis, Western Blot was conducted. Antibodies against LTF (Proteintech, 10933-1-AP) and MMP8 (ABclonal, A1963) were used.

Validation in cell injury models

We selected the human lung epithelial (BEAS-2B) and mouse macrophage (RAW264.7) cell lines [29, 30] for further validation. The cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The experimental groups were as follows: Control group (PBS); Experimental group (Lipopolysaccharide, LPS); Treatment group 1: LPS + Methylprednisolone (MP) 1 µmol/L; Treatment group 2: LPS + MP 5 µmol/L; Treatment group 3: LPS + MP 10 µmol/L. The LPS doses were: BEAS-2B cells, 5 µg/mL; RAW264.7 cells, 1 µg/mL [31–33]. The treatment duration was 24 h for BEAS-2B and 6 h for RAW264.7. For groups requiring MP, it was added two hours before the LPS. Total RNA and proteins for LTF and MMP8 were extracted from the treated cells for subsequent gene and protein expression analyses.

Validation in mouse lung injury model

Healthy C57BL/6 mice were used according to the guidelines of the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee. The mice were randomly divided into three groups (N = 5 per group): control group (PBS), LPS group, and LPS + MP group. Lung injury model was induced by intratracheal LPS injection, while the control group received an equivalent volume of sterile PBS. The LPS dose was 8 mg/kg, administered via 50 µL intratracheal instillation, and the mice were sacrificed 6 h later for sample collection [32, 33]. In the LPS + MP group, MP was administered intraperitoneally (5 mg/kg) 2 h prior to LPS challenge [33, 34]. Blood samples were collected for the detection of LTF and MMP8 by ELISA. Total RNA and proteins were extracted from the lung tissues for subsequent gene and protein expression analyses. Additionally, to assess the pathological changes in lung tissue, part of the lung tissue was fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The pathological changes in the lung tissue were observed under a microscope. Immunohistochemical staining was also performed for LTF and MMP8. This study was approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (approval number ZN2022115).

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

To investigate the interaction between LTF and MMP8, co-immunoprecipitation assays were performed in HEK293T and BEAS-2B cells under exogenous and endogenous conditions, respectively. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding HA-LTF and Flag-MMP8. After 48 h, cells were lysed with IP lysis buffer. The supernatants were incubated with either anti-HA (ABclonal, AE008) or anti-Flag (ABclonal, AE092) antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads for 5 h to capture immune complexes. After thorough washing, bound proteins were eluted with SDS loading buffer. Both total protein lysates and immunoprecipitated samples were analyzed by Western blot using anti-Flag and anti-HA antibodies. To detect the endogenous interaction, BEAS-2B cells were first stimulated with LPS for 24 h before collection. Total protein lysates were prepared using the same lysis buffer. Immunoprecipitation was performed with an anti-LTF antibody (Proteintech, 10933-1-AP) following the same procedure as the exogenous assay, using Protein A/G magnetic beads for capture. Finally, co-precipitated endogenous MMP8 protein was detected by Western blot analysis using an anti-MMP8 antibody. To eliminate potential interference from the heavy and light chains of the immunoprecipitating antibody, Western blot detection was performed using a conformation-specific secondary antibody (Vazyme, RA1008-01).

Statistical analysis

Image J was used for densitometric quantification of Western blot bands and immunohistochemical staining. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22 software, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for pairwise comparisons. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Differential gene expression analysis and WGCNA screening

The overall data screening workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1A. Differential gene expression analysis identified 506 DEGs (471 upregulated,35 downregulated). The distribution of DEGs is visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps (Fig. 1B–C). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that the DEGs were predominantly enriched in immune-related biological processes, including leukocyte-mediated immunity and neutrophil extracellular trap formation (Fig. 1D–E, Figure S1A-B). DO enrichment analysis further showed significant associations with inflammatory and immune-related diseases (Fig. 1F, Figure S1C). An appropriate threshold was selected based on the scale-free topology fit index and mean connectivity (Fig. 1G). Following gene clustering and module division (Fig. 1H), WGCNA identified 13 gene co-expression modules (Fig. 1I, Figure S1D–G, Figure S2). Among these, the grey module exhibited the strongest correlation with the systemic inflammation phenotype and contained 360 genes. Intersection of these genes with the 506 DEGs yielded 24 co-expressed genes, as shown in the Venn diagram (Fig. 1J). Detailed clustering and screening procedures are provided in Supplementary Figure S1G–H.

Machine learning analysis

LASSO regression of the 24 DEGs identified 12 significant genes (Fig. 1K, Figure S1I). During recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (SVM-RFE-CV), the relationship between the number of features and the root mean square error (RMSE) was evaluated, and the optimal predictive model was determined to contain 22 features (Fig. 1L). Intersection of the SVM-RFE-selected genes and LASSO-identified genes yielded two hub genes: LTF and MMP8 (Fig. 1M). The five‑fold cross‑validation performance of LASSO is shown in Figure S3A and Table S2, with a mean AUC of 0.861 (95% CI: 0.794–0.922). SVM‑RFE achieved a mean AUC of 0.692 (95% CI: 0.590–0.785) (Figure S3B and Table S2). The intersection model showed a balanced performance with a mean AUC of 0.769 (95% CI: 0.670–0.859) (Figure S3C and Table S2). Subsequent validation confirmed significantly higher expression of LTF and MMP8 in the systemic inflammation group of the human dataset GSE32707 (Fig. 2A). Consistent with this, analysis of an independent murine model (GSE216943) of LPS-induced acute lung injury remarkably showed a significant upregulation of the mouse orthologs Ltf and Mmp8 (Fig. 2B). Additionally, both genes demonstrated strong diagnostic performance in systemic inflammation (Fig. 2C–D).

To further investigate their expression patterns, we performed a sensitivity analysis comparing patients with sepsis alone to those with sepsis plus ARDS in GSE32707. The results showed that the expression levels of LTF and MMP8 did not further increase in patients with sepsis complicated by ARDS compared to those with sepsis alone (Figure S3D, S3E). These observations suggest that the upregulation of LTF and MMP8 may represent an early event in the course of systemic inflammation. Their marked elevation already at the sepsis stage may indicate a critical transitional phase from systemic inflammation to substantive lung injury.

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune infiltration analysis showed that, compared with the control group, the systemic inflammation group exhibited increased proportions of neutrophils, monocytes, and naïve CD4⁺ T cells, and decreased proportions of naïve B cells, plasma cells, activated dendritic cells, activated mast cells, and eosinophils (Fig. 2E, Figure S4A). Correlation analysis indicated strong negative associations between naïve B cells and both memory B cells and neutrophils, whereas a strong positive correlation was observed with eosinophils, suggesting complex interactions among immune cell populations (Fig. 2F).

We performed immune infiltration analysis on cells from both high and low LTF expression groups and observed a significant association between LTF expression levels and specific immune feature scores. The macrophage score was significantly higher in the high LTF expression group compared to the low expression group. In contrast, scores for cytolytic activity, CD8⁺ T cells, helper T cells, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) were lower in the high LTF expression group (Figure S4B). LTF expression correlated with M0 macrophages, resting mast cells, follicular helper T cells, neutrophils, and resting NK cells (Fig. 2G, Figure S5A). More immune cell types were correlated with MMP8 expression (Fig. 2H, Figure S5B). Both molecules were associated with M0 macrophages, resting NK cells, and resting mast cells, suggesting their involvement in regulating the immune microenvironment.

GSEA of LTF expression

GSEA revealed that LTF expression was significantly associated with ribosome-related GO terms, suggesting a potential role in protein synthesis (Fig. 2I). KEGG pathway analysis further showed that LTF expression was associated with multiple immune-related pathways, including Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 2J).

Validation with human specimens

Levels of LTF and MMP8 were significantly elevated in serum samples from lung injury patients compared to controls (Fig. 3A). In lung tissue specimens obtained from patients with benign lesions (controls) and lung injury, immunoblotting confirmed markedly higher expression of LTF and MMP8 in injured tissues (Fig. 3B–C). qRT-PCR further validated these findings (Fig. 3D).

Validation in cell models

In BEAS-2B and RAW264.7 cell models of lung injury (Fig. 3E), western blot and qRT-PCR analyses showed that LPS stimulation significantly upregulated LTF and MMP8 expression (Fig. 3F–K). MP treatment modulated their expression, supporting their important roles in lung injury (Fig. 3F–K).

Validation in mouse lung injury model

In LPS-induced lung injury mice (Fig. 4A–B), serum and lung tissue levels of LTF and MMP8 were significantly higher than in controls (Fig. 4C–F). Immunohistochemical staining confirmed elevated protein levels of LTF and MMP8 in lung tissues after LPS exposure (Fig. 4G–H). MP treatment reduced their expression, indicating that the modulation of LTF and MMP8 might be involved in the therapeutic effects of MP.

Protein interaction and regulatory networks of LTF and MMP8

We analyzed the 3D structures of LTF and MMP8 (Fig. 5A-B and Supplementary Figure S6). Computational structural modeling (AlphaFold) suggested the presence of a potential interaction interface between the two proteins. Protein-protein interaction network analysis further indicated a potential association between LTF and MMP8, with several shared functional partners (Fig. 5C). To validate this prediction, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays. Co-IP assays demonstrated that LTF and MMP8 co-precipitate under exogenous overexpression conditions in HEK293T cells (Fig. 5D). More importantly, endogenous Co-IP in LPS-stimulated BEAS-2B cells demonstrated that LTF and MMP8 can form a complex under pathophysiologically relevant inflammatory conditions (Fig. 5E). Together, these data support the existence of a physical association between LTF and MMP8.

Additionally, molecular docking predicted binding affinities of -5.1 kcal/mol for LTF and − 6.4 kcal/mol for MMP8 with methylprednisolone (Fig. 5G-H). These computational predictions suggest a potential physical association that warrants further investigation; however, experimental validation is required to determine whether this interaction contributes to the anti-inflammatory effects of MP.

Discussion

This study employed a research pathway from systemic level to target organ: we began with bioinformatic analysis of blood transcriptomes from patients with systemic inflammation to screen for common key molecular signals, rather than starting directly with injured lung tissues. We hypothesized that genes showing consistent dysregulation in blood might represent core hubs driving damage in distal organs. Based on this approach, we successfully identified LTF and MMP8 as candidate key genes. This discovery was further substantiated through differential gene expression analysis, WGCNA, and machine learning algorithms, which confirmed their significant upregulation in systemic inflammation and indicated their potential roles in inflammatory response [35, 36]. Subsequent experimental validation using independent clinical specimens and murine lung injury models consistently demonstrated significant upregulation of both LTF and MMP8 in injured lung tissues and associated blood samples. This research pathway reveals LTF and MMP8 as molecular bridges connecting systemic inflammation to lung injury.

GO, KEGG, and DO enrichment analyses demonstrated that the differentially expressed genes screened from the systemic inflammation cohort were significantly enriched in pathways related to inflammatory response, immune regulation, and extracellular matrix organization (Fig. 1, Figure S1). The functional relevance of these findings is supported by existing literature. LTF is a multifunctional protein widely present in various body fluids, with antibacterial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties [37, 38]. Most research on LTF has focused on its role as a nutritional protein supplement for infants [39, 40]. In studies of various diseases, LTF has been shown to have significant protective effects, particularly in infectious and inflammatory diseases [41]. LTF exerts its effects by binding to iron ions, thereby inhibiting the growth of pathogens and modulating the host’s immune response to reduce inflammation and tissue damage [42]. In the context of lung injury, the protective role of LTF has gained increasing attention, though its precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood [43]. In this study, LTF was identified through screening of blood samples from patients with systemic inflammation, and its significant upregulation was confirmed in lung tissues and blood samples from both patients and murine models with lung injury. These findings provide new evidence supporting the role of LTF in pulmonary inflammatory processes. MMP8 is a neutrophil-derived protease involved in extracellular matrix degradation, regulation of inflammatory responses, and tissue remodeling [44, 45]. In typical inflammatory responses, MMP8 plays a dual role [45, 46]: on one hand, it promotes tissue repair and regeneration by degrading damaged extracellular matrix; on the other hand, excessive MMP8 activity can lead to excessive matrix degradation and inflammation spread, exacerbating tissue damage. In our study, we observed a significant upregulation of MMP8 in systemic inflammation and focal lung injury. Thus, regulating MMP8 activity may represent a crucial strategy for treating lung injury.

Our immune infiltration analysis revealed that MMP8 correlates with a substantially broader range of immune cell subsets than LTF (Fig. 2G–H). This disparity reflects their distinct cellular origins. MMP8, originally identified as a neutrophil‑derived collagenase, is now known to be inducible in multiple cell types under inflammatory conditions, including vascular endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, monocytes, and macrophages [47, 48]. Beyond its matrix‑remodeling role, MMP8 actively modulates immune responses by promoting M2‑macrophage polarization and regulating dendritic cell tolerance [49, 50]. Thus, circulating MMP8 serves as a broad sensor of activation across diverse immune lineages. In contrast, LTF is produced by a more restricted set of cells. It is a major constituent of secondary granules of neutrophils and is also constitutively expressed and secreted by exocrine glandular epithelial cells [51]. During systemic inflammation, LTF is primarily released from degranulating neutrophils. This relatively restricted cellular origin, centered on neutrophils and epithelial cells, likely explains why LTF exhibits a narrower correlation pattern with immune cell subsets, whereas MMP8, with its broader inducible expression across both immune and vascular cell types, correlates with a much wider spectrum of immune cells.

Interestingly, although the proportion of neutrophils is markedly elevated in the systemic inflammation group, LTF expression is significantly negatively correlated with neutrophil infiltration. This seemingly paradoxical finding likely reflects a negative feedback regulatory mechanism. LTF is a highly positively charged protein [52], enabling it to bind to negatively charged surfaces of apoptotic cells. Upon release from activated neutrophils, LTF has been shown to inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis and reduce neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) release [53], thereby limiting excessive neutrophil‑mediated tissue damage. Thus, elevated LTF in the circulation during systemic inflammation may serve as an intrinsic counter‑regulatory signal that suppresses further neutrophil recruitment and activation.

Both LTF and MMP8 exhibit strong positive correlations with M0 macrophages. M0 macrophages represent a plastic population poised for differentiation. MMP8 expression increases during monocyte‑to‑macrophage differentiation, and surface‑bound MMP8 on macrophages contributes to pericellular proteolysis [49, 54]. For LTF, beyond its iron‑binding function, it can act as a bridging molecule that coats apoptotic neutrophils and facilitates their efferocytosis by macrophages, thereby promoting inflammation resolution [52]. Furthermore, when LTF forms immune complexes with anti‑LTF autoantibodies, these complexes can potently activate monocytes and macrophages [55]. Given the physical interaction between LTF and MMP8 demonstrated in this study, their concurrent association with M0 macrophages raises the possibility that the LTF‑MMP8 complex may cooperatively regulate macrophage priming and functional polarization during inflammation, a hypothesis that warrants further experimental investigation.

PPI network analysis suggested a potential interaction between LTF and MMP8 (Fig. 5C). This predicted interaction was subsequently validated by co-immunoprecipitation, confirming that LTF and MMP8 form a complex both exogenously and under inflammatory conditions (Fig. 5D-E). While these results established their co‑association, its functional consequences remain unclear. Notably, while prior biochemical work has shown that LTF can inhibit MMP2 activity through zinc chelation, it did not demonstrate a similar inhibitory effect on MMP8 [56]. This suggests that the LTF‑MMP8 interaction we observed may operate through a mode distinct from classical protease inhibition. Thus, the LTF‑MMP8 complex may mediate inflammation‑specific, non‑catalytic functions, such as regulating protein turnover, localization, or signal transduction. Future studies employing mutagenesis, activity assays, or genetic perturbations will help clarify the precise functional role of this association in lung injury. Meanwhile, ceRNA network analysis highlighted the importance of LTF in post-transcriptional regulatory networks (Fig. 5F). These genes may influence the expression of other inflammation-related genes by regulating miRNA expression and function, forming a complex regulatory network. However, it is important to note that ceRNA interactions are often cell-type specific, and this network was predicted from blood-derived data. Therefore, these findings are preliminary and require experimental validation in relevant cell types. This predictive work provides candidate molecules for subsequent mechanistic research.

Molecular docking prediction [57] generated testable hypotheses regarding potential physical interactions between MP and LTF or MMP8. MP is an important drug commonly used in the treatment of inflammation [58]. It is important to note that these in silico findings do not establish that the anti-inflammatory effects of MP are mediated by direct binding to these proteins. Further biochemical and pharmacological experiments are required to validate direct binding and to determine whether such interactions contribute to the therapeutic effects of MP. Consistently, our cellular and animal experiments demonstrated that MP pretreatment could significantly downregulate the LPS-induced upregulation of both LTF and MMP8. Collectively, our findings reveal that MP can modulate LTF and MMP8 in a prophylactic model, while their definitive therapeutic potential and the precise underlying mechanisms require further validation in therapeutic dosing models.

While LTF and MMP8 have been individually implicated in inflammation and lung injury, our study provides two main novel insights. First, using an integrated machine learning pipeline on blood transcriptomes from patients with systemic inflammation, we identified LTF and MMP8 as top candidates and subsequently validated their roles in lung injury, offering a blood-to-lung discovery strategy. Second, our co‑immunoprecipitation assays demonstrate, for the first time, a physical interaction between LTF and MMP8 in the context of lung injury.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of the GSE32707 dataset may introduce platform-specific biases and sample heterogeneity, which could limit the generalizability of our findings. For example, the ceRNA predictions were derived from heterogeneous blood samples; therefore, validation in specific cell types is necessary to confirm the functional interactions between the predicted miRNAs and lncRNAs and LTF (e.g., via luciferase reporter assays). Second, variations in etiology, severity, and comorbidities among patients with lung injury may affect the universal applicability of the identified biomarkers. While our multi-tiered validation supports the involvement of LTF and MMP8 in lung injury, bulk RNA-seq analysis could not discern changes in gene expression from shifts in cellular abundance. Subsequent research should apply high-resolution techniques like flow cytometry or scRNA-seq to resolve this ambiguity.

Conclusions

This study identified LTF and MMP8 through integrative bioinformatic and machine learning analysis of systemic inflammation and subsequently validated their dysregulation in lung injury. These findings establish these molecules as key contributors to pulmonary damage and highlight their potential as therapeutic targets.
