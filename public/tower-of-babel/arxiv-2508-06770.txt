Bounds on skew dimensions and characters of symmetric groups via thick hook decompositions
Lucas Teyssier

Abstract
We introduce a new decomposition of Young diagrams into thick hooks. These thick hook decompositions enable having a better control over hook products for excited diagrams in the Naruse hook length formula, which leads to improved bounds on the number of standard tableaux of skew shapes. Combining this with elementary counting arguments in the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule, we establish a uniform bound on characters of symmetric groups 𝔖 n \mathfrak{S}_{n} . In the case of balanced representations, this improves on the character bounds of Féray and Śniady for permutations with support size at least n 2 / 3 n^{2/3} , and is sharp for permutations with support size of order n n . Finally, we recover some bounds of Pak and Panova on Kronecker coefficients with very short proofs that rely on characters.

Introduction

Let
\lambda
be an irreducible representation of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
. Denote the dimension of
\lambda
by
d_{\lambda}
, the character of
\lambda
at
\sigma
by
\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)
, the associated renormalized character by
\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma):=\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)/d_{\lambda}
, the minimal number of transpositions one needs to multiply to obtain
\sigma
by
|\sigma|
, and the support size of
\sigma
by
\supp(\sigma)
.
The irreducible representations of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
are in bijection with integer partitions
\lambda\vdash n
, which can be represented by Young diagrams, and we use the same notation
\lambda
for these three notions. The first row (resp. column) of a diagram
\lambda
is denoted by
\lambda_{1}
(resp.
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}
), and the maximal hook length of
\lambda
is
s(\lambda):=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{1}^{\prime}-1
.
Finally, for
C>0
we say that
\lambda\vdash n
is
C
-balanced if
s(\lambda)\leq C\sqrt{n}
.

Building on the work of Kerov
[
8
]
, Biane
[
1
,
2
]
proved, alongside factorization formulas for characters, that for balanced diagrams
\lambda
and permutations
\sigma
with
\supp(\sigma)=O(1)
,

|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)|\leq\left(\frac{O(1)}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{|\sigma|}.

(1.1)

This bound on characters for balanced diagrams was later extended to
\supp(\sigma)=O(\sqrt{n})
by Rattan and Śniady
[
27
]
. Moore and Russell conjectured (
[
27
, Conjecture 3]
) that (
1.1
) holds for any support size. Note that we always have
\supp(\sigma)/2\leq|\sigma|\leq\supp(\sigma)-1
, so
\supp(\sigma)\asymp|\sigma|
.

The known bounds for
\supp(\sigma)=\omega(\sqrt{n})
are however still relatively weak, especially for larger support sizes. Rattan and Śniady
[
27
]
proved that for permutations with
\supp(\sigma)=\omega(\sqrt{n})
,

|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)|\leq\left(O(1)\frac{|\sigma|^{2}}{n^{3/2}}\right)^{|\sigma|},

(RŚ)

which is trivial if
\supp(\sigma)=\omega(n^{3/4})
.

The strongest known character bound in this setting is due to Féray and Śniady
[
5
]
, who proved the following bound, which applies to all shapes of diagrams:

|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)|\leq\left(O(1)\max\left(\frac{|\sigma|}{n},\frac{s(\lambda)}{n}\right)\right)^{|\sigma|}.

(FŚ)

For balanced diagrams, (
FŚ
) coincides with the bound of Rattan and Śniady if
|\sigma|=O(\sqrt{n})
; these bounds are excellent and led to optimal applications to the number of standard diagrams of balanced skew Young tableaux in
[
4
]
. If
|\sigma|=\omega(\sqrt{n})
, and still for balanced diagrams
\lambda
, (
FŚ
) can be rewritten as

|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)|\leq\left(O(1)\frac{|\sigma|}{n}\right)^{|\sigma|}.

(FŚbal)

This bound is stronger than (
RŚ
) since
|\sigma|/n=o(|\sigma|^{2}/n^{3/2})
.

Our main result is the following character bound.

Theorem 1.1
.

As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all representations
\lambda\vdash n
and all permutations
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash\left\{\Id\right\}
, we have

|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)|\leq\left(\frac{O(1)}{\sqrt{|\sigma|}}\right)^{|\sigma|}\max\left(1,\frac{s(\lambda)\sqrt{|\sigma|}}{n}\right)^{\supp(\sigma)}.

(1.2)

Theorem
1.1
has the following simpler form for balanced representations.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
C>0
. As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
C
-balanced representations
\lambda\vdash n
and all permutations
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash\left\{\Id\right\}
, we have

|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)|\leq\left(\frac{O(1)}{\sqrt{|\sigma|}}\right)^{|\sigma|}.

(1.3)

An interesting aspect of our results is that they are more precise for permutations with larger support sizes, contrary to the bounds of
[
27
]
and
[
5
]
. More precisely,
Theorem
1.2
is stronger than (
FŚbal
) if
|\sigma|=\omega(n^{2/3})
, but weaker if
|\sigma|=o(n^{2/3})
.
As such, Theorem
1.1
complements the Féray–Śniady character bounds, as we illustrate in Figure
1
.
Moreover, Theorem

The first one is a bound on the number

2
Bounds on skew dimensions

Let
\lambda
and
\mu\subset\lambda
be Young diagrams. We denote the number of standard tableaux of the skew diagram
\lambda\backslash\mu
by
d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}
, and refer to this quantity as
skew dimension
.
Different explicit formulas have been found to compute skew dimensions, see for instance
[
23
,
29
]
.

A decade ago, Naruse
[
22
]
found a way to compute skew dimensions via excited diagrams, a notion introduced in
[
6
]
. This powerful formula, now called Naruse hook length formula, has seen other proofs and generalizations, see
[
18
,
11
]
, and has enabled computing improved bounds on skew dimensions in
[
17
,
20
,
24
]
as well as explicit formulas for various skew shapes
[
9
,
19
,
10
]
.
In this section we are concerned in proving general bounds on the quantity
\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}
for all shapes, in function of the sizes of the diagrams
\lambda
and
\mu
and the maximal hook length of
\lambda
.

2.1
The Naruse hook length formula

Let us now define excited diagrams and state the Naruse hook length formula. We represent boxes by the coordinates of their top-right corner in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
If
u=(i,j)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
is a box, we define its upper right square by
\textup{URS}(u)=\left\{(i,j),(i+1,j),(i,j+1),(i+1,j+1)\right\}
. Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram, and let
E
be a subset (of the boxes) of
\lambda
. A box
u\in E
is said to be
(\lambda,E)
-
excitable
if
\textup{URS}(u)\subset\lambda
and
E\cap\textup{URS}(u)=\left\{u\right\}
. A
simple excitation
(of
E
in
\lambda
) consists of transforming the set of boxes
E
into the diagram
E^{\prime}:=(E\backslash\left\{u\right\})\cup\left\{(i+1,j+1)\right\}
, where
u=(i,j)
is an excitable box of
E
in
\lambda
. In words,
E^{\prime}
has the same boxes as
E
except one that was moved diagonally towards north-east. Finally an
excited diagram
of a set of boxes
\mu
in
\lambda
is a diagram obtained by applying a (possibly empty) sequence of simple excitations. We denote the set of all excited diagrams of
\mu
in
\lambda
by
{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)
, and illustrate this in Figure
2
.
We refer to
[
24
]
or
[
18
]
for a more detailed description and further examples.

Figure 2:
The set
{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)
, with
\lambda=[5,5,5,2]
and
\mu=[3,2,1,1]
, consists of the following diagrams. We represent the excitable boxes light gray and the other boxes of excited diagrams in dark gray.

We denote the hook length of a box
u
in
\lambda
by
H(\lambda,u)
. If
E\subset\lambda
is a subset of the boxes of
\lambda
we set
H(\lambda,E)=\prod_{u\in E}H(\lambda,u)
. In particular, the hook length formula rewrites as
d_{\lambda}=|\lambda|!/H(\lambda,\lambda)
.
We use the falling factorial notation: if
0\leq k\leq n
are two integers, we write

n^{\downarrow k}=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.

(2.1)

Finally, let
\mu\subset\lambda
be two Young diagrams. We define the
excited sum
of
\mu
in
\lambda
by

S(\lambda,\mu)=\sum_{E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)}H(\lambda,E).

(2.2)

The Naruse hook length formula can then be written as the following identity:

\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}=\frac{S(\lambda,\mu)}{|\lambda|^{\downarrow|\mu|}}.

(2.3)

2.2
Motivation towards decompositions of diagrams

A first way to bound excited sums
S(\lambda,\mu)
is to bound each term of the sum by the maximal summand. Then we obtain the bound

S(\lambda,\mu)\leq|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)|H(\lambda,\mu).

(2.4)

Let us illustrate the (non-)sharpness of this bound when
\mu=[1]
consists of only one box. Then have
d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}=d_{\lambda}
and (equivalently)
S(\lambda,\mu)=n
. For example, the identity
S(\lambda,\mu)=n
follows from the fact that the union of the hooks of the boxes with coordinates
(1,1),(2,2),\ldots
in
\lambda
is exactly
\lambda
.

Example 2.1
.

In the following 3 cases, we omit integer parts, and the equivalents are as
n\to\infty
.

(a)

Balanced shape, represented here by the square diagram. If
\lambda=[\sqrt{n}^{\sqrt{n}}]
, then
|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)|=\sqrt{n}
and
H(\lambda,\mu)=2\sqrt{n}-1
, so (
2.4
) gives
S(\lambda,\mu)\leq 2n
, which is rather good.

(b)

Thin shape, represented here by a diagram with only two rows, of the same length. If
\lambda=[n/2,n/2]
, then
|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)|=2
and
H(\lambda,\mu)=n/2+1
, so (
2.4
) gives
S(\lambda,\mu)\leq n(1+o(1))
, which is very good.

(c)

Hybrid shape: union of a long row and a square. If
\lambda=[3n/4,m^{m}]
, where
m:=\sqrt{n/4}
, then
|{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,\mu)|=(1+o(1))\sqrt{n}/2
and
H(\lambda,\mu)=(1+o(1))3n/4
, so (
2.4
) gives
S(\lambda,\mu)\leq(3/8+o(1))n^{3/2}
, which is off by a factor of order
n^{1/2}
.

This illustrates that (
2.4
) is good for diagrams whose rows have comparable length, but can be wasteful for diagrams with hybrid shapes.

In Example
2.1
(c), it is not hard to modify the argument to obtain a precise estimate: we can split the excited diagrams depending on whether the excited box is in position
(1,1)
or in position
(i,i)
for some
i\geq 2
. Writing again
m=\sqrt{n/4}
, we obtain this way the very good estimate

S(\lambda,\mu)=S(\lambda^{1},[1])+S([m^{m}],[1])=(3/4+o(1))n+m^{2}=(1+o(1))n.

(2.5)

Now we want to generalize this idea to non-trivial cases, where
\lambda
and
\mu
can have any size and shape. We will decompose the diagram
\lambda
into parts that have comparable contributions to
S(\lambda,\mu)
.

2.3
Thick hook decomposition of Young diagrams

Recall that we denote the hook of the diagonal box
(i,i)
in
\lambda
by
\lambda^{i}
. For
j\leq k
we set
\lambda^{j\to k}=\cup_{j\leq i\leq k}\lambda^{i}
. We illustrate this in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
The diagram
\lambda=[11,10,8^{3},7,5,2,1]
. The subset
\lambda^{5}
is represented on the left and
\lambda^{2\to 4}
is represented on the right, both in gray.

2.3.1
Definition of thick hook decompositions

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram, and
1\leq a\leq b
. An
(a,b)
thick hook decomposition of
\lambda
is a sequence of thick hooks
(T_{j})_{1\leq j\leq p}=(\lambda^{(i_{j-1}+1)\to i_{j}})_{1\leq j\leq p}
, where
0=i_{0}<i_{1}<\ldots<i_{p}=\delta(\lambda)
, such that for every
1\leq j\leq p
we have

a\leq|T_{j}|\leq b.

(2.6)

By definition
\lambda
is the disjoint union of the thick hooks
T_{j}
. We illustrate this in Figure
4
.

Figure 4:
A
(18,36)
thick hook decomposition of the diagram
\lambda=[24,19,14,12,11,10,9,7,6,3,1]
. Here we have
(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4})=(1,2,4,7)
and
(|T_{j}|)_{1\leq j\leq 4}=(34,26,33,23)
. In this case this is the unique such decomposition.

2.4
Minimally excited diagrams in a thick hook decomposition

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram. Let
1\leq a\leq b
such that there is at least one thick hook decomposition of
\lambda
, and let
\boldsymbol{T}=(T_{j})_{1\leq j\leq p}
be such a decomposition. Let
1\leq\ell\leq\lambda_{1}
be an integer,
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{j})_{1\leq j\leq p}
be a sequence of non-negative integers such that
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\ell_{j}=\ell
, and let

{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell})=\left\{E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])\mid E\cap T_{j}=\ell_{j}\text{ for all }1\leq j\leq p\right\}

(2.7)

be the set of excited diagrams of
[\ell]
in
\lambda
with
\ell_{j}
boxes in the thick hook
T_{j}
, for each
1\leq j\leq p
.

If
{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell})
is non-empty, all its diagrams are excited diagrams of a minimally excited diagram which is a union of shifted rows.
Indeed, let
(i_{j})_{0\leq j\leq p}
be such that
T_{j}=\lambda^{(i_{j-1}+1)\to i_{j}}
for
1\leq i\leq p
, and let
m=\max\left\{q\geq 1\mid(\ell+q-1,q)\in\lambda\right\}
. By the bijection with flagged tableaux from
[
18
, Section 3.3]
, the diagrams in
{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell})
correspond exactly to sequences of integers
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell})
such that
1\leq u_{1}\leq\ldots\leq u_{\ell}\leq m
, and such that [
u_{k}\geq i_{j-1}+1
whenever
\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\ell_{i}<k\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}\ell_{i}
for some
1\leq j\leq p
], and the coordinate-wise partial order on vectors of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
corresponds to the partial order on excited diagrams (where
E
is smaller than
E^{\prime}
if
E^{\prime}
can be obtained from
E
after applying excited moves). Among such sequences of integers, the sequence
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell})
defined by
u_{k}=i_{j-1}+1
whenever
\sum_{i=1}^{j}\ell_{j}<k\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}\ell_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq p
is clearly the unique minimizer.

We denote this minimally excited diagram by
E_{\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell}}
, and illustrate this in Figure
5
.

2.5
A general bound for line-shaped diagrams

We first give a sufficient condition for thick hook decompositions to exist.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram and
a\geq s:=s(\lambda)
. Then there exists an
(a,4a)
thick hook decomposition of
\lambda
.

Proof.

First build a
(1,4a)
thick hook decomposition
\boldsymbol{T}
of
\lambda
by maximizing the size of
T_{1}
, then
T_{2}
, and so on up to a last thick hook
T_{p}
. Since
s\leq a
, by construction we have
3a\leq|T_{j}|\leq 4a
for all
1\leq j\leq p-1
, and
|T_{p}|\leq 4a
. If
|T_{p}|\geq a
, then
\boldsymbol{T}
is already a
(a,4a)
thick hook decomposition of
\lambda
. Otherwise, using again that
s\leq a
, we can decrease the value of
i_{p-1}
to transfer between
a
and
2a
boxes from
T_{p-1}
to
T_{p}
, and after doing that we have
a\leq|T_{p-1}|\leq 3a
and also
a\leq|T_{p}|\leq 3a
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We now extend a counting argument from
[
24
, Section 3.1]
to thick hook decompositions.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram. Let
a\geq s:=s(\lambda)
, where we recall that
s(\lambda)
is the maximal hook length in
\lambda
. Let
\boldsymbol{T}=(T_{j})_{1\leq j\leq p}
be an
(a,4a)
thick hook decomposition of
\lambda
. Let
1\leq\ell\leq\lambda_{1}
. Then there are at most

\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor}{\ell}

sequences of non-negative integers
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{j})_{1\leq j\leq p}
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{p}\ell_{j}=\ell
and
{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell})
is non-empty.

Proof.

Each
\boldsymbol{\ell}
such that
{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell})
is non-empty corresponds to exactly one minimally excited diagram
E_{\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell}}
, which is determined by the successive lengths
\ell_{j}
and how many thick hooks they jump over. Moreover, we can represent each
E_{\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell}}
denoting successive boxes by
*
and the jumps by
|
. (For example in Figure
5
, the configuration of black boxes can be represented by
*||***|*
.) Therefore since we have
\ell
stars and
p-1
bars, the number of such
\boldsymbol{\ell}
is at most
\binom{\ell+(p-1)}{\ell}
, which is less than
\binom{\ell+p}{\ell}
.
Finally, each
T_{j}
contains at least
a
boxes, and
p
is an integer, so we have
p\leq\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram, and denote
n=|\lambda|
. Let
a\in[s(\lambda),n]
, where we recall that
s(\lambda)
is the maximal hook length in
\lambda
. Let
1\leq\ell\leq\lambda_{1}
. Then

S(\lambda,[\ell])\leq\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor}{\ell}(4a)^{\ell}.

(2.8)

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{T}=(T_{j})_{1\leq j\leq p}
be an
(a,4a)
thick hook decomposition of
\lambda
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{j})_{1\leq j\leq p}
such that
{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell})
is non-empty.
Let
1\leq j\leq p
. Denote the part of
E_{\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell}}
which is in
T_{j}
by
\nu_{j}
.
For any set of boxes
\rho\subset T_{j}
, extending the notion of excited diagrams to any set of boxes, we set

S^{*j}(\lambda,\rho)=\sum_{E}H(\lambda,E),

(2.9)

where the sum is over all excited diagrams of
\rho
whose boxes are all in
T_{j}
.
Denote the bottom-left corner of
T_{j}
by
u_{0}(j)
.
Then for each
1\leq j\leq p
we have,

S^{*j}(\lambda,\nu_{j})\leq\prod_{u\in\nu_{j}}S^{*j}(\lambda,u)\leq\prod_{u\in\nu_{j}}S^{*j}(\lambda,u_{0}(j))=\prod_{u\in\nu_{j}}|T_{j}|=|T_{j}|^{\ell_{j}}\leq(4a)^{\ell_{j}},

(2.10)

and therefore

\prod_{1\leq j\leq p}S^{*j}(\lambda,\nu_{j})\leq\prod_{1\leq j\leq p}(4a)^{\ell_{j}}=(4a)^{\ell}.

(2.11)

We conclude, denoting the empty set of boxes by
\emptyset
, that

S(\lambda,[\ell])=\sum_{E\in{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda,[\ell])}H(\lambda,E)=\sum_{\underset{{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda;\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{\ell})\neq\emptyset}{\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{j})_{1\leq j\leq p}{\;:\;}}}\prod_{1\leq j\leq p}S^{*j}(\lambda,\nu_{j})\leq\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor}{\ell}(4a)^{\ell}.\qed

(2.12)

2.6
Specific bounds for line-shaped diagrams

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer. Let
\lambda\vdash n
. Recall that
s:=s(\lambda)
denotes the maximal hook length in
\lambda
. Let
1\leq\ell\leq\lambda_{1}
.

(a)

Assume that
\ell\leq n/s
. Then

S(\lambda,[\ell])\leq\left(8\frac{n}{\ell}\right)^{\ell}.

(2.13)

(b)

Assume that
\ell\geq n/s
. Then

S(\lambda,[\ell])\leq\left(4e^{2}\right)^{\ell}s^{\ell}.

(2.14)

Proof.

(a)

Set

. By assumption we have

. Moreover
\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\ell\right\rfloor=\ell
, so
\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor}{\ell}=\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\leq 2^{\ell}
and we conclude using Proposition
2.4
that

S(\lambda,[\ell])\leq\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor}{\ell}(4a)^{\ell}\leq(8a)^{\ell}=(8n/\ell)^{\ell}.

(2.15)

(b)

Set
a=s
and
q=\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor
. In particular we have
\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor\geq 2
so
q\geq(n/a)/2
. Since we also have
q\leq\ell
by assumption, it follows, writing
\gamma:=q/\ell
, that

\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor}{\ell}=\binom{q+\ell}{q}\leq\binom{2\ell}{q}=\frac{(2\ell)^{\downarrow q}}{q!}\leq\frac{(2\ell)^{q}}{(q/e)^{q}}=\left(\frac{2e\ell}{q}\right)^{q}=\left(\left(\frac{2e}{\gamma}\right)^{\gamma}\right)^{\ell}.

(2.16)

But
(2e/\gamma)^{\gamma}\leq e^{2}
since for any

the maximum of the function

is
e^{A/e}
(reached at

). It follows that

\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor}{\ell}\leq e^{2\ell},

(2.17)

and we conclude using Proposition
2.4
that

S(\lambda,[\ell])\leq\binom{\ell+\left\lfloor n/a\right\rfloor}{\ell}(4a)^{\ell}\leq\left(4e^{2}\right)^{\ell}s^{\ell}.\qed

(2.18)

2.8
Bounds on skew dimensions for general shapes

Proposition 2.7
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that the following holds. Let
1\leq k\leq n
be integers. Let
\lambda\vdash n
and
\mu\vdash k
such that
\mu\subset\lambda
. Then

S(\lambda,\mu)\leq\left(C\max\left(\frac{n}{\sqrt{k}},s\right)\right)^{k}.

(2.20)

Proof.

Denote by
(\ell_{i})_{1\leq i\leq q}
the parts in the stairs decomposition of
\mu
, where we recall that the number of parts satisfies
q\leq 2\delta(\mu)\leq 2\sqrt{k}
. In this proof we use the notation
\ell_{i}
for both the subsets of boxes in the stairs slicing and the lengths of these parts.

Let
I=\left\{i\in[q]\mid\ell_{i}\leq n/s\right\}
and
J=[q]\backslash I
, where
[q]=\left\{1,2,\ldots,q\right\}
. Set
\ell_{I}=\sum_{i\in I}\ell_{i}
and
\ell_{J}=\sum_{i\in J}\ell_{i}
. Note that
k=\ell_{I}+\ell_{J}
. Set also
C=\max\left(8,4e^{2}\right)=4e^{2}
. By Proposition
2.5
, we can reduce the problem to line-shaped diagrams, which leads to

S(\lambda,\mu)\leq\prod_{i}S(\lambda,\ell_{i})\leq\left(\prod_{i\in I}\left(C\frac{n}{\ell_{i}}\right)^{\ell_{i}}\right)\left(\prod_{i\in J}\left(Cs\right)^{\ell_{i}}\right)=C^{k}n^{\ell_{I}}s^{\ell_{J}}\prod_{i\in I}\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{i}}\right)^{\ell_{i}}.

(2.21)

Without loss of generality we may assume that
|I|\geq 1
. By convexity we have

\prod_{i\in I}\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{i}}\right)^{\ell_{i}}\leq\prod_{i\in I}\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{I}/|I|}\right)^{\ell_{I}/|I|}=\left(\frac{|I|}{\ell_{I}}\right)^{\ell_{I}}.

(2.22)

Therefore, since
|I|\leq q\leq 2\sqrt{k}
,

S(\lambda,\mu)(2C)^{-k}\leq s^{\ell_{J}}\left(\frac{n|I|}{2\ell_{I}}\right)^{\ell_{I}}=s^{k}\left(\frac{n|I|}{2s\ell_{I}}\right)^{\ell_{I}}\leq s^{k}\left(\frac{n\sqrt{k}}{s\ell_{I}}\right)^{\ell_{I}}=s^{k}\left(\frac{n}{s\sqrt{k}}\frac{k}{\ell_{I}}\right)^{\ell_{I}}

(2.23)

Now there are two cases.

(a)

If
\frac{n}{s\sqrt{k}}\leq e
, then by (
2.23
) and since
(A/x)^{x}\leq e^{A/e}
for
A,x>0
, we have

S(\lambda,\mu)(2C)^{-k}\leq s^{k}\left(\frac{ek}{\ell_{I}}\right)^{\ell_{I}}\leq s^{k}e^{k}.

(2.24)

(b)

If
\frac{n}{s\sqrt{k}}\geq e
, then the function
\ell\in[1,k]\mapsto\left(\frac{n}{s\sqrt{k}}\frac{k}{\ell}\right)^{\ell}
reaches its maximum at
\ell=k
, and therefore we have

S(\lambda,\mu)(2C)^{-k}\leq s^{k}\left(\frac{n}{s\sqrt{k}}\right)^{k}=\left(\frac{n}{\sqrt{k}}\right)^{k}.

(2.25)

We conclude that in all cases we have

S(\lambda,\mu)\leq(2eC)^{k}\left(\max\left(s,\frac{n}{\sqrt{k}}\right)\right)^{k}.\qed

(2.26)

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Recall from (
2.3
) that
\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}=\frac{S(\lambda,\mu)}{n^{\downarrow k}}
, and that
n^{\downarrow k}\geq(n/e)^{k}
.
The result then follows immediately from Proposition
2.7
.
∎

3
Bounds on characters

The most canonical way to compute the characters of symmetric groups is via the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule. This is an explicit formula for characters (explained below in this section) which involves sums over ribbon tableaux and sign cancellations that can be very delicate to understand for large diagrams. The Murnaghan–Nakayama rule led to fruitful
exponential
character bounds
[
28
,
21
,
12
]
, i.e. bounds uniform on
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}
and
\sigma
is in
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
(or a large subset of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
) of the form

|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)|\leq d_{\lambda}^{-(a+o(1))\frac{\ln(n/f(\sigma))}{\ln n}},

(3.1)

where
f(\sigma)
is the number of fixed points of
\sigma
(or 1 if
\sigma
is fixed point free), and
a>0
is a constant to optimize. These character bounds are related to
mixing times
of random walks on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, and more specifically to generalizations of results of Diaconis and Shahashahani
[
3
]
on random transpositions.
Recently, the analytic technique of
hypercontractivity
(see for instance
[
7
]
) was successfully used to obtain exponential character bounds
[
14
]
. These bounds essentially show that (
3.1
) holds with
a=1
for permutations with at most
n/(\ln n)^{O(1)}
cycles, which improved on the Larsen–Shalev bounds for these permutations. It turned out that improving some of the technical estimates of
[
12
]
led to stronger bounds
[
31
, Theorem 1.7, see also Remark 5.1]
, which are suited for permutations with
o(n)
fixed points. For permutations with order
n
fixed points, we obtained the sharpest possible exponential character bounds in
[
24
]
, namely that (
3.1
) holds uniformly with
a=1
and no
o(1)
error term.

The character bounds considered in the present paper follow the line of work of
[
8
,
1
,
2
,
27
,
5
]
. They have a different form and are not comparable to exponential character bounds.
For exponential character bounds the Young diagrams that are limiting for the bound are those with a very long first row. Such shapes impose strong constraints on the ribbon tableaux and it makes it
more
possible to handle them using the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule.
However the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule seemed to be little suited for diagrams with short rows and columns (and in particular for balanced diagrams), where there are less constraints on ribbon tableaux: Féray and Śniady
[
5
]
described it as being “cumbersome and hence intractable for computing characters corresponding to large Young diagrams”. It might therefore be surprising that the proof of Theorem
1.1
(via that of Theorem
1.4
) relies on the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule, and leads to precise character bounds in some regimes not previously covered in
[
27
]
or
[
5
]
.

3.1.3
The Murnaghan–Nakayama rule

The Murnaghan–Nakayama rule (

are actually integers, but also provides us with a way to compute them. It can be stated as follows.

Theorem 3.1

(Murnaghan-Nakayama rule)
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
\lambda\vdash n
be a partition of
n
, and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
Denote by
\alpha
the cycle type of
\sigma
, that is, the list of cycle lengths of
\sigma
sorted in decreasing order. Then, we have

\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\sum_{T\in\RT(\lambda,\alpha)}(-1)^{\height(T)}.

(3.2)

Since the character
\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)
depends only on the cycle type
\alpha
of
\sigma
, we may write
\ch^{\lambda}(\alpha)
instead of
\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)
.

A useful property of the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule is that the value of the sum does not depend on the order of the entries of
\alpha
(see
[
30
, Corollary 7.17.4 and the comment below the proof of Proposition 7.17.6]
, or the proof of
[
15
, Proposition 3.10]
).
When computing characters, it can be convenient to permute some entries of
\alpha
to reduce the number of diagrams to consider. Let us illustrate this on two examples.

The Murnaghan–Nakayama rule is actually fairly simple to use for small diagrams, but using it to estimate characters of large diagrams can be very complicated.

There are two main difficulties to overcome. The first one is that there could be significant cancellations between ribbon tableaux since the summands
(-1)^{\height(T)}
can be positive or negative. A possibility to handle this is to forget about compensations by applying the triangle inequality in (
3.2
), which leads to the bound

\left|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|=\left|\sum_{T\in\RT(\lambda,\alpha)}(-1)^{\height(T)}\right|\leq\left|\RT(\lambda,\alpha)\right|.

(3.3)

One then only has to estimate the number of ribbon tableaux of
\lambda
of weight
\alpha
. Applying the triangle inequality in the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule (
3.2
) seems wasteful at first glance but can be surprisingly precise in practice.
The second and main difficulty is that when permutations have many small cycles and diagrams do not have a long first row, the number of associated ribbon tableaux is large and difficult to control.

]
. We observed in
[
24
]
that this identity is useful to leverage character bounds for fixed point free permutations to permutations of any support size, and will also use this to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Let
n
and
k
be integers such that
2\leq k\leq n
, and let
\sigma
is a permutation of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support
k
. Let
\sigma^{*}\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{k}
be a permutation with as many cycles of length
i
as
\sigma
for
i\geq 2
, but no fixed point; to define it uniquely we fill the cycles of
\sigma^{*}
in (weakly) decreasing order of their lengths with the numbers from 1 to
k
.
For example if
\sigma=(1\;3\;5\;8)(2\;7)(4)(6)(9)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{9}
, then
\sigma^{*}=(1\;2\;3\;4)(5\;6)\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{6}
.

A way to view the following iterated branching rule is that it corresponds to peeling first the
n-k
ribbons of size 1 in the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
2\leq k\leq n
,
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
with support of size
k
, and
\lambda\vdash n
. Then

\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}.

(3.4)

Equivalently, we have

\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}.

(3.5)

Proof.

Let
\ell
be the length of the longest cycle in
\sigma
. We may assume without loss of generality that
\ell\geq 2
. Let
\alpha_{i}
be the number of
i
-cycles in
\sigma
, and set
\alpha=\left(1^{\alpha_{1}},\ell^{\alpha_{\ell}},(\ell-1)^{\alpha_{\ell-1}},\ldots,2^{\alpha_{2}}\right)
, the cycle type of
\sigma
, written with fixed points first and the other cycles in (weakly) decreasing order of their lengths. Let also
\alpha^{*}=\left(\ell^{\alpha_{\ell}},(\ell-1)^{\alpha_{\ell-1}},\ldots,2^{\alpha_{2}}\right)
be the cycle type of
\sigma^{*}
, where the cycles are written in (weakly) decreasing order of their lengths. Then by the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule, peeling the
\alpha_{1}
ribbons of length 1, we have

\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)=\sum_{T\in\RT(\lambda,\alpha)}(-1)^{\height(T)}=\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}\left(\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\RT(\mu,\alpha^{*})}(-1)^{\height(T^{\prime})}\right)d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}=\sum_{\mu\subset_{\vdash k}\lambda}\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}.

(3.6)

The second part of the lemma is obtained from the first one by dividing both sides by
d_{\lambda}
.
∎

3.2
Elementary character bounds in terms of the number of cycles

Larsen and Shalev
[
12
]
developed an elaborate inductive peeling process to prove exponential character bounds which are especially precise for fixed point free permutations and representations with a long first row. In this section show that applying a simpler peeling process that relies only on counting arguments leads to more precise bounds for diagrams that do not have a long first row, and in particular for balanced diagrams.

If
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
and
j\geq 1
,
\cyc_{j}(\sigma)
is the number of cycles of length
j
of
\sigma
,
\cyc_{\geq j}(\sigma)=\sum_{i\geq j}\cyc_{i}(\sigma)
is the number of cycles of length at least
j
of
\sigma
, and
\cyc(\sigma)=\cyc_{\geq 1}(\sigma)
is the total number of cycles of
\sigma
.
Then
\sum_{j\geq 1}j\cyc_{j}(\sigma)=n
and
\sum_{j\geq 2}j\cyc_{j}(\sigma)=\supp(\sigma)
is the support size of
\sigma
.
Also, for
\lambda\vdash n
,
\delta(\lambda)
is the diagonal length of
\lambda
, that is,
\delta(\lambda)=\max\left\{i\geq 1\mid\min(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i}^{\prime})\geq i\right\}
, and
\ell(\lambda)
is the number of rows of
\lambda
, that is,
\ell(\lambda)=\lambda_{1}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\lambda
be a Young diagram. Let
r\geq 1
.

(a)

There are at most
\ell(\lambda)
ways to peel a ribbon of length
r
from
\lambda
.

(b)

There are at most
2\delta(\lambda)
ways to peel a ribbon of length
r
from
\lambda
.

Proof.

(a) A ribbon, given its length, is determined by the position of its bottom-right-most box, which must be at the end of a row of
\lambda
. This is illustrated in Figure
19
.
Hence the number of ribbons of length
r
of
\lambda
is at most its number of rows, which is
\ell(\lambda)
.

Figure 19:
The Young diagram
\lambda=[24,15,12,8,8,5,3,3,3,1,1]
. The right-most box of each row is in dark gray. These boxes are the bottom-right-most possible positions of boxes of ribbons of
\lambda
.

(b) Pushing further the previous argument, a ribbon must satisfy at least one of the following two conditions: having its bottom-right-most box at the end of one of the
\delta(\lambda)
first rows of
\lambda
; or having its top-left-box at the end of one of the first
\delta(\lambda)
columns of
\lambda
. These boxes are illustrated in Figure
20
.

Figure 20:
The Young diagram
\lambda=[24,15,12,8,8,5,3,3,3,1,1]
. Here
\delta(\lambda)=5
. The right-most box of each of the first
\delta(\lambda)
rows is in dark gray. The top box of each of the first
\delta(\lambda)
columns is in light gray.

∎

We can now prove the desired character bound.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
\alpha
be any ordering of the cycle lengths of
\sigma
, for example in (weakly) increasing order. Then applying the triangle inequality in the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule gives
|\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)|\leq|\RT(\lambda,\alpha)|
, as written in (
3.3
).

We therefore only need to bound
|\RT(\lambda,\alpha)|
. We note that peeling a ribbon
\rho
from
\lambda
cannot increase its number of rows or its diagonal length. Hence, applying Lemma
3.5
iteratively to the
\cyc(\sigma)
ribbons that we peel gives
|\RT(\lambda,\alpha)|\leq\ell(\lambda)^{\cyc(\sigma)}
and
|\RT(\lambda,\alpha)|\leq(2\delta(\lambda))^{\cyc(\sigma)}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

3.3
Bounds on characters for general shapes of diagrams

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
n
be a large integer. Let
\lambda\vdash n
and
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\backslash\left\{\Id\right\}
. Denote the support size of
\sigma
by
k
. By Lemma
3.4
, the triangle inequality, and since the number of partitions of the integer
k
is
e^{O(k)}
(actually even
e^{O(\sqrt{k})}
), we have

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq\sum_{\mu\subset\lambda{\;:\;}|\mu|=k}\left|\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})\right|\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\leq e^{O(k)}\max_{\mu\subset\lambda{\;:\;}|\mu|=k}\left|\ch^{\mu}(\sigma^{*})\right|\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}.

(3.7)

We deduce from Theorem
1.4
(using that for any
\mu\vdash k
the diagonal length of
\mu
satisfies
\delta(\mu)\leq\sqrt{k}
) and Theorem
1.3
that

\left|\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq e^{O(k)}\sqrt{k}^{\cyc(\sigma^{*})}\left(\max\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}},\frac{s}{n}\right)\right)^{k}=e^{O(k)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)^{k-\cyc(\sigma^{*})}\max\left(1,\frac{s\sqrt{k}}{n}\right)^{k}.

(3.8)

This concludes the proof since
|\sigma|\asymp k
,
e^{O(k)}=O(1)^{k}
, and
k-\cyc(\sigma^{*})=|\sigma^{*}|=|\sigma|
.
∎

4
Some comments on random compression probabilities

Let
n
and
k
be two integers such that
1\leq k\leq n
. Let
\lambda\vdash n
and
\mu\vdash k
be two diagrams such that
\mu\subset\lambda
.
A natural quantity to consider is the probability

P_{\lambda}(\mu)=\frac{d_{\mu}d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}

(4.1)

that the first
k
integers fill
\mu
in a random tableau of
\lambda
. This can also be interpreted as a random compression probability, see the survey
[
16
]
. Denote the Plancherel measure evaluated at a Young diagram
\nu\vdash k
by

\Pl_{k}(\nu)=\frac{d_{\nu}^{2}}{k!}.

(4.2)

Dousse and Féray
[
4
]
studied the asymptotics of the quantity

A_{\lambda,\mu}:=k!\frac{d_{\lambda\backslash\mu}}{d_{\mu}d_{\lambda}}=\frac{P_{\lambda}(\mu)}{\Pl_{k}(\mu)},

(4.3)

when
\lambda
and
\mu
are balanced Young diagrams, and proved precise bounds when
k=O(\sqrt{n})
.

An interesting question is to understand how
close
the two measures
P_{\lambda}(\cdot)
and
\Pl_{k}(\cdot)
are, for different shapes of
\lambda
and sizes
k
, and different notions of distances.
The quantity
\sum_{\mu\vdash k}|P_{\lambda}(\mu)-\Pl_{k}(\mu)|
is (twice) the total variation distance between the two measures, while

\max_{\mu\vdash k}|A_{\lambda,\mu}-1|

(4.4)

is a form of
L^{\infty}
distance between
P_{\lambda}(\cdot)
and
\Pl_{k}(\cdot)
. (If we view the Plancherel measure as the stationary measure of a Markov chain, this is how the
L^{\infty}
distance from stationarity defined, see
[
13
, Section 4.7]
.) Dousse and Féray restricted their attention to diagrams
\lambda
and
\mu
that are balanced. Their results are therefore bounds of
L^{\infty}
-type restricted to balanced diagrams.

Dousse and Féray
[
4
, Conjecture 6]
conjectured that for any
C>0
there exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that as
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
1\leq k\leq n
, and all
C
-balanced diagrams
\lambda\vdash n
and
\mu\vdash k
such that
\mu\subset\lambda
, we have

-C^{\prime}\frac{k^{3/2}}{n^{1/2}}\leq\ln A_{\lambda,\mu}\leq C^{\prime}\frac{k^{3/2}}{n^{1/2}}.

(4.5)

They proved (
4.5
) for
k=o(n^{1/3})
in
[
4
, Corollary 2]
, and that the upper bound of (
4.5
) holds for
k=O(n^{1/2})
in
[
4
, Theorem 4]
. In order to prove the upper bound of (
4.5
) via the strategy of Dousse and Féray, one would need to prove the conjecture of Moore and Russell.

The probabilistic point of view on the quantity
A_{\lambda,\mu}
leads to the following elementary improvement of
[
4
, Theorem 5]
: the assumption that
\lambda
is balanced can be removed, and the exponent is improved by a logarithmic factor.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
C>0
. As
n\to\infty
, uniformly over all
\lambda\vdash n
and all
C
-balanced diagrams
\mu\subset\lambda
, we have

A_{\lambda,\mu}\leq(C^{2}e)^{k}.

(4.6)

Proof.

Let
n
be a large integer, let
\lambda\vdash n
, and let
\mu\subset\lambda
be a
C
-balanced Young diagram. By the hook length formula we have

d_{\mu}=\frac{k!}{H(\mu,\mu)}\geq\frac{k!}{(C\sqrt{k})^{k}}.

(4.7)

Moreover
k!\geq(k/e)^{k}
and
P_{\lambda}(\mu)\leq 1
since
P_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is a probability. We conclude that

A_{\lambda,\mu}=\frac{P_{\lambda}(\mu)}{\Pl_{k}(\mu)}\leq\frac{1}{\Pl_{k}(\mu)}=\frac{d_{\mu}^{2}}{k!}\leq\frac{\left((C\sqrt{k})^{k}\right)^{2}}{k!}\leq(C^{2}e)^{k}.\qed

(4.8)

5
Bounds on Kronecker coefficients via characters

Let
n\geq 2
. Let
X
be a uniform permutation on
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.
The Kronecker coefficient for the representations
\lambda,\mu,\nu\vdash n
is given by

g(\lambda,\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\ch^{\lambda}(\sigma)\ch^{\mu}(\sigma)\ch^{\nu}(\sigma)={\mathbb{E}}\left[\ch^{\lambda}(X)\ch^{\mu}(X)\ch^{\nu}(X)\right].

(5.1)

We refer to
[
26
]
for more details and history on Kronecker coefficients.

Using Theorem
1.4
(proved in Section
3.2
), we obtain the following simple bounds on Kronecker coefficients. Recall that
\delta(\lambda)
and
\ell(\lambda)
respectively denote the diagonal length and the number of rows of a diagram
\lambda
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Let
\lambda,\mu,\nu\vdash n
. We have the following.

(a)

g(\lambda,\mu,\nu)\leq\binom{n+K-1}{n}
, where
K=\ell(\lambda)\ell(\mu)\ell(\nu)
.

(b)

g(\lambda,\mu,\nu)\leq\binom{n+K-1}{n}
, where
K=8\delta(\lambda)\delta(\mu)\delta(\nu)
.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality and Theorem
1.4
, for both (a) and (b) we have

g(\lambda,\mu,\nu)\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[|\ch^{\lambda}(X)||\ch^{\mu}(X)||\ch^{\nu}(X)|\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[K^{\cyc(X)}],

(5.2)

where
X
is a uniform permutation of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
.
Since the generating function of
\cyc(X)
is given, for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, by
{\mathbb{E}}[z^{{\cyc(X)}}]=\frac{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n-1)}{n!}
, which is equal to
\binom{n+z-1}{n}
if
z
is a positive integer, we conclude that

g(\lambda,\mu,\nu)\leq{\mathbb{E}}[K^{\cyc(X)}]=\binom{n+K-1}{n}.\qed

(5.3)

We note that Proposition
5.1
gives essentially the same bound as
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
, and also implies that if
K=\ell(\lambda)\ell(\mu)\ell(\nu)\leq n
we have
g(\lambda,\mu,\nu)\leq 4^{n}
, since
\binom{n+K-1}{n}\leq\binom{2n-1}{n}\leq 4^{n}
.

The following corollary of Proposition
5.1
recovers that Kronecker coefficients for diagrams with a bounded diagonal length are polynomial. Our constant 8 in the exponent (which we did not try to optimize) is weaker than the constant 4 that of
[
26
, Theorem 1.4]
, but our proof is significantly simpler.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
n\geq 4
. Let
\lambda,\mu,\nu\vdash n
and set
k=\max(\delta(\lambda),\delta(\mu),\delta(\nu))
. Then

g(\lambda,\mu,\nu)\leq n^{8k^{3}}.

(5.4)

Proof.

Set
K=8k^{3}
. By Proposition
5.1
, we have

g(\lambda,\mu,\nu)\leq\binom{n+K-1}{n}\leq\binom{n+K}{n}.

(5.5)

Now if
K\geq n
we have
\binom{n+K}{n}\leq\binom{2K}{n}\leq 2^{2K}=4^{K}\leq n^{K}
, and if
K\leq n
, using that
K\geq 4
so that
K!\geq 2^{K}
, we have

\binom{n+K}{n}=\binom{n+K}{K}\leq\binom{2n}{K}\leq\frac{(2n)^{K}}{K!}\leq n^{K}.\qed

(5.6)
