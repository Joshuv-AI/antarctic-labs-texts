Synthesis, Characterization, and Biological Assessment of 2‐Methoxynicotinonitrile Derivatives
Authors: Eman Sulaiman, Eman Al‐Masri, Maram B. Alhawarri, Mohammad M. Ibrahim, Mahmoud Al‐Refai, Basem F. Ali, Ali Shuaib
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study presents the formation of 2‐methoxy‐3‐cyanopyridine derivatives 3(a–g) that occurred while attempting to synthesize furo[2,3‐b]pyridines. The structures of all newly synthesized compounds were confirmed by IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, 2D‐NMR, HRMS, and crystal x‐ray structure analysis. In the crystal structure of 3f, the molecules are connected by C―H···N≡C, C―Cl···O―C, and C―S···N≡C interactions to form layers, which are further connected through π···π and Cl···π interactions, consolidating a 3‐dimensional structure. The antioxidant activities of all synthesized compounds were tested in a DPPH (2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl) free radical scavenger assay. Compound 3d displayed the highest level of activity with an IC50 of 57.47 ± 1.17 µg/mL. Moderate levels of antibacterial activity were found against Escherichia coli, Coagulase‐Negative Staphylococci (CONS), and Streptococcus species. These findings indicate that the 2‐methoxy nicotinonitrile is an adaptable scaffolding to provide both tunable electronic properties and potential antioxidant and antibacterial properties. This new synthetic conversion described here demonstrates a practical method for preparing structurally diverse cyanopyridines and provides a platform for further lead optimization toward future multifunctional bioactive compounds.

Introduction

Nitrogen‐containing heterocycles are the mainstay of current medicinal chemistry and serve as privileged frameworks for designing new drug candidates with bioactivity. Among heterocycles with nitrogen atoms, the pyridine ring is one of the most commonly used, and the pyridine‐3‐carbonitrile (nicotinonitrile) framework is among the most researched because it represents a highly versatile pharmacological profile. The wide range of biological activity exhibited by derivatives of nicotinonitrile includes but is not limited to anti‐Alzheimer [1], anticonvulsant [2], antioxidant [3], and anticancer properties [4]. The clinical significance of the nicotinonitrile is underscored by its presence in several FDA‐approved therapeutic agents [5, 6], such as the androgen receptor inhibitor Apalutamide (A), the tyrosine kinase inhibitor Neratinib (B), and the isocitrate dehydrogenase‐1 (IDH1) inhibitor Ivosidenib (C) (Figure 1).

FDA‐approved drugs contain cyanopyridine.

In parallel, the combination of the sulfur‐containing heterocyclic rings with the pyridine structure will provide an opportunity to produce beneficial synergistic effects in terms of biological activity. The thiophene ring, a bioisostere of the aromatic phenyl group, can be used to alter the physiochemical properties and metabolic stability of potential drugs [7, 8]. Therefore, compounds that combine a halogenated thiophene group with a nicotinonitrile scaffold appear to be interesting candidates for pharmacological research and could have increased potency against targets of microbial and oxidative stress.

A second major approach for optimizing pharmacokinetics involves enhancing lipophilicity by introducing alkoxy groups. Alkoxy groups at the C‐2 position of the pyridine ring are known to increase both the ability to penetrate membranes and the receptor binding affinity of the compound [9]. As a result, the 2‐alkoxy‐3‐cyano‐pyridines are recognized as an important subclass of heteroaromatic compounds with demonstrated cholinesterase inhibiting properties [10], anti‐inflammatory [11], antiviral [10], and anticancer activities [12].

In our previous investigations, we reported the efficient synthesis of 2‐methoxy‐3‐cyanopyridines via the base‐catalyzed condensation of chalcones with malononitrile, where the resulting derivatives demonstrated promising antiproliferative activity [13]. Building upon this work, our initial synthetic strategy aimed to construct the fused furo[2,3‐b]pyridine system of 2‐cyanomethoxynicotinonitrile precursors. Intriguingly, this attempt yielded an unusual reaction pathway. Instead of the anticipated ring closure, the reaction conditions promoted a distinctive substitution, affording the corresponding 2‐methoxynicotinonitrile derivatives exclusively.

Although 4‐aryl‐6‐(halogenated‐3‐thiophenyl)‐2‐methoxynicotinonitriles are highly biologically interesting, little information exists regarding 4‐aryl‐6‐(2,5‐dichlorothiophen‐3‐yl)‐2‐methoxynicotinonitriles in which a halogenated thiophene unit, a C‐2 methoxy group, and differently substituted aryl groups are present on the same scaffold. This gap is important in its own right, as each of these structural modifications could affect the physicochemical and biological properties of the molecule, and the combined effect on this focused nicotinonitrile series has not been thoroughly investigated. In this study, a new series of 4‐aryl‐6‐(2,5‐dichlorothiophen‐3‐yl)‐2‐methoxynicotinonitriles 3a–g was synthesized from 2‐cyanomethoxynicotinonitrile precursors, using base‐mediated methodology. This series consisted of a 2‐methoxynicotinonitrile‐based scaffold where the aryl group at the C‐4 position was varied with methoxy, fluoro, chloro, bromo, bromo‐methoxy, and nitro‐substituted phenyl rings, while the 2,5‐dichlorothiophen‐3‐yl moiety was kept constant. A focused design enabled the assessment of the relationship between the observed antioxidant and antibacterial profile and various electronic and steric characteristics of the aryl substituent. All synthesized compounds were fully characterized by spectroscopic methods, and the structure of compound 3f was confirmed by single‐crystal x‐ray diffraction. The series has also been screened for DPPH radical‐scavenging and antibacterial activity to assess the relationship of the different aryl substituents and the observed potency.

Results and Discussion

Chemistry

The 4‐aryl‐2‐(cyanomethoxy)‐6‐(2,5‐dichlorothiophene‐3‐yl)nicotinonitrile 2(a–g) derivative was synthesized in 50%–90% yield in our research group by treatment of the 2(1H)‐pyridone derivatives 1(a–g) with chloroacetonitrile and K2CO3 in dimethylformamide (DMF) (Scheme 1) [14]. An unexpected reaction occurred when 2‐cyanomethoxynicotinonitrile 2(a–g) was treated with sodium methoxide under reflux, where 2‐methoxynicotinonitrile 3(a–g) was formed in moderate to excellent yields (58%–95%) instead of the expected furo[2,3‐b]pyridines. So, a series of the last 2‐methoxynicotinonitrile was prepared due to the efficiency of the new method for the synthesis of these compounds (Scheme 1).

Antioxidant Activity

The DPPH radical‐scavenging assay revealed that the newly synthesized compounds 3(a–g) display a broad spectrum of antioxidant activities, with % inhibition ranging from 38% to 86% at a concentration of 200 ppm, while the most potent compounds (3d, 3a, and 3e) achieved IC50 values (57.47–73.39 ppm) that are within the same order of magnitude as those reported for structurally related nicotinonitrile derivatives [28, 29, 30]. Ascorbic acid was used as a positive control, with an IC50 of 15.14 ppm, underscoring that, while our compounds are less potent than the classical ascorbic acid scaffold, they still possess comparable percentage inhibition to positive control, particularly compounds 3a, 3d, 3e, and 3g (Table 2 and Figure 5). However, the percentage inhibition of the compounds 3a–g was determined at 200 µg/mL, but the ascorbic acid was obtained at 100 µg/mL; therefore, these values of percentage inhibition were not directly compared. Accordingly, antioxidant activity was assessed mainly by the concentration‐dependent IC50 values, and a moderate radical‐scavenging activity was obtained for the most active derivatives.

Compounds 3a, 3d, 3e, and 3g were shown to be the most active in terms of antioxidant activity, with % inhibition ranging from 80.10% to 85.68%. This percent inhibition value was comparable to or greater than that of the positive control ascorbic acid (77.69%). Of these four compounds, compound 3d showed the greatest activity and thus exhibited the lowest IC50 (57.47 ± 1.17 ppm). Although it remained less potent than ascorbic acid (IC50 = 15.14 ± 0.52 µg/mL). In contrast, compound 3f showed very poor activity (% inhibition = 38.15%) and also showed a very high IC50 (> 200 ppm), indicating its inability to effectively scavenge free radicals.

The dichlorothiophene ring present in all derivatives acts as a hydrophobic anchor that may enhance lipophilicity and cellular uptake, while also providing a conjugated system that stabilizes the radical adduct. Therefore, the antioxidant behavior of these compounds is due to their ability to influence how electrons move within them (electronic effects) [31], the relative polarities of substituents attached to the ring, and the degree to which the unpaired electron is stabilized by resonance [31]. Compound 3d (Br‐phenyl) was found to be the most active compound tested, as it contains a moderately electron‐withdrawing but very polarizable group (the Br) [32]. Therefore, we would expect that this compound would better stabilize radicals than other members of the series via inductive effects and by allowing for better delocalization of the unpaired electron. Both 3b (F‐phenyl) and 3c (Cl‐phenyl) displayed weaker activity with IC50 values greater than 100 ppm. We suspect that the stronger electron‐withdrawing properties of F and Cl decrease electron density on the aromatic ring [33], thus reducing its ability to donate an H or an electron to the DPPH radical.

Compounds 3a and 3e that had methoxy groups present moderate activity as antioxidants with an IC50 value of approximately 70–73 ppm. Methoxy groups may increase antioxidant potential by contributing electron density via resonance to facilitate either hydrogen atom or electron transfer to neutralize free radicals [34]. The fact that compounds 3a and 3e exhibited similar levels of activity suggests that having one or multiple methoxy groups present in the structure of the compound is beneficial. The compound 3g (Ar = nitrophenyl), although displaying a high level of % inhibition (80.10%), exhibited a higher IC50 (91.21 ppm) than both 3a and 3e. Although nitro groups have significant electron‐withdrawing properties, they can also contribute to resonance stabilization, which can result in the ability for the nitro group to scavenge radicals at higher concentration levels while exhibiting less efficiency at lower concentrations.

Compound 3f was inactive (IC50 > 200 ppm) due to steric inhibition resulting from the combination of an ortho‐bromo and a para‐methoxy group on the phenyl ring, which would prevent the phenyl ring from achieving sufficient flatness to allow for the phenyl ring to adequately overlap with the nicotinonitrile core. Additionally, the electron‐withdrawing effect of the bromine could cancel out the electron‐donating effect of the methoxy, resulting in a net lower radical‐scavenging capacity. The DPPH assay shows clearly that the substitution on the aryl moiety has a significant impact on the antioxidant activity; compounds with electron‐donating or polarizable groups have shown higher free‐radical scavenger capacities than those with large or strong deactivation groups. In this series, compound 3d was found to be the most active antioxidant; thus, it could be a useful reference structure for subsequent optimization and biological assays.

Recent research on the antioxidant properties of small molecules containing catechol groups has also highlighted the substituent‐controlled antioxidant behavior with a focus on hydrogen atom transfer (HAT), single electron transfer (SET), and catechol–quinone conversion pathways [35, 36]. While the present nicotinonitrile derivatives are not catechol based, these results provide general confirmation of the idea that electronic distribution and the nature of the aryl substituents appear to have a significant effect on the radical‐scavenging properties.

The present results were also compared to those of other research groups for nicotinonitrile derivatives. The DPPH method was used for assessing a library of thiophene‐based nicotinonitrile, triazolopyridine, pyridotriazine, and pyridine–pyrazole compounds [37]. The antioxidant response was quite different in their series. The 5‐oxo‐3‐phenyl‐ and 5‐oxo‐3‐methyl‐dihydropyrazole conjugates were the most active, while the hydrazinonicotinonitrile precursor was moderately active, and most of the fused triazolopyridine and pyridotriazine conjugates exhibited weak or very weak activity. Together, all these data demonstrate the wide range of antioxidant activities found in the nicotinonitrile series and indicate that the type of substituents attached to the pyridine framework has a significant effect on the antioxidant activity.

Recent studies on natural antioxidant systems, including Balanites aegyptiaca aqueous extract and green tea flavonoids, have also used the DPPH assay to demonstrate measurable radical‐scavenging activity, supporting its value as a preliminary antioxidant screening method, although direct comparison with the present synthetic nicotinonitriles is limited by differences in chemical class and sample composition [38, 39, 40].

Antimicrobial Activity

The preliminary screening of the antimicrobial activity of compounds 3a–g showed that they displayed moderate to weak activity against Escherichia coli with MICs ranging from 100 to 200 µg/mL, coagulase‐negative staphylococci with MICs ranging from 100 µg/mL to Streptococcus species with MICs ranging from 50 to 100 µg/mL. Any of the results obtained are, however, not absolute values of comparable potency to established antibacterial drugs, as a reference antibiotic was not tested at the same time [41, 42].

An antimicrobial screening of the 2‐methoxypyridine‐3‐carbonitrile series (3a–g) demonstrates a particular pattern of SAR that strongly depends upon the electronic and steric character of the aryl substituents. The series showed a general baseline MIC of 100 ppm against the gram‐negative host E. coli except for compound 3e, which showed much smaller potencies (MIC 200 ppm). The latter can be explained by the steric and electronic hindrance caused by the 2,4‐dimethoxyphenyl substituent. The presence of two electron‐donating methoxy groups increases electron density of the aryl ring and may decrease the electrophilicity of the cyanopyridine core, and the ortho‐methoxy group probably causes the aryl ring to be out of coplanarity [43], thus creating steric bulk, which inhibits passage through the size‐restricted channels of the gram‐negative outer membrane [44]. However, 3e had a strictly bactericidal profile (MBC/MIC ratio of 1) [45], whereas the 4‐chloro (3c) and 4‐bromo (3f) analogues showed a bacteriostatic profile (MBC > 200 ppm) (Table 3).

The result with the gram‐positive Streptococcus species was the most sensitive to the tested series (3a‐‐g) in which most of the derivatives had an MIC of 50 ppm. This increased vulnerability confirms the hypothesis that the lipophilic 2‐methoxy‐pyridine scaffold is highly permeative with regard to the gram‐positive bacteria peptidoglycan layer [46], but the lack of a similar deviation in the SAR was noted with compound 3c (4‐chlorophenyl), which exhibited a low potency of 100 ppm compared to that of 4‐fluoro (3b) and 4‐bromo (3d) counterparts.

Coagulase‐negative Staphylococcus (CoNS) activity matched remarkably across the series (MIC 100 ppm); the 2‐methoxy group greatly increases lipophilicity over 2‐amino or 2‐oxo precursors, which is essential in membrane transport, and the oxygen atom is a hydrogen bond acceptor essential in active site binding [47, 48]. The dichlorothienyl group serves as a lipophilic anchor, which causes hydrophobic collapse of the enzyme pockets and may cause a disruption of the lipid bilayer. This is supported by Sarojini et al., who found that a series of 2,5‐dichlorothienyl‐substituted thiazole derivatives had measurable antibacterial and antifungal activity, suggesting that the 2,5‐dichlorothienyl group may be responsible for antimicrobial activity when attached to appropriate heterocyclic structures [49]. Moreover, adoption of electron‐withdrawing groups (EWG) like nitro (3g) and halogenated (3b, 3d) groups tended to maintain or enhance potency and is probably due to the increased acidity of nearby protons or the ability to form a synergistic effect with partial enzyme inhibition by the electrophile combined with membrane stress to a lethal point [50].

The antibacterial activity of pyridine‐3‐carbonitrile derivatives is very different depending on the substituents and microorganisms. For instance, the 2‐(methyldithio)pyridine‐3‐carbonitrile exhibited MICs between 0.5 and > 64 µg/mL for various bacterial strains, with an MIC of 32 µg/mL for E. coli and 4 µg/mL for methicillin‐sensitive and methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus [51]. The compounds tested in the present study, on the other hand, exhibited MIC of 50–200 µg/mL, which was relatively low antibacterial activity. However, caution should be used when making direct numerical comparisons between the different studies, as the MIC may be influenced by the bacterial strain, inoculum size, growth medium, incubation conditions, and the testing protocol. Therefore, the present results are considered as preliminary screening results to be confirmed with a wider panel of bacteria and standard antibiotics, which are tested at the same time under the same conditions [52]. Recent studies have emphasized the value of combined antioxidant and antibacterial screening in different bioactive systems and support the broader relevance of evaluating both radical‐scavenging and antibacterial properties during preliminary biological assessment [52, 53, 54].

Conclusions

The present research demonstrates an unusual base‐induced nucleophilic substitution reaction that results in the production of 4‐aryl‐6‐(2,5‐dichlorothiophen‐3‐yl)‐2‐methoxy nicotinonitriles as major products instead of the expected fused furo[2,3‐b]pyridine derivatives. This synthetic method provides highly functionalized nicotinonitriles in good to excellent yields. The structural characterization of these compounds was accomplished using standard spectroscopic techniques and further confirmed by single‐crystal x‐ray diffraction studies on 3f, which revealed a distorted molecular geometry due to the twisting of both the dichlorothiophene and 3‐bromo‐4‐methoxyphenyl groups relative to the plane of the central pyridine ring, with angles of 7.93° and 36.98°, respectively.

This distortion is thought to be critical to how these molecules pack in the solid state and their ability to cross biological membranes and interact with potential receptor sites. The biological evaluation of this series has also identified compound 3d, which contains a polarizable bromine atom at the aryl position, as the most active antioxidant in the series (IC50 = 57.47 ± 1.17 ppm). These data suggest that the inductive effect of the aryl group plays a key role in stabilizing free radicals. In addition to demonstrating antioxidant activity, this series has shown good bacteriostatic activity toward gram‐positive bacteria, especially toward Streptococcus species, with minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) as low as 50 ppm. The incorporation of the hydrophilic 2‐methoxy group into the 2‐methoxynicotinonitrile structure and the lipophilic dichlorothienyl group may contribute to the enhanced membrane permeability and target site interaction of these compounds. Overall, the 2‐methoxynicotinonitrile scaffold has been established as a useful template for future modifications to produce multi‐targeted antimicrobial/antioxidant agents. Future studies will include a larger scaffold expansion, drug testing in wider and more resistant microbial panels with suitable reference antibiotics, and new cytotoxicity, selectivity, solubility, and preliminary pharmacokinetic parameters. These studies will be essential to specify the optimization potential and biological relevance of this series of 2‐methoxynicotinonitrile.

Study Limitations

This study has effectively established a new synthesis pathway along with preliminary biochemical activity; however, there are several issues to be considered in future studies. The biological assessment was limited to three bacterial strains, highlighting the need for broader assessments using multidrug‐resistant (MDR) clinical isolates and fungal pathogens in order to determine the full range of therapeutic applications. In addition, the lack of in vitro cytotoxicity testing utilizing mammalian cell lines limits the ability to establish a selectivity index, which is important to ensure the compounds do not exhibit non‐specific toxicity.

In addition to the electronic influences provided by the 4‐aryl‐substituted aryls, the 4‐dichlorothiophenyl moiety has been a constant structural feature in these compounds; therefore, the extent of the contributions to the SAR of the pharmacophore from the dichlorothiophenyl moiety relative to the other sulfur‐containing bioisosters is still unknown. Lastly, while the lipophilic character provided by the 2‐methoxy and halo groups is advantageous for the penetration of bacteria into their membranes, the high lipophilicity may present problems in terms of aqueous solubility and in vivo pharmacokinetic delivery that will need to be investigated in future formulation studies.

Experimental Section

Materials

Chemicals were all purchased from commercial supplies. Solvents were dried and purified according to the standard procedures. 4‐Aryl‐6‐(2,5‐dichlorothiophen‐3‐yl)‐1,2‐dihydro‐2‐oxopyridine‐3‐carbonitrile derivatives 1(a–g) were synthesized according to the literature procedures [55, 56]. Nicotinonitriles 2(a–g) were prepared according to the literature procedure [14].

Instrumentation

1H (500 MHz) and 13C (125 MHz) NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker Ultra Shield 500 MHz spectrometer, Switzerland. Deuterochloroform (CDCl3) was used as the internal standard. The splitting patterns are reported as (m: multiplet, sext: sextet, pent: pentet, q: quartet, t: triplet, dd: doublet of a doublet, d: doublet, s: singlet). Chemical shifts δ are reported in parts per million (ppm). Infrared spectra of samples were recorded using an FT‐IR spectrophotometer (Nicolet Impact‐400). Reactions were monitored using silica gel (60 F254, Merck)‐coated thin layer chromatography (TLC).

Synthesis

Synthesis of 4‐Aryl‐2‐(Cyanomethoxy)‐6‐(2,5‐Dichlorothiophene‐3‐yl)Nicotinonitrile 2(a–g)

According to the literature procedure [14], a mixture of appropriate 2(1H)‐pyridone (1a–g, 1.0 mmol) and potassium carbonate (117 mg, 1.1 mmol) was dissolved in DMF (dry) and stirred at room temperature for 1 h. Chloroacetonitrile (127 µL, 2.0 mmol, in dry DMF) was dropwise added and stirred for 24 h at room temperature. The solution mixture was then poured into ice water. The formed solid was filtered off, washed with distilled water, and dried without any additional purification.

Synthesis of 4‐Aryl‐6‐(2,5‐Dichlorothiophen‐3‐yl)‐2‐Methoxynicotinonitrile 3(a–g)

In a round‐bottom flask, 2‐cyanomethoxynicotinonitrile (2a–g, 1.0 mmol) in methanolic solution of sodium methoxide (0.04 M, 25 mL) was refluxed at 65°C for 6–8 h. The precipitate formed was filtered off, washed with methanol, dried, and purified using column chromatography by CHCl3:cyclohexane (40:60) as a mobile phase to obtain 2‐methoxynicotinonitrile 3(a–g).

Assessment of Antioxidant Activity

The free radical scavenging capability of the synthesized nicotinonitriles 3(a–g) was evaluated in vitro using the 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay, following previously established protocols [60]. A stock solution of the stable radical DPPH (0.1 mM) was freshly prepared in methanol and kept in a dark container at room temperature. Stock solutions of synthesized compounds 3(a–g) were prepared using methanol and subjected to serial dilution to yield a concentration range of 3.125 to 200 µg/mL2.

For the assay, 50 µL of each compound concentration was mixed with 150 µL of the 0.1 mM DPPH solution using a 96‐well microplate. The reaction mixtures were shaken and incubated for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The reference compound was ascorbic acid and was used in a range of 1.562 to 100 µg/mL. The percent DPPH free radical scavenging activity was determined by the following equation: %inhibition=Acontrol−AsampleAcontrol∗100 where A control is the absorption of the DPPH solution without the test compound, and A sample is the absorption of the test compound/DPPH solution mixture. The IC50 value, which is defined as the concentration required to inhibit the scavenging of 50% of the DPPH radicals, is obtained through graphically plotting the percent inhibition versus the compound concentration.

Assessment of Antimicrobial Activity

The antimicrobial activity of the synthesized compounds 3(a–g) was tested against three different bacterial strains to demonstrate both gram‐negative (Escherichia coli) and gram‐positive bacteria (coagulase‐negative Staphylococcus and Streptococcus species).

Determination of Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)

The MIC for synthesized compounds was determined using the broth microdilution method using 96‐well microtiter plates, following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [14]. Stock solutions of the synthesized compounds were prepared in methanol and subsequently diluted with sterile Mueller‐Hinton broth to obtain a series of test concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 200 µg/mL.

A standardized bacterial culture was established for use as a starting point for achieving an initial inoculum density of approximately 1× 105 CFU/mL. Each well with the tested compound was inoculated with 100 µL volume of this standardized bacterial culture. The plates were then placed in an incubator set at 37°C for a period of 24 h. The MIC was determined as the lowest concentration of the compound where there was no visible evidence of bacterial growth (turbidity) relative to the growth control. All assays contained solvent control wells and sterility control wells so that valid results could be obtained from the data collected.

Determination of Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC)

To assess the bactericidal potential of the synthesized compounds, the MBC was evaluated by sub‐culturing samples from the MIC wells that showed no visible growth. Aliquots (10 µL) from these clear wells were spot‐inoculated onto fresh nutrient agar plates and incubated at 37°C for an additional 24 h. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration of the compound that resulted in no colony formation on the agar plate. Based on the ratio of MBC to MIC, the compounds were characterized as exhibiting either bacteriostatic or bactericidal activity.
