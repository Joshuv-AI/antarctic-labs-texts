Neutrophil Secretory Proteins Inhibit Calcium Oxalate Crystallisation and Crystal Growth, but Promote Crystal Aggregation
Authors: Chanettee Lertprapai, Paleerath Peerapen, Visith Thongboonkerd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNeutrophil secretory proteins are frequently found in calcium oxalate (CaOx) kidney stone matrix, suggesting their involvement in stone pathogenesis, but with unclear mechanisms. We therefore investigated the effects of secretome (a set of secretory proteins) from CaOx monohydrate (COM)‐exposed versus control dHL‐60 (neutrophil‐like) cells on crystal nucleation (crystallisation), growth, aggregation and invasion. Quantitative proteomics was also performed to identify significantly altered secretory proteins, followed by analyses of their physicochemical properties and biological relevance. The data demonstrated that both COM‐treated and control secretomes inhibited crystallisation and crystal growth, but the inhibitory effects from the COM‐treated secretome were slightly weaker. By contrast, both of them promoted crystal aggregation, with the more potent effect from the COM‐treated secretome. However, neither of them had a modulatory effect on crystal invasion. Quantitative proteomics revealed 20 decreased and 9 increased proteins in the COM‐treated secretome compared with the control. Analyses of physicochemical properties showed that the increased secretory proteins tended to have a lower instability index and a smaller number of oxalate‐binding motifs/protein. Main molecular functions of the increased group were catalytic, hydrolase and transporter activities, whereas those of the decreased group included RNA binding, molecular adaptor activity and catalytic activity. These data indicate that the neutrophil secretome inhibits crystallisation and crystal growth but promotes crystal aggregation. The COM‐treated secretome exerts weaker inhibitory effects on crystallisation and growth but has a stronger promoting effect on crystal aggregation. These findings enhance our understanding of the roles of neutrophils in kidney stone pathogenesis.Keywords: immune, kidney stone, modulators, proteomics, secretome, stone modulation

Introduction

Kidney stone disease, characterised by intrarenal accumulation of crystalline materials together with organic substances, is a common disease with an average prevalence of ~10% of the world population [1, 2, 3, 4]. Its incidence varies roughly from 54.2 to 900 affected individuals per 100 000 annually [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Calcium oxalate (CaOx), especially its monohydrate form (COM), is the most common stone type (70%–80% of all kidney stone cases) [6, 7]. While Randall's plaque is a common priming event for CaOx stones, crystallisation, crystal growth, crystal aggregation and crystal invasion are also essential for stone development, with supersaturated urine and imbalance of urinary stone modulators as critical driving forces [8, 9].

Neutrophils are the most abundant, short‐lived, circulating leukocytes with a crucial responsibility to protect the human body as the first‐line defence to cope with invading microbial organisms [10]. In response to infectious agents and non‐infectious stimuli, neutrophils produce and release several groups of mediators, such as cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, extracellular vesicles (EVs) and granule proteins [11], to generate immune responses and get rid of invading agents and toxic substances. Cytokines (interleukin (IL)‐1α, IL‐1β, IL‐6, IL‐16, IL‐18, etc.), chemokines (chemokine (C‐X‐C motif) ligand (CXCL)‐1, CXCL‐2, CXCL‐8, etc.), and growth factors (granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF), etc.) are preformed and later stored in secretory granules or de novo synthesised via endoplasmic reticulum (ER)‐Golgi network [11, 12]. Neutrophils can secrete these mediators constitutively and upon activation [12]. The release may be mediated through the classical secretory pathway for de novo synthesised proteins or upon ligand‐receptor signalling for preformed mediators [13].

Similarly, neutrophil‐derived EVs can be produced and released both constitutively and upon activation [14]. There are two subtypes of neutrophil EVs. The small EVs or exosomes are generally 30–100 nm in size and are formed by inward membrane invagination, endosomal fusion, multivesicular body formation and release to the extracellular space [14, 15]. Another subtype is the large EVs or microvesicles (also known as microparticles or ectosomes) that are generally 100–1000 nm in size and are formed by outward plasma membrane budding or shedding of cellular materials such as cytoskeleton, granules, mitochondria and so on [14, 16]. Among these, the majority of neutrophil‐derived EVs are microvesicles with sizes of 100–800 nm [14, 16].

As a member of granulocytic leukocytes, neutrophils contain several groups of intracellular granules that are formed during neutrophil differentiation in bone marrow [17]. These cytoplasmic granules contain many types of proteins, including membrane receptors, antimicrobial glycoproteins and potent glycosylated hydrolytic enzymes. Based on their compositions and functions, these granules can be classified as azurophilic granules, specific granules, gelatinase granules and secretory vesicles [18, 19]. Azurophilic granules comprise peroxidase‐positive proteins, of which the majority are myeloperoxidase, elastase, α‐defensin and azurocidin, with a main function to kill microbes in phagosomes [17, 18, 19, 20]. Myeloperoxidase reacts with hydrogen peroxide, producing hypochlorous acid and other chlorinated compounds that attack and aggregate proteins, lipids and DNA of microbes [21, 22]. Specific granules contain peroxidase‐negative proteins. The well‐known proteins in this group are lactoferrin and neutrophil gelatinase‐associated protein (NGAL), which have broad‐spectrum antimicrobial activity by depleting iron that is essential for bacterial growth [17, 19, 22, 23, 24]. Gelatinase granules are also peroxidase‐negative. Matrix metalloproteases (MMPs) are the major proteins in this group [17, 18, 19, 23]. Gelatinase granules are released to the cell surface to degrade ECM for neutrophil transmigration and extravasation through endothelium [17, 18, 19, 23]. Unlike the others, secretory vesicles do not bud from the Golgi but are formed through an endocytic process [17, 23]. Their contents are mainly plasma proteins (such as albumin) that are necessary for firm adherence to vascular endothelium [17, 23].

Degranulation, the release of neutrophil granule proteins, is receptor‐mediated [25, 26]. Differences in the signal threshold of each granule type to Ca2+ activation cause the selective release of granule proteins [27, 28]. Among various types of neutrophil granules, secretory vesicles are most sensitive (lowest threshold) to Ca2+ activation, whereas azurophilic granules have the highest threshold to Ca2+ activation [27]. The selective release process is to prevent tissue damage from unnecessary degranulation that can release potent enzymes or inducer proteins.

In addition to microbes, other stimuli and inorganic materials such as CaOx can activate neutrophils [29]. In stone formers' renal papillary biopsies, CaOx stones seem to come along with tissue inflammation. Almost all of CaOx stone formers have tissue inflammation at stone deposition sites [30, 31]. In addition to tissue macrophages, neutrophils have been proven to get involved in the inflammation associated with kidney stones as their specific genes and proteins have been found in the stone matrix, renal papillary tissue and urine of stone formers [32, 33, 34]. Interestingly, neutrophil granule proteins (i.e., myeloperoxidase, cathepsin G, lactoferrin and defensin) have been found in the stone matrix [34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41], suggesting roles for neutrophil secretory proteins in kidney stone pathogenesis. However, the mechanisms by which neutrophil secretory proteins play in kidney stone development remain unknown.

This study, therefore, investigated the effects of secretome (a set of secretory proteins) from COM‐exposed neutrophils (termed as “COM‐treated secretome”) on crystal nucleation (crystallisation), growth, aggregation and invasion compared with those secreted from control neutrophils without COM exposure (termed as “control secretome”). Quantitative proteomics was also performed to determine significantly altered secretory proteins in the COM‐treated secretome compared with the control secretome. Physicochemical properties, biological processes, molecular functions and cellular compartments of the significantly altered secretory proteins were also analysed.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Differentiation

HL‐60 cells (ATCC) were employed to generate human neutrophil‐like (dHL‐60) cells using a protocol published recently [42]. Briefly, HL‐60 cells were maintained and grown in complete RPMI medium (Gibco/Invitrogen) containing 10% heat‐inactivated foetal bovine serum (Gibco), 60 U/mL penicillin G (Sigma‐Aldrich), and 60 mg/mL streptomycin (Sigma‐Aldrich) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The cells were then differentiated into dHL‐60 (neutrophil‐like) cells by incubating with 1.25% (vol/vol) dimethyl sulfoxide (Sigma‐Aldrich) in the complete medium for 6 days. Phenotypes and markers specific for neutrophils were also confirmed [42].

COM Crystal Preparation

COM crystals were prepared following a standard protocol published recently [43]. Details are also provided in Supporting Information Methods.

COM Treatment and Cell Death Assay

The dHL‐60 cells were seeded into each well of a 6‐well plate (1 × 107 cells/well) in serum‐free RPMI medium without or with COM crystals at 100 μg/mL. The cells were then incubated at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 2, 4, 6, 8 or 10 h. At the indicated time points, the cells were washed three times with PBS, incubated with FITC‐conjugated annexin V (BD Biosciences) in annexin V‐binding buffer (BD Biosciences) for 15 min, and then with propidium iodide (BD Biosciences) for 2 min. Cell death was then quantified by using the BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Secretome Collection

The dHL‐60 cells were cultured in the serum‐free medium without or with COM crystals as mentioned above. At the optimal time‐point (2 h), at which cells remained healthy without a significant increase in cell death under a serum‐free environment, culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min to remove debris. The clear supernatant was then dialysed with deionised water to remove salts/contaminants, lyophilised and dissolved in a crystallisation buffer (90 mM NaCl in 10 mM Tris–HCl; pH 7.4). Protein concentration was equally adjusted to 1 μg/μL across all secretome samples.

COM Crystallisation Assay

Crystallisation assay was performed with 4 μL of 1 μg/μL control or COM‐treated secretome or 4 μL crystallisation buffer without proteins (blank control). See greater detail in [44, 45] and Supporting Information Methods.

COM Crystal Growth Assay

Crystal growth assay was performed with 4 μL of 1 μg/μL control or COM‐treated secretome or 4 μL crystallisation buffer without proteins (blank control). See greater detail in [46, 47] and Supporting Information Methods.

COM Crystal Aggregation Assay

Crystal aggregation assay was performed with 4 μL of 1 μg/μL control or COM‐treated secretome or 4 μL crystallisation buffer without proteins (blank control). See greater detail in [48, 49] and Supporting Information Methods.

COM Crystal Invasion Assay

Crystal invasion assay was performed with 4 μL of 1 μg/μL control secretome, COM‐treated secretome, albumin (positive control) or lysozyme (negative control), or 4 μL crystallisation buffer without proteins (blank control). See greater details in [50, 51] and Supporting Information Methods.

In‐Solution Tryptic Digestion and Quantitative Proteomics

The control and COM‐treated secretome samples (30 μg each) were subjected to in‐solution tryptic digestion and label‐free quantitative proteomics as described in [52, 53] and Supporting Information Methods.

Validation of MS/MS Data by Western Blotting

The control and COM‐treated secretome samples (30 μg each) were subjected to Western blotting as described previously [54, 55]. Primary antibodies included mouse monoclonal anti‐human cyclophilin A, anti‐human α‐enolase, and anti‐GAPDH antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) (1:1000–2000 in 1% skim milk/PBS), whereas the secondary antibody was horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated rabbit anti‐mouse IgG (Sigma‐Aldrich) (1:20000 in 1% skim milk/PBS).

Analyses of Physicochemical Properties and Biological Relevance of Significantly Altered Secretory Proteins

Physicochemical properties, including isoelectric point (pI) and instability index, of significantly altered secretory proteins were examined using the ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/), whereas the number of oxalate‐binding motifs/protein was analysed using the OxaBIND tool (https://www.stonemod.org/oxabind.php). Calcium‐binding motifs/protein and the relevance of these altered secretory proteins in biological processes, molecular functions, and cell components were evaluated through the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/). Reactome pathway enrichment analysis of all significantly altered proteins was performed using the ShyniGO tool (v.0.85) (https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/).

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative analyses were performed using the data derived from three independent experiments. Unpaired Student's t‐test was used for comparisons between two groups, whereas ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. p‐value less than 0.05 was set as a significant threshold.

Results

Optimal Time‐Point for the Secretome Collection

The optimal time‐point for the collection of secretome from dHL‐60 cells was carefully defined to avoid contamination from proteins associated with cell death or disruption under a toxic stress, particularly in a serum‐free environment. Cell death was thus quantified after the cells were incubated without or with COM crystals for 2, 4, 6, 8 or 10 h. Without crystals, cell death remained unchanged till the end of the observation (Figure 1). However, COM crystals caused a progressive increase in cell death, starting from 4‐h incubation. The 2‐h time‐point, at which cell death remained unchanged even with COM crystal exposure, was selected as the optimal time‐point to collect the secretome for subsequent investigations in the present study (Figure 1). Note that the 2‐h duration is sufficient to determine changes in secretory proteins that can occur very early (within minutes) [56, 57].

Physicochemical Properties and Biological Relevance of Significantly Altered Secretory Proteins

All significantly altered secretory proteins (Figure 7A) were subjected to analyses of their physicochemical properties. Analyses using the ProtParam tool revealed that pI values and proportions of acidic and basic proteins of the increased secretory proteins and the decreased secretory proteins were comparable (Figure 7B,C). However, the increased secretory proteins had a lower instability index (Figure 7D). As a result, the increased secretory proteins had a greater proportion of stable proteins (Figure 7E). Analyses using the OxaBIND tool revealed that the number of oxalate‐binding motifs per protein tended to be smaller in the increased secretory proteins (Figure 7F). Also, the proportion of oxalate‐binding proteins was lower in the increased secretory proteins (Figure 7G). Analyses of calcium‐binding sites per protein and proportion of calcium‐binding proteins showed no significant differences between the two groups (Figure 7H,I).

Discussion

The involvement of neutrophils and their secretory proteins in kidney stone disease is well documented. Evidence from gene microarrays, proteomic analysis and immunohistochemical staining has shown that neutrophil secretory proteins (NGAL, elastase, S100A8, etc.) and their genes are strongly expressed in renal papillary tissues of CaOx stone formers and are also found in their urine [32, 33, 34]. By proteomic analysis, several proteins have been identified from CaOx stone matrix. These proteins can be categorised into three groups, including blood/plasma proteins (albumin, prothrombin, protein Z, etc.), renal cellular proteins (Tamm‐Horsfall protein, heat shock proteins, etc.), and inflammatory proteins [58, 59]. Interestingly, a variety of proteins unique in neutrophil azurophilic granules (e.g., myeloperoxidase, defensin, lactoferrin and cathepsin G) are the major inflammatory proteins found in CaOx stone matrix [34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41]. These findings correlate well with the results from our recent in vitro study, demonstrating that COM crystals potently activate degranulation of azurophilic granules, as shown by the up‐regulation of CD63 (an azurophilic membrane marker) and the increased α‐defensin secretion (azurophilic granule protein) in the COM‐exposed neutrophils [42].

In the present study, we examined the effects of secretory proteins from dHL‐60 (neutrophil‐like) cells on the individual steps of COM stone formation. From crystal assays, secretory proteins inhibited COM crystallisation and crystal growth, but promoted COM aggregation. However, secretory proteins had no effects on COM crystal invasion. COM crystallisation is initiated by the supersaturation of Ca2+ and C2O4 2− in the urine. In addition, Ca2+ and C2O4 2− ions can be associated into ion pairs or pre‐nucleation clusters (PNCs), which dynamically break off into ion pairs or smaller clusters and grow further by either restructuring or agglomeration [60]. As shown by structural analysis, amorphous CaOx nanoparticles (50–500 nm in diameter) are formed via agglomeration of ion pairs or PNCs [61, 62]. The agglomerates then further enlarge to a critical size and develop into a crystal shape [60, 61, 62]. It has been shown that polyanionic molecules (citrate, polyacrylic acid, etc.) can exert the “colloidal stabilisation effect” that prevents agglomeration of PNCs and amorphous COM nanoparticles [60, 61, 62, 63]. Negative charges of polyanionic molecules adhered to PNCs and amorphous COM nanoparticle surfaces cause electrostatic repulsion, allowing disagglomeration of PNCs and amorphous COM nanoparticles [60, 61, 62, 63].

Crystal growth is a process following crystallisation caused by the addition of Ca2+ and C2O4 2− ions to the crystal surface, resulting in crystal size enlargement [64]. These ions diffuse onto the crystal surface and favour incorporation into particular binding sites, called “kink” and “step,” due to immediate bonding with neighbouring ions rather than the other types of binding sites, at which the incorporated ions are easy to dissociate [64]. Therefore, a tight binding to the crystal surface occurs, facilitating further deposition of Ca2+ and C2O4 2− ions and crystal enlargement. Crystal growth can be inhibited in several ways. Blocking kink and step pinning are a few examples [65]. These two inhibitory mechanisms are mediated by occupying the inhibitors at the kink or step binding site, thereby blocking further attachment of Ca2+ and C2O4 2− ions. Interestingly, it has been shown that several COM crystal growth inhibitors, which comprise negatively charged groups (carboxylic, phosphate, etc.), may compete with the negatively charged C2O4 2− to conjugate with Ca2+ on COM crystal surfaces [66].

Related to these circumstances, analyses of physicochemical properties of significantly altered proteins in the COM‐treated secretome revealed that the dHL‐60 cells secreted a variety of mostly small proteins (median molecular weight at 35.7 kDa) with anionic pI (median pI of 5.99) in response to COM crystal exposure. However, the proportion of acidic proteins and basic proteins in control and COM‐treated secretomes did not significantly differ. Therefore, other factors were more likely to be involved in differences in the degree of inhibitory activities of the control and COM‐treated secretomes. We therefore investigated other physicochemical properties of these proteins. The analysis showed that the instability index and the proportion of oxalate‐binding proteins in both groups significantly differed.

The instability index is the in vivo [67] and in vitro [68] protein stability prediction based on dipeptide composition of proteins. Stable proteins (instability index < 40) have longer half‐life (> 16 h), whereas unstable proteins (instability index > 40) tend to enter autoproteolysis or self‐cleavage, causing the shorter half‐life (< 5 h). N—O(S) acyl shift mechanism is suggested to get involved in this self‐cleavage process. This mechanism is initiated when the sidechain of threonine, serine or cysteine reacts to the preceding residue, generating intermediates (such as transient ester and thioester) and later end‐products including N‐ and C‐termini moieties [69, 70, 71]. The greater proportion of unstable proteins in the control secretome could generate a greater number of these intermediates and end‐products, all of which might play roles in the inhibition of COM crystallisation and crystal growth, resulting in the more potent inhibitory effects of the control secretome on COM crystallisation and crystal growth compared with the COM‐treated secretome.

The binding of C2O4 2− ions by oxalate‐binding proteins is another mechanism that is supposed to inhibit COM crystallisation and crystal growth by the formation of the complex between C2O4 2− ions and oxalate‐binding proteins, leading to a lower degree of C2O4 2− ion saturation or even its unsaturation [72]. Using the OxaBIND tool, we found that the proportion of oxalate‐binding proteins was significantly different between control and COM‐treated secretomes. The greater proportion of oxalate‐binding proteins in the control secretome might help form complexes with oxalate ions, causing significantly decreased C2O4 2− ions and a greater degree of inhibitory effects of the control secretome on COM crystallisation and growth compared with the COM‐treated secretome.

Crystal aggregation is the process by which crystals adhere to others and form crystal aggregates. Generally, aggregation or dispersion of particles is determined by the summation of repulsive (e.g., electrical double layer force) and attractive (e.g., van der Waals force, electrostatic force) forces [73, 74]. Particle aggregation can be mediated by several kinds of macromolecules (surfactants, polymers, proteins, etc.) through different mechanisms (either decreasing repulsive forces or increasing attractive forces) [75, 76, 77, 78]. For protein‐mediated particle aggregation, bridging aggregation is the most popular model that has been studied in a variety of particles, including COM crystals [75]. This aggregation occurs when proteins, which are oppositely charged to the crystals, adhere onto the crystal surface but with partial surface coverage (e.g., at low protein concentrations) [73, 74, 77, 78]. The adhered proteins generate charge patches or charge heterogeneity on the crystal surface that interact with the bare surface of the other crystals, resulting in the increased attractive electrostatic interaction and crystal aggregation [73, 74, 78]. This mechanistic model has been shown in the previous study that desialylated Tamm‐Horsfall protein induces COM crystal aggregation at low protein concentrations, but interferes with the aggregation at higher concentrations [77, 79, 80, 81]. In the present study, the secretome from the neutrophil‐like cells contained a variety of positively and negatively charged proteins with various concentrations that could promote the occurrence of bridging aggregation.

Among the significantly increased secretory proteins identified in the present study, S100A8, also known as calgranulin A, is an EF‐hand calcium‐binding protein in the S100 family. It is expressed mainly in the cells of myeloid origin and those involved in the innate immunity, especially neutrophils [82, 83]. S100A8 and some other proteins in this family comprise approximately 40% of neutrophil cytosolic proteins [82, 83]. After activation, they are secreted extracellularly as damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and then interact with several target proteins and receptors such as toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4) and receptor for advanced glycation end‐products (RAGE), resulting in inflammatory responses [82, 83]. The predominant presence of S100A8 in CaOx stone matrix and stone formers' urine indicates its clinical relevance in CaOx kidney stone formation [34, 40]. Neutrophils secrete S100A8 and other S100 member proteins through non‐classical secretion, passive release from necrotic cells, and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation [82, 83]. In both intracellular and extracellular environments, S100A8 usually forms a homodimer and is also associated (non‐covalently) with S100A9 (calgranulin B) to create a heterodimer called calprotectin [82, 83]. In our present study, S100A9 was also identified from both control and COM‐treated secretomes, but did not show a significant difference between the two groups (Table S1). In the presence of Ca2+, S100A8/A9 heterodimers further form a heterotetramer upon Ca2+‐binding, and each heterotetramer can bind 8 Ca2+ ions [82, 83]. With this unique feature, the S100A8/A9 oligomer is an essential component of a fibrillar amyloid complex, which is highly stable and protease‐resistant in a Ca2+‐dependent manner [84]. Interestingly, the strongly expressed calprotectin and amyloid complexes have been found in calcified areas of prostatic corpora amylacea and calculi [85, 86] and atherosclerotic arteries [87]. These findings signify the involvement of S100A8 and its oligomer in the dystrophic calcification process. With these properties, we therefore postulate that S100A8 and its oligomer may act as a cross‐linker to bring adjacent COM crystals together, thereby promoting crystal aggregation.

Peptidylprolyl isomerase A (cyclophilin A) is another significantly increased secretory protein identified in the present study. It is a ubiquitous catalytic enzyme produced and secreted extracellularly by several cell types (e.g., endothelial cells, macrophages and renal tubular cells) under stress conditions [88]. Cyclophilin A plays roles in protein folding and intracellular/extracellular signalling [88]. An electrostatic interaction between cyclophilin A and CaOx crystals can promote CaOx crystal aggregation [89]. The increase of cyclophilin A in the neutrophil secretome found in the present study and its interactions with CaOx crystals [89] and presence in kidney stone matrix [90, 91] observed previously indicate its pathogenic roles in kidney stone disease.

Finally, the roles of neutrophil EVs should not be overlooked. Membranes of intact neutrophil EVs have negative charges and carry a variety of molecules, such as adhesion molecules (integrin, selectin, etc.), receptors and other proteins [14]. Their large surface‐to‐volume ratio enhances their efficacy for surface interaction. A previous study has shown that bacteria‐activated neutrophil EVs help bacterial aggregation via integrin‐ and actin‐dependent mechanisms [92]. Therefore, neutrophil EVs might also help promote COM crystal aggregation.

Although promising results were obtained, limitations in the present study should be noted. First, we used neutrophil‐like (dHL‐60) cells to investigate the effects of COM‐treated neutrophil secretome on kidney stone formation, mainly because of their advantage in long‐term manipulation and the large number of cells available for all experiments. The dHL‐60 cells provide a gene expression pattern closely resembling that of human neutrophils [93]. They express degranulation markers (CD63 and CD107a) and release multiple types of secretory granules typical for human neutrophils. However, not all the dHL‐60 cells behave with such a characteristic secretion of human neutrophils, as they were not fully differentiated [94]. Therefore, the interpretation of our results is limited to the neutrophil‐like cells, whereas validation in primary human neutrophils is still required.

Next, extracellular calcium influx is a regulator of neutrophil activation, governing key effector functions, including degranulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and secretion of inflammatory mediators. In the present study, dHL‐60 cells were incubated with COM crystals in serum‐free RPMI medium for 2 h to collect the secretome. Although COM is the most thermodynamically stable form of CaOx crystals (compared with dihydrate and trihydrate forms), characterised by its low solubility under physiologic conditions [95, 96], the potential for partial dissolution of COM crystals in the RPMI medium could not be entirely excluded. Therefore, the subtle amount of soluble Ca2+ ions released from the solid‐phase COM crystals should be considered. This raised the possibility that the observed dHL‐60 secretome was not solely driven by direct crystal‐cell interactions, but might also be partially mediated by the increased extracellular Ca2+ ions.

From an anatomical and physiological perspective, CaOx crystallisation is generally understood to occur within the tubular lumen, particularly in distal tubules and collecting ducts, as well as the renal pelvis, rather than in the renal interstitium, where neutrophils are recruited during inflammation. However, intratubular crystal formation can cause tubular epithelial injury, leading to disruption of epithelial integrity that can induce crystal translocation into the interstitium, thereby enabling direct contact with infiltrating neutrophils [97]. In addition, CaOx crystals adhered to the apical surface of tubular epithelial cells can trigger the release of chemokines and DAMPs, promoting recruitment and activation of neutrophils in adjacent areas [98]. Furthermore, under inflammatory conditions, neutrophils can undergo transepithelial migration into the tubular lumen, as observed in models of kidney injury and infection, providing another potential route for direct crystal‐neutrophil interactions [99]. In the present study, dHL‐60 cells were stimulated by COM crystals in serum‐free RPMI medium, which does not fully recapitulate the physicochemical environment of distal tubular fluid and urine. While studying the effects of COM crystals on neutrophil secretome using the serum‐free RPMI medium allowed a confident interpretation of the data that were neither contaminated by the serum proteins that were introduced during the culture nor the effects of other confounding factors that could also affect neutrophil cell biology (e.g., urinary and tubular fluid constituents), the direct physiological translation was limited. To address this limitation, future studies should incorporate urine‐mimetic or distal tubular fluid‐like medium to better approximate the in vivo conditions. Comparative analyses of neutrophil secretome profiles under RPMI versus physiologically relevant media would also provide important insights into how the environmental factors modulate neutrophil responses to COM crystals.

Conclusions

Both COM‐treated and control secretomes inhibited crystallisation and crystal growth, but the inhibitory effects from the COM‐treated secretome were slightly weaker. By contrast, both of them promoted crystal aggregation, with the more potent effect from the COM‐treated secretome. However, neither of them had a modulatory effect on crystal invasion. Composition analyses via quantitative proteomics revealed 20 decreased and 9 increased proteins in the COM‐treated secretome compared with the control. The increased secretory proteins tended to have a lower instability index and a smaller number of oxalate‐binding motifs/protein. Main molecular functions of the increased group were catalytic, hydrolase and transporter activities, whereas those of the decreased group included RNA binding, molecular adaptor activity and catalytic activity. These data indicate that the secretome derived from the neutrophil‐like cells inhibits crystallisation and crystal growth but promotes crystal aggregation. The COM‐treated secretome exerts weaker inhibitory effects on crystallisation and growth but has a stronger promoting effect on crystal aggregation. These findings enhance our understanding of the roles of neutrophils in kidney stone pathogenesis.
