Impact of Helicobacter pylori on immunotherapy in gastric cancer
Authors: Jing Li, Zenghong Wu, Rong Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study reviews the contrasting finding regarding the impact of Helicobacter pylori infection on the efficacy of immunotherapy in gastric cancer (GC). While a large retrospective study reported a positive association between H. pylori infection and progression-free survival (PFS)/overall survival (OS) in patients with GC undergoing PD-1/PD-L1 therapy, previous studies had given rise to a contrary outcome. Potential explanations of the inconsistency include the divergent immune responses induced by different H. pylori status (current infection, postinfection and negative status) directly, as well as the indirect influence of gut microbiota alterations. Tumor molecular subtypes, particularly Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection status, may also play a role in the discrepancy by altering the profile of immune infiltration. However, data on EBV status are absent in studies showing H. pylori infection as an unfavorable factor for GC immunotherapy. Moreover, a combined positive score (CPS) <1 might exert a positive influence, as more patients with CPS<1 enrolled in the study reported a positive association between H. pylori infection and immunotherapy. The review highlights the need for further research to clarify the complex interplay between H. pylori infection, immune response, and immunotherapy efficacy in GC, aiming to develop more targeted and personalized treatment strategies.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Gastric Cancer

We have read with great interest the recent publication titled ‘H. pylori and immunotherapy for gastrointestinal cancer’ by Jia et al.1 It is the largest retrospective study to assess the impact of H. pylori on immunotherapy in patients with gastrointestinal cancer to date. The study supports that the presence of H. pylori infection in patients with GC is significantly associated with longer immune-related PFS and OS than the H. pylori-negative patients treated with anti-programmed cell death protein 1/PD ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) therapy by shaping hot tumor microenvironments (TME). This observation is indeed intriguing and noteworthy. We extend our sincere appreciation to the authors for their valuable contribution to the pivotal realm of cancer research. Nevertheless, previous retrospective studies have shown H. pylori infection is associated with inferior survival in patients with GC treated with immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI), giving rise to a diametrically contrary outcome. Magahis et al2 and Che et al3 reported that patients with GC with H. pylori infection exhibit significantly shorter median PFS and OS post-ICI treatment compared with their H. pylori-negative counterparts. Meanwhile, Magahis et al also suggested that patients with prior infection have significantly shorter PFS and OS than the negative group, showing a similar detrimental response to immunotherapy. Regarding the influence of H. pylori infection to immunotherapy in GC, certain factors might contribute to the inconsistency.

One potential explanation given top priority to is the divergent immune responses in different H. pylori status, including current H. pylori infection (acute and chronic infection), postinfection and negative status. Acute H. pylori infection is characterized by heavy infiltrations of neutrophils, proinflammatory M1 macrophages and CD4+ T lymphocytes that produce cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-2, IFN-γ, IL-12. The immune responses exhibited a predominant TH1 and TH17 type response in gastric mucosa.4 Specific subtype of neutrophiles could affect the immunotherapy response in return.5 The swift infiltration in gastric mucosa from neutrophiles to monocytes is a symbol of chronic H. pylori infection, accompanied by the balance between TH1, TH17 and immune suppressor cells. At this stage, H. pylori infection can induce immune tolerance by regulating the tumor immune microenvironment, accompanied by PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint activation, which could weaken T-cell-mediated cytotoxic effect and lead to immune evasion. The influence of H. pylori on immune response would persist even after H. pylori eradication, showing a higher CD8+ T cells infiltration than uninfected controls, which remains unclear of the changes to PD-1/PD-L1 expression and immunotherapy. Besides the direct effect of H. pylori status to the immune system, it can also exert an indirect effect through gut microbiota. H. pylori infection leads to the reduced richness and evenness of gastric bacteria, and H. pylori eradication only partially restored microbial diversity. Several studies have verified that the altered microbiota and their metabolites can increase the release of cytokines and promote the infiltration of T cell, subsequently influencing anti-GC immunotherapy.6 It is reasonable to assume that the status and length of H. pylori infection may influence the effectiveness of immunotherapy by affecting the immune system to exhibit different response patterns both directly and indirectly. However, only Magahis et al enrolled a few postinfection patients. Meanwhile, the H. pylori-negative patients in Jia et al and Che et al might consist of post-infection participants, which may lead to different immune responses, resulting in different responses to ICIs treatment in GC. This emphasizes that we should evaluate the immune response related to H. pylori, rather than H. pylori infection, before using ICI therapy.

Another potential explanation for this discrepancy comes from the tumor molecular subtypes, including EBV subtype with extreme DNA hypermethylation. As an estimated 8%–10% GCs attributed to EBV infection, there is no correlation between EBV infection rate and H. pylori infection rate in GCs. EBV infection can alter the profile of immune infiltration and TME in the process of gastric carcinogenesis, exhibiting an inflamed-immune phenotype with increased T-cell and B-cell infiltration. The status of EBV is an important prognostic factor for GC immunotherapy. Patients with GC with EBV infection may derive more benefits from anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy. The study of Jia et al included 636 EBV-negative and microsatellite-stable patients with GC. The subgroup analysis revealed no significant differences in neither PFS nor OS between H. pylori-positive and H. pylori-negative patients in either EBV-negative patients or microsatellite instability-high patients. However, data on EBV status are absent in Magahis et al and Che et al, which show H. pylori infection as an unfavorable factor for GC immunotherapy. We speculate that the EBV status of enrolled patients might be a potential factor leading to different outcomes.

CPS has been used in clinical trials investigating the ICI to identify patients who could benefit from anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. Baseline CPS characteristics differ in studies regarding the interaction between H. pylori infection and ICI treatment. Jia et al enrolled 36.3% (231/636) patients with CPS<1. The subgroup analysis showed H. pylori-positive patients had longer PFS and OS than H. pylori-negative patients in the PD-L1 CPS<1 patients, whereas no significant differences in both PFS and OS between H. pylori-positive and H. pylori-negative patients in patients with GC with CPS≥1. Limited patients of CPS<1 were included in Magahis et al and Che et al, with a percentage of 21% (45/215) and 22% (17/77), respectively. We have reason to speculate that CPS<1 might exert a positive influence on H. pylori infection and ICI treatment. Therefore, when formulating treatment plans, it is necessary to comprehensively consider the specific situation and patient’s condition in order to develop personalized treatment plans.

In conclusion, the study by Jia et al adds valuable insights into the prognostic significance of H. pylori in the context of immunotherapy, emphasizing the need for a deeper appreciation of the complex interplay between H. pylori infection and the effectiveness of immunotherapy in GC, and paving the way for more targeted and effective treatment strategies. Future research should clarify the specific role of H. pylori in the immune response to therapy and explore the complex relationship between H. pylori infection and the immune microenvironment of GC, as well as the specific mechanisms by which H. pylori infection affects the efficacy and prognosis of immunotherapy. This will help to provide more precision and personalized medicine plans for patients with GC.
