Pattern and prognosis of perioperative deep vein thrombosis after hip fracture: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jenny Shin, Hyung-jin Cho, Ji-sum Moon, Seung-chan Kim, Kang-woong Jun, Jeong-kye Hwang, Mi-hyeong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePatients undergoing hip fracture surgery are at high risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) despite standard prophylaxis due to advanced age, surgery or the fracture itself, and immobilization. We investigated the incidence, pattern, and clinical course of DVT in this population.MethodsConsecutive patients who underwent hip fracture surgery at a single institute were included. Lower extremity vein duplex ultrasonography (DUS) was performed preoperatively and again on postoperative day 7. Pharmacologic and mechanical prophylaxis were applied. Treatment-indicated DVT (TI-DVT) was defined as proximal DVT or isolated distal DVT (IDDVT) with a large thrombus burden (TI-IDDVT).ResultsAmong 154 patients, 34 (22.1%) had preoperative DVT. Twelve patients had TI-DVT (11 TI-IDDVT) and were excluded from further analysis. In the remaining 142 patients, 39 (27.4%) postoperative DVTs were diagnosed, including 19 (13.3%) TI-DVT. Of these, 3 were proximal DVT and 16 were TI-IDDVT. The multivariate analysis identified operation time and the presence of preoperative IDDVT as risk factors for postoperative TI-DVT. The majority of DVT cases were IDDVT, and we further analyzed the prognosis of IDDVT before and after surgery. Twenty-two non-TI-IDDVT cases were identified in preoperative DUS, half of which became TI-DVT after surgery.ConclusionMost cases of perioperative DVT after hip fracture occurred as IDDVT. Half of these cases progressed to clinically significant DVT. Preoperative IDDVT represents an important predictor of postoperative TI-DVT. Therefore, preoperative DUS needs to be considered in very high-risk patients to allow earlier identification and timely management.Keywords: Anticoagulants, Hip fractures, Ultrasonography, Venous thromboembolism

INTRODUCTION

There is a well-known high risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) in patients who undergo major orthopedic surgery. Using the Caprini scoring system, the most widely used VTE risk assessment model, patients commonly encountered in clinical practice can also be classified as high risk [1]. For example, a 70-year-old hip fracture patient would be classified as high risk for VTE development by scoring 11 points for advanced age, fracture, scheduled major surgery, and immobilization. If the same patient had a central venous catheter or heart or lung comorbidities, they would score more than 17 points. In this context, the guidelines released in 2009 by the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) recommend pharmacological prophylaxis for 7–12 days starting 12–24 hours postoperatively [2].

Before the 1980s, when VTE prophylaxis was not performed, the incidence of symptomatic deep vein thrombosis (DVT) after hip fracture surgery reached 40%–60% [3,4]. As a prevalent postoperative complication, DVT significantly escalates mortality rates, the incidence of subsequent medical issues, and the financial burden on healthcare systems. The introduction of prophylactic anticoagulation in the 1980s markedly reduced the incidence of VTE to 5%, and by the 2000s, it was reportedly 1%–2% [4,5,6,7]. However, hip fracture is still the most common disease in which VTE occurs. According to studies that analyzed nationwide data in Korea in 2014 and 2017, the VTE incidence after hip fracture surgery was 1.43%–2.3% [8,9]. This is more than twice as high as the incidence among surgical oncology patients and 34 times higher than that of benign surgery patients.

Against this background, we aimed to determine how often and in what form DVT occurs in hip fracture patients and how it progresses before and after surgery.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Eunpyeong St. Mary’s Hospital, College of Medicine, The Catholic University of Korea (No. PC24RISI0068). It was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and written informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

Patient and data collection

We retrospectively analyzed the medical records of patients who underwent hip fracture surgery in a single institute between January 2023 and December 2023. All patients underwent duplex ultrasonography (DUS) 1 or 2 days before surgery and follow-up DUS 1 week after surgery. DUS examination was performed on the entire leg by 2 registered vascular technologists and included the common femoral vein, femoral vein, popliteal vein, crural veins (tibial, peroneal, and calf veins), and superficial veins. Patients who did not receive preoperative or follow-up DUS or who were evaluated for VTE with different image modalities were excluded. Screening tests for pulmonary artery embolism (PE) were not performed, and pulmonary CT angiography was selectively performed in patients with symptoms such as dyspnea, hypoxia, and hypotension. Patients who could not follow standard pharmacological prophylaxis or treatment due to a high risk of bleeding were excluded from the study.

Definition of deep vein thrombosis

DVT was defined in detail according to the amount and extent of the thrombus (Table 1). A thrombus that formed in the iliac, femoral, or popliteal vein was defined as proximal DVT. If the thrombus was confined to tibial, peroneal, and calf veins without proximal DVT, it was defined as isolated distal DVT (IDDVT). Proximal DVT was considered an indication for anticoagulation treatment upon diagnosis. In the case of IDDVT, treatment-indicated IDDVT (TI-IDDVT) was defined in the presence of a large thrombus burden (i.e., thrombosed vein greater than 7 mm in diameter or 5 cm in length or with more than 3 lesions) according to the American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) and European Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines, and anticoagulant therapy was performed [10,11]. IDDVT with a small thrombus burden, which does not carry a treatment indication, was defined as non-TI-IDDVT, and progression was tracked by weekly DUS.

Deep vein thrombosis prevention and treatment

Mechanical prophylaxis using an intermittent pneumatic compression device (IPCD) was performed on the fracture-free leg before surgery; after surgery, it was performed on both legs, avoiding the surgical incision. The IPCD with 3 cuffs was used; on the operated limb, only the ankle and calf cuffs were applied, and the thigh cuff was not used. The prophylactic dose of anticoagulant (20 mg enoxaparin once a day) was initiated the day after surgery and maintained until 7 days after surgery, at which point aspirin was initiated until 14 days after surgery. When proximal DVT or TI-IDDVT was diagnosed by preoperative DUS, anticoagulation therapy was performed at a dose of 1 mg enoxaparin/kg twice a day. If IDDVT was found in preoperative DUS but did not meet the TI-IDDVT criteria, standard prophylactic treatment was followed, as in patients without DVT.

Data analysis and statistics

The main findings analyzed in this study were the frequency and pattern of DVT occurrence before surgery, the frequency and pattern of DVT progression after surgery, and the risk factors that could predict DVT occurrence and progression.

Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate, and are presented as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Student t-test and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

For risk factor analysis, variables showing a P-value <0.20 in the initial group comparison were considered candidates for further regression analysis to avoid excluding potentially relevant predictors. Univariate logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed to estimate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Variables demonstrating statistical significance and/or clinical relevance were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model.

To minimize overfitting given the limited number of outcome events, the number of predictors included in the final multivariable model was restricted. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). A VIF value >5 was considered indicative of significant multicollinearity.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software ver. 3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and a 2-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Preoperative deep vein thrombosis prevalence and pattern

A total of 154 patients were included in the study, and DVT was diagnosed in 34 patients (22.1%) as a result of preoperative DUS (Fig. 1A). Twelve of the 34 DVT patients had TI-DVT, one of whom had proximal DVT, and 11 of whom had TI-IDDVT. Anticoagulation therapy was started for these 12 patients at the therapeutic dose, and they were excluded from the analysis of DVT progression after surgery.

Flowchart of perioperative duplex ultrasonography (DUS) and patterns of deep vein thrombosis (DVT). IDDVT, isolated distal DVT; TI-IDDVT, treatment-indicated IDDVT.

Postoperative deep vein thrombosis incidence and pattern

The postoperative DUS findings of 142 patients were analyzed, excluding the 12 cases of TI-DVT diagnosed on preoperative DUS (Fig. 1B). Postoperative DVT was diagnosed in 39 patients (27.4%). There were 19 cases (13.3%) of TI-DVT, of which 3 were proximal DVT and 16 were TI-IDDVT. There were no cases of symptomatic PE. To determine the risk factors that progress to TI-DVT, the 19 cases of TI-DVT were compared with the remaining 123 cases. There was no significant difference in the baseline characteristics and preoperative laboratory findings between the 2 groups (Tables 2, 3). The operation information of the 2 groups was analyzed (Table 4), and there was no difference in the type of surgery between the 2 groups. There were many cases of general anesthesia or intraoperative transfusion in the TI-DVT group, but the difference from the non-TI-DVT group was not statistically significant. The operation time was longer in the TI-DVT group, but there was no statistical significance in the results using the Student t-test method. The postoperative laboratory findings were then analyzed; the D-dimer was numerically higher in the TI-DVT group compared with the non-TI-DVT group, but the difference was not statistically significant (Table 5). Logistic regression analysis was performed on the factors discussed earlier (Table 6) and showed a significantly increased risk of developing postoperative TI-DVT in patients with longer operation times and those diagnosed with DVT in preoperative DUS.

Prognosis of perioperative deep vein thrombosis

Most of the perioperative DVTs appeared in the form of IDDVT, and we analyzed in detail how they changed after surgery (Fig. 2). There were 33 cases of preoperatively diagnosed IDDVT, 11 of which showed a large thrombus burden and required anticoagulation at the time of diagnosis. The remaining 22 cases were IDDVT with a small thrombus burden; in these cases, postoperative DUS revealed that 11 were in a stable state (4 cases resolved, 7 cases unchanged) and the remaining 11 showed an increase in thrombus burden, including 2 cases of progression to proximal DVT. In the end, 2/3 of patients diagnosed with IDDVT in preoperative DUS required anticoagulant therapy as the therapeutic dose.

DISCUSSION

This study is significant in that both preoperative and postoperative ultrasound were performed in all hip fracture patients included in the study, and the changes in DVT before and after surgery were analyzed based on the results. This study had 2 main notable findings. The first is that DVT occurred in 27.4% of patients after hip fracture surgery, even though mechanical and pharmacological prophylaxis were performed, and the second is that most cases of DVT were IDDVT.

In previous nationwide data studies, the prevalence of DVT after hip fracture surgery was reported to be approximately 1.4%–2% [8,9]. However, in this study, we reported a prevalence of 27.4%, which is more than 10 times higher than these previous reports as this study included asymptomatic DVT diagnosed with DUS screening, not symptomatic DVT. However, despite actively implementing prophylaxis, it is surprising that the prevalence of DVT, which is actually undiagnosed and hidden, was as high as 27.4%. Looking at the pattern of DVT, of the 39 postoperatively diagnosed DVTs, only 3 were proximal DVT and 36 (92.3%) were IDDVT. These 2 findings indicate that asymptomatic IDDVT occurs mainly in hip fracture patients; thus, it may not be considered terribly dangerous and can be well controlled by the current treatment policy. However, IDDVT progresses to proximal DVT in 20%–40% of cases and causes PE in 5%–15% of cases if not treated properly [12,13]. In the presented study, half of the 22 patients diagnosed with small thrombus burden IDDVT on preoperative DUS also progressed to proximal DVT or large thrombus burden IDDVT after surgery. Therefore, DVT is still a potentially high-risk complication that frequently occurs in hip fracture patients.

In this study, 34 (22%) cases of DVT were diagnosed on preoperative DUS and 39 (27%) cases of DVT were diagnosed on postoperative DUS. In addition, in the multivariate analysis, DVT presence in preoperative DUS was analyzed as a risk factor for the occurrence of postoperative TI-DVT and was found to increase the risk 18-fold compared to cases without preoperative DVT presence. Therefore, performing an ultrasound before and after surgery in patients with hip fractures to monitor the development of DVT is helpful for early diagnosis and appropriate treatment of DVT.

Nevertheless, the 2012 ACCP guideline and the 2009 and 2011 AAOS guidelines do not recommend screening using DUS in patients scheduled for elective hip fracture surgery [2,4]. The differences between the results of the present study and the guidelines need to be discussed. First, this study was conducted in Korea, and the cost of DUS screening in Korea is rather inexpensive: the cost varies slightly from institution to institution, but it is approximately $70 for researchers. The process of explaining the DUS implementation to the patient and obtaining consent proceeded smoothly. Therefore, performing DUS tests may be challenging in countries with reimbursement systems different from that of Korea. Second, VTE in patients with major orthopedic surgery is assessed according to the risk of developing PE, not DVT. Because the ACCP and AAOS guidelines consider the cost-effectiveness of PE prevention, they have determined that DUS screening has insufficient evidence. In fact, even in this study, none of the 154 patients were observed with symptomatic PE. Therefore, it is difficult to argue for the effectiveness of DUS screening in preventing PE based on the results of this study. However, it is also difficult to say that the potential risk of developing PE is small, given that 12 cases of TI-DVT had already been diagnosed before surgery, half of the non-TI-IDDVT progressed to TI-DVT after surgery, and 19 of 142 patients (13.3%) had TI-DVT after surgery.

We expected that this study could help identify high-risk patients by finding factors that could predict the occurrence of TI-DVT after surgery through the patients’ baseline characteristics, preoperative laboratory findings, and operation information, but we failed to find meaningful factors other than operation time. Among the items used for VTE risk assessment in the Caprini scoring model, except for the common risk factors (i.e., fracture, scheduled major surgery, immobilization) in hip fracture patients, those such as old age, previous VTE history, comorbidity of the heart or lungs, and coagulopathy remain, and these can be considered as additional risk factors [1]. One study analyzing the risk factors for PE after hip arthroplasty used the Charlson Comorbidity Index, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, anemia, depression, and body mass index as PE risk stratification criteria [14]. In a study based on nationwide data, female sex, body mass index, age of at least 70 years, and anemia (protective effect, −2 points) were selected as risk factors for PE [15]. Therefore, if the patient has the mentioned risk factors and is judged to exceed the average risk of the hip fracture patient, we think it would be helpful to consider DUS screening in the perioperative period.

However, we must address certain aspects of our study protocol that may have influenced the results. The prophylactic dose of enoxaparin used (20 mg once daily) is lower than the 40 mg dose recommended by the ACCP and the manufacturer’s insert [4]. This conservative approach was chosen given the advanced age and high bleeding risk of hip fracture patients in our clinical setting, as well as the smaller body size and lower VTE incidence in the Asian population compared to Western cohorts [16,17]. While this dose is within the range suggested by the Korean guideline [18], we acknowledge that this relatively lower-intensity prophylaxis, combined with our rigorous DUS screening for asymptomatic clots, likely contributed to the high postoperative DVT incidence (27.4%) observed.

Furthermore, our definition of ‘treatment-indicated IDDVT’ focused primarily on sonographic thrombus burden. Because nearly all hip fracture patients carry clinical risk factors (e.g., recent major surgery, immobilization) that are also considered treatment indications by the ACCP, many cases we classified as‘non-treatment-indicated’ might have met treatment thresholds under a stricter clinical interpretation [4]. Therefore, the 50% progression rate reported here should be understood as a transition into a higher-thrombus-burden category as defined by our study’s specific threshold, reflecting both biological worsening and our classification criteria. This transparent acknowledgement ensures a balanced interpretation of the predictive value of preoperative IDDVT identified in our study.

In conclusion, perioperative DVT after hip fracture occurred frequently, even under pharmacological prophylaxis. Most cases of DVT presented as IDDVT, and half progressed to problematic DVT after surgery. Preoperative IDDVT can be a predictor of postoperative TI-DVT. Thus, preoperative DUS needs to be considered for patients with a very high risk of VTE development.
