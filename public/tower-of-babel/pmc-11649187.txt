Referral patterns for common surgical procedures in Ontario: a cross-sectional population-level study
Authors: Pardis Seyedi, Dionne Aleman, Nancy Baxter, Chaim Bell, Merve Bodur, Andrew Calzavara, Robert Campbell, Michael Carter, Pieter de Jager, Scott Emerson, Anna Gagliardi, Jonathan Irish, Danielle Martin, Samantha Lee, Marcy Saxe-Braithwaite, Julie Takata, Suting Yang, Claudia Zanchetta, David Urbach
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Little is known about the existing structure and function of referral networks in the prevalent referral system for specialized surgical care in Canada, which is based on direct physician referral to specialists in a largely unmanaged referral marketplace. Our objective was to describe and analyze the referral networks of referring physicians and surgeons for common surgical procedures in Ontario, to better understand potential barriers to single-entry models.Methods:We analyzed referral networks for patients between referring physicians and surgeons for 9 common scheduled surgical procedures from 2016 to 2019 using administrative data sources in Ontario. We described the connectedness of referring physician–surgeon pairs using descriptive measures and graphical social network analysis.Results:The median number of surgeons connected to a referring physician for patients having a particular surgical procedure ranged from 1 (interquartile range [IQR] 1–3) for spine surgery to 3 (IQR 1–4) for knee arthroplasty and 3 (IQR 2–5) for noncancer uterine procedures. Referral network structure varied according to the procedure studied. Spine surgery was highly clustered with a small number of larger groups; gallbladder, inguinal hernia, and noncancer uterine surgery were highly distributed with many small groups within the referral network. Breast cancer surgery occurred in a largely distributed network, but with a skewed distribution reflecting a few small groups with large numbers of patients.Conclusion:Improving surgical wait times by coordinating surgical referrals will require approaches that address the structure of existing referral networks. Most physicians refer their patients to a very small number of surgeons, suggesting that referring physicians largely do not individualize referrals to multiple different surgeons based on specific patient characteristics.

Long wait times for scheduled surgical procedures have long been a concern in Canadian health systems. 1 Access to health services is determined by the demand for services, the supply of services, and the coordination between supply and demand.2 Single-entry referral models, also called central intake, have been proposed as a potential solution to improve long wait times for surgery in Canada.3 However, little is known about the existing structure and function of referral networks in the prevalent referral system for specialized care in Canada, which is commonly based on direct physician referral to specialists in a largely unmanaged referral marketplace.

A number of criticisms have been applied to single-entry models. For example, some have argued that they disrupt relationships between referring physicians and surgeons, prevent discretion in identifying a preferred surgeon for a particular patient, and reduce patient autonomy in choosing their own specialists. Resistance to single-entry models might be perpetuating inequities in access to care in Canadian health systems, since existing market-based referral networks do not ensure that patients are seen as quickly as possible by a next available provider and exacerbate referral biases that discriminate against female surgeons who receive fewer referrals, and fewer referrals resulting in surgery, than male surgeons.4

To better address the barriers to implementing new models for surgical referral, it is important to understand the current structure of Canadian referral networks and therefore the extent to which coordinated care structures such as single-entry models would necessitate changes in referral practices. For example, if primary care physicians refer their patients to a wide variety of specialists, it is possible that they are tailoring referrals to specific surgeons based on their patients’ needs, and concerns about introducing single-entry models might be valid. However, if primary care physicians refer their patients to only 1 or 2 surgeons, it is less likely that referrals are tailored to specific specialists because of unique clinical factors or that they are driven by patient choice. The objective of this study was to describe and analyze the referral networks of referring physicians and surgeons across 9 common surgical procedures in Ontario using administrative data sources, to better understand potential barriers to single-entry models.

Methods

In accordance with best practices for reporting observational studies, we followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.5 The STROBE guidelines provide a comprehensive framework for transparent and complete reporting of study methods and results.

Data sources

We analyzed referral systems for patients between referring physicians and surgeons using administrative data sources in Ontario. These sources included the Ontario Health Insurance Plan for physician claims6 to identify referring physicians and surgeons; the Registered Persons Database for patient demographic characteristics,7 including area of residence; the Corporate Provider Database for physician demographic characteristics; and the Wait Time Information System, a registry of most nonurgent surgical procedures in Ontario,8 to select surgeries and identify referring physicians. These data sets were linked using unique encoded identifiers based on each patient’s health card number and analyzed at ICES, a not-for-profit research institute encompassing a community of research, data and clinical experts, whose legal status under Ontario’s health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze health care and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. The unit of analysis was the surgical procedure.

Procedures studied

We selected 9 common surgical procedures across Ontario from January 2016 to December 2019 (cataract extraction, knee arthroplasty, hip arthroplasty, knee arthroscopy, spine surgery, gallbladder removal, inguinal hernia repair, noncancer uterine surgery, and breast cancer surgery). We selected these procedures because they are performed frequently and represent procedures for which wait times are a public concern, making them services for which coordinated referral systems may be appropriate. Patients of all ages were included.

We excluded all emergency and urgent cases (2.8% of the data) as well as procedures performed on the same day (3.6%), which lack statistical independence. Additionally, we excluded procedures that did not occur following referral from a referring physician to a surgeon (19.6%), because the purpose of this study was to describe referral networks between referring physicians and surgeons for non-emergency procedures.

Analysis

We described attributes of the procedures and patients for the 9 procedures, including types of procedures, number of operations, patient sex, age, region (Eastern, Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area [GTHA], Toronto, Western, and Northern), and rural residence status. There were no significant blank values for any attributes (less than 0.2%) in the data; all of them were excluded from the analysis.

We sought to describe relationships between referring physicians and surgeons, such as the number of surgeons a particular physician referred to, the number of referring physicians who referred to a particular surgeon, and the number of patients shared. The term “degree” indicates the number of physicians (referring physicians or surgeons) linked to another physician. The degree of a referring physician is measured by the number of surgeons who shared at least 1 patient with them during the study period, and the degree of a surgeon is determined by counting the number of referring physicians who shared at least 1 patient during the study period. We were especially interested in studying how many surgeons a primary care physician referred their patients to, because of the importance of this measure to the suitability of single-entry referral models. We analyzed relationships according to procedure type, sex, region, and years of practice.9

Social network analysis10 examines connections and relationships among actors within a system, and offers a valuable approach for depicting referral patterns between referring physicians and surgeons. In the graphical representations of referring physician–surgeon networks, nodes represent individual referring physicians (dots) and surgeons (squares) in the network, and edges (line segments) represent the presence of shared patients between a referring physician and a surgeon. The weight of edges varies in proportion to the number of shared patients.

We used the Girvan–Newman algorithm11 to identify clusters within the network of surgeons and referring physicians. The algorithm uses edge betweenness centrality (the number of times the edge occurs in the shortest path between all pairs of nodes) to quantify the strength of connections or bridges between different nodes within the network. The Girvan–Newman algorithm iteratively removes edges that have high betweenness centrality, until the network is divided into separate, more interconnected groups. In lay terms, these edges are the most likely candidates whose removal will separate the parts.

The algorithm is computationally intensive for large networks. To produce interpretable results for data visualizations, we limited the data set to the most recent 54 000 procedures out of the total of 387 533, including the most recent 6000 procedures of each type. We included 6000 procedures because this was the approximate number of the least common procedure (spine); using a similar sample for each procedure facilitates visual comparisons of the graphical representations of the referral networks. We measured the mean and median density of groups (the number of referring physicians and surgeons who formed a closely connected community based on shared patients). The median value is less sensitive to extreme values compared with the mean. The absolute difference between the median and average density of groups, along with skewness, can show disparities and asymmetries in the network of referring physicians and surgeons in a network analysis. Small groups were defined as those with fewer than 10 surgeons and referring physicians. These network attributes are helpful to understand how optimally distributed referrals are within existing networks, and the potential benefit of balancing any potential inefficiencies through single-entry referral models. For example, does the size of communities of referring physicians and surgeons (“group density”) vary widely or is it similar in size across regions? Is there a skewed distribution with a small number of very large groups? Are there many small groups scattered around the province?

Referring physicians were typically connected with a relatively small number of surgeons on average, ranging from a low of 1.6 surgeons for spine surgery to a high of 3.4 for surgeons performing uterine procedures. Surgeons were typically connected with a much larger number of referring physicians. Cataract surgeons and knee and hip arthroplasty surgeons had connections with more referring physicians than surgeons performing the other procedures. Male surgeons were connected to more referring physicians than female surgeons, except for breast cancer surgery, in which female surgeons had connections with more referring physicians. On average, the connection degree of male spine surgeons (83) was twice that of female spine surgeons (43). In breast cancer, even though the number of male surgeons (277) was larger than the number of female surgeons (133), the average connection degree for female surgeons (58) was more than that of males (32).

The years of practice of referring physicians did not appear to affect their connection degree with surgeons. However, the number of referring physicians connected with surgeons was higher after 9 years of practice across all procedures except for spine surgery.

Whereas the connection degree of referring physicians did not vary across different regions, surgeons’ degree showed more variation depending on the type of procedure. For example, breast cancer, cataract, and knee arthroscopy surgeons in Toronto and GTHA tended to have more connections than surgeons in other regions for the same procedures. Northern and GTHA spine surgeons showed a higher degree of connection than surgeons in other regions. Hip and knee arthroplasty surgeons in the Western and Eastern regions were connected with more referring physicians. Inguinal hernia and breast cancer surgeons in the North region had the lowest connection degree among all regions and procedures.

Referring physician–surgeon social network analysis

The network of referring physicians and surgeons across Ontario is visualized in Figure 1 and is quantified in Table 3. The social network analysis illustrates interconnected groups of referring physicians and surgeons. Hip arthroplasty, spine, and cataract networks show the lowest number of small groups (< 10 referring physicians and surgeons), suggesting a more concentrated market, whereas more than 50% of noncancer uterine, gallbladder, and inguinal hernia networks are distributed into smaller groups, reflecting a more distributed market. Procedures with more small groups are more amenable to coordination within small geographic regions. Procedures with fewer small groups may reflect highly specialized types of care that are provided in very few centres (e.g., spine surgery) or, alternatively, may reflect inefficient market concentration whereby a relatively small number of surgeons attract a substantial proportion of referrals and other surgeons are relatively idle. In the latter scenario, single-entry referral models would equalize the workload among specialists and improve access to services through greater efficiency.

Skewness measures the asymmetry of data distribution, with a right-leaning distribution (greater skewness) reflecting there are a few groups with a significantly larger number of members.

Mean–median deviation quantifies the difference between the mean and median, helping gauge the data’s central tendency; a small deviation implies symmetry, and a large deviation implies potential outliers or skewness.

The referring physician:surgeon ratio represents the balance of referring physicians to available surgeons in a medical network, in which a high ratio means fewer available surgeons compared with referring physicians.

Spine surgery and hip arthroplasty networks had the same number of groups and percentage of small groups, and the ratio of referring physicians to spine surgeons was twice that of hip arthroplasty surgeons, suggesting a more centralized and concentrated spine surgery network. The difference between the mean and median for spine surgery was twice as large as for hip arthroplasty, indicating a more imbalanced and less evenly distributed structure in the spine surgery network, in which a few highly connected groups play a key role.

The breast cancer network showed the highest skewness and mean–median deviation among all networks, indicating an uneven distribution of connections influenced by a few highly connected groups. Additionally, breast cancer surgery had a large number of small groups (45.3%), suggesting diverse referral dynamics and potentially reflecting various specialties among surgeons or more localized connections in breast cancer care. In comparison, the knee arthroscopy network was similar to the breast cancer network in terms of the ratio of physicians and average density of groups but had fewer small groups and less asymmetry.

Discussion

We identified significant variations in referral networks across different surgical procedures and demographic groups in Ontario. The degree analysis and network structures highlight the different levels of connectedness among health care providers and their effects. For example, there is a highly interconnected network for spine surgery in Ontario, owing to a few surgeons with exceptionally high numbers of connections. Such a centralized pattern could influence patient access and wait times for spine surgeries in Ontario, and potentially create disparities among surgeons.

Our study provides important information related to the potential use of single-entry referral models, whereby patients are referred into a common queue and see the next available surgeon in consultation, rather than being referred directly to a specific surgeon. A striking finding of our study is the discovery that over an extended period, primary care physicians refer patients to a very small number of surgeons. If different patients have unique clinical needs that are best addressed by a specific surgeon, or if a patient’s choice of a specific specialist is an important determinant of the surgeon who provides their surgery, then we would expect to see a single referring physician refer patients to many different surgeons. However, primary care physicians do not appear to send patients to a number of different surgeons; they tend to send their patients to the same 1, 2, or few surgeons repeatedly. Although the reasons for the narrow network of destinations for surgical referrals are unclear, it does suggest the possibility that busy primary care physicians refer to the surgeon with whom they have prior experience and can remember handles this type of case, rather than to the surgeon most likely to meet particular needs of an individual patient. A common argument against single-entry models — that the “choice” of a specific surgeon among referring physicians or patients is important in referral networks and should be preserved — is not supported by our data.

This research provides a detailed view of referral dynamics, particularly emphasizing gender disparities and regional differences among physicians. It builds on the existing body of literature that recognizes the complex nature of referral systems and extends it by quantifying these relationships in the context of surgical procedures.12,13 Single-entry models could help reduce some of the inequities we identified that favoured referrals to male surgeons compared with female surgeons, as other research has shown.14 Single-entry referral models smooth out imbalances in wait times between surgeons by more equitable load-sharing and can reduce gender disparities in referrals by ensuring that the probability of referral to a surgeon who participates in a single-entry referral model is independent of the surgeon’s gender.

Analysis of referral networks can help inform how referral models can be improved. For example, a highly interconnected network, such as that for spine surgery, suggests an opportunity for increasing the number of regions and surgeons that provide these services, or centralizing care by creating high-volume centres of excellence that serve geographic regions. More distributed procedures, such as uterine and hernia surgery, are best provided in multiple settings within geographic regions, ideally with coordinated referral models to equalize wait times among centres.

Further research should delve into how these referral patterns affect patient outcomes, particularly in terms of access to care and wait time for surgery. Exploring the potential influence of technological advancements, such as telemedicine, or other referral system strategies, such as single-entry referral models, on changing these patterns could also provide valuable insights for future health care policies.

Limitations

We used data from Ontario and studied a limited number of surgical procures, and we considered only referrals to surgeons that resulted in a surgical procedure being performed. Referral networks may also be more complex than simple relationships between a family physician and surgeon; for example, many patients having cataract surgery are referred to an ophthalmologist by an optometrist. However, these limitations are unlikely to have influenced our main conclusions.

Conclusion

Our study found that the existing referral networks in Ontario are not optimally efficient, and primary care physicians refer their patients to a relatively small number of surgeons. Our findings suggest that interventions to address potential bottlenecks in patient access to surgical services, such as single-entry referral models and regionalized networks for surgical care, could improve efficiency and reduce inequities such as gender disparities in surgical referrals.
