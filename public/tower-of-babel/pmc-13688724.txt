The economic burden of cervical cancer in Addis Ababa: a matched case-control study
Authors: Tariku Shimels, Biruck Gashawbeza, Teferi Gedif Fenta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical cancer poses a major global health challenge with significant financial burdens, especially in advanced care settings. In developing countries like Ethiopia, limited evidence exists on its economic impact. The objective of this study was to evaluate the economic burden of cervical cancer in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsA matched comparative cost-of-illness study using a matched case-control framework was employed at tertiary oncology centers from July 1st to September 30th, 2023. Participants were consecutively recruited. Data was collected using an interviewer-administered questionnaire developed after a literature review, and medical records were abstracted. Data entry was done via Epi Info v.7.2.5, followed by cleaning, transformation, and export to IBM SPSS v.26 for further analysis. Descriptive statistics, Student’s t-test, and Tukey’s biweight regression were applied using SPSS and R (v4.2.2). Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval.ResultsA total of 932 participants were enrolled in the study, comprising 466 cases and 466 controls. The findings indicated that the average total annual cost for cervical cancer per patient was INT$3196.27 in the first year, decreasing to INT$2235.86 in subsequent years. Direct medical costs were the primary contributors, amounting to INT$2638.05 in the first year and INT$1898.02 thereafter. Specifically, diagnosis and treatment represented the largest expenses in both the first year and the post-first-year periods. In the first year, diagnosis costs were the highest, averaging INT$1548.16 (± SD INT$1946.87), followed by treatment costs at INT$1004.18 (± SD INT$1403.45). In the post-first-year period, diagnosis costs remained significant at INT$1020.91 (± SD INT$2344.55), while treatment costs averaged INT$796.26 (± SD INT$1001.36). Key factors independently associated with higher total annual costs included larger household size (β = 492 0.82, p < 0.05), presence of comorbidities (β = 834.96, p < 0.001), lack of social support (β = 627.48, p < 0.001), poor diet (β = 740.27, p < 0.001), and advanced cancer stage (β = 1324.12, p < 0.001).ConclusionCervical cancer imposes a significant economic burden, with average annual costs of 3196.27 INT$ in the first year and 2235.86 INT$ afterward. Direct medical costs dominate expenses, with household size, comorbidities, lack of social support, poor diet, treatment timing, and advanced-stage disease were associated with higher total annual costs. Targeted interventions like early diagnosis and financial support are essential to reduce this burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15213-7.

Background

The term “cost of illness” (COI), also known as “burden of disease” (BOD) or “economic burden,” encompasses a wide range of expenses and financial challenges faced by countries, regions, communities, or individuals [1]. Depending on the methods employed, cost assessments typically utilize either incidence or prevalence-based approaches to capture costs and indicate the impact on longevity, morbidity, and even the health-related quality of life (QoL) for patients [2]. Expanding the measurement of the cost of illness could involve considering financial losses related to both direct and indirect expenditures resulting from premature death, disability, or injury caused by the associated disease and/or its comorbidities [1, 3].

The expenses associated with illness subsequent to cervical cancer constitute a significant share of the overall burden of the disease, both in the general population and specifically within this particular group. As reported in the United States [4], the average annual medical cost of treating cervical cancer shows considerable variability depending on the treatment phase. For instance, the mean cost of care during the last year of life could reach up to United States Dollars (USD) 118,000 for patients below 65 years and up to USD 79,000 for those aged 65 and above. In Ontario, a population-based estimate from 2016 revealed that the average overall care cost for cervical cancer was USD 39,137 in the first year following diagnosis. By the fifth year after diagnosis, the mean unadjusted cost increased to USD 63,131 [5].

Regarding the burden of cervical cancer in Ethiopia, an estimated 7,884 women (95% CI: 5,759–11,765) are living with the disease. Cervical cancer also accounts for approximately 162,776 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) (95% CI: 119,900–239,116) [6]. The incidence and mortality rates are approximately 17.3% and 16.5%, respectively [7]. Annually, around 6,300 new cases are diagnosed, with approximately 4,884 women succumbing to the disease each year. This makes cervical cancer the second most common and deadly cancer within this population in Ethiopia. Reflecting the scale of this issue, a previous study revealed that the mean outpatient cost per patient for cervical cancer was USD 407.2, with the direct outpatient cost constituting the largest portion [8].

The expenses incurred by patients in advanced care settings encompass various components across the care continuum. Currently, there is a lack of evidence regarding the economic impact of the disease in many developing contexts, including Ethiopia. Notably, there is no documented report to date that compares the incremental costs of the disease with other non-communicable diseases in a single study using a matched sample. Similarly, the patient attributes that predominantly influence the total cost in cervical cancer are not clearly understood.

This study endeavors to evaluate the economic repercussions of cervical cancer, specifically aiming to discern direct medical costs, direct non-medical costs, and indirect costs. Moreover, the study aimed to estimate the incremental costs associated with the disease and identify the factors associated with the total annual cost within this vulnerable group. The research is concentrated on patients residing in and visiting oncology units at leading referral and tertiary care hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Methods

Context and settings

The study was conducted at Saint Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC) and Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), the main public oncology centers in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. These leading referral hospitals provide comprehensive healthcare and medical education. SPHMMC serves around 200,000 patients annually, with a catchment population of 5 million, while TASH treats nearly 500,000 patients annually through its 700 beds. Both hospitals offer basic and specialized oncology services, making them crucial for cancer treatment in the country.

Study design and period

The study employed a matched comparative cost-of-illness design using a prevalence-based, patient-level approach from a societal perspective. A matched case-control framework was adopted to improve comparability between women with cervical cancer (cases) and women with hypertension (controls), rather than to investigate causation. Hypertension was selected as the comparison condition because it is a common chronic disease requiring regular healthcare utilization, thereby providing a more appropriate comparator than healthy individuals for estimating the incremental economic burden attributable to cervical cancer. Cases and controls were individually matched by age (± 5 years), residence, health facility, admission status (outpatient/inpatient), and follow-up period to reduce potential confounding related to healthcare access and utilization. Because the primary outcome was healthcare cost rather than disease occurrence, the analyses focused on comparing healthcare costs between the matched groups rather than estimating disease risk. The study was conducted from July 1 to September 30, 2023.

Source population

The source population for the cases consisted of all women diagnosed with cervical cancer who sought care at both public and private health facilities in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Likewise, the source population for the controls included all women diagnosed with hypertension who visited the same health facilities.

Study population

The study population consisted of all women with a pathologically and clinically confirmed cervical cancer diagnosis who either had started treatment or were on follow-up care in the selected hospitals during the study period. Controls were age- (± 5 years), sex-, residence-, hospital- and admission status- and follow-up period-matched known hypertensive patients. The controls were similar in most aspects of their background except that they were free from any form of confirmed cancer.

Inclusion criteria

All adult cases and controls (18 years of age or older) who lived in Addis Ababa city, began treatment and had been followed for a minimum of one month, were able to provide study-related information, had proxies or people with whom medical records and history could be verified, and provided verbal informed consent to take part in the study were eligible.

Exclusion criteria

Patients below the specified age and those with suspected diagnoses were excluded from the study. This includes cases (those with cancers other than cervical cancer), controls (those suspected of having any type of cancer), and proxies (those representing non-consenting patients).

A minimal standardized mean difference (SMD) of costs was estimated to be 0.2, despite the fact that population or sample mean estimates and standard deviations for the proposed research question are not currently available. Cohen [9] proposed three levels of this measure, 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 or more, as small, medium, and large impact sizes, respectively. The SMD is also known as Cohen’s d or effect size, and the value of 0.2 was taken to increase the sample size and precision of estimates. Sampling technique

The sampling technique was guided by careful consideration of the similarity of the populations being studied, the flow of cases, and the accessibility of healthcare facilities when selecting hospitals. Accordingly, a consecutive sampling method was adopted to include cases in the study. Then, the selection of controls was performed in a similar manner, ensuring that eligibility and matching criteria were met at each individual hospital to effectively achieve the study objectives. The selection process continued for both samples until the required sample size was achieved.

Epidemiologic approach and perspective

A prevalence-based approach was considered to evaluate the economic impact of illness from cervical cancer on society per patient per year. This examination adopted a societal perspective, encompassing all associated expenses. This includes direct medical costs (covering medications, diagnostics, supplies, hospitalization, and physician consultations), direct non-medical costs (encompassing transportation, food, and caregiver expenses), and indirect costs (accounting for lost earnings due to job absenteeism and productivity reductions incurred by caregivers).

Instrument design

Following a review of the literature on cancer [8, 10, 11] and other chronic illnesses [12–14], the data collection format for the cost of illness was designed (Supplementary Material 1). The purpose of the instrument was to capture data on patients’ direct medical and nonmedical expenses as well as any indirect costs incurred during the previous three months. The tool included various components, such as sociodemographic and economic factors, behavioral aspects, and the clinical profiles of participants. Additionally, it featured a format documenting the history of all medical expenses incurred over the past 90 days. The locally validated MD Anderson symptom inventory (MDSAI) tool was employed to capture the patient-reported inventory of common symptoms among cases [15]. Through face-to-face interviews and observation of medical records, data on patients’ profiles and all expenditures was collected retrospectively through a bottom-up approach. The projected costs of illness were based on net salary or wages and market earnings. We assessed indirect costs with the human capital technique [16]. This is the money that would be lost as a result of morbidity at the hospital, home, or in other healthcare settings for patients or their companions (family members or caregivers). Because of this, the number of days that the patients or their caregivers have lost was used to assess the expenses associated with absenteeism and patient productivity loss through interviews. For working participants, the daily average per capita net earnings were used; however, for laborers, housemaids, students, or housewives who were employed elsewhere, the market price of daily wages was taken into consideration [17]. Women over 60 or in retirement had no productivity losses because the human capital method is based, only, on market earnings.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted by six trained data collectors, including two general practitioners and four individuals with master’s degrees in public health. The team underwent extensive training on the data collection tool, ethical considerations, and effective communication techniques to ensure accurate responses. Eligible participants were approached at selected healthcare facilities, where interviews were conducted in private settings to ensure confidentiality and comfort. Informed consent was obtained prior to participation. All costs were captured in Ethiopian birr to ensure consistency and avoid errors.

Valuation of costs and sensitivity analysis

Costs were collected using a three-month recall period and annualized by multiplying quarterly expenditures by four. Annual costs were estimated separately for patients in the first year after treatment initiation and those beyond the first year, consistent with the prevalence-based costing approach. Resource utilization was obtained from patient interviews, medical records, hospital billing records, and receipts where available. Unit costs were derived from hospital charges and patient-reported expenditures. Following data collection and entry in local currency units (Ethiopian birr), the costs were computed and documented in International Dollar (INT$), considering a purchasing power parity (PPP) of 20.0 local currency units (LCUs) per INT$ for Ethiopia. This PPP was derived from the 2022 reported LCU for the country, which stood at 17.8, factoring in an annual growth rate of 12.45% [18]. When real-time comparisons were required (such that studies reported in local currencies) a USD was used considering the exchange rates that were in effect during the study period (1USD=55ETB) in Ethiopian birr (ETB). No discounting was applied since it was anticipated that all expenses would be incurred within a year. Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a ± 20% range for the main cost drivers, including diagnosis, treatment, palliative care, as well as direct non-medical and indirect costs. This approach was used to account for variability and potential non-reported factors that could affect the total annual costs. The analysis helped to assess the robustness of our findings and determine which cost components had the greatest influence on overall expenses under different scenarios.

Data quality assurance

Before designing the data collection tool, a comprehensive literature review was conducted to inform the development process and ensure the quality of the data collected. To enhance data quality and accuracy, we implemented a system of supervision, provided thorough training sessions for data collectors, and conducted pretesting of the data collection instrument. Additionally, a three-month timeframe was established for data collection to minimize the potential for recall bias in cost estimation. Data were checked for completeness before analysis. Missing data were minimal (< 5%); therefore, complete-case analysis was performed and no imputation was undertaken while the sensitivity analysis was used to assess the impact of variability in major cost drivers and unreported factors.

Definitions of terms

Physical Exercise

This behavior was assessed based on the recommendations provided by the US Physical Activity Advisory Committee [19], the American Cancer Society (ACS) [20] and the World Health Organization (WHO) [21]. These guidelines suggest that patients with chronic illnesses, particularly those with cancer, should engage in either 150–300 minutes of moderate exercise or 75–100 minutes of vigorous physical activity per week. Patients were classified as ‘good’ if they met either recommendation, and ‘poor’ if they do not.

Dietary Practice

Following the guidelines of the American Cancer Society [20], it is recommended to consume foods high in nutrients to maintain a healthy body weight. This includes a variety of vegetables (dark green, red, and orange), fiber-rich legumes, fruits (especially whole fruits in different colors), and whole grains. Patients were classified as having ‘good’ dietary practices if they follow any combination of these recommended diets, and ‘poor’ dietary practices if they engage in high-risk diets or lack access to an adequate diet.

Current Use of Excess Alcohol

Current use of excess alcohol was defined based on a participant’s response to the question of whether they currently consume more than one bottle of alcohol per day, as set by the American Cancer Society [20].

Current Use of Cigarettes

Current use of cigarettes, as per the World Health Organization [21] definition, refers to the use of cigarettes (smoked or non-smoked) reported by respondents during the data collection period.

Current Use of Khat or Other Stimulant Substances

Current use of khat or other stimulant substances is defined based on whether a patient reports using khat or other stimulant substances (such as Hashish, Ganja, etc.) during the data collection period.

Clinical Symptoms

The common clinical symptoms namely, pain, fatigue, distress, sadness and numbness were selected from the MDASI questionnaire as they have been reported to be common experiences among cancer patients in the current study settings. The Amharic version of the tool has been validated earlier for Ethiopian patients [15].

Data analysis

Data entry was done using Epi-Info v.7.2.5 and exported to computer languages such as MS Excel, Statistical Packages for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 [22] and R programming version 4.2.2 for additional cleaning, recoding, transformation, and modeling. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data and are presented as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Student’s independent t-tests were used to compare mean costs between cases and controls. Because cost data were positively skewed, bootstrapping with 1,000 replications was performed to estimate the uncertainty around incremental cost estimates. As the bootstrapped estimates were consistent with the observed results, only the original sample estimates are presented. The total annual cost data were positively skewed and contained influential outliers. Therefore, preliminary ordinary least squares (OLS) regression diagnostics, including Cook’s distance, leverage values, and standardized residuals, were used to identify influential observations before fitting the final model. Tukey’s biweight MM robust regression was then used to identify factors associated with total annual costs while reducing the influence of any remaining extreme observations through robust weighting. Model diagnostics were performed, and the performance of the robust regression model was compared with that of the OLS regression model. Results are presented using tables and figures. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05 and a 95% confidence interval.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Prior to starting the data collection, the study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Saint Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (Ref. no: PM23/284) and the School of Pharmacy, Addis Ababa University (Ref. no: ERB/SOP/543/15/2023). A letter of support was sent to the chosen cancer centers. Each study participant provided verbal informed consent. The verbal informed consent was documented by the research team through written records during each interview. Verbal informed consent was used because the study involved interviewer-administered questionnaires and medical record review without invasive procedures, and because documenting written consent could impose an unnecessary burden on participants. The use of verbal informed consent was reviewed and explicitly approved by the respective Institutional Review Boards/Ethics Committees. Involvement in the study was entirely voluntary. No personal identifiers were used in the study; instead, data was analyzed in aggregate. Both patient privacy and confidentiality of the data collected were safeguarded. All study procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Results

Sociodemographic and economic characteristics

A total of 932 participants, comprising 466 cases and 466 controls with a 1:1 case-to-control ratio, were recruited and included in the study. Among the case-control pairs, 53.6% (250 pairs) were from SPHMMC, with the remaining participants from TASH. The mean age for cases was 51.7 (± 10.6) years, and for controls, it was 52.8 (± 10.9) years, both groups averaging approximately to 52 years. A slightly higher percentage of cases (58.2%, n = 271) belonged to the age group 52 years or less compared to controls (48.3%, n = 225). In terms of marital status, more than two-thirds of both cases (74.7%, n = 348) and controls (69.5%, n = 324) were married. Additionally, a similar proportion of cases (69.7%, n = 325) and controls (71.5%, n = 333) were able to read and write. Similarly, almost equal proportions of cases (37.3%, n = 174) and controls (37.8%, n = 176) had not attended formal education, representing the predominant category in both samples. Geographically, a significant number of cases were from Addis Ketema sub-city (14.4%, n = 67) and Kolfe Keranio sub-city (13.5%, n = 63), while controls were primarily from Lideta (15.0%, n = 70) followed by Addis Ketema (14.8%, n = 69) sub-cities. The majority of cases (55.8%, n = 260) and controls (46.6%, n = 217) were housewives. Additionally, a substantial portion of cases (68.9%, n = 321) and controls (61.4%, n = 286) reported having some form of social support within their community. Regarding family size, approximately two-thirds of cases (79.6%, n = 371) and the majority of controls (86.3%, n = 402) had households with five or fewer members. The mean (± SD) average monthly income for households was 5656.6 (± 5055.9) Ethiopian Birr (ETB) for cases and 5404.9 (± 3918.9) ETB for controls, equivalent to 102.8 (± 91.9) and 98.3 (± 71.3) USD for cases and controls, respectively. A significant proportion, 61.4% of cases (n = 286) and 62.4% of controls (n = 291), reported earnings above the poverty line of 3567 ETB or 65 USD per month. Lastly, community-based health insurance emerged as the primary source of funding for medical expenses, with nearly two-thirds of both cases (70.0%, n = 326) and controls (67.4%, n = 314) reporting its utilization (Table 1).

Behavioral characteristics

The evaluation of the current behavioral characters of the respondents indicates that a relatively small percentage of both cases and controls reported consumption of substances, including tobacco products, excessive alcohol, and khat (a stimulant leaf also called ‘Catha edulis’ that contains the chemical compound known as ‘cathinone’ among others). Notably, more than half of both cases (56.9%, n = 265) and controls (51.9%, n = 242) reported engaging in either moderate or vigorous exercises. Likewise, a higher percentage of both cases (59.2%, n = 276) and controls (59.0%, n = 275) reported maintaining good dietary practices (Fig. 1).

Clinical profiles

Most of the case-control pairs were from the outpatient department (95.3%, n = 444). More number of cases reported history of hospitalization during the past 90 days (75, 16.1%) compared to the controls which was statistically significant (X2 = 20.61, p < 0.001). More than half of both the cases (54.3%, n = 253) and controls (57.5%, n = 268) did not have any chronic comorbidity. Among cases with comorbidities (n = 213), HIV/AIDS (19.5%, n = 91) followed by hypertension (13.7%, n = 64) constituted the highest proportions. In contrast, type 2 diabetes mellitus (17.6%, n = 82) followed by cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) (10.9%, n = 51) were the prevalent conditions among the controls with comorbidities (n = 198) (Table 2).

In the current study, the assessment of clinical symptoms using the MDASI focused on five commonly reported dimensions: pain, fatigue, distress, sadness, and numbness [15]. The results revealed that a significant majority of both cases (87.6%, n = 408) and controls (69.5%, n = 324) experienced mild to severe pain. Moreover, a higher percentage of cases reported mild to severe fatigue (91.2%, n = 425) compared to controls (85.4%, n = 398). In terms of psychological symptoms, 88.4% of cases (n = 412) and 82.8% of controls (n = 386) reported mild to severe distress. Similarly, a substantial proportion experienced mild to severe sadness, with rates of 89.1% for cases (n = 415) and 80.5% for controls (n = 375). Lastly, it was observed that cases had a notably higher occurrence of mild to severe numbness (85.8%, n = 400) compared to controls (61.8%, n = 288) (Fig. 2).

For the cases, the mean (± SD) duration from diagnosis to treatment initiation was 91.2 (± 111.3) days, ranging from 0 to 762. Factors associated with total annual cost in cervical cancer care

A Tukey’s biweight regression analysis was conducted in R to identify factors independently associated with total annual cost. Several steps were taken to refine the fitness of the final model. Preliminary OLS diagnostics identified nine influential observations, which were excluded before fitting the final robust regression model. Diagnostic assessment of the final model indicated that seven additional observations received robustness weights of zero, minimizing their influence on the estimated coefficients. Dummy variables with minimal contribution, such as marital status and level of education categories, were excluded. Additionally, three dummy coded variables with a variance inflation factor (VIF) exceeding 2.5 were recoded to mitigate multicollinearity among predictors. The residual standard error (RSE) of the robust model was found to be lower than that of the OLS model (RSE = 1332.04 vs. 1881.64, for the OLS and Tukey’s models respectively), indicating a favorable fit. The diagnosis of heteroscedasticity using the studentized Breusch-Pagan (BP) test suggested that the variance of residuals was constant (BP = 22.001, df = 15, p = 0.108) (Supplementary Material 3).

The final model revealed that, keeping other variables constant, patients with households larger than 5 members are likely to incur a higher total annual cost of 492.82 INT$ compared to those with fewer members (β = 492.82, p = 0.016). Patients with comorbid conditions were more likely to incur an additional total annual cost of 834.96 INT$ compared to those without comorbidity (β = 834.96, p < 0.001). The absence of social support significantly increases the total annual cost of patients by 627.48 INT$ (β = 627.48, p = 0.001). Patients with poor dietary habit are more likely to expend an additional 740.27 INT$ annually compared to those with good dietary practices (β = 740.27, p < 0.001). The total annual cost of cervical cancer during the first year of starting treatment increases by an average of 967.73 INT$ compared to the estimate during the post-first-year time frame (β = 967.73, p < 0.001). An advanced stage of cancer during diagnosis is associated with an additional total annual cost of 1324.12 INT$ compared to those at an early stage (β = 1324.12, p < 0.001) (Table 7).

Discussion

This research investigated the economic impact of cervical cancer in an urban context of Ethiopia. It placed emphasis on comprehending the direct medical costs, direct non-medical costs, and indirect costs faced by patients in accessing a diverse array of advanced healthcare services. The study also analyzed the incremental costs associated with cervical cancer compared to hypertension, along with identifying the factors independently associated with total annual costs incurred in this population.

The main direct medical costs for cervical cancer cases at the study hospitals were driven by expenses for drugs, imaging diagnostics, and hospital stays, making them the top contributors to total costs. These diagnosis and treatment costs were the primary drivers in both the first year and post-first-year periods. This is largely due to the high cost of medications commonly used in cancer management, which significantly elevates the overall cost of care. Additionally, the intricate nature of clinical decision-making in cancer treatment necessitates a comprehensive approach, with imaging diagnoses—primarily utilizing ultrasound, CT, and MRI evaluations—playing a significant role. Despite inpatient admissions constituting a substantial portion of direct medical costs, there was no statistically significant difference between outpatient and inpatient cases (p = 0.060). This lack of significance could be primarily attributed to the smaller proportion of patients assessed in the inpatient group. However, the costs associated with hospitalization were markedly higher among the cases compared to the controls, potentially linked to frequent emergency unit admissions or visits by the cases. The results indicate a significantly higher incidence of hospitalization among cases, either at present or in the past 90 days, compared to the controls (X2 = 20.61, p < 0.001). A report from China also highlighted that inpatient care accounted for 58.6% of cancer treatment costs [24]. Even though medications stood out as the primary cost contributors for both cases and controls in this study, the challenge regarding the availability and the elevated expense of individual drugs presents a substantial hurdle, particularly for cancer patients compared to controls. This is in line with reports indicating that anticancer medicines are poorly available [25] or more costly than other classes of drugs [26]. A study conducted by Kasahun and colleagues highlighted that nearly three-fourths of cancer patients in Addis Ababa faced catastrophic health expenditures [27]. Additionally, the high-cost may also be associated with the clinical symptoms experienced by patients, as a larger proportion of cases reported mild to severe clinical symptoms in contrast to the controls. A recent report underscored that addressing the relief of cancer-related symptoms significantly contributed to the financial burden borne by patients in terms of out-of-pocket expenditures [28].

The analysis of costs incurred outside the present hospitals indicates that, among cases, imaging services took the top position, whereas costs related to consultation visits were highest among controls. This observation underscores the substantial reliance on common cross-sectional imaging modalities, specifically CT and MRI in the diagnosis and monitoring of cancer [29]. Cases not only accumulated significant expenses for imaging services at the current hospitals but also sought similar services at other hospitals or centers during periods of non-functionality or extended waiting times in the present facilities. The challenges of high costs and prolonged waiting times for diagnostic tests have been reported as prominent obstacles facing cancer patients in Ethiopia [30]. Meanwhile, both outpatients and inpatients experienced substantial expenses, with a slight increase observed among the inpatients. However, both figures are notably lower when compared to findings reported a decade ago in the same setting by Hailu and Mariam [8]. The observed difference could be attributed to various factors, including changes in health infrastructure, changes in patient load, economic inflation, and methodological variations between the current study and the one reported a decade ago.

In the present study, we found that the estimated average of total annual expenses for cervical cancer per patient were 3196.27 INT$ or 1162.3 USD in the initial year of treatment commencement. For the subsequent post-first-year period following treatment initiation, the cost decreased to 2235.86 INT$ (813.04 USD). Upon comparing the cost estimates with controls (824.85 INT$ or 299.94 USD), a statistically significant difference emerged across all assessed cost objects, indicating an almost fourfold increase in total annual costs among cases. A nationwide study examining the costs associated with four prevalent cancers in the United States similarly reported a fourfold higher cost for cancer patients compared to non-cancer controls [31]. Another study in the same country highlighted that costs among cases were significantly higher compared to controls [32].

Cases consistently incurred higher costs, both in the first year of treatment initiation and in subsequent time frames. Interestingly, it was revealed that the per capita total annual costs among cases significantly decreased during the post-first-year time frame (1162.30 USD vs. 813.04 USD). The accuracy of this estimate may vary throughout the later stages of patients’ lives since the assessments conducted did not account for end-of-life care or mortality costs. Consequently, there is a potential for an upward trend in expenses or an increased likelihood of higher costs during the final year of life. Numerous studies have illustrated that both the initial and concluding years of life for individuals with cancer are linked to elevated healthcare expenditures [33–35]. Conversely, among controls, there was a slight increase in the same figure (299.94 USD vs. 341.29 USD) (Table 5). This notable difference could be primarily attributed to the resource-intensive nature of initial aggressive treatments that patients receive during the early phases of cancer care, including surgeries, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy (either neo-adjuvant or adjuvant). Additionally, patients may undergo various diagnostic processes and repeated hospital treatments, such as chemotherapy, contributing to higher costs. This financial burden may decrease in subsequent phases of cancer care, essentially before end-of-life, as well as in comparison to other chronic illnesses. Because the comparison group consisted of women with hypertension, the reported incremental costs should be interpreted as the additional economic burden associated with cervical cancer relative to another chronic disease population rather than to the general healthy population.

It is noteworthy that, despite variations in years of follow-up, the overall cost in our study was considerably lower compared to a similar estimate reported in Ontario, which amounted to 29,027.7 USD [5]. Several factors may explain the observed differences, including variations in healthcare system structures and payment models, differences in treatment protocols and standards of care, and economic disparities. Additionally, differences in healthcare spending across populations could also play a role, among other influencing factors. These diverse elements may collectively influence the overall cost landscape and financial burden associated with cervical cancer care in the two settings. It is interesting to note that a study in Brazil reported a slightly higher figure (3591.72 INT$) for the total annual costs of cervical cancer per patient [36]. Additionally, this study emphasized that direct medical costs accounted for 81.2%, which is comparable to our finding of 82.5%. This similarity in the distribution of costs suggests a common trend in the dominance of direct medical costs in the overall economic burden of cervical cancer in both settings.

An analysis of factors independently associated with the total annual cost of cervical cancer indicated that certain patient characteristics—such as having more than five family members in the household, experiencing concomitant chronic comorbidity, lacking social support, practicing poor dietary habits, being in the initial year of treatment initiation, and being in an advanced stage of cancer—exhibited a statistically significant positive association. Larger households may incur higher transportation, food, caregiver and opportunity costs during repeated treatment visits. Another study in the current research setting indicated that individuals with a household size of five or more were more prone to encountering poor dietary diversity [37], which may have an indirect effect on total cost of cancer management. It is probable that chronic comorbidity can impact the overall costs of cervical cancer, either directly or indirectly. Directly, patients may face intricate treatment regimens, prolonged hospitalizations, or additional imaging tests, all of which may contribute to the total cost. Indirectly, there might be related costs associated with frequent transportation to hospitals, lost productivity, or expenses for paid caregivers, among other factors. A recent study in Taiwan demonstrated that cancer patients with mental health disorders incurred costs that were 50% higher than those without such issues [38]. A separate study conducted in China found that comorbidities in individuals with lung cancer led to frequent outpatient visits, hospitalizations, and increased expenditures [39].

The association between absence of social support and poor dietary habit with higher total annual costs in cervical cancer appears to be quite intricate and interactive. From the sample, patients with poor social support were found to be about 1.78 times more likely to have poor dietary habit (crude odds ratio: 1.78: 95% CI: 1.19–2.67). The fact that cancer patients reported poor dietary diversity has been documented in a recent study [37]. This, in turn, may compromise patients’ immune system and overall health, leading to increased side effects from cancer treatments. Prolonged recovery times and additional medical interventions to manage side effects can contribute to the higher costs reported. Poor dietary status may reflect poorer nutritional status, greater disease severity, or lower socioeconomic status, all of which may be associated with higher treatment costs [40]. These associations should be interpreted cautiously because residual confounding and unmeasured differences in disease severity or healthcare utilization may have influenced the observed relationships.

Furthermore, the implicit correlation between an advanced stage of cancer and increased overall costs is evident. This connection is influenced by various factors such as the necessity for sophisticated diagnostic methods, personalized treatment strategies, frequent hospitalizations, and the adverse effects of treatments. The progression to an advanced-stage of cancer could lead to end-of-life care in cancer management which, indirectly, exacerbates total healthcare expenditures in this population [35] On a similar note, it is probable that the initial year of cancer diagnosis necessitates repetitive diagnostic procedures and extended treatment regimens spanning several months. Consequently, these factors are linked to increased overall costs. Despite potential uncertainty in the cost estimates arising from recall bias, disease progression, and cost projections, this study provides a comprehensive and up-to-date assessment of the economic burden of cervical cancer in Ethiopia. The findings support strengthening cervical cancer prevention through HPV vaccination and early screening, improving referral pathways to reduce diagnostic delays, expanding financial risk protection mechanisms for cancer care, and ensuring equitable access to essential oncology medicines and diagnostic services.

Several measures were undertaken to enhance the validity and robustness of the findings. Cost data were collected using a 90-day recall period to minimize recall bias, and analyses were stratified by time since treatment initiation (first year versus post-first-year) to account for differences in healthcare utilization over the disease course. In addition, sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the cost estimates under alternative assumptions.

Nevertheless, the study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited consecutively from two tertiary hospitals in Addis Ababa; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all women with cervical cancer in Ethiopia, particularly those receiving care in primary or secondary facilities or in other regions. Second, face-to-face interviews may have introduced recall and social desirability bias, while medical record abstraction may have been affected by incomplete documentation. These potential biases were minimized through the use of standardized questionnaires, trained data collectors, and verification of patient-reported information against medical records whenever possible. Third, patients residing outside Addis Ababa were excluded, and their cost experiences may differ from those of the study population. Fourth, productivity losses due to premature mortality and presenteeism were not included in the cost estimates. Fifth, intangible costs, such as pain, suffering, and reduced quality of life, were not quantified because these outcomes are difficult to value monetarily, and willingness-to-pay methods were not employed due to ethical considerations. Finally, although matching improved comparability between cases and controls, residual confounding may remain because cervical cancer and hypertension differ in their disease characteristics and patterns of healthcare utilization.

Conclusion

The costs associated with cervical cancer in Addis Ababa were calculated to represent the yearly total expenditure, encompassing direct medical, direct non-medical, and indirect cost components. During the first and post-first-year of diagnosis, the direct medical costs were assessed at 2638.05 and 1898.02 INT$, respectively, holding the highest rank among all cost categories and covering 82.5% and 84.9% of the average in total annual costs. In comparison to the control group, individuals with cervical cancer accrued additional mean total annual costs of 1961.27 and 1297.39 INT$ during the first and post-first-year periods, respectively. Various patient characteristics, including having more than five family members in the household, experiencing concurrent chronic comorbidities, lacking social support, adopting poor dietary habits, being in the initial year of treatment initiation, and being diagnosed at an advanced stage of cancer were independently associated with total annual costs. It is crucial to implement policies that prioritize ensuring the availability and affordability of medicines, encourage the prescription of generic drugs, and emphasize the prevention and early detection of cervical cancer. Furthermore, there is a need to expand tertiary treatment center facilities, coupled with the introduction of cost-sharing schemes to guarantee the accessibility of services.
