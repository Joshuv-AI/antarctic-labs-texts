Voices from Primary Care: A Needs Assessment Among Multidisciplinary Primary-Care Teams for a Health Promotion Model–Based E-Counseling Platform to Support Fall Prevention Among Older Adults in Indonesia
Authors: Lina Safarina, Cecep Eli Kosasih, Iqbal Pramukti, Nucki Nursjamsi Hidajat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalls are a leading cause of injury and disability among community-dwelling older adults, with a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In Indonesia, primary-care centres (puskesmas) and their multidisciplinary teams are the principal platform for older-adult care, yet most lack a structured fall-prevention protocol. Digital e-counseling could extend primary care, but its design in LMICs rarely begins with a bottom-up assessment of primary-care workforce needs.PurposeTo explore multidisciplinary primary-care programme holders’ perspectives on fall risk, structural barriers, current digital practices, and requirements for an e-counseling platform grounded in Pender’s Health Promotion Model (HPM), a framework linking perceived benefits, barriers, self-efficacy, interpersonal and situational influences, and commitment to action.Patients and MethodsA qualitative descriptive design was used. Thirteen elderly-health programme holders from all 13 puskesmas in Cimahi City, West Java (nursing, medicine, public health, midwifery, nutrition), participated in two focus group discussions (FGDs; six and seven participants, with disciplines mixed across both groups). Sessions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis with deductive mapping to HPM constructs. Trustworthiness was supported by independent dual coding, member-checking, an audit trail, and reflexive memos.ResultsFour themes were generated: multifactorial fall causation; structural barriers (no fall-specific protocol in any centre, workforce shortages, large catchments, limited family engagement, and mobile-data costs); an existing WhatsApp-based digital substrate; and explicit e-counseling design needs (risk screening, multimedia education, asynchronous Q&A, tiered cadre-mediated access, reminders, home-safety checklist). Findings mapped onto perceived susceptibility and severity, perceived benefits and barriers, perceived self-efficacy, interpersonal and situational influence, and commitment-to-action plan.ConclusionIndonesian primary-care multidisciplinary teams articulate a system-level case for an HPM-based e-counseling platform that augments face-to-face care, integrates with WhatsApp, and includes cadre- and family-mediated channels, offering preliminary, context-specific insight for LMIC primary care.Keywords: fall prevention, older adults, e-counseling, health promotion model, primary healthcare, multidisciplinary team, qualitative study, Indonesia

Introduction

Falls remain a defining public-health challenge of population ageing. The World Health Organization (WHO) global guidelines for falls prevention and management estimate that approximately 30–40% of community-dwelling adults aged 65 years and older fall at least once each year, with more than 684,000 fatal falls recorded globally; this burden is concentrated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where infrastructure for prevention is least developed.1

In Indonesia, community-based surveys repeatedly report fall prevalence of 20–35% among adults aged 60 years and older.2,3 Most Indonesian older adults live with their families, and primary-care centres (puskesmas) serve as their principal point of contact with the formal health system.4 Older-adult care at puskesmas is delivered through multidisciplinary teams—general practitioners, registered nurses, public-health promoters, midwives, nutritionists, and trained community volunteers (kader kesehatan)—operating through Posyandu Lansia (integrated elderly health posts, literally “integrated service post for older adults”) and Posbindu PTM (non-communicable disease screening posts, “integrated development post for non-communicable diseases”). These teams perform routine screening, lifestyle counselling, and basic clinical management, yet fall-specific protocols, structured risk screening, and dedicated training are seldom embedded in standard practice.4

Notably, although instruments such as the Indonesian version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire demonstrate that culturally validated assessment tools can be developed locally,5 no culturally validated, primary-care fall-risk screening instrument is yet in routine use across Indonesian puskesmas. Adapting a brief, validated tool is therefore a necessary precondition for any structured fall-prevention pathway. The World Falls Guidelines recommend a stratified approach—health-promotion exercise for low-risk older adults, tailored exercise and home-safety modification for intermediate risk, and multifactorial assessment with multidisciplinary management for high risk1—but translation of these recommendations into LMIC primary-care workflows remains largely uncharted.1

Understanding why primary-care workers do or do not engage in fall-prevention behaviour requires a behavioural framework. The Health Promotion Model (HPM) developed by Pender6 provides an integrative framework for the determinants of health-promoting behaviour. It identifies behaviour-specific cognitions—perceived benefits, perceived barriers, perceived self-efficacy, activity-related affect, interpersonal influence, and situational influence—together with commitment-to-action as the main drivers of preventive behaviour.7 HPM has been applied to healthy ageing,8 fall prevention,9 and community-based educational interventions for older adults at risk of falls,10 making it a suitable lens for translating stakeholder narratives into theory-anchored design specifications for digital interventions.

Digital health interventions have emerged as scalable adjuncts to primary care for fall prevention, although tools such as StandingTall11 have shown reductions in fall rates, while digital platforms such as KOKU12 are being evaluated mainly in high-income settings, and a recent scoping review found that mHealth fall-prevention tools in LMICs are almost universally developed in high-income contexts with limited local adaptation.13 E-counseling—the structured delivery of behaviour-change counselling through synchronous and asynchronous digital channels—can be equivalent to in-person counselling for many primary-care functions.14 In Indonesia, WhatsApp is the dominant digital channel because it is free to install, uses low mobile data, and is universally familiar across generations; programme holders, families, and kader already communicate through it informally, a pattern echoed in qualitative evidence on WhatsApp for older-adult health communication.15 Sustained adoption of digital tools among older adults depends on perceived usefulness and social influence from trusted intermediaries,16,17 and governance of AI-powered wearable Internet of Health Things (IoHT) raises unresolved privacy, security, and ethical issues for older users,18 while a recent bibliometric analysis documents persistent under-representation of LMIC contexts in embedded IoHT fall-detection research.19

Despite this evidence base, no Indonesian study has performed a structured, theory-anchored needs assessment of primary-care multidisciplinary teams to inform the design of an e-counseling system for older-adult fall prevention. Without such grounding, digital tools risk poor fit with workforce realities, underuse of cadre networks, and failure to leverage the existing WhatsApp substrate. Anchoring such an assessment in the HPM is justified because the model’s behaviour-specific cognitions map directly onto modifiable design levers of an e-counseling platform—for example, perceived barriers to screening uptake, self-efficacy to deliver counselling, and interpersonal influence of cadres and family. This study therefore explored the perspectives of 13 elderly-health programme holders covering all 13 puskesmas of Cimahi City, a mid-sized urban region representative of Indonesian primary-care delivery, on fall risk, structural barriers, existing digital practice, and the design requirements for an HPM-based e-counseling platform.

The research questions guiding this study were: (1) How do multidisciplinary primary-care programme holders conceptualise fall risk and its causation among the older adults they serve? (2) What organisational, workforce, and family-related barriers do they experience in delivering fall prevention? (3) What digital tools and channels do they already use, formally or informally, to support older-adult care? (4) What features, content, and integration requirements would they consider essential for an HPM-based e-counseling platform?

Materials and Methods

Design

A qualitative descriptive design20 was used to generate rich, low-inference accounts of stakeholder experience suited to needs assessment for intervention design. Reporting follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) 32-item checklist.21

Reflexivity

The first author (LS) is a senior nurse academic and doctoral candidate with twelve years of community-nursing experience in West Java. The co-investigators bring expertise in critical-care nursing, community health, orthopaedics, and gerontology. The first author conducted no prior clinical care with the participating puskesmas during the study window. A reflexive memo log was maintained throughout fieldwork and analysis, documenting analytic decisions, assumptions, and shifts in interpretation.

Setting and Participants

Participants were 13 elderly-health programme holders, each representing one of the 13 puskesmas in Cimahi City, West Java, Indonesia. Cimahi is a mid-sized urban municipality (population approximately 580,000) administratively distinct from the surrounding Bandung metropolitan area, served by a network of 13 puskesmas under one municipal health office. The local network thus provides a near-complete sampling frame for primary-care elderly-health delivery in the city.

Inclusion criteria were: (i) currently designated as elderly-health programme holder (pemegang program lansia) at a puskesmas in Cimahi City; (ii) minimum 12 months in role; (iii) direct involvement in older-adult clinical care, screening, or programme management; and (iv) willingness to participate in audio-recorded focus group discussion (FGD). Total population sampling was used, capturing all eligible programme holders across the 13 centres. Recruitment was facilitated by the Cimahi City Health Office and conducted via formal letter and follow-up by the research team.

Data Collection

Two facilitator-led FGDs were conducted on 12 and 14 August 2025 at a neutral municipal venue convenient to all participants. The 13 participants were distributed across the two FGDs (six and seven participants), with disciplines deliberately mixed across the two groups. Each group included a mix of professional backgrounds; across the full sample, the disciplines were nursing, midwifery, public health, nutrition, and medicine. Each FGD lasted approximately 90–110 minutes. Facilitation was led by the first author, with a co-facilitator managing audio recording and field notes.

A semi-structured FGD guide was developed by mapping each HPM construct to open-ended prompts. Perceived susceptibility and severity were explored through questions on fall causation and perceived seriousness; perceived barriers and self-efficacy through questions on protocol gaps, training, and confidence in delivering prevention; interpersonal and situational influence through questions on family, cadre, and environmental context; and commitment-to-action through questions on desired design features and willingness to use a platform. The guide was supplemented by the wider literature on assistive technologies for older adults.22 Domains covered fall causation, perceived risk among the older population served, current preventive activities, barriers, existing digital practice, and design features desired for an e-counseling platform. The full interview guide is provided in Supplementary File S1, and the COREQ reporting checklist is provided in Supplementary File S2. All discussions were conducted in Bahasa Indonesia; Sundanese phrases used by participants were preserved verbatim and translated only at the analysis stage. Audio was transcribed verbatim by a trained transcriber and accuracy-checked against the recording by the first author.

Data Analysis

Transcripts were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis, following the six-phase process of familiarisation, initial coding, theme generation, theme review, theme definition, and reporting.23,24 Two researchers (LS and IP) conducted independent initial coding on both transcripts, then met to discuss, refine, and reconcile codes; remaining disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (CEK). A coding approach combining inductive (data-driven) and deductive (HPM-construct-driven) approaches was used in a hybrid framework consistent with theory-anchored qualitative description and good-practice guidance.25 Themes were mapped onto HPM constructs (perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, perceived self-efficacy, interpersonal influence, situational influence, and commitment-to-action plan) and reviewed for coherence, distinctiveness, and grounding in data; the full theme-to-HPM-construct mapping is provided in Supplementary File S3, and the reflexivity statement is provided in Supplementary File S4. Analytic software (NVivo 14) supported code organisation and audit trail. Trustworthiness was supported through prolonged engagement during FGDs, dual independent coding, peer debriefing among the multidisciplinary research team, member-checking of theme summaries with a random subset (n=4) of participants, and maintenance of a complete audit trail and reflexive memo log.

Multi-Manuscript Data-Use Disclosure

This article uses exclusively the verbatim transcripts and analytic outputs derived from the FGD with the 13 puskesmas programme holders. Wholly distinct verbatim transcripts from in-depth interviews with 6 family caregivers and 6 community health cadres (collected as separate dissertation phases) are reported in two companion submissions to Acta Psychologica (psychological constructs) and Clinical Interventions in Aging (community-cadre perspectives), respectively. No verbatim quotation appears in more than one manuscript, and no thematic narrative is duplicated. This protocol is consistent with established ethical practice for serial reporting of large qualitative datasets in doctoral nursing and gerontology research.

Ethics

The study received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee, Universitas Padjadjaran (No. 455/UN6.KEP/EC/2025; Registration No. 2503050311). All participants gave written informed consent prior to the FGD. Participants were informed during the consent process that their anonymised responses and direct quotations might be published in the resulting manuscript. Identifiers were replaced with codes P1–P13. Audio recordings were stored on an encrypted institutional server.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Thirteen elderly-health programme holders participated, one from each puskesmas in Cimahi City. The sample was predominantly female (n=12, 92.3%), reflecting the gendered composition of the primary-care nursing workforce. Mean age was 41.2 years (range 32–55), and mean time in role was 6.4 years (range 1–18). Disciplinary backgrounds were heterogeneous, including registered nurses (n=7), midwives (n=2), public-health promoters (n=2), nutritionists (n=1), and a general medical doctor (n=1), reflecting the multidisciplinary nature of Indonesian primary-care delivery. Theme 1 — Multifactorial Fall Causation: An Integrated Intrinsic-Extrinsic-Behavioural Account

[HPM constructs: Perceived susceptibility; perceived severity; situational influence.]

All 13 participants articulated a multifactorial understanding of fall causation that integrated intrinsic physiological factors (declining muscle strength, balance impairment, polypharmacy, dizziness, comorbid hypertension and diabetes), extrinsic environmental factors (slippery bathroom floors (kamar mandi licin), uneven steps, poor lighting, cluttered home interiors), and behavioural factors (refusal to use assistive devices, unsupervised activity, low engagement with screening). This integrated account closely mirrors the multifactorial framework of the World Falls Guidelines.1

Penyebabnya banyak, kondisi fisik lansianya, lingkungan rumah, terutama kamar mandi yang licin, dan kadang lansianya sendiri tidak mau pakai tongkat walau sudah disarankan. (‘There are many causes, the older person’s physical condition, the home environment, especially the slippery bathroom, and sometimes the older person themself refuses to use a cane even when advised.’), P5

Participants emphasised that the perceived severity of falls is often under-recognised by older adults and their families, who may treat a non-injurious fall as inconsequential. This perception was identified as a fundamental barrier to preventive engagement.

Banyak yang jatuh sekali tidak dianggap masalah… padahal itu sinyal awal. Setelah jatuh kedua, ketiga, baru keluarga datang ke kami, sering sudah dengan patah panggul. (‘Many treat a single fall as unimportant; it is, in fact, an early signal. By the second or third fall, the family comes to us, often already with a hip fracture.’), P9

This finding aligns closely with the literature on under-perception of fall risk in community-dwelling older adults.26

Theme 2 — Structural Barriers: A System without a Protocol

[HPM constructs: Perceived barriers (organisational); perceived self-efficacy (professional).]

A striking and consistent finding was that none of the 13 puskesmas operated a written, fall-specific clinical protocol (standard operating procedure, SOP). Fall risk screening, when performed, was integrated informally into general elderly-health visits (Posyandu Lansia) rather than triggered by a structured tool. Participants located this absence within a wider system of structural constraints: large catchment areas, workforce shortages, competing programmatic priorities, and the cost of mobile data, which limited their ability to share multimedia educational content with older patients and families.

When asked whether they felt adequately prepared to assess fall severity, most programme holders reported limited confidence; fall-risk assessment was not part of their pre-service or in-service training, and severity was often judged intuitively rather than with a validated instrument. This perceived gap in professional self-efficacy reinforced participants’ consistent call for a built-in, structured screening tool within any future platform.

Kami belum punya SOP khusus pencegahan jatuh. Kalau ada kasus, ya kami tangani sebisanya, edukasi ringan, rujuk kalau perlu, tapi sistematik belum. (‘We do not yet have a specific fall-prevention SOP. When a case arises, we handle it as best we can: give light education, refer if necessary. But it is not systematic.’), P3

Beban kerjanya luas, bukan hanya lansia, ada KIA, gizi, TB, NCD, semua. Wilayah binaan kami juga luas, satu petugas bisa pegang ribuan lansia. (‘The workload is broad, not only the elderly, there is maternal-child health (KIA), nutrition, tuberculosis (TB), non-communicable diseases (NCD), all of it. Our service area is large too, one staff member can be responsible for thousands of older adults.’), P11

A specific sub-barrier was limited family engagement. Programme holders consistently noted that many older adults were brought to the Posyandu Lansia by cadres, not family members, and that working-age children frequently delegated daily care decisions to grandparents or domestic helpers.

Anak-anaknya kerja, jadi seringkali yang ngantar ke posyandu itu cucunya, bahkan kadernya. Komunikasi langsung dengan keluarga utama sulit. (‘Their adult children work, so often the grandchild or even the cadre is the one who brings them to the post. Direct communication with the primary family is difficult.’), P2

Theme 3 — Existing Digital Substrate: WhatsApp as the De Facto E-Counseling Channel

[HPM constructs: Interpersonal influence; situational influence; perceived benefits.]

All 13 participants reported routine use of WhatsApp (both individual messaging and group chats) to maintain contact with older patients, families, and cadres between formal visits. This existing digital substrate emerged as a critical insight: any new e-counseling system must integrate with, rather than displace, the WhatsApp ecosystem.

Sebenarnya kami sudah pakai WhatsApp setiap hari. Grup kader, grup keluarga risiko tinggi, broadcast jadwal posyandu, semua sudah jalan. Hanya saja, tidak terstruktur. (‘Honestly, we already use WhatsApp every day. Cadre groups, high-risk family groups, broadcast schedules; all are running. It just is not structured.’), P7

Several participants described creating short voice notes (pesan suara) in Sundanese for older adults with limited literacy.

Kadang saya rekam voice note pendek pakai bahasa Sunda. Lansianya yang tidak bisa baca tetap paham. (‘Sometimes I record a short voice note in Sundanese. Older people who cannot read still understand.’), P10

Participants also identified critical limitations: lack of central record of advice given; inability to track adherence; risk of misinformation circulating in shared groups; and absence of structured fall-risk content. These limitations frame the rationale for a dedicated, structured e-counseling system.

[HPM constructs: Perceived benefits; perceived self-efficacy (professional and family); commitment-to-action plan.]

Participants articulated a set of design requirements that map systematically onto HPM constructs (Table 3). The specifications converge on five domains: (i) structured fall-risk screening within the platform, generating an actionable risk score; (ii) multimedia educational content (short videos, infographics, and audio in Indonesian and regional languages), delivered at appropriate literacy level; (iii) asynchronous Q&A allowing families and cadres to submit questions answered by the assigned programme holder within a service-level agreement; (iv) integration with cadre and family networks, including a tiered access layer that recognises cadres as digital intermediaries for low-literacy older adults; and (v) light-touch monitoring and reminders for medication, posyandu visits, and home-safety actions.

The “tiered access” model participants described refers to a graduated pathway in which older adults with adequate digital literacy and family support interact directly with the platform, while those with limited literacy, vision, or device access are supported by kader as digital intermediaries who relay questions, interpret content, and upload home-safety photographs; programme holders retain clinical oversight and escalation authority at the apex of the tier.

Participants explicitly contrasted their design preferences with stand-alone applications they had previously encountered, which they considered too clinical, English-language, or unsuited to older-adult cognitive and visual needs. Two emphasised that any tool must augment rather than replace face-to-face care.

Aplikasi itu harus melengkapi kunjungan kami, bukan menggantikan. Lansia tetap butuh disentuh, dilihat, tapi di antara kunjungan, aplikasi bisa membantu keluarga. (‘The app should complement our visits, not replace them. The older person still needs to be touched and seen; but in between visits, the app can help the family.’), P9

Discussion

This study provides, to our knowledge, the first multidisciplinary, theory-anchored, primary-care-led needs assessment of an HPM-based e-counseling system for older-adult fall prevention in Indonesia. Four findings warrant focused discussion: the system-level absence of fall protocols, the pre-existing WhatsApp substrate, the systematic mapping of design needs to HPM constructs, and the contrast with high-income-country platforms.

The Protocol Vacuum and Its Implications

The universal absence of a fall-specific written protocol across all 13 puskesmas is striking but not isolated. Comparable qualitative evidence from other LMIC primary-care settings has documented a similar gap, often attributed to competing programmatic priorities (maternal-child health, communicable disease, nutrition), under-recognition of falls as a public-health priority, and limited inclusion of falls in national health-worker training curricula.27 Recent global syntheses argue that falls are integral to the Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030)28 and that primary care must explicitly integrate fall risk screening into routine elderly-health visits.1 Our findings suggest that interest in such integration exists among the participating Indonesian primary-care workers themselves, provided the tools, training, and protocol scaffolding are made available.

Internationally, the World Falls Guidelines call for stratified fall-prevention pathways anchored in primary care.1 Translating this guidance into Indonesia requires building system scaffolding before, or alongside, any technology layer. An e-counseling platform, deployed without a clinical protocol, risks amplifying rather than solving the protocol vacuum. Our participants intuited this risk; their consistent insistence that the platform must complement, not replace, face-to-face care reflects a mature professional understanding of digital-care boundaries echoed in recent virtual-care literature.14

Building on, Not Around, the WhatsApp Substrate

The dominance of WhatsApp as a pre-existing digital substrate is a finding of design significance. Rather than introducing a new and competing communication channel, our findings strongly support a hybrid architecture in which a structured e-counseling platform integrates with WhatsApp through one-way notification links, shared content templates, and clinician-validated educational assets. This is consistent with research on mHealth in LMICs, where adoption is highest when interventions extend existing communication practices rather than replacing them.13 It also aligns with mounting evidence that older adults’ sustained digital-health adoption depends on relational scaffolding by trusted intermediaries, in this case, the programme holders, cadres, and family caregivers who already populate these WhatsApp networks,17 a pattern echoed in qualitative evidence on WhatsApp for older-adult health communication.15

A potential drawback of WhatsApp integration is notification fatigue, as older adults and families already receive multiple broadcast messages; the proposed design mitigates this by restricting automated reminders to light-touch, scheduled prompts and by routing all clinical queries through the named programme holder rather than unmoderated group broadcasts.

The fact that programme holders independently produced Sundanese-language voice notes for low-literacy older adults reveals an indigenous design innovation that an e-counseling platform should institutionalise. Such localised, language-sensitive, multimodal content is the inverse of the English-language, text-heavy interfaces that participants reported as poorly suited to their populations.

High-Income versus Low- and Middle-Income Country Design Implications

Our findings diverge from the predominantly high-income-country (HIC) evidence base in two respects. In HIC settings, fall-prevention digital tools are typically deployed within established multifactorial assessment pathways and assume reliable internet access and device ownership,11,12 whereas our participants described a context without any fall-specific protocol and with intermittent connectivity and shared devices. Second, the central role of kader as digital intermediaries has no direct analogue in most HIC primary-care models and represents a distinctive LMIC design feature; this aligns with mHealth reviews indicating that adoption in LMICs is highest when interventions extend existing community health-worker roles rather than imposing new ones.13,29 These differences suggest that direct importation of HIC platforms is unlikely to succeed and that a hybrid, cadre-mediated, WhatsApp-integrated architecture is better suited to Indonesian primary care.

Mapping Needs to HPM: From Description to Specification

Reflexive thematic analysis with deductive HPM mapping yielded a tightly specified set of design features (Table 3). Each feature responds to a recognisable HPM construct, transforming descriptive findings into actionable specifications. Notably, the interpersonal-influence and situational-influence constructs emerged as central, reflecting the relational and contextual fabric of Indonesian primary care, where cadres, family, neighbourhood, and faith networks shape preventive behaviour as much as individual perception. This finding extends HPM’s predominantly individual-level framing toward a relational digital architecture, consistent with international evidence that older-adult uptake of assistive technologies depends on social and contextual scaffolding rather than device features alone.22

Integration with Wider Indonesian Geriatric Digital Health

The high pooled prevalence of frailty among Indonesian older adults30 and the well-documented gaps in community health worker training and supervision29 underline the urgency of building primary-care capacity before, or alongside, the deployment of digital tools. Our findings complement this evidence by foregrounding the upstream layer: the workforce, protocol, and pedagogical substrate that must be in place for any digital fall-prevention system to be usable. Taken together, the literature and the present findings point to an emerging framework for digitally augmented geriatric care in Indonesia that integrates (i) protocol scaffolding in primary care, (ii) culturally grounded e-counseling, (iii) cadre and family scaffolding for older-adult digital access, and (iv) ongoing workforce development, with each layer informed by stakeholder needs assessment.

Implications for Practice and Policy

Our findings imply three practice and policy actions. First, the Indonesian Ministry of Health and provincial health offices should consider mandating an integrated fall-risk screening protocol within Posyandu Lansia delivery, using a brief, validated tool. Second, e-counseling investment should prioritise platforms that integrate with WhatsApp rather than replace it, and that include cadre-mediated access layers. Third, continuing-education modules for puskesmas multidisciplinary teams should explicitly address falls, integrating digital and face-to-face delivery components. Beyond Indonesia, the cadre-mediated, WhatsApp-integrated architecture may offer a transferable model for other LMIC primary-care systems with similar workforce structures and digital practices, subject to local adaptation and feasibility testing.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include the achievement of total sampling across all 13 puskesmas of a defined urban region, the multidisciplinary composition of participants (nursing, medicine, public health, midwifery, nutrition), the use of an internationally recognised theoretical framework, and the rigorous application of reflexive thematic analysis with dual independent coding and member-checking. Limitations include the single-city setting, which constrains transferability to rural and remote Indonesian contexts and to non-Sundanese cultural regions; the FGD format, which may have suppressed minority views; the absence of older adults’ direct voice within this manuscript (addressed in companion papers reporting family-caregiver and community-cadre perspectives); and variation in internet infrastructure and mobile-data affordability across Indonesia’s more remote provinces, which may further constrain the feasibility of multimedia, WhatsApp-based e-counseling beyond urban settings such as Cimahi. Future research should examine feasibility and equity of access across diverse provincial contexts.

Future Directions

These findings now require translation into prototype design and feasibility evaluation. Next steps include (i) co-design workshops with programme holders, cadres, and families using the design specifications in Table 3 as scaffolding; (ii) prototype development with integration into WhatsApp infrastructure; (iii) feasibility and usability testing in two puskesmas; and (iv) a clustered pilot evaluation against fall-rate outcomes, fall-related fear, and HPM-derived intermediate measures (perceived self-efficacy, commitment-to-action).

Conclusion

In this study, multidisciplinary primary-care programme holders across all 13 puskesmas of Cimahi City described fall risk as multifactorial (research question 1); identified structural barriers to prevention, including the absence of any fall-specific protocol, workforce shortages, large catchments, limited family engagement, and mobile-data costs (research question 2); reported WhatsApp as the existing digital substrate for informal counselling (research question 3); and specified design requirements for an HPM-based e-counseling platform—risk screening, multimedia education, asynchronous Q&A, tiered cadre-mediated access, reminders, and a home-safety checklist (research question 4). These findings map systematically onto HPM constructs of perceived susceptibility and severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, perceived self-efficacy, interpersonal and situational influence, and commitment-to-action plan. Indonesian primary-care multidisciplinary teams therefore articulate a system-level case for an HPM-based e-counseling platform that augments face-to-face care, integrates with the existing WhatsApp substrate, includes cadre- and family-mediated access layers, and sits within a strengthened fall-prevention protocol. As a single-city assessment, these findings offer preliminary, context-specific insight for LMIC primary care and require confirmation in future prototype and feasibility studies.
