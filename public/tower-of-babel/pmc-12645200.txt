Transient cardiac arrest due to trigeminocardiac reflex during aneurysm clipping: illustrative case

The trigeminocardiac reflex (TCR) is a well-established phenomenon that causes bradycardia, hypotension, and cardiac arrest due to mechanical stimulation of the trigeminal nerve distribution.1 This reflex is mediated through a pathway involving trigeminal sensory nerve terminals that transmit signals via the gasserian ganglion to the trigeminal sensory nucleus, with subsequent activation of the vagal motor nucleus through brainstem reticular formation neurons.2 TCR has been predominantly reported during posterior fossa surgeries, particularly cerebellopontine angle tumor resections and microvascular decompression for trigeminal neuralgia, as well as transsphenoidal procedures.1,3,4 Although TCR is recognized as a known complication that can result from stimulation of various trigeminal nerve–innervated intracranial structures, its occurrence during supratentorial aneurysm clipping is exceedingly rare, and effective preventive strategies for this specific scenario remain poorly defined.

Here, we describe a case of transient cardiac arrest during internal carotid–posterior communicating artery (IC-PC) aneurysm clipping, possibly related to dural stimulation by the aneurysm clip blade. This case demonstrates the importance of appropriate clip selection and surgical techniques in preventing this potentially serious complication, thereby providing valuable educational insights for neurosurgeons performing aneurysm clipping procedures.

Illustrative Case

An 88-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with sudden onset of severe headache. Her medical history included hypertension, with no history of cardiac disease. Her medication included amlodipine (5 mg daily). Physical examination revealed a blood pressure of 184/76 mm Hg, heart rate of 90 beats per minute, and Glasgow Coma Scale score of E4V5M6, with no neurological deficits. An initial electrocardiogram revealed no abnormalities.

CT of the head revealed a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), predominantly in the basal cisterns and right sylvian fissure (Fig. 1A). CT angiography revealed three intracranial aneurysms: a 6-mm right IC-PC aneurysm, a 3-mm anterior communicating artery (Acom) aneurysm, and a 5-mm right distal anterior cerebral artery aneurysm (Fig. 1C). The patient underwent craniotomy via the right pterional approach for clipping of the ruptured IC-PC aneurysm and prophylactic clipping of the Acom aneurysm on the day following the onset of SAH. Anesthesia was induced with 150 μg of fentanyl, 60 mg of propofol, and 50 mg of rocuronium and maintained with remifentanil at 0.1 μg/kg/min and sevoflurane at 1.2%.

During application of the aneurysm clip to the right IC-PC aneurysm, a slightly curved Sugita Titanium Aneurysm Clip II no. 65 standard type (11 mm) (Mizuho Corporation), was inserted from the lateral aspect of the internal carotid artery (ICA), with the clip blade oriented along the dura mater of the sphenoid ridge (Fig. 2A). Cardiac arrest occurred immediately during the clip insertion and before clip closure (Fig. 2B). Clip application was discontinued and spontaneous cardiac rhythm resumed without cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Intraoperative monitoring demonstrated a complete loss of electrocardiographic waveforms and arterial pressure for approximately 8 seconds (Fig. 2E). Considering the possibility that the clip blade had contacted the dura mater of the sphenoid ridge or the clip tip had contacted the tentorial dura, the clip was changed to a straight Sugita Titanium Aneurysm Clip II no. 15 standard type (15 mm) (Mizuho Corporation) for better tip control (Fig. 2C). The straight clip was applied in a more horizontal orientation toward the suprasellar cistern to avoid dural contact, allowing successful clip closure without cardiac arrest (Fig. 2D). Clipping of the Acom aneurysm was performed without any complications. The right distal anterior cerebral artery aneurysm was not treated. The patient recovered uneventfully, without any neurological deficits. Fasudil hydrochloride hydrate and ozagrel sodium were initiated on postoperative day 1 (day 2 after SAH onset) to prevent cerebral vasospasm and continued for 2 weeks. Fasudil, a Rho-kinase inhibitor, and ozagrel, a thromboxane A2 synthetase inhibitor, are commonly used in Japan for vasospasm prevention following SAH.5,6 Postoperative contrast-enhanced CT imaging confirmed that the clip blade was appropriately directed toward the suprasellar cistern, avoiding contact with deeper dural structures including the tentorium that could potentially trigger TCR (Fig. 1D). There was no evidence of cerebral vasospasms on imaging studies. On postoperative day 10, the patient developed COVID-19, resulting in disorientation and somnolence. Although these symptoms subsequently improved, the COVID-19 infection led to a decline in activities of daily living, and the patient was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital on postoperative day 41. No bradyarrhythmias were detected during the hospital stay.

Discussion

Observations

While TCR during neurosurgical procedures is well recognized, this case provides novel insights into preventive strategies specific to supratentorial aneurysm clipping. We describe an extremely rare instance of cardiac arrest due to TCR, possibly related to dural stimulation during aneurysm clipping. TCR is typically defined as a 20% decrease in mean arterial pressure accompanied by a heart rate reduction to less than 60 beats per minute.1 However, this case presented with 8 seconds of complete cardiac arrest during clip insertion, representing a more severe manifestation of the reflex. Immediate withdrawal of the clip resulted in rapid spontaneous recovery without requiring atropine administration, which may be considered persistent bradycardia.1,7

TCR has been well documented during posterior fossa surgeries, particularly cerebellopontine angle tumor resections and microvascular decompression procedures.1,3 In aneurysm surgery, several cases of TCR have been reported during extracranial procedures, particularly dural manipulation around the sphenoid ridge during craniotomy.8–11 However, TCR during intracranial procedures, such as clip application, is extremely rare, with only two IC-PC aneurysm cases reported in the literature. One case occurred during temporary clipping12 and another during permanent clip application, although the latter case also experienced TCR during craniotomy dural manipulation.10 Cardiac arrest occurring exclusively during permanent clip application without preceding dural manipulation–related episodes, as in this case, has not been previously reported.

The anatomical basis for TCR induction associated with IC-PC aneurysm clipping lies in the extensive trigeminal nerve innervation of intracranial structures, including the tentorium cerebelli by the recurrent meningeal branch of the first division of the trigeminal nerve (Arnold’s recurrent nerve), the sphenoid ridge dura by the second and third divisions of the trigeminal nerve, and trigeminal nerve endings distributed in the arterial walls of the ICA.13–15 Notably, in contrast to a previous report that implicated direct arterial stimulation as the primary mechanism for TCR,12 the present case demonstrated that altering the clip geometry—thus avoiding dural contact while maintaining the same vascular contact at the aneurysm neck—successfully prevented recurrent cardiac arrest. This observation strongly suggests that dural stimulation, rather than direct vascular stimulation, was responsible for the TCR in this case.

Two potential mechanisms may have contributed to dural stimulation in this older adult patient. First, atherosclerotic changes may have caused the ICA to assume a more horizontal course, narrowing the space available for clip insertion. In such confined spaces, applying the curved clip along the dural surface resulted in a broader contact area between the clip blade and the sphenoid ridge dura during insertion, potentially triggering the TCR through increased stimulus intensity. However, unlike previous reports in which TCR occurred during both sphenoid ridge dural manipulation during craniotomy and subsequent clip application,10 this case demonstrated TCR only during clip application, suggesting that the extent or intensity of dural contact during clip insertion was a critical factor. Second, the inherent tendency of the curved clip to deflect posterosuperiorly during insertion may have caused inadvertent contact between the clip tip and the tentorial edge, particularly given the proximity of the horizontally oriented ICA to the tentorium in this patient.

The most distinctive feature of this case was that complications were successfully avoided by changing the clip geometry and approach. In contrast to the curved clip, the straight Sugita no. 15 clip provided better tip control and allowed more predictable linear trajectory control, even with a longer clip length. The straight clip was applied in a more horizontal orientation toward the suprasellar cistern without following the dural surface during insertion, enabling intentional avoidance of both tentorial and sphenoid ridge dural contacts. This experience demonstrates that clip selection for IC-PC aneurysm clipping should consider not only aneurysm size and morphology but also spatial constraints, clip trajectory control, and potential contact with surrounding anatomical structures.

Several factors are known to predispose patients to TCR, including hypoxemia, hypercapnia, light anesthesia, pediatric age, β-blocker or calcium channel blocker use, and certain anesthetic agents.7 The patient’s preoperative use of amlodipine, a calcium channel blocker, may have served as an additional predisposing factor. Furthermore, remifentanil has been reported to lower the threshold for vagal reflexes,16 which may have contributed to the TCR activation in this case.

Based on this experience, several preventive measures should be implemented during aneurysm clipping. Preoperative assessment should include a detailed imaging evaluation of the spatial relationship between aneurysms and the surrounding structures, particularly the tentorium, in IC-PC aneurysm cases. Careful consideration of the clip geometry and its directional implications relative to the surrounding anatomy is essential, with preference given to clip orientations that minimize contact with innervated structures. Continuous electrocardiographic and hemodynamic monitoring throughout the procedure allows for the early detection of TCR. Surgical techniques should emphasize meticulous avoidance of dural stimulation during clip application, particularly in older patients, where anatomical relationships may be altered. Anesthetic management should consider the potential for TCR, especially when using agents known to lower vagal stimulation thresholds.

This case report had some limitations. Cardiac arrest occurred only during the use of the slightly curved clip, limiting the ability to definitively establish causation. Additionally, the precise dural region responsible for triggering the TCR could not be determined intraoperatively; it remains unclear whether the sphenoid ridge dura, tentorial dura, or both contributed to the reflex. Additionally, the patient’s advanced age and multiple comorbidities may have influenced the severity of the TCR response, potentially limiting the generalizability of these findings to younger populations.

Lessons

Although TCR is a recognized phenomenon, this case demonstrates that TCR can occur during supratentorial aneurysm clipping via dural stimulation using clip blades. The complication was successfully avoided by switching from a curved clip that followed the dural surface to a straight clip applied horizontally toward the suprasellar cistern, thereby avoiding contact with the tentorial dura. These findings underscore the importance of careful clip geometry selection and precise trajectory control relative to adjacent anatomical structures. This consideration is particularly critical in older adult patients, whose anatomical changes may increase the risk of inadvertent dural contact. Prompt intraoperative recognition of TCR and appropriate technical modifications can mitigate the risk of serious cardiovascular complications.
