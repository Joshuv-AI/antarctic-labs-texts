Differentiability and overlap concentration in optimal Bayesian inference
Hong-Bin Chen, Victor Issa

Abstract
. In this short note, we consider models of optimal Bayesian inference of finite-rank tensor products. We add to the model a linear channel parametrized by h h . We show that at every interior differentiable point h h of the free energy (associated with the model), the overlap concentrates at the gradient of the free energy and the minimum mean-square error converges to a related limit. In other words, the model is replica-symmetric at every differentiable point. At any signal-to-noise ratio, such points h h form a full-measure set (hence h = 0 h=0 belongs to the closure of these points). For a sufficiently low signal-to-noise ratio, we show that every interior point is a differentiable point. Keywords and phrases: statistical inference, Hamilton–Jacobi equation, replica symmetry MSC 2020: 82B44, 82D30, 35D40



1.
Introduction

Recently, there has been tremendous progress in understanding the information-theoretical aspect of statistical inference models. These works often utilize the toolbox from the mean-field spin glass theory. In particular, the Bayesian inference of finite-rank tensor products in the optimal case (where the posterior is known) can be seen as a simple variant of a mean-field spin glass model. One important feature of the optimal model is that the system is always in the replica symmetry regime. This means that the spin-glass order parameter, the overlap, always concentrates (under a small perturbation).

In this note, we clarify the connection between the differentiability of the limit free energy associated with the inference model and the concentration of the overlap as well as the convergence of minimal mean-square errors. The theme is closely related to the so-called generic model in the spin glass (see
[
54
, Section 15.4]
and
[
47
, Section 3.7]
).
In the context of optimal Bayesian inference, we show that at any differentiable point (with respect to the parameter
h
for an additional linear channel (or external field)) of the limit free energy, the overlap concentrates.
In other words, the model is
replica-symmetric at such points.
This is different from the result by Barbier in
[
3
]
that shows the concentration of the overlap under an average of small perturbation (which corresponds to the same phenomenon in general non-generic spin glass models).

The parameter
h
also corresponds to the spatial parameter in the Hamilton–Jacobi equation representation of the limit free energy.
Since the limit free energy is Lipschitz, Lebesgue-almost-every point is differentiable.
Moreover, under our assumption, the initial condition (namely, the free energy associated with the linear channel) is smooth. Hence, we can solve the equation using characteristic lines for a short time (small signal-to-noise ratio) and the solution is smooth. Therefore, in this regime, every interior point
h
is a differentiable point.

This short-time regularity result is less obvious in our case because the equation is not posed on the entire Euclidean space but on a closed convex cone. Hence, it is important that the nonlinearity “points in the right direction” so that the characteristics do not leave the domain.

Setting and the main result

Throughout, we write
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
and
\mathbb{R}_{++}=(0,\infty)

For any matrices

We can identify

We consider the statistical inference problem of general tensor products as in
[

Fix

Fix any

Fix any deterministic

Let

The

\displaystyle Y=\sqrt{\frac{2t}{N^{{\mathsf{p}}-1}}}X^{\otimes{\mathsf{p}}}A+W

In addition to

For

,

, and

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{N}(t,h,\mathbf{x})=&\sqrt{\frac{2t}{N^{{\mathsf{p}}-1}}}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\otimes p}A\right)\cdot Y-\frac{t}{N^{{\mathsf{p}}-1}}\left|\mathbf{x}^{\otimes{\mathsf{p}}}A\right|^{2}\\

Here,

is the matrix square root of

.
The randomness in

We define the associated Gibbs measure by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{N,t,h}\propto\exp\left(H_{N}(t,h,\mathbf{x})\right)\mathrm{d}P_{N}(\mathbf{x}).

By the Bayes rule, the Gibbs measure satisfies

(1.4)

\displaystyle\left\langle g\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\rangle_{N,t,h}=\mathbb{E}\left[g\left(X\right)\,\big|\,Y,\overline{Y}\right]

for any bounded measurable function

.
Slightly abusing the notation, we denote still by

\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
from
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{N,t,h}

let

be the enriched free energy (see
[
21

) defined by

(1.5)

\displaystyle F_{N}(t,h)=\frac{1}{N}\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}}\exp\left(H_{N}(t,h,\mathbf{x})\right)\mathrm{d}P_{N}(\mathbf{x}).

For each

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}(q)=\left(AA^{\intercal}\right)\cdot q^{\otimes{\mathsf{p}}},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall q\in S^{D}.

Theorem 1.1

(
[

]

Assume

,
(H2)
, and

.
The function

to the unique Lipschitz viscosity solution of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f-\mathsf{H}(\nabla_{h}f)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\mathbb{R}_{+}\times S^{D}_{+}$}

.

Moreover,

(1.8)

\displaystyle f(t,h)=\sup_{h^{\prime}\in S^{D}_{+}}\left\{h^{\prime}\cdot h-\psi^{*}(h^{\prime})+t\mathsf{H}\left(h^{\prime}\right)\right\},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,h)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times S^{D}_{+};

if in addition

\displaystyle f(t,h)=\sup_{h^{\prime}\in S^{D}_{+}}\left\{\psi(h+h^{\prime})-t\mathsf{H}^{*}(h^{\prime}/t)\right\},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,h)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times S^{D}_{+}.

combined with other results. We give a detailed explanation in Section

Remark 1.2

(Almost everywhere differentiability)

Since

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

h+sa\in S^{D}_{++}
for every
s\in[-r_{0},r_{0}]
.
For
r\in(0,r_{0}]
, we set

\displaystyle\delta_{N}(r)=\left|F_{N}(h-ra)-\overline{F}_{N}(h-ra)\right|+\left|F_{N}(h)-\overline{F}_{N}(h)\right|+\left|F_{N}(h+ra)-\overline{F}_{N}(h+ra)\right|.

By (
4.15
) from Lemma
4.5
and the Taylor expansion, there is a constant
C
depending only on
h
,
a
, and
r_{0}
such that, for every
r\in(0,r_{0}]
,

\displaystyle F_{N}(h+ra)-F_{N}(h)\geqslant ra\cdot\nabla F_{N}(h)-Cr^{2}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}|Z|,

\displaystyle F_{N}(h-ra)-F_{N}(h)\leqslant ra\cdot\nabla F_{N}(h)+Cr^{2}N^{-\frac{1}{2}}|Z|.

The above two displays together give

\displaystyle a\cdot\nabla F_{N}(h)-a\cdot\nabla\overline{F}_{N}(h)\leqslant\frac{\overline{F}_{N}(h+ra)-\overline{F}_{N}(h)}{r}-a\cdot\nabla\overline{F}_{N}(h)+\frac{\delta_{N}(r)}{r}+CrN^{-\frac{1}{2}}|Z|,

\displaystyle a\cdot\nabla F_{N}(h)-a\cdot\nabla\overline{F}_{N}(h)\geqslant\frac{\overline{F}_{N}(h)-\overline{F}_{N}(h-ra)}{r}-a\cdot\nabla\overline{F}_{N}(h)-\frac{\delta_{N}(r)}{r}-CrN^{-\frac{1}{2}}|Z|.

The concentration assumption
(H3)
ensures
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\delta_{N}(r)=0
.
Recall from (
1.1
) that
Z
is an
N\times{D}
matrix with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries. Therefore, we have
\mathbb{E}|Z|\leqslant\sqrt{N{D}}
. Also,
\overline{F}_{N}
converges to
f
pointwise as given by Theorem
1.1
.
Using these and (
3.3
), we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left|a\cdot\nabla F_{N}(h)-a\cdot\nabla\overline{F}_{N}(h)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\frac{f(h+ra)-f(h)}{r}-a\cdot\nabla f(h)\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{f(h)-f(h-ra)}{r}-a\cdot\nabla f(h)\right|+Cr\sqrt{{D}}.

Now, sending
r\to 0
and using the differentiability of
f
at
h
, we deduce that the left-hand side in the above vanishes. Finally, inserting this to (
4.22
), we arrive at (
4.21
).

Since (
4.18
) and (
4.21
) together yield (
4.17
), the proof is complete.
∎

Now, we are ready for the main task.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Recall
Q
from (

).
Fix any
(t,h)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times S^{D}_{++}
such that
f(t,\cdot)
is differentiable at

.
We verify (
4.1
) and (
4.2
). First, (
4.1
) follows from (
3.3
), (
3.5
), (
4.4
). Then, we turn to (
4.2
).
Recall
\ell_{0}(N)
and
\ell_{1}(N)
from (
4.10
). It is straightforward to see
\ell_{0}(N)\leqslant 2\ell_{1}(N)
. Hence, (
4.2
) follows from (
4.13
) in Lemma
4.3
provided
\lim_{N\to\infty}\ell_{1}(N)=0
. In the following, we deduce this from Lemma
4.4
.
Using (
4.6
), (
4.8
), and assumption
(H1)
, there is a constant depending only on

and

such that

\displaystyle\left|a\cdot{\mathcal{L}}\right|\leqslant C\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}|Z|+1\right)|a|

which immediately implies

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{L}}\right|\leqslant C\left(N^{-\frac{1}{2}}|Z|+1\right).

We write
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle=\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{N,t,h}
for simplicity.
Using this, we have that, for any

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\langle\left|{\mathcal{L}}-\mathbb{E}\left\langle{\mathcal{L}}\right\rangle\right|^{2}\right\rangle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\left|{\mathcal{L}}-\mathbb{E}\left\langle{\mathcal{L}}\right\rangle\right|^{2}\right\rangle\mathbf{1}_{N^{-\frac{1}{2}}|Z|\leqslant r}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\left|{\mathcal{L}}-\mathbb{E}\left\langle{\mathcal{L}}\right\rangle\right|^{2}\right\rangle\mathbf{1}_{N^{-\frac{1}{2}}|Z|>r}\right]

5.
Short-time regularity

Recall that
\psi
is the initial condition given in
(H2)
. In this section, we assume that

(5.1)

\psi
is twice differentiable with bounded derivatives (both first and second order),

as assumed in the last part of Theorem
1.3
. First, we show that this condition is satisfied under the stronger assumption
(HS)
. Note that under the condition in (
5.1
) the gradient of
\psi
is a Lipschitz function. In this section, again, we write
\nabla=\nabla_{h}
for brevity.

Lemma 5.1
.

Under
(HS)
, the condition in (
5.1
) holds.

Proof.

Under
(HS)
, we have
\psi=\overline{F}_{1}(0,\cdot)
. As previously, we write
\langle\cdot\rangle
in place of
\langle\cdot\rangle_{N=1,t=0,h}
. We let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}
denote independent copies of
\mathbf{x}
under
\langle\cdot\rangle
. Then, we can directly compute the derivatives of
\overline{F}_{1}(0,\cdot)
using Gaussian integration by parts (as proven in
[
41
, (3.8) and (3.10)]
) and the Nishimori identity (
4.4
) (also
[
41
, (3.3)]
). More precisely we will use the following identities, which hold for any
\mathbb{R}
-valued bounded measurable function
g
and any
\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
-valued bounded measurable function
G
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\langle g(x,x^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\langle g(x,X)\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\langle Z\cdot G(\mathbf{x},X)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\langle(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime})\sqrt{2h}\cdot G(\mathbf{x},X)\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\langle Z\cdot G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},X)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\langle(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-2\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime})\sqrt{2h}\cdot G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},X)\right\rangle.

For
a\in S^{D}
, we denote by
a\cdot\nabla
the operator defined by taking the directional derivative in direction
a
with respect to
h
, that is

(a\cdot\nabla)g(h)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{g(h+\varepsilon a)-g(h)}{\varepsilon}.

Recall from (
4.7
) that
\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{h}}(a)
denotes the derivative in the direction
a
of the square root function at
h
.
Then, we have

(a\cdot\nabla)H_{1}(0,h,\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{2}\mathcal{D}_{\sqrt{h}}(a)\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}Z+2a\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}X-a\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}

Differentiating
\psi
in direction
a
we obtain

(a\cdot\nabla)\psi(h)=\mathbb{E}\langle(a\cdot\nabla)H_{1}(0,h,\mathbf{x})\rangle.

Using the first Gaussian integration by part identity as recalled above, we recover
[
21
, (2.2)]
, namely

(a\cdot\nabla)\psi(h)=\mathbb{E}\langle a\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}X\rangle.

The boundedness of the signal yields that
\left|(a\cdot\nabla)\psi(h)\right|\leqslant C|a|
and thus the first derivative of
\psi
is bounded. Furthermore, differentiating the expression in the previous display in direction
b\in S^{D}
yields

(b\cdot\nabla)(a\cdot\nabla)\psi(h)=\mathbb{E}\left\langle a\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}X\left((b\cdot\nabla)H_{1}(0,h,\mathbf{x})-(b\cdot\nabla)H_{1}(0,h,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\right\rangle.

Again using the Gaussian integration by part identities we obtain, like in
[
41
, (3.27)]
, an expression of the form

(b\cdot\nabla)(a\cdot\nabla)\psi(h)=\mathbb{E}\langle\left(a\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}X\right)b\cdot P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime},X)\rangle,

where
P
is some explicit homogeneous polynomial of degree 2. Again, from the boundedness of the signals, we see that

|(b\cdot\nabla)(a\cdot\nabla)\psi(h)|\leqslant C|a||b|.

This proves that the second derivative of
\psi
is bounded.
∎

We consider the nonlinearity
\mathsf{H}
as in (
1.6
). For each
h\in S^{D}_{+}
, the characteristic line
t\mapsto X(t,h)
emitting from
h
is defined as

\displaystyle X(t,h)=h-t\nabla\mathsf{H}(\nabla\psi(h)),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Under (
5.1
), the goal is to show that the solution of (
1.7
) is twice differentiable for a short time. For equations defined on the entire Euclidean space, this is a standard result
[
26
, Exercise 2.10]
. But in our case, since the domain is the convex cone
S^{D}_{+}
, this standard argument does not apply directly. We need to ensure that the characteristics in the backward direction do not leave
S^{D}_{+}
.
Fortunately, we have

\displaystyle\nabla\mathsf{H}(a)\in S^{D}_{+},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall a\in S^{D}_{+}

which is a result of the monotonicity of
\mathsf{H}
proved in
[
21
, Lemma 4.2]
. Also,
\nabla\psi
takes value in
S^{D}_{+}
since
\overline{F}_{N}(0,\cdot)
is increasing in the direction of
S^{D}_{+}
as
\nabla\overline{F}_{N}(0,h)\in S^{D}_{+}
(see
[
21
, Lemma 2.1]
) and
\psi
is the limit of
\overline{F}_{N}(0,\cdot)
. Hence, we always have
\nabla\mathsf{H}(\nabla\psi(h))\in S^{D}_{+}
, which means that the characteristics go further into the cone
S^{D}_{+}
when traced backward.

Recall
L
from (
1.13
).
We first show that when
t<L^{-1}
the characteristics form a diffeomorphism.

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume (
5.1
). Let
t\in[0,L^{-1})
, then the following holds.

(1)

The map
X(t,\cdot):S^{D}_{+}\to X(t,S^{D}_{+})
admits a differentiable inverse function
Z(t,\cdot)
.

(2)

We have
X(t,S^{D}_{+})\supset S^{D}_{+}
.

By an inverse function, we mean
X(t,Z(t,h))=h
for every
h\in S^{D}_{+}
here.

Proof.

Due to the definition of
L
and
t<L^{-1}
, we can see that
\det(\nabla X(t,h))>0
for every
h\in S^{D}_{+}
. Therefore, in each local neighborhood,
X(t,\cdot)
is invertible. The definition of
L
and
t<L^{-1}
also ensure that the preimage of any bounded set under
X(t,\cdot)
is still bounded. Therefore, we can resort to classical results on the invertibility of maps (e.g.
[
2
, Theorem 1.8 in Chapter 3]
) to get the existence of a continuous map
Z(t,\cdot)
that serves as the inverse of
X(t,\cdot)
. Again using
t<L^{-1}
, we can first verify that
Z(t,\cdot)
is Lipschitz. Then, using the smoothness of
X(t,\cdot)
and the invertibility condition, we can verify that
Z(t,\cdot)
is differentiable everywhere. This verifies Part (
1
).

We show that for each
k\in S^{D}_{+}
there is
h
such that
X(t,h)=k
.
Let
\mathfrak{C}_{t,k}\subseteq S^{D}_{+}
denote the closed convex envelope of
\left\{k+t\nabla\mathsf{H}(\nabla\psi(h))\big|\,h\in S^{D}_{+}\right\}
and let

\Phi_{t,k}:\begin{cases}\mathfrak{C}_{t,k}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{C}_{t,k}\\
h\longmapsto k+t\nabla\mathsf{H}(\nabla\psi(h))\end{cases}.

Since
\Phi_{t,k}
is continuous and
\mathfrak{C}_{t,k}
is a compact convex set (as
\nabla\psi
is bounded), it follows from Brouwer’s fixed point theorem that
\Phi_{t,k}
admits a fixed point
h^{*}\in\mathfrak{C}_{t,k}
which satisfies
k=h^{*}-t\nabla\mathsf{H}(\nabla\psi(h^{*}))=X(t,h^{*})
. Hence, Part (
2
) is valid.
∎

Lemma
5.2
allows us to consider the restriction of
Z(t,\cdot)
to
S^{D}_{+}
. Then, we can proceed via the standard argument of characteristics to show the existence of a smooth solution for a short time.

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume (
5.1
).
Let
L
be given as in (
1.13
) and let
f
be the unique Lipschitz viscosity solution of (
1.7
) with initial condition
f(0,\cdot)=\psi
. Then, the restriction of
f
to
[0,L^{-1})\times S^{D}_{+}
is twice differentiable everywhere.

The proof is classical but we choose to include it here for completeness.

Proof.

It is sufficient to construct a twice-differentiable Lipschitz increasing solution
u
of (
1.7
) on
[0,L^{-1})\times S^{D}_{+}
with
u(0,\cdot)=\psi
. Indeed, we can conclude that
f
coincide with
u
on the prescribed domain from the uniqueness of solution on
[0,L^{-1})\times S^{D}_{+}
(see
[
22
, Corollary 3.2]
which can be easily adapted to the domain
[0,L^{-1})\times S^{D}_{+}
from
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times S^{D}_{+}
).

With this explained, we turn to the construction of
u
.
We define

\displaystyle U(t,h)=\psi(h)-t\nabla\mathsf{H}(\nabla\psi(h))\cdot\nabla\psi(h)+t\mathsf{H}(\nabla\psi(h)),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,h)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times S^{D}_{+}.

Let
Z
be given by Lemma
5.2
and we can restrict it to
[0,L^{-1})\times S^{D}_{+}
. Then, we set

\displaystyle u(t,h)=U(t,Z(t,h)),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,h)\in[0,L^{-1})\times S^{D}_{+}.

We first verify the initial condition. Since
U(0,\cdot)=\psi
and
Z(0,\cdot)
is the identity map, we get
u(0,\cdot)=\psi
as desired.

From the definition of
u
, it is clear that
u
is differentiable everywhere. Then, we verify that
u
satisfies the equation (
1.7
), the procedure of which will also show that
u
is twice-differentiable.
To carry out computations, we introduce some notation. Let
d={D}({D}-1)/2
and we fix any orthogonal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
for
S^{D}
. For any suitable function
g
, we write
g_{t}
for the derivative in
t
and
g_{i}
for the directional derivative in
h
along
e_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.

For convenience, we identify the linear inner product space
S^{D}
with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and think of
S^{D}_{+}
as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This allows us to think of the gradient of a scalar function
g
(such as
\psi
,
\mathsf{H}
,
U
, and
u
) as a column vector
\nabla g=(g_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; its Hessian
\nabla^{2}g=(g_{ij})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}
as a symmetric
d\times d
matrix; an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued function
G=(G^{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}
(such as
X
and
Y
) as a column vector; and its gradient
\nabla G=(G^{i}_{j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}
as a
d\times d
matrix. In the following, we view
\cdot
as the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and always evaluate matrix multiplication (including matrix multiplied by a vector) before evaluating the inner product.
For
(t,h)
clear from the context, we also write
\nabla g^{Z}
or
g_{t}^{Z}
to indicate that we evaluate
\nabla g
or
g_{t}
at the spatial variable
Z(t,h)
.

In the following computations, we keep
(t,h)\in[0,L^{-1})\times S^{D}_{+}
implicit.
We first compute the spatial derivative of
u
. We start with some basic computations.

\displaystyle\nabla U

\displaystyle=(U_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}=\nabla\psi-t\left(\nabla^{2}\mathsf{H}(\nabla\psi)\nabla^{2}\psi\right)^{\intercal}\nabla\psi,

\displaystyle\nabla X

\displaystyle=(X^{i}_{j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant d}=\mathbf{I}-t\nabla^{2}H(\nabla\psi)\nabla^{2}\psi,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\nabla u

\displaystyle=(\nabla Z)^{\intercal}\nabla U^{Z}.

where
\mathbf{I}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. The first two relations give

\displaystyle\nabla U=(\nabla X)^{\intercal}\nabla\psi.

This along with (
5.2
) and the fact that
Z
is the inverse of
X
implies

(5.3)

\nabla u=\nabla\psi^{Z}.

Next, we compute the derivative of
u
in
t
. We start with

\displaystyle U_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla H(\nabla\psi)\cdot\nabla\psi+H(\nabla\psi)

(5.4)

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla H(\nabla\psi),

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=U^{Z}_{t}+\nabla U^{Z}\cdot Z_{t}

Therefore,

(5.5)

u_{t}=-\nabla\mathsf{H}\left(\nabla\psi^{Z}\right)\cdot\nabla\psi^{Z}+\mathsf{H}\left(\nabla\psi^{Z}\right)+\left(\nabla X^{Z}\right)^{\intercal}\nabla\psi^{Z}\cdot Z_{t}.

Notice that the last term is of the form
A^{\intercal}v\cdot v^{\prime}
for a matrix
A
and vectors
v,v^{\prime}
. We can rearrange it into
Av^{\prime}\cdot v
.
Since
Z
is the inverse of
X
, we get

\displaystyle X^{Z}_{t}+\nabla X^{Z}Z_{t}=0.

Using this, (
5.4
) and the aforementioned rearrangement to cancel the first and third terms in (
5.5
), we get

\displaystyle u_{t}=\mathsf{H}\left(\nabla\psi^{Z}\right).

This along with (
5.3
) implies that
u
satisfies the equation (
1.7
) in the classical sense. Moreover, we can also infer from them that
u
is twice differentiable everywhere. Finally, from (
5.3
) and the monotony of
\psi
, we get that
\nabla u\in S^{D}_{+}
, so
u(t,\cdot)
is an increasing function.
∎
